Generic small-scale creation in the two-dimensional Euler equation
Thomas Alazard, Ayman Rimah Said

Abstract
. The Cauchy problem for the two-dimensional incompressible Euler equation is globally well-posed for smooth initial data. In this paper, we show that for a dense G δ G_{\delta} set of initial data, the solutions lose regularity in infinite time, thereby confirming a long-standing conjecture of Yudovich in the smooth setting.



1.
Introduction

The global well-posedness of the Cauchy problem for the two-dimensional incompressible Euler equations for smooth initial data is rooted in the transport
of the vorticity, denoted by
\omega
, by the fluid flow map.
Let
\Phi_{t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the flow generated by the velocity field.
The vorticity satisfies the Lagrangian identity

(1.1)

\omega(t,\Phi_{t}(x))=\omega_{0}(x).

This identity implies the conservation
of all Lebesgue norms
L^{p}
for
p\in[1,\infty]
. However, this conservation
law is also believed to drive
a generic growth of higher-order norms of the solution (see the discussion in §
1.2
).
The main purpose of this paper is to establish this fact. We denote by
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
(respectively
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
) the
Sobolev space of index
s
(respectively the space of smooth functions)
on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
consisting of functions with vanishing mean value.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
s>4
be a real number.
The set of initial data
\omega_{0}\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

such that the corresponding unique solution of the two-dimensional incompressible Euler equations satisfies

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\left\lVert\omega(t,\cdot)\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}=+\infty,

is a dense
G_{\delta}
set in
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

As a corollary, smooth solutions generically lose regularity in infinite time.

Corollary 1.2
.

The set of initial data
\omega_{0}
in the space
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
such that there exists an integer
k\in\mathbb{N}

such that

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\left\lVert\omega(t,\cdot)\right\rVert_{C^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}=+\infty,

is a dense
G_{\delta}
set in
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

It is worth emphasizing that Theorem
1.1
holds in a domain
without boundary and without imposing any symmetry constraints.
This contrasts with previous generic growth results,
such as those by Yudovich
[
41
]

and Morgulis, Shnirelman and Yudovich
[
31
]
,
where the growth mechanism relies on boundary
effects (specifically, fluid particles approaching stagnation points
on the boundary). Similarly, it differs from the work
of Elgindi, Murray and Said
[
18
]
,
which relies on specific symmetry classes where the point on the axis of symmetry are naturally stagnation points.

We also establish generic small-scale creation
for solutions defined on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
rather than the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
This change in geometry simplifies the
analysis. Indeed, through the scaling symmetries
of the Euler equations, spatial infinity can effectively act as a stagnation point
(at least from the perspective of the Lagrangian flow, as observed by Shankar
in
[
36
]
). We show in Section
11
that this
phenomenon induces a generic growth of the Lipschitz norm of the flow,
which in turn yields the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
s>1
be a real number. The set of initial data
\omega_{0}\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

satisfying the zero-mean condition
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega(x)\diff\!x=0
and such that

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\left\lVert\omega(t,\cdot)\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=+\infty,

is a dense
G_{\delta}
set in
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Remark 1.4
.

A closer inspection of the arguments in Section
9
reveals that the growth rate of the Sobolev norms (and even Hölder norms)
in Theorem
1.3
is bounded from below by
t^{s}
.

Remark 1.5
.

Returning to the periodic setting, the absence of spatial infinity or physical
boundaries complicates significantly the analysis. To the best of our knowledge,
Theorem
1.1
provides the first rigorous confirmation of generic
turbulent behavior for Euler equations
driven solely by the internal nonlinear dynamics
,
independent of boundary geometry or symmetry properties that inherently create
small scales.

While we cannot rule out the existence of a
hidden identity on the torus, analogous
to the one presented in Section
11
, that could simplify
the analysis of pointwise growth, we believe
that the microlocal theory
developed here offers further applications where finer information
on fluid dynamics is required. In particular those techniques can be applied in various geometrical settings (on any compact manifold and potentially higher dimensions for instance)
and for many active scalar equations (notably for the SQG equation), which will be the subjects of further works.

The proof strategy builds upon the seminal work of Shnirelman
[
37
]
,
who pioneered the use of Alinhac’s paracomposition operators to investigate the
geometry of the group of diffeomorphisms. This framework was recently revisited
by the second author
[
33
]
to construct a Lyapunov functional
detecting small-scale formation. Independently, analogous paradifferential transformations
have been used in
[
2
]
to solve small divisor problems in KAM theory.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
then relies on three ideas.
The first one is the introduction of a convenient paradifferential modification
of the flow
\Phi_{t}
that completely paralinearizes the Lagrangian dynamics and allows to identify
new structures. The second one is to probe the dynamics of vortices through
a microlocal analysis of Hölderian cusps.
The third one is the construction of a specific trajectory exhibiting unbounded
growth, obtained via a Schauder fixed point argument.
Finally, this yields a dense set of solutions blowing-up in infinite time by
adapting firstly an idea of Koch
[
27
]
, which shows that Lagrangian
stretching drives Eulerian growth, and secondly a Baire category argument introduced
by Hani
[
20
]

in the context of dispersive equations.

In the remainder of this introduction, we discuss related results and outline the proof.

1.1.
The equations

The two-dimensional incompressible Euler equation reads

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p=0,\\
&\cn u=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where
u=(u^{1},u^{2})
and
p
denote the fluid velocity and pressure, respectively.
The variables are the time
t\in\mathbb{R}
and the spatial
position
x=(x^{1},x^{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We consider bi-periodic functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, satisfying

f(x_{1}+2\pi,x_{2})=f(x_{1},x_{2}+2\pi)=f(x_{1},x_{2}).

Such functions are identified with functions defined on the
2
-torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}\coloneqq(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}
, and the
Sobolev spaces of these bi-periodic functions are denoted by
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

for
s\in\mathbb{R}
. We denote by
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
the subspace of functions
f

with mean value zero
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}f(x)\diff\!x=0
.

A key quantity in 2D fluid dynamics is the scalar vorticity, defined by

\omega\coloneqq\partial_{1}u^{2}-\partial_{2}u^{1}
.
Taking the curl of the momentum equation eliminates the pressure term
and yields a transport equation for the vorticity:

(1.3)

\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla\omega=0.

The incompressibility condition allows one to recover the velocity field

u
from
\omega
by introducing a stream function
\psi
, such that

u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi\coloneqq(-\partial_{2}\psi,\partial_{1}\psi)
.
Solving for
\psi
gives the explicit non-local relation between velocity
and vorticity, known as the Biot-Savart law (see (
2.2
) below for more details):

(1.4)

u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega.

The system formed by (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) is self-contained:
the evolution of
\omega
depends on
u
, which is itself determined by
\omega
.
Hence, equation (
1.3
) shows that vorticity is transported by the very
flow it generates.
The transport nature of (
1.3
) is most apparent from a Lagrangian perspective.
We trace the path of a fluid particle
\Phi_{t}(x)
originating at

x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
by solving the ordinary differential equation

\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}\Phi_{t}(x)=u(t,\Phi_{t}(x)),\quad\text{with}\quad\Phi_{0}(x)=x.

Equation (
1.3
) implies that vorticity is constant along these
particle trajectories, which yields
\omega(t,\Phi_{t}(x))=\omega_{0}(x)
. Consequently,
the
L^{p}
norms are conserved for all
p\in[1,\infty]
:

(1.5)

\left\lVert\omega(t,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}=\left\lVert\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{p}}.

1.2.
The Yudovich Conjecture

The conservation law (
1.5
) leaves open the possibility that the derivatives
of vorticity may grow.
This is the object of the following

Conjecture 1.6

(Yudovich (1974),
[
40
,
41
]
, quote from
[
31
]
)
.

There is a “substantial set” of inviscid incompressible flows whose
vorticity gradients grow without bound.
This set is dense enough to cause a loss of smoothness for arbitrarily
small perturbations of any steady flow.

Remark 1.7
.

This conjecture has two distinct components: one
concerning the
topological size
of the set of initial data leading
to turbulence, and the other concerning the
regularity
of the flow.
Regarding the size, our result gives a precise answer by proving that it forms a dense
G_{\delta}
set.
Regarding regularity, the challenge is twofold. The first aspect is to prove
that growth occurs regardless of smoothness, distinguishing this phenomenon
from instabilities that might be facilitated by limited regularity (such
as the
C^{1,\alpha}
framework used in the pioneering works of
Zlatoš
[
42
]
or Elgindi
[
17
]
). Our theorem
addresses this by establishing instability for all high-regularity Sobolev
spaces, which is strikingly illustrated in Corollary
1.2
.
The second aspect is to lower the regularity requirement to the
optimal threshold
s>1
or
C^{0,\alpha}
with
\alpha>0
. We solve this question (see Theorem
1.3
)
for solutions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For periodic solutions our proof extends to
s>3
,
which would require working with negative regularity cusps (see Section
5
)
and would burden
the presentation with technical difficulties.
Closing the threshold
1<s<3
remains a challenging open question.

The heuristic mechanism driving this growth is the phenomenon of
vorticity filamentation
.
We refer the reader to the recent review articles
[
14
,
25
]

for a detailed exposition on this phenomena.
The fluid flow acts to stretch and fold initially smooth patches of vorticity
into increasingly thin and complex structures.
While the magnitude of
\omega
is preserved along trajectories (see (
1.1
)),
the spatial gradient across these filaments increases inversely proportional
to their thickness.
This geometric deformation induces a transfer of energy to higher frequencies, which characterizes
turbulent behavior.

Historically, the pioneering examples of unbounded
growth for the vorticity gradient were constructed
by Bahouri and Chemin
[
5
]
in the context
of singular initial data, specifically for vortex patches with corners.
In the realm of smooth solutions, a landmark breakthrough by Kiselev and Šverák
[
26
]
established
the existence of double-exponential growth on the disk,
a result subsequently extended to other domains by Xu
[
39
]
. Building on
these advances, Zlatoš
[
42
]

demonstrated exponential growth on the torus for solutions
with limited smoothness and,
more recently
[
43
]
proved that double-exponential
growth does occur for smooth solutions in the half-plane.
Even setting aside the question of genericity,
constructing solutions with superlinear growth in a domain
without boundary remains a formidable challenge, with the notable exception
of the work by Denisov
[
13
]
.
In contrast, the regime of linear growth near
steady states and traveling waves is now well-understood thanks to the
recent breakthroughs of Nadirashvili
[
32
]
, Choi and Jeong
[
12
]
, Drivas, Elgindi
and Jeong
[
15
]
, Bedrossian and Masmoudi
[
8
]
,
Masmoudi and Zhao
[
29
]
, and Ionescu and Jia
[
23
,
24
]
.

1.3.
Strategy of the proof and plan of the paper

In this section, we focus on the proof of Theorem
1.1
, which combines paradifferential calculus with Lagrangian analysis and topological arguments. While the proof of Theorem
1.3
shares the same Lagrangian
instability mechanism (see Section
9
) as Theorem
1.1
,
the proof is much simpler, due to Shankar’s identity (which is
specific to the scaling symmetries of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
), and is done in Section
11
.

Remark on the use of paradifferential calculus

We have made a substantial effort to ensure this
paper is self-contained by employing
a streamlined version of paradifferential calculus.
We rely exclusively on elementary results
on paraproducts (recalled in Appendix
A
),
thereby bypassing the machinery of general paradifferential operators.
Notably, we adopt an elementary definition of Alinhac’s paracomposition
(see Section
3
).

Step 1: Paralinearization of the Lagrangian flow (Sections
3
and
4
)

Following the framework initiated by Shnirelman
[
37
]
, we introduce the vector field

X(t)\coloneqq T_{(D\Phi_{t})^{-1}}\Phi_{t}.

Our first observation (Proposition
4.3
) establishes that
X
satisfies a closed evolution equation of the form

\partial_{t}X+\nabla^{\perp}QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot X=\nabla^{\perp}Q\omega_{0}-\mathcal{R},

where
Q
is an explicit parametrix for the paradifferential
Laplace–Beltrami operator and
\mathcal{R}
is a smoothing remainder.

The goal: Small scale creation

Using this evolution equation, we will demonstrate that the creation of
small scales is inevitable for any initial
data possessing
finite regularity
.
The novelty here lies in the precise nature of this blow-up.
While a previous result by the second author
[
33
]

established an alternative between the blow-up of the vorticity in
H^{s}

or the flow in
H^{s+1}
(in a more general setting up to the critical regularity
s>1
),
we establish in Proposition
7.1
a sharper
alternativefor
s>4
:

(1.6)

•

Either the vorticity
\omega(t)
itself is unbounded in
H^{s}
;

•

Or the inverse flow map
\Phi_{t}^{-1}
is unbounded in
H^{s}
.

Securing the same regularity index
s
for both the vorticity and the
flow
is pivotal. Loosely speaking, the idea is the following:
since
\omega=\omega_{0}\circ\Phi^{-1}
, a
blow-up
1
1

1

Hereafter, we say that a function
t\mapsto f(t)
defined for all time
t\geq 0

blows up in a space
X
if
\limsup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert f(t)\right\rVert_{X}=+\infty
.
of
\Phi^{-1}
solely in
H^{s+1}
might remain invisible to the
H^{s}
-norm of
\omega
(if
\nabla^{s+1}\Phi^{-1}

blows up while
\nabla^{\leq s}\Phi^{-1}
remains bounded). By aligning the indices,
we ensure the possibility that the instability of the flow can be
transferred
to the vorticity.

Having outlined our objective, two problems have to be solved.

(i)

First, deriving small-scale creation requires obtaining a
quantitative lower bound on the growth of the Lagrangian unknown
X
. The
difficulty here is to find a suitable test function (which we shall identify as a
Hölderian cusp) capable of extracting growth information.

(ii)

Second, proving that this Lagrangian stretching indeed translates
into an Eulerian blow-up of the vorticity.
Indeed, making the vorticity "feel" the stretching effect of the inverse flow typically requires perturbing the initial
data. However, such a construction
would normally rely on stability estimates, which are precisely the properties
precluded by the very instability we intend to establish.

The subsequent
analysis is devoted to resolving these two difficulties by
combining microlocal analysis
with various topological arguments.

Step 2: A microlocal analysis of Hölderian cusps (Sections
5
and
6
)

To characterize the nature of the singularities arising in the Euler dynamics,
our study relies on two distinct approaches.
First, we establish an elementary result stating that generic
H^{s}

functions exhibit a “heavy-tail” property in their Fourier distribution.
More precisely, we show that, in any ball centered around an initial datum, one may find a
function
\omega_{0}
satisfying the following two properties:

(i)

There exist a constant
c>0
and an integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that,
for all
|n|\geq N
,

(1.7)

(1+|n|^{2})^{s/2}\RE\hat{\omega_{0}}(n)\geq\frac{c}{|n|\ln(|n|)}.

(ii)

\omega_{0}
attains its global infimum at a unique point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
,
and the Hessian matrix
D^{2}\omega_{0}(x_{0})
is positive definite.

This setup is complemented by a systematic study of Hölderian cusps,
which act as prototypes for non-smooth behavior. While
the techniques
themselves remain relatively standard, the novelty of
this singularity-tracking approach prompts us to outline the philosophy in detail.

The evolution equation for
\curl X
provides the starting point for a
quantitative analysis of small-scale creation. However, extracting growth
information from this identity requires a carefully chosen test function.
In a sub-critical regime, where the flow
\Phi_{t}
is assumed to be bounded
in time in
H^{s+\nu}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for some
\nu>0
, one could directly test
the equation against the initial vorticity
\omega_{0}
. At the critical
threshold
\nu=0
, handling the interaction between the flow and the initial
data demands a more refined strategy.

This motivates the introduction of Hölderian singularities, specifically
“cusps” of the form
|x|^{2\alpha}
with
\alpha>0
, to probe the dynamics.
Conceptually, we test the equation for
\curl X
against the singular profile

H\coloneqq(\omega_{0}-m)^{\alpha}
, where
m\coloneqq\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\omega_{0}
.
The choice of a cusp-like profile is dictated by its analytical plasticity: not only
does it exhibit a predictable decay in the Fourier domain, but it also yields a
direct gain of regularity when multiplied by functions vanishing
at the singularity’s core.
Specifically, the product of
|x|^{\alpha}
with any
smooth function vanishing at the origin generates a milder singularity of order
|x|^{\alpha+1}
.

Simultaneously, cusps possess a perfectly identifiable
spectral signature
.
Unlike generic functions in
H^{s}
, the frequency-domain behavior of a
power-law singularity is explicit, which allows for sharp estimates of the
Fourier tails. This dual nature (that is, tractability in physical space and
explicitness in frequency space) makes the cusp a pivotal tool for computing
interactions in both variables. By calibrating the cusp exponent
\alpha

against the initial regularity index
s
, the qualitative question of
the emergence of small scales is reduced to a concrete
analytic lower bound in Section
7
.

Step 3: A Dichotomy (Section
8
)

Having established the core alternative (
1.6
),
our next objective is to upgrade this result into a
stronger dichotomy. Specifically, we prove in Proposition
8.1
that for any
\omega_{0}
satisfying (
1.7
):

•

Either the flow map is unstable in the
H^{s}
topology (meaning that, for arbitrarily large
N
and for
any neighborhood of
\omega_{0}
, there exists a perturbed solution satisfying
\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert\omega^{\prime}(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}>N
);

•

Or the inverse flow map
\varphi_{t}=\Phi_{t}^{-1}
blows up in the Lipschitz norm
L^{\infty}
.

We proceed by contradiction. Assume that the solution remains stable in
H^{s}
(Eulerian stability)
and that the inverse flow remains uniformly Lipschitz (Lagrangian stability).
As shown in Step 2, the assumption (
1.7
) forces the
H^{s}
Sobolev norm
of the flow to grow. We exploit this Sobolev growth to derive a contradiction.

To this end, we employ a topological argument based on Schauder’s fixed point theorem.
We construct a map
\mathcal{F}
acting on the space of initial data by adding a specific
perturbation, here a wave packet tailored to resonate with the stretching of the flow. This
map is designed to enforce two conflicting properties: strict stability (the image by
\mathcal{F}

remains trapped within a small ball) and norm inflation
(the trajectory eventually escapes). The construction
ensures that the existence of a fixed point would imply the
cancellation of a specific Fourier coefficient of the perturbation.
We prove that this cancellation is impossible,
thereby disproving the stability assumption.

Step 4: Construction of a growing perturbation (Section
9
)

We are now in a position to conclude the proof of instability. We reason again by
contradiction: suppose the vorticity remains locally bounded. By the dichotomy principle (Step 3),
this forces the derivative of the inverse flow map to blow up in
L^{\infty}
.

We then exploit this Lagrangian stretching to construct
a destabilizing perturbation,
adapting an argument of Koch
[
27
]
.
Unlike the topological argument in Step 3,
this construction is explicit and geometric.
Roughly speaking, the idea is that if the Jacobian
D\varphi

of the inverse flow stretches fluid elements by a factor
\Lambda

in a direction
e
, we insert an initial perturbation
\eta_{\textrm{init}}

oscillating in the direction
e
with a frequency proportional to
1/\delta
and amplitude
\delta^{s-1}
.
By Faà di Bruno’s formula applied to the
s
-th derivative
of
\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi
, this scaling isolates the term
where all derivatives act on the profile
\eta_{\textrm{init}}
,
which generates
s
factors of
D\varphi
.
We then exploit the fact that the velocity is one
derivative more regular than the vorticity
to show the stability of
D\varphi
with
respect to such high-frequency wave packet
perturbations. Thus, we capture a
factor
\Lambda^{s}
in the growth of
\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi
,
which results in norm growth for the vorticity.

Step 5: A Baire argument

The previous steps show that for generic finite regularity
data
\omega_{0}
,
stability implies a contradiction. Thus, instability is dense by a Baire
argument adapted from a proof introduced by Hani
[
20
]

in the context of dispersive equations. Since the set of initial data
leading to blow-up in infinite time (
\limsup\left\lVert\omega(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}=+\infty
) is a
G_{\delta}

set (a countable intersection of open sets), the density of the instability
implies that it is generic in the sense of Baire category.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notations and conventions

Let us fix some definitions and notational conventions that will be used in the sequel.

Vectors and Matrices

We adopt the Einstein summation convention over repeated indices and adhere to
the following conventions regarding vector and matrix calculus.

Vectors are identified with column matrices.
The gradient operator in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, denoted by
\nabla
, transforms a scalar
function
f
into the column vector field:

\nabla f\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}f\\
\partial_{2}f\end{pmatrix}.

The perpendicular gradient

\nabla^{\perp}
is defined by:

\nabla^{\perp}f\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}-\partial_{2}f\\
\partial_{1}f\end{pmatrix}=J\nabla f,\quad\text{with}\quad J\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

The components
a^{i}_{j}
of a matrix
A
are indexed such that
i
corresponds
to the row (target component) and
j
to the column (source component).
Consequently, the Jacobian matrix
Du
of a vector field
u=(u^{1},u^{2})^{\top}

is defined by:

(Du)^{i}_{j}\coloneqq\partial_{j}u^{i}\quad\implies\quad Du=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}u^{1}&\partial_{2}u^{1}\\
\partial_{1}u^{2}&\partial_{2}u^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The transpose of a matrix is denoted by the superscript
⊤
.
The components of the transposed Jacobian are
((Du)^{\top})^{i}_{j}=\partial_{i}u^{j}
.
We will use the shorthand notation
(Du)^{-\top}
to denote the transpose
of the inverse matrix, i.e.
((Du)^{-1})^{\top}
.

Function Spaces

We work with periodic functions, defined on the
2
-torus

\mathbb{T}^{2}\coloneqq(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}.

The Sobolev spaces of these periodic functions are denoted by
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
For an index
s\in[0,+\infty)
,
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
consists
of those functions
u
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for which the following norm is finite:

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\coloneqq\biggl(\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\big(1+\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{2}\big)^{s}\left\lvert\hat{u}(\xi)\right\rvert^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
C^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
the usual space of functions on

\mathbb{T}^{2}
whose derivatives of order
\leq k
are continuous.
For a non-integer
r>0
,
C^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
denotes the space of functions whose
derivatives up to order
[r]
are bounded and uniformly Hölder continuous
with exponent
r-[r]
, where
[r]
is the integer part of
r
.
The Hölder norm is defined as

\left\lvert u\right\rvert_{C^{r}}\coloneqq\left\lvert u\right\rvert_{C^{[r]}}+\sum_{|\alpha|=[r]}\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{T}^{2},x\neq y}\frac{\left\lvert\partial^{\alpha}u(x)-\partial^{\alpha}u(y)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{r-[r]}}.

Paradifferential Calculus

We shall make extensive use of the paradifferential calculus introduced by Bony
[
9
]
.
We denote by
T_{a}u
the paraproduct of
u
by
a
and by
R_{\mathcal{B}}(a,u)
the associated remainder.
The precise definitions of the Littlewood-Paley decomposition and of these operators, along with their
main continuity properties, are recalled in Appendix
A
.

2.2.
Well-posedness and Flow Map

We consider the two-dimensional incompressible Euler equations with zero-mean vorticity on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
:

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla\omega=0,\\
u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Functional setting.

In the sequel, we denote by
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
the subspace of Sobolev functions with zero mean:

H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\coloneqq\left\{f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2}):\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}f(x)\diff\!x=0\right\}.

The Biot-Savart operator
\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}

is readily defined as a Fourier multiplier. Its action on a frequency
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
is given by the symbol

(2.2)

m(k)=-\frac{ik^{\perp}}{|k|^{2}},\quad\text{where }k^{\perp}=(-k_{2},k_{1}).

This linear operator is bounded from
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
into
H^{s+1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

The Flow Map.

Associated with the velocity field
u
, we define the flow map
\Phi
as the solution to the ordinary differential equation:

(2.3)

\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}\Phi(t,x)=u(t,\Phi(t,x)),\quad\Phi(0,x)=x.

Throughout this paper, we denote by
\Phi^{\omega_{0}}_{t}
the flow map associated with the unique
solution generated by the initial data
\omega_{0}
.
Consequently, the solution map
S(t)
, which maps the initial data to the vorticity at time
t
, satisfies the transport formula:

S(t)\omega_{0}=\omega_{0}\circ(\Phi^{\omega_{0}}_{t})^{-1}.

We recall the standard sub-critical well-posedness result
in Sobolev spaces (see e.g. Bertozzi and Majda
[
28
]
or Chemin
[
11
]
).

Theorem 2.1

(Global Well-posedness)
.

Let
s>1
. For any initial vorticity
\omega_{0}\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, there exists a unique global solution

\omega\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})).

Moreover, there exists a constant
C_{s}>0
, depending only on
s
, such that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the estimate:

(2.4)

\left\lVert S(t)\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\left\lVert\Phi^{\omega_{0}}_{t}-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}\leq C_{s}\left\lVert\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\exp\left(e^{C_{s}\left\lVert\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}|t|}\right).

Furthermore, for any fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the solution map
S(t)
and the
flow map
\omega_{0}\mapsto\Phi^{\omega_{0}}_{t}
are continuous
from
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
H^{s+1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
respectively.

3.
Alinhac’s Paracomposition Operators

A key ingredient of our proof is paradifferential
calculus. To analyze the nonlinear operation

f\circ\chi
(the composition of a function
f

with a diffeomorphism
\chi
), Alinhac introduced
[
3
,
4
]

the paracomposition operator, denoted
\chi^{*}
.
Much like Bony’s paraproduct for function multiplication,
this operator provides a paralinearization of the composition.

In this section, we introduce a simple variant of this
paracomposition operator. We then establish fundamental
identities describing its interaction with the gradient
and perpendicular gradient operators. This variant is
advantageous, as it permits an elementary derivation
of these identities and leads to simpler final formulas
due to a crucial cancellation between remainder terms.

3.1.
The Paracomposition Operator

We work with diffeomorphisms of the

2
-torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and identify such diffeomorphisms with their
lifts to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the context of incompressible hydrodynamics, we restrict
our attention to diffeomorphisms
that preserve the Lebesgue measure and are homotopically equivalent to the identity. This leads to the following
definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Consider a real number
s>2
.
We say that a map
\chi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
belongs to
\mathrm{SDiff}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

if it is a diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
admitting the decomposition

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad\chi(x)=x+\tilde{\chi}(x)\quad\text{with }\tilde{\chi}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})^{2},

and satisfies the incompressibility constraint:

(3.1)

\det(D\chi)=\det(\Id+D\tilde{\chi})=1.

Remark 3.1
.

i)
The condition (
3.1
) implies that the Jacobian determinant is strictly positive
everywhere. Since
\mathbb{T}^{2}
is a compact manifold and
\chi
is homotopic to the
identity, this local invertibility is sufficient to guarantee
that
\chi
is a global diffeomorphism.

ii)
Although some identities could be extended to more general diffeomorphisms,
we restrict our attention to this setting for the sake of simplicity.

iii)
The assumption
s>2
is the minimal requirement to ensure,
via Sobolev embedding, that
\chi-\Id\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

As in
[
1
]
we
now define the paracomposition operator, relying
solely on the concept of paraproducts
(we refer to Appendix
A
for the definition
of the paraproduct
T_{a}u
and the Bony remainder
R_{\mathcal{B}}(a,u)
).

Definition 3.2
.

Let
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
be a periodic function
and
\chi\in\mathrm{SDiff}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for some
s>2
.
The
paracomposition
of
f
by
\chi
,
denoted
\chi^{*}f
, is defined by the formula

\chi^{*}f\coloneqq f\circ\chi-T_{\nabla f\circ\chi}\cdot\chi.

This definition relies on several conventions which we clarify.

i)

The
T_{a}\Id=0
convention.

The definition is based on the convention that the
paraproduct of any function
a
with the identity
map
\Id
vanishes:

(3.2)

T_{a}\Id=0.

Since
\chi=\Id+\tilde{\chi}

where
\tilde{\chi}
is periodic,
this implies
T_{\nabla f\circ\chi}\chi=T_{\nabla f\circ\chi}(\Id+\tilde{\chi})=T_{\nabla f\circ\chi}\tilde{\chi}
. The definition is
thus equivalent to

\chi^{*}f=f\circ\chi-T_{\nabla f\circ\chi}\cdot\tilde{\chi},

a form which involves only periodic functions.

ii)

Scalar Paraproduct.

The notation
T_{\nabla f\circ\chi}\cdot\chi

stands for the component-wise contraction. In
accordance with the Einstein summation convention, its coordinate expression is:

T_{\nabla f\circ\chi}\cdot\chi\coloneqq T_{(\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi}\chi^{j}.

The subsequent analysis of
\chi^{*}f
will involve
matrix-vector operations. We thus extend the paraproduct
and remainder notations to matrices and vectors on a
component-wise basis.

•

For two matrix fields
A
(components
a^{i}_{j}
)
and
B
(components
b^{j}_{k}
),
T_{A}B
and
R_{\mathcal{B}}(A,B)

are the matrices defined by

(T_{A}B)^{i}_{k}\coloneqq T_{a^{i}_{j}}b^{j}_{k},\qquad(R_{\mathcal{B}}(A,B))^{i}_{k}\coloneqq R_{\mathcal{B}}(a^{i}_{j},b^{j}_{k}).

•

In particular, for a matrix
field
A
(components
a^{i}_{j}
) and
a column vector
y
(components
y^{j}
),

T_{A}y
and
R_{\mathcal{B}}(A,y)
are the column vectors

(T_{A}y)^{i}\coloneqq T_{a^{i}_{j}}y^{j},\qquad(R_{\mathcal{B}}(A,y))^{i}\coloneqq R_{\mathcal{B}}(a^{i}_{j},y^{j}).

•

For a row vector
w
(components
w_{i}
)
and a matrix
A
(components
a^{i}_{j}
),
T_{w}A
and
R_{\mathcal{B}}(w,A)
are the row vectors

(T_{w}A)_{j}\coloneqq T_{w_{i}}a^{i}_{j},\qquad(R_{\mathcal{B}}(w,A))_{j}\coloneqq R_{\mathcal{B}}(w_{i},a^{i}_{j}).

3.2.
Main Identities

It follows from the chain rule that

\nabla(f\circ\chi)=(D\chi)^{\top}(\nabla f)\circ\chi,

recalling our convention

((D\chi)^{\top})^{i}_{j}=\partial_{i}\chi^{j}
.
Our first main identity establishes a similar
commutation rule between the gradient operator
and the paracomposition operator
\chi^{*}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
\chi\in\mathrm{SDiff}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for some
s>2
.
The following identity holds:

(3.3)

\nabla(\chi^{*}f)=T_{(D\chi)^{\top}}(\chi^{*}(\nabla f))+R_{1}+R_{2}

where

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\chi)^{\top},(\nabla f)\circ\chi),

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(T_{(D\chi)^{\top}}T_{(D\nabla f)\circ\chi}-T_{(D\chi)^{\top}((D\nabla f)\circ\chi)}\right)\chi.

Remark 3.3
.

The terms
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are smoothing remainders
in paradifferential calculus. To clarify the notation,
let us rewrite them in terms of components:

\displaystyle(R_{1})^{i}

\displaystyle=R_{\mathcal{B}}(\partial_{i}\chi^{j},(\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi),

\displaystyle(R_{2})^{i}

\displaystyle=T_{\partial_{i}\chi^{j}}T_{(\partial_{\ell}\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi}\chi^{\ell}-T_{\partial_{i}\chi^{j}((\partial_{\ell}\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi)}\chi^{\ell}.

Note that
(D\nabla f)
corresponds to the Hessian matrix of
f
, consistent with our matrix-vector multiplication rules.

Proof.

To simplify notations, we work in coordinates.
The argument proceeds by direct differentiation
of
\chi^{*}f\coloneqq f\circ\chi-T_{\nabla f\circ\chi}\cdot\chi
.
Applying the chain rule, the partial derivatives
of
f\circ\chi
are given by

\partial_{i}(f\circ\chi)=\partial_{i}\chi^{j}(\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi.

We then use Bony’s decomposition to write

\partial_{i}\chi^{j}(\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi=T_{\partial_{i}\chi^{j}}(\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi+T_{(\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi}\partial_{i}\chi^{j}+R_{\mathcal{B}}(\partial_{i}\chi^{j},(\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi).

We now compute the partial derivatives of the
second term
T_{\nabla f\circ\chi}\cdot\chi
. Since
\partial(T_{a}u)=T_{\partial a}u+T_{a}\partial u
,
we have

\partial_{i}(T_{\partial_{j}f\circ\chi}\chi^{j})=T_{(\partial_{i}\chi^{\ell}(\partial_{j}\partial_{\ell}f)\circ\chi)}\chi^{j}+T_{\partial_{j}f\circ\chi}\partial_{i}\chi^{j}.

By combining these expressions, the terms

T_{\partial_{j}f\circ\chi}\partial_{i}\chi^{j}
cancel, which leads to the intermediate identity:

\partial_{i}(\chi^{*}f)=T_{\partial_{i}\chi^{j}}(\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi+R_{\mathcal{B}}(\partial_{i}\chi^{j},(\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi)-T_{(\partial_{i}\chi^{\ell}(\partial_{j}\partial_{\ell}f)\circ\chi)}\chi^{j}.

Then, by applying the definition of
\chi^{*}(\partial_{j}f)
to the first term, we may rewrite it as

T_{\partial_{i}\chi^{j}}(\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi=T_{\partial_{i}\chi^{j}}\chi^{*}(\partial_{j}f)+T_{\partial_{i}\chi^{j}}T_{(\partial_{\ell}\partial_{j}f)\circ\chi}\chi^{\ell},

from which we deduce the desired result (
3.3
).
∎

Building on the previous result, we now derive
the corresponding identity for the perpendicular
gradient
\nabla^{\perp}
. This plays an essential
role in our analysis, as the Biot-Savart law
relates the velocity
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
to
the vorticity
\omega
via
\Delta\psi=\omega
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
\chi\in\mathrm{SDiff}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for some
s>2
.
The following identity holds:

(3.4)

\nabla^{\perp}(\chi^{*}f)=T_{(D\chi)^{-1}}(\chi^{*}\nabla^{\perp}f)+R_{1}^{\prime}+R_{2}^{\prime},

where

\displaystyle R_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\chi)^{-1},\nabla^{\perp}f\circ\chi),

\displaystyle R_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(T_{(D\chi)^{-1}}T_{(D\nabla^{\perp}f)\circ\chi}-T_{(D\chi)^{-1}(D\nabla^{\perp}f)\circ\chi}\right)\chi.

Proof.

On applying the matrix
J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\phantom{-}0\end{smallmatrix}\right)

to the identity (
3.3
) from Lemma
3.2
, we get

\displaystyle\nabla^{\perp}(\chi^{*}f)=J\nabla(\chi^{*}f)=JT_{(D\chi)^{\top}}(\chi^{*}\nabla f)+JR_{1}+JR_{2}.

The proof proceeds by transforming each of the three
terms on the right-hand side. The key tool is the algebraic
identity valid for any
2\times 2
matrix
A
with
\det(A)=1
:

JA^{\top}=A^{-1}J.

Since
J
is a constant matrix, it commutes with the
frequency localization operators used in the paraproduct.
Consequently, the algebraic identity implies the corresponding
operator identities:

(3.5)

JT_{A^{\top}}=T_{A^{-1}}J\quad\text{and}\quad JR_{\mathcal{B}}(A^{\top},\cdot)=R_{\mathcal{B}}(A^{-1}J,\cdot).

Using this rule with
A=D\chi
, the transformation
of the main term is straightforward. Since
J
is
constant, we have
J(\chi^{*}\nabla f)=\chi^{*}(J\nabla f)=\chi^{*}\nabla^{\perp}f
. Thus:

JT_{(D\chi)^{\top}}(\chi^{*}\nabla f)=T_{(D\chi)^{-1}}J(\chi^{*}\nabla f)=T_{(D\chi)^{-1}}(\chi^{*}\nabla^{\perp}f).

Similarly, we transform the remainder
R_{1}
.
The identity (
3.5
) gives

\displaystyle JR_{1}

\displaystyle=JR_{\mathcal{B}}((D\chi)^{\top},(\nabla f)\circ\chi)

\displaystyle=R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\chi)^{-1}J,(\nabla f)\circ\chi).

Since the Bony remainder is defined component-wise,
we may use the obvious identity
(AJ)v=A(Jv)
for any
matrix
A
and any vector
v
, to write

\displaystyle JR_{1}

\displaystyle=R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\chi)^{-1},J((\nabla f)\circ\chi))

\displaystyle=R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\chi)^{-1},(\nabla^{\perp}f)\circ\chi)=R_{1}^{\prime}.

It remains to prove that
JR_{2}=R_{2}^{\prime}
. Recall from Lemma
3.2
that

R_{2}=\left(T_{(D\chi)^{\top}}T_{(D\nabla f)\circ\chi}-T_{(D\chi)^{\top}((D\nabla f)\circ\chi)}\right)\chi.

We transform the two parts of
R_{2}
separately.
First, for the composition of operators, we use (
3.5
)
to get

\displaystyle JT_{(D\chi)^{\top}}T_{(D\nabla f)\circ\chi}\chi

\displaystyle=T_{(D\chi)^{-1}}JT_{(D\nabla f)\circ\chi}\chi

\displaystyle=T_{(D\chi)^{-1}}T_{J(D\nabla f)\circ\chi}\chi

\displaystyle=T_{(D\chi)^{-1}}T_{(D\nabla^{\perp}f)\circ\chi}\chi,

where we used the identity
J(D\nabla f)=D(J\nabla f)=D(\nabla^{\perp}f)
.

Second, for the term involving the
product of symbols, we use the algebraic identity

A^{\top}J=JA^{-1}
(which is equivalent to

JA^{\top}=A^{-1}J
by taking the inverse on both sides
and using
J^{-1}=-J
). Using
J^{2}=-\Id
, this allows
us to rewrite the symbol of the second part of
R_{2}
:

\displaystyle(D\chi)^{\top}(D\nabla f\circ\chi)

\displaystyle=-(D\chi)^{\top}J^{2}(D\nabla f\circ\chi)

\displaystyle=-(D\chi)^{\top}J(D\nabla^{\perp}f\circ\chi)

\displaystyle=-J(D\chi)^{-1}(D\nabla^{\perp}f\circ\chi).

Applying the operator
J
to the paraproduct with this symbol
yields:

JT_{(D\chi)^{\top}(D\nabla f)\circ\chi}\chi=T_{-J^{2}(D\chi)^{-1}(D\nabla^{\perp}f)\circ\chi}\chi=T_{(D\chi)^{-1}(D\nabla^{\perp}f)\circ\chi}\chi.

Subtracting the second transformed part from the
first confirms that
JR_{2}=R_{2}^{\prime}
, which completes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Paracomposition of the Laplacian

Our next goal is to express
\chi^{*}(\Delta f)
in terms
of
\chi^{*}f
. To do so,
we first recall the pullback of the Laplacian by
\chi
.
This operator, denoted by
\Delta^{\chi}
, is defined by the identity:

\Delta^{\chi}(f\circ\chi)=(\Delta f)\circ\chi.

It is the Laplace–Beltrami operator associated with the pullback metric

g\coloneqq(D\chi)^{\top}(D\chi)
.
In coordinates,
\Delta^{\chi}
is given by the general formula

\Delta^{\chi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_{i}\left(\sqrt{|g|}\,g^{ij}\partial_{j}\right),

where
|g|\coloneqq\det(g)
and
g^{ij}
are the entries of the inverse metric
matrix
g^{-1}=(D\chi)^{-1}(D\chi)^{-\top}
.
Assuming that
\det(D\chi)=1
, it follows that
|g|=1
, and hence this formula
simplifies to the divergence form:

\Delta^{\chi}v=\cn\left(g^{-1}\nabla v\right)=\partial_{i}\left(g^{ij}\partial_{j}v\right).

This motivates the following definition of the paradifferential Laplace–Beltrami operator.

Definition 3.3
.

Consider a
C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
diffeomorphism
\chi
satisfying
\det(D\chi)=1
.
We define the paradifferential operator
\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
by:

\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}\coloneqq\cn\big(T_{(D\chi)^{-1}(D\chi)^{-\top}}\nabla\cdot\big).

The following proposition asserts that
\chi^{*}

conjugates the paradifferential
Laplacian to the standard Laplacian, up to a
remainder that is as regularizing
as the smoothness of
\chi
permits.

Proposition 3.5
.

Consider two real numbers
s>2
and
\mu\in(3,s+3]
. Let
f\in H^{\mu}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

and let
\chi\in\mathrm{SDiff}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
be an
H^{s}
-diffeomorphism satisfying
\det(D\chi)=1
.
Then, the following identity holds:

\chi^{*}(\Delta f)=\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}(\chi^{*}f)+R_{\Delta}(\chi,f),

where the remainder term
R_{\Delta}(\chi,f)
satisfies the following estimate:

(3.6)

\left\lVert R_{\Delta}(\chi,f)\right\rVert_{H^{\mu+s-4}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert\chi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{\mu}},

for some function
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
depending only on
\mu
and
s
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in three steps. We first derive an algebraic identity for
the conjugated Laplacian. We then identify the principal part with the
divergence form operator
\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
. Finally, we establish the required
regularity estimates for the remainder.

Step 1: Algebraic identity.

We begin by expressing
\chi^{*}(\nabla f)
in terms of
\nabla(\chi^{*}f)
.
Recall the identity from Lemma
3.2
:

\nabla(\chi^{*}f)=T_{(D\chi)^{\top}}(\chi^{*}(\nabla f))+R_{1}+R_{2}
.
Although the operator
T_{(D\chi)^{\top}}
is not strictly invertible, it admits
an approximate inverse
T_{(D\chi)^{-\top}}
. Indeed, since the symbol

(D\chi)^{\top}
belongs to
C^{r-1}
with
r=s-1>1
, the composition calculus (Theorem
A.3
)
ensures that
T_{(D\chi)^{-\top}}T_{(D\chi)^{\top}}=\Id+\mathcal{E}
,
where the error
\mathcal{E}
is smoothing of order
-(r-1)
.
This yields the expression:

(3.7)

\chi^{*}(\nabla f)=T_{(D\chi)^{-\top}}\nabla(\chi^{*}f)+r(f),

where the term
r(f)
collects all regularizing remainders:

(3.8)

r(f)\coloneqq-T_{(D\chi)^{-\top}}(R_{1}+R_{2})+(\Id-T_{(D\chi)^{-\top}}T_{(D\chi)^{\top}})\chi^{*}(\nabla f).

Since
\det(D\chi)=1
, we have

(D\chi)^{-\top}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{2}\chi^{2}&-\partial_{1}\chi^{2}\\
-\partial_{2}\chi^{1}&\partial_{1}\chi^{1}\end{pmatrix}.

Consequently, the paradifferential operator
D\coloneqq T_{(D\chi)^{-\top}}\nabla

can be written as a vector of operators
D=(D_{1},D_{2})^{\top}
defined by:

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq T_{\partial_{2}\chi^{2}}\partial_{1}-T_{\partial_{1}\chi^{2}}\partial_{2},

\displaystyle D_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-T_{\partial_{2}\chi^{1}}\partial_{1}+T_{\partial_{1}\chi^{1}}\partial_{2}.

Denoting the components of the remainder by
r(f)=(r_{1}(f),r_{2}(f))^{\top}
,
equation (
3.7
) reads component-wise as

\chi^{*}(\partial_{i}f)=D_{i}(\chi^{*}f)+r_{i}(f)
for
i=1,2
.
We now iterate these identities to compute
\chi^{*}(\Delta f)
.
Applying the decomposition to
g=\partial_{1}f
yields:

\displaystyle\chi^{*}(\partial_{1}^{2}f)=\chi^{*}(\partial_{1}g)

\displaystyle=D_{1}(\chi^{*}g)+r_{1}(g)

\displaystyle=D_{1}(D_{1}(\chi^{*}f)+r_{1}(f))+r_{1}(\partial_{1}f)

\displaystyle=D_{1}^{2}(\chi^{*}f)+D_{1}(r_{1}(f))+r_{1}(\partial_{1}f).

A similar identity holds for
\chi^{*}(\partial_{2}^{2}f)
. Summing the two, we obtain

\chi^{*}(\Delta f)=(D_{1}^{2}+D_{2}^{2})(\chi^{*}f)+R_{\triangle}(\chi,f),

where the total remainder is given by

R_{\triangle}(\chi,f)\coloneqq D_{1}(r_{1}(f))+r_{1}(\partial_{1}f)+D_{2}(r_{2}(f))+r_{2}(\partial_{2}f).

Step 2: Geometric equivalence.

We now verify that the operator
P\coloneqq D_{1}^{2}+D_{2}^{2}
coincides, up to
smoothing terms, with
\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
. Let us examine the structure of
D_{1}
.
Using the Leibniz rule
\partial_{i}T_{a}=T_{a}\partial_{i}+T_{\partial_{i}a}
,
we rewrite it in divergence form:

D_{1}=\partial_{1}T_{\partial_{2}\chi^{2}}-\partial_{2}T_{\partial_{1}\chi^{2}}-(T_{\partial_{1}\partial_{2}\chi^{2}}-T_{\partial_{2}\partial_{1}\chi^{2}}).

The zero-order term vanishes identically thanks to Schwarz’s theorem.
Thus,
D_{1}
acts as a divergence. Specifically, if we define the vector
fields
V_{1}\coloneqq(\partial_{2}\chi^{2},-\partial_{1}\chi^{2})^{\top}
and

V_{2}\coloneqq(-\partial_{2}\chi^{1},\partial_{1}\chi^{1})^{\top}
, we have

D_{k}=\cn(T_{V_{k}}\cdot)
for
k=1,2
.
Notice that
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
correspond exactly to the columns of
(D\chi)^{-1}
.
It follows that the sum of squares becomes:

\displaystyle D_{1}^{2}+D_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\cn\left(T_{V_{1}}(T_{V_{1}}\cdot\nabla)+T_{V_{2}}(T_{V_{2}}\cdot\nabla)\right)

\displaystyle=\cn\left((T_{V_{1}}T_{V_{1}}^{\top}+T_{V_{2}}T_{V_{2}}^{\top})\nabla\right).

Since the rows of
(D\chi)^{-\top}
are
V_{1}^{\top}
and
V_{2}^{\top}
, the
matrix product satisfies

T_{(D\chi)^{-1}}T_{(D\chi)^{-\top}}=T_{V_{1}}T_{V_{1}}^{\top}+T_{V_{2}}T_{V_{2}}^{\top}.

Using the composition calculus (Theorem
A.3
) to replace the
product of paraproducts with the paraproduct of the product (up to a
smoothing error of order
-(r-1)
whose operator norm is bounded
by the right-hand side of (
3.6
)), we conclude that
D_{1}^{2}+D_{2}^{2}

is equivalent to
\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
.

Step 3: Estimates.

We finally address the regularity of
R_{\triangle}(\chi,f)
. We first establish a
bound for the auxiliary remainder
r(g)
as defined by (
3.8
).

Claim.

Let
\rho\in[2,s+2]
be a real number.
For any
g\in H^{\rho}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, the remainder
r(g)
belongs to
H^{\rho+s-3}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

and satisfies

\left\lVert r(g)\right\rVert_{H^{\rho+s-3}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert\chi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{\rho}}.

Proof of the claim.

The term
R_{1}(g)=R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\chi)^{\top},(\nabla g)\circ\chi)

involves
(D\chi)^{\top}\in H^{s-1}

and
(\nabla g)\circ\chi\in H^{s-2}
(see Theorem
B.1

and Proposition
B.2
). Applying the Bony remainder
estimate (Theorem
A.2
), which maps
H^{s-1}\times H^{\rho-1}

to
H^{s+\rho-3}
, we obtain:

R_{1}\in H^{\rho+s-3},\quad\text{with}\quad\left\lVert R_{1}\right\rVert_{H^{\rho+s-3}}\lesssim\left\lVert\chi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{\rho}}.

We now estimate
the term

R_{2}(g)=\left(T_{(D\chi)^{\top}}T_{(D\nabla g)\circ\chi}-T_{(D\chi)^{\top}((D\nabla g)\circ\chi)}\right)\chi.

Firstly, we use the fact that a paraproduct by a function in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is an operator of order
1
(see (
A.5
)) to get

\displaystyle\left\lVert R_{2}(g)\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\big\lVert T_{(D\chi)^{\top}}T_{(D\nabla g)\circ\chi}\chi\big\rVert_{H^{s-1}}+\big\lVert T_{(D\chi)^{\top}((D\nabla g)\circ\chi)}\chi\big\rVert_{H^{s-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\big\lVert(D\chi)^{\top}\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert(D\nabla g)\circ\chi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\big\lVert(D\chi)^{\top}((D\nabla g)\circ\chi)\big\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\left\lVert\chi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert\chi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{2}}.

On the other hand, consider a real number
3<\mu\leq s+2
and
g\in H^{\mu}
.
Then
\partial^{2}g\circ\chi
belongs to
H^{\mu-2}\subset C^{\mu-3}
. Then,
according to the composition calculus for
paraproducts (see Theorem
A.3
), the remainder
R_{2}(g)
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert R_{2}(g)\right\rVert_{H^{s+\mu-3}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big\lVert(D\chi)^{\top}\big\rVert_{C^{\mu-3}}\left\lVert D\nabla g\circ\chi\right\rVert_{C^{\mu-3}}\left\lVert\chi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert\chi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{\mu}}.

By interpolating the two previous inequalities we have

\forall\rho\in[2,s+2],\quad\left\lVert R_{2}(g)\right\rVert_{H^{s+\rho-3}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert\chi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{\rho}}.

Finally, using again Theorem
A.3
, we estimate the inversion error

(\Id-T_{(D\chi)^{-\top}}T_{(D\chi)^{\top}})\chi^{*}(\nabla g)

as the action of a smoothing operator of order
-(s-2)
to a function in
H^{\rho-1}
,
resulting in regularity
H^{\rho+s-3}
. This proves the claim.

We now apply this claim to the terms composing
R_{\triangle}(\chi,f)
with
f\in H^{\mu}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
where
\mu\in(3,s+3]
.

•

For the terms
r_{i}(\partial_{i}f)
, we set
g=\partial_{i}f
which
belongs to
H^{\rho}
with
\rho=\mu-1\in(2,s+2]
. Then, we apply
the claim to deduce that
r(\partial_{i}f)\in H^{(\mu-1)+s-3}=H^{\mu+s-4}
.

•

For the terms
D_{i}(r_{i}(f))
, we set
g=f\in H^{\mu}
. The claim implies

r(f)\in H^{\mu+s-3}
. Since
D_{i}
is a paradifferential operator
of order 1, it maps
H^{\mu+s-3}
to
H^{\mu+s-4}
.

Combining these estimates, we conclude that
R_{\triangle}(\chi,f)\in H^{\mu+s-4}
.
∎

3.4.
Parametrix Construction

We seek a parametrix (that is, an approximate inverse)
for the operator
\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
. Instead of using the general
symbolic calculus of para-differential operators, we present
here a self-contained construction based on the specific
structure of homogeneous symbols in dimension
2
. This
construction is simpler and allows us to obtain sharp results
in terms of regularity.

3.4.1.
Preliminaries

Before proceeding, we recall the definition of the order
of an operator in the Sobolev scale.

Definition 3.4

(Operator of order
m
)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{R}
. A linear operator
T

is said to be of
order
m
if,
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
, it defines a bounded
operator from
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to
H^{s-m}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

We also introduce the Beurling transform,
which serves as the elementary building block
for our parametrix. Following
classical definitions in complex analysis
(see e.g.
[
7
]
), this transform
is a singular integral operator which acts as the
complex analogue of the Hilbert transform.
In the periodic setting, it is most easily defined as a Fourier multiplier.

Definition 3.5
.

The Beurling transform
B

is the Fourier multiplier with symbol:

\sigma(B)(\xi)=\frac{(\xi_{1}+i\xi_{2})^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}}=e^{2i\theta},\quad\text{where }\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\text{ and }\theta=\arg(\xi).

It can be expressed via the classical Riesz transforms
R_{j}
(the Fourier multipliers with symbols
-i\xi_{j}/|\xi|
) as:

B=R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}-2iR_{1}R_{2}.

Since
|\sigma(B)(\xi)|=1
,
the operator
B
defines an isometry on all Sobolev spaces.

3.4.2.
The Elliptic Parametrix

We can now construct the parametrix for
\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
.

Lemma 3.6

(Elliptic Parametrix)
.

Assume
\chi\in\mathrm{SDiff}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for some
s>2
. There exists a sequence of bi-periodic functions
(m_{k}(x))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}

belonging to
H^{s-2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
such that the operator

Q\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{m_{k}}B^{k}\Delta^{-1}

is a parametrix for
\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
. Specifically, we have

Q\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}=\Id+S,

where
S
is a regularizing operator of order
-\min\{s-2,1\}
.
Explicitly, for any
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a function
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that:

\left\lVert Sf\right\rVert_{H^{\sigma+\min\{s-2,1\}}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert\chi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{\sigma}}.

Moreover, there exists
\delta^{\prime}>0
such that the coefficients decay exponentially:

(3.9)

\left\lVert m_{k}\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}\leq Ce^{-\delta^{\prime}|k|}\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert\chi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}}),

which ensures the absolute convergence of the series in the operator topology.

Proof.

The proof is decomposed into three steps.

1. Symbol Analysis

The principal symbol of
\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
is
the quadratic form
p(x,\xi)=\big|(D\chi(x))^{-\top}\xi\big|^{2}
. Let us denote the
matrix
M(x)=(D\chi)^{-1}(D\chi)^{-\top}
. We can write

p(x,\xi)=\langle M(x)\xi,\xi\rangle_{L^{2}}=M_{11}\xi_{1}^{2}+2M_{12}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}+M_{22}\xi_{2}^{2}.

We introduce the angular profile
\tilde{p}(x,\theta)
defined by restriction to the circle:
in polar coordinates (
\xi_{1}=|\xi|\cos\theta,\xi_{2}=|\xi|\sin\theta
),
we have
p(x,\xi)=|\xi|^{2}\tilde{p}(x,\theta)
with:

\tilde{p}(x,\theta)=\frac{M_{11}+M_{22}}{2}+\frac{M_{11}-M_{22}}{2}\cos(2\theta)+M_{12}\sin(2\theta).

This formula shows that
\theta\mapsto\tilde{p}(x,\theta)
is a trigonometric polynomial of degree 2, which is
\pi
-periodic (and not only
2\pi
-periodic).

Moreover, since
\chi
is a diffeomorphism,
there exists a constant
c>0
such that
\tilde{p}(x,\theta)\geq c
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and for
all
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We define the symbol of the parametrix as the inverse of this profile:

m(x,\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tilde{p}(x,\theta)}.

By construction, the function
\theta\mapsto m(x,\theta)
is real-analytic and
\pi
-periodic.
Consequently, its Fourier series only involves even frequencies. We write:

m(x,\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}m_{k}(x)e^{2ik\theta},\quad\text{where }m_{k}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}m(x,\theta)e^{-2ik\theta}\diff\theta.

Standard results on analytic periodic functions
imply the exponential decay of the coefficients.
The regularity in
x
follows from the
H^{s-1}

regularity of the metric coefficients
M(x)

(since
D\chi\in H^{s-1}
) and standard nonlinear
estimates (see Theorem
B.1
). Which gives the
desired estimate (
3.9
).

2. Definition of the approximate inverse

We define the operator
Q
by quantizing this series. Observe
that the symbol of
B
satisfies
\sigma(B)(\xi)=e^{2i\theta}
.
We identify the term
e^{2ik\theta}
with the iterate
B^{k}
and set

Q\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{m_{k}}B^{k}\Delta^{-1}.

Since
\sigma(B)
has modulus 1,
B
is an
isometry on Sobolev spaces (
\|B^{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H^{\sigma})}=1
).
The absolute convergence of the series for
Q
thus follows
directly from the exponential decay of
\left\lVert m_{k}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}

and the operator norm estimate for paraproducts (cf Theorem
A.1
) implies that
Q
is of order
-2
.

3. Remainder Estimate

We explicitly compute the composition
Q\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
to isolate the remainder.
To do this, first, we decompose
\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
using the angular Fourier expansion
of its symbol. Recall that
\tilde{p}(x,\theta)

is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
2
.
We can write it as
\tilde{p}(x,\theta)=\sum_{j=-1}^{1}p_{j}(x)e^{2ij\theta}
.
By definition of the quantization, we have:

\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}=\left(\sum_{j=-1}^{1}T_{p_{j}}B^{j}\right)\Delta+R_{\text{lin}},

where
R_{\text{lin}}=T_{\partial_{i}M_{ij}}\partial_{j}
corresponds
to the sub-principal term. By using the estimate (
A.6
) with
s_{1}=s-2>0
,
we verify that this operator satisfies

\left\lVert R_{\text{lin}}f\right\rVert_{H^{\sigma}}\lesssim\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{\sigma+1+\max\{3-s,0\}}}.

We now apply
Q=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{m_{k}}B^{k}\Delta^{-1}
to this decomposition:

Q\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=-1}^{1}T_{m_{k}}B^{k}\Delta^{-1}T_{p_{j}}B^{j}\Delta+QR_{\text{lin}}.

Our aim is to show that the
remainder
Q\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}-\Id

is of order
\min\{s-2,1\}
. Notice that, since
Q
is
of order
-2
,
the term
QR_{\text{lin}}
is clearly
of order
-1+\max\{3-s,0\}
(regularizing), so we need only to focus on the double sum.
We commute the operator
B^{k}\Delta^{-1}
(which is of order
-2
) directly with the paraproduct
T_{p_{j}}
. We write:

B^{k}\Delta^{-1}T_{p_{j}}=T_{p_{j}}B^{k}\Delta^{-1}+[B^{k}\Delta^{-1},T_{p_{j}}].

The commutator
[B^{k}\Delta^{-1},T_{p_{j}}]
is of order
-2-\min\{s-2,1\}
with an operator norm that grows at
most polynomially in
k
(due to the derivatives of the symbol
e^{2ik\theta}
).
Indeed, according to the commutator estimate
(Theorem
A.5
in Appendix),

(3.10)

\left\lVert[B^{k}\Delta^{-1},T_{p_{j}}]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(H^{\sigma},H^{\sigma+2+\min\{s-2,1\}})}\leq P(k)\left\lVert p_{j}\right\rVert_{C^{s-1}},

for some polynomial
P
. Therefore, the term involving this commutator, once
composed with
\Delta
(order 2), is of order
-\min\{s-2,1\}
.
For the principal part, we use the fact that the Fourier multipliers
commute to write
B^{k}\Delta^{-1}B^{j}\Delta=B^{k+j}
.

Thus, up to order
-1
remainders, the general term simplifies to
T_{m_{k}}T_{p_{j}}B^{k+j}
.
Using the composition rule for paraproducts (Theorem
A.3
in Appendix),
we have
T_{m_{k}}T_{p_{j}}=T_{m_{k}p_{j}}+R_{\text{alg}}(m_{k},p_{j})
, where the remainder is of order
-(s-1)
, satisfying

(3.11)

\left\lVert R_{\text{alg}}(m_{k},p_{j})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(H^{\sigma},H^{\sigma+s-1})}\lesssim\left\lVert m_{k}\right\rVert_{C^{s-2}}\left\lVert p_{j}\right\rVert_{C^{s-1}}.

Substituting these expansions back into the sum and re-indexing with
n=k+j
, we obtain:

Q\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{c_{n}}B^{n}+R_{\text{total}}\quad\text{where}\quad c_{n}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{j=-1}^{1}m_{n-j}(x)p_{j}(x).

The remainder
R_{\text{total}}
collects all the error terms.

Now the exponential decay of the coefficients
m_{k}

(see (
3.9
)) compensates for the polynomial
factor
P(k)
in (
3.10
) and controls the other error terms. This
ensures that the series defining
R_{\text{total}}
converges absolutely in the operator
norm
\mathcal{L}(H^{\sigma},H^{\sigma+\min\{s-2,1\}})
.

To conclude the proof, it remains only to prove that

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{c_{n}}B^{n}=\Id.

To see this, notice that
c_{n}(x)
is exactly the
n
-th
Fourier coefficient of the product
m(x,\theta)\tilde{p}(x,\theta)

(relative to the variable
2\theta
).
Since
m=1/\tilde{p}
by construction, this product is identically equal
to
1
. Therefore, its Fourier coefficients are
c_{0}(x)=1
and
c_{n}(x)=0
for
n\neq 0
. The proof is complete.
∎

4.
The vector field behind Shnirelman’s unknown

Following the approach of Shnirelman
[
37
]
, our analysis
centers on the vector field
2
2

2

We may clarify the interpretation of
X
as follows.
Recall the identity
\Phi^{-1}\circ\Phi=\Id
. Applying the paracomposition definition to
the vector field
\Phi^{-1}
yields

\Phi^{*}(\Phi^{-1})+T_{D(\Phi^{-1})\circ\Phi}\Phi=\Id.

By the chain rule,
D(\Phi^{-1})\circ\Phi=(D\Phi)^{-1}
. Substituting this gives the identity

X=\Id-\Phi^{*}(\Phi^{-1})
.

X\coloneqq T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}\Phi.

While related works
[
37
,
34
]

focus on the properties of
\curl X
, a finer analysis
of the full vector field
X
is required here. Our main
new observation is that the evolution equation simplifies
significantly when the velocity field has the specific structure
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The evolution equation for
X=T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}\Phi
simplifies to

\partial_{t}X=\nabla^{\perp}(\Phi^{*}\psi)-R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\Phi)^{-1},u\circ\Phi).

Remark 4.2
.

Two remarks are in order.

(i)
The fact that the remainder admits such a simple and
compact form is due to a cancellation inherent
to our definition of the paracomposition operator.

(ii)
It is worth noting that this remainder
exhibits a significant gain in regularity compared to the main term.
Specifically, while the main term is in
H^{s+1}
provided that
\psi\in H^{s+2}

and
\Phi
is Lipchitz, the remainder belongs to
H^{2s}
, thus possessing nearly double the regularity.

Proof.

The proof relies on two steps. First, we derive the general evolution equation for
X
.
Second, we apply the specific structure
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
to reveal the cancellation.

Step 1: General evolution equation.

This step is parallel to the analysis in
[
37
,
34
]
.
We show that the dynamics of
X
is governed by
the para-pulled-back velocity,
T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}(\Phi^{*}u)
, up to a smoothing remainder.
We begin by applying the product rule for differentiation:

(4.1)

\partial_{t}X=\partial_{t}\left(T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}\Phi\right)=T_{\partial_{t}(D\Phi)^{-1}}\Phi+T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}(\partial_{t}\Phi).

We analyze each term on the right-hand side of (
4.1
).
For the first term, we compute
\partial_{t}(D\Phi)^{-1}
. Using the
identity
\partial_{t}(A^{-1})=-A^{-1}(\partial_{t}A)A^{-1}
with
A=D\Phi

and
\partial_{t}\Phi=u\circ\Phi
, we have

\partial_{t}(D\Phi)^{-1}=-(D\Phi)^{-1}D(\partial_{t}\Phi)(D\Phi)^{-1}=-(D\Phi)^{-1}D(u\circ\Phi)(D\Phi)^{-1}.

Since
D(u\circ\Phi)=(Du\circ\Phi)D\Phi
, this simplifies to

\partial_{t}(D\Phi)^{-1}=-(D\Phi)^{-1}(Du\circ\Phi).

The first term on the right-hand side of (
4.1
) thus becomes
-T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}Du\circ\Phi}\Phi
.

For the second term on the right-hand side of (
4.1
), we use
\partial_{t}\Phi=u\circ\Phi

and apply the paracomposition definition
u\circ\Phi=\Phi^{*}u+T_{Du\circ\Phi}\Phi
to write

\displaystyle T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}(\partial_{t}\Phi)

\displaystyle=T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}(u\circ\Phi)

\displaystyle=T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}(\Phi^{*}u+T_{Du\circ\Phi}\Phi)

\displaystyle=T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}(\Phi^{*}u)+T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}T_{Du\circ\Phi}\Phi.

Combining these results in (
4.1
), we find

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}\Phi\right)

\displaystyle=-T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}Du\circ\Phi}\Phi+T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}(\Phi^{*}u)+T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}T_{Du\circ\Phi}\Phi

\displaystyle=T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}(\Phi^{*}u)+R_{C}(u,\Phi),

where
R_{C}(u,\Phi)\coloneqq\left(T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}T_{Du\circ\Phi}-T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}Du\circ\Phi}\right)\Phi
.
This concludes the first step.

Step 2: Simplification for
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
.

We have proved that

(4.2)

\partial_{t}X=T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}(\Phi^{*}u)+R_{C}(u,\Phi).

We now apply Lemma
3.4
(with
\chi=\Phi
and
f=\psi
) to rewrite the first term on the right-hand side.
Rearranging (
3.4
) gives:

T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}(\Phi^{*}\nabla^{\perp}\psi)=\nabla^{\perp}(\Phi^{*}\psi)-R_{1}^{\prime}-R_{2}^{\prime},

where
R_{1}^{\prime}=R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\Phi)^{-1},\nabla^{\perp}\psi\circ\Phi)
and
R_{2}^{\prime}=\left(T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}T_{D\nabla^{\perp}\psi\circ\Phi}-T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}D\nabla^{\perp}\psi\circ\Phi}\right)\Phi
.

Substituting this into (
4.2
) yields

\partial_{t}X=(\nabla^{\perp}(\Phi^{*}\psi)-R_{1}^{\prime}-R_{2}^{\prime})+R_{C}(u,\Phi).

We now observe that
R_{C}
exactly cancels
R_{2}^{\prime}
. Since
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
,
we have
Du=D\nabla^{\perp}\psi
so that, by their respective definitions,
R_{C}
and
R_{2}^{\prime}
are identical.
The evolution equation for
X
thus simplifies to

\partial_{t}X=\nabla^{\perp}(\Phi^{*}\psi)-R_{1}^{\prime},

which is the desired result with
R_{1}^{\prime}=R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\Phi)^{-1},u\circ\Phi)
.
∎

We are now in a position to derive the main equation governing the evolution of the vector field
X
.
We begin by relating the paracomposed vorticity to the stream function.

Proposition 4.3
.

The vector field
X=T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}\Phi
solves the closed evolution equation

(4.3)

\partial_{t}X+\nabla^{\perp}QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot X=\nabla^{\perp}Q\omega_{0}-\mathcal{R},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\nabla^{\perp}\left(S(\Phi^{*}\psi)+QR_{\triangle}(\Phi,\psi)\right)-R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\Phi)^{-1},u\circ\Phi)

\displaystyle-\nabla^{\perp}Q\left(R_{\mathrm{alg}}\left((D\Phi)^{-\top},\nabla\omega_{0}\right)\cdot\Phi\right).

and
S
is the smoothing remainder introduced in Lemma
3.6
.

Remark 4.4
.

The equation (
4.3
) highlights the linear driving mechanism: the growth of
X

is governed by the operator
\nabla^{\perp}QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot
, forced by the initial
vorticity profile
\nabla^{\perp}Q\omega_{0}
, up to smoothing terms.

Proof.

Recall that
\Delta\psi=\omega
. Applying the paracomposition operator and using the
conjugation formula for the Laplacian from Proposition
3.5
(with
\chi=\Phi
), we obtain

\Phi^{*}\omega=\Phi^{*}(\Delta\psi)=\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}(\Phi^{*}\psi)+R_{\triangle}(\Phi,\psi).

We now apply the parametrix
Q
constructed in Lemma
3.6
to invert the
operator
\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}
. Using the identity
Q\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}=\Id+S
, where
S
is the smoothing remainder, we find

\displaystyle Q(\Phi^{*}\omega)

\displaystyle=Q\Delta^{\chi}_{\mathrm{para}}(\Phi^{*}\psi)+QR_{\triangle}(\Phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=(\Id+S)(\Phi^{*}\psi)+QR_{\triangle}(\Phi,\psi).

Solving for the stream function term yields:

(4.4)

\Phi^{*}\psi=Q(\Phi^{*}\omega)-S(\Phi^{*}\psi)-QR_{\triangle}(\Phi,\psi).

This expression can be substituted directly into the evolution equation for
X

derived in Proposition
4.1
. Since
\partial_{t}X=\nabla^{\perp}(\Phi^{*}\psi)-R
, we have:

(4.5)

\partial_{t}X=\nabla^{\perp}Q(\Phi^{*}\omega)-\nabla^{\perp}\left(S(\Phi^{*}\psi)+QR_{\triangle}(\Phi,\psi)\right)-R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\Phi)^{-1},u\circ\Phi).

We must express the source term
\Phi^{*}\omega
in terms of the initial data
and the unknown
X
. By definition,
\Phi^{*}\omega=\omega\circ\Phi-T_{\nabla\omega\circ\Phi}\Phi
.
Recall that, using the Lagrangian conservation of vorticity, we have
\omega\circ\Phi=\omega_{0}
.
Furthermore, the chain rule implies
(\nabla\omega)\circ\Phi=(D\Phi)^{-\top}(\nabla\omega_{0})
.
We can therefore expand the paraproduct as follows:

\displaystyle T_{(\nabla\omega)\circ\Phi}\cdot\Phi

\displaystyle=T_{(D\Phi)^{-\top}(\nabla\omega_{0})}\cdot\Phi=T_{(D\Phi)^{-\top}}T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\Phi+R_{\mathrm{alg}}\left((D\Phi)^{-\top},\nabla\omega_{0}\right)\cdot\Phi

\displaystyle=T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}\Phi+R_{\mathrm{alg}}\left((D\Phi)^{-\top},\nabla\omega_{0}\right)\cdot\Phi

\displaystyle=T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot X+R_{\mathrm{alg}}\left((D\Phi)^{-\top},\nabla\omega_{0}\right)\cdot\Phi,

where we have used the composition estimate (Theorem
A.3
)
and recognized the definition of
X=T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}\Phi
.
Substituting this back into the expression for
\Phi^{*}\omega
, we conclude that:

(4.6)

\Phi^{*}\omega=\omega_{0}-T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot X-R_{\mathrm{alg}}\left((D\Phi)^{-\top},\nabla\omega_{0}\right)\cdot\Phi.

Injecting this into (
4.5
) gives the desired result.
∎

5.
On cusps and generic properties of singularities

In this section, we develop the quantitative tools
required to extract a strict growth lower bound from
the paradifferential evolution equation for
X
.

First, in Section
5.1
, we introduce a dense class of generic initial data,
denoted
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, which is characterized by a strictly
non-degenerate global minimum and a slowly decaying Fourier tail.
Second, in Section
5.2
, we isolate the singular behavior of these functions near their minimum
by defining localized Hölderian cusps. We provide a
comprehensive description of these profiles, establishing both their explicit
high-frequency asymptotics and the regularizing effect of their remainders in physical space.
Third, Section
5.3
is devoted to the microlocal
interaction between these cusps and the parametrix
\Delta Q
.
We prove a symbol-freezing lemma, showing that the operator acts on
the leading-order singularity essentially as a constant-coefficient multiplier.
We use these tools in Section
6.3
, where we evaluate
the high-frequency inner product
\langle\Delta Qg,P_{\varepsilon}(g-m)^{\alpha}\rangle_{L^{2}}
.
This yields the fundamental lower bound driving the small-scale generation argument.

5.1.
The set of unstable initial data

Let
s>3
. We introduce the class of functions
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

that exhibit both a slow Fourier decay and a generic landscape
for their global minimum.

Definition 5.1
.

We denote by
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
the set of real-valued functions
g\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

satisfying the following four properties:

(i)

There exist a constant
c>0
and an integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that,
for all
|n|\geq N
,

(5.1)

(1+|n|^{2})^{s/2}\RE\hat{g}(n)\geq\frac{c}{|n|\ln(1+\left\lvert n\right\rvert)}.

(ii)

The function
g
attains its global infimum at a unique point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

(iii)

The minimum is non-degenerate: the Hessian matrix
D^{2}g(x_{0})

is positive definite.

(iv)

The function has zero average, i.e.,
\hat{g}(0)=0
.

Remark 5.1
.

The spectral lower bound (
5.1
) ensures that the regularity of
g

is sharp: indeed,
g\notin H^{s^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for any
s^{\prime}>s
. Consequently, we shall refer
to the elements of
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
as functions with
exact

H^{s}
regularity.

We begin by proving the following

Proposition 5.2
.

For all
s>3
, the set
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is dense in the space
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

Proof.

Let
f\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
r>0
. We first show the existence of a function
satisfying the spectral lower bound (
5.1
) in the ball
B_{H^{s}}(f,r)
.
To this end, we employ a construction that extracts a sequence
decaying arbitrarily slowly relative to the Fourier coefficients
of
f
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. There exists a
square-summable sequence of positive real numbers

(l_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}
such that

(1+|n|^{2})^{s/2}|\hat{f}(n)|=o(l_{n})\quad\text{as }|n|\to\infty,

while satisfying
l_{n}=l_{-n}
and the lower bound
l_{n}\geq(|n|\ln(1+|n|))^{-1}

for all
|n|\geq 1
.

Proof.

Define the weighted coefficients
a_{n}\coloneqq(1+|n|^{2})^{s/2}|\hat{f}(n)|

and, for
N\geq 1
, the tail of the series

R_{N}\coloneqq\sum_{|j|\geq N}a_{j}^{2},

which converges monotonically to zero. We define the sequence
(l_{n})
by

l_{n}\coloneqq\frac{a_{n}}{R_{|n|}^{1/4}}+\frac{1}{|n|\ln(1+|n|)},

where the first term is understood to be zero if
a_{n}=0
.
The required lower bound on
l_{n}
is satisfied by construction. Since
f
is real-valued, we have
a_{n}=a_{-n}
.
Therefore, the sequence
(l_{n})
is real-valued and satisfies
l_{n}=l_{-n}
. Moreover, the required lower bound on
l_{n}
is satisfied by construction.

To verify square-summability, note that the logarithmic term is in
\ell^{2}
.
Moreover, since
\sum_{|n|=N}a_{n}^{2}=R_{N}-R_{N+1}
, an integral comparison
for the decreasing function
x\mapsto x^{-1/2}
yields

\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{\sqrt{R_{|n|}}}=\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\frac{R_{N}-R_{N+1}}{\sqrt{R_{N}}}\leq\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\int_{R_{N+1}}^{R_{N}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\diff\!x=\int_{0}^{R_{1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\diff\!x=2\sqrt{R_{1}}.

Thus
(l_{n})\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
. Finally, since
a_{n}\leq l_{n}R_{|n|}^{1/4}

and
R_{|n|}\to 0
, we have
a_{n}=o(l_{n})
.
∎

We now define the perturbation
g
satisfying
g\in B_{H^{s}}(f,r)
and
properties (
i
)–(
iv
).

We define
l_{f}
as the function with Fourier coefficients

\hat{l_{f}}(n)=l_{n}(1+|n|^{2})^{-s/2}
for
n\neq 0
, and
\hat{l_{f}}(0)=0
.
Notice that
l_{f}
is real-valued since
l_{n}=l_{-n}
.
By construction
\left\lVert l_{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}=\left\lVert l_{n}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}
and
l_{f}
has zero average.
We then define the
perturbed function
g_{0}
by

g_{0}\coloneqq f+\frac{r}{2\left\lVert l_{f}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}}l_{f}.

It follows that
g_{0}\in B_{H^{s}}(f,r)
, and
g_{0}
satisfies property (
i
) and (
iv
).

Now, let
m_{0}\coloneqq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}g_{0}(x)
. If the set of
points
X\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}:g_{0}(x)=m_{0}\}
is not a singleton, we select
an arbitrary point
x_{0}\in X
and, by applying a spatial translation,
we may assume
x_{0}=0
. Consider a non-negative function
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

supported in a small neighborhood of the origin and such that
\psi(0)=1>\hat{\psi}(0)
.
For any
\eta>0
sufficiently small, we define
g_{1}\coloneqq g_{0}-\eta(\psi-\hat{\psi}(0))
,
ensuring property (
ii
).
To satisfy property (
iii
), we ensure that the Hessian
D^{2}g(0)
is positive
definite by adding a quadratic-like perturbation:

g\coloneqq g_{1}-\varepsilon\cos(x^{1})-\varepsilon\cos(x^{2}),

where
\varepsilon>0
is chosen sufficiently small. Notice that
\hat{g}(0)=\hat{g_{1}}(0)=0
.
Furthermore, at the origin, the Hessian of the perturbation is
\varepsilon\mathrm{Id}
.
This ensures the non-degeneracy of the global minimum for any
\varepsilon>0
,
as
D^{2}g_{1}(0)
is necessarily non-negative. In addition, this perturbation preserves
property (
ii
) for
\varepsilon
small enough.

The final function
g
belongs to
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and has zero average.
For
\eta
and
\varepsilon
sufficiently small,
g
belongs to the ball
B_{H^{s}}(f,r)
.
As the centering constants and smooth terms only affect a finite number of Fourier
coefficients or decay rapidly, the spectral lower bound (
5.1
) remains valid
for
|n|
large enough. This completes the proof of density.
∎

Now consider a function
g\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
s>3
.
By applying a spatial translation,
we may assume without loss of
generality that
x_{0}=0
, that is

g(0)=m\coloneqq\inf_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}g(x).

Furthermore, by definition of
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
the Hessian
matrix
D^{2}g(0)
is positive definite, and we shall
denote its eigenvalues by
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
.

For our problem, a key point is to study the local regularity of
(g-m)^{\alpha}
near
x=0
.
This analysis will be performed in depth in the next section;
as a preparation, we establish the following elementary result
characterizing its Hölder regularity.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
s>3
and
g\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and set

m=\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}g
.
If
0\leq\alpha<(s-1)/2
,
then the function
(g-m)^{\alpha}

belongs to
C^{2\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

Proof.

Since
g\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, at the point
x_{0}=0
where the infimum is attained, a Taylor
expansion of the non-degenerate minimum yields the local
behavior

g(x)-m=\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{q}(x)+o(|x|^{2}),

where
\mathfrak{q}
is a positive definite quadratic form. It follows that,
in a neighborhood of the origin, the function
(g-m)^{\alpha}
behaves
asymptotically like
|x|^{2\alpha}
, which precisely defines the

C^{2\alpha}
regularity.
Conversely, at any point
x\neq 0
, we have
g(x)>m
, and since the mapping
u\mapsto(u-m)^{\alpha}
is
locally smooth, we deduce that
(g-m)^{\alpha}
is locally
in
H^{s}\subset C^{2\alpha}
by the Sobolev embedding theorem.
∎

5.2.
Some results about cusps

When performing Taylor expansions on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, one has to pay attention to
the non-periodicity of the global coordinate
x
. To remain within the space of
periodic distributions, we introduce the following notation.

Definition 5.2
.

We fix the following objects for the remainder of this section:

(1)

A cut-off:
Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
be a bump function
such that
\chi\equiv 1
and such that the support of
\chi
is strictly
contained within the fundamental cell
(-\pi,\pi]^{2}
.

(2)

A periodic coordinate:
We introduce a periodic
function
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})

such that
\sigma(x)=x
for all
x\in\supp\chi
. This map allows
us to define the "distance to the origin" in a smooth, periodic manner.

(3)

A quadratic form:
Given the Hessian
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
of
g
at the origin,
we define
\mathfrak{q}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
by:

(5.2)

\mathfrak{q}(x)\coloneqq\lambda_{1}\sigma_{1}(x)^{2}+\lambda_{2}\sigma_{2}(x)^{2}.

So,
\mathfrak{q}
is a smooth, periodic function that coincides
with
\mathfrak{q}_{\textrm{eucl}}(x)\coloneqq\lambda_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}x_{2}^{2}

in a neighborhood of
0
.

The following lemma identifies the principal part of the Fourier expansion.
The key observation is that the high-frequency behavior is entirely
determined by the singularity at the origin.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\alpha>0
such that
2\alpha\notin\mathbb{N}
. The singular profile

H(x)\coloneqq\chi(x)\mathfrak{q}(x)^{\alpha}
belongs to
C^{2\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\cap H^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for all
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
. Moreover, there exists a non-zero
constant
C_{\alpha}
such that its Fourier coefficients satisfy:

(5.3)

\hat{H}(n)=\frac{C_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}}\left(\frac{n_{1}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}+\frac{n_{2}^{2}}{\lambda_{2}}\right)^{-(1+\alpha)}+O(|n|^{-\infty}).

Remark 5.6
.

Up to considering
H/C_{\alpha}
instead of
H
,
we will assume without loss of generality that
C_{\alpha}=1
.

Proof.

By a linear change of variables
y_{j}=\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}x_{j}
, we reduce
the problem to the radial case where
\mathfrak{q}(x)=|y|^{2}
.
Since
\chi
is
supported within the fundamental cell, the periodic
coefficients
\hat{H}(n)
coincide with the Fourier transform on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\hat{H}(n)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\chi(y)|y|^{2\alpha}e^{-in\cdot y}\diff\!y.

Now we observe that
D(y)=(1-\chi(y))\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2\alpha}
is a
smooth function with polynomial growth at infinity and
hence its Fourier transform contributes to the
O(|n|^{-\infty})

remainder. On the other hand, it is a classical result (see
[
35
]
) that
the Fourier transform of
\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2\alpha}
is equal to
\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{-2-2\alpha}
and we obtain (
5.3
).

The Sobolev regularity follows from
\hat{H}(n)\sim|n|^{-(2+2\alpha)}

which implies
\sum|n|^{2s}|\hat{H}(n)|^{2}<\infty
for

s<1+2\alpha
.
∎

To justify the cusp extraction, we must show that any term vanishing more rapidly
than
\mathfrak{q}
at the origin results in a more regular distribution.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
t>2
and
G\in H^{t}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
be such that
G(0)=0
.
For all real number
\alpha>-1/2
, the function
f

defined by

f(x)\coloneqq\chi(x)\mathfrak{q}(x)^{\alpha}G(x)

belongs to
H^{\min(2\alpha^{\prime}+2,t-1)}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for all
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
.

Proof.

Since
G\in H^{t}
and
G(0)=0
,
we represent
G
on
\supp\chi

using the periodic coordinate map
\sigma
:

G(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sigma_{j}(x)u_{j}(x),\quad\text{where}\quad u_{j}(x)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{j}G(\tau\sigma(x))\diff\!\tau.

The condition
\nabla G\in H^{t-1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
implies
u_{j}\in H^{t-1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

by standard composition and integration arguments.
We then write
f=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\chi w_{j}u_{j}

with
w_{j}(x)\coloneqq\chi(x)\mathfrak{q}(x)^{\alpha}\sigma_{j}(x)
.

The regularity of
w_{j}
follows from the fact that near
the origin it is positively homogeneous of
degree
2\alpha+1
. In dimension
d=2
,
a distribution that is homogeneous of degree
\beta

belongs to
H^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}
if and only if
s<\beta+1
.
Substituting
\beta=2\alpha+1

yields
w_{j}\in H^{2\alpha^{\prime}+2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for all
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
.

Finally, since
t>2
, we have
t-1>1
.
Moreover,
2\alpha^{\prime}+2>1
for
\alpha>-1/2
.
In dimension
d=2
, the product of two functions
in
H^{s_{1}}
and
H^{s_{2}}
with
s_{1},s_{2}>1

belongs to
H^{\min(s_{1},s_{2})}
.
Applying this multiplication theorem to
each term
\chi w_{j}u_{j}
yields the claimed regularity.
∎

Let
s>4
and consider a function
g
in
the class
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

introduced in Definition
5.1
. This ensures that
g

attains a unique non-degenerate minimum
m

at the origin, where the Hessian matrix admits positive
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
.

We are now at a position to provide a refined
analysis of the profile
(g-m)^{\alpha}
.
The following result shows that
(g-m)^{\alpha}
can be decomposed into a
singular profile, whose Fourier transform is known explicitly, and a remainder
that is more regular in the Sobolev scale.

Proposition 5.8
.

Consider
\alpha
and
s
such that
0<\alpha<(s-4)/2

and
g\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
Then, provided the cut-off function

\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is supported in a sufficiently small neighborhood
of the origin,

(5.4)

(g-m)^{\alpha}=H(x)+R(x),

where
H=\chi\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}
is the singular profile from
Lemma
5.5
and the
remainder satisfies
R\in H^{\min(2\alpha^{\prime}+2,s-3)}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for
any
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
.

In particular,
(g-m)^{\alpha}
belongs to
H^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for all
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
F\coloneqq g-m
. We decompose the function into a singular component
concentrated near the origin and a smoother remainder:

F(x)^{\alpha}=\chi(x)F(x)^{\alpha}+(1-\chi(x))F(x)^{\alpha}.

Since
m
is the unique minimum of
g
,
the function
F
is strictly positive on the support
of
1-\chi
. We may therefore
represent
(1-\chi)F^{\alpha}
as
\Psi(x,F(x))

for some smooth function

\Psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R})
. It then
follows from standard Moser composition rules
that
(1-\chi)F^{\alpha}
belongs to

H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
and it is therefore absorbed into the remainder
R
.

We now focus on the local component
\chi F^{\alpha}
.
By choosing an appropriate orthonormal basis, we may assume
that the Hessian
D^{2}g(0)
is diagonal. Since
\nabla g(0)=0
,
we use the periodic coordinate
\sigma
to write the Taylor expansion
of
F=g-m
on
\supp\chi
:

F(x)=\sum_{j,k=1}^{2}\sigma_{j}(x)\sigma_{k}(x)M_{jk}(x),\quad\text{where}\quad M_{jk}(x)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)\partial_{j}\partial_{k}g(\tau\sigma(x))\diff\!\tau.

Recalling the definition (
5.2
) of the globalized
quadratic profile
\mathfrak{q}(x)=\sum\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}(x)^{2}
,
we note that
M_{jk}(0)=\delta_{jk}\lambda_{j}
. We define
the increment

\Delta(x)\coloneqq F(x)-\mathfrak{q}(x)=\sum_{j,k=1}^{2}\sigma_{j}(x)\sigma_{k}(x)[M_{jk}(x)-M_{jk}(0)].

Observe that, provided the cut-off function

\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is supported in a sufficiently small neighborhood
of the origin, we ensure that the quadratic form
\mathfrak{q}

dominates the higher-order remainder
\Delta
. Specifically, since

\mathfrak{q}(x)\geq c|x|^{2}
and
\Delta(x)=O(|x|^{2+\min(1,s-3)})
, taking

\supp\chi\subset B(0,\delta)
with
\delta
small enough guarantees that

\mathfrak{q}(x)+\theta\Delta(x)\geq\underline{c}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}>0
for all
\theta\in[0,1]
and
x\neq 0
.

For
x\neq 0
on the support of
\chi
, the strict
positivity
\mathfrak{q}(x)+\theta\Delta(x)\geq c|x|^{2}/2>0

ensures that the straight line segment connecting
\mathfrak{q}(x)
and
F(x)
lies entirely
within the interval
(0,+\infty)
where the map
u\mapsto u^{\alpha}
is
C^{\infty}
.
Applying the fundamental theorem of calculus thus yields

\displaystyle\chi(x)F(x)^{\alpha}=\chi(x)\mathfrak{q}(x)^{\alpha}+R_{\textrm{loc}}(x),\quad\text{where}

\displaystyle R_{\textrm{loc}}(x)=\alpha\chi(x)\Delta(x)\int_{0}^{1}(\mathfrak{q}(x)+\theta\Delta(x))^{\alpha-1}\diff\!\theta.

On the other hand, at
x=0
, we have

\chi F^{\alpha}=0=\chi\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}
and
R_{\textrm{loc}}(x)=O(|x|^{2\alpha+\min(1,s-3)})
,
meaning that the remainder vanishes continuously as
x\to 0
and hence the identity holds also at the origin.

The principal term
\chi\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}
is precisely the profile
H

characterized in Lemma
5.5
.
Consequently, to complete the proof, it remains only to obtain the
claimed Sobolev regularity for the remainder
R_{\textrm{loc}}
. To do this, we
begin by observing
that by the non-degeneracy of the minimum, the term
(\mathfrak{q}+\theta\Delta)^{\alpha-1}

behaves locally like
|\sigma(x)|^{2\alpha-2}
. The product

\Delta(\mathfrak{q}+\theta\Delta)^{\alpha-1}
thus behaves as
|\sigma(x)|^{2\alpha}G(x)
,
where
G(x)

is a linear combination of the terms
M_{jk}(x)-M_{jk}(0)
. Since
g\in H^{s}
,
the second derivatives
\partial_{j}\partial_{k}g
are in
H^{s-2}
,
and consequently the matrix elements
M_{jk}
also belong to
H^{s-2}
.

Furthermore, since
M_{jk}(x)-M_{jk}(0)
vanishes at the origin,
we may apply Lemma
5.7
to each component of the
expansion of
R_{\textrm{loc}}
. This implies that
R_{\textrm{loc}}

belongs to
H^{\min(2\alpha^{\prime}+2,s-3)}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
which completes the proof.
∎

5.3.
Microlocal stability of cusps

In this section, we study the interaction between the paradifferential parametrix
Q

(as constructed in Lemma
3.6
) and the singular cusp
H=\chi\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}
.
We show that the action of the operator
\Delta Q
is equivalent, up to a smoother remainder,
to a constant-coefficient Fourier multiplier obtained by freezing the principal symbol at the origin.

Recall from the proof of Lemma
3.6
that the principal symbol of
\Delta Q
is

a(x,\xi)\coloneqq\frac{|\xi|^{2}}{\xi\cdot M(x)\xi}\quad\text{where}\quad M(x)\coloneqq(D\phi(x))^{-1}\big(D\phi(x)\big)^{-\top}.

By the properties of the diffeomorphism,
M
is a smooth, symmetric,
and positive definite matrix on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Although
\phi
generally depends
on time, its temporal evolution is irrelevant for the following symbolic estimates;
we thus omit the time variable from our notation. The positive definiteness of
M(x)

ensures the ellipticity of
\Delta Q
; specifically,
there exist constants
C\geq c>0
such that
c\leq a(x,\xi)\leq C

for all
(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

Lemma 5.9

(Symbol freezing)
.

Let
\alpha
and
s
be such that
0<\alpha<(s-4)/2
.
Consider the singular
profile
H=\chi\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}
from Lemma
5.5
and
let
Q
be the parametrix constructed in Lemma
3.6
. The action of
\Delta Q

on
H
satisfies

(5.5)

(\Delta Q)H=a(0,D)H+\mathcal{R},

where the remainder
\mathcal{R}

belongs to
H^{\min(2\alpha^{\prime}+2,s-3)}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for all
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
.

Proof.

By definition of the parametrix (Lemma
3.6
), we have
Q=\sum_{k}T_{m_{k}}B^{k}\Delta^{-1}
.
The operator
A\coloneqq\Delta Q
can be written as:

(5.6)

A=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Delta T_{m_{k}}B^{k}\Delta^{-1}.

We first commute the Laplacian with the paraproduct. Using
the identity
\Delta T_{m_{k}}=T_{m_{k}}\Delta+2T_{\nabla m_{k}}\cdot\nabla+T_{\Delta m_{k}}
,
and observing that Fourier multipliers
\Delta
,
B^{k}
, and
\Delta^{-1}
commute,
we get

A=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{m_{k}}B^{k}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(2T_{\nabla m_{k}}\cdot\nabla+T_{\Delta m_{k}}\right)B^{k}\Delta^{-1}.

Since
B^{k}\Delta^{-1}
is an operator of order
-2
and
T_{\nabla m_{k}}\cdot\nabla
is an
operator of order
1
, the composition in the second sum defines an operator
of order
-1
. Its action on
H\in H^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}
results in a remainder in
H^{2+2\alpha^{\prime}}
,
which is consistent with the required regularity for the remainder
R
.

We now focus on the principal sum
\sum T_{m_{k}}B^{k}H
. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we freeze
the coefficient
m_{k}
at the origin by writing
m_{k}(x)=m_{k}(0)+\tilde{m}_{k}(x)
,
where
\tilde{m}_{k}(0)=0
. Since the paraproduct
T_{m_{k}(0)}
coincides with the
multiplication by the constant
m_{k}(0)
up to a smooth low-frequency truncation term
(which we absorb into the remainder
R
and omit hereafter for brevity), we have:

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{m_{k}}B^{k}H=\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}m_{k}(0)B^{k}\right)H+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{\tilde{m}_{k}}B^{k}H.

The first term on the right-hand side is exactly
a(0,D)H
. The terms in the second sum
involve the paraproduct
T_{\tilde{m}_{k}}
with a coefficient
\tilde{m}_{k}\in H^{s-2}
that
vanishes at the origin. Since
B^{k}
is a Fourier multiplier of order
0
,
B^{k}H

possesses the same cusp structure as
H
, being a homogeneous distribution of
degree
2\alpha
localized by
\chi
. We apply the Bony decomposition to each term:

(5.7)

T_{\tilde{m}_{k}}(B^{k}H)=\tilde{m}_{k}(B^{k}H)-R_{\mathcal{B}}(\tilde{m}_{k},B^{k}H)-T_{B^{k}H}\tilde{m}_{k}.

As established in Lemma
5.7
, since
\tilde{m}_{k}(0)=0
and
B^{k}H
behaves
locally like
|\sigma(x)|^{2\alpha}
, the product
\tilde{m}_{k}(B^{k}H)
gains one full
derivative compared to
H
. Specifically, this term behaves as
|\sigma(x)|^{2\alpha+1}

and belongs to
H^{\min(2\alpha^{\prime}+2,s-3)}
. The other two terms in (
5.7
)
are similarly regular: the Bony remainder
R_{\mathcal{B}}
gains derivatives from the sum of
indices, and the paraproduct
T_{B^{k}H}\tilde{m}_{k}
inherits the
H^{s-2}
regularity of
\tilde{m}_{k}
.

Finally, the exponential decay of the Sobolev norms
\|m_{k}\|_{H^{s-2}}
established
in (
3.9
) ensures that the series of remainders converges absolutely
in
H^{\min(2\alpha^{\prime}+2,s-3)}
. Summing all error terms from the
Laplacian commutation and the coefficient freezing yields the desired result.
∎

6.
A paradifferential Poisson structure

In this section, we establish the paradifferential Poisson structure. Our analysis is structured as follows.

In subsection
6.1
, we gather several properties
of Friedrichs mollifiers
which are needed to localize in the frequency
space. Then, we establish in subsection
6.2
an upper bound
for the scalar product
\langle f,P_{\varepsilon}(g-m)^{\alpha}\rangle_{L^{2}}
, which serves as a necessary counterpart
to the subsequent growth estimates in subsection
6.3
, where
we establish the spectral tail lower bound
for the scalar
product
\langle\Delta Qg,P_{\varepsilon}(g-m)^{\alpha}\rangle_{L^{2}}
.
In subsection
6.4
, we analyze a paradifferential
variant of the cancellation
\int(v\cdot\nabla f)f=0
provided that
\cn v=0
.
This result,
summarized in Lemma
6.4
, allows us to treat the advection term as
a remainder despite the limited regularity of the vorticity.

This combination of lower bounds for the parametrix
and cancellation for the transport term provides the quantitative
estimates needed in the next section
to close the small-scale generation argument.

6.1.
Friedrichs mollifiers

Let
\theta
be a
C^{\infty}

function of
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, satisfying

0\leq\theta\leq 1,\quad\theta(\xi)=1\text{ for }|\xi|\leq 1/2,\quad\theta(\xi)=0\text{ for }|\xi|\geq 1,\quad\theta(\xi)=\theta\left(-\xi\right).

Set
\theta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)=\theta(\varepsilon\xi)
, for
0<\varepsilon\leq 1

and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
; so that
\theta_{\varepsilon}
is supported in the
ball of radius
1/\varepsilon
about the origin. Then we define
J_{\varepsilon}

as the Fourier multiplier with symbol
\theta_{\varepsilon}
:

J_{\varepsilon}=\theta(\varepsilon D_{x}).

The operator
J_{\varepsilon}
is self-adjoint
and
J_{\varepsilon}u
is real-valued for any real-valued function
u
.
We will often use the simple observation that

(6.1)

J_{\varepsilon}=J_{\varepsilon}J_{c\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for all}\,\,\,\,0\leq c\leq 1/2.

We make extensive use of the following estimates.

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider the Friedrichs mollifiers
J_{\varepsilon}=\theta(\varepsilon D)
.

(i)

Let
s\geq 0
.
There exists a constant
C
such that, for all
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,

(6.2)

\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{s}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

(ii)

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{R}

and
(r,\rho)\in[0,1]^{2}
be such that
r+\rho\leq 1
. There exists a constant

C
such that, for all
v\in C^{r+s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,

(6.3)

\left\lVert[(\Id-J_{\varepsilon}),T_{v}]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(H^{\mu},H^{\mu+r})}\lesssim_{\mu}\varepsilon^{\rho}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{r+\rho}}.

(iii)

The spectrum of the
paraproduct
T_{a}(J_{\varepsilon}b)
is contained in the low-frequency region:

(6.4)

\mathcal{S}_{low}\coloneqq\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|\xi|<\frac{9}{2\varepsilon}\right\}.

Proof.

(i)
By Plancherel’s theorem and the support
property of
\theta
(which equals
1
for
|\xi|\leq 1/2
), the
symbol
1-\theta(\varepsilon k)
vanishes unless
|k|>(2\varepsilon)^{-1}
.
On this frequency set, we have
(1+|k|^{2})^{-s}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2s}
. Thus,

\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{|k|>(2\varepsilon)^{-1}}(1-\theta(\varepsilon k))^{2}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}\lesssim\sum_{|k|>(2\varepsilon)^{-1}}\varepsilon^{2s}(1+|k|^{2})^{s}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2},

which yields the result.

(ii)
Consider
the scaled operator
A_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\varepsilon^{-\rho}(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})
.
Its symbol is
a_{\varepsilon}(\xi)=\varepsilon^{-\rho}(1-\theta(\varepsilon\xi))
. We verify
that
A_{\varepsilon}
is a Fourier multiplier of order
\rho
, uniformly in
\varepsilon
.
Indeed, for any multi-index
\alpha
, the derivative
\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}a_{\varepsilon}(\xi)

is supported in the region
|\xi|\sim\varepsilon^{-1}
. On this support, we have:

|\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}a_{\varepsilon}(\xi)|=\varepsilon^{-\rho}\varepsilon^{|\alpha|}|(\partial^{\alpha}\theta)(\varepsilon\xi)|\lesssim\varepsilon^{|\alpha|-\rho}\lesssim(1+|\xi|)^{\rho-|\alpha|}.

We now apply the standard commutator estimate (Theorem
A.5
):
the commutator of a Fourier multiplier of order
m=\rho
with the paramultiplication
by a function
v\in C^{r+\rho}
maps
H^{\mu}
to
H^{\mu-m+r+\rho}=H^{\mu+r}
. This gives:

\left\lVert[A_{\varepsilon},T_{v}]\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(H^{\mu},H^{\mu+r})}\lesssim\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{r+\rho}}.

This implies the desired estimate for
[(\Id-J_{\varepsilon}),T_{v}]=\varepsilon^{\rho}[A_{\varepsilon},T_{v}]
.

(iii)
Recall that
T_{a}(J_{\varepsilon}b)=\sum_{j\geq 3}S_{j-3}a\,\Delta_{j}(J_{\varepsilon}b)
.
The spectrum of each term
S_{j-3}a\,\Delta_{j}v
is contained in the ball
of radius
2^{j-2}+2^{j+1}=\frac{9}{8}2^{j+1}
.
For the term
\Delta_{j}(J_{\varepsilon}b)
to be non-zero, the dyadic
annulus
\{|\xi|\sim 2^{j}\}
must intersect the support of
J_{\varepsilon}
,
which is
\{|\xi|\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\}
, so
2^{j-1}<\varepsilon^{-1}
. We find
that the spectrum is contained in
\{|\xi|<\frac{9}{8}\cdot 4\varepsilon^{-1}=\frac{9}{2\varepsilon}\}
,
which concludes the proof.
∎

6.2.
An upper bound

Consider a function
g
in
the class
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

introduced in Definition
5.1
. This ensures that
g

attains a unique non-degenerate minimum
m

at the origin, where the Hessian matrix admits positive
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
.

Now, introduce the Fourier multiplier

P_{\varepsilon}=\Id-J_{\varepsilon}=\Id-\theta(\varepsilon D_{x}),

where
\theta
is as in the previous paragraph, so that
P_{\varepsilon}
localized at frequencies larger than
1/\varepsilon
.
Our first objective
is to estimate the scalar
product

\left\langle f,P_{\varepsilon}(g-m)^{\alpha}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}.

We want to characterize
the maximal decay rate allowed by the cusp exponent
\alpha
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Consider
\alpha
and
s
such that
0<\alpha<(s-4)/2
.
Assume that
g\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
For all
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
, there exists a
constant
C=C(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}},\alpha^{\prime})
such that, for all
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
for all
0<\varepsilon<1
,

(6.5)

\left|\left\langle f,P_{\varepsilon}(g-m)^{\alpha}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\right|\leq C\varepsilon^{s+1+2\alpha^{\prime}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

Proof.

Recall from Proposition
5.8
that
the function
V=(g-m)^{\alpha}
belongs to the Sobolev space
H^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
Now, as above, with
\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}=P_{2\varepsilon}
, there holds
P_{\varepsilon}=\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}P_{\varepsilon}
so

\left\lvert\langle f,P_{\varepsilon}V\rangle_{L^{2}}\right\rvert=\big\lvert\langle\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}f,P_{\varepsilon}V\rangle_{L^{2}}\big\rvert\leq\big\lVert\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}f\big\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert P_{\varepsilon}V\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

We then write that

\left\lVert P_{\varepsilon}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{s}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{s}},

and, similarly,

\left\lVert P_{\varepsilon}V\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{H^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}}.

Combining these estimates, we obtain the wanted inequality.
∎

6.3.
Spectral tail estimates

We now establish a quantitative lower
bound for the quantity

\left\langle\Delta Qg,P_{\varepsilon}((g-m)^{\alpha})\right\rangle_{L^{2}},

where
Q
is the paradifferential parametrix
constructed in Lemma
3.6
.
Before stating the following result, let us observe that the operator norm
of the parametrix
Q
inherently depends on the Sobolev norm
\left\lVert\Phi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}}
.
Nevertheless, for the sake
of notational clarity, we shall systematically absorb this dependence into
the generic constants without explicit mention.

Proposition 6.3
.

Consider three real numbers
s,\alpha,\beta
, satisfying

(6.6)

2\alpha<s-4,\quad 2\alpha\notin\mathbb{N}\quad\text{and}\quad 0<\alpha<\beta.

Let
g\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. There exist constants
C=C(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}})>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0

such that, for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, the following lower bound holds:

(6.7)

\left\langle\Delta Qg,P_{\varepsilon}(g-m)^{\alpha}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\geq C\varepsilon^{s+1+2\beta}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
the exponent
\beta

satisfies the condition
\beta<\min\{\alpha+1/2,(s-4)/2\}
.
Throughout the following estimates, we denote by
C
various
positive constants depending solely on the
H^{s}
-norm of
g
,
and we employ the notation
A\lesssim B
as a shorthand for

A\leq CB
for such a constant
C
.

By exploiting the self-adjointness
of the spectral projector
P_{\varepsilon}
, we decompose
the expression
into a principal term
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
and a commutator
remainder
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}
as follows:

\left\langle\Delta Qg,P_{\varepsilon}(g-m)^{\alpha}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\left\langle\Delta QP_{\varepsilon}g,(g-m)^{\alpha}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\left\langle[P_{\varepsilon},\Delta Q]g,(g-m)^{\alpha}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}+\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}.

Step 1: Estimate of the commutator term
\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}
.

We first establish the following bound for the commutator contribution:

(6.8)

\left\lvert\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}\right\rvert\lesssim\varepsilon^{s+2+2\alpha^{\prime}}.

To this end, we introduce a slightly wider spectral projector

\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}=P_{\lambda\varepsilon}
, with
\lambda>1
chosen large
enough so that
P_{\varepsilon}\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}=P_{\varepsilon}
.

By virtue of the third statement in Lemma
6.1
,
when
\lambda
is large enough, one has

P_{\varepsilon}T_{a}b=P_{\varepsilon}T_{a}\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}b.

Given that the parametrix
Q
is constructed as a sum of
operators involving paraproducts and Fourier multipliers,
the commutator with
\Delta Q
inherits this spectral localization.
Specifically, we have

[P_{\varepsilon},\Delta Q]g=\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}[P_{\varepsilon},\Delta Q]g.

We now proceed by duality to write

\left\lvert\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}\right\rvert\leq\big\lVert\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}[P_{\varepsilon},\Delta Q]g\big\rVert_{H^{-1-2\alpha^{\prime}}}\left\lVert(g-m)^{\alpha}\right\rVert_{H^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}}.

Invoking the high-frequency decay estimate provided in the
first statement of Lemma
6.1
, we find that

\left\lVert\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}[P_{\varepsilon},\Delta Q]g\right\rVert_{H^{-1-2\alpha^{\prime}}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{s+1+2\alpha^{\prime}}\left\lVert[P_{\varepsilon},\Delta Q]g\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

Furthermore, applying the commutator estimate of
Lemma
6.1
-(ii) (with
r=0
and
\rho=1
),
it follows that

\left\lVert[P_{\varepsilon},\Delta Q]g\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\lesssim\varepsilon\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

Combining these bounds, and noting
that
\left\lVert(g-m)^{\alpha}\right\rVert_{H^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}}\leq C(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}})

(see Proposition
5.8
), we conclude
that
\left\lvert\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}\right\rvert
satisfies (
6.8
), as required.

Step 2: estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
.
We now address the
estimate of
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
. This is where we shall rely
on the microlocal analysis of cusps established in Section
5
.

According to Proposition
5.8
, the function
(g-m)^{\alpha}

decomposes as
(g-m)^{\alpha}=H+R
, where
H=\chi\mathfrak{q}^{\alpha}

is the singular profile analyzed in Lemma
5.5
. The remainder
R

belongs to
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
k=\min(2\alpha^{\prime}+2,s-3)
for any

\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
. The scalar product (
6.7
) thus splits as:

(6.9)

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}=\left\langle\Delta QP_{\varepsilon}g,H\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\left\langle\Delta QP_{\varepsilon}g,R\right\rangle_{L^{2}}.

The second term in (
6.9
) is estimated by
H^{-k}\times H^{k}

duality. Since
\Delta Q
is an operator of order
0
and
P_{\varepsilon}

is a spectral projector localized at frequencies
|n|\geq 1/\varepsilon
, we have:

\left\lvert\langle\Delta QP_{\varepsilon}g,R\rangle_{L^{2}}\right\rvert\lesssim\left\lVert P_{\varepsilon}g\right\rVert_{H^{-k}}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{H^{k}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{s+k}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\left\lVert R\right\rVert_{H^{k}}.

By assumptions on
\beta
, we have
k>1+2\beta
and hence

s+k>s+1+2\beta
, so that this term is
o(\varepsilon^{s+1+2\beta})

and is absorbed into the final error.

We now focus on the first term in the right-hand side of (
6.9
).
By the self-adjointness of
P_{\varepsilon}
and the symbol-freezing
result (Lemma
5.9
), we have:

(6.10)

\langle\Delta QP_{\varepsilon}g,H\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle P_{\varepsilon}g,(\Delta Q)^{*}H\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle P_{\varepsilon}g,a(0,D)H\rangle_{L^{2}}+\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}.

Here
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}=\langle P_{\varepsilon}g,\mathcal{R}\rangle_{L^{2}}

where
\mathcal{R}
is the smoothing remainder in
H^{k}
from
Lemma
5.9
. As with the term in
R
, duality
ensures
|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}|=O(\varepsilon^{s+k})
so
|\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}|=o(\varepsilon^{s+1+2\beta})
.

Using Parseval’s identity and the fact that both
a(0,n)

and
\hat{H}(n)
are real and even, the main term in (
6.10
) satisfies:

(6.11)

\langle P_{\varepsilon}g,a(0,D)H\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(1-\theta(\varepsilon n))\big(\RE\hat{g}(n)\big)a(0,n)\hat{H}(n),

where recall that
P_{\varepsilon}=\Id-\theta(\varepsilon D_{x})
. We now apply the lower bounds established for each factor:

•

From property (
i
) of
Definition
5.1
,
\RE\hat{g}(n)\geq c|n|^{-s}(|n|\ln|n|)^{-1}
.

•

From Lemma
5.5
,
\hat{H}(n)\geq c^{\prime}|n|^{-2(1+\alpha)}
.

•

From the ellipticity of the parametrix (cf Lemma
3.6
),
a(0,n)\geq c^{\prime\prime}>0
.

•

By definition of
\theta
,
1-\theta(\varepsilon n)=1
for
|n|\geq 1/\varepsilon
.

Substituting these into (
6.11
), every term in the sum is positive for large
|n|
, and we obtain:

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}\geq C\sum_{|n|\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{|n|^{s+3+2\alpha}\ln|n|}.

Applying an integral comparison in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}\geq C\int_{1/\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{r}{r^{s+3+2\alpha}\ln r}\diff\!r\geq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\ln(1/\varepsilon)}\varepsilon^{s+1+2\alpha}.

Since
\beta>\alpha
, the term
\varepsilon^{s+1+2\alpha}|\ln\varepsilon|^{-1}

is dominant over
\varepsilon^{s+1+2\beta}
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.
Combined with the fact that the remainders are of order
O(\varepsilon^{s+k})

with
k>1+2\beta
, we conclude that (
6.7
) holds.
∎

6.4.
A
\nabla^{\perp}
cancellation

Here, we prove an estimate which allows us to handle the most troublesome
term in the equation for
\curl X
coming from the linear
propagator
X\mapsto\nabla^{\perp}Q(T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot X)
.
The analysis relies upon the following observation:

(6.12)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\nabla^{\perp}f\cdot\nabla g)f\diff\!x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\nabla^{\perp}(f^{2})\cdot\nabla g\diff\!x=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\nabla\cdot(\nabla^{\perp}f^{2})g\diff\!x=0.

Lemma 6.4
.

Consider three real numbers
s,\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
such that

s-4>2\alpha,\quad\alpha>\alpha^{\prime}>0.

There exists a non-decreasing function
C\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

such that, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and for all
\sigma\in H^{s+1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

and
g\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,

\left\lvert\langle T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla\sigma,P_{\varepsilon}(g-m)^{\alpha}\rangle_{L^{2}}\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon^{s+\mu}C(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}},

where
m=\inf g
and
\mu=\min\{2+2\alpha^{\prime},s-3\}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
6.3
, we use a slightly wider spectral projector
\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}=P_{\lambda\varepsilon}

where
\lambda>1
is chosen large enough to ensure that

P_{\varepsilon}\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}=P_{\varepsilon}
as well as

(6.13)

T_{a}P_{\varepsilon}b=\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}T_{a}P_{\varepsilon}b,\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\varepsilon}T_{a}b=P_{\varepsilon}T_{a}\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}b.

Setting
h\coloneqq(g-m)^{\alpha}
and observing that
P_{\varepsilon}
is
self-adjoint, we decompose the integral

I\coloneqq\langle P_{\varepsilon}h,T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla\sigma\rangle_{L^{2}}

by introducing a commutator term as follows:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}P_{\varepsilon}h\,(T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla\sigma)\diff\!x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}h\,T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla(P_{\varepsilon}\sigma)\diff\!x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}h\,[P_{\varepsilon},T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}]\cdot\nabla\sigma\diff\!x

\displaystyle\coloneqq I_{1}+I_{2}.

We first address the commutator term
I_{2}
. Setting
\Sigma\coloneqq\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}\sigma
,
we observe that
identity (
6.13
) implies that

[P_{\varepsilon},T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}]\cdot\nabla\sigma=[P_{\varepsilon},T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}]\cdot\nabla\Sigma.

Since
g\in H^{s}
with
s>4
, the vector field
\nabla^{\perp}g

belongs to
C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Applying Lemma
6.1
-(ii)
with the parameters
r=1
and
\rho=0
, we observe that
[P_{\varepsilon},T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}]\cdot\nabla

defines a bounded operator from
H^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

with an operator norm
O(1)
relative to
\varepsilon
. Consequently, there exists a constant
K>0
, independent of
\varepsilon
,
such that

(6.14)

\left\lVert[P_{\varepsilon},T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}]\cdot\nabla\Sigma\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq K\big\lVert\nabla^{\perp}g\big\rVert_{C^{1}}\left\lVert\nabla\Sigma\right\rVert_{H^{-1}}\lesssim\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{C^{2}}\left\lVert\Sigma\right\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Recalling Lemma
6.1
, we have

\left\lVert\Sigma\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{s+1}\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}
.
Similarly, for the singular profile
h
, we have

\big\lVert\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}h\big\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{H^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}}
.
Combining these bounds, we obtain

|I_{2}|\lesssim\varepsilon^{s+2+2\alpha^{\prime}}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{C^{2}}\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{H^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}}.

Now, remembering
from Proposition
5.8
that

\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{H^{1+2\alpha^{\prime}}}\leq C(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}})
, we conclude that

(6.15)

|I_{2}|\leq C\varepsilon^{s+2+2\alpha^{\prime}}\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}

for some constant
C
depending only on
\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}}
.

Analysis of the principal term
I_{1}
.

We now proceed to estimate the term
I_{1}
. Using again (
6.13
) and the fact that
\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}

is self-adjoint and satisfies
\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}P_{\varepsilon}=P_{\varepsilon}
, we can rewrite
I_{1}
as

I_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}h\,T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla(P_{\varepsilon}\sigma)\diff\!x.

We now invoke Bony’s paralinearization formula to decompose

h=H(g)
into two components:

h=\underbrace{T_{H^{\prime}(g)}g}_{h_{\textrm{LF}}}+\underbrace{\left(H(g)-T_{H^{\prime}(g)}g\right)}_{h_{\textrm{HF}}}.

The contribution of
h_{\textrm{LF}}
is estimated
directly. Let
\gamma\coloneqq H^{\prime}(g)=\alpha(g-m)^{\alpha-1}
.
Notice that since
\alpha-1>-1
, the function
H^{\prime}(g)
belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
Since paraproducts by
L^{\infty}
functions define operators of order zero
while
paraproducts by
L^{1}
functions are operators of order
2
(cf. Theorem
A.1
), we have:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\langle T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla P_{\varepsilon}\sigma,\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}T_{\gamma}g\rangle_{L^{2}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla P_{\varepsilon}\sigma\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}T_{\gamma}g\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{s}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{C^{1}}\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}\cdot\varepsilon^{s-2}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{L^{1}}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2s-2}C(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}.

The analysis of the high-frequency remainder
h_{\textrm{HF}}\coloneqq H(g)-T_{H^{\prime}(g)}g

requires a more refined treatment. We first perform a
paradifferential integration by parts. Specifically, we observe that, for the divergence-free
vector field
v=\nabla^{\perp}g
, the adjoint of the transport
operator
T_{v}\cdot\nabla
satisfies:

\displaystyle(T_{v}\cdot\nabla)^{*}u

\displaystyle=-\cn(T_{v}^{*}u)

\displaystyle=-\cn(T_{v}u)-\cn((T_{v}^{*}-T_{v})u)

\displaystyle=-T_{v}\cdot\nabla u-(T_{v}^{*}-T_{v})\cdot\nabla u,

where the vanishing of
\cn v
has been used to identify the
principal part. This identity yields the following decomposition
for the high-frequency integral:

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla P_{\varepsilon}\sigma\,\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}h_{\textrm{HF}}\diff\!x=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}P_{\varepsilon}\sigma\,T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla(\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}h_{\textrm{HF}})\diff\!x\\
+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}P_{\varepsilon}\sigma\,(T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}-T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}^{*})\cdot\nabla(\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}h_{\textrm{HF}})\diff\!x.

The second term on the right-hand side, denoted hereafter
R_{\text{adj}}
, is controlled by

\displaystyle|R_{\text{adj}}|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert P_{\varepsilon}\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{-2\alpha^{\prime}-(s-2)}}\left\lVert(T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}-T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}^{*})\cdot\nabla(\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}h_{\textrm{HF}})\right\rVert_{H^{2\alpha^{\prime}+(s-2)}}.

From Lemma
6.1
, we have
\left\lVert P_{\varepsilon}\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{-2\alpha^{\prime}-(s-2)}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{s+1+(2\alpha^{\prime}+s-2)}\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}
.
Moreover, Theorem
A.3
implies that

\left\lVert(T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}-T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}^{*})\cdot\nabla(\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}h_{\textrm{HF}})\right\rVert_{H^{2\alpha^{\prime}+(s-2)}}\lesssim\left\lVert\nabla g\right\rVert_{C^{s-2}}\left\lVert\nabla(\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}h_{\textrm{HF}})\right\rVert_{H^{2\alpha^{\prime}}}\leq C(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}}).

Combining these bounds, we arrive at

(6.16)

|R_{\text{adj}}|\leq\varepsilon^{2s+2\alpha^{\prime}-1}C(\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}.

Analysis of the principal term and Poisson cancellation.

It remains to estimate the principal contribution to
I_{1}
:

(6.17)

\mathcal{J}\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}P_{\varepsilon}\sigma\,\big(T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla h_{\textrm{HF}}\big)\diff\!x=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}P_{\varepsilon}\sigma\,\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}\big(T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla h_{\textrm{HF}}\big)\diff\!x.

The control of this term relies on a paradifferential variant
of the geometric cancellation (
6.12
). We
first expand the gradient of the high-frequency remainder

h_{\textrm{HF}}=H(g)-T_{H^{\prime}(g)}g
. By the paralinearization
formula (Theorem
A.2
), the derivative of
H(g)
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla(H(g))

\displaystyle=(H^{\prime}\circ g)\nabla g

\displaystyle=T_{H^{\prime}(g)}\nabla g+T_{\nabla g}H^{\prime}(g)+R_{B}\left(\nabla g,H^{\prime}(g)\right).

Substituting this into the definition of
h_{\textrm{HF}}
, and
noting that
\nabla T_{H^{\prime}(g)}g=T_{\nabla(H^{\prime}(g))}g+T_{H^{\prime}(g)}\nabla g
,
we obtain:

\displaystyle\nabla h_{\textrm{HF}}

\displaystyle=T_{\nabla g}H^{\prime}(g)+R_{B}\left(\nabla g,H^{\prime}(g)\right)-T_{\nabla(H^{\prime}(g))}g.

We make the following claim regarding the regularity of the
advected remainder.

Claim.

For any
\nu>0
, the following
estimate holds:

(6.18)

\left\lVert T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla h_{\textrm{HF}}\right\rVert_{H^{s-3-\nu}}\lesssim_{s,\nu}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}\left\lVert H^{\prime}(g)\right\rVert_{L^{1}}.

Proof of the claim.
We analyze each
component of
\nabla h_{\textrm{HF}}
individually. Since
H^{\prime}(g)
belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, by the
continuity of the Bony remainder (Theorem
A.2
),
we have

\left\lVert R_{B}\left(\nabla g,H^{\prime}(g)\right)\right\rVert_{H^{s-3}}\lesssim\left\lVert\nabla g\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}\left\lVert H^{\prime}(g)\right\rVert_{L^{1}}.

Furthermore, we recall that Bernstein’s inequality ensures the
embedding
L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\hookrightarrow C^{-2}_{*}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
It follows that the distribution
\nabla(H^{\prime}(g))
belongs to
C^{-3}_{*}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
Consequently,
the continuity properties of the paraproduct stated in
Theorem
A.1
yield the following estimate:

\left\lVert T_{\nabla(H^{\prime}(g))}g\right\rVert_{H^{s-3}}\lesssim\left\lVert\nabla(H^{\prime}(g))\right\rVert_{C^{-3}_{*}}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\lesssim\left\lVert H^{\prime}(g)\right\rVert_{L^{1}}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

It remains to estimate the composition of
paraproducts
T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot T_{\nabla g}H^{\prime}(g)
.
By the symbolic calculus for paraproducts (Theorem
A.3
),
the product of two paraproducts is the paraproduct of the
product of their symbols, up to a smoothing remainder:

T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot T_{\nabla g}=T_{\nabla^{\perp}g\cdot\nabla g}+R_{\mathrm{CM}}(\nabla^{\perp}g,\nabla g).

Now observe that
\nabla^{\perp}g\cdot\nabla g\equiv 0
, thus the composition is determined solely by the commutator
remainder
R_{\mathrm{CM}}
. Since
H^{\prime}(g)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and since
L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\subset H^{-1-\nu}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for any
\nu>0
,
we deduce that

\left\lVert T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot T_{\nabla g}H^{\prime}(g)\right\rVert_{H^{s-3-\nu}}\lesssim\left\lVert\nabla g\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}^{2}\left\lVert H^{\prime}(g)\right\rVert_{L^{1}}.

This establishes the claim (
6.18
).

Conclusion of the estimate.
Once
the claim is granted, we write

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{J}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert P_{\varepsilon}\sigma\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\tilde{P}_{\varepsilon}\big(T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla h_{\textrm{HF}}\big)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{s+1}\left\lVert\sigma\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}\cdot\varepsilon^{s-4}\left\lVert T_{\nabla^{\perp}g}\cdot\nabla h_{\textrm{HF}}\right\rVert_{H^{s-4}}.

Substituting (
6.18
) with
\nu=1
into the above, we
conclude that the principal term
\mathcal{J}
is of the
order
O(\varepsilon^{2s-3})
, which completes the proof of the
Lemma.
∎

7.
Small scale creation

We are now at a position to prove the following proposition, which shows that
for
all
initial vorticity in the space
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
(see Definition
5.1
) of functions with
finite regularity,
small scale creation is inevitable for the flow.

Proposition 7.1
.

Consider a real number
s>4
and an initial data
\omega_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Then

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\left\lVert|D|^{s}\Phi_{t}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=+\infty.

Proof.

We prove this proposition by contradiction. Assume that

(H)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert|D|^{s}\Phi_{t}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}<+\infty.

Step
1
: Preliminary estimates.

We claim that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert\Phi_{t}-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<+\infty.

Indeed, observe that

\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}\Phi=u\circ\Phi\Longrightarrow\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\Phi-\Id)\diff\!x=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u\circ\Phi\diff\!x=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u\diff\!x=0,

where we used
\det(D\Phi)=1
and the fact that and
u
has mean-value
zero since
u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega
. Now
the claim follows from the Poincaré inequality for
functions with mean-value zero on the torus.

Since the diffeomorphism
\Phi_{t}
belongs to
\mathrm{SDiff}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
(see
Definition
3.1
), the incompressibility constraint

\det(D\Phi_{t})=1
ensures that,
in the
two-dimensional setting, the entries of
(D\Phi_{t})^{-1}
are merely
a signed rearrangement of those of
D\Phi_{t}
, which allows for a
direct control of
D(\Phi_{t}^{-1})
in
H^{s-1}

by
D\Phi_{t}
(see Proposition
B.2
). Given
the transport identity
\omega(t,\cdot)=\omega_{0}\circ\Phi_{t}^{-1}
, this
yields a uniform estimate for the inhomogeneous Sobolev norm
of the vorticity:

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert\omega(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<+\infty,

which in turn implies that the velocity
u
,
defined by
u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega
, satisfies

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}<+\infty.

We shall make repeated use of the fact that we have a similar
estimate for
u\circ\Phi
. Namely, it follows from
Proposition
B.2
and the previous estimates that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert u\circ\Phi_{t}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<+\infty.

Hereafter, we set

(7.1)

M\coloneqq\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert\omega(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert\Phi_{t}-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert u\circ\Phi_{t}\right\rVert_{H^{s}},

and denote by
C=C(M)
various constants depending only on
M
and
s
.

Step
2
: The subprincipal terms.

The
\nabla^{\perp}
structure highlighted in Proposition
4.1
naturally suggest to
study of the curl and divergence
of
X=T_{(D\Phi)^{-1}}\Phi
.
Focusing first on the curl, which was studied
in
[
37
,
34
]
, the evolution equation
established in Proposition
4.3
reads

\partial_{t}\curl X+\Delta QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot X=\Delta Q\omega_{0}-\curl\mathcal{R},

where we used
the identity
\curl(\nabla^{\perp}f)=\Delta f
.
The next step consists in decomposing
the term
\Delta QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot X

into components depending on
\curl X
and
\cn X
.
To do this, we use the Helmholtz decomposition (remembering that
X
has zero average):

X=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\curl X+\nabla\Delta^{-1}\cn(X),

thus

\partial_{t}\curl X+\Delta QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\curl X=\Delta Q\omega_{0}-\curl\mathcal{R}-\Delta QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}\cn(X).

As we saw in Section
6
,
there is a key cancellation due to the operator

T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}
. To exploit
this, we commute this operator with
\Delta Q
, to get

(7.2)

\partial_{t}\curl X+T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\Delta Q\Delta^{-1}\curl X=\Delta Q\omega_{0}-R_{\textrm{tot}},

with

(7.3)

R_{\textrm{tot}}=\left[\Delta Q,T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\right]\Delta^{-1}\curl X+\curl\mathcal{R}+\Delta QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}\cn(X).

Step
3
: Estimate of the remainder.

Let

\delta=\min\{s-3,1\}>0.

Our goal here is to show the estimate

(7.4)

\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})R_{\textrm{tot}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C(M)\left(\varepsilon^{s+\delta}+t\varepsilon^{s+1+\delta}\right),

where recall that
M
is as defined in (
7.1
).
We will make extensive use of classical results about Friedrichs mollifiers gathered in the previous section.
In particular,

(7.5)

\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})f\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{\sigma}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{\sigma}}.

We begin by estimating the divergence term in (
7.3
). To do so,
we first apply the high-frequency
decay estimate (Lemma
6.1
-(i)) with index
2s-1
.
Since the operator
\Delta QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}
is of order
-1
, we have:

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})[\Delta QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}\cn(X)]\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C\varepsilon^{2s-2}\left\lVert\Delta QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}\cn(X)\right\rVert_{H^{2s-2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C\varepsilon^{2s-2}\left(1+\left\lVert\Phi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\right)\left\lVert\nabla\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert\cn(X)\right\rVert_{H^{2s-3}}.

To estimate the divergence of
X
, we recall from Proposition
4.1
that

\partial_{t}X=\nabla^{\perp}(\Phi^{*}\psi)-R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\Phi)^{-1},u\circ\Phi).

Since
\cn(\nabla^{\perp}\cdot)=0
and
X(0)=T_{\Id}\Id=0
(by convention (
3.2
)), integrating in time yields:

\cn(X(t))=-\int_{0}^{t}\cn\left(R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\Phi)^{-1},\nabla^{\perp}\psi\circ\Phi)\right)\diff\!\tau.

We now apply the Bony remainder estimate (Theorem
A.2
).
Under hypothesis (
H
), we have
(D\Phi)^{-1}\in H^{s-1}

and
u\circ\Phi\in H^{s}
uniformly in time. Since
s>2
,
the remainder belongs to
H^{(s-1)+s-1}=H^{2s-2}
.
Taking the divergence, we see that the integrand is in
H^{2s-3}
and hence

\left\lVert\cn(X)\right\rVert_{H^{2s-3}}\leq C(M)t.

Combining these estimates, and since the norms are bounded by
M
under hypothesis (
H
), we conclude:

\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})[\Delta QT_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla\Delta^{-1}\cn(X)]\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C(M)t\varepsilon^{2s-2}\leq C(M)t\varepsilon^{s+1+\delta}.

We now move to the commutator term in (
7.3
). By using
the representation of
Q
given by Lemma
3.6
, we see that

[\Delta Q,T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}]=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}[\Delta T_{m_{k}}B^{k}\Delta^{-1},T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}],

applying Theorem
A.5

for the commutators of Fourier multipliers with paraproducts gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[\Delta Q,T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\right]\Delta^{-1}\curl X\right\rVert_{H^{s+\delta}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k^{4}\left\lVert m_{k}\right\rVert_{C^{\delta}}\right)\left\lVert\nabla\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{C^{\delta}}\left\lVert\curl X\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k^{4}e^{-k\delta^{\prime}}\right)\mathcal{F}\left(\left\lVert\Phi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\right)\left\lVert\nabla\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}\left\lVert\curl X\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}},

where we used the Sobolev injection and the estimate (
3.9
)
for
m_{k}
. Now, by definition of
X
, and the continuity of
paraproducts (see (
A.3
)) and Proposition
B.2
, we have

\left\lVert\curl X\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}\lesssim\left\lVert(D\Phi)^{-1}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert D\Phi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}\leq C(M).

Thus by Hypothesis (
H
) and the estimate (
7.5
), we get

\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})\left[\Delta Q,T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\right]\Delta^{-1}\curl X\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C(M)\varepsilon^{s+\delta}.

It remains only to estimate
\curl\mathcal{R}
in (
7.3
).
The latter contains four terms given in Proposition
4.3
that we
recall here:

\displaystyle\curl\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=\Delta\left(S(\Phi^{*}\psi)+QR_{\triangle}(\Phi,\psi)+Q\left(R_{\mathrm{alg}}\left((D\Phi)^{-\top},\nabla\omega_{0}\right)\cdot\Phi\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\curl R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\Phi)^{-1},u\circ\Phi),

and estimate them all separately. For the first term we recall from (
4.4
)

\Delta\left(S(\Phi^{*}\psi)\right)=\Delta\left(S\left(Q(\Phi^{*}\omega)-S(\Phi^{*}\psi)-QR_{\triangle}(\Phi,\psi)\right)\right),

hence by Lemma
3.6

\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})\Delta\left(S(Q(\Phi^{*}\omega))\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{s+\delta}\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert\Phi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C(M)\varepsilon^{s+\delta},

and applying Proposition
3.5
with
f=\psi
and
\mu=s+2

\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})\Delta\left(SQR_{\triangle}(\Phi,\psi)\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C\varepsilon^{2s-2}\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert\Phi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert_{H^{s+2}}\leq C(M)\varepsilon^{s+\delta},

for the term
\Delta\left(S^{2}(\Phi^{*}\psi)\right))
we iterate the previous computation to get

\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})\Delta\left(S(\Phi^{*}\psi)\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C(M)\varepsilon^{s+\delta}.

For the term after we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})\Delta\left(Q\left(R_{\mathrm{alg}}\left((D\Phi)^{-\top},\nabla\omega_{0}\right)\cdot\Phi\right)\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(M)\varepsilon^{s+1}\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert\Phi-\Id\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\big\lVert(D\Phi)^{-\top}\big\rVert_{C^{\delta}}\left\lVert\nabla\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{C^{\delta}}\left\lVert\Phi\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C(M)\varepsilon^{s+\delta},

and finally for the last term again by the Bony remainder estimate

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\Id-J_{\varepsilon})\curl R_{\mathcal{B}}((D\Phi)^{-1},u\circ\Phi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{2s-3}\left\lVert(D\Phi)^{-1}\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}\left\lVert u\circ\Phi\right\rVert_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq C(M)\varepsilon^{s+\delta}.

This completes the analysis of the remainder term
R_{\textrm{tot}}
.

Step
4
: Conclusion.

Once the estimate of
R_{\textrm{tot}}
is granted, we conclude the proof of Proposition
7.1

by exploiting the structure identified in Section
6
, that is the equation

(7.6)

\partial_{t}\curl X+T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\Delta Q\Delta^{-1}\curl X=\Delta Q\omega_{0}-R_{\textrm{tot}}.

Denote by
m_{0}
the unique non degenerate minimum
of
\omega_{0}

and consider a
cusp exponent
\alpha
satisfying

0<2\alpha<s-4,\quad 2\alpha\notin\mathbb{N}.

Set
V\coloneqq(\omega_{0}-m_{0})^{\alpha}
.
By combining the preceding estimates, we are now at a position to
deduce that the time derivative of the
L^{2}
inner product of
\curl X
and
P_{\varepsilon}V

grows without bound, which contradicts the hypothesis (
H
). Indeed, let us
consider the functional

y(t)\coloneqq\langle\curl X(t),P_{\varepsilon}V\rangle_{L^{2}}

whose time derivative satisfies

y^{\prime}(t)=\langle\Delta Q\omega_{0},P_{\varepsilon}V\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle R_{\textrm{tot}},P_{\varepsilon}V\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle T_{\nabla\omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla^{\perp}\Delta Q\Delta^{-1}\curl X,P_{\varepsilon}V\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Using the lower bound established in
Proposition
6.3
, the first term on the right-hand side
provides a strictly positive driving term of order

\varepsilon^{s+1+2\beta}
for any
\beta>\alpha
. Conversely, the results of
Proposition
6.2
and Lemma
6.4
ensure that the
remaining terms are of higher order in
\varepsilon
. Specifically, there
exist constants
\gamma>0
,
c>0
and
\nu>0
such that, for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small
and
t\leq\varepsilon^{-1-\nu}
, we have

y^{\prime}(t)\geq c\varepsilon^{s+1+2\beta}-C(M)(\varepsilon^{s+1+2\alpha+\gamma}+t\varepsilon^{s+2+2\alpha+\gamma})\geq\frac{c}{2}\varepsilon^{s+1+2\beta}.

By integrating this inequality over the interval
[0,\varepsilon^{-1-\nu}]
,
we obtain the lower bound

(7.7)

\sup y(t)\geq\frac{c}{2}\varepsilon^{s+2\beta-\nu}.

However, under the stability hypothesis (
H
), the vector
field
X(t)
remains bounded in
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

(uniformly in time), which implies
that
\curl X(t)
is bounded in
H^{s-1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. A direct
application of Proposition
6.2
then yields that, for any
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
and any time
t
,

\left\lvert y(t)\right\rvert\leq C(M)\varepsilon^{s+2\alpha^{\prime}}.

Comparing this with (
7.7
), we arrive at a contradiction as
soon as
2\beta-\nu<2\alpha
, provided
\varepsilon
is chosen
sufficiently small. It follows that the Sobolev norm of the
flow cannot remain bounded in time, which concludes the proof.
∎

8.
Proof of the main theorem: the dichotomy

In this section, we analyze the long-time behavior of the solution starting
from a specific initial vorticity
\omega_{0}
in
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

(see Definition
5.1
). Our objective is to establish the following dichotomy: either the vorticity
blows up in
H^{s}
, or
D\Phi
blows up in the
L^{\infty}
norm (which is equivalent
to saying that the differential of the inverse flow
\varphi=\Phi^{-1}
blows up).
Recall that we say that a function
t\mapsto f(t)
defined for all time
t\geq 0
blows
up in a space
X
if
\limsup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert f(t)\right\rVert_{X}=+\infty
.

Recall from paragraph
2.2
the following notation.
For any
g\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, we denote by
\Phi_{t}^{g}
the flow of the
Euler equation associated with an initial data
u_{0}\in H^{s+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

satisfying
\cn u_{0}=0
and
\curl u_{0}=g
. Hereafter, we denote by
\varphi_{t}^{g}
the inverse flow given by

\varphi_{t}^{g}=(\Phi_{t}^{g})^{-1}
. In particular,
\omega=g\circ\varphi_{t}^{g}

solves the Euler equation (in vorticity formulation):

\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t}+u\cdot\nabla\omega=0\quad\text{with}\quad u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega=(\partial_{t}\Phi^{g})\circ\varphi_{t}^{g}.

Proposition 8.1
.

Consider an initial data
\omega_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. For any radius
r>0

and any threshold
N>0
, one of the following two
assertions holds:

(1)

Growth of the vorticity:
There exists an initial data

f_{0}\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)
such that

(8.1)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert f_{0}\circ\varphi_{t}^{f_{0}}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}>N.

(2)

Growth of the flow:
The inverse flow
\varphi_{t}^{\omega_{0}}
satisfies

(8.2)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert D\varphi_{t}^{\omega_{0}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=+\infty.

Strategy of the proof.

Before entering into the details of the proof, we explain its strategy.
We proceed by contradiction. Let us fix
r>0
and
N>0
. Assume that
neither condition holds. Specifically, we suppose that for all

f_{0}\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)
, the solution satisfies

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\lVert f_{0}\circ\varphi_{t}^{f_{0}}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq N
, and that
the reference flow satisfies
\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert D\varphi_{t}^{\omega_{0}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}<+\infty
.
As established in the previous sections (see Proposition
7.1
),
if the vorticity remains bounded while the initial data belongs to
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
the flow map must exhibit unbounded
growth in
H^{s}
. Specifically, we have the following result
for any
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\cap\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)
, there holds

(8.3)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\lVert\Phi_{t}^{f_{0}}-\Id\big\rVert_{H^{s}}=+\infty.

Note that, in light of Proposition
B.2
(see (
B.4
)), the blow-up (
8.3
) is equivalent to

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\lVert\varphi_{t}^{f_{0}}-\Id\big\rVert_{H^{s}}=+\infty.

Now by writing

\varphi_{t}^{f_{0}}\circ\Phi_{t}^{f_{0}}=\Id\Longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\varphi_{t}^{f_{0}}\circ\Phi_{t}^{f_{0}}\diff\!x=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\Id\diff\!x\Longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\varphi_{t}^{f_{0}}-\Id)\diff\!x=0,

we get by the Poincaré inequality

(8.4)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\lVert|D|^{s}\varphi_{t}^{f_{0}}\big\rVert_{L^{2}}=+\infty.

It is this explosion of the flow map in the Sobolev norm that we shall
exploit to derive a contradiction.

The proof relies on a topological argument involving the Schauder fixed
point theorem. Let
P
be the orthogonal projector in
H^{s}
onto the space spanned
by the function
(x^{1},x^{2})\mapsto\cos(x^{1})
.
For any
f\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)
, we define a "regularized" reference vorticity

\tilde{f}\coloneqq\omega_{0}+P(f-\omega_{0}).

Notice that, for
r
sufficiently small (which can be assumed without loss of generality),

\omega_{0}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
if and only if
\tilde{f}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

Let
\tilde{\varphi}_{t}=(\Phi_{t}^{\tilde{f}})^{-1}
be the inverse of the
flow generated by
\tilde{f}
. By hypothesis, the solution
t\mapsto\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{\varphi}_{t}

remains bounded in
H^{s}_{0}
. We claim that we can construct a specific
perturbation
\eta=\eta(\tilde{f})\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
of the initial data tailored to resonate
with the stretching of
\tilde{\varphi}_{t}
.

Claim 1.

There exists a perturbation
\eta\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
satisfying
the three properties:

(1)

Strict Stability:

\big\lVert\tilde{f}+\eta-\omega_{0}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}<r
.

(2)

Norm Inflation:

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\lVert(\tilde{f}+\eta)\circ\tilde{\varphi}_{t}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}>N
.

(3)

Phase condition:

\hat{\eta}(e_{1})\neq 0
where
e_{1}=(1,0)
.

Assuming this claim holds, we consider the map
\mathcal{F}\colon\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)\to H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

defined by

\mathcal{F}(f)\coloneqq\tilde{f}+\eta(\tilde{f})=\omega_{0}+P(f-\omega_{0})+\eta(\tilde{f}).

We further claim that one can choose the perturbation so that

Claim 2.

The mapping
f\mapsto\mathcal{F}(f)
is continuous
and its image is contained in a compact subset of
\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)
.

Schauder’s fixed point theorem then ensures the existence of a fixed point

f^{*}\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)
such that
\mathcal{F}(f^{*})=f^{*}
.
Substituting the definition of
\mathcal{F}
, this identity reads:

\omega_{0}+P(f^{*}-\omega_{0})+\eta(\tilde{f}^{*})=f^{*}.

Rearranging the terms, we find that
\eta(\tilde{f}^{*})=f^{*}-\omega_{0}-P(f^{*}-\omega_{0})
.
Observing that the right-hand side is simply
(\Id-P)(f^{*}-\omega_{0})
,
we apply the projector
P
to the equation. Since
P(\Id-P)=0
, we obtain

P\eta(\tilde{f}^{*})=0.

However, the third property of Claim 1 implies that
\hat{\eta}(\tilde{f}^{*})(e_{1})\neq 0
, which ensures
P\eta\neq 0
and
this is the wanted contradiction.
The proof of Proposition
8.1
is thus reduced to establishing Claim 1
and Claim 2, which we address in the following subsections. Claim 1
is proved in subsection
8.2
and Claim 2 is proved in subsection
8.3
.
As a preparation, in subsection
8.1
, we construct an auxiliary function used to define the perturbation.
∎

8.1.
Construction of the Perturbation

We start by defining an auxiliary function used to define a perturbation of the initial data.

Let
\theta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a standard radial cut-off function,
satisfying
\theta(y)=1
for
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\leq 1/2
and
\theta(y)=0
for
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq 1
.
We define the scalar localized perturbation
\zeta_{\delta,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})}\colon\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

associated with a position
y_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
, an amplitude
\delta>0
,
a localization scale
\rho\in(0,1)
, and a direction vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(with
\left\lvert\mathbf{v}\right\rvert=1
) by the formula:

(8.5)

\zeta_{\delta,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})}(y)\coloneqq\delta\rho^{s-2}\theta\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\rho}\right)\mathbf{v}\cdot(y-y_{0}).

Here, we identified the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with the periodic box
[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
.
Since the support of the perturbation is of size
\rho<1
, the use of
Euclidean coordinates is unambiguous. The following proposition summarizes the key properties of this
perturbation.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
s>2
be a real number.
The perturbation
\zeta_{\delta,\rho}
satisfies the following properties.

(1)

Sobolev Control:
There exists a constant
C_{s}>0
such that for all
\delta>0
,

\rho\in(0,1)
, all
y_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
and all unit vectors
\mathbf{v}
:

\left\lVert\zeta_{\delta,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq C_{s}\delta.

(2)

Geometric Lower Bound:
if
\varphi\in\mathrm{SDiff}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and

x_{0}=\varphi^{-1}(y_{0})
, then there exist constants
c_{s}>0
(depending only on
s
) and
c_{\varphi}>0

(depending on
\left\lVert D\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
) such that

\left\lVert\zeta_{\delta,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})}\circ\varphi\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\geq c_{s}\,\delta\,\rho^{s-2}\,\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\cdot\varphi\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}(B(x_{0},c_{\varphi}\rho))}.

(3)

Generic Scales:
For almost every scale
\rho\in(0,1)
,
the Fourier coefficients satisfy the non-vanishing condition

\hat{\zeta}_{\delta,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})}(k)\neq 0\quad\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }\mathbf{v}\cdot k\neq 0.

Proof.

For the sake of notational simplicity, we simply write
\zeta
instead of
\zeta_{\delta,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})}
.

1. Sobolev Control.

Since
\rho<1
, the support of
\zeta

is contained in a ball of radius smaller than
\pi
.
Consequently, we may identify
\zeta
with a
function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\left\lVert\zeta\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}=\|\tilde{\zeta}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
with

\tilde{\zeta}(y)=\delta\rho^{s-1}G\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\rho}\right),

where
G(z)\coloneqq\theta(z)\mathbf{v}\cdot z

is a smooth function with compact support in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
We can now compute the homogeneous Sobolev norm
using the standard scaling property in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\left\lVert u(\cdot/\rho)\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\rho^{1-s}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
.
This yields:

\left\lVert\zeta\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}=\|\tilde{\zeta}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\delta\rho^{s-1}\cdot\rho^{1-s}\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\delta\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

For the lower order
L^{2}
norm, the scaling
gives

\|\tilde{\zeta}\|_{L^{2}}=\delta\rho^{s-1}\cdot\rho\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=\delta\rho^{s}\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
.
Combining these estimates gives

\left\lVert\zeta\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C_{s}\delta
.

2. Geometric lower bound.

Introduce the set
\Omega_{\rho}\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}(B(y_{0},\rho/2))
.
For any point
x
in
\Omega_{\rho}
, the cut-off function satisfies

\theta(\rho^{-1}(\varphi(x)-y_{0}))=1
, hence

\zeta(\varphi(x))=\delta\rho^{s-2}\mathbf{v}\cdot(\varphi(x)-y_{0}).

Consequently,

\left\lVert\zeta\circ\varphi\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\geq\left\lVert\zeta\circ\varphi\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}(\Omega_{\rho})}=\delta\rho^{s-2}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\cdot\varphi\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}(\Omega_{\rho})}.

To conclude, it remains to relate the domain
\Omega_{\rho}
to a ball centered at

x_{0}=\varphi^{-1}(y_{0})
. Since
\varphi
is a diffeomorphism of class
C^{1}
,
standard estimates ensure the existence of a radius
r>0
such that

B(x_{0},r)\subset\varphi^{-1}(B(y_{0},\rho/2))
. Specifically, this inclusion holds
provided that

r\leq\frac{\rho}{2\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left\lvert D\varphi(x)\right\rvert},

where
\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert
denotes the operator norm. By choosing
c_{\varphi}
sufficiently small
with respect to
\left\lVert D\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{-1}
, we ensure that the ball

B(x_{0},c_{\varphi}\rho)
is contained in
\Omega_{\rho}
. The desired inequality follows
by restricting the integration domain to this ball.

3. Generic Scales.
Finally, we address the generic
non-vanishing of Fourier coefficients. Since
\rho<1
, the support of
\zeta

is localized in
B(y_{0},\rho)
, allowing us to compute the Fourier transform as an
integral over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The Fourier transform is given by the integral formula

\hat{\zeta}(k)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\delta\rho^{s-2}\theta\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\rho}\right)\mathbf{v}\cdot(y-y_{0})e^{-ik\cdot y}\diff\!y.

We perform the change of variables
y=y_{0}+\rho z
, to get

\hat{\zeta}(k)=\frac{\delta\rho^{s+1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{-ik\cdot y_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\theta(z)(\mathbf{v}\cdot z)e^{-ik\cdot(\rho z)}\diff\!z.

Observing that multiplication by
z_{j}
corresponds to
i\partial_{\xi_{j}}
in frequency domain, the integral
is equal to
i\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}\widehat{\theta}(\rho k)
.
Since
\theta
is radial, its Fourier transform is of the form

\widehat{\theta}(\xi)=\Psi(\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert)
. Computing the gradient, we obtain

(8.6)

\hat{\zeta}(k)=i\frac{\delta\rho^{s+1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{-ik\cdot y_{0}}(\mathbf{v}\cdot k)\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\rho\left\lvert k\right\rvert)}{\left\lvert k\right\rvert}.

Since
\mathbf{v}\cdot k\neq 0
by assumption, the coefficient
\hat{\zeta}_{\delta,\rho}(k)

vanishes if and only if
\Psi^{\prime}(\rho\left\lvert k\right\rvert)=0
. Now, since
\theta
has compact support,
the profile
\Psi
is real-analytic on
\mathbb{R}
, which proves that the zeros of
\Psi^{\prime}
form a discrete set.
As a result, for any fixed
k
such that
\mathbf{v}\cdot k\neq 0
, the set

\mathcal{B}_{k}\coloneqq\left\{\rho\in(0,1):\Psi^{\prime}(\rho\left\lvert k\right\rvert)=0\right\}
is countable (and finite away from 0).
The union of these sets over all relevant
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
has Lebesgue measure zero. This completes the proof.
∎

8.2.
Proof of Claim 1 (Construction of the Perturbation)

We now turn to the proof of Claim 1. Let
N>0
be the required growth threshold.
Consider an arbitrary function
f\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)
. We define the
defect
u
and the regularized profile
\tilde{f}
by

u\coloneqq P(f-\omega_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{f}\coloneqq\omega_{0}+u.

Since the operator norm of the orthogonal projector
P
is bounded by
1
,
we have
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq\left\lVert f-\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq r
, which implies
that
\tilde{f}\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)
. Furthermore, by our specific
choice of projector,
u
takes the explicit form

u(x)=\alpha\cos(x^{1}),

for some coefficient
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
|\alpha|\left\lVert\cos(x^{1})\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq r
.

We seek a perturbation
\eta
of the form:

\eta=\lambda\zeta_{\delta,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})},

where
\lambda\in\{-1,1\}
is a sign,
\rho\in(0,1)
is a scale,

\delta>0
is an amplitude,
y_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
is a center, and

\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a unit direction vector.
These parameters must be chosen to satisfy the stability condition

\tilde{f}+\eta\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)
and the norm inflation
condition for the flow
\tilde{\varphi}_{t}
.

Step 1: The Interaction Function.

We first compute the scalar product between the defect
u
and the perturbation profile.
Let us fix a scale
\rho\in(0,1)
outside the negligible set of "bad scales"
identified in Proposition
8.2
, ensuring that the Fourier
coefficients of the profile do not vanish.
For any center
y_{0}
and direction
\mathbf{v}
, we define the
function
J(y_{0},\mathbf{v})
as the inner product:

J(y_{0},\mathbf{v})\coloneqq\big\langle u,\zeta_{1,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})}\big\rangle_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}.

To avoid notational confusion, we recall that we denote the first
coordinate by
v^{1}=\mathbf{v}\cdot e_{1}
and
y_{0}^{1}=e_{1}\cdot y_{0}
.
Also,
\zeta_{1,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})}
stands for
\zeta_{\delta,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})}
with
\delta=1
.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
u(x)=\alpha\cos(x^{1})
. Then

J(y_{0},\mathbf{v})=\alpha\,\mathcal{A}(\rho)\,v^{1}\sin(y_{0}^{1}),

where
\mathcal{A}(\rho)\neq 0
is a real coefficient depending only on the profile
\theta
, the scale
\rho
, and the regularity
s
.

Proof.

By Parseval’s identity,
J(y_{0},\mathbf{v})=\sum_{k}(1+|k|^{2})^{s}\hat{u}_{k}\overline{\hat{\zeta}_{k}}
,
where
\hat{\zeta}_{k}
denotes the Fourier coefficient of
\zeta_{1,\rho}^{(y_{0},\mathbf{v})}
.
The function
u
is supported on frequencies
k=\pm e_{1}
, with
\hat{u}_{\pm e_{1}}=\alpha/2
.
Using the explicit formula (
8.6
) from the proof of Proposition
8.2
and noting that
|e_{1}|=1
, we have:

\hat{\zeta}_{e_{1}}=i\frac{\rho^{s+1}}{(2\pi)^{2}}(\mathbf{v}\cdot e_{1})\Psi^{\prime}(\rho)e^{-ie_{1}\cdot y_{0}}.

Set
C_{\rho}\coloneqq(2\pi)^{-2}\rho^{s+1}\Psi^{\prime}(\rho)
, so that
\hat{\zeta}_{e_{1}}=iC_{\rho}v^{1}e^{-iy_{0}^{1}}
.
Since the perturbation
\zeta
is real-valued, we have
\hat{\zeta}_{-e_{1}}=\overline{\hat{\zeta}_{e_{1}}}
and hence

\displaystyle J(y_{0},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=(1+|e_{1}|^{2})^{s}\left(\hat{u}_{e_{1}}\overline{\hat{\zeta}_{e_{1}}}+\hat{u}_{-e_{1}}\overline{\hat{\zeta}_{-e_{1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=2^{s-1}\alpha C_{\rho}v^{1}\left(ie^{-iy_{0}^{1}}-ie^{iy_{0}^{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=2^{s-1}\alpha C_{\rho}v^{1}\left(2\sin(y_{0}^{1})\right).

Setting
\mathcal{A}(\rho)\coloneqq 2^{s}C_{\rho}
, and recalling that
\Psi^{\prime}(\rho)\neq 0
by our choice of scale, we conclude that
\mathcal{A}(\rho)\neq 0
.
∎

Step 2: Stability and Amplitude Selection.

We must ensure that the perturbation satisfies the strict stability condition
\left\lVert u+\eta\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<r
.
Recall from Proposition
8.2
that
\left\lVert\zeta_{\delta,\rho}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C_{s}\delta
.
Expanding the squared norm, the condition reads:

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}+2\lambda\delta J(y_{0},\mathbf{v})+\left\lVert\zeta_{\delta,\rho}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}<r^{2}.

We define the "admissible amplitude"
\delta(y_{0},\mathbf{v})
as follows:

•

Case 1:
u\equiv 0
.
We choose a constant amplitude
\delta_{0}=r/(2C_{s})
.
Then
\left\lVert u+\eta\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C_{s}\delta_{0}=r/2<r
. The sign
\lambda
is irrelevant;
we set
\lambda=1
.

•

Case 2:
u\not\equiv 0
.
We choose the sign
\lambda\coloneqq-\operatorname{sgn}(J(y_{0},\mathbf{v}))

to make the cross-term negative. The stability condition is satisfied if
-2\delta|J|+C_{s}^{2}\delta^{2}<0
, which
holds if
\delta<\frac{2}{C_{s}^{2}}|J|
.
To ensure strict stability, we set

\delta(y_{0},\mathbf{v})\coloneqq\frac{1}{C_{s}^{2}}|J(y_{0},\mathbf{v})|=\frac{1}{C_{s}^{2}}|\alpha\mathcal{A}(\rho)v^{1}\sin(y_{0}^{1})|.

With this choice, the squared norm of the perturbation decreases strictly:

\left\lVert u+\eta\right\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}^{2}-\frac{1}{C_{s}^{2}}J(y_{0},\mathbf{v})^{2}.

Thus,
\left\lVert u+\eta\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq r
.

Step 3: Optimization and Conclusion.

It remains to show that we can choose the parameters so that the norm inflation occurs, while
ensuring the spectral support condition.

By the reverse triangle inequality it suffices to find parameters such that
\left\lVert\eta\circ\tilde{\varphi}_{t}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}>2N
.
Using the geometric lower bound (Proposition
8.2
, item 2), we have:

\left\lVert\eta\circ\tilde{\varphi}_{t}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\geq c_{s}\delta(y_{0},\mathbf{v})\rho^{s-2}\left\lVert|D|^{s}(\mathbf{v}\cdot\tilde{\varphi}_{t})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(x_{0},c_{\rho}))},

where
x_{0}=\tilde{\varphi}_{t}^{-1}(y_{0})
. Substituting the definition of
\delta(y_{0},\mathbf{v})
, we seek to maximize the functional:

\mathcal{Q}(t,y_{0},\mathbf{v})\coloneqq|\sin(y_{0}^{1})|\cdot|v^{1}|\cdot\left\lVert|D|^{s}(\mathbf{v}\cdot\tilde{\varphi}_{t})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(x_{0},c_{\varphi}\rho))}.

By hypothesis (
8.4
), there exists a sequence of times
t_{n}\to\infty
such that the
global norms diverge:

\Lambda_{n}\coloneqq\left\lVert\tilde{\varphi}_{t_{n}}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}\to+\infty.

An elementary covering argument implies the existence of a constant
c>0
and a sequence
of balls
B(x_{n},r)
on which the local norms satisfy

(8.7)

\left\lVert\tilde{\varphi}_{t_{n}}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(B(x_{n},r))}\geq c\Lambda_{n}.

Let
y_{n}=\tilde{\varphi}_{t_{n}}(x_{n})
. We introduce the set
\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{T}^{2}:\sin(y^{1})=0\}
,
where the weight
|\sin(y^{1})|
vanishes. Since
\mathcal{Z}
has empty
interior, the continuity of the map

y\mapsto\left\lVert\tilde{\varphi}_{t_{n}}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}(\tilde{\varphi}_{t_{n}}^{-1}(B(y,\rho)))}

allows us to select a perturbed center
y_{n}^{*}

satisfying
\operatorname{dist}(y_{n}^{*},\mathcal{Z})\geq\varepsilon>0
, while ensuring that the pullback
ball centered at
\tilde{\varphi}_{t_{n}}^{-1}(y_{n}^{*})
still
captures a comparable amount of energy to that in (
8.7
).
Finally, the diagonal directions
\mathbf{v}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,\pm 1)
satisfy
the constraint
|v^{1}|>0
as well as the algebraic identity

||D|^{s}\varphi|^{2}=||D|^{s}(\mathbf{v}_{+}\cdot\varphi)|^{2}+||D|^{s}(\mathbf{v}_{-}\cdot\varphi)|^{2}.

Consequently, there exists a direction
\mathbf{v}^{*}\in\{\mathbf{v}_{+},\mathbf{v}_{-}\}

such that the associated term captures at least half of the total gradient energy.
For
n
large enough, the functional
\mathcal{Q}(t_{n},y_{n}^{*},\mathbf{v}^{*})

thus exceeds the required threshold
2N
.

Finally, we address the spectral support. The topological argument in the next
step requires the perturbation to satisfy
\hat{\eta}(e_{1})\neq 0
.
From Lemma
8.3
, this coefficient is proportional to
\sin(y_{0}^{1})
.
In the construction above, we explicitly selected the center
y^{*}

such that
\operatorname{dist}(y^{*},\mathcal{Z})\geq\varepsilon
.
Since
\mathcal{Z}
is the zero-locus of the sine function,
this geometric separation guarantees that the interaction coefficient
is strictly non-zero.
Thus, the condition
\hat{\eta}(e_{1})\neq 0
is naturally satisfied.

8.3.
Proof of Claim 2 (Continuity and Compactness)

We finally establish the properties of the mapping
\mathcal{F}
required to apply
Schauder’s fixed point theorem. Recall that the map is defined by

\mathcal{F}(f)=\tilde{f}+\eta(\tilde{f}),\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{f}=\omega_{0}+P(f-\omega_{0}).

Since
P
is a finite-rank projector, the set of regularized profiles

\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\left\{\omega_{0}+P(f-\omega_{0}):f\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}}(\omega_{0},r)\right\}

is a compact subset of
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. To construct a continuous assignment

\tilde{f}\mapsto\eta(\tilde{f})
, we rely on a partition of unity argument.

For every profile
g\in\mathcal{K}
, Claim 1 guarantees the existence of a
perturbation
\eta_{g}
satisfying three properties:

(1)

Strict Stability:
The perturbed profile lies strictly inside the ball, that is

\left\lVert g+\eta_{g}-\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<r.

(2)

Norm Inflation:
There exists a time
t_{g}\geq 0
such that

\big\lVert(g+\eta_{g})\circ\varphi_{t_{g}}^{g}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}>N,

where
\varphi^{g}
denotes the inverse flow generated by
g
.

(3)

Phase condition:

\hat{\eta}_{g}(e_{1})\neq 0
where
e_{1}=(1,0)
.

Since
\left\lVert g+\eta_{g}-\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<r
, by the triangle inequality, there exists
an open neighborhood
V_{g}
of
g
in
\mathcal{K}
such
that for all
\tilde{f}\in V_{g}
, we have
\big\lVert\tilde{f}+\eta_{g}-\omega_{0}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}<r
.

Similarly, we can ensure the growth condition is preserved.
The map which associates the solution at time
t_{g}
to the
initial data is continuous
from
H^{s}
to
H^{s}
(owing to the well-posedness of the Euler equations).
Therefore, the function

\tilde{f}\mapsto\big\lVert(\tilde{f}+\eta_{g})\circ\varphi_{t_{g}}^{\tilde{f}}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}

is continuous. Since this value is strictly greater than
N
at
\tilde{f}=g
,
it remains strictly greater than
N
in a neighborhood of
g
.
By intersecting these neighborhoods, we may assume without loss of generality that
for all
\tilde{f}\in V_{g}
, the growth condition holds.

Since
\mathcal{K}
is compact, we can extract a finite subcover
V_{g_{1}},\dots,V_{g_{m}}
.
Let
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{m}
be a continuous partition of unity subordinate to this cover.
(Recall that such a partition can be constructed explicitly using distance functions,
for instance by setting
\chi_{j}=w_{j}/\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}w_{i}

with
w_{j}(\tilde{f})\coloneqq\operatorname{dist}(\tilde{f},\mathcal{K}\setminus V_{g_{j}})
).
We define the map
\eta:\mathcal{K}\to H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
by

\eta(\tilde{f})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\chi_{j}(\tilde{f})\eta_{g_{j}}.

This map is continuous by construction.
It remains only to verify that it satisfies the required properties.

Stability.
First, we verify that
\mathcal{F}(f)\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}}(\omega_{0},r)
.
To do so, write

\mathcal{F}(f)-\omega_{0}=\tilde{f}+\eta(\tilde{f})-\omega_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\chi_{j}(\tilde{f})\left(\tilde{f}+\eta_{g_{j}}-\omega_{0}\right),

where we used
\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}\chi_{j}=1
. For any index
j
such that
\chi_{j}(\tilde{f})>0
,
we have
\tilde{f}\in V_{g_{j}}
. By our choice of neighborhoods, this implies

\left\lVert\tilde{f}+\eta_{g_{j}}-\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<r
.
By convexity of the norm, the sum remains in the ball:

\left\lVert\mathcal{F}(f)-\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\chi_{j}(\tilde{f})\left\lVert\tilde{f}+\eta_{g_{j}}-\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<r.

Compactness.
The image of the map
f\mapsto\eta(\tilde{f})

is contained in the finite-dimensional vector space spanned by
\{\eta_{g_{1}},\dots,\eta_{g_{m}}\}
.
Consequently, the image of
\mathcal{F}
lies in the finite-dimensional affine subspace

E\coloneqq\omega_{0}+\operatorname{Im}(P)+\operatorname{Span}\left(\{\eta_{g_{1}},\dots,\eta_{g_{m}}\}\right).

Since
\mathcal{F}
is continuous and bounded, its closure is compact.

Non-Vanishing Spectral Support.

Finally, we address the spectral support. Recall that for the contradiction argument,
we must ensure that the projection
P\eta
does not vanish.
The construction above guarantees that
\hat{\eta_{g}}(e_{1})
is strictly non-zero.
Moreover, since
P
projects onto the one-dimensional space spanned by
\cos(x^{1})
,
the projection
P\eta
is a real scalar function. Its non-vanishing implies that it has a
strictly positive or strictly negative sign.
By continuity of the flow with respect to the initial data
f
, this sign is locally constant
in
\bar{B}_{H^{s}}(\omega_{0},r)
.
By choosing the covering
\{V_{g_{j}}\}
fine enough, we ensure that on any overlap,
the perturbations
\eta_{g_{j}}
share the same spectral sign, preventing any cancellation
in the partition of unity. This ensures that
P\eta(\tilde{f})\neq 0
for all
\tilde{f}\in\mathcal{K}
.

This establishes Claim 2. As mentioned above, applying Schauder’s fixed point theorem to
\mathcal{F}
,
we obtain a fixed point
f^{*}
which must satisfy
P\eta(\tilde{f}^{*})=0
.
This contradicts the spectral support property.
The proof of Proposition
8.1
is complete.

9.
Lagrangian stretching implies instability

After the dichotomy proven in Proposition
8.1
, the next main step
to prove Theorem
1.1
it suffices to prove the following

Proposition 9.1
.

Consider
\omega_{0}\in H_{0}^{s}
with
s>1
such that the inverse flow
\varphi_{t}^{\omega_{0}}
satisfies

(9.1)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert D\varphi_{t}^{\omega_{0}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=+\infty.

Then for any radius
r>0
and any threshold
N>0
, there exists an initial data

f_{0}\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)
such that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert f_{0}\circ\varphi_{t}^{f_{0}}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}>N.

Remark 9.2
.

The proof of this proposition is rooted in the
instability mechanism identified by Koch in
[
27
]
which shows that Lagrangian
stretching drives Eulerian growth. Specifically, our proof highlights
that this mechanism is allowed by the stability of the data-to-solution map under high frequency perturbation.
This allows to introduce regularized initial data to bypass the fact that the data-to-solution
map is not
C^{1}
for quasilinear problems.

The end of this section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
9.1
.

We proceed by contradiction and suppose that for all
f_{0}\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(\omega_{0},r)

(9.2)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert f_{0}\circ\varphi_{t}^{f_{0}}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq N.

Henceforth
\Phi
will denote the flow associated to
\omega_{0}
.

9.1.
Construction of the perturbation

We aim to construct an initial perturbation
\eta_{\textrm{init}}
satisfying

(9.3)

\forall\mu\leq s,\quad\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\right\rVert_{H^{\mu}}\leq r\delta^{s-\mu},

where
r
is the radius of the ball and
\delta
is a small parameter (
\delta\ll 1
),
while ensuring that at some target time
T
:

(9.4)

\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\Phi_{T}^{-1}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\geq 5N.

By the blow-up hypothesis (
9.1
), there exists a time
T>0
, a point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}

and a unit vector
e\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that:

(9.5)

\left|(D\Phi_{T}(x_{0}))^{-\top}e\right|>\left(10^{3}\frac{N}{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}.

Once
T
,
x_{0}
and
e
have been determined, we rely on the construction from
Subsection
8.1
with a slight change of notation: the localization scale
is denoted by
\delta
(instead of
\rho
) and the amplitude is determined by the radius
r
.
Also, we need to introduce a high-frequency sinusoidal profile (parameterized by a
frequency
\mu\geq 1
) instead of a simple linear one. Namely, given a standard
radial cut-off function
\theta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

satisfying
\theta(y)=1
for
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\leq 1/2
and
\theta(y)=0
for
\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq 1
, we set

\eta_{\textrm{init}}(x)=\frac{r}{5}\delta^{s-1}\theta\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\delta}\right)\sin\left(\mu\frac{x-x_{0}}{\delta}\cdot e\right).

Again, we identify the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with the periodic box
[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
and, since we will
choose
\delta\ll 1
, the support of the perturbation is localized and the use of Euclidean
coordinates is unambiguous. Note that by symmetry of the sine term, the mean vanishes:
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\eta_{\textrm{init}}(x)\diff\!x=0
.

Remark 9.3
.

Analogous to the first statement of Proposition
8.2
, we have the following estimates:

(9.6)

\left\lVert\eta_{\text{init}}\right\rVert_{H^{\nu}}\leq C_{\mu}r\delta^{s-\nu}.

These estimates imply that for
\nu<s
, the
H^{\nu}
norms of
\eta_{\text{init}}
are small.
This scaling contrasts fundamentally with the construction in the previous section, where
the various Sobolev norms were comparable.
Indeed, while the estimate
\left\lVert\nabla^{k}\zeta\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\delta\rho^{s-k}

in Section 6 is formally similar to (
9.6
) (with
\rho
playing the role of
\delta
), the scale
\rho

was there fixed of order
1
. Consequently, the growth mechanisms differ. Considering the Faà di Bruno
formula for the
s
-th derivative of
\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi
, the growth is presently generated by the term where all
derivatives act on the profile
\eta_{\textrm{init}}
, whereas in the previous
section, the growth was driven by the term where derivatives acted on the inverse flow

\varphi
. These two terms evolve through drastically
different regimes, necessitating distinct arguments.

Lemma 9.4
.

There exists a constant
C_{s}
depending only on
s
such that, if

\delta C(T)\leq 1\quad\text{where}\quad C(T)=C_{s}\exp(\exp(C_{s}\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}T)),

then

\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\Phi^{-1}(T)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\geq 5N.

Proof.

Let
K_{T}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left\lvert D\Phi_{T}(x)\right\rvert
and consider the
domain
\Omega\coloneqq B(y_{0},2K_{T}\delta)
. Thanks to
the assumption
\delta C(T)\leq 1
, we can assume that the
radius
2K_{T}\delta
is strictly smaller than
1
. This allows us to identify
the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{T}^{2}
with a Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
without
ambiguity regarding periodicity. On this ball, we approximate the inverse
flow
\varphi\coloneqq\Phi_{T}^{-1}
by its first-order Taylor expansion at
y_{0}
:

\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}(y)\coloneqq x_{0}+A(y-y_{0}),\quad\text{where }A\coloneqq D\varphi(y_{0})=(D\Phi_{T}(x_{0}))^{-1}.

Note that the support of the linearized composition
\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}

is also contained in
\Omega
since
|A^{-1}|\leq K_{T}
.
We write
\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi=\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}+\mathcal{R}
.

We first establish the lower bound for the linear part. Recall that

\eta_{\textrm{init}}(x)=\frac{r}{5}\delta^{s-1}G\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\delta}\right),\quad\text{with }G(z)=\theta(z)\sin(\mu z\cdot e).

Substituting the linear map, we
have
\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}(y)=\frac{r}{5}\delta^{s-1}G(A(y-y_{0})/\delta)
.
Observe that

\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}=\frac{r}{5}\delta^{s-1}\cdot\delta^{-(s-1)}\left\lVert G(A\cdot)\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}=\frac{r}{10\pi}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|A^{\top}k|^{2s}|\hat{G}(k)|^{2}\diff\!k\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Now, define the constant
c\in(0,1)
by

c\coloneqq\left(\inf_{k\in\supp\hat{G}}\frac{|A^{\top}k|^{2s}}{|A^{\top}e|^{2s}|k|^{2s}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\left(\inf_{k\in\supp\hat{G}}\frac{|k\cdot e|^{2s}}{|k|^{2s}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Using this lower bound in the integral, we get

\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}\geq c\frac{r}{10\pi}|A^{\top}e|^{s}\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}.

Since
\hat{G}(k)=\frac{1}{2i}\big(\hat{\theta}(k-\mu e)-\hat{\theta}(k+\mu e)\big)
, the spectrum is
concentrated around
\pm\mu e
. Thus,
for
\mu
large enough (depending on the support
of
\hat{\theta}
), we have
|k\cdot e|\approx|k|
on the support, ensuring
c\geq 1/2
. Assuming further
that
\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}\approx 1
(by normalization of
\theta
), and using the hypothesis (
9.5
)
on
|A^{\top}e|
, we obtain:

\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}\right\rVert_{\dot{H}^{s}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{r}{10\pi}\left(10^{3}\frac{N}{r}\right)\geq 6N.

It remains to bound the remainder term

\mathcal{R}\coloneqq\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi-\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}
.
Recall that the supports of both
\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi
and
\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}

are strictly contained in
\Omega
.
Since the functions vanish outside
\Omega
, we can apply the difference estimate
for composition directly on this domain:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{R}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathcal{R}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{s}\left(\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}\left\lVert\varphi-\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\right\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\left\lVert\varphi-\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\right).

Now, observe that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\sim\delta^{s-1}\cdot\delta^{1-s}=O(1),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}

\displaystyle\sim\delta^{s-1}\cdot\delta^{1-(s+1)}=O(\delta^{-1}),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\right\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{-1}\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\sim\delta^{-1}\cdot\delta^{s-1}=\delta^{s-2}.

Fix
0\leq\nu\ll 1
, we directly estimate

\left\lVert\varphi-\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|\varphi\right\|_{C^{1+\nu}}(2K_{T}\delta)^{1+\nu}.

since
\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}
is the first-order Taylor approximation of
\varphi

this allows for the estimate for highest-order derivatives

\left\lVert\varphi-\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}|\Omega|^{1/2}\lesssim\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}\delta.

Plugging these explicit bounds back into the difference estimate, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{R}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{s}\left[(\delta^{-1})\cdot(\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{C^{1+\nu}}\delta^{1+\nu})+(\delta^{s-2})\cdot(\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}\delta)\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{s}\left[\delta^{\nu}\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{C^{2}}+\delta^{s-1}\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}\right].

Now, recall that the flow norms grow at most double exponentially in time:

\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{C^{1+\nu}}\leq\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}\leq C(T)\coloneqq C_{s}\exp(\exp(C_{s}\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}T)).

Thus, assuming that
\delta C(T)\leq 1
with
C_{s}
sufficiently large, we infer that

\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\geq\left\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi_{\textrm{lin}}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}-\left\lVert\mathcal{R}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\geq 6N-N=5N.

This concludes the proof.
∎

9.2.
Stability of the growth

We begin by regularizing the initial data.
Introduce a Friedrichs mollifier, that is an approximation of the
identity of the form
\theta(\delta^{\prime}D_{x})

where
\theta\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\theta(\xi)=1
for
|\xi|\leq 1/2

as above, and define
\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}=\theta(\delta^{\prime}D_{x})\omega_{0}
.
Standard approximation estimates yield

\big\lVert\omega_{0}-\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\big\rVert_{H^{s^{\prime}}}\leq C\big\lVert\omega_{0}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\delta^{\prime s-s^{\prime}}\quad\text{and}\quad\big\lVert\omega_{0}-\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}=o_{\delta^{\prime}\to 0}(1).

We consider the perturbed initial vorticity defined by

\omega^{\prime}_{0}\coloneqq\omega^{\delta^{\prime}}_{0}+\eta_{\textrm{init}}.

Notice that
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\omega^{\prime}_{0}\diff\!x=0
. Let
\Phi
,
\Phi^{\prime}

and
\Phi^{\delta^{\prime}}
be the flows associated with the solutions
arising from initial data
\omega_{0}
,
\omega^{\prime}_{0}
and
\omega^{\delta^{\prime}}_{0}

respectively. We denote their inverses by
\varphi\coloneqq\Phi^{-1}
,
\varphi^{\prime}\coloneqq(\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}

and
\varphi^{\delta^{\prime}}\coloneqq(\Phi^{\delta^{\prime}})^{-1}
. At time
T
,
the perturbed vorticity is given by
\omega^{\prime}(T)=\omega^{\prime}_{0}\circ\varphi^{\prime}(T)
. We decompose it as

\displaystyle\underbrace{\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\circ\varphi^{\delta^{\prime}}(T)}_{=(1)}+\underbrace{\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\circ\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\circ\varphi^{\delta^{\prime}}(T)}_{=(2)}+\underbrace{\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi(T)}_{=(3)}+\underbrace{\eta_{\textrm{init}}\circ\varphi(T)}_{=(4)}.

We proceed to estimate each term.
First, we choose the regularization
parameter
\delta^{\prime}
sufficiently small such that

\big\lVert\omega_{0}-\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq\frac{r}{4}.

Consequently, it follows from the stability hypothesis (
9.2
)
that the evolution of the regularized profile remains bounded:

\left\lVert(1)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<N.

On the other hand, by the linear amplification constructed in
Lemma
9.4
, we have the lower bound

\left\lVert(4)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\geq 5N.

It remains to control the error terms (2) and (3).
Henceforth,
C_{T}
denotes a large constant depending only on the bound
M(T)
defined by

M(T)\coloneqq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big\lVert u(t)\big\rVert_{H^{s+1}}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big\lVert u^{\prime}(t)\big\rVert_{H^{s+1}}.

To estimate the term
(2)
, again, we write the difference as an integral:

(2)=[\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\varphi^{\delta^{\prime}}(T)]\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\left((1-\tau)\varphi^{\prime}(T)+\tau\varphi^{\delta^{\prime}}(T)\right)\diff\!\tau.

Applying Moser-type product estimates (see (
B.1
)) and the composition estimate from Proposition
B.2
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\big\lVert(2)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\lesssim C_{T}\big\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\varphi^{\delta^{\prime}}(T)\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\big\lVert\nabla\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}+\big\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\varphi^{\delta^{\prime}}(T)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\big\lVert\nabla\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}}.

Through the mollification we have

\big\lVert\nabla\omega_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\big\rVert_{H^{k}}\lesssim(\delta^{\prime})^{-1}\big\lVert\omega_{0}\big\rVert_{H^{k}}
. Thus:

\big\lVert(2)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C_{T}\left({\delta^{\prime}}^{-1}\big\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\varphi^{\delta^{\prime}}(T)\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\big\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\varphi^{\delta^{\prime}}(T)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\right).

To bound the flow difference, we use the stability estimate
for the inverse flow (
B.5
) and Proposition
B.4
. For any integer
\mu\leq s
:

\big\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\varphi^{\delta^{\prime}}(T)\big\rVert_{H^{\mu}}\leq C_{T}\big\lVert\Phi^{\prime}(T)-\Phi^{\delta^{\prime}}(T)\big\rVert_{H^{\mu}}\leq C_{T}\big\lVert u_{0}^{\prime}-u_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\big\rVert_{H^{\mu}}.

Recall that
u_{0}^{\prime}-u_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}
is the velocity associated with
\eta_{\textrm{init}}
.
By the Biot-Savart law and the estimates (
9.6
),
we have
\big\lVert u_{0}^{\prime}-u_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\big\rVert_{H^{\mu}}\leq Cr\delta^{s+1-\mu}
.
Applying this with
\mu=s
and
\mu=1+\nu
, for some
0<\nu\ll 1
(using the Sobolev
embedding
H^{1+\nu}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
), we get:

\big\lVert(2)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C_{T}r\left({\delta^{\prime}}^{-1}\delta^{s-\nu}+\delta\right).

The estimate of
(3)
is similar. Using the product estimates (
B.1
)
and Proposition
B.2
, we find that

\displaystyle\big\lVert(3)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C_{T}\Big(

\displaystyle\big\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\varphi(T)\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\big\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\big\rVert_{H^{s+1}}

\displaystyle+\big\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\varphi(T)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\big\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\big\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\Big).

The distance between the initial velocities is bounded by triangle inequality:

\big\lVert u_{0}^{\prime}-u_{0}\big\rVert_{H^{\mu}}\leq\big\lVert u_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}-u_{0}\big\rVert_{H^{\mu}}+\big\lVert u^{\prime}_{0}-u_{0}^{\delta^{\prime}}\big\rVert_{H^{\mu}}.

Using the approximation properties of the mollifier and the size of the perturbation (
9.6
), we have:

\big\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(T)-\varphi(T)\big\rVert_{H^{\mu}}\leq C_{T}\big\lVert u_{0}^{\prime}-u_{0}\big\rVert_{H^{\mu}}\leq C_{T}\left(\delta^{\prime s+1-\mu}+r\delta^{s+1-\mu}\right).

Using the scaling
\big\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\big\rVert_{H^{s+1}}\sim\delta^{-1}
and

\big\lVert\eta_{\textrm{init}}\big\rVert_{W^{1,\infty}}\sim\delta^{s-2}
, we find:

\big\lVert(3)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C_{T}\left[(\delta^{\prime s-\nu}+r\delta^{s-\nu})\delta^{-1}+(\delta^{\prime}+r\delta)\delta^{s-2}\right].

We are now at a position to complete the proof. We
set the regularization parameter
\delta^{\prime}=\delta
. With this choice,
and since
s>1
, the error terms
(2)
and
(3)
vanish as
\delta\to 0
.
Explicitly, combining the previous bounds:

\left\lVert(2)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\lesssim\delta^{s-1-\nu}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert(3)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\lesssim\delta^{s-1-\nu}.

By choosing
\delta
sufficiently small (specifically
\delta\ll(N/C_{T})^{\frac{1}{s-1-\nu}}
),
we ensure that
\left\lVert(2)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\left\lVert(3)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq N
.
Combining all estimates, we conclude:

\big\lVert\omega^{\prime}_{T}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\geq\big\lVert(4)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}-\big\lVert(1)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}-\big\lVert(2)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}-\big\lVert(3)\big\rVert_{H^{s}}\geq 5N-N-N=3N.

Since we constructed a solution starting arbitrarily close to
\omega_{0}

(in terms of
r
and
\delta
) that exits the ball of radius
2N
,
this contradicts the stability hypothesis (
9.2
).

10.
Proof of the main theorem: Generic unboundedness

In this final section, we
combine the results of the previous sections
to conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
To upgrade the existence of blowing-up solutions
to a genericity result, we rely on a Baire category
argument introduced by Hani
[
20
]

in the context of dispersive equations (see also
[
18
,
19
]
).

Recall that we aim to prove that the set of initial data
leading to an unbounded growth of the
H^{s}
-norm
is a dense
G_{\delta}
subset of
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
We define the set of blowing-up initial data as:

\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\left\{\omega_{0}\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2}):\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\left\lVert S(t)\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}=+\infty\right\}.

(Recall that the solution map
S(t)
, which maps the initial
data to the vorticity at time
t
, is as defined in Section
2.2
.)
Then, we rewrite
\mathcal{B}
as a countable intersection:

\mathcal{B}=\bigcap_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\mathcal{U}_{N},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{U}_{N}\coloneqq\left\{\omega_{0}\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2}):\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert S(t)\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}>N\right\}.

Since
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is a complete
metric space, the Baire Category Theorem
ensures that if each
\mathcal{U}_{N}
is open and dense,
then their intersection
\mathcal{B}
is a dense
G_{\delta}
set.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
therefore reduces to the
following two lemmas.

Lemma 10.1
.

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the set
\mathcal{U}_{N}

is open in
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

Proof.

If
\omega_{0}\in\mathcal{U}_{N}
then
there exists a time
t_{0}\geq 0
such that

\left\lVert S(t_{0})\omega_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}>N
.
According to the classical well-posedness
theory (Theorem
2.1
), the
solution map
S(t_{0}):H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is continuous.
Consequently, the map

\omega\mapsto\left\lVert S(t_{0})\omega\right\rVert_{H^{s}}
is continuous.
There exists a small neighborhood
V
of
\omega_{0}
in
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
such that
\left\lVert S(t_{0})\omega\right\rVert_{H^{s}}>N

for all
\omega\in V
. This proves that
\mathcal{U}_{N}
is open.
∎

Lemma 10.2
.

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the set
\mathcal{U}_{N}
is dense in
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

Proof.

Let
f\in H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
be an arbitrary initial
data and let
\delta>0
. We seek a function

h\in\mathcal{U}_{N}
such that
\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<\delta
.

Proposition
5.2
ensures that there exists a function
f_{r}
in
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

such that
\left\lVert f-f_{r}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<r
.
Now, we can apply the Dichotomy Theorem
(Proposition
8.1
) and hence, choosing a radius

r<\delta-\left\lVert f-f_{\delta}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}
, we conclude that one of the
following two assertions holds:

(1)

Instability of the vorticity:
There exists
an initial data
h\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(f_{\delta},r)

such that the solution exceeds
N
.
Then
h\in\mathcal{U}_{N}
and
\left\lVert h-f\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<\delta
.
The proof is
done.

(2)

Blow-up of the flow:

The inverse flow
\varphi_{t}^{f_{\delta}}
blows up
in the Lipschitz norm:

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\lVert D\varphi_{t}^{f_{\delta}}\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=+\infty.

In this case, we apply the Koch argument
(Proposition
9.1
). This
proposition ensures that there exists a
perturbation
h\in\bar{B}_{H^{s}_{0}}(f_{\delta},r)

such that the solution grows arbitrarily large. Specifically,
there exists
h
such that
\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert S(t)h\right\rVert_{H^{s}}>N
.

In both scenarios, we have found an
element
h\in\mathcal{U}_{N}
such
that
\left\lVert f-h\right\rVert_{H^{s}}<\delta
. Thus
\mathcal{U}_{N}
is dense.
∎

This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

10.1.
Proof of Corollary
1.2

The Baire’s argument explained in the previous section applies here
in verbatim
,
hence it is sufficient to prove that any smooth function can
be approximated in the Fréchet space
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
by an initial data generating a
solution that blows up in a certain
C^{k}
space, or equivalently in a Sobolev space
H^{m}
.

Proposition 10.3
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be a non-empty open subset of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
There exist a function
h\in\mathcal{O}
and a real number

m>3
such that:

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\left\lVert S(t)h\right\rVert_{H^{m}}=+\infty.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{O}
. By definition of the Fréchet topology of

C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, the openness of
\mathcal{O}
implies the
existence of an index
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and a radius
\varepsilon>0
such that

B_{k_{0}}(f,\varepsilon)\coloneqq\left\{g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}):\left\lVert f-g\right\rVert_{H^{k_{0}}}<\varepsilon\right\}\subset\mathcal{O}.

Next, we fix a real number
m>\max(k_{0},3)
. For any integer

N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we define the set of smooth functions whose
trajectories exceed the threshold
N
in
H^{m}
:

\mathcal{V}_{N}\coloneqq\left\{u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}):\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert S(t)u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}>N\right\}.

Since the map
\Phi\colon u\mapsto\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert S(t)u\right\rVert_{H^{m}}

is lower semi-continuous on
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, the set

\mathcal{V}_{N}=\Phi^{-1}((N,+\infty])
is open in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
We now prove that these sets are also dense. Consider an element

g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and let
\delta>0
. Theorem
1.1
asserts
that the set of initial data blowing up in
H^{m}
is dense in
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.
Therefore, there exists an unstable function
u^{*}\in H^{m}
satisfying

\left\lVert g-u^{*}\right\rVert_{H^{m}}<\delta/2
. Since the strict inequality

\sup\left\lVert S(t)u^{*}\right\rVert_{H^{m}}>N
is an open condition in
H^{m}
, it holds
on a small ball around
u^{*}
. By the density of
C^{\infty}
in
H^{m}
,
this ball contains a smooth function
h\in\mathcal{V}_{N}
arbitrarily
close to
u^{*}
, and thus close to
g
. This proves that
\mathcal{V}_{N}

is dense in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

Finally, since
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is a Fréchet space, it is a Baire space.
The countable intersection of dense open sets is therefore dense:

\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\bigcap_{N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\mathcal{V}_{N}\quad\text{is dense in }C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

It follows that the intersection
\mathcal{I}\cap B_{k_{0}}(f,\varepsilon)
is
non-empty. Thus, there exists a function
h\in\mathcal{O}
such that
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert S(t)h\right\rVert_{H^{m}}>N
.
This is equivalent to

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\left\lVert S(t)h\right\rVert_{H^{m}}=+\infty,

which concludes the proof.
∎

11.
Proof of Proposition
1.3

The proof of Proposition
1.3
follows directly by applying the
results of Section
9
and the following proposition.

Proposition 11.1
.

For any non-zero initial data
\omega_{0}
in
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{0}(x)\diff\!x=0
,
the associated flow satisfies

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert D\Phi_{t}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=+\infty.

To prove this proposition, we first
require standard velocity field estimates on
\mathbb{R}^{2}

(see e.g.,
[
28
,
11
]
).

Lemma 11.2
.

For any
\omega\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the associated velocity
u
given by the Biot-Savart law
satisfies

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{L^{1}}^{1/2}\left\lVert\omega\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{1/2}.

If, moreover,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega(x)\diff\!x=0
, then for all

x
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq 2\left\lvert y\right\rvert
for all
y\in\supp\omega
,

\left\lvert u(x)\right\rvert\leq\frac{C}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert y\right\rvert\left\lvert\omega(y)\right\rvert\diff\!y\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lvert Du(x)\right\rvert\leq\frac{C}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert y\right\rvert\left\lvert\omega(y)\right\rvert\diff\!y.

In particular,
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Next, we establish identities inspired by the study by Shankar
[
36
]
of the
conserved quantities associated with the scaling symmetry of the Euler equations on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Lemma 11.3
.

For
\omega_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{0}(x)\diff\!x=0
,
let
G(t)
be defined by

G(t)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\partial_{t}\Phi_{t}(x)\cdot Y(t,x)\diff\!x,

where
Y(t,x)=\Phi_{t}(x)-(x\cdot\nabla_{x})\Phi_{t}(x)
. Then we have

\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}G(t)=2\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

This identity implies the lower bound:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert(x\cdot\nabla_{x})\xi(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x\geq\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\,t^{2},

where
\xi(t,x)\coloneqq\Phi_{t}(x)-x
is the displacement field.

Proof.

Differentiating
G(t)
with respect to time, we obtain:

\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}G(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\partial_{t}^{2}\Phi_{t}\cdot Y\diff\!x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\partial_{t}\Phi_{t}\cdot\partial_{t}Y\diff\!x=\mathcal{I}_{p}+\mathcal{I}_{k}.

From the Euler equations,
\partial_{t}^{2}\Phi_{t}=-(\nabla p)(t,\Phi_{t})
, hence

\mathcal{I}_{p}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\nabla p)(t,\Phi_{t}(x))\cdot Y(t,x)\diff\!x.

By setting
W(t,y)\coloneqq Y(t,\Phi_{t}^{-1}(y))
and using the fact that
\det(D\Phi_{t})=1
,
the change of variables
y=\Phi_{t}(x)
yields:

\mathcal{I}_{p}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla_{y}p(y)\cdot W(t,y)\diff\!y=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}p(y)\,(\nabla_{y}\cdot W(t,y))\diff\!y.

Let
X(x)=x
be the identity vector field. We observe that
W(t,y)=y-Z(t,y)
,
where
Z\coloneqq(\Phi_{t})_{*}X
is the push-forward of
X
by the flow
\Phi_{t}
.
Since the flow is volume-preserving, the divergence transforms
as
\nabla_{y}\cdot((\Phi_{t})_{*}X)=(\nabla_{x}\cdot X)\circ\Phi_{t}^{-1}
.
Since
\nabla_{x}\cdot X=2
, we obtain
\nabla_{y}\cdot Z=2
.
This implies
\nabla_{y}\cdot W=\nabla_{y}\cdot y-\nabla_{y}\cdot Z=2-2=0
,
which proves that
\mathcal{I}_{p}=0
.

It remains to compute the term
\mathcal{I}_{k}
. Let
v(t,x)=\partial_{t}\Phi_{t}(x)
, then:

\mathcal{I}_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert v\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}v\cdot((x\cdot\nabla_{x})v)\diff\!x.

Since
\det(D\Phi_{t})=1
, the first term equals
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert v\circ\Phi_{t}\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x=\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
.
For the second one, an integration by parts gives:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}v\cdot((x\cdot\nabla_{x})v)\diff\!x=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\nabla_{x}\cdot x)\left\lvert v\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert v\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x=-\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Finally, we obtain
\mathcal{I}_{k}=\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-(-\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2})=2\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
.
We have thus proved that

\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}G(t)=2\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since
Y(0,x)=0
, this implies
G(t)=2\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}t
. By definition of
G(t)
, the
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies:

\left\lvert G(t)\right\rvert^{2}\leq\left\lVert\partial_{t}\Phi_{t}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert Y(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x.

It follows that
4\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{4}t^{2}\leq\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert Y(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x
,
which is equivalent to:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert\Phi_{t}-(x\cdot\nabla_{x})\Phi_{t}\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x\geq 4\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}t^{2}.

With
\xi(t,x)=\Phi_{t}(x)-x
, we have
\Phi_{t}-(x\cdot\nabla_{x})\Phi_{t}=\xi-(x\cdot\nabla_{x})\xi
,
and by developing the square and integrating by parts:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert\xi(t,x)-(x\cdot\nabla_{x})\xi(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x=3\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert\xi(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert(x\cdot\nabla_{x})\xi(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x.

In dimension
2
, we use the inequality

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert(x\cdot\nabla_{x})\xi\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x
,
which follows from the relation
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}x\cdot\nabla\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x
and Cauchy-Schwarz.
We thus deduce:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert(x\cdot\nabla_{x})\xi(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x\geq\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\,t^{2},

which is the wanted result.
∎

Proof of Proposition
11.1
.

Assume for contradiction that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert D\Phi_{t}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=A<+\infty.

Then
\left\lvert\Phi(t,x)-\Phi(t,y)\right\rvert\leq A\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert
for any
x,y
and
t
.
Now, denote by
K(t)
the support of
\omega(t,\cdot)
. Then

\left\lvert K(t)\right\rvert=\left\lvert K_{0}\right\rvert\text{ and }\diam(K(t))\leq A\diam(K_{0}),\text{ where }\diam(K)\coloneqq\sup_{x,y\in K}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert.

We assume, without loss of generality, that
A\geq 1
,
\diam(K_{0})\geq 1
and
t\geq 1
.

Next, we claim that the displacement at the origin,
\xi(t,0)=\Phi_{t}(0)-0
, satisfies the following pointwise estimate:

(11.1)

\left\lvert\xi(t,0)\right\rvert\leq 2\sqrt{t}\left(\frac{(A+1)\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right)^{1/2}.

To establish this, we first bound the
L^{2}
norm of the displacement field.
Using the conservation of kinetic energy and the measure-preserving property of the flow, we have:

\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}\left\lVert\xi(t,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\xi\cdot(u\circ\Phi_{t})\diff\!x\leq 2\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}},

which integrates to
\left\lVert\xi(t,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq t\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}
.
On the other hand, the Lipschitz bound
\left\lVert D\Phi_{t}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq A
implies that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\left\lvert\xi(t,x)\right\rvert\geq\left\lvert\xi(t,0)\right\rvert-(A+1)\left\lvert x\right\rvert.

In particular, on the ball
B_{R}
of radius
R=\frac{\left\lvert\xi(t,0)\right\rvert}{2(A+1)}
,
the displacement is bounded from below by
\left\lvert\xi(t,x)\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\lvert\xi(t,0)\right\rvert
.

Integrating this lower bound over
B_{R}
yields:

\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\int_{B_{R}}\left\lvert\xi(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x\geq\left(\pi\frac{\left\lvert\xi(t,0)\right\rvert^{2}}{4(A+1)^{2}}\right)\frac{\left\lvert\xi(t,0)\right\rvert^{2}}{4}=\frac{\pi\left\lvert\xi(t,0)\right\rvert^{4}}{16(A+1)^{2}}.

Comparing this with the
L^{2}
upper bound, we find:

\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\left\lvert\xi(t,0)\right\rvert^{2}}{4(A+1)}\leq\left\lVert\xi(t,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq t\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}},

which leads to the desired inequality.

Now, to reach a contradiction, we partition the domain
\Omega\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B(0,M)
into two regions:

\Omega_{1}(t)\coloneqq\Omega\cap B(\xi(t,0),2A\diam(K_{0})),\quad\text{and}\quad\Omega_{2}(t)\coloneqq\Omega\setminus B(\xi(t,0),2A\diam(K_{0})).

We then decompose the integral as:

\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert x\cdot\nabla\xi\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x=\int_{\Omega_{1}(t)}\left\lvert x\cdot\nabla\xi\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x+\int_{\Omega_{2}(t)}\left\lvert x\cdot\nabla\xi\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x.

In view of (
11.1
), the first integral is bounded by

\left\lvert\Omega_{1}(t)\right\rvert\cdot\sup_{\Omega_{1}(t)}\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}\cdot\sup\left\lvert D\xi\right\rvert^{2}\lesssim\diam(K_{0})^{2}A^{4}t.

We now move to the second integral. Observe that
\big\lVert D\Phi_{t}^{-1}\big\rVert\leq A
so
\left\lvert\Phi_{t}(x)\right\rvert\gtrsim\left\lvert x\right\rvert/A
.
Then, using
\frac{\diff}{\diff\!t}D\xi=(Du\circ\Phi_{t})D\Phi_{t}
and Lemma
11.2
, we deduce that

\left\lvert D\xi(t,x)\right\rvert\leq t\frac{CA^{4}}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{3}}\implies\int_{\Omega_{2}(t)}\left\lvert x\cdot\nabla\xi\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x\leq t^{2}\frac{CA^{8}}{M^{2}}.

Now, Lemma
11.3
implies the following lower bound:

\int_{B(0,M)}\left\lvert x\cdot\nabla\xi\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x\geq\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\,t^{2}-t^{2}\frac{CA^{8}}{M^{2}}-C\diam(K_{0})^{2}tA^{2}.

On the other hand, the most straightforward estimates give

\int_{B(0,M)}\left\lvert x\cdot\nabla\xi\right\rvert^{2}\diff\!x\lesssim M^{4}A^{2}.

Remembering that
A
is fixed, choosing
M
and
t
sufficiently large provides the desired contradiction.
∎

Appendix A
Review of Paradifferential Calculus

In this appendix, we review the basic notations and results
of Bony’s paradifferential calculus
[
9
]
.
We refer to
[
21
,
38
,
30
]
for
the general theory.
For simplicity, we restrict our attention to the
2
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}\coloneqq(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}
.

A.1.
Littlewood-Paley Decomposition

We represent a function
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
by its Fourier series, which
converges in the
L^{2}
topology:

u(x)=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\hat{u}(\xi)e^{i\xi\cdot x},\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{u}(\xi)\coloneqq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}u(x)e^{-i\xi\cdot x}\diff\!x.

The Littlewood-Paley decomposition is constructed using a smooth
cut-off function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
supported in
the annulus
\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:1/2\leq|\xi|\leq 2\}
, such that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varphi(2^{-j}\xi)=1\quad\text{for all }\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{0\}.

Using this partition of unity, we decompose any function
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

as a sum of spectrally localized blocks
u=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Delta_{j}u
.
The
dyadic block operators

\Delta_{j}
are defined by their Fourier multipliers:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta_{0}u}(\xi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\varphi(2^{-j}\xi))\hat{u}(\xi),

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta_{j}u}(\xi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\varphi(2^{-j}\xi)\hat{u}(\xi)\quad\text{for }j\geq 1.

We also define the partial sum operator
S_{j}
by

S_{j}u\coloneqq\sum_{0\leq k\leq j}\Delta_{k}u.

This decomposition characterizes the standard function spaces.
For any real number
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is the
set of those periodic distributions
u
such that

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\approx\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{2js}\left\lVert\Delta_{j}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}<+\infty.

Similarly, for
r\in(0,+\infty)\setminus\mathbb{N}
, the Hölder space
C^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is characterized by

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{r}}\approx\sup_{j\geq 0}2^{jr}\left\lVert\Delta_{j}u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}.

By the Sobolev embedding theorem, the continuous
embedding
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\hookrightarrow C^{s-1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
holds whenever
s>1
and
s-1\not\in\mathbb{N}
.

Finally, given
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
C^{\sigma}_{*}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
the Zygmund space consisting of
those periodic distributions
u
which satisfy

(A.1)

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{\sigma}_{*}}\coloneqq\sup_{j\geq 0}2^{j\sigma}\left\lVert\Delta_{j}u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}<+\infty.

We recall that for

1\leq p\leq+\infty
, the continuous embedding

L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\hookrightarrow C^{-2/p}_{*}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
holds in
dimension two as a consequence of Bernstein’s inequality.

A.2.
Paraproducts

Given two functions
a
and
u
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, the paraproduct
T_{a}u
is a
bilinear operator defined by isolating the part of the product where the frequencies
of
a
are much lower than those of
u
.

Definition A.1
.

Given a function
a\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, the paraproduct operator
T_{a}
is defined by

(A.2)

T_{a}u\coloneqq\sum_{j\geq 3}S_{j-3}a\,\Delta_{j}u.

We also recall the following definition.

Definition A.2
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{R}
. A linear operator
T

is said to be of
order
m
if,
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, it defines a bounded
operator from
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to
H^{s-m}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

We collect below several classical continuity properties for the
paraproduct. The main property is that the
operator
T_{a}
is
of order
0
on Sobolev spaces provided
a\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
;
however, it acts as an operator of positive order (thereby
inducing a loss of derivatives) when
a
is assumed only to
belong to a Zygmund space of negative
regularity.

Theorem A.1

(Theorem 2.82 in
[
6
]
)
.

(1)

If
a\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, then
T_{a}
is of order
0
:
For all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, it maps
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with

(A.3)

\left\lVert T_{a}u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\lesssim_{s}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

(2)

Let
\sigma\in(0,+\infty)
.
If
a\in C^{-\sigma}_{*}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, then
T_{a}
is of order
\sigma
:
For all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, it maps
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
to
H^{s-\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with

(A.4)

\left\lVert T_{a}u\right\rVert_{H^{s-\sigma}}\lesssim_{s,\sigma}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{C^{-\sigma}_{*}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

In particular, there holds

(A.5)

\left\lVert T_{a}u\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}\lesssim_{s}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert T_{a}u\right\rVert_{H^{s-2}}\lesssim_{s}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{1}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

(3)

Let
s_{0},s_{1},s_{2}
be such that

s_{0}\leq s_{2}
and
s_{0}<s_{1}+s_{2}-1
,
then

(A.6)

\left\lVert T_{a}u\right\rVert_{H^{s_{0}}}\lesssim_{s_{0},s_{1},s_{2}}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{H^{s_{1}}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s_{2}}}.

A.3.
Symbolic Calculus and Commutators

Paradifferential calculus allows us to replace standard
algebraic operations with paraproducts, up to smoothing error terms.

If
s>1
then
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is an algebra and we can
decompose a product of two functions as follows:

uv=T_{u}v+T_{v}u+R_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v),

where the term
R_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v)
is called the Bony remainder.

Theorem A.2

(Theorem 2.85 in
[
6
]
)
.

(1)

If
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
v\in H^{s^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
s,s^{\prime}>1
,
then
R_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v)\in H^{s+s^{\prime}-1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with

\left\lVert R_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v)\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}}\lesssim_{s}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

(2)

More generally, for any
r>0
and
s\in(-r,+\infty)
,
if
u\in C^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
then
R_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v)\in H^{s+r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with

\left\lVert R_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v)\right\rVert_{H^{s+r}}\lesssim_{s,r}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{r}}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

(3)

Let
\sigma\in(0,+\infty)
and
s\in(\sigma,+\infty)
.
If
u\in C^{-\sigma}_{*}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, then

R_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v)\in H^{s-\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with

\left\lVert R_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v)\right\rVert_{H^{s-\sigma}}\lesssim_{s,r}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{-\sigma}_{*}}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

We refer the reader to
[
30
]
for the proofs of the
subsequent three results, which
arise as special cases of general
paradifferential calculus.

The first theorem establishes that the composition
of two paraproducts coincides with the paraproduct of their product,
up to a remainder that is regularizing.

Theorem A.3
.

Let
a
and
b
be two functions in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and define the
algebraic remainder

R_{\mathrm{alg}}(a,b)=T_{a}\circ T_{b}-T_{ab}.

If we further assume that
a
and
b
belong to
C^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for some real
number
r>0
, then
R_{\mathrm{alg}}(a,b)
is a smoothing operator of order
-r
. Specifically, for all

s\in\mathbb{R}
, there holds

\left\lVert R_{\mathrm{alg}}(a,b)u\right\rVert_{H^{s+r}}\lesssim_{s,r}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{C^{r}}\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{C^{r}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

In a similar vein, the operation of taking the adjoint
(T_{a})^{*}
of a paraproduct
T_{a}

behaves essentially as the complex conjugation of its symbol. Consequently,
the paraproduct associated with a real-valued function is self-adjoint,
up to a remainder exhibiting the exact same regularizing properties.

Theorem A.4
.

Consider a real-valued function
a
belonging to
C^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for
some real number
r>0
. Then, the
difference

(T_{a})^{*}-T_{a}

is smoothing of order
-r
.
Specifically, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, there holds

\left\lVert((T_{a})^{*}-T_{a})u\right\rVert_{H^{s+r}}\lesssim_{s,r}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{C^{r}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

Finally, we address the commutation properties between paraproducts
and Fourier multipliers. We consider Fourier multipliers with symbols
homogeneous of degree
0
,
such as the Beurling transform appearing in the study of the parametrix for the
paradifferential Laplace–Beltrami operator.

Theorem A.5
.

Let
m(D)
be a Fourier multiplier whose symbol
m(\xi)
is homogeneous of
degree
0
and smooth outside the origin. Let
r\in[0,1]
and
a\in C^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Then the commutator

[T_{a},m(D)]\coloneqq T_{a}m(D)-m(D)T_{a}

is a smoothing operator of order
-r
. Specifically, for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
:

\left\lVert[T_{a},m(D)]u\right\rVert_{H^{s+r}}\lesssim_{s}\mathcal{N}_{r}(m)\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{C^{r}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}},

where the constant
\mathcal{N}_{r}(m)
depends on the symbol
m
via
the semi-norm involving derivatives up to order
N_{r}\coloneqq[r]+3
:

\mathcal{N}_{r}(m)\coloneqq\sup_{|\beta|\leq N_{r}}\sup_{|\xi|\in[1/2,2]}|\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}m(\xi)|.

Appendix B
Composition Estimates

We begin with the standard algebra property and the tame
estimate for the composition of functions.

Theorem B.1

(Moser-type estimates)
.

Consider a real number
s>1
.

(1)

The Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is a Banach algebra and for all
u,v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,

(B.1)

\left\lVert uv\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\lesssim_{s}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{s}}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}.

(2)

Let
F\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be a smooth
function with
F(0)=0
. For any
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, the composition
F\circ u
belongs to
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})

and satisfies

(B.2)

\left\lVert F\circ u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}})\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}},

where
C(\cdot)
is a non-decreasing function depending
on
F
and
s
.

We now turn to analogous estimates for the
right-composition
,
namely the composition of a function
v
with a diffeomorphism
\varphi
.
For a comprehensive treatment of these results, we refer the reader
to
[
16
,
22
,
10
]
.

Proposition B.2

(Right Composition)
.

Consider a real number
s>2
. There exists a non-decreasing
function
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that the following
properties hold for all
\phi\in\mathrm{SDiff}^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

i)
Consider
\sigma\in[0,s]
. For all
v\in H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
,
the composition
v\circ\phi
belongs to
H^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and satisfies

(B.3)

\left\lVert v\circ\phi\right\rVert_{H^{\sigma}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert D\phi\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}})\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{\sigma}}.

ii)
Let
\varphi=\phi^{-1}
. The norms of the displacements

u=\phi-\Id
and
v=\varphi-\Id
satisfy

(B.4)

\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq\mathcal{F}(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{s}}).

By using the previous result, we deduce the following lemma.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
s>2
. Consider a zero-mean velocity field
u\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
.
The associated flow map
\Phi_{t}
satisfies the following estimate for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
:

\left\lVert D\Phi_{t}\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}}\leq C_{s}\exp\left(C_{s}\int_{0}^{|t|}\left\lVert\nabla u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\diff\!\tau\right)\left(1+|t|\sup_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left\lVert u(\tau)\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\right).

We also need to estimates the differences. To do so, consider two
diffeomorphisms
\Phi
and
\Phi^{\prime}
and denote their inverses by
\varphi

and
\varphi^{\prime}
. Set
Y=\Phi\circ\varphi^{\prime}
and
X=\Id-Y
, to get

\varphi-\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi\circ(Y+X)-\varphi\circ Y=\left(\int_{0}^{1}D\varphi(Y+\tau X)\diff\!\tau\right)\cdot X.

We then apply Proposition
B.2
and the Moser estimate (
B.1
), to get

(B.5)

\left\lVert\varphi-\varphi^{\prime}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}\leq C(\left\lVert D\varphi\right\rVert_{H^{s}})\left\lVert\Phi-\Phi^{\prime}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}.

This leads to the following result.

Proposition B.4
.

Let
s>2
. Consider two initial velocities
u_{0},u_{0}^{\prime}\in H^{s+1}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and their associated velocity fields
u,u^{\prime}
. For any
T\geq 0
, we define

M(T)\coloneqq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert u^{\prime}(t)\right\rVert_{H^{s+1}}.

The differences satisfy the following estimates:

(B.6)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big\lVert\omega(t)-\omega^{\prime}(t)\big\rVert_{H^{s-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{F}_{s}(T,M(T))\left\lVert u_{0}-u_{0}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{H^{s}},

(B.7)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big\lVert\Phi^{\omega_{0}}_{t}-\Phi^{\omega_{0}^{\prime}}_{t}\big\rVert_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{F}_{s}(T,M(T))\left\lVert u_{0}-u_{0}^{\prime}\right\rVert_{H^{s}},

where
\mathcal{F}_{s}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a continuous function, non-decreasing in its arguments.
