Seasonal variation in chemical composition, insecticidal and repellent activities of Conioselinum smithii (Apiaceae) essential oils against stored-product insects
Authors: Boya Li, Chen Gao, Zitong Shi, Yahui Wang, Min Jin, Shushan Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Widely used chemical insecticides pose significant risks to food safety through residue accumulation. Consequently, the development of safe and effective bio-insecticides has become a research priority. Essential oils (EOs) from medicinal plants have demonstrated notable insecticidal activity against stored-product pests. This study characterized the chemical composition of EOs from the aerial parts and roots of Conioselinum smithii (Apiaceae) collected from May to October and evaluated their insecticidal and repellent activities against Tribolium castaneum and Liposcelis bostrychophila. The highest EO yield was observed in September (root: 2.13%; aerial parts: 1.02%). Chemical analysis revealed that the aerial EOs were dominated by sesquiterpenoids and phthalides, whereas the root EOs primarily contained phthalides and monoterpenoids. Furanoeudesma-1,4-diene (23.52%–48.25%) and neocnidilide (30.30%–34.99%) exhibited the highest relative percentages in samples. The chemical compositions of the EOs collected in different months were correlated, with samples 7LD, 9LD, and 10LD exhibiting similar profiles, as did 6LG, 8LG, 9LG, and 10LG. A significant positive correlation was observed between 6LG and 22LG, 5LG, 8LG, and 9LG (p < 0.001). The EOs exhibited contact toxicity against both insects. For L. bostrychophila, the root EO collected in May showed the strongest toxicity (LC₅₀ = 17.68 µg/cm²). For T. castaneum, the aerial EO collected in May exhibited the strongest contact toxicity (LD₅₀ = 41.55 µg/adult). Certain C. smithii EO samples exhibited repellent activity superior to that of DEET: root EOs collected from June to September and aerial EOs from September showed enhanced repellency against T. castaneum, whereas root EOs collected in May, June, and August, along with the aerial EO from May, exhibited stronger repellent effects against L. bostrychophila. These findings indicate that C. smithii EOs exhibit promising insecticidal and repellent properties, supporting their potential as plant-based agents for stored-product pest management.

Introduction

Stored-product pests represent a major threat to global food security, causing substantial economic losses estimated at 10–40% of postharvest agricultural commodities worldwide1. Among these, Tribolium castaneum (Herbst) (Coleoptera: Tenebrionidae) and Liposcelis bostrychophila (Badonnel) (Psocoptera: Liposcelididae) are two of the most prevalent and destructive species, infesting stored cereals, processed grain products, and various dried food commodities2. T. castaneum is well-documented for its remarkable adaptability to diverse environmental conditions and its resistance to multiple classes of insecticides3. Similarly, L. bostrychophila, a parthenogenetic species with extraordinary adaptive capacity, can rapidly colonize storage facilities and cause significant qualitative and quantitative losses4. Infestations by these pests not only result in direct product damage through feeding but also lead to contamination with fecal matter, exuviae, and webbing, rendering stored products unfit for human consumption5.

Research on bioactive compounds from medicinal plants as alternatives to chemical pesticides is of considerable importance for environmental sustainability, biodiversity conservation, and human health protection6. Residues and metabolites of chemical pesticides pose a significant risk of contaminating soil, water, and air7. Airborne pesticide drift from spraying or volatilization can contaminate non-target crops, adversely affect plant diversity, and cause economic losses8. Farmers and workers in the agrochemical industry are considered high-risk groups; studies have shown that occupational exposure to chemical pesticides can increase indicators of genetic damage, such as chromosome aberrations, micronuclei formation, and DNA damage, thereby posing a significant threat to their health9.

Considerable research has been conducted on plant-based pesticides. Extracts from the whole plant, leaves, and fruits of Piper yunnanense possess varying degrees of toxicity against pests such as T. castaneum and L. bostrychophila. The fruit EO exhibits the strongest lethal effect against T. castaneum, whereas the whole-plant EO is most effective against L. bostrychophila10. Similarly, the pesticidal efficacy of Zanthoxylum myriacanthum fruit EOs varies with extraction time: EOs obtained after 1 h of extraction show pronounced activity against L. bostrychophila, whereas those extracted after 5–7 h are most effective against T. castaneum11.

The Apiaceae family includes a variety of medicinal and aromatic plants known for their high EO yields and easy availability of raw materials, which are used in various industrial applications12. Conioselinum smithii (syn. Ligusticum sinense) is a perennial herbaceous plant indigenous to high-altitude regions of China, traditionally valued for its medicinal roots. Despite the documented insecticidal potential of Apiaceae family plants, investigations on C. smithii EOs remain limited, particularly concerning seasonal variations in chemical composition and biological activity. Furthermore, the pest management potential of aerial parts—typically discarded as agricultural byproducts—has not been systematically evaluated. To address these knowledge gaps, the present study systematically investigated the chemical composition and insecticidal activity of EOs derived from both aerial parts and roots of C. smithii collected monthly over a growing season.

The specific objectives of this study were to (1) characterize seasonal variations in EO yield and chemical composition from aerial parts and roots of C. smithii using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS); (2) evaluate the contact toxicity of monthly collected EOs against T. castaneum and L. bostrychophila using topical application and filter paper methods, respectively; and (3) assess the repellent activity of these EOs against both pest species. The EO samples from aerial parts and roots were designated as “month LD” and “month LG”, respectively. The aerial parts and roots collected in 2022 were labeled “22LD” and “22LG”, respectively. The findings of this study may inform the development of sustainable, plant-based pest management strategies for stored-product protection while promoting the valorization of agricultural byproducts derived from medicinal plant cultivation.

Results

EO extraction

Details of plant sample collection and EOs extraction are summarized in Table 1. Figure 1 illustrates the monthly variation in EO yield from aerial parts and roots of C. smithii. Samples collected in September 2023 exhibited the highest oil yield. The three replicate extractions showed significant repeatability. Correlation analysis between EO yield, and collection month is presented in Table 2. The mean EO yield from roots (3.94% ± 3.91%) was numerically higher than that from aerial parts (1.06% ± 0.79%). However, this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05), likely due to the limited sample size and high variability. Collection month demonstrated no significant correlation with the EO yield (p > 0.40), and linear regression models accounted for less than 12% of yield variance. Similarly, one-way ANOVA confirmed no significant seasonal effect on oil yield (F = 0.625, p > 0.05).

Chemical composition of EOs

The chemical composition and relative percentage content of the EOs from the aerial parts and roots of C. smithii are presented in Tables 3 and 4. A total of 66 compounds were identified in the EOs collected monthly from the aerial parts, accounting for 91.76–99.27% of the total relative EO percentage. Meanwhile, 50 compounds were identified in the root EOs, representing 94.47–99.49% of the total relative percentage. In the aerial EOs, furanoeudesma-1,4-diene was the most abundant component in samples 5LD, 6LD, 8LD, and 9LD (23.52%–48.25%), whereas neocnidilide predominated in samples 7LD (34.99%) and 10LD (30.30%). E-Ligustilide was another relatively abundant compound, accounted for approximately 20% of samples 5LD, 10LD, and 22LD. E-Ligustilide was the predominant component in the root EOs of C. smithii collected monthly in 2023, with relative percentages ranging from 33.09% to 67.80% across samples. However, in sample 7LG, Z-ligustilide exhibited the highest relative percentage (45.11%). In addition, samples 5LG and 6LG contained the sesquiterpene compound furanoeudesma-1,4-diene at relative percentages exceeding 20%.

The major compound classes identified in aerial EOs were sesquiterpenes, phthalides, and esters. Sesquiterpenes predominated in samples 5LD, 6LD, 8LD, and 9LD, with the highest proportion (69.41%) recorded in 6LD. Phthalides constituted the major compound class in samples 22LD, 7LD, and 10LD, reaching a maximum of 56.09% in 10LD. In root EOs, monoterpenes and phthalides were the dominant compound classes. Monoterpene content exceeded 30% in 5LG and 8LG, reached 46.39% in 10LG, and peaked at 68.83% in 22LG. Phthalide content in root EOs was highest in 9LG (73%), though no statistically significant trend with collection month was detected (p > 0.05).

Pearson correlation analysis revealed that ester content was significantly positively correlated with collection month (r = 0.850, p = 0.015) (Table 5), indicating a progressive increase throughout the growing season. All other chemical classes demonstrated no significant correlation with collection month (p > 0.05). These findings suggest that seasonal factors specifically modulate ester biosynthesis, whereas variations in other chemical classes may be attributed to additional factors such as plant developmental stage or environmental conditions.

Inter-class correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between sesquiterpene hydrocarbons and phthalides (r = -0.843, p = 0.017) (Table 6), indicating an inverse relationship between these two major chemical classes. The negative correlation between sesquiterpene hydrocarbons and phthalides suggests a metabolic trade-off between these two biosynthetic pathways, possibly reflecting substrate competition or regulatory shifts during plant development. All other pairwise correlations were not significant (all p > 0.05), indicating relatively independent accumulation patterns.

PCA was performed on the chemical components with relative content > 2% in the aerial parts and roots of C. smithii. The results are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. The compound numbers in Figs. 2 and 3 correspond to those in Tables 3 and 4. The PCA results of aerial parts EOs explained 50.55% of the variance in chemical components, with PC1 accounting for 26.17% and PC2 for 24.38%. Samples 6LD, 8LD, and 9LD were located on the positive side of PC1, 7LD was positioned near the origin, and the remaining samples were scattered on the negative side of PC1. Samples 7LD, 9LD, and 10LD were relatively close to each other, indicating a degree of similarity between them. Sample 7LD appeared on the positive half-axis of PC2 and was strongly correlated with ester components. Sample 5LD was negatively correlated with PC1 and positively correlated with PC2, whereas 6LD was positively correlated with both PC1 and PC2. Both samples were associated with sesquiterpene components.

The biplot reveals that the primary compositional gradient (PC1) separates sesquiterpene-rich early-season aerial samples (5LD, 6LD) from phthalide-rich late-season and overwintering samples (10LD, 22LD), reflecting a metabolic shift from sesquiterpene biosynthesis during the vegetative period to phthalide accumulation during the reproductive and senescence phases. The intermediate positioning of 7LD and 9LD near the origin suggests transitional chemical profiles, consistent with the flowering period (July–September) during which both sesquiterpene and phthalide pathways remain active. Notably, 22LD and 10LD cluster in the same quadrant, indicating relative consistency of autumn aerial EOs composition across years.

The PCA of the monthly C. smithii root EO collections showed that PC1 explained 32.95% of the variance and PC2 explained 24.87%, together accounting for 64.82% of the variability in chemical composition. The sample points for 5LG and 8LG were relatively clustered, as were those for 6LG, 9LG, and 10LG; all these samples were located on the negative half-axis of PC2. The first two samples (5LG and 8LG) were positioned on the positive half-axis of PC1, whereas the latter three (6LG, 9LG, and 10LG) were positioned on the negative half-axis of PC1. Samples 8LG, 6LG, 9LG, and 10LG showed correlations with monoterpene components; among these, 6LG and 10LG were also associated with the phthalide compound ligustilide. Sample 7LG was located on the negative half-axis of PC2 and the positive half-axis of PC1. The considerable distance from the other sample points and chemical compositions indicated a significant difference in composition.

The root biplot indicates that PC1 primarily separates samples by monoterpene versus phthalide dominance: 22LG and, to a lesser extent, 5LG are positioned toward the positive PC1 direction, associated with monoterpene variables, whereas 9LG and 10LG are located in the negative PC1 region, aligned with phthalide (ligustilide) vectors. This distribution suggests a progressive shift in root secondary metabolism from monoterpene prevalence in the early growing season to phthalide accumulation as the season advances. The distinct isolation of 7LG in the upper-left quadrant, strongly associated with ester variables, implies that July represents a unique metabolic state in root EOs biosynthesis, possibly associated with the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth. The clustering of 6LG with 9LG and 10LG in the lower-left region suggests that root EOs composition becomes relatively stable during the latter half of the growing season.

Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between 6LG and 22LG, 5LG, 8LG, and 9LG at the 0.001 level; between 6LD and 5LD; between 10LG and 9LG at the 0.01 level; and between 10LG and 5LG and 22LG at the 0.05 level.

Hierarchical clustering analysis classified the 14 C. smithii samples into two groups: Category I included 6LG, 22LG, 5LG, 10LG, 8LG, and 9LG, and Category II comprised the remaining eight samples. Within Category I, 22LG and 5LG were closely associated, as were 8LG and 9LG. In Category II, pairs 5LD and 9LD, as well as 7LG and 8LD, showed closer associations, consistent with the PCA results. These findings indicate that, except for 7LG, there were significant differences in the chemical composition between the aerial parts and roots of C. smithii throughout the annual growth cycle. The chemical composition of the aerial parts fluctuated more markedly from month to month, whereas that of the roots remained strongly correlated during the spring and summer collection periods in this study.

Discussion

Botanical insecticides have emerged as promising alternatives to synthetic pesticides for stored-product pest management, offering advantages such as reduced environmental persistence, lower mammalian toxicity, and reduced risk of pest resistance development13. EOs, as complex mixtures of volatile secondary metabolites, exhibit diverse bioactivities against storage insects through multiple modes of action, rendering them particularly valuable for integrated pest management programs14. Within the Apiaceae family, numerous species have demonstrated considerable potential as sources of bioactive EOs with insecticidal and repellent properties15. The present study investigated the seasonal variation in chemical composition and insecticidal activity of C. smithii EOs.

Table 10 presents the meteorological monitoring data from the Benxi station in Liaoning Province, obtained from the China Meteorological Data Network (http://data.cma.cn/). According to these data, July experiences the highest number of rainy days locally, whereas August records the greatest precipitation throughout the year. Although statistical analysis revealed no significant seasonal effect on EO yield (p > 0.05), the oil yield from both the aerial parts and roots appeared to increase from July onward. As noted in the Flora of China, the flowering period of C. smithii occurs in August, followed by a fruiting period from September to October. Previous studies have reported higher EO yields during flowering stages in certain perennial herbs16. Changes in climatic factors, such as temperature, sunshine duration, and precipitation, directly affect the oil yield of harvested plants17. Low temperatures result in the highest EO yield of Ocimum basilicum in winter, which may explain the high oil yield of the roots of the perennial herb C. smithii collected in November 2022 and October 202318. Environmental factors—including light, nutrients, water, temperature, and CO₂—influence plant growth and metabolism19. As the samples were collected from the same planting base and irrigation was provided, the influence of nutrient and water availability was excluded from the experiment. Therefore, the impact of light and temperature on plant EOs should be emphasized. High temperatures increase the allocation of photosynthetic products to leaves while simultaneously reducing the accumulation of assimilates in roots20. This phenomenon may partly account for the observed numerical EO yield gap between the aerial parts and roots across months, although these patterns were not statistically significant.

The composition of the C. smithii EO has been extensively investigated. Li et al. conducted a chemical recognition model experiment on 28 batches of C. smithii EO collected from different regions. Their results showed that the substances with the highest average relative content were neophenolactone (2.27–57.60%) and phthalides (34.62–70.28%). In addition, β-carvone, elemin, n-butylphthalide, Z-butylphthalide, ligusticolactone, and Z-ligusticolactone were identified as potential characteristic components through unpaired t-tests21. In this study, the phthalide content in the EOs of C. smithii collected in June and July showed notable variation. Medium-wave ultraviolet (UV-B) radiation, a component of solar radiation, has been used as an influencing factor to examine the variation patterns of phthalide compounds in Angelica sinensis. A previous study concluded that irradiation at a dose of 1.870 W/m2, as measured by a radiometer, did not significantly increase phthalide content. However, exposure to a higher dose of 2.253 W/m2 induced an adverse stress response, leading to a significant increase in phthalide components22. Therefore, light exposure is an important factor influencing the accumulation of phthalide components in Apiaceae plants, which may partially contribute to the variation in phthalide content observed in C. smithii EOs collected in June and July. Occasionally, some detected EO components are not inherently present in the plants but are produced through the hydrolysis of compounds within the plant tissues. In addition, certain EO components result from the decomposition of precursor compounds during distillation. The mevalonate (MVA) pathway produces sesquiterpenes, the methylerythritol 4-phosphate (MEP) pathway yields monoterpenes and diterpenes, and the shikimic acid pathway generates phenylpropenes23. The mechanisms by which these compounds accumulate via different pathways and transform into one another may explain the correlations observed among the components of the C. smithii samples collected in different months.

The C. smithii samples in this study exhibited varying degrees of toxicity against T. castaneum and L. bostrychophila. Among the monthly samples, those collected in May exhibited the strongest toxic activity against T. castaneum and the root EO against L. bostrychophila. Except for 8LD, the EOs extracted from the aerial parts in other months exhibited similar activity against L. bostrychophila (LC50 = 22.68–30.76 µg/cm2). Contrary to our findings, Qi et al.24 reported that the Cinnamomum glanduliferum EO collected in September had the most significant toxic effect on both T. castaneum and L. bostrychophila. Contact toxicity tests of Hedychium longipetalum EO on L. bostrychophila have indicated that the plant collected in December exhibits the strongest toxic effect25. Additionally, the active effect of Mesosphaerum suaveolens EO against storage pests was found to have seasonal variations, with samples collected during the dry season demonstrating the most pronounced toxic effects17,26. These findings suggest that the optimal collection time for different plant species to achieve the strongest control effect on storage pests varies, thus warranting further investigation.

Insecticidal activity results from a series of complex interactions between toxic substances and insect tissues. These complex toxicokinetics can be categorized into three main processes: penetration, activation, and detoxification27. Toxic substances must penetrate the insect’s outer cuticle and the membranes of target organs to exert their lethal effects. However, some toxin components may be metabolized and neutralized by the insect’s defense mechanisms. The ultimate dose of the toxin that acts on insects determines their toxicity. The insecticidal and repellent activities of C. smithii EOs are likely related to their major chemical constituents, which likely act through multiple neurotoxic mechanisms. Furanoeudesma-1,4-diene, the dominant sesquiterpene in samples 5LD, 6LD, 8LD, and 9LD (23.52–48.25%), has been reported to exhibit significant insecticidal activity against stored-product insects28. By structural analogy, furanoeudesma-1,4-diene possesses a furanodiene scaffold characteristic of eudesmane-type sesquiterpenes; eudesmane-derived sesquiterpenoids have been demonstrated to inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE) in a non-competitive manner, with molecular docking studies confirming their binding to both the catalytic and peripheral sites of the enzyme29. However, whether furanoeudesma-1,4-diene itself directly inhibits insect AChE remains to be experimentally verified. Consistent with the reported insecticidal activity, the elevated contact toxicity observed in samples with high furanoeudesma-1,4-diene content (particularly 5LD and 6LD against T. castaneum) suggests that this compound may contribute to the observed bioactivity. Neocnidilide, which predominated in samples 7LD and 10LD (30.30–34.99%), is a well-characterized phthalide with documented insecticidal properties30. Neocnidilide and the structurally related phthalide butylidenephthalide have both been reported to exhibit insecticidal activity. Butylidenephthalide showed contact toxicity against imported fire ants (LC₅₀ = 11–16.4 µg/g) and repellent activity against mosquitoes31. Ligustilide isomers (E- and Z-), the major constituents in root EOs, possess a cross-conjugated diene system that provides multiple reactive sites for biological interactions32. Z-Ligustilide, the predominant isomer, has been reported to exhibit anticholinergic activity32. By extension, ligustilide may disrupt insect cholinergic neurotransmission, although direct evidence in insects is lacking. Root EOs generally exhibited stronger contact toxicity than aerial EOs against L. bostrychophila, which may be attributed to the high ligustilide content. The LC₅₀ of 5LG (17.68 µg/cm²) was comparable to that of the positive control pyrethrins (18.72 µg/cm²).

Beyond AChE inhibition, EO constituents may exert insecticidal effects through additional neurotoxic pathways. Monoterpene and sesquiterpene components have been shown to positively modulate insect GABA-gated chloride channels, resulting in hyperexcitation and mortality33. The repellent activity of C. smithii EOs may also involve octopamine receptors, which are specific to invertebrates and represent an attractive target for selective insect control34. Although the major constituents of C. smithii EOs have not been directly tested for octopaminergic activity, other EO components—such as eugenol, α-terpineol, and geraniol—have been demonstrated to act as octopamine receptor agonists, increasing intracellular cAMP and Ca²⁺ levels and thereby disrupting normal behavioral and physiological processes in insects34. As octopamine receptors are absent in mammals, this mechanism offers a favorable selectivity profile for bio-insecticide development. The enhanced repellent activity observed at 4 h compared to 2 h may be attributed to cumulative exposure effects, where EO vapors gradually saturate the test environment and overwhelm insect olfactory systems. Furthermore, the complex chemical composition of EOs enables multi-target action—simultaneously affecting AChE, GABA receptors, and potentially octopamine receptors—which may contribute to broad-spectrum insecticidal activity and delay resistance development compared with single-target synthetic insecticides28. It should be noted, however, that EOs as complex mixtures may exhibit bioactivity that differs from the sum of their individual components, and synergistic or antagonistic interactions among minor constituents cannot be excluded30.

To contextualize the observed bioactivity, comparison with a standard synthetic insecticide is informative. Dichlorvos, a widely used organophosphate insecticide for stored-product protection, exhibited an LD₅₀ of 0.040 µg/adult against T. castaneum by topical application, approximately 1,000-fold more potent than the most active C. smithii EO sample (5LD, LD₅₀ = 41.55 µg/adult)35. For L. bostrychophila, dichlorvos showed an LC₅₀ of 54.4 µg/m² by surface contact assay, substantially more active than the C. smithii root EO (5LG, LC₅₀ = 17.68 µg/cm²), although direct comparison is limited by the different bioassay methods employed36. Notably, the contact toxicity of the most active C. smithii EO samples against L. bostrychophila (e.g., 5LG: LC₅₀ = 17.68 µg/cm²) was comparable to that of the botanical positive control pyrethrins (LC₅₀ = 18.72 µg/cm²), suggesting that C. smithii EOs possess contact toxicity within the range of established botanical insecticides. The lower acute toxicity of EOs relative to synthetic compounds is well-documented and expected, as EOs are complex mixtures in which the active constituents typically constitute only a fraction of the total mass34. However, EOs offer several compensatory advantages, including reduced mammalian toxicity, lower environmental persistence, multi-target modes of action that may delay resistance development, and the potential for synergistic interactions among constituents34. Furthermore, formulation strategies such as nanoemulsification, encapsulation, or combination with synergists have been shown to substantially enhance EO efficacy against stored-product pests, narrowing the performance gap with synthetic alternatives.

C. smithii is suitable for growth in shady, humid areas at altitudes of 1,250–2,500 m, such as under forests, in meadows, and along gullies37. It has a broad geographical range under these conditions and exhibits a high plant survival rate, making it suitable for large-scale cultivation. This provides a plant-based foundation for the industrial development of insecticides. This study demonstrated that the EOs of C. smithii collected from May to October exhibited insecticidal and repellent activity against the target pests. Among the monthly samples, those collected in May exhibited the strongest insecticidal activity, suggesting that this period may be preferable for harvesting when bioactivity is the primary consideration. Moreover, the growing consumer demand for reduced pesticide residues in stored products supports the continued exploration of plant-based insecticides as complementary tools for integrated pest management.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the EOs were obtained by steam distillation, which may not fully preserve heat-sensitive constituents and could potentially affect bioactivity; alternative extraction methods such as supercritical CO₂ extraction may yield distinct chemical profiles. Second, although GC–MS analysis identified the major compounds, the bioactivity of individual components was not evaluated through isolation or bioassay-guided fractionation. Consequently, the contribution of specific compounds to overall insecticidal activity remains to be elucidated. Third, the sampling was conducted in a single geographical area and the collection period did not cover the entire growing season. The observed patterns of composition and bioactivity may reflect the specific environmental conditions at a single location during a single year, rather than consistent seasonal trends. Therefore, the universality of the reported seasonal variations remains to be validated through multi-year, multi-location investigations. Finally, insecticidal activity was evaluated exclusively against adult insects, effects on other developmental stages (eggs, larvae, and pupae) were not investigated.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and extraction of EOs

Plant identification and voucher specimen

The plant materials were collected from the medicinal plant cultivation area in Qingyuan County, Liaoning Province, China (E 124°55′, N 42°05′). The aerial parts and roots of C. smithii were collected in November 2022 and monthly from May to October 2023, specifically on the 25th of each month. The plant was identified by a qualified botanist, Dr. Liu, from the College of Life Science at Beijing Normal University, Beijing, China. Voucher specimens have been deposited in the herbarium of Beijing Normal University, Beijing, China. The voucher specimen deposition number for aerial parts and roots are recorded as “BNU-DSS-2023-Month-LD” and “BNU-DSS-2023-Month-LG”, respectively. The aerial parts and roots samples collected in 2022 were labeled as “BNU-DSS-2022-LD” and “BNU-DSS-2022-LG”, respectively. This temporal coverage was designed to span the phenological cycle of C. smithii, including the vegetative phase (May–July), flowering phase (August–September), and fruiting/senescence phase (October).

EOs extraction

The whole plant was divided into two parts: aerial and root. After drying, the samples were crushed and weighed. The processed samples were divided into three equal-weight portions and placed in a modified Clevenger extractor. Water was added to cover the sample liquid at an approximate ratio of 10:1 (v/w). The EOs were extracted using the water distillation method. After the water boiled, the extraction was continued for 5 h (when no more EO was obtained). The volumes of the obtained EOs were recorded. The EOs were collected and stored in a refrigerator at 4℃.

Chemicals

n-Hexane (97%) was obtained from Beijing Chemical Works (Beijing, China). Teflon was obtained from Beijing Sino-Rich Material Science (Beijing, China).

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry

Alkane series for RI determination

For retention index determination, a homologous series of n-alkanes (C₈–C₄₀, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was analyzed under identical chromatographic conditions. Retention indices (RI) were calculated relative to the retention times of n-alkanes according to the Kovats method. Compound identification was performed by comparing the experimental RI values with literature data and by matching mass spectra with the WILEY 275 and NIST 05 libraries. The mass spectral similarity threshold for compound identification was set at > 85%.

GC–MS conditions

The chemical compositions of the EOs were analyzed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: a quartz capillary column (DB-5MS, 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) was used with high-purity helium (99.999%) as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The injection volume was 1 µL, with a split ratio of 1:20. The injection port temperature was maintained at 250℃. The initial column temperature was 60℃, maintained for 5 min, then increased to 290℃ at a rate of 7℃ /min, and held for 5 min. The ion-source temperature was 200℃. The mass spectrometry conditions included electron impact ionization mode with an ionization energy of 70 eV. The transmission line temperature was set to 250℃. The mass scan range was 50–550 m/z and operated in the full scan mode.

Insect rearing

The target insects were reared in waterproof incubators at 28–30 °C and 70–80% relative humidity. The diet of T. castaneum consisted of flour mixed with yeast at a ratio of 10:1, and the insects were kept in 250 mL wide-mouth bottles. L. bostrychophila was fed a mixture of 80-mesh sifted wheat flour, yeast, and milk powder in a 10:1:1 ratio, contained in 100 mL conical bottles. Teflon was applied to the inner upper quarter of the bottles to prevent insects from escaping.

Contact toxicity

Due to the distinct biological characteristics of the two target insects, different bioassay methods were employed. The contact activity of T. castaneum was determined using the topical application method38. A total of 0.5 µL of diluted EO was applied to the front thorax or dorsal plate of the insect. Ten adult insects were then transferred to a glass bottle measuring 2.5 cm in diameter and 5.5 cm in height, and the screw cap was tightened to prevent their escape. The results are expressed as LD₅₀ (µg/adult). The contact activity of L. bostrychophila was assessed using the filter paper method39. A total of 300 µL of the diluted EO was evenly applied to a circular filter paper with a diameter of 5.5 cm. After the reagent dried naturally, the filter paper was firmly adhered to the bottom of a Teflon-coated Petri dish. Ten adult insects, 1–2 weeks post-emergence and not yet sexually differentiated, were introduced into a Petri dish, which was sealed with a lid. The results are expressed as LC₅₀ (µg/cm²).

Each EO was diluted with n-hexane, which served as the negative control for the assays. Pyrethrins were used as a positive control, as they represent a widely adopted botanical insecticide standard in stored-product pest bioassays. The positive control was tested at the same concentration gradients as the EO samples under identical experimental conditions. Three concentrations were used in the preliminary experiment (T. castaneum: 50%, 10%, and 2%; L. bostrychophila: 5%, 1%, and 0.2%). For the formal experiment, five concentration gradients were established (the concentrations are shown in Table 11), with five replicates for each. The treated test insects were placed in a water bath incubator maintained at a constant temperature of 28–30℃ and relative humidity of 70–80%. Mortality was assessed after 24 h, insects showing no movement when gently prodded with a soft brush were recorded as dead.

Repellent effect

Adult insects that emerged 1–2 weeks prior were selected for repellent activity tests. The repellent activity assay for T. castaneum was conducted using the area preference method40. The EOs were diluted with n-hexane to concentrations of 78.63, 15.73, 3.15, 0.63, and 0.13 nL/cm2 (the equivalent concentration percentages are 0.5%, 0.1%, 0.02%, 0.004%, and 0.0008%). Five replicates were performed for each sample. A circular filter paper with a diameter of 9 cm was cut into two halves. A total of 500 µL of the diluted sample solution was evenly applied to one half of the filter paper to saturation, whereas the other half was treated with an equal volume of n-hexane as a negative control. The filter paper was placed in a room-temperature (25 ± 2°C) environment and left to stand for 3 min. After all the n-hexane had evaporated completely, the two halves of the filter paper were reassembled and affixed to the bottom of a culture dish using solid glue. Twenty test insects were placed at the center of each dish. The treated insects were maintained in a constant-temperature incubator under the same culture conditions. The distribution of insects between the treated and control halves was recorded after 2 and 4 h. N, N-Diethyl-meta-toluamide (DEET), a widely used synthetic repellent standard, was included as a positive control at the same concentration gradients as the EO samples.

The method for determining the repellent activity of L. bostrychophila was similar to that used for T. castaneum, except that the EO was diluted to concentrations of 63.17, 12.63, 2.53, 0.51, and 0.10 nL/cm2 (the equivalent concentration percentages are 0.5%, 0.1%, 0.02%, 0.004%, and 0.0008%). The inner wall of the culture dish was coated with Teflon to prevent the insects from escaping. Petri dishes and circular filter papers (5.5 cm in diameter) were used, with a chemical dosage of 150 µL applied to one side of each filter paper. The filter paper was left at room temperature (25 ± 2°C) for 1.5 min to allow complete evaporation of the n-hexane solvent before the test insects were introduced.

Statistical analysis

EOs yield and chemical composition analysis

The EOs yield and chemical composition were analyzed using Python (version 3.12) and RStudio (v1.4.3, Boston, MA, USA). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using the FactoMineR and factoextra packages41. Spearman’s correlation analysis and visualization were performed using the linkET, psych, and ggplot2 packages.

Insect resistance activity analysis

The mortality rate curves for the contact activity of T. castaneum and L. bostrychophila at varying concentrations or doses were calculated using probit regression, with the median lethal dose (LD50) or concentration (LC₅₀) as the evaluation criterion.
