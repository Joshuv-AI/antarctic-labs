Active ROP2 triggers leaf senescence and orchestrates the growth-senescence trade-off under nitrogen starvation
Authors: Tian Zhang, Heng Ye, Ying Zhang, Jianfei Wang, Jing Wang, Shiyi Lixiang, Zhixi Lin, Yan Xiong, Yanlin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNitrogen (N) deficiency-induced leaf senescence is a genetically programmed process that facilitates plant adaptation to nutrient-limited conditions. Although numerous transcription factors (TFs) involved in N deficiency-induced leaf senescence have been identified, how they are regulated remains largely unknown. Here, we discovered that a plant-specific small GTPase, RHO of Plant 2 (ROP2), acts as an upstream positive regulator that bridges N starvation to leaf senescence by modulating key leaf senescence-associated TFs. Plants expressing constitutively active ROP2 (CA-ROP2) promote cotyledon early senescence under N-deficient conditions, by inducing the expression of key senescence-promoting NAC family TFs (ORE1, NAP, ANAC005), while strongly suppressing the expression of these TFs' inhibitory regulators (HASTY and NLA). On the contrary, CA-ROP2 suppresses the expression of the negative regulation TFs of N limitation-induced leaf senescence, like WRKY53 and NIGT1. The two opposing regulatory axes of CA-ROP2 coordinately accelerate premature cotyledon senescence under N starvation. Furthermore, phenotypic and time-series transcriptomic analysis revealed that CA-ROP2, relative to the wild type (WT), maintains shoot growth and elevated expression of growth- and development-related genes during early N starvation, yet later induces premature cotyledon senescence and drives a pronounced transcriptional switch to upregulate senescence-associated genes. Together, ROP2 converts from a “growth promoter” to a “senescence-initiating regulator” depending on environmental nitrogen status, unveiling a novel mechanism by which plants balance growth and senescence to adapt to nutrient stress.

Introduction

Leaf senescence is a genetically programmed, highly ordered process of organ degradation and nutrient remobilization, crucial for plant adaptation to environmental stresses and completion of their life cycle. 1 , 2 This process is coordinately regulated by internal developmental signals, such as developmental age and hormones, and external environmental factors, like light, nutrients, and biotic or abiotic stresses. 1 , 3

Nitrogen (N) is the most abundant mineral nutrient in plants and serves as vital element for biological macromolecules formation. 4 , 5 The N nutritional status significantly affects plant growth, development, and adaptability. 6 N starvation markedly induces leaf senescence to promote N remobilization from older leaves to younger leaves. 7 , 8 The molecular mechanisms of N starvation-induced leaf senescence have been widely studied. In Arabidopsis, a central advance has been the identification of numerous transcription factors (TFs) that orchestrate this process. 9‐11 Among these TFs, the NAC-family transcription factor ORESARA1 (ORE1)/ANAC092 is not only acts as a key regulator of developmental and dark-induced leaf senescence, 12 , 13 but also serves as a core positive regulator in N starvation-induced leaf senescence. 14 Under N starvation the expression of ORE1 was highly induced. In addition, PHOSPHATE 2 (PHO2) and NITROGEN LIMITATION ADAPTATION (NLA) form a polyubiquitination module to destabilize ORE1 protein under N starvation conditions. 14‐16 On the other hand, the ubiquitin-specific proteases 12 and 13 (UBP12/13) decrease the polyubiquitination of ORE1 to restore its stability. 17 , 18 Thus, UBP12/13 counteract the NLA–PHO2 module to modulate ORE1 protein level during N starvation-induced leaf senescence. Interestingly, similar to the inducing of ORE1, the expression of NLA and PHO were also highly induced by N starvation. 15 In addition, the HASTY protein, a key factor in miRNA processing and export, generates miRNAs that directly target and degrade ORE1, NAP, and ANAC055 transcripts, thereby delaying leaf senescence under N starvation. 19 These findings suggest that plants have evolved a hierarchical regulatory mechanism for ORE1 to control N starvation-induced leaf senescence. Moreover, N starvation destabilizes another TF, growth, development and splicing 1 (GDS1), via the anaphase-promoting complex subunit 10 (APC10), which in turn reactivates the expression of phytochrome-interacting transcription factors 4 and 5 (PIF4 and PIF5), thereby inducing leaf senescence. 20 Interestingly, NIGT1, a TF negatively regulating nitrate-inducible gene expression, also acts as a negative regulator in the N starvation-induced leaf senescence. 21‐23 Furthermore, WRKY53, a key nodal transcription factor, has been reported to integrate multiple senescence signals and can also suppress the senescence process by activating ATL31 expression under N starvation conditions. 24 , 25 However, the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms by which N starvation affects leaf senescence are still fragmentary, especially the upstream signaling mechanisms through which plants perceive N starvation signals and integrate the downstream transcriptional regulation of leaf senescence remain poorly understood.

RHO of Plant 2 (ROP2) is a plant-specific small GTPase that functions as a pivotal molecular switch in regulating plant morphogenesis, like anisotropic growth, cell division orientation, and cell adhesion. 26‐28 Our previous work and other labs' studies have shown that ROP2, by sensing auxin, activates the target of rapamycin (TOR) pathway, thereby promoting cell proliferation, leaf development, and root growth. 29‐31 Notably, diverse N sources could activate ROP2, which in turn enhances TOR activity to promote shoot growth. 32 These findings indicate that ROP2 is involved in the N-TOR-regulated shoot growth. However, whether ROP2 is also involved in other plant physiological processes regulation under fluctuating N supply remains largely unknown.

In this study, we revealed that activated ROP2 not only functions as a key regulator to promote shoot growth, but also serves as an upstream positive regulator for inducing premature leaf senescence under N starvation. Further, we found that constitutively active ROP2 (CA-ROP2) highly upregulates the expression of key leaf senescence-promoting transcription factors (e.g., ORE1, NAP, ANAC005), while strongly suppressing the expression of leaf senescence-inhibitory transcription factors (e.g., HASTY, NIGT1, WRKY53) under prolonged N starvation. The combined effect of these opposing regulatory actions drives CA-ROP2-induced premature leaf senescence. In contrast, the rop2/4/6 mutant exhibited delayed leaf senescence and attenuated the N starvation-induced upregulation of key senescence-promoting TF genes relative to WT plants. These data suggested that ROP2 acts as an upstream integrator, linking N starvation signals to the downstream leaf senescence-related key TF regulators. To assess the genome-wide impact of ROP2 on plant gene expression, we conducted time-course transcriptomic profiling of WT and CA-ROP2 seedlings under both N-sufficient and -deficient conditions. Strikingly, we found that compared to WT, CA-ROP2 maintained elevated expression of growth- and development-related genes in the early phase of N starvation, yet later drove a pronounced shift toward the upregulation of senescence-associated genes. The unique gene regulatory pattern observed in CA-ROP2 plants aligns perfectly with their growth and senescence phenotypes under N starvation conditions, indicating that ROP2 is a key regulator coordinating the balance between plant growth and senescence. Together, this study redefines the functional role of ROP2, from a general growth promoter to a critical N-responsive environmental sensor that coordinates the balance between leaf growth and senescence.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The Columbia-0 (Col-0) ecotype of Arabidopsis thaliana was used as the wild type (WT) in this study. The constitutively active ROP2 (CA-ROP2) transgenic plant and rop2/4/6 mutant in Col-0 background have been described previously. 33 , 34 The Arabidopsis seeds were surface-sterilized by 75% ethanol for 5 minutes at room temperature, followed by three rinses with sterile water for 5 minutes each, and then stratified in darkness at 4 °C for 72 hours. Subsequently, the seeds were uniformly sown on 1/4 solid Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 0.8% agar, pH 5.7), with N (+N) or without N (-N) according to the experimental setup. The seedlings were incubated in a growth chamber at 22 °C, 65% humidity, and 75 μmol·m−2·s−1 light intensity under a 12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod unless otherwise stated. The plants were grown vertically on the medium for 6 to 24 d, and photos were taken or samples were collected at the indicated time points. All phenotypic observations and sample collections were conducted under the controlled conditions described above, with at least three independent biological replicates.

Measurement of chlorophylls

Chlorophyll content was determined using the extraction method (acetone: anhydrous ethanol: H₂O = 4.5:4.5:1, v/v/v). 35 WT, CA-ROP2, and rop2/4/6 mutant seedlings were harvested at 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 d after the onset of N-deficient treatment, with six biological replicates per time point. Fresh tissue (20 mg) was homogenized in 400 μL of extraction buffer and incubated in the dark at 4 °C for 24 h until the tissues were completely bleached. Following extraction, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, and their absorbance was measured at 663 and 645 nm using a spectrophotometer, with the extraction buffer serving as a blank control. Total chlorophyll concentrations were calculated according to the Arnon equation. 36 Pigment content was expressed as mg·g−1 fresh weight (FW) based on the tissue fresh weight and extraction buffer volume. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Graphing and statistical analyzes were performed using GraphPad Prism software.

Quantitative RT‒PCR

The expression levels of genes were detected using real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‒PCR). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen), and first-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit (Promega). The qRT‒PCR reactions were performed using the SYBR Green dye method (Bio-Rad) in a CFX96 real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad). The reaction system had a total volume of 12 µL, containing 1 µL of cDNA template, 0.5 µL each of forward and reverse primers, 6 µL of SYBR Green master mix, and was made up to 12 µL with ddH₂O. Primers were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST online tool and synthesized by General Biol (Anhui) Co., Ltd (Supplementary Table 1). TUB4 was used as an internal reference gene. Three biological replicates and three technical replicates were set for each sample. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and graphs were plotted and statistically analyzed by using GraphPad Prism.

RNA-seq analysis

To perform transcriptome analysis, RNA-seq technology was employed. WT and CA-ROP2 seedlings were grown on solid medium under N-sufficient and -deficient conditions. Whole seedlings were harvested at three time points (6, 12, and 18 d) during growth. Each sample included three independent biological replicates. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen). RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. Library construction and sequencing were completed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

For data analysis, raw sequencing reads were first filtered to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads, obtaining clean reads. The clean reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis thaliana reference genome (TAIR10). Gene expression levels were quantified and normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). After obtaining the normalized data, differential expression analysis was performed between WT and CA-ROP2 at each time point under both N-sufficient and -deficient conditions. Genes with |log₂(Fold Change)| ≥ 1 and an adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed using the DAVID Bioinformatics Resources, with GO terms having padj < 0.05 considered significantly enriched. The TBtools software package was used to generate heatmaps to visualize the expression patterns of selected growth- and senescence-related genes. The heatmaps in the article were plotted using log₂(ratio of gene expression) to compare gene expression differences.

Results

Active ROP2 enhances shoot growth while simultaneously triggering premature cotyledon chlorosis under N starvation

Our previous study has demonstrated that the small GTPase ROP2 acts as a key upstream regulator that integrates N signals to activate TOR kinase, thereby promoting shoot growth. 32 Notably, CA-ROP2 exhibits shoot activation phenotypes even in the absence of N. 32 The ability of CA-ROP2 to activate shoot growth under N starvation conditions has led us to consider whether such growth promotion may come at a cost to other physiological processes in the plants. To investigate this possibility, we continuously observed the shoot growth phenotypes of WT and CA-ROP2 plants under both N-sufficient and -deficient conditions. Consistent with our previous findings, 32 CA-ROP2 plants displayed resistance to N starvation from day 6 to 12, with larger cotyledons and true leaves, whereas WT plants exhibited strong growth inhibition (Figure 1A and B). Both genotypes showed similar normal growth under N-sufficient conditions (Figure 1A). Interestingly, with prolonged cultivation, we observed that WT plants remained in a shoot growth inhibition state while retaining cotyledon greening until 18 d. In contrast, after 12 d of growth, CA-ROP2 plants exhibited attenuated shoot growth rate and were accompanied by cotyledon chlorosis, indicative of a premature senescence phenotype (Figure 1A–C​​​​​). In contrast, the rop2/4/6 triple mutant, lacking ROP2 and its closely homologous ROP4 and ROP6, 34 delayed cotyledon chlorosis and leaf senescence compared to WT under N starvation (Supplementary Figure 1A-B). These results reveal that active ROP2-driven plant growth indeed exacts a trade-off in the form of accelerating cotyledon chlorosis under N starvation conditions.

CA-ROP2-induced premature cotyledon chlorosis depends on leaf senescence under N starvation

The premature cotyledon chlorosis phenotype observed in CA-ROP2 plants under N starvation closely resembled leaf senescence and cell death phenotypes. 3 , 37 Therefore, we speculated that CA-ROP2 may induce plant premature senescence or cell death under N-deficient conditions. To test this hypothesis, we detected the chlorophyll contents in the WT, CA-ROP2, and rop2/4/6 plants after 6 to 18 d of cultivation under N starvation conditions. Consistent with the cotyledon chlorosis phenotypes, total chlorophyll content showed no significant difference among WT, CA-ROP2, and rop2/4/6 plants at day 6; however, compared to WT, it declines from day 6 to day 18 faster in CA-ROP2 but slower in the rop2/4/6 triple mutant (Figure 2A). Furthermore, we examined the leaf senescence-associated genes' expression level in WT, CA-ROP2, and rop2/4/6 plants at day 12 under N starvation conditions. We found that the transcript levels of senescence-related genes SAG12, SAG13, and SGR1 were strongly induced in CA-ROP2 plants, but remained largely unchanged in the rop2/4/6 triple mutant, compared to WT (Figure 2B). Moreover, we compared the expression dynamics of the SAG12 and the senescence-repressed gene LHCB1.2 in WT, CA-ROP2 and rop2/4/6 plants under N starvation. The results also show that, compared to WT, CA-ROP2 exhibits a more pronounced and earlier shift in their expression, whereas the rop2/4/6 triple mutant shows a less pronounced and delayed shift (Figure 2C–D). These data demonstrate that CA-ROP2-induced cotyledon chlorosis is related to leaf senescence and active ROP2 acts as a positive regulator of leaf senescence under N starvation conditions.

CA-ROP2 drives leaf senescence under N starvation by upregulating senescence-promoting NAC family transcription factors

ROP GTPases in plants are central molecular switches that regulate many growth-related biological processes and mediate auxin and N signals to activate shoot apexes activation by promoting downstream genes' expression. 29 , 31 , 32 To determine whether CA-ROP2-promoted cotyledon senescence depends on the leaf senescence-related key TFs, we first tested the involvement of CA-ROP2 in regulating the expression of senescence-promoting TFs, such as NAC family TFs (ORE1, NAP, ANAC055) and PIF4/5 under N starvation conditions. Interestingly, consistent with previous reports, most of these TF genes were induced in WT plants under N starvation. Among them, only NAC family TFs showed significantly higher induction in CA-ROP2 plants (Figure 3A–C). In contrast, their expression remained largely unchanged in the rop2/4/6 triple mutant (Figure 3A–C), whereas no such expression pattern was observed for PIF4 and PIF5 (Figure 3D–E). These results suggest that CA-ROP2 may act upstream of the NAC family TFs to promote cotyledon senescence under N starvation.

Previous studies reported that HASTY, a key protein involved in miRNA processing and export, generates miRNAs that directly target and degrade NAC family TFs transcripts, such as ORE1, NAP, and ANAC055, thereby delaying leaf senescence under N starvation. 19 In addition, NLA, an E3 ligase, mediates the ubiquitination-associated degradation of the ORE1 protein to repress leaf senescence under N starvation conditions. 14‐16 We investigated whether CA-ROP2 upregulates NAC family TFs through repressing HASTY and NLA. We next tested the expression of HASTY and NLA in WT, CA-ROP2, and rop2/4/6 plants and found that they were both highly induced in WT but their inductions were markedly attenuated in CA-ROP2 and rop2/4/6 plants under N starvation conditions (Figure 3F–G). These data suggest that CA-ROP2 promotes premature cotyledon senescence by inhibiting the gene expression of HASTY and NLA to upregulate NAC family TFs at both transcriptional and protein levels.

CA-ROP2 induces leaf senescence under N starvation by repressing senescence-inhibitory transcription factors

To investigate whether leaf senescence-inhibitory transcription factors are also under the control of CA-ROP2 under N starvation conditions, we tested the effect of CA-ROP2 on the key senescence-repression transcription factor genes WRKY53 and NIGT1. Under high carbon/low N conditions, WRKY53 is highly induced, which in turn enhances the expression of ATL31, an E3 ubiquitin ligase with senescence-suppressing activity. 24 , 25 Therefore, we examined the effect of CA-ROP2 and rop2/4/6 on the WRKY53-ATL31 module under N starvation. As expected, both WRKY53 and ATL31 were markedly induced in WT plants; however, their induction was substantially attenuated in CA-ROP2 and rop2/4/6 plants (Figure 3H and I). Furthermore, we detected the effects of CA-ROP2 and rop2/4/6 on NIGT1, a TF that is involved in nitrate signaling and acts as a negative regulator in the N starvation-induced leaf senescence, 21 , 22 and found that its expression pattern was also repressed by CA-ROP2 and rop2/4/6 under N starvation conditions (Figure 3J). These data suggest that active ROP2 also inhibits the expression of the key leaf senescence-inhibitory TFs, WRKY53 and NIGT1, to induce leaf senescence under N starvation conditions.

CA-ROP2 drives transcriptomic landscape shifts as N starvation progresses

To systematically dissect the genome-wide mechanism by which active ROP2 regulates gene expression under N starvation, we performed time-series RNA-sequencing analysis on WT and CA-ROP2 seedlings grown under N-sufficient and N-deficient conditions for 6, 12, and 18 d. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that, irrespective of genotype, the transcriptomes of N-sufficient and N-deficient samples were clearly separated with 74% variance (Supplementary Figure 2A). Interestingly, we found that the transcriptomes of CA-ROP2 and WT were nearly identical under N-sufficient conditions; they became clearly separated over time under N starvation (Supplementary Figure 2A). This pattern was mirrored by the CA-ROP2-altered differentially expressed genes under N-sufficient and N-starvation conditions (Supplementary Figure 2B-C). Compared to WT, CA-ROP2 significantly altered the expression of 4,667, 7,543, and 8,001 genes under N starvation at 6, 12, and 18 d, respectively (Figures 4A, 5D and G; Supplementary Table 2). Among these, 1,945, 2,194, and 3,478 genes, respectively, were classified as N-responsive; their expression levels remained relatively unaffected by N status in CA-ROP2 plants (Figure 4A-B, D-E, G-H; Supplementary Table 2). These data indicates that CA-ROP2 reprogramed the N-responsive gene expression landscapes under N starvation progresses. We further detailed analyzed these N-responsive genes' functional classification. At the early stage of N starvation (day 6), the upregulated genes in CA-ROP2 were enriched in meiotic cell cycle, cell division, microtubule-based movement, and DNA repair, whereas the downregulated genes were enriched in negative regulation of biosynthetic process, jasmonic acid signaling, response to starvation, and miRNA silencing, indicating that CA-ROP2 maintains plant growth programs and repress stress responsive processes induced by N-deficient (Figure 4C). At day 12, the upregulated genes were shifted to response to water deprivation, response to hypoxia, and defense response while the downregulated genes mainly enriched in cell wall organization and secondary metabolism (Figure 4F). At the later stage of N starvation (day 18), the upregulated genes were significantly enriched in photosystem II disassembly and singlet oxygen-mediated programmed cell death; while the downregulated genes were involved in ROS scavenging and lignin biosynthesis (Figure 4I). Time-series transcriptome analysis revealed that CA-ROP2 drives a stage-specific transcriptional reprogramming during N starvation, progressing sequentially from “growth maintenance” to “defense activation” and ultimately to “metabolic breakdown”.

ROP2 orchestrates the balance of plant growth and senescence under N starvation

We found that CA-ROP2 maintained plant growth during early-stage N starvation but induced premature cotyledon senescence at the later stage (Figures 1 and 2). However, the genome-wide effects of CA-ROP2 on gene expression that regulate plant growth and senescence during N starvation remain unknown. To test the effects of CA-ROP2 on plant growth and senescence under N starvation, we examined the growth and development promotion related genes 37‐39 and the senescence-inducing related genes (Supplementary Table 3) 40‐42 expression dynamics in WT and CA-ROP2 plants over the course of N starvation. The results showed that CA-ROP2 plants exhibited significantly higher expression levels of growth- and development-related genes than WT plants at day 6 of N starvation (Figure 5A). However, as a compensatory effect, CA-ROP2 shifted the dramatic induction of senescence-associated genes under N starvation from day 18 in WT to day 12 (Figure 5B). These findings indicate that CA-ROP2 primarily sustains growth during the early stage of N starvation but subsequently accelerates the onset of senescence, highlighting its role as a key regulatory node in balancing growth and senescence.

CA-ROP2 modulates hormone-associated pathways to accelerate cotyledon senescence under N starvation

Plant hormones are intricately connected with leaf senescence. Through synergistic or antagonistic actions, hormones precisely orchestrate the initiation and progression of the senescence process. 43‐45 In addition to promoting senescence-inducing TFs and repressing senescence-inhibitory TFs (Figures 3 and 5C), our time course transcriptomic data under N starvation further revealed that CA-ROP2 globally modulated the expression of hormone-responsive senescence-associated genes, thereby accelerating premature cotyledon senescence (Figure 6A, Supplementary Table 3). Specifically, ROP2 promotes senescence mainly by upregulating the expression of genes that positively regulate senescence, 46‐52 such as CK degradation related genes (CKX3/4), SL related genes (MAX1/2/3), ABA related genes (PYL2/6, BGLU18, RD29B, RAB18), ethylene related genes (ACO2/3/5, EIN3), JA related genes (LOX2, MYC5), SA related genes (TGA3, PAL1), and PAO2/5, while downregulating the expression of senescence suppressor genes, 46 , 53 , 54 including auxin related genes (YUC1/2/6, IAA7/12), CK synthesis related genes (IPT3/5/7), GA related genes (GA20OX1, GA2OX1/8, RGL2), BR related genes (DET2, BRL1, BSK6, EXO, BEN1), SL related genes (SMXL8, SPX1/2), and APX1/2 (Figure 6A, Supplementary Table 3). These results indicate that CA-ROP2 coordinately regulates senescence-associated genes through multiple hormone pathways, thereby precisely orchestrating the progression of N starvation-induced cotyledon senescence. These results indicate that CA-ROP2 finely orchestrates the progression of N starvation-induced cotyledon senescence by coordinately regulating senescence-associated genes across multiple hormone pathways.

Gene expression ofROP2 is induced by early but repressed by late N starvation

ROP2 serves as an essential regulator to orchestrate plant growth-senescence trade-off under N starvation. Previously, we demonstrated that the activity of ROP2 gradually decreased during the progression of N starvation, and it could be reactivated upon nitrogen resupply. 32 However, how N starvation effects ROP2 gene expression remains unknown. To investigate the effect of N starvation on ROP2 gene expression, we systematically examined its expression dynamics in wild-type plants grown under N-sufficient and -deficient conditions over a time course. Compared to N-sufficient conditions, N starvation induced ROP2 expression at day 6 to 9, showed no significant difference at day 12 to 15, whereas it markedly repressed it at day 18 (Figure 6B). Taken together, these findings imply that under early nitrogen starvation, plants upregulate ROP2 to support growth and development. As starvation continues, ROP2 expression is kept at a medium level to orchestrate the trade-off between growth and senescence. At later, prolonged stages, ROP2 expression is dampened to mitigate senescence.

Discussion

To grow or to senesce is a critical decision plant organs must make in response to the changes of developmental stages or environmental conditions. 3 In this study, we first identified CA-ROP2 as an essential regulator of N starvation-induced leaf senescence. CA-ROP2 functions upstream of key senescence-related transcription factors by both promoting the expression of senescence-promoting NAC family members (ORE1, NAP, and ANAC005), inhibiting the expression of senescence-suppressing factors (HASTY, WRKY53, and NIGT1), and altering the expression of hormone-responsive senescence-associated genes under prolonged N starvation. Furthermore, we revealed that ROP2 serves as a critical N-responsive regulator that coordinates the balance between leaf growth and senescence under N starvation. Specifically, CA-ROP2 sustains higher TOR kinase activity 33 and elevated expression of growth-related genes to promote plant growth during early N starvation, but subsequently orchestrates a drastic transcriptional shift to upregulate senescence-associated genes, thereby promoting premature cotyledon senescence under prolonged N starvation. These findings expand the function of ROP2 from a “growth promoter” to a “senescence-initiating regulator” depending on environmental N status, unveiling a novel mechanism by which plants balance growth and senescence to adapt to N starvation (Figure 6C).

ROP2 acts as an upstream positive regulator of leaf senescence under N starvation

N starvation-induced leaf senescence involves a range of downstream TFs, such as ORE1, NAP, ANAC055, HASTY, WRKY53, and NIGT1; 9‐25 however, the upstream signaling components that link N starvation to the regulation of these TFs have not yet been identified. Our study identifies the constitutively active ROP2 as a positive upstream regulator that bridges N starvation with key senescence-associated TFs to regulate leaf senescence (Figures 3, 5C). Furthermore, we found that CA-ROP2 also could affect multiple hormones signaling components to modulate leaf senescence under N starvation (Figure 6A). These findings offer a new way for understanding how plants translate N limitation into a coordinated leaf senescence response. ROP2 has been previously established as a molecular switch that regulates plant growth, development and cell morphology by integrating multiple upstream signals, including auxin, light, and nutrients. 26‐33 Our discoveries expand the biological function of ROP2 from growth-promotion to senescence-modulation under N limitation conditions. Future progress will likely elucidate the mechanisms by which N starvation regulates ROP2 activity and how ROP2 in turn modulates key senescence-associated TFs. This work also highlights potential roles of ROPs in adaptation to diverse stresses, including nutrient limitation, as well as abiotic and biotic stresses. 55‐57

ROP2 orchestrates trade-off of growth and senescence under N starvation

Plants facing nutrient stress must maintain a delicate balance between sustaining growth and initiating senescence. Our previous study revealed that under N starvation conditions, ROP2 promotes growth by activating the TOR signaling pathway. 32 In this study, we further revealed that CA-ROP2 maintained plant growth during the early stages of N starvation but exhibit premature cotyledon senescence after prolonged N starvation (Figure 1), highlighting the causal relationship between “continued growth under resource limitation” and “accelerated compensatory senescence”. 58‐60 Genome-wide expression profiling further confirms that CA-ROP2 sustains high expression of growth-related genes at day 6 of N starvation, but significantly activates senescence-related genes by day 12 (Figure 5). This precise temporal transition suggests that under N starvation conditions, ROP2 functions like a timer, coordinating the programmatic shift from growth to senescence. The goal is to maximize the growth period before nutrient depletion and, when growth can no longer be sustained, to timely initiate senescence to facilitate nutrient recycling as a survival mechanism. 1‐3 Future work will likely focus on elucidating how ROP2 orchestrates the growth-senescence balance, as well as whether TOR and other ROP family members contribute to the growth-senescence trade-off under diverse nutrient limitations and environmental stress conditions. 31 , 32
