Comparative outcomes of endoscopic dacryocystorhinostomy under local versus general anesthesia in elderly patients
Authors: Yin-Hsi Chang, Yi-Lin Liao, Yueh-Ju Tsai, Yen-Chang Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndoscopic dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) is a common procedure, typically performed under general anesthesia (GA), while performing it under local anesthesia (LA) may offer benefits to elderly or high-risk patients. This study evaluates the feasibility and surgical outcomes of endoscopic DCR under LA, with comparison to GA.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients aged 18 years or older with acquired nasolacrimal duct obstruction who underwent Endoscopic DCR at a tertiary referral center between January 2019 and December 2024. Surgical outcomes were evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age and sex, with propensity score–matched analyses as sensitivity analyses. Secondary outcomes included operative time, intraoperative bleeding, complications, predictors of failure and revision surgery. Furthermore, potential predictors were screened by univariable analyses, and variables with P < 0.15 were entered into multivariable models.ResultsThe study enrolled 210 eyes of 162 patients, with 117 patients (159 eyes) in the GA group, and 45 patients (51 eyes) in the LA group. The mean age was 61.7 ± 12.2 years in the GA group, and 72.3 ± 12.7 years in the LA groups. Surgical success was comparable between groups, with the LA group exhibiting a shorter operative time (P < 0.001). No major adverse events were reported. Small lacrimal sac size was identified as an independent associated factor of surgical failure (P < 0.001).ConclusionsLA offers a feasible alternative to GA for endoscopic DCR, particularly in elderly patient. Potential drawbacks include patient discomfort and an uncontrollable surgical field, which can be mitigated with appropriate patient selection.

Introduction

Endoscopic dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) is a common procedure performed to relieve nasolacrimal duct obstruction (NLDO) [1]. The goal of surgery is to restore normal tear drainage, alleviating symptoms such as epiphora and recurrent infections by creating a new passageway between the lacrimal sac and the nasal cavity. Various modified techniques, with or without intubation, have been used to achieve comparable success rates of up to 85–95% [2–4]. Intraoperative bleeding and patient discomfort commonly arise during nasal mucosa manipulation and osteotomy creation. Thus, anesthesia plays a crucial role in ensuring optimal surgical conditions. Traditionally, endoscopic DCR has been performed under general anesthesia (GA), but the use of local anesthesia (LA) with or without sedation offers the advantage of avoiding the risks associated with GA, particularly in elderly patients. It remains unclear whether differences in outcomes and complication rates exist depending on the type of anesthesia used. While LA is widely believed to be a safer approach, concerns about intraoperative bleeding and increased patient discomfort have led many surgeons to avoid the option [5]. The purpose of this study is to compare the outcomes and assess the risks and benefits of performing endoscopic DCR under GA versus LA.

Materials and methods

Study design

Patient data were extracted from a structured electronic medical record database at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Linkou Medical Center. A retrospective chart review was performed for patients who underwent endoscopic DCR between January 2019 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (no. 202500286B0), and all procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients

Adults aged 18 years or older with acquired nasolacrimal duct obstruction were included. Patients were excluded if they had congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction, a history of previous external or endoscopic DCR, or incomplete medical records. Patient demographics, medical history, preoperative evaluations, surgical parameters, and postoperative outcomes were collected for analysis.

Anesthesia procedure and surgical technique

The choice of anesthesia was determined jointly by the surgeon and anesthesiologist, considering patient age, systemic comorbidities, ASA classification, and patient preference. GA was conducted via standard endotracheal intubation by experienced anesthesiologists, with perioperative intravenous administration of cefazolin, tranexamic acid, and dexamethasone. LA was achieved by injecting 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine for infraorbital, intratrochlear and anterior ethmoidal nerve block. The remaining preparation and surgical steps were performed identically by the same surgeon in both GA and LA groups. After the nasal cavity was packed with 1:10,000 Bosmin gauze, the patient was placed head elevated to minimize venous congestion and bleeding. A 30-degree rigid endoscope is introduced to identify the axilla of the middle turbinate, where additional 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine is infiltrated.

A curvilinear incision is made in the nasal mucosa to expose lacrimal bone and the frontal process of the maxillary bone. The osteotomy is meticulously created using a Kerrison punch and osteotome to a size of at least 10 mm. Once the entire lacrimal sac is exposed and tented with a Bowman lacrimal probe, it is opened using a crescent knife. The posterior flaps of the lacrimal sac are opposed to the nasal mucosa, followed by packing with Surgifoam® (Ethicon, Inc., Somerville, NJ, USA) soaked in triamcinolone to reduce postoperative inflammation and fibrosis in all cases. Crawford silicone stents may be optionally inserted only in cases with a small lacrimal sac or stenosis of the common canaliculus. Postoperatively, patients received topical antibiotics and steroid along with Budesonide nasal spray for a period of 4 weeks. The stents are generally removed 2–3 months after surgery.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome measure was surgical success, defined as complete resolution of epiphora with patent lacrimal drainage on irrigation performed at the final follow-up visit. Resolution of epiphora was reported by patient subjectively at each follow-up visit. Lacrimal drainage patency was evaluated by lacrimal syringing at scheduled follow-up visits and was defined as free flow of saline through the nasolacrimal system without reflux or resistance. Surgical success required both criteria to be met simultaneously — symptomatic resolution AND objective patency on syringing— at the follow-up visit.

Secondary outcome measures included operative time, intraoperative bleeding, complications, predictors of failure and need for revision surgery. Bleeding severity was graded from 1 to 5 based on the amount of blood obscuring the surgical field and need for intervention, similar to the Modena bleeding score [6]. A subjective difficulty score from 1 to 5 was given by the surgeon based on overall operative complexity. In addition, the anatomical features of the nasal cavity were also documented. Lacrimal sac size was assessed intraoperatively by the surgeon using endoscopic visualization. A small lacrimal sac was defined as having a vertical diameter of < 5 mm [7].

Statistical analysis

The distributions of continuous variables (e.g., age and length of follow-up) and categorical variables (e.g., sex and comorbidities) between the GA and LA groups were compared using independent-samples t tests and Fisher’s exact tests, respectively. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Characteristic analyses were also performed at the eye level for variables related to surgery.

To compare the risk of surgical failure between the GA and LA groups, we first conducted a Cox proportional hazards model adjusted for age and sex. As an alternative approach, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching based on age and sex and repeated the same analysis to compare surgical failure between groups. Next, we conducted a series of Cox models to preliminarily evaluate potential factors associated with surgical failure. Variables with a P value < 0.15 in the univariate Cox models were subsequently included in the final multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. All statistical tests were two-tailed with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

A total of 210 eyes of 162 patients were included for analysis, with 117 patients (159 eyes) undergoing surgery under GA, while 45 patients (51 eyes) under LA (Table 1). The mean age of patients in the LA group was significantly older than that of the GA group (72.3 vs. 61.7 years, P < 0.001). The mean follow up duration was 10.6 ± 14.1 months. The overall surgical success rate was 91.4% at 6 months and 88.6% at the final follow-up. Success rates were comparable between the GA and LA groups at both 6 months (90.6% vs. 94.1%, respectively; P = 0.571) and final follow-up (87.4% vs. 92.2%, respectively; P = 0.454). There was no significant difference in surgical failure between the groups (P of log-rank test = 0.828, Fig. 1). Noticeably, the average duration of surgery was significantly shorter in the LA group than that of the GA group (67.3 vs. 40 min, P < 0.001). Intraoperative bleeding scores and surgeon-graded difficulty scores were not significantly different in both groups (Table 2). No major postoperative complication such as intractable epistaxis, orbital fat prolapses or cerebrospinal fluid leak was observed in either group.

Revision surgery was required in 15 eyes (9.4%) in the GA group and 4 eyes (7.8%) in the LA group, with no significant difference. Of the 19 revision cases, 15 eyes (78.9%) underwent repeated endoscopic DCR, 1 eye (6.7%) received balloon dacryocystoplasty for early ostium obstruction, and 3 eyes (20%) received Crawford silicone stents for common canalicular obstruction. No case was converted to an external DCR. The main cause of failure was granulation tissue formation at orifice, followed by mucosal adhesions in the nasal cavity and common canalicular obstruction. Functional epiphora despite a patent ostium occurred in 4 eyes.

Anesthesia type (GA vs. LA) was not significantly associated with surgical failure (hazard ratio [HR], 1.19; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.39–3.65; P = 0.755) after adjustment for age and sex (Table 3). Similarly, there was no significant difference in surgical failure between the GA (n = 46) and LA (n = 46) groups based on propensity score matching for age and sex (HR, 1.19; 95% CI, 0.32–4.45; P = 0.799; Table 3). Furthermore, univariate Cox models were applied to identify potential variables associated with surgical failure, which indicated that greater bleeding severity, and small lacrimal sac were significantly associated with an increased risk of surgical failure. The multivariable Cox analysis identified small lacrimal sac as an independently associated factor of surgical failure (HR, 11.94; 95% CI, 3.14–45.47; P < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated comparable surgical success rates between endoscopic DCR performed under GA and LA, with no significant differences observed in complication or revision rates. These findings suggest that LA may represent a feasible alternative in appropriately selected elderly patients. Furthermore, a small lacrimal sac size is independently associated with surgical failure, regardless of anesthesia type.

Traditionally, endoscopic DCR has been predominantly performed under GA to optimize patient comfort and maintain a stable surgical field, allowing the surgeon to perform meticulous dissection and create a large, well-defined ostium. However, GA is not without risk, such as respiratory depression, cardiovascular instability, and prolonged cognitive dysfunction [8–10], which may render some high-risk patients unsuitable candidates for surgery. In real-world practice, anesthesia selection is often individualized, taking into account patient age, systemic comorbidities, ASA physical status, and patient preference. In our cohort, local anesthesia was preferentially offered to older individuals as an alternative approach.

Recent trends toward outpatient surgery have accelerated since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, accompanied by an increasing preference for the use of local anesthesia. This preference is also driven by a shortage of anesthesia personnel in subsequent years [11]. Together, these factors reflect a shift toward more efficient approaches with shortened operative times to preserve hospital capacity. A few surgeons have demonstrated satisfactory surgical outcomes and tolerable pain with the added benefits of reduced hospital stays and healthcare cost savings in both external [12]and endoscopic DCR [13–15]. However, direct comparisons between LA and GA in endoscopic DCR remain limited. The present study was therefore designed to evaluate the outcomes of two anesthetic methods in selected patients.

The shorter operative time observed in the LA group should be interpreted cautiously. Age-related changes in bone density may partially explain this finding. Osteoporosis affects approximately 20.6% of women and 5.0% of men aged 40 years and older [16], and rises to 45% among women over age 60–65 due to estrogen deficiency [17]. The systemic bone density elsewhere in the body is likely mirrored in the orbital region [18]. Therefore, thinner lacrimal bone in elderly patients may facilitate more efficient osteotomy creation during surgery. This is particularly relevant in postmenopausal women. On the other hand, differences in operative workflow and anesthetic recovery phase can also contribute to the observed time difference. Therefore, this finding does not necessarily imply superiority of one anesthetic method over the other.

With advances in surgical instruments and techniques, LA can be applied to a broader range of conditions. For example, the advent of laser-assisted DCR has expanded the feasibility of performing lacrimal surgeries under LA [19]. Several authors have reported successful outcomes with diode or Holmium: Yag laser in outpatient settings using locally infiltrative anesthesia or nerve block [20–22]. The operation time was approximately 20 to 30 min per eye. While laser-assisted DCR often face criticism for lower success rates compared to traditional endoscopic techniques, optimizing laser settings, maximize osteotomy size, careful case selection, and surgeon experience may improve the outcomes. It may serve as a useful adjunct when combined with traditional approach in cases where GA facilities are limited, or when the patient’s condition presents contraindications.

Disadvantages of LA include patient anxiety and discomfort, especially in those with a low pain threshold. The possibility of unexpected movements, or sneezing reflexes can reduce surgical field control, potentially complicating the procedure. Moreover, the posterior nasal influx of blood can result in frequent coughing, and rarely, aspiration. Unlike GA, which ensures complete immobility and airway protection, LA demands continuous communication and cooperation, which may be feasible in selected patients. In our cohort, bleeding scores and subjectively graded difficulty scores did not differ significantly between groups, which is likely attributable to the presence of an experienced surgeon. However, this might not be the case for all surgeons.

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as older patients may have been preferentially selected for LA. However, we intended to evaluate the risks and benefits of these two methods using real-world data, which can reflect actual practice. We also conducted analyses with adjustment for age and sex as well as propensity score matching, which demonstrated comparable risk of surgical failure between the two groups. Second, standardized assessments of pain, anxiety, and satisfaction were unavailable, limiting direct evaluation of patient experience under LA. We can only observe that no LA procedure required conversion to GA, which indirectly suggests procedural tolerability. Therefore, validated patient-reported outcome measures should be incorporated in further studies. Third, the surgical difficulty grading was based on the operator’s subjective perception. However, all the surgeries were performed by a single surgeon, which minimizes bias. Fourth, the progressive attrition in eyes available for assessment during follow-up may have introduced bias. Later time-point estimates should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, most failure events were observed within the first 6 months after surgery, and the 6-month success rates were comparable to those at the final follow-up visit in both groups. Both 6-month and final follow-up success rates are therefore reported. Finally, cost analysis was not included; future prospective studies should assess patient-reported outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and long-term success rates in greater detail.

Conclusion

Endoscopic DCR performed under LA demonstrates comparable surgical success rates to GA, with the added benefits of healthcare resource savings. With careful patient selection and meticulous control of the surgical field, LA may serve as a viable alternative, particularly when GA poses increased risks or is unavailable. However, the choice of anesthesia should be individualized based on patient-specific factors, including comorbidities, anxiety level, surgeon and patient preference, surgical complexity, and institutional capabilities.
