EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The One Earth Energy (OEE) plant in Gibson City, Illinois produces ethanol through a corn
fermentation process. For this project, the maximum possible ethanol production rate for this
facility is 160 million gallons per year (MMgal/yr) based on discussions with plant personnel
regarding recent annual production rates and any foreseeable plans or possibilities to increase
ethanol production at this facility. Yeast ferments the corn mash to produce ethanol and produces
carbon dioxide (CO2) as a by-product at the same time. The CO2 bubbles out of the mash and flows
through a packed bed water scrubber to remove volatile organic compounds before the gas vents
to atmosphere. CO2 will be captured after it has passed through the existing scrubber.
Recent federal and state government incentives have made capturing CO2 from high purity
industrial CO2 sources an economically attractive process and OEE is considering capturing the
CO2 vented from their facility wet scrubber. Trimeric and AECOM are working with the Illinois
State Geological Survey (ISGS) to develop a CO2 capture plant design and cost estimate for the
OEE facility. This report summarizes the pre-feasibility design work and cost estimation for the
CO2 capture facility completed by Trimeric and AECOM. The pipeline that transports the captured
CO2 from OEE to the injection well, the injection well(s), monitoring well(s), in addition to costs
for these parts of the project are not included in this report, but will be detailed in separate reports.
The nominal CO2 production rate from the ethanol facility is estimated at 458,000 tonne (metric
tons) per year based on 160 MMgal/yr of ethanol production. This is 1,290 tonne/day of CO2
product based on operating 355 days/year. The nominal CO2 feed gas rate entering the surface
facilities (capture plant) is 25.6 MMSCFD.
The capture facility will be a CO2 compression and dehydration system, with a centrifugal blower
first compressing the raw CO2 from near atmospheric pressure to about 15 psig and then two four-
stage reciprocating compressors operating in parallel to continue compression of the CO2 up to
about 1,500 psig. Between the 3rd and 4th stages of the reciprocating compressors (at about 600
psig), a triethylene glycol (TEG) dehydration unit will remove most of the remaining water vapor
from the CO2 and then the dehydrated CO2 will be returned to the reciprocating compressors for
the final compression step. The estimated purchased equipment cost for the major equipment in
the CO2 capture unit is shown in Table 1.

PREVIOUS WORK
In previous phases of the project, Trimeric worked with the ISGS and OEE to develop a basic
process design and initial cost estimate for the CO2 capture facility and also to characterize the
feed gas composition coming from the fermenter wet scrubber. In the prior pre-feasibility work on
the project, removal of almost all of the oxygen present in the feed gas was thought to be necessary.
As a result, the original process design included refrigeration and other equipment to liquefy and
then distill the CO2 to remove the oxygen and nitrogen impurities.
During the prior phase of the project, the ISGS conducted research on the expected conditions in
the injection well and subsurface formation and concluded that it was not necessary for the oxygen
to be removed from the CO2. This eliminated the need for the surface facility to liquefy and distill
the CO2. Previous work that details anticipated capital cost estimates and operating cost estimates
was disregarded and replaced with the work completed in the current phase of the project since the
type of facility has changed and since the current work is more detailed and more recent.
Earlier in the project, plant personnel considered the possibility of storing additional CO2 produced
at other sites and transported to the OEE injection well(s). Trimeric advised that the most practical
way to do this would be to purify, dehydrate, and compress the CO2 at the other site locations and
transport dehydrated, high pressure, high purity CO2 in underground carbon steel pipelines to the
OEE site. CO2 is typically transported in pipelines at pressures above the critical pressure for CO2
(> 1,100 psig) to ensure a homogeneous high density, low viscosity, low pressure drop fluid that
cannot separate into distinct gas and liquid phases. Transporting raw produced CO2 from other
ethanol plants or similar sources at lower pressures would require much larger diameter pipelines
leading to much higher pipeline costs and most likely, higher operating costs due to increased
pressure drop in the pipelines. The lower pressure CO2 would also be susceptible to forming liquid
CO2 in colder temperatures leading to safety concerns as liquid CO2 can rapidly overpressure if it
is trapped between two valves or otherwise isolated without proper pressure relief devices. This
can cause the piping to violently rupture. Transporting CO2 in pipelines below the critical pressure
could also result in two-phase (gas and liquid) flow and create issues with slug flow including a
“water-hammer” effect at bends or turns in the pipeline. Transporting CO2 in a pipeline without
first dehydrating it would lead to significant corrosion concerns that would likely require coated
carbon-steel or stainless steel, which are more expensive than regular carbon steel typically used
for transporting high pressure, dehydrated CO2 in pipelines.
The concept of transporting CO2 as a liquid from remote locations to the OEE site was also
considered. Converting CO2 to liquid is a proven commercial process that uses refrigeration to
liquefy CO2 at approximately 300 psig, and 0 °F. The capture facility is roughly 15% higher in
capital cost than the compression and dehydration process proposed at OEE. It has about 50%
higher operating costs as additional electricity is required for refrigerant compressors. Truck and
rail CO2 transportation is done routinely, but at much smaller scale than pipeline CO2 transport.
The typical capacity for an insulated trailer truck is 18 tonnes of CO2.
Insulated rail cars carry about 83 tonnes of CO2, but in either case the cost for a facility to liquefy
CO2 and the transportation costs for truck or rail typically lead to CO2 costs on the order of $110
per tonne of CO2 delivered or higher. Costs increase with the distance between the source and the
destination and so do CO2 losses due to evaporation of liquid CO2 and fuel costs during transport.
Pipeline transportation will nearly always be the safest, most economical, and logistically

 1

manageable onshore transportation mode for long-term CO2 sequestration projects with quantities
of CO2 in the hundreds of thousands of tonnes per year or higher.

PROCESS SIMULATION DEVELOPMENT AND RESULTS
Trimeric developed a process simulation to model the CO2 capture process in Symmetry, which is
a process simulation program owned by Schlumberger (VMGSim was the previous name for this
program). The simulation used the “Advanced Peng-Robinson for Natural Gas 2” equation of
state, which Trimeric uses for many other CO2 projects as we have thoroughly vetted it for this
application. This equation of state models CO2 physical properties across a range of pressures and
temperatures with a reasonable degree of accuracy. The process simulation is also used to estimate
liquid water quantities produced by the compression system.
Surface facilities for the OEE project consist mostly of compression units and a dehydration unit
required to meet the specifications shown in Table 6. Process flow diagrams can be found in
Appendix A. The triethylene glycol (TEG) dehydration unit is a typical technology utilized in the
natural gas treating industry and in CO2 applications such as the one in this project. A typical TEG
dehydration unit process flow diagram is shown in Appendix A. A heat and material balance for
the main compression process is shown in Appendix B and utility estimates from the process
simulation are shown in Appendix C.
Reciprocating compressors are a very common type of compressor used in the oil and gas industry
and related CO2 enhanced oil recovery (EOR) industries. They can be designed for a range of
flows and pressures, but each stage of a reciprocating compressor will usually not exceed a
compression ratio of 4 in CO2 applications. In between each stage of compression, the gas is cooled
and is routed to a two-phase separator (scrubber) to remove any condensed liquids. The
reciprocating compressor type considered for OEE is a medium speed compressor, running at
approximately 700 rotations per minute (RPM). These are typical for the oil and gas industry and
related CO2 EOR industries, with typical manufacturers being Ariel and Siemens (formerly
Dresser-Rand).
Medium speed compressors are usually limited to six cylinders (or throws) on a single compressor
frame. The compression ratio (approximately 104) and flow rate required for OEE cannot be
accomplished in a single medium speed compressor. The low density of the atmospheric pressure
CO2 feed gas would also require multiple, large-bore cylinders on a reciprocating compressor,
which would add significant cost to the project. A multistage centrifugal blower is therefore
installed upstream of the reciprocating compressor. The blower increases the pressure of the CO2
enough (by approximately 15 psi) to limit the number of 1st stage cylinders required in the
reciprocating compressor and allows the rest of the pressure rise to be completed in a total of four
stages of compression in the reciprocating compressors. A heat and material balance for the
compression process is provided in Appendix B while a run sheet of an example compressor from
Ariel is in Appendix D.
In between the 3rd and 4th stages of the compressor, the gas is dehydrated to meet the pipeline water
specification by contacting the gas with liquid TEG in a packed tower. The pressure here is about
600 psig where the water content has already been minimized by multiple compression and cooling

 3

steps. This reduces capital and operating costs for the dehydration unit, but the pressure is still low
enough to avoid excessive losses of TEG into the CO2 gas stream caused by evaporation resulting
from increased solubility of TEG in CO2 at higher pressure. Water-lean TEG flows to the top of
packed tower and flows downward while the gas flows up the tower countercurrent to the TEG.
The water-rich TEG from the bottom of the packed tower flows to a regeneration skid where it is
heated to liberate water as a vapor vent stream and then the regenerated lean-TEG is pumped back
to the packed tower in a closed loop.
Trimeric and AECOM developed a complete set of Piping and Instrumentation Diagrams (P&IDs)
for the proposed capture process that includes all utilities required for the process along with the
tie-points required within the existing ethanol plant. The P&IDs include all the equipment
necessary for the capture facility, the required instrumentation, and a clear delineation between
what equipment the packaged equipment vendors will provide and what equipment will need to be
provided as part of the balance of plant. The P&IDs are in Appendix E while an equipment list is
in Appendix F.

design and assemble these types of skidded equipment systems. The equipment is largely skid-
mounted in a shop and then shipped to the site for installation on a concrete foundation. As a result,
the facility is mostly modular in design, and a design-build or EPC firm may be utilized to complete
the balance of plant engineering and then start to build the facility once the major equipment is

Due to the cold weather in Illinois, it is recommended that the reciprocating compressors be located
inside a building. The blower system may be located indoors or outdoors; if outdoors, all liquid
containing vessels and lines should be heat-traced and insulated. The contactor for the dehydration
system can be installed outdoors. The regeneration skid for the dehydration system can be installed

and lean glycol) will need to be heat traced and insulated. The building will come equipped with

The blower skid will be located in the existing One Earth facility, close to the CO2 Scrubber. The
remainder of the capture equipment will be located on the northwest corner of the facility, just to
the north of the existing peaking power plant. A large aboveground stainless steel pipe will
transport CO2 from the blower skid to the reciprocating compressors. Primary access to the blower
skid will be through existing roads in and out of the plant. Primary access to the remainder of the
capture facility will be from N 300E Road. A new road will be required from N 300E Road into
the capture facility. In addition to the main transfer line from the blower skid to the compressors,
an additional pipe rack will be required that runs along the north end of the four-cell cooling tower
of the existing facility. This pipe rack will carry additional utility lines that are necessary for the
capture unit. A complete overview of the site layout is provided in Appendix J. Mechanical,
electrical, and foundation layout drawings may be found in Appendix K.

The expected accuracy range of a Class 4 Equipment Factored Cost estimate is between -30% to
+50%. Several sources of uncertainty for the costs are as follows:
 • The level of project definition – Material take-offs and competitive general contractor bids
 have not been prepared yet, so the cost estimate is fairly high level. Bids received for major
 packaged equipment were not competitively bid, nor was a detailed specification provided for
 the budgetary packaged equipment cost estimates obtained for this project.
 • Recent supply chain issues have caused volatile pricing and delivery times for engineered
 equipment. As mentioned above, recent price spikes in metals markets have made future
 prices for raw materials difficult to determine and may cause equipment manufacturers to
 escalate prices to avoid risk.
 • Value engineering opportunities – The current pre-engineered metal building (PEMB) is
 configured to be a wide span, open building to allow bridge crane access to the compressor
 skids. This design could be re-configured to reduce costs while still maintaining access to the
 equipment. Some equipment, such as heat exchangers associated with the compressors, could
 be located outdoors, which would reduce the price of the building.
At the current level of the cost estimate (Class 4), the range of the fixed capital investment will be
from -30% to +50% of the fixed capital investment shown in Table 11.

schedule. Once certified vendor data is received, the balance of the plant (e.g., piping, controls,
wastewater collection, utilities) can be designed. The detailed engineering effort will produce a
General Construction Package deliverable that can be used to engage a general contractor (GC).
The GC can mobilize to the construction site and begin site work and foundations. Weather may
be a factor at this and other points in the project schedule. Once long lead equipment skids have
been shop fabricated and shipped to the site, the GC can lift them onto their foundations, install
interconnecting piping, power distribution, and instrumentation and controls. The PEMB, control
room, and electrical room can be sealed in and finished. Once the Injection Permit and Operating
Permit have been approved, the CO2 Capture Facility can be commissioned, operators trained, and
compressed CO2 can be sent to the pipeline.
