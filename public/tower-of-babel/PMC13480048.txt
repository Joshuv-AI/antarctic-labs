Clozapine-associated systemic inflammatory reaction with adult-onset Still’s disease-like features and marked interleukin-18 elevation in a patient with schizophrenia: a case report
Authors: Chiharu Sugimori, Ryota Kawajiri, Katsumi Urata, Noriko Akiyama, Katsuji Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClozapine can trigger early inflammatory adverse reactions ranging from transient fever to organ-dominant syndromes. It remains unclear whether this spectrum can also present as a systemic adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD)-like phenotype.Case presentationA 54-year-old Japanese woman with long-standing treatment-resistant schizophrenia, psychogenic polydipsia with water intoxication, and overweight/obesity underwent olanzapine-to-clozapine cross-titration. Under the standard Japanese Clozaril Patient Monitoring Service procedure, clozapine was increased to 75 mg/day by day 23. Psychosis improved, but on day 27 she developed spike fever to 39.2 °C and fever-associated arthralgia. Infection was initially suspected, and empiric piperacillin/tazobactam was started while clozapine was continued because no neutropenia, marked eosinophilia, or severe organ-dominant reaction was evident. During days 27–30, no infectious focus was identified, and an evening spike-fever pattern with arthralgia and sore throat increased suspicion for clozapine-associated inflammation. Ferritin and interleukin-18 (IL-18), an autoinflammatory cytokine associated with Still’s disease, were measured after a Still-like phenotype was suspected. Clozapine was discontinued on day 35. However, the syndrome evolved with recurrent fever up to 40.2 °C, evanescent rash, edema/hypoxemia, neutrophil-predominant inflammation, hypoalbuminemia, ferritin elevation, and serial IL-18 increase from 470 pg/mL on day 33 to 1,160 pg/mL on day 49. Infection, established autoimmune disease, drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, and neuroleptic malignant syndrome were not favored. Overt myocarditis was not supported by normal troponin/creatine kinase and nonspecific electrocardiographic findings, but echocardiography was not performed. She fulfilled Yamaguchi and Fautrel classification criteria for AOSD-like illness. Consequently, hydrocortisone and supportive care were used, and no further spike fever occurred after day 49.ConclusionsThis case expands the clozapine inflammatory spectrum to a systemic AOSD-like phenotype with marked serial IL-18 elevation. When spike fever with systemic inflammation emerges during clozapine titration, early temporary interruption of clozapine should be considered, and persistent Still-like inflammation after discontinuation may warrant anti-inflammatory supportive treatment, including corticosteroids in selected cases.

Background

Clozapine remains the most effective antipsychotic for treatment-resistant schizophrenia, but its use is constrained by early adverse reactions that are often inflammatory in nature. Contemporary literature increasingly conceptualizes clozapine-associated fever, myocarditis, serositis, DRESS-like syndromes, and other early inflammatory events as a clozapine inflammatory spectrum rather than as entirely separate toxicities [1, 2]. Even apparently benign clozapine fever belongs to this early inflammatory differential because it overlaps temporally and clinically with more severe syndromes [3]. This framework is clinically relevant because cytokine release during inflammation can reduce cytochrome P450 activity, especially CYP1A2, thereby increasing clozapine exposure relative to dose and amplifying further toxicity [1, 2, 4].

Adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD) is a systemic autoinflammatory disorder characterized by high spiking fever, arthralgia or arthritis, evanescent rash, sore throat, neutrophilic leukocytosis, and hyperferritinemia. Yamaguchi and Fautrel classification criteria remain widely used. Among candidate biomarkers, interleukin-18 (IL-18) is closely linked to Still’s disease biology and may help distinguish Still-like hyperinflammation from sepsis in clinically ambiguous settings, although it is not specific to AOSD and can increase in other inflammatory states [5–8]. We report a patient with schizophrenia who developed a clozapine-associated systemic inflammatory reaction with marked serial IL-18 elevation and a clear AOSD-like phenotype after clozapine initiation.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old Japanese woman whose schizophrenia had begun at age 18 had persistent persecutory delusions, erotomanic delusions, auditory hallucinations, passivity phenomena, and chronic functional impairment despite multiple antipsychotic trials, including paliperidone and olanzapine. Her longitudinal course also included psychogenic polydipsia with episodes of water intoxication. She had no personal or family history of autoinflammatory disease, Still’s disease, periodic fever syndrome, or rheumatic disease. At clozapine initiation, her height was 148.0 cm, weight 62.4 kg, and body mass index was 28.5 kg/m². She had a remote history of heavy smoking (> 20 cigarettes/day from ages 20 to 24) but no current smoking and no caffeine intake.

Because of persistent treatment resistance, olanzapine was cross-titrated to clozapine. Following the standard Japanese initiation procedure, she was registered in the Clozaril Patient Monitoring Service (CPMS), complete blood counts were checked regularly, and clozapine was increased stepwise from 12.5 mg/day on day 1 to 25 mg/day on day 9, 50 mg/day on day 16, and 75 mg/day on day 23. All clozapine doses were divided twice daily after breakfast and dinner. In parallel, olanzapine was tapered from 20 mg/day on day 1 to 15 mg/day on day 9, 10 mg/day on day 16, 5 mg/day on day 23, and discontinued on day 30. Other regular medications throughout the relevant course were brotizolam 0.25 mg/day, lemborexant 10 mg/day, sodium valproate 800 mg/day, pregabalin 50 mg/day, fexofenadine 120 mg/day, and atorvastatin 5 mg/day. During the early clozapine phase, her prayer-like repetitive behavior diminished and she reported improvement of hallucinations.

On day 27, she developed abrupt spike fever to 39.2 °C together with dull arthralgia involving both shoulders, both elbows, and both wrists. At that time, infection, benign clozapine fever, clozapine-associated inflammatory reaction, and autoimmune disease were considered. Because infection was clinically the most frequent and immediately treatable possibility, blood cultures and other infection-focused evaluations were started, and piperacillin/tazobactam (TAZ/PIPC) was begun empirically. Clozapine was initially continued because psychotic symptoms had improved and there was no neutropenia, marked eosinophilia, or severe organ-dominant presentation suggesting DRESS or overt myocarditis. During days 27–30, however, no infectious focus was identified: she had mild sore throat but no cough or sputum, no localized abdominal tenderness or diarrhea, and no urinalysis findings supporting urinary tract infection. Fever tended to occur at night and resolve by morning, and the arthralgia was most prominent from the febrile period until shortly after defervescence. These features gradually shifted our working impression from infection toward clozapine-associated inflammation.

On the night of day 30, hydrocortisone 100 mg was given as symptomatic treatment for abrupt fever with respiratory distress, and defervescence was prompt. This response later influenced the choice of hydrocortisone when inflammatory spikes recurred. Fever usually occurred once in the evening at first, but at the height of illness the interval shortened to approximately 18 h. The highest recorded temperature was 40.2 °C on day 43. Two episodes of fine drug-eruption-like but clinically evanescent rash were documented during febrile periods, first on day 33 and again during the later recurrence. Because the first rash appeared while TAZ/PIPC was being administered, antibiotic contribution to that first episode cannot be completely excluded. TAZ/PIPC was stopped on day 34.

During the same inflammatory period, she developed generalized edema, particularly in the legs, and hypoxemia. SpO2 had begun to decline on day 27, and oxygen therapy was started on day 31 when SpO2 reached 92%. Oxygen was initially given by nasal cannula at 4 L/min and was soon reduced to 2 L/min. Thereafter, SpO2 transiently decreased to 92% on days 37 and 39 but generally remained between 94% and 97% under oxygen, sometimes increasing to 98% during afebrile periods. Oxygen was continued mainly for safety and symptomatic relief, particularly at night, rather than being adjusted strictly in proportion to each SpO2 value, and it was discontinued after complete defervescence on day 51. Because heart-failure physiology and fluid retention were suspected to contribute to hypoxemia, intravenous furosemide 20 mg/day and potassium canrenoate 100 mg/day were given from days 31–36, followed by oral furosemide 20 mg/day until day 55, when pleural effusions had almost disappeared on chest radiography. Bisoprolol 0.625 mg/day was also given from days 34–55.

By days 27–32, the combination of spike fever and fever-associated arthralgia led us to suspect an AOSD-like inflammatory phenotype. Ferritin and IL-18 were therefore measured on day 33; broad cytokine profiling was not pursued because such testing is generally outside standard insurance-covered clinical practice in Japan, whereas IL-18 is a recognized marker in Still’s disease. Ferritin elevation was confirmed on the following day, and clozapine was discontinued on day 35 because clozapine-associated inflammatory reaction had become the leading diagnosis. Day 36 was afebrile, but fever recurred on days 37–38. When IL-18 elevation also became available, our confidence in an AOSD-like syndrome increased further. Because infection had not been supported and TAZ/PIPC had already been stopped, recurrent spikes were treated with hydrocortisone and supportive care rather than renewed antibiotics.

Olanzapine was restarted on day 40 at 5 mg/day because persecutory statements had re-emerged from approximately day 38 after clozapine discontinuation. The fever on day 40 occurred before olanzapine was restarted. Olanzapine was increased to 10 mg/day on day 44, 15 mg/day on day 52, and 20 mg/day on day 60. The fever pattern was not temporally linked to twice-daily clozapine dosing or to the other regular medications, which were administered continuously while the fever evolved from predominantly nocturnal spikes to a shorter approximately 18-hour cycle during the peak inflammatory phase.

Selected acute diagnostic findings are summarized in Table 1, and the overall time course is shown in Fig. 1. Serial laboratory testing showed CRP elevation up to 15.0 mg/dL on day 49, ferritin elevation from 686.3 ng/mL on day 33 to 933.5 ng/mL on day 49 (upper limit of normal [ULN] 88 ng/mL), marked hypoalbuminemia with a nadir of 2.1 g/dL, and neutrophil-predominant inflammation. IL-18 increased serially from 470 pg/mL on day 33 to 926 pg/mL on day 40, 990 pg/mL on day 44, and 1,160 pg/mL on day 49 (ULN 211 pg/mL). Soluble interleukin-2 receptor was 4,420 U/mL on day 44 (ULN 613 U/mL). Eosinophil percentages fluctuated between 1.8% and 7.3%, with transient absolute eosinophil counts above 500/µL during days 40–44. Liver injury was minimal, with peak AST/ALT values of 44/61 IU/L on day 33, and renal dysfunction was absent.

Infectious work-up did not support bacterial or viral infection. Blood cultures and urine culture were negative, COVID-19 and influenza antigen tests were negative, and cytomegalovirus antigenemia (C7-HRP) was also negative. Urine findings did not support urinary tract infection, and chest computed tomography excluded pneumonia; there was no pleural or pericardial thickening. D-dimer was not strongly suggestive of thromboembolism. Autoimmune serologies were unrevealing: antinuclear antibody was < 40 by indirect immunofluorescence, rheumatoid factor was negative, anti-dsDNA antibody was negative, MPO-ANCA was negative, and PR3-ANCA was negative.

Cardiac involvement was considered because NT-proBNP was elevated to 2,921 pg/mL and bilateral pleural effusions were present. However, high-sensitivity troponin I was 0.013 ng/mL (ULN 0.014 ng/mL), creatine kinase never exceeded the reference range (peak 147 IU/L), chest pain was absent, persistent hypotension was not seen, and electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia with T-wave flattening but no ST-segment elevation or depression suggestive of overt myocarditis. Echocardiography was not performed.

The patient fulfilled Yamaguchi classification criteria for Still’s disease-like illness through fever ≥ 39 °C for longer than 1 week, arthralgia for longer than 2 weeks, leukocytosis with neutrophil predominance, sore throat, mild liver dysfunction, and negative antinuclear antibody/rheumatoid factor after exclusion of infection and connective tissue disease [5]. She also fulfilled Fautrel criteria through spiking fever, arthralgia, transient rash, pharyngitis, and neutrophils ≥ 80% [6].

Pharmacokinetic monitoring supported reduced clozapine clearance during the inflammatory phase. The trough clozapine concentration on day 29 was 357.1 ng/mL at a dose of 75 mg/day (concentration-to-dose ratio [C/D ratio] 4.76), rising to 477.3 ng/mL on day 35 at the same dose (C/D ratio 6.36). Clozapine and norclozapine were both below the assay sensitivity limit by day 44 after discontinuation. Hydrocortisone 100 mg was administered on days 30 and 33, followed by 200 mg on day 40 and additional 100-mg boluses on days 42–48 in response to recurrent spike fever. No further spike fever occurred after day 49. Ferritin and IL-18 declined gradually during follow-up, with ferritin 274.4 ng/mL and IL-18 393 pg/mL on day 80, ferritin 184.3 ng/mL and IL-18 291 pg/mL on day 113, and ferritin 67.4 ng/mL and IL-18 288 pg/mL on day 163. At follow-up on day 254, the inflammatory syndrome had not recurred, but her psychiatric symptoms and global functioning had returned to their pre-clozapine baseline under olanzapine 20 mg/day.

Discussion and conclusions

This case is best interpreted as a clozapine-associated systemic inflammatory reaction with AOSD-like features rather than as straightforward idiopathic AOSD. The syndrome emerged soon after clozapine initiation, progressed further despite discontinuation, and ultimately resolved with discontinuation, supportive care, and corticosteroid treatment. The clinical picture reproduced core Still-like features - spike fever, fever-associated arthralgia, sore throat, evanescent rash, neutrophil-predominant inflammation, hyperferritinemia, and marked serial IL-18 elevation - yet the overall chronology and pharmacokinetic pattern strongly implicated clozapine as the trigger.

The differential diagnosis is critical and was refined over time. Infection was initially considered the most likely and most treatable diagnosis, which justified blood cultures, infection-focused evaluation, and empirical TAZ/PIPC on day 27. However, infection was not supported by cultures, viral antigen tests, urinalysis, chest computed tomography, bilirubin, or the overall clinical course. DRESS was considered but not favored: mild later eosinophil fluctuations did occur, with eosinophil percentages up to 7.3% and transient absolute eosinophil counts above 500/µL on days 40–44; however, there was no lymphadenopathy, no persistent rash, no clinically important liver or kidney injury, and no eosinophilic organ-dominant phenotype [9]. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome was unlikely because creatine kinase remained normal, rigidity was absent, and there was no altered consciousness. Myocarditis could not be completely excluded because echocardiography was not performed, but the syndrome was not myocarditis-predominant: troponin remained below the ULN, creatine kinase did not rise, chest pain was absent, and electrocardiographic changes were nonspecific [1, 10]. We therefore view any myocardial component as, at most, subclinical and secondary within a broader systemic inflammatory syndrome.

IL-18 should also be interpreted cautiously. It is a biologically plausible and clinically informative marker in Still’s disease, but it is not disease-specific and may increase in infections, macrophage activation, and other drug-related inflammatory states [7, 8]. In addition, ferritin elevation in this case was moderate compared with classic hyperferritinemic AOSD. Therefore, IL-18 and ferritin do not prove idiopathic AOSD. Rather, their serial pattern strengthened the interpretation of a Still-like autoinflammatory phenotype within a clozapine-associated systemic inflammatory reaction.

The present case fits well within the evolving concept of the clozapine inflammatory spectrum. Historically, clozapine fever, myocarditis, serositis, hepatitis, nephritis, colitis, dermatologic reactions, and DRESS-like syndromes were often described as separate adverse reactions. de Leon proposed that many of these phenomena are better understood as manifestations of early clozapine-induced inflammation during titration [2]. In that model, titration that is effectively too rapid for a given patient - because the schedule is aggressive, because the patient behaves as a poor metabolizer, or because metabolic reserve is reduced by host factors - induces cytokine release. The resulting cytokines can inhibit CYP1A2 and related pathways, creating a positive feedback loop in which clozapine exposure rises relative to dose and the inflammatory response intensifies [2, 4]. If the process continues, more organ-predominant phenotypes may emerge.

Leung and colleagues further synthesized the literature by showing that clozapine behaves as a proinflammatory drug especially during the first 4–8 weeks of exposure, with CRP and several cytokines reported to rise in this early window [1]. Their review also emphasized that the clinically visible phenotypes range from apparently benign fever and isolated CRP elevations to myocarditis and other organ-specific inflammatory syndromes, supporting a spectrum rather than a single entity [1, 3]. Within that framework, phenotype likely reflects the interaction between the cytokine milieu and host vulnerability of particular organs or tissues. In our patient, the dominant signature was not eosinophilic myocarditis or isolated fever, but a systemic Still-like pattern characterized by spike fever, arthralgia, sore throat, evanescent rash, hyperferritinemia, and marked serial IL-18 elevation. We therefore interpret this case not as proof that clozapine caused idiopathic Still’s disease, but as evidence that clozapine-associated inflammation can mimic a Still-like syndrome with an IL-18-dominant signature.

Published clozapine-associated inflammatory case reports with partial overlap have included lupus-like syndromes or drug-induced lupus [11–13], polyserositis or pleural-effusion syndromes [14–16], and Sweet’s syndrome [15, 17]; DRESS-like presentations have also been summarized in systematic reviews [9]. These reports support the view that clozapine can trigger heterogeneous systemic inflammatory phenotypes extending beyond isolated myocarditis or self-limited fever. However, we did not identify a prior clozapine case explicitly framed as a drug-induced AOSD or Still-like syndrome with documented IL-18 elevation. The combination in our patient - AOSD-like classification criteria, marked serial IL-18 elevation, hyperferritinemia, and dose-relative accumulation of clozapine - therefore appears distinctive within the available literature.

Host vulnerability in this patient was probably multifactorial rather than singular. As a Japanese woman who was not smoking at the time of clozapine initiation, she belonged to a low-CYP1A2 subgroup for clozapine dosing, and her body mass index of 28.5 kg/m² may have further reduced clearance relative to dose [2, 4]. Ongoing olanzapine during cross-titration may also have contributed, because olanzapine shares substantial CYP1A2-dependent metabolism and has been discussed as a potential complicating cofactor during early clozapine inflammation [2]. Although our titration was approximately 25 mg/week after the first week and slower than the Japanese package-insert schedule, Japanese data and recommendations indicate that inflammatory adverse events can still occur even with slow titration and that patients with risk factors such as obesity or concomitant olanzapine may require even more gradual schedules, such as 6.25–12.5 mg/week [18, 19]. By contrast, her remote smoking history is unlikely to have provided meaningful CYP1A2 induction at clozapine initiation because she had not smoked for decades. A final speculative possibility is target-organ susceptibility to capillary leak or serosal/cardiopulmonary manifestations once the cytokine cascade is triggered. In our patient, that type of susceptibility may help explain why the phenotype featured edema, pleural effusions, hypoalbuminemia, and heart-failure physiology rather than isolated fever alone.

The pharmacokinetic findings support this interpretation. Absolute clozapine concentrations were not dramatically supratherapeutic, yet they were high for a daily dose of only 75 mg, and the C/D ratio rose further between days 29 and 35 while inflammatory manifestations continued. A recent Japanese study found that higher clozapine C/D ratios on day 8 after initiation were associated with subsequent clozapine-associated fever [19]. Our C/D ratios were measured later, after inflammation had become clinically evident, so they should not be interpreted as a pre-onset risk marker. Instead, they are best interpreted as pharmacokinetic support for inflammation-associated reduction in clozapine metabolic capacity during the event [1, 2, 4, 19].

The delayed discontinuation of clozapine is an important clinical lesson. On day 27, continuing clozapine was understandable because psychosis had improved, infection was initially the leading concern, and there were no clear signs of agranulocytosis, DRESS, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, or overt myocarditis. However, the subsequent course suggests that earlier temporary interruption of clozapine once spike fever, arthralgia, and absence of an infectious focus became apparent might have mitigated progression. This reflective interpretation is consistent with recent recommendations emphasizing early reassessment of dose escalation, inflammatory markers, and clozapine exposure during titration [3, 18].

Another clinically interesting aspect is the apparent response to corticosteroids. Hydrocortisone boluses were repeatedly followed by defervescence, and the final illness course may have been shorter and less organ-destructive than it otherwise would have been. Because clozapine had already been stopped and other supportive treatments were provided, the effect of corticosteroids cannot be isolated. Still, the temporal association raises the possibility that corticosteroids attenuated progression within the inflammatory spectrum. In retrospect, we regard the illness as a single inflammatory wave that began around day 27, peaked around days 40–45, and subsided around day 50. Early hydrocortisone may have temporarily suppressed the reaction, making the course appear biphasic, but it was not sufficient to abort the underlying inflammatory process.

This case has several limitations. It is a single case, and rechallenge was not attempted for ethical and clinical reasons. Echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, skin biopsy, glycosylated ferritin, procalcitonin, HHV-6 testing, and broad cytokine profiling were not performed. The first rash may have been partly influenced by TAZ/PIPC. IL-18 elevation is not specific to AOSD, and ferritin elevation was moderate. Hydrocortisone treatment and natural disease evolution cannot be separated. These limitations argue against overclaiming causality, but they do not negate the main clinical observation that clozapine-associated inflammation can present with a Still-like systemic phenotype.

A practical message from this case is that clinicians should think beyond infection, benign clozapine fever, or myocarditis alone when spike fever and systemic inflammation develop after clozapine initiation [3]. A Still-like pattern - high spiking fever, fever-associated arthralgia, sore throat, evanescent rash, neutrophil-predominant leukocytosis, ferritin elevation, and marked serial IL-18 elevation - may represent another expression of the clozapine inflammatory spectrum. Early temporary interruption of clozapine should be considered when such a pattern emerges during titration, even when the clozapine dose is relatively low. If Still-like inflammation persists after discontinuation, corticosteroid treatment may be considered on a case-by-case basis while infection and organ-dominant inflammatory syndromes are reassessed.

In conclusion, this case broadens the recognizable phenotype of clozapine-associated inflammation to include an AOSD-like systemic presentation with marked serial IL-18 elevation. It also supports a clinically practical message: early temporary interruption of clozapine may be safer when spike fever and systemic inflammation emerge during titration, and anti-inflammatory treatment may be useful when Still-like inflammation persists after discontinuation. In that sense, this case shows another face of the clozapine inflammatory spectrum.

Patient perspective

The patient and treating team had hoped that clozapine would improve symptoms that had been resistant to several prior antipsychotics. She noticed early improvement in hallucinations but accepted discontinuation because of the severity of the inflammatory episode. She agreed to publication in the hope that the experience may help clinicians recognize similar complications earlier.
