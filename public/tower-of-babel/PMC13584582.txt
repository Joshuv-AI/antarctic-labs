Prevalence of Salmonella species in live bird markets and processing environments in Plateau State, Nigeria
Authors: Chidinma Ejike-Okongwu, Hashimu Zakari, Anayochukwu Chibuike Ngene, Saje Emmanuel Dangyang, Michael Macvren Dashen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalmonella remains a major foodborne pathogen of public health importance, particularly in developing countries where poor hygiene and biosecurity practices persist along the poultry value chain. This study investigated the prevalence and distribution of Salmonella species in live bird markets and processing environments in Plateau State, Nigeria. A total of 1,120 samples, including fecal droppings, soil, carcass rinse water, defeathering water, slaughtering table swabs, and eviscerated organs, were collected across eight markets during both dry and wet seasons. Isolation and identification were conducted using standard microbiological and biochemical techniques, of ISO 6579 and WOAH while statistical analysis was performed using SPSS. Overall, Salmonella was detected in 121 samples, yielding a prevalence of 10.80%. A significantly higher prevalence was observed during the wet season (12.68%) (p < 0.05). Geographically, Plateau North recorded the highest prevalence (13.75%), whereas Plateau Central and South showed lower rates (5.50% each). Among study locations, Chobe market had the highest contamination level (17.22%), while based on sample type, slaughtering table swabs exhibited the highest contamination (22.50%), indicating critical points for cross-contamination. Lower prevalence was observed in fecal samples, particularly from indigenous birds. Statistical analysis revealed significant associations between the occurrence of Salmonella and season, location, senatorial district, and sample type (p < 0.05), while no significant association was found with eviscerated organ type or bird category. The relatively high prevalence rate of Salmonella in this study suggest that slaughtering tables, carcass rinse water, fecal droppings, eviscerated organs and poor infrastructural facilities could be potential transmission routes. Introduction

Salmonellosis is recognized as one of the leading bacterial foodborne diseases globally and remains a major challenge to public health, food security, and poultry production industries [1, 2]. The disease is caused by bacteria belonging to the genus Salmonella, particularly non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS), which are frequently linked to poultry and poultry-derived food products [3, 4]. Human infection commonly occurs through the consumption of contaminated poultry meat, eggs, and water, as well as through direct exposure to infected birds or contaminated environments [5, 6]. Worldwide, NTS infections contribute substantially to disease burden, mortality, and economic losses, with the greatest impact observed in low- and middle-income countries where food safety systems, biosecurity practices, and disease surveillance programs are often insufficient [7–10].

Poultry and poultry-processing environments are recognized as major reservoirs of Salmonella because of the organism’s ability to colonize the gastrointestinal tract of birds and persist within farm and market ecosystems [11–15]. In many developing countries, including Nigeria, live bird markets (LBMs) serve as important interfaces where birds from multiple production systems are aggregated, slaughtered, processed, and distributed under conditions that often favor bacterial transmission and environmental contamination [16–18]. Consequently, LBMs play a crucial role in the epidemiology and dissemination of Salmonella throughout the poultry value chain and among individuals exposed through occupational activities or consumption of contaminated poultry products.

Several studies in Nigeria have documented the occurrence of Salmonella in poultry and poultry-associated environments, although prevalence estimates vary depending on geographical location, sample type, production system, and laboratory detection methods. Fagbamila et al. [19] reported widespread Salmonella contamination in commercial layer farms across Nigeria, while Agbaje et al. [20] identified multidrug-resistant non-typhoidal Salmonella serovars in chicken feces from Ogun State. Similarly, Raji et al. [21] detected Salmonella in slaughtered chickens and ready-to-eat chicken gizzards in Kwara State, whereas Sati et al. [22] reported contamination in indigenous poultry droppings and drinking water sources in North Central Nigeria. Beyond Nigeria, environmental contamination has been extensively documented in poultry-processing environments. Kunadu et al. [23] reported high contamination rates in poultry carcasses (61%), rinse water (77%), and fecal matter (81%) in informal poultry-processing systems in Ghana, while Siddiky et al. [24] identified slaughter surfaces and processing environments as important reservoirs of Salmonella in wet markets in Bangladesh. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that environmental contamination contributes substantially to the persistence and transmission of Salmonella within poultry production and processing systems.

The persistence of Salmonella in poultry environments is largely attributed to its remarkable environmental survival capabilities. The organism possesses several adaptive mechanisms that facilitate prolonged survival under adverse conditions, including biofilm formation, resistance to desiccation, tolerance to temperature fluctuations, and persistence in organic matter-rich environments [25, 26]. Biofilm formation on slaughter tables, processing equipment, water containers, and market surfaces protects bacterial cells from disinfectants and environmental stressors, thereby promoting long-term survival and recurrent contamination [25]. Furthermore, Salmonella can persist in contaminated soil, feces, wastewater, feathers, and dust for extended periods, increasing opportunities for indirect transmission [26, 27]. These survival strategies enhance the ability of the organism to circulate continuously within live bird markets and poultry-processing environments.

Contamination pathways in poultry-processing systems are complex and multifactorial. During slaughter and processing, intestinal contents from infected birds may contaminate carcasses, slaughter tables, knives, rinse water, de-feathering equipment, and surrounding surfaces [13–15]. Cross-contamination may occur through repeated use of contaminated water, inadequate cleaning and disinfection of processing equipment, poor waste management practices, and direct contact between contaminated carcasses and processing surfaces [24, 28, 29]. Environmental contamination is further amplified in LBMs where birds from different sources and species are mixed under crowded and often unsanitary conditions [18, 29]. Such conditions facilitate continuous transmission of Salmonella among birds, humans, equipment, water sources, and the surrounding environment.

Environmental reservoirs are increasingly recognized as critical components of Salmonella epidemiology and surveillance. Contaminated soil, wastewater, processing surfaces, and poultry feces may function as long-term reservoirs capable of sustaining bacterial circulation even in the absence of clinically infected birds [25, 30]. Environmental surveillance therefore provides valuable information regarding pathogen persistence, transmission dynamics, and critical control points within poultry-processing systems [28, 31]. In addition, monitoring environmental contamination can facilitate the early detection of emerging Salmonella strains and support the implementation of targeted interventions aimed at reducing contamination throughout the poultry value chain [10, 32].

Despite the growing public health significance of Salmonella in Nigeria, limited information exists regarding its simultaneous occurrence in poultry, environmental samples, and processing environments within live bird markets in Plateau State. Most previous Nigerian studies have focused primarily on poultry feces, meat products, or farm-level investigations, with comparatively little attention given to integrated environmental contamination pathways involving soil, rinse water, slaughter surfaces, de-feathering water, and eviscerated organs within market settings [19–22]. Furthermore, there is limited information on the seasonal and geographical distribution of Salmonella contamination across the senatorial districts of Plateau State.

This study provides the first integrated assessment of Salmonella contamination across multiple environmental, processing, and poultry-associated sample types within live bird markets and poultry-processing environments in Plateau State, Nigeria. Unlike most previous Nigerian investigations that focused primarily on poultry feces, carcasses, or farm-level samples, the present study simultaneously evaluated soil, slaughter-table swabs, rinse water, de-feathering water, fecal droppings, and eviscerated organs across different senatorial districts and seasons. By examining multiple contamination pathways within a single epidemiological framework, the study offers new insights into the seasonal and geographical distribution of Salmonella in informal poultry markets. The identification of slaughter tables, rinse water, and environmental soil as major contamination hotspots highlights critical control points that may sustain pathogen transmission within the poultry value chain. In addition, the study provides important baseline epidemiological data for Plateau State, a major poultry-trading hub in North Central Nigeria. The demonstration of concurrent Salmonella contamination in environmental samples, processing equipment, poultry organs, and fecal materials further strengthens understanding of the interconnected transmission dynamics operating within live bird markets. These findings provide valuable evidence to support targeted surveillance programs, improved biosecurity measures, enhanced sanitation practices, and effective public health interventions in Nigeria and other resource-limited settings.

Plateau State is of particular epidemiological importance because it serves as a major poultry-trading hub linking poultry movement between northern and southern Nigeria. The state hosts numerous live bird markets characterized by intensive poultry trade, aggregation of birds from multiple production systems, and informal slaughtering practices that may facilitate the amplification and dissemination of foodborne pathogens. The relatively cool climate and high levels of human and poultry traffic within urban centers such as Jos and Bukuru may further promote environmental persistence and transmission of Salmonella. These factors create an important ecological interface for the circulation of zoonotic pathogens within poultry production and processing systems.

Therefore, this study was conducted to determine the prevalence and distribution of Salmonella species in live bird markets and poultry-processing environments across Plateau State, Nigeria, using environmental samples, poultry fecal samples, processing water, slaughter surfaces, and eviscerated organs collected from different senatorial districts during both dry and wet seasons. The study also evaluated the influence of sample type, geographical location, and season on the occurrence of Salmonella in order to generate baseline epidemiological data relevant for surveillance, risk assessment, and the development of effective control strategies within poultry-processing environments.

Materials and methods

Laboratory quality assurance and contamination-control measures

To ensure the reliability and validity of the laboratory findings, several quality assurance and contamination-control measures were implemented throughout the study. Laboratory benches and equipment were routinely disinfected with 70% ethanol before and after use to maintain a clean and contamination-free working environment. All media preparation and culture handling procedures were conducted within a Class II biosafety cabinet under aseptic conditions. Culture media were sterilized appropriately before use, and sterility checks were performed by incubating uninoculated batches to confirm the absence of microbial contamination. Isolation of Salmonella was carried out in accordance with the ISO 6579 standard protocol, which provides validated procedures for the detection of Salmonella spp.; therefore, reference strains were not included in the analysis. Furthermore, presumptive isolates were subjected to standard biochemical identification tests and confirmed using Rambach chromogenic agar, a highly specific medium for the detection and differentiation of Salmonella species. These measures collectively minimized the risk of laboratory contamination and ensured the accuracy of the results obtained.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Animal Ethics Committee of the National Veterinary Research Institute (NVRI), Vom, Nigeria, with approval number AEC/02/218/26. This study did not involve experimental manipulation of live animals. Samples, including fecal droppings, environmental materials, and eviscerated organs, were collected from live bird markets rather than controlled poultry farms or laboratory animal facilities.

Study Area

The study was conducted in Plateau State, the North Central part of Nigeria. It has an area of 26,899 square kilometers, the state is located on latitude 8024’N to 10004’N and longitude 8°32’E and 10°38’E [32]. Presently, the state has seventeen (17) Local Government Areas within three Senatorial districts namely: Plateau North, Central and South Senatorial. It has a tropical climate of rainy season (April to October) and dry season (November to March). Plateau has a population of around 3,206,531(2006 census), 4,200,442 (2016 forecast) (Figs. 1 and 2).

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted to isolate Salmonella from live bird markets and processing environments between February and July, 2024. Chicken droppings from indigenous and broilers, eviscerated organs, swabs from carcass slaughtering tables, soil, defeathering water and rinse water were randomly collected from eight (8) live bird markets and processing environments i.e., Railway/Terminus markets, Yankagi, Chobe, Bukuru, Pankshin, Bokkos, Shendam and Namo (Qua’anpan) live bird markets within Plateau State, Nigeria.

Sample collection

Samples were randomly collected across the three Senatorial Districts, focusing on major LBMs and poultry processing sites between February and July, 2024. One thousand one hundred and twenty (1,120) samples, which includes defeathering water (80), carcass rinse water (80), fecal droppings (280 indigenous), fecal droppings (80 broilers), soil (280), slaughtering table swabs (80) and eviscerated organs 240 (40 liver, 40 spleen, 40 caeca, 40 small intestine,40 gizzard and 40 proventriculus). Sampling was carried out using sterile spatulas, swab sticks, syringes and polythene bags, properly labeled. Samples were collected and immediately transported to the National Reference Laboratory of the National Veterinary Research Institute Vom, Plateau State Nigeria using a cooler with ice packs for analysis. The samples were analysed for the presence of Salmonella using standard microbiological and biochemical processes.

Isolation of Salmonella species

Isolation of salmonella was carried out according to the International Organization of Standardization ISO 6579 [33] and World Organization for Animal Health [34] recommendations using pre-enrichment in buffered peptone water, enrichment in Rappaport Vassiliadis and Tetrathionate broth and primary isolation on Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar (OXOID, UK) and Brilliant Green agar (OXOID, UK). Biochemical identification was also carried out according to the recommendations of ISO 6579 [33] and World Organization for Animal Health [34] using comprehensive biochemical and cultural identification procedure.

Pre-enrichment

For the solid samples, 1gram of solid samples (fecal, soil, table swab sticks and eviscerated organs) were inoculated into 10mL of buffered peptone water (Oxoid, UK) while 1mL of the liquid samples (de-feathering hot and rinse water) were also inoculated into 10mL of buffered peptone water and incubated at 37℃ between 18 and 24 h (ISO 6579).

Enrichment

Ten mL of enrichment broth (Rappaport Vassiliadis Soya broth) (OXOID, UK) and Tetrathionate broth + iodine (OXOID, UK) were prepared according to manufacturer instructions and dispensed into universal bottles. While Rappaport Vassiliadis Soya broth was autoclaved at 115℃ for 15 min, Tetrathionate broth + iodine was prepared by boiling and then after cooling to 45℃, iodine iodide solution was added according to manufacturer instruction without sterilization. 1mL from the inoculated buffered peptone water was inoculated into 10mL of the enrichment broth and incubated at 37℃ for the Tetrathionate + iodine and 0.1mL was inoculated into 10mL of Rappaport Vassiliadis and incubated at 42℃ between 18 and 24℃.

Subculturing on selective medium

Selective agar, Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar (OXOID, UK) and Brilliant Green agar (OXOID, UK) were prepared according to manufacturer’s instruction. A sterile wire loop was used to pick a loopful from the enrichment broths and streaked onto the XLD agar plates and Brilliant Green agar. The plates were incubated at 37℃ for 18–24 h.The appearance of pink or pink ring colonies with black centers on the XLD plates were regarded as presumptive Salmonella species while a pinkish-white or red colonies surrounded by red halo in the medium were regarded as presumptive salmonella species on Brilliant Green Agar.

Biochemical test

The suspected Salmonella isolates were identified using comprehensive biochemical and cultural identification test procedures according to the recommendations of ISO 6579 [33]. All suspected Salmonella colonies were picked, purified and inoculated into the following biochemical tubes for identification: Triple Sugar Iron (OXOID, UK) agar, Urea (OXOID, UK) agar and Lysine Iron Decarboxylase (OXOID, UK) agar. The presumptive salmonella isolates were also inoculated onto Rambach agar (CHROMagar, Paris) and a red appearance on Rambach indicates the presence of salmonella.

Statistical analysis

Results from prevalence, distribution of the pathogen across senatorial districts, seasons, specific geographical locations, and sample matrices of Salmonella were presented and entered into Microsoft Excel sheet and analyzed using descriptive statistics. Results were presented as frequency and percentages where applicable. A Chi-square value was generated based on this comparison. From this value, a p-value was calculated using statistical software SPSS version 25. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant, indicating that the observed difference was unlikely to be due to chance. A p-value greater than 0.05 (p > 0.05) indicated that the difference was not statistically significant.

Among the different sample types examined, table swabs recorded the highest overall prevalence of Salmonella species at 22.50% (18/80), followed by rinse water with 17.50% (14/80). Soil samples also showed considerable contamination, with a prevalence of 10.36% (29/280). In contrast, faecal droppings from indigenous chickens recorded the lowest prevalence of 4.64% (13/280), while faecal droppings from broilers had a prevalence of 5.00% (4/80).

With respect to seasonal distribution, several environmental and organ samples showed higher prevalence rates during the wet season compared to the dry season. Rinse water exhibited a marked increase from 5.00% in the dry season to 30.00% in the wet season, while de-feathering water recorded no positive sample during the dry season but increased to 20.00% during the wet season. Similarly, table swabs showed an increase from 20.00% in the dry season to 25.00% in the wet season.

Among the eviscerated organs, caeca recorded one of the highest wet season prevalence rates at 30.00%, with no positive sample detected during the dry season. Liver samples increased from 5.00% in the dry season to 20.00% in the wet season, while gizzard and proventriculus each increased from 10.00% in the dry season to 20.00% during the wet season. Spleen and small intestine maintained equal prevalence rates of 15.00% across both seasons.

Statistical analysis showed that the distribution of Salmonella species across sample types was highly significant overall (χ² = 32.790; p = 0.001), indicating that the occurrence of the organism varied significantly among the different sample sources. Seasonal stratification further revealed that the variation observed during the dry season was not statistically significant (χ² = 19.457; p = 0.053), whereas the wet season showed a highly significant association between sample type and occurrence of Salmonella species (χ² = 43.953; p < 0.001).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated an overall Salmonella prevalence of 10.80% in live bird markets and poultry processing environments in Plateau State, Nigeria. This prevalence agrees with the findings of Raji et al. (2021) [21], who reported an 8.6% prevalence among slaughtered chickens and ready-to-eat gizzards in Kwara State, Nigeria. Similarly, Sati et al. [22] documented relatively lower prevalence rates in indigenous poultry and drinking water sources from North Central Nigeria, while Ndlovu et al. [35] also reported lower contamination levels in poultry offal in South Africa. However, considerably higher prevalence rates have been reported in several Nigerian and African studies, ranging from 20.2% to 41.2% [19, 20, 24, 36, 37], with extremely high contamination levels documented in Ghana and South Africa [23, 38]. These differences may reflect variations in hygiene practices, market infrastructure, climatic conditions, poultry density, slaughtering procedures, sanitation practices, and regulatory enforcement across study locations [39].

The significantly higher prevalence observed in Plateau North senatorial district (13.75%) compared with Plateau Central and Plateau South (5.50% each) suggests that Salmonella contamination is strongly influenced by geographical and operational characteristics of the markets. Markets located within Plateau North, particularly Chobe, Bukuru, and Yankaji, are characterized by higher poultry traffic, greater human activity, and increased aggregation of birds sourced from multiple farms and transport routes. Such conditions facilitate pathogen introduction, circulation, and environmental persistence [40, 41]. The dense urban poultry trade system within Jos and Bukuru may also contribute to prolonged holding times and repeated contamination of processing surfaces and surrounding environments. In contrast, the relatively lower prevalence recorded in Plateau Central and South may reflect lower market intensity and reduced poultry movement.

Seasonal variation was another important finding in this study. The wet season prevalence (12.68%) was significantly higher than the dry season prevalence (8.93%), consistent with reports from Ghana and Ethiopia [42, 43]. Increased humidity and moisture during the rainy season likely enhance bacterial survival in contaminated soil, water, slaughter equipment, and processing surfaces [26]. Rainwater runoff may additionally facilitate the spread of fecal contaminants within market environments, especially where drainage systems are poor. The marked increase in prevalence observed in Bukuru, Yankaji, and Chobe during the wet season further supports the role of environmental moisture in promoting Salmonella persistence and transmission. Interestingly, some locations such as Bokkos and Shendam recorded no wet-season isolates, indicating that local sanitary practices, market size, or reduced poultry throughput may modify seasonal effects.

The analysis of sample types revealed important epidemiological insights regarding contamination pathways within live bird markets and poultry processing environments. Slaughter table swabs showed the highest prevalence (22.50%), indicating that processing tables are major reservoirs and transmission points for Salmonella. Similar observations have been reported in poultry processing environments in Bangladesh and Ghana, where contaminated slaughter surfaces acted as major sources of carcass contamination [23, 24]. In many live bird markets, slaughter tables are inadequately cleaned between processing cycles, allowing bacterial biofilms and organic residues to accumulate and sustain contamination. Frequent contact between contaminated carcasses and processing surfaces therefore creates opportunities for cross-contamination.

Rinse water also demonstrated high contamination levels (17.50%), with prevalence increasing sharply to 30.00% during the wet season. This finding suggests that communal rinse water functions as an important vehicle for dissemination of Salmonella between carcasses during processing. Repeated reuse of contaminated water without replacement or disinfection allows bacterial load to increase progressively during slaughter activities. Similar findings have been reported in informal poultry processing systems in Ghana and Bangladesh [23, 29]. The high contamination observed in de-feathering water during the wet season (20.00%) further supports the importance of contaminated processing water in the transmission cycle of Salmonella. Elevated moisture and organic matter in processing water may enhance bacterial survival and facilitate transfer to carcasses and equipment.

Environmental contamination was also evident from the recovery of Salmonella from soil samples (10.36%). Soil contamination was particularly high in Plateau North, where prevalence exceeded 22% in both seasons. This finding indicates persistent environmental contamination around live bird markets and slaughter points. Poultry feces, blood, wastewater discharge, and accumulation of organic wastes likely contribute to contamination of surrounding soil. Contaminated soil may subsequently act as a reservoir for reinfection of birds and contamination of equipment, footwear, and processing materials. Similar environmental persistence of Salmonella in poultry market environments has been described previously [25, 30]. The lower prevalence observed in Plateau Central and South may reflect differences in market sanitation and environmental exposure.

Among eviscerated organs, Salmonella was consistently isolated from liver, spleen, caeca, small intestine, gizzard, and proventriculus, with prevalence ranging from 12.50% to 15.00%. Although no statistically significant differences were observed among organ types, the detection of Salmonella across multiple organs indicates systemic dissemination and intestinal colonization within infected birds. The high prevalence in caecal samples during the wet season (30.00%) is particularly important because the caeca serve as a major reservoir for intestinal carriage and fecal shedding of Salmonella [30]. During slaughter and evisceration, rupture of intestinal contents can easily contaminate carcasses, processing water, and slaughter surfaces. The widespread organ contamination observed in this study therefore highlights poor hygienic handling during slaughter and inadequate farm-level biosecurity.

The fecal carriage analysis further demonstrated differences between poultry production systems. Broiler fecal droppings showed a higher prevalence (5.00%) than indigenous chicken droppings (1.25%) in Plateau North. Intensive broiler production systems often involve high stocking densities, stressful housing conditions, and frequent antimicrobial exposure, factors known to favor Salmonella colonization and fecal shedding [22, 44]. In contrast, indigenous chickens raised under extensive scavenging systems may experience lower transmission pressure because of reduced stocking density and less continuous contact with contaminated litter and equipment [45, 46]. Nevertheless, the recovery of Salmonella from indigenous chicken droppings across all senatorial districts still demonstrates that free-range birds may contribute to environmental dissemination of the pathogen within market settings.

The observed variation across sample matrices emphasizes that Salmonella contamination within live bird markets is multifactorial and sustained by interactions between infected birds, contaminated environments, slaughter equipment, processing water, and poor hygienic practices. The simultaneous contamination of environmental samples, processing surfaces, rinse water, and internal organs demonstrates the existence of interconnected transmission pathways that permit continuous circulation of the organism throughout the poultry value chain. These findings highlight the urgent need for improved sanitation practices, regular disinfection of slaughter equipment, provision of clean water, proper waste disposal systems, and strengthened biosecurity measures in Nigerian live bird markets and poultry processing environments.

Conclusion

The study concludes that Salmonella contamination represents a substantial public health concern in Plateau State’s live bird markets and processing environments, with an overall prevalence of 10.80%. Statistically there was significant association between the occurrence of Salmonella and season, senatorial districts, study location and sample type. In contrast, there was no significant association between the occurrence of Salmonella with eviscerated organs and type of chickens (broilers and indigenous). The prevalence of Salmonella species from this study shows that the bacteria is still a potential threat to both local and international countries.

Limitation of study

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study was limited to selected live bird markets and poultry processing environments within Plateau State; therefore, the findings may not fully represent all poultry production and marketing systems across Nigeria. Second, the cross-sectional design only reflects contamination patterns at the time of sampling and may not capture long-term temporal fluctuations in Salmonella circulation. Third, molecular characterization, serotyping, and genomic analysis of the Salmonella isolates were not performed, limiting the ability to determine circulating serovars, virulence profiles, transmission dynamics, and genetic relatedness between environmental and poultry-associated isolates. In addition, antimicrobial susceptibility profiling was beyond the scope of the present study and therefore the resistance patterns of the recovered isolates could not be assessed. Although multiple environmental and poultry-associated samples were analyzed, the study did not evaluate specific farm-level risk factors, biosecurity practices, or hygienic behaviors contributing to contamination within the live bird markets. Nevertheless, the study provides important baseline epidemiological data on Salmonella occurrence across environmental, processing, fecal, and organ samples in Plateau State and highlights critical contamination points relevant for surveillance and control interventions.

Recommendations

The findings of this study highlight significant public health concerns arising from the occurrence of Salmonella in live bird markets and poultry-processing environments in Plateau State. The detection of the pathogen in slaughter tables, rinse water, environmental samples, and poultry organs indicates that cross-contamination during slaughter and processing is a major route of transmission to poultry handlers and consumers. Given the role of live bird markets as major poultry distribution centers, contaminated birds and poultry products may facilitate the wider dissemination of Salmonella across the poultry value chain.

To mitigate these risks, improved hygiene and biosecurity measures should be implemented and enforced within live bird markets and poultry-processing facilities. Routine cleaning and disinfection of processing equipment and surfaces, provision of clean potable water, proper waste management, and the elimination of rinse-water reuse are essential. Regular environmental surveillance, adoption of Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point (HACCP)-based interventions, and continuous training of poultry handlers on food safety and sanitation practices should be strengthened. Furthermore, a One Health approach involving collaboration among veterinary, environmental, and public health sectors is recommended to enhance Salmonella surveillance and control, while future studies should incorporate antimicrobial resistance profiling and molecular characterization to better understand the epidemiology and public health significance of circulating Salmonella strains in Nigeria.
