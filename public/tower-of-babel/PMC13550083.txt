Long-term Outcomes of Hypo- and Conventional Fractionated Stereotactic Radiotherapy for Non-vestibular Schwannomas: A Retrospective Cohort Study from Ramathibodi Hospital
Authors: Siripa BUMRUNGTIEN, Putipun PUATAWEEPONG, Wasawat MUNINTHORN, Ake HANSASUTA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although vestibular schwannomas are common, benign intracranial tumors with similar histopathology originating from other nerves are rare. For non-vestibular schwannomas, surgery remains standard treatment, albeit associated with morbidities. Radiotherapy offers a safe and effective alternative treatment modality. Nevertheless, the previous publication had a limited follow-up period and included outcomes from both stereotactic radiosurgery and radiotherapy. To investigate our long-term results, a retrospective chart review was conducted in patients who received stereotactic radiotherapy for non-vestibular schwannomas using hypo- or conventional fractionated regimen, from 2000 to 2020, with a minimum follow-up of 24 months. Treatment failure was defined as a ≥20% tumor growth, necessitating additional treatment. Radiation-induced sequelae were also observed. During the study period, 66 patients underwent stereotactic radiotherapy for non-vestibular schwannomas. After excluding non-eligible cases, 57 patients were analyzed. The tumor's median volume was 6.5 milliliters (interquartile range 2.85-11.04). Hypofractionated stereotactic radiotherapy, delivered in 3, 5, or 10 sessions, was used in 53 patients, while 4 patients received 25-day conventional fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy. With a median follow-up of 121 months (interquartile range 54-159), tumor control was achieved in 55 subjects (96.5%), with surgical resection necessary in 2 patients after radiation. The actuarial control rates were 100%, 97%, and 92% at 5, 10, and 15 years, respectively. Tumor shrinkage was observed in 27 individuals (47.37%). Regarding complications, one patient reported worsening of facial numbness after radiation. With extended follow-up compared to prior reports, our long-term outcomes support the efficacy and safety of hypo- and conventional fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy for non-vestibular schwannomas.

Introduction

Intracranial schwannomas are common benign neoplasms that account for approximately 8% of all primary central nervous system (CNS) tumors.1) It frequently arises from the eighth cranial nerve (CN), known as vestibular schwannoma (VS). Other CNs, such as CN III-VII and IX-XII, can also be the origins of these neoplasms, recognized as non-vestibular schwannomas (nVSs). Because they are discovered in 5%-10% of all intracranial schwannomas, nVSs are considered uncommon.2-5)

Like VS, many nVSs tend to grow slowly but can enlarge to a significant size, warranting treatment.2,6) With sufficient volume and brain compression, patients may develop neurological deficits. Hence, the primary goals of treatment are to alleviate compressive symptoms, reduce mass effect, and preserve CN function. Current treatment options include observation, surgery, and radiation utilizing stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) or stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT).2,7-9)

Surgical resection is considered the standard treatment for these tumors.3,5-7,10-12) However, complete removal is not always possible without complications.13) Thus, weighing the risks and benefits, radiotherapy is often considered in patients who are poor surgical candidates, have residual tumors after surgery, or experience tumor recurrence/regrowth. Studies have shown that SRS and SRT offer effective local control with low risks for nVSs. Nevertheless, larger tumors or those adjacent to critical organs may not be appropriate for a single-session SRS. Hypofractionated SRT (HFSRT) or conventional fractionated SRT (CFSRT) serve as an alternative to SRS in these situations.5,14,15)

Previous reports of nVSs utilizing HFSRT or CFSRT were limited to 7 publications and 1 conference abstract.15-22) D'Astous et al.16) demonstrated the highest number of nVSs, with 95 targets (SRT = 55, SRS = 40) from 88 patients; however, subjects with as short as 1 month of follow-up after radiation were also included. While all studies have shown favorable local control with linear accelerator (LINAC)- based and Gamma Knife treatments, the relatively short follow-up durations in some series raise questions about their long-term durability and safety.14,16-22) Moreover, most of the literature combined the pre-radiation parameters and/or post-radiation outcomes of SRS and SRT,15-18,20) creating difficulty in understanding the effects of SRS vs SRT when examined separately. Because of the limitations mentioned above, we analyzed data on nVS cases who underwent HFSRT and CFSRT to address these gaps and evaluate the long-term outcomes of these patients at our institution. Treatment parameters, local tumor control, and treatment-related toxicity were examined to support their role as safe and effective treatment modalities for nVSs.

Materials and Methods

Study design

Following approval by the Human Research Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University (Approval No. MURA2025/765), a retrospective medical record review was conducted. Data of patients with nVSs undergoing HFSRT or CFSRT at our center from 2000 to 2020 were retrieved. Written informed consent for the study was waived due to the retrospective design and the use of de-identified patient data.

Patient's characteristics, including age, gender, previous surgery or radiation, diagnosis of neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF-2), and pre-existing neurological deficits, were recorded. Concerning the nVSs, the locations, appearances (solid or cystic), volume, evidence of brainstem compression, and sides were documented. In patients with NF-2 who received radiotherapy simultaneously for multiple schwannomas, only the nVS target(s) were included in the analysis.

Radiotherapy equipment, planning, and technique

At our institution, SRT was delivered to patients with nVSs using 2 LINAC-based platforms as follows.

The frame-based LINAC radiosurgery (X-Knife, Varian Clinac 600SR, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) consisted of a 6-MV dedicated LINAC with fixed circular cones, planned using X-Knife forward-planning software (versions 3 and 4, Radionics, Burlington, MA, USA). The treatment protocol employed the relocatable Gill-Thomas-Cosman frame. A collimator size covering ≥90% of the target volume was selected, and multiple isocenters were used for irregular lesions. High conformity was achieved using non-coplanar arcs with varying beam weighting. The X-Knife was utilized for both the HFSRT and CFSRT from 2000 to 2014.

The frameless or mask-based robotic radiosurgery (CyberKnife Model G4, Accuray Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA) consisted of a 6-MV LINAC mounted on a fully articulated robotic arm. Inverse planning was executed using the Multiplan software (Accuray, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Patients were immobilized in the supine position with a customized thermoplastic facemask. The CyberKnife was used exclusively for HFSRT from 2009 onward.

Both systems acquired images from thin-cut computed tomography co-registered with radiosurgery protocol magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for delineation of the nVSs and critical structures. Parameters of the SRT, including the prescribed and maximum doses, number of fractions (Fx), and planning target volume, were retrieved from the radiation oncology treatment-planning medical record archive. Apart from the date of treatment, which largely determined the availability of SRT systems, the procedure was primarily guided by tumor size, location, and proximity to critical structures, with strict dose constraints being applied. Patients were categorized into 2 groups based on the number of Fx. The HFSRT group received >2 Gray (Gy) per Fx delivered in ≤10 Fx, while the CFSRT group had ≤2 Gy/Fx for more than 20 Fx.

Outcome assessments

After the SRT, all patients underwent MRI and were evaluated for new neurological deficits or radiation-induced complications at 3, 6, and 12 months during the first year. For unremarkable cases, the interval was increased to 12 and 24 months, respectively. Patients with less than 24 months of follow-up, incomplete radiotherapy information, or unretrievable clinical or radiographic data were excluded from the study.

The results of SRT were assessed by reviewing medical records from outpatient clinic visits. Documented comprehensive neurological data was collected for neurological status. Any new or worsening condition was considered post-radiation CNS toxicity based on Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) CNS toxicity criteria. The development of such was carefully noted. Clinically significant CNS toxicity was considered if there were grade ≥2 events, corresponding to symptomatic toxicity requiring medical management.23) Due to the retrospective nature of this study, clinical evaluations were based on available records and were not blinded.

Concurrently with clinical assessment at outpatient department visits, serial MRI was performed to evaluate tumor control. Based on radiographic interpretation, treatment outcome was determined by the changes in tumor volume or maximal diameter. Shrinkage was defined as any measurable reduction from pre-SRT imaging; stable disease as no significant change; and progression as ≥20% increase in tumor volume relative to baseline.24) Treatment failure was defined as tumor progression requiring additional surgery or repeat radiation. Radiographic changes attributable to radiation, consistent with RTOG criteria, were recorded.23)

For each patient, if no additional treatment was required at the last clinic visit, the date of that visit was recorded as the duration of follow-up. In cases requiring subsequent surgery or repeat radiation, the date was censored at that time point, indicative of failure. Predictor variables for outcome, including patients' demographics, tumors' volume/appearance/location, as well as previous surgery/radiation, along with potential confounders such as NF-2 status, pre-existing neurological deficits, different SRT systems, and fractionation, were also recorded.

Data analysis

Patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment details, and clinical/radiological outcomes were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and were examined by the Chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test, depending on the sample size. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the unpaired t-test for normally distributed variables. Non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians and interquartile range (IQR) and were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U test. Univariate analysis was performed to identify potential predictors of treatment failure and to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was implemented to estimate the time to treatment failure. All statistical analyses were computed using Stata version 18 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), with statistical significance defined as a p-value < 0.05.

Results

Between 2000 and 2020, 66 patients underwent SRT for 66 nVSs at our institute. Nine patients were excluded due to inadequate follow-up or missing crucial data in the retrieved medical records. Hence, 57 patients, encompassing 28 males and 29 females, were included in the analysis. The average age at the time of SRT was 46 (±14.23) years. Forty-three patients (75.44%) had prior surgery, none had prior radiation, and 6 (10.53%) were diagnosed with NF-2. Almost 3-quarters had pre-existing neurological deficits. The overall median tumor volume was 6.5 mL (IQR 2.85-11.04). Trigeminal schwannoma was the most common tumor in 27 cases (47.37%), followed by 17 glossopharyngeal origins (29.82%).

Table 1 illustrates the treatment parameters stratified by the number of SRT sessions. For HFSRT, the median tumor volumes for the 3-, 5-, and 10-Fx regimens were 1.41, 6.81, and 9.75 mL, respectively, with mean prescription doses of 17.25 (±2.12), 24.05 (±1.64), and 31.53 (±2.93) Gy, respectively. The most frequently employed plan was 5 Gy per session delivered over 5 days (n = 35, 61.4%), with a median prescribed dose of 25 Gy (IQR 22.2-25). Four patients (7%) were scheduled for 25 sessions of CFSRT. Their nVS volumes were 0.75, 1.88, 10.7, and 41.6 mL. Because of the functioning optic nerves, the 2 small nVSs received CFSRT for their adherence to the structure. The total prescribed dose of 45 Gy was delivered to 3 cases, and the largest lesion received 50 Gy. The statistically significant increase in the number of fractions accompanying larger tumor sizes most likely reflected this concept, as applied to our decision-making process. The overall mean tumor coverage was 96.40 (±1.63).

The median follow-up of our cohort was 121 months (IQR 54-159), with 2 patients (4%) exhibiting tumor growth necessitating surgical intervention at 80 and 132 months, respectively. (Table 2) A male patient with an 11.45 mL post-resection trigeminal schwannoma failed the 10-session regimen at 132 months. His tumor grew to 13.8 mL, resulting in worsening radiographic compression of the brainstem, although without additional neurological difficulties. Another occurred after a 5-fraction SRT for a post-surgical glossopharyngeal schwannoma. Despite a stable neurological status, the tumor enlarged from 11.81 to 14.62 mL with concerning radiographic brainstem compression. Although further observation was offered as an alternative, both patients chose surgery due to the absence of typical pseudoprogression features on MRI and continued growth beyond the usual time frame.

Discussion

Non-vestibular schwannomas are uncommon benign tumors. Microsurgical resection has been considered the first choice in symptomatic cases of neural compression. However, complete removal is frequently limited by the complex relationship between these neoplasms and nearby critical structures, risking permanent morbidity. In this context, stereotactic radiation has been established as an effective and safe option for patients with residual or recurrent disease or for those in whom the risks of resection are unacceptable.5,6)

Although single-fraction SRS works well in appropriately selected cases, multisession or conventional fractionated SRT offers a wider range of sizeable tumors close to critical organs. For over 2 decades, our tertiary center has received complex nVSs from across the country for radiation. Due to the moderate to large size of most nVSs referred to our institute and tailored planning based largely on target volume with dose constraints to critical organs, SRS was only appropriate in 2 patients. Hence, we elected to examine the more complex neoplasms that required HFSRT or CFSRT, which have consistently represented our most frequent challenges, rather than conflating the data with SRS, to serve the purpose of this research and clarify the unanswered questions that had only been partly addressed by previous reports.

The existing literature evaluating SRT for nVSs remains limited. A search of English-language publications identified 7 clinical series and 1 meeting abstract specifically addressing this treatment modality. (Table 4) However, most studies combined SRS and SRT results and reported them heterogeneously. Interpretation of the pooled outcomes must be made with caution, as the pre-radiation parameters would not have been identical.15-18,20) While these studies consistently demonstrated favorable tumor control, most were limited by relatively short follow-up durations, restricting assessment of long-term durability, which is an essential consideration for benign tumors with a slow-growing pattern. In addition, the varying, but not simplified, formats of illustrations, definitions of success/failure, and data representation using means (ranges) and medians (ranges) create heterogeneity across several articles, making it somewhat difficult to compare or provide an overall statistical summary of success/failure or toxicity.

In a meeting abstract by Laufer et al.,15) the median follow-up of 92.5 months and the maximum follow-up of 306 months represented the longest to date. However, the inclusion of patients with surveillance as short as 5 months introduced considerable disparity. Moreover, their median tumor volume was smaller than that of our cohort (4.72 vs 6.5 mL).15) Other literature also included similarly short follow-up periods of 1-7 months in their series.16-18,21) These limitations underscore the need for long-term outcome data in patients with more challenging tumors, particularly when SRS is not combined in the analysis.

To address these limitations, this study contributes to the existing literature by providing data from our large cohort, thereby adding to the only series with more than 50 SRT subjects, previously reported by D'Astous et al.16) Our research, encompassing 57 patients, has demonstrated excellent tumor control, consistent with earlier literature. The median follow-up of 121 months (IQR 54-159) represents the longest duration of surveillance in these uncommon neoplasms. These results highlight the durability of HFSRT and CFSRT, supporting their role as an effective treatment modality for nVSs. Because the details of unsuccessful cases, i.e., type/location/volume of the nVSs or dose/fractionation regimen, from most reports were not completely narrated, and some of the failures were from SRS cases, the actual efficacy of SRT could not be precisely determined. In contrast, our comprehensive description of this issue is informative for future systematic reviews and meta-analyses, particularly for subgroup evaluations.

Beyond tumor control, the radiographic response provides additional insight. Almost half of the nVSs (47.37%) exhibited radiographic regression, which is consistent with earlier studies reporting shrinkage in 40%-55% of treated lesions.17,19,22) The regression observed in these nVSs reiterates the biological effectiveness of fractionated SRT. As advances in imaging enable earlier detection of smaller tumors, the verified efficacy of stereotactic radiotherapy further supports its role as a primary, minimally invasive treatment option in carefully selected patients.

Our clinical outcomes following radiation were favorable, with the majority of patients (80%) maintaining their neurological status and 9 patients (16%) demonstrating improvement in cranial nerve-related symptoms. Only 1 patient (2%) experienced subjective worsening of facial numbness despite radiographic tumor regression. The occurrence was considered grade 1 CNS toxicity; hence, no patients experienced grade ≥2 acute or late CNS toxicity.23) This single case of neurological worsening did not meet criteria for clinically significant radiation-related toxicity and is consistent with prior reports demonstrating a low incidence of post-treatment morbidity following hypofractionated and conventional fractionated SRT for nVSs.5,14,16,23,25) Finally, clinical decision-making often centers on a comprehensive understanding of treatment options along with the patient's individual judgment based on facts. In cases with significant and symptomatic brain compression, HFSRT or CFSRT cannot replace microsurgical procedures but can effectively manage postoperative residual nVSs after maximal safe resection.

This study has limitations related to its retrospective design and the incomplete clinical and imaging data available in medical records. Neurological and CN function was determined from narrative clinical documentation rather than using graded systems. Additionally, the lack of blind assessments may have introduced individual bias or inter-observer variation, similar to that noted in the existing publication. Next, due to the low numbers of failures and toxicity, examination of possible influential patients' demographics, tumor characteristics, or radiation parameters by univariate analysis did not yield any statistically significant results. Ideally, a larger cohort (n > 100) with a prospective study design and validated neurological outcome measures could help clarify this matter and better define long-term functional outcomes after SRT.

Conclusion

In summary, our long-term institutional experience supports hypo- and conventional fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy as safe and effective treatment options for moderate to large non-vestibular schwannomas adjacent to critical organs. The comprehensive examination of our extended follow-up in these patients reiterates durable tumor control with a low incidence of clinically significant toxicity.
