Investigation of enamel surface changes in teeth with artificially produced white spot lesions after debonding: an in vitro study
Authors: Mahmut Tankuş, Tülin Ufuk Toygar Memikoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate enamel surface changes after debonding of teeth with metal and ceramic brackets exhibiting varying severities of white spot lesions (WSLs).MethodsForty healthy premolars were divided into two groups based on the use of metal or ceramic brackets. Mild and severe lesions were established to examine the effects of WSLs on enamel damage and bracket type. All the teeth were subjected to the same bonding protocol. The cavitation areas on the enamel surface were examined using stereomicroscopy before application (T0), after debonding (T1), and after finishing (T2).ResultsThe ceramic bracket group demonstrated significantly greater adhesive residue on the tooth surface after debonding compared with the metal bracket group (P = 0.004). A significant main effect of time was noted, with a substantial increase in the total cavitation area from T1 to T2 (P < 0.001). A significant interaction was observed between time and lesion severity (p = 0.006). When the lesion severity was mild, there was no statistically significant difference in the average total cavitation area between T1 and T2 in either the metal or ceramic bracket groups (p > 0.05). However, when the lesion was severe, the difference was statistically significant in both the metal and ceramic bracket groups (p < 0.001 and p = 0.004, respectively). No significant interaction was found between the time and bracket type (p = 0.214).ConclusionsA WSL surrounding the entire surface of the bracket carries the risk of cavitation during finishing. Therefore, care should be taken to avoid cavitation in the enamel when removing adhesive residue from these teeth.Keywords: Debonding, Enamel damage, White spot lesion, Metal bracket, Ceramic bracket

Background

Brackets used in fixed orthodontic treatment must adhere to the teeth with a certain amount of force. This force should be strong enough to prevent tearing during oral functions but also weak enough to avoid damaging the enamel when the bracket is removed at the end of the treatment [1, 2]. An increase in bond strength requires a greater force when removing the braces. This increases the risk of enamel damage, such as cracking or fracturing [2, 3]. Debonding procedures such as bracket removal and adhesive residue cleaning have been reported to cause enamel loss and tears [4].

White spot lesions (WSLs) that form around brackets are common complications associated with orthodontic treatment [5]. WSLs are subsurface enamel pores characterized by a white opaque clinical appearance, which is considered the initial stage of caries [6–9]. WSLs have been reported in approximately 45–68% of patients during orthodontic treatment, with prevalence may increase to as high as 97% following treatment completion [8]. A recent meta-analysis reported that the pooled prevalence of WSL among patients undergoing orthodontic treatment was approximately 55% [10]. This problem predominantly affects the cervical region of the tooth and has been associated with bacterial plaques resulting from inadequate oral hygiene [6]. This occurs when the balance between demineralization and remineralization shifts towards demineralization. WSLs have the potential to compromise the primary goal of orthodontic treatment, which is to achieve optimal aesthetic results [11]. As the severity of this problem increases, aesthetic concerns arise for both orthodontists and their patients [12].

In contrast to the intact and mineralized outermost enamel layer (6), WSLs have been found to have demineralized enamel regions that are softer and more prone to abrasion than the normally mineralized enamel [13]. Early diagnosis of WSLs before the onset of cavitation is considered essential, as it allows the application of minimally invasive management strategies and supports preservation of tooth integrity [7]. As studies examining the effects of debonding have generally been conducted on healthy teeth, the effect of the force applied for debonding on this less resistant layer is unknown. To the best of our knowledge, only one previous study has investigated the effects of finishing procedure on teeth with WSLs. The aforementioned study evaluated the enamel loss in teeth with experimentally created WSLs and bonded metal brackets by assessing volume and depth following adhesive removal with low-speed finishing burs or discs [13]. However, the effects of ceramic bracket finishing on teeth with WSL remain unclear. In addition, the effect of cavitation area measurement, which has the potential to be directly related to tooth aesthetics, is yet to be determined. Furthermore, it is unknown how the WSL severity affects these variables. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the enamel surface changes that occur after debonding in teeth with carious lesions created after bonding metal and ceramic brackets. The null hypothesis was that surface cleaning after removal of metal or ceramic brackets from teeth with artificially induced WSL would not increase cavitation areas on the enamel surface.

Methods

This in vitro study was approved by the İnönü University Malatya Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval Number: 2017/15), which confirmed the adherence to ethical guidelines. A priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) based on a repeated-measures ANOVA (within–between interaction) design. Assuming an effect size of f = 0.40, alpha error probability of 0.05, and statistical power of 0.97, with four groups and two repeated measurements (T1–T2), the minimum required sample size was calculated as 36. In this study, 40 teeth were included to compensate for possible irregularities. The samples were randomly divided into equal groups using a computer algorithm (www.random.org).

Samples

To examine the effects of metal and ceramic bracket types, as well as experimentally induced WSL severity on enamel damage, four experimental groups were established: mild lesions with metal brackets (n = 10), severe lesions with metal brackets (n = 10), mild lesions with ceramic brackets (n = 10), and severe lesions with ceramic brackets (n = 10). WSL areas were subsequently measured as outcome parameters.

Healthy teeth with intact enamel surfaces, free from decay or filling, and without structural defects or damage during extraction were used in this study. Despite exhibiting these characteristics during inspection, teeth that did not exhibit these characteristics during initial stereomicroscopic examination (T0) were excluded from the study. The teeth were stored in a 0.1% thymol solution (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) at room temperature prior to the experiment to prevent bacterial growth and dehydration. To facilitate manipulation during the study and ensure that the teeth were examined in the same direction during the stereomicroscopic examination, they were placed vertically in hexagonal prism molds designed by the researcher (M.T.) with 8-mm sides and 18-mm length. Acrylic (Orthocryl, Dentaurum, Ispringen, Germany) was poured into the mold, and the tooth was placed in the center. This ensured that the buccal surfaces were parallel to one side of each hexagon. The teeth embedded in acrylic molds were placed in distilled water. This study did not apply any artificial ageing protocols (e.g., water storage, thermocycling or fatigue loading), as the primary aim was to evaluate the immediate effects of bracket debonding and subsequent enamel surface cleaning.

Preparation of working areas on the tooth surface and stereomicroscopic examination

To objectively compare the changes after debonding, labels were created to form standard WSL areas around the teeth. To standardize the demineralization areas, rectangular labels with a 1.5-mm margin around the brackets were prepared. In groups described as the severe lesion (metal and ceramic), the labels covered the entire bracket with a 1.5-mm margin on all sides. In the groups described as the initial lesion, the labels included a 1.5-mm margin around the bracket, starting from half of its occlusogingival dimension. Accordingly, label dimensions were 6.5 × 6.8 mm for the metal severe lesion group, 6.5 × 7.0 mm for the ceramic severe lesion group, and 3.5 × 6.5 mm for both the metal and ceramic initial lesion groups. WSLs were classified as mild or severe according to the quantity of the enamel surface exposed to the demineralization protocol rather than based on post hoc statistical differences in the measured lesion area values. The standardized WSLs are schematically shown in Fig. 1.

Labels were applied to the enamel surface considering the area where the brackets were placed. After the labels were applied, the entire tooth surface was coated with a double layer of an acid-resistant varnish (nail polish). The varnish was allowed to dry, and the labels were removed (Fig. 2).

Teeth were examined under a stereomicroscope (Olympus SZX16, Tokyo, Japan). The first stereomicroscopic examination was performed immediately before bonding and after embedding the teeth in acrylic molds and was recorded as T0. The second stereomicroscopic examination was conducted following the removal of the brackets and prior to the removal of the residual adhesive and was recorded as T1. After cleaning of the remaining adhesive, a final stereomicroscopic examination was performed and recorded as T2. All stereomicroscopic measurements were performed by the same researcher (M.T.). Flowchart of the experiment

All measurements were performed at least twice. Statistical analyses were performed using the averages (µ) of the values obtained from these measurements.

Bonding procedure

In the experiment, 0.022 slot MBT premolar brackets (Equilibrium® 2, Dentaurum, Ispringen, Germany) were used as metal brackets, and 0.022 slot MBT ceramic brackets (Radiance, American Orthodontics, Sheboygan, WI, USA) with a polycrystalline structure were used as ceramic brackets.

The teeth were etched with 37% orthophosphoric acid for 20 s, rinsed with oil-free water for 20 s, and subsequently dried with oil-free air for 20 s. A thin layer of primer was applied using a fine brush. The bonding process continued by applying a composite adhesive to the bracket base (Transbond XT; 3 M Unitek, Monrovia, California, USA). A portable light device producing 1200 Mw/cm2 of light (Elipar S10, 3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany) was used to polymerize the adhesive. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, metal brackets were light-cured for 10 s, with 5 s applied to both the mesial and distal surfaces, whereas ceramic brackets were light-cured for 5 s on the buccal surface of the teeth.

Formation of WSL

Artificial WSLs were produced using an acid buffer (chemical) demineralization model as described by Reynolds [14], which provides controlled and reproducible demineralization conditions, whereas bacterial models require complex culture conditions that are difficult to standardize. This technique, which falls under the category of caries formation models using acid buffers (chemical), was created by adding 0.264 g NaH2PO4, 0.244 g CaCI2, and 2.86 ml acetic acid to 1 L of deionized water. NaOH was added to the solution to adjust the pH to 4.4. Specimens embedded in acrylic blocks, bonded brackets, and areas where lesion formation was not desired, were immersed in a demineralization solution. All teeth were stored in the same sealed container for the same duration. The beaker was gently agitated several times daily to promote homogeneous lesion formation. Therefore, differences in the formation of mild and severe lesions were related to the area exposed to the solution.

The pH and temperature of the demineralization solution were monitored daily for four days using a pocket pH meter (ADWA AD12; Adwa Instruments, Szeged, Hungary). The initial pH was 4.37. At the end of day 1, the pH was 4.45 and the temperature was 25.1 °C; at the end of day 2, the pH remained 4.45 and the temperature was 25.2 °C. Although no increase in pH was observed at the end of day 2, the solution was replaced. At the end of day 3, the pH was 4.43 and the temperature was 25.1 °C, and at the end of day 4, the pH was 4.47 with a temperature of 25.7 °C. Debonding and finishing

For debonding the metal brackets, the same company’s metal bracket removal pliers (Dentaurum, Bracket Removing Pliers, REF 004-349-00, Ispringen, Germany) were used, and for the ceramic brackets, the same company-recommended plastic-tipped removal pliers were used. (American Orthodontics, Radiance Plus Debonding Pliers, + 001-343E, Sheboygan, WI, USA). Bracket removers were placed occlusogingivally on the bracket wings and force was applied. Moreover, molds used to fabricate the acrylic hexagonal prisms were used to stabilize the teeth during the debonding procedure, thereby allowing partial standardization of the debonding forces. To assess the amount of residual adhesive after debonding, both the four-stage index developed by Artun and Bergland and direct micrometer-level measurements performed using a microscope-integrated image analysis program (Olympus cellSens Entry, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) were employed, as shown in Fig. 5. According to this index, 0 indicates that there is no adhesive on the tooth, 1 indicates that 0–50% of the adhesive is present on the tooth, 2 indicates that 50–100% of the adhesive is present on the tooth, and 3 indicates that all adhesives are present on the tooth [15].

Measurement of WSL and residual adhesive areas of a specimen from the metal bracket–severe lesion group using a microscope-integrated image analysis program

After removing the brackets, the remaining adhesive was cleaned using a 6-bladed, conical round micromotor carbide bur (HM23R 014, Meisinger, Neuss, Germany). The bur was replaced with a new one after every six teeth. Cleaning under air cooling continued at a low speed until all the adhesives were removed. After this process, pumice was applied to each tooth using a brush bur for 5 s. The samples were placed in distilled water for stereomicroscopic examination. Final stereomicroscopic examination was conducted (T2). Blinding of the operator during adhesive removal was not feasible because the severity of the lesion was clearly visible during the procedure. Consequently, all the procedures were conducted by the same operator (M.T.) to maintain methodological consistency.

Statistics

Statistical analyses of the spatial measurements of the T0, T1, and T2 images were performed using SPSS Version 16 statistical software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The total cavitation area was compared at T1 and T2 according to the bracket type and WSL level using three-factor repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Bonferroni multiple comparison test was applied to the same data. The amount of adhesive was compared according to bracket type and WSL level using a two-way analysis of variance (factors: (1) Bracket type, (2) WSL level). The results of the analysis are presented as the mean ± standard deviation for quantitative data and frequency (percentage) for categorical data. Statistical significance level was set at p < 0.050.

Discussion

One of the most common complications associated with orthodontic treatment is WSLs on teeth at the end of treatment. Studies have demonstrated that the incidence of WSLs affecting at least one tooth after fixed orthodontic treatment can reach up to 97% [16]. There is limited data available regarding debonding properties in the presence of this highly common complication. The present study is the first to investigate changes in the tooth enamel surface following bracket removal in teeth with WSLs that have metal and ceramic brackets. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the interactions between various WSL levels and different bracket types. The results showed that time had a significant main effect on both bracket groups, indicating that cavitation significantly increased between the debonding and finishing stages. Additionally, as the level of the WSL increased in both the metal and ceramic bracket groups, there was a substantial increase in the amount of cavitation during the finishing phase.

The amount of adhesive remaining on the tooth surface can be calculated by direct measurements using dental models [17], scanning electron microscopy [18, 19], or stereomicroscopy [4, 19, 20]. In the present study, the use of a computer-assisted stereomicroscope was found to be both convenient and practical in terms of the application and evaluation of the results, which is consistent with the results of Kitahara-Céia et al. [21]. The advantages of this method include the ability to examine and store images obtained with an integrated digital camera in detail on a computer screen and to perform linear and areal measurements at the micron level. Moreover, the hexagonal prism-shaped acrylic blocks used as test samples in the present study allowed for a detailed and repeatable examination of the buccal, mesiobuccal, and distobuccal surfaces of the tooth under a stereomicroscope. However, volumetric measurements could not be performed; therefore, the true three-dimensional structure of the cavitation could not be evaluated. Nevertheless, given that aesthetic concerns increase with the severity of WSL [12], the surface area of the tooth affected by the lesion may be considered at least as important as its volume.

The Adhesive Remnant Index (ARI) is a commonly used metric to quantify the amount of adhesive residue remaining on the enamel surface of orthodontic brackets following debonding. However, it should be noted that the ARI is not a true quantitative measure, but rather a categorical scoring system that reflects a range of values [22]. Despite this limitation, the ARI score is clinically significant from two perspectives. First, a high ARI score indicates that bond failure occurs predominantly at the bracket–adhesive interface, which is associated with better preservation of enamel integrity and a lower risk of structural damage [23–25]. Second, particularly in teeth affected by WSLs, as demonstrated by the findings of the present study, the elimination of residual adhesive using rotary instruments may itself result in the formation of cavitation.

In the present study, in addition to the ARI scoring, the residual adhesive on each tooth was quantitatively measured in terms of the surface area to allow for a more precise evaluation. Accordingly, the amount of adhesive remaining on the tooth surface owing to debonding was significantly higher in the ceramic bracket group than in the metal bracket group. This finding contrasts with those of previous studies reporting that ceramic brackets, particularly those with chemical retention, exhibit higher bond strength and tend to show lower ARI scores, indicating that less adhesive remains on the tooth surface and potentially increase the risk of enamel damage during debonding. However, this pattern of failure may allow easier and safer removal of residual resin after debonding [26]. Conversely, Theodorakopoulou et al. demonstrated that in almost all samples of two different types of ceramic brackets removed with pliers, the entire adhesive remained on the tooth surface [27]. In agreement with this observation, our results showed that in the ceramic bracket group, all adhesive remained on the tooth surface in 85% of the samples, whereas a substantial amount remained in the remaining 15%. This finding, in contrast to those reported for early generation ceramic brackets, suggests a shift in debonding from the enamel–adhesive interface toward the bracket–adhesive interface, which is more favorable for enamel preservation. Considering the severe enamel damage reported in studies involving early ceramic brackets [28, 29], it can be inferred that modern ceramic brackets have undergone considerable transformation and have become safer during debonding, particularly owing to modifications made to the bracket base with advances in technology. Furthermore, variations in adhesive selection, bracket characteristics, and debonding techniques may partially account for the inconsistent findings in the literature regarding the amount of residual adhesive.

Despite the absence of a statistically significant main effect of lesion severity, the significant interaction between lesion severity and time suggests that its impact was evident after adhesive removal and surface cleaning. This suggests that the progression of cavitation is not solely determined by baseline lesion characteristics but also by how these lesions respond to post-debonding procedures. The study results revealed that the cavitation on the enamel surface increased during cleaning. Therefore, contrary to the traditional belief that adhesive retention on the tooth surface indicates less enamel damage [23–25] in teeth with WSLs, the remaining adhesive represents a clinical paradox; while it may reduce the risk of enamel damage during the removal of the bonding process, its subsequent removal may inadvertently contribute to cavitation formation. Notably, the type of bracket did not affect this relationship. This emphasizes that the observed increase in cavitation was due to the severity of the lesion and not the type of bracket used. The rigidity and brittleness of ceramic brackets may lead to fractures when pressure is applied to the bracket base. Therefore, debonding methods specifically designed for each type of ceramic bracket have been recommended [27]. In the present study, although no fractures occurred in the metal bracket group during debonding, two ceramic brackets in the mild lesion group exhibited fractures. Despite the use of special debonding pliers for ceramic brackets, it is believed that fractures occurred because of the rigid structure of the brackets, as mentioned above. However, owing to the limited number of samples, this finding could not be subjected to statistical analysis; consequently, its significance could not be determined.

In addition to potential damage during bracket debonding, enamel damage can occur when burs are used to clean residues from the enamel surface [30]. Despite the existence of numerous methodologies for the removal of adhesive residues, tungsten carbide burs are widely regarded as a more expeditious and efficacious alternative to other instruments, and are thus considered the gold standard [23, 31]. However, on the contrary, there are also studies that do not recommend this method [32]. All removal methods resulted in a certain degree of enamel loss [19]. Cesur et al. reported that enamel damage was observed with all methods used to remove the resin after debonding, regardless of the cleaning method, materials used, or type of bracket (metal or ceramic) [32]. Diedrich et al. stated that approximately 150 μm of local enamel loss was expected during the debonding and surface cleaning phase of the brackets [33]. These data were sourced from intact teeth and were related to alterations in the depth of the enamel surface rather than to the width of the area. The superficial layer of a WSL is non-resistant to force and is affected even when examined using a sharp probe. The importance of the integrity of this layer for remineralization has been discussed above. In the study most closely related to this topic, Tüfekçi et al. investigated the effects of finishing with a bur and Sof-Lex disc in teeth bonded with metal brackets, including both sound enamel and enamel with WSLs. While no difference was found between the disc and bur in healthy teeth in their study, teeth with WSL had less enamel wear when finished with a bur than those in the disc group. The aforementioned study, which measured maximum depth and volume, did not include area analysis [13].

The paucity of research in the field of debonding studies on teeth with WSL poses a significant challenge when comparing the cavitation findings of the present study. In our study, the cavitation areas after finishing (T2) did not show a statistically significant increase in the groups with mild lesions compared to those immediately after debonding. However, a statistically significant increase was observed in the groups with severe lesions. It has been suggested that the increase in cavitation areas in severe lesion groups is due to the WSLs being susceptible to abrasion and having weak mineral structures. Another potential explanation is that, in the severe lesion group, the lesion extended from the middle of the bracket to the incisal edge. This finding indicates that the WSLs were located within the area contacted by the bur during removal of the residual adhesive. Therefore, alternative finishing methods should be considered in cases with severe WSL. Moreover, methods such as the application of casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate or topical fluoride, which increase the mineral content of the lesion, may be considered prior to the debonding procedure.

Previous studies have demonstrated that naturally occurring and artificially induced WSLs exhibit comparable histological architectures consisting of four distinct zones [34, 35]. In this context, chemical demineralization models provide a standardized and reproducible approach for generating such lesions. However, they do not fully replicate the complex biological conditions of the oral cavity. The absence of biofilms, salivary interactions, and organic components may influence lesion structure, mineral distribution, and progression dynamics. Consequently, although these models are useful for controlled comparisons, their direct clinical applicability remains limited and their translation to clinical practice should be interpreted with caution.

The present study had several limitations. Most notably, although quantitative spatial measurements were obtained, three-dimensional volumetric assessment could not be performed. Although the primary objective of this study was to quantitatively identify cavitation areas on the enamel surface rather than to analyze nanoscale surface properties, it may be possible to provide more detailed microstructural or chemical characterizations using advanced techniques such as Raman spectroscopy, micro-CT, non-contact surface profilometry, and FE-SEM. Future studies using these methods may provide further insights into the microstructure of dental enamel. Another limitation of this study was that the debonding force could not be completely standardized. Although the molds used to fabricate the acrylic hexagonal prisms were used to stabilize the teeth and the manufacturer-recommended pliers were used to apply force in the same direction, these measures provided only partial standardization. Moreover, this study did not evaluate the effectiveness of the different cleaning methods. Considering current studies involving carbide burs, it would be useful to test different finishing methods for teeth with WSLs from a clinical management perspective. Finally, the results should be interpreted with caution because no artificial aging protocol was employed between bonding and debonding. Consequently, the results only reflect the immediate response of the enamel rather than long-term intraoral conditions. Further subclinical and clinical studies are required to confirm or refute the results of this in vitro study.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, cavitation increased significantly during the finishing phase in teeth with WSLs, and this increase was influenced by WSL severity rather than bracket type. Ceramic brackets exhibited higher levels of residual adhesive, whereas bracket type did not influence the progression of cavitation. In teeth particularly affected by severe WSLs, removal of the residual adhesive can lead to enamel damage in the form of cavitation; therefore, caution is important in finishing protocols for these teeth. Methods that reduce the risk of cavitation, such as the application of enamel-strengthening agents before debonding, and the use of alternative finishing methods other than burs, should be carefully investigated for these teeth. The results of this in vitro study should be confirmed in clinical studies. Therefore, it is important to use evidence-based decision-making processes in clinical practice and to assess each patient’s situation on a case-by-case basis.
