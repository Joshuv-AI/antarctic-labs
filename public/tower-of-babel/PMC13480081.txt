ScEnsemble: weighted hypergraph ensemble clustering for single-cell RNA sequencing
Authors: Beste Uncu, Idil Yavuz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSingle-cell RNA sequencing enables detailed profiling of cellular heterogeneity, with clustering serving as a critical step for identifying distinct cell populations. However, no single clustering algorithm consistently outperforms others across diverse datasets, creating uncertainty in robust cell population identification. Existing ensemble methods either treat all algorithms equally or employ simple filtering strategies, failing to account for varying solution quality across different datasets.ResultsWe present ScEnsemble, a weighted hypergraph ensemble clustering framework that integrates multiple base algorithms through quality-based weighting. ScEnsemble constructs a hypergraph where edges represent cluster co-assignments, weighted by internal validation indices including Silhouette coefficient, Calinski-Harabasz index, Davies-Bouldin index, and Dunn index. Multiple consensus algorithms partition the weighted hypergraph to produce final clusters, including CSPA variants with hierarchical clustering and community detection methods, MCLA with multiple consensus strategies, and hypergraph spectral clustering. Benchmarking across five scRNA-seq datasets demonstrates that the best ensemble configuration matched or exceeded the best individual algorithm in 23 of 25 metric–dataset combinations (92%). Quality-based weighting further improved upon unweighted ensembles in 21 of 25 combinations (84%). Biological validation on a breast cancer tumor microenvironment dataset demonstrated that ScEnsemble clusters correspond to known cell types as confirmed by independent gene-set scoring.ConclusionsScEnsemble provides a principled solution for scRNA-seq clustering that leverages algorithmic diversity rather than forcing selection of a single method. The framework enables researchers to optimize for either mathematical cluster quality or biological interpretability based on their analytical priorities, addressing a fundamental challenge in single-cell data analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12859-026-06525-w.Keywords: Single-cell RNA sequencing, Ensemble clustering, Weighted hypergraph partitioning, Quality-based weighting, Multi-metric optimization

Background

Single-cell RNA sequencing has revolutionized our understanding of cellular heterogeneity, enabling researchers to profile gene expression at unprecedented resolution [1, 2]. Unlike bulk RNA sequencing, which averages gene expression across populations of cells, scRNA-seq reveals the transcriptomic landscape of individual cells, providing critical insights into cell type identification, developmental trajectories, and cellular responses to various stimuli [3, 4]. The rapid advancement of scRNA-seq technologies has generated massive datasets containing expression profiles for thousands to millions of cells. However, this wealth of data presents significant computational challenges, particularly in identifying distinct cell populations within heterogeneous samples [5]. The inherent complexity of scRNA-seq data, characterized by high dimensionality, dropout events, technical noise, and sparse expression patterns, necessitates sophisticated analytical approaches that can effectively navigate these challenges while preserving biological signal [6, 7].

Clustering analysis has emerged as a fundamental step in scRNA-seq data processing pipelines, serving as the foundation for downstream analyses including cell type annotation, trajectory inference, and differential expression analysis [8]. The primary objective is to group cells with similar transcriptomic profiles into biologically meaningful clusters corresponding to distinct cell types, developmental stages, or functional states. Numerous algorithms have been developed, each employing distinct mathematical frameworks. Graph-based methods construct cell-cell similarity networks and apply community detection algorithms; Seurat implements k-nearest neighbor graphs with Jaccard similarity refinement combined with Louvain or Leiden clustering [9, 10], demonstrating scalability to millions of cells through modularity optimization [11, 12]. Recent graph-based advances incorporate deep learning, with scGNN introducing graph neural network frameworks modeling heterogeneous gene expression patterns [13], and multi-view approaches like scMCGF integrating multiple graph representations to capture diverse cellular relationships [14]. Recent advances also focus on integrating multi-omics data using deep learning architectures to improve clustering resolution, as seen in models like scMultiNet [15]. Notably, hypergraph-based representations have emerged to address limitations of traditional pairwise networks by preserving higher-order information [16]. Hierarchical approaches organize cells into tree-like structures revealing multi-resolution relationships; CIDR addresses dropout events through Epanechnikov kernel-based imputation followed by principal coordinates analysis and hierarchical clustering [17], while SC3 employs consensus clustering across multiple transformations and parameters to achieve robust identification [18]. BackSPIN implements divisive hierarchical clustering iteratively partitioning data along principal components [19], and DendroSplit utilizes feature selection-based hierarchical clustering with modified Welch t-tests to determine optimal splitting points [20]. Contemporary hierarchical developments include self-supervised deep clustering for rare cell detection [21]. Partitioning methods divide cells by optimizing specific objective functions; SIMLR learns similarity matrices using multiple kernels followed by dimensionality reduction and k-means clustering [22], while specialized methods like pcaReduce integrate PCA with iterative k-means to create hierarchical structures [23], and RaceID combines k-medoids clustering with outlier detection mechanisms employing noise models to distinguish biological heterogeneity from technical artifacts [24]. Advanced partitioning algorithms like scAIDE facilitate rare cell type identification on global population scales [25]. Model-based approaches assume data originate from probability distribution mixtures; BISCUIT implements hierarchical Bayesian models simultaneously performing normalization, imputation, and clustering [26], TSCAN combines Gaussian mixture models with minimum spanning trees for cluster identification and pseudotemporal ordering [27], and Monocle integrates density-based clustering with trajectory inference through reverse graph embedding [28, 29]. Population-scale Bayesian models like BAMM-SC address droplet-based data from multiple individuals [30], while deep learning integration exemplified by scGMAI employs autoencoder-based imputation with Gaussian mixture models [31], and model-based deep embedding methods incorporate domain knowledge for constrained clustering [32]. Density-based methods identify high-density regions without geometric assumptions; GiniClust utilizes the Gini index focusing on highly variable genes and applies DBSCAN for rare cell detection [33], DensityCut estimates densities using directed k-nearest neighbor graphs with random walk refinement [34], and Galapagos provides straightforward workflows performing clustering directly on t-SNE embeddings [35].

Despite this algorithmic sophistication, comprehensive benchmarking studies demonstrate that no single method consistently outperforms others across diverse experimental conditions and dataset characteristics [36, 37]. Different methods optimize distinct aspects of cluster quality, with systematic evaluations showing substantial variation in cell type estimates and cluster assignments across methods [38, 39]. This algorithmic diversity reflects fundamental differences in mathematical frameworks rather than inadequacy of individual approaches, creating a persistent methodological challenge for robust cell population identification.

Ensemble clustering approaches have emerged to address these limitations by integrating multiple algorithms. Early hypergraph-based methods like SAFE-clustering pioneered hypergraph partitioning for scRNA-seq data, integrating solutions from multiple base methods [40], while mixture model approaches like SAME-clustering employ expectation-maximization procedures to integrate algorithms and selectively filter poor performers based on Adjusted Rand Index [41]. Memory-efficient strategies exemplified by SHARP utilize random projection with weighted meta-clustering [42], and consensus approaches like conCluster combine t-SNE-based dimensionality reduction with k-means across varying parameters [43]. Recent developments emphasize sophisticated combination strategies; feature selection-based ensemble methods like scEFSC demonstrate that strategic preprocessing enhances performance on high-dimensional data [44], and multi-scale frameworks like scMSCF combine multi-dimensional PCA with weighted metaclustering to handle multi-resolution cellular heterogeneity [45]. However, these approaches share fundamental limitations: most treat all base algorithms equally or employ simple binary filtering, failing to systematically weight algorithms based on comprehensive quality assessment.

We present ScEnsemble, a weighted hypergraph ensemble clustering framework addressing these limitations through three key innovations (Fig. 1). (i) We develop a hypergraph representation where edges are scaled based on a comprehensive quality assessment, ensuring that high-performing algorithms contribute substantially while poor performers are minimized. (ii) We implement a multi-criteria internal validation framework integrating four complementary metrics (Silhouette coefficient, Calinski-Harabasz, Davies-Bouldin, and Dunn indices). These metrics capture cluster compactness, separation, and connectivity to establish both individual and combined (averaged) weighting schemes. (iii) We integrate diverse consensus approaches spanning hierarchical clustering, community detection, meta-clustering, and hypergraph spectral methods enabling researchers to optimize for either mathematical cluster quality or biological interpretability based on their analytical priorities.

Overview of the ScEnsemble methodology. Single-cell RNA-seq data is processed by base clustering algorithms (SC3, CIDR, Seurat, SIMLR, Monocle, RaceID). Quality metrics (Silhouette, Calinski-Harabasz, Davies-Bouldin, Dunn) are computed for each partition and used to construct a weighted hypergraph. Different weighting schemes (Standard, Average, CH, Silhouette, DB) determine edge weights. Consensus methods (CSPA-HC, CSPA-Leiden, CSPA-Louvain, MCLA, MCLA-Leiden, MCLA-Louvain, HGSC) integrate the weighted partitions to generate the final consensus clustering

Methods

Datasets

We evaluated ScEnsemble using five publicly available benchmark scRNA-seq datasets that represent diverse biological contexts and sequencing technologies. All datasets were retrieved using the scRNAseq Bioconductor package[46], ensuring reproducible data access and consistent preprocessing across experiments. The datasets include Pollen [47], Kolodziejczyk [48], Muraro [49], Tasic [50], and Zeisel [19]. These datasets vary in size, complexity, and biological context, providing a comprehensive evaluation framework for clustering methods.

Base clustering algorithms

We employed six state-of-the-art scRNA-seq clustering algorithms as base methods: SC3, CIDR, Seurat, SIMLR, Monocle, and RaceID. These methods were selected based on their widespread adoption in the community and their complementary algorithmic approaches (graph-based, hierarchical, model-based, and density-based clustering paradigms). Each algorithm was run with parameters as recommended by the original authors, however, the number of clusters was estimated automatically in each algorithm for fair comparison. Specific implementation details: SC3 (version 1.36.0, Bioconductor package 3.21), CIDR (version 0.1.5), Seurat (version 5.3.1, resolution parameter optimized via silhouette score), SIMLR (version 1.34.0), Monocle (version 1.4.25), and RaceID (version 0.3.9).

Data preparation and algorithm configuration

Each base clustering algorithm received the raw count matrix as input and applied its own internal preprocessing pipeline, consistent with its original implementation and the recommendations of its authors. This design preserves the methodological integrity of each algorithm: imposing a common external transformation prior to clustering would override the preprocessing assumptions embedded in each method and potentially distort the structure it was designed to recover.

Internal validity indices (Silhouette, Calinski-Harabasz, Davies-Bouldin, Dunn) were subsequently computed within the native output representation of each algorithm using Euclidean distance, following a model-intrinsic evaluation strategy. SC3 produces a consensus probability matrix across subsampled runs; CIDR derives a dropout-corrected dissimilarity matrix via its scDissim procedure; Seurat embeds cells in the top ten principal components of the scaled, variable-feature expression matrix; SIMLR learns a multi-kernel similarity matrix through its iterative optimisation; Monocle retains the top fifty principal components computed during preprocess_cds; and RaceID constructs a Pearson correlation-based distance matrix via compdist. Validity indices are computed within each of these representations so that each algorithm is assessed on how coherently it partitioned the data within the geometric structure it was designed to exploit, rather than within an externally imposed projection. Applying a single common reference space would systematically disadvantage algorithms whose clustering objectives are defined over nonlinear manifolds or kernel-induced similarity spaces, constituting a representational bias that would render the resulting weights unfair across methods.

Raw metric values obtained from these heterogeneous spaces are normalised to a common scale prior to hypergraph weighting. This normalisation pipeline ensures that scale differences arising from different geometric spaces do not introduce systematic bias in the final weighting.

Algorithm-specific configurations were as follows. CIDR cluster number was selected by evaluating and retaining the value that maximised the mean Silhouette coefficient computed on CIDR’s own dissimilarity matrix. Seurat community detection was performed via the Louvain algorithm at resolution 0.7, with cluster number emerging from graph modularity optimisation. SIMLR was run with internal feature normalisation enabled (normalize = TRUE), and cluster number was estimated using SIMLR_Estimate_Number_of_Clusters over [22]. Monocle was preprocessed with num_dim = 50 principal components followed by Leiden community detection [28]. RaceID applied quality filtering (mintotal = 1000, minexpr = 5, minnumber = 1), Pearson-based distance computation, and k-medoids clustering with clustnr = 30 candidate values and bootnr = 50 bootstrap iterations [24]. Prior to hypergraph construction, any partition yielding fewer than two distinct clusters was excluded to prevent undefined metric values.

External and internal validation metrics and normalization framework

A critical contribution of this work lies in the systematic integration of multiple internal clustering validation metrics to weight the contribution of each base clustering algorithm. We employ four complementary metrics: Silhouette coefficient, Calinski-Harabasz index, Davies-Bouldin index, and Dunn index. Since these metrics operate on different scales and optimization directions, we develop a comprehensive normalization framework to enable fair comparison and integration. We assessed clustering performance using both external and internal validation metrics. External validation quantifies agreement between predicted clusters and known cell type labels, while internal validation evaluates cluster quality based solely on data structure without requiring ground truth annotations.

Algorithm integration and evaluation framework

Our comprehensive ensemble clustering framework integrates three complementary strategies, each tailored to different structural assumptions and clustering characteristics. W-CSPA is particularly effective for approximately spherical cluster structures and scenarios where interpretable pairwise sample similarities are essential. W-MCLA is better suited for capturing overlapping or heterogeneous cluster configurations, as it operates at the hyperedge level and enables detailed meta-cluster analysis. Finally, W-HGSC is designed to handle complex, non-convex cluster shapes by operating directly on hypergraph-derived similarity structures, making it especially advantageous when preserving the native topology of the hypergraph is critical for accurate consensus formation.

All methods benefit from our weighted hypergraph construction framework, ensuring that reliable clustering solutions dominate the consensus generation process while poor-performing algorithms contribute minimally to the final result. This systematic integration of internal validation metrics provides a principled mechanism for automated algorithm selection and adaptive ensemble weighting.

The performance of our weighted hypergraph ensemble methods is evaluated primarily using internal validation metrics for both hyperparameter optimization and weighting scheme determination. The comprehensive evaluation framework ensures robust assessment of the proposed methodology across diverse clustering scenarios and datasets.

All algorithms are implemented with systematic hyperparameter optimization, including automatic cluster number selection for hierarchical methods and resolution parameter tuning for community detection approaches. The integration of multiple internal validation metrics in the weighting scheme provides a principled approach to ensemble clustering that adapts to the intrinsic characteristics of different clustering algorithms and datasets. Results

We evaluated ScEnsemble across five benchmark scRNA-seq datasets representing diverse biological contexts: Pollen neural progenitor cells (301 cells, 5 cell types), Kolodziejczyk embryonic stem cells (704 cells, 3 cell types), Muraro pancreatic islets (3,072 cells, 11 cell types), Tasic adult mouse cortex (1,809 cells, 8 cell types), and Zeisel mouse brain (3,005 cells, 7 cell types). The evaluation employed both external validation using the Adjusted Rand Index to measure agreement with known cell type annotations and internal validation metrics including Silhouette coefficient, Calinski-Harabasz index, Davies-Bouldin index, and Dunn index to assess cluster quality independent of ground truth labels. Complete performance results for all 175 configurations are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S5.

Internal validation metrics revealed a markedly different performance landscape. This divergence between external and internal validation performance underscores a fundamental challenge in scRNA-seq clustering: algorithms optimising mathematical cluster properties do not necessarily produce partitions that best match biological annotations, and vice versa. The systematic variation in optimal algorithm choice across datasets and metrics motivated our ensemble approach, which leverages this algorithmic diversity through quality-based weighting.

For biological concordance, ensemble methods achieved the highest ARI scores in four of five datasets (Supplementary Table S6). The most notable gain occurred on the Muraro dataset, where MCLA-Louvain with DB weighting achieved an ARI of 0.936 compared to 0.920 for Monocle, a 1.7% improvement. On the Tasic dataset, CSPA-Leiden with average weighting matched the best individual algorithm, achieving an ARI of 0.904 compared to 0.902 for CIDR. On Pollen, CSPA-Louvain achieved 0.723 compared to 0.718 for Monocle, a 0.7% improvement. The Zeisel dataset represented the single case where Monocle marginally outperformed ensemble approaches, with an ARI of 0.879 versus 0.874 for CSPA-Leiden, a difference of only 0.6%. Weighted configurations further matched or exceeded unweighted configurations on ARI in four of five datasets, confirming that quality-based weighting is beneficial even for external validation (Supplementary Table S7).

Internal validation metrics revealed more pronounced advantages. On Pollen, CSPA-HC with CH weighting achieved a Silhouette coefficient of 0.917 compared to 0.652 for SC3 (40.7% improvement), a Calinski-Harabasz index of 5,852.5 versus 689.7 (748.6% increase), and a Dunn index of 2.689 versus 0.208for SIMLR (1,192.6% improvement). The Kolodziejczyk dataset demonstrated a notable convergence phenomenon: multiple ensemble configurations including CSPA-HC, CSPA-Leiden, CSPA-Louvain, MCLA, and MCLA-Leiden all reached an identical optimal clustering with Silhouette coefficient of 0.998, Davies-Bouldin index of 0.002, and Dunn index of 206.611. This convergence reflects the unambiguous biological structure of embryonic stem cell states, where quality-based weighting guides diverse algorithmic paths toward the same globally optimal partition. Across all datasets, weighted configurations outperformed their unweighted counterparts in 21 of 25 combinations (84%), confirming that quality-based weighting represents a robust and broadly effective enhancement (Supplementary Table S7).

Integrative analysis of weighting strategies and ensemble architectures

The choice of weighting scheme influenced optimisation outcomes, with different schemes favouring distinct aspects of cluster quality (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S7).

CH-based weighting was the most frequently selected scheme for internal validation optimisation, achieving the best weighted result in 13 of 25 metric–dataset combinations. On Kolodziejczyk, CH weighting produced a Calinski-Harabasz index of 59,900,781 compared to 2,252 for the best unweighted configuration, while on Pollen, the Silhouette coefficient improved from 0.577 (best unweighted) to 0.917 (+58.9%) and the Dunn index from 0.333 to 2.689 (+706.8%). DB-based weighting was also effective, achieving the best Silhouette score on three of five datasets (Muraro: 0.972, Tasic: 0.764, Zeisel: 0.674). These improvements suggest an alignment between the CH criterion, which favours high between-cluster variance relative to within-cluster variance, and the ensemble integration goal of identifying structurally coherent partitions.

On the Muraro dataset, DB weighting achieved the best ARI (0.936) via MCLA-Louvain, while CH weighting produced the best Davies-Bouldin index (0.319) through CSPA-HC. On Pollen, DB-weighted CSPA-Leiden achieved an ARI of 0.719, closely trailing the best configuration (CSPA-Louvain, standard, 0.723), indicating that different weighting schemes can yield comparable external concordance.

Average weighting, which combines all four internal metrics, achieved competitive biological concordance on datasets with complex cellular hierarchies. On the Tasic dataset, featuring hierarchically organised cortical neuronal subtypes, average-weighted CSPA-Leiden achieved an ARI of 0.904, comparable to the best individual algorithm (CIDR, 0.902). On Kolodziejczyk, average-weighted CSPA-Leiden produced the best ensemble ARI (0.838), slightly exceeding Monocle (0.834). These results indicate that balanced multi-metric optimisation can be effective for datasets where no single geometric criterion fully captures the underlying cluster structure.

Among ensemble architectures, CSPA variants showed consistent performance across datasets. MCLA variants performed well on biologically complex datasets, with MCLA-Louvain distinguishing alpha, beta, delta, and gamma pancreatic cell subtypes on Muraro (ARI = 0.936). The meta-clustering strategy, which operates on cluster prototypes rather than individual cells, appears suited to datasets where cell types exhibit gradual transcriptional transitions. W-HGSC achieved competitive Davies-Bouldin indices on Tasic (0.596 vs. 0.795 for SC3, + 25.0%) and Zeisel (0.874 vs. 1.074 for Seurat, + 18.6%), suggesting that spectral decomposition of the hypergraph Laplacian can offer advantages for datasets with complex community structure.

These results suggest two complementary principles for weighting scheme selection. CH-based weighting tends to maximise geometric cluster quality and may be preferred when cluster structure optimisation is the primary goal, while average or DB weighting can be more effective when biological concordance with known cell type annotations is the objective, particularly for datasets with hierarchical cellular organisation. Biological validation on a complex tumor microenvironment dataset

ScEnsemble weights base clusterers using internal validity indices, including the Calinski-Harabasz, Davies-Bouldin, and silhouette criteria, which measure geometric properties of a partition without requiring ground-truth labels. To examine whether optimizing for these internal criteria produces biologically interpretable structure, we applied ScEnsemble to a real-world dataset and assessed cluster identity using independent gene-set scoring that relies on neither internal indices nor ground-truth annotations. We used the Wu et al. (2021) breast cancer scRNA-seq dataset [63], which profiles the tumor microenvironment (TME) across 28 annotated cell type categories. The full dataset exceeds 100,000 cells, which is beyond the practical scale of several base algorithms in ScEnsemble. We therefore constructed a stratified subsample of n = 2,000 cells by sampling each cell type proportionally to its abundance in the full dataset, with a minimum of 10 cells per type to retain all 28 categories. This design preserves the cell type composition of the original dataset and does not introduce selection bias toward any particular clustering outcome; subsampling of this scale is standard practice in single-cell clustering benchmarks [59]. The CSPA-HC variant with CH weighting, which achieved the highest ARI across the benchmark datasets, was used for all downstream analyses.

The ScEnsemble UMAP embedding closely mirrors the ground-truth cell type structure: immune populations, stromal cells, and epithelial clusters occupy distinct regions of the embedding, consistent with the annotated celltype_major labels (Fig. 4, Panel A). To evaluate biological coherence independently of the ground-truth annotation, we applied AUCell gene-set scoring [64] using curated marker gene signatures for 15 TME cell type categories. AUCell ranks genes by expression within each cell and computes an enrichment score for a given gene set without consulting either the clustering result or the cell type labels. Aggregating AUCell scores per predicted cluster shows that each ScEnsemble cluster selectively enriches for a single dominant TME signature: clusters containing predominantly immune cells scored highest on T cell (CD3D, CD8A), B cell (CD19, MS4A1), and NK cell (GNLY, NKG7) gene sets; stromal clusters scored highest on CAF (FAP, COL1A1) and endothelial (PECAM1, VWF) signatures; and epithelial clusters were distinguished by Cancer Epithelial (EPCAM, KRT8) and Normal Epithelial (KRT14, KRT5) scores (Fig. 4, Panel B). The one-to-one correspondence between predicted clusters and dominant AUCell signatures was obtained without any reference to ground-truth labels at any step.

B AUCell gene-set enrichment heatmap showing the dominant TME signature per predicted cluster. At the individual gene level, expression of two canonical markers per cell type across predicted clusters confirms the same pattern: marker genes are concentrated in the clusters that score highest for the corresponding AUCell signature, and show low expression in unrelated clusters (Fig. 4, Panel C). The marker enrichment is consistent across all 15 cell type categories represented in the subsample.

The CH-weighted CSPA-HC variant, which yielded the highest Silhouette coefficient and Calinski-Harabasz scores on Pollen (Sil = 0.917, CH = 5,852.5) and Kolodziejczyk (Sil = 0.998, CH = 59,900,781) among the benchmark datasets, also produced the clearest TME cell type separation in the Wu dataset. This parallel outcome indicates that maximizing within-cluster compactness relative to between-cluster separation, as captured by the CH index, is a practical criterion for selecting base clusterers in the absence of ground-truth labels, and that doing so yields partitions with interpretable biological structure.

Comparison to existing ensemble approaches

To evaluate the performance of ScEnsemble against established single-cell clustering methods, we conducted a comprehensive benchmark across the five aforementioned scRNA-seq datasets. For each dataset, 2,000 Highly Variable Genes (HVGs) were selected to optimize biological signal detection. ScEnsemble was compared against SHARP and SAME, two representative ensemble and consensus clustering methods. Performance was assessed using three complementary metrics: ARI, NMI, and Silhouette.

ScEnsemble demonstrated consistently strong performance across the majority of datasets. On the Kolod dataset, multiple ScEnsemble variants achieved similar scores (ARI = NMI = 1.000), outperforming SHARP (ARI = 0.697) and SAME (ARI = 0.723). Notably, in the Kolod dataset, the standard and silhouette weightings in the CSPA_Louvain variant, the std, sil, and avg weightings in the CSPA_Leiden variant, and the std, sil, and avg weightings in the HGSC variant simultaneously yielded the best results across ARI, NMI, and Silhouette evaluation indices (Table 4).

Discussion

Single-cell RNA sequencing enables researchers to study gene expression at single-cell resolution, but choosing the right clustering algorithm remains a practical challenge. With a growing number of clustering methods available for scRNA-seq data [65], selecting an appropriate approach requires careful consideration. Our weighted hypergraph ensemble framework offers a structured approach by combining multiple algorithms through quality-based weighting, rather than requiring researchers to select a single method.

ScEnsemble introduces two complementary design choices. First, it integrates a multi-criteria internal validation framework directly into the hypergraph edge weighting process, so that base partitions with higher structural quality contribute proportionally more to the consensus. Second, it diversifies the consensus partitioning step by applying hierarchical clustering, Louvain, and Leiden community detection on the similarity and meta-cluster matrices derived from the quality-weighted hypergraph, ensuring that both the edge weights and the partitioning strategy reflect clustering quality. Together, these choices enable a systematic examination of architecture–weighting interactions.

These design choices address limitations shared by current scRNA-seq ensemble methods. In our direct benchmark (Sect. 4.5), SAME-clustering [41], which selectively filters algorithms via binary ARI thresholding, and SHARP [42], which employs weighted meta-clustering based on random projection features, were both outperformed by ScEnsemble on four of five datasets. ScEnsemble addresses the gap left by these approaches by providing continuous quality-based weighting and multiple consensus architectures within a single framework.

Our results illustrate a systematic trade-off between biological concordance and geometric cluster quality. Monocle achieved the highest ARI on four of five datasets, yet SC3 and CIDR consistently produced better-separated clusters by internal metrics. This is consistent with the benchmark by Duò et al. [36], who found that no single method dominated across all conditions and that pairwise ensemble combinations often produced results between the better and worse individual methods. ScEnsemble mitigates this averaging effect: across the 25 metric–dataset combinations (after excluding degenerate configurations), the best ensemble configuration matched or exceeded the best individual algorithm in 23 cases (92%; Supplementary Table S6). Quality-based weighting further improved upon unweighted ensembles in 21 of 25 combinations (84%; Supplementary Table S7).

The weighting scheme determines what the ensemble optimises, and our results indicate that this choice can be aligned with specific analytical priorities. CH-based weighting was the most frequently optimal scheme for internal validation, improving Silhouette coefficients by up to 58.9% (from 0.577 to 0.917), Dunn indices by up to 706.8% (from 0.333 to 2.689), and Davies-Bouldin indices by up to 56.3% (from 0.731 to 0.319) over unweighted baselines (Supplementary Table S7). Average weighting, which integrates all four internal metrics simultaneously, achieved competitive biological concordance on datasets with hierarchically organised cellular populations. On the Tasic cortical neuron dataset, average-weighted CSPA-Leiden achieved an ARI of 0.904, comparable to the best individual algorithm (CIDR, 0.902). Average weighting similarly produced the best ensemble ARI on Kolodziejczyk (0.838 vs. 0.834 for Monocle). These observations are consistent with the view that integrating complementary geometric criteria can be a useful strategy for datasets with complex cellular organisation, and align with a practical need identified in recent reviews [66]: a single consensus strategy may not serve all analytical purposes equally well.

Dataset characteristics influenced which configuration performed best. The Kolodziejczyk embryonic stem cell dataset exhibited a convergence phenomenon: five architectures independently reached an identical optimal partition with a Silhouette coefficient of 0.998 and a Dunn index of 206.611 under CH weighting. This convergence suggests that when the underlying biological structure is well-separated, quality-guided weighting can guide diverse algorithmic paths toward the same solution. The Muraro pancreatic dataset, where cell types share transcriptional programmes while maintaining distinct identities, performed best with DB-weighted MCLA-Louvain (ARI = 0.936), suggesting that prototype-based consensus may be suited to datasets with overlapping transcriptional signatures.

A related concern is the potential divergence between internal validation metrics and biological correctness. As our results show, Monocle achieved the highest ARI on four of five datasets, yet SC3 and CIDR dominated internal metrics. The quality-based weighting mechanism therefore optimises geometric compactness, which does not always coincide with the biologically most meaningful partition. ScEnsemble mitigates this through averaged weighting, which balances multiple geometric criteria, and through diversified consensus architectures. Importantly, however, our biological validation analyses on both the Wu tumor microenvironment and Muraro pancreatic datasets demonstrate that even when geometric optimization diverges from raw label agreement, the resulting partitions still preserve robust biological meaningfulness. The clusters recovered by ScEnsemble consistently align with distinct functional marker gene signatures, confirming that the structural compactness optimized by internal metrics translates into biologically coherent subpopulations. Nevertheless, users should be aware that internal-metric-guided weighting may not be optimal for all biological questions.

Several additional limitations should be acknowledged. The weighting approach assigns quality scores based on the full dataset partition and does not adapt locally: an algorithm performing well overall but poorly on a specific cell subpopulation will receive a uniformly high weight, potentially biasing the consensus for that subpopulation. Our failure-case analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2) confirms that populations representing less than 10% of the dataset may experience reduced F1 scores compared to specialised base algorithms; iterative or region-adaptive weighting strategies could address this in future work. Additionally, ScEnsemble produces deterministic hard partitions without per-cell confidence scores; soft membership outputs indicating assignment uncertainty would be informative for cells near cluster boundaries. Evaluation was conducted on five benchmark datasets with well-defined cell type labels; extension to spatial transcriptomics and multi-modal single-cell assays, where ground truth is less well-defined, remains an important direction.

Regarding computational scalability, all experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with a 12th Gen Intel Core i7-12650H processor (2.30 GHz) and 32 GB RAM running Windows 1164-bit. A systematic profiling using subsamples of (500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 3000) cells from a breast cancer scRNA-seq dataset confirmed that the ScEnsemble consensus step accounts for a minor fraction of total runtime relative to the base clustering algorithms (Supplementary Fig. S1). The computational cost is dominated by the base algorithms, particularly SIMLR and SC3, rather than the hypergraph construction and partitioning steps.

In summary, ScEnsemble provides principled control over what the ensemble optimises through quality-proportional weighting and diversified consensus partitioning, offering a practical tool for single-cell clustering applications.

Conclusions

Clustering is a cornerstone of single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, yet the persistent diversity of methods reflects a genuine truth: no single algorithm captures all aspects of biological heterogeneity equally well. ScEnsemble transforms this diversity from a source of uncertainty into a methodological advantage by integrating multiple clustering algorithms through quality-proportional weighting, ensuring that the ensemble is guided by the most informative partitions rather than treating all contributions as equal. The framework allows researchers to select a weighting scheme that aligns with their specific biological question, whether prioritising geometric cluster quality or biological concordance. This addresses a practical limitation of existing ensemble approaches and provides a principled basis for method selection in varied analytical contexts. The systematic characterisation of interactions between weighting scheme and consensus architecture offers concrete guidance for single-cell clustering practice. As the scale and complexity of single-cell data continue to grow, approaches that combine algorithmic diversity with principled integration will be essential for producing robust, reproducible, and biologically meaningful cell type discoveries.
