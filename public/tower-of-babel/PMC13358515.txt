Impact of prior malignancy on non-small cell lung cancer presentation, management, and survival
Authors: Gabriella R. Rasmussen, Eric Klipsch, Rupak Mukherjee, Zachary Matthews, Ian C. Bostock, Kathryn E. Engelhardt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs survival after an initial cancer diagnosis improves, an increasing number of patients are diagnosed with lung cancer after a prior malignancy. These patients represent a clinically distinct population, yet population-level data describing lung cancer presentation, treatment patterns, and outcomes in this group remain limited.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study using the National Cancer Database (NCDB), including adults diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) between 2010 and 2021. Patients whose lung cancer represented their first malignancy were compared with those diagnosed with lung cancer after a prior malignancy of any type. Outcomes of interest included tumor size and stage at diagnosis, treatment strategy, and overall survival (OS).ResultsAmong 1,304,953 patients with NSCLC, 266,458 (20.4%) had a history of prior malignancy. Compared with patients with first-primary NSCLC, those with prior malignancy were older at diagnosis and more frequently presented with smaller tumors (≤3 cm: 57.2% vs. 43.8%) and earlier-stage disease (stage 0–I: 42.2% vs. 27.7%; all P<0.001). Among surgically treated patients, sublobar resection was more common in patients with prior malignancy (30.5% vs. 20.4%), including for tumors ≤2 cm (43.3% vs. 31.6%). Despite earlier stage at presentation, prior malignancy was independently associated with worse OS on adjusted analysis [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) 1.07, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.06–1.08].ConclusionsPatients diagnosed with lung cancer after a prior malignancy present with earlier-stage disease and are more frequently managed with parenchyma-sparing surgical approaches, yet experience worse adjusted OS. These findings highlight meaningful differences in presentation and management in a growing population of cancer survivors.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In a national cohort of 1,304,953 patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), 20.4% were diagnosed with lung cancer after a prior malignancy. Compared with patients whose lung cancer represented their first malignancy, those with prior malignancy presented more frequently with smaller tumors and earlier-stage disease and were more likely to undergo parenchyma-sparing surgical approaches, including sublobar resection. Despite this earlier stage at presentation, prior malignancy remained independently associated with worse overall survival (OS) after adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors.

What is known and what is new?

• Patients who develop lung cancer after a prior malignancy represent a clinical scenario that is growing more common as survival across malignancies improves. Prior studies have largely focused on survival outcomes in patients with multiple cancers and have not comprehensively evaluated stage at presentation or treatment patterns.

• This study demonstrates that patients with prior malignancy present with earlier-stage NSCLC and are more frequently treated with sublobar resection, yet experience worse adjusted OS, highlighting important differences in both presentation and management within this population.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest that tumor stage alone may not fully capture prognosis in patients with lung cancer after a prior malignancy. Competing mortality and the physiologic consequences of prior cancer treatment likely influence outcomes and treatment selection. As the population of cancer survivors continues to grow, treatment decisions and prognostic counseling for patients with subsequent lung cancers should incorporate patient-level factors such as comorbidity burden and prior treatment exposure in addition to tumor characteristics.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide and accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer diagnoses. Five-year survival is approximately 28% overall but varies substantially by stage. Patients with localized disease have survival approaching 65%, whereas those with distant metastases have survival near 9%. Because outcomes decline sharply once disease extends beyond the lung, stage at diagnosis is a primary determinant of prognosis and treatment.

Advances in surgery, systemic therapy, targeted agents, and immunotherapy have improved survival across many malignancies, including NSCLC. As survival improves, the number of cancer survivors continues to increase. A growing proportion of patients are subsequently diagnosed with a new primary malignancy, including lung cancer (1). Patients who develop lung cancer after a prior malignancy differ from those with first-primary NSCLC. They may have reduced physiologic reserve from prior surgery, radiation, or systemic therapy. They often have higher comorbidity burden and face competing risks of mortality. These factors influence treatment selection and survival.

Most prior studies examining patients with multiple malignancies have focused on survival and are frequently limited to institutional series. National-level data describing how lung cancer presents in patients with prior malignancy, how management differs, and how outcomes compare with first-primary NSCLC remain limited. It is unclear whether differences in stage at diagnosis and surgical approach reflect treatment era effects, physiologic constraints related to prior therapy, or increasing use of sublobar resection.

To address this gap, we analyzed patients diagnosed with NSCLC between 2010 and 2021 using the National Cancer Database (NCDB) (2,3). We compared patients whose lung cancer occurred after a prior malignancy of any type with those whose lung cancer represented their first malignancy. Our objective was to evaluate differences in stage at diagnosis, treatment patterns, and overall survival (OS) in this population. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0639/rc).

Methods

We performed a retrospective cohort study using the NCDB, a nationwide hospital-based registry sponsored by the American College of Surgeons and the American Cancer Society (2,3). The NCDB captures approximately 70% of newly diagnosed cancers in the United States (2). We included patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2021. The dataset is de-identified. This retrospective analysis is subject to selection bias and unmeasured confounding inherent to registry-based studies. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Adult patients (≥18 years) with histologically confirmed NSCLC were identified using NCDB histology codes for adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, and NSCLC not otherwise specified. Missing data were handled as recorded in the NCDB and included in analyses where applicable. Patients with missing diagnosis date, vital status, or tumor size were excluded. A total of 1,304,953 patients met inclusion criteria for the analysis. This retrospective analysis is subject to selection bias and unmeasured confounding that is inherent to registry-based studies.

Prior malignancy status was defined using the NCDB “sequence number” variable. A value of 0 indicated NSCLC was the only malignancy. A value of 1 indicated NSCLC was the first of multiple malignancies. A value of 2 indicated NSCLC occurred after one prior malignancy. Patients with sequence numbers greater than 2 were excluded to avoid additional heterogeneity from multiple prior cancers. The NCDB does not include information on the site, stage, or treatment of prior malignancies, and we could not determine whether the preceding cancer was pulmonary or extrathoracic. The sequence number variable does not reliably distinguish between a second primary lung cancer and recurrence when the prior malignancy was pulmonary.

Demographic variables included age at diagnosis, sex, race/ethnicity, and insurance status. Socioeconomic variables were derived from zip code-level data and included median household income, education level, urban or rural residence, and distance to treatment facility. Hospital characteristics included facility type, geographic region, and annual lung cancer volume, stratified into quartiles. Clinical variables included year of diagnosis, histologic subtype, tumor size (categorized by centimeter groups), clinical stage [American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition] (4), Charlson-Deyo comorbidity index (0 vs. ≥1) (5), and lymphovascular invasion. The Charlson-Deyo comorbidity index in the NCDB does not explicitly capture prior malignancy history or treatment-related effects.

Treatment variables included extent of resection (no surgery, sublobar resection, lobectomy, pneumonectomy), surgical approach (open or minimally invasive), and receipt of radiation, systemic therapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, or hormone therapy.

Statistical analysis

OS was defined as time from diagnosis to death or last contact and was censored at 120 months. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s Chi-squared test. Cramér’s V >0.10 was considered potentially meaningful. Trends in extent of resection were evaluated using the Jonckheere-Terpstra test. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier estimates with log-rank testing and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Analyses were performed in Stata version 19.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), with P<0.05 considered significant.

Results

A total of 1,304,953 patients were diagnosed with NSCLC between 2010 and 2021. Of these, 1,038,495 (79.6%) had NSCLC as their first malignancy and 266,458 (20.4%) were diagnosed with NSCLC after a prior malignancy.

Age differed between groups. Patients with prior malignancy were more likely to be older than 70 years (56.9% vs. 42.1%), whereas patients with first-primary NSCLC were more frequently 70 years or younger (57.9% vs. 43.1%) (P<0.001; Cramér’s V =0.138). Other demographic and socioeconomic variables, including sex, race, comorbidity score, insurance type, income quartile, education level, urban or rural residence, facility type, and facility volume, also differed between groups (all P<0.001), although the strength of association was weak (Cramér’s V <0.10 for each). There were no significant differences in operative approach (robotic, thoracoscopic, or open).

Tumor size and stage at diagnosis differed between groups. Patients with prior malignancy more frequently presented with tumors <3 cm (57.2% vs. 43.8%), whereas patients with first-primary NSCLC more often had tumors >3 cm (56.2% vs. 42.8%; P<0.001; Cramér’s V =0.126). Patients with prior malignancy were more likely to present with stage I disease (41.7% vs. 27.4%), while patients with first-primary NSCLC more frequently presented with stage III or IV disease (64.2% vs. 49.2%; P<0.001; Cramér’s V =0.132). Treatment patterns differed between groups (Table 2). Nonoperative management was more common among patients with first-primary NSCLC compared with those with prior malignancy (73.9% vs. 70.3%; P<0.001; Cramér’s V =0.064). Sublobar resection was more frequent in patients with prior malignancy (9.03% vs. 5.28%; P<0.001; Cramér’s V =0.064). Among tumors ≤2 cm, sublobar resection remained more common in the prior malignancy group (20.1% vs. 15.7%; P<0.001; Cramér’s V =0.081). When restricted to patients who underwent surgery, sublobar resection was more frequent in patients with prior malignancy (30.5% vs. 20.4%; P<0.001; Cramér’s V =0.101), including for tumors ≤2 cm (43.3% vs. 31.6%; P<0.001; Cramér’s V =0.109).

OS differed between groups. Patients with prior malignancy had lower survival compared with those with first-primary NSCLC (log-rank P<0.001; Figure 1). This difference persisted in analyses restricted to tumors ≤2 cm and stage I disease (both P<0.001). On unadjusted Cox regression, prior malignancy was associated with a lower hazard of death [hazard ratio (HR) 0.92; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.91–0.93]. After adjustment for age, sex, race, insurance, comorbidity, income, education, tumor stage, tumor size, and distance to facility, prior malignancy was associated with higher mortality (adjusted HR 1.07; 95% CI, 1.06–1.08; P<0.001). Stage IV disease (HR 5.09; 95% CI: 5.04–5.14), tumor size >7 cm (HR 1.81; 95% CI: 1.79–1.83), and Charlson-Deyo comorbidity score ≥1 (HR 1.24; 95% CI: 1.23–1.25) were among the strongest independent predictors of mortality (Table S1).

Discussion

Patients who develop lung cancer after a prior malignancy represent a substantial subgroup within thoracic oncology practice. In this national cohort, these patients were older and more frequently presented with smaller tumors and stage I disease compared with those whose lung cancer represented their first malignancy. Despite earlier stage at diagnosis, prior malignancy remained independently associated with worse OS, including among patients with tumors ≤2 cm and those with stage I disease.

Prior registry analyses have reported mixed survival patterns in patients with subsequent lung cancers. Verma and Alam, using NCDB data, demonstrated survival differences among patients with multiple malignancies but did not examine stage distribution or treatment selection (6). SEER-based analyses by Cassivi et al. similarly emphasized survival outcomes without detailed characterization of operative management (7). In contrast, our study evaluates stage at presentation, surgical approach, and adjusted survival within a large national cohort.

The reversal from an apparent survival advantage on unadjusted analysis to increased mortality after adjustment is central to interpreting these findings. Earlier stage likely drives the univariate association, whereas adjustment for age and comorbidity reveals the influence of competing mortality. The Charlson-Deyo comorbidity index did not differ meaningfully between groups, which may reflect limitations of the NCDB comorbidity variable, as it does not fully capture prior cancer history, treatment-related toxicity, or functional status. In this population, baseline health status exerts greater influence on OS than lung cancer stage alone. Stage shift does not fully offset the risks associated with prior malignancy. Earlier stage does not translate into better OS in this population.

Sublobar resection was more common in patients with prior malignancy. This pattern parallels increasing adoption of segmentectomy for small peripheral NSCLC following randomized data supporting its oncologic equivalence in selected patients (8,9). Prior institutional and multi-institutional series examining second primary lung cancers have also demonstrated acceptable oncologic outcomes following limited resection and repeat pulmonary surgery in carefully selected patients (10-12). However, greater use of parenchyma-sparing resection in this cohort may also reflect reduced physiologic reserve related to prior surgery, radiation, or systemic therapy (13). Because the NCDB does not include pulmonary function data, smoking history, or operative rationale, we cannot determine whether operative strategy was driven primarily by patient-level limitation or oncologic preference.

Differences in stage at presentation should be interpreted cautiously. The NCDB does not record the type, stage, or treatment of prior malignancies, nor does it capture how lung cancers were detected. When the prior malignancy was pulmonary, the sequence number variable cannot reliably distinguish a new primary from recurrence. Smoking exposure, pulmonary function, and detailed operative indications are not available and likely influence both management and survival. While some cases may represent recurrence rather than new primary disease, this limitation would be expected to bias results toward worse stage and survival rather than earlier stage at diagnosis (1).

This study carries the limitations inherent to registry-based analyses. The NCDB is hospital-based and may not reflect patients treated outside Commission on Cancer-accredited centers. Missing data may introduce bias. We evaluated OS rather than cancer-specific survival, and deaths from competing causes are likely more common in an older cohort with prior malignancy. This study does not evaluate lung cancer screening effectiveness; rather, it characterizes presentation, management, and outcomes in patients defined by prior malignancy history.

As survival across malignancies improves, more patients will present with lung cancer after prior treatment. Our findings demonstrate that these patients differ not only in stage at diagnosis but also in operative management and adjusted survival. Stage alone may be insufficient to guide operative decision-making in patients with prior malignancy. Risk stratification in this group should incorporate comorbidity burden, prior treatment exposure, and competing mortality when determining candidacy for resection and counseling regarding prognosis.

Conclusions

Patients who develop NSCLC after a prior malignancy represent a large and distinct clinical population. In this national cohort, these patients presented with smaller tumors and earlier-stage disease and more frequently underwent parenchyma-sparing resection. Despite this stage shift, prior malignancy remained independently associated with worse adjusted OS. These findings suggest that competing mortality and the physiologic impact of prior cancer treatment influence outcomes beyond tumor stage alone. As cancer survivorship increases, these factors should be incorporated into risk stratification and operative decision-making for patients with subsequent lung cancers.
