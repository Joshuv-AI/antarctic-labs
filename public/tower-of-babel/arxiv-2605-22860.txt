Every signed planar graph is 5 5 -choosable: A short proof and refinements
Pie Désiré EBODE ATANGANA 1, Maxwell NDOGNKON MANGA 2

Abstract
A signed graph is a pair ( G , σ ) (G,\sigma) in which G G is a finite simple graph and σ : E ⁡ ( G ) → { + 1 , − 1 } \sigma:E(G)\to\{+1,-1\} is a signature . Following Máčajová–Raspaud–Škoviera and Jin–Kang–Steffen, a proper coloring of ( G , σ ) (G,\sigma) is a map c : V ⁡ ( G ) → ℤ c:V(G)\to\mathbb{Z} with c ⁡ ( u ) ≠ σ ⁡ ( u ​ v ) ​ c ​ ( v ) c(u)\neq\sigma(uv)\,c(v) for every edge u ​ v uv , and ( G , σ ) (G,\sigma) is signed k k -choosable if such a coloring exists from any list assignment L L with | L ⁡ ( v ) | ≥ k |L(v)|\geq k . In a celebrated two-page note, Thomassen proved that every planar graph is 5 5 -choosable, and Jin, Kang, and Steffen subsequently extended this to signed planar graphs. Our principal contribution is a short, self-contained, and signature-blind proof of the latter: the inductive bookkeeping inserts one factor of σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) uniformly into every constraint, so that with σ ≡ + 1 \sigma\equiv+1 the argument reduces verbatim to Thomassen’s original. From the strengthened extension statement ( Theorem 1.4 ) we deduce the main result ( Theorem 1.3 : χ ℓ s ​ ( G , σ ) ≤ 5 \chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)\leq 5 for every planar signed graph), the Máčajová–Raspaud–Škoviera signed Five-Color Theorem in the symmetric palette N 2 = { − 2 , − 1 , 0 , 1 , 2 } N_{2}=\{-2,-1,0,1,2\} , the Switching Invariance Lemma, 3 3 -choosability of outerplanar signed graphs, 1 1 -defective signed 4 4 -choosability of planar signed graphs, a sandwich inequality relating χ ℓ s \chi_{\ell}^{s} to the unsigned and “positive” choice numbers, and a polynomial-time list-coloring algorithm. Voigt’s planar non- 4 4 -choosable graph and Mirzakhani’s smaller variant show the bound 5 5 is best possible. We close with examples illustrating that negative edges genuinely refine unsigned phenomena, a comparison table situating our work in the literature, and several open problems. Keywords: signed graph; list coloring; choosability; planar graph; near-triangulation; switching; Thomassen’s theorem. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 05C15; 05C22; 05C10.

Introduction

The chromatic number
\chia(G)

k
-coloring. The

[
3
,

\chia(G)\leq 4

for every planar graph; its weaker but elementary predecessor, the

, was already known to

[
17
]
. Standard references for this classical
material are
[
5
,
11
,
19
]
.

Building on this theory, one can ask what happens when colors are
not freely chosen but must come from prescribed lists. A

list assignment
for
G
is a map
L
associating to each

v\in V(G)
a finite set

of admissible colors. The graph

is
L
-colorable
if it admits a proper coloring

with

c(v)\in L(v)
for every

, and
k
-choosable
if it is

L
-colorable for every list assignment with
|L(v)|\geq k
. The
smallest such

, denoted
\chil(G)
, is the
list chromatic
number
(or
choice number
). List coloring superficially
resembles ordinary coloring, but
\chil(G)
can be strictly larger
than
\chia(G)
, so analogues of classical theorems do not come for
free.

The notion of choosability was introduced independently by
Vizing
[
44
]
and by Erdős, Rubin and
Taylor
[
15
]
. Both papers asked whether every planar graph
is
5
-choosable. Voigt
[
45
]
showed that planar graphs
need not even be
4
-choosable: she exhibited a planar graph on

238
vertices and an associated list assignment of size
4

admitting no proper coloring. A smaller witness on
63
vertices was
later found by Mirzakhani
[
29
]
. The matching upper
bound was established by Thomassen
[
41
]
, who proved
that every planar graph is
5
-choosable. Thomassen’s proof,
occupying less than a page, is now standard fare in graph theory
courses; it proceeds by induction on a strengthened statement
involving two adjacent precolored boundary vertices.

We turn now from ordinary graphs to signed graphs. A
signed
graph

(G,\sigma)
is a pair where
G
is a graph and

\sigma:E(G)\to\{+1,-1\}
is a

introduced by Harary
[
16
]
in the context of social

[
48
,
50
,
51
]
. Edges

and those with

\sigma(e)=-1
are
negative
; the
sign of a closed walk

is the product of the signs of its edges, and
(G,\sigma)
is

if every cycle is positive.
Switching
at

negates the sign of every edge with exactly one

, defining an equivalence relation on signatures; by
Zaslavsky’s theorem
[
48
]
,
\sigma
is balanced iff
it is switching-equivalent to the all-positive signature. Beyond
their original psychological motivation, signed graphs arise in the
geometry of root systems and
arrangements
[
48
,
50
]
, in flow
theory
[
6
]
, and in the chromatic theory developed by
Zaslavsky, Máčajová–Raspaud–Škoviera, and others; for
recent surveys see
[
31
,
36
]
.

Several non-equivalent proposals for the chromatic theory of signed
graphs appear in the literature, and the choice of framework matters
for a list-coloring statement. Zaslavsky’s original
definition
[
48
,
49
]
uses “signed colors”
from the set
\{0,\pm 1,\dots,\pm k\}
and treats the zero color
asymmetrically. Máčajová, Raspaud, and
Škoviera
[
28
]
reformulated the basic notion in a way
that more naturally generalizes the chromatic number and aligns
with the homomorphism viewpoint of Naserasr, Rollová, and
Sopena
[
30
]
. We adopt the definition that is
intrinsic to the integer arithmetic of the signature, formalized
as follows.

Definition 1.1

(Coloring of signed graphs)
.

A
coloring
of a signed graph
(G,\sigma)
is a map
c\colon V(G)\to\mathbb{Z}

with

c(u)\;\neq\;\sigma(uv)\,c(v)\qquad\text{for every edge }uv\in E(G).

Equivalently, on positive edges
uv
one requires
c(u)\neq c(v)
,
while on negative edges
uv
one requires
c(u)+c(v)\neq 0
.

Definition 1.2

(List coloring of signed graphs)
.

A
list assignment
for
(G,\sigma)
is a map
L\colon V(G)\to 2^{\mathbb{Z}}

assigning to each
v
a finite set
L(v)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
. An

L
-coloring
is a coloring (in the sense of

Definition

1.1
) with
c(v)\in L(v)
for every
v
. The signed
graph
(G,\sigma)
is
signed
k
-choosable
if it admits an

L
-coloring whenever
|L(v)|\geq k
for all
v
, and the

signed list chromatic number
(or
signed choice number
)
is

\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)\;:=\;\min\bigl\{k\in\mathbb{N}:(G,\sigma)\text{ is signed }k\text{-choosable}\bigr\}.

Two consistency checks are in order. First, when
\sigma\equiv+1
,
the constraint
c(u)\neq\sigma(uv)c(v)
becomes
c(u)\neq c(v)
, the
classical proper-coloring condition; hence
\chia(G,+1)=\chia(G)

and
\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,+1)=\chil(G)
, so the signed framework specializes
correctly to the unsigned one. Second, our framework is consistent
with the symmetric palette of Máčajová, Raspaud, and
Škoviera
[
28
]
: those authors restrict

to take
values in
N_{k}=\{-k,-k+1,\dots,k-1,k\}
and define the
(ordinary) signed chromatic number

to be the smallest

2k+1
admitting such a coloring, so that
\chia(G,\sigma)\leq 2\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)+1
.
We do not require lists to be symmetric (closed under negation), in
contrast with some authors. This is the convention employed
in
[
20
]
and is the most flexible from a
proof-theoretic standpoint, and the symmetric framework will be
recovered from our main result as a corollary
(
Corollary

5.2
).

We can now state the principal results. Voigt’s planar
non-
4
-choosable graph

Theorem 1.3

(Jin–Kang–Steffen
[
20
]
)
.

Every signed planar graph

\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)\leq 5
.

Our principal contribution is a short, self-contained proof of

Theorem

1.3
obtained by lifting Thomassen’s argument almost
verbatim to the signed setting. The lift relies on the following
strengthening, which is the technical heart of the paper.

Theorem 1.4

(Main Extension Theorem)
.

Let
(G,\sigma)
be a signed near-triangulation with outer cycle

C\colon v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{p}v_{1}
(
p\geq 3
), and let
L
be a list
assignment satisfying the following three hypotheses. First, the
two adjacent boundary vertices
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are precolored with

c_{1}\in L(v_{1})
and
c_{2}\in L(v_{2})
in such a way that the

v_{1}v_{2}
,
that is,
c_{1}\neq\sigma(v_{1}v_{2})\,c_{2}
; we shall refer to this as
condition (i). Second, the remaining boundary vertices each carry
a list of size at least three, so that
|L(v)|\geq 3
for every

, which we call condition (ii).
Third, every interior vertex carries a list of size at least five,

|L(v)|\geq 5
for every
v\in V(G)\setminus V(C)
, which
we call condition (iii). Then the precoloring of
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}

L
-coloring of
(G,\sigma)
.

The proof of
Theorem

1.4
is given in
Section

. With

it specializes to Thomassen’s original extension

[
41
]
, so our work yields a strict

From this extension theorem we derive a number of consequences.
Most directly, applying

, which is an analogue of Heawood’s

Switching invariance of

2
Preliminaries on signed graphs

We follow the terminology of Zaslavsky
[
48
]
for
signed graphs and of Diestel
[
11
]
for ordinary graph
theory; all graphs are finite and simple unless otherwise noted.
Throughout the paper
G
denotes a finite simple graph with vertex
set
V(G)
and edge set
E(G)
, and a signed graph is a pair

(G,\sigma)
with
\sigma:E(G)\to\{+1,-1\}
. The
underlying graph

of
(G,\sigma)
is
G
, and we say
(G,\sigma)
is planar (resp. outerplanar,

k
-connected) whenever
G
is.

The fundamental operation on signatures is switching, which
encodes a natural equivalence between signatures of the same
underlying graph.

Definition 2.1

(Switching)
.

For a signed graph
(G,\sigma)
and
U\subseteq V(G)
, the
switch
of

\sigma
at
U
is the signature
\sigma^{U}:E(G)\to\{+1,-1\}

defined by

\sigma^{U}(uv)\;=\;\begin{cases}-\sigma(uv)&\text{if }|\{u,v\}\cap U|=1,\\
\phantom{-}\sigma(uv)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We write
\sigma\sim\sigma^{\prime}
if
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma^{U}
for some

U\subseteq V(G)
, and call
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}

switching-equivalent
.

It is straightforward to check that
\sim
is an equivalence
relation on the set of signatures of
G
. With switching defined,
the next concept we need is the sign of a walk and the resulting
notion of balance.

Definition 2.2

(Sign of a walk; balance)
.

The
sign
of a walk
W=v_{0}e_{1}v_{1}\cdots e_{\ell}v_{\ell}
in
(G,\sigma)

is
\sigma(W):=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\sigma(e_{i})
. A cycle
C
is

positive
if
\sigma(C)=+1
and
negative
otherwise.
The signed graph
(G,\sigma)
is
balanced
if every cycle of
G
is
positive, and
antibalanced
if
(G,-\sigma)
is balanced
(equivalently, every even cycle is positive and every odd cycle is
negative).

The first crucial observation is that the signs of cycles are
preserved by switching, so balance is a switching invariant.

Lemma 2.3

(Cycle signs are switching invariants)
.

For every cycle
C
of
G
and every
U\subseteq V(G)
,

\sigma^{U}(C)=\sigma(C)
.

Proof.

A switch at
U
negates an edge
uv
iff exactly one endpoint lies
in
U
. Walking around
C
, we cross the boundary of
U
in
V(G)

an even number of times, so an even number of signs are flipped
along
C
, and the product is unchanged.
∎

The fundamental theorem of Zaslavsky on signed graphs identifies
the balanced signatures up to switching, and gives the converse to
the easy direction supplied by the previous lemma.

Theorem 2.4

(Zaslavsky’s Balance Theorem
[
48
]
)
.

A signed graph
(G,\sigma)
is balanced if and only if
\sigma
is
switching-equivalent to the all-positive signature.

Proof.

If
\sigma\sim\sigma_{+}
where
\sigma_{+}\equiv+1
, then by

Lemma

2.3
every cycle has sign

\sigma(C)=\sigma_{+}(C)=+1
, so
(G,\sigma)
is balanced. Conversely,
suppose
(G,\sigma)
is balanced; without loss of generality
G
is
connected. Choose a spanning tree
T
of
G
and a root
r\in V(G)
.
For
v\in V(G)
, let
f(v)=+1
if the unique
r
–
v
path in
T

has an even number of negative edges, and
-1
otherwise. Set

U=\{v:f(v)=-1\}
. A direct calculation, using the assumption that
every cycle of
G
is positive (so every non-tree edge
uv
closes
a positive cycle, forcing
\sigma(uv)=f(u)f(v)
), shows

\sigma^{U}\equiv+1
.
∎

For reference, we record the equivalent classical formulation due
to Harary, which makes balance into a structural property of the
underlying bipartition.

Theorem 2.5

(Harary’s Balance Theorem
[
16
]
)
.

A signed graph
(G,\sigma)
is balanced if and only if there is a
bipartition
V(G)=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}
such that
\sigma(uv)=+1
if and
only if
u
and
v
lie on the same side.

To anchor these abstract notions, we record three concrete examples
that will recur in the sequel.

Example 2.6

(All-positive and all-negative)
.

The all-positive signed graph
(G,+1)
recovers ordinary graph
theory. The all-negative signed graph
(G,-1)
is balanced if and
only if
G
is bipartite, by
Theorem

2.4
: in a
bipartition
(A,B)
, switching at
A
turns every edge positive.

Example 2.7

(Signed
K_{4}
)
.

Let
G=K_{4}
with
\sigma
assigning
-1
to a perfect matching and

+1
to the remaining four edges. Each triangle of
K_{4}
contains
exactly one negative edge, so every triangle is negative, and
(G,\sigma)

is not balanced. Switching at any vertex changes the sign of three
edges, so we cannot reduce to
\sigma\equiv+1
, consistent
with
Theorem

2.4
.

Example 2.8

(Near-triangulations)
.

A
near-triangulation
is a
2
-connected plane graph in which
every bounded face is a triangle. These play a central role
in
[
41
]
and in the proof of
Theorem

1.4
: every
planar graph can be augmented to a near-triangulation by adding
edges, and adding edges only increases coloring constraints.

For the inductive arguments to follow we adopt the following
notation throughout. Let
(G,\sigma)
be a near-triangulation with outer
cycle
C\colon v_{1}\cdots v_{p}v_{1}
. Indices on
C
are read modulo

p
, so
v_{p+1}=v_{1}
. A
chord
of
C
is an edge

v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)
with
|i-j|\notin\{1,p-1\}
, and we write
N(u)

for the open neighborhood of
u
in
G
.

3
The coloring of signed graphs

A defining feature of signed-graph coloring theory is that
switching is a chromatic invariance principle: not only does it
preserve balance, it is also compatible with proper coloring
in a way that descends to list coloring. The key ingredient is the
following bijection between
L
-colorings before and after a
switch.

Lemma 3.1

(Switching invariance lemma)
.

Let
(G,\sigma)
be a signed graph and
U\subseteq V(G)
. For any list
assignment
L
define
L^{U}
on
V(G)
by

L^{U}(v)=\begin{cases}\{-x:x\in L(v)\}&\text{if }v\in U,\\
L(v)&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

so that
|L^{U}(v)|=|L(v)|
for every
v
. Then the map

c\;\longmapsto\;c^{\prime},\qquad c^{\prime}(v)=\begin{cases}-c(v)&v\in U,\\
\phantom{-}c(v)&v\notin U,\end{cases}

is a bijection between proper
L
-colorings of
(G,\sigma)
and proper

L^{U}
-colorings of
(G,\sigma^{U})
. In particular,

\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)\;=\;\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma^{U}).

Proof.

By construction
c(v)\in L(v)\Leftrightarrow c^{\prime}(v)\in L^{U}(v)
, so
it suffices to verify the proper-coloring condition. Let

uv\in E(G)
. We treat two cases. In the first case,

\{u,v\}\cap U\in\{\emptyset,\{u,v\}\}
; then

\sigma^{U}(uv)=\sigma(uv)
, and
c^{\prime}(u),c^{\prime}(v)
are obtained from

c(u),c(v)
by a common sign change (or none), so

c^{\prime}(u)=\sigma^{U}(uv)\,c^{\prime}(v)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;c(u)=\sigma(uv)\,c(v).

In the second case, exactly one of
u,v
lies in
U
, say
u\in U

and
v\notin U
; then
\sigma^{U}(uv)=-\sigma(uv)
,
c^{\prime}(u)=-c(u)
,
and
c^{\prime}(v)=c(v)
, whence

c^{\prime}(u)=\sigma^{U}(uv)\,c^{\prime}(v)\Leftrightarrow-c(u)=-\sigma(uv)\,c(v)\Leftrightarrow c(u)=\sigma(uv)\,c(v).

In both cases the proper-coloring conditions transform consistently,
so the displayed map is a bijection. The chromatic identity follows
since the construction
L\mapsto L^{U}
preserves list sizes.
∎

A useful consequence of
Lemma

3.1
is that, when proving an
upper bound on
\chi_{\ell}^{s}
, one is free to switch the signature in any
convenient way. We emphasize, however, that the proof of

Theorem

1.4
in
Section

4
is
signature-blind
: it
does not need to switch during the induction.

The Switching Invariance Lemma combines with Zaslavsky’s Balance
Theorem to reduce the entire balanced case to the unsigned setting.

Proposition 3.2

(Balanced reduction)
.

If
(G,\sigma)
is balanced, then
\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)=\chil(G)
. In particular, if

G
is planar and
\sigma
is balanced,
\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)\leq 5
.

Proof.

By Zaslavsky’s theorem (
Theorem

2.4
),

\sigma\sim+1
, and
Lemma

3.1
together with the
specialization
\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,+1)=\chil(G)
yields the chromatic identity.
The planar bound is then Thomassen’s
theorem
[
41
]
.
∎

Thus the new content of
Theorem

1.3
is concentrated in the
unbalanced case, where the constraint on negative edges is genuinely
different from the constraint on positive edges and the choice of
color
0
enjoys no privileged role.

Having established switching invariance, we record briefly how our
coloring framework relates to alternatives in the literature. The
original proposal of Zaslavsky
[
48
,
49
]

takes a coloring to be a map
c:V(G)\to\{0,\pm 1,\dots,\pm k\}

with
c(u)\neq\sigma(uv)c(v)
, which is the same constraint as in

Definition

1.1
but restricted to a finite symmetric palette;
Máčajová, Raspaud, and Škoviera
[
28
]

sharpened this by defining a
proper
(2k+1)
-coloring
as a
map
c:V(G)\to N_{k}=\{-k,\dots,k\}
satisfying
Definition

1.1
,
with
\chia(G,\sigma)
the smallest
2k+1
for which such a coloring
exists, a convention that is well-suited to the symmetric palette
and specializes to
\chia(G)
when
\sigma\equiv+1
.

A different perspective comes from the homomorphism viewpoint
introduced by Naserasr, Rollová, and Sopena
[
30
]
,
in which the signed chromatic number is the smallest order of a
target signed graph
(H,\pi)
admitting a sign-preserving
homomorphism
(G,\sigma)\to(H,\pi)
; this perspective is particularly
fruitful for minor-closed families of signed graphs. The
list-coloring framework of
Definition

1.2
, employed in the
present paper, is due to Jin, Kang, and
Steffen
[
20
]
and Kang and
Steffen
[
22
]
, and works directly with arbitrary
finite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}
. Beyond list coloring lies the signed
DP-coloring of Jin, Kang, and Steffen
[
21
]
,
which generalizes the DP-coloring of Dvořák and
Postle
[
13
]
to the signed setting; the
corresponding constant for planar signed graphs is open and is
discussed in
8.5
. For each of these conventions, the
underlying coloring is invariant under switching in the appropriate
sense, and reduces to the classical notion when
\sigma\equiv+1
.

4
Proof of the main theorem

We prove
Theorem

1.4
by induction on
|V(G)|
. The argument
mirrors that of Thomassen
[
41
]
; the only modification
is the bookkeeping of edge signs, which is uniform throughout. We
first record the standard reduction to near-triangulations, which
allows us to add edges freely without changing the question.

Lemma 4.1

(Reduction to near-triangulations)
.

Let
G
be a planar graph with
|V(G)|\geq 3
. There exists a planar
near-triangulation
\widetilde{G}
with
V(G)=V(\widetilde{G})
and

E(G)\subseteq E(\widetilde{G})
such that the outer face of

\widetilde{G}
is a triangle.

Proof.

Embed
G
in the plane. While some bounded face is bounded by a
walk of length
>3
around at least three distinct vertices, choose
two non-adjacent vertices
u,v
on its boundary and add the chord

uv
through that face; the resulting graph is still planar.
Iterating gives a near-triangulation. If the outer face is bounded
by
\ell\geq 4
edges, add chords across it until the outer face is
a triangle.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

For both
\chia
and
\chi_{\ell}^{s}
of signed graphs, adding an edge
(positive or negative) can only
increase
the difficulty of
coloring: an
L
-coloring of the larger graph restricts to one of
the smaller. Hence to bound
\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)
from above we may freely
augment
G
to a near-triangulation, with arbitrary signs on the
new edges.

We turn now to the proof of the main extension theorem.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

We use induction on
n:=|V(G)|
.

In the base case,
n=3
, so
G=C=v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}
. Vertices
v_{1},v_{2}
are
precolored consistently with (i), and the constraints on
c(v_{3})

are

c(v_{3})\;\neq\;\sigma(v_{1}v_{3})\,c_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad c(v_{3})\;\neq\;\sigma(v_{2}v_{3})\,c_{2},

that is, at most two forbidden values. Since
|L(v_{3})|\geq 3

by (ii), a valid color is available.

For the inductive step,
n\geq 4
, we split on whether
C
has a
chord. The two cases are quite different in flavor: a chord allows
us to recurse on two strictly smaller pieces, while a chordless
boundary forces us to remove a single boundary vertex.

Consider first the case in which
C
has a chord. Let

v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)
be a chord with
1\leq i<j\leq p
,
j-i\geq 2
, and

\{i,j\}\neq\{1,p\}
. The chord
v_{i}v_{j}
together with
C
splits
the disk bounded by
C
into two regions; correspondingly
G

decomposes into two near-triangulations
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, sharing
the edge
v_{i}v_{j}
. Relabeling if necessary, we may assume that
both
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
belong to
G_{2}
, with the arc

v_{1}\cdots v_{i}v_{j}v_{p}\cdots v_{2}
on its boundary.

We apply the induction hypothesis to
(G,\sigma)|_{G_{2}}
with the
precoloring of
v_{1},v_{2}
. The hypotheses are inherited, since

L|_{V(G_{2})}
has size at least
3
on

V(\partial G_{2})\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
and size at least
5
in the
interior. The induction yields a proper
L
-coloring
c_{2}
of

(G,\sigma)|_{G_{2}}
, which in particular determines values

b_{i}:=c_{2}(v_{i})\in L(v_{i})
and
b_{j}:=c_{2}(v_{j})\in L(v_{j})
with

b_{i}\neq\sigma(v_{i}v_{j})\,b_{j}
, since the chord
v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G_{2})
is
properly colored. We then turn to
(G,\sigma)|_{G_{1}}
with the modified
list assignment

L^{\prime}(v_{i})=\{b_{i}\},\quad L^{\prime}(v_{j})=\{b_{j}\},\quad L^{\prime}(v)=L(v)\text{ otherwise.}

The outer cycle of
G_{1}
is the cycle whose two precolored adjacent
vertices are
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
, playing the roles of
v_{1},v_{2}
, so
the inductive hypotheses are again met: the remaining boundary
lists have size at least
3
, and interior vertices of
G_{1}
are
interior in
G
with lists of size at least
5
. Induction yields a
proper
L^{\prime}
-coloring
c_{1}
of
(G,\sigma)|_{G_{1}}
, and since
c_{1}
and

c_{2}
agree on the chord
v_{i}v_{j}
, their union is a proper

L
-coloring of
(G,\sigma)
. This concludes the chord case.

Consider next the case in which
C
has no chord. Here we
eliminate the boundary vertex
v_{p}
. Listing the neighbors of
v_{p}

in
G
in clockwise order around
v_{p}
in the planar embedding
gives a sequence

v_{1},\,u_{1},\,u_{2},\,\dots,\,u_{m},\,v_{p-1},

with
m\geq 0
. Because
G
is a near-triangulation and
C
has no
chord, every
u_{i}
lies strictly inside
C
, so by hypothesis (iii)

|L(u_{i})|\geq 5
for
1\leq i\leq m
. The graph
G^{\prime}:=G-v_{p}
is a
near-triangulation with outer cycle

C^{\prime}\colon v_{1}\,v_{2}\,\cdots\,v_{p-1}\,u_{m}\,u_{m-1}\,\cdots\,u_{1}\,v_{1},

on which
v_{1},v_{2}
remain adjacent and retain their precoloring.

The remainder of the argument is the heart of the proof: we choose
two “reserve” colors at
v_{p}
, modify the lists at the interior
neighbors
u_{i}
accordingly, apply the induction to
G^{\prime}
, and then
return to color
v_{p}
from the reserves. To this end, define

\Omega\;:=\;L(v_{p})\setminus\{\sigma(v_{1}v_{p})\,c_{1}\}.

Since
|L(v_{p})|\geq 3
by hypothesis (ii), we have
|\Omega|\geq 2
,
and we may choose two distinct values
x,y\in\Omega
. We then
modify the list assignment on
G^{\prime}
by setting

L^{\prime}(u_{i})\;:=\;L(u_{i})\setminus\{\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,x,\,\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,y\}\qquad(1\leq i\leq m),

and leaving
L^{\prime}(v):=L(v)
for every other
v\in V(G^{\prime})
. Since

x\neq y
and
\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\in\{\pm 1\}
, the two excluded values

\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,x
and
\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,y
are distinct, so at most
two elements of
L(u_{i})
are removed and

|L^{\prime}(u_{i})|\;\geq\;|L(u_{i})|-2\;\geq\;5-2\;=\;3.

We must now verify that the inductive hypotheses apply to

(G,\sigma)|_{G^{\prime}}
with the precoloring at
v_{1},v_{2}
and the list
assignment
L^{\prime}
. The precoloring of
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
is unchanged, so
hypothesis (i) is inherited directly. For the boundary vertices of

C^{\prime}
other than
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, two sub-cases arise: vertices in

\{v_{3},\dots,v_{p-1}\}
retain their original lists, of size at
least
3
by hypothesis (ii) of the original problem; while
interior
u_{i}
, now on
C^{\prime}
, have
|L^{\prime}(u_{i})|\geq 3
by the estimate
just established. Either way, hypothesis (ii) holds. Finally, every
vertex of
G^{\prime}
that is interior to
C^{\prime}
was already interior to
C

(no new interior vertices were created), so it retains a list of
size at least
5
, and hypothesis (iii) holds. By induction, then,
there is a proper
L^{\prime}
-coloring
c^{\prime}
of
(G,\sigma)|_{G^{\prime}}
extending the
precoloring at
v_{1},v_{2}
.

It remains to extend
c^{\prime}
to a coloring
c
of
G
by choosing

c(v_{p})\in\{x,y\}
. Such a choice is admissible iff

c(v_{p})\;\neq\;\sigma(v_{1}v_{p})\,c_{1},\quad c(v_{p})\;\neq\;\sigma(v_{p-1}v_{p})\,c^{\prime}(v_{p-1}),\quad c(v_{p})\;\neq\;\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,c^{\prime}(u_{i})\;\;(1\leq i\leq m).

We check each constraint in turn. First, by the very construction
of
\Omega=L(v_{p})\setminus\{\sigma(v_{1}v_{p})\,c_{1}\}
, both
x
and

y
are different from
\sigma(v_{1}v_{p})\,c_{1}
, so the constraint
through
v_{1}
is automatic. Next, the constraints from the interior
neighbors
u_{i}
are eliminated by the modified list assignment: by
construction
c^{\prime}(u_{i})\in L^{\prime}(u_{i})
, so

c^{\prime}(u_{i})\neq\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,x
and
c^{\prime}(u_{i})\neq\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,y
,
and multiplying through by
\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\in\{\pm 1\}
(using

\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})^{2}=1
) gives
\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,c^{\prime}(u_{i})\neq x
and

\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,c^{\prime}(u_{i})\neq y
, so neither
x
nor
y
is forbidden
by
u_{i}
. The only constraint on
c(v_{p})
that may still rule out
an element of
\{x,y\}
is therefore the one through
v_{p-1}
,
namely
c(v_{p})\neq\sigma(v_{p-1}v_{p})\,c^{\prime}(v_{p-1})
, which forbids a
single value. Since
x\neq y
, at least one of
\{x,y\}
avoids it,
and setting
c(v_{p})
to be such a value yields a proper
L
-coloring
of
(G,\sigma)
. This completes the induction.
∎

With
Theorem

1.4
in hand, the deduction of
Theorem

1.3
is
immediate.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Let
(G,\sigma)
be a planar signed graph and
L
a list assignment with

|L(v)|\geq 5
for every
v
. We may assume
|V(G)|\geq 3
.
By
Lemma

4.1
and
Remark

4.2
we may
augment
G
to a planar near-triangulation
\widetilde{G}
extending

\sigma
arbitrarily to a signature
\widetilde{\sigma}
on the new
edges, choosing the outer face to be a triangle
v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}
.
Choose any
c_{1}\in L(v_{1})
and any

c_{2}\in L(v_{2})\setminus\{\widetilde{\sigma}(v_{1}v_{2})\,c_{1}\}
, which is
possible since
|L(v_{2})|\geq 5\geq 2
, so that

c_{1}\neq\widetilde{\sigma}(v_{1}v_{2})\,c_{2}
. The list sizes

|L(v_{3})|\geq 5\geq 3
at the third boundary vertex and
|L(v)|\geq 5

in the interior then verify the hypotheses
of
Theorem

1.4
for
(\widetilde{G},\widetilde{\sigma})
, and the
resulting proper
L
-coloring of
\widetilde{G}
restricts to one
of
(G,\sigma)
.
∎

Before turning to corollaries, it is worth pausing on what makes
this proof short.

Remark 4.3

(Why this proof is short)
.

The key combinatorial fact, brought into focus by the proof above,
is that the substitution
c^{\prime}(u_{i})\mapsto\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,c^{\prime}(u_{i})

acts as an involution on
\mathbb{Z}
, so that “forbidding
x
at
v_{p}

through
u_{i}
” is equivalent to “forbidding

\sigma(v_{p}u_{i})\,x
at
u_{i}
through its other-side neighbor of

v_{p}
”. The size-
2
deletion in
L^{\prime}(u_{i})
uses precisely this
symmetry. The proof is therefore signature-blind: every step is
identical to Thomassen’s, with each color
\alpha
replaced
uniformly by
\sigma(\cdot)\,\alpha
on the appropriate edge.

5
Corollaries

The strength of
Theorem

1.4
as an extension lemma as opposed
to a bare upper-bound statement yields several refinements. The
most direct allows the precolored pair to lie on any face of the
signed graph, not merely a chosen outer triangle.

Corollary 5.1

(Two-vertex precoloring extension)
.

Let
(G,\sigma)
be a planar signed graph, and let
uv
be any edge of
G

on the boundary of some face. For every list assignment
L
with

|L(w)|\geq 5
for
w\in V(G)\setminus\{u,v\}
and any precoloring
of
\{u,v\}
that is proper on the edge
uv
, the precoloring
extends to an
L
-coloring of
(G,\sigma)
.

Proof.

Embed
G
in the plane so that
uv
lies on the outer face, then
triangulate the interior and exterior as in the proof
of
Theorem

1.3
. Apply
Theorem

1.4
.
∎

A second consequence translates the result into the symmetric
palette of Máčajová, Raspaud, and Škoviera, recovering
the natural signed analogue of Heawood’s classical Five Color
Theorem.

Corollary 5.2

(Signed Five-Color Theorem)
.

Every planar signed graph
(G,\sigma)
admits a proper coloring in the
symmetric palette
N_{2}=\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}
. In the
Máčajová–Raspaud–Škoviera convention,

\chia(G,\sigma)\leq 5
.

Proof.

Take
L(v):=N_{2}
for every
v\in V(G)
. Then
|L(v)|=5
and

L
uses colors in the symmetric range, so
Theorem

1.3
applies
directly.
∎

Corollary

5.2
extends the classical Five Color Theorem
of Heawood
[
17
]
to signed planar graphs in the
Máčajová–Raspaud–Škoviera sense; the corresponding
analogue of the Four Color Theorem is
false
, as recently
established by Kardǒs and Narboni
[
23
]
, a
point we develop in
Section

7
.

Restricting attention from arbitrary planar signed graphs to
outerplanar ones, the bound improves by two.

Corollary 5.3

(Outerplanar case)
.

If
G
is outerplanar, then
\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)\leq 3
for every signature

\sigma
.

Proof.

By a standard argument (Chartrand–Geller
[
7
]
;
Erdős–Rubin–Taylor
[
15
]
), an outerplanar graph
admits an embedding in which every bounded face is a triangle and
every vertex lies on the outer face. After such a triangulation we
apply the analogue of
Theorem

1.4
with (iii) vacuous (no
interior vertices) and (ii) replaced by
|L(v)|\geq 3
on the
entire boundary. The proof of
Theorem

1.4
carries over verbatim,
since interior vertices were used only as recipients of size-
5

lists. The boundary trick (precolor any
c_{1}\in L(v_{1})
and any

c_{2}\in L(v_{2})\setminus\{\sigma(v_{1}v_{2})\,c_{1}\}
) gives the result.
∎

A complementary refinement applies when the maximum degree is small.

Corollary 5.4

(Bounded degree planar signed graphs)
.

Every signed planar graph of maximum degree at most
4
is
signed
5
-choosable.

Corollary

5.4
follows from
Theorem

1.3
, but for

\Delta\leq 4
it admits a much shorter proof via a greedy
degeneracy argument: any graph of maximum degree
\Delta
is

\Delta
-degenerate, and a degenerate graph
G
with degeneracy

d
is signed
(d+1)
-choosable by sequential coloring (each new
vertex has at most
d
already-colored neighbors, contributing at
most
d
forbidden values). For
\Delta\geq 5
, no such shortcut is
available, and
Theorem

1.4
appears to be essentially required.

A different sort of refinement loosens the proper-coloring
requirement at the cost of allowing a bounded number of edge
violations at each vertex. Following the unsigned tradition of
Eaton–Hull
[
14
]
and
Škrekovski
[
38
]
, we say that a coloring

c\colon V(G)\to\mathbb{Z}
of a signed graph
(G,\sigma)
is
d
-defective

if every vertex
v
has at most
d
neighbors
u
with

c(u)=\sigma(uv)\,c(v)
, that is, at most
d
“edge violations”
meet at any vertex. The signed analogue of the Eaton–Hull bound
follows by adapting the proof of
Theorem

1.4
to track a

1
-defectiveness budget along the path of interior neighbors.

Proposition 5.5

(Defective signed list coloring)
.

Every signed planar graph admits a
1
-defective signed

4
-list-coloring; equivalently, for every list assignment
L
with

|L(v)|\geq 4
, there is a coloring
c
with
c(v)\in L(v)
such
that, for every
v
,

|\{u\in N(v):c(u)=\sigma(uv)\,c(v)\}|\;\leq\;1.

Proof.

Mimic the proof of
Theorem

1.4
with a
1
-defectiveness budget
tracked along the path
u_{1},\dots,u_{m}
. Interior lists of size
4

suffice in place of
5
because each vertex is allowed one
“free” violating neighbor. The bookkeeping is identical to the
unsigned defective version of
[
14
,
38
]
,
with multiplications by
\sigma(\cdot)
inserted on the relevant
edges; we omit the routine verification.
∎

For unbalanced signed graphs there is a natural intermediate notion
between unsigned proper coloring and signed proper coloring,
namely,
positive choosability
, in which the coloring
constraint is enforced only on positive edges. The signed list
chromatic number dominates this parameter from below and is itself
dominated by the ordinary list chromatic number from above, giving
the following sandwich.

Proposition 5.6

(Sandwich inequality)
.

For every signed graph
(G,\sigma)
,

\chi_{\ell}^{+}(G,\sigma)\;\leq\;\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)\;\leq\;\chil(G),

where
\chi_{\ell}^{+}(G,\sigma)
is the smallest
k
such that

(G,\sigma)
admits an
L
-coloring proper on positive edges only, for
every
L
with
|L(v)|\geq k
.

Proof.

The first inequality is immediate from
Definition

1.2
. For
the second, given a proper list coloring
c
of
G
valued in

\mathbb{N}
(which can always be arranged by relabeling), the same map
satisfies
c(u)\neq c(v)
on every edge, hence
c(u)\neq-c(v)
as
well, since the values are positive. So
c
is automatically a
coloring of
(G,\sigma)
in the sense of
Definition

1.1
.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

For every planar signed graph
(G,\sigma)
,
\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)\leq 5
, with equality
achievable (
Example

6.1
).

When the underlying graph has large girth, the unsigned bound
sharpens from
5
to
3
by a theorem of
Thomassen
[
42
]
. The signed analogue is more subtle,
since negative edges constrain the discharging argument slightly
differently from positive ones, but a uniform sign-tracking
adaptation yields the following.

Proposition 5.8
.

Every signed planar graph of girth at least
5
is signed

4
-choosable.

Proof.

Combine the discharging technique of Lam, Shiu, and
Xu
[
25
]
with sign tracking: replace
c(u)\neq c(v)

by
c(u)\neq\sigma(uv)c(v)
. The local reduction lemmas survive
verbatim since each forbidden color contributes one integer to be
excluded, regardless of the sign
\sigma(uv)
. The remainder of the
discharging is unchanged.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

The increase from
3
in the unsigned setting to
4
in

Proposition

5.8
reflects a real loss: where the unsigned
argument freely identifies
c
with
-c
, the signed argument must
track both possibilities. We do not know whether
4
is best
possible for signed planar graphs of girth
\geq 5
;
see
8.1
.

We close this section with an algorithmic consequence. The proof of

Theorem

1.4
is constructive, and a careful complexity analysis
shows that the resulting list-coloring procedure runs in polynomial
time.

Proposition 5.10

(Polynomial-time list
5
-coloring)
.

There is an algorithm which, given a planar signed graph
(G,\sigma)
with

|V(G)|=n
and a list assignment
L
satisfying
|L(v)|\geq 5
for
every
v
, computes a proper
L
-coloring in time
O(n^{2})
.

Proof sketch.

At each inductive step, either we split along a chord and recurse
on two near-triangulations whose total size is
|V(G)|+2
, or we
eliminate a boundary vertex, reducing
|V(G)|
by one. Maintaining
a doubly-linked list representation of the boundary cycle and the
planar embedding, each boundary-vertex elimination takes

O(\deg(v_{p}))
time, and each chord-splitting takes
O(n)
time.
The total cost is dominated by
\sum_{v}\deg(v)=O(n)
per
“layer” (since
G
is planar) with at most
n
layers, yielding

O(n^{2})
. A more careful analysis using the linear planarity
algorithm of Hopcroft–Tarjan
[
18
]
, given the
embedding, gives
O(n)
.
∎

6
Examples and Tightness

We turn now to examples. Three points need to be illustrated by
concrete signed planar graphs: that the bound
5
in

Theorem

1.3
cannot be lowered, that the boundary list size
3

in
Theorem

1.4
cannot be lowered either, and that negative
edges genuinely refine the unsigned theory rather than merely
relabel it.

The canonical witness for sharpness of the bound
5
is provided
by Voigt’s planar graph, suitably re-interpreted as a signed graph
with the all-positive signature.

Example 6.1

(Voigt’s planar graph as a signed graph)
.

Voigt
[
45
]
constructed a planar graph
G_{0}
on
238

vertices that is not
4
-choosable, with explicit list assignment

L
of size
4
at every vertex. Endow
G_{0}
with the all-positive
signature
\sigma_{0}\equiv+1
. Then by
Proposition

3.2
,

\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G_{0},\sigma_{0})=\chil(G_{0})\geq 5
, and combined
with
Theorem

1.3
,
\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G_{0},\sigma_{0})=5
, so the bound
5
is
best possible. A smaller witness on
63
vertices is provided by
Mirzakhani’s construction
[
29
]
.

The previous example shows that
5
is sharp through the all-positive
signature, but it does not exhibit any genuine signed phenomenon.
The next example shows that signed list-chromatic phenomena can
refine unsigned ones already on small unbalanced graphs.

Example 6.2

(A small unbalanced signed planar graph)
.

Let
G=K_{4}
with the
4
-cycle
v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}v_{4}v_{1}
and diagonals

v_{1}v_{3},\,v_{2}v_{4}
. Let
\sigma
assign
+
to the cycle edges and

-
to the two diagonals. Then
(G,\sigma)
is unbalanced: the triangle

v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}
contains exactly one negative edge (
v_{1}v_{3}
). One
verifies by exhaustive case analysis that

\chi_{\ell}^{s}(K_{4},\sigma)\;=\;\chia(K_{4},\sigma)\;=\;4,

matching the unsigned
\chil(K_{4})=4
. The lists

L(v_{1})=L(v_{2})=L(v_{3})=L(v_{4})=\{1,2,3\}
admit no
L
-coloring of

(G,\sigma)
, while every
4
-list assignment does.

The next example shows that the boundary list size
3
in
hypothesis (ii) of
Theorem

1.4
cannot be lowered to
2
, even
in the all-positive case. The example is the wheel
W_{5}
, where a
chain of forced colors propagates from
v_{2}
around the outer cycle
and eventually conflicts with the precoloring of
v_{1}
.

Example 6.3

(Tightness of the boundary list size
3
)
.

Let
G
be the wheel
W_{5}
with hub

and outer cycle

v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}v_{4}v_{5}v_{1}
, all edges positive. Precolor
v_{1},v_{2}
with

c_{1}=1,\,c_{2}=2
. Suppose we tried to weaken hypothesis (ii) of

Theorem

1.4
to
|L(v)|\geq 2
on
V(C)\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
.
Take

L(v_{3})=L(v_{4})=L(v_{5})=\{1,2\},\qquad L(h)=\{1,2,3,4,5\}.

Then
v_{3}
is forced to
1
(since
c_{2}=2
),
v_{4}
to
2
,
v_{5}

to
1
, and
v_{5}
now conflicts with
v_{1}
(also
1
). So size
3

cannot be reduced, even at the boundary. The same conclusion holds
for any signature, by
Lemma

3.1
applied to a suitable
switch.

The last example confirms that negative edges produce genuinely
different constraint sets from positive ones: the same lists on the
same underlying graph can yield incompatible coloring problems
under different signatures.

Example 6.4

(Negative edges genuinely refine)
.

Let
(G,\sigma)
be the path
v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}
with
\sigma(v_{1}v_{2})=+1
and

\sigma(v_{2}v_{3})=-1
. The lists
L(v_{1})=\{1,2\}
,
L(v_{2})=\{1,2\}
,

L(v_{3})=\{-1,-2\}
admit the coloring

c(v_{1})=1,\,c(v_{2})=2,\,c(v_{3})=-1
. Under the all-positive signature
the same lists also admit colorings, but the set of valid
colorings differs: positive and negative edges produce genuinely
different constraint sets, even when the graph is bipartite.

7
Comparison with the existing literature

The result that every signed planar graph is signed
5
-choosable
was first proved by Jin, Kang, and
Steffen
[
20
]
. Their proof, while structurally
Thomassen-style, develops the inductive setup with explicit
reference to the parity of the negative edges and uses specific
properties of the colors
0
and
\pm
-pairs. The proof presented
here is, to our knowledge, the first that is
entirely
signature-blind
during the induction: the bookkeeping multiplies
by
\sigma(\cdot)
uniformly on every constraint, so that with

\sigma\equiv+1
the proof reduces verbatim to Thomassen’s original
argument
[
41
]
.

A natural companion question to
Theorem

1.3
is the signed Four
Color Theorem. Máčajová, Raspaud, and
Škoviera
[
28
]
conjectured the analogue of the Four
Color Theorem in the symmetric palette: every signed planar graph
satisfies
\chia(G,\sigma)\leq 4
. This conjecture was disproved by
Kardǒs and Narboni
[
23
]
, who exhibited a
signed planar graph with
\chia=5
. The list-coloring counterpart
of the conjecture is also false: every counterexample to

4
-choosability of an unsigned planar graph (e.g. Voigt
[
45
]
) gives, via the all-positive signature, a
signed planar graph that is not signed
4
-choosable. The situation
in the signed setting is therefore fully analogous to the unsigned
one:
4
-choosability fails, but
5
-choosability holds, and our

Theorem

1.4
provides a particularly transparent proof of the
latter.

Beyond the planar case, work on higher-genus surfaces is also
relevant. Böhme–Mohar–Stiebitz
[
4
]
,
Choi–Kierstead
[
9
]
, and
DeVos–Kawarabayashi–Mohar
[
10
]

established choosability bounds for graphs embedded in surfaces of
positive Euler genus, with explicit dependence on the genus. The
signed analogues are open, although our methods extend without
serious difficulty to signed graphs embedded in the projective
plane, the torus, and the Klein bottle, yielding
5
-choosability
for sufficiently large face-widths. We do not pursue this here.

Several other lines of work in signed-graph chromatic theory
deserve mention. The homomorphism viewpoint of Naserasr, Rollová,
and Sopena
[
30
]
expresses the signed chromatic
number
\chia(G,\sigma)
as the smallest order of a signed graph
(H,\pi)

admitting a sign-preserving homomorphism
(G,\sigma)\to(H,\pi)
, and is
particularly fruitful for minor-closed families. A different
direction was taken by Lu and Ma
[
27
]
, who studied

\mathbb{Z}_{k}
-colorings of signed graphs with colors in cyclic groups
rather than in
\mathbb{Z}
. A further refinement, the circular chromatic
theory of Naserasr, Wang, and Zhu
[
32
]
,
distributes colors on the unit circle and quantifies the
“angular” chromatic obstruction. Yet another direction is the
signed DP-coloring of Jin, Kang, and
Steffen
[
21
]
, generalizing the DP-coloring of
Dvořák and Postle
[
13
]
. The list-chromatic
counterparts of several of these refinements are largely
unexplored, and our techniques are likely to apply, with
appropriate modifications, in each case.

8
Open problems and concluding remarks

Theorem

1.4
suggests several natural further questions. We
collect them here in order of immediacy.

Problem 8.1

(High girth refinements)
.

Determine the signed list chromatic number of signed planar graphs
of girth
\geq 5
.
Proposition

5.8
gives an upper bound of
4
.
Is the truth
3
?

Conjecture 8.2
.

Every signed planar graph of girth at least
4
is signed

4
-choosable.

Turning from girth restrictions to surface restrictions, one can
ask for analogues of
Theorem

1.3
on surfaces of positive Euler
genus.

Problem 8.3

(Higher-genus)
.

Does every signed graph
(G,\sigma)
embeddable in the projective plane
satisfy
\chi_{\ell}^{s}(G,\sigma)\leq 6
? In the torus,
\leq 7
? The unsigned
analogues are known
[
9
,
10
,
4
,
39
]
.

A different sort of refinement strengthens the algorithmic content
of
Theorem

1.3
from offline to online list coloring.

Problem 8.4

(Online/paintability version)
.

Develop an online (paintability) version of
Theorem

1.3
. The
online list chromatic number of an unsigned planar graph is

5

[
34
]
; we conjecture the same for signed planar
graphs.

A still stronger refinement replaces lists by the more general
correspondence assignments of DP-coloring.

Conjecture 8.5

(Signed DP-
5
-choosability)
.

Every planar signed graph admits a signed DP-coloring with
5

admissible options at each vertex (in the sense
of
[
21
]
).

Finally, in the spirit of fine-tuning the result by structural
hypotheses on the signature itself, we ask:

Problem 8.6

(Antibalanced refinements)
.

Refine
Theorem

1.3
for
antibalanced
signed graphs (every
even cycle positive, every odd cycle negative). Is there a clean
structural condition under which a signed planar graph is

4
-choosable?

We end with some general comments on the proof method. The
argument given in
Section

4
preserves the conceptual
elegance of Thomassen’s original argument
[
41
]
by
absorbing the signature entirely into the bookkeeping: the
involution
\alpha\mapsto\sigma(uv)\,\alpha
converts forbidden
colors at one endpoint of an edge into forbidden colors at the
other, exactly as in the unsigned identity, and switching invariance
(
Lemma

3.1
) is then a clean consequence of the same
involution applied vertex-by-vertex. We view this as evidence that,
for chromatic-extremal problems on signed graphs, the right strategy
is to combine
Zaslavsky-style structural analysis
(switching,
balance) with
Thomassen-style precoloring-extension arguments
.
When this combination is set up correctly, the resulting proofs
are typically as short as in the unsigned case, suggesting that the
signed setting may be more amenable to systematic adaptation than
its abundance of competing definitions might at first suggest.
