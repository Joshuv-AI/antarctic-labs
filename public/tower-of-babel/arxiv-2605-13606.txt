Lieb-Schultz-Mattis theorem from gauge constraints
Bhandaru Phani Parasar

Abstract
We construct a ℤ 2 × ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2} gauge theory coupled to matter on a one-dimensional chain, aiming to study the ground-state physics in the Gauss law subspace. We show that the theory in the Gauss law subspace has a U ( 1 ) (1) symmetry whose generator commutes with lattice translations, but anticommutes with the lattice reflection operator. This leads to a Lieb-Schultz-Mattis (LSM) theorem that always rules out a trivial gapped ground state in the Gauss law subspace, if the hamiltonian is invariant under translations and reflection. Any point in the parameter space must realize either a spontaneously symmetry broken (SSB) ground state, or a gapless ground state. Imposing the Gauss law is pivotal for the existence of the U ( 1 ) (1) symmetry, and hence of the LSM theorem. We thus demonstrate a novel mechanism to obtain an LSM-type theorem, wherein the symmetry responsible for the theorem originates from the kinematic constraints of a gauge theory. We identify a point in the parameter space at which the system is gapless. At the gapless point, the excitations admit a description in terms of free Dirac fermions with a constraint on the total fermion number. The asymptotic behavior of the two-point correlation function of the simplest local gauge-invariant quantity at the gapless point is found to be ∝ cos ( π r ) r − 2 / 9 \propto\cos{(\pi r)}\,r^{-2/9} , where r r is the lattice separation between the two points. This model is also a natural platform to study phase diagram topological defects residing in families of SSB phases.

When applicable, an LSM-type theorem prohibits a trivial gapped ground state for the system: the ground state must spontaneously break a symmetry under consideration, or be gapless, or have a nontrivial degeneracy on the torus. Recent studies reveal a compelling link between LSM-type theorems and quantum anomalies

Gauge theories

play an indispensable role in condensed matter physics. Gauging a global symmetry of “matter” degrees of freedom involves promoting the global symmetry to a local one by introducing auxiliary “gauge” degrees of freedom and imposing a Gauss law constraint. The study of many strongly correlated systems

In this paper, we answer the above-raised questions in the affirmative by demonstrating how an LSM-type theorem arises in the Gauss law subspace of a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory in one spatial dimension. We construct a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory coupled to matter on a one-dimensional chain hosting a three-dimensional Hilbert space on each site and link. We show that the theory in the Gauss law subspace has a U
(1)
symmetry, whose generator commutes with the lattice translation operator, but
anticommutes
with the lattice reflection operator. This gives rise to an LSM theorem for the theory in the Gauss law subspace. At all values of the parameters, the theory is prohibited to realize a trivial gapped ground state. The origin of the U
(1)
symmetry, and hence the LSM theorem, is in imposing the Gauss law. We thus uncover a novel mechanism to realize an LSM-type theorem, where the symmetry responsible for the theorem originates from the kinematic structure of a gauge theory (Gauss law).
We identify a gapless point in the parameter space with the aid of a nonlocal mapping to fermionic degrees of freedom. At the gapless point, the excitations are described by free Dirac fermions. We also study the asymptotic behavior of correlation functions at the gapless point using results from the theory of Toeplitz determinants.

LSM
—
We are now ready to state and prove the main result of this work: the translation and reflection invariant hamiltonian
H_{G}
of Eq. (
8
) does not admit a trivial gapped ground state for any values of the parameters
t_{\alpha},K_{\alpha}
. We prove this statement using two facts established thus far: (1) In the Gauss law subspace,
H_{G}
has a U
(1)
symmetry,
[H_{G},U(\theta)]=0
, where
U(\theta)=\exp{(\mathrm{i}\theta M)}
. (2) The generator of the U
(1)
symmetry,
M
, anticommutes with the reflection operator
R
. Generally, LSM theorems arising from a global U
(1)
symmetry prohibit a trivial gapped ground state only when the filling-factor is not an integer
Oshikawa et al. 1997
. We emphasize that, however, in the LSM theorem obtained here, a trivial gapped ground state is prohibited for
any
value of
M
in a ground state. This is possible because of the additional property (2) listed above.

The proof is simple, and proceeds as follows: Let
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
be a ground state of
H_{G}
in the Gauss law subspace. Since
H_{G}
,
M
, and the translation operator
T
all commute with each other in the Gauss law subspace, we can always choose
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
to also be a simultaneous eigenstate of
T
and
M
:
H_{G}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}=E_{0}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
,
M{|\psi_{0}\rangle}=M_{0}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
,
T{|\psi_{0}\rangle}=e^{\mathrm{i}q}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
,
q\in[0,2\pi)
. If
M_{0}\neq 0
,
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
is not invariant under reflection
R
since
MR{|\psi_{0}\rangle}=-M_{0}R{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
. Thus, if
M_{0}\neq 0
,
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
and
R{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
are orthogonal and are eigenstates of
H_{G}
with the same eigenvalue
E_{0}
, and our proof is complete. Hence, in the following, we can assume
M_{0}=0
. Now, following the standard LSM proof
Oshikawa et al. 1997
, we define a variational state
{|\psi\rangle}=\mathcal{O}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
, where
\mathcal{O}=\exp{\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{L}j\mu_{j}\right)}
. The translation operator
T
acts on
\mathcal{O}
as
T\mathcal{O}{T}^{-1}=\mathcal{O}e^{\mathrm{i}\pi(\mu_{1}-M/L)}=-\mathcal{O}e^{-\mathrm{i}\pi M/L}
as
\mu_{1}=\pm 1
. Since
M_{0}=0
,
T{|\psi\rangle}=-e^{\mathrm{i}q}{|\psi\rangle}
, so
{|\psi\rangle}
is orthogonal to
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
. Also,
\langle\psi|H_{G}|\psi\rangle-E_{0}\leq\frac{\text{const.}}{L}
, since
H_{G}
is a local hamiltonian. Thus, the hamiltonian
H_{G}
for any
t_{\alpha},K_{\alpha}
realizes either a spontaneously symmetry-broken, or a gapless ground state.

It should be stressed that the implementation of the Gauss law is crucial for the existence of the U
(1)
symmetry, and hence for the LSM theorem. Indeed, in the unconstrained combined Hilbert space of the matter and the gauge degrees of freedom, the hamiltonian

admits a trivial gapped ground state

.

For the ground state, we have half-filling i.e.,

(or

(13)

Some results on a spin-
1/2
model
—
Before we conclude, we present some additional results concerning the spin-
1/2
version of the hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
). i.e., consider the hamiltonian
H_{S=1/2}=\sum_{j,\alpha}t_{\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}_{j}\tau^{\alpha}_{j}\sigma^{\alpha}_{j+1}
, where the sum is over
\alpha=x,y,z
and
\sigma^{\alpha}_{j}
and
\tau^{\alpha}_{j}
are Pauli operators on site
j\sigma
and link
j\tau
respectively. This hamiltonian has local symmetries
\tilde{A}^{\alpha}_{j}=\tau^{\alpha}_{j-1}\sigma^{\alpha}_{j}\tau^{\alpha}_{j}
. Interestingly,

do not all commute with one another.
\tilde{A}^{\alpha}_{j},\tilde{A}^{\beta}_{j^{\prime}}
anticommute with each other if

and

, but commute otherwise. A scenario where the local symmetries do not all commute with each other is known

to give rise to an extensive degeneracy in each energy eigenvalue. Showing that the existence of these local symmetries with this algebra amounts to having a
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{L}
symmetry with a projective representation (see
SM
, Sec.
S7
), we prove that each eigenvalue is at least
2^{L-2}
degenerate. In this process, we develop a systematic procedure for computing degeneracies of hamiltonians with such local symmetries.

Discussion
—
In this paper, we have illustrated how kinematic constraints in a gauge theory can manifest an LSM-type theorem. In the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
theory of matter coupled to gauge degrees of freedom we construct, imposing the Gauss law gives rise to a U
(1)
symmetry, whose generator anticommutes with the lattice reflection operator, leading to the LSM theorem in the Gauss law subspace. The theorem forces the ground state to either spontaneously break a symmetry, or be gapless.

Performing a nonlocal transformation, we have identified a gapless point, at which the model admits a description in terms of noninteracting Dirac fermions with a constraint on the fermion number. The model is a natural platform to study phase diagram topological defects
Prakash et al. 2023
;
Hsin et al. 2020
;
Manjunath and Else 2026
in families of SSB phases. In particular, it will be interesting to study the nature of the singularity in the phase diagram at the gapless point
t_{x}=t_{y}=t_{z}=1
,
K_{x}=K_{y}=K_{z}=0
. In the parameter space
(\delta_{x},\delta_{y},K_{x},K_{y})
, where
t_{x}=1+\delta_{x},t_{y}=1+\delta_{y},t_{z}=1-\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}
, is the gapless point an isolated critical point, or does it lie on a higher-dimensional critical manifold? It will also be interesting to explore the dynamics of the system in the constrained Hilbert space in connection with quantum scar states and Hilbert space fragmentation
Mukherjee et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Mohapatra and Balram 2023
.

(2^{L}+2\times(-1)^{L})/3
, giving us
D(L)=2^{L}+2\times(-1)^{L}
as before.

Let us now discuss the operator content on this side of the mapping. The Pauli-
3
operators
\mu^{(3)}_{j}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{0\tau}
are valid operators of the theory. We define the Pauli-
1
and Pauli-
2
operators
\mu^{(1)},\mu^{(2)}
via their action on the eigenstates of
\mu^{(3)}
,
\{{|\mu\rangle},\mu=\pm 1\}
, as
\mu^{(1)}{|\mu\rangle}={|-\mu\rangle}
,
\mu^{(2)}{|\mu\rangle}=(\mathrm{i})^{\mu}{|-\mu\rangle}
. Importantly, the operators
\mu^{(1)}_{j}
and
\mu^{(2)}_{j}
are not allowed in the Gauss law Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}_{G}
since they do not respect the constraint Eq. (
S4.21
): these operators change
M=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{j}\mu_{j}
by
\pm 2/3
. However, as we will see below, combinations like
\mu^{(1)}_{j}\mu^{(2)}_{j+1}-\mu^{(2)}_{j}\mu^{(1)}_{j+1}
are allowed. Now, what is an operator that acts as a “cyclic raising operator” on
\mathcal{Z}_{0\tau}
, but leaves all
\mu^{(3)}_{j}
invariant? Clearly, such an operator must act as a raising operator on all
\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}
. It is
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}=\prod_{j}(\mathcal{X}_{j\tau})
. We have
\mathcal{Z}_{0\tau}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}=\omega\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{Z}_{0\tau}
and
[\tilde{\mathcal{X}},\mu^{(s)}_{j}]=0
for
s=1,2,3
. Now, we are ready to see how the hamiltonian of Eq. (
S3.19
) transforms under this mapping.

First, consider the potential term
(S^{\alpha}_{j\tau})^{2}=1-P^{\alpha}_{j\tau}
, where
P^{\alpha}_{j\tau}=(1+\xi_{\alpha}^{\ast}\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}+\xi_{\alpha}\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}^{\dagger})/3
is the projector on to the state
\alpha
on the link
j\tau
. Hence we have

\begin{split}P^{\alpha}_{j\tau}&=1-(S^{\alpha}_{j\tau})^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\left(\xi_{\alpha}^{\ast}\mathcal{Z}_{0\tau}\exp{\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{3}\sum_{1\leq k\leq j}\mu^{(3)}_{k}\right)}+\text{h.c}\right)\end{split}

(S4.23)

Now, consider the action of the hopping term
{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha}_{j}=-P^{\alpha}_{j-1\tau}S^{\alpha}_{j\tau}P^{\alpha}_{j+1\tau}
for
1\leq j<L
on the state
{|\psi\rangle}={|v_{0};\mu_{1}^{\prime}\dots\mu_{L}^{\prime}\rangle}
.
{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha}_{j}{|\psi\rangle}\neq 0
only if
P^{\alpha}_{j-1\tau}{|\psi\rangle}=P^{\alpha}_{j+1\tau}{|\psi\rangle}={|\psi\rangle}
. Or, equivalently, only if
P^{\alpha}_{j-1\tau}{|\psi\rangle}={|\psi\rangle}
and
\mu_{j}^{\prime}+\mu_{j+1}^{\prime}=0
. We have

{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha}_{j}{|v_{0};\mu_{1}^{\prime}\dots\mu_{j}^{\prime}\mu_{j+1}^{\prime}\dots\mu_{L}^{\prime}\rangle}=P^{\alpha}_{j-1\tau}(-\mathrm{i}\mu_{j}^{\prime})\delta_{\mu_{j}^{\prime},-\mu_{j+1}^{\prime}}{|v_{0};\mu_{1}^{\prime}\dots\mu_{j+1}^{\prime}\mu_{j}^{\prime}\dots\mu_{L}^{\prime}\rangle}
for
1\leq j<L
. Hence,

{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}P^{\alpha}_{j-1\tau}\left(\mu^{(1)}_{j}\mu^{(2)}_{j+1}-\mu^{(2)}_{j}\mu^{(1)}_{j+1}\right),\,\,\,1\leq j<L

(S4.24)

where
P^{\alpha}_{j\tau}
is defined in Eq. (
S4.23
). The case
{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha}_{L}
must be treated separately since it can change the eigenvalue of
\mathcal{Z}_{0\tau}\equiv\mathcal{Z}_{L\tau}
. We have
{h^{\prime}}^{\alpha}_{L}{|v_{0};\mu_{1}^{\prime}\mu_{2}^{\prime}\dots\mu_{L-1}^{\prime}\mu_{L}^{\prime}\rangle}=P^{\alpha}_{L-1}(-\mathrm{i}\mu_{L}^{\prime})\delta_{\mu_{L}^{\prime},-\mu_{1}^{\prime}}{|\omega^{-\mu_{1}^{\prime}}v_{0};\mu_{L}^{\prime}\mu_{2}^{\prime}\dots\mu_{L-1}^{\prime}\mu_{1}^{\prime}\rangle}
, hence,

h^{\alpha}_{L}=\frac{1}{2}P^{\alpha}_{L-1}\left(\mu^{(1)}_{L}\mu^{(2)}_{1}-\mu^{(2)}_{L}\mu^{(1)}_{1}\right)\tilde{\mathcal{X}}^{-\mu_{1}}.

(S4.25)

Hence, under the mapping to qubit operators, the hamiltonian Eq. (
S3.19
) (up to the addition of a constant term) transforms to

H_{G}^{(\text{q})}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\sum_{\alpha}t_{\alpha}P^{\alpha}_{j-1\tau}\left(\mu^{(1)}_{j}\mu^{(2)}_{j+1}-\mu^{(2)}_{j}\mu^{(1)}_{j+1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha}t_{\alpha}P^{\alpha}_{L-1\tau}\left(\mu^{(1)}_{L}\mu^{(2)}_{1}-\mu^{(2)}_{L}\mu^{(1)}_{1}\right)\tilde{\mathcal{X}}^{-\mu_{1}}+\sum_{j,\alpha}K_{\alpha}P^{\alpha}_{j\tau}

(S4.26)

where
P^{\alpha}_{j\tau}
consists of an operator string involving the operators
\{\mu^{(3)}_{j}\}
as given in Eq. (
S4.23
). We note that for general values of the parameters
t_{\alpha},K_{\alpha}
, the hamiltonian Eq. (
S4.26
) is not local in terms of the qubit operators
\{\mu^{(s)}\},s=1,2,3
. Let us do a Jordan-Wigner (JW) transformation on the qubit operators
\mu^{(s)}_{j},s=1,2,3
. The JW transformation is

\begin{split}\mu^{(1)}_{j}&=(c_{j}+c_{j}^{\dagger})\prod_{1\leq k<j}\mu^{(3)}_{k}\\
\mu^{(2)}_{j}&=-\mathrm{i}(c_{j}-c_{j}^{\dagger})\prod_{1\leq k<j}\mu^{(3)}_{k}\end{split}

(S4.27)

Then
\mu^{(3)}_{j}=1-2n_{j}
, where
n_{j}=c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}
is the fermion number. Let
\mathcal{N}=\sum_{j}n_{j}=(L-3M)/2
be the total fermion number operator. The constraint Eq. (
S4.21
) expressed in terms of the fermion operators then becomes

\exp{\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{3}(L+\mathcal{N})\right)}=1

(S4.28)

The fermion creation and annihilation operators
c_{j},c_{j}^{\dagger}
are not valid operators in the theory because of this constraint. The hamiltonian Eq. (
S4.26
) after JW transforms to

H_{G}^{(\text{f})}=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\sum_{\alpha}t_{\alpha}P^{\alpha}_{j-1\tau}\left(-\mathrm{i}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j+1}+\mathrm{i}c_{j+1}^{\dagger}c_{j}\right)+\sum_{\alpha}t_{\alpha}P^{\alpha}_{L-1\tau}\left(\mathrm{i}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}c_{L}^{\dagger}c_{1}-\mathrm{i}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}^{\dagger}c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{L}\right)\exp{\left(\mathrm{i}\pi\mathcal{N}\right)}+\sum_{j,\alpha}K_{\alpha}P^{\alpha}_{j\tau}

(S4.29)

The appearance of the factor
\exp{(\mathrm{i}\pi\mathcal{N})}
in the
L^{\text{th}}
term when performing JW transformation with periodic boundary conditions is well known. The projector
P^{\alpha}_{j\tau}
in terms of the fermion operators is

P^{\alpha}_{j\tau}=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\left(\xi_{\alpha}^{\ast}\mathcal{Z}_{0\tau}e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi j}{3}}\exp{\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{3}\sum_{1\leq k\leq j}n_{k}\right)}+\text{h.c}\right)

(S4.30)

This exact mapping to the fermionic hamiltonian offers us another way to see the existence of the conserved quantity
M=(L-2\mathcal{N})/3
in the Gauss law subspace, since the above hamiltonian in Eq. (
S4.29
) clearly conserves the total fermion number
\mathcal{N}
.

When
t_{x}=t_{y}=t_{z}(=1)
and
K_{x}=K_{y}=K_{z}(=0)
, because of the identity
\sum_{\alpha}P^{\alpha}_{j\tau}=1
,
H_{G}^{(\text{q})}
is local in the qbit operators. At this point,
H_{G}^{(\text{f})}
is also local and quadratic in the fermion operators. They are given by

\begin{split}H_{\text{gl}}^{(\text{q})}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\left(\mu^{(1)}_{j}\mu^{(2)}_{j+1}-\mu^{(2)}_{j}\mu^{(1)}_{j+1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mu^{(1)}_{L}\mu^{(2)}_{1}-\mu^{(2)}_{L}\mu^{(1)}_{1}\right)\tilde{\mathcal{X}}^{-\mu_{1}}\\
H_{\text{gl}}^{(\text{f})}&=\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}\left(-\mathrm{i}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j+1}+\mathrm{i}c_{j+1}^{\dagger}c_{j}\right)+\left(\mathrm{i}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}c_{L}^{\dagger}c_{1}-\mathrm{i}\tilde{\mathcal{X}}^{\dagger}c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{L}\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\pi\mathcal{N}}\end{split}

(S4.31)

S6
Correlation functions at the gapless point

As discussed in the main text, to diagonalize the hamiltonian at the gapless point, we perform a transformation
c_{j}\to e^{\mathrm{i}\phi_{j}}c_{j}
so that the coefficient of
c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j+1}
is equal to
e^{\mathrm{i}\Phi/L}
for all
j
. The spin correlation functions we calculate below involve only the fermion particle numbers
n_{j}
, which are anyway invariant under this transformation. Hence, we can work with the correlation functions of the transformed
c
operators.

S6.1
Correlation function as a Toeplitz determinant

In the ground state, consider the correlation function
\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{q}e^{-\mathrm{i}q(j-k)}\left\langle n_{q}\right\rangle
. Clearly,
\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}\right\rangle=1/2
. For
L
a multiple of
4
,
\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{m=-L/4}^{L/4-1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi m}{L}(j-k)}.
For
j\neq k
,

\begin{split}&\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle=\frac{\sin{\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(j-k)\right)}}{L\sin{\left(\frac{\pi}{L}(j-k)\right)}}e^{\mathrm{i}\pi(j-k)/L}\\
&\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle-\left\langle c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{j}\right\rangle=\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{L}\sin{\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(j-k)\right)}\end{split}

(S6.32)

If
L
is even, but not a multiple of
4
(for one of the ground states),
\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{m=-(L+2)/4}^{(L-6)/4}e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi m}{L}(j-k)}
, and for
j\neq k

\begin{split}\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle&=\frac{\sin{\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(j-k)\right)}}{L\sin{\left(\frac{\pi}{L}(j-k)\right)}}e^{2\mathrm{i}\pi(j-k)/L}\\
\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle-\left\langle c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{j}\right\rangle&=\frac{4\mathrm{i}}{L}\sin{\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(j-k)\right)}\cos{\left(\frac{\pi}{L}(j-k)\right)}\end{split}

(S6.33)

In any case, in the thermodynamic limit
L\gg|j-k|
, (we first take the limit
L\to\infty
), the correlation function

\left\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c c}1/2&\text{if }j=k\\
\frac{\sin{\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(j-k)\right)}}{\pi(j-k)}&\text{if }j\neq k\end{array}\right.

(S6.34)

Let us express the spin correlation functions in terms of the fermion operators. We have
(S^{\alpha}_{j\tau})^{2}-2/3=-(\xi_{\alpha}^{\ast}\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}+\xi_{\alpha}\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}^{\dagger})/3
, where
\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}=\mathcal{Z}_{0\tau}e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{3}j}\exp{\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{3}\sum_{1\leq k\leq j}n_{k}\right)}
(Eq. (
S4.30
)). At the gapless point, the ground state is an eigenstate of
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
, hence
\left\langle\mathcal{Z}_{0\tau}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}\right\rangle=0
and
\left\langle(S^{\alpha}_{j\tau})^{2}\right\rangle=2/3
in the ground state. Now, consider

\begin{split}&\left\langle(S^{\alpha}_{j\tau})^{2}(S^{\beta}_{j+r\tau})^{2}\right\rangle-4/9=\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\frac{1}{9}\left(\xi_{\alpha}^{\ast}\xi_{\beta}\left\langle\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}\mathcal{Z}_{j+r\tau}^{\dagger}\right\rangle+\text{c.c}\right).\end{split}

(S6.35)

We have

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}\mathcal{Z}_{j+r\tau}^{\dagger}\right\rangle=e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{3}r}\left\langle\exp{\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{3}\sum_{j+1\leq k\leq j+r}n_{k}\right)}\right\rangle

(S6.36)

We can write
\exp{\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{3}n_{k}\right)}=(c_{k}+\omega c_{k}^{\dagger})(c_{k}+c_{k}^{\dagger})
. Defining
a_{k}=c_{k}+\omega c_{k}^{\dagger}
,
b_{k}=c_{k}+c_{k}^{\dagger}
,
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle a_{k}a_{l}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\omega\left\langle b_{k}b_{l}\right\rangle=\omega\delta_{kl}+\omega\left\langle c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{l}\right\rangle-\omega\left\langle c_{l}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle

(S6.37)

\displaystyle\left\langle a_{k}b_{l}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{kl}+\omega\left\langle c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{l}\right\rangle-\left\langle c_{l}^{\dagger}c_{k}\right\rangle

(S6.38)

Using Eq. (
S6.34
), we have in the thermodynamic limit
L\to\infty
(
L\gg|k-l|
),

\begin{split}\left\langle a_{k}a_{l}\right\rangle&=\omega\left\langle b_{k}b_{l}\right\rangle=\omega\delta_{kl}\\
\left\langle a_{k}b_{l}\right\rangle&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c c}\frac{\omega+1}{2}&\text{if }k=l\\
\frac{(\omega-1)}{\pi(k-l)}\sin{\frac{\pi}{2}(k-l)}&\text{if }k\neq l\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(S6.39)

Now,
\left\langle\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}\mathcal{Z}_{j+r\tau}^{\dagger}\right\rangle=e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{3}r}\left\langle\prod_{k=j+1}^{j+r}(a_{k}b_{k})\right\rangle
. Using Wick’s theorem and Eq. (
S6.39
), we can write in the thermodynamic limit

\left\langle\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}\mathcal{Z}_{j+r\tau}^{\dagger}\right\rangle=e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi r}{3}}\text{det}(F^{\prime})
, where
F^{\prime}
is the
r\times r
Toeplitz matrix with the entries
F^{\prime}_{k,l}=F^{\prime}_{k-l}=\left\langle a_{k}b_{l}\right\rangle
for
0\leq k,l\leq r-1
. Factoring out
e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{2\pi}{3}}e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{2}k}
from every row
k
and
e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{2}l}
from every column
l
,
\left\langle\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}\mathcal{Z}_{j+r\tau}^{\dagger}\right\rangle=\cos{(\pi r)}\,\text{det}(F)
, where
F
is a real Toeplitz matrix,
F_{k,l}=F_{k-l}
with

\displaystyle F_{k-l}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c c}1/2&\text{if }k=l\\
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi(k-l)}\sin^{2}{\left(\frac{\pi}{2}(k-l)\right)}&\text{if }k\neq l\end{array}\right.

\text{det}(F)
is real since
F
is real.

S6.2
Asymptotic limit

To understand the asymptotic behavior of the correlation function when the separation between the lattice points
r\to\infty
(note we have already taken the limit
L\to\infty
), we need to know the asymptotic behavior of
\text{det}(F)
as the dimension of the matrix
r\to\infty
. We now state the Fisher-Hartwig conjecture (see Ref.
Deift et al. 2013
for a review), which can be used to determine the asymptotic behavior of Toeplitz determinants.

Let
f:S^{1}\to\mathbb{C}
, and let
\{F_{p}\}
be the Fourier coefficients of
f
:

\begin{split}f(\theta)&=\sum_{p=-\infty}^{\infty}F_{p}e^{\mathrm{i}p\theta}\\
F_{p}&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\text{d}\theta f(\theta)e^{-\mathrm{i}p\theta}\end{split}

(S6.42)

Let
f(\theta)
, with
z=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
, be of the form

f(\theta)=e^{W(z)}z^{\sum_{s=1}^{Q}\eta_{s}}\prod_{s=1}^{Q}|z-z_{s}|^{2\nu_{s}}w_{z_{s},\eta_{s}}(\theta)z_{s}^{-\eta_{s}},

(S6.43)

where

w_{z_{s},\eta_{s}}(\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{\mathrm{i}\pi\eta_{s}},0\leq\theta<\theta_{s}\\
e^{-\mathrm{i}\pi\eta_{s}},\theta_{s}\leq\theta<2\pi\end{array}\right.,

(S6.44)

and
W:S^{1}\to\mathbb{C}
is a smooth function. Also,
z_{s}=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{s}}
,
0<\theta_{s}<2\pi
,
\eta_{s}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\text{Re}(\nu_{s})>-1/2
for
s=1,\dots Q
. Then, according to the Fisher-Hartwig conjecture, the determinant
\text{det}(F^{(r)})
of the
r\times r
Toeplitz matrix
F^{(r)}_{k,l}=F_{k-l},0\leq k,l\leq r-1
has the asymptotic form

\text{det}(F^{(r)})\sim C\times e^{rW_{0}}\times r^{\sum_{s}(\nu_{s}^{2}-\eta_{s}^{2})},

(S6.45)

where
C
is a constant,
W_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\text{d}\theta\,W(e^{\mathrm{i}\theta})
. This conjecture has been proven for
|\text{Re}({\eta_{s}})|<1/2,|\text{Re}({\nu_{s}})|<1/2
, which includes the case in which we use this below. Now, for the Toeplitz matrix Eq. (
S6.1
) we are concerned with,

\begin{split}f(\theta)&=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p}\sin^{2}{\left(\frac{\pi p}{2}\right)}\left(e^{\mathrm{i}p\theta}-e^{-\mathrm{i}p\theta}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\left(\left(\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2p-1}}{2p-1}\right)-\left(\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{1-2p}}{2p-1}\right)\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}(\log{(1+z)}-\log{(1-z)}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\log{(1+1/z)}+\log{(1-1/z)})\end{split}

(S6.46)

Which gives

\displaystyle f(\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c c}e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{3}}&\,\,0<\theta<\pi\\
e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{3}}&\,\,\pi<\theta<2\pi\\
\end{array}\right.

f
can be cast in the Fisher-Hartwig form Eq. (
S6.43
) with
W(e^{\mathrm{i}\theta})=0
,
Q=2
,
\eta_{1}=1/3,\eta_{2}=-1/3
,
\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}=0
,
\theta_{1}=\pi,\theta_{2}=0
:

\displaystyle f(\theta)=w_{-1,1/3}(\theta)w_{1,-1/3}(\theta)

(S6.49)

Using Eq. (
S6.45
), we conclude that asymptotically, for large
r
,
\text{det}(F)\sim C\frac{1}{r^{2/9}}
. Hence,
\left\langle\mathcal{Z}_{j\tau}\mathcal{Z}_{j+r\tau}^{\dagger}\right\rangle\sim C\frac{\cos{(\pi r)}}{r^{2/9}}
. Using the formula given for the constant
C
in Ref.
Deift et al. 2013
,
C=\left(2^{-1/9}G(4/3)G(2/3)\right)^{2}
, where
G(z)
is the Barnes-G function, is a positive constant. Finally, from Eq. (
S6.35
), we have
\left\langle(S^{\alpha}_{j\tau})^{2}(S^{\beta}_{j+r\tau})^{2}\right\rangle-4/9\sim C_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\cos{(\pi r)}}{r^{2/9}}
, where
C_{\alpha\beta}=2C/9
,
-C/9
for
\alpha=\beta
,
\alpha\neq\beta
respectively.

S7
Spin-
1/2
model

The spin-
1/2
model we consider is

H_{S=1/2}=\sum_{j,\alpha}t_{\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}_{j}\tau^{\alpha}_{j}\sigma^{\alpha}_{j+1}

(S7.50)

with periodic boundary conditions. This hamiltonian has local symmetries
\tilde{A}^{\alpha}_{j}=\tau^{\alpha}_{j-1}\sigma^{\alpha}_{j}\tau^{\alpha}_{j}
. These operators do not all commute with each other. If
\alpha\neq\beta
and
j^{\prime}-j=0,\pm 1
,
\tilde{A}^{\alpha}_{j},\tilde{A}^{\beta}_{j^{\prime}}
anticommute. Otherwise, they commute. We show this implies that the symmetry group
\mathbbm{G}=(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{L}
is realized as a projective representation on the Hilbert space.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{e,a,b,c=ab\}
.
Consider the projective representation of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
on the Hilbert space of each site and link with the multiplier system
\omega_{0}
:
u_{j\sigma}(e)=1,u_{j\sigma}(a)=\mathrm{i}\sigma^{x}_{j},u_{j\sigma}(b)=\mathrm{i}\sigma^{y}_{j},u_{j\sigma}(c)=\mathrm{i}\sigma^{z}_{j}
,
u_{j\tau}(e)=1,u_{j\tau}(a)=\mathrm{i}\tau^{x}_{j},u_{j\tau}(b)=\mathrm{i}\tau^{y}_{j},u_{j\tau}(c)=\mathrm{i}\tau^{z}_{j}
. The symmetry group associated with the set of local symmetries discussed above is
\mathbbm{G}\cong(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{L}
. Let
\mathbbm{g}=\left(g_{1},g_{2},\dots g_{L}\right)\in\mathbbm{G}
, where each
g_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. The action of the symmetry group is

U(\mathbbm{g})=\prod_{j}\left(u_{j\sigma}(g_{j})u_{j\tau}(g_{j}g_{j+1})\right)

(S7.51)

and the multiplier system associated with this representation is

\Omega(\mathbbm{g},\mathbbm{h})=\prod_{j}\left(\omega_{0}\left(g_{j},h_{j}\right)\omega_{0}\left(g_{j}g_{j+1},h_{j}h_{j+1}\right)\right).

(S7.52)

The object of interest for us is the subgroup
{\cal M}<\mathbbm{G}
defined as

\displaystyle{\cal M}=\{\mathbbm{g}\in\mathbbm{G}\,\,|\,\,[U(\mathbbm{g}),U(\mathbbm{h})]=0\,\,\forall\mathbbm{h}\in\mathbbm{G}\}

(S7.53)

For a certain class of projective representations which includes the present case, we show below that the dimension of any irreducible representation of the projective representation is
d=\sqrt{|\mathbbm{G}|/|\mathcal{M}|}
. In the present case,
(g_{1},g_{2},\dots g_{L})\in{\cal M}\implies g_{j-1}g_{j}g_{j+1}=1
for all
j
. Hence, if
L
is not a multiple of
3
, then
{\cal M}=1
is the trivial group, and the degeneracy of each eigen space is (at least)
2^{L}
. If
L
is a multiple of
3
, then
{\cal M}=\{\left(g_{1},g_{2},g_{1}g_{2},g_{1},g_{2},g_{1}g_{2},\dots,g_{1},g_{2},g_{1}g_{2}\right)|g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}\}

|{\cal M}|=4^{2}

S7.1
Dimension of irrep

Let
\mathbbm{G}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}
for some
N
. Then the Schur multiplier
H^{2}\left(\mathbbm{G},\text{U}(1)\right)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N(N-1)/2}
. Let
U
be a projective representation of
\mathbbm{G}
on a Hilbert space with
\Omega
as the associated multiplier system. Also, let
\Omega(\mathbbm{g},\mathbbm{h})^{2}=1

\forall\mathbbm{g},\mathbbm{h}\in\mathbbm{G}
. We want to find the irreducible representations contained in the direct sum decomposition of
U
.

Now, the projective representation
U
can be lifted to a linear representation
\rho
of a central extension
X
of
\mathbbm{G}
by
V\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{E,F\}
. i.e.,
X/V=\mathbbm{G}
. As a set,
X=\{(\mathbbm{g},a)|\mathbbm{g}\in\mathbbm{G},a\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}\}
. The group multiplication in
X
is

\displaystyle(\mathbbm{g},a_{1})(\mathbbm{h},a_{2})

\displaystyle=(\mathbbm{g}\mathbbm{h},a_{1}a_{2}\alpha(\mathbbm{g},\mathbbm{h}))

(S7.54)

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathbbm{g},\mathbbm{h})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}E&\text{if }\Omega(\mathbbm{g},\mathbbm{h})=1\\
F&\text{if }\Omega(\mathbbm{g},\mathbbm{h})=-1\end{array}\right.

\alpha
is an element of the second cohomology group
H^{2}\left(\mathbbm{G},{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\right)
. The linear representation
\rho
of
X
is
\rho((\mathbbm{g},a))=\lambda(a)U(\mathbbm{g})
, where
\lambda
is the nontrivial linear representation of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
;
\lambda:E\mapsto 1,F\mapsto-1
.

We want to find the dimensions of linear irreducible representations of
X
that descend to a projective representation of
\mathbbm{G}
with the same multiplier system
\Omega
. Let
\rho_{0}
be a linear irrep of
X
. Any irreducible linear representation when restricted to the group center acts as a scalar representation (due to Schur’s lemma). In fact, the same is true for any subgroup of the center. If a linear irrep
\rho_{0}
of
X
has to descend to a projective irrep of
\mathbbm{G}
with the multiplier system
\Omega
, then it should act as the nontrivial linear representation
\lambda
on the central subgroup
V\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(\mathbbm{g})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}1&\text{if }\mathbbm{g}=(1_{\mathbbm{G}},E)\\
-1&\text{if }\mathbbm{g}=(1_{\mathbbm{G}},F)\end{array}\right.

Now, the central subgroup
V
is also the commutator subgroup
[X,X]
of
X
. This can be seen by noting that
\mathbbm{G}=X/V
is abelian. Thus, any commutator
xy{x}^{-1}{y}^{-1}\in V
, where
x\in X,y\in X
. If
x,y
do not commute, then
xy{x}^{-1}{y}^{-1}=(1_{\mathbbm{G}},F)
and
\rho_{0}(x)\rho_{0}(y){\rho_{0}(x)}^{-1}{\rho_{0}(y)}^{-1}=-1
. If
x\notin\mathcal{Z}\left(X\right)
(center of
X
) then
\rho_{0}(x)=-\rho_{0}(y)\rho_{0}(x){\rho_{0}(y)}^{-1}
for some
y\in X
. Hence
\text{tr}\rho_{0}(x)=0

\forall x\notin\mathcal{Z}\left(X\right)
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\chi_{\rho_{0}}(x)=\text{tr}\rho_{0}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}\gamma(x)d_{\rho_{0}}&\text{if }x\in\mathcal{Z}\left(X\right)\\
0&\text{if }x\notin\mathcal{Z}\left(X\right)\end{array}\right.

where
\gamma
is a one-dimensional complex representation of the center
\mathcal{Z}\left(X\right)
, and
d_{\rho_{0}}
is the dimension of the irreuducible representation
\rho_{0}
. i.e.,
|\chi_{\rho_{0}}(x)|=d_{\rho_{0}}
if
x\in\mathcal{Z}\left(X\right)
,
0
otherwise. Using the relation
\sum_{x\in X}|\chi_{\rho_{0}}(x)|^{2}=|X|
,

d_{\rho_{0}}=\sqrt{\frac{|X|}{|\mathcal{Z}\left(X\right)|}}
.
Let
X=(\mathbbm{g},a)
. Then

x\in\mathcal{Z}\left(X\right)\iff xy=yx\,\forall y\in X\iff\alpha(\mathbbm{g},\mathbbm{h})=\alpha(\mathbbm{h},\mathbbm{g})\,\forall\mathbbm{h}\in\mathbbm{G}\iff\Omega(\mathbbm{g},\mathbbm{h})=\Omega(\mathbbm{h},\mathbbm{g})\,\forall\mathbbm{h}\in\mathbbm{G}\iff U(\mathbbm{g})U(\mathbbm{h})=U(\mathbbm{h})U(\mathbbm{g})\forall\mathbbm{h}\in\mathbbm{G}
.
Define
\mathcal{M}=\{\mathbbm{g}\in\mathbbm{G}|U(\mathbbm{g})U(\mathbbm{h})=U(\mathbbm{h})U(\mathbbm{g})\forall h\in\mathbbm{G}\}<\mathbbm{G}
, a subgroup of
\mathbbm{G}
. Then
\mathcal{Z}\left(X\right)=\{(\mathbbm{g},a)|\mathbbm{g}\in\mathcal{M},a\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}\}
,
|\mathcal{Z}\left(X\right)|=2\times|\mathcal{M}|
. Hence the dimension of any irrep is

\displaystyle d_{\rho_{0}}=\sqrt{\frac{|\mathbbm{G}|}{|{\cal M}|}}
