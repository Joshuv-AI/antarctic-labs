ABSTRACT
Repurposing depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs for carbon dioxide (CO2) storage is a promising
option, however, research is needed to credibly forecast pressure and saturation responses and to
ensure effective long-term containment. This study uses numerical simulation to forecast pressure
and saturation changes in response to CO2 injection and storage in depleted hydrocarbon and saline
reservoirs, considering factors such as initial depletion levels and saturation states, injection
strategies, and boundary condition assumptions to provide insights for stakeholders. The spatial
extent of reservoir free-phase CO2 saturation and substantially elevated pressure are considered as
a proxy for potential unwanted fluid migration to overlying receptors of concern. These
assessments highlight that the depletion level during CO2-enhanced oil recovery (CO2-EOR)
operations significantly impacts miscibility and CO2 plume behavior. Additionally, the modeling
domain and injection strategies influence pressure buildup differently between dedicated storage
and EOR injection, providing valuable insights for stakeholders in transitioning to CO2 storage.

1. INTRODUCTION
Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is a key technology for mitigating anthropogenic gas emissions
which involves the process of obtaining carbon dioxide (CO2) from industrial sources or directly
from the atmosphere and securely storing it in subsurface geological formations. The geological
storage mainly targets several types of injection zones: saline formations, depleted oil and gas
reservoirs, non-mineable coal beds, and basalt formations (Raza et al., 2022). There are four major
mechanisms used for CO2 storage: (1) structural trapping, where CO2 is physically trapped under
impermeable caprocks; (2) residual trapping, where CO2 is immobilized in pore spaces by capillary
forces; (3) solubility trapping, where CO2 dissolves in formation water over time; and (4) mineral
trapping, where CO2 reacts with formation minerals to form stable carbonates, providing long-
term security.
The storage of CO2 in depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs is gaining traction as a viable CO2
mitigation strategy. Leveraging their geological properties and existing infrastructure, these
reservoirs provide opportunities for large-scale CO2 sequestration while presenting distinct
challenges, particularly in reservoir transitioning, and economic viability. Such opportunities and
benefits are summarized as:
 1. Proven geological capacity with established geological knowledge: Depleted reservoirs are
 characterized by geological formations that have securely trapped hydrocarbons for
 millions of years. Extensive exploration and production data make depleted reservoirs
 well-characterized for CO2 injection while known reservoir structures, porosity,
 permeability, and caprock integrity increase confidence in long-term storage potential. This
 proven containment integrity is advantageous for long-term CO2 storage (Global CCS
      Institute, 2023).
 2. Infrastructure reutilization: Existing infrastructure, such as wells, pipelines, and processing
 facilities, significantly reduces the initial project expense and accelerates project timelines
 and cost savings (CER, 2023). The existing wells and deployed technologies can be reused
 for monitoring purposes that further reduce capital expenditure. A recent example is the
 Alberta Carbon Trunk Line in Canada which utilizes legacy pipelines for cost-effective
 CO2 transport and injection (CER, 2023).
 3. Large global storage potential: Depleted reservoirs are estimated to have a global storage
      capacity of 800-1,200 Gt CO2 (IPCC, 2023).
 4. Dual benefits with enhanced oil recovery (EOR): Integration of EOR with CO2 storage
 generates revenue while permanently sequestering CO2 or making up offset of the storage
 cost. One example is the Weyburn-Midale project in Canada which stored over 31 Mt of
 CO2 while recovering additional hydrocarbons (IEAGHG, 2023). Such opportunities can
 be also extended to offshore storage potential such as in the North Sea, providing
 opportunities for large-scale storage with minimal impact on populated areas. Norway’s
 Sleipner project has stored over 20 Mt CO2 offshore, demonstrating technical success
 (Equinor, 2023). Additionally, Project Greensand in Denmark aims to store 400,000 tons
 of CO2 annually (Adomaitis, 2024).

 2

One of the most popular discussions of storing CO2 in the depleted hydrocarbon formations is the
transition of existing CO2-EOR potentials to dedicated CO2 storage for leveraging the existing
wells, infrastructure, and known knowledge and practices for potential benefits to CO2 storage,
especially cost reduction and offset. However, there are some technical challenges for permanent
CO2 storage in depleted hydrocarbon fields such as:
 1. Well integrity: Over relatively long operation history, abandoned and/or legacy wells pose
 a significant risk of CO2 flow through the wellbore. Poorly sealed or degraded wells may
 compromise containment integrity. Advanced well plugging and abandonment
 technologies are necessary to address this integrity (Chiaramonte et al., 2008).
 2. Induced seismicity and pressure management: CO2 injection into depleted reservoirs can
 lead to pressure buildup because of the operational schemes changing from source-sink in
 EOR to sink focused dedicated CO2 storage, increasing the risk potential of fault
 reactivation. Therefore, the strategic injection and pressure buildup study help to mitigate
 potentials impacts.
 3. Uncertainty in CO2 behavior: Interactions between CO2, residual hydrocarbons, and
 formation water can affect storage efficiency and lead to unintended chemical reactions.
 Dissolution and mineral trapping processes vary significantly with reservoir conditions
      (Bachu, 2003).
 4. High monitoring costs: Long-term monitoring to ensure containment integrity and detect
 potential leaks is resource intensive.
Han et al. (2010) presents a comprehensive analysis of the CO2 trapping mechanisms in geologic
sequestration at the Scurry Area Canyon Reef Operator's Committee (SACROC) Unit in the
Permian Basin. They focused on predicting and comparing major trapping mechanisms such as
hydro-stratigraphic, residual, brine-solubility, oil-solubility, and mineralization, and also
considered the impact of initial fluid saturation assumption. The present study builds upon this
foundation by incorporating scenario-based simulations that reflect EOR-to-storage transitions,
pressure buildup, and Area of Review (AoR). In addition, the present study aims to quantify the
impacts of model domain size, boundary conditions, reservoir fluids, and reservoir depletion on
the effectiveness of CO2 storage in depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs.

2. CO2-EOR REVIEW, MODEL SELECTION, AND DESCRIPTION

2.1 CO2-EOR REVIEW
CO2 injected into subsurface oil formations for tertiary EOR purposes has been practiced for more
than half of a century. The first large-scale project of CO2-EOR in SACROC began in 1972 in
Texas, (Gill, 1982). The first commercial CO2 storage project for mitigating anthropogenic gas
emissions was the Sleipner project which began in 1996 in Norway(Furre et al., 2017). Several
other CO2 storage projects have been implemented worldwide including: the Weyburn and
Aquistore projects in Canada (Brown et al., 2017; Whittaker and Worth, 2011); the CO2CRC
Otway project in Australia (Sharma et al., 2011); the Ketzin project in Germany (Martens et al.,
2013); the Tomakomai project in Japan (Sawada et al., 2018); and the Salah project in Algeria
(Bjørnarå et al., 2018). The topic of CO2 storage in depleted hydrocarbon fields has been the focus
of many researchers. Table 1 shows a brief summary of currently operating major CO2-EOR fields,
CO2 sources, and pipeline infrastructure in the United States.

2.2 MODEL SELECTION AND GENERALIZATION
After reviewing the major current CO2-EOR fields, a typical representation of the Canyon Reef
was employed as the base of the geological model for this case study based on data availability.
The geological structure of the SACROC Unit is shown in Figure 1. This sector lies in the
southeastern segment of the Horseshoe Atoll within the Midland Basin, Texas. The Horseshoe
Atoll represents a carbonate reef composed of bioclastic debris and bound stone. The carbonate
reef system features bedded bioclastic limestone with thin shale beds. The reservoir structure
includes deposits from Strawn, Canyon, and Cisco Formations of the Pennsylvanian and the
Wolfcamp Series of the Lower Permian. The hydrocarbon production mainly comes from the
Canyon and Cisco Formations, which are of the Pennsylvanian age. The carbonate reef system
was developed in the early Strawn (Desmoinesian), when the basin was near the equator.
Carbonate deposition continued through the Canyon (Missourian) and Cisco (Virgilian) periods.
Accumulation of carbonate sediments ceased during the Wolfcampian due to a significant influx
of fine-grained clastics. The Canyon and Cisco Formations are mainly composed of limestone,
with limited amounts of anhydrite, sand, chert, and shale present locally, as shown in Figure 1.
The low-permeability Wolfcamp shale of the lower Permian serves as a seal above the
Pennsylvanian Cisco and Canyon Formations (Feder, 2021; Han et al., 2010; Kalteyer, 2020).

 Figure 1: Canyon (Missourian) and Cisco lithology for the reviewed model in West Texas
 (Isdiken, 2013). Left: The areal map. Middle: The wells placement majorly in 5-spot pattern;
 “P” indicates producer, and “I” indicates injectors. Right: Formation structure example.

Over the past decades of operation, data, knowledge, and practices were gained, and are now
accessible. This includes geology, fluids dynamics, fluid-rock interactions, injection and
production measurements, and evolutions of the reservoir responses. Such detailed information
can be referred to the well documented references (Isdiken, 2013). This case study uses the
advantages and the available geological model and simulation findings to study the transition
objectives. The version of the accessible model has been adapted multiple times to generalize
geological and fluid features over the past two decades of CO2 feasibility studies in various projects
(Isdiken, 2013), shown as part of the north side as highlighted in the right inset in Figure 1. The
generalized model used in this study conserves the majority of the geological representations for

CO2-EOR fields but not the indications for any site-specific operations, impacts, and related results
(Isdiken, 2013).

2.3 MODEL MIRRORING EXTENSION
The generalized case study model as stated above is part of the entire sector with a size of 5 km
width and 10 km length. The geological representation shows the boundaries relatively isolated on
three sides, but one is open as shown on the left side in Figure 2. With the nature of the CO2-EOR,
there are sources and sinks which help balance the reservoir pressure. This means that the boundary
impacts may not be as significant as the one in dedicated CO2 storage, which considers the
injection without extraction, particularly the assumption taken for this use case. Thus, the CO2-
EOR model domain may not be large enough for transition needs such as pressure front study and
to quantify the impacts, especially, for the model with three sides of closed and one side of open
boundary conditions. To overcome the potential limitations of the model domain, a simple but
relatively practical step is to mirror the model along with Y/Z plan (at X-minimum) adding the
same model on the open boundary side based on the original domain as shown on the right side in
Figure 2. Through this simple innovation, the extended model conserves the same geological
representations as the original one, even varying it locally. The populated geological properties
and parameters of the model such as porosity, permeabilities, net to gross, fluid compositions,
pressures, and saturations are the same as original ones.

3. SCENARIOS DESIGN FOR CASE STUDY
Based on the generalization and mirrored extension models, several simulation scenarios were
designed (Table 2). The main objective of these simulation scenarios is to quantify the impact of
the model domain size, boundary conditions, reservoir fluids, and reservoir depletion on the
viability of CO2 storage in depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs and their impacts. The first two cases
are used as base cases along the original operation, under the business-as-usual (BAU) water
alternating gas (WAG) CO2 injection. The base cases also discuss the pressure development, the
migration of the gas plume, and the AoR which has been defined for protecting the underground
sources of drinking water (USDW). The first case (Case 1) for BAU is to cease the WAG EOR
operations at the simulation’s beginning time (simulation time=0) for use cases in this study. This
case plays as a baseline for comparison to other cases regarding reservoir responses, particularly
CO2 plume and pressure buildup. The second BAU case is Case 2, where 30 years of WAG
operations are applied until simulation time =30 years. This case offers a foundation for the
purpose of comparing the dedicated storage cases regarding CO2 plume movement and reservoir
pressure buildup for other cases. Case 3 is the first dedicated CO2 storage simulation to help
understand the CO2 fluid behaviors under the minimum miscibility pressure (MMP), which is
1,856 psi (~128 bar), due to depletion level. This case is designed to examine the depleted
hydrocarbon reservoir behavior under dedicated CO2 storage. The interpretation and comparison
of the results include the CO2 storage capacity, the buildup in the reservoir pressure due to CO2
injection, and the gas plume expansion over time. Case 4 studies the extended hydrocarbon
reservoir model after it is mirrored. This case can help understand the effect of the extended model
versus the aquifer/open boundary model (Case 3) on AoR, storage capacity, and pressure buildup.
Case 5 replaces the hydrocarbon reservoir fluids with saline, which is used to compare the CO2
plume movement and reservoir pressure buildup in the same geological settings as used in the
original model’s domain size. Case 6 employes the extended model to discuss the CO2 storage in
the saline reservoir. It is worth mentioning that the boundary conditions in all cases in this study
remain open (aquifer) on one side and closed on the other sides, in the original size model (Figure
2). However, the extended model has outer boundaries that are closed on all sides. The depletion
level in Case 7 varies as it is raised above the MMP for comparison with other MMP cases.

4. RESULTS AND ANALYSIS
Initial results and simulation insights from the field case study were presented at the 2025 Carbon
Capture Utilization and Storage Conference (Liu et al., 2025). This study expands upon that work
with full methodological detail and extended scenario analyses. The results and analysis here cover
CO2 injection and pressure profile, CO2 plume, and pressure movement over time as well as entire-
domain histogram for quantitative visualization. In this study, Layer #10 is selected for results
visualization and interpretation, as the layer is in the middle of the reservoir, and the gas plume
and pressures are significant in that layer. Furthermore, the contour line highlighted in red
represents the critical pressure threshold.

4.1 BUSINESS AS USUAL: CASE 1 AND CASE 2
This section presents the first two cases shown in Table 2: Case 1 represents the baseline case
where no injection or production occurs, and Case 2 is the BAU case. The BAU case continues
the WAG process, monitoring changes in saturation, pressure, and carbon storage. The water
injection rate of the WAG operations is 8,100 m3/day (50,947.5 STB/day) for the full field and the
total CO2 injection rate is 3,230,000 sm3/day (114 mmscf/day). The WAG cycle is 2 years (1 year
of water injection followed by 1 year of gas injection). The injection wells operate under a
controlling limit of 227.5 bars (3,300 psi) as a maximum bottomhole pressure (BHP). The WAG
operational duration in the BAU case is 30 years (until simulation time = 30 years). The pressure
and saturation developments are observed for an additional 50 years, until simulation time = 80
years.
The cumulative amounts of injection and production for the BAU case (Case 2) are shown in
Figure 3, along with the development of average reservoir pressure of both Cases 1 and 2. Figure
3 presents the total CO2 and water injection as well as the total liquid and gas production on the
left, while the pressure curves are shown to the right. The results indicate that the cumulative
amount of CO2 injection is 1.17E10 sm3 and the cumulative water injection is 2.33E7 sm3.
Meanwhile, the total gas and liquid production quantities are 7.13E9 and 2.4E7 sm3, respectively.
This means that the gas injection volume exceeds the cumulative gas production by 64% (4.57 E9
sm3), while there is a small difference between the total injection and production of liquid. The
total amount of water injected into the formation is less than the cumulative liquid production by
3% (7E5 sm3). Although the gas and liquid amounts of Case 2 are reported in surface conditions,
the pressure curves show an increase in the average reservoir pressure over time during the WAG
process. This implies that the WAG operations impose the pressure increase of the BAU case (Case
2) as a result of a net positive fluid injection/storage. The pressure curves further indicate that the
reservoir pressure in Case 1 (no injection or production) stabilizes at approximately 127 bars
(1,841 psi) with minor variations as the pressure reaches equilibrium. In Case 2, the average
reservoir pressure tends to stabilize in the post-injection period at approximately 179 bars (2,595
psi). This means that the 30-year WAG operations introduce a pressure increase of 52 bars (754
psi).

The amounts of CO2 (in tons and sm3) that are produced and stored in the reservoir as a result of
the WAG process are shown in Figure 4. The figure presents the total CO2 injection (red), the
cumulative CO2 production (blue), and the amounts of carbon storage (green). The total CO2
amount injected into the formation in tons is 2.19 E7 (1.17E10 sm3). As multiple wells are set to
produce, CO2 breakthrough occurs in these production wells, leading to a cumulative CO2
production of 1.15 E7 tons and a cumulative net carbon storage of 1.04E7 tons. This means that
the WAG EOR operations contribute to CO2 storage by retaining 47.5% of the total injected CO2;
however, the remaining 52.5% of the total CO2 injection amount circulates through the reservoir
and is eventually produced from operating wells. This CO2 breakthrough rate (52.5%) presents a
challenge for long-term CO2 retention, as a large portion of the injected CO2 circulates rather than
being permanently stored. This highlights the need for optimized well placement and injection
strategies to enhance CO2 trapping. The figure also shows that the net carbon storage curve follows
a logarithmic growth, characterized by rapid increases in the early period, as CO2 is initially
trapped in the formation, followed by slower increases in the later period, indicating that storage
efficiency declines over time.

Figures 5-9 discuss the AoR based on critical pressure and gas saturation highlights. The AoR is
defined as the overlap area of the gas plume and the area where the critical pressure is exceeded.
The critical pressure is the pressure required to lift the brine to the nearest USDW through the
wells (EPA, 2010). The critical pressure concept is introduced to ensure that the injected CO2 does
not leak though wells into the upper USDW. Based on the calculations from the use case based on
this method, the critical pressure is estimated to be approximately 176 bars (2,552 psi). The area
where pressures exceed this value is the most emphasized as AoR for the pressure front and
pressure movement.

the next best alternative. It is worth noting that the BAU case operates under a multi-well injection
and production schedule, which likely contributes to its high storage capacity. In contrast, Case 4
relies on a single well for carbon injection, yet still achieves a storage capacity close to the BAU

This means that pressure development and the gas plume behavior are highly dependent on the
reservoir fluids, boundary conditions, depletion level, and injection strategy. The hydrocarbon
reservoirs generally have higher CO2 storage capacities and more expansive gas plumes, due to
higher compressibility and solubility of hydrocarbons compared to saline reservoirs. The boundary
conditions act differently in hydrocarbon and saline reservoirs. The aquifer boundaries in the saline
reservoir provide higher carbon storage capacity, larger gas plume, and pressures below critical

gas plume and lead to high reservoir pressures, requiring careful monitoring to ensure pressure

6. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
This study was conducted on a real field dataset and comprehensively evaluated the CO2 storage
performance across seven distinct scenarios using generalized and mirrored/extended reservoir
models. The scenarios varied in reservoir fluid types (hydrocarbon versus saline), domain sizes
(original versus extended), boundary conditions (open versus closed), and initial pressure levels
(under and above MMP), capturing a wide spectrum of operational and geological conditions for
CO2 storage assessment.
The findings of this study highlight that long-term WAG operations increase CO2 storage, but with
diminishing efficiency over time due to CO2 breakthrough into production wells. This could also
result in operational challenges related to CO2 circulation. Moreover, the hydrocarbon reservoirs
provide significantly larger carbon storage capacities than the saline reservoirs of the same
dimensions, due to the high compressibility and CO2 solubility of hydrocarbon fluids compared to
formation water. The plume size in the hydrocarbon cases is larger than the plume of the saline
reservoirs, due to the higher storage capacity of the hydrocarbon reservoirs.
The results show the importance of evaluating the AoR through pressure and CO2 plume
development over the injection and post-injection periods. It is evident that reservoir domain size,
reservoir fluid type, and depletion level largely influence the plume-induced pressure distribution
and the storage capacity performance. In addition, this study enables quantification of the effects
of physical parameters important for evaluating reservoir suitability, storage performance, and
plume containment.
Future work will build on these case studies to consider more detailed sensitivity analysis and
conduct long-term containment effectiveness assessment using computational tools developed
under the U.S. DOE’s National Risk Assessment Partnership (NRAP).
