Effects of Smoothened Agonist Exposure on Murine Craniofacial Development
Authors: Chuanqing Mao, Yuanjing Jiang, Zuhui Li, Wenjie Zhou, Yongzhen Lai, Chengyong Wang, Meng Lu, Weihui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWhile inhibition of Hedgehog (Hh) signalling is known to cause cleft lip, the effects of its hyperactivation on craniofacial development remain systematically unexplored.Materials and methodsPregnant mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of Smoothened Agonist (SAG) (25 mg/kg) at various time points between E7.5 and E12.5. A median cleft lip model was established at E10.5. Mechanisms were assessed by analysing cell proliferation (PHH3, Ki67), apoptosis (TUNEL), and expression of cell cycle regulators (Cyclin A, B1, D1, E1).ResultsSAG administration induced craniofacial defects – including cranial bone abnormalities, hematomas, and cleft lip/palate – in a time-dependent manner. The critical window for cleft lip induction was E9.5–E10.5. Mechanistically, SAG-induced cleft lip was associated with reduced proliferation and altered expression of selected cell-cycle markers, consistent with delayed G0/G1 progression. No significant change in apoptosis was observed.ConclusionSAG is a potent teratogen that can induce cleft lip during a critical developmental window, potentially through suppression of cell proliferation and perturbation of cell-cycle progression. While its prenatal use poses significant risks, SAG also provides a useful experimental tool for generating congenital craniofacial defect models.Key words: Hedgehog, Lip cleft, Craniofacial malformations, Smoothened agonist, Cell cycle, Hematomas

Introduction

Congenital developmental defects are a significant cause of infant mortality globally, with an estimated incidence of 2-4%.1 Craniofacial malformations are common congenital developmental defects.2 Normal craniofacial development depends on the coordinated fusion and growth of facial tissues during embryogenesis, particularly between the maxillary, medial nasal, and lateral nasal processes, where fusion defects are prone to occur. Disruption or delay in the fusion of these tissues can result in cleft lip and cleft palate (CLP) formation.3,4 CLP are one of the most common congenital malformations, with an incidence of approximately 1 in 500 to 1000 live births.5,6 Although the precise aetiology of many congenital developmental defects remains unknown, these defects are frequently attributed to interactions between genetic mutations and environmental influences.

The normal development of craniofacial tissues is tightly regulated by numerous signalling pathways, among which the Hh signalling pathway plays a critical role in the development of many organs and tissues in mammals.7 In mice, the formation of the medial and lateral nasal processes (MNP and LNP), which eventually give rise to the upper lip and nose, begins at embryonic day (E)10 when the frontonasal prominence is still relatively flat. By E10.5, the MNP and LNP start to develop from the frontonasal prominence, and then fuse with the maxillary processes to shape the facial structure; this fusion is initiated rostrally/anteriorly and subsequently extends posteriorly as facial morphogenesis proceeds.8 Before the substantial growth of the MNP and LNP, Sonic hedgehog (Shh) is expressed in the ventral neural tube, where mesenchymal cells of the frontonasal prominence receive Shh signalling, activating the Hh pathway. Around E11.0, Shh expression is observed in specific regions of the oral ectoderm, followed by increased patched1 (Ptch1) expression primarily in the MNP.8 Abnormal expression of this pathway during development can lead to craniofacial malformations. For instance, conditional knockout of Ptch1 in cranial neural crest cells results in persistent activation of the Hh signalling pathway, leading to failed fusion of the MNP and LNP and subsequent cleft palate formation.9 Furthermore, transgenic mice with ectopic Shh expression in Krt14-positive basal epithelial cells exhibit extended survival compared to non-transgenic controls, along with severe craniofacial defects, including complete cleft palate.10 In chick embryos, Shh overexpression also leads to abnormal craniofacial development, affecting cell proliferation and ultimately causing CLP.11

The Hh signalling family consists of three ligands – Shh, Indian hedgehog (Ihh), and Desert hedgehog (Dhh) – as well as two receptors (Ptch1 and Ptch2) and three glioblastoma-associated protein (Gli) transcription factors (Gli1, Gli2, and Gli3). Unlike these ligands and receptors, smoothened (Smo) is the only member involved in all forms of Hh signalling across all vertebrates, including Drosophila, mice, and zebrafish. In mice, global knockout of Smo results in embryonic lethality at around E9.5.12 In mouse embryos, cranial neural crest cells lacking Smo undergo normal migration and form facial primordia but exhibit increased apoptosis between E9.5 and E10.5, resulting in decreased proliferation and impaired growth of the facial primordia by E11.5.13,14 Moreover, studies using vismodegib, an Smo receptor inhibitor, at different embryonic stages have demonstrated spatiotemporal differences in the effects on craniofacial development, including CLP and craniofacial bone malformations.15, 16, 17 Interestingly, overexpression of Smo in K14- or Osr2-positive cells reduces apoptosis in palatal cells and impairs palatal bone development, thereby inducing cleft palate in foetal mice.18,19 In addition, persistent activation of Smo in cranial neural crest cells leads to slight overgrowth of the facial primordia.20 Collectively, these findings highlight the critical role of Smo and other Hh signalling pathway components as regulators of craniofacial tissue development, thereby emphasizing their importance in understanding congenital craniofacial defects.

SAG is a small molecule agonist that can cross the gastrointestinal tract, placenta, and blood-brain barriers, directly binding to Smo and enhancing Hh pathway activity. Studies have shown that a single injection of SAG effectively enhances Hh signalling, improving cerebellar morphology and partially restoring hippocampal function in Down syndrome models.21,22 Perinatal administration of SAG has also been found to alleviate neural defects in triploid mice and influence the morphology and size of craniofacial tissues.23,24 Furthermore, SAG injection during the embryonic stage can lead to polydactyly in fetuses,25 and treatment of endocrine-cerebro-osteodysplasia mice with SAG can rescue several congenital malformations, including CLP.26 Additionally, SAG has demonstrated efficacy in ameliorating skeletal defects in an animal model of skeletal dysplasia induced by Ihh knockout, suggesting its potential for treating cartilage-related conditions.27 These studies underscore the significant therapeutic potential of SAG for congenital malformations. However, the effects of SAG administration during different embryonic stages on foetal craniofacial development have not yet been systematically studied. Therefore, the objective of this study is to investigate whether and how hyperactivation of the Hh pathway by SAG induces craniofacial malformations, thereby completing the picture of Hh signalling dosage in craniofacial morphogenesis.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The use of ICR mice in this study strictly adhered to the relevant ethical regulations for animal experimentation and was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (IACUC FJMU 2024-0228). The mice were housed in groups at 23 °C under a 12-hour light-dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. For mating, six-week-old female and male ICR mice were paired at a ratio of 1:1. The day a vaginal plug was detected was designated as E0.5. Pregnant mice at E7.5 to E12.5 were given a single intraperitoneal injection of 200 μL of an aqueous solution containing the SAG (25 mg/kg) for the experimental group, whereas the control group received 200 μL of sterile water. Each group included three pregnant mice. SAG HCl was purchased from Selleck (catalogue number: S7779).

Overview of experimental design

Pregnant mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of SAG or vehicle at E7.5–E12.5. For experimental groups across this developmental window, gross morphology and craniofacial measurements were evaluated at E16.5, whereas foetal survival was summarized at E18.5 and micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) was performed at E18.5 in the E9.5–E12.5 exposure groups. To investigate earlier events associated with cleft lip formation, E9.5-treated foetuses were collected at E11.5 and E12.5 for scanning electron microscopy, and E10.5-treated foetuses were collected at E11.5 for immunohistochemistry (IHC), immunofluorescence (IF) , terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labelling (TUNEL), and reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analyses. Unless otherwise stated, each exposure group comprised three pregnant mice, and the number of foetuses analysed for each assay is indicated in the relevant Methods section, Results section, figure legend, or table. (Supplementary Table 1)

Morphological observations and measurements under a stereomicroscope

Pregnant mice in the experimental and control groups were euthanized at E16.5. The uteruses were isolated, and the foetuses were removed for morphological examination under a stereomicroscope. Foetal viability, individual developmental status, and craniofacial tissue development were recorded, and the total number of foetuses recovered from each treatment group was summarized at the group level (Table 2). For craniofacial measurements, two foetuses were randomly selected from each of the three pregnant mice using a random number table. The skulls of these foetuses were dissected, and craniofacial development was measured under a stereomicroscope to assess craniofacial tissue growth. These gross measurements were developed for the present study to provide reproducible external comparisons of craniofacial growth at E16.5 using landmarks that could be consistently identified under stereomicroscopy. Because direct bony maxillary breadth could not be reliably measured in intact foetuses under this approach, the distance between the eyes was used as a surrogate gross index and is referred to as interorbital distance in Figure 2. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Foetuses from the experimental and control groups at different time points (E7.5, E8.5, E9.5, E10.5, E11.5, and E12.5) were collected at E16.5, with three foetuses randomly selected from each time point using a random number table. The isolated head tissues were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C, followed by washing with running water for several hours to remove excess fixative. A graded series of alcohol (30%-100%) was used for dehydration, with each concentration for 1 hour, followed by butanol clearing and paraffin embedding. Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned into 4 μm continuous slices, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated using a graded series of alcohol (each concentration for 5 minutes). H&E staining was then performed according to standard procedures, and morphological changes were observed and photographed under a light microscope. Morphological changes in craniofacial tissue development in response to drug administration at different time points were analysed.

Micro-CT scanning

Cranial samples from foetuses at E18.5, obtained from pregnant mice treated at E9.5, E10.5, E11.5, and E12.5, were scanned using NEMO Micro-CT (NMC-200, PINGSENG Healthcare (Kunshan) Inc.). Three-dimensional reconstruction was performed using Avatar software, and identical axial images were compared to observe the effects of SAG treatment at different time points on foetal cranial tissue development.

Alcian blue and alizarin red staining

Cranial skeletal specimens were collected from E18.5 foetuses derived from six different pregnant mice in both the experimental and control groups after treatment at E9.5, E10.5, E11.5, or E12.5. After removal of the skin under a stereomicroscope, the specimens were fixed in 95% ethanol overnight at room temperature, followed by staining with Alcian Blue solution overnight at room temperature. The samples were then washed twice with 70% ethanol and destained in 95% ethanol overnight at room temperature. After removal of ethanol, the specimens were pre-cleared in 1% KOH for 1 h at room temperature, then stained with Alizarin Red solution for 3 to 4 h at room temperature. Finally, the Alizarin Red solution was replaced with 20% glycerol/KOH, and the samples were incubated at room temperature until the tissues became transparent. The specimens were then transferred to 100% glycerol for long-term storage and photographed under a stereomicroscope. Mandibular length as well as maxillary width and length of the foetal skull were measured using ImageJ software.16 Subsequently, statistical comparisons were performed between the experimental and control groups at each of the indicated time points.

Scanning electron microscopy of lip tissue development

Exploiting the reliable E9.5 cleft phenotype, dams received SAG at E9.5 to generate an SEM-ready model for tracking lip malformation . Foetuses from pregnant mice treated at E9.5 in both the experimental and control groups were dissected at E11.5 and E12.5 under a stereomicroscope, and craniofacial tissues were isolated. The tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. After fixation, a graded series of alcohol (30%-100%) was used for dehydration, with each concentration for 30 minutes. The tissues were dried for 24 hours using a thermostatic vacuum dryer and then adhered to conductive adhesive for gold coating. Finally, the gold-coated samples were scanned using a scanning electron microscope (Xplore20, Oxford Instruments) to observe morphological changes in lip tissue development in both groups.

IHC staining

Because lip tissue is morphologically inconspicuous and very small at E10.5 after E9.5 treatment. Upper lip tissues were collected from foetal samples 24 hours after administration at E10.5 and sectioned into 4 μm slices for further analysis and staining. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated with a graded alcohol series. Antigen retrieval was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions for the primary antibodies, and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 minutes. The sections were blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 minutes, followed by incubation with the primary antibodies – Gli1 (1:200, NBP1-78259, NOVUSBIO), Ptch1 (1:200, ab53715, Abcam), PHH3 (1:1000, ab47297, Abcam), Ki67 (1:200, ab16667, Abcam), Cyclin D1 (1:200, ET1601-31, Huabio), Cyclin E1 (1:200, ET1612-16, Huabio), Cyclin A (1:200, ER63152, Huabio), and Cyclin B1 (1:200, ER1906-93, Huabio) at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS the following day, incubation was performed using the UltraSensitive SP Kit (KIT-9707, MXB Biotechnologies) as per the manufacturer's instructions. DAB was used for visualization, and the slides were mounted with neutral gum and observed under a light microscope. The positive cell rates of Cyclin A, Cyclin B1, Cyclin E1, Cyclin D1, PHH3 and Ki67 were analysed using ImageJ software.

IF staining

Upper lip tissues were collected from foetal samples 24 hours after administration at E10.5 and sectioned into 4 μm slices. They were subjected to high-pressure EDTA antigen retrieval, blocked with goat serum, and stained overnight at 4 °C with Acetyl-α-Tubulin (Lys40) (D20G3) Xp® Rabbit mAb (Alexa Fluor® 488 Conjugate) (1:1000,35652S,CST). Nuclear counterstaining was performed with DAPI, and samples were imaged using a laser confocal fluorescence microscope.

TUNEL staining

Pregnant mice at E10.5 received 25 mg/kg SAG to establish a cleft-lip model. At E11.5, foetal upper-lip tissues from both experimental and control groups were sectioned into 4-μm slices, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated with a graded alcohol series. The sections were treated with 20 μg/mL proteinase K at 37 °C for 20 minutes, followed by two PBS washes for 5 minutes each. TUNEL reaction solution (UElandy, T6014) was added, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 2 hours. After washing with PBS, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 minutes. The slides were mounted and photographed under a confocal laser scanning microscope.

8. RT-qPCR analysis

For RT-qPCR, pregnant mice at E10.5 were administered 25 mg/kg SAG to establish a cleft-lip model. Upper lip tissue from one foetus per pregnant mouse was collected from six independent pregnant mice in both the experimental and control groups at E11.5, and total RNA was extracted from each sample. RT-qPCR was performed according to the instructions provided by the Takara TB Green Premix Ex Taq reverse transcription kit and PCR kit to detect the expression of relevant mRNAs. Briefly, total RNA (1 μg) was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit (code No. RR037A, Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RT-qPCR was then performed using a LightCycler 480 System and TB Green® Premix EX Taq (code No. RR420A, Takara, Japan). The reference gene used was ACTB, and the 2-ΔΔCT method was used to compare the expression levels of target genes between groups. The final data are presented as mean ± standard deviation in bar graphs. The primer information for the RT-qPCR is shown in Table 1. All data were analysed using an unpaired t-test with SPSS software. A P-value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

For the measurements of craniofacial tissues, including maxillary and mandibular lengths, interorbital distance, mandibular width, and oral fissure width, as well as the positive cell ratios of Cyclin A, Cyclin B1, Cyclin E1, Cyclin D1, PHH3, Ki67, and TUNEL assay results, all data were analysed using an unpaired t-test with SPSS software. A P-value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Foetal mortality following SAG (25 mg/kg) administration SAG at different embryonic stages

Pregnant mice in both experimental and control groups received a single intraperitoneal injection of either 25 mg/kg SAG solution or an equivalent volume of solvent between E7.5 and E12.5. No maternal deaths were observed during the experiment. However, all foetuses were unable to survive beyond one day after birth because of severe congenital developmental defects. To further analyse foetal development, the observation endpoint was set at E18.5. Table 2 summarizes the number of pregnant mice per group together with the total foetuses recovered at E18.5. At this endpoint, foetal mortality was observed to varying degrees between E8.5 and E10.5. Notably, the group treated at E8.5 exhibited the highest rate of foetal death (see Table 2).

Additionally, when examining the dose-dependent effect of different SAG concentrations on foetal development at E10.5, it was found that administration of 50 mg/kg led to maternal death. In the 20 mg/kg group, foetuses exhibited milder malformations, with approximately 50% of the foetuses presenting with conditions such as cleft palate, median cleft lip, and polydactyly. In contrast, the 15 mg/kg group showed no significant developmental abnormalities in the foetuses (Table 3).

Time-dependent effects of SAG on craniofacial malformations in foetal mice

To investigate the potential effects of administering SAG at different developmental stages on craniofacial and other tissue development in foetal mice, foetuses were examined at E16.5 after exposure at E7.5-E12.5. In the control group, the craniofacial tissues of E16.5 foetuses developed normally, with fully fused bilateral upper lips, symmetrical eyes, and normally shaped limbs. In the experimental group administered SAG at E7.5, most foetuses exhibited the absence of parietal and maxillary bones, accompanied by cranial exposure and either absent or single-eye malformations. At E8.5, foetuses primarily showed a narrowed anterior maxilla, along with anophthalmia and polydactyly. In the E9.5 group, foetuses predominantly displayed bilateral or unilateral complete cleft lip and palate, partial spinal bifurcation, and polydactyly.

At E10.5, the experimental group exhibited more complex abnormalities, including cleft palate, exophthalmos, median cleft lip, localized subcutaneous hematoma, and spinal bifurcation. In the E11.5 group, subcutaneous hematoma and polydactyly were also observed in the craniofacial region, with mild cleft lip malformations. At this stage, subcutaneous hematomas were mainly concentrated in the area anterior to both tragus regions. By E12.5, approximately 50% of the foetuses in the experimental group exhibited subcutaneous hematomas, primarily in the median maxillary region, and the occurrence of median cleft lip had largely disappeared. These results indicate that the regulation of craniofacial development by SAG is time-dependent (Figure 1).

Quantitative craniofacial measurements

To evaluate the effects of SAG administration at different developmental stages, craniofacial measurements were performed on E16.5 foetuses from experimental groups treated at E9.5, E10.5, E11.5, and E12.5, as well as from the control group (N = 6, P < .05). Interorbital distance, used here as a surrogate gross measure of maxillary width, was significantly greater in the E9.5, E10.5, and E11.5 experimental groups than in the control group (Figure 2A, N = 6, P < .05). In contrast, mandibular width measurements indicated that only the E9.5 and E11.5 experimental groups exhibited significantly wider bilateral mandibular widths compared with the control group. For the E12.5 experimental group, no significant differences were observed in either interorbital distance or mandibular width compared with the control group (Figure 2B, N = 6, P < .05). In terms of maxillary length, the E10.5, E11.5, and E12.5 experimental groups exhibited significantly longer maxillae compared with the control group (Figure 2C, N = 6, P < .05). Conversely, mandibular length measurements showed that the mandibular length in the E10.5 experimental group was the shortest, being significantly shorter than that of the control group. However, the mandibular length gradually increased at subsequent stages, and by E12.5, it was slightly longer in the experimental group than in the control group, with statistically significant differences (Figure 2D, N = 6, P < .05). Finally, measurements of oral fissure width revealed that the E9.5, E10.5, and E11.5 experimental groups had significantly wider oral fissures compared with the control group, with the E10.5 group showing the greatest width. In contrast, no significant differences in oral fissure width were observed between the E12.5 experimental group and the control group (Figure 2E, N = 6, P < .05).

Histological characterization of stage-specific craniofacial defects

To further investigate the craniofacial developmental abnormalities caused by experimental interventions at different time points, E16.5 tissue samples from each group were subjected to H&E staining. The results revealed substantial stage-specific differences. In the E7.5 experimental group, pronounced malposition of the upper maxillary tissues was observed, along with underdeveloped cranium, eyeballs, and maxilla, as well as cleft palate and disorganized tongue musculature. The molar tooth germ and mandible appeared relatively preserved in the illustrated sections, and no obvious abnormality of the adjacent Meckel’s cartilage was observed. In the E8.5 experimental group, complete absence of the eyeballs was noted, whereas the cranium, maxilla, mandible, palate, tongue, and tooth germ tissues appeared grossly preserved. By the E9.5 experimental group, abnormalities in the cranium and eyeballs appeared less severe than at earlier stages; however, distinct cleft lip and palate abnormalities persisted, while the mandible, tongue, and tooth germ tissues appeared relatively preserved. In the E10.5 experimental group, prominent abnormalities were observed, including reduced palatal bone development, abnormal elevation of the palatal shelves, impaired maxillomandibular separation, disorganized tongue musculature, and a midline cleft. The E11.5 experimental group primarily exhibited hematoma formation with red blood cell accumulation in the subcutaneous tissues of both cheeks, reduced palatal bone development resulting in a submucosal cleft, and abnormal palatal elevation. By E12.5, the experimental group displayed craniofacial tissue morphology that was largely comparable to that of the control group in the illustrated sections (Figure 3).

The results revealed that in the E9.5 group, the cranial and palatal bones were underdeveloped, and the mandible was significantly shortened. In the E10.5 group, the mandible remained shorter than that of the control group, and premature fusion of the maxilla and mandible was observed. In the E11.5 and E12.5 groups, failure of midline fusion was observed in the palatal bone (Figure 4A).

Furthermore, Alcian blue/Alizarin red staining of the E18.5 craniofacial skeleton revealed that, in the control group, the maxilla and mandible were essentially fully developed, whereas the sagittal, coronal, frontal, and lambdoid sutures remained unfused at this stage. In the E9.5 experimental group, abnormal development of the premaxilla associated with bilateral cleft lip was observed. In addition, both the maxilla and mandible were significantly smaller and shorter than those in the control group, and the sagittal suture had already fused (Figure 4B, E, F; N = 6; P < .05). In the E10.5 experimental group, not only were the maxilla and mandible markedly reduced in size, but fusion of the sagittal suture and fusion between the maxilla and mandible were also observed, accompanied by narrowing of the maxillary width (Figure 4B, D-F; N = 6; P < .05). Although the superior and lateral views of the skull in the E11.5 and E12.5 groups showed no obvious morphological differences compared with the control group, the lengths of both the maxilla and mandible were still significantly shorter than those in the control group (Figure 4B, D-F; N = 6; P < .05).

SEM analysis of upper lip morphogenesis after E9.5 exposure

SAG exposure between E9.5 and E11.5 induced bilateral, unilateral, and midline cleft lip phenotypes. To characterize the associated surface morphological changes, SEM was performed on E9.5-treated foetuses collected at E11.5 and E12.5. At E11.5, the control group showed closely aligned maxillary, lateral nasal, and medial nasal prominences with a subtle midline depression. In the experimental group, these prominences were significantly elongated outward, with pronounced nostril and midline depressions, and the epithelial junction between the LNP and MNP exhibited a marked collapse. By E12.5, the control group showed nearly complete upper lip formation, with the midline depression resolving and the maxillary prominence fusing with the MNP. In contrast, the experimental group exhibited additional cleft at nasal base, with lateral nasal and maxillary prominences extending outward, resulting in a complete CLP phenotype (Figure 5).

At E11.5, the experimental group exhibited significantly reduced expression of PHH3 and Ki67-positive cells compared to the control group, with statistically significant differences (Figure 6, A-D, N = 6, P < .05). These findings suggest that SAG administration suppresses cell proliferation in upper lip tissues. Apoptosis was then evaluated using TUNEL staining. At E11.5, the number of TUNEL-positive cells did not differ significantly between the experimental and control groups (Figure 6, E-F, N = 6, P>0.05).

Discussion

The Hh signalling pathway is a critical regulator of craniofacial tissue development, with Smo serving as a key transducer within this pathway.7,29 SAG, a Smo receptor agonist, holds potential therapeutic value in addressing congenital malformations. In this study, SAG was administered to pregnant mice at key time points during early craniofacial development to assess its effects on embryogenesis. The results revealed that SAG administration at different stages induced congenital defects in foetal mice, including CLP, polydactyly, spinal bifida, and mandibular fusion. These findings establish a foundation for generating stable animal models to further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying craniofacial abnormalities associated with Hh pathway overexpression.

SAG-induced cleft lip and palate deformities

Non-syndromic cleft lip with or without cleft palate (NSCL/P) is a prevalent congenital defect, affecting 1 in 500 to 1 in 1400 newborns globally. Evidence from various animal models has demonstrated that NSCL/P is influenced by genetic, hereditary, and environmental factors.30 Transgenic mouse studies have shown that upper lip and palate development is tightly regulated by signalling pathways such as Wingless-related integration site (WNT), Shh, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), and fibroblast growth factor (FGF). Dysregulation of these pathways can impair cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition, ultimately leading to CLP.31, 32, 33 Environmental stimuli, modelled in animals or studied through epidemiological investigations, have also been identified as significant risk factors for CLP. These include maternal smoking, alcohol consumption, drug use, infections, and advanced maternal age during early pregnancy.34, 35, 36, 37 For example, retrospective studies have indicated that folic acid and vitamin supplementation during pregnancy can help reduce the risk of CLP.38 Additionally, the administration of vismodegib, a clinically approved drug for basal cell carcinoma, has been shown to inhibit the Hh signalling pathway during embryogenesis, reducing mesenchymal cell proliferation in the palate and resulting in cleft palate in foetal mice.39 The development of CLP involves complex physiological processes requiring the precise regulation of cell migration, growth, differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis.40 In the present study, SAG, a small-molecule Smo receptor agonist, was associated with reduced proliferation and altered expression of selected cell-cycle markers in upper lip tissues between E9.5 and E11.5, consistent with delayed G0/G1 progression rather than definitive global cell-cycle arrest. This was accompanied by the formation of various CLP phenotypes, including bilateral complete cleft lip and palate, unilateral complete cleft lip and palate, midline cleft lip, and submucosal cleft palate. These results successfully established robust animal models for different CLP types. Furthermore, the peak occurrence of unilateral CLP induced by vismodegib has been reported at E8.75.41 Using a comparable gestational age framework, this study identified the peak occurrence of bilateral CLP induced by SAG at E9.0. By integrating the vismodegib model with the SAG-induced CLP models developed in this study, researchers gain valuable tools for exploring the pathogenic mechanisms underlying congenital defects caused by both overexpression and inhibition of Smo. These models provide new opportunities to elucidate shared and distinct mechanisms in these conditions, paving the way for targeted therapeutic strategies.

Our findings, together with previous studies using vismodegib, demonstrate that craniofacial development requires tightly regulated Hh signalling activity. Both inhibition and overactivation disrupt morphogenesis, albeit through potentially distinct cellular mechanisms (apoptosis versus altered proliferation/cell-cycle progression).

SAG affects bone formation and leads to craniofacial skeletal abnormalities

The Hh signalling pathway, mediated by Ptch1 and its downstream effector Smo, plays a crucial role in bone and cartilage development during craniofacial skeletal formation.15,16 Conditional deletion of Smo, a critical molecule for Hh signalling activity, in mesenchymal cells (Smofl/fl; Wnt1Cre) has been shown to result in ectopic mandibular bone formation, whereas Smo mutations lead to significant mandibular shortening.42 Additionally, activation of ciliary Hh signalling by SAG has been reported to significantly accelerate growth plate repair following injury.43 Similarly, the application of SAG in cranial defect repair promotes bone regeneration and angiogenesis, facilitating the healing of bone defects.44 However, during palatal bone development, overexpression of the Hh signalling pathway has been shown to cause developmental defects in the palate.45 Consistent with these findings, this study demonstrates that SAG administration at E7.5 causes abnormalities and partial loss of parietal and maxillary bone development. Furthermore, SAG administration at E9.5 and E10.5 leads to mandibular shortening and palatal bone developmental defects, highlighting the time-dependent regulatory effects of SAG on craniofacial bone development. Because Von Kossa staining was not performed in the present study, the extent of ossification and calcification abnormalities should be interpreted primarily from the histological and micro-CT findings and will require further validation in future work.

Furthermore, craniosynostosis is a common congenital developmental disorder caused by mutations in multiple genes. It is typically characterized by premature fusion of one or more craniofacial sutures, resulting in abnormal skull growth and, in some cases, neurological deficits secondary to increased intracranial pressure.46 The four major sutures associated with craniosynostosis are the sagittal, coronal, frontal, and lambdoid sutures. In mice, precursor cells of the coronal suture are already present within the cephalic mesoderm at E7.5–E8.0.47 These precursor cells subsequently migrate to the supraorbital ridge during E8.5–E9.0, followed by apical migration between E11.5 and E13.5. By E13.5, they give rise to the coronal suture as well as the overlapping parietal and frontal bones.46 During calvarial osteogenesis in embryonic mice, GLI3, a potent repressor of the Hh signalling pathway, restricts osteogenic differentiation and maintains suture patency by suppressing the expression of Runx2 and Ihh; accordingly, loss of GLI3 results in Ihh-independent craniosynostosis.48 In addition, studies using a Ptch1 haploinsufficient mouse model have demonstrated that excessive activation, rather than suppression, of Hh signalling can also induce lambdoid craniosynostosis, thereby expanding our understanding of the regulatory role of Hh signalling in suture development.49 Moreover, duplication of an upstream enhancer cluster of the Ihh gene leads to Ihh overexpression at cranial sutures, which in turn induces craniosynostosis through excessive activation of the Hh signalling pathway.50 Finally, coronal suture fusion has also been reported in Curry–Jones syndrome caused by constitutive SMO activation.51 In the present study, SAG treatment at E9.5 and E10.5, which led to overactivation of the Hh signalling pathway, also resulted in marked sagittal suture fusion. These findings further support a critical regulatory role for Hh signalling in cranial suture and Craniofacial Skeletal development. However, the precise mechanism by which Hh pathway overactivation promotes suture fusion in our model remains to be elucidated in future studies.

Congenital maxillomandibular fusion is a rare and severe developmental anomaly characterized by fusion of the maxilla and mandible, alveolar bone, or gingiva.52 The specific pathogenic mechanisms underlying this condition remain poorly understood. Previous studies have identified abnormal expression of genes such as Foxc1, FGF8, GATA3, and BMP4 as contributing factors to maxillomandibular fusion.53,54 In this study, SAG administration at E10.5 also induced maxillomandibular fusion in foetal mice, suggesting that the Hh signalling pathway is implicated in the pathogenesis of this condition. Interestingly, the phenotypes observed in this study are similar to those found in mice with ICK gene deficiencies, which model Endocrine-cerebro-osteodysplasia (ECO) syndrome. However, previous research has shown that SAG administration can partially alleviate congenital abnormalities caused by ICK deficiencies in mouse models.26 These findings indicate that the timing of SAG administration is critical for its effectiveness in treating related congenital developmental abnormalities.

The relationship between SAG exposure and vascular disturbance

Vascular development consists of two primary stages: vasculogenesis and angiogenesis, which are fundamental to embryonic development and organogenesis.55,56 In 1998, Pepicelli et al and, later, Rowitch et al first identified the regulatory role of the Hh signalling pathway during embryogenesis.57,58 Subsequent research has shown that various molecules within the Hh pathway consistently promote angiogenesis during the development of multiple organs.59 For example, the absence of vascular formation in Smo-deficient yolk sacs or embryos confirmed the crucial role of the Hh pathway in embryonic vasculogenesis.60 Additionally, overactivation of the Hh pathway, either through Ptch1 knockout or SAG administration, has been shown to result in arterial enlargement.61,62 In this study, SAG administration led to the formation of subcutaneous hematomas in craniofacial tissues of foetal mice. Conversely, in Smo-deficient mice, excessive development of superficial ocular blood vessels was observed.63 Similarly, inhibition of the Shh signalling pathway using the Shh antibody 5E1 has been shown to cause vascular developmental defects and subcutaneous hematoma formation.64 Therefore, the mechanism by which SAG leads to hematoma formation in embryonic maxillofacial development requires further investigation.

Angiogenesis, defined as the sprouting of new blood vessels from pre-existing ones, occurs with spatial and temporal specificity during development,65 aligning with the variations in hematoma locations observed at E11.5 and E12.5 in this study. Furthermore, Hh-induced vasculogenesis and angiogenesis primarily rely on the activation of mesenchymal cells, while the regulation of endothelial cell barrier integrity depends on the activation of the endothelial Hh signalling pathway.59 In Six1-deficient mouse embryos, persistent upregulation of Shh expression results in vascular smooth muscle cell defects, leading to hematoma formation.66 During vascular development, molecules such as vascular endothelial cadherin are essential for maintaining tight intercellular contacts among endothelial cells, ensuring vascular stability. Disruption of these interactions can lead to hematomas during embryogenesis or postnatally. The above studies suggest that the mechanism by which SAG causes hematoma formation during embryonic development may be related to this. The Hh signalling pathway also plays a critical role in regulating vascular maturation, stability, and arterial differentiation during embryonic development.67, 68, 69 For example, SAG has been shown to enhance the expression of tight junction proteins, reducing blood-brain barrier permeability. In peripheral vasculature, SAG inhibits TNFα-induced increases in vascular permeability and the expression of Vcam-1.70 The angiogenic properties of Hh pathway agonists, such as SAG, make them promising candidates for the treatment of ischemic diseases. For example, Hh proteins can induce pro-angiogenic responses in endothelial cells through either canonical or non-canonical Hh signalling pathways.71,72 In vitro studies have shown that exogenous Shh promotes the expression of VEGF, ANG-1, and ANG-2, as well as cellular proliferation, in dermal fibroblasts.73 In cultured primary human dermal fibroblasts, the SAG compound 10c was also shown to potently induce VEGF expression, thereby contributing to the regulation of angiogenesis.74 Therefore, it is essential to fully understand the regulatory mechanisms of SAG in blood vessels before its formal clinical application. Our project research has found an association between embryonic exposure to SAG and vascular stability. Consequently, in future research and applications of SAG for promoting angiogenesis, attention should also be paid to whether it concurrently alters vascular maturation and reduces vascular integrity.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that SAG administration at various embryonic stages induces distinct congenital craniofacial defects in foetal mice, including cranial defects, CLP, maxillomandibular fusion, and subcutaneous hematomas. These stable animal models offer a cost-effective, technically accessible, and time-efficient approach for investigating related diseases. In addition, our data indicate that SAG induced cleft lip associated with reduced proliferation and altered cell cycle marker expression within a critical developmental window. Further work will be required to define the precise cell-cycle and vascular mechanisms involved.
