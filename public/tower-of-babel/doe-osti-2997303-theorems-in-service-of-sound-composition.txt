ABSTRACT
As Sandia undertakes to secure new, high-consequence systems against cyber security threats we are
compelled to build verification tools that support scalable automated analysis, verified component
reuse, and rapid modeling through division of work. In our favor we have demonstrated that the
high-consequence systems of interest at Sandia are ideally suited to formal verification. However,
as Sandia aims to verify more and larger systems, we are confronted with the scaling limitations of
the state of the art. Tools like TLA [4] and QQ [5] require the user to compose component models
using arbitrary data sharing and this can lead to new behaviors that did not exist in the constituent
models. That in turn invalidates any assurance case developed for those models in isolation (i.e.
free sharing, no guarantees). Unfortunately, our only recourse is to carry out verification of all
components together as a full system. Not only are larger models harder for users to work with
but the time required by automated verification is exponential in the size of the full model’s state
space.
Our insight is that we can retain the assurance cases of individual components through automatically
enforced data sharing patterns which govern how data is allowed to be manipulated outside a
component. But! We must strike a careful balance between the design of data sharing patterns and
the ability of users to accurately model systems of interest. To wit, if we require two models to share
no data then, when they are composed, they are effectively behaving in isolation and their properties
are retained (i.e. no sharing, free guarantees) but this precludes most systems of interest.
Here we extend the state of the art in state machine modeling with modules and automatically
enforced data sharing patterns. We then prove sound several forms of composition where component
models define data sharing restrictions such that they retain their individual assurance cases when
composed with any other model.
We have hypothesized that existing, well understood data sharing patterns from literature, like
one-writer many-readers, will be sufficient to strike the necessary balance between the needs of
users and automated guarantees of assurance for real systems under verification at Sandia.
Ultimately this project provides a machine checked, formal mathematical foundation for usable,
checkable, and rapid modeling of Sandia’s high consequence systems as well as a deeper under-
standing of how Sandia models and verifies high consequence systems. Practically speaking, this
will enable Sandia to develop components on completely different timelines and even for different
purposes while retaining their assurance cases when composed with other components.

1. INTRODUCTION

A key problem with the current state of the art in formal verification using state machine models is
that it lacks forms of composition that accurately represent how real components will work together
and that provides users ways to restrict data sharing such that that component assurance cases are
preserved in the larger system. Without these properties all verification must take place with the
entire system making the task exponentially harder.
The state of the art supports two structural forms of composition, parallel and nested. Parallel
composition may be used to model distributed components, components communicating over a
bus, or the relationship between a component and an attacker. Within parallel composition there
are at least two types: synchronous and asynchronous. The former requires that both components
take actions simultaneously while the later allows the two components to additionally perform work
on their own.

1.1. Examples

To see the issue with parallel composition, consider the interaction in Figure 1. Assuming x is
initialized to 0, the component on the right would never enter the state Err in isolation and it comes
equipped with a proof of that fact which shows the value of x is always less than 2 (dashed box).
Unfortunately, when composed with the component on the left the possible values for the variable x
includes 2 because the component on the left is allowed to x to have the value 2 as the components
take turns operating within the composed system. As a result, the Err state can be reached. Thus,

this requirement for the value of x, which is satisfiable when checked in isolation, is not retained
under composition.
Figure 2 shows nested composition. Intuitively, when the parent model is in the top state it waits
while the child model does its work. This is used at Sandia today to separate levels of detail in
models much like a procedure call in an everyday programming language. Much as with the parallel
example, the property that x always be less than 2 may be violated when nested inside a parent
machine that manipulates the value of x when the child is not performing work.
In both examples we may recover the assurance case of the right component by restricting the way
that the variable x is allowed to be manipulated when it is composed. If external components can
only ever read x, then we are assured that x will always be less than 2 and will never enter the Err
state. This is the one-writer many-readers pattern. These simple examples illustrate that issue at
hand: without some restriction of the way data is shared between components we are obligated to
check their assurance cases at the whole system level and thereby incur the human reasoning and
performance penalty of working with more complex models.

1.2. State of the Art

State of the art systems for modeling and verification like TLA [4], Event B [1], and QQ [5] provide
forms of composition like those described previously but do not provide facilities for specifying
how data should be shared between components and make no attempt to preserve assurance cases
in the presence of composition. Thus, when verifying systems these tools are inherently limited to
verification over the full system.
Where data sharing patterns are concerned there are long lines of research in type systems, static
analysis and program verification that attempt to make verification of programs composable [2].
Rust’s [6] borrow checker ensures that there is only ever one writer to a mutable resource so that
systems composed in parallel may not introduce data-race bugs.

 12

There is also extensive study in concurrency literature of verification of programs in the presence
of locking disciplines and more intricate data sharing protocols for manual verification of lock free
algorithms [3]. The key insight of this line of research is that modular verification of concurrent
processes can be characterize using protocols. Protocols govern the ways in which the variable can
evolve and requires that there is a method by which those protocols can be enforced. Beyond our
initial hypothesis we expect that the right balance of user needs and guarantees will land somewhere
between the one-writer many-readers pattern described above and these protocols.

1.3. Data Sharing Patterns

The key enabling insight of this work is that there are existing data sharing patterns and language
constructs in the aforementioned literature that we can take as inspiration for our approach and
then work with existing users of these tools at Sandia to strike the right balance between usability
and automatically verified composition. We have hypothesized that the one-writer, many readers
pattern will be sufficient for verifying most systems at Sandia, i.e. when only one component in a
system is allowed to make modifications to a shared variable.
To begin the project we interviewed 8 subject matter experts (SMEs) at Sandia and asked them
about the one-writer many-readers data sharing pattern in their work. In particular we asked them
three questions:

 1. Are you familiar with the one-writer many-readers pattern?
 2. Is it possible in your designs to confine them to the one-writer many-readers approach for
 shared variables?
 3. Are there examples in your designs where it is more natural for many components to write to
 a variable?

Universally the first question was answered in the affirmative when an example was provided for
clarity. The second question similarly had a clear answer in the affirmative. In fact many of the
interviewed SMEs suggested that they take pains to adhere to such a design restriction for the same
reason we hypothesize it’s success for this project, namely that it provides some hope of modular
reasoning about a system.
The final question was the most varied. Many designs can be proven correct with shared state
variables and the most direct way to communicate in modeling state machines is with a directly
shared variable (as opposed to a pair of one-sided one-writer variables). Moreover at least on of
the SMEs reported on a queue data structure shared between threads where they were unable to see
how one might model such a structure with one sided writes.
This suggests that future work extending these approaches with more general constructs may provide
value to users.

1.4. Desiderata

There are several desiderata for both the semantics and the metatheory of this project. For the
semantics we want to capture the semantics of the QQ tool as closely as possible so that we can be
confident that our reasoning applies to the tool. Second we want to ensure that our semantics does
not preclude the compilation of state charts to the various backends of the QQ tool. For example,
compiling the state charts to SMV for model checking pushes us toward a notion of state chart with
constraints over variables that can otherwise take arbitrary values within their declared type but we
have a semantics that preserves the values when they are not modified in the state chart.
For the metatheory we have three idealized theorems in Figure 1-3 that we would like to prove
about our data pattern and type system. Each provides a way to preserve properties in the presence
of composition for the parent or child of the composition. In a very important sense these three
theorems represent what we believe to be table stakes for state chart semantics because of their
implications for scaling analysis.
The first theorem provides for the refinement of the parent when nesting any child as long as the
two composed state machines satisfy the one-writers many-readers restriction and that the child is
a refinement of whatever state machine it’s replacing. With a suitable notion of refinement that
means that Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) properties will apply to the newly composed parent. The
impact is that a person who conducts a proof about the parent state machine can do so with a simpler
more abstract child (usually a Plain) and know that whatever machine replaces that Plain the new
composition will retain the proven properties.

Theorem 1.4.1 (Parent Refinement, Eqn. 1.1). If a machines 𝑚 1 and 𝑚 2 are well typed in the
parent 𝐶, and the set of traces for 𝑚 1 is contained within 𝑚 2 , then the set of traces for 𝐶[𝑚 1 ], i.e.
𝑚 1 composed in 𝐶, is contained within the set of traces for 𝐶[𝑚 2 ].

The second theorem addresses the issue outlined in Figure 1-2. If the composition of the child
machine is well-typed according to our one-writer many-readers discipline then any invariant about
the child will hold while executing in the parent, where invariants are defined as the global LTL
operator G followed by a propositional statement in LTL lacking temporal operators. The reason
for the invariant restriction is due to the fact that the traces of a nested child may be truncated by

the parent and also repeated in the traces of the parent. Thus any composition involving nesting
can only preserve invariants.

Theorem 1.4.2 (Invariants Preserved, Eqn. 1.2). If a machine 𝑚 is well typed within 𝐶, for any
linear temporal logic invariant 𝑃inv that holds for 𝑚 and which also holds for the initial states of
𝐶[𝑚] then 𝑃inv also holds for 𝑃(𝐶[𝑚]).

Finally, the third theorem addresses the issue outlined in Figure 1-1 for parallel composition.
Here we are able to recover all LTL properties when the composed machine follows our typing
discipline.

Theorem 1.4.3 (Parallel Preserves, Eqn. 1.3). If a machines 𝑚 1 and 𝑚 2 are well typed when
composed in parallel, then for any linear temporal logic property 𝑃 that holds for 𝑚 1 , 𝑃 also holds
for Par 𝑚 1 𝑚 2 .

2. REFERENCE STATE MACHINE SEMANTICS

After extensive consultation with the team members of the QQ project [5] we have created an
operational semantics for the state charts of the QQ2 tool. We have included the key addition of
variable scoping with public and private variables for the purposes of facilitating modular design
and defining the observable behaviors of the system.

2.1.       Syntax

The syntax of our term language appears in Figure 2-1. Machines are defined inductively with one
of three forms: variable declarations with a child machine for which the variable is intended to be
scoped, parallel machines with two children, sequential machines with a transitions between a set
of children 𝑚¯ (denoted as 𝑒¯𝑡𝑟 ) and inner transitions when any of those children step (denoted as
𝑒¯𝑠𝑡 ), and a plain machine which represents a chaotic base case. We discuss the static and dynamic
semantics for these machines in the next two sections.

2.2. Static Semantics

To start we define the static semantics of the one-writer many-readers data pattern in terms of
ownership. The idea is that this type system will enforce that only one machine in the hierarchy of
a state machine definition will be permitted to write to a variable and thus "own" it.

We define an operator ▷ for reconciling our ownership types, that captures the montonic progression
of ownership toward being owned where it cannot be revoked. We lift the same operator to
type environments Γ that are mappings from identifiers to type and ownership information. We
interchangeably treat Γ as an associate list and mapping where it makes sense. We denote the
ownership of a variable with the subscripts Owned and Free. The operations take and give assign
the appropriate the ownership of a variable.

3. LTS SEMANTICS

This is the code artifact for the so-called “LTS semantics” approach, as part of a literate Rocq
formalization that is available for viewing upon request.
During the time that this approach was still being pursued, the strategy was to build up a domain-
specific language for (suitable generalizations of) labeled transition systems (LTS’s), and prove
characteristic theorems for compositional deduction of these LTS’s. We would proceed to define
the operational semantics of QQ machine models via a series of compilation passes down to
these LTS’s, and the theorems proven at the level of LTS’s would carry back up to the level of
QQ machine models. This envisioned strategy is not unlike how large proof developments like
CompCert organize their program models by a hierarchical series of compilation passes through
intermediary languages of lesser and lesser complexity.
In our case, the LTS’s that we would be working with at the lowest level would have for state
space 𝑇 × 𝐸𝑛𝑣, where 𝑇 is some index type of locations for the machine (that is, user-defined states
in the underlying finite state machine) and 𝐸𝑛𝑣 is some abstraction whose instances carry all the
dynamic, run-time data associated with an execution at a given point in time (such as variable types
and assignments).
We recognize that it should be possible to combine such LTS’s, e.g. parallel composition of LTS’s
with state spaces 𝑇1 × 𝐸𝑛𝑣 and 𝑇2 × 𝐸𝑛𝑣 should yield an LTS with state space (𝑇1 × 𝑇2 ) × 𝐸𝑛𝑣. The
way we chose to achieve this was by creation of an intermediate abstraction referred to as an ELTS
(envigorated LTS), which with reference type 𝑇 referred to (and could be forgetfully compiled down
to) an LTS with state space 𝑇 × 𝐸𝑛𝑣.
The structure of the code presented here is as follows:

 • In Syntax, the syntax of a 𝑀𝑎𝑐ℎ𝑖𝑛𝑒 (an abstract syntax tree close to the user-facing structure
 of a QSpec) is developed;
 • In Static, the base for static analysis of 𝑀𝑎𝑐ℎ𝑖𝑛𝑒s with respect to a well-typing/variable
 ownership scheme was being developed; much of it became deprecated as underlying layers
 of the semantics were changed, and while these semantics were still changing, the static
 analysis algorithms and theory were not a focus to update;
 • In Env, the core API of the environment/context type 𝐸𝑛𝑣 is developed, as well as its dynamic
 semantics with respect to 𝐴𝑐𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛s;
 • In LTL, traditional theory about linear temporal semantics is developed;

 • In LTS, the theory of LTS’s and ELTS’s are developed, with the desired composition and
 deduction theorems;

 • In Chart, the compilation of 𝑀𝑎𝑐ℎ𝑖𝑛𝑒 → 𝐸 𝐿𝑇 𝑆 → 𝐿𝑇 𝑆 is defined, and it is demonstrated

An abstraction refinement simulation is a relationship between ELTS’s. It relates snapshots of the
underlying LTS such that steps in the refinement can always be taken in the abstraction (backward
simulation), and such that initializations in the refinement can always be matched by initializations
in the abstraction (also a form of backward simulation).

refinement from invariants on the abstraction.

4. ACTION SEMANTICS

Here we explore another point in the design space of structural operational theories for statecharts.
Instead of having a relation which includes the current state and the next state, the action semantics
is defined by a relation 𝑚 ⊢ (𝑠, 𝑙) Ð→ 𝑙 ′ where the machine 𝑚 determines the transition on locations
 𝐴

𝑙 to 𝑙 ′ with the input state 𝑠 and the proposed actions 𝐴 to be performed for the next step. This
enables a simpler step relation avoiding the tracking of registers and skipping. We can then build a
trace for a machine by applying actions before the next step depending on whether or not they are
registers.
The action semantics does not have a standard notion of typing, but we will define the standard
notion of free variable as well as statechart specific notion of touched variables.

4.1.        Syntax

The syntax of machines and of dynamic structures is modified under this new approach, though
we try to maintain as much similarity of syntax as possible. The first change is that a lot of the
annotations are dropped; variables are merely the placeholders 𝑥, 𝑦, and 𝑧. We use the letters 𝑖, 𝑗,
𝑘, and ℎ for indices of maps.

The SPlain rule shows that ○ steps to ○ with any store and the empty action. This is a point of

in the location and that part of the machine action is removed. Finally, there are two rules for

the initial states of the codomain of the transition. Note again there are two possible sources of
internal non-determinism: the choice of transition and the choice of initial state. The last transition
SSeqSub is where the child of a sequential composition takes a step; note that this only occurs when
none of the parent transitions are satisfiable.

5. CONCLUSION

This project’s express goal was to build a semantics for state machines that supported the three
key desired theorems. For various instantiatons of the semantics we have achieved this goal and
provided a basic set of constraints that any state machine semantics should adhere to.

5.1. Future Work

One-writer-many-readers is not always the most direct way to define communication between com-
ponents. We hypothesize that more direct communication can be modeled and proven automatically
using state machines restricted to resemble resource algebras [Iris] while still allowing for proofs
to be retained for components when added to a larger system. Further, during our research, we have
discovered several variants of the one-writer-many-readers data pattern that support variations of
the component proofs. For example, instead of one nested child being allowed to own a particular
variable, many adjacent children might share a variable. In future work we wish to pursue these
alternate and more expressive notions of data sharing for the purposes of proving our desired
theorems for more models.
