Finite element thermoelastic analysis of functionally graded spherical shells
Authors: Anil Kumar, Gopal Kumar Gupta, Ajit Kumar Singh, Achche Lal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGraphene, a remarkable material, has numerous efficient applications due to its enhanced chemical, mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties. By gradually spreading graphene nanoplatelets into a matrix, graphene functionally graded materials combine remarkable strength and low weight. Thermoelastic analysis is crucial for forecasting performance and guaranteeing dependability in sophisticated structural applications since it assesses how they react to combined mechanical stresses and temperature changes. In the present work, we consider two functionally graded materials: one made of Graphene and Copper, and the other of Titanium and Zirconium. The problem is formulated for a spherical shell under two generalized thermoelastic theories to examine the thermoelastic behavior, specifically for the functionally graded material of Graphene. We use the Laplace transform technique to eliminate the time dependency from the governing equations and the finite element method to address the non-linearity of the governing equations, which is generated by the dependency of thermal parameters on spatial coordinates. A detailed comparative study of the results obtained is discussed to focus on the functionally graded material and the necessity of the finite element method for generalized thermoelasticity. The numerical values of the field variables are shown through different graphs to provide a more compact analysis of generalized thermoelasticity for functionally graded materials.Keywords: Graphene, Spherical Shell, Generalized thermoelasticity, Finite Element method, Functionally graded materialSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

There are two main problems with classical thermoelasticity, often known as the theory of uncoupled thermoelasticity. The first is that, contrary to what we observe in physical tests, it implies that the temperature of an elastic body is independent of its mechanical state. The second problem is that the parabolic heat conduction equation in the theory falsely implies that temperature changes can occur at an unlimited speed, which is not a real-world occurrence. The first of these issues was effectively resolved by Biot’s1 linked thermoelasticity theory, but because it also made use of a parabolic-type heat conduction equation, it continued to have problems with infinite speed. Researchers such as Lord and Shulman2, Green and Lindsay3, and Green Naghdi4–6 developed generalized thermoelasticity theories to produce a more realistic model. These new theories predicted a limiting speed for thermal waves in solids by including one or two relaxation intervals into the thermoelastic process. Either the energy equation and the Neuman–Duhamel connection were changed, or Fourier’s heat conduction equation was modified. The thermoelasticity with dual-phase-lag effects hypothesis, which included two phase-lag factors, was later presented by Tzou7 and Chandrasekharaiah8. Roychoudhuri9 then added three distinct phase-lags to the GN-III model, substantially altering it. One imortant property of GN-II model is that most of the recent developed thermoelasticity theories predict finite speeds, but these models are dissipative, whereas GN-II is conservative. The well-posedness of the solutions to these phase-lag thermoelasticity theories has been the subject of numerous investigations. Hetnarski and Ignaczak10 have shown the theoretical significance of these models, while Quintanilla and Racke11 have examined their stability. Quintanilla’s12,13 most recent reworking of the exact three-phase-lag model resulted in a heat conduction equation with a single delay term. To make the DPL equation easier to solve, most researchers employ a first or second-order Taylor expansion. Quintanilla contends that these approximations may result in unstable solutions, where the temperature increases indefinitely, that are absent from the ” exact” model. He concentrates on the mathematical well-posedness, demonstrating that the behavior of the ”exact” phase-lag model differs from that of its truncated approximations and is frequently more stable.

In engineering, common structural shapes like spheres, cylinders, and disks are crucial design components, so it is crucial that they are made to endure the heavy stresses they will experience. In 1984, a spaceplane project in Japan gave rise to the idea of Functionally Graded Materials (FGMs). The goal was to create a new thermal barrier material that could withstand a temperature decrease of 1000 K across a thickness of less than 10 mm and a surface temperature of 2000 K. Since then, a lot of research has been done on FGMs, especially as thin films, and they are almost ready for commercial use. These materials are considered state-of-the-art, heat-resistant choices due to their remarkable thermal characteristics and ability to withstand extremely high thermal loads. Additionally, they have outstanding resistance to corrosion and erosion as well as great fracture toughness. The basic idea behind FGMs is to create a continuously changing composite material by mixing metal and ceramic. The spatial composition is designed to enable a gradual change in material qualities from one component to the next, such as thermal conductivity or Young’s modulus. As a result, the mechanical and thermal coefficients that describe the distribution of stress and temperature in generalized thermoelasticity equations are not constant; rather, they depend continuously on the material’s spatial position.

Understanding how FGMs respond to different mechanical and thermal loads has garnered a lot of attention throughout the last ten years. However, because the governing equations for temperature and the related thermoelastic fields are frequently nonlinear, it is difficult to find exact solutions. The idea of laminated composites has been used by numerous researchers as a practical means of approximating solutions for FGM thermoelastic problems in order to get around this. This method has been applied in a number of studies to examine transient thermoelastic problems in FGMs. Other approaches have also been investigated. One-dimensional, steady-state, and transient thermal stresses in functionally graded circular hollow cylinders and spheres were investigated by Obata and Noda14,15 using the perturbation method. Assuming a linear composition of the constituent materials, Lutz and Zimmerman16,17 offered an analytical solution for stresses in thick, radially graded spheres under radial thermal loads. Additionally, they came up with a precise solution for a uniformly heated cylinder in which the thermal expansion coefficient and elastic moduli vary linearly with radius. Shabana and Noda’s18 work, which examined thermoelastoplastic stresses in a fully functionally graded plate under a thermal load, is an example of further research. In the meanwhile, Sankar and Tzeng19 calculated the axial stress distribution in a FGM beam by solving the thermoelastic equilibrium equations.

When examining the thermal stress in FGM hollow circular cylinders, Liew et al.20 adopted a novel methodology. They created a revolutionary technique that adapts answers from smaller, homogeneous cylinders in place of the intricate equations of non-homogeneous thermoelasticity. In order to make inferences regarding the overall behavior of thermal stresses in these FGM cylinders, they subsequently examined a number of numerical instances. Darabseh and Bani Salameh21 employed a parabolic heat conduction model to study the transient thermal stresses in a FGM cylinder. They made the assumption that the radial direction of the material qualities varied exponentially. They employed an implicit finite difference numerical technique with various thermal boundary conditions to compute the temperature, radial displacement, and stress distributions. Kothari and Mukhopadhyay22 used a variety of thermoelasticity theories to study a functionally graded hollow disk. They used a volume-fraction rule to represent the material properties of the disk as a power-law function of the radial direction. The coupled equations that resulted from their research were solved using a Galerkin type finite element approach.

The Earth’s crust contains a lot of carbon, and people can benefit greatly from its different forms, or allotropes. Graphene, a two-dimensional sheet of carbon with atoms organized in a honeycomb lattice by sp2 hybridization, is one prominent allotrope. Graphene is unique because of its exceptional qualities. At 2630 square meters per gram, it has a remarkably high specific surface area. With a high elastic modulus of 1.02 TPa and an inherent strength of 130 GPa, it is exceptionally strong mechanically. With a high charge carrier mobility of 15,000 /V s at room temperature and a thermal conductivity of 5000 W/mK, it is also a very good conductor of heat and electricity.

We must examine graphene’s elastic properties in order to comprehend its special mechanical qualities. These consist of its mass density, specific heat, Poisson’s ratio, Lame’s constants, elastic modulus, and negative coefficient of thermal expansion. Graphene is frequently seen as a two-dimensional structure due to its extreme thinness: just one atom thick. This indicates that its in-plane tensile behavior: that is, how it stretches and pulls within that plane: is its most crucial mechanical characteristic. Its elastic modulus and Poisson’s ratio are the two most basic mechanical properties that characterize this behavior.

Lee et al.23,24 determined that the elastic modulus of a single layer of graphene was roughly 1.02 GPa using an atomic force microscope and a nanoindentation technique and estimated Young’s modulus of graphene by Raman spectroscopy. Similarly, Zhang and Pan25 obtained a somewhat lower result of roughly 0.89 GPa for monolayer graphene using nanoindentation as well. Zhang and Pan went one step farther and demonstrated that the elastic modulus drops with the number of layers. For two layers, the value was 0.393 TPa; for three levels, it was 0.051 TPa; and for four or more layers, it was 0.027 TPa. They came to the conclusion that as the number of graphene sheets rises, the elastic modulus decreases. In a different work, Frank et al.26 used an atomic force microscope hanging over trenches to test the elastic modulus of graphene stacks (less than five layers). They stated that the figure was roughly 0.5 TPa. The Young’s modulus of graphene is strong at ambient temperature but decreases as the temperature rises, according to Georgantzinos et al.27 curiously, a linear growth is seen at absolute zero. Additionally, an ongoing experiment was used to validate the study’s conclusions.

Under normal circumstances, pristine graphene exhibits a positive Poisson’s ratio, according to early numerical simulations by Zakharchenko et al.28. However, their study also revealed that when the temperature exceeds about 1700 K, this value turns negative. Los et al.29 corroborated this conclusion by stating that the experimental system size is responsible for the Poisson’s ratio, which can vary between 0.15 and 0.3. This range is in line with Gui et al.30’s numerical simulation results, which showed a value of about 0.17. There is a contradiction in the scientific literature, as several other research31–36 have claimed a negative Poisson’s ratio for graphene in spite of these findings.

Anisotropic graphene will have important theoretical investigations in the thermo-mechanical and mechanical domains in recent years reported by scientists such as Sfyris et al.37–40, Favata et al.41, and Delfani et al.42. The theoretical and practical analysis of thin graphene sheets on substrates is a particularly challenging subject. Several mathematical models have been presented to better understand the behavior of monolayer graphene. A sixth-order structural tensor can be used to characterize the zigzag and armchair orientations of graphene, which is treated as an anisotropic hexagonal 2-lattice in one such model. Researchers were able to obtain closed-form solutions for the nonlinear theory by using a finite elasticity model. By developing models for freestanding monolayer graphene as a 2D 2-lattice, further work43–45 built upon this. According to these theories, the shift vector and the strain and curvature tensors have a quadratically dependent effect on the material’s characteristics.

Graphene and Copper can be fabricated using powder metallurgy, where layers with varying concentrations of Graphene are combined and sintered. This approach allows for a gradient in properties, such as hardness, across the material. FGMs of graphene and copper have potential applications in areas requiring materials with tailored mechanical and thermal properties, such as in aerospace and other high-performance applications.

The Finite Element Method (FEM) is more than simply a computational tool in the context of Functionally Graded Materials; it is the link that turns the hypothetical ”super-material” into a manufacturable reality. The material must typically be uniform in order to use traditional analytical solutions. But with FGMs, the density ϱ (r), thermal conductivity K(r), Young’s modulus E(r) and other elastic parameters vary at each point along the space variables. Abbas et. al46. has used the FEM to address the plane waves in generalized thermo-microstretch elastic solid with thermal relaxation. Further, Othman et. al47–49. have studied the effect of rotation on plane waves in generalized thermomicrostretch elastic solid, fundamental solution of generalized thermo-viscoelasticity and generalized magneto- thermo-microstretch analysis in elastic solids, respectively using FEM. Laplace transform method is also applied simultaneously here to tackle the dependency of the problem on time. The combined results are obtained here using Laplace and FEM technique. Othman et. al50. have adopted an analytical technique of the Eigen value expansion method to analyze on plane waves through magneto-thermoelastic microstretch rotating medium with temperature dependent elastic properties. A detailed analysis of the effects of the phase-lags, the angular frequency of thermal vibration and the variability thermal conductivity parameter on the displacement, incremental temperature, and the thermal stresses are presented by Othman and Abouelregal in51.

Abouelregal152had examine thermomechanical interactions within a functionally graded unbounded non-homogeneous solid with a spherical hole in a unified way using the Moore–Gibson–Thompson thermoelasticity (MGTE) model.. Thermoelastic analysis of functionally graded materials for fractional heat transfer models and MGT has been carried out by Abouelregal et. al53–56.. Alhashash and Abouelregal57 have presented an analytical framework to investigate the photothermal response of functionally graded semiconductor materials under laser pulse excitation, utilizing a nonlocal thermoelastic model.

In the present work, an investigation of thermoelastic behavior on a functionally graded hollow sphere constituted with graphene and Copper under an exact heat conduction model proposed by Quintanilla et. al12,13. together with another generalized thermoelastic models has carried out. The inner boundary of the disk is assumed to be under the thermal shock and stress free. The other boundary is taken insulated with temperature and fixed with some rigid support. We uses the Laplace transform technique to remove the dependency of governing equations on time and finite element method to tackle the non-linearity of governing equations generated due to dependency of thermal parameters on spacial Coordinate. A detailed study on the obtained result is discussed to focus on the functionally graded material and the necessity of the finite element method to the generalized thermoelasticity. The numerical values of the field variables has shown by the different graphs to give the more compact analysis of the generalized thermoelasticity for functionally graded materials.

The current study highlights the following points. Unlike classical models where the heat flux is proportional to the temperature gradient, GN-II model makes the heat flux proportional to the thermal displacement gradient. It predicts that thermal waves travel at a finite speed (undamped thermal waves). This is critical for modeling materials that operate at cryogenic temperatures or under extremely fast laser-pulse heating, where the ”lag” in heat response significantly affects structural integrity. Quintanilla’s models account for the micro-structural delay between the application of a heat gradient and the actual movement of heat carriers (electrons or phonons). It introduces two distinct time lags: The time, it takes for heat to start moving and the time, it takes for the material’s lattice to respond. This allows for a more ”memory-dependent” simulation of materials, where the current state of stress depends on the history of thermal loading.

Although copper has a high coefficient of thermal expansion and is heavy, it is the gold standard for conductivity. One can make a material that can withstand conditions where conventional copper components would distort or melt by grading it with graphene. Several important real-world implications can be simulated using solution with the Finite Element Method for this particular FGM. Massive heat fluxes are applied to materials in nuclear fusion environments (such as the divertor plates in ITER) while one side is cooled by water. The ”mismatch” in thermal expansion is a common cause of failure for standard copper-to-steel couplings.

Displacement

Figure 4(a, b) illustrate the changes in deformation u for FGM-1 and FGM-2 under the Quintanilla and GN-II models with a fixed value of homogeneity index, n at time t=0.2 and 0.3. For FGM-1, it is shown that u first declines before beginning to rise and going to zero towards outer boundary of the spherical shell; for FGM-2, this pattern is entirely the opposite. This reaction is explained by the fact that FGM-1 contains Graphene as an ingredient that behaves differently than most materials. Compared to FGM-2, the degree of deformation for FGM-1 is smaller. The greater hardness of the Graphene material found in FGM-1 makes this difference obvious. Additionally, as seen in Figure 4(a, b), it is noted that the displacement’s effective area and magnitude both grow over time. In the models’ context, the Quintanilla model exhibits greater moduli deformation than the GN-II model for FGM-2. On the other hand, the middle region shows a reversal tendency in the case of FGM-1.

When the fluctuation of n is taken into account, Figure 5(a, b) illustrate how displacement u varies under the GN-II model for t=0.2 and 0.3, whereas Figure 5(c, d) give the similar description for the Quintanilla model. While there is almost no change for FGM-2 because of the smaller impact of the non-homogeneity index n, Figures 5(a, b) show a noticeable variation in deformation for FGM-1, demonstrating the unique behavior of Graphene in comparison to other materials. The variations in deformation u for both FGMs, however, become increasingly noticeable as time t increases. The Quintanilla model shows a result (shown in Figures 5(c, d)) that is comparable to that shown in Figure 5(a, b) for the GN-II model. Temperature

Conclusions

In this study, a thermoelastic analysis has been performed on two types of FGMs: one made of Graphene and Copper, and the other consisting of Titanium and Zirconium.

In conventional shells composed of two distinct layers (such as a ceramic coating over metal), the layers frequently delaminate at the joint. Because FGMs change smoothly, there is no ”joint.” This prevents stress accumulation and increases the shell’s strength in extremely hot conditions.
