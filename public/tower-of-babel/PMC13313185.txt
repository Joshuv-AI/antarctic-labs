Efficient inference of non-polyreactive antibody variants dependent on local fine-tuning
Authors: Matthew Saunders, Sophie Comyn, Borka Medjo, Zahra Anderson, Chase LaCombe, Jake Ripley, Kyra Chapman, Tim Ma, Abigail Ventura, Alyssa Ruelos, Alec Jaeger, Chase Poindexter, Ophelia Rebanal, Nemanja Vucic, Sean M. West, David Eavarone, John M. Sutton, Douglas Ganini da Silva, David A. Spencer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntibody polyreactivity, which is characterized by broad, low-affinity off-target binding, is an undesirable drug property associated with rapid clearance. Existing in vitro screening methods often suffer from interassay variability, while current in silico models lack the generalizability needed to efficiently identify non-polyreactive variants within the localized sequence spaces typical of drug‑optimization campaigns. Here, we address both challenges through refining low‑ and high‑throughput assays suitable for generating standardized datasets and quantifying the impact of localized protein language model (PLM) tuning in a specific drug‑optimization context. We demonstrate that range‑normalized summation scores derived from multi-concentration monoclonal antibody ELISA provide highly reproducible ground-truth measurements with inter-experiment Pearson r ≥ 0.99. We verified a previously developed yeast-display high-throughput approach for polyreactivity data generation and screened a library of 240,000 single-chain variable fragment heavy‑chain complementarity-determining region variants, then used this data to fine-tune two PLMs. As expected, local fine-tuning dramatically improved model performance on a test set in the relevant sequence space, while performance on an out-of-distribution set of 80 clinical antibodies was largely unchanged. The top‑performing base and tuned models were tasked with inferring 18 low or non-polyreactive variants. The observed inference success rates were 0% and 66.6%, respectively, demonstrating the practical utility of local tuning in a drug development context. Together, this work provides a reproducible, integrated framework combining robust in vitro assay methodology with locally tuned in silico models to efficiently resolve polyreactivity during antibody optimization.

Introduction

Since the first approval in 1986, monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have emerged as a leading class of clinical‑stage therapeutics due to their aptitude for exquisite target specificity and humoral tolerance with potential for dual functionality through direct target engagement and lymphocyte recruitment.1–3 Despite this success, ideal antibody therapeutics consist of a range of diverse properties that confer the desired functional, safety, and developability profile, and the collation of which continues to impede the rapid development of novel antibody drugs.4 This motivates continued efforts to develop multi-property programmable approaches that can be applied early in the drug‑creation process and that generalize across development campaigns.

Antibody polyreactivity – defined as broad, low‑affinity off-target interactions generally driven by patches of positive charge or hydrophobicity5,6 – is a particularly undesirable drug property that drives rapid clearance and is associated with poor pharmacokinetic profiles.7–9 During the development of endogenous antibody responses, the expansion of polyreactive B‑cell clones is mitigated through rounds of positive and negative selection in the lymphoid tissues. Synthetic antibodies derived from in vitro screening of libraries based on the natural repertoire or computational methods undergo no such physical selection filter, and numerous first-generation approved antibodies are polyreactive, with suboptimal clearance noted in the clinic.8 To ameliorate this, an array of in vitro assays has been developed to inform down-selection of non-polyreactive variants, most notably the mAb enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) against the heterogeneous protein mixtures displayed on baculovirus particles (BVP) or from cell lysate (polyspecificity reagent (PSR)), or against single reagents such as DNA, insulin, or ovalbumin (OVA).10 Orthogonal assay alternatives include screening mAbs immobilized on beads or displaying antibody sequences, generally in single-chain variable fragment (scFv) or VHH format on yeast.6,11–14 A persistent limitation of each of these screening methods is that the inherent noisiness of reporting weak interactions (KDs > 1 µM), gives rise to inter- and intra-assay variability, often reflected by low to moderate correlations observed between datasets spanning different experimental days, labs-of-origin, assay formats, or reagents.10,15,16

In silico filtering offers the potential for a more rapid, scalable, and cost-effective method to supplement in vitro screening. To date, zero-shot approaches for polyreactivity predictions are non-performant, and autoregressive or protein language model (PLM)-based methods are dependent on the amount, quality, and proximity of the training data.6,11,13,17 Notably, models trained on library-scale yeast surface display (YSD) polyreactivity datasets encompassing the human germline breadth can achieve moderate performance on clinical antibodies in both VHH and mAb formats,6,11 but these may poorly resolve sequences with low to moderate polyreactivity.13 In many optimization contexts, it is critical to avoid designating false positives for sequences with otherwise favorable development profiles. Thus, while of some use for binary scoring of non- or extremely polyreactive antibody sequences, existing models may lack the necessary training data to interrogate a localized sequence space with high accuracy.13,18

To address these gaps, here we rigorously characterize the reproducibility of the mAb ELISA assay and demonstrate the use of range‑normalized summation (RNS) scores as a robust single readout capturing multi-concentration replicates and ideally suited for building high-quality expandable datasets for modeling. We evaluate score variability between reagents and recommend the use of OVA as the most consistently scoring reagent between ELISA and YSD formats. Additionally, we benchmark the performance of two polyreactivity models against public and internal datasets. Fine-tuning with a YSD-derived dataset composed of variants within the localized sequence space of interest substantially elevated model performance and enabled efficient identification of novel non-polyreactive variants. Together, this work informs the use of both in vitro and in silico polyreactivity screening tools that can be integrated into antibody optimization workflows.

Results

ELISA RNS scores are highly reproducible

We first sought to adapt published mAb ELISA methodology10,12,19 with the aim of providing a single, reproducible score well-suited for building a continuously expandable dataset for modeling. We selected a set of 32 mAbs,18 of which were clinical antibodies with a previously reported range of low–high polyreactivity, and the remaining composed of internal sequences. Plates were pre-coated with the same reagent lot of OVA, insulin, DNA, and PSR, then mAbs were tested at four concentrations in triplicate over three different experiment days. Polyreactivity scores (RNS) were derived as a single value to capture both dose effects and control for plate-to-plate variability as detailed in the Methods.

Exquisite reproducibility was observed for RNS values across multiple experiments, with interexperiment Pearson correlation coefficients ≥ 0.99 for each reagent (Figure 1(A)). Notably, RNS values demonstrated reproducible and strong correlations between reagents, with Pearson correlation coefficients ≥ 0.90 for each experiment (Figure 1(B)). Additionally, we examined these values for consistency with external measurements for the shared subset of 18 mAbs. As the ELISA values reported in Jain et al.10 are measured with different reagents than those used in this study – specifically versus six recombinant proteins (reported there as “PSR” and here as “Jain PSR”) or BVP – we used the average Jain PSR and BVP values for benchmarking, considering this most representative of a heterogenous protein pool comparable to the PSR derived from Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell lysate used here. A strong correlation was found between the Jain et al. 10 composite value and PSR and OVA RNS scores measured here, with Pearson r = 0.859 and 0.808, respectively. Moderate correlations were observed with the remaining reagents (Figure 1(C)). Together, high interexperiment reproducibility, consistency between reagents, and agreement with external datasets provide confidence that the plate ELISA protocol adapted for use here is well positioned to generate high-quality and continuously expandable “ground truth” datasets for both training and validating polyreactivity models. Staining yeast-displayed scFvs with OVA is suitable for high-throughput polyreactivity data generation.

We next sought to optimize a high-throughput flow cytometry-based approach for both polyreactivity screening and data generation for modeling, testing incubation time, reagent concentration, and potential variability from two induction batches using 16 standards expressed as scFvs on yeast. Staining conditions were selected to strike a balance between minimizing an upward shift in signal for the negative controls while maximizing differentiation of fluorescence intensity between the positive and negative standards. We found these criteria were most satisfactorily met with 0.13 mg/ml of each reagent, with incubation times of 1 h for OVA and PSR and overnight for insulin (Supplemental Figures S1–2). Expression levels were generally similar for scFvs expressed from two induction batches, with 14/16 standards showing expression levels within 10% of each other in each fermentation run, and this was deemed an unlikely potential source of major variability for experiments run at different times (Supplemental Figure S3). High standard deviations in geometric mean fluorescence intensity (gMFI) values were observed across replicates for all three polyreactivity reagents. To minimize day-to-day variation, we adapted a previously reported method whereby gMFI values are first normalized for expression and then range-normalized to positive (Briakinumab scFv = 1) and negative (Trastuzumab scFv = 0) controls.6 Following assay optimization, YSD scFv staining demonstrated variable consistency with values measured in mAb ELISA dependent on the reagent, with only OVA signals well-correlated with mAb ELISA RNS scores (Pearson r = 0.787). High-throughput data generation within a localized variant space for model evaluation

A YSD sorting protocol similar to that performed by Chen et al.6 was performed to benchmark high-diversity library polyreactivity data generation and model performance with and without fine-tuning on a local sequence space. Briefly, a 240,000-member scFv library composed of variants in the same framework with ≤4 heavy‑chain complementarity-determining region (HCDR) mutations from the parental antibody was iteratively sorted against OVA, PSR, and insulin, enriching the sequences with the top and bottom 10% fluorescent signals each round (sort Rounds 1–3, respectively). A Round 4 sort was performed twice against each reagent to generate Bio-replicate 1 and Bio-replicate 2 (Supplemental Figure S4). Post‑sort processing, 32,790 and 24,972 high-confidence sequences were designated polyreactive or non-polyreactive, derived from the gates collecting variants with the top and bottom 10% signal, respectively. All 11 of the recovered standards built into the library were designated as high or low polyreactive as expected based on their single-strain stain profiles (Supplemental Figure S5).

ESM with Chen data generally outperformed Chen for most metrics across the Rounds 1–4 datasets, most pointedly in Recall and F1 Score (Figure 3(A), Supplemental Figure S6). We next checked model performance with or without additional tuning with the union of Round 3 and Round 4 data using a 80/20 train/validation split on a holdout set of 3,998 sequences as described in the methods (Figure 3(B), Supplemental Figure S7). These further tuned models are herein referred to as ESM with local tuning, ESM with Chen data and local tuning, and Chen with local tuning. As expected, the base ESM model was non-performant, labeling all sequences as zero, or non-polyreactive, with a randomly derived accuracy of 0.39 and uncalculable precision, recall, and F1. The base Chen model was moderately performant, consistent with the expectation that it has some utility as a general‑purpose polyreactivity model6; however, it substantially benefited from tuning with the local dataset derived here, which boosted its comparatively poor performance on accuracy, recall, and F1 to ≥0.95 for each of these metrics. Similar high performance was observed in the other cases where local tuning was applied, with both ESM with local tuning and ESM with Chen data and local tuning performing near equivalently well (Figure 3(B), Supplemental Figure S7).

To further underscore the substantial impact of local tuning on model performance, each of these models was next used to assign polyreactivity scores to 80 clinical mAbs, and these were compared to in vitro bead-based polyreactivity readouts vs OVA or soluble membrane proteins (SMP) previously reported using binary‑classification thresholds (Dataset S8 from Chen et al.).6,21 Consistent with previous evaluation on out-of-distribution test sets – those with substantial framework and CDR diversity – the models that had been pretrained with polyreactivity data demonstrated moderate performance at binary classification. For the models pretrained with the Chen dataset (Chen and ESM with Chen data), the additional tuning with the data generated here, which in this context is “out-of-distribution,” had negligible impact (Figure 3(C), Supplemental Figure S8). Consistently, generally weak correlations were observed across the distribution of in vitro scores (Supplemental Figure S9). Together, these results support the premise that local tuning may be desirable to enable efficient identification of non-polyreactive variants for a given drug development program.

Tuned ESM efficiently infers low or non-polyreactive sequences

To categorize polyreactivity in a drug development context, we classify molecules on a five-point scale derived from the observed range of RNS scores, with RNS score ≤ 6 = None; 6–12 = Low; 12–20 = Moderate; 20–30 = High; >30 = Extremely High. Using these definitions, we next sought to evaluate the impact of using local tuning data on model inference in a specific drug development context. Starting with a parental molecule with low-moderate polyreactivity, a range often observed on pre-optimized leads in drug development campaigns, we tasked the top-performing “base” model (ESM with Chen data) with and without local tuning to infer new sequence variants with high (N = 6 / model) or minimal-no (N = 18 / model) polyreactivity, then measured these variants in mAb ELISA (Figure 4). Poor separation was observed for variants inferred by the base model with zero inferred variants showing no or low polyreactivity vs all three reagents (0% success; Figure 4(A-B)). In this case, the fine-tuned model demonstrated substantially improved performance and provided an efficient solution to finding non-polyreactive variants, with 14 of 18 inferred variants showing no polyreactive vs OVA and 12 variants that showed no or low polyreactivity to all three reagents (66.6% success; Figure 4(A-B)). Taken together, these results demonstrate the limitations of applying off-the-shelf models within some drug development context that can be overcome with further tuning with local data. Discussion

While avoiding or resolving polyreactivity is broadly important in drug development applications, the development of standardized approaches is impeded by an array of in vitro methods with varying degrees of reproducibility between groups. The exquisite reproducibility of mAb ELISA RNS scores (Figure 1(A-B)), which account for both dose effects and control for plate-to-plate variability with a standard set of controls, provides ground truth measurements suitable for combining datasets collected over time and by separate groups. As such, we recommend this method for wider use in the field.

For generating display-scale datasets, we independently validated the sort-seq method performed by Chen et al.6 Standards displayed as scFvs on yeast had variable reagent-dependent correlations with the mAb ELISA values, mostly consistent with variable relationships between scFv display and purified mAb assays reported elsewhere.10,11,14,21 The moderate to poor correlation between YSD scFv staining and mAb ELISA readouts may in part be driven by the lack of potential for avidity in monovalent format, which is expected to appreciably support observed bi-valent polyreactivity.22,23 While this effect provided the rationale for using iterative sorts vs multiple reagents to score library variants with a composite polyreactivity metric for modeling,6 the OVA reagent consistently demonstrated strong agreement between formats and may be a sufficient proxy for scoring polyreactivity in display formats for most drug development applications. This may be due to its comparatively greater net negative charge mitigating an avidity requirement for detection in some contexts.24

High‑fidelity and expandable datasets generated by these methods are desirable for both selection and modeling, as existing developability models lack meaningful generalizability. For example, a seminal benchmarking exercise that compared model outputs sourced from 113 groups spanning academia, pharma, and biotech found that the most performant model had a Spearman ⍴ of 0.559 vs a more sequence-similar holdout set (labeled GDPa1), and that this dropped to a Spearman ⍴ of 0.337 against a more diverse test set of 80 antibodies spanning a wide range of germline and CDR diversity selected from the OAS database (labeled GDPa3).25 Stronger classifiers can be obtained with PLMs tuned on display data in both VHH11 and scFv6; however, these remain imprecise predictors if individual sequences, as, in the latter case, we found a Spearman ⍴ of 0.57 vs a similarly sized test set of 80 clinical mAbs (Supplemental Figure S9).

To quantify the impact of local tuning, we selected the ESM models for inference and in vitro validation due to their slightly higher classification metrics on the lead variant library (Figure 3), although both ESM and Chen models showed substantially improved performance with local tuning. This is consistent with observations for other antibody-specific PLMs IgBert and IgT5, for which pre-training with variants in the localized space (the random split set) boosted performance, an effect not observed with out-of-domain tuning (the cluster split set).26 This trend showcasing the beneficial impact of more localized tuning was again observed in a separate study for antiBERTy, AntiBERTa2, IgBert, ProtBert, and ProtT5 when trained on the Chen et al.6 Dataset 1 and tested on a Dataset 1 holdout subset (in-domain) or Dataset 2 (less in-domain).18 Together, these studies further solidify the understanding that the quantity, quality, and sequence proximity of the tuning data are more determinant than PLM architecture for improving performance in bespoke settings.

Materials and methods

mAb generation

Antibodies were produced by WuXi Biologics (Wuxi, Jiangsu, China) using a CHO-based system at a 1 mL culture volume scale and purified from culture supernatants via protein A affinity chromatography. Antibodies were stored in a sodium acetate buffer containing 20 mM histidine, 150 mM NaCl, at pH 5.5. In certain cases, formulations were further supplemented with 150 mM arginine and 60 mM succinic acid. Endotoxin levels were maintained below 0.020 EU/mg for all samples.

ELISA

Polyreactivity ELISA was adapted from previously described methods.10,12,19 Here, 384‑well Nunc MaxiSorp plates (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA) were coated with 50 µL per well of DNA from salmon testes (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, catalog number D1626), insulin (Millipore, Burlington, MA, catalog number I9278), OVA (Millipore, Burlington, MA, catalog number A7641), or PSR. DNA was coated at 10 µg/mL in 0.25 M calcium chloride (VWR, Radnor, PA), 2.5 M sodium chloride (VWR, Radnor, PA), 0.05 M sodium acetate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) buffer, pH 3.5. Insulin was coated at 5 µg/mL in 0.1 M carbonate/bicarbonate buffer (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA), and OVA was coated at 500 µg/mL in 0.1 M carbonate/bicarbonate buffer. A large lot of biotinylated PSR was generated from CHO cell extract for dual use in ELISA and flow cytometry using approximately 1 billion ExpiCHO cells (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA), pelleting cells at 550 ×g and washing twice with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were suspended in 10 mL of 1× PBS +0.1% Tween-20 (PBS-T) with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA, catalog number A32963) added. Biotinylation was performed by adding 330 µL of 100 mM EZ-Link LC-biotin (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA) in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and incubating for 3 hours at 4°C ± 2°C with gentle rotation. Cells were lysed using a glass dounce with 50 dounce stroke steps performed on the 10 mL volume. Excess LC-biotin was removed using a 3,500 Mw Cutoff Slide-A-Lyzer cartridge (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA) in 2 L of 1× PBS with stirring at 300 rpm, with one buffer exchange at 4°C ± 2°C, with a minimum of 4 hours per buffer exchange.

Plates were incubated with each reagent at 4°C ± 2°C with overnight shaking at 525 rpm on a digital microplate shaker (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA). Overnight coated plates were washed three times with 100 µL per well using PBS with 0.1% Tween-20 (PBS-T) (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA) and 1 mM EDTA (Crystalgen, Commack, NY) and blocked for 1 hour using the same buffer, shaking at 525 rpm at room temperature for 1 hour. Antibody concentrations were determined using a SoloVPE instrument (Repligen, Waltham, MA) with extinction coefficients calculated using protein sequence information. Samples were prepared at 10 µg/mL and plated in triplicate at 10, 2, 0.4 and 0.08 µg/mL using a Hamilton Star instrument (Hamilton, Reno, NV). After blocking an additional wash step was performed, with 50 µL of each sample added to assay plates and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature, shaking at 525 rpm. Positive controls Bococizumab (iChor Bio, Syracuse, NY, catalog number ICH51420 and Briakinumab (Leinco Technologies, Fenton, MO, catalog number LT500) and negative controls Herceptin (Genentech, South San Francisco, CA, NDC 50,242–132-01) and Cetuximab (iChor Bio, Syracuse, NY, catalog number ICH4004) were included on every 384-well plate run at the same dilution levels as all sample antibodies. Additionally, 12 “blank” wells containing PBS-T were included per plate. After sample incubation an identical wash step was performed and 50 µL per well of a 1:5,000 peroxidase‑conjugated anti-Fcƴ antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, catalog number 128–035-232), diluted in PBS-T, was added with another 1 hour incubation, shaking at 525 rpm. An identical wash was performed and ELISA was developed using 50 µL per well TMB-Ultra (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA) for 12 minutes, followed by quenching with an additional 50 µL per well 2 N sulfuric acid (Ricca Chemical Company, Arlington, TX).

Plates were read on a SpectraMax iD5 instrument (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) at 450 nm absorbance settings, and data reported using SoftMax Pro v 7.1 software. Sample normalization was calculated by averaging the absorbance value for triplicate sample preparations at each concentration, masking outlying wells giving coefficient of variance (CV) value above 15%. Each averaged value was divided by the averaged value of the blank wells (PBS-T) to give a per‑concentration polyreactivity score. These scores were then normalized on a plate‑by‑plate basis using the equation (((“Score”-1)/(Plate Maximum “Score”)-1)*10)+1 to control for potential plate-to-plate variability. After normalization, an RNS value was determined for each sample by adding the normalized score for all four dilution levels for each unique antibody variant.

Flow reagents

OVA and insulin were biotinylated using a 20:1 molar ratio of EZ-Link NHS biotin (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA), suspended in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO) in 1× PBS (VWR, Radnor, PA) incubating for 1 hour at room temperature. Excess biotin was removed by dialyzing in PBS using 3,500 Mw cutoff Slide-A-Lyzer Mini (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA) dialysis chambers at 4°C ± 2°C with three 15 mL buffer changes, for a minimum of 4 hour incubation per buffer change. Biotinylated PSR was generated as described above for use in ELISA.

Yeast strains

Yeast surface display experiments were performed with the EBY100 Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain using methods described previously.27 The scFv/Aga2p fusion was expressed on a single-copy ARS/CEN plasmid under the galactose-inducible GAL1 promoter. The ARS/CEN plasmid contained an auxotrophic selection marker (TRP1). Plasmids expressing the scFv polyreactivity standards were purchased as Clonal Genes (Twist Bioscience). To generate scFv-expressing strains, circular plasmid DNA and Carrier DNA (Invitrogen Salmon Sperm DNA) were used to transform electrocompetent EBY100 using the Gene Pulser Xcell Electroporator (BioRad). Transformed cells were rescued in YPD Media for 2 hours at 30°C and centrifuged at 600 ×g for 2 minutes. YPD Media was removed and pellets were resuspended in CM Glucose minus Tryptophan Media. Resuspended cells were plated on CM glucose minus tryptophan plates (Teknova) and grown at 30°C for 2 d to isolate single colonies.

Individual yeast strains were seeded into SD-CAA (2% glucose) media at OD600 = 0.1 and grown for 16 hours at 30°C with shaking (250 rpm). Yeast cultures were then induced in SG-CAA (0.2% glucose) media at OD600 = 0.5 and grown for 22–24 hours at 20°C with shaking (250 rpm). Post‑induction, cultures were stored at 4°C until used in flow cytometry or sorting experiments.

Yeast library construction

Library sequences were ordered as an Oligo Pool (Twist Bioscience). The Oligo Pool was amplified by PCR using Platinum SuperFi II Polymerase (Thermo Fisher), and gel purified using a 1% agarose gel and the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Cleanup Kit (Macherey-Nagel). The library was initially transformed into an intermediate E. coli cloning strain to recover and amplify the library DNA. Thus, the library was first assembled into a linearized medium‑copy E. coli-compatible plasmid using HIFI Assembly Mix (NEB). The assembly reaction was purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Cleanup Kit (Macherey-Nagel) and transformed into Transformax EPI300 electrocompetent cells (BioResearch Technologies) using a Micro Pulser Electroporator (Biorad) and rescued for 1 hour at 37°C in SOC media. Transformed E. coli was grown overnight in liquid LB culture at 30°C with kanamycin selection. Plasmid DNA was extracted from E. coli cells using the ZymoPURE II Plasmid Midiprep Kit (Zymo).

Sequencing of the library post-midiprep confirmed the presence of library members. Sequencing results were generated by amplifying the region of diversity using Platinum SuperFi II Polymerase (Thermo Fisher). Amplicons were purified in the same manner as the oligo pools and prepped with ThruPLEX DNA-Seq Kit (Takara Bio USA) and sequenced on the Miseq Sequencer (Illumina). To prepare for transformation in EBY100 cells, the library was linearized by a restriction enzyme digestion with I-SceI (NEB) and I-CeuI (NEB). Digested DNA and purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Cleanup Kit (Macherey-Nagel).

To generate the full yeast expression plasmid, the linearized library was co-transformed with an equimolar amount of linearized yeast plasmid backbone and assembled in vivo. Linearized library/plasmid DNA and Carrier DNA (Invitrogen Salmon Sperm DNA) were transformed into electrocompetent EBY100 cells using the Gene Pulser Xcell Electroporator (BioRad). Transformed EBY100 cells were rescued for 2 hours in YPD media and centrifuged at 600 ×g for 2 minutes. YPD media was removed and pellets were resuspended in SD-CAA (2% glucose) media and grown overnight at 30°C. Overnight culture was diluted to an OD600 of 0.5 in SD-CAA (2% glucose) media and grown for 6 hours prior to induction. To capture the library diversity during the EBY100 transformation, the number of transformants generated was > 10x of the theoretical diversity of the library. The number of transformants was determined by plating a small percentage of the rescued and resuspended cells on CM Glucose minus Trp Plates (Teknova) and counting colony-forming units.

Flow cytometry analysis and FACS

To measure the polyreactivity of individual scFv-expressing yeast strains, 4 × 106 induced cells were washed once with PBS buffer and then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 3 minutes. Cells were resuspended in PBS (100 uL volume) containing 0.13 mg/mL biotinylated polyreactivity reagent (OVA, PSR, or insulin), anti-HA (1:50, BioLegend, 901,525), and anti-cmyc AF488 (1:25, Abcam, ab202008). Staining reactions were incubated at room temperature with rotation for 1 hour (OVA and PSR) or overnight (insulin). Following staining, samples were washed twice in ice-cold PBS and then resuspended in PBS (100 uL volume) containing Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated streptavidin (1:100, Invitrogen, S21374). Samples were incubated with rotation at 4°C for 1 hour, then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 3 minutes, and washed once with ice‑cold PBS. Cell pellets were resuspended in 100 uL PBS and run on a SONY ID7000 spectral cell analyzer. All experiments were performed in biological triplicate and 100,000 events were analyzed using FlowJo 10.10.0 software. We used the normalization method described by Chen et al. to compare the level of polyreactivity between different scFv strains. Briefly, the ratio of the gMFI of polyreactivity reagent binding to cmyc expression gMFI was normalized between two control scFvs (Trastuzumab set at 0 and Briakinumab set at 1).

The scFv library was stained as per the flow cytometry method above with the following modifications: 1.6 × 108 cells were stained in a 2 mL volume permitting a minimum 45× coverage of the library diversity, following secondary staining, the cell pellet was resuspended in 4 mL PBS and passed through a 70 uM filter. All sorting was performed on a FACSymphony S6 Cell Sorter using a 70 uM nozzle. A YSD sorting protocol similar to that of Chen et al. was performed to generate datasets for polyreactivity model testing. Briefly, the scFv library was sequentially sorted for high (top 10%) and low (bottom 10%) polyreactivity using OVA (sort 1), PSR (sort 2), and insulin (sort 3) reagents to produce broadly high and low polyreactive populations. Following the insulin sort, the top and bottom populations were sorted with each of the polyreactivity reagents in duplicate (bioreplicates 1 and 2). Collected cells were cultured in SD-CAA (2% glucose) media with penicillin–streptomycin at 30°C with shaking (250 rpm) until reaching a minimum density of OD600 = 1.0.

Deep sequencing

Sequencing was performed on all 18 sorted populations individually. Plasmid DNA is extracted from post-sort yeast outgrowth cultures as per the Zymoprep Plasmid Miniprep II Kit instructions (Zymo Research). Miniprepped DNA is used as the input to a PCR reaction with primers designed to amplify the HCDR123-encoding region of the plasmid. From miniprep DNA material, 4 µL of supernatant is used within SuperFi II mastermix to create 50 µL of material. Samples are incubated at 98°C for 30 seconds, followed by 25 cycles of amplification: denaturing for 10 seconds at 98°C, annealing for 30 seconds at 60°C, and extension for 1 minute per 1kb at 72°C. A final extension for 5 minutes at 72°C following the 25 PCR cycles. The final PCR product is cleaned up using a 0.72× bead-based cleanup, followed by a 1.08× cleanup for the final PCR amplicon product. PCR amplicons are then prepared for sequencing with the ThruPLEX DNA-Seq Kit (Takara Bio USA) and sequenced on the Nextseq1000 sequencing platform. PCR amplicons are incubated with template preparation buffer to repair fragmented dsDNA at 22°C for 25 minutes, then at 55°C for 20 minutes. Stem‑loop adaptors are added to the repaired PCR amplicon fragments, incubating at 22°C for 40 minutes. Addition of index and Illumina‑based primers to finalize the sequencing amplicon library, followed with a PCR library amplification: 2 minutes at 72°C, denaturing for 2 minutes at 98°C, PCR amplification for 4–10× cycles (depending on starting input) at 98°C for 20 seconds, at 67°C for 20 seconds, at 72°C for 40 seconds, and a final extension after amplification cycle at 72°C for 1 minute. A 1× bead-based clean-up is performed on the amplified sequencing library before loading on the sequencer.

Modeling

Dataset construction

The constructed display library contained 240,000 variants with 1–4 edits within 24 positions of the HCDRs paired with a single light chain sequence. To construct the localized polyreactivity dataset, sequences recovered from all three sequential YSD sorts (OVA, PSR, and insulin) were merged and de-duplicated. Variants present in the top binding gate across all three reagents were labeled as broadly high polyreactive (label = 1), and variants present in the bottom binding gate across all three reagents were labeled as broadly low polyreactive (label = 0). Sequences with ambiguous gate membership were filtered out.

To assess whether binding data from all three reagents is necessary for polyreactivity prediction, reagent-specific subsets were also constructed. In each subset, labels were assigned using appropriate reagent’s sort data (e.g., label 1 for top 10% OVA binders, label 0 for bottom 10% OVA binders).

Model evaluation and training

Four models were evaluated for polyreactivity prediction. The first, the Chen model, is a random forest classifier reimplemented according to Chen et al.,6 which takes as input a set of custom-calculated sequence features rather than raw sequences. The second, the Chen-Tuned model, uses the same random forest architecture and feature set, but was retrained on the Chen dataset combined with the localized polyreactivity dataset.

The third, the ESM model, is an ESM-2 transformer protein language model (esm2_t30_150M_UR50D, 150 M parameters) fine-tuned on Dataset S1 from Chen et al.6 which includes both heavy and light chain sequences. The dataset was split 80/20 into 197,034 training and 49,259 validation sequences. Training was performed using the Adam optimizer with focal loss as the objective function, a batch size of 128, and a learning rate of 3 × 10−4. The last transformer layer and the classification head were unfrozen during training, while the rest of the ESM backbone remained frozen. The Chen, Chen-Tuned, and ESM models were all evaluated using the localized polyreactivity dataset as an external test set.

The fourth model, the ESM-Tuned model, was derived from the ESM model through additional fine-tuning on the localized polyreactivity dataset. Several fine-tuning strategies were explored. In the first two, an active‑learning approach was taken, using only the subset of localized sequences misclassified by the ESM model. In the first strategy, these sequences were merged with the Chen dataset and a model was trained from scratch. In the second, they were used to fine-tune the ESM model directly. In the third strategy, the ESM model was fine-tuned on the full localized dataset. The third approach yielded the highest performance and was adopted as the final model. For this final model, a holdout test set of 3,998 sequences was first separated and reserved for final performance evaluation. The remaining 53,764 sequences were split 80/20 into 43,011 training and 10,753 validation sequences. Training hyperparameters were identical to those used for the ESM model.

All models were trained using binary labels (0 or 1) as supervision. At inference, both the discrete classification outputs and the predicted probability of a sequence being polyreactive were used for evaluation.
