ABSTRACT

 The United States power grid will continue to experience stress from aging

 infrastructure, extreme weather, and increasing loads. In order to strengthen

 resiliency, researchers need to run tests and simulations that model real-life

 infrastructure. Idaho National Laboratory (INL) is creating a digital twin of its

 Critical Infrastructure Test Range Complex (CITRC). CITRC is an at-scale grid

 testbed which can be configured in utility-realistic distribution scenarios for a

 variety of tests, such as advanced grid protection and control. A digital twin of this

 infrastructure would support in-depth tests/simulations without risking physical

 consequences, before trialing devices under test at-scale. This project focuses on

 the data acquisition part of constructing a digital twin. It investigates the

 Supervisory Data Acquisition and Control (SCADA) system of CITRC and

 explores ways to utilize this system for the twin. Future “ideal” at-scale

 implementations such as upgraded equipment, modern communication protocols,

 and automation applications are also explored.

I. INTRODUCTION

 Growing demand on the United States power grid is proving the need for increasingly
reliable, abundant, and secure critical infrastructure. By 2030, the U.S load is predicted to
increase by 25%, following into an even higher 78% by 2050.1 To combat this, industries are
focused on creating more flexible, advanced, and diverse forms of power generation. Nuclear,
geothermal, natural gas, hydropower, energy storage, solar, and wind are all generation strategies
being implemented for future integration.1 As the deployment of these systems increases, it is
vital to understand potential disturbances that come with frequent grid usage. Extreme weather
and aging infrastructure can cause faults leading to outages and decreased generation. Idaho
National Laboratory (INL) utilizes its Critical Infrastructure Test Range Complex (CITRC), for
research in these areas.

 CITRC is a test distribution system that uses at-scale utility assets to evaluate different
generations and loads.2 Researchers use field data gathered at the site to run simulations, testing
different aspects of this system to strengthen system resilience. Resiliency has become the core
research of CITRC as it not only evaluates the cause of a disturbance but can be used to
iteratively test disturbance prevention technologies at-scale. This research can require high risk,
complex tests that may be accident and hazard prone. To combat this, INL is creating a digital
twin of CITRC. Digital twins “. . . replicate everything in the physical world in the digital space
and provide engineers with feedback from the virtual world.” 3 They are important because they
enable researchers to test the system before at-scale deployment without physical safety and
equipment risk. Industries like power utilities would be able to use a digital twin to sandbox
optimal grid monitoring, planning, and risk management.3 In order to complete accurate tests on
a digital twin, researchers need to be confident that the system they are using is based on
reputable amounts of data. CITRC data comes from a variety of devices that can be routed to a
digital twin data warehouse.

 This paper focuses on the Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) system
for CITRC, and how it can be adopted into the digital twin. A SCADA system uses Intelligent
Electronic Devices (IEDs) coupled with software to monitor, control, and poll for data across the
entire site. These devices generate event reports, measure and calculate desired quantities
(voltage, current, power), and serve as the interface to control breakers and switches, thus
monitoring the system, maintaining efficiency and safety.4 This project consisted of gathering
and documenting the SCADA data and device layout, as well as changing some of the device
settings to better support the needs of the twin. Having a well-established SCADA system
provides groundwork understanding and documentation for project use as well as foundations for
future Artificial Intelligence (AI) and automation applications.

A. Data acquisition

1. RTAC configuration

 Through the diagrams/one-lines it was found that CITRC uses an ecosystem of
Schweitzer Engineering Laboratories (SEL) devices: the SEL-735 Power Quality and Revenue
Meter, SEL- 651R-2 Advanced Recloser Control, SEL-2240 Axion, and SEL-3555-2 Real Time
Automation Controller (RTAC). Detailed below in Figure 4 is a simplified visual of the
foundational data flow between devices, based on the device layout in Figure 3.

 6

 FIG. 4. Foundational data flow between CITRC devices.

 Beginning this flow, instrumentation power and current transformers (PTs and CTs) take
in voltage and current and send it to the meters and reclosers. Additionally, the reclosers also
receive switch status information. All SEL devices are configured by the user to determine what
SCADA points they will then transmit. In other words, field analog and binary information is
ingested, the device reads that data and executes local protection algorithms plus populates its
data points. Along with that, it uses analog and binary information to generate its own reports.
The RTAC (Real Time Automation Controller) is the hub for all transmitted SCADA points.
Ideally the RTAC would then load all points into a project database, but it is also easy to access
through its human machine interface (HMI). Presently, INL has a lab in town that has a direct
connection to all of the devices on-site, including the RTAC. The SEL-2240 Axion can be used
in the system but all on-site are currently not configured or used. They have the possibility to
control more switches, connect to the meters to provide more control and facilitate RTAC data
flow if needed.

 To understand the specific points being sent across devices, a configuration was pulled
from the RTAC. This was run using the acSELerator RTAC SEL-5033 Software. This software
is simple to navigate and is used to configure different SEL RTACs.6 It can configure different
communications protocols between the devices, process data tags, and create the logic code used
to control devices.6 The data tag section of the configuration was the most significant to this
stage in the process. Shown below in Figure 5 is a sample of some of the points collected from

 7

the RTAC. These points were taken from one of the SEL 735 meters (pad A) using the
acSELerator software to review the current RTAC configuration.

 FIG. 5. Sample list of points for a SEL 735 (pad A) at CITRC. 7

 Total points from all devices (binary inputs, binary outputs, and analog inputs) were
copied from the software project and added to an Excel Sheet. This sheet functioned like a
“points” list, detailing what each tag was, and the function it served. The Axions were still
included in this points list for potential future use. After the Excel sheet was made, a visual data
flow showing visual inputs and outputs was created.

2. On-site collection

 Through the process of putting the points list and the visual flow together, some
questions started to arise. Certain devices were hard to locate on the one-lines, making it a
challenge to verify where they were getting their data from. It also was not clear if some devices
were active or dormant. Working out at the physical site and conversing with INL power
management got these questions answered. Seeing the physical equipment and layout showed a
clear picture of how everything was connected. Notes were added to the data flow Excel Sheet to
reflect these findings.

3. Communication Protocols and Standards

 Another important aspect of a SCADA system is the communication protocols and
standards that are being utilized for data retrieval, transmission, reporting, and management. A
plain language reference guide was created to explain and visualize each protocol being used at
the site and how it could apply to the digital twin project. One of the main goals of this guide
was to explain how Distributed Network Protocol 3 (DNP3) is used to transmit all SCADA data
points across devices. This is how data flows throughout the entire site, through the RTAC, and
then to the main server in town. SCADA points through DNP3 can be accessed through serial
connections at the device or by using Transmission Control or Internet Protocol (TCP/IP) over
Ethernet. Along with all the streaming data points, this server can also ping for multiple types of
files and reports using file transfer protocol (FTP) over TCP/IP. See Figure 6 below for a section
of the guide used to visualize this:

 FIG. 6. Different file types decision diagram.

 9

One other critical aspect of this protocol and standards research was how it could apply to the
future applications of CITRC and the digital twin. Detailed research was done to see how newer,
more advanced protocols could be utilized to help with data management as well as
AI/automation plans with the project.

B. Meter settings

 Once the current data structure had been explored, it was time to change the meter
settings for the SEL 735s. The current settings on the meters were from previous projects and
were set more to what a common utility would use. A digital twin will need the meters to pick up
sensitive changes in voltage and current as well as generate a higher volume of reports. This
creates a larger volume of data to observe and use in the digital twin process. The other devices
were not as known with how they were routed or configured so they were not the main focus.

 By going out to each test pad on site, settings for each meter were pulled for
modification. Once the settings were pulled, they were opened up in acSELerator Quickset
Software. The old settings were kept in every section except for the events and reporting section.
The main things changed were harmonic and voltage, sag, swell, interrupt (VSSI) information.
Harmonic activity up to the fifteenth harmonic was turned on and the meters were set to detect
when harmonic activity reached 3% of the base frequency. VSSI reports were modified to be
more sensitive, see Figure 7 below for a table outlining those changes directly from the settings
comparison:

 10

 FIG. 7. SEL 735 VSSI settings.8

The above figure shows that VSSI were changed to become detectable at smaller deviations from
the normal voltage. Their hysteresis percents were lowered to shorten event capture to help the
meter record more events. The base voltage averaging time was also lengthened to prevent the
base voltage from updating its value as easily.

 The other important area of the settings change was the load profiles. The previous meter
settings had most profile generation happening every 15 minutes and recorded over every few
weeks. As the digital twin will need to access large amounts of data frequently, these were
changed to data being taken in seconds, recorded over every few days. This might need to be
adjusted in the future to balance the needs of the project with those of storage/memory.
However, at the time these settings were changed it was deemed the space was adequate. See
Figure 8 below for one of these settings examples:

 11

 FIG. 8. Load profile SEL 735s illustrating data capture timing. 8

 Lastly, the meters have DNP3 maps to illustrate what points they are utilizing for their
input/output (I/O) systems. These were compared with the Excel Sheet made previously, and a
couple of points for the meters were removed to match the current set-up. Once these settings
were finished, they were pushed back to the physical meters on site. See Appendix for a
complete list of the settings changes.

III. OUTCOMES

A. Future improvements

 Establishing the SCADA system went successfully but led to some discoveries for future
improvement:

 1. Not all equipment on the one-lines is labeled with a unique identifier. Some devices
like transformers and relays are drawn but have no numerical tag to associate with them. This
makes them hard to identify and a challenge to integrate into a data warehouse. They should all
be given names so they can become objects for DeepLynx9 and stay consistent with current
documentation.

 2. The four-way switches are drawn with three axions inside when they all only contain
one. Along with that, the axions drawn next to the meters are not in use. Indicating the precise
number of axions as well as what devices are in use is important when trying to document data
flow and the amount of data one can poll for. See Figure 9 below for where these axions are in
the device diagram.

 3. The switches inside each big transformer box are documented as S & C Electric
Company switches, but after visiting the site, they were discovered to be G & W Electric
switches. Different manufacturers make things differently; it is crucial that all equipment is
recorded accurately. Future tests that utilize these switches will depend on what kind they are.
See Figure 9 below for the location of those switches in the device diagram.

 12

 FIG. 9. Indication of axion and switch area for improvement, representing the entire column.

 4. When cross referencing the RTAC configuration with the old SEL 735 DNP3 maps,
there were quite a few inconsistencies when they should have matched. This likely meant that
things were copied over between devices, even if the data points were not relevant. While this
problem was quickly remedied, having an awareness that this is possible when the other devices
are documented is important. Those are the points carried across the system, having confusion
about what those points are puts control and monitoring at risk, as well as makes the SCADA
muddled.

 5. Illustrating this system was also very useful for the team in understanding the
differences between event reports and streaming data. Event reports generate when a disturbance
happens, like a fault. They provide a brief snapshot of what was going on in the system at that
time and sample at 164/sec. Streaming data is not as indicative of disturbances, but provides a
real-time, wide scope of system data, typically sampling at 4/sec or 1/sec. When these are
coupled together disturbances can be seen, and then better understood system wide. Updating
meter settings in the future to better balance these needs may need to be done if memory/storage
or over-writing time is a concern. It would also be a benefit to gather reports and points from the
reclosers and axions by bringing them online and routed through the RTAC as well.

B. Future applications

 SCADA systems are the foundation of control, analysis, and diagnostics of the power
system industry. Aside from providing accurate data to a digital twin, having a well-established
SCADA system can provide a window of future opportunities. One of those being an Advanced
Distribution Management System (ADMS). “[An] ADMS is a software that provides utilities
with real-time, integrated view of the distribution grid.” 10 While SCADA systems provide
control and supervision of devices, an ADMS would take that system and integrate it with other
applications to provide a wider scope of autonomous decision making and risk management.10
By having a variety of data to poll from, autonomous training and machine learning are within
reach. The complexity of power grids is already increasing from higher power needs and the
commonality of distributed energy resources (DERs). An ADMS could be implemented at
CITRC and used for future tests. It would be able to handle increasingly complex and higher-risk
projects. Coupling an ADMS with the digital twin would also lead to higher level simulation,
and accurate predictions.

 To create an ADMS, more modern communication protocols and standards should be
implemented at the site. One such standard is IEC 61850. Created by the International
Electrotechnical Commission, this standard “. . . govern[s] the interoperability in the power
utility automation domain.” 11 This standard has its own data semantics, System Description
Language, communication protocols, and security standard.11 It can be layered on to DNP3,
providing cohesive and simple communication between devices, and cyber security. By making
the CITRC site compatible with IEC 61850 an ADMS would already have a foundation, and
critical infrastructure tests could be run with simpler communication and integrated cyber
security.

IV. CONCLUSIONS

 The DOE has set ambitious goals, enhancing the testing, security, and abundance of
critical infrastructure. Creating a digital twin of CITRC will assist in meeting these goals through
the ability to run tests/simulations for prevention and analysis. This project focused on
establishing and enhancing the SCADA system being used at CITRC. It documented all data
points travelling through DNP3 Protocol, the communication protocols and standards woven into
that, and updated device settings to better meet the needs of the digital twin. By having an
illustrated data system to use as a guide, current and future researchers for the digital twin project
will be able to have a sea of accurate data to investigate and apply to the project. They have
access to understanding the current configuration of devices and could change them for future
needs. This SCADA system also provides a foundation for future development, such as AI
modules and putting together an Advanced Distribution Management System.
