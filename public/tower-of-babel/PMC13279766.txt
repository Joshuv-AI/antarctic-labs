Multi-omics reveals goat milk improves glucose homeostasis in type 2 diabetes mellitus mice by gut–liver axis
Authors: Cheng Pan, Xiaoying Chen, Longhui Liu, Zhifei Zhang, Weiwei Yao, Lan Wu, Jun Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging evidence suggests that goat milk exerts beneficial effects on insulin levels, insulin sensitivity, and glucose homeostasis in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). However, the underlying mechanisms need further investigation. Here, the metabolic profiles of goat milk, goat milk powder, and cow milk were compared, revealing distinct compositional signatures. In a high-fat diet/streptozotocin-induced T2DM mouse model, goat milk intervention significantly improved glucose homeostasis (fasting blood glucose decreased from 13.87 mmol/L to 8.08 mmol/L, P < 0.05), alleviated hepatic injury, and remodeled serum bile acid metabolism. Further investigation revealed that goat milk intervention altered the gut microbiota composition, characterized by an increased relative abundance of Akkermansia and Lachnoclostridium. Additionally, transcriptomic analysis suggested that goat milk intervention up-regulated Bcl-2 and PI3K and down-regulated G6pc in the hepatic PI3K/AKT pathway. Collectively, these findings suggest that goat milk has the potential to alleviate T2DM and may serve as a dietary intervention for T2DM patients.Keywords: Goat milk, Type 2 diabetes, Gut microbiota, Liver, Multi-omics

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction(Hudish et al., 2019). It affects over 500 million individuals globally(Ahmad et al., 2022). Driven by rising obesity rates, sedentary lifestyles, and population aging, the prevalence of T2DM is escalating rapidly(Chatterjee et al., 2017), underscoring the urgent need for enhanced prevention and management strategies. However, given that T2DM is currently incurable, patients often rely on long-term pharmacological hypoglycemic therapy, which is frequently associated with a range of adverse drug reactions(Hussain & Chowdhury, 2019). Therefore, identifying safe and effective dietary interventions that lower blood glucose could reduce medication dependence, facilitate improved disease management, and enhanc quality of life for patients with T2DM.

The liver, as a central organ in glucose homeostasis(W. Liu et al., 2014; Titchenell et al., 2017), exhibits metabolic dysfunction that is a key risk factor for T2DM(Lim et al., 2021). Under diabetic conditions, hepatic aberrations typically include increased gluconeogenesis, suppressed glycogen synthesis, and elevated free fatty acids resulting from exacerbated lipolysis, thereby driving lipotoxicity and subsequent hepatocyte injury(Jin et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2023). Previous studies have shown that goat milk enhances AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activity in the liver of rats fed a high-fat diet, thereby improving abnormal glucose metabolism, suggesting that goat milk may exert a direct regulatory effect on hepatic metabolic disorders under diabetic conditions(W. Liu et al., 2019). However, whether goat milk can exert systemic liver protection by simultaneously improving the abnormal pathways of hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism remains to be elucidated, as does its underlying mechanism.

In recent years, the role of the gut-liver axis in regulating host glucose homeostasis has attracted considerable attention(El Hage et al., 2020; Zeng et al., 2024). For example, bacterially derived pyrrolidine propionic acids, such as 3-indolepropionic acid, can alleviate hepatic insulin resistance by suppressing the mTORC1 signaling pathway(Koh et al., 2018). Moreover, the gut microbiota also enhances the production of secondary bile acids through the activation of the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and the transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5), consequently improving hepatic inflammation, glucose homeostasis, and lipid metabolism(Duparc et al., 2017; Nie et al., 2024). These findings point to a mechanistic bridge for dietary intervention, whereby particular dietary components may directly affect the liver through the portal circulation by altering the gut microbiota's composition and metabolic output, hence mitigating metabolic problems associated with diabetes.

Goat milk, recognized as a functional food, has garnered increasing attention due to its potential to modulate gut microbiota and improve metabolic health(Chen et al., 2025; S. Liu et al., 2025). Goat milk is rich in bioactive proteins, peptides, and oligosaccharides that differ from those in cow milk(Park, 2010). As early as 1552–1578 CE, the beneficial effects of goat milk in alleviating diabetes-like symptoms were identified. The classic Chinese materia medica, Compendium of Materia Medica, documents that goat milk consumption relieves diabetes-related symptoms, including weight loss, polydipsia, polyphagia, and polyuria. Recent studies have suggested that the abundance of taurine, peptides, and medium-chain triglycerides in goat milk is closely associated with diabetes prevention, positioning it as a promising dietary intervention for diabetes management(Verma et al., 2026). Notably, in high-fat diet/streptozotocin (HFD/STZ)-induced diabetic rats, goat milk effectively restores glucose homeostasis by activating the hepatic AMPK and GSK3-GYS signaling axis(Han et al., 2022). However, little is known about the precise mechanisms by which goat milk improves T2DM by the gut-liver axis.

Therefore, we hypothesized that goat milk may regulate host glucose homeostasis by modulating the gut–liver axis. In this study, we established a T2DM mouse model and treated with goat milk. An integrated multi-omics approach was employed to systematically assess its effects on host physiological parameters, gut microbial community, serum metabolome, liver histology, and liver transcriptome. Our findings provide a reference for the potential of goat milk as a nutritional intervention for adjunctive management strategies in T2DM.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and experimental design

Ethics statement: All animal experiments conducted in this study was performed in strict accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and are formally approved by the Experimental Animal Management and Use Committee of Northwest A&F University, Yangling, Shaanxi, China (Approval No. 15–516). Furthermore, this study was conducted strictly in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

The experimental dairy cows and dairy goats used for sample collection were sourced from the Livestock Teaching Base of Northwest A&F University. Six healthy dairy cows and six healthy dairy goats of the same parity and in peak lactation period were selected. For three consecutive days, 40 mL milk samples were collected using a 50 mL collection tube before morning feeding and before afternoon feeding each day. Subsequently, milk samples from the same animal were thoroughly mixed and aliquoted, with a mixing ratio of morning and evening milk samples of 6:4. The mixed samples were stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer at −80 °C. Subsequently, samples of cow milk, goat milk, and goat milk powder (from Shaanxi Meiliyuan Dairy Company) were sent to Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for metabolomics testing and analysis.

Three-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice (Production License No. SCXK (Sichuan) 2020–030) were purchased from Chengdu Dosh Experimental Animals Co., Ltd. Establishment of the T2DM mouse model and the five experimental groups followed our previous study(X. Chen et al., 2024). Briefly, male C57BL/6 J mice were first fed a 60% high-fat diet (HFD) for 5 weeks, followed by intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 40 mg kg−1) for three consecutive days(Magalhães et al., 2019). Mice with fasting blood glucose levels ≥11.1 mmol/L(Srinivasan et al., 2005) were considered diabetic and randomly assigned to one of five groups: normal control (NC, n = 16), normal control plus goat milk (NC-GM, n = 16), T2DM model (T2DM, n = 18), T2DM plus goat milk intervention (T2DM-GM, n = 18), and T2DM plus metformin positive control (T2DM-MET, n = 18). The diet for the goat milk intervention group was supplemented with 10.3% goat milk powder, calculated based on the dry matter content of goat milk. The metformin group received metformin hydrochloride (300 mg kg−1 day−1)(Chen et al., 2022; X. Zhou et al., 2024) administered via drinking water. The detailed composition of the mouse diet was as previously described(X. Chen et al., 2024).

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test and histopathological analysis

An intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) was performed at weeks 8 and 10 of the experiment, following the detailed procedures described in previous study(X. Chen et al., 2024). At week 10, mice were fasted (with free access to water) for 12 h and then euthanized by cervical dislocation. Liver and colon tissues, as well as intestinal contents, were collected immediately.

The collected liver tissues were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution at 4 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned for further analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Oil Red O staining were performed on the mouse liver tissue sections. Histological score was conducted as described by Puchakayala et al.(Puchakayala et al., 2015). Lipid droplet area was quantified using ImageJ.

Metabolomic analysis of goat milk, cow milk, and goat milk powder

The extraction procedure was conducted as follows. Samples were thawed on ice and vortex-mixed. Then, 50 μL of each sample was transferred to a labeled centrifuge tube, and internal standard working solution (2-chlorophenylalanine, 1 ppm) was added to each tube. Then, 300 μL of a methanol solution containing 20% acetonitrile was added, followed by vortexing for 3 min and centrifugation at 4 °C and 12,000 r/min for 10 min. Next, 200 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a new labeled centrifuge tube and kept at −20 °C for 30 min. After a second centrifugation step at 4 °C and 12,000 r/min for 3 min, 180 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a vial for subsequent instrumental analysis. Following extraction, untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed using an Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) system coupled with Tandem Mass Spectrometry (MS/MS) for separation and data acquisition. This analysis was conducted by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B), delivered at 0.4 mL/min with a 2 μL injection volume. The gradient elution was: 0 min, 5% B; 11.0 min, 90% B; 12.0 min, 90% B; 12.1 min, 5% B; and 14.0 min, 5% B. Mass spectrometry was conducted using an ESI source in both positive and negative modes with the following settings: temperature, 500 °C; ion spray voltage, ±5500/4500 V; gas I/II, 55/60 psi; curtain gas, 25 psi; and high CAD. Ion pairs were monitored using optimized declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE). A quality control (QC) sample was analyzed after every 5–15 samples to monitor instrument stability, and only variables with a relative standard deviation (RSD) ≤ 30% were retained. Raw data were processed using Progenesis QI software. Multivariate data analysis and modeling were performed using the Ropls software package. All data were centered and scaled before analysis. Three complementary models were constructed: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for unsupervised clustering and outlier detection; Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) for supervised classification and variable selection; and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) to separate predictive variation from orthogonal noise, thereby enhancing model interpretability. Identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/). The annotated metabolites were then mapped to the KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) for pathway analysis. Statistically significant differential metabolites were defined as those satisfying both of the following criteria: log2 Fold Change (log2 FC) > 1 and p-value <0.05.

Serum metabolomics analysis

Serum samples were transported on dry ice to Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for metabolomic profiling. Metabolite detection was performed using an UPLC-MS/MS platform, following the experimental procedures detailed in the reference(Ying et al., 2019). Metabolite screening was based on a combination of univariate statistical analysis (p-value) and the variable importance in projection (VIP) score from orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) models. The inclusion criteria were a fold change > 2 or < 0.5, accompanied by P < 0.05 and VIP > 1.

Hepatic transcriptomic analysis and qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues using the Trizol method. RNA quality was assessed as follows: (a) integrity and potential DNA contamination were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis; (b) concentration was measured with high precision using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer; and (c) RNA integrity was verified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. All samples passed the quality controls before proceeding with library preparation.

Libraries were constructed utilizing the structural feature of polyA tails present on most eukaryotic mRNAs. Briefly, mRNA containing polyA tails was enriched using Oligo(dT) beads. The enriched mRNA was fragmented using a fragmentation buffer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using these short RNA fragments as templates, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis using buffer, dNTPs (dUTP, dATP, dGTP, and dCTP), and DNA polymerase I. The double-stranded cDNA was then purified. The purified cDNA underwent end repair, polyA-tailing, and sequencing adapter ligation. Fragments of the desired size were selected using AMPure XP beads, and the cDNA library was finally amplified by PCR. The quality of the constructed libraries was inspected, and only those meeting the required standards were subjected to sequencing.

Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.20.0) to compare gene expression profiles between groups, with three biological replicates per group. Genes meeting the following criteria were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs): |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05. Subsequently, the clusterProfiler R package (version 3.8.1) was employed to perform Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis on the identified DEGs. Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) and the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Sequencing and analysis of the cecum microbiota

Genomic DNA was extracted from samples using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. The purity and concentration of the DNA were assessed by spectrophotometry. The DNA was then diluted with sterile water to a concentration of 1 ng/μL. Using the diluted genomic DNA as a template, PCR amplification was performed with barcode-specific primers and a high-fidelity DNA polymerase, targeting the selected sequencing region. 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing of mouse intestinal contents was conducted on the NovaSeq6000 platform by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Raw sequencing data were assembled and filtered to obtain high-quality clean data. Denoising analysis was performed based on the valid data to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Mothur (version 1.48) was used to annotate the ASV sequences at multiple taxonomic levels (phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species) for taxonomic profiling and community composition analysis. To identify shared and unique ASVs among different samples and to assess species richness and evenness, various alpha diversity indices were calculated. To examine differences in microbial community structure between groups, beta diversity analyses including principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), principal component analysis (PCA), and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were performed. Additionally, LEfSe statistical analysis was used to test for significant differences in species composition and community structure between groups.

Correlation analysis

The Spearman correlation coefficient was calculated using the SciPy package in Python, and the results were visualized with Matplotlib to explore the associations among gut microbiota, serum bile acids, and hepatic gene expression.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Unless otherwise stated, comparisons among groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's post hoc test. Feed intake and body weight data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Differences in bile acid profiles between the T2DM and T2DM-GM groups were analyzed using Student's t-test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Metabolomic profiling and functional analysis of goat milk

To elucidate the intrinsic metabolic advantages of goat milk and assess the impact of processing, untargeted metabolomics was performed on fresh goat milk (GMM), goat milk powder (GMP), and fresh cow milk (CMM) (Fig. 1A). PCA revealed distinct clustering among the three groups, indicating substantial differences in their metabolic profiles (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, OPLS-DA further revealed differences in the metabolic profiles among different groups (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Permutation test results showed high R2 and Q2 values (0.998 and 0.992, respectively), indicating that the model had a good fit and accurate predictive ability (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

The GMM group represents the goat milk group (n = 6); the GMP group represents the goat milk powder group (n = 6); the CMM group represents the cow milk group (n = 6). B: PCA analysis. Differential metabolite analysis (C: GMM VS CMM, D: GMM VS GMP). E: Clustering analysis of differential metabolites. KEGG enrichment analysis (F: GMM VS CMM, G: GMM VS GMP).

In comparison to CMM, GMM exhibited 285 significantly differential metabolites, comprising 125 up-regulated and 160 down-regulated metabolites (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 1C). The top up-regulated metabolites in GMM included Leu-Trp, Pro-Ala, 2-Acetylfuran, Dimethylmalonic acid, and glutaconic acid (Supplementary Table 2). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these differential metabolites showed that GMM was significantly enriched in pathways associated with the biosynthesis of cofactors, metabolic pathways, purine metabolism, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (P < 0.05, Fig. 1G). This distinct metabolic profile, particularly the enrichment of amino acids and precursors for cofactor synthesis, underscores the unique biochemical composition and potential bioactivity of goat milk at its source.

We next evaluated how the spray-drying process to produce GMP affected this bioactive signature. Compared with the GMP group, 306 differential metabolites were identified in the GMM group, of which 258 were significantly up-regulated and 58 were significantly down-regulated (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 1D). The top up-regulated metabolites in GMP included Pyrrolidine, Quinoline-4-carboxylic acid, Pro-Asp, 3-Dehydroshikimate and Xanthurenic Acid (Supplementary Table 3). Compared to GMM, GMP exhibited a significant down-regulation in pathways such as the AMPK and FoxO signaling pathways (Fig. 1F), potentially indicating the loss of certain heat-labile or volatile components. However, pathways associated with lipid and energy metabolism(Salas-Salvado et al., 2006; H. Sun et al., 2018), including linoleic acid metabolism and carbon metabolism, were significantly up-regulated or preserved in GMP (P < 0.05, Fig. 1G). Additionally, clustering analysis revealed that the overall metabolic profile of GMP remained distinct from that of CMM, clustering more closely with GMM (Fig. 1E).

Construction of the T2DM model and measurement of biochemical parameters

In the preliminary phase of this study(X. Chen et al., 2024), we established an HFD/STZ- induced type 2 diabetic mouse model (Fig. 2A). Mice in the T2DM group exhibited a significant increase in food intake (P < 0.05, Fig. 2B) but a significantly lower body weight compared to the NC group (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C), indicating severe glucose metabolism disorder and impaired energy utilization. At week 10, there was no significant difference in body weight between the T2DM and T2DM-GM groups; however, the liver-to-body weight ratio was significantly increased in the T2DM-GM group (P < 0.05, Fig. 2D). Fasting blood glucose levels in the T2DM group were significantly higher than those in the NC group (13.87 vs. 6.28 mmol/L, P < 0.01, Fig. 2E). In contrast, the T2DM-GM group exhibited a significant reduction in fasting blood glucose levels(to 8.08 mmol/L, P < 0.05, Fig. 2E). Moreover, the results of theOGTT results at week 10 showed improvements in the T2DM-GM group compared to the T2DM group (Fig. 2F). We next examined whether the goat milk diet could ameliorate pancreatic function and structure in T2DM mice. The results showed that the goat milk diet significantly alleviated islet damage in T2DM mice, as evidenced by intact islet morphology and evenly distributed cells(X. Chen et al., 2024). Collectively, these results demonstrate that dietary supplementation with goat milk effectively improves metabolic health in type 2 diabetic mice, indicating a protective role.

Protective effect of goat milk on liver histology in T2DM mice

To evaluate the protective effect of goat milk against liver injury in T2DM, we conducted a histopathological analysis of liver samples. In T2DM mice, a hyperglycemic environment can induce abnormal lipid accumulation in the liver, promote oxidative stress in hepatocytes, and trigger inflammatory infiltration. In this study, mouse livers (n = 3 per time point) were collected at the 4th, 8th, and 10th weeks of the experiment. Paraffin sections were prepared and subjected to H&E staining and Oil Red O staining.

The results of H&E staining showed that the NC and NC-GM groups maintained intact hepatic lobular architecture, characterized by uniform hepatocyte size, orderly arrangement, clear cell borders, and no obvious vacuolation or inflammation(Fig. 3A). In contrast, the T2DM group exhibited progressive liver injury typical of diabetes: at week 4, blurred cell boundaries and vacuolation began to appear; by week 8, hepatocytes were disorganized, nuclei were pyknotic, and inflammatory cell infiltration was observed; at week 10, hepatocytes were markedly swollen, heavily vacuolated, and the hepatic architecture was highly disrupted (Fig. 3A). At week 10, by comparison, the T2DM-GM group showed only mild disorganization of hepatocytes, with overall architecture notably better than that of the T2DM group, and significantly reduced vacuolization and inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 2 A). The T2DM-MET group also exhibited a similar protective effect, with relatively preserved lobular structure and markedly decreased cell swelling and vacuolation (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 2 A).

Effects of goat milk intervention on liver tissue in T2DM mice. A: H&E staining. B: Oil Red O staining.

Oil Red O staining revealed minimal and uniformly distributed lipid droplets in the livers of both the NC and NC-GM groups (Fig. 3B). In the T2DM group, focal lipid deposition became apparent by week 4; by week 8, the number of lipid droplets had increased, occupying the cytoplasm. By week 10, a substantialaccumulation of lipids was observed, with several hepatocytes exhibiting characteristic “white rings” around the nuclei (Fig. 3B). The T2DM-GM group displayed reduced lipid deposition compared to the T2DM group at all time points: only faint red droplets were observed at week 4, and the areas of red lipid staining were significantly reduced at week 10, indicating that goat milk effectively alleviated hepatic steatosis (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 2B). Similarly, the T2DM-MET group exhibited a reduction in both the number and size of lipid droplets, characterized by light red staining and relatively preserved hepatocyte morphology (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Goat milk modulates hepatic gene expression profiles in T2DM mice to alleviate metabolic disorders

To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms by which goat milk improves health in T2DM mice, we analyzed gene expression profiles among the NC, NC-GM, T2DM, T2DM-GM, and T2DM-MET groups using transcriptome sequencing. PCA showed clear separation between the T2DM and T2DM-GM groups (Fig. 4A). OPLS-DA further revealed differences in transcriptomic profiles among different groups, and permutation tests showed high R2 (0.994) and Q2 (0.837) values, indicating good model fit and accurate predictive ability (Supplementary Fig. 3 A–B). We identified 46 DEGs between the NC and NC-GM groups; 1479 DEGs between the NC and T2DM groups; 2747 DEGs between the T2DM and T2DM-GM groups; and 95 DEGs between the T2DM and T2DM-MET groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that DEGs between the NC and NC-GM groups were primarily enriched in terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, steroid biosynthesis, and metabolic pathways. Furthermore, DEGs between the NC and T2DM groups were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways, the PPAR signaling pathway, the MAPK signaling pathway, and the p53 signaling pathway; and DEGs between the T2DM and T2DM-MET groups were enriched in protein digestion and absorption (P < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). Notably, DEGs between the T2DM and T2DM-GM groups exhibitedsignificant enrichment in focal adhesion, the relaxin signaling pathway, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, all of which were markedly up-regulated in the T2DM-GM group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 4D). Among these pathways, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway is a central regulator of cell survival, proliferation, metabolism, and insulin sensitivity. Our findings revealed that PI3K and its downstream targets (e.g., Bcl-2, Cyclin) were significantly up-regulated in the T2DM-GM group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4D), suggesting that goat milk may enhance PI3K-Akt signaling to inhibit apoptosis, promote cell cycle progression, and improve glucose-metabolism-related processes such as G6Pc-regulated gluconeogenesis. To validate these findings, we confirmed the expression of four randomly selected genes from the DEGs using RT-qPCR, which corroborated the sequencing results (Fig. 4F).

Goat milk restores serum bile acid metabolic disorders in T2DM

This study employed untargeted metabolomics to analyze blood metabolic profiles and evaluate whether the beneficial effects of goat milk arise from alterations in metabolites. PCA results revealed distinct separation among the NC, T2DM, and T2DM-GM groups (Fig. 5A). OPLS-DA further revealed differences in serum metabolic profiles among the groups, with permutation tests showing high R2 (0.979) and Q2 (0.94) values, indicating good model fit and accurate predictive ability (Supplementary Fig. 5A–B). A total of 176 differentially expressed metabolites were identified between the NC and T2DM groups, with 125 significantly up-regulated and 51 significantly down-regulated in the T2DM group. In the comparison between the T2DM and T2DM-GM groups, 184 differentially expressed metabolites were detected, with 122 significantly up-regulated and 62 significantly down-regulated in the T2DM-GM group (P < 0.05, Fig. 5B). Clustering analysis of differential metabolites revealed that the T2DM-GM group exhibited a distinct metabolic profile compared to the NC and T2DM groups (Fig. 5C). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the differentially expressed metabolites between the T2DM and T2DM-GM groups were primarily enriched in pathways including the Pentose phosphate pathway, Carbon metabolism, Cholesterol metabolism, Bile secretion, and Primary bile acid biosynthesis. Notably, both Bile secretion and Primary bile acid biosynthesis were significantly enriched in the T2DM-GM group (P < 0.05, Fig. 5D). Previous studies have shown that bile acids can effectively reduce blood glucose through various pathways, such as BA-FXR-SHP, BA-FXR-FGFR15/19, BA-TGR5-GLP-1, and BA-TGR5-cAMP(Hou et al., 2023). Accordingly, analysis of metabolites within the Bile secretion and Primary bile acid biosynthesis pathways revealed significant up-regulation of several bile acid metabolites in the T2DM-GM group, including Glycoursodeoxycholic Acid, 23-Nordeoxycholic acid, Deoxycholic acid, and Taurocholic acid (Fig. 6). These findings suggest that goat milk can improve the expression profile of serum bile acid metabolites, thereby contributing to blood glucose reduction.

Goat milk restores intestinal homeostasis in T2DM mice by reshaping the gut microbiota

Given the significance of gut microbiota as a key target in T2DM research, its composition and dynamics profoundly impact host health. Therefore, to systematically evaluate the regulatory effect of goat milk intake on gut microbiota in T2DM mice, we performed 16S rRNA sequencing on cecal contents from each group. Alpha diversity analysis revealed no significant differences in the Chao1 index and Shannon index among the groups (P > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 3 A). Regarding beta diversity, PCoA revealed that the NC and NC-GM groups were clearly separated from the T2DM, T2DM-GM, and T2DM-MET groups, indicating significant differences in community structure (P = 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 3B). At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were the dominant phyla in the NC, NC-GM, and T2DM-MET groups, whereas Firmicutes and Proteobacteria were predominantin the T2DM and T2DM-GM groups (Fig. 7A). At the genus level, Ligilactobacillus, Akkermansia, Lachnoclostridium, Streptococcus, and Bacteroides were the most abundant genera across all groups (Fig. 7B).

Effects of goat milk intervention on the gut microbiota in T2DM mice. A: Microbial composition at the phylum level. B: Microbial composition at the genus level. C: LDA score analysis (effect size analysis LDA > 4.0, P < 0.05). Microbial co-occurrence network analysis (D:NC, E:T2DM, F:T2DM-GM).

To identify differentially abundant microbial taxa at the phylum, class, order, family, and genus levels among the groups, we performedLinear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis. The results identified a total of 41 discriminative features (Fig. 7C). The NC group was enriched in 20 feature microbes, including g_Dubosiella, g__Lactobacillus, and g_Allobaculum. The T2DM group exhibited 5 feature microbes, including s_Lactobacillus_murinus, g_Ligilactobacillus, f_Rhizobiaceae, and s_Acutalibacter_muris. The T2DM-GM group showed 16 feature microbes, primarily including c_Clostridia, g_Lachnoclostridium, s_Akkermansia_muciniphila, and g_Akkermansia.

To investigate the effect of goat milk on gut microbial interactions in T2DM mice, a network analysis was constructed (Fig. 7D-F). The results showed that, in comparion to the NC group, the T2DM group exhibited a reduced network complexity, characterized by 277 nodes and 1577 edges. Furthermore, the T2DM-GM group showed a further decrease in network complexity following goat milk intervention, with 233 nodes and 1346 edges. These findings suggest that goat milk may alleviate T2DM-associated dysbiosis by remodeling the microbial interaction network and screening core functional microbiota.

Correlation analysis

To investigate the potential associations among gut microbiota, serum bile acids, and key liver genes, a correlation analysis was conducted between the top 10 most abundant gut microbial genera (at the genus level), bile acid profiles, and liver gene expression. As illustrated in Fig. 8A, Akkermansia was significantly positively correlated with glycohyocholic acid, glycochenodeoxycholic acid, glycoursodeoxycholic acid, and 23-nordeoxycholic acid (P < 0.05). Ligilactobacillus was significantly negatively correlated with glycine deoxycholic acid, glycocholic acid, and 3-epideoxycholic acid (P < 0.01). Furthermore, hyodeoxycholic acid and gamma-muricholic acid were significantly positively correlated with the expression of liver genes Gng5, Gng2, and Bcl-2 (P < 0.01, Fig. 8B). In contrast, glycine deoxycholic acid and glycochenodeoxycholic acid were significantly negatively correlated with the expression of MMP9, MMP2, and G6pc (P < 0.05, Fig. 8B).

Association analysis among gut microbiota, serum bile acids, and hepatic genes. A: Correlation analysis between gut microbiota and serum bile acids. B: Association analysis of serum bile acids with hepatic genes. Note: * indicates P < 0.05, ** indicates P < 0.01.

Discussion

The global prevalence of T2DM has exceeded 589 million adults(Duncan et al., 2026). This situation is particularly severe in China, where the total number of diabetic patients has reached 233 million, representing a significant increase of 163.36% between 2005 and 2023(Y.-C. Zhou et al., 2025). Although goat milk intervention has been shown to alleviate T2DM symptoms(X. Chen et al., 2024), its precise mechanisms remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether goat milk, as a dietary intervention, can improve T2DM by modulating the gut–liver axis. Using an HFD/STZ-induced T2DM mouse model, we found that goat milk intervention significantly reduced fasting blood glucose and improved glucose tolerance. Integrated multi-omics analysis further revealed that goat milk was highly correlated with the enrichment of beneficial gut bacteria such as Akkermansia, alterations in host bile acid metabolism, and activation of the hepatic PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. These changes may contribute to the suppression of gluconeogenesis, enhancement of cell survival, and alleviation of inflammatory responses.

Goat milk is rich in various bioactive components, including small-molecule bioactive proteins, casein, and neutral oligosaccharides, which exhibit potential for modulating immune function, enhancing systemic immunity, and exerting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects(Prosser, 2021). Studies have shown that in type 2 diabetic rat models, goat milk intervention can significantly improve fasting blood glucose, glucose tolerance, lipid metabolism, and systemic glucose homeostasis by activating hepatic signaling axes such as GSK3–GYS or the AMPK pathway(Han et al., 2022; W. Liu et al., 2019). In the present study, the metabolomic results showed that goat milk contains abundant metabolites, including L-aspartic acid, taurolithocholic acid, hyodeoxycholic acid, and allopurinol. Among these, L-Aspartic acid, a natural amino acid, has been shown to exert hepatoprotective effects by regulating metabolic activity in hepatocytes(Jain et al., 2022), and taurolithocholic acid modulates bile acid levels and alters glycosphingolipid metabolism to mitigate diabetes(B. Chen et al., 2023). Furthermore, we found that goat milk intervention increased the liver organ index in T2DM mice, while alleviating hepatic inflammation and reducing lipid deposition. Although an increased liver index is typically associated with pathological changes(Lv et al., 2022), in this study this change may be attributed to the relative decrease in body weight of the T2DM-GM group. Therefore, we hypothesize that these active substances enriched in goat milk may contribute to ameliorating hepatic metabolic disorders in T2DM mice. However, further in vitro functional validation is required to elucidate the specific mechanisms involved.

Recent studies have demonstrated that gut microbiota dysbiosis affects glucose homeostasis, contributing to T2DM development and complications(Greiner & Bäckhed, 2011; Holst et al., 2016; Howard et al., 2022). In T2DM mice fed goat milk, the relative abundance of Verrucomicrobiota, which helps maintain glucose homeostasis and exhibits anti-inflammatory properties(Diez-Sainz et al., 2022), increased, while the relative abundance of the potentially pathogenic Proteobacteria decreased(Vasques-Monteiro et al., 2021). Furthermore, at the genus level, the probiotic Akkermansia and Lachnoclostridium, both possessing mucus-modulating, gut-barrier-repairing, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic-improving potential(J. Chen et al., 2021; Macchione et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2023), were significantly increased in the T2DM-GM group. At the species level, we observed a significant reduction in the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium pseudolongum in T2DM mice, while goat milk intervention specifically enriched the probiotic Akkermansia muciniphila. Chronic low-grade inflammation, a key feature of T2DM, is characterized by elevatedpro-inflammatory cytokines(e.g.,IL-6 and TNF-α). These cytokines interfere with insulin signaling and exacerbate ROS-mediated liver damage (Chen et al., 2025; Gurung et al., 2020). Notably, beneficial bacteria like Akkermansia muciniphila, Lactobacillus plantarum, and Bifidobacterium animalis improve glucose homeostasis by inducing anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 secretion(Chang et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2014; Li et al., 2016; Plovier et al., 2017; Shen et al., 2018); Additionally, Lactobacillus species and Akkermansia have been demonstrated to effectively suppress TNF-α expression(Chen et al., 2014; Singh et al., 2017; K.-Y. Sun et al., 2017), thereby alleviating systemic inflammation. Collectively, these microbial shifts indicate that goat milk intervention promotes an anti-inflammatory gut phenotype and restores glucose-lipid homeostasis.

To further elucidate the systemic metabolic effects resulting from goat milk-induced gut microbiota remodeling, this study analyzed host serum metabolites. Bile acids are critical signaling molecules in metabolic regulation and have been proposed as key mediators in the modulation of type 2 diabetes(Cadena Sandoval & Haeusler, 2025). The results demonstrated that goat milk intervention significantly upregulated bile secretion and the primary bile acid biosynthesis pathways in T2DM mice. Notably, serum levels of bile acids such as glycoursodeoxycholic acid, deoxycholic acid, and taurocholic acid were significantly elevated in goat milk-treated T2DM mice. This finding contrasts with previous clinical observations of reduced serum glycoursodeoxycholic acid in patients with type 2 diabetes(B. Chen et al., 2023), suggesting that goat milk intervention may contribute to restoring this specific bile acid level, thereby regulating glucose and lipid metabolism and exerting beneficial effects on type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, there exists a close bidirectional regulatory relationship between bile acid metabolism and the gut microbiota. Gut microorganisms can modulate the synthesis and transformation of bile acids by influencing the expression of rate-limiting enzymes (e.g., CYP7A1, CYP27A1, CYP7B1) in bile acid biosynthesis pathways(Wahlström et al., 2016). Previous studies have demonstrated that Akkermansia muciniphila can alleviate high-fat diet-induced metabolic fatty liver disease by modulating gut microbiota structure and bile acid metabolism (Wu et al., 2023). In our findings, we observed a significant positive correlation between the abundance of Akkermansia and the levels of several bile acids, including glycohyocholic acid, glycochenodeoxycholic acid, glycoursodeoxycholic acid, and 23-nordeoxycholic acid. Based on these correlational results, we hypothesize that goat milk may improve metabolic disorders by modulating gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism, with Akkermansia potentially acting as an intermediary factor. However, the relationship between the Akkermansia and bile acid metabolism remains to be further validated(Hagi et al., 2020).

The liver is a crucial organ that regulates glucose uptake, gluconeogenesis, and glycogen synthesis(Radziuk & Pye, 2001). Recent studies have increasingly emphasized the significant role of the gut–liver axis in the onset and progression of T2DM(Tong et al., 2024; Y. Zhang et al., 2025). Therefore, we hypothesized that goat milk-induced gut microbiota remodeling, particularly the enrichment of Akkermansia muciniphila, may transmit signals to the liver via metabolites such as bile acids. To test this “gut–liver axis” hypothesis, we performed transcriptomic analysis of liver tissues. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant activation of the Focal adhesion, Relaxin signaling, and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways. Existing studies have shown that bile acids can activate the PI3K/AKT pathway(Ren et al., 2025), suggesting that alterations in bile acid levels induced by goat milk may mediate regulatory effects on hepatic function through this pathway. The PI3K/AKT pathway is a classic signaling cascade involved in regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, glucose transport, and other functions(Savova et al., 2023). In the focal adhesion pathway, the up-regulation of Prkcb activate the PI3K/AKT signaling, thereby inhibiting apoptosis(Patergnani et al., 2013). Moreover, the increased expression of Shc4 and Shc2 down-regulates the pro-apoptotic gene Bad and up-regulates the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2, promoting cell survival and proliferation(Niba et al., 2013; X. Zhang et al., 2021). In the Relaxin signaling pathway, goat milk intervention significantly up-regulates the expression of Gnb5, Gng2, and Gng7, promoting the assembly of heterotrimeric G-protein βγ subunits, which in turn activates the PI3K/AKT pathway. This pathway further induces the expression of matrix metalloproteinases MMP9 and MMP2, exerting anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects in the liver(Ghanim et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023; C. Zhou et al., 2023). Within the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, goat milk intervention activates the cascade by up-regulating the Toll-like receptors Tlr2/4, which suppresses Chuk expression, thereby up-regulating the transcription factor Myb and promoting cell proliferation(Tsui et al., 2022). Concurrently, PI3K/AKT activation down-regulates the cell-cycle inhibitor P27 and up-regulates cyclin Ccnd2, collectively inhibiting apoptosis and promoting regeneration of injured liver tissue(Yang et al., 2021). Furthermore, this pathway alleviates excessive hepatic gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance by suppressing the expression of the key gluconeogenic enzyme gene G6pc(Althurwi et al., 2022). However, the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway identified in this study was based solely on transcriptomic analysis, lacking direct validation at the protein level, which warrants further confirmation in future studies.

In summary, this study suggests that goat milk intervention may modulate the gut–liver axis in a HFD/STZ-induced mouse model of T2DM. This modulation is closely associated with the enrichment of the probiotic Akkermansia muciniphila, remodeling of host bile acid metabolism, and activation of the hepatic PI3K/Akt signaling pathway(Fig. 9). Consequently, goat milk intervention reduces fasting blood glucose and improves liver condition by suppressing gluconeogenesis, promoting hepatocyte survival, and alleviating inflammatory responses.

Schematic illustration of the potential mechanisms by which goat milk alleviates hyperglycemia in T2DM.

This study presents several experimental limitations: microbiota transplantation was not performed to confirm whether Akkermansia muciniphila is the functional driver of the therapeutic effects; the collected serum samples were limited and used solely for metabolomic extraction, which precluded quantitative analysis of bile acid metabolism; and validation of the hepatic PI3K/Akt signaling pathway at the protein level was not conducted. Despite these limitations, this study holds important translational implications, suggesting that goat milk can serve as a functional food adjuvant for managing T2DM, offering a natural and potentially safer alternative or complementary option. Future research should prioritize randomized controlled trials in humans to validate these effects, optimize dosing regimens, and investigate the long-term impacts on diabetic complications.
