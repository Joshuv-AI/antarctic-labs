Convergence analysis of a fully discrete observer for data assimilation of the barotropic Euler equations
Aidan Chaumet, Jan Giesselmann

Abstract
. We study the convergence of a discrete Luenberger observer for the barotropic Euler equations in one dimension, for measurements of the velocity only. We use a mixed finite element method in space and implicit Euler integration in time. We use a modified relative energy technique to show an error bound comparing the discrete observer to the original system’s solution. The bound is the sum of three parts: an exponentially decaying part, proportional to the difference in initial value, a part proportional to the grid sizes in space and time and a part that is proportional to the size of the measurement errors as well as the nudging parameter. The proportionality constants of the second and third parts are independent of time and grid sizes. To the best of our knowledge, this provides the first error estimate for a discrete observer for a quasilinear hyperbolic system, and implies uniform-in-time accuracy of the discrete observer for long-time simulations.

1.
Introduction

The barotropic Euler equations are a system of quasilinear hyperbolic balance laws that model the evolution of compressible, inviscid fluids, for example in aerodynamics and gas transport in pipes. Often, only part of the state can be measured. For example, in aerodynamics, flow velocities can be measured relatively easily using
particle image velocimetry
(PIV)
[
21
]
. However, measuring densities is not straightforward, especially without altering the flow dynamics.
Then,
data assimilation
combines measurement data with the underlying physical evolution equations, to reconstruct the full system state, including density, from the velocity data alone.

We study a
distributed Luenberger observer

[
20
]
approach for data assimilation, also commonly known as
nudging

[
17
]
. This approach introduces a forcing term, scaled by an arbitrary but fixed nudging parameter, that drives the system towards the measurements. Motivated by PIV, we assume that the complete velocity field is measured.

In this scenario, on the continuous level,
[
16
]
proves exponential
synchronization
for the barotropic Euler equations in one space dimension, that is, convergence of the solution to the observer system towards the original system state in time, provided that solutions are sufficiently smooth and satisfy suitable smallness assumptions for the state and its time derivatives. However, in practice, there are sources of errors, such as discretization errors from solving the observer system numerically and measurement errors, whose influence must be understood. We choose a discretization that allows us to prove that by combining simulations with data, the observer is uniformly accurate for long times.

We discretize the Euler system in space using a mixed finite element method (MFEM), which is based on rewriting the equations as a port-Hamiltonian system, and use implicit Euler integration in time
[
10
]
. A similar MFEM approach is also used for other hyperbolic systems that can be rewritten as a port-Hamiltonian system, such as the shallow water equations
[
5
]
.
Extending the error estimate from
[
10
]
without making use of the nudging term gives an error bound that blows up exponentially in time. However, using that the nudging term drives the discrete observer towards the original system’s state, we are able to prove that errors are at most amplified by a constant factor that is independent of time, instead.

Our main result is given by Corollary
30
, a uniform-in-time error bound that compares the original system state with the numerical solution of the observer system. The bound is the sum of three parts: the first part decays exponentially in time and is proportional to the size of the initial error of the observer. The second part is the discretization error, which is proportional to the grid sizes in space and time, and the third part is proportional to the size of the measurement errors as well as the nudging parameter. The proportionality constants for the second and third terms are independent of time. This is the best estimate one can expect, up to improvements of the constants, because the scheme is only consistent at first order in space and time, and in a single step of the scheme, measurement errors are already multiplied by the nudging parameter.

The proof of Corollary
30
follows the synchronization proof from
[
16
]
, which shows the decay of a modified relative energy. This approach is inspired by
[
9
]
and
[
11
]
, which use similar techniques to show exponential stability of a damped linear hyperbolic system on networks and exponential stability for a class of scalar nonlinear hyperbolic problems, respectively. Showing the decay of the relative energy in
[
16
]
uses the variational structure of the port-Hamiltonian formulation. The MFEM discretization retains this structure, so many steps of the proof can be easily adapted for the numerical scheme, up to additional consistency errors. This justifies using the MFEM scheme over conservative schemes for hyperbolic balance laws, such as finite volume schemes. While the relative energy technique is well-suited to handle the present nonlinearity, it is limited to sufficiently smooth solutions. To move beyond smooth solutions, one would have to apply some different stability framework suitable for comparing discontinuous solutions, such as
[
4
]
or
[
22
]
. However, currently it is unclear to us how to perform this step.

Overall, our results justify using an observer in practice. In comparison to other widely used data assimilation approaches, such as variational or statistical techniques
[
1
]
, observers have the lowest computational cost.
Solving the discrete observer system is typically as costly as solving the original system, because the data is introduced to the PDE on the continuous level and discretization is performed subsequently.

In contrast, variational approaches, such as the 3D-Var and 4D-Var algorithms, are based on minimizing some cost functional that measures the mismatch between model prediction and measured data
[
1
]
. The minimization typically requires computing gradients repeatedly by solving an adjoint system, which is expensive.
Statistical approaches use Bayes’s formula to estimate the system’s state and estimate the uncertainty of the prediction, which is costly. For example, the ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF), going back to
[
12
]
, samples an ensemble of system states to estimate the system’s statistical properties. In each step, the dynamics of the underlying equations must be solved for each state in the ensemble, making the EnKF many times more expensive than an observer.

The remainder of this work is structured as follows. Section
2
gives an overview of some related works. In Section
3
we introduce the barotropic Euler equations, summarize the convergence result of
[
16
]
for the continuous observer and discretize the observer system. Section
4
summarizes our proof strategy and estimates the modified relative energy. In Section
5
we complete the proof of the error bound of the discrete scheme. Section
6
gives some numerical examples illustrating the convergence result.

2.
Related Work

While observers are well established for data assimilation,
we are not aware of any other works proving uniform-in-time error bounds for discrete observers for quasilinear hyperbolic balance laws. Here, we give an overview of some related works, focusing on fluids and hyperbolic balance laws, that study the synchronization of observers or prove error bounds for discrete observers. In the following overview, we use “synchronization” to refer to the long-time convergence of both continuous and discrete observer systems towards the original system with respect to time, and “convergence” to refer to error bounds for discrete observers that decrease under mesh refinement.

For the linear wave equation, in
[
6
]
the synchronization of an observer using field measurements in some subset of the computational domain is shown on the continuous level. Then,
[
7
]
proves the convergence of a numerical discretization of the proposed observer. Additionally,
[
7
]
extends the observer to other linear wave-like equations. In
[
3
]
the synchronization of a Luenberger observer for a kinetic formulation of the Saint-Venant system, using measurements of the full system state, is shown. Some extensions, such as measurements only on some subset of the domain or noisy measurements are also discussed. For a linearized water waves model,
[
19
]
investigates the synchronization of a Luenberger observer using only measurements of the free surface height, in some subset of the spatial domain, and shows convergence of the low-frequency modes of the observer after discretization.

Further examples of observers for fluids include
[
18
]
where synchronization for the surface quasi-geostrophic equation using blurred-in-time measurements of the full system state is shown on the continuous level, and
[
14
]
which proves the synchronization of a nudging approach for 3D Rayleigh-Bénard flow in a weak solution framework.

On the discrete side, some examples include a computational study of 2D Rayleigh-Bénard flow with coarse-grained velocity data
[
13
]
, a proof of the convergence and long-time accuracy of a
C^{0}
interior penalty method for the Cahn-Hilliard equation using coarse measurements
[
8
]
and
[
15
]
proving uniform-in-time error bounds for a mixed finite-element semidiscretization in space of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equation with velocity measurements on a coarse spatial mesh.

A key part of the analysis in
[
15
]
is that the viscosity damps the fine scales of the observer system, such that one does not need measurement data on an arbitrarily fine spatial mesh to show exact synchronization of the discrete observer.
In contrast, one cannot expect the same behavior for the barotropic Euler equations. Because there is no viscosity, errors made on fine scales persist. This also poses an additional challenge for discretization, because the discretization error occurs at the fine scales. Indeed,
[
23
]
shows that for non-dissipative systems, such as the Korteweg-de Vries equation, with only sparse measurement data, one should not generally expect exact synchronization with the original system state, when the high frequency modes are not captured by the measurements. For the linearized water waves model,
[
19
]
observes that high-frequency modes reduce the speed at which the observer system achieves synchronization, and can even reduce the rate from exponential to polynomial. Throughout this work, we assume that we have measurements of the velocity at arbitrarily fine resolution, such that the data resolves even the high-frequency modes, avoiding these issues. In practice, this is unrealistic. While data may be available at high resolution (e.g. PIV), ultimately one only has discrete sensor locations that cannot capture all frequencies. A natural extension of the nudging approach to this situation is given by using interpolated data for nudging
[
2
]
. The interpolation error then gives an additional contribution to the bound from Corollary
30
, which does not decay over time, because the Euler system has no damping for the frequencies which are not resolved by the measurements, see Remark
14
for a more quantitative explanation.

In
[
11
]
, the semi-discretization in time of linear, exponentially stable systems is studied using energy techniques. As justified by
[
6
]
, one can view an observer for the linear wave equation as a special case of such an exponentially stable system. Similar to the results in
[
15
]
, the analysis in
[
11
]
shows that one has to introduce suitable numerical viscosity to dissipate the fine scales of such exponentially stable systems and show convergence of the discretization.

While we consider a quasilinear equation in this work, due to the smallness conditions we require, the analysis shares similarities with the linear case. From
[
16
]
, we know that the observer system for the barotropic Euler equations is exponentially stable. The ideas from
[
11
]
and
[
15
]
are reflected in our choice of discretization. In particular, using the implicit Euler method for time integration naturally introduces numerical dissipation into our scheme, which is essential to our convergence proof.

3.
Problem Outline and Summary of Previous Results

In this section we briefly summarize the assumptions and results from
[
16
]
, where the convergence of the continuous distributed observer system towards the original state is shown.

Let
(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{v})
be a solution of the barotropic Euler equations on a single pipe, with a quadratic friction term and unit cross-sectional area.
We write the Euler system as a port-Hamiltonian system:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widehat{\rho}+\partial_{x}(\widehat{\rho}\widehat{v})

\displaystyle=\hat{s}_{1}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widehat{v}+\partial_{x}{\symscr{h}}(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{v})+\gamma\absolutevalue{\hvel}\widehat{v}

\displaystyle=\hat{s}_{2},

for
x\in\Omega:=[0,\ell]
and
t\in[0,\infty)
, with source terms
\hat{s}_{1},\hat{s}_{2}\in W^{1,\infty}([0,\infty);W^{1,2}(\Omega))
. This formulation is equivalent to the conservative form for Lipschitz-solutions and enables the variational arguments from
[
16
]
and
[
10
]
.
While the source terms
\hat{s}_{1}
and
\hat{s}_{2}
are not physically relevant, the subsequent analysis is possible even with source terms, and the source terms make it possible to use manufactured solutions in numerical examples. The solution of system (
3.1
) – (
3.2
) will serve as a reference solution for the observer system, which is why we denote its components with hats. We define the enthalpy
{\symscr{h}}(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{v})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\widehat{v}^{2}+P^{\prime}(\widehat{\rho})
, where
P
is the pressure potential, connected to the pressure law by
p^{\prime}(\widehat{\rho})=\widehat{\rho}P^{\prime\prime}(\widehat{\rho})
. For brevity, we define
\widehat{\symscr{h}}={\symscr{h}}(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{v})
. For subsonic states, one may prescribe one boundary condition at each end of the pipe. We prescribe
\widehat{m}_{\partial}=\widehat{\rho}\widehat{v}
at
x=0
and
\widehat{\symscr{h}}_{\partial}=\widehat{\symscr{h}}
at
x=\ell
.
The variable that is prescribed at either end of the pipe can be changed, but we always need one boundary condition on
\widehat{m}
and
\widehat{\symscr{h}}
each, to eliminate boundary contributions in some estimates, see Remark
24
for more details.

For the rest of this work, we assume that we measure only
\widehat{v}
.
Additionally, real measured data has measurement errors, which we will denote by
\hat{\eta}
.
Given measurements of
\widehat{v}
on the whole pipe, one wishes to reconstruct the full system state
(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{v})
on the pipe. We use a Luenberger observer

(3.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}(\rho v)

\displaystyle=\hat{s}_{1}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v+\partial_{x}{\symscr{h}}(\rho,v)+\gamma\absolutevalue{\vel}v

\displaystyle=\hat{s}_{2}-\mu(v-\widehat{v})+\mu\eta,

with some nudging parameter
\mu>0
, subject to the same boundary conditions as the original system.

In
[
16
]
, it is shown that under the following assumptions, the observer system (without measurement error or source terms) converges towards the original system state exponentially in time:

(A1)

There exists a solution of the original system (
3.1
) – (
3.2
) with the following regularity:

(3.5)

\widehat{\rho},\widehat{v}\in W^{2,\infty}\left([0,\infty);W^{1,1}(\Omega)\right)\cap W^{1,\infty}\left([0,\infty);W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)\right).

Further, there exist constants
\underline{\rho},\overbar{\rho},\tilde{v}>0
, such that the following uniform bounds are satisfied pointwise:

(3.6)

0<\underline{\rho}\leq\widehat{\rho}(x,t)\leq\overbar{\rho}\quad\text{and}\quad\absolutevalue{\hvel(x,t)}\leq\tilde{v},

for all
x\in\Omega
and
t\in[0,\infty)
.

(A2)

The pressure potential
P:\symbb{R}^{+}\to\symbb{R}
is smooth and strongly convex, i.e. there exists a constant
\underline{C}_{P^{\prime\prime}}>0
with

(3.7)

P^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\geq\underline{C}_{P^{\prime\prime}},

for all densities
\underline{\rho}\leq\rho\leq\overbar{\rho}
.

(A3)

The solution is subsonic. More precisely, we have

(3.8)

\rho P^{\prime\prime}(\rho)\geq 4\absolutevalue{\tilde{\vel}}^{2},\quad\text{for all}\quad\underline{\rho}\leq\rho\leq\overbar{\rho}.

(A4)

The solution
(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{v})
of the original system has sufficiently small time derivatives and both solutions have small velocities, i.e. there exist sufficiently small constants
C_{t},\overbar{v}>0
, such that

(3.9)

\displaystyle||\partial_{t}\widehat{\rho}||_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+||\partial_{t}\widehat{v}||_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{t},

(3.10)

\displaystyle||v||_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{\infty}(\Omega))},||\widehat{v}||_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\overbar{v}.

These constants will be further specified in later computations.

For the remainder of this paper, we abbreviate
||\cdot||_{2}\coloneqq||\cdot||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
The central result of
[
16
]
is given by

Theorem 1
.

(Convergence, c.f.
[
16
, Lemma 4.6]
)

Under the assumptions
(A1)
–
(A4)
, for any
\mu>0
, the constants
C_{t}
and
\overbar v
can be chosen small enough, such that there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, such that for any
t\in[0,\infty)
, it holds that

||{u}(t,\cdot)-\widehat{u}(t,\cdot)||_{2}^{2}\leq C_{1}||{u}(0,\cdot)-\widehat{u}(0,\cdot)||_{2}^{2}\exp(-C_2 t),

with
{u}=(\rho,v)
and
\widehat{u}=(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{v})
.

For the later treatment of the source terms and measurement errors, we add the following additional assumption:

(A5)

The source terms have the regularity
\hat{s}_{1},\hat{s}_{2}\in W^{1,\infty}([0,\infty);W^{1,2}(\Omega))
. Further, the measurement error has the regularity
\hat{\eta}\in C\left([0,\infty)\times\Omega\right)\cap L^{\infty}\left([0,\infty)\times\Omega\right)
, i.e. that the measurement error be continuous in space and time and pointwise bounded.

The continuity requirement on the measurement error is a technical requirement to simplify the presentation of the estimates, that is, to show that measurement errors will give a contribution proportional to
||\hat{\eta}||_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}
. However, the numerical scheme and its analysis only depend on the linear interpolant of the measurement errors. Thus, more realistic settings with discrete sensors, each with their own error described by an independent random variable, are a straightforward generalization. The assumption that the measurement error is bounded in space and time is also realistic, because many measurement devices are rated to have both some bounded relative error and bounded maximum error within their operational regime.

We discretize system (
3.3
) – (
3.4
) using the scheme from
[
10
]
. Let
M\in\symbb{N}
and set
\mathrm{h}=\ell/M
. Let
\symcal{T}_{\mathrm{h}}=\{x_{i}=i\mathrm{h},0\leq i\leq M\}
be a uniform grid in space. Let

\symcal{P}_{k}(\symcal{T}_{\mathrm{h}})\coloneqq\left\{p:\Omega\mapsto\symbb{R}\>\bigl\lvert\>\forall i=0,\dotsc,M-1:p|_{[x_{i},x_{i+1}]}\text{ is a polynomial of degree k}\right\}.

We then define the discrete spaces

Q_{\mathrm{h}}\coloneqq\symcal{P}_{0}(\symcal{T}_{\mathrm{h}}),\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\mathrm{h}}\coloneqq\{r\in\symcal{P}_{1}(\symcal{T}_{\mathrm{h}}):r(0)=0\}\cap H^{1}(\Omega),

that is, piecewise constant and piecewise linear continuous functions on the grid
\symcal{T}_{\mathrm{h}}
. For later use, we define projection operators. Let
\Pi_{\mathrm{h}}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to Q_{\mathrm{h}}
be the
L^{2}
–projection onto the piecewise constant functions and let
I_{\mathrm{h}}:H^{1}(\Omega)\to\symcal{P}_{1}(\symcal{T}_{\mathrm{h}})\cap H^{1}(\Omega)
be the piecewise linear interpolation operator.

Choose
\tau>0
as the time step size. We define the time steps
t^{n}=n\tau
for all
n\in\symbb{N}
. Let
\overline{\partial_{\tau}}{u}^{n}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{1}}{{\tau}}({u}^{n}-{u}^{n-1})
. Lastly, we define the linear interpolants of the velocity and the measurement error function respectively as

(3.11)

\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}\coloneqq I_{\mathrm{h}}(\widehat{v}(t^{n},\cdot))\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\eta}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}=I_{\mathrm{h}}(\hat{\eta}(t^{n},\cdot)).

Note that the measurement error does not need to be known explicitly for the observer system, because the nudging term requires only the measured data
\widehat{v}+\hat{\eta}
, however we track the term separately to see its influence throughout the following analysis.
With this, we are ready to state the fully discrete scheme.

Numerical Scheme.
Let
{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle}
denote the
L^{2}
-inner product on
\Omega
. Let
\rho_{\mathrm{h}}^{0}\in Q_{\mathrm{h}}
and
m_{\mathrm{h}}^{0}
with
m_{\mathrm{h}}^{0}-\widehat{m}_{\partial}(t^{0})\in R_{\mathrm{h}}
be given.
For
1\leq n\leq N
, find
\rho_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}\in Q_{\mathrm{h}}
and
m_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}
with
m_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-\widehat{m}_{\partial}(t^{n})\in R_{\mathrm{h}}
, such that

(3.12)

\displaystyle{\langle\overline{\partial_{\tau}}\rho_{\mathrm{h}}^{n},q_{\mathrm{h}}\rangle}+{\langle\partial_{x}m_{\mathrm{h}}^{n},q_{\mathrm{h}}\rangle}=

\displaystyle{\langle\Pi_{\mathrm{h}}(\hat{s}_{1}^{n}),q_{\mathrm{h}}\rangle},\quad\forall q_{\mathrm{h}}\in Q_{\mathrm{h}},

(3.13)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\langle\overline{\partial_{\tau}}v_{\mathrm{h}}^{n},r_{\mathrm{h}}\rangle}-&{\langle\symscr{h}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n},\partial_{x}r_{\mathrm{h}}\rangle}+{\symscr{h}}_{\partial}^{n}r_{\mathrm{h}}\lvert^{x=\ell}_{x=0}\\
&+\gamma{\langle\absolutevalue{\numvel^n}v_{\mathrm{h}}^{n},r_{\mathrm{h}}\rangle}+\mu{\langle v_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-(\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}+\hat{\eta}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}),r_{\mathrm{h}}\rangle}\end{aligned}=

\displaystyle{\langle\hat{s}_{2}^{n},r_{\mathrm{h}}\rangle},\quad\forall r_{\mathrm{h}}\in R_{\mathrm{h}},

with
v_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}\coloneqq\frac{m_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}}{\rho_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}}
and
\symscr{h}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(v_{\mathrm{h}}^{n})^{2}+P^{\prime}(\rho_{\mathrm{h}}^{n})
.

We define
{\symscr{h}}_{\partial}^{n}r_{\mathrm{h}}\lvert^{x=\ell}_{x=0}\coloneqq{\symscr{h}}_{\partial}(t^{n})r_{\mathrm{h}}\lvert_{x=\ell}-\symscr{h}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}r_{\mathrm{h}}\lvert_{x=0}
, which corresponds to weakly enforcing the boundary condition on
{\symscr{h}}
at
x=\ell
. Note that
\symscr{h}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}r_{\mathrm{h}}\lvert_{x=0}=0
, because
r_{\mathrm{h}}\lvert_{x=0}=0
for all
r_{\mathrm{h}}\in R_{\mathrm{h}}
. The boundary condition on
m_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}
at
x=0
is enforced strongly.

For the numerical analysis, we will compare the numerical solution to the projection of the solution of the original system in conservative variables,
(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{m})
with
\widehat{m}\coloneqq\widehat{\rho}\widehat{v}
. We set

(3.14)

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Pi_{\mathrm{h}}(\widehat{\rho}(t^{n},\cdot)),\quad\widehat{m}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}\coloneqq I_{\mathrm{h}}(\widehat{m}(t^{n},\cdot)),\quad\text{and}

(3.15)

\displaystyle\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n},\nicefrac{{\widehat{m}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}}}{{\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}}}).

With these definitions, importantly,
\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}\neq\widehat{m}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}
. Instead, one has
||\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-\widehat{m}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}||_{\infty}\leq C\mathrm{h}
with some constant
C
independent of
n
, which we show in Lemma
4
. Additionally we define
\widehat{\symscr{h}}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\nicefrac{{\widehat{m}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}}}{{\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}}})^{2}+P^{\prime}(\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n})
.

In
[
10
]
, it is shown that, under some additional assumptions, the above scheme converges uniformly in time, at first order in
\tau
and
\mathrm{h}
. More precisely, one can show that, for all
n
such that
t^{n}\leq T
for some
T\in\symbb{R}
, one gets
||{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-{u}^{n}||_{2}^{2}\leq C(\mathrm{h}^{2}+\tau^{2})
, with
C\sim\exp(cT)
, coming from a discrete version of Gronwall’s inequality. In the convergence proof from
[
10
]
, one cannot expect to obtain a better bound than this exponentially large constant.

A naive approach for obtaining an error bound comparing the discrete observer to the original system could be combining the continuous synchronization result and the error bound for the numerical scheme using the following splitting of the error:

(3.16)

||{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-\widehat{u}^{n}||_{2}\leq||{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-{u}^{n}||_{2}+||{u}^{n}-\widehat{u}^{n}||_{2}.

The first term can be estimated using the error estimate of the numerical scheme (after some simple modifications to include the nudging term of the observer system). The second term can be controlled using the synchronization of the continuous observer system, Theorem
1
. Thus, one gets an overall error estimate of
||{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-\widehat{u}^{n}||_{2}^{2}\lesssim\left(\mathrm{h}^{2}+\tau^{2}\right)\exp(C_1 T)+\exp(-C_2T)
at time
t=T
. However, this estimate is not uniform-in-time, which leads to much more restrictive bounds on the grid sizes for long-time simulations. To guarantee
||{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-\widehat{u}^{n}||_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon
for some prescribed error tolerance
\epsilon>0
one requires
T\gtrsim-\log(\epsilon)/C_{2}
and
(\mathrm{h}^{2}+\tau^{2})\lesssim\epsilon^{1+\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}}
. From the proofs of
[
10
]
and
[
16
]
, one finds that
C_{1}\gg C_{2}
, such that the estimate scales poorly for
\epsilon\to 0
.

We improve the error bound by using a different splitting of the error. By comparing the solution of the discrete observer directly to the projection of the original system state instead, we can make use of the nudging term and obtain an error bound that does not deteriorate over time. This leads to the following splitting:

(3.17)

||\widehat{u}^{n}-{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}||_{2}\leq||\widehat{u}^{n}-\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}||_{2}+||\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}||_{2}.

Due to Assumption
(A1)
, and in particular, since
\widehat{u}
is Lipschitz uniformly in time, the projection error can be bounded by
||\widehat{u}^{n}-\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}||_{2}\leq C\mathrm{h}
for some
C>0
independent of
T
.
For the second term, the nudging term makes it possible to prove an error estimate with a constant independent time.

4.
Convergence of the discrete observer.

In this section, we will prove the convergence of the discrete observer system.
Our final error estimate is given by the following corollary, which we will prove later:

Corollary
30
.

Let
\widehat{u}
be a solution of system (
3.1
) – (
3.2
) and
{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}
be a solution of (
3.12
) – (
3.13
). Further, let Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A4)
,
(A5)
and
(A1h)
hold. Let
\alpha,\tau_{0}\in\symbb{R}
be chosen small enough, such that
C_{1}C_{0}^{-1}>(\alpha+\tau_{0})C_{2}c_{0}^{-1}
, with the constants as introduced in Theorem
28
.
Then, for all
0<\tau\leq\tau_{0}

Corollary

We use a relative energy framework, as presented in
[
16
]
, and adapt the estimates therein to the discrete setting. In order to motivate the more technical discrete computations, we summarize the strategy from
[
16
]
in the following section.

The system (
3.1
) – (
3.2
) is equipped with the energy

(4.2)

\hamil(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{v})=\int_{0}^{\ell}\frac{1}{2}\widehat{v}^{2}+P(\widehat{\rho})\differential{x}

and the state variables
(\widehat{\rho},\widehat{v})
are connected to the co-state variables

by the variational derivatives of this energy, that is,
(\widehat{\symscr{h}},\widehat{m})=(\delta_{\rho}\hamil(\widehat{u}),\delta_{v}\hamil(\widehat{u}))

We use the energy to define the relative energy between two functions
u
and
\widehat{u}
, given by

(4.3)

\hamil({u},\widehat{u})\coloneqq\hamil({u})-\hamil(\widehat{u})-{\langle\hamil^{\prime}(\widehat{u}),{u}-\widehat{u}\rangle},

The relative energy amounts to the difference of the energy of state
{u}
and the linear Taylor expansion of the energy around
\widehat{u}
. Under the uniform bounds from assumption
(A1)
and the convexity of the pressure potential
(A2)
, the relative energy can be used to measure the distance between two functions. To make this more precise, we recall

and

Lemma 2

(Norm equivalence)
.

Let

satisfy the bounds from equations (
3.6
). Further, let Assumption
(A2)
hold. Then , there exist

with

(4.4)

c_{0}||{u}-\widehat{u}||_{2}^{2}\leq\hamil({u},\widehat{u})\leq C_{0}||{u}-\widehat{u}||_{2}^{2}.

The constants
c_{0}
and
C_{0}
depend only on the bounds in the assumptions.

To control the norm
||{u}-\widehat{u}||_{2}
, one can thus bound the relative energy. This is done using a Gronwall-inequality type argument, which requires estimates for the time derivative of the relative energy.

Now, let
\widehat{u}
be a solution of system (
3.1
) – (
3.2
) and let
{u}
be a solution to (
3.3
) – (
3.4
). Estimating the time derivative of the relative energy, one only obtains dissipation in the difference of velocities, i.e.

\partial_{t}\hamil({u},\widehat{u})\leq-\frac{1}{2}\mu\underline{\rho}||v-\widehat{v}||_{2}^{2}+C||{u}-\widehat{u}||_{2}^{2}

for some constant

independent of
\mu

\overbar{v}
and the time derivatives of the state
\widehat{u}
, c.f. Assumption

can be made arbitrarily small. However, because there is no dissipation in

Instead, the idea is to find a modified relative energy for which dissipation in both variables can be shown. One ansatz for constructing such a modified relative energy is to find a functional
\contG
and some small parameter

with the following properties:

(R1)

For
\delta

For any choice of

and the bounds in Assumption

such that

(4.5)

\partial_{t}\left(\hamil({u},\widehat{u})+\delta\contG({u},\widehat{u})\right)\leq-C\left(\hamil({u},\widehat{u})+\delta\contG({u},\widehat{u})\right),

with some constant

depending on the bounds in Assumptions

–
(A4)
.

Applying Gronwall’s lemma then proves exponential-in-time convergence of the observer state towards the original system state as in Theorem

[
16
]
, a suitable functional

In this work we extend these arguments to the discrete observer (

4.2.
Projection errors.

Before we start estimating the discrete time derivative of the relative energy, we summarize some properties of the projection operators.

We use the following lemma to estimate projection errors:

Lemma 3

Let

||\Pi_{\mathrm{h}}z||_{\infty}\leq||z||_{\infty},\quad\text{and}\quad||I_{\mathrm{h}}z||_{\infty}\leq||z||_{\infty}.

Lastly, there exists a constant

such that for any

||z-\Pi_{\mathrm{h}}z||_{p}\leq c\mathrm{h}||\partial_{x}z||_{p},\quad\text{and}\quad||z-I_{\mathrm{h}}z||_{p}\leq c\mathrm{h}||\partial_{x}z||_{p}.

is independent of

and
\mathrm{h}

Remark 10
.

The previous lemma means that the perturbation term (IV) from Lemma
7
gives three types of contributions:

•

contributions that are proportional to
||{u}_{\mathrm{h}}-\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{h}}||_{2}^{2}
, but can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
\alpha
small enough,

•

contributions that are proportional to
||{u}_{\mathrm{h}}-\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{h}}||_{2}^{2}
and
\tau
, and

•

contributions at order
\symcal{O}(\tau^{2})
.

The first type of contributions does not spoil the constants one would get when estimating the time derivative of the relative energy in the continuous case, and for sufficiently small
\tau
the second type of contributions will not spoil the exponential decay of the relative energy. Finally, the last type contributes to the base level of error the observer can achieve. Choosing small
\alpha
gives worse constants on these terms.
We will show similar results for the remaining terms of the discrete time derivative
\overline{\partial_{\tau}}\hamil({u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n},\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n})
, too.

Remark 27
.

The previous lemma gives some insight on how to choose
\mu
. When setting the parameter
\mu
, there are three main goals:

•

obtaining a fast exponential decay rate, i.e. having
C_{1}(\alpha,\tau_{0})
as large as possible,

•

having a wide range of applicability, that is, being able to prescribe large values of
\overbar{v}
and
C_{t}
from Assumption
(A4)
, and

•

balancing the amplification of measurement errors.

Contrary to the intuition one might have, that larger
\mu
will always provide faster convergence, because the velocity component is nudged more strongly towards to the data, the above proofs indicate that overly large
\mu
will be suboptimal. In particular, a larger choice of
\mu
requires a smaller choice of
\delta
in the estimate (
5.4
). A smaller choice of
\delta
then also needs more restrictive bounds on
\overbar{v}
and
C_{t}
to show estimate (
5.5
).

Further, choosing larger values of
\mu
also amplifies measurement errors more strongly, reducing the maximum possible accuracy of the observer. This is in line with the motivation of using an observer in the first place. In the formal limit of
\mu\to\infty
, the observer system corresponds to setting
v=\widehat{v}
and reconstructing
\widehat{\rho}
using the mass conservation equation of the Euler system. This means the relevant measurement error is the derivative
\partial_{x}\hat{\eta}
. Typically, measurement errors for the derivatives of some quantity are much larger than those of that quantity itself. In our analysis, we have no regularity assumptions on the derivatives of the measurement error, and thus, our estimate blows up as
\mu\to\infty
.

Next, we move on to the final convergence result.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}a_{n}\leq 2\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}{w}_{j}^{-1}\right)a_{0}+\frac{\tau}{1+\tilde{C}_{2}c_{0}^{-1}\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\prod_{j=k}^{n}{w}_{j}^{-1}\tilde{g}_{k}+C\mathrm{h}^{2}.

In particular,
C
is independent of
n
,
\tau
and
\mathrm{h}
. Then inserting the definitions of
\tilde{g}_{n}
, the fact that
\prod_{j=k}^{n}{w}_{j}^{-1}\leq 1
and using
\symcal{D}({u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{k},\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{k})\geq c||v_{\mathrm{h}}^{k}-\nicefrac{{\widehat{m}_{\mathrm{h}}^{k}}}{{\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{h}}^{k}}}||_{3}^{3}
, we obtain

(5.20)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}a_{n}+c\tau\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\prod_{j=k}^{n}{w}_{j}^{-1}\right)||v_{\mathrm{h}}^{k}-\nicefrac{{\widehat{m}_{\mathrm{h}}^{k}}}{{\widehat{\rho}_{\mathrm{h}}^{k}}}||_{3}^{3}\leq 2\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}{w}_{j}^{-1}\right)a_{0}+C(\tau^{2}+\mathrm{h}^{2})+C||\hat{\eta}||_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}.

Then, using
a_{n}\geq c_{0}||{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}||_{2}^{2}
and
a_{0}\leq C_{0}||{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{0}-\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{0}||_{2}^{2}
completes the proof.
∎

6.
Numerical Experiments

In this section we present several numerical experiments to illustrate the error bound. In particular, we want to highlight the following three points:

•

We observe an initial exponential decay of the error of the numerical solution, followed by a plateau in error, which does not increase in time.

•

We observe that the height of the plateau is proportional to
\mathrm{h}
and
\tau
.

•

How quickly the error approaches the plateau depends on the nudging parameter
\mu
, with overly large
\mu
leading to slower synchronization, see Remark
27
.

To compare the numerical solution of the observer system to the original system state, we cannot solve the original system using some numerical scheme. Unlike the observer system, the reference solution computed this way could develop large errors over time, making it unsuitable for comparison.
Because of this, we instead use manufactured solutions, that is, we prescribe a function
\widehat{u}
and then compute source terms
\hat{s}_{1}
and
\hat{s}_{2}
such that equations (
3.1
) – (
3.2
) are satisfied.

6.1.
Implementation details

A reference implementation of the numerical scheme for the observer and the required data to generate the plots is located at
https://git-ce.rwth-aachen.de/publications_chaumet/observers

For the source term
\hat{s}_{1}
, the numerical algorithm requires evaluating the
L^{2}
projection
\Pi_{\mathrm{h}}
, i.e. it is required to compute cell-wise averages of
\hat{s}_{1}
. We do this using the trapezoid rule. Consider a cell
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
with
0\leq i\leq M-1
, then we have
\Pi_{\mathrm{h}}(\hat{s}_{1}^{n})\big\lvert_{[x_{i},x_{i+1}]}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{s}_{1}^{n}(x_{i})+\hat{s}_{1}^{n}(x_{i+1}))+\symcal{O}(\mathrm{h}^{2})
. Handling the
\symcal{O}(\mathrm{h}^{2})
terms similarly to the measurement error, one can see that these errors will not accumulate, and because they are at second order in
\mathrm{h}
, they will not significantly influence the plateau of the error. Computing the
L^{2}
errors
||{u}_{\mathrm{h}}^{n}-\widehat{u}^{n}||_{2}
requires numerical integration and we use the trapezoid rule for this as well.

We solve the nonlinear system of equations (
3.12
) – (
3.13
) using the Newton method, taking the discrete solution of the previous time step as an initial guess in each new time step. We prescribe a solver tolerance of at least
10^{-12}
on the residuals with respect to the maximum norm, however, in the following experiments, we always achieve residuals less than
10^{-15}
for each time step.

7.
Discussion

In this paper we have proved an error bound for a fully discrete observer for the barotropic Euler equations. We show that the observer term stabilizes the system in such a way that one has an error bound that is uniform-in-time. Thus, the discrete observer stays accurate even for long-time simulations. In particular, we show that the observer converges exponentially with respect to the error in the initial guess of the state, up to additive contributions due to the discretization and measurement errors, which do not increase with time.

Our proof is based on a relative energy approach, which limits it to solutions that are uniformly Lipschitz in time and we require some smallness assumptions to show decay for arbitrary
\mu
. However, the intermediate proof steps also give some insight and criteria on how to choose a “good” value for
\mu
, showing that one has to balance the increase of measurement errors, the applicability of the estimate and the convergence speed. As an extension, one might also consider a time-dependent nudging parameter, that decreases over time, to benefit from a fast exponential decay rate initially, but obtain a more accurate prediction once measurement errors become a significant source of error.

We illustrate our error estimate in a numerical study. While our estimates are certainly not sharp in general and only present an upper bound, the qualitative features of our error estimate are visible in the numerical study, regardless.

To extend the range of applications for observer-based data assimilation for hyperbolic balance laws, it would be of interest to further study how to extend our results to discontinuous solutions.
In similar spirit, in this work, we have only focused on the barotropic Euler equations in one space dimension. The next step is to consider either two dimensions, or an extension to networks. For the network case,
[
16
]
shows that the results can be extended to star-shaped networks, and we think the same should be possible for the discrete observer. However, an extension to more general network topologies seems challenging and would require additional understanding at the continuous level first.
