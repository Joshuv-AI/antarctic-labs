Ultra slow sub-logarithmic diffusion of a sluggish random walker subject to resetting with memory
Satya N. Majumdar

Abstract
We solve a model of sluggish stochastic motion in which a Brownian particle diffuses with a diffusion coefficient that decays algebraically with the distance to the origin, as | x | − α |x|^{-\alpha} . Additionally, the particle resets with a constant rate r r to positions previously visited in the past, so that frequently visited regions are more likely to be revisited. An exact expression is obtained at all times for the position distribution in arbitrary spatial dimensions. At late times, the typical displacement of the walker from the origin grows extremely slowly, as [ ln ⁡ ( r ​ t ) ] 1 / ( α + 2 ) [\ln(rt)]^{1/(\alpha+2)} , and the position distribution tends to a scaling law. For any α > 0 \alpha>0 , the scaling function has a bimodal shape with a minimum at x = 0 x=0 and has non-Gaussian tails. Although the mean square displacement is hard to compute, some generalized moments of this process can be calculated exactly at all times in one dimension, and are shown to be closely related to the moments of the well-studied model with a constant diffusion coefficient.



1
Introduction

Resetting with memory has been demonstrated to slow down the dynamics of a diffusing particle
considerably. Consider, for instance, a single Brownian particle on a line
with a diffusion constant
D
. In addition
to diffusion, the particle undergoes a resetting process with rate
r

to previously visited positions.
Resetting follows a stochastic rule that incorporates memory effects: the particle that
resets at some time
t
chooses uniformly at random a preceding time
0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t
, i.e., with probability density
1/t
, and occupies the position at which it was located at

t^{\prime}
again. This resetting move with memory favours revisit to frequently visited locations and therefore tends
to localize the particle, while the normal diffusion tends to de-localize it.
Eventually de-localization wins but at the cost of a considerable slowing down
of the dynamics. The position distribution of this particle at time
t
was first computed exactly
on a lattice
[
1
]
, and later in a continuous space-time version of the model
[
2
]
.
At late times, the position distribution
P_{r}(x,t)
turns out to be a simple Gaussian, albeit with
a variance that grows logarithmically with increasing time
[
1
,
2
]

P_{r}(x,t)\approx\sqrt{\frac{r}{4\,\pi\,D\,\ln(rt)}}\,\exp\left[-\frac{r\,x^{2}}{4\,D\,\ln(rt)}\right]\,.

(1)

This slow dynamics can be traced back to the fact that
the resetting induced memory makes the walker likely to re-visit frequently visited locations and thus growth via visiting fresh sites gets severely hindered.

This simple model of diffusion with resetting to a previously visited location
via a uniform memory kernel has been generalized in various directions.
This includes incorporating a fading memory
[
3
]
, generalization to Lévy flights
[
4
]
,
generalization to a broad memory kernel going beyond the uniform case
[
2
]
and more
recently to active run and tumble particles
[
5
]
. A Brownian particle in one dimension in the presence of a
uniform resetting induced memory and an external confining potential
U(x)
has been exactly solved recently
[
6
]
.
This study demonstrated that, somewhat unexpectedly, the system reaches a Gibbs-Boltzmann stationary state

P^{\rm st}(x)\propto e^{-U(x)/D}
at long times, but the relaxation to this stationary state is rather slow: the deviation from
the stationary state decays non-exponentially in time, as a power law with an exponent that depends continuously on the system parameters, namely

r
and
D

[
6
]
. This result for the uniform kernel in the presence of a confining potential was recently generalized to the case
of wider range of kernels
[
7
]
.
On the mathematical side, a central limit theorem and an anomalous
large deviation principle have also been established for a class of memory walks of this type for a broad range of memory
kernels
[
8
,
9
]
. The rigorous proofs of these results are based on a connection between the resetting process to
previous sites and the growth of weighted random recursive trees.
In a more applied context, random walks with long range memory have become increasingly useful in ecology for the description and
analysis of animal mobility. There is mounting evidence that animals do not follow purely Markov processes but use their memory and
tend to revisit preferred places during ranging
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. The model above was able to
describe quantitatively the movement patterns of Capuchin monkeys in the wild
[
1
]
, as well as of individual elks released
in an unknown environment
[
16
]
. Another feature shared by many animal trajectories is spatial localization, by opposition
to unbounded diffusion, as home ranges are often clearly identifiable.
The range-resident behaviour of animals can be modelled by incorporating a central place toward which
they are attracted (such as a den), which produces an effective piece-wise linear
potential and therefore a stationary ’equilibrium’ state
[
17
]
. Recent studies have
considered harmonic potentials and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) processes as the starting point of models of
animal motion of increasing complexity
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

Another simple random walk lattice model leading to subdiffusive growth at late times has been introduced recently, known
as the ‘sluggish random walk’ (SRW)
[
21
]
. In the SRW model, e.g., in one dimension, the hopping rate out of a site located
at
x
decreases as
\sim|x|^{-\alpha}
as
|x|
increases (with
\alpha>0
), making the walker sluggish the further it goes from the
origin. In the continuum limit, the position of the walker evolves via the Langevin equation

\frac{dx}{dt}=\sqrt{2\,D(x)}\,\eta(t)\,,

(2)

where
\eta(t)
is a zero mean Gaussian white noise with delta correlations:
\langle\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})

and
D(x)\sim|x|^{-\alpha}
is a space dependent diffusion constant that falls off algebraically
with the distance from the origin. The Langevin equation (
2
) is interpreted
in the Itô sense, since in the associated lattice model the hopping rate depends only on the departure site, and
not on the destination site. The associated Fokker-Planck equation for the position distribution reads
[
21
]

\frac{\partial P(x,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\left[D(x)\,P(x,t)\right]\,.

(3)

Note that
D(x)
appears inside the Laplacian in Eq. (
3
) due to the Itô rule.
The choice
D(x)\approx|x|^{-\alpha}
for
|x|\gg 1
creates an effective drift away from the origin,
but the diffusion rate also falls off as a power law with
distance. The solution of the Fokker-Planck equation admits a scaling form at late times
[
21
]
,

P(x,t)\approx\frac{1}{t^{\nu}}\,G_{\alpha}\left(\frac{x}{t^{\nu}}\right)\,\quad{\rm with}\quad\nu=\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\,,

(4)

where
G_{\alpha}(z)
is a symmetric but non-Gaussian scaling function (for any
\alpha>0
). It is actually non-monotonous with respect to
|x|
and given by

G_{\alpha}(z)=A_{\alpha}\,|z|^{\alpha}\,\exp\left(-\nu^{2}\,|z|^{1/\nu}\right)\,.

(5)

The normalization constant
A_{\alpha}
can be explicitly computed and is given by
[
21
]

A_{\alpha}=\frac{\nu^{1-2\nu}}{2\,\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,\quad{\rm with}\quad\nu=\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\,.

(6)

The scaling form (
4
) of
P(x,t)
shows that the typical position of
the walker spreads subdiffusively as
x\sim t^{1/(\alpha+2)}
, recovering the diffusive spread only for
\alpha=0
. The scaling function
G_{\alpha}(z)
is shown on Fig.
1
.
Various other observables such as the survival probability up to time
t
and the distribution of the maximal
displacement up to time
t
can be computed explicitly for all
\alpha\geq 0

[
21
]
. Recently, distributions of other functionals
of this process such as the occupation time, the time at which the maximum occurs and the last-passage time before
t

have been computed explicitly, leading to non-trivial generalization (for
\alpha>0)
of the arcsine laws valid
for
\alpha=0

[
22
]
.

Figure 1:
Scaling functions of the sluggish random walker in one dimension, given by Eq. (
5
), for various parameter values. The walker follows a Brownian motion where the diffusion coefficient decreases with the distance from the origin as
D(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
.

Diffusion of a particle with a space-dependent diffusion coefficient
D(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
and subject to
stochastic resetting to a fixed position (with rate
r
) has been studied in the anti-Itô interpretation

[
23
]
. Due to resetting, the position distribution tends asymptotically to a non-equilibrium
steady state, which can be obtained exactly, as well as the mean square displacement at all times. The
mean first-passage time of the particle at the origin can also be calculated
[
23
]
. Some exact
results on first-passage observables can also be obtained for an arbitrary profile
D(x)
in the
Stratonovitch sense, while approximate expressions at large and small
r
are available in the other
interpretations
[
24
]
.
The sluggish random walker model with stochastic resetting
to the initial position has also been studied recently in the context of an adaptive and efficient search
strategy called ‘proxitaxis’
[
25
]
.

Here, we wish to investigate how the memory dependent resetting with rate
r
combined with the sluggishness in the diffusion via

D(x)\sim|x|^{-\alpha}
leads to a drastic slowdown of the late time dynamics of the random walker.
In the former case, the typical distance of the walker grows as
\sim\sqrt{\ln(rt)}
at late times even though the position distribution
remains Gaussian. In the latter case, the typical distance grows as a subdiffusive power law
\sim t^{1/(\alpha+2)}
, but
the position distribution becomes highly non-Gaussian for any
\alpha>0
. It is then natural to ask what happens to
a sluggish random walker with
D(x)\sim|x|^{-\alpha}
, if in addition it undergoes memory dependent resetting with rate
r
.
How does the typical position grow with time and what is the position distribution at late times? In this paper,
we provide an exact solution for the position distribution in this model with both sluggishness and memory dependent resetting.
We show that these two moves lead to an ultra slowdown in the dynamics and the typical distance of the walker
from the origin grows sub-logarithmically,
x\sim\left[\ln(rt)\right]^{1/(\alpha+2)}
at late times. However, the
scaling function describing the position distribution remains the same as in Eq. (
5
), i.e., unchanged from
the scaling function
G_{\alpha}(z)
of the sluggish walker without resetting.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we define the
model precisely and derive the exact position distribution at all times in one dimension.
In Section
3
, we derive the exact generalized moments of the position distribution
and verify the analytical predictions numerically. Section
4
provides
the derivation of the exact position distribution in arbitrary dimensions. We conclude with
a summary and outlook in Section
5
. Details of the numerical simulations are
provided in
A
.

2
The model and its exact solution in one dimension

We start with a sluggish random walker on a line evolving via the Langevin equation (
2
) with
a space dependent diffusion rate
D(x)
(symmetric in
x
), starting at the origin at
t=0
.
For the moment we consider a general
D(x)
, but eventually we will focus on

D(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
. With this choice, the model remains well behaved
in the continuum even in the vicinity of
x\to 0
.
We now incorporate the memory driven resetting moves to this sluggish dynamics.
At time
t
, with rate
r
the particle chooses any previous time in the past
0\leq t^{\prime}<t
with
uniform probability density
1/t
and resets to the position that it occupied at that previous instant
t^{\prime}
. Let
P_{r}(x,t)

denote the position distribution at time
t
. To take into account the memory effect, we now need to define the
two-point function
P_{r}(x,t\,;x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
, which is the joint probability density for the particle to be at
x^{\prime}
at
t^{\prime}

and at
x
at
t
, with
t^{\prime}\leq t
. Clearly, if we integrate over one of the positions, we recover the
marginal one-point probability density

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{r}(x,t\,;x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}=P_{r}(x,t)\,\ {\rm and}\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{r}(x,t\,;x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,dx=P_{r}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,.

(7)

Subsequently, the Fokker-Planck equation for the evolution of the one-point
position distribution
P_{r}(x,t)
can be written as

\partial_{t}P_{r}(x,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}\left[D(x)\,P_{r}(x,t)\right]-r\,P_{r}(x,t)+\frac{r}{t}\,\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx^{\prime}\,P_{r}(x^{\prime},t;x,t^{\prime})\,.

(8)

The first term on the right hand side (rhs) describes the space-dependent diffusion via the Itô rule.
The second term
describes the loss of probability density from position
x
at time
t
due to resetting to other positions.
The last term on the rhs describes the gain in the probability density at
x
at time
t
due to resetting
from other positions labelled by
x^{\prime}
. If the particle has to reset to
x
from
x^{\prime}
at time
t
, it must have been
at
x
at a previous time
t^{\prime}\leq t
and the probability density of this event is simply the
two-point function
P_{r}(x^{\prime},t;x,t^{\prime})
. Finally, we need to integrate over all times
t^{\prime}
where
x
may have been visited, and all positions
x^{\prime}
from
which the particle may arrive at
x
at time
t
via resetting. The reason for the solvability for the one-point
position distribution in this model can then traced back to the second relation in Eq. (
7
), which
allows us to write a closed equation for the one-point function (without involving multiple-point functions).
We then obtain

\partial_{t}P_{r}(x,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}\left[D(x)\,P_{r}(x,t)\right]-r\,P_{r}(x,t)+\frac{r}{t}\,\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P_{r}(x,t^{\prime})\,.

(9)

By integrating Eq. (
9
) over
x
and if no absorbing boundaries are present, it is easy to check that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{r}(x,t)\,dx
is independent of time, i.e., the total probability is
conserved and equals unity since the initial condition, e,g.,
P_{r}(x,0)=\delta(x-x_{0})
normalizes the
probability to unity. One also needs to impose the vanishing boundary conditions as
x\to\pm\infty
, i.e.,

P_{r}(x\to\pm\infty,t)=0
for all
t
.
Evidently, for
r=0
, Eq. (
9
) reduces to the
Fokker-Planck equation (
3
). Note that
P_{r}(x,t)
of course depends on the choice of
D(x)
, but we
suppress the explicit dependence of
P_{r}(x,t)
on
D(x)
for the moment to keep notational simplicity, and
we will restore it later at an appropriate place.

To solve the Fokker-Planck equation (
9
), we use the
method of separation of variables with the ansatz

P_{r}(x,t)=\phi(x)\,f_{r}(t)\,.

(10)

Substituting (
10
) in Eq. (
9
), dividing both sides by
\phi(x)f_{r}(t)
and assembling
the only-time dependent and only-space dependent parts separately gives

\frac{{\dot{f}_{r}}(t)}{f_{r}(t)}+r-\frac{r}{t\,f_{r}(t)}\,\int_{0}^{t}f_{r}(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\phi(x)}\,\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\left[D(x)\,\phi(x)\right]=-\lambda\,,

(11)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a constant independent of
x
and
t
. Note that the time-dependent part

f_{r}(t)
is completely independent of
D(x)
, while the
space-dependent function
\phi(x)
is independent of
r
. We now solve the time and space dependent parts
separately.

2.1
Time-dependent part

In fact, the time-dependent part
f_{r}(t)
, which is independent of
D(x)
, was already solved in Ref.
[
6
]
, which
we recall briefly here. For a given
\lambda
, we get from Eq. (
11
)
a second order ordinary differential equation for
f_{r}(t)

{\dot{f}_{r}}(t)+(r+\lambda)\,f_{r}(t)-\frac{r}{t}\,\int_{0}^{t}f_{r}(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}=0\,.

(12)

Multiplying this equation by
t
and taking the time derivative, one obtains

t{\ddot{f}_{r}}(t)+[1+(r+\lambda)t]\,\dot{f_{r}}(t)+\lambda f_{r}(t)=0\,.

(13)

Through the change of variable
z=-(\lambda+r)t
with
g(z)\equiv f(t)
, Eq. (
13
) can be recast as a confluent hypergeometric equation,

z\frac{d^{2}g(z)}{dz^{2}}+(b-z)\frac{dg(z)}{dz}-ag(z)=0,

(14)

with
b=1
and
a=\lambda/(\lambda+r)
. The differential equation (
14
) has two
linearly independent solutions denoted by
M(a,b,z)
and
U(a,b,z)

[
26
]
.
The function
M(a,b,z)
is known as Kummer’s function and has a simple power series expansion

M(a,b,z)=1+\frac{a}{b}\,z+\frac{a(a+1)}{b(b+1)}\,\frac{z^{2}}{2!}+\ldots\,.

(15)

Its linearly independent cousin
U(a,b,z)
is called the confluent hypergeometric function and
has a more complicated expression
[
26
]
.
Hence, the most general solution of Eq. (
13
) can be expressed as

f_{r}(t)=c_{1}\,M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)+c_{2}\,\,U\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)\,,

(16)

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are two arbitrary constants. The solution must be finite at all
t
, in particular at
t=0
. The small argument asymptotics of the two solutions are
given by
[
26
]

\displaystyle M(a,1,z)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle 1\,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,{\rm as}\ z\to 0

(17)

\displaystyle U(a,1,z)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\,\ln z\,\quad{\rm as}\ z\to 0\,.

(18)

Consequently, the logarithmic divergence as
t\to 0
in Eq. (
16
) is avoided by setting
c_{2}=0
.
Then the solution simply reads

f_{r}(t)=M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)\,.

(19)

where we have set
c_{1}=1
by absorbing this constant into the amplitude of
the space-dependent eigenmode
\phi(x)
.

2.2
Space-dependent part

The space dependent part
\phi(x)
in Eq. (
11
) evidently depends on
\lambda
. Hence
we denote it by
\phi_{\lambda}(x)
and it satisfies the eigenvalue equation

\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\left[D(x)\,\phi_{\lambda}(x)\right]+\lambda\,\phi_{\lambda}(x)=0\,.

(20)

Note that since
D(x)
is symmetric in
x
, so is
\phi_{\lambda}(x)
.
To proceed further, we need to
make a choice for
D(x)
. Following the sluggish random walker model
studied in Ref.
[
21
]
,
we choose
D(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
. We next define

h_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{\phi_{\lambda}(x)}{|x|^{\alpha}}\,,

(21)

which satisfies the ordinary second order differential equation

\frac{d^{2}h_{\lambda}(x)}{dx^{2}}+\lambda\,|x|^{\alpha}\,h_{\lambda}(x)=0\,.

(22)

Since
h_{\lambda}(x)
is symmetric around
x=0
, we will henceforth consider only
x\geq 0
.
This differential equation (
22
) can fortunately be solved exactly for any

\alpha\geq 0
. The general solution for
x\geq 0
reads

h_{\lambda}(x)=B_{1}(\lambda)\,\sqrt{x}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)+B_{2}(\lambda)\,\sqrt{x}\ J_{\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,,

(23)

where

\nu=\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\,.

(24)

Here
J_{\pm\nu}(z)
is the standard Bessel function of index
\pm\nu
and argument
z
. The two unknown
constants (i.e., independent of
x
)
B_{1}(\lambda)

and
B_{2}(\lambda)
need to be fixed from the boundary conditions. Let us first consider the
boundary
x=0
. It is clear from Eq. (
21
) that
h_{\lambda}(x)
is symmetric in
x

and its second derivative must exist as
x\to 0
.
Let us now take the
x\to 0
limit of
the solution (
23
) using the following asymptotic behaviour of the Bessel function
[
26
]

J_{\nu}(z)\to\frac{2^{-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\,z^{\nu}\quad{\rm as}\ z\to 0\,.

(25)

This gives

h_{\lambda}(x)\to\left[\frac{\nu^{-\nu}\,\lambda^{-1/(2\nu)}}{\Gamma(1-\nu)}\right]\,B_{1}(\lambda)+\left[\frac{\nu^{\nu}\,\lambda^{1/(2\nu)}}{\Gamma(1+\nu)}\right]\,B_{2}(\lambda)\,x\,\quad{\rm as}\ x\to 0\,.

(26)

Since
h_{\lambda}(x)
is symmetric in
x
, it can not have a linear slope as
x\to 0
, since that would lead
to a cusp at
x=0
and the second derivative
h_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(0)
will not exist. To rule out the linear slope at

x=0
, we therefore need to set
B_{2}(\lambda)=0
, leading to

h_{\lambda}(x)=B_{1}(\lambda)\,\sqrt{x}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,.

(27)

Consequently, the space dependent part of the
eigensolution
\phi_{\lambda}(x)
, using Eq. (
21
), is given by

\phi_{\lambda}(x)=B_{1}(\lambda)\,x^{\alpha+1/2}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,,

(28)

where we recall that
\nu=1/(\alpha+2)
.
Since
J_{\nu}(z)\sim\cos(z-\pi\nu/2-\pi/4)/\sqrt{z}
as
z\to\infty
, it follows
that
\phi_{\lambda}(x)
in Eq. (
28
) diverges as
x^{3\alpha/4}
at large
x
.
This, however, is not a problem for the full solution
P_{r}(x,t)
when all
the modes are summed over (see the next subsection). This is due to non-trivial cancellations at large argument that
occur due to the oscillatory nature of the Bessel function.

2.3
The full solution

For any fixed
\lambda
, we have now determined both the time dependent part
f_{r}(t)
and the space dependent part
\phi_{\lambda}(x)

of the eigensolution characterized by the eigenvalue
\lambda\geq 0
. Their product, for any fixed
\lambda\geq 0
, is a solution of the
original Fokker-Planck equation. In fact the most general solution of the Fokker-Planck equation (
9
) can be
expressed as a linear combination over all possible values of
\lambda\geq 0
. Since the position distribution now
depends on the two parameters
(r,\alpha)
and the variables
x
and
t
, we will denote, henceforth, the full solution
by
P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)
to make the dependence on the two parameters explicit.

Hence,
using the time-dependent part
f_{r}(t)
in Eq. (
19
)
and the space-dependent part
\phi_{\lambda}(x)
in Eq. (
28
), we can express, for any
t
and any
x\geq 0
,
the full solution
P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)
in the form of
the following exact spectral decomposition

P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)=x^{\alpha+1/2}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,B_{1}(\lambda)\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)\,,

(29)

where

\nu=\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\,.

(30)

The only unknown quantity is the spectral density
B_{1}(\lambda)
which is determined from the initial condition as follows.
We assume that initially the particle is located at
x=0
.
Since the solution
P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)
, with this initial condition is symmetric in
x
, we must have

\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)dx=1/2
. In particular, at
t=0
and focusing only on
x\geq 0
, we have

P_{r,\alpha}(x,0)=\frac{1}{2}\,\delta(x)\,.

(31)

We set
t=0
in Eq. (
30
) and use
M(a,b,z=0)=1
. Comparing it to
(
31
) then leads to

x^{\alpha+1/2}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,B_{1}(\lambda)\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,\delta(x)\,.

(32)

To determine
B_{1}(\lambda)
from Eq. (
32
) we proceed as follows. We first multiply both sides of (
32
)
by the factor
\sqrt{x}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)
with
\lambda^{\prime}>0
and integrate over
x\in[0,\infty]
.
On the rhs this integral gives

{\rm rhs}=\frac{1}{2}\,\lim_{x\to 0}\sqrt{x}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,.

(33)

Using the asymptotic behaviour of the Bessel function in Eq. (
25
) we then get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,B_{1}(\lambda)\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{\alpha+1}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\nu^{-\nu}}{\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,\left(\lambda^{\prime}\right)^{-\nu/2}\,,

(34)

where we recall that
\nu=1/(\alpha+2)
. Making the substitution
2\nu x^{1/(2\nu)}=y
and simplifying the integral, we get

\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,B_{1}(\lambda)\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dy\,y\,J_{-\nu}\left(\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,y\right)\,J_{-\nu}\left(\sqrt{\lambda}\,y\right)=\frac{\nu^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,\left(\lambda^{\prime}\right)^{-\nu/2}\,.

(35)

To extract
B_{1}(\lambda)
, we next use the following identity
[
27
]

\int_{0}^{\infty}dy\,y\,J_{m}(ky)\,J_{m}(k^{\prime}\,y)=\frac{1}{k}\,\delta(k-k^{\prime})\,,

(36)

valid for any
m
.
Setting
k=\sqrt{\lambda}
and
m=-\nu
gives

\int_{0}^{\infty}dy\,y\,J_{-\nu}\left(\sqrt{\lambda}\,y\right)\,J_{-\nu}\left(\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,y\right)=2\,\delta\left(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}\right)\,.

(37)

Using this identity in Eq. (
35
) fixes
B_{1}(\lambda)
as

B_{1}(\lambda)=\frac{\nu^{1-\nu}}{2\,\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,\lambda^{-\nu/2}\,\quad{\rm with}\quad\nu=\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\,.

(38)

Plugging this expression for
B_{1}(\lambda)
in Eq. (
30
) gives the
full exact position distribution, valid for all

x\geq 0
(and equivalently for
x<0
by symmetry) and all time
t

\displaystyle P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)=

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{\nu^{1-\nu}}{2\,\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,x^{\alpha+1/2}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,\lambda^{-\nu/2}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\nu\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)\,.

As a useful check, let us first demonstrate that our general exact solution (
39
) valid for arbitrary
r\geq 0
does
reproduce the known solution for
r=0

[
21
]
. To proceed, we first note that for
r=0
, the time-dependent part
of the solution in Eq. (
39
) greatly simplifies since

\lim_{r\to 0}M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)=M\left(1,1,-\lambda t\right)=e^{-\lambda\,t}\,.

(40)

This last identity follows from the definition of
M(a,b,z)
in Eq. (
15
). Hence, in the limit
r\to 0
, our result
(
39
) reduces to

P_{0,\alpha}(x,t)=\frac{\nu^{1-\nu}}{2\,\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,x^{\alpha+1/2}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,\lambda^{-\nu/2}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,e^{-\lambda\,t}\,.

(41)

Amazingly, the integral over
\lambda
can be done explicitly using the identity (Mathematica can perform
this integral explicitly)

\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,\lambda^{-\nu/2}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,e^{-\lambda\,t}=\nu^{-\nu}\,x^{-1/2}\,t^{\nu-1}\,\exp\left(-\frac{\nu^{2}}{t}\,x^{1/\nu}\right)\,,

(42)

valid for any
\nu>0
and
x\geq 0
. Using this identity, we get from Eq. (
41
)

P_{0,\alpha}(x,t)=\frac{\nu^{1-2\nu}}{2\,\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,t^{\nu-1}\,x^{\alpha}\,e^{-\frac{\nu^{2}}{t}\,x^{1/\nu}}\,\quad{\rm where}\quad\nu=\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\,.

(43)

The solution (
43
) can be written in the scaling form

P_{0,\alpha}(x,t)=\frac{1}{t^{\nu}}\,G_{\alpha}\left(\frac{x}{t^{\nu}}\right)\,,

(44)

where the scaling function
G_{\alpha}(z)
has precisely the same expression as given in Eq. (
5
).
This then reproduces the known result in the limit
r=0
.
Indeed, this result for
r=0
was derived
originally in Ref.
[
21
]
by directly substituting the scaling ansatz (
44
) in the Fokker-Planck
equation (
3
) (with
D(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
) and solving the resulting ordinary differential equation for
G_{\alpha}(z)
.
Here, our method using spectral decomposition thus provides an alternative derivation of this result for
r=0
.

Another important check corresponds to choosing
\alpha=0
, i.e.,
\nu=1/2
, where the exact result for

P_{r,0}(x,t)
is known
[
2
]
. Setting
\nu=1/2
in our general result in Eq. (
39
), we get

P_{r,0}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\sqrt{x}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,\lambda^{-1/4}\,J_{-1/2}\left(\sqrt{\lambda}\,x\right)\,M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)\,.

(45)

Setting
\lambda=k^{2}
and using
J_{-1/2}(kx)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi kx}}\,\cos(kx)
gives

P_{r,0}(x,t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\cos(kx)\,M\left(\frac{k^{2}}{r+k^{2}},1,-(r+k^{2})t\right)\,,

(46)

which coincides with the solution found in Ref.
[
2
]
by solving directly the Fokker-Planck equation
in the Fourier space.

Thus, our general result for
P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)
in Eq. (
39
),
valid for arbitrary
r
and
\alpha\geq 0
,
contains both the known limiting results when
r\to 0
and
\alpha\to 0
.
Let us also remark that although here we assumed
\alpha\geq 0
to model
the ‘sluggishness’ of the walker (so that the diffusion coefficient decreases with
increasing
|x|
), the result for the position distribution in Eq. (
39
)
is actually valid for any
\alpha>-1
. For
\alpha<-1
, the solution in Eq. (
39
)
is not normalizable.

2.4
Asymptotic behaviour at late times for general
r
and
\alpha

Let us now derive the asymptotic late time behaviour of the position distribution
P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)
in Eq. (
39
).
At late times, the argument
-(r+\lambda)t
of
M(a,b,-(r+\lambda)\,t)
in Eq. (
39
) becomes a large
negative number. We can then use the following asymptotic behaviour
[
26
]

M(a,b,-z)\approx\frac{\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(b-a)}\,z^{-a}\,\quad{\rm as}\quad z\to\infty\,.

(47)

Consequently,

\displaystyle M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{r}{r+\lambda}\right)}\,\left[(r+\lambda)t\right]^{-\lambda/(r+\lambda)}

(48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{r}{r+\lambda}\right)}\,e^{-\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}\,\ln\left((r+\lambda)t\right)}\,.

We substitute this large
t
behavior in Eq. (
39
),
rescale
\lambda=r\,\tilde{\lambda}/\ln(rt)
and at the same time rescale also

x=\left(\frac{1}{r}\,\ln(rt)\right)^{\nu}\,\tilde{x}
in the integral.
The resulting rescaled integral over
\tilde{\lambda}
(with
\tilde{x}
fixed) can be performed exactly using
the identity (
42
). Taking into account all factors carefully, it is then easy to see that
P_{r}(x,t)
, for large
t

and large
x
, but with the ratio
\tilde{x}=x\,r^{\nu}/\left(\ln(rt)\right)^{\nu}
fixed, approaches the following scaling form

P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)\approx\left(\frac{r}{\ln(rt)}\right)^{\nu}\,G_{\alpha}\left(\frac{r^{\nu}\,x}{\left(\ln(rt)\right)^{\nu}}\right)\,\quad{\rm with}\quad\nu=\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\,,

(49)

where the scaling function
G_{\alpha}(z)
is precisely the one given in Eq. (
5
). Thus the effect of a non-zero resetting
rate
r
is to slow down the dynamics drastically with
x\sim\left(\ln(rt)\right)^{1/(\alpha+2)}
at late times instead of
t^{1/(\alpha+2)}
, but
remarkably the associated scaling function
G_{\alpha}(z)
retains the same form as
r=0
.

3
Generalized moments of the position distribution

The spectral representation of the position distribution in Eq. (
39
) is valid at all
t
and for all
r
and

\alpha\geq 0
. It helped us to derive explicitly the late time scaling behaviour of
P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)
in Eq. (
49
).
However, plotting
P_{r}(x,t)
at finite
t
using the spectral decomposition (
39
) is not easy, since the integral over

\lambda
is difficult to do explicitly. Hence, it would be nice to find an observable that can be explicitly computed at all

t
and compared to numerical simulations. A natural candidate for such an observable is the quantity

\mu_{r,\alpha}(m,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)\,|x|^{m}\,dx=2\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)\,|x|^{m}\,dx\,,

(50)

where we used the symmetry
P_{r,\alpha}(-x,t)=P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)
of the distribution. For
m
an even integer,

\mu_{r,\alpha}(m,t)
is indeed the
m
-th integer moment of the distribution. However,
\mu_{r,\alpha}(m,t)
is well defined
for any
m\geq 0
, not necessarily an integer and we will call
\mu_{r,\alpha}(m,t)
as the generalized
m
-th moment.

Direct computation of
\mu_{r,\alpha}(m,t)
defined by the rhs of (
50
) from the
spectral decomposition (
39
) is however delicate.
This is because, if we multiply both sides of (
39
) by
x^{m}
and then integrate over
x
(after interchanging
the order of integration over
\lambda
and
x
), we encounter a problem: the integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{m+\alpha+1/2}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\nu\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)

(51)

is unfortunately not convergent since the Bessel function decays as
x^{-1/2}
for large
x
(with oscillations) which
makes the integral divergent. This means that we are not allowed to interchange the order of
\lambda
-integration
and
x
-integration. To circumvent this problem, we use a different trick. Let us first multiply both sides
of Eq. (
39
) by the factor
\sqrt{x}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\nu\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)
and
then integrate over
x\in[0,\infty]
. This makes both the
x
and the
\lambda
integration on the rhs
convergent and there is no problem in interchanging the order of integration. This gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)\,\sqrt{x}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\nu\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,dx=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu^{1-\nu}}{2\,\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,\lambda^{-\nu/2}\,M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)\,I(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\,,

(52)

where

I(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{\alpha+1}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\nu\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\nu\sqrt{\lambda}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,.

(53)

Now, we have encountered this integral before in Eq. (
34
). Upon making the change of variable

2\nu x^{1/(2\nu)}=y
and using the identity (
37
), we simply get

I(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\nu}\,\delta(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})\,.

(54)

Substituting this result in (
52
) and carrying out the integral over
\lambda
gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)\,\sqrt{x}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\nu\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,dx=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu^{-\nu}}{2\,\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,(\lambda^{\prime})^{-\nu/2}\,M\left(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{r+\lambda^{\prime}},1,-(r+\lambda^{\prime})t\right)\,.

(55)

Let us now investigate the left hand side (lhs) of Eq. (
55
). We first use the following series expansion
of Bessel function
[
26
]

J_{-\nu}(z)=2^{\nu}\,z^{-\nu}\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}\,z^{2n}}{2^{2n}\,n!\,\Gamma(-\nu+n+1)}\,,

(56)

on the lhs of Eq. (
55
) and then integrate term by term of the sum. This gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)\,\sqrt{x}\,J_{-\nu}\left(2\nu\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,x^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,dx=

\displaystyle\nu^{-\nu}\,(\lambda^{\prime})^{-\nu/2}\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}\,\nu^{2n}}{2\,n!\,\Gamma(-\nu+n+1)}\,(\lambda^{\prime})^{n}\,\mu_{r,\alpha}(n/\nu,t)\,,

(57)

where the generalized moment
\mu_{r,\alpha}(m,t)
for general
m\geq 0
(not necessarily integer) is defined
in Eq. (
50
). Equating this result with the rhs of (
55
) gives

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}\,\nu^{2n}}{n!\,\Gamma(-\nu+n+1)}\,(\lambda^{\prime})^{n}\,\mu_{r,\alpha}(n/\nu,t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,M\left(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{r+\lambda^{\prime}},1,-(r+\lambda^{\prime})t\right)\,.

(58)

Now, the
M
function on the rhs in (
58
) can, in principle, be expanded in a power series in
\lambda^{\prime}
using
the definition in Eq. (
15
) and let us define the coefficients
a_{n}(t)
by

M\left(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{r+\lambda^{\prime}},1,-(r+\lambda^{\prime})t\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(t)\,(\lambda^{\prime})^{n}\,.

(59)

Substituting this on the rhs of (
58
) and matching the coefficients of the identical powers of
\lambda^{\prime}
on both
sides, we get the exact relation

\mu_{r,\alpha}(n/\nu,t)=\frac{(-1)^{n}\,n!\,\Gamma(n+1-\nu)}{\nu^{2n}\,\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,a_{n}(t)\,,

(60)

where
a_{n}(t)
is defined in Eq. (
59
). By using the definition of the Kummer’s function (
15
) and expanding it at first order in
\lambda^{\prime}
, one obtains

M\left(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{r+\lambda^{\prime}},1,-(r+\lambda^{\prime})t\right)=1+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{r}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-rt)^{m}}{m\,m!}+{\cal O}(\lambda^{\prime 2})\,.

(61)

As a first check, we deduce
a_{0}(t)=1
from Eq. (
61
) and setting
n=0
in Eq. (
60
), we get

\mu_{r,\alpha}(0,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{r,\alpha}(x,t)\,dx=1\,,

(62)

that confirms the normalization of the distribution.

A slightly more non-trivial result corresponds to choosing
n=1
in Eq. (
60
). This gives

\mu_{r,\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{\nu},t\right)=-\frac{(1-\nu)}{\nu^{2}}\,a_{1}(t)\,,

(63)

where
a_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-rt)^{m}}{m\,m!}
from the series expansion (
61
).
Indeed, the sum appearing in the coefficient
a_{1}(t)

was computed explicitly in a similar calculation in Ref.
[
2
]
,

a_{1}(t)=-\frac{1}{r}\,\left[E_{1}(rt)+\gamma_{E}+\ln(rt)\right]\,\quad{\rm where}\quad E_{1}(z)=\int_{z}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-x}}{x}\,dx\,,

(64)

and
\gamma_{E}=0.5772\ldots
is the Euler constant.
Substituting
a_{1}(t)
in Eq. (
63
) then gives, using
\nu=1/(\alpha+2)
, the explicit expression valid for any
t
,

\mu_{r,\alpha}(\alpha+2,t)=\frac{(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)}{r}\,\left[E_{1}(rt)+\gamma_{E}+\ln(rt)\right]\,.

(65)

\alpha+2
rescaled by

. The thin lines correspond to simulations with

, a time-step
\Delta t=10^{-5}
and averages performed over

trajectories, see

and follow the evolution predicted by Eq. (

).

Note that for
\alpha=0
, we then recover the result for the variance derived in Ref.
[
2
]
with
D=1
. Figure
2
displays
\mu_{r,\alpha}(\alpha+2,t)/[(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)]
as a function of
rt
for several values of
\alpha
, where the moments are obtained from numerical simulations of many trajectories following the model rules, see
A
for a description of the method. As predicted by Eq. (
65
), the different curves collapse onto a single function independent of
\alpha
and following closely
[E_{1}(rt)+\ln(rt)+\gamma_{E}]/r
.

Finally, let us notice another interesting moment relation in this problem. Consider the position distribution
P_{r,0}(x,t)

for
\alpha=0
given in Eq. (
46
). The Fourier transform of this distribution reads then

\tilde{P}_{r,0}(k,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{r,0}(x,t)\,e^{i\,k\,x}\,dx=M\left(\frac{k^{2}}{r+k^{2}},1,-(r+k^{2})\,t\right)\,.

(66)

Expanding in powers of
k
on the lhs and rhs (using (
59
)) gives

\mu_{r,0}(2n,t)=(-1)^{n}\,(2n)!\,a_{n}(t)\,.

(67)

Eliminating
a_{n}(t)
between Eqs. (
60
) and (
67
) provides an interesting relation

\frac{\mu_{r,\alpha}(n(\alpha+2),t)}{\mu_{r,0}(2n,t)}=\frac{n!\,\Gamma(n+1-\nu)\,(\alpha+2)^{2n}}{(2n)!\,\Gamma(1-\nu)}\,,

(68)

that is totally independent of time
t
and the resetting rate
r
. Of course, one can check easily that for
\alpha=0
, the rhs equals unity, as it should. In Figs.
3
a-b-c, the lhs of Eq. (
68
) obtained from numerical simulations is actually consistent with a constant behaviour in time. This ratio fluctuates around a value which is close to the rhs of Eq. (
68
). The fluctuations arise from the finite number of trajectories taken to compute the moments and become greater at larger
n
.

4
Generalization to higher dimensions

Our main result for the position distribution
P_{r}(x,t)
in Eq. (
39
) for one dimension can be easily extended to higher
dimensions
d\geq 1
. In
d\geq 1
, we choose
D(\vec{x})=D(R)
where
R=|\vec{x}|
in order to preserve the spherical
symmetry. The associated Fokker-Planck equation for the position distribution

P_{r}(\vec{x},t)
reads

\partial_{t}P_{r}(\vec{x},t)=\nabla^{2}\left[D(R)\,P_{r}(\vec{x},t)\right]-r\,P_{r}(\vec{x},t)+\frac{r}{t}\,\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,P_{r}(\vec{x},t^{\prime})\,,

(69)

starting from the initial condition

P_{r}(\vec{x},t=0)=\delta(\vec{x})\,.

(70)

Since
D(R)
depends only on the radial distance
R
from the centre, the solution
P_{r}(\vec{x},t)=P_{r}(R,t)

also depends only on the radial distance. Using this spherical symmetry, Eq. (
69
) then reduces to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P_{r}(R,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{\partial R^{2}}+\frac{(d-1)}{R}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial R}\right]\left[D(R)\,P_{r}(R,t)\right]-r\,P_{r}(R,t)

(71)

\displaystyle+\frac{r}{t}\,\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,P_{r}(R,t^{\prime})\,.

The method of separation of variables works exactly as in
d=1
. Writing,
P_{r}(R,t)=f_{r}(t)\,\phi(R)
, one finds that
the time-dependent part
f_{r}(t)
is exactly the same as in Eq. (
19
) for
d=1
, namely

f_{r}(t)=M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)\,.

(72)

where
M(a,b,z)
is the Kummer’s function. We now choose
D(R)=R^{-\alpha}
The space-dependent part
\phi(R)
depends on
d
as well as
\lambda
.
As in
d=1
, we define

h_{\lambda}(R)=\frac{\phi_{\lambda}(R)}{R^{\alpha}}\,,

(73)

which then satisfies the ordinary differential equation

\frac{{\rm d}^{2}h_{\lambda}(R)}{{\rm d}R^{2}}+\frac{(d-1)}{R}\,\frac{{\rm d}h_{\lambda}(R)}{{\rm d}R}+\lambda\,R^{\alpha}\,h_{\lambda}(R)=0\,.

(74)

Note that the function
h_{\lambda}(R)
implicitly depends on
d
, but for notational simplicity we do not display this explicitly.

The differential equation (
74
) can be solved exactly and the most general solution is given by

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}(R)=B_{1}(\lambda)\,R^{(2-d)/2}\,J_{(d-2)\,\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,R^{1/(2\nu)}\right)

\displaystyle+B_{2}(\lambda)\,R^{(2-d)/2}\,\ J_{-(d-2)\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,R^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,\quad{\rm where}\ \nu=\frac{1}{\alpha+2}\,,

(75)

where
B_{1}(\lambda)
and
B_{2}(\lambda)
are two unknown constants (independent of
R
).
Once again, the constants
B_{1}(\lambda)
and
B_{2}(\lambda)
implicitly depends on
d
, but we do not display
this explicitly for simplicity.
Using the small
z
behaviour
of
J_{\nu}(z)
in Eq. (
25
), one finds that while the term multiplying
B_{1}(\lambda)
in Eq. (
75
)
is perfectly analytic as
R\to 0
, the second term multiplying
B_{2}(\lambda)
is non-analytic as
R\to 0
, and behaves as

R^{2-d}
. Thus the second derivative of
h_{\lambda}(R)
diverges as
R^{-d}
as
R\to 0
, if

B_{2}(\lambda)
is non-zero. Since the second derivative must remain finite
as
R\to 0
[the basic assumption that leads to the differential equation (
74
)], we must have

B_{2}(\lambda)=0
. Taking the product of the space and time-dependent parts and integrating over all possible
\lambda\geq 0
,
we then arrive at the exact spectral decomposition

\displaystyle P_{r,\alpha}(R,t)=

(76)

\displaystyle R^{\alpha+(2-d)/2}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,B_{1}(\lambda)\,J_{(d-2)\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,R^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)\,,

where we have restored the dependence on
\alpha
explicitly in the position distribution by denoting it
by
P_{r,\alpha}(R,t)
.

The spectral density
B_{1}(\lambda)
is again determined from the initial condition (
70
) as in
d=1
. Setting
t=0
in
Eq. (
76
) and using
M(a,b,0)=1
we get

R^{\alpha+(2-d)/2}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,B_{1}(\lambda)\,J_{(d-2)\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,R^{1/(2\nu)}\right)=\delta(\vec{x})\,.

(77)

We now multiply both sides by
R^{d/2}\,J_{(d-2)\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,R^{1/(2\nu)}\right)

and integrate over
R\in[0,\infty]
. This gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,B_{1}(\lambda)\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dR\,R^{\alpha+1}\,J_{(d-2)\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,R^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,J_{(d-2)\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}\,R^{1/(2\nu)}\right)

\displaystyle=\int\delta(\vec{x})\,R^{d/2}\,J_{(d-2)\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,R^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,dR\,.

(78)

The main contribution to the integral on the rhs of (
78
)
comes from the
R\to 0
region. Using the small argument expansion (
25
)
we get

{\rm rhs}=\frac{\nu^{(d-2)\nu}\,\left(\lambda^{\prime}\right)^{(d-2)\nu/2}}{\Gamma\left(1+(d-2)\nu\right)}\,\int\delta(\vec{x})\,R^{d-1}\,dR\,.

(79)

However, the integral
\int\delta(\vec{x})\,R^{d-1}\,dR=1/\Gamma_{d}
where
\Gamma_{d}=2\,\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)
is the
volume of a
d
-dimensional unit sphere. Hence, we finally get the rhs as

{\rm rhs}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\,\pi^{d/2}}\,\frac{\nu^{(d-2)\nu}\,\left(\lambda^{\prime}\right)^{(d-2)\nu/2}}{\Gamma\left(1+(d-2)\nu\right)}\,.

(80)

Similarly, the lhs of Eq. (
78
) can be performed exactly, as in
d=1
, by first making the change of variable

2\,\nu\,R^{1/(2\nu)}=y
and then using the identity
(
37
). This finally determines
B_{1}(\lambda)
explicitly in arbitrary dimension

B_{1}(\lambda)=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\,\pi^{d/2}}\,\frac{\nu^{(d-2)\nu+1}\,\lambda^{(d-2)\nu/2}}{\Gamma\left(1+(d-2)\nu\right)}\,.

(81)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda\,\lambda^{(d-2)\nu/2}\,J_{(d-2)\nu}\left(2\,\nu\,\sqrt{\lambda}\,R^{1/(2\nu)}\right)\,M\left(\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda},1,-(r+\lambda)t\right)\,,

It is easy to check that for

5
Summary and Conclusion

We have studied a sluggish random walk model consisting of a Brownian particle starting from
the origin and whose diffusion coefficient decays algebraically with the distance from this
point, or
D(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
. In addition, the walker uses from time to time (with a rate

r
) a memory rule whereby it resets to positions previously visited in the past. These
revisits are preferential, i.e., the probability to reset to a small region of space is
proportional to the total amount of time spent there up to the present time. This model can
be considered as a simple animal movement model, where an individual is reluctant to move
away from a central place and at the same time exploits known places in its environment.

We have obtained the exact solution of the position distribution of the particle
x(t)
at
all times in arbitrary spatial dimensions and have focused our analysis on the one-dimensional
case, which already captures the main properties. While the model in the absence of memory (
r=0
)
exhibits a subdiffusive behaviour, with
x(t)\sim t^{1/(\alpha+2)}
typically, the combined
effects of the vanishing diffusion coefficient at large distances and of the returns to
frequently visited positions lead to an extremely slow, sub-logarithmic diffusion at late
times, as
x(t)\sim[\ln(rt)]^{1/(\alpha+2)}
. Despite this sluggishness, the walker does
not become localized in space asymptotically: its position distribution keeps depending on
time and tends to a scaling law. The associated scaling function is the same as in the
memory-less case with
r=0
. However, contrary to this latter problem, when
r>0
scaling
does not hold at all times but only asymptotically. Due to the form of
D(x)
, the particle
is repelled from the origin, hence the scaling function is a non-monotonous function of the
distance.

Obtaining the moments of the position, such as the mean square displacement, at all times is
rather difficult in this problem even in one dimension. However, rather interestingly, it is
possible to compute at all times the moment of order
\alpha+2
, i.e.,
\langle|x|^{\alpha+2}\rangle
explicitly in
d=1
. It closely resembles at all times the mean
square displacement of the problem with
\alpha=0
(a diffusion coefficient constant in
space), to which it is simply related by a proportionality factor. Similarly, the moments of
order
n(\alpha+2)
are proportional at all time to those of order
2n
of the problem with

\alpha=0
. These analytical results in
d=1
have been verified via numerical simulations.

It would be interesting to study the localized steady states and localization dynamics of
the model presented here in the presence of a specific resource site, thus extending
previous work with a diffusion coefficient constant in space
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
.

Appendix A
Numerical Simulations

We have generated stochastic trajectories by implementing the following numerical scheme (see also
[
6
]
). Time is discrete,
t=n\Delta t
with
n=0,1,2,\ldots
and
\Delta t\ll 1
a fixed time-step. As
n
increases by one unit, with probability
1-r\Delta t
, the position
x(n\Delta t)\equiv X_{n}
of the particle is updated via the discretized version of the Langevin equation (
2
) in one dimension,

X_{n+1}=X_{n}+\sqrt{2D(X_{n})\Delta t}\,\xi_{n+1}\,,

(85)

where
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n=1,2,\ldots}
are i.i.d. Gaussian random variables with zero mean and unit variance. To avoid a diverging diffusivity near
x=0
and therefore very large increments, we choose

D(x)=\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}+\epsilon^{\alpha}}\,,

(86)

with
\epsilon
a crossover length such that
\epsilon\ll 1
and
\epsilon^{-\alpha}\Delta t\ll 1
. For practical purpose
\Delta t
is fixed to
10^{-5}
, and we choose
\epsilon=10^{-2}
if
1\leq\alpha\leq 2
, while
\epsilon=10^{-4}
if
\alpha=1/2
. We expect the long term dynamics to be independent of the choice of
\epsilon
as soon as it fulfils the above conditions.

With the complementary probability
r\Delta t
, the next position is determined by the memory rule: a time
t^{\prime}
is chosen uniformly in the interval
[0,t]
, i.e., an integer
n^{\prime}
is chosen uniformly among
\{0,1,2,\ldots,n\}
, and the walker is reset to the position it occupied at that time in the past,

X_{n+1}=X_{n^{\prime}}\,.

(87)
