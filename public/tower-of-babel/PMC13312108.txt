Dual modal pathomics model for colorectal cancer early recurrence prediction and mutation landscape analysis
Authors: Wenyu Luo, Runze Liu, Xiaomei Yang, Wendan Si, Jinglei Qu, Ling Xu, Ying Chen, Na Song, Jin Wang, Yu Cheng, Xiujuan Qu, Xiaojiao Guan, Ruichuan Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPatients with stage II/III colorectal cancer remain at substantial risk of early postoperative recurrence, yet accurate risk stratification remains challenging. Here, we developed a dual-modal pathomics model integrating hematoxylin-eosin and Ki-67 whole-slide images to predict early recurrence in 362 patients from two medical centers. Among multiple pretrained feature encoders, the integrated hematoxylin-eosin plus Ki-67 model using the UNI encoder achieved the best performance, with an area under the curve of 0.902 in the external validation cohort. The model consistently stratified patients into distinct prognostic groups across clinical subgroups. Attention map visualization further suggested that high-risk predictions were associated with tumor invasive fronts, whereas low-risk predictions were linked to immune infiltration and fibrotic stromal regions. These findings highlight the potential of multimodal pathology artificial intelligence for clinically interpretable prognostic assessment and personalized postoperative management in colorectal cancer.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC), as the third most prevalent malignant tumor and the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, continues to increase its disease burden and faces multiple challenges in clinical diagnosis and treatment.1 Epidemiological data show that the sex stratified incidence rate and mortality of CRC in the cancer statistics of the United States in 2024 are both among the top five.2 Notably, significant heterogeneity exists in clinical outcomes among patients with stage II-III CRC receiving standard postoperative adjuvant therapy: The 5-year survival rate for high-risk stage II patients is 75–85%, while for stage III patients it is approximately 50–70%.3,4,5,6 These findings highlight the notable limitations of the current TNM staging system and traditional high-risk factors in assessing recurrence risk. Early recurrence (ER), defined as recurrence or metastasis within 1 year after radical surgery, confers a poor prognosis in patients with CRC, with a 5-year survival rate of only 38.2%, significantly lower than the 65.9% of patients with non-ER.7 This not only reflects the essential characteristics of tumor-invasive biological behavior but also highlights the urgent need for precise prediction tools in clinical practice.

Currently, postoperative adjuvant therapy strategies for patients with stage II/III CRC are primarily guided by high-risk factors and microsatellite status, which may not provide adequate information to assess potential treatment benefits. Although these indicators can partially reflect the degree of tumor malignancy, it is difficult to quantify tumor microenvironment (TME) heterogeneity, spatial morphological distribution, and multidimensional interaction characteristics. In recent years, pathology technology driven by artificial intelligence (AI) has enabled automated extraction and quantitative analysis of large-scale pathological features via whole-slide image (WSI) digitization and deep learning algorithms.8,9,10 Among these technologies, the multi-layer perceptron (MLP), with its excellent universal approximation theory, has laid a theoretical foundation for solving complex nonlinear problems and serves as an indispensable core component of deep learning models.11 Previous studies have demonstrated that the combination of pathology and AI algorithms can be utilized to evaluate the prognosis of gastrointestinal diseases such as gastric cancer.12 In the field of CRC, AI-aided pathology has yielded breakthrough model development, with significant advancements in tissue typing, lymph node metastasis prediction, microsatellite status determination, and even evaluation of neoadjuvant therapy response.13,14,15,16,17,18 Notably, a dual-modal model integrating hematoxylin-eosin (HE) morphological features and Ki-67 proliferation markers exhibited superior predictive performance of pathological staging compared to single indicators (area under the curve, AUC: 0.814 vs. 0.703/0.767), validating the clinical value of the dual-modal fusion strategies.18

Based on these advancements, this study aims to construct a pathological model integrating HE with Ki-67-stained WSI data for the prediction of ER in CRC. This model will not only unravel key TME characteristics overlooked by traditional pathology but also provide a quantitative decision-making basis for individualized adjuvant therapy selection and adjustment of postoperative monitoring intensity. Ultimately, the visualization of attention and analysis of mutation profiles enhanced the interpretability of the optimal model.

Results

Clinical and pathological characteristics of patients

A total of 362 patients were enrolled in this study, including 216 (59.7%) males, with a median age of 62 years (interquartile range [IQR], 54–67 years). The flowchart of the enrolled patients is shown in Figure 1. This study included data from 253 patients at the First Hospital of China Medical University from January 2019 to June 2023. They were randomly divided into a training cohort (N = 178) and an internal validation group (N = 75) at a ratio of 7:3. In addition, an external cohort includes 109 patients who underwent radical surgery from January 2019 to June 2023 at Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University. The baseline characteristics of the study population stratified by the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts are presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed in the distribution of age, gender, T stage, N stage, venous emboli and/or lymphatic invasion, perineural invasion, TNM stage, Ki-67 protein expression, and ER status among the three cohorts (all p > 0.05). Construction and comparison of prediction models

The multivariate regression analysis revealed that N stage (p < 0.001) and perineural invasion (p = 0.012) were significantly associated with ER. For these two clinical indicators (N stage and perineural invasion) with p < 0.05 in multivariate analysis, a linear regression clinical prediction model was constructed, as shown in Table S1. ResNet/UNI/CONCH were used to perform transfer learning on patches of HE and Ki-67 slides, constructing models for HE, Ki-67, and HE + Ki-67, respectively. The overall architecture of the integrated HE + Ki-67 model is shown in Figure S1. The performance evaluation of the single-modal HE and Ki-67 model is presented in Table S2. The predictive performance of the integrated HE + Ki-67 model based on CONCH/ResNet/UNI is shown in Table 2. The integrated HE + Ki-67 model based on UNI achieved the best performance, with AUC of 0.949 (95% CI: 0.898–0.986), 0.934 (95% CI: 0.840–0.993), and 0.902 (95% CI: 0.820–0.964) in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively (Figure 2A). Pairwise DeLong test comparisons for the integrated HE + Ki-67 models using different encoders and cohorts were illustrated in Figure S2. No significant differences were observed between the ResNet/UNI/CONCH HE + Ki-67 models established through the three encoders, suggesting that the optimal model is not susceptible to variations in feature extraction methods. The integrated HE + Ki-67 model was significantly superior to the Clinical model across all cohorts (DeLong test for AUC: adjusted p < 0.05). The accuracy (training: 95.5%, internal validation: 94.7%, external validation: 87.2%), specificity (training: 97.0%, internal validation: 96.8%, external validation: 90.9%), and PPV (training: 91.1%, internal validation: 84.6%, external validation: 65.2%) of the integrated HE + Ki-67 model using UNI as patch encoder are superior to those of other models (Table 2). Therefore, we selected this model as the optimal model for the following analysis (hereinafter referred to as the integrated HE + Ki-67 model). Clinical utility and prognosis value of the integrated HE + Ki-67 model

The integrated HE + Ki-67 model exhibited the highest net benefit in all three cohorts (Figure 2B). The precision-recall (PR) curve is presented in Figure 3, and its performance trend is consistent with that of the ROC curves, achieving the highest area under the PR curve (AUPRC) across all cohorts. The calibration curve indicated a high degree of consistency between the integrated HE + Ki-67 model’s predicted probability and the true ER status (Figure S3). Based on the four optimal cutoffs for different clinical scenarios, the prediction performance of the integrated HE + Ki-67 model remained robust. Under the DCA-optimized threshold, the model achieved specificity of 96.8% in the internal validation cohort and 90.9% in the external validation cohort, with high accuracy (94.7% and 88.1%, respectively; Table S4). The predictive ability of the integrated HE + Ki-67 model in different subgroups is shown in Figures 4 and S4. In the T4 subgroup, the AUC of the integrated HE + Ki-67 model reached 0.972 (Figure S4C). Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups with a cutoff value of 0.5. The integrated HE + Ki-67 model effectively discriminated the DFS differences between two groups of patients, whether in the training (HR = 24.16, 95% CI: 13.11–44.52, p < 0.01), internal validation (HR = 8.59, 95% CI: 3.87–19.04, p < 0.01), or external validation cohorts (HR = 5.86, 95% CI: 2.90–11.82, p < 0.01, Figure 5). The integrated HE + Ki-67 model exhibited superior discriminatory ability compared with the other five clinical-pathological factors. As visualized in the sankey diagrams, these plots intuitively demonstrated the associations between the five factors and ER status (Figures 6G and 6H). Among stage II patients with at least one conventional risk factor (T4 and/or invasion present) but without an ER event, the integrated HE + Ki-67 model reclassified 96.8% (90/93) into the low-risk group. Conversely, among stage II patients without conventional risk factors but with an ER event, 77.8% (7/9) were reclassified into the high-risk group. In stage III patients without an ER event, 94.4% (117/124) were reclassified as low risk by the integrated model.

Pathological interpretability and the mutation landscape of patients with CRC

To improve the interpretability of the integrated HE + Ki-67 model, we mapped the attention values onto corresponding patches of the WSI (Figure 7A). Patient PA101017 (DFS = 2 months) was accurately classified into the high-risk group for ER by the integrated HE + Ki-67 model. The attention heatmap showed that high-attention regions were concentrated in the highly aggressive tumor front defined as the outermost area where solid tumor tissue contacts and interacts with adjacent normal tissues and stroma, and serves as the “front line” for tumor invasion and infiltration into surrounding tissues.19 Patient PA101160 (DFS not reached; follow-up duration: 32 months) was accurately classified into the low-risk group for ER by the integrated HE + Ki-67 model. This patient presented with malignant transformation of a tubular adenoma, which is a pathological type with inherently lower malignancy. To characterize the somatic mutation landscape of the cohort, next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed on resected tumor tissue using a panel of 425 cancer-related genes (Table S5). The mutation landscape of all 14 patients is visualized in Figure 7B. A total of 601 somatic mutations were identified, including single-nucleotide variations (SNVs), small insertions/deletions (Indels), and copy number variations (CNVs). The most frequently mutated genes were TP53 (mutated in all 14 patients), adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) (N = 13, 92.9%), and methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) (N = 9, 64.3%). The main type of mutation observed was a missense mutation, with TP53 missense mutations being particularly common. Notably, the Oncoplot highlighted potential differences in the mutation landscape between patients at high risk of ER (N = 7) and those at low risk (N = 7). For instance, the MTHFR gene was mutated in 85.7% (6/7) of the high-risk group, compared to 42.9% (3/7) in the low-risk group. The dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPYD) gene was mutated in 71.4% (5/7) of the high-risk group, compared with 28.6% (2/7) in the low-risk group.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically explored risk stratification strategies for postoperative ER in stage II/III CRC by constructing a dual-modal prediction model based on the digital pathological images of HE and Ki-67. Furthermore, the attention heatmap highlights histopathologically meaningful regions driving predictive decisions, which aligns with the principles of pathological assessment. Additionally, exploration of the mutation landscape revealed tentative links between genetic alterations and model-predicted ER risk.

The integration of pathomics and multiple instance learning (MIL) has demonstrated significant advantages in digital pathology, with its core being the efficient processing of sub-pixel level WSI. Notably, only weakly supervised slice-level labels are required for model training, significantly reducing the cost of manual annotation. Meanwhile, attention mechanisms enable the automatic identification of key regions, thereby transforming the black-box decision-making process of the model into interpretable feature distributions. At the application level, this technology has been widely used for cancer diagnosis and prognosis prediction.21,22 To our knowledge, few studies have evaluated the association of a pathomics model based on the digital pathological images of HE and Ki-67 with ER in stage II/III CRC. In this study, three distinct pre-trained pathological foundation models, namely ResNet50, UNI, and CONCH, were utilized as encoders to extract features from HE and Ki-67 WSIs of each patient. The UNI-based integrated HE + Ki-67 model exhibited the optimal predictive performance, achieving AUCs of 0.949, 0.934, and 0.902 in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively. This integrated HE + Ki-67 model achieved the highest ACC, sensitivity, and PPV among all cohorts. Stratified bootstrap analysis demonstrated its robust performance across validation cohorts. Therefore, this model can identify patients with high-risk ER who truly require enhanced intervention, enabling more proactive treatment and monitoring to improve their survival outcomes. Meanwhile, patients with low ER risk can avoid the harm and unnecessary consumption of medical resources caused by overtreatment, while alleviating the psychological burden. The advantages of dual-modal fusion in this study also highlight the unique value of Ki-67 as a cell proliferation marker for predicting ER. A recent study constructed a pathological model to predict the grading of 385 gastrointestinal and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors. The grading accuracy of the HE-based classification framework reached 77.5%, and the accuracy was further improved to 83.0% when combined with Ki-67 markers for classification.23 In addition, another study also reported that Ki-67 has predictive value for postoperative pathological staging of CRC.18 Although Ki-67 protein expression failed to serve as an independent predictor of ER status in the univariate analysis, the integrated HE + Ki-67 model did exhibit performance improvements compared to both the Clinical model and single-modality HE model. Critically, the integrated HE + Ki-67 model demonstrated robust risk stratification capabilities. As evidenced by Kaplan-Meier survival analyses, model-stratified high-risk patients exhibited significantly poorer DFS than low-risk patients across all cohorts. This predictive power remained consistent across key clinical subgroups defined by T stage, N stage, and conventional risk factors, reinforcing its potential utility in diverse patient populations. The integrated HE + Ki-67 model’s patient re-stratification capability carries significant clinical implications, particularly for stage II CRC. Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend adjuvant chemotherapy for stage III patients, while treatment decisions for stage II patients heavily rely on traditional risk factors (e.g., T4, obstruction, perforation, poorly differentiated histology, venous emboli and/or lymphatic invasion, perineural invasion, examination of <12 lymph nodes, and positive surgical margins). The integrated HE + Ki-67 model identified a subset of stage II patients who were stratified as high-risk despite the absence of these traditional high-risk factors. The DFS of predicted low-risk patients in stage II was significantly higher than that of predicted high-risk patients. Similar results were obtained for stage II patients who have venous emboli/lymphatic invasion/perineural invasion, or without any invasion. The DFS of predicted low-risk patients in stage III was also significantly higher than that of predicted high-risk patients. This critical finding underscores the integrated HE + Ki-67 model’s potential to overcome the limitations of conventional TNM staging and traditional high-risk definitions, providing a biologically driven paradigm for precision adjuvant therapy decision-making. Identifying these “hidden high-risk” stage II patients, who might otherwise be undertreated, could significantly impact their outcomes.

Notably, the attention heatmap and preliminary genomic analysis provided valuable insights into potential biological underpinnings of the model’s predictions. The attention mapping-based interpretability enhancement of the integrated HE + Ki-67 model validates the model’s predictive rationality by linking its decision-making to biologically meaningful pathological regions (e.g., invasive tumor front, immune infiltration, and fibrotic areas). Distinct mutation profiles (e.g., a higher prevalence of MTHFR and DPYD mutations in the high-risk group) were observed between model-predicted high-risk and low-risk patients, alongside ubiquitous mutations in key CRC driver genes (e.g., TP53 and APC). These findings are consistent with previous research on CRC-related genes. MTHFR mutations may influence cancer cell proliferation through the modulation of local folate metabolism.24 The DPYD mutation is significantly associated with cancer metastasis and chemotherapy toxicity.25,26 These results suggest the associations between the digital pathology images and molecular mechanisms, which warrant further investigation in larger cohorts to elucidate the molecular correlates of the AI-derived risk signatures.

In conclusion, the findings of this multi-center study suggest that an AI-based dual-modal pathomics model, which integrates HE and Ki-67 WSIs with UNI as the feature extractor, exhibits superior performance in predicting ER risk of stage II/III CRC relative to conventional clinical-pathological assessment. Besides, visualization of the attention heatmap and analysis of the mutation landscape in CRC radical resection specimens identified potential associations between TME and mutation profiles and model-predicted ER risk groups, thereby strengthening the biological interpretability of our pathomics model and laying a foundation for future translational investigations.

Limitations of the study

Although the research results have clinical implications, our limitations still need to be carefully considered. Firstly, although this study is based on the retrospective collection of multicenter data, potential selection bias cannot be completely avoided. Secondly, the sample size is relatively limited and primarily consists of the Chinese population. The universality of the results requires validation in larger-scale studies with broader populations. Thirdly, the causal relationship between pathological histology and prognostic mechanisms needs further validation at the molecular level.27,28 Fourthly, future studies addressing tissue deformation and section misalignment, incorporating tumor region segmentation and cell-level quantification, will be required. Finally, the predictive performance for long-term clinical outcomes should be further validated.

Experimental model and study participant details

This retrospective study included 362 patients with stage II/III colorectal cancer who underwent radical surgery. The cohort included 216 males and 146 females, with a median age of 62 years (IQR, 54–67 years).

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Committee of the First Hospital of China Medical University (Approval No. 2025-786). Owing to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Sex was recorded as a biological variable and reported in the baseline clinic-pathological characteristics of the study cohorts (Table 1). Sex-specific analyses were not performed because the primary aim of this study was to evaluate pathology image-derived recurrence risk prediction rather than sex-associated biological differences.

Method details

Study design

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Committee of the First Hospital of China Medical University (Approval No.2025-786). The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study. The inclusion criteria for CRC patients in this study were as follows: (1) undertaking radical resection of colorectal cancer, with postoperative clinical stage II/III; (2) availability of pathological tissue sections with paired HE staining and Ki-67 staining; (3) having completed at least one year of follow-up after surgery; (4) having basic clinical and pathological characteristic data. We excluded patients who had received neoadjuvant therapy before surgery or had a history of multiple primary cancers. Baseline clinical data, including age, sex, venous emboli and/or lymphatic invasion, perineural invasion, depth of invasion (T stage), lymph node metastasis (N stage), and disease free survival (DFS), were collected.

Digital pathological image acquisition

The process of model construction is shown in the Figure S1. Firstly, two gastrointestinal pathologists who had no knowledge of the patient’s basic information and recurrence status conducted preliminary examinations on all HE-stained sections of each patient. They re-evaluated the TNM staging based on the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging criteria and selected the most representative infiltration depth in each slice for subsequent analysis. Then, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples corresponding to representative glass slides were subjected to consecutive sectioning at a thickness of 4 μm. These consecutive sections were separately processed for HE staining and immunohistochemical staining with the Ki-67 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, CST, catalog number 12202) at a dilution ratio of 1:400. Quality control is carried out by the director of the pathology department. If the stained-glass slide has fragments, overlaps, is missing, or is contaminated, it needs to be replicated and rechecked. Slice scanning is performed using an Aperio ScanScope scanner (Leica Biosystems) with a magnification of 400x. The obtained digital pathological images are stored in SVS format. Then, these WSIs were divided into small blocks of 1024 × 1024 pixels and color standardized using the Macenko method for further analysis.29 This study strictly adhered to a patient-level stratification strategy, with all samples grouped by patient ID to ensure that all WSIs and corresponding patches from the same patient were uniquely assigned to the training cohort, internal validation cohort, or external validation cohort, with no cross-set allocation.

Pathomics feature extraction and model construction

WSIs were divided into patches (instances), with image-level labels indicating recurrence status (negative: non early recurrent, positive: early recurrent), while instance-level labels remained unannotated. The model integrates instance dependencies and spatial information to improve classification accuracy. Each patch was processed via three pre-trained encoders (ResNet50, UNI, and CONCH) to extract high-level features, followed by a fully connected layer to generate instance-specific scores.30,31 All three encoders were used in their pre-trained state without fine-tuning to avoid overfitting on the present data and to leverage their general feature extraction capabilities learned from large-scale datasets. We employed a weighted aggregation strategy, incorporating positional encoding (to capture spatial relationships) and cosine similarity-based weighting to emphasize diagnostically relevant regions. As shown in Figure S1, we developed a pathology-based prediction model for ER of CRC using a multiple instance learning (MIL) framework.32 Aggregated features were passed through an MLP with a sigmoid activation function to generate the final image-level prediction.33 Integrated models were generated based on early fusion at the feature level. Spatial dependencies between patches were encoded using a grid-based positional system, enhancing contextual analysis. In addition, instance contributions were dynamically adjusted via similarity-based weights, improving robustness to noise.

For a given pathology image, it is assumed that each image can be considered as a whole, containing multiple instances, where each instance corresponds to a patch of the image. The task is to predict the binary classification label Yi ∈ {0,1} for each image, where the image-level label Yi is known, but instance-level labels are unavailable. Specifically, let {xi,1,xi,2, …,xi,n}represent all instances in the i-th image, and Yi be the label of the image. In the binary classification MIL framework, the image-level label Yi isYi={0,1,if∑yi,j=0yi,j∈{0,1},j=1…notherwise

Here, Yi,j ∈ {0,1} represents the unknown instance-level labels. The model predicts the image-level label Yi using a scoring function Yi = S(Xi). The scoring function S aggregates the information from multiple instances within the WSI and outputs the final prediction for the entire image.

Each patch xi,j is passed through a pre-trained encoder. To evaluate the impact of different feature representations on the performance of the models, this study employed ResNet50 and two state-of-the-art pathology foundation models, UNI and CONCH, as feature extractors. We encoded non-overlapping image patches extracted from WSIs, mapping them into low-dimensional, high-level feature vectors. UNI is a general-purpose foundation model for pathology image analysis developed by the Mahmood Lab.34 Architecturally, UNI is built upon a Vision Transformer backbone. To capture a broad spectrum of histomorphological features, UNI was pre-trained on the Massive Open Pathology dataset. UNI employs the DINOv2 self-supervised learning framework. Through a synergy of Knowledge Distillation and Masked Image Modeling, the model is capable of learning highly generalizable histopathological visual representations from unlabeled data. In our experiments, we utilized the 1024-dimensional feature vectors output by UNI as inputs for the downstream MIL network. In contrast to UNI, which relies solely on visual information, CONCH functions as a vision-language foundation model. It is designed to leverage rich textual knowledge from pathology reports and textbooks to enhance the semantic alignment of visual features.35 CONCH utilizes the Contrastive Captioners architecture, integrating a ViT-B-based image encoder with a text decoder. For this study, we extracted the 512-dimensional visual feature vectors generated by CONCH for subsequent analysis. The output goes through a fully connected layer to generate a score for each instance, reflecting its relevance to the overall classification.

After calculating instance scores, we perform weighted aggregation of instance scores. LetS(Xi)=∑jwi,j∗xi,jwhere wi,j is the weight computed based on the similarity between instances, and ∑jwi,j=1.

The aggregated instance scores S(Xi) are passed through MLP: Yi=σ(MLP(S(Xi)))

where σ is the activation function (usually sigmoid). The output of MLP is mapped to a probability between 0 and 1. We selected a threshold of 0.5 to classify patients into high and low ER risk groups.

Evaluation of the effectiveness of models

The model performance was compared through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall (PR) curves. Calibration curves were used to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed probabilities of early recurrence. We employed decision curve analysis (DCA) to assess clinical application value. The prediction performance of the optimal model based on the selected cutoffs for different clinical scenarios was also evaluated. DFS differences between high-risk and low-risk patients were evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared via the log rank test. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) of the model-predicted risk score for DFS. Sankey diagrams were used to compare the predictive ability of the model versus clinicopathological factors for ER risk.

Visualization and interpretability of models

To validate the model’s decision-making process and provide clinically relevant insights, we employ an attention-based visualization technique. Since the proposed MIL framework relies on a weighted aggregation of instance features to form the slide-level representation, the attention scores directly reflect the contribution of each patch to the final classification.

For a test WSI Xi consisting of n patches {xi,1,xi,2, …,xi,n}, the trained model outputs a corresponding set of attention weights Ai = {wi,1,wi,2, …,wi,n}. These weights indicate the importance of each patch.

Specifically, within the TransMIL architecture, a learnable class token (CLS) is appended to the sequence of patch embeddings to aggregate global information. The combined sequence, enhanced with conditional positional encodings, is processed through stacked Transformer layers employing Nyström attention, which reduces computational complexity while capturing long-range dependencies. To interpret the model’s focus, we extract the attention weights from the final Transformer block, which represent the learned relationships between the global context and local instances.

The raw attention scores are obtained by isolating the specific attention row corresponding to the learnable class token’s interaction with all other instance tokens in the last layer. This vector quantifies the semantic affinity between the global slide representation and each local patch, effectively highlighting regions.

To generate the visualization heatmap, we first normalize the raw attention weights to the range [0,1] using Min-Max scaling to ensure contrast and comparability:wi,jˆ=wi,j−min(Ai)max(Ai)−min(Ai)

Next, each normalized score wi,jˆ is mapped back to the spatial coordinates (uj,vj) of the j-th patch in the original WSI grid. We generate a probability map by assigning these scores to their respective spatial locations. This map is then smoothed and converted into a heatmap using a color map, where “hot” colors (red) represent high-attention regions and “cold” colors (blue) represent low-attention regions.

Finally, the heatmap is upsampled to the original resolution and overlaid on the WSI. This visualization allows pathologists to verify whether the model focuses on histologically significant areas, thereby enhancing the interpretability and trust in the prediction system.

Next-generation sequencing

To characterize the somatic mutation landscape of the patients with different risk of ER, the next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed on tumor tissue using a commercially available 425-gene panel (Geneseeq Technology Inc., Nanjing, China). The specific procedure can be found in DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gendis.2024.101245.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed at the patient level. The exact sample sizes (N) for each analysis are provided in the results, figure legends, and tables; N represents the number of patients unless otherwise specified. For pooled data, continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Baseline clinicopathological characteristics were compared among cohorts. Age and Ki-67 protein expression were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, T stage was compared using Fisher’s exact test, and other categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

Model discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and precision-recall (PR) curves. Areas under the ROC curve (AUCs) and 95% CIs were calculated using DeLong’s method. Pairwise comparisons of AUCs were performed using DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves. Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment was applied within each cohort. Areas under the PR curve (AUPRCs) were calculated from patient-level predicted probabilities. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1 score were calculated using prespecified probability cutoffs.

Calibration was evaluated by comparing predicted and observed early recurrence (ER) probabilities using calibration curves. Hosmer-Lemeshow statistics and Brier scores were calculated to summarize calibration performance. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA) across threshold probabilities. For scenario-specific evaluation of the optimal integrated HE + Ki-67 model, thresholds were derived in the training cohort and then applied unchanged to the validation cohorts. The DCA-optimized threshold was selected by maximizing net benefit in the training cohort; the Youden-optimized threshold was selected by maximizing sensitivity + specificity - 1. Additional high-sensitivity and high-specificity thresholds were evaluated according to prespecified target criteria. Model robustness in the validation cohorts was assessed using stratified bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations and replacement, preserving the original ER class distribution in each resampled dataset; 95% CIs were estimated using percentile intervals.

Disease-free survival (DFS) was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival differences between model-predicted high-risk and low-risk groups were compared using the log rank test. Patients were classified as high risk or low risk using a predicted probability cutoff of 0.5 unless otherwise specified. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression. The continuous model-predicted risk score was also evaluated in univariable Cox regression, with HRs reported per 0.1 increase in predicted risk score.

All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise stated. No formal sample size calculation was performed because this was a retrospective multicenter study including all eligible patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period.

Analyses and model training were performed using R software version 4.3.3 and Python scripts with standard scientific computing and deep learning packages, including readxl, pROC, PRROC, survival, survminer, ggplot2, rmda, pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, PyTorch, and PyTorch Lightning.
