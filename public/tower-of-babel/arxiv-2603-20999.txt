OrbitStream: Training-Free Adaptive 360° Video Streaming via Semantic Potential Fields
Aizierjiang Aiersilan Zhangfei Yang

Abstract
. Adaptive 360° video streaming for teleoperation faces dual challenges: viewport prediction under uncertain gaze patterns and bitrate adaptation over volatile wireless channels. While data-driven and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) methods achieve high Quality of Experience (QoE), their "black-box" nature and reliance on training data can limit deployment in safety-critical systems. To address this, we propose OrbitStream, a training-free framework that combines semantic scene understanding with robust control theory. We formulate viewport prediction as a Gravitational Viewport Prediction (GVP) problem, where semantic objects generate potential fields that attract user gaze. Furthermore, we employ a Saturation-Based Proportional-Derivative (PD) Controller for buffer regulation. On object-rich teleoperation traces, OrbitStream achieves a 94.7% zero-shot viewport prediction accuracy without user-specific profiling, approaching trajectory-extrapolation baselines ( ∼ \sim 98.5%). Across 3,600 Monte Carlo simulations on diverse network traces, OrbitStream yields a mean QoE of 2.71. It ranks second among 12 evaluated algorithms, close to the top-performing BOLA-E (2.80) while outperforming FastMPC (1.84). The system exhibits an average decision latency of 1.01 ms with minimal rebuffering events. By providing competitive QoE with interpretability and zero training overhead, OrbitStream demonstrates that physics-based control, combined with semantic modeling, offers a practical solution for 360° streaming in teleoperation.

Adaptive Bitrate Streaming

Traditional adaptive bitrate (ABR) algorithms tackle quality adaptation through varying strategies

33
)
.
Rule-based methods
, including buffer-based
(
10
;
32
)
and rate-based approaches
(
38
;
22

Optimization-based approaches
, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC)
(
42

43

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)

(
14

and Fugu
(
40

Semantic-aware predictors

Distinction of OrbitStream.

OrbitStream

3.
System Model

3.1.
End-to-End Architecture

We consider a real-time teleoperation loop comprising four subsystems. A
360° Camera
captures the remote environment at 4K resolution (30 fps). An
Edge Encoding Server
performs object detection and HEVC encoding
(
34
)
, partitioning frames into spatial tiles (grid size
N\times M
). A
Wireless Downlink
transmits these tiles to the
Operator Terminal
, where a viewport-adaptive player renders the content on a Head-Mounted Display (HMD).

3.2.
Video and Semantic Representation

The video is temporally segmented into chunks of duration
T_{s}=2
seconds. Spatially, the equirectangular frame is divided into an
8\times 4
tile grid (32 total tiles)
(
31
)
. The equivalent full-projection video targets
K=6
global bitrate tiers
\mathcal{R}=\{1.2,2.5,5.0,10.0,20.0,40.1\}
Mbps. The actual per-tile bitrate is inherently fractionalized from these global budgets based on spatial area and content complexity.

The system maintains a real-time
Semantic Scene State
. For every frame, a set of
L
objects
\{O_{1},\dots,O_{L}\}
is detected, simulated via YOLOv5 inference traces
(
11
;
27
)
. Each object
O_{\ell}
is defined by its
Position

(\theta_{\ell},\phi_{\ell})
in spherical coordinates, and its
Semantic Mass

M_{\ell}\in[0,1]
. This scalar weight encodes task-relevance based on vulnerability-based priority ordering. Specifically, pedestrians receive the highest mass (
M=1.0
) reflecting extreme collision risk, followed by vehicles (
M=0.8
) and traffic signs (
M=0.75
). Background elements receive low mass (
M<0.2
). This task-driven weighting maps directly into the gravitational predictor.

3.3.
Channel and Buffer Dynamics

The client playback buffer
B(t)
(measured in seconds of video) evolves via a continuous fluid model parameterized by network delivery and continuous video playout:

(1)

\frac{dB(t)}{dt}=\frac{C(t)}{R(t)}-\mathbf{1}_{\{B(t)>0\}}

where
C(t)
is the available channel capacity,
R(t)
is the aggregate selected chunk bitrate, and the indicator function
\mathbf{1}_{\{B(t)>0\}}
formally captures playback stalling during buffer underflow. A
Rebuffering Event
occurs if
B(t)=0
and content is unavailable, critically halting control feedback. We assume a noisy capacity estimator
\hat{C}(t)
with a bounded prediction error
|\hat{C}(t)-C(t)|\leq\epsilon_{C}
, where we empirically cap
\epsilon_{C}=2.0
Mbps.

3.4.
QoE Objective Formulation

We adopt a generalized linear QoE model tailored for safety-critical tasks
(
42
;
14
)
:

(2)

\text{QoE}=\sum_{t}\Big[\underbrace{u(r_{t})}_{\text{Quality}}-\underbrace{\mu\cdot T_{\text{stall}}(t)}_{\text{Reliability}}-\underbrace{\lambda\cdot|r_{t}-r_{t-1}|}_{\text{Smoothness}}-\underbrace{\nu\cdot E_{\text{view}}(t)}_{\text{Accuracy}}\Big]

The model features four components. The
Utility

u(r_{t})=\log(r_{t}/r_{\min})
(with
r_{\min}=1.2
Mbps) follows the Weber-Fechner law of diminishing returns. We assign a
Stall Penalty

\mu=10
to reflect the negative impact of video freezing. A
Smoothness
penalty
\lambda=0.5
curtails visual flickering to help mitigate operator fatigue. Lastly, the
Viewport Error
penalty relies on
E_{\text{view}}(t)=1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{t}|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{V}_{t}}\frac{q_{k}}{q_{\max}}
, capturing the normalized quality deprivation within the specific tiles comprising the user’s actual rendered viewport
\mathcal{V}_{t}
. This computes the risk of missing visual details due to prediction inaccuracies.

4.
Gravitational Viewport Prediction

Potential Field Formulation.
The GVP model treats visual attention as a test particle moving on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
under forces generated by semantic objects. This draws inspiration from biological visual foraging models
(
23
;
28
;
30
)
. Each object
O_{\ell}
with semantic mass
M_{\ell}
at position
(\theta_{\ell},\phi_{\ell})
creates an attractive potential well. The total attention potential at an arbitrary coordinate
(\theta,\phi)
evaluates to:

(3)

U(\theta,\phi)=-G\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{M_{\ell}}{d(\theta,\phi,\theta_{\ell},\phi_{\ell})+\delta}

where
G=1.0
is a saliency weighting factor,
\delta=1.0
averts singular division precisely at object overlap, and
d(\cdot)
signifies the haversine angular distance properly accounting for spherical geometry:

(4)

\begin{split}d(\theta_{1},\phi_{1},\theta_{2},\phi_{2})&=2\arcsin\Bigg(\Bigg[\sin^{2}\Big(\frac{\phi_{2}-\phi_{1}}{2}\Big)\\
&\quad+\cos\phi_{1}\cos\phi_{2}\sin^{2}\Big(\frac{\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}}{2}\Big)\Bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg)\end{split}

The haversine formulation supports gradient computations across polar boundaries where Euclidean approximations may fail
(
2
)
. This continuously shifting potential landscape guides predicted gaze. As detailed in
section

7
, this methodology achieves a 94.7% viewport prediction accuracy on our teleoperation traces.

Viewing Probability Distribution.
Operator attention to a tile
k
centered at
(\theta_{k},\phi_{k})
is modeled by a Boltzmann distribution over the field
(
17
)
:

(5)

P_{k}=\frac{\exp(-\beta U_{k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{NM}\exp(-\beta U_{j})}

where
\beta=1/T_{\text{att}}
signifies inverse temperature (
T_{\text{att}}=2.0
, yielding
\beta=0.5
). While the continuous SDE below models the operator’s central gaze progression
\vec{g}(t)
, this Boltzmann distribution independently captures the spatial uncertainty around the field. The potential gradient directs centralized visual locus shifting, while the Boltzmann map outlines spatial bandwidth allocations scaled by
\beta
.

Gaze Dynamics and Evolution.
Gaze progression is dictated by a Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) resolving deterministic potentials and exploratory noise:

(6)

d\vec{g}(t)=-\nabla_{\vec{g}}U(\vec{g}(t))dt+\sigma dW(t)

where
\vec{g}(t)
denotes the unit gaze vector,
\nabla_{\vec{g}}U
is the spherical gradient, and
dW(t)
mimics spontaneous ocular saccades modeled as Brownian motion with volatility
\sigma=0.05
. Discretization uses the Euler-Maruyama method encompassing momentum to model eye inertia:

(7)

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\gamma\vec{v}_{t}-\eta\nabla_{\vec{g}}U(\vec{g}_{t})+\sqrt{2\eta\sigma}\vec{\xi}_{t}

(8)

\displaystyle\vec{g}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\text{normalize}(\vec{g}_{t}+\Delta t\cdot\vec{v}_{t+1})

with
\gamma=0.8
governing smooth pursuit decay,
\eta=0.1
directing descent responsiveness, and
\vec{\xi}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
acting as standard Gaussian noise. The
\text{normalize}(\cdot)
operation projects the vector back onto the unit sphere surface
\mathbb{S}^{2}
to preserve geometric constraints.

Convergence Logic.
Eliminating momentum and noise (
\gamma,\sigma=0
) simplifies the system to gradient descent yielding Lyapunov convergence (
dU/dt=-\|\nabla U\|^{2}\leq 0
) into local minima. Conversely, introducing realistic parameters allows for momentary overshoot or peripheral surveillance, supporting context awareness.

1
visualizes this dynamic landscape.

5.
PD Controller with Tanh Saturation

Control Law Formulation.
The rate controller targets a stable buffer state
B_{\text{ref}}=4.0
s. Defining the error
e(t)=B(t)-B_{\text{ref}}
, we employ a Proportional-Derivative (PD) law to formulate a dimensionless control signal
u(t)

(
1
)
:

(9)

u(t)=K_{p}e(t)+K_{d}\frac{de(t)}{dt}

where
K_{p}=0.5
provides correction and
K_{d}=0.2
provides damping to help suppress oscillation.

Saturation-Based Rate Selection.
To support continuous adaptability under channel volatility, we map the unbounded
u(t)
into a bounded bandwidth scaling factor via a hyperbolic tangent constraint:

(10)

R^{*}(t)=\hat{C}(t)\cdot\left(1+\tanh(u(t))\right)\cdot\rho

where
\hat{C}(t)
is the estimated capacity and
\rho=0.9
ensures a safety headroom. Because
1+\tanh(u)
scales within
(0,2)
, the preliminary target
R^{*}(t)
can exceed
\hat{C}(t)
. Thus, we apply a hard saturation constraint
R(t)=\min(R^{*}(t),\hat{C}(t))
. The continuous modulation helps bridge operational regimes. When
e(t)\to 0
,
R^{*}(t)\approx\hat{C}(t)\rho
, managing connection utility. As the buffer depletes (
e(t)<0
),
u(t)
becomes negative, lowering the bitrate request
R^{*}(t)
toward zero to encourage buffer replenishment. Conversely, during buffer surplus (
e(t)>0
), the requested rate scales up to utilize available capacity. This nonlinearity aims to reduce "bang-bang" fluctuations often seen in threshold-based rule-based methods (see
fig.

2
).

Stability Analysis.
To outline closed-loop tracking stability, we perform a Lyapunov analysis
(
13
)
. Defining the positive definite candidate
V(e)=\frac{1}{2}e(t)^{2}
, the time derivative under active playback (
B(t)>0
) relies on
\dot{e}(t)=\dot{B}(t)=\frac{C(t)}{R(t)}-1
. Thus:

(11)

\dot{V}=e(t)\dot{e}(t)=e(t)\left(\frac{C(t)}{R(t)}-1\right)

Embedding the saturation constraint
R(t)=\min(R^{*}(t),\hat{C}(t))
and sector bounding the
\tanh(u)
function
(
13
)
, the control input guides the system against underflow: when
e(t)<0
(buffer deficit), the map explicitly restricts
R(t)<C(t)
(provided estimation error
\epsilon_{C}
is bounded by safety margin
\rho
), making the quantity
\frac{C(t)}{R(t)}-1>0
, thereby contributing to
\dot{V}<0
. When
e(t)>0
(buffer surplus), the controller scales
R(t)
to match the available capacity limit
\hat{C}(t)
. This pushes
\frac{C(t)}{R(t)}\to 1
, helping to regulate buffer growth. This establishes practical boundedness within a neighborhood of
B_{\text{ref}}
, parameterized by the channel estimation error
\epsilon_{C}
.

Tuning Strategy.
Gains
K_{p},K_{d}
are calibrated leveraging a modified Ziegler-Nichols methodology
(
47
)
. Deriving the critical oscillation threshold (
K_{cr}
, period
T_{cr}
), we parameterize
K_{p}=0.6K_{cr}
to prioritize stability, supplemented by
K_{d}=K_{p}\cdot T_{cr}/8
for damping logic.

Tile Quality Allocation.
We allocate the budget

6.
Implementation

The
OrbitStream
framework is implemented as a modular control loop (
algorithm

1
) executing once per video chunk. The architecture decouples perception tasks from control logic to support low-latency operation.

6.1.
Perception-Control Decoupling

The framework operates in two concurrent stages.
Perception
runs on an edge-co-located server, executing object detection on the incoming 360° stream to extract bounding boxes and class labels (incurring
\sim
12.8 ms latency per frame on an NVIDIA T4).
Control
executes locally at the Operator Terminal, using object metadata to update the semantic potential field, simulate gaze dynamics, and compute the PD rate allocation.

6.2.

O(L\cdot N\cdot M)
for
L

N\times M
tiles. For typical scenarios (
L\approx 8

To improve efficiency, we optimize sections by
Vectorizing
distance/potential arrays using BLAS/LAPACK backends, and leveraging a tailored
Haversine Distance Computation
for spherical angular evaluations. These optimizations bring the average control loop execution time to
1.01\text{ ms}
(measured on an Intel Core i7), making the overhead small compared to standard network RTT or frame intervals.

7.4.
Result 3: Real-Time Feasibility

Minimizing feedback latency is an essential requirement for effective robotic control. Benchmarking computational overhead (
table

3
) demonstrates that rule-based pipelines execute nearly instantaneously (
\sim 3\,\mu\mathrm{s}
), while numerical optimization routines like MPC incur slightly elevated, but still very low, costs (
\sim 13
–
19\,\mu\mathrm{s}
).

In contrast,
OrbitStream
logs an average control-loop decision time of roughly
1.01\,\mathrm{ms}
. This represents a multi-order-of-magnitude increase in computational overhead compared to the evaluated baselines. This added latency is a direct consequence of evaluating dense semantic potential fields and propagating Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs) over spherical coordinates at each step. By explicitly sacrificing microsecond-level computational agility,
OrbitStream
trades execution speed for semantic awareness and training-free interpretability.

While
1.01\,\mathrm{ms}
remains comparatively large against baseline algorithms, it still fits within soft real-time constraints for most teleoperation environments. For instance, it remains well below typical visual rendering intervals (
\approx 33\,\mathrm{ms}
at 30 fps) and standard wireless Round-Trip Times (RTTs of
20
–
50\,\mathrm{ms}
). Nevertheless, this computational penalty is a clear limitation that must be carefully considered before deploying on severely resource-constrained edge hardware.

Flattening the semantic mass assignments (

proved sensitive. Elevating

The Stability-Efficiency Trade-off.
As shown in

While DRL agents can sometimes overfit specialized training distributions,
OrbitStream
does not require prior training data. The physics-based modeling parameters provide stable behavior across broadband and mobile domains without retraining. 8.
Conclusion

We proposed
OrbitStream
, an adaptive streaming framework tailored for 360° teleoperation. By formulating viewport prediction as test-particle dynamics within semantic potential fields, alongside a PD rate controller, we provide a control logic alternative to deep learning paradigms.
Empirical evaluations demonstrate
OrbitStream
’s capability to deliver competitive QoE (2.71, secondary to BOLA-E) alongside buffer stability (
\sigma_{B}=0.42
s) and semantic-aware viewport prediction (94.7% accuracy). By showing that interpretable, physics-based control can operate within viable soft real-time margins (1.01 ms) while maintaining competitive quality and stability,
OrbitStream
provides a practical methodology for teleoperation systems where analyzability is beneficial.

8.1.
Limitations & Future Work

A primary limitation of the proposed approach is its marked computational overhead relative to conventional rule-based or MPC algorithms. As noted in the evaluation, computing the potential fields and evaluating stochastic differential equations elevates control latency to roughly
1.01\,\mathrm{ms}
. While this is generally acceptable for
30
-fps rendering loops, it poses a direct bottleneck for deployment on highly resource-constrained micro-controllers that require microsecond-level efficiencies.

Furthermore, our framework relies heavily on the accuracy of upstream object detection. Because it operates strictly downstream of the vision models, any detection failures—caused by occlusion, poor lighting, or environmental noise—will inherently cascade into the control loop and degrade viewport prediction accuracy. Lastly, assuming a static semantic mass hierarchy restricts flexibility; it prevents the system from autonomously adapting to specialized, user-specific teleoperation behaviors that might prioritize different scene elements over time.

Future efforts will explore: (1) tuning semantic weights from operator corrections, (2) stress-testing the architecture against severely occluded detector pipelines, and (3) migrating the stability constraints into sampled-data equivalents to tighten computational boundaries.
