Easy and versatile preparation of 68Ga- and Al18F-labelled arylphosphonium salts from a common precursor: stability and preliminary in vivo PET/CT evaluation
Authors: Joseph A Ioppolo, Leila R Hill, Alexander W E Sadler, Amit Hetsron, Laurence Morandeau, John Doan, Kenneth K C Hong, Andrew T King, Tzong-Tyng Hung, Carl Power, Louis M Rendina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe primary aim of this work was to develop and optimise a facile synthesis of radiolabelled arylphosphonium compounds [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 from the common macrocyclic precursor 1-(4-(((triphenylphosphonium)yl)methyl)benzyl)-4,7-bis(carboxymethyl)-1,4,7-triazacyclononane acetate (1). A series of reaction conditions, including temperature, time, ethanol content and reagent concentration were tested to arrive at optimised conditions for labelling with either 68Ga or Al18F. Preclinical evaluations including stability in formulation and in serum, and preliminary imaging in healthy wild type C57BL/6 mice were completed.ResultsThe target compounds [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 could be prepared in near quantitative yields using the optimised conditions. Although the stability of both tracers was acceptable in formulation containing 10% ethanol and 0.5 mg/mL sodium ascorbate, and the stability of [18F]3 in serum was ideal, the stability of [68Ga]2 in serum was sub-optimal. Dynamic PET/CT imaging in mice over 60 min revealed no uptake in bone, rapid renal excretion and more prolonged uptake in the liver and intestine for [18F]3 compared to [68Ga]2.ConclusionThe simple synthesis of [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 from a common precursor was achieved and was easily translatable. Although the utility of [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 for myocardium or cancer imaging is limited, this successful labelling strategy may be applied to a library of other compounds for use in imaging other targets in the body.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41181-026-00466-8.Keywords: Gallium-68, Fluorine-18, Aluminium fluoride, PET imaging, Phosphonium salt

Background

The rapid increase in demand for positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) using [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 (gallium-68 gozetotide) has been driven not only by this tracer’s great utility for imaging prostate cancer (Chow et al. 2023; Clore and Scott 2024; Roberts et al. 2025) and its application in theranostics (Fakiri et al. 2021) but also by a straightforward production process that can be replicated at most PET imaging sites without need of an on-site cyclotron (Lenzo et al. 2018). That is, elution of the 68Ga radiometal from a 68Ge/68Ga generator followed by addition of a precursor in buffered solution, similar to the process used to prepare many technetium-99m tracers. Currently, “cold-kits” for [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 are commercially available from Telix (Illuccix and Gozellix) and Novartis (Locametz) (Hennrich et al. 2025). The supply of generators can be vulnerable to disruptions in manufacturing and transportation (Ballinger 2010; Krijger et al. 2013; Sadri et al. 2021; Small et al. 2013; Thomas and Maddahi 2010; World Nuclear News 2018; Scully 2019), however gallium-68 can also be supplied locally from medical cyclotrons that are normally used to prepared fluorine-18 (Clore and Scott 2024; Ashhar et al. 2022). Commercial cold-kits for fluorine-18 tracers are not yet available, but this has been a target for nuclear medicine researchers over the years (McBride et al. 2009; Wan et al. 2013).

Fluorine-18 is routinely prepared on a cyclotron via the 18O(p,n)18F reaction and is supplied as fluoride ion in oxygen-18 enriched water ([18O]H2O). The subsequent process involving formation of a carbon–fluorine bond (such as in the Hamacher process for preparing [18F]FDG (Hamacher et al. 1986)) requires a significant portion of synthesis time to first remove all traces of water from nucleophilic [18F]fluoride ion. The subsequent reaction steps (in basic, anhydrous solution and at high temperatures) and acid/base deprotection of protecting groups are unacceptably harsh for radiofluorination of bioactive molecules, such as peptides, proteins, and oligonucleotides (Jacobson et al. 2015). The development of new fluorine-18 tracers is an intensive process (Sharma and Aboagye 2011; Hicks et al. 2006; Mukherjee et al. 1995). Significant time and resources are required for creation of methods tailored to each novel tracer, particularly for those prepared using the conventional route outlined above. For these reasons, the more amenable routes utilising the water-compatible single-step incorporation of fluorine-18 using an aluminium-, boron- or silicon-fluoride bond have been explored (Bernard-Gauthier et al. 2018). Some notable examples are [18F]Alfatide, which was the first aluminium-fluoride labelled tracer to be studied in humans (Wan et al. 2013), [18F]SiFAlinate (Lindner et al. 2020) which can be prepared from kits currently available from ABX (Germany), and [18F]AlF-FAPI-74 (Lindner et al. 2021), a fluoride-18 analogue developed to help meet the increasing demand for [68Ga]Ga-FAPI tracers used in imaging various cancers and other diseases.

A common precursor for incorporating either gallium-68 or fluorine-18 would allow even greater flexibility for researchers. The chelated [GaF]2+ modality (either as [68Ga][GaF]2+ or [18F][GaF]2+) has been explored, and applied to tracers that could be used for Alzheimer’s disease imaging using PET (Koay et al. 2025; Bhalla et al. 2014, 2015). This is a particularly attractive goal as in this case both labelled options are chemically identical. However, there have been Ga-F bond stability issues at physiological pH which have limited this strategy for imaging (Koay et al. 2025; Bhalla et al. 2015). Alternatively, targeting vectors incorporating a suitable macrocycle can be used to chelate either [68Ga]Ga3+ or [18F][AlF]2+ ion. This route has been demonstrated for efficiently preparing labelled [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-74 and [18F]AlF-FAPI-74 from the common FAPI-74 precursor (Giesel et al. 2021). Further, the tracer [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-74 has been recently prepared from the same precursor (Penawou et al. 2026). The preparation of radio-labelled lipophilic phosphonium salts for use in myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) and cancer imaging is another area that has typically required multi-step syntheses (Kim and Min 2016; Zielonka et al. 2017). A key example is [18F]FBnTP (Lin et al. 2024; Madar et al. 2006; Waldmann et al. 2018; Werner et al. 2019). Though a single-step labelling was recently presented, this does require preliminary steps to dry the [18F]fluoride and a final purification step by semi-preparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Lin et al. 2024). In our hands, the single step 18F-labelling of a phosphonium salt containing a C2B10H11 closo-carborane cage was unworkable and required the preparation of a prosthetic group, followed by semi-preparative HPLC purification (Ioppolo et al. 2017). This multi-step route precluded translation to a secondary site for preclinical imaging studies, particularly when short-lived radioisotopes were being considered.

The principal goal of this study was to achieve the facile single-step synthesis, optimisation, and evaluation of representative labelled arylphosphonium compounds [68Ga]2 (by complexation with generator-sourced [68Ga]GaCl3 in 0.1 M HCl) and [18F]3 (using an Al18F strategy using cyclotron-sourced [18F]fluoride in water), using a common precursor 1-(4-(((triphenylphosphonium)yl)methyl)benzyl)-4,7-bis(carboxymethyl)-1,4,7-triazacyclononane acetate 1 (Fig. 1). A further aim of this study was to explore the feasibility of a general and versatile 68Ga/Al18F labelling strategy for arylphosphonium salts so that it may be applied to a library of other similar compounds. Both [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 were evaluated for stability in formulation and in serum. A preclinical in vivo PET/CT imaging study in a small cohort of mice was performed to study and compare their biodistributions over 60 min. A secondary aim of this study was to identify any significant uptake in the heart myocardium, a characteristic of radiolabelled arylphosphonium tracers.

Methods

General

The arylphosphonium precursor ligand 1 containing the NODA (1,4-bis(t-butoxycarbonylmethyl)-1,4,7-triazacyclononane) macrocycle, and non-radioactive standards [natGa]2 and [19F]3 were prepared at the University of Sydney. Briefly, the synthesis of ligand 1 was achieved using a convergent modular synthesis whereby the cationic arylphosphonium-linker species and the NODA macrocycle were first prepared separately and then coupled together prior to final deprotection and purification. The synthesis of [natGa]2 was adapted from the method of Kim et al. (2012). The synthesis of [19F]3 was adapted from the method of D’Souza et al. (2011). In both cases the acetate salts were isolated by semi-preparative HPLC. LogP determinations were completed using an analytical HPLC method as described previously (Wilkinson et al. 2014; Hall et al. 2021; Morrison et al. 2014a). The precursor 1 was labelled with [68Ga]Ga3+ and [18F]AlF2+ ([68Ga]2 and ([18F]3, respectively) at the Radiopharmaceutical Production and Development (RAPID) Laboratory, Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital, a partner with the Western Australian National Imaging Facility Node (WA NIF). The effects of reaction time, temperature and acetate buffer pH, as well as the amount and molar ratio of precursors on the yield of the products, were investigated in detail. The optimised radiochemistry was then translated to the University of New South Wales (UNSW) Biological Resources Imaging Laboratory (BRIL), a NIF Node partnered with the Prince of Wales Hospital (POWH) in Sydney. Here, the reactions were scaled up to higher activities, and small animal imaging in healthy wild type C57BL/6 mice was performed. Detailed non-radioactive synthesis, characterisation data, general radiolabelling and quality control methods are presented in the Supplementary Information.

Optimised manual 68Ga labelling method

The preparation of [68Ga]2 proceeded according to the scheme in Fig. 1. In a typical optimised small-scale synthesis, [68Ga]GaCl3 was eluted from an Eckert & Ziegler IGG100 68Ge/68Ga generator using hydrochloric acid (0.1 M, 5 mL). The generator was fractionally eluted, with the first 2 mL discarded, the second 2 mL reserved, and the last 1 mL discarded. From the reserved 2 mL 68Ga stock, 50 µL (ca. 16 MBq) was transferred to a clean low protein binding 2 mL microcentrifuge tube containing a mixture of sodium acetate solution (0.25 M, 70 μL) and precursor 1 solution (150 μM in water, 10 μL, 1.5 nmol). The tube was closed, gently shaken to ensure mixing, and placed in a heating block (Eppendorf) set at 95 °C for 10 min. The tube was removed from the heating block and allowed to cool. The crude material was tested by TLC before purification (radiochemical conversion (RCC) > 95%, n = 5). For purification, a μC18 cartridge (ORTG) fitted with silicone tubing and a Luer connector was preconditioned with ethanol (3 mL) and water for injection (5 mL). The solution was then drawn up into a syringe containing water (1 mL), then passed gently through the conditioned μC18 cartridge. The product retained on the cartridge was washed with acetate buffer (pH 5, 1 mL) before eluting off with ethanol (0.05 mL) and diluting with 0.9% saline (0.45 mL). The product was then filtered through a sterile PVDF filter (Merck Millex-GV, 13 mm) to give 5–7 MBq [68Ga]2 in ca. 0.5 mL 10% v/v ethanol in 0.9% saline formulation (n = 5, 73 ± 2% decay corrected, 35–40 min, tR(HPLC-Method1) 7.8 min, RCPHPLC-Method1 > 99%, RCPTLC-Method1 > 99%). Analytical HPLC and TLC data are shown in Fig. 4. Further testing confirmed acceptable pH (6, by pH strip) and ethanol concentration (< 100,000 ppm by GC-FID). Characterisation data and experimental details for each optimisation experiment are given in Fig. S1-6 and Table S1.

Automated 68Ga labelling method on the TRASIS AllinOne

The automated synthesis of [68Ga]2 was performed using a TRASIS AllinOne synthesiser at POWH, using hardware consumables from TRASIS, with in-house reagents and procedures adopted from a general protocol for 68Ga research tracers. In a typical synthesis, two Eckert & Ziegler IGG100 68Ge/68Ga generators were simultaneously eluted using hydrochloric acid (0.1 M, 5 mL each) and the [68Ga]Ga3+ was trapped on a Chromafix cation exchange cartridge (1.2–1.4 GBq). The 68Ga was then eluted off as [68Ga]GaCl3 with acidified 4.4 M sodium chloride solution into a reactor vessel charged with sodium acetate solution (1.5 M, 3.0 mL), ascorbic acid solution (44 mg/mL, 40 μL) and precursor 1 solution (1500 µM in water, 20 μL, 30 nmol). The mixture was heated at 95–100 °C for 10 min. The reactor was diluted with saline for injection (4 mL) and trapped on an OASIS® PRIME-HLB cartridge (10 mg). The cartridge was further washed with 10 mL of saline for injection to remove any unlabelled [68Ga]Ga3+. The automation sequence was then stopped. The cartridge was removed and manually eluted with ethanol (100 μL) into a clean plastic microcentrifuge tube and diluted with 0.9% saline (900 μL) and ascorbic acid solution (44 mg/mL, 11 μL) to give 574–875 MBq of [68Ga]2 in 1 mL 10% v/v ethanol + 0.5 mg/mL ascorbic acid in 0.9% saline formulation (n = 5, 43–55% ndc over 19–42 min, tR(HPLC-Method2) = 7.2 min, RCPHPLC-Method2 > 95%, RCPTLC-Method1 > 95%). Representative HPLC traces are given in Fig. S7-8.

Optimised manual 18F labelling procedure

The preparation of [18F]3 proceeded according to the scheme in Fig. 1. For preparation of a concentrated [18F]fluoride stock solution, a μQMA cartridge fitted with silicone tubing and a Luer connector was first pre-conditioned by gentle elution with 0.9% saline (2 mL), then TraceSELECT water (5 mL) before drying with air. For small scale reactions, target wash activity was collected immediately following a routine cyclotron 18F production (18O(p,n)18F). This water (2 mL) containing the residual 18F was transferred directly to a shielded hotcell under argon gas pressure (2 bar), through the conditioned μQMA (in a shielded container) and into a vented collection vial. The μQMA containing the trapped 18F activity was then retrieved and the activity eluted into a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube with 0.9% saline (0.2 mL), yielding a ca. 10 GBq/mL stock solution. For larger scale reactions at UNSW, raw [18F]fluoride in water was obtained in a glass vial. A portion of this was drawn up by syringe and purified on a μQMA as above to give a ca. 10 GBq/mL stock solution.

In a typical optimised small-scale synthesis, a portion of the collected [18F]fluoride stock (5 μL, 9–17 MBq) was transferred to a clean low protein binding plastic 2 mL microcentrifuge tube containing a mixture of precursor solution 1 (1500 µM in water, 10 μL, 15 nmol), AlCl3 solution (2000 μM in water, 7.5 μL, 15 nmol), acetate buffer (0.2 M, pH 3.5, 25 μL) and ethanol (145 μL). The tube was closed, gently shaken to ensure mixing, and placed in a heating block (Eppendorf) set at 95 °C for 20 min. The tube was removed from the heating block and allowed to cool. The crude material was tested by TLC before purification (RCC > 95%, n = 5). For purification, a μC18 cartridge (ORTG) fitted with silicone tubing and a Luer connector was preconditioned with ethanol (3 mL) and water for injection (5 mL). The crude reaction solution was then drawn up into a syringe containing acetate buffer for dilution (0.2 M, pH 4, 10 mL), then passed gently through the conditioned μC18 cartridge. The product retained on the cartridge was washed with acetate buffer (0.2 M, pH 5, 1 mL) before eluting off with ethanol (0.05 mL) and diluting with 0.9% saline (0.45 mL). The product was then taken up in a fresh syringe and filtered through a sterile PVDF filter (Merck Millex-GV, 13 mm) into a clean tube to give 6–11 MBq [18F]3 in ca. 0.5 mL 10% v/v ethanol in 0.9% saline formulation (n = 5, 69 ± 2% decay corrected, 40–45 min, tR(HPLC-Method1) 9.7 min, RCPHPLC-Method1 > 99%, RCPTLC-Method2 > 99%). Analytical HPLC and TLC data are shown in Fig. 4. Further testing confirmed acceptable pH (6, by pH strip) and ethanol concentration (< 100,000 ppm by GC-FID). Characterisation data and experimental details for each optimisation experiment are given in Fig. S9-14 and Table S2.

For the larger scale preparations used for animal imaging, the above procedure was followed with the following modifications: A portion of the collected 18F stock, (80 μL, ca. 1 GBq) was transferred to a 3 mL glass vial (Thermo Scientific Reacti-vial) containing a mixture of precursor solution 1 (1500 µM in water, 80 μL, 120 nmol), AlCl3 solution (2000 μM in water, 60 μL, 120 nmol), acetate buffer (0.2 M, pH 3.2, 60 μL) and ethanol (900 μL). The tube was closed (using a screw-cap PTFE seal), briefly shaken to ensure mixing, and placed in a heating block (Thermo Scientific Reacti-Therm) set at 95 °C for 20 min. Following cooling and testing of the crude by TLC (RCC > 90%), the mixture was diluted in 40 mL 0.9% saline and trapped, washed, eluted and filtered as above, and further stabilised with ascorbic acid solution (44 mg/mL, 6 μL). This process gave 202–294 MBq of [18F]3 in 0.5 mL 10% v/v ethanol + 0.5 mg/mL ascorbic acid in 0.9% saline formulation (n = 3, 21–55% decay corrected, 63–70 min, tR(HPLC-Method3) 4.9 min, RCPHPLC-Method3 > 97%, RCPTLC-Method2 > 97%). Representative HPLC traces are given in Fig. S15-16.

To investigate the hypothesis that some unspecified impurities in the prepared [18F]fluoride stock were impacting on radiochemical conversion, an 18F target wash was obtained immediately after a routine cyclotron run, and was directed into a glass vial without purification. The solution was separated into four equal portions of ca. 0.5 mL. One portion was used neat, without any purification (Stock A). A second portion was purified on a μQMA using the procedure outlined above (Stock B). A third portion was trapped in the same way, then washed with TraceSELECT water prior to eluting with commercial 0.9% saline (Stock C). This was to remove any residual metal ions from the target remaining on the resin. A fourth portion was trapped, washed and eluted as for Stock C, using 0.9% saline prepared in-house from TraceSELECT sodium chloride and TraceSELECT water (Stock D). This was to investigate the possibility of trace impurities in commercial 0.9% saline ampoules. To check if radiochemical conversion varied according to the cyclotron run, another [18F]fluoride stock (Stock E) was prepared using the procedure outlined above, similar to Stock B, on a separate day.

Animal imaging

After the optimised radiochemistry was translated to the UNSW NIF Node, small animal imaging in healthy wild type C57BL/6 mice was completed using a Bruker Si78 microPET/CT instrument. Four mice (2 male and 2 female) were imaged with each tracer—8 total. While under 1–2% isoflurane gas anaesthesia, each mouse was administered with 10–15 MBq of the radiopharmceutical (either [68Ga]2 or [18F]3, by i.v. tail vein injection) at the start of a 60 min dynamic PET imaging acquisition, followed by microCT imaging for PET co-registration and attenuation correction. PET images were reconstructed using Paravision 360 V3.5 and the settings employed were MLEM algorithm, 18 iterations, voxel size of 0.5 mm. Standardised uptake values (SUV) were used to quantify activity uptake (%ID/mL). The resulting data was binned and reconstructed into 60-time frames (60 × 60 s). Three-dimensional regions of interest (ROI) covering the heart (approximating blood-pool activity), bladder (approximating urine activity) and representative portions of the liver, bone (femur), intestine and kidneys, and high intensity injection site voxels were drawn for subsequent data analysis with PMOD V4.4. The mean value in each ROI was used to generate regional time-activity curves. Representative ROIs drawn are presented in Fig. S17.

Results

The preparation and characterisation of precursor 1 and non-radioactive standards [natGa]2 and [19F]3 are described in detail in the Supplementary Information. The logP values for 1, [natGa]2 and [19F]3 at pH 7.4 were experimentally determined to be 1.47 ± 0.01, 1.41 ± 0.01 and 1.42 ± 0.01, respectively (n = 3 for each value).

Manual preparations of [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 were successfully optimised giving > 90% radiochemical conversion, prior to purification. In many cases the radiochemical conversion was > 95%, but due to the small injection volumes required for animal imaging (ca. 150 μL), a cartridge was still required to concentrate the dose and reduce the ethanol content. The radiochemical conversion for [68Ga]2 was optimised at 1 μM precursor concentration, with pH 5.0 acetate buffer, 10 min reaction time and 95 °C reaction temperature. The radiochemical conversion for [18F]3 was optimised at 100 μM precursor and 100 μM Al3+ concentrations, with pH 3.5 acetate buffer, 20 min reaction time and 95 °C reaction temperature. The effects of temperature, time, precursor concentration, acetate buffer pH, ethanol content, the aluminium:precursor ratio and the amount of [18F]fluoride stock used on the radiochemical conversion are presented in Figs. 2 and 3. Radiochemical conversion for each data point was tested in triplicate and measured by TLC.

Using ORTG 10 mg micro-C18 cartridges for purification and 13 mm PVDF syringe filters for terminal filtration of the formulation, the isolated yields for [68Ga]2 were 5–7 MBq (n = 6, 73.0 ± 2.2% decay corrected, 30–35 min) and for [18F]3 were 5–20 MBq (n = 6, 68.8 ± 2.2% decay corrected, 35–40 min). The radio-HPLC and radio-TLC traces confirmed > 95% radiochemical purity (Fig. 4). Radiochemical identity was confirmed by comparison of the retention time in the radio-traces with the retention time of the non-radioactive standards [natGa]2 and [19F]3 in the UV-traces. The radio-HPLC trace for [68Ga]2 showed the presence of a small side-product, presumably either the OH– or OAc– complex as a result of the unsaturated coordination at the metal centre.

The translation of procedures and scaled-up syntheses of [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 at UNSW/POWH were successful. Preparation of [68Ga]2 was developed on the TRASIS AllinOne synthesiser, giving consistently high yields. However, the final steps were performed manually to ensure the final volumes were sufficient for animal injection. The synthesis of [18F]3 using raw [18F]fluoride was also successful in preparation of animal doses.

Stability assays in 0.9% saline formulation were completed (5–10% v/v ethanol, with and without ascorbic acid stabilizer) (Fig. 5). For activity concentrations below ca. 400 MBq/mL both [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 remained > 95% pure after 2 h with the addition of 0.5 mg/mL ascorbic acid to the formulation. In this case the stability was more than sufficient for an initial study in mice. Tracer [18F]3 demonstrated excellent stability in mouse serum at 37 °C (> 99% purity at 2 h, Fig. 6). Tracer [68Ga]2 demonstrated sub-optimal stability in serum after 30 min, though no free [68Ga]Ga3+ ion was detected in the serum supernatant solutions.

Stability of [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 in mouse serum

Dynamic PET/CT imaging of [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 was performed over 60 min (Figs. 7, 8). Both tracers demonstrated primarily urinary excretion. The activity in the liver was greater and more prolonged for [18F]3 than for [68Ga]2 (ca. 10 vs. 5 %ID/mL in liver after 20 min). The activity in the intestine ROI was the most variable of all the ROIs. Activity appeared to transit through the intestine over time, which itself moved naturally over the course of imaging. This made segmenting a representative portion of the intestine quite challenging and the data for this ROI are taken to be indicative only. The key differences in liver and intestine activity between [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 over 5–60 min are evident by comparison of the maximum activity projections (MIPs) presented in Fig. 7, where the 18F-activity in the intestine relative to the bladder was most pronounced (panel (d)). Blood pool activity (approximated by the heart ROI) peaked after the first few minutes and fell dramatically thereafter, revealing minimal blood-background of both tracers after 1 h (Fig. 8). Over time, there was no prolonged activity in the heart ROI, indicating that there may not be persistent uptake in the myocardium. There was also no elevated uptake in the skeleton when imaging with [18F]3 compared to [68Ga]2, demonstrating no measurable loss of [18F]fluoride in vivo over 60 min.

Discussion

Preparation of [68Ga]2 was straightforward, and optimisation of the reaction did not require addition of ethanol adjuvant or any other species for stabilisation during the reaction. Quantitative conversion was obtained with a comparatively lower concentration of precursor than that used for [18F]3, as expected (1 vs. 100 μM precursor for [68Ga]2 and [18F]3, respectively). However, as the NODA macrocycle is more suitably designed for Al18F labelling (5 available donor sites to complex AlF2+, vs. 6 sites required for Ga3+) there is a free coordination site which is taken up by either OAc– or OH– in solution. In either case, the final complex possesses a net + 1 charge, and so this was not a concern. The radio-HPLC trace for [68Ga]2 (Fig. 4) was found to contain a small side-product at tR ~ 10 min, likely either the OAc− or OH− complex. A similar side-product is also observed in the HPLC of purified [natGa]2 standard (prepared as the OAc− complex) when dissolved in water instead of buffer solution, as confirmed by MS analysis of the collected fractions. When [68Ga]2 was prepared, the relative peak areas in the radio-HPLC trace did not change with increased reaction temperature, also suggesting this is dependent on the OAc– concentration in solution. The lability of this ligand is most likely responsible for the apparent instability of the metal complex in mouse serum. In the radio-HPLC trace of the serum supernatant, an additional shoulder peak is observed but no free [68Ga]Ga3+ was detected. It is likely the labile OH–/OAc– ligand exchanges readily with other anionic species in solution, e.g. citrate.

As expected, following previous work for the optimisation of Al18F-labelling of NOTA-octreotide (Laverman et al. 2012), the yield of [18F]3 increased significantly with an increase in temperature and time of reaction. No impact on product integrity was observed in the HPLC data. Near-quantitative yields could be obtained by heating at 95 °C for 20 min, though 15 min was acceptable (Fig. 2b). The yield did increase with the amount of precursor added (keeping the Al3+-precursor ratio constant at 1:1). However, there was no significant benefit to addition of greater than 15 nmol to the reaction (final concentration ca. 100 μM, Fig. 2d). Although previous works have employed an excess of AlCl3 relative to the precursor (Lutje et al. 2019), the data indicate a 1:1 Al3+:precursor ratio was optimal (Fig. 3a). The radiochemical conversion decreased dramatically as excess AlCl3 was added (2–4 molar equiv.), in agreement with previous work using the HBED chelator (Malik et al. 2015). The optimum pH of the buffer in this reaction appeared to be 3.0–3.5, although pH 4.0 gave only a slightly lower yield than for pH 3.5 under the conditions tested (Fig. 2f). An optimal pH no lower than 3 is consistent with the pKa of HF (Ory et al. 2015), below which [18F]fluoride would be present predominantly as [18F]HF and presumably unavailable for reaction. Earlier work suggested the ideal pH for [18F]AlF2+ complexation is 4, as this favours a 1:1 aluminium-fluoride complex (McBride et al. 2013). However, later works have indicated a pH closer to 2 is optimal in practice (Carroll et al. 2024), and this is consistent with the data trend presented in Fig. 2f. The radiochemical conversion for the reaction in this study increased with increasing ethanol concentration, with 75% (v/v) ethanol content giving near-quantitative conversions for the conditions tested (Fig. 3b). This value is appreciably high. Although other co-solvents such as acetonitrile and DMSO could be used in preference to ethanol, they must be separated from the product by an additional step. The impurity specifications for acetonitrile and DMSO are much lower than ethanol due to their higher toxicity threshold. It is possible that by using ethanol co-solvent, “cold-kit” formulations for Al18F-labelled tracers could be available in the future (analogous to those available for 99mTc and 68Ga radiopharmaceuticals), as ethanol is acceptable for i.v. administration following dilution to 10% v/v. However, working with higher concentrations of [18F]fluoride and precursor in necessarily small volumes may be challenging.

Of particular interest in this work was the effect of the amount of [18F]fluoride stock added to the reaction on the radiochemical conversion. In our experience, increasing the scale of Al18F complex reactions can result in a sporadically less efficient conversion, as was observed in our previous efforts to prepare [18F]AlF-PSMA-11 (Ioppolo et al. 2020). Trace non-radioactive impurities in the stock (e.g. [19F]fluoride liberated from plastic tubing like PTFE, other impurities originating from the transfer lines, fittings and valves, or metallic species from the cyclotron target body or windows) may have an effect on the final yields. These impurities can be elevated depending on the age or type of cyclotron target used, the time since the last target service, the age or type of transfer lines used, or the quality of the cyclotron run that produced the activity (Gillies et al. 2006; Kilian et al. 2014; Link et al. 2012). This in turn may be exacerbated by use of “rinse” or “target wash” [18F]fluoride, where the activity concentration would be reasonably lower than in [18O]water from a dedicated run. Interestingly, each of the stock solutions prepared from the same target rinse activity gave similar results for the small-scale reactions (Stocks A-D, Fig. 3c). Stock A, which was used unprocessed, gave similar results to Stocks B-D, which were purified on a QMA. Stock C (prepared with a wash of the QMA cartridge prior to elution with 0.9% saline) resulted in a slightly lower yield for the 20 μL addition than for Stock B (which was not washed prior to elution). Stock E however, which was prepared identically to Stock B on a different day, gave much poorer results. Sporadically low yields for the Al18F reactions are likely related to impurities that are only present in the 18F stock on each occasion. It should be stressed there was no other indication of subpar quality in the bulk [18F]fluoride produced, and the FDG produced from the same run was prepared in excellent yield and passed all quality control tests thereafter. Furthermore, when the optimised parameters were used for the scaled-up syntheses for animal work, the radiochemical conversion was typically > 90% using [18F]fluoride from a dedicated cyclotron run (not target rinse). This suggests that though target rinse [18F]fluoride can be quite safe and useful for low-activity research and development, the [18F]fluoride obtained from a dedicated cyclotron run (and perhaps [18F]NaF injectable product normally used for PET/CT bone scanning) would be more reliable for human imaging using Al18F radiopharmaceuticals.

As shown in Fig. 5, once [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 were formulated in 0.9% saline containing 10% (v/v) ethanol and 0.5 mg/mL ascorbic acid, the radiochemical purity remained high (> 95%) for activity concentrations less than 100 MBq/mL, which is typical for theoretical human doses prepared within a few hours of use. For the higher concentrations required for injection into mice (10–15 MBq in < 200 μL volume, 1–2 h after tracer synthesis), the stability up to 2 h was acceptable as long as the activity concentration at end of synthesis stayed below ca. 400 MBq/mL. To accommodate this, the animal imaging experiments were scheduled alongside the radiosyntheses and all mice were injected promptly after the products were prepared, with quality control testing being completed while the tracer was being transported to the animal imaging facility.

The results of the dynamic imaging experiment and ROI analysis revealed that both [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 were excreted mostly through the urine. Bladder activity for both tracers reached ca. 100 %ID/mL after 20 min and remained at 100–150 %ID/mL for the extent of the imaging period (data not shown on Fig. 8 due to scale). The main difference in excretion was that [18F]3 displayed slightly prolonged liver and intestine uptake. The key molecular difference between [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 is the metal ion, and while both the [68Ga]Ga3+ and [18F]AlF2+ species are retained in the complex in serum, and there was no apparent bone uptake in mice injected with [18F]3, it is possible the labile OH−/OAc− binding site in [68Ga]2 may lead to a more efficient urinary excretion pathway for [68Ga]2 over [18F]3. Alternatively, there is a possibility that [68Ga]2 was metabolised in vivo and then free 68Ga was excreted in the urine, however an in vivo radio-metabolite analysis would be required to investigate this.

Though this dynamic PET/CT imaging experiment was designed more for a comparative biodistribution and not specifically to image myocardium tissue, no prolonged uptake in the heart tissue was evident in the PET data, as may be anticipated for delocalised lipophilic cations (DLCs). DLCs are known to accumulate strongly in the mitochondria of cardiomyocytes and in many cancers. A few explanations are possible for this negative result. First, this is unlikely due to their hydrophilicities alone, as the logP values for each complex (1.41 ± 0.01 and 1.42 ± 0.01 for [natGa]2 and [19F]3, respectively) are similar in lipophilicity to the clinical MPI tracer [99mTc]Tc-sestamibi (logP 1.1) and actually indicate increased lipophilicity relative to 18F-labelled phosphonium salts [18F]FBnTP (logP − 0.38) and [18F]FTPMP (logP 0.91) which also image myocardium effectively (Tominaga et al. 2016; Zeng et al. 2016). The measured logP values for [natGa]2 and [19F]3 are similar to related Gd-DOTA-phosphonium complexes prepared previously in our group which have shown promising uptake in T98G (glioma) cells in vitro (Hall et al. 2021; Morrison et al. 2014a, 2014b). In this cell line, as in many cancers, the mitochondrial membrane potential is elevated relative to non-cancerous cells. However, the uptake of these tracers in vivo depends also on many other factors, including metabolism and excretion pathways that may not be evident in an in vitro assessment. The key reasons proposed here for lack of uptake of [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 in the heart may be related to molar activity and the PET imaging technique used. The apparent molar activities of [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 (ca. 20 GBq/μmol and ca. 2 GBq/μmol, respectively) were not optimised and so there is a possibility of their uptake in mitochondria being blocked by non-radioactive precursor 1, which was not separated from the products. However, at least for tracer [68Ga]2, the apparent molar activity is not too dissimilar to the clinical tracers [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 and [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-TATE (ca. 50 GBq/μmol), which are taken up by receptor-mediated processes highly sensitive to molar activity. Concerning the PET imaging technique, an electrocardiogram (ECG) gated PET imaging study would be optimal for studying uptake in myocardium alone. Distinct tracer uptake in myocardium in a coronal PET image from a gated imaging acquisition would otherwise appear as a lower intensity ‘smear’ of activity in the 3D MIP image when acquired over a continuous acquisition. Using gated imaging there is a small possibility we might have identified a period of very low uptake in the myocardium. However, the dynamic PET/CT study we chose best-suited our primary aim of a comparative biodistribution study, where if there was strong persistent uptake in the heart this could also be identified. Unfortunately, this wasn’t observed. Following a more detailed radiometabolite analysis and re-designing the labelling strategy to maximise apparent molar activity, the use of [68Ga]2 or [18F]3 for imaging T98G tumour xenografts (or in myocardium using gated imaging) may be a subject of a future study.

Conclusions

A simple preparation for arylphosphonium tracers containing 68Ga and 18F has been developed and optimised. The syntheses were easily translated to a second site. Although the stability of both tracers was acceptable in formulation containing 10% ethanol and 0.5 mg/mL sodium ascorbate, and the stability of [18F]3 in serum was ideal, the stability of [68Ga]2 in serum was sub-optimal. Dynamic PET/CT imaging in mice over 60 min revealed no uptake in bone, rapid renal excretion and more prolonged uptake in the liver and intestine for [18F]3 compared to [68Ga]2. Although the utility of [68Ga]2 and [18F]3 for myocardium or cancer imaging may be limited, this versatile 68Ga/Al18F pathway to labelled phosphonium salts may be applied to a library of other compounds. Their application in imaging other targets such as solid tumours will be explored in due course.
