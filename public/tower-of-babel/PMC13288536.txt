Targeted activation of PPARG or AKT1 alleviates liver injury in mice with type 2 diabetes and sepsis by modulating inflammatory and metabolic pathways
Authors: Jie Ma, Xin Qi, Wendi Li, Xiaoli Feng, Junbai Ma, Hongmei Ma, Jia Man, Tian Ma, Tianyu De, Jian Liu, Huifang Li, Jinyu Li, Mei Liu, Shan Li, Jun Gao, Hao Wang, Gang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe simultaneous presence of sepsis and type 2 diabetic mellitus (T2DM) has a synergistic effect on hepatic damage. Although the activation of peroxisome proliferator-activate receptor gamma (PPARG) and AKT serine/threonine kinase 1 (AKT1) have been shown to confer protection alone in isolated models of sepsis or T2DM. The present study was designed to systematically assess the effects of PPARG or AKT1 activation on inflammatory responses and glucose and lipid metabolism as well as intestinal barrier function in a murine model of T2DM and sepsis (T2DM/sepsis), and understand the molecular pathways involved.MethodsMice were treated with either the PPARG agonist pioglitazone or the AKT1 activator SC79 and we then evaluated survival, inflammatory indicators, metabolic changes, tissue pathology, intestinal barrier integrity, and gut microbiota composition. Mechanistic studies focused on the PPARG/AMPK and PI3K/AKT1/mTOR signaling pathways.ResultsActivation of either PPARG or AKT1 was associated with significantly increased survival rates, reduced systemic and hepatic levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, increased the expression of anti-inflammatory mediators, ameliorated the abnormalities of the glucose and lipid metabolism, and partially improved hepatic, pulmonary, and intestinal injury. Mechanistically, PPARG activation mainly activated the PPARG/AMPK pathway and AKT1 activation enhanced the PI3K/AKT1/mTOR pathway. Furthermore, the intestinal barrier integrity and gut microbiota composition was partially improved.ConclusionThese results suggest that targeting PPARG or AKT1 is associated with protection against sepsis-associated liver injury in mice with T2DM by modulating the PPARG/AMPK axis or the PI3K/AKT1/mTOR pathway, respectively, which may collectively contribute to anti-inflammatogenic, metabolic, and gut-barrier protective mechanisms.Keywords: PPARG, AKT1, sepsis, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, inflammation, glucolipid metabolism

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a pervasive chronic metabolic condition which causes organ dysfunction and immune deficiency [1]. In 2023, 11.6% of the nations population of the United States has been diagnosed with DM and for the rest of the world, estimates of prevalence of this effect expect a growing number to about 643 million people by 2030 [2]. Persistently elevated glucose levels promote neutrophil dysfunction and disturb the signaling pathways for cytokines and thus engender a state of immunosuppression [3]. DM is a significant risk factor for sepsis and an independent predictor of increased disease severity and mortality [4,5]. Clinical data has also shown that patients with DM are at an increased rate of incidence of sepsis, increased mortality and extended hospitalizations [6].

Sepsis is a severe disease that arises through a disordered host inflammatory response with the manifestation of multi-organ dysfunction [7]. The condition is linked to high rates of prevalence and mortality and a large burden of healthcare [8,9]. The liver being the principal organ of immunometabolism is especially prone to the pathophysiological evolution of sepsis [10,11]. Clinical investigations have reported hepatic dysfunction in 34%–46% of patients with sepsis with mortality rates of 54%–68% [12,13]. When sepsis occurs in people with pre-existing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) the odds of acute liver failure occurring in the critical first 3 days of hospitalization increase almost twice, correspondingly impacting fatal outcomes [14]. T2DM and sepsis share overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms (excessive inflammation and oxidative stress) that might be synergistic in worsening the progression of the disease [14,15]. Hepatic injury and multi-organ failure, common and serious complications of sepsis, are mainly caused by high levels of important pro-inflammatory cytokines including tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-a, interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-1β [16–19]. Consequently, therapeutic approaches that act concurrently on these two pathological processes, i.e. on the over-production of inflammatory mediators as well as metabolic dysregulation, are urgently needed to reduce liver injury and therefore improve the outcome of patients with T2DM complicated with sepsis (T2DM/sepsis).

Our previous investigation made use of transcriptomic profiling and Mendelian randomization analyses to identify peroxisome proliferator-activate receptor gamma (PPARG) and AKT serine/threonine kinase 1 (AKT1) as pivotal biomarkers for glucose metabolism in sepsis and then investigated their related signaling pathways in a systematic matter [20]. Gene enrichment analysis placed AKT1 within the mTOR signaling pathway, while PPARG was predominantly enriched in AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-related processes (Fig. S1A). Using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), we found AKT1 significantly enriched in upstream phosphoinositide signaling pathways. Conversely, PPARG displayed no direct enrichment in the AMPK pathway (Fig. S1B, C). To date, the exact role of the PPARG/AMPK axis in the setting of sepsis is incompletely delineated. Nevertheless, it is well documented that PPARG and also AMPK are important regulators of energy metabolism in cells. Experimental data show activation of PPARG benefits glucose homeostasis, improves insulin resistance in T2DM [21] and protects against sepsis caused organ failure, especially hepatic injury [22,23]. Similarly, the activation of the pathway of AMPK helps to control metabolic disturbances, decreases the number of complications associated with these disturbances and hinders the damage in the liver caused by sepsis [24–26]. In addition, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling network is critically involved in modulating the processes of insulin sensitivity, hepatic lipid metabolism and attenuation of inflammatory reactions and cellular apoptosis during sepsis [27–30]. However, within the complex pathophysiology of T2DM/sepsis, direct in vivo evidence remains limited regarding whether activation of PPARG or AKT1 exerts hepatoprotective effects through modulation of metabolic-inflammatory signaling pathways.

This study aimed to elucidate the independent roles of PPARG and AKT1 in regulating AMPK- and mTOR-dependent signaling pathways to suppress excessive inflammation, partially improve metabolic homeostasis, preserve intestinal barrier integrity, and ultimately ameliorate liver injury. These findings may provide preclinical evidence to support the development of personalized therapeutic strategies for T2DM/sepsis.

Methods and materials

Animal models

T2DM murine model was developed in accordance with prior research [31,32]. C57BL/6J male mice (4 to 5 weeks) maintained at the Laboratory Animal Centre of Ningxia Medical University, procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. After a 1 week of acclimatization, mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD, D12492, Research Diets, USA) for 6 weeks. Subsequently, streptozotocin (STZ, S6050F, Biotopped, Beijing, China) was administered to produce diabetes mellitus. STZ was freshly dissolved at a concentration of 1% (w/v) in sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5) under dark conditions and maintained on ice until use. Mice were starved for 12 h before to the first injection and received STZ at a dose of 40 mg/kg for 5 consecutive days. Subsequent injections were performed without fasting. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels at 6 h were evaluated after injection on days 3, 10, and 17. To ensure accuracy, food was withdrawn at 9:00 am, and glucose measurements were conducted at 3:00 pm following overnight fasting, as previously reported [33]. T2DM was confirmed by FBG ≥11.1 mmol/L in combination with polydipsia, polyuria, and polyphagia [34].

Sepsis mouse modeling: The standard cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) technique was employed to establish the sepsis model [35–37]. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and maintained at 36–37 °C. The cecum was ligated 1.5 cm from the distal tip and punctured twice with a 21-gauge needle. A small amount of fecal material was extruded before repositioning the cecum into the abdominal cavity. The incision was closed in layers. Postoperative analgesia was provided by infiltration of 0.2% ropivacaine, and fluid resuscitation was performed using pre-warmed saline (50 mL/kg, 37 °C). Sham-operated mice experienced laparotomy and cecal exposure without ligation and puncture. The identical surgeon performed all procedures to ensure uniform surgery duration across all groups. Animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Clinical Medical College of Ningxia Medical University (IACUC–H2025066).

Grouping and intervention for the experiment

Following acclimatization, a total of 110 mice were randomized to one of four experimental groups: Control, Sepsis, PIO+Sepsis, and SC79 + Sepsis (Figure 1A). The T2DM model was successfully established in approximately 90% of mice; animals failing to meet diagnostic criteria were excluded. Control mice underwent sham surgery without CLP. The sepsis group performed CLP alone. The PIO+Sepsis group received of pioglitazone (20 mg/kg, PIO, MedChemExpress). The drug was prepared in a 50% PEG300/50% saline and administered intragastrically once daily for 7 consecutive days starting 1 week before CLP to ensure full activation of PPARG [38,39]. The SC79 + Sepsis group was injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with a single dose of SC79 (10 mg/kg, MedChemExpress) 30 min prior to CLP. SC79 was prepared in the same vehicle to achieve AKT1 activation [40]. To avoid bias, mice were divided into two independent cohorts. The experimental cohort was euthanized 24 h after surgery under anesthesia induced by an i.p. injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital for blood collection and harvesting of pancreatic, hepatic, pulmonary, and colonic tissues. A separate cohort was followed for 7 days post-CLP to assess survival, with no overlap between experimental groups. All experimental procedures (treatments, surgeries) and outcome measurements were conducted in an order that was not systematically linked to group identity, to avoid batch or order effects.

Cytokine and insulin quantification

Serum levels of IL-6, IL-1β, interleukin-10 (IL-10), and TNF-α were quantified using commercial ELISA kits: Mouse TNF-α ELISA Kit (KE10002), Mouse IL-10 ELISA Kit (KE10008) (Biotechnology, Wuhan, China), Mouse IL-6 ELISA Kit (ab222503), and Mouse IL-1β ELISA Kit (ab197742) (Abcam, UK). Serum insulin levels were measured using an Insulin Assay Kit (H203-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng, China). All assays were performed strictly according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Biochemical analyses

FBG levels were measured after a 6 h fast using a handheld glucose meter (ACCU-CHEK, Germany). The Triglyceride Assay Kit (A110-1-1), the High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Assay Kit (A112-1-1), the Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Assay Kit (A113-1-1), the Alanine Aminotransferase Assay Kit (C009-1-1), and the Aspartate Aminotransferase Assay Kit (C010-2-1) (Nanjing Jiancheng, China) were obtained to quantify triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), ALT, and AST levels in mouse serum. All tests were conducted precisely as specified by the manufacturers.

Quantitative reverse-transcription RCR

We used TRNzol Universal RNA Reagent (DP424, Tiangen, Beijing, China) to extract total RNA from mouse liver tissue. A micro-volume spectrophotometer (K5800, Kaiao Technology, Beijing, China) was employed to assess concentration and purity. Total RNA (10 μL) was reverse transcribed into cDNA in a 20 μL reaction using the SweScript All-in-One SuperMix for qPCR (Sikejie Biotechnology, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using 2× Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Pre-mix (AH0105-B, Sikejie Biotechnology, Shandong, China) to measure target gene expression levels, with GAPDH serving as the endogenous control. Table 1 presents the primer sequences for the indicators. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. All experiments were conducted in triplicate using independent biological samples. Specifically, primers for GAPDH, PPARG, and AKT1 were taken from our published study [20], while all others were custom-designed.

Histology and immunofluorescence

To preserve their architecture, diverse tissues including pancreatic, hepatic, lung, and colonic tissues were sourced from mice and treated in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. A gradient ethanol solution was used to dehydrate the tissue sample, followed by xylene for clarification, enabling immersion in paraffin for embedding. Paraffin blocks were sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices using a microtome.

For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, sections were dried at 60 °C for 40 min, deparaffinized, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and stained using an H&E staining kit (HY-K0315, MedChemExpress, USA). Sections were subsequently dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. An optical microscope (APX100, Olympus, Japan) revealed Histopathological changes in the tissue.

The fluorescent staining of colon sections was performed in accordance with established protocols. Briefly, sections were dried at 60 °C for 120 min, then dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. The tissue sections were subsequently incubated in a blocking solution at 37 °C for 1 h. After blocking at 37 °C for 1 h, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against occludin (1:100, sc-133256) and claudin-4 (1:150, sc-376643) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA). The mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, Sections were then incubated with FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:150, GB22301, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (S2110, Solarbio, Wuhan, China) was applied to encapsulate and stain the nuclei of the sections. Fluorescence images were captured using an Olympus APX100 microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Western blot analysis

Mechanical homogenization of liver tissue was performed in lysis buffer supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitors using the Protein Extraction Kit for Liver Tissue (KGB5303-100, KeyGen Biotech, Jiangsu, China). The lysates underwent centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C) to collect the supernatants. The BCA Quantification Kit (KGB2101-50, KeyGen Biotech, Jiangsu, China) was adopted for measuring the protein concentration. Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Separation was performed using SDS-PAGE gels of varying concentrations to achieve optimal resolution based on molecular weight. Then, proteins were blotted onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA) using the wet transfer technique. This established the foundation for the subsequent phase of the analysis. The membrane was gently agitated in TBST buffer containing skimmed milk powder (ST677, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 2 h to completely blocking and incubated with the primary antibody solution at 4 °C overnight. The following morning, a suitably diluted additional antibody was prepared and combined with the cleaned membrane at ambient temperature for 2 h. The membrane was ultimately generated and imaged using an ultra-sensitive ECL chemiluminescent substrate (BMU102-CN, Abbkine Scientific, China) within a gel imaging system. Primary antibodies against p-AKT1 (1:1000; cst4060, CST, USA), AKT1 (AF0836), AMPK (AF6423), p-AMPK (AF3423), PPARG (AF6284) (all 1:1000; Affinity, Changzhou, China), PI3K (bs-10657R), p-PI3K (bs-6417R) (both 1:1000; Bioss, Beijing, China), as well as mTOR (66888-1-Ig) and GAPDH (66009-1-Ig) (1:1000 and 1:5000, respectively; Proteintech, Wuhan, China) were used. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Anti-Mouse, SA00001-1; Anti-Rabbit, SA00001-2; both 1:5000) were from Proteintech Group, Inc.

Metagenomic sequencing and comprehensive analysis of the gut microbiota

Isolation of genomic DNA from fecal samples: The Stool DNA Isolation Kit (Magnetic Bead System) (NGB-63100, Norgen Biotek, Canada) was utilized to extract total genomic DNA from 0.2 g of mouse feces in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s standard operating procedure. A NanoDrop OneC Microvolume UV Spectrophotometer (840–317500, Thermo Scientific, USA) was adopted for the assessment of product concentration and purity. This procedure was executed to ensure compliance with the concentration requirements for the subsequent experiment and to eliminate any potential impurities, such as proteins and phenolic chemicals. Electrophoretic analysis on a 1% agarose gel was performed to verify DNA integrity. This confirmed that the samples were not degraded and were of sufficient quality for subsequent molecular research.

Metagenome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis: The Covaris M220 system (Gene Company Limited, China) successfully facilitated DNA fragmentation to around 350 bp. The NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq technique (Bioo Scientific, USA) was permitted to construct the binomial library. Major Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) provided us with the DNBSEQ-T7 sequencing platform from MGI Tech (Shenzhen, China). The DNBSEQ-T7RS Reagent Kit (FCL PE150) version 3.0 was utilized for metagenomic sequencing, following the guidelines outlined in the operation handbook.

The Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://www.majorbio.com/) offers complimentary online analysis. The Fastp (version 0.20.0) [41] was applied to examine the quality of the initial sequencing data. The adaptor sequences and low-quality reads were eliminated, namely those with read lengths less than 50 bp and quality scores below 20. Subsequently, BWA (version 0.7.17) [42] was implemented for matching the reads to the murine genome reference. All reads that aligned effectively with the host genome were designated as contamination and eliminated to ensure specificity in the target sequence analysis.

We used MEGAHIT (v 1.1.2) [43] to assemble the quality-controlled sequences de novo. The final assembly output was composed of contigs with a minimum length of 300 base pairs. Prodigal (v2.6.3) [44] was used to anticipate open reading frames (ORFs). Genes with nucleotide lengths of 100 bp or greater were retained and converted into corresponding amino acid sequences. The CD-HIT method (v 4.6.1) [45] was adopted for the purpose of clustering sequences, establishing consistency and coverage thresholds at 90% to obtain a non-redundant gene set. Ultimately, SOAPaligner (version soap2.21 release) [46] was chosen to quantify gene abundance. The quantification was conducted on samples using a 95% sequence identity criterion.

Blinding

To minimize assessment bias, blinding was implemented during key outcome evaluations. While the investigators performing the animal modeling, drug administration, and surgeries were necessarily aware of group allocations, all subsequent analyses were conducted by personnel blinded to the treatment groups. Specifically, tissue collection, histological processing and scoring, Western blot imaging, and quantitative densitometric analysis were performed using numerically coded samples. The blinding was maintained until after all data were quantified and compiled for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.2) and the MyGeneCloud web-based analysis platform (https://www.majorbio.com/) were deployed for data analysis and processing. Data conforming to normality assumptions were given to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with Tukey’s post hoc test employed for multiple comparisons. To analyze the longitudinal repeated measures data, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was employed. In this analysis, ‘group’ was designated as the between-subjects factor and ‘time point’ was assigned as the within-subjects factor. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with comparisons between groups assessed by the log-rank test. Depending on the number of groups compared, non-parametric tests were applied to mouse fecal microbiome data: the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (two groups) and the Kruskal-Wallis test (four groups). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to evaluate pairwise associations between gut microbiota composition, inflammatory cytokines, ALT and AST. A two-tailed P value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Activation of PPARG or AKT1 improves survival outcomes and alleviates glucose metabolism abnormalities in mice with T2DM/sepsis

In this study, a type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) model was established using a HFD combined with multiple low-dose intraperitoneal injections of STZ, while sepsis was induced via caecal ligation and puncture (CLP). After 6 weeks of HFD feeding, mice exhibited an elevation in both body weight and 6 h FBG levels. However, no significant differences were observed among the four experimental groups (p > 0.05) (Figure 1B, C). At 17 days post-STZ administration, FBG levels reached ≥11.1 mmol/L and were accompanied by classical diabetic symptoms, including polydipsia, polyuria, and polyphagia. No significant intergroup differences in blood glucose or body weight were detected at this stage (Figure 1D, E), confirming successful and consistent establishment of the T2DM model across all groups. At 24 h post-CLP, mice in the sepsis group exhibited marked weight loss and profound hypoglycemia, with 100% mortality occurring within 72 h (p < 0.05). In contrast, pretreatment with PIO or SC79 significantly improved the 7 days survival rates and partially alleviated sepsis-induced hypoglycemia compared with the untreated sepsis group (p < 0.05) (Figure 1F-H). These findings suggest that activation of PPARG or AKT1 is associated with markedly improved survival outcomes and reduced glucose metabolism disturbances in mice with T2DM/sepsis.

Activation of PPARG or AKT1 suppresses inflammatory responses in mice with T2DM/sepsis

Sepsis is characterized by profound dysregulation of systemic inflammatory responses, leading to immune imbalance [7]. In contradistinction to the control group, the sepsis group exhibited statistically increased serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, accompanied by a compensatory elevations in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (p < 0.0001). Treatment with PIO or SC79 significantly reduced circulating pro-inflammatory cytokine levels while further enhancing IL-10 expression compared to the sepsis group (p < 0.05) (Figure 2A-D). Consistently, sepsis markedly raised hepatic mRNA expression of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in T2DM mice (p < 0.0001). Administration of PIO or SC79 statistically suppressed hepatic expression of these pro-inflammatory genes and concomitantly elevated IL-10 expression (p < 0.01) (Figure 2E-H). Collectively, the above data suggest that activation of PPARG or AKT1 appears to shift the inflammatory balance toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype at both systemic and hepatic levels in T2DM/sepsis mice.

Activation of PPARG or AKT1 improves lipid profiles in mice with T2DM/sepsis

Serum insulin levels were evaluated at 24 h post-surgery across different groups. Compared with the control group, insulin levels were statistically elevated in the sepsis group (p < 0.0001), whereas both the PIO+sepsis and SC79 + sepsis groups exhibited significantly lower insulin levels than the untreated sepsis group (p < 0.01) (Figure 3A). To further evaluate metabolic alterations, plasma lipid profiles were analyzed. The sepsis group displayed markedly increased TG levels and statistically reduced HDL-C and LDL-C levels compared with controls (p < 0.0001). In contrast, mice treated with PIO or SC79 showed significantly decreased TG levels (p < 0.05) and increased HDL-C and LDL-C concentrations relative to the sepsis group (p < 0.01) (Figure 3B-D). Thus, activation of PPARG or AKT1 mitigates CLP-induced insulin shortage and lipid profile dysregulation, signifying their role in rectifying metabolic imbalance in T2DM/sepsis models.

Activation of PPARG or AKT1 alleviates organ injury in mice with T2DM/sepsis

Histopathological and biochemical analyses were carried out to determine preventive effects of PIO and SC79 on organ injury. Lung tissue is particularly susceptible to early injury during CLP-induced sepsis [47]. H&E staining revealed serious pulmonary damage in sepsis-T2DM mice, characterized by widespread inflammatory cell infiltration, thickened alveolar septa, and interstitial hemorrhage. Treatment with either PIO or SC79 effectively attenuated these pathological alterations (Figure 4A). Hepatic injury was evaluated using histological examination and serum liver enzyme levels. T2DM/sepsis mice exhibited pronounced hepatic inflammation, which was substantially alleviated by PIO or SC79 treatment (Figure 4B). Correspondingly, serum ALT and AST levels were significantly elevated in the sepsis group compared with controls (p < 0.0001). Treatment with either PIO or SC79 considerably decreased liver enzyme levels (p < 0.01) (Figure 4D, E). Pancreatic histology revealed mild inflammatory infiltration in T2DM/sepsis mice, which was mitigated by both interventions (Figure 4C). At the molecular level, hepatic PPARG mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in the sepsis group compared with controls (p < 0.0001). PIO markedly increased PPARG expression relative to both the sepsis and SC79 + Sepsis groups (p < 0.0001), whereas SC79 had no significant effect (Figure 4F). Conversely, AKT1 mRNA expression was statistically reduced in sepsis mice (p < 0.0001). SC79 treatment robustly upregulated AKT1 expression (p < 0.01), while PIO further suppressed it relative to the sepsis group (p < 0.01) (Figure 4G). Together, these findings suggest that the amelioration of multi-organ injury and dysfunction in T2DM/sepsis mice by PIO or SC79 is associated, at least in part, with specific modulation of their respective transcriptional programs in the liver.

Effects of PPARG or AKT1 activation on signaling pathway proteins

To investigate the molecular mechanisms through which PIO-mediated PPARG activation or SC79-induced AKT1 activation improves T2DM/sepsis liver injury, we tracked the phosphorylation levels of key proteins in the PPARG/AMPK and PI3K/AKT1/mTOR signaling pathways in liver tissue via western blot, based on prior research and relevant literature [20–30]. The results confirmed that, relative to controls, sepsis markedly reduced hepatic levels of p-PI3K, p-mTOR, and PPARG proteins, while significantly increasing those of p-AKT1 and p-AMPK (Figure 5A-F). SC79 treatment significantly enhanced phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT1, and mTOR (p < 0.001) (Figure 5A-D). Conversely, PIO selectively upregulated PPARG expression and increased AMPK phosphorylation (p < 0.001) (Figure 5A, E, and F). In aggregate, these findings imply that selective activation of PPARG or AKT1 is associated with differential modulation of dysregulated signaling pathways, which may participate in hepatic recovery in T2DM/sepsis.

Activation of PPARG or AKT1 preserves colonic barrier integrity in mice with T2DM/sepsis

Sepsis pathogenesis for many comes from intestinal barrier impairment, so it leads to systemic inflammation and organ failure [48]. Moreover, T2DM and its complications are closely related to impaired intestinal barrier function and dysbiosis of gut microbiota [49]. To assess the impact of PIO or SC79 stimulation of intestinal barrier function, we quantified the expression of important tight junction proteins, as well as histological changes. Immunofluorescence staining showed a significant downregulation of claudin-4 and occludin in the colonic epithelium of septic mice when compared to the controls (p < 0.001). Treatment by PIO or SC79 caused a significant recovery of expression of both proteins (p < 0.05) (Figure 6A-D). H&E staining showed marked inflammatory infiltration in septic mice and this was significantly reduced by both interventions (Figure 6E). Collectively, these results suggest that activation of PPARG or AKT1 promotes colonic barrier integrity in T2DM/sepsis mice through the recovery of tight junction protein expression and the inhibition of intestinal inflammation to ameliorate the colonic damage induced by sepsis.

Activation of PPARG or AKT1 modifies gut microbiota in mice with T2DM/sepsis

Gut microbiota dysbiosis is involved in the susceptibility to sepsis through its role in supporting pathogenic expansion, excessive inflammation, and impaired generation of protective metabolites [50]. To examine the association between activation of PIO or SC79 and microbial dysbiosis in T2DM/sepsis, fecal samples were collected at baseline and 24 h after surgery (following both sham surgery and cecal ligation and puncture) and metagenomic sequencing was performed. After severe quality controls, each sample produced between 66,435,862 and 81,177,338 high-quality clean reads. Prior to surgery, α-diversity indices revealed no significant differences in diversity among groups of mice with T2DM, at both the genus- and species-level. At 24 h post-CLP, SC79 but not PIO effectively changed α-diversity of T2DM/sepsis mice (p < 0.05), while no differences were found between controls and septic groups (Figure 7A-D). Thus, the altered gut microbiota α-diversity in T2DM mice points to a combined impact of SC79 pretreatment and subsequent sepsis induction. β-Diversity analyses showed similar microbial community structures within groups before surgery. In contrast, strong compositional changes occurred after 24 h of surgery with SC79 causing a strong restructuring of the gut microbiota components, while PIO showed more moderate effects (Figure 7E-H). At the species level, community typing data indicated that whereas the gut microbiota of T2DM mice prior to surgery aggregated into two basic kinds, the profiles after 24 h post-CLP differed according to experimental group. The septic group and each treatment group (PIO or SC79) each formed distinct, treatment-specific clusters (Figure 7I, J).

Venn analysis revealed a sepsis-induced reduction in common operational taxonomic units (OTUs) among groups. The common OTUs at the genus level decreased from 2,063 (pre-surgery) to 1,839 (24 h post-surgery), and at the species level from 7,661 to 6,581 (Figure 8A-D), indicating substantial gut microbiota remodeling that was subject to pharmacological modulation. Before surgery, the overall structure and dominant taxa (e.g. Bacteroides, Duncaniella at the genus level; corresponding species at the species level) of the gut microbiota were similar across all groups (Figure 8E, G). At 24 h post-surgery, sepsis induced a consistent shift at both taxonomic levels, characterized by a greater number of potentially pro-inflammatory taxa (genera involving Bacteroides and Escherichia; including species like Bacteroides_sp. and Escherichia_coli) and a decrease in beneficial taxa (e.g. Roseburia, Akkermansia, Acetatifactor) (Figure 8F, H). PIO selectively enriched Muribaculum and Akkermansia, while reducing Alistipes and Eubacterium. In contrast, SC79 markedly promoted Akkermansia and Escherichia_coli, but suppressed Duncaniella, Alistipes, and Oscillibacter (Figure 8F, H). Circos plot analysis revealed that the sepsis-induced microbiota remodeling was primarily driven by a limited consortium of core taxa (10 key genera and species) (Figure 8I-L).

Intergroup differential abundance analysis showed that sepsis in T2DM mice caused significant dysbiosis in the gut, with a substantial decrease in short chain fatty acid (SCFA) producing genera (e.g. Roseburia, Blautia) and species (e.g. Eubacterium_sp., Roseburia_sp.) (Figure 9A, D; Tables S1 and S4). Pharmacological interventions partly restored microbe composition but showed different patterns. PIO did increase abundance of Enterococcus at genus level (Figure 9B; Table S2) but decreased abundance of Enterococcus_sp. and enriched abundance of Acetobacter sp._ CAG:977 at the species level (Figure 9E; Table S5). In contrast, SC79 strongly enriched for beneficial genera, involving Akkermansia and Parabacteroides (Figure 9C; Table S3), which is attributable to an increase in Akkermansia_muciniphila and Parabacteroides_goldsteinii abundance (Figure 9F; Table S6). We caution that these findings are exploratory, as some associations did not remain significant after FDR correction―likely attributable to limited statistical power. A group-specific microbial signature was further encountered in the LefSe analysis: SCFA-producing taxa (e.g. s_Eubacterium_sp_) were found among the controls; s_Duncaniella_sp_, s_Duncaniella_dubosii, and s_Alistipes_sp_ were enriched in the sepsis group; s_Oscillibacter_sp_ and s_Odoribacter_sp_ characterized the PIO-treated group; and s_Klebsiella_sp_ and s_Escherichia_sp_ were predominant in the SC79-treated group (Figure 9G). To functionally contextualize the microbial alterations, correlation analyses were carried out between the gut microbial abundance and key host parameters. Taxa depleted by sepsis group (mostly Firmicutes genera like Roseburia and Blautia) were negatively correlated to the liver injury markers (ALT/AST), whereas sepsis enriched taxa (like Alistipes and Duncaniella) correlated positively with pro-inflammatory cytokines and liver enzymes. Most importantly, the SC79-enriched taxa Parabacteroides showed a prominent positive association with the anti-inflammatory cytokine, IL-10 and this was confirmed at the species level (Figure 9H, I). These exploratory data suggest that sepsis is associated with alterations in the gut microbiota of T2DM mice, whereas stimulation of PPARG or AKT1 guides microbial community toward treatment-specific topologies that are linked to improvement of systemic inflammation and hepatic damage.

Discussion

The comorbidity between T2DM and sepsis results in dysregulated inflammatory reactions and metabolic disorders that interact to increase hepatic damage and multi-organ dysfunction [14,51]. In this study, we found that targeted activation of PPARG (with PIO) or AKT1 (with SC79) was associated with improved survival, reduced systemic and hepatic inflammation, partial correction of glucolipid abnormalities, ameliorated intestinal barrier dysfunction, and altered gut microbiota composition in a murine model of T2DM/sepsis. At the molecular level, PIO was associated with increased the PPARG/AMPK axis, while SC79 was associated with enhanced PI3K/AKT1/mTOR signaling. The study presents essential preclinical data that can be used in the creation of specific treatment measures to address T2DM/sepsis patients.

At 24 h after CLP, mice with T2DM exhibited severe weight loss, hypoglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, and abnormal hepatic lipid profile (elevated TG, reduced HDL-C and LDL-C). These changes are consistent with previous reports in non-diabetic models of sepsis, which suggests the septic insult may per se be a profound disrupting force for glucose homeostasis irrespective of basal glycaemic status [52]. Hypoglycaemia and weight loss are probably result from rapid hepatic glycogen depletion, impaired hepatic function, excessive consumption of glucose, decreased food intake, and failure of the intestinal barrier [52,53]. Hyperinsulinemia may reflect stress hormones and cytokines stimulation of β cells with an on top of stress-related compensation for impaired insulin signalling [54,55]. Activation of PPARG or AKT1 was associated with significant improvement of these disturbances. PIO-mediated PPARG activation improves insulin sensitivity [56] and as demonstrated in the current study, reduces the effect of inflammation on insulin signalling pathways by inhibiting excessive overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This effect leads to an improvement in hepatic lipid metabolism and to the restoration of glucose equilibrium which reduces the demand for insulin [57]. SC79-mediated AKT1 activation induces peripheral glucose uptake, inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis and enhances lipid metabolism via downstream insulin signalling [58–61]. Although the two interventions act through different molecular entry points, they converge on partially restoring metabolic-inflammatory homeostasis―highlighting the centrality of this crosstalk in T2DM and sepsis.

As reported previously [52,62], sepsis led to a significant elevations of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in serum and liver of T2DM mice, accompanied by increased transaminases and hepatic histopathology. Even though levels of IL-10 increased but were insufficient to counteract the overwhelming inflammation. Treatment with PIO or SC79 resulted in reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and liver enzymes, increased IL-10 expression, improved lipid profiles and reduced hepatic injury. The results of the current research show that the targeted activation of either PPARG or AKT1 defines an anti-inflammatory and pro-repair regulatory system that provides organ protection. Our mechanistic data reveal that PIO selectively upregulated PPARG and increased AMPK phosphorylation in the liver, without affecting AKT1 phosphorylation. PPARG is a cytokine-activated nuclear receptor that regulates inflammation, glucose and lipid metabolism, and tissue repair [63–66]. Its overexpression suppresses the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-kB) and inflammasome signalling [22,38], alleviating septic liver injury and enhancing insulin sensitivity [67,68]. AMPK, a major cellular energy sensor, inhibits inflammation and stimulates glucose utilisation and fatty acid oxidation [69–72]. AMPK deficiency worsens sepsis-induced organ damage, whereas its activation is protective [73–75]. AMPK also blocks NF-kB via SIRT1, FOXO and PGC-1a [76,77]. Thus, the protective effects associated with PIO may involve stimulation of the PPARG/AMPK pathway in T2DM/sepsis, although direct evaluation of NF-kB activity and genetic loss-of-function studies are needed to confirm this mechanism. Conversely, SC79 enhanced PI3K, AKT1, and mTOR phosphorylation without affecting PPARG expression. AKT1 is an integrator of both metabolic and immunological signals downstream of PI3K essential for controlling glucose and lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses [59,78,79]. Its activation improves multi-organ injury in sepsis and insulin sensitivity [59,80,81]. In the liver, AKT1 promotes lipid homeostasis while the inhibition of AKT1 aggravates steatosis and lipotoxicity [61]. mTOR, a downstream effector, limits excessive inflammation, improves IL-10 production and survival in sepsis models [82]. Moreover, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling promotes anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization, while mTOR inhibition favors pro-inflammatory M1 polarization [83–85]. These findings suggest that SC79-mediated hepatoprotection is associated with reactivation of the PI3K/AKT1/mTOR axis, although macrophage-specific genetic validation is warranted.

Sepsis and T2DM affect intestinal barrier integrity, which result in microbial dysbiosis and bacterial translocation [86–88]. In our study, both interventions partially restored colonic tight junction proteins (claudin-4 and occludin) and reduced inflammatory infiltration. Exploratory metagenomic sequencing suggested that PIO and SC79 induced distinct but partially overlapping shifts in gut microbiota composition. Activation of the PPARG revealed trends toward higher relative abundance of Muribaculum and Akkermansia, while activation of AKT1 showed trends toward higher relative abundance of Akkermansia and Parabacteroides (Figure 8F, H). These taxa are linked to better glucose metabolism, lipid homeostasis and anti-inflammatory effects [89–95]. Correlation analysis further showed that beneficial bacteria enriched during AKT1 activation correlated favourably with IL-10, whereas combined taxa expanded during sepsis correlated positively with pro-inflammatory cytokines and indicators of liver damage. These changes may contribute to reduced bacterial translocation and systemic inflammation, but this remains speculative without functional validation. However, we caution that correlational data do not prove causality; fecal microbiota transplantation or germ-free mouse experiments are required to establish a causal role.

Although PIO and SC79 were given via different routes (oral 7 days pretreatment vs. single intraperitoneal injection), both clearly improved outcomes compared with the sepsis alone group. This difference in regimen came from their distinct pharmacological properties. Pioglitazone, a PPARG agonist, requires sustained receptor occupancy for stable metabolic effects, which matches its standard daily oral use in both preclinical and clinical settings [38,39,96]. By contrast, SC79 rapidly activates AKT1 signaling; a single intraperitoneal injection reaches effective blood levels quickly, making it suitable for acute sepsis intervention [40]. We acknowledge the lack of route-matched vehicle controls as a limitation. This difference prevents any direct comparison of efficacy between the two agents, because the observed effects could be influenced by the distinct pharmacokinetics and dosing schedules rather than target specificity alone. However, our main goal was to test whether activating each target separately provides benefit, not to directly compare the two drugs. From a translational standpoint, our findings suggest that T2DM/sepsis patients might be stratified according to baseline PPARG or AKT1 expression – for instance, those with low PPARG activity could receive pioglitazone, whereas those with suppressed AKT1 signaling might be candidates for AKT1 activators. Future studies with standardized protocols are still needed to compare the relative efficacy of these two agents. Several other limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observation period was limited to 24 h post-CLP. While this time point captures the peak of the initial hyperinflammatory response and early organ injury, it provides no insight into the durability of the observed protective effects of PPARG or AKT1 activation. It remains unknown whether the improvements in survival, metabolic profiles, and organ histology translate into long-term benefits, such as prevention of chronic liver fibrosis, sustained restoration of gut barrier function, or resolution of the immunosuppressive phase of sepsis. Future studies extending the observation period to 72 h and beyond post-CLP, including serial assessments of organ function and inflammation, are necessary to evaluate the sustained therapeutic potential of PIO and SC79. Second, our mechanistic conclusions rely primarily on protein phosphorylation and expression changes following pharmacological activation. We did not employ loss-of-function approaches (e.g. PPARG antagonist, conditional knockout of PPARG or AKT1, or AKT1-specific inhibitors) to definitively establish the necessity of these pathways. Therefore, while our data support a strong association, causal inference regarding the PPARG/AMPK and PI3K/AKT1/mTOR pathways should be made with caution. Future mechanistic studies are required to confirm the indispensable roles of these molecules. Third, This study used only male mice. While this design minimizes variability associated with the female estrous cycle, it inevitably limits the generalizability of our conclusions. In sepsis, substantial sex-related differences exist in both immune and metabolic responses. Male individuals (both mice and humans) typically show higher sepsis incidence, disease severity, and mortality, which may relate to stronger innate immune activation and impaired disease tolerance in males [97–99]. In contrast, females often exhibit a survival advantage, possibly due to estrogen-mediated immunomodulation, X-chromosome mosaicism, and stronger adaptive immune responses [99,100]. Moreover, during sepsis, male mice display impaired switching to mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, whereas females maintain better metabolic flexibility [97]. Therefore, future studies should include female animals, with careful consideration of estrous cycle or hormonal status, to fully assess the sex-specific effects of these therapeutic strategies. Fourth, our microbiota findings are largely correlative, and the causal relationship between gut microbiota remodelling and protection of organs remains to be validated by fecal microbiota transplantation, antibiotic-induced microbiota depletion, or germ-free mouse experiments. Fifth, we did not include a post-sepsis treatment model (drug administration after CLP), which would be more clinically relevant. Despite these limitations, the current study offers novel findings on therapeutic strategies in T2DM/sepsis and provides a basis for prospective clinical application.

Conclusion

Sepsis superimposed on T2DM increases damage to the liver due to the development of a self-sustaining relationship between metabolic dysfunction and inflammation. As illustrated in Figure 10, this investigation suggests that targeted activation of PPARG or AKT1 is associated with survival and liver function in T2DM/sepsis mice, accompanied by reduced excessive inflammation, partially improvement of glucose and lipid metabolism, and enhanced intestinal barrier integrity. Mechanistically, the observed protective effects are associated with engagement of the PPARG/AMPK axis and increased activity of the PI3K/AKT1/mTOR signaling cascade, respectively. Collectively, these data suggest that metabolically targeted immunomodulation represents a promising therapeutic strategy in the setting of sepsis complicated by T2DM, not only providing a new framework for individualistic treatment, but also mechanistic insight into molecular underpinnings of liver injury for this high-risk comorbidity.
