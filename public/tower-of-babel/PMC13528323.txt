Academia and industry in the age of artificial intelligence: a narrative review
Authors: Elmer Victor Bernstam, Todd R Johnson, Fares Alahdab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have fuelled a boom phase and promise to revolutionise many domains, including the traditional activities of academic medical centres (AMCs): biomedical research, education and healthcare services. Progress in AI is driven by three factors: (1) faster and cheaper processors (compute), (2) increasingly efficient training algorithms and neural network architectures and (3) an increase in electronically readable data. Outside of healthcare, industry investment dwarfs AI investment by governments. The largest computer clusters and datasets are now managed by industry. Similarly, most PhDs specialising in AI research are employed by industry. Therefore, it is not surprising that most of the largest and best-performing models are now developed by industry.Despite the industry lead in the general domain, we argue that AMCs have a key advantage in healthcare; they steward high-value biomedical data, a key driver of AI progress. Data are a strategic asset for AMCs. However, to capitalise on that asset, AMCs must invest in data ecosystems that improve data quality and utility, including the processes that produce data as well as data management. AMCs must also implement policies and procedures that lower the barriers to data use and reuse. With burgeoning interest in AI, AMCs should take on the role of careful, yet efficient, stewards of healthcare data to maximise its potential, while acting in the public’s best interests.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Data Management, Electronic Data Processing, Electronic Health Records, Informatics

Introduction

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) are driven by three forces (AI inputs): (1) faster and cheaper processors; (2) improvements in algorithms and neural network architectures; and (3) enormous amounts of electronically readable human-generated data on the internet/web. Statistical models with hundreds of billions of parameters can now be trained to perform as well or even better than humans on a wide variety of previously challenging tasks, including handwriting and speech recognition, image recognition and code generation.1 In healthcare, specific AI systems performed better than the average human on medical licensing exams.2 Similarly, some AI systems outperformed humans on specific, well-defined tasks, including the interpretation of radiological images (eg, chest X-rays,3 4 mammograms5) and diagnosis.6 7 Interestingly, in some cases and contexts, humans assisted by AI performed better than humans without AI but not as well as AI alone. In this narrative review, we address the relationship between academia and industry in the context of AI research and its application to healthcare.

Academia versus industry

Academic medical centres (AMCs) have long driven progress in biomedical research, and the application of research advances to patient care. Consider drug development as an example. Industry regularly partners with academia and public funders such as the US National Institutes of Health. Since pharmaceutical companies do not provide direct healthcare, they depend on others to conduct clinical trials, most commonly AMCs. Industry-sponsored studies of promising drug candidates and marketed products are a substantial portion of an AMC’s portfolio.8 Once a drug is approved, industry handles manufacturing, marketing and distribution, at a profit, of course. Funding for drug development is roughly evenly split between academia and industry.8 9

In contrast to drug development, just as AI seems poised to revolutionise many domains, including healthcare, industry has far outpaced academia in many AI metrics. AI research investment is dominated by industry; in 2021, the US and European governments invested less than $3 billion compared with $340 billion invested by industry. Indeed, investment by Alphabet alone, the parent company of Google, in 2019 was similar to US Government investment in 2021.

Consider the ‘AI inputs’: compute, algorithm and data. Industry has enormous computer clusters capable of running far larger models than academia. Nearly 75% of AI PhDs who develop novel algorithms are employed by industry.10 As a result, the ranks of AI PhDs in industry have grown enormously while computer science research faculty numbers have been relatively stable.10 Industry also has access to large commercial datasets. For example, Amazon and Google have enormous repositories of proprietary data not available to academia.

Not surprisingly, ‘AI outputs’ are also increasingly dominated by industry. In 2021, AI models in industry were 29 times larger than academic models, and 96% of the largest models were built by corporations.11 The computational resources used to train milestone systems have doubled roughly every 5–6 months, with a distinct large-scale trend since 2015 dominated by corporate training runs that are 10–100× larger than academic efforts.12 Nearly 90% of the frontier state-of-the-art model releases came from industry in 2024, up from 60% in 2023.13

Notably, industry is also increasing ‘basic’ AI research, not just model building and applications. Although academia remains the leading producer of highly cited AI papers,13 the share of publications by industry at leading AI conferences in 2020 approached 40%, approximately double their share in 2016.11 Further, on average, industry-affiliated papers have received more citations and online attention.14

For another perspective on the changing relationship between academia and industry, consider that the core technology that transformed web search in 1998, the PageRank algorithm,15 was developed at Stanford by Larry Page and Sergey Brin, who then dropped out of the PhD programme to start Google. In contrast, the founders of OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, in 2015 were not academics. Sam Altman and Elon Musk are technology entrepreneurs; Ilya Sutskever was formerly employed by Google; Gregory Brockman and Trevor Blackwell were tech CEOs, etc.

Certain exceptions to the industry dominance emerge as well, such as BigScience’s BLOOM 175B multilingual model,16 an example of the pooling of compute and funding resources by academia and civil society for an open, cross-sector effort. Government efforts have also been set up in response to the current resource imbalance and to support academics and non-profits with shared compute and data access including the US National Science Foundation’s National Artificial Intelligence Research Resource.17

AI and machine learning in healthcare

In contrast to the broader ‘general domain’, healthcare AI has multiple cautionary tales for industry. Two prominent examples are Google Flu Trends and the Epic sepsis prediction algorithm. Google Flu Trends was an effort to use search terms to predict and track influenza outbreaks. At first, the system worked very well and accurately identified outbreaks well ahead of traditional public health surveillance methods. However, over time, accuracy dropped, and the system was quietly retired in 2015.18 19 Similarly, Epic Systems bundled a machine learning algorithm trained to predict sepsis in hospitalised patients with their widely used electronic health record (EHR) system. However, when the system was evaluated at the University of Michigan, performance was very poor.20

Unlike the general models where industry dominates, AMCs and public consortia have released most of the open datasets and early methods for task-specific medical models. These are models that focus on certain data modalities or specialty areas, such as ECG, clinical risk prediction using EHR data, radiology, pathology and ophthalmology. For example, PBT-XL is the largest 12-lead ECG dataset and is used in benchmarks and many research studies.21 22 PBT-XL was released by academic partners and PhysioNet. Recent multi-million-ECG foundation model23 efforts have also been led by AMCs. Similar trends are seen for the largest datasets and open AI modelling efforts around chest X-rays,24–26 CT imaging,27 MRI imaging,28–30 pathology evaluation of biopsies to detect cancer,31 32 retinal disease diagnosis,33 34 echocardiography35 36 and others. Many large vision-language medical foundation models37 38 are academic or academic-consortium efforts with open code and publicly available preprints. Open, community-supported, physiology-aware and physics-aware modelling frameworks for deep learning on healthcare data39 40 have also been on the rise. Notably, many of these academic AI models later get commercialised and licensed to commercial companies to bring the models to market and to the bedside.41 42 Companies organise the resulting evidence for regulatory submissions, workflow integration and sales. Not surprisingly, publication incentives and regulatory-market incentives encourage different outputs.43 44

Opportunities for AMCs

One area where academia, particularly AMCs, has an advantage is healthcare data. This includes routinely collected data, including clinical, administrative and financial data that are by-products of healthcare, as well as research data that are generated by research protocols.45 Importantly, AMCs also have the expertise to understand how these data were created, their context, provenance, interpretation and limitations. For example, the rate of a particular disease can be low or high at an institution because of differences in population, care processes or organisational factors that have nothing to do with biology. A free-standing cancer centre may draw patients with cancer away from a nearby ‘general’ AMC. Although there are multiple very large commercial healthcare datasets, these data are limited by deidentification and selection biases and generally lack unstructured data (eg, clinical notes, waveforms, clinical images) that represent clinical reality more fully than ubiquitous billing codes that are a poor reflection of what is actually going on with the patient.46 AMCs also drive systematic data collection studies, including patient registries as well as large cohort studies, such as the Nurses’ Health Study47 that may be valuable for AI training.

Importantly, AMCs including both publicly funded and private institutions have traditionally prioritised the public good over profit. Patients are more willing to share data with AMCs than commercial entities.48–50 Therefore, AMCs are well positioned to address the many complex ethical issues related to AI including bias, transparency and accountability.

In short, to drive AI progress in healthcare, AMCs must maximally use their data resources. First, AMCs must recognise that their data are strategic institutional assets. Anecdotally, many leading institutions are doing so and are thoughtfully considering mutually beneficial data-enabled collaborations with outside entities, such as allowing corporations to train or evaluate AI models on data from the AMC. AMCs should develop data governance and set policies for access, quality, lineage, retention and value capture, which require formal roles for data stewards, honest-broker services and contract templates for safe sharing and licensing.51–53

A corollary is that institutions should also maximise the value of their data. AMCs should work to improve their understanding of their data, including the limitations of these data. For example, before 2020, telehealth encounters were much less frequent; therefore, telehealth metadata and remote patient monitoring were much less available prior to 2020. AMCs must invest in ‘data improvement projects’ that standardise processes and harmonise data curation so that data are comparable and interpretable across the enterprise. Every clinic in the enterprise should collect vital signs in the same way or, if that is not appropriate (eg, specialty-specific vital signs), this should be systematically documented and understood by the institution.

Second, AMCs must intentionally lower the barriers to the use of their data by appropriate parties, particularly their own researchers. This might include investments in informatics service units that bridge the gap between databases and clinical researchers as well as mapping their data to standard data models such as the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership54 and PCORnet Common Data Model (CDM).55 Audited self-service tools for feasibility analysis and cohort discovery (eg, i2b2 or ATLAS56 57) can help translate clinical questions into computable cohorts and reproducible extracts.

AMCs can lower barriers to data use by adhering to the Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable (FAIR) principles.58 In contrast to data that do not adhere to the FAIR principles, FAIR data can be reanalysed to answer new questions (eg, test an algorithm that was not developed when the data were created), be linked to internal or external datasets facilitating collaboration within the AMC and between institutions. However, FAIR data require intentional investment of resources, most notably the effort of skilled informaticians, and strong leadership. It is often expedient to implement a process that meets a particular need without considering future applications such as later reuse of the data resulting from that process for research or quality improvement. Similarly, workarounds that damage data (eg, test patients intermingled with real patients, blocking meeting times by scheduling ‘fake’ patients with names like ‘Mr. Block Meeting’) should be strictly avoided.45

AMCs should adopt and enforce shared vocabularies and transport standards so that clinical data remain reusable outside the originating system.59 60 AMCs should maintain ownership and oversight of patient data that feed into purchased vendor products (eg, radiology or pathology information systems) to keep these data accessible and usable by authorised institutional researchers.

Strategically, these investments should be aimed at realising a ‘virtuous cycle’ between informatics services and research. In this cycle, service requests (eg, by an investigator to identify patients with diabetes mellitus) may generate a research project (eg, applying advanced phenotyping methods to more accurately identify patients with diabetes). Thus, completing the research project benefits future service projects that inspire future research projects and so on.

AMCs should also work to lower the procedural friction and regulatory barriers to data reuse for research, quality improvement and AI development. This includes in-house and collaborative efforts with other AMCs or industry partners. Several tested mechanisms help. One strategy to lower regulatory barriers to data use and reuse while maintaining appropriate institutional review board (IRB) oversight is to implement umbrella protocols that obviate the need for project-specific review by IRBs for projects that meet specific criteria. The University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston has developed protocols that apply to projects that keep the data on specific institutional servers and do not allow researchers to contact patients or publish/release protected health information.61 These protocols have been adopted by several other institutions and are publicly available for adoption by others. AMCs may also use the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act pathways that allow privacy-preserving62 reuse at scale: deidentification by expert determination or safe harbour63 and limited datasets64 released only under a data use agreement.65 Finally, to facilitate collaboration between academia and industry, when the external partners need data access, AMCs should follow a ‘bring-the-algorithm-to-the-data’ approach on institutional infrastructure, with business associate agreements when required.

The recommendations above require resources and collaboration between historically separate units within AMCs, including clinical operations, clinical information technology (IT) and informatics. The operating model should align these entities. Each unit has expertise that is vital to the overall effort. Clinical operation units understand how the data are generated and the tasks that must be supported (eg, tracking specific quality metrics). Clinical IT maintains EHRs and enforces security. Informatics can help translate clinical questions into computable phenotypes and reproducible extracts, run data quality checks and curate CDM stores to bridge the gap between technology, clinical care and the research enterprise. Often, clinical operations and clinical IT focus solely on the information needed for immediate care and billing. However, a learning healthcare system66 requires a carefully maintained data fabric enabling routinely collected healthcare data to be used for secondary purposes, such as estimating the causal effects of interventions. Informatics can help bridge the gap between the information that is collected during routine clinical care and the information that clinical researchers need to later use those data for discovery. By intentionally improving the data collected throughout care, AMCs can provide better data for AI models and research use. Informatics can also build connections to other institutions (eg, via research networks or federated learning consortia) that increase sample sizes for research projects, allowing analyses that would not be feasible at a single institution (eg, rare diseases). This data-enabled ecosystem cannot, and perhaps should not, be established by industry alone; it must involve AMCs that create, manage and contextually understand healthcare data, while keeping the patients’ and the public’s interests at heart.
