 SUMMARY
 The Department of Energy’s Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE),
 through the National Reactor Innovation Center (NRIC), is accelerating
 the deployment of advanced reactor technologies by establishing both
 physical and virtual environments for testing systems and components. A
 central initiative within this effort is the Virtual Test Bed (VTB), which aims
 to expedite reactor development and licensing by making advanced
 modeling and simulation tools readily accessible online. This is
 accomplished by offering a centralized platform where stakeholders can
 engage with high-fidelity reactor models to evaluate performance and
 safety.
 Beyond serving as an online repository, the VTB also addresses
 modeling and simulation gaps. One such initiative is the development of a
 virtual model of the Demonstration of Microreactor Experiments (DOME)
 shield. This model supports confirmatory analyses by providing an
 adaptable framework capable of interfacing with user-supplied reactor
 designs. The model focuses on simulating concrete temperatures during
 both steady-state and transient operations, assessing radiation dose
 distributions within the DOME facility, and enabling easy updates for
 configuration changes. Open-source tools are employed throughout the
 development to promote transparency and flexibility.
 Previous work introduced a two-step approach that decouples neutron
 and gamma heating calculations in the core from thermal modeling in the
 shield. This method, implemented in Cardinal using OpenMC and the
 MOOSE heat transfer module, was verified to provide results in good
 agreement with a one-step approach using a publicly available heat-pipe
 microreactor design in place of vendor proprietary designs. The current
 report builds upon that foundation by enhancing the workflow associated
 with the two-step calculation process. It further verifies the two-step
 methodology using a gas-cooled microreactor and presents new dose
 rate maps, transient analyses, and thermal model improvements.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 The Department of Energy, Office of Nuclear Energy’s (DOE-NE) National Reactor
Innovation Center (NRIC) accelerates the deployment of novel reactor concepts by establishing
both physical and virtual spaces for building and testing various components, systems, and
complete pilot plants. The NRIC Virtual Test Bed (VTB) mission is to accelerate the deployment
of advanced reactors by facilitating the adoption of cutting-edge advanced modeling and
simulation (M&S) tools to design, evaluate, and license reactors. This is primarily achieved by
providing a central web repository for reactor developers and stakeholders to access and
leverage state-of-the art M&S models of advanced reactors to evaluate performance and safety.
 In addition to serving as an online repository, the VTB project identifies and fills M&S gaps.
The VTB is thus developing a virtual model of the Demonstration of Microreactor Experiments
(DOME) shield to accelerate confirmatory analysis by providing a model of the DOME reactor
supplemental shield that can interface with a DOME user reactor model. The following
requirements have been identified for that model:
• Couples with any reactor design
• Evaluates the maximum concrete temperature in the shield for both steady-state operation
 and transient scenarios
• Evaluates radiation doses in the DOME facility
• Uses open-source codes
• Model should be easy to modify to accommodate shield configuration changes.
 Previous work has been documented in References [1] and [2] and includes a Cardinal
model coupling OpenMC for neutron and gamma heating with the MOOSE heat transfer
module. A two-step approach for the neutron and gamma heating calculation was developed to
decouple the reactor calculation from the shielding calculation. This decoupling permits new
models of the microreactor and/or shield to be more easily substituted into the workflow. This
two-step workflow has been verified against a one-step standard workflow in terms of energy
deposited in the shield using a heat-pipe microreactor design that is available in the open
literature.
 This report summarizes updates made to the DOME shield model in Fiscal Year (FY) 2025.
Section 2 presents the workflow improvement streamlining the two-step approach calculation.
This workflow is then used to verify the two-step approach using a gas-cooled microreactor in
Section 3. Section 4 presents dose rate maps. Section 5 and Section 6 present the transient
analyses and improvements made to the thermal model.
 The technical details of the work presented in this report have been published in References
[3], [4], [5], and [6].

 2. AUTOMATED WORKFLOW
 The prototype code used to develop the two-step workflow in Reference [1] for an OpenMC
model of a heat-pipe reactor has been fully refactored and updated. The main objective of this
refactoring was to facilitate the integration of any OpenMC reactor models inside the DOME
shield model to generate calculations needed for the two-step approach and the reference
approach. This was achieved by designing a generic interface for every OpenMC model based
on the native modular approach of Python. Now, the code acts as an automated framework that
can produce evaluations for verification of the two-step approach for the DOME shielding
calculations. Figure 1 shows a visual representation of the OpenMC model for the DOME shield
with the heat-pipe reactor model used in Reference [1] and the newly added gas-cooled
microreactor (GCMR) model derived directly from a VTB model in Reference [7]. More details
on the verification process and the model encapsulation principle are available in Reference [3].

gas-cooled microreactor (GCMR) model [7].

 3. GAS-COOLED MICROREACTOR CASE STUDY
 The two-step methodology for shield heating calculations must be accurate for a range of
microreactor designs because multiple vendors are slated to test their concepts in DOME.
Therefore, a gas-cooled microreactor core should be tested to supplement previous conclusions
obtained with a heat-pipe-cooled reactor model. In FY-25, a new OpenMC reactor model was
developed and integrated into the verification framework based on the GCMR design developed
at Argonne National Laboratory under the DOE Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and
Simulation (NEAMS) Program [7].
 The OpenMC GCMR model was adapted from the Serpent model available in the VTB
repository (commit 97d290b5780 model version was used). Figure 2 shows a radial
representation of the GCMR core model and its added shielding layer by zooming in on the
three types of assemblies used in the core. Figure 3 shows a radial representation of the inner
assembly type with a description of its main components. Two representations have been
implemented for modeling the elements filled with tristructural Isotropic (TRISO) particles for the
GCMR model in OpenMC. A first representation considers every particle explicitly and the
second representation only considers a repeated pattern of a given set of particles. While the
second approach introduces geometric discontinuities in TRISO particles at the border between
two repeated cells, it is a necessary simplification to obtain results in a reasonable time with
OpenMC using surface tracking. The simplified representation for TRISO particles (second
approach) was therefore selected for the verification study.
 Consistent with the heat-pipe reactor study [1], the new study for the GCMR model reported
in Reference [3] shows that the Enhanced Step 1 (ES1) approach gives the closest results to
the single-step reference calculation with a relative difference of only 0.66% in estimated heat
deposited in the shield. The Generic 2-Step (G2S) approach gives the worst results with a
relative difference of 10%. The Enhanced Step 2 (ES2) approach shows a relative difference of
2.45%. More details on the OpenMC model and the verification study are available in Reference
[3]. Both the gas-cooled and heat-pipe-cooled reactor studies indicate that the ES1 and ES2
approaches are acceptable for estimating the energy deposited from radiation in the shield,
which is the most important quantity for estimating concrete shield temperatures.

 4. DOSE RATE MAPS
 A tool was developed that facilitates dose rate maps computations. This tool helps users
demonstrate that their designs can be operated and tested safely in the DOME facility with
regard to radiological protection.
 However, computing dose rate maps is a challenging task. Traditional analog simulations
generate very few particles that penetrate deeply into the shielding, resulting in limited statistics
at the periphery of the shield. This limitation hinders the accurate evaluation of dose rate maps.
To improve these statistics, a variance reduction workflow is necessary.
 A variance reduction workflow was developed for dose calculations using a fixed-source
problem to generate separate weight windows for neutrons and photons. The problem was
rerun twice with the respective weight windows, tallying the dose per source in cubic volumes of
1 cubic foot, with peripheral bins just outside the shield (see Figure 4). Dose maps were
rescaled for a core power of 5 MW and presented in terms of mrem/h. The workflow details are
in Reference [4], but further optimization is needed to reduce 100% uncertainty in some
peripheral bins.
 These preliminary results demonstrate that accurate computation of photon-induced doses
is crucial for the accuracy of dose rate maps. Future work will focus on refining the variance
reduction workflow to achieve acceptable convergence of photon-induced doses. This step is
essential for delivering a reliable dose calculation tool.

 5. MULTIPHYSICS REACTOR MODEL FOR THERMAL
 SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS
 The thermal model presented in References [1] and [2] uses a metallic box with a 50 kW
heat source to simulate the reactor thermal leakage behavior. It ensures that the shield structure
can absorb the 50 kW; however, the actual reactor might have a different geometry, a different
thermal conductivity and thermal inertia, which could potentially affect the results in both steady-
state and transient regimes.
 To evaluate these effects, the multiphysics core model of the gas-cooled microreactor
design mentioned in section 4 (directly available on the VTB repository [7]) was extended to
include additional core external components. Because they are placed around the core, they
participate in the thermal coupling of the core and the shield. The geometry of the core is the
same as the reactor presented in Section 3. For simplicity, the 20 MW reactor power is
prescribed to use a constant power density. The geometry was extended to include the core
barrel, upcomer, reactor pressure vessel (RPV), and external insultation and shielding. A square
box was then added to allow the use of a gap heat transfer between the reactor external faces
and the shield cavity inner surface. The effects of these geometry changes were evaluated by
performing numerous sensitivity studies. The details of this model are described in [5].
 The temperature distribution obtained in steady state is shown in Figure 5. The core with
external components resulted in higher concrete temperatures than the core with the simpler
box model. The core with external components experienced azimuthally symmetrical
temperatures, and there was no heat redistribution to the colder surfaces as was observed with
the core placed in a steel box. The temperatures outside the reactor are mostly driven by the
upcomer and plenum temperatures, leading to higher temperatures in the shield concrete.
However, before drawing final conclusions, it should be noted that the outer box and gap heat
transfer methods used in this model add some conservatism and need to be revised and
improved using the method described later in Section 6.

Figure 5. Temperature distribution of the DOME shield during normal operation when coupled
with the multiphysics reactor model: Concrete temperature (left) and shielding and reactor
temperatures (right).

 This model was then used to simulate a protected loss-of-flow scenario. The resulting
reactor fuel temperature, shield water tank and shield concrete temperatures are shown in
Figure 6, Figure 7, and Figure 8, respectively. It should be noted that for this transient, a decay

 6. IMPROVED THERMAL MODEL
 This section presents improvements made to the thermal model of the DOME shield. In the
previous version of the model ([1] and [2]), the cavity between the reactor core and the shield
was not meshed; instead, a simplified gap heat transfer model was used. In that approach, heat
transfer between each core surface and its corresponding shield surface was modeled using an
effective gap conductance, based on the assumption of two infinite parallel plates. While
computationally efficient, this simplification tended to overpredict the maximum temperature in
the shield. To address this limitation, a new methodology was introduced and is described in
detail in [6]. It enhanced radiation modeling within the cavity by calculating view factors between
the cavity surfaces and applying the net radiation method, resulting in a more accurate
temperature distribution. In the current work, we extend the model described in [6] through
several key modifications:
• Replaced the box-shaped reactor with a horizontally oriented cylindrical reactor to better
 represent the geometry of microreactors intended for DOME testing.
• Included the effect of air conduction and convection by explicitly meshing the cavity and
 solving for heat conduction using an effective thermal conductivity that accounts for both
 modes of heat transfer.
• Subdivided the radiating surfaces between the reactor and the shield into multiple surface
 patches, enabling more precise view factor calculations and improved temperature
 predictions.
• Replaced the four thin alternating insulation layers with a single thick aluminum layer, which
 better reflects the updated design and reduces the maximum concrete temperature in the
 shield.
 To evaluate the impact of geometry and radiation modeling on the shield’s temperature
distribution, three models were developed and compared:
• Model 1: Uses a gap heat transfer approach with a box-shaped reactor.
• Model 2: Implements the net radiation method while retaining the box reactor geometry.
• Model 3: Applies the net radiation method with a horizontally oriented cylindrical reactor.
 To ensure a fair comparison, all models share the same reactor volume, internal heat
source, boundary conditions, insulation configuration, and effective thermal conductivity.
 Figure 9 shows the temperature distribution for Model 1, Figure 10 for Model 2, and Figure
11 for Model 3. All figures use the same temperature scale.
 When comparing Figure 9 and Figure 10, it is evident that the net radiation model (Model 2)
yields a lower maximum concrete temperature, approximately 19°C lower than the gap model
(Model 1) for the same box geometry. This reduction highlights the improved accuracy of the net
radiation method in capturing radiative heat transfer.
 When comparing Model 2 and Model 3, both of which use the net radiation method but differ
in geometry, the cylindrical reactor (Model 3) results in a further reduction in maximum concrete
temperature by approximately 19°C compared to the box reactor. This difference arises from
geometric view factor effects. In the box geometry, the view factor from the bottom reactor
surface to the shield floor insulation is nearly unity due to the small gap between both surfaces,
resulting in more radiative heat reaching the floor. In contrast, the cylindrical surface in Model 3
has a significantly smaller view factor to the floor, leading to reduced radiative heat transfer and
lower concrete temperatures.

 This comparison between models focuses solely on the effect of thermal radiation under
different methods and geometries. In practice, the differences in the concrete maximum
temperature would be smaller when accounting for natural convection of the air in the cavity and

these effects will be presented in a future publication.

 7. CONCLUSION
 This report summarizes updates made to NRIC-DOME shield computational models in
FY-25. The computational models being developed are intended to support reactor vendors by
providing multiphysics workflows for estimating shield concrete temperatures, dose rate maps,
and other quantities of interest. During FY-25, the two-step methodology separating reactor core
and shielding geometry calculations was automated using Python workflows. The two-step
model was also successfully applied to a gas-cooled microreactor design, extending confidence
in the method’s accuracy for a range of designs. A methodology to calculate dose rate maps
has been demonstrated, with preliminary results for neutron and photon-based doses.
Additional work is required to reduce the variance on the dose rate estimation from photons. A
multiphysics model of the gas-cooled microreactor design was coupled with a simplified reactor
shield design to assess assumptions regarding thermal heat transfer to the shield in the two-
step model. Finally, the thermal model for transporting heat from the reactor to the DOME shield
was improved, resulting in a lower concrete temperature estimation.
