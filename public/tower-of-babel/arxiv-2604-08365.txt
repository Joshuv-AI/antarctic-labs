Equivalences of promise compactness principles
Bertalan Bodor

Abstract
. For a pair of finite relational structures ( 𝔄 , 𝔅 ) (\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}) such that 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} homomorphically maps to 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} we denote by K ( 𝔄 , 𝔅 ) K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})} the following statement: for all structures ℑ \mathfrak{I} with the same signature as 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} if all finite substructures of ℑ \mathfrak{I} homomorphically maps to 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} then ℑ \mathfrak{I} homomorphically maps to 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} . In this article, we show that if ( 𝔄 , 𝔅 ) (\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}) has no Olšák polymorphism, then K ( 𝔄 , 𝔅 ) K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})} is equivalent to the ultrafilter principle over ZF \operatorname{ZF} . This includes the statements K ( K 3 , K 5 ) K_{(K_{3},K_{5})} and K ( H 2 , H c ) K_{(H_{2},H_{c})} for all c ≥ 2 c\geq 2 where K n K_{n} denotes the clique of size n n and H k H_{k} denotes the ternary not-all-equal structure on a k k -element set. This means, for example, that in any ZF \operatorname{ZF} model, if every finitely 3-colourable graph can be coloured by 5 colours then all these graphs can in fact be coloured by 3 colours.



1.
Introduction

For a finite relational structure
\mathfrak{A}
we write
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
for the following statement.

•

For every relation structure
\mathfrak{I}
with the same signature as
\mathfrak{A}
if all finite

substructures of
\mathfrak{I}
homomorphically map to
\mathfrak{A}
then so does
\mathfrak{I}
.

We refer to the statement
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
as the
compactness principle
over
\mathfrak{A}
. Note that all statements
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
follow from the compactness theorem of propositional logic, and thus they are all theorems of
\operatorname{ZFC}
. In fact, we know that the compactness theorem (even for first-order logic), and thus all compactness principles
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
, follow already from the ultrafilter lemma which is strictly weaker than the axiom of choice
[
Jec77
]
over
\operatorname{ZF}
. However, different compactness principles have different strengths in choiceless set theory. For instance, by a theorem of Läuchli
[
Läu71
]
we know that
K_{K_{3}}
is in fact equivalent to the ultrafilter lemma over
\operatorname{ZF}
meaning that
K_{K_{3}}
is (one of) the strongest compactness principles. On the other hand, it is fairly easy to see that
K_{K_{2}}
is equivalent to
\operatorname{AC}(2)
, i.e., that axiom of choice for families of 2-element sets which is much weaker than the ultrafilter principle
[
Jec77
]
.

1.1.
CSPs and compactness principles

For a relational structure
\mathfrak{A}
the CSP (constraint satisfaction problem) over
\mathfrak{A}
, denoted by
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{A})
, is the computation problem where the input is any structure
\mathfrak{I}
with the same signature of
\mathfrak{A}
, and we need to decide whether
\mathfrak{I}
homomorphically maps to
\mathfrak{A}
.
As has been observed at many places in the literature, the strength of
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
is closely related the computation complexity of the CSP over
\mathfrak{A}
; in general the harder the CSP is over
\mathfrak{A}
the stronger the statement
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
seems to be
[
RTW17
,
KTV23
,
Tar25
]
. For example
\operatorname{CSP}(K_{3})
, i.e., 3-colourability of graphs, is
\mathbf{NP}
-complete whereas
K_{K_{2}}
, i.e., 2-colourability is polynomial time decidable. This observation can be formalized using the notion of
primitive positive (or pp-) constructions
: we say that a structure
\mathfrak{A}

pp-constructs
a structure
\mathfrak{B}
if
\mathfrak{B}
is homomorphically equivalent to some pp-power of
\mathfrak{A}

[
BOP18
]
. One of the most important features of pp-constructions is that they give reductions between complexity of CSPs. More specifically, if
\mathfrak{A}
pp-constructs
\mathfrak{B}
then
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{B})
polynomial time (in fact LOGSPACE) reduces to
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{A})

[
BOP18
]
. Following the terminology of
[
BPS25
]
we say that finite structure
\mathfrak{A}
is
omniexpressive
if it pp-constructs all finite structures. We know, for instance, that
K_{3}
is omniexpressive, and thus omniexpressivity is also equivalent to the pp-constructability of
K_{3}
. In particular, if
\mathfrak{A}
pp-constructs
K_{3}
then
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{A})
is
\mathbf{NP}
-complete. On the other hand, it was shown independently by Bulatov
[
Bul17
]
and Zhuk
[
Zhu20
]
that all CSPs over not omniexpressive finite structures are solvable in polynomial time, thereby confirming the famous dichotomy conjecture by Feder and Vardi
[
FV99
]
.

As for compactness principles some analogous results were obtained in a recent work of Kátay, Tóth, and Vidnyánszky
[
KTV23
]
. Here, it has been shown that if
\mathfrak{A}
pp-constructs
\mathfrak{B}
then
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
implies
K_{\mathfrak{B}}
. This implies immediately that all compactness principles over finite omniexpressive structures are equivalent to each other, and thus by the theorem of Läuchli, they are also equivalent to the ultrafilter lemma. On the other hand, we know if
\mathfrak{A}
is finite and not omniexpressive then
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
is strictly weaker than
K_{K_{3}}
. In fact, the following result is shown in the aforementioned paper.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
KTV23
]
, Theorem 2.18)
.

There exists a model of
\operatorname{ZF}
where for a finite structure
\mathfrak{A}
the compactness principle
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
holds if and only if
\mathfrak{A}
is not omniexpressive.

Note that combining the finite-domain CSP dichotomy theorem with Theorem
1.1
we obtain that
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{A})
is
\mathbf{NP}
-hard if and only if
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
is equivalent to the ultrafilter lemma, unless
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{NP}
.

1.2.
Promise CSPs

We call a pair of structures
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
a
promise template
if
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
have the same signature and there is a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathfrak{B}
. A promise template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is called finite if both
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are finite.
The
promise CSP
, or
PCSP
, over a finite promise template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
, denoted by
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
, is the decision problem where for an input
\mathfrak{I}
with the same signature as
\mathfrak{A}
(or
\mathfrak{B})
we need to output

•

YES if
\mathfrak{I}
homomorphically maps to
\mathfrak{A}
, and

•

NO if
\mathfrak{I}
does not homomorphically map to
\mathfrak{B}
.

The “promise” in this setting is that we are given an input where one of the two cases above holds. Note that in this notation
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{A})
is the same as
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{A})
. The notion of promise PCSPs was introduced in
[
AGH17
]
, although some variations, such as approximate graph colourings (i.e., PCSPs over
(K_{k},K_{c})
) already appeared earlier in the literature
[
GJ76
,
DRS05
,
GK04
,
Hua13
,
BG16
]
. As it turns out, pp-constructions, and thus also omniexpressivity, can be generalized to promise templates, and they still imply complexity reductions in the same way
[
BBKO21
]
. For example, it follows from the results of
[
BG16
]
that
(K_{k},K_{2k-2})
is omniexpressive for all
k\geq 3
, and therefore
\operatorname{PCSP}(K_{k},K_{2k-2})
is
\mathbf{NP}
-complete. However, as opposed to finite-domain CSPs, there are many non-omniexpressive finite promise templates which are known to have hard PCSPs. Some notable examples are

•

(K_{k},K_{2k-1})
for
k\geq 3

[
BBKO21
]
,

•

(K_{k},K_{c})
where
k\geq 4
and
c={k\choose\lfloor k/2\rfloor}-1

[
KOWŽ23
]
,

•

(C_{2k+1},K_{4})
for
k\geq 1
where
C_{\ell}
denotes the cycle of length
\ell

[
AFO+25
]
, and

•

(H_{2},H_{c})
for
c\leq 2
where
H_{k}
denotes the structures on a
k
-element set with a single ternary not-all-equal relation
[
DRS05
]
.

Further hardness results have been shown in
[
AGH17
,
FNO+23
]
. We remark that all PCSPs over
(K_{k},K_{c})
where
3\leq k\leq c
are in fact conjectured to be hard but already the complexity of
\operatorname{PCSP}(K_{3},K_{6})
is open
[
BBKO21
]
.

1.3.
Promise compactness

Following the idea of promise CSPs, we introduce the promise version of compactness principles in the following way. Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a promise template. We then write
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
for the following statement, to which we refer as the
promise compactness principle
over
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
.

•

For every relation structure
\mathfrak{I}
with the same signature as
\mathfrak{A}
if all finite

substructures of
\mathfrak{I}
homomorphically map to
\mathfrak{A}
then
\mathfrak{I}
homomorphically maps to
\mathfrak{B}
.

It follows from a straightforward generalization of the proofs of
[
KTV23
]
that if
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
pp-constructs
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
then
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
implies
K_{(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})}
(over
\operatorname{ZF}
) (see Section
3
). Thus, it still holds that if
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is omniexpressive then
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
is equivalent to the ultrafilter lemma. By the results of
[
BG16
]
mentioned above, this includes all promise templates of the form
(K_{k},K_{c})
where
3\leq k\leq c\leq 2k-2
. This means, for example, that if in a
\operatorname{ZF}
model every finitely 3-colourable graph can be coloured by 4 colours then all these graphs can in fact be coloured by 3 colours. So we can get rid of one of the colours without any choice axiom.

Since in the promise setting omniexpressivity does not correspond to hardness of PCSPs this motivates the question as to which promise compactness principles are equivalent to
K_{K_{3}}
, or more concretely: is it true that if
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is hard, then
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
is equivalent to the ultrafilter lemma? In this article, we settle this question in the following cases.

Theorem 1.2
.

Any of the following statements are equivalent to the ultrafilter lemma over
\operatorname{ZF}
.

(1)

K_{(K_{k},K_{2k-1})}
with
k\geq 3
.

(2)

K_{(H_{k},H_{c})}
with
2\leq k\leq c
.

As for finitely 3-colourable graphs this means that we can also get rid of a second colour without any choice axiom.

1.4.
Polymorphisms

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a promise template. Then a map
f:A^{k}\rightarrow B
is called a
polymorphism
of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
if it is a homomorphisms from the categorical power
\mathfrak{A}^{k}
to
\mathfrak{B}
. A polymorphism of a single structure
\mathfrak{A}
is a polymorphism of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{A})
. Polymorphisms of a promise template always form a
minion
, i.e., a minor-closed set of operations, and polymorphisms of a single structure from a clone. We write
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
for the polymorphism minion of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
, and we write
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{A})\coloneqq\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A})
. Polymorphisms play an essential role in the study of CSPs and PCSPs. For example, we know by the results of
[
BBKO21
]
that for finite
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D}
the promise template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
pp-constructs
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
if and only if there exists a
minion homomorphism
from
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
to
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
. By the results of
[
BK12
]
we know the following algebraic dichotomy for finite structures.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a finite structure. Then
\mathfrak{A}
is not omniexpressive if and only if
\mathfrak{A}
has a cyclic polymorphism, i.e., an
h\in\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A})
satisfying the identity
h(x_{0},\dots,x_{k-1})=h(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{0})
for some
k\geq 2
.

We remark that Theorem
1.3
, in fact, plays a crucial role in both Zhuk’s and Bulatov’s proof of the finite-domain CSP dichotomy theorem, as well as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, although in the former two proofs a weaker version of Theorem
1.3
(with
weak near-unanimity
polymorphisms) is sufficient.

In a recent work of Barto and Kozik
[
BK22
]
a new reduction between promise CSPs was introduced which is strictly more powerful than pp-constructions. This reduction involves a generalization of minion homomorphisms which we will simply call
weak minion homomorphisms
(originally called
(d,r)
-minion homomorphisms
, see Definition
5.2
). This reduction together with the results of
[
NVWŽ25
]
gives a new proof of the hardness of PCSPs over
(H_{k},H_{c}):2\leq k\leq c
and
(K_{k},K_{2k-1}):k\geq 3
. Note however that this reduction is purely algebraic in nature, and as of now there is no clear description on the level of structures as to when this reduction is possible. Nevertheless, in this article, we show that this reduction is robust enough that it can also be used to infer implication between the corresponding promise compactness principles provided some weak version of
\operatorname{AC}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that for all
k\in\omega
the axiom of choice holds for all families of
k
-element sets, and let us assume that there exists a weak minion homomorphism from
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
to
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
. Then if
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
holds, then so does
K_{(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})}
.

Even though Theorem
1.4
uses a choice assumption in its formulation, we show that in the concrete cases where we need to apply it, this assumption automatically holds. More specifically, we show the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let us assume that
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is finite promise template which has no cyclic polymorphism and
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
holds. Then for all
k\in\omega
the axiom of choice holds for all families of
k
-element sets.

An operation
f:A^{6}\rightarrow B
is called
Olšák
if it satisfies the identities

f(x,x,y,y,y,x)=f(x,y,x,y,x,y)=f(y,x,x,x,y,y).

Using Theorems
1.4
and Theorem
1.5
we will show the following.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let us assume that
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is finite promise template which has no Olšák polymorphism. Then
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
is equivalent to the ultrafilter lemma.

Outline of the paper

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we recall all notions and results in the literature that are relevant for this article. In Section
3
we introduce the notion of promise compactness and prove some results that follow easily from what is already available in the literature. In Section
4
we discuss the relation of promise compactness and certain choice axioms for finite sets, and we prove Theorem
1.5
. In Section
5
we introduce weak minion homomorphisms and we show Theorem
1.4
by going through the proofs of the main results of
[
BK22
]
with a slight modification. This is the most technical part of the paper. Here, we also discuss how Theorems
1.2
and
1.6
can be derived from the two previously mentioned theorems. Finally, in Section
6
we discuss some open problems and possible future research questions about promise compactness.

2.
Preliminaries

We denote by
\omega
the set of natural numbers
\{0,1,\dots\}
, and we use the usual set theoretical convention that
n=\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
for
n\in\omega
. For any set
A
and
B
we use the notation
\prescript{B\!}{}{A}
for the set of functions from
B
to
A
. We think of
n
-tuples from a set
A
as functions from
n
to
A
, and if it does not cause confusion, we write
A^{n}
(as opposed to
\prescript{n\!}{}{A}
) for the set of
n
-tuples from
A
. For a function
\prescript{B\!}{}{A}
and
C\subseteq B
, we write
f|_{C}
for the restriction of
f
to
C
, i.e.,
f|_{C}=f\cap(C\times A)
. For a subset
\mathcal{S}
of
\prescript{B\!}{}{A}
we write
\mathcal{S}|_{C}\coloneqq\{f|_{C}:f\in\mathcal{S}\}
. For a set
A
we write
\mathcal{P}(A)
for the set of all subsets of
A
, and for an
n\in\omega
we write
{A\choose n}
for the set of
n
-element subsets of
A
.

By a
relational signature
we mean a set of relational symbols
\mathcal{R}
together with an arity function
\operatorname{ar}:\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\omega
. In this paper all signatures will be assumed to be relational and finite. A
relational structure
is a triple
\mathfrak{A}=(A,\sigma,i)
where
A
is a set, called the
domain set
of
\mathfrak{A}
,
\sigma
is a relational signature, called the
signature of
\mathfrak{A}
, and
i
maps each
R\in\mathcal{R}
to a subset of
A^{\operatorname{ar}(R)}
. We will write
R^{\mathfrak{A}}
for
i(R)
, and
\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{A})
for the domain set of
\mathfrak{A}
. We say that two structures
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are
similar
if they have the same signature. We use the notational convention that structures are denoted by Fraktur letters, and their domain sets are denoted by the corresponding Latin letters. When talking about finite structures, we usually assume that their domain is
n
for some
n\in\omega
.

For a structure
\mathfrak{A}
and a subset
B\subseteq A=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{A})
, we write
\mathfrak{A}|_{B}
for the
substructure of
\mathfrak{A}
induced on
B
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{A}|_{B}
is the structure whose domain set is
B
, is similar to
\mathfrak{A}
, and for all relational symbols
R
in their signature we have
R^{\mathfrak{A}|_{B}}=R^{\mathfrak{A}}\cap B^{\operatorname{ar}(R)}
.

If
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are similar structures then a map
h:A\rightarrow B
is called a
homomorphism
from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathfrak{B}
if for all relational symbols
R
in their signature whenever
(a_{1},\dots,a_{\operatorname{ar}(R)})\in R^{\mathfrak{A}}
then
(h(a_{1}),\dots,h(a_{\operatorname{ar}(R)}))\in R^{\mathfrak{B}}
.

For a
k\in\omega
and a relational structure
\mathfrak{A}
the
k
-th power of
\mathfrak{A}
, denoted by
\mathfrak{A}^{k}
, is defined to be the structure whose domain is
A^{k}
, similar to
\mathfrak{A}
, and for any relational symbol
R
with
\operatorname{ar}(R)=n
we have
((a_{11},\dots,a_{1k}),\dots,((a_{n1},\dots,a_{nk}))\in R^{\mathfrak{A}^{k}}
if and only if
(a_{1i},\dots,a_{ni})\in R^{\mathfrak{A}}
for all
i=1,\dots,k
.

We denote by
K_{n}
the complete graph with vertex set
n
, and we write
H_{n}
for the structure on
n
with a single ternary
not-all-equal
relation
\operatorname{NAE}
, i.e.,

\operatorname{NAE}\coloneqq\{\prescript{3}{}{n}\setminus\{(i,i,i):i\in n\}\}.

2.1.
Primitive positive constructions and minion homomorphism

In this subsection we are giving a quick introduction to pp-constructions of promise templates and recall some important results that are necessary for the proofs of this paper. Most of the material in this subsection are taken from
[
BKO19
]
.

We assume that the reader is familiar with the usual notions in first-order logic.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a first-order formula
\varphi
is
primitive positive
, or
pp
for short, if
\varphi
can be built up from atomic formulas by only using conjunctions and atomic formulas.

Definition 2.2
.

We write
\mathfrak{A}\rightarrow\mathfrak{B}
if there exists a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathfrak{B}
.

A
promise template
is a pair
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
of similar structures with
\mathfrak{A}\rightarrow\mathfrak{B}
. A promise template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is called finite is both
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are finite. In this paper all promise templates will be finite.

The
PCSP
(P=promise) over
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
, in notation
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
, is the decision problem where the input is a finite structure similar to
\mathfrak{A}
and we need to output
YES
if
\mathfrak{I}\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
and
NO
if
\mathfrak{I}\nrightarrow\mathfrak{B}
. The CSP over
\mathfrak{A}
, in notation
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathfrak{A})
, is
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{A})
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
and
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
be promise templates. Then

(1)

(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
is called a
homomorphic relaxation
of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
if all structures
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D}
are similar,
\mathfrak{C}\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}
.

(2)

(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
is called an
(
n
th) pp-power
of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
if
C=A^{n},D=B^{n}
, and for every relational symbol
R
of arity
k
in the signature of
\mathfrak{C}
there exists a pp-formula
\varphi_{R}
in the signature of
\mathfrak{A}
with free variables
x_{i}^{j}:1\leq i\leq k,1\leq j\leq n
such that

•

for all
a_{i}^{j}\in A:1\leq i\leq k,1\leq j\leq n
we have
((a_{1}^{1},\dots,a_{1}^{n}),\dots,(a_{k}^{1},\dots,a_{k}^{n}))\in R^{\mathfrak{C}}
iff
\varphi_{R}(a_{1}^{1},\dots,a_{k}^{n})
holds in
\mathfrak{A}
, and

•

for all
b_{i}^{j}\in B:1\leq i\leq k,1\leq j\leq n
we have
((b_{1}^{1},\dots,b_{1}^{n}),\dots,(b_{k}^{1},\dots,b_{k}^{n}))\in R^{\mathfrak{D}}
iff
\varphi_{R}(b_{1}^{1},\dots,b_{k}^{n})
holds in
\mathfrak{B}
.

We say that
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
is
pp-constructible
from
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
, or
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})

pp-constructs

(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
, if
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
is a homomorphic relaxation of a pp-power of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
.

Note that for templates of the form
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{A})
homomorphic relaxation collapses to homomorphic equivalence and pp-power is just the usual pp-power for single structures.

Definition 2.3
.

We denote by
\pi_{i}^{k}
the
i
th projection map
on
k
-tuples, i.e., the map which assigns to any
k
-tuple its
i
th coordinate.

Let us fix some sets
A
and
B
. Then a set of functions
\mathcal{M}
from some finite power
A^{k}:k\in\omega\setminus\{0\}
to
B
is called a
(function) minion
if it is closed under taking minors, i.e., for any
f\in\mathcal{M},f:A^{k}\rightarrow B
and
\pi:k\rightarrow\ell
we have
f_{\pi}\in\mathcal{M}
where

f_{\pi}:(x_{0},\dots,x_{\ell-1})\mapsto f(x_{\pi(0)},\dots,x_{\pi(k-1)}).

We say that a minion
\mathcal{M}
has
a finite domain
if
A
and
B
can be chosen to be finite.

If
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
are minions, then a map
\xi:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
is called a
minion homomorphism
if
\xi
preserves the arities of functions, and for all
k
-ary
f\in\mathcal{M}
and
\pi:k\rightarrow\ell

\xi(f_{\pi})=(\xi(f))_{\pi}
.

We say that a map
f:A^{k}\rightarrow B
is a

of a PCSP template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
if

is a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}^{k}
to
\mathfrak{B}
. The set of polymorphisms of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is denoted by
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
. We write
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A})
for
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{A})
.

We write
\mathscr{P}
for the set of all projections on

(which is clearly a minion).

Note that the set of polymorphisms of a promise template always forms a minion. The main correspondence between pp-constructions and minion homomorphisms is the following.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
BKO19
]
, Theorem 4.12)
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
and
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
be finite promise templates. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
pp-constructs
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
.

(2)

There exist a minion homomorphism from
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
to
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
.

Note that Theorem
2.4
also implies that the pp-constructability relation is transitive which is not immediate from our definition. We mention that the non-promise version of Theorem
2.4
(i.e. when

and

) was already shown in

]
.

Definition 2.5
.

We say that a promise template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is

if

pp-constructs all finite promise templates. We say that
\mathfrak{A}
is

if
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{A})
is.

Using this notion, an important special case of Theorem

is the following.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a finite promise template. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is omniexpressive.

(2)

(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
pp-constructs
(K_{3},K_{3})
.

(3)

(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
pp-constructs
(K_{3}^{*},K_{3}^{*})
where
K_{3}^{*}
denote the expansion of

by all constants.

(4)

There exists a minion homomorphism from
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
to
\mathscr{P}
.

Proof.

The implication
1
\rightarrow

2
is trivial and the implication
4
\rightarrow

follows from Theorem

. By Lemma 3.9 in
[

]
we know that
K_{3}
pp-constructs

is a core structure. This implies the equivalence of items

and

. Finally, the equivalence of items

and

follows from Theorem

]

Remark 2.7
.

The term
omniexpressive
itself was only introduced recently in
[
BPS25
]
but the concept itself already appeared in many different places and forms in the literature, many of which are also cited in this article.

Remark 2.8
.

We know that if
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
pp-constructs
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
then
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
is LOGSPACE-reducible to
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
. Since
\operatorname{CSP}(K_{3})
, i.e., 3-colouring of graphs is

-complete, this means that omniexpressivity implies
\mathbf{NP}
-hardness of the corresponding PCSP. By the finite-domain CSP dichotomy theorem

,

]
we know that if
\mathfrak{A}
is not omniexpressive then

is tractable, but this is no longer the case in the promise setting, as we will see in the next subsection.

2.2.
Minor conditions

A further characterization of the existence of minion homomorphisms, and in turn pp-constructability, can be given in terms of minor conditions.

Definition 2.9
.

A
minor condition
is a finite set
\Sigma
of identities of the form
f_{\sigma}\approx g_{\tau}
where
f
and
g
are functional symbols with some designated arities
k
and
\ell
, respectively, and
\sigma\in\prescript{k}{}{n},\tau\in\prescript{\ell}{}{n}
for some
n\in\omega
.

A minor condition
\Sigma
is satisfied in a minion
\mathcal{M}
if and only if we can find an assignment
f\mapsto f^{\mathcal{M}}\in\mathcal{M}
for symbols
f
occurring in
\Sigma
such that whenever
f_{\sigma}\approx g_{\tau}
is an identity in
\Sigma
then
f_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{M}}\approx g_{\tau}^{\mathcal{M}}
holds.

A minor condition is called
trivial
if it is satisfied by
\mathscr{P}
.

Remark 2.10
.

Formally speaking, the number
n
is not specified by the identity
f_{\sigma}\approx g_{\tau}
as in the first line above, but the choice of its value does not change the satisfiability of
\Sigma
because we can add or drop dummy variables in
f_{\sigma}^{\mathcal{M}}
or
g_{\tau}^{\mathcal{M}}
for each of the identities
f_{\sigma}\approx g_{\tau}
.

The correspondence between minion homomorphisms and minor conditions in the case of finite-domain minions is the following.

Theorem 2.11

(
[
BKO19
]
, Theorem 4.12)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
be minions with finite domains. Then there exists a minion homomorphism from
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{N}
if and only if all minor conditions satisfied by
\mathcal{M}
are also satisfied by
\mathcal{N}
.

It is worth pointing out that “only if” direction in Theorem
2.11
follows from a routine calculation (and it does not even require finiteness), so the real strength of this theorem lies in the other direction. In practice, the non-existence of a minion homomorphism from one minion to another (and in turn non-pp-constructability of structures) is often shown by finding a minor condition which separates the two minions. Theorem
2.11
tells us that this is always possible in the case of finite-domain minions. One of the special cases of this observation is the following.

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a finite promise template. Then
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is omniexpressive if and only if all minor conditions satisfied by
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
are trivial.

Proof.

Direct consequence of Corollary
2.6
and Theorem
2.11
.
∎

In this paper we are considering the following types of operations defined by minor conditions.

Definition 2.13
.

A map
f:A^{k}\rightarrow B
is called

•

cyclic
if
k\geq 2
, and it satisfies the identity
f(x_{0},\dots,x_{k-1})=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{0})
,

•

a
area-rare
(or
4-ary Siggers
) operation if
k=4
and it satisfies the identity
f(a,r,e,a)=f(r,a,r,e)
,

•

a
Siggers
operation if
k=6
, and it satisfies the identity
f(x,y,x,z,y,z)=f(y,x,z,x,z,y)
,

•

an
Olšák
operation if
k=6
, and it satisfies the minor condition
f(x,x,y,y,y,x)=f(x,y,x,y,x,y)=f(y,x,x,x,y,y)
.

It is easy to check that all minor conditions in Definition
2.13
are nontrivial, thus by Corollary
2.12
the existence of any of the operations above in
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
implies that
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is not omniexpressive.

The following proposition is folklore, for the sake of completeness we provide a proof.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be any minion. Then

(1)

If
\mathcal{M}
contains a cyclic operation of any arity then
\mathcal{M}
also contains a area-rare operation.

(2)

If
\mathcal{M}
contains an area-rare operation then it contains both a Siggers and an Olšák operation.

Proof.

(
1
) Let
f\in\mathcal{M}
be a cyclic operation of arity
3k+r
where
r\in\{0,1,2\}
. If
r=0
then let

g(x,y,z,w)\coloneqq f(\underbrace{y,\dots,y}_{k\text{ times}},\underbrace{z,\dots,z}_{k\text{ times}},\underbrace{w,\dots,w}_{k\text{ times}}).

Then we have

g(a,r,e,a)=f(\underbrace{r,\dots,r}_{k\text{ times}},\underbrace{e,\dots,e}_{k\text{ times}},\underbrace{a,\dots,a}_{k\text{ times}})=f(\underbrace{a,\dots,a}_{k\text{ times}},\underbrace{r,\dots,r}_{k\text{ times}},\underbrace{e,\dots,e}_{k\text{ times}})=g(r,a,r,e).

If
r=1
then
k\geq 1
In this case let

g(x,y,z,w)\coloneqq f(\underbrace{y,\dots,y}_{k+1\text{ times}},\underbrace{w,\dots,w}_{k-1\text{ times}},x,x,\underbrace{z,\dots,z}_{k-1\text{ times}}).

Then

\displaystyle g(a,r,e,a)=

\displaystyle f(\underbrace{r,\dots,r}_{k+1\text{ times}},\underbrace{a,\dots,a}_{k+1\text{ times}},\underbrace{e,\dots,e}_{k-1\text{ times}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\underbrace{a,\dots,a}_{k+1\text{ times}},\underbrace{e,\dots,e}_{k-1\text{ times}},\underbrace{r,\dots,r}_{k+1\text{ times}})=g(r,a,r,e).

Finally, if
r=2
then let

g(x,y,z,w)\coloneqq f(\underbrace{y,\dots,y}_{k+1\text{ times}},\underbrace{w,\dots,w}_{k\text{ times}},x,\underbrace{z,\dots,z}_{k\text{ times}}).

Then

\displaystyle g(a,r,e,a)=

\displaystyle f(\underbrace{r,\dots,r}_{k+1\text{ times}},\underbrace{a,\dots,a}_{k+1\text{ times}},\underbrace{e,\dots,e}_{k\text{ times}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\underbrace{a,\dots,a}_{k+1\text{ times}},\underbrace{e,\dots,e}_{k\text{ times}},\underbrace{r,\dots,r}_{k+1\text{ times}})=g(r,a,r,e).

(
2
) Let
f
be an operation satisfying the identity
f(a,r,e,a)=f(r,a,r,e)
. Then
g(x,y,z,u,v,w)\coloneqq g(x,y,w,z)
is a Siggers operation and
h(x,y,z,u,v,w)\coloneqq g(v,y,x,z)
is an Olšák operation as shown by the following calculation.

\displaystyle g(x,y,x,z,y,z)

\displaystyle=f(x,y,z,x)=f(y,x,y,z)=g(y,x,z,x,z,y)

\displaystyle h(x,x,y,y,y,x)

\displaystyle=f(y,x,x,y)=f(x,y,x,x)=h(x,y,x,y,x,y)

\displaystyle h(x,y,x,y,x,y)

\displaystyle=f(x,y,x,x)=f(y,x,y,x)=h(y,x,x,x,y,y).\qed

(2)

\mathfrak{A}

(4)

(5)

\rightarrow

\rightarrow

∎

2.3.
Finite versions of the axiom of choice

Let us consider the following axioms.

•

\operatorname{AC}
(Axiom of choice). For every family of nonempty sets
X
there is a function
X\rightarrow\bigcup X
such that
f(x)\in x
for all
x\in X
.

•

\operatorname{UL}
(Ultrafilter lemma). Every filter is contained in an ultrafilter.

•

\operatorname{AC}(<\!\omega)
(Axiom of finite choice). For every family of nonempty finite sets
X
, there is a function
X\rightarrow\bigcup X
such that
f(x)\in x
for all
x\in X
.

•

\operatorname{AC}(k)
. For every family
X
of sets of size exactly
k
there is a function
X\rightarrow\bigcup X
such that
f(x)\in x
for all
x\in X
.

•

\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
. For all
k\in\omega
,
\operatorname{AC}(k)
holds.

•

\operatorname{KW}
(Kinna-Wagner principle). For every family
X
of sets of size at least 2 there is a function
X\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\bigcup X)
such that for all
x\in X
,
f(x)
is a proper subset of
x
.

•

\operatorname{KW}(k)
. For every family
X
of sets of size exactly
k
there is a function
X\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\bigcup X)
such that for all
x\in X
,
f(x)
is a proper subset of
x
.

We know that over
\operatorname{ZF}

(1)

\operatorname{AC}
is strictly stronger than
\operatorname{UL}
,

(2)

\operatorname{UL}
is strictly stronger than
\operatorname{AC}(<\!\omega)
, and

(3)

\operatorname{AC}(<\!\omega)
is strictly stringer than
\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
,

see for instance
[
Jec77
]
.

Clearly,
\operatorname{AC}(k)
implies
\operatorname{KW}(k)
. Note that in formulation of the axiom
\operatorname{KW}(k)
we can assume without loss of generality that
|f(x)|\leq k/2
for all
x
since for all values of size bigger than
k/2
we can switch to the complement. From this observation it is easy to see that
\operatorname{KW}(k)\wedge\bigwedge_{\ell\leq k/2}\operatorname{KW}(\ell)
implies
\operatorname{AC}(k)
for all
k\in\omega
, in particular
\operatorname{KW}(k)
and
\operatorname{AC}(k)
are equivalent for
k=2
and
k=3
. It is also easy to see that
\operatorname{AC}(kn)
implies
\operatorname{AC}(k)
for all
k,n\in\omega
. For prime numbers the converse also holds in the following sense. For any prime
p
there is a model of
\operatorname{ZF}
in which
\operatorname{AC}(p)
fails but
\operatorname{AC}(k)
holds for all
k
not divisible by
p
. In fact, the exact pairs of sets
(Z,k)\in\mathcal{P}(\omega)\times\omega
for which the implication
\bigwedge_{\ell\in Z}\operatorname{AC}(\ell)\rightarrow\operatorname{AC}(k)
is provable in ZF have been classified
[
Gau70
]
. From this we only need the following nontrivial implication in this paper.

Theorem 2.19

(
[
Jec77
]
, Theorem 7.15)
.

Let
n,m\in\omega
and let us assume that
n
cannot be written as a sum of primes bigger than
m
. Then
\bigwedge_{k\leq m}\operatorname{AC}(k)
implies
\operatorname{AC}(n)
.

Theorem
2.19
immediately implies the following.

Corollary 2.20
.

The smallest
k
for which
\operatorname{AC}(k)
fails (if there is any) is prime.

Corollary 2.21
.

The following are equivalent.

(1)

\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
.

(2)

\operatorname{AC}(p)
holds for every prime number
p
.

(3)

\operatorname{KW}(p)
holds for every prime number
p
.

Proof.

The implications
1
\rightarrow

2
and
2
\rightarrow

3
are trivial.

Now let us assume that item
3
holds but item
1
does not. By Corollary
2.20
we know that there exists a prime
p
such that
\bigwedge_{k<p}\operatorname{AC}(k)
holds but
\operatorname{AC}(p)
does not. By our assumption we know that
\operatorname{KW}(p)
holds, thus
\bigwedge_{k\leq p}\operatorname{KW}(k)
also holds. However, as we have seen, this implies
\operatorname{AC}(p)
, a contradiction.
∎

3.
Promise compactness

In this section we introduce the promise version of compactness principles and examine some their basic properties. In particular, we show that pp-constructions lead to implications between the compactness principles over the corresponding templates.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a finite promise template. Then we write
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
, called the
compactness principles
over
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
, for the following statement.

For every structure
\mathfrak{I}
with the same signature as
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
if for every finite substructure of
\mathfrak{I}
admits a homomorphism to
\mathfrak{A}
then
\mathfrak{I}
admits a homomorphism to
\mathfrak{B}
.

We write
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
for
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{A})}
, and we call it the
compactness principle
over
\mathfrak{A}
.

Note that all compactness principles
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
are theorems of
\operatorname{ZFC}
. In this paper, however, all results are understood over
\operatorname{ZF}
, unless indicated otherwise.

In
[
KTV23
]
it is shown that if
\mathfrak{A}
pp-constructs
\mathfrak{B}
then
K_{\mathfrak{A}}
implies
K_{\mathfrak{B}}
over
\operatorname{ZF}
. We will show that this result generalizes to promise templates as well.

The following definition is motivated by Definition 2.6 in
[
KTV23
]
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
and
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
be finite promise templates. Then we say that
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})

finitely reduces
to
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
if there exists an operation
\Gamma
mapping the instances of
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
to instances of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
such that

(1)

for every structure
\mathfrak{I}
of
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
if
\mathfrak{I}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}
then
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}

(2)

for every structure
\mathfrak{I}
of
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
if
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})\rightarrow\mathfrak{B}
then
\mathfrak{I}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}
, and

(3)

if there exists a finite substructure
\mathfrak{H}
of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
that does not admit a homomorphism to
\mathfrak{A}
then there exists a finite substructure of
\mathfrak{F}
of
\mathfrak{I}
that does not admit a homomorphism to
\mathfrak{D}
.

Remark 3.3
.

We remark that our definition of finite reductions slightly differs from the one given in
[
KTV23
]
in that in the latter it was also assumed that there exist concrete operations mapping homomorphisms to homomorphisms as in item
1
. However, this strong version is not required for any of our arguments regarding compactness statements.

Similarly to the argument in
[
KTV23
]
we can show the following.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let us assume that
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D},\Gamma
be as in item
1
in Definition
3.2
, and let us assume that for every finite substructure
\mathfrak{H}
of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
there exists a finite structure
\mathfrak{F}
of
\mathfrak{I}
such that
\mathfrak{H}\rightarrow\Gamma(\mathfrak{F})
. Then item
3
in Definition
3.2
also holds.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{I}
be an instance of
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
, and let
\mathfrak{H}
be a finite substructure of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
that does not admit a homomorphism to
\mathfrak{A}
. We know that there exists some finite substructure of
\mathfrak{F}
such that
\mathfrak{H}\rightarrow\Gamma(\mathfrak{F})
. Then
\Gamma(\mathfrak{F})\nrightarrow\mathfrak{A}
, and thus
\mathfrak{F}\nrightarrow\mathfrak{C}
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
and

3.2.
Compactness principles provable in

We close this section off by characterizing those finite promise templates

\operatorname{ZF}

if and only if the

has

. In this subsection we show that the analogous characterization also holds for finite promise templates.

We first recall the definition of width 1 for promise templates.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be an arbitrary relational structure. Then we denote by
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{A})
, called the

\mathfrak{A}
, the relational structure similar to
\mathfrak{A}
whose domain set is
\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{A})\setminus\{\emptyset\}
and for each relational symbol

of arity

we have
(S_{0},\dots,S_{k-1})\in R^{\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{A})}
if and only if for all
j\in k
and

there exists a

such that
(a_{0},\dots,a_{j-1},b_{j},a_{j+1},\dots,a_{k})\in R^{\mathfrak{A}}

We say that a promise template is
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})

\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{A})
homomorphically maps to
\mathfrak{B}

We note the width 1 templates are usually defined differently, for instance by solvability the corresponding PCSP by arc consistency, however the equivalence to our definition can be seen easily, see the discussion in
[

]

We write
\mathfrak{H}
for the structure with

and whose relations are

is usually called

Theorem 3.12

(
[

]
, Theorem 7.4)
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a finite promise template. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
has width 1.

(2)

(\mathfrak{H},\mathfrak{H})
pp-constructs
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
.

.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a finite promise template which has width 1. Then

is provable in
\operatorname{ZF}
.

Proof.

The case when
\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{B}
is shown in
[

]
. In particular,

is provable in
\operatorname{ZF}
.

Now let

be an arbitrary finite promise template. Then by Theorem

we know that

.
∎

Remark 3.14

We remark that the proof of

4.
Finite choice from the absence of cyclic polymorphisms

In this section we show that if a promise template does not have a cyclic polymorphism then the corresponding compactness principle implies the axiom
\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
.

The heart of the proof is the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p
be a prime and let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a finite promise template which does not have a cyclic polymorphism of arity
p
. Then
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
implies
\operatorname{KW}(p)
.

Proof.

Let
X
be family of sets of size
p
. We will find a function
X\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\bigcup X)
such that
f(C)
is a proper subset of
C
for all
C\in X
. We can assume without loss of generality that the elements of
X
are pairwise disjoint. We define a structure
\mathfrak{M}
with
\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{M})=M\coloneqq\bigcup_{C\in X}\prescript{C\!}{}{A}
similar to
\mathfrak{A}
as follows. Let
R
be a relation of
\mathfrak{A}
of arity
k
. Then for each
C\in X
the tuple
((x_{c}^{1})_{c\in C},\dots,(x_{c}^{k})_{c\in C})\in(\prescript{C\!}{}{A})^{k}
is contained in
R^{\mathfrak{M}}
if and only if
(x_{c}^{1},\dots,x_{c}^{k})\in R
for all
c\in C
. No other tuples are in the relation
R^{\mathfrak{M}}
. Note that
\mathfrak{M}
is a disjoint union of copies of
\mathfrak{A}^{p}
. If
F\subseteq M
is finite then
F
is contained in a structure isomorphic to some finite disjoint union of copies
\mathfrak{A}^{p}
. Clearly, these structures homomorphically map to
\mathfrak{A}
, for instance we can take any projection map on each copy of
\mathfrak{A}^{p}
. Therefore, by our assumption,
\mathfrak{M}
admits a homomorphism
h
to
\mathfrak{B}
.

Now, let
\prec
be any ordering of the
p
-ary polymorphisms of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
. Such an ordering exists since both
A
and
B
, and thus also
\prescript{\prescript{p\!}{}{A}\!}{}{B}
are finite. Now let
C\in X
be arbitrary. We define
f(C)
as follows. For a map
\alpha:C\rightarrow p
and
f\in\prescript{\prescript{C\!}{}{A}\!}{}{B}
we write
f_{\alpha}
for the map from
\prescript{p\!}{}{A}\rightarrow B
which maps each tuple
x\in\prescript{p\!}{}{A}
to
f(x\circ\alpha)
. Then
({h|}_{\prescript{C\!}{}{A}})_{\alpha}
is a polymorphism of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
for all bijections
\alpha
from
C
to
p
. We define
g(C)
to be the
\prec
-smallest polymorphism which can be obtained this way and we define

f(C)\coloneqq\{\beta^{-1}(0)\mid\beta\colon C\rightarrow p,({h|}_{\prescript{C\!}{}{A}})_{\beta}=g(C)\}.

We show that this
f
suffices.

Clearly,
\alpha^{-1}(0)\in f(C)
for the map
\alpha
in the definition of
g(C)
. Thus,
f(C)
is a nonempty subset of
C
for all
C\in X
. We need to show that
f(C)\neq C
. Let
\Gamma\coloneqq\{\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}(p):g(C)_{\sigma}=g(C)\}
. Then clearly
\Gamma
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(p)
. We claim that
\Gamma
is not transitive. Suppose on the contrary that
\Gamma
is transitive. Then the stabilizer of each element is an index
p
subgroup of
\Gamma
. In particular
p\mid|\Gamma|
. Thus, by Cauchy’s theorem it follows that
\Gamma
contains some element
\gamma
of order
p
. This is only possible if
\gamma
is a
p
-cycle, say
\gamma=(i_{0}\,\dots\,i_{p-1})
. Let
g^{\prime}\coloneqq(x_{0},\dots,x_{p-1})\mapsto g(C)(x_{i_{0}},\dots,x_{i_{p-1}})
. Then we have

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x_{1},\dots,x_{p-1},x_{0})=

\displaystyle g(C)(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{p-1}},x_{i_{0}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(C)(x_{\gamma(i_{0})},\dots,x_{\gamma(i_{p-1})})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(C)(x_{i_{0}},\dots,x_{i_{p-1}})=g^{\prime}(x_{0},\dots,x_{p-1})

where the second-to-last equality follows from the definition of
\Gamma
. This implies that
g^{\prime}
is a cyclic polymorphism of
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
(of arity
p
) which contradicts our assumption.

Now let
\alpha
be a bijection
p\rightarrow C
as in the definition of
g(C)
. Then we have

\displaystyle f(C)=

\displaystyle\{\beta^{-1}(0)\mid\beta\colon C\rightarrow p,({h|}_{\prescript{C\!}{}{A}})_{\beta}=g(C)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\alpha^{-1}(\sigma^{-1}(0))\mid\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}(p),({h|}_{\prescript{C\!}{}{A}})_{\sigma\circ\alpha}=g(C)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\alpha^{-1}(\sigma^{-1}(0))\mid\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}(p),g(C)_{\sigma}=g(C)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{-1}(\Gamma(0)).

Since
\Gamma
is not transitive, this implies that
f(C)\neq\alpha^{-1}(p)=C
which finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Let us denote by
C_{n}
the
n
-cycle digraph for
n\in\omega
. It was shown in
[
RTW17
]
, Proposition 6.2 that
K_{C_{p}}
implies
\operatorname{KW}(p)
for any prime number
p
. This now also follows from our general result using the following observation.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}
be a finite loopless graph such that
C_{p}\rightarrow\mathfrak{G}
. Then
(C_{p},\mathfrak{G})
does not have a cyclic polymorphism of arity
p
.

Proof.

Let us assume for contradiction that
f\in\operatorname{Pol}(C_{p},\mathfrak{G})
is cyclic and of arity
p
. Then since
f
is a polymorphism, it follows that
f(0,1,\dots,p-1)
is connected to
f(1,\dots,p-1,0)
in
\mathfrak{G}
. On the other hand, by the cyclicity of
f
we get
f(0,1\dots,p-1)=f(1,\dots,p)
which would mean that
\mathfrak{G}
has a loop.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{G}
be a finite loopless graph such that
C_{p}\rightarrow\mathfrak{G}
. Then
K_{(C_{p},\mathfrak{G})}
implies
\operatorname{KW}(p)
.

By Proposition 6.1 in
[
RTW17
]
we know that
\operatorname{AC}(n)
implies
K_{C_{n}}
for all

. Considering that for
p=2
and

the axioms
\operatorname{KW}(p)
and
\operatorname{AC}(p)
are equivalent, this means that in these cases the statement of Corollary
4.3
can also be reversed.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
p\in\{2,3\}
and
\mathfrak{G}
be a finite loopless graph such that
C_{p}\rightarrow\mathfrak{G}
. Then
K_{(C_{p},\mathfrak{G})}
is equivalent to
\operatorname{AC}(p)
.

In the rest of the paper we are only interested in the case when Lemma
4.1
can be applied to all prime numbers

.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a finite promise template which does not have a cyclic polymorphism. Then
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
implies
\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
.

Proof.

Follows from Lemma
4.1
and Corollary
2.21
.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

One can notice that in Theorem

it is enough to assume that

does not have a cyclic polymorphism of any prime arity. However, this does not make any difference because in any minion the existence of a cyclic operation also implies the existence of a cyclic operation of prime arity.

is uninteresting since in this case it follows from Theorem

that the assumption of Corollary

can only hold if

. However, in the promise setting we have several interesting examples for templates without cyclic polymorphisms.

•

By item

5.
Reductions from weak minion homomorphisms

In this section we consider a more general notion of minion homomorphisms, called
minion homomorphisms
(or
(d,r)
-minion homomorphisms) which was originally introduced in
[
BBKO21
]
. The main purpose of this notion was to give a general algebraic framework for proving some hardness results for PCSPs which do not follow from omniexpressivity. This includes the templates
(K_{k},K_{2k-1})
for all
k\geq 3
and
(H_{k},H_{c})
for all
2\leq k\leq c
, see our discussion below. We will show how this algebraic approach can be adapted in the context of compactness principles, and as a result we will obtain that over
\operatorname{ZF}
all compactness principles corresponding to the aforementioned promise templates are equivalent to
\operatorname{UL}
.

Notation 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a minion. A
chain of minors
is a sequence of minors

t_{0}\xrightarrow{\pi_{0,1}}t_{1}\xrightarrow{\pi_{1,2}}\dots\xrightarrow{\pi_{r-1,r}}t_{r}

in
\mathcal{M}
. For such a sequence for
i<j
we write
\pi_{i,j}
for the composition
\pi_{j-1,j}\circ\dots\circ\pi_{i,i+1}
. Note that in this case we have
t_{i}\xrightarrow{\pi_{i,j}}t_{j}
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}
be minions, and let
d,r\in\omega
. Then a map
\xi:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{N})
is a
(d,r)
-minion homomorphism
if

(1)

\xi
preserves arities, i.e., for all
t\in\mathcal{M}
all elements of
\xi(t)
have the same arity as
t
,

(2)

|\xi(t)|\leq d
for all
t\in\mathcal{M}
, and

(3)

for all chains of minors
t_{0}\xrightarrow{\pi_{0,1}}t_{1}\xrightarrow{\pi_{1,2}}\dots\xrightarrow{\pi_{r-1,r}}t_{r}
in
\mathcal{M}
there exists
i<j,g\in\xi(t_{i}),h\in\xi(t_{j})
such that
g\xrightarrow{\pi_{i,j}}h
.

We say that a map
\xi:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{N})
is a
weak minion homomorphism
if it is a
(d,r)
-minion homomorphism for some
d,r\in\omega
.

Remark 5.3
.

Note that a minion homomorphism is the same as a
(1,1)
-minion homomorphism.

We know by the combination of Theorem
2.4
and Lemma
3.8
that if there exists a minion homomorphism from
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
to
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
then
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
finitely reduces to
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
, and thus by Lemma
3.5

K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
implies
K_{(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})}
over
\operatorname{ZF}
. When trying to generalize this implication for weak minion homomorphisms we run into several challenges. The first one is that at the moment there is no known corresponding description of weak minion homomorphisms on the level of relational structures (similar to pp-constructions). For this reason, we have to work with the minions directly. Fortunately, the complexity reduction presented in Theorem B2 in
[
BBKO21
]
can easily be adapted to also give a finite reduction. Another slight technical issue is that in these constructions it is not clear how to avoid choice entirely. However, as we will see, we can make our proofs work assuming
\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
which will be enough in our concrete applications using Corollary
4.8
.

Theorem 5.4

(
\operatorname{ZF}+\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
)
.

Let us assume that there exists a weak minion homomorphism from
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
to
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
. Then
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
finitely reduces to
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
.

Before proving Theorem
5.4
, we discuss some of its most important consequences.

The following result, although not explicitly stated, is included in the proof of Theorem 12 in
[
NVWŽ25
]
.

Theorem 5.5
.

For all
c\geq 2
there exists a weak minion homomorphism from
\operatorname{Pol}(H_{2},H_{c})
to
\mathscr{P}
.

Combining this with Theorem
5.4
we obtain the following.

Theorem 5.6

(
\operatorname{ZF}
)
.

Let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a finite promise template which does not have an Olšák polymorphism. Then
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
is equivalent to
\operatorname{UL}
.

Proof.

We have already seen that
\operatorname{UL}
implies all compactness principles over
\operatorname{ZF}
.

For the other direction let
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
be a promise template without an Olšák polymorphism and assume that
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
holds. By item (6) of Theorem
2.17
we know that
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
pp-constructs
(H_{2},H_{c})
for some

, and thus by Corollary

we know that
K_{(H_{2},H_{c})}
holds. Then by Corollary
4.8
we know that
\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
holds and thus we can apply Theorem

. By Theorems

and

we can conclude that
(K_{3},K_{3})
finitely reduces to

which means that

, and thus also

holds.
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

Any of the following statements are equivalent to
\operatorname{UL}
over
\operatorname{ZF}
.

(1)

K_{(K_{k},K_{c})}
for any

(2)

Follows from Theorems

We finally mention that by a relatively easy set theoretical argument one can also remove the uniform upper bounds in item

Definition 5.8

For a

is

if it has a homomorphism to

free structure of
\mathcal{M}
generated by
\mathfrak{S}
, denoted by
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathfrak{S})
, is the structure similar to
\mathfrak{S}
whose domain set is the set of
n
-ary functions in
\mathcal{M}
and for a
k
-ary relational symbol
R
in the signature of
\mathfrak{S}
we define
R^{\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathfrak{S})}
to be the set of those
k
-tuples
(f_{0},\dots,f_{k-1})
from
\mathcal{M}^{(n)}
such that there exists an
m
-ary function
g\in\mathcal{M}
satisfying

f_{i}(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1})=g(x_{r_{0}(i)},\dots,x_{r_{m-1}(i)})

where
m=|R^{\mathfrak{S}}|
and
r_{0},\dots,r_{m-1}
are tuples enumerating the relation
R^{\mathfrak{S}}
.

We know that for any
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathcal{M}
as above there exists a minion homomorphism from
\mathcal{M}
to
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{S},\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathfrak{S}))
, see for instance the discussion after Definition 4.1 in
[
BBKO21
]
. Thus, by Theorem
2.4
we know that if
\mathcal{M}=\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
and
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are finite then
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
pp-constructs
(\mathfrak{S},\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathfrak{S}))
. Combining this with Theorem
3.8
, we can conclude the following.

Lemma 5.23

(
\operatorname{ZF}
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{S}
be finite structures with
\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{S})\in\omega
. Then
(\mathfrak{S},\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathfrak{S}))
finitely reduces to
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
5.4
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
. Then we construct an appropriate structure
\mathfrak{S}
for which we can show that
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
finitely reduces to
(\mathfrak{S},\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathfrak{S}))
. By Lemma
5.23
this implies Theorem
5.4
.

By our assumption there exists a
(d,r)
-minion homomorphism
\xi
from
\mathcal{M}
to
\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
for some
d,r\in\omega
. Let
k_{0},\dots,k_{r}
be as in the conclusion of Theorem
5.13
applied in the case when
n=|\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{D})|
and
m
is the maximal arity of the relations of
\mathfrak{C}
(and thus also of
\mathfrak{D})
. By Theorem
5.14
the existence of such numbers is provable in
\operatorname{ZF}+\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
.

We define the structure
\mathfrak{S}
as follows. The domain set of
\mathfrak{S}
is
S\coloneqq|C|^{k_{0}}
and its relations are all partial functions from
S
to
S
(each of them represented by itself). Then we define an operation
\Gamma
which maps instances of
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
to
(\mathfrak{S},\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathfrak{S}))
. Let
\mathfrak{I}
be an instance of
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
. The domain set of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
is
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=0}^{r}{V\choose k_{i}}
. For an
U\in\mathcal{X}
we write
D_{U}
for the set of homomorphisms from
\mathfrak{I}|_{U}
to
\mathfrak{C}
. Then for each
U\in\mathcal{X}
we pick some bijection
\sigma_{U}\coloneqq D_{U}\rightarrow|D_{U}|
, note that
|D_{U}|\leq S
. This is possible to do simultaneously by
\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
. Then for all pairs
U,W\in\mathcal{X}
with
W\subseteq U
we put the pair
(U,W)
in the relation
\{(\sigma_{U}(f),\sigma_{W}(f|_{W})):f\in D_{U}\}
in
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
.

We claim that
\Gamma
gives a finite reduction from
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
to
(\mathfrak{S},\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathfrak{S}))
.

Let us first observe that if
h:\mathfrak{I}\rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
is a homomorphism then then
U\mapsto\sigma_{U}(h|_{U})
is a homomorphism from
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
to
\mathfrak{S}
. Next we check
\Gamma
that satisfies item
3
in Definition
3.2
by using Lemma
3.4
. If
\mathfrak{H}
is a finite substructure of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
then we define
F\coloneqq\bigcup_{U\in H}U
, and we write
\mathfrak{F}
for the substructure of
\mathfrak{I}
induced on
F
. Then
\mathfrak{F}
is finite, and
\mathfrak{H}
is a substructure of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{F})
. Therefore, the hypotheses of Lemma
3.4
hold, and thus
\Gamma
satisfies item
3
.

It remains to show that item
2
in Definition
3.2
holds for
\Gamma
, i.e, if
s\colon\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})\rightarrow\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathfrak{S})
then
\mathfrak{I}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}
. This essentially follows from the same argument as the one used for proving soundness in the proof of Theorem B.2 in
[
BBKO21
]
. For the sake of completeness, we present this argument here as well.

We use the notation
\iota_{m}
for the map
\operatorname{id}_{m}\cup\,((S\setminus m)\times\{0\})
. Let
U\in{V\choose k_{i}}
. Then we define the
D(U)
-ary function
t(U)
in
\mathcal{M}
by
t(U)\coloneqq(s(U))_{\iota_{|D(U)|}}
, i.e.,

t(U)(x_{1},\dots,x_{|D(U)|})=s(U)(x_{1},\dots,x_{|D(U)|},x_{0},\dots,x_{0}).

We claim that if
W\subseteq U
then
t(U)\xrightarrow{\pi_{U,W}}t(W)
where
\pi_{U,W}:|D(U)|\rightarrow|D(W)|,m\mapsto\sigma_{W}((\sigma_{U}^{-1})(m)|_{W})
. By definition
(U,W)
is contained in the relation
\{(\sigma_{U}(f),\sigma_{W}(f|_{W})):f\in D_{U}\}
and
s
is a homomorphism, it follows that there exists some
g\in\mathcal{M}
of arity
|D(U)|
such that

•

s(U)(x_{0},\dots,x_{S-1})=g(x_{0},\dots,x_{|D(U)|-1})
and

•

s(W)(x_{0},\dots,x_{S-1})=g(x_{\pi_{U,W}(0)},\dots,x_{\pi_{U,W}(|D(U)|-1)})
.

This also implies that
s(U)
only depends on the first
|D(U)|
and
s(W)
only depends on the first
|D(W)|
coordinates. In particular,
g=t(U)
and
g_{\pi_{U,W}}=t(W)
which finishes the proof of our claim.

We construct a sequence
(\mathcal{I}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{r})
of PASes of arities
k_{0},\dots,k_{r}
on
V=\operatorname{Dom}(\mathfrak{I})
as follows. Let
U\in{V\choose k_{i}}
. Then for a
g\in\xi(t(U))
we define

Z_{U}(g):U\rightarrow S,u\mapsto g(\sigma^{-1}_{U}(0)(u),\dots,\sigma^{-1}_{U}(|D(U)|-1)(u)),

and we put

\mathcal{I}_{i}(U)\coloneqq\{Z_{U}(g):g\in\xi(t(U))\}.

It is clear from the definition that all
\mathcal{I}_{i}
has value at most
d_{i}
. Moreover, since
\xi(t(U))\in\operatorname{Pol}(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{D})
it follows that all elements of
\mathcal{I}_{i}
are partial homomorphisms to
\mathfrak{D}
. We now show that the sequence
(\mathcal{I}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{r})
is consistent. Let
V\supseteq U_{0}\supseteq\dots\supseteq U_{r}
with
|U_{i}|=k_{i}
. Then, as we have seen,

t(U_{0})\xrightarrow{\pi_{U_{0},U_{1}}}t(U_{1})\xrightarrow{\pi_{U_{1},U_{2}}}\dots\xrightarrow{\pi_{U_{r-1},U_{r}}}t(U_{r})

is a sequence of minors in
\mathcal{M}
. Since
\xi
is a
(d,r)
-minion homomorphism, it follows that there exist
i<j,g\in\xi(t(U_{i})),h\in\xi(h(U_{j}))
such that
g\xrightarrow{\pi_{U_{i},U_{j}}}h
. Then for all
u\in U_{j}
we have

\displaystyle Z_{U_{j}}(h)(u)=

\displaystyle h(\sigma^{-1}(0)(u),\dots,\sigma^{-1}(|D(W)|-1)(u))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{\pi(U_{i},U_{j})}(\sigma^{-1}(0)(u),\dots,\sigma^{-1}(|D(W)|-1)(u))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(\sigma^{-1}(0)(u),\dots,\sigma^{-1}(|D(U_{i})|-1)(u))=Z_{U_{i}}(g)(u).

This means that
Z_{U_{i}}(g)|_{U_{j}}=Z_{U_{j}}(h)
, and thus
\mathcal{I}_{i}|_{U_{j}}\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}\neq\emptyset
which shows that
(\mathcal{I}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{r})
is consistent.

By the choice of
k_{0},\dots,k_{r}
we can conclude that
\mathcal{I}_{i}
has an
m
-solution
h
for some
i
. Since
\mathcal{I}_{i}
consist of partial homomorphisms to
\mathfrak{D}
it follows that the restriction of
h
to every at most
m
-element substructure of
\mathfrak{I}
is a homomorphisms to
\mathfrak{D}
. Since all relations of
\mathfrak{D}
has arity at most
m
this means that
h
is in fact a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{I}
to
\mathfrak{D}
.
∎

6.
Conclusion and open problems

As we have seen, a lot of analogies can be drawn between reduction between promise CSPs and the reduction between the corresponding compactness principles, and we have shown that for several promise templates which are known to have hard CSPs, the corresponding compactness principles are as strong as possible, i.e., they are equivalent to the ultrafilter lemma. It would be interesting to see how far these analogies can be pushed, for example one can ask for any given promise template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
for which
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is known or conjectured to be
\mathbf{NP}
-complete, whether
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
is still equivalent to the ultrafilter lemma.

Question 6.1
.

Is
K_{(K_{3},K_{6})}
equivalent to
\operatorname{UL}
over
\operatorname{ZF}
?

More generally, we can make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 6.2
.

For all
3\leq c\in\omega
the compactness principle
K_{(K_{3},K_{c}})
is equivalent to
\operatorname{UL}
over
\operatorname{ZF}
.

Recall that by Corollary
2.18
we know for any
3\leq k\leq c
there exists some
d\in\omega
such that
(K_{k},K_{c})
pp-constructs
(K_{3},K_{d})
. Therefore, by Corollary
3.9
we can conclude that
K_{(K_{k},K_{c})}
implies
K_{(K_{3},K_{d})}
. This means that a positive answer to Conjecture
6.2
would imply the same conclusion for all compactness principles
K_{(K_{k},K_{c})}:3\leq k\leq c
.

It could also be worthwhile to look at some of the other methods for proving hardness of PCSPs in some of the papers mentioned in the introduction (
[
KOWŽ23
,
AFO+25
,
AGH17
,
FNO+23
]
), to see if they can be adapted in the context of compactness principles.

We can also turn around the questions above, and ask whether there exists any promise template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
which has a tractable PCSP but
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
still implies
\operatorname{UL}
. Note that by the results of
[
KTV23
]
we know that this is impossible in the case when
\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{B}
. A promise template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is called
finitely tractable
if there exists some finite, not omniexpressive structure
\mathfrak{C}
such that
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is a homomorphic relaxation of
(\mathfrak{C},\mathfrak{C})
. Note that if
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is finitely tractable then it follows from the finite-domain CSP dichotomy theorem
[
Bul17
,
Zhu20
]
that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
is tractable. Moreover, in this case
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
is true in a model of
\operatorname{ZF}
as in Theorem
1.1
, in particular it does not imply
\operatorname{UL}
. The prototypical example for a promise template which is not finitely tractable, but has a tractable PCSP is
(T,H_{2})
where
T
is the template structure for 1-in-3-SAT, i.e.,
\operatorname{Dom}(T)=2
, and its only (ternary) relation is
\{(0,0,1),(0,1,0),(1,0,0)\}
[
BG18
,
BKO19
]
.

Question 6.3
.

Is
K_{(T,H_{2})}
equivalent to
\operatorname{UL}
over
\operatorname{ZF}
?

A slightly unsatisfactory aspect of the results of Section
5
is that in order to make the general reductions work we need to invoke
\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
. This motivates the following question.

Question 6.4
.

Is Theorem
5.4
or Theorem
5.13
provable in
\operatorname{ZF}
?

Finally, we can ask whether the converse of any of the main results of Section
4
holds.

Question 6.5
.

Does there exist a promise template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
which has a cyclic polymorphism of arity
p
for some prime
p
and
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
implies
\operatorname{KW}(p)
over
\operatorname{ZF}
.

Question 6.6
.

Does there exist a promise template
(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})
which has a cyclic polymorphism and
K_{(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})}
implies
\operatorname{AC}^{*}(<\!\omega)
over
\operatorname{ZF}
?
