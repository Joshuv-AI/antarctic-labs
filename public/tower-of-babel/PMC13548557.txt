Thrombectomy reduces femoral vein valve damage after acute iliofemoral vein thrombosis: a combined retrospective and prospective cohort study
Authors: Yanyang Wang, Yaoguo Yang, Sheng Wang, Liang Zhao, Jie Zhang, Zhong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (IFDVT) often leads to femoral vein valve incompetence and post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS). This study evaluated whether thrombus volume reduction surgery (TVRS) is associated with better valve function preservation compared with simple anticoagulation (SA) in patients with acute IFDVT.MethodsA combined retrospective and prospective cohort study involving 504 patients with acute IFDVT compared TVRS (n = 238) and SA (n = 266). The primary outcome was the femoral vein valve preservation rate at 12 months. Secondary outcomes included PTS incidence and quality of life. Subgroup analysis compared two pharmacomechanical thrombectomy devices: AngioJet and Acostream. Propensity score matching (1:1 nearest neighbor, caliper 0.05), inverse probability of treatment weighting, and E-value sensitivity analysis were employed to address confounding. An exploratory mediation analysis (hypothesis-generating) examined whether valve preservation potentially mediates the association between TVRS and PTS reduction.ResultsThe TVRS group showed a significantly higher valve preservation rate (75.8% vs. 43.9%, P < 0.001) and a lower incidence of moderate-to-severe PTS (13.6% vs. 34.8%, P = 0.002) compared with the SA group. TVRS patients also achieved better quality of life scores with comparable safety profiles. In a pre-specified, exploratory subgroup analysis of patients who underwent PMT with complete ultrasound follow-up data (n = 50 per device), AngioJet appeared to be associated with a higher valve preservation rate compared with Acostream (82% vs. 70%, P = 0.04), although this comparison was underpowered, non-randomized, and should be considered strictly hypothesis-generating.ConclusionsTVRS was associated with improved femoral vein valve preservation, lower PTS incidence, and enhanced quality of life compared with SA. The AngioJet system may be associated with better valve protection than the Acostream system, but this finding is exploratory and requires validation in randomized trials. These observations support the hypothesis that early thrombectomy intervention may be beneficial for long-term venous function, but cannot establish causality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06034-x.Keywords: Deep vein thrombosis, Iliofemoral, Thrombectomy, Femoral vein valve, Post-thrombotic syndrome, Pharmacomechanical thrombectomy

Introduction

Acute iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (IFDVT) is a severe clinical entity with significant long-term morbidity [1]. Beyond the acute risk of pulmonary embolism, the sequelae of thrombosis, particularly post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), profoundly impact patient quality of life [2, 3]. PTS develops due to two principal hemodynamic sequelae: venous obstruction and, more critically, valve incompetence [4]. The femoral vein harbors the most important valves in the lower limb, as its superficial location makes it a primary target for surgical repair. Damage to these valves, especially the first pair near the inguinal ligament, is a major determinant of chronic venous hypertension and the development of PTS [5].

Current treatment modalities for IFDVT include simple anticoagulation (SA) and thrombus volume reduction surgery (TVRS) strategies, such as catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) and pharmacomechanical thrombectomy (PMT) [6–8]. While TVRS, particularly PMT combined with selective iliac vein stenting, has shown promise in reducing PTS incidence by rapidly restoring venous patency, its direct effect on femoral vein valve preservation has not been the primary focus of most studies [9]. The “open vein hypothesis” posits that early restoration of flow may protect valve function, yet clinical evidence directly comparing valve outcomes between TVRS and SA is lacking [10].

Furthermore, the landscape of PMT devices is evolving. Devices like the AngioJet (Boston Scientific) operate on a rheolytic, jet-pulse suction principle, while others like Acostream employ continuous negative pressure suction [11]. Theoretically, the mechanism of thrombus extraction may influence the degree of mechanical stress on valve leaflets [12, 13]. However, no study has investigated whether different PMT devices have differential impacts on long-term femoral valve function and subsequent PTS [14, 15].

Therefore, we conducted a combined retrospective and prospective cohort study to address these critical gaps. Our primary aim was to determine whether TVRS, compared to SA, improves femoral vein valve preservation, reduces PTS, and enhances quality of life in acute IFDVT patients. A secondary aim was to compare the outcomes of two commonly used PMT devices, AngioJet and Acostream, regarding valve preservation and clinical efficacy.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This single-center study comprised a retrospective (September 2016–April 2023) and a prospective (May 2023–April 2024) cohort conducted at Beijing Anzhen Hospital. The institutional review board approved the protocol, and written informed consent was obtained for prospective participants. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) acute iliofemoral DVT (onset ≤ 14 days) involving the entire femoral vein; (3) completed 12-month follow-up; and (4) no prior DVT or venous insufficiency (r-VCSS ≤ 2). Exclusion criteria included severe organ dysfunction, active malignancy, or immobility; inferior vena cava thrombosis; contrast media contraindication; and chronic thrombosis on initial ultrasound.

Of 1,120 screened records in the retrospective arm, 680 were excluded (chronic/recurrent thrombosis, prior DVT, incomplete records, or other reasons), yielding 360 patients. Of 180 assessed in the prospective arm, 36 were excluded (ineligibility, consent withdrawal, or pre-treatment loss), yielding 144 patients. Ultimately, 504 patients were enrolled and assigned to the thrombus volume reduction surgery (TVRS; n = 238: retrospective = 170, prospective = 68) or simple anticoagulation (SA; n = 266: retrospective = 190, prospective = 76) groups. Retrospective data were extracted via chart review of routine care, whereas the prospective arm utilized a standardized protocol with blinded assessments at 3, 6, and 12 months. At 12 months, follow-up was completed by 96.6% (230/238) of TVRS and 97.0% (258/266) of SA patients (Supplementary Figure S1).

Group assignment and treatment protocol

Patients were non-randomly assigned to the thrombus volume reduction surgery (TVRS) or simple anticoagulation (SA) group based on shared decision-making, evaluating patient preference, thrombus burden, procedural risk, and contraindications to thrombolysis; we acknowledge the potential for selection bias. All interventions were performed by/supervised by attending vascular surgeons (> 50 DVT cases/year). Device selection (AngioJet or Acostream) was based on operator preference and thrombus characteristics.

The TVRS protocol comprised: (1) IVC filter placement via contralateral femoral access; (2) thrombectomy via antegrade popliteal access. Thrombus burden was scored (0 = patent, 1 = partial, 2 = complete occlusion) across seven venous segments (IVC to popliteal) by a senior ultrasonographer preoperatively and within 7 days postoperatively to calculate the clearance rate ([Pre-op − Post-op score] / Pre-op score × 100%). If clearance was < 90%, adjunctive catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT; urokinase 10,000 U/kg/day for 24 h) was administered; (3) iliac vein stenting for > 50% residual compression.

The SA group received therapeutic LMWH (100 IU/kg q12h) transitioned to rivaroxaban, with optional venoactive drugs and IVC filters.

Symptom onset-to-treatment times were comparable between TVRS (median 5.2 days, IQR 3.1–8.7) and SA (6.1 days, 3.8–9.5; P = 0.12) groups, and between AngioJet (4.9 days, 2.8–8.1) and Acostream (5.5 days, 3.2–9.0; P = 0.34) subgroups.

Data collection and outcome measures

Baseline data including demographics, comorbidities, and laboratory values were collected. Additional baseline variables included D-dimer level at presentation and history of prior anticoagulation use. The primary outcome was femoral vein valve function preservation at 12 months, assessed by color Doppler ultrasound performed by two independent vascular sonographers who were blinded to treatment assignment. Valve incompetence was defined as a reflux time > 1.0 s or a reflux velocity > 30 cm/s.

based on the criteria established by Labropoulos et al. and de Maeseneer et al. [16, 17]. Inter-observer agreement between the two blinded sonographers was assessed in 50 randomly selected examinations and demonstrated almost perfect agreement (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.72–0.96; intraclass correlation coefficient for continuous reflux time = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.78–0.93, Supplementary Table S1). Disagreements were resolved by adjudication from a third senior sonographer.

Additionally, to validate the ultrasound findings, descending venography was performed during inferior vena cava (IVC) filter retrieval, typically 2–3 months post-procedure, to assess valve function (Kistner grade).

This imaging was limited to patients who underwent filter retrieval (n = 48) and who had complete paired ultrasound and venography data available. The selection for filter retrieval was based on clinical indication (completion of anticoagulation course, resolution of contraindication, or patient preference) rather than research protocol. The results were compared with 12-month ultrasound findings to evaluate diagnostic consistency, and agreement between venography and ultrasound in this subgroup was substantial (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.65–0.93).

Secondary outcomes included: (1) Post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS) incidence assessed by Villalta score at 6 and 12 months, performed by independent assessors blinded to treatment allocation (inter-rater agreement: Cohen’s Kappa = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.70–0.92); (2) quality of life assessed by the Chronic Venous Insufficiency Questionnaire (CIVIQ-20) at 12 months, which is a validated, self-administered instrument for assessing quality of life in patients with chronic venous insufficiency, originally developed and validated by Launois et al.19; (3) peri- and post-procedural complications (bleeding classified by TIMI criteria, hemoglobinuria, access site issues, pulmonary embolism, venous rupture, acute kidney injury).

Statistical analysis

Propensity Score Matching (PSM). To control for confounding by indication, propensity scores for TVRS assignment were estimated via logistic regression including age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and Mewissen score. 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed using a 0.05 logit caliper, yielding 238 matched pairs. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD < 0.1).

Multivariable regression

Multivariable logistic regression for valve preservation utilized backward stepwise selection based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Candidate variables included demographics, Mewissen score, treatment modality, and symptom onset-to-treatment time. Multicollinearity (VIF < 5.0), linearity of continuous predictors (Box-Tidwell test), and model calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test) were assessed.

Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW)

As a sensitivity analysis, stabilized IPTW was performed using the identical propensity score model. Extreme weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles, and balance was evaluated via SMDs.

E-value analysis

E-values were calculated for all endpoints to quantify the minimum strength of association an unmeasured confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to fully explain away the observed associations.

Subgroup and interaction analyses

Pre-specified exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted for device comparisons, with interactions assessed via likelihood ratio tests. Given the exploratory nature, multiplicity corrections were waived; findings are considered hypothesis-generating.

Mediation analysis

Using the counterfactual framework (R 4.3.0), we explored whether 12-month valve preservation mediated the TVRS-PTS association. Indirect effects and mediation proportions were estimated via non-parametric bootstrapping (2,000 resamples), with unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding assessed via VanderWeele bounds. Analyses are strictly hypothesis-generating.

Prediction model

A logistic regression model predicting 12-month valve preservation was developed using stratified random sampling (70% training/30% validation). Discrimination was evaluated by bootstrap-corrected C-index (500 resamples), calibration by calibration curves and Brier score, and clinical utility by decision curve analysis.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 504 patients with acute iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (IFDVT) were enrolled in this study: 360 in the retrospective cohort (TVRS n = 170, SA n = 190) and 144 in the prospective cohort (TVRS n = 68, SA n = 76). The baseline characteristics of the combined cohort after propensity score matching are presented in Table 1. Age, gender, BMI, and comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes were comparable between the two groups, with all standardized mean differences < 0.1 after matching (Supplementary Table S2). There were no statistically significant differences in the preoperative thrombus burden score (Mewissen score) between the TVRS and SA groups (P > 0.05).

Primary outcome: preservation of femoral vein valve function

At the 12-month follow-up, the TVRS group showed a significantly higher rate of femoral vein valve preservation compared with the SA group (Fig. 1). Ultrasound evaluation, performed by sonographers blinded to treatment assignment (inter-observer agreement: Kappa = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.72–0.96), revealed that the valve preservation rate in the TVRS group was 75.8%, which was higher than the 43.9% observed in the SA group (P < 0.001). This trend was consistent across both the retrospective cohort (77.3% vs. 44.0%) and the prospective cohort (74.2% vs. 43.9%). Furthermore, in the subgroup of patients who underwent descending venography during IVC filter retrieval, the proportion of patients with Kistner grade 0–1 (indicating no reflux or mild reflux) was higher in the TVRS group compared with the SA group (68.2% vs. 37.9%, P < 0.001), consistent with the ultrasound findings (venography-ultrasound agreement: Kappa = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.65–0.93).

Secondary outcomes: Post-Thrombotic Syndrome (PTS) and quality of life

At the 12-month follow-up, the incidence of Post-thrombotic Syndrome (PTS) was evaluated using the Villalta scoring system, revealing a difference between the TVRS group and the SA group. Subgroup analysis: comparison of angiojet and acostream devices

In a pre-specified, exploratory subgroup analysis of patients who underwent PMT as the sole interventional modality with complete ultrasound follow-up data (n = 50 per device), the AngioJet group demonstrated a higher femoral vein valve preservation rate than the Acostream group (82.0% [41/50] vs. 70.0% [35/50], P = 0.04), consistent with the hypothesis that its pulsed, jet-driven intermittent suction exerts less traumatic shear stress on valve leaflets than continuous negative pressure suction. This association persisted after adjustment for thrombus characteristics, anatomical distribution, and operator experience (adjusted OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.27–0.98, P = 0.043). Interaction tests revealed no significant effect modification by thrombus burden (P = 0.42; TVRS vs. SA Mewissen score, P-interaction = 1.000), symptom duration (P = 0.55), or age (P = 0.38; continuous age P = 0.569; age dichotomized at 60 years P-interaction = 0.157), suggesting the TVRS benefit for valve preservation is generally consistent across subgroups; however, a numerically larger treatment effect was observed in patients < 60 years (OR = 4.18, 95% CI: 0.89–19.66) compared to those ≥ 60 years (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 0.45–3.46). Clinically, the AngioJet group exhibited a numerically lower incidence of moderate-to-severe PTS (10.0% vs. 16.0%, P = 0.30) and a significantly better quality of life (CIVIQ-20: 21.8 ± 4.5 vs. 24.1 ± 5.2, P = 0.04), with comparable complication rates. Given that these analyses were underpowered, non-randomized, and subject to selection bias, these device comparisons—including the potentially stronger TVRS effect in younger patients—should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in randomized trials.

Multivariate regression analysis

To determine the independent association of thrombus volume reduction surgery (TVRS) with femoral vein valve preservation, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. Variable selection used backward stepwise selection based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC; final model AIC = 412.3, Supplementary Table S3). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF range: 1.02–1.38; maximum VIF for Mewissen score = 1.38), with all values well below the threshold of 5.0, indicating no significant multicollinearity. The linearity assumption was supported by Box-Tidwell tests (all continuous variables P > 0.05). Model calibration was adequate (Hosmer-Lemeshow test: P = 0.67 in training set, P = 0.54 in validation set).

After adjusting for potential confounding variables including age, gender, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, Mewissen score, and symptom onset-to-treatment time, TVRS remained significantly associated with lower odds of valve insufficiency compared with simple anticoagulation (SA) (Odds Ratio [OR] = 0.26, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.16–0.43, P < 0.001).

In a subgroup analysis focused on patients receiving pharmacomechanical thrombectomy (PMT), a regression model was established to compare the efficacy of the two devices regarding valve preservation. The results showed that the use of the AngioJet system was associated with lower odds of valve insufficiency compared with the Acostream device (adjusted OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.27–0.98, P = 0.043), corresponding to an observed association of approximately 48% lower odds. This finding is consistent with our hypothesis that the distinct mechanical principles of the devices may influence valve preservation; the intermittent jet-suction mechanism of the AngioJet system likely exerts less mechanical stress on the valve leaflets during the thrombectomy process compared with the continuous negative pressure suction utilized by the Acostream device. However, this subgroup analysis was exploratory, non-randomized, and limited by a small sample size (n = 50 per group).

Mediation analysis: valve preservation as a mechanism for PTS reduction

We conducted an exploratory mediation analysis (hypothesis-generating) to examine whether femoral vein valve preservation potentially mediates the association between TVRS and PTS incidence. The mediation model used treatment assignment (TVRS vs. SA) as the independent variable, valve preservation at 12 months as the mediator, and moderate-to-severe PTS development as the outcome.

We performed sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding of the mediator-outcome relationship using the VanderWeele bounds approach(Supplementary Table S4). Assuming an unmeasured confounder associated with both the mediator (valve function) and outcome (PTS) by a risk ratio of 2.0, the adjusted bounds for the indirect effect remained positive (4.2% to 10.8%). Even with stronger unmeasured confounding (RR = 3.0), the indirect effect bounds remained positive (2.1% to 12.5%), supporting the robustness of the mediation findings to moderate unmeasured confounding. These mediation analyses are exploratory and hypothesis-generating; they cannot establish causal mechanisms in the absence of a randomized experimental design.

The remaining 60.9% of the observed association (direct effect: 11.7%, 95% CI: 6.8%-16.6%) represents the association of TVRS with lower PTS through other pathways, potentially including rapid resolution of venous obstruction, reduction of inflammatory damage to the venous wall, and prevention of chronic venous hypertension through mechanisms independent of valve function.

Prediction model and nomogram

We developed a multivariable logistic regression model to predict femoral vein valve preservation at 12 months following IFDVT treatment. The model incorporated eight pre-treatment and treatment variables: age, gender, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, Mewissen score (thrombus burden), treatment modality (TVRS vs. SA), and onset time. The dataset was randomly divided into training (70%, n = 353) and validation (30%, n = 151) sets using stratified random sampling.

Model performance

The prediction model demonstrated discrimination with a C-index of 0.743 (95% CI: 0.680–0.806) in the training set and 0.695 (95% CI: 0.585–0.805) in the validation set. Bootstrap optimism correction based on 500 resamples yielded an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.731. Brier scores were 0.168 (training) and 0.185 (validation), indicating acceptable calibration. Calibration curves showed reasonable agreement between predicted and observed probabilities in both training and validation cohorts.

Decision curve analysis

Decision curve analysis revealed that the nomogram provided greater net benefit than treating all patients or treating none across a wide range of threshold probabilities (10%-80%), suggesting potential clinical utility for treatment decision-making, pending external validation.

Nomogram

A user-friendly nomogram was constructed based on the multivariable model. To use the nomogram, clinicians locate the patient’s value on each variable axis, draw a line upward to obtain the corresponding points, sum all points to get total points, and then draw a line downward to determine the predicted probability of valve preservation.

Sensitivity analyses

Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW)

As a sensitivity analysis to propensity score matching, we performed stabilized IPTW using the same baseline covariates. After weighting, all covariate standardized mean differences were < 0.1, indicating excellent balance. The IPTW-adjusted results were consistent with the primary PSM analysis: valve preservation (OR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.17–0.46, P < 0.001), moderate-severe PTS (OR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.18–0.58, P < 0.001), and CIVIQ-20 score (beta = -7.1, 95% CI: -9.3 to -4.9, P < 0.001, Supplementary Table S5).

IVC filter sensitivity analysis

To address the potential influence of IVC filter placement on valve outcomes, we performed two complementary analyses. First, repeating the primary analysis after excluding all patients who received IVC filters (n = 42 excluded, 133 remaining) yielded consistent results: TVRS was associated with a higher valve preservation rate compared with SA (72.1% vs. 57.8%; OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 0.86–4.15, P = 0.161, Supplementary Table S7), though statistical significance was attenuated due to reduced sample size. Second, including IVC filter placement as a covariate in multivariable logistic regression (n = 152 with complete data) showed that TVRS remained associated with directionally favorable valve preservation outcomes after adjustment (OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 0.62–3.91, P = 0.351, Supplementary Table S8). IVC filter placement itself was not significantly associated with valve preservation (OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 0.61–3.86, P = 0.359), supporting the hypothesis that filter placement does not confound the treatment-outcome association. (Figure S2, Figure S3).

Age interaction analysis

To evaluate whether the treatment effect of TVRS on valve preservation varied by age, we performed interaction analyses using both dichotomous (< 60 vs. >=60 years) and continuous age specifications. No statistically significant interaction was found between TVRS treatment and dichotomous age on valve preservation (P-interaction = 0.157). However, a numerically larger treatment effect was observed in patients < 60 years (OR = 4.18, 95% CI: 1.42–12.30) compared with those > = 60 years (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 0.68–2.30), suggesting a potential age-related gradient that merits investigation in larger cohorts (Figure S2, Supplementary Table S9).

Thrombus burden interaction analysis

To assess whether the treatment effect varied by initial thrombus burden, we performed an interaction analysis using the Mewissen score. Limited by the small sample size with available Mewissen scores (n = 38), no significant interaction was detected (P-interaction = 1.000). The point estimates were consistent with a TVRS benefit across both low and high thrombus burden subgroups (Figure S2, Supplementary Table S10).

Cohort-stratified analysis

To assess the consistency of findings between the retrospective and prospective cohorts, we performed stratified analyses. The odds ratios for valve preservation were similar in the retrospective cohort (OR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.14–0.41, P < 0.001) and the prospective cohort (OR = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.15–0.62, P = 0.001). The Breslow-Day test for interaction was non-significant (P = 0.68), supporting the consistency of the observed association across cohorts.

E-value sensitivity analysis

To assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values for the primary and secondary endpoints. For valve preservation, the point estimate E-value was 3.5 (corresponding RR = 2.04), and the 95% CI lower bound E-value was 2.1 (corresponding RR = 1.38). This indicates that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both TVRS treatment assignment and valve preservation outcome by a risk ratio of at least 3.5-fold to fully explain away the observed association. For moderate-to-severe PTS reduction, the point estimate E-value was 3.2 and the CI lower bound E-value was 1.8. These values suggest that substantial unmeasured confounding would be required to explain away the observed associations.

Safety outcomes

The safety profiles of TVRS and SA are summarized in Table 5. Major bleeding (TIMI criteria) occurred in 8 (3.4%) TVRS patients and 5 (1.9%) SA patients (P = 0.31)( Supplementary Table S6). Minor bleeding was more frequent in the TVRS group (5.5% vs. 2.3%, P = 0.05). Pulmonary embolism occurred in 2 (0.8%) TVRS patients and 1 (0.4%) SA patient (P = 0.60). Acute kidney injury occurred in 5 (2.1%) TVRS patients (all AngioJet-related, attributed to hemoglobinuria-related tubular injury; all resolved with conservative management) and 0 SA patients (P = 0.03). Access site complications occurred in 6 (2.5%) TVRS patients and 0 SA patients (P = 0.01). Venous perforation occurred in 1 (0.4%) TVRS patient (P = 0.48). Thirty-day mortality was 1 (0.4%) in the TVRS group (PE-related, day 3 post-procedure) and 2 (0.8%) in the SA group (intracranial hemorrhage on day 18 and advanced malignancy progression on day 25) (P = 0.62).

Discussion

This combined retrospective and prospective cohort study provides evidence that thrombus volume reduction surgery (TVRS) is associated with better femoral vein valve preservation, a lower incidence of post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), and improved quality of life compared with simple anticoagulation (SA) in acute iliofemoral DVT (IFDVT). Notably, an exploratory mediation analysis suggests that approximately 39.1% of the observed association between TVRS and lower PTS incidence may be attributable to valve preservation. While hypothesis-generating, this highlights the importance of valve protection in mediating clinical outcomes.

The pathophysiology of PTS is driven by persistent venous obstruction and valvular incompetence. While TVRS is known to achieve early vein patency, its direct association with valvular function has been underexplored. Our findings extend the “open vein hypothesis,” suggesting that early thrombectomy benefits extend beyond simple recanalization. We postulate that TVRS rapidly eliminates the inflammatory thrombus milieu. In SA, slow endogenous thrombolysis exposes valve leaflets to prolonged inflammatory stress, leading to fibrosis and adhesion. Conversely, TVRS facilitates immediate flow restoration, minimizing intraluminal pressure and preventing the structural remodeling underlying valvular incompetence.

Our findings must be interpreted within the broader context of existing RCT evidence and guideline recommendations. The ATTRACT trial reported no overall difference in PTS between pharmacomechanical thrombolysis and standard anticoagulation; however, a pre-specified subgroup analysis suggested greater benefit with thrombus removal in iliofemoral DVT—consistent with our observations, albeit accompanied by a higher incidence of bleeding complications. The CaVenT trial demonstrated that catheter-directed thrombolysis reduced PTS at 24 months, whereas the CAVA trial found no significant benefit. These divergent efficacy outcomes, together with the well-documented bleeding and procedural risks associated with catheter-directed and pharmacomechanical thrombolysis, explain why current ACCP and SVS guidelines maintain a cautious stance, recommending selective rather than routine application of thrombus removal strategies. As an observational study without randomization or blinding, our findings are strictly hypothesis-generating and cannot alter existing guidelines. Rather, they identify femoral vein valve preservation as a mechanistic target that warrants prospective investigation in future randomized trials with dedicated valve-specific endpoints and adequate safety monitoring.

A novel aspect is the quantitative dissection of the treatment association via mediation analysis. The observation that valve preservation mediates nearly 40% of the PTS risk difference provides mechanistic insight: valve protection may be a functional therapeutic target, not merely a surrogate marker. The remaining direct effect likely reflects rapid venous obstruction resolution and attenuated wall inflammation independent of valve function. This underscores the multifaceted benefits of an interventional strategy, although the mediation analysis relies on strong assumptions unverified in observational settings.

Furthermore, our exploratory subgroup analysis comparing pharmacomechanical thrombectomy devices offers preliminary insights. The AngioJet system, utilizing a pulsed rheolytic mechanism, was associated with a higher valve preservation rate compared with the Acostream system employing continuous suction. We hypothesize that intermittent suction mitigates sustained mechanical stress on leaflets, whereas continuous suction risks entrapping delicate valve structures. This association remained after confounder adjustment (adjusted OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.27–0.98). However, this comparison was non-randomized, limited to 50 patients per device, subject to selection bias, and borderline significant (P = 0.043), requiring validation in randomized settings.

Clinically, the correlation between preserved valve function, reduced Villalta scores, and better CIVIQ-20 scores underscores the tangible impact of TVRS on patients’ daily lives. Given the socioeconomic burden of moderate-to-severe PTS, the absolute risk reduction observed supports the hypothesis that early TVRS may benefit eligible IFDVT patients, pending RCT confirmation.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, despite rigorous adjustments (PSM/IPTW) and relatively robust E-values (≥ 3.5 for valve preservation; ≥3.2 for PTS), the non-randomized design cannot entirely exclude residual confounding by indication from unmeasured variables (e.g., anatomical complexity, precise thrombus chronicity, operator expertise). The single-center, tertiary referral design with high operator volumes further limits generalizability to broader clinical practices, and the combined retrospective-prospective design introduces potential temporal heterogeneity in data collection, despite consistent stratified effects (P for interaction = 0.68). Second, power and data availability constraints impacted several analyses; the exclusion of IVC filter patients reduced the sample size by 24%, Mewissen score data were available for only 38 patients (severely underpowering the thrombus burden interaction analysis), and the absence of symptom onset-to-treatment time precluded the corresponding interaction analysis. Additionally, restricting the device comparison to a PMT-only subset with complete data introduces potential selection bias, rendering the borderline finding (P = 0.043) purely hypothesis-generating. Third, regarding outcome assessment, the inherent subjectivity of ultrasound and Villalta scoring—despite excellent inter-observer agreement (Kappa > 0.80)—and venographic validation limited to a select subgroup may affect endpoint accuracy. Similarly, the mediation analysis relies on untestable assumptions (e.g., no unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding), precluding definitive causal inference despite sensitivity analyses. Finally, multiple exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted without multiplicity correction, increasing the risk of Type I error. Consequently, these secondary findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require cautious interpretation and independent validation in larger, randomized cohorts.

Conclusions

In conclusion, TVRS was associated with improved femoral vein valve preservation, lower PTS incidence, and better quality of life compared with SA in patients with acute IFDVT. The AngioJet system was associated with a higher valve preservation rate than the Acostream system in an exploratory subgroup analysis, but this finding is non-randomized, underpowered, and requires validation in randomized trials. These findings support the hypothesis that early thrombectomy may be beneficial for long-term venous function and suggest that valve preservation should be considered as a therapeutic target in the management of acute IFDVT. However, causality cannot be established from this observational study, and our findings should not alter current guideline recommendations pending randomized confirmatory evidence.
