Toward Reference-Grade neuron models
Authors: Alon Korngreen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Computational neuroscience increasingly produces detailed, data-driven neuron models, yet compressing biological variability and measurement uncertainty into single-parameter sets can blur the line between data-supported claims and modeling choices. Here, a reference-grade culture is proposed, adapting the traceable standards, explicit uncertainty budgets, and ensemble predictions of analytical chemistry to computational neuroscience.

In their 1945 paper, Rosenblueth and Wiener argued that scientific progress depends on abstraction: a model replaces a portion of the universe with a deliberately simpler structure chosen to answer a particular class of questions1. Their argument implies a caution that remains useful today: when a model is treated as a literal copy rather than an instrument, one can no longer distinguish claims supported by data from consequences of modeling choices. That warning is newly salient as computational neuroscience increasingly pursues comprehensive, data-driven reconstructions of cortical tissue, from the Blue Brain neocortical microcircuit2 to Allen Institute models of the mouse primary visual cortex3 and standardized model-generation pipelines built from the Allen Cell Types Database4. Such efforts integrate heterogeneous datasets, standardize modeling pipelines, and enable circuit-level hypotheses that would otherwise be difficult to formulate. Yet a key methodological question remains: which additional scientific claims become supportable as biological detail increases, and which gains primarily reflect the scale of the reconstruction rather than tighter constraints on mechanism?

Despite advances in inference, many workflows still reduce rich electrophysiological recordings to a single representative parameterization before building upward. Voltage-clamp recordings yield a best-fit set of channel parameters; those parameters are assembled into a deterministic multicompartment neuron; additional parameters are optimized to match firing features; and the result is presented as a detailed model. These steps are individually legitimate, but they repeatedly compress biological variability and measurement uncertainty into a single representative set. That variability is rarely carried forward as part of the scientific object5,6. Parameter degeneracy means that distinct parameter combinations can produce indistinguishable outputs under limited fitting protocols, so a fitted model is one member of a large equivalence class7–9. This is the signature of an inverse problem: limited observations admit multiple mechanistic explanations. When the equivalence class is not characterized, claims about mechanisms become claims about a particular parameterization rather than about the underlying biology. Large-scale reconstructions often respond by compartmentalizing parameters; once fit at one level, they are not treated as free at higher levels. These practices, however, relocate rather than resolve the identifiability problem5,6. Generalizations across cells, regions, or recording conditions are themselves modeling steps that introduce uncertainty, even when every submodel uses deterministic values. Crucially, if two parameter combinations are truly equivalent for all inputs, that degeneracy need not propagate upward. But if equivalence was established on a restricted set of protocols, the uncertainty is real and consequential.

A practical way forward is to adopt an explicit culture of traceability and reference standards, borrowing from analytical chemistry, where reliability rests on a hierarchy of standards rather than the apparent realism of any single measurement. In chemistry, a primary standard carries a certified value accompanied by an explicit uncertainty budget traceable to fundamental units. The logic of calibration, provenance, and declared uncertainty can be adapted to computational neuroscience without importing the literal materials of titration. The analogues are curated datasets, protocol suites, and model forms that allow claims to remain traceable as components are reused across larger models. The right-hand panel of Fig. 1 illustrates this reference-grade pipeline: curated observations feed into primary standards with explicit uncertainty budgets; a sensitivity gate prioritizes identifiable degrees of freedom; validated secondary modules are assembled; and ensemble propagation yields predictions expressed as ranges with declared scope and failure modes.

A primary standard in this framework corresponds to voltage-gated channel kinetics derived from carefully designed experiments, with sufficient replication to characterize biological variability and to constrain the relevant transitions through protocols. Critically, a primary standard must separate the system being modeled from the measurement process, so that uncertainty introduced by observation is tracked rather than folded invisibly into fitted parameters10. The model form, whether Hodgkin–Huxley, Markov, or another formalism, should be chosen with identifiability in mind and tested against plausible alternatives. Global sensitivity analysis serves as a gatekeeper: it identifies which parameters actually control the measured currents and therefore which degrees of freedom merit reference-grade effort10. Open-source frameworks such as SciUnit11 for model validation and Uncertainpy12 for uncertainty quantification and sensitivity analysis can operationalize parts of these requirements. Only when sensitivity analysis confirms that important degrees of freedom are constrained should the resulting parameter distributions be treated as reference-grade candidates, not single numbers but probability distributions with documented uncertainty.

Secondary standards are modular components assembled from validated primaries. The proposal is most immediately actionable at the level of ion-channel kinetics, synapses, and reusable cellular modules; circuit-scale reconstructions should inherit this uncertainty structure rather than invent it after assembly. Consider, for example, a reusable module for the axon initial segment of a pyramidal neuron: it combines validated sodium and potassium channel primaries with morphological information and empirical distributions of channel densities, and is released with an uncertainty budget and a sensitivity profile that clarifies which parameter combinations control the firing threshold. Dendritic modules can be treated similarly, each with a declared scope and failure modes. These components can then be assembled into larger neurons or networks, with uncertainty propagated via Monte Carlo sampling and Bootstrap variance estimation. Secondary standards should specify the ranges of stimuli, temperatures, and recording conditions for which they reproduce experimental behavior within uncertainty bounds, and indicate when new data are required. Standards repositories, analogous to NIST’s Standard Reference Materials, should be openly accessible, with documentation linking raw data, code, parameter distributions, and validation targets. This aligns directly with the FAIR Guiding Principles13 (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) and extends them by elevating uncertainty propagation to a first-class deliverable.

This framework pushes the field toward ensemble predictions rather than single-parameter-set models7,8. If the inverse problem admits broad equivalence classes, the scientific object is an ensemble induced by measured variability, not a single best parameter set. In an ensemble, predictions are distributions: rheobase becomes a range, spike timing a probability density, and robustness quantifiable rather than asserted. Degeneracy, the phenomenon in which distinct parameter combinations yield similar function, whether in excitability, synaptic integration, or circuit output, is a feature of biological systems9, and ensemble thinking is therefore not methodological purism but descriptive adequacy. Importantly, this does not prohibit phenomenological or abstract models at higher levels of description; it requires only that uncertainty be declared at each level and that the mapping between levels be explicit rather than implicit.

Adopting a reference-grade culture also changes how models are shared and reviewed. Descriptors such as detailed or large-scale are more informative than the catch-all realistic, because they prompt the follow-up question: realistic with respect to what, under what constraints, and within what scope of validity? Reference-grade standards are therefore complementary to format and interoperability standards such as NeuroML14: they do not prescribe a syntax or model class but specify that uncertainty, validation, provenance, and scope must accompany a reusable model. A compact documentation object accompanying each primary or secondary standard would describe data sources, protocols, model form, parameter distributions, uncertainty budgets, sensitivity profiles, and known failure modes, operationalizing the requirements articulated by Crook and colleagues6. Review then becomes an evaluation of evidential support: do key conclusions persist across admissible variability, and is the scope of inference declared rather than implied?

As Rosenblueth and Wiener whimsically put it, the best model of a cat is another cat1, a reminder that complete fidelity collapses the distinction between model and world. The issue is not ambition but calibration: without traceability and uncertainty budgets, added detail can obscure rather than strengthen inference. Reference-grade modeling is not the abandonment of ambitious simulation but its maturation: models as calibrated instruments whose components are traceable, whose uncertainties are explicit, and whose conclusions are stated as distributions when the inverse problem demands distributions.
