Temporal Conductance and Bounds on the Voter Model for Dynamic Networks
Tatiana Rocha Avila, Holger Dell, John Lapinskas

Abstract
The voter model is a classical stochastic process that models how opinions might spread through a network: at each step, every node lazily adopts the opinion of a random neighbour; eventually all nodes share the same opinion ( consensus ). Stronger connectivity should yield faster consensus. Berenbrink, Giakkoupis, Kermarrec, and Mallmann-Trenn (ICALP 2016) make this precise via the network’s conductance: if the network has m m edges, minimum degree d min d_{\min} , and conductance at least ϕ \phi , then the voter model reaches consensus in expected O ⁡ ( m / ( d min ​ ϕ ) ) O(m/(d_{\min}\phi)) steps. Their results extend to dynamic networks with fixed vertex degrees by considering the network’s conductance at each time step. We introduce temporal conductance Φ \Phi , a more general connectivity measure for dynamic networks. Unlike static conductance, which collapses to 0 0 whenever some snapshot is disconnected, Φ \Phi captures connectivity through edges that appear at different times. We generalise the results of Berenbrink et al. from static conductance to temporal conductance, showing that the expected consensus time of the standard voter model is at most O ⁡ ( m / ( d min ​ Φ ) ) O(m/(d_{\min}\Phi)) . Moreover, we prove that this bound is tight up to constant factors. We expect temporal conductance to be a useful primitive for analysing other dynamics on temporal networks, and potentially time-inhomogeneous Markov chains more generally.

It is most interesting not because it is realistic—it is too simplistic for that—but because it resists rigorous mathematical analysis despite its simplicity.
If we cannot analyze the voter model, then we are unlikely to be able to analyze realistic models of opinion dynamics, which are more complex.
The voter model serves as a testbed for methods in network dynamics more generally, and has been the subject of a large body of work in the mathematical physics

Consensus time.

One of the most fundamental questions about the voter model is this: How long does it take for the process to reach consensus, that is, for all vertices to have the same opinion?
Results span a wide range of graph families: on the complete graph
K_{n}
the expected consensus time is
\Theta(n)

[
22
]
; on random regular graphs it is also
\Theta(n)

[
10
]
; on heterogeneous (configuration-model) graphs it depends on the degree distribution
[
27
]
; and on subcritical scale-free graphs the exponent varies in a rich phase diagram
[
12
]
.

An important result by Berenbrink et al.
[
5
]
shows that the expected consensus time of the voter model is captured by the
conductance
of the underlying graph, which is a measure of how well-connected the graph is. In particular, they show that the consensus time is at most
O(m/(d_{\min}\phi))
, where
m
is the number of edges in the graph,
d_{\min}
is the minimum degree of any vertex, and
\phi
is the conductance of the graph. They also show that there are graphs where this upper bound is asymptotically tight.

Voter model in temporal graphs.

When it comes to real-world social networks, the underlying graph is not static; it changes over time. For example, people make new friends, they interact with different subsets of people at different times, and during pandemics, lockdowns may be imposed or lifted. This motivates the study of the voter model as a test-bed for network dynamics on
temporal graphs
\mathcal{G}
, which are sequences of graphs that change over time:
\mathcal{G}=(G_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{N})
. In the context of spreading processes like the voter model, this is particularly useful in settings where the graph structure is independent from the spreading process being studied and can be observed in detail, such as epidemic modelling in livestock markets
[
2
]
or schools
[
28
]
.

The results of
[
5
]
are stated for the temporal setting as well, in the setting where vertex degrees are fixed with respect to time, by considering the conductance
\phi_{t}
of the graph at each time step
t
. They show that if
\sum_{t=0}^{T}\phi_{t}\geq Cm/d_{\min}
for an appropriate constant
C
, then with probability at least
1/2
the absorption time is at most
T
; in particular, if
\phi_{t}\geq\phi
for all
t\geq 0
then this generalises their result in the static case. However, both bounds are proved in an adaptive setting, where an adversary can choose each graph
G_{t}
with full knowledge of the state of the voter model at time
t-1
, rather than the non-adaptive paradigm described above where the graph and voter model evolve independently. While their upper bound carries over to this setting immediately, their lower bound does not.

As a simple example, consider an
n
-vertex temporal graph consisting of one uniformly-random perfect matching per time step, drawn independently. It is not hard to show that such a graph has
O(\log n)
expected absorption time in the voter model, but it is disconnected at every time step so
\sum_{t=0}^{T}\phi_{t}=0
for all
T
— that is, the results of
[
5
]
do not give any upper bound on the expected absorption time.

Our contribution.

In this paper, we study the voter model on temporal graphs with fixed vertex degrees in the non-adaptive setting. We define a novel parameter
\Phi(\mathcal{G})
of
temporal conductance
which measures the connectivity of such graphs.
We generalize the results of
[
5
]
and show that with probability at least
1/2
the consensus time is at most
O(m/(d_{\min}\Phi(\mathcal{G})))
, and that there are temporal graphs for which this upper bound is asymptotically tight. Further, we generalise the stepwise bound of
[
5
]
to an interval-wise bound — if time interval
I_{i}
contains at least one time
t
such that
G_{t}
has conductance at least
\phi_{i}
, and
\sum_{i=0}^{I}\phi_{i}\geq Cm/d_{\min}
for some suitable constant
C
, then we prove absorption with probability
1/2
after
I
intervals have passed. We prove this bound is asymptotically tight even if instead of requiring
maximum
conductance at least
\phi_{i}
over each interval
I_{i}
, we require
average
conductance at least
\phi_{i}
.

In order to prove our results, we perform a drift analysis with a potential function inspired by
[
5
]
.
Dealing with temporal conductance turns out to pose significant challenges, which we overcome by splitting time into
stable intervals
(during which the volume of each opinion class remains roughly constant, allowing conductance to be exploited) and

unstable intervals
(during which the volume of the minority opinion changes significantly, causing rapid decline of the potential even if conductance is poor).

1.1
Related work

Conductance in static graphs.

The
conductance
of a Markov chain was defined by Jerrum and Sinclair
[
17
]
in 1988 to prove their seminal result: counting the number of perfect matchings in bipartite graphs admits a randomized polynomial-time approximation scheme.
In order to do this, they proved that the conductance is closely tied to the Markov chain’s
mixing time
, and used this to show that randomly switching between perfect and near-perfect matchings in bipartite graphs is enough to approximately uniformly sample perfect matchings in polynomial time.
Sinclair and Jerrum
[
26
]
then developed this connection into a general framework for approximate counting and uniform generation, now central to the analysis of MCMC algorithms
[
21
]
.
Conductance is also equivalent (up to squaring) to the spectral gap of the random walk matrix—a discrete Cheeger inequality established by Alon and Milman
[
1
]
and Mihail
[
24
]
—linking it to expander graph theory, spectral graph partitioning
[
3
]
, and the mixing of reversible Markov chains more broadly
[
19
]
.

Spreading and random walks on dynamic graphs.

Random walks and spreading processes on dynamic graphs have been studied in various models.
Clementi et al.
[
7
]
studied flooding time in the
edge-Markovian
model—where each edge appears and disappears independently as a two-state Markov chain—and proved near-tight bounds.
Giakkoupis, Sauerwald, and Stauffer
[
14
]
studied push-pull rumor spreading under adversarial rewiring, allowing degrees to vary by at most a constant factor, and showed that the protocol broadcasts to all vertices whenever the cumulative conductance satisfies
\sum_{i}\phi_{i}=\Omega(\log n)
, where
\phi_{i}
is the conductance at round
i
.
Avin, Koucký, and Lotker
[
4
]
showed that an oblivious adversary can force exponential cover time on a simple random walk even when every snapshot is well-connected, but that the lazy random walk achieves polynomial cover and mixing time against any oblivious adversary.
Sauerwald and Zanetti
[
25
]
proved
O(n^{2})
mixing and hitting time bounds for random walks on any sequence of
d
-regular connected graphs via a local expansion argument.
Recently, Galanis, Goldberg, and Mifsud
[
13
]
studied random walks on dynamic graphs where edges evolve according to Glauber dynamics for the random-cluster model, and prove
O(\log n)
mixing time in the subcritical regime, matching that of a static random regular graph.

Absorption time of the voter model.

It is well-known (see e.g.
[
23
]
) that the voter model on static graphs is dual to a random walk model — the
n
-opinion case on an
n
-vertex graph is dual to
n
independent random walks, one starting on each vertex, with distinct walks coalescing into a single walk on collision. The absorption time of the voter model then corresponds to the time at which only one walk remains. As such, upper bounds on absorption time are often proved in terms of random walks and their properties. For example, Hassin and Peleg prove
[
15
]
that in static graphs the absorption time is
O(t_{\textrm{meet}}\log n)
, where
t_{\textrm{meet}}
is the expected time for two independent random walks to meet on the base graph; this was recently
[
18
]
sharpened to
O(t_{\textrm{meet}}(1+\sqrt{t_{\textrm{mix}}/t_{\textrm{meet}}}\log n))
, where
t_{\textrm{mix}}
is the mixing time of a single random walk. Meanwhile, Cooper et al.
[
9
]
bound the expected absorption time in terms of the second-largest eigenvalue of the random walk’s transition matrix and its stationary distribution. None of these bounds are directly comparable to the
O(m/(d_{\min}\phi))
bound from
[
5
]
; see their introduction for a more detailed discussion.

There is one other general upper bound on absorption in terms of conductance. In
[
5
]
, in addition to their main result of expected absorption time
O(m/(d_{\min\phi}))
(the one stated in their abstract), Berenbrink et al. also prove expected absorption time
O(n\log n/\phi^{2})
. This is a strictly weaker result for the important case of regular graphs, but can be stronger for graphs which are far from regular and have high conductance. We do not generalise this result to the non-adaptive setting, and consider it an interesting open question — in
[
5
]
the result falls out relatively easily from their main proof, but in our setting this approach breaks.

Other notions of temporal conductance.

DiTursi, Ghosh, and Bogdanov
[
11
]
define a temporal conductance for community detection in dynamic networks by aggregating cut and volume over a time interval with a normalisation factor; their definition is motivated by data-mining considerations and differs from ours.
Berenbrink et al.
[
5
]
and Giakkoupis et al.
[
14
]
use per-round conductances as input parameters, with bounds in terms of their minimum or cumulative sum.
Our
\Phi(\mathcal{G})
instead optimises the window length automatically, yielding a single intrinsic parameter of the temporal graph.

1.2
The voter model on temporal graphs

As discussed earlier, the dynamic networks we consider in this paper are
temporal graphs with fixed degrees
, which are arbitrary sequences of graphs on a common vertex set, with the only restriction being that the degree of each vertex does not depend on time.
The fixed degree assumption makes stochastic processes on the temporal graph much more well-behaved; for example, the lazy random walk becomes
strongly ergodic
and has a unique stationary distribution.
The same assumption was also made by Berenbrink et al.
[
5
]
, because without it, the expected consensus time can be exponential in the number of vertices
[
5
, Observation 1]
.
We give a formal definition next.

Definition 1.1

(Temporal graph with fixed degrees)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a sequence
(G_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
of undirected graphs on a common vertex set
V(\mathcal{G})
, where
G_{t}
is the graph at time
t
. We say
\mathcal{G}
is a
temporal graph with fixed degrees
if the degree of each vertex
v\in V(\mathcal{G})
in
G_{t}
does not depend on
t
. We denote this common degree by
d(v)
, and the (time-independent) number of edges of
\mathcal{G}
by
m
, so that
m=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V(\mathcal{G})}d(v)
holds.
For convenience, we assume
d(v)\geq 1
for all
v\in V(\mathcal{G})
, that is, there are no isolated vertices in
\mathcal{G}
.

We remark that the degree of each vertex is
fixed
across time, but not necessarily
constant
, that is, the degree of a vertex may depend on the number
n
of vertices but not on
t
.

In the voter model, each vertex holds an opinion and repeatedly copies the opinion of a randomly chosen neighbour in the current graph; the temporal graph thus governs which interactions are available at each time step.

Definition 1.2

(Standard voter model)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(G_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
be a temporal graph, let
\kappa
be a positive integer, and let
\sigma_{0}\colon V(G)\to\{0,\dots,\kappa-1\}
. The
standard
\kappa
-opinion voter model
on
\mathcal{G}
is a stochastic process
(\xi_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
, where
\xi_{t}\colon V(\mathcal{G})\to\{0,\dots,\kappa-1\}
is a random function that assigns an opinion to each vertex at time
t
, and
\xi_{0}=\sigma_{0}
holds deterministically for the initial state.

At each time step
t
, we sample
\xi_{t+1}(v)
as follows: every vertex
v\in V(\mathcal{G})
independently and synchronously chooses a neighbour
w
in
G_{t}
uniformly at random, and adopts the opinion
\xi_{t}(w)
with probability
1/2
; otherwise,
v
keeps its current opinion
\xi_{t}(v)
. This process continues until all vertices share the same opinion, which is called
consensus
.
The
consensus time
is the first time
t
such that
\xi_{t}
is a constant function.

Note that
(\xi_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
is a time-inhomogeneous Markov chain on the state space of all functions
V(\mathcal{G})\to\{0,\dots,\kappa-1\}
, and that the absorbing states are precisely the constant functions.

Although our results extend to arbitrary
\kappa
, the proof reduces to the two-opinion case, so we mostly focus on
\kappa=2
.
In this setting, we write

A_{t}

for the set
\xi_{t}^{-1}(0)
of vertices with opinion
0
at time
t
, and we write

\overline{A_{t}}\coloneqq V(\mathcal{G})\setminus A_{t}
for the complement.
Consensus is reached when
A_{t}=\emptyset
or
A_{t}=V(\mathcal{G})
holds.

1.3
Temporal conductance

Before introducing a temporal analogue of conductance, we begin by recalling the standard definition of conductance for a static graph.
For a graph
G
with
m
edges and a vertex set
S\subseteq V(G)
, we define the
volume
of
S
and the
conductance
of
G
, via

\displaystyle\textnormal{Vol}(S)\coloneqq\sum_{v\in S}d(v)\,,

\displaystyle\phi(G)\coloneqq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq V(G)\\
0<\textnormal{Vol}(S)\leq m\end{subarray}}\frac{e_{G}(S,\overline{S})}{\textnormal{Vol}(S)}\,.

(1)

Here
\textnormal{Vol}(S)
counts each internal edge of
S
twice and each edge between
S
and
\overline{S}
once, so it measures the edge mass incident to
S
; and
e_{G}(S,\overline{S})
denotes the number of edges of
G
crossing the cut.
We remark that
m=\tfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{Vol}(V(G))
holds, so the condition
\textnormal{Vol}(S)\leq m
is equivalent to
\textnormal{Vol}(S)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{Vol}(V(G))
.
Among other things, the conductance determines how fast opinions can propagate across the cut in the voter model.

Now let
\mathcal{G}=(G_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
be a temporal graph with fixed degrees.
The volume of a set
S\subseteq V(\mathcal{G})
is time-invariant, but the edges crossing a cut are not.
For disjoint subsets
S,N\subseteq V(\mathcal{G})
, we write
e_{t}(S,N)
for the number of edges of
G_{t}
between
S
and
N
, and we abbreviate
e_{t}(v,N)\coloneqq e_{t}(\{v\},N)
for a single vertex
v
.
The
conductance of the set
S
at time
t
is:

\displaystyle\phi^{t}(S)\coloneqq\frac{e_{t}(S,\overline{S})}{\textnormal{Vol}(S)}\,.

(2)

The
conductance of
\mathcal{G}
on an integer interval
[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is defined by

\displaystyle\phi^{[a,b]}(\mathcal{G})\coloneqq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq V\\
0<\textnormal{Vol}(S)\leq m\end{subarray}}\phi^{[a,b]}(S)

where

\displaystyle\phi^{[a,b]}(S)\coloneqq\max_{a\leq t\leq b}\phi^{t}(S)\,.

(3)

For
a=b=t
, we have
\phi^{[a,b]}(\mathcal{G})=\phi(G_{t})
.
What
\phi^{[a,b]}(S)
captures for
a<b
is the
best
conductance of
S
across the interval
[a,b]
:
Indeed
\phi^{[a,b]}(S)\geq\phi
holds if and only if
S
has conductance
\phi^{t}(S)\geq\phi
for at least one
t\in[a,b]
.
Moreover,
\phi^{[a,b]}(\mathcal{G})\geq\phi
means that every set
S
with
0<\textnormal{Vol}(S)\leq m
has conductance
\phi^{t}(S)\geq\phi
for at least one
t\in[a,b]
.
Note already here that
\phi^{[a,b]}(\mathcal{G})
can be large even if
\phi(G_{t})=0
holds for all
t\in[a,b]
.

Then for any
\Delta\geq 1
, we define the
\Delta
-window conductance of
\mathcal{G}
by

\displaystyle\phi^{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\coloneqq\min_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\phi^{[t,t+\Delta-1]}(\mathcal{G}).

(4)

Now
\phi^{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\geq\phi
means that, for every time interval of length
\Delta
, every set
S
with
0<\textnormal{Vol}(S)\leq m
has at least one “good step”, that is, it has conductance at least
\phi
in at least one graph
G_{t}
in that interval.

Since
\phi^{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})\leq 1
holds for all
\Delta
, we have
\phi^{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})/\Delta\to 0
as
\Delta\to\infty
, so the supremum of
\Delta\mapsto\phi^{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})/\Delta
is attained at some positive integer
\Delta^{\ast}
.
We can therefore define the
conductance
\Phi(\mathcal{G})
of the temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
via

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathcal{G})\coloneqq\max_{\Delta\geq 1}\frac{\phi^{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})}{\Delta}\,.

(5)

If the temporal graph is static, that is, if we have
G_{1}=G_{2}=\cdots=G
, then
\Delta^{\ast}=1
and thus
\Phi(\mathcal{G})=\phi(G)
, so the definitions coincide. The motivation for dividing by
\Delta
in (
5
) is that the one “good step” in the length-
\Delta
interval may be all we get before
S
has been changed beyond recognition, and so we must amortise its effect over the entire interval length. At first this may seem like a very pessimistic assumption — however, in the next section we provide a matching lower bound even in the case where we have lower bounds on the
average
conductance within an interval (rather than the maximum), and so we argue that it is justified.

Example.

Recall our example from earlier — suppose
\mathcal{G}=(M_{1},M_{2},\dots)
, where each
M_{i}
is a perfect matching drawn independently and uniformly at random. Then with high probability over the
M_{i}
’s,
\Phi(\mathcal{G})\geq\phi^{3}(\mathcal{G})/3=\Omega(1)
, even though
\phi(G_{t})=0
for all
t
. Effectively, for
\Phi^{\Delta}(\mathcal{G})
to be high, we require that every set
S
will be well-cut by
some
graph in a length-
\Delta
window, but this “good step” may differ within the window from one set to the next.

1.4
Our Model and Results

We prove bounds on the expected consensus time for the
standard voter model
in a temporal undirected graph whose vertex degrees are fixed by using our notion of temporal conductance.

Main Theorem 1

(Upper bound)
.

There exists a constant
b^{\prime}>0
such that the following holds. Consider the standard voter model on an
n
-vertex temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
with fixed vertex degrees,
m
edges, and minimum degree
d_{\min}
, with arbitrary initial state. Then with probability at least
1/2
, the consensus time is at most
b^{\prime}m/(d_{\min}\Phi(\mathcal{G}))
.

Main˜Theorem

1
is our simple bound advertised in the introduction.
It is a consequence of the following more general statement, which works with an arbitrary partition of time into windows of lengths
\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1},\dots
and the conductance
\phi^{I_{j}}(\mathcal{G})
attained on each window. One recovers
Main˜Theorem

1
by choosing the windows that realise
\Phi(\mathcal{G})
.

Theorem 1.3

(Upper Bound)
.

There exists a constant
b>0
such that the following holds. Consider the standard voter model on a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
with fixed vertex degrees,
m
edges, and minimum degree
d_{\min}
, with arbitrary initial state. Let
\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1},\dots\geq 1
be integers, and let
\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\dots
be real numbers in
[0,1]
. For all
j\geq 0
, let
I_{j}^{-}=\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{j-1}
, let
I_{j}^{+}=I_{j}^{-}+\Delta_{j}-1
, and let
I_{j}=[I_{j}^{-},I_{j}^{+}]
. Suppose that for all
j\geq 0
,
\phi^{I_{j}}(\mathcal{G})\geq\phi_{j}
. Let
J=\min\{j\colon\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\phi_{\ell}\geq bm/d_{\min}\}
(where we require
J<\infty
). Then with probability at least
1/2
, the consensus time is at most
\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{J}
.

These upper bounds are essentially tight: the dependence on
\Phi(\mathcal{G})
cannot be improved in general, as our matching lower bounds show.

Main Theorem 2

(Lower bound)
.

There exist arbitrarily large
n
-vertex 3-regular temporal graphs
\mathcal{G}
, and constant
c
with the following property. From some initial state, with probability at least
1/2
, the standard voter model running on
\mathcal{G}
does not absorb within time
cn/\Phi(\mathcal{G})
.

The construction behind
Main˜Theorem

2
in fact yields a stronger obstruction: it rules out fast consensus even against a weaker, window-averaged notion of conductance, and for every odd degree
d
.

Theorem 1.4

(Lower bound)
.

There exist
c>0
,
\phi\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\Delta\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
with
\phi(n)=\Theta(1/n)
and
\Delta(n)=\Theta(n)
as
n\to\infty
such that the following holds. For all odd integers
d
, there exist arbitrarily large
n
-vertex
d
-regular temporal graphs
\mathcal{G}
such that:

(i)

For all time intervals
I
of length
\Delta(n)
and all sets
S\subseteq V(\mathcal{G})
with
0<\textnormal{Vol}(S)\leq m
, we have
\tfrac{1}{\lvert I\rvert}\sum_{t\in I}\phi^{t}(S)\geq\phi(n)
.

(ii)

From some initial state, with probability at least
1/2
, the standard voter model on
\mathcal{G}
does not absorb within time
cn\Delta(n)/\phi(n)
.

These results should be read against those of
[
5
]
. All their results are proved in the same adversarial model: the adversary is allowed to rewire the entire graph at each time step subject to fixed vertex degrees, and the results apply to the total conductance summed over all time steps. Naturally, their main upper bound of
[
5
, Theorem 1.1(i)]
transfers immediately to the non-adaptive setting while their main lower bound of
[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
breaks in the non-adaptive setting.

This important difference aside,
[
5
, Theorem 1.1(i)]
is precisely the restriction of
Theorem

1.3
to length-1 intervals, while
Main˜Theorem

1
can be read as a restriction of
Theorem

1.3
to
\phi_{0}=\phi_{1}=\dots=\phi
and
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{1}=\dots=\Delta
, with optimised choices of
\phi
and
\Delta
. Meanwhile,
[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
provides a graph for which the upper bound is tight with interval length
1
, while the interval length of
Theorem

1.4
is
\Theta(n)
; otherwise, they both provide tight lower bounds in their respective settings. Throughout we retain the fixed-degree assumption of
[
5
]
.

1.5
Proof sketches

We now sketch the proofs of our two main results.

Section

1.5.1
treats the upper bound (
Main˜Theorems

1
and
1.3
): a drift analysis of a Lyapunov potential that generalises the argument of Berenbrink et al.
[
5
]
by splitting time into
stable
and
unstable
intervals.

Section

1.5.2
treats the lower bound (
Main˜Theorems

2
and
1.4
), constructing temporal graphs on which the voter model is slow to reach consensus and explaining why the upper bound remains tight even when the conductance is averaged over each window rather than maximised.

1.5.1
Upper bound

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an
n
-vertex
m
-edge temporal graph with fixed vertex degrees and minimum degree
d_{\min}
. For simplicity, fix
\phi
and
\Delta
, divide time into intervals
I_{0},I_{1},\dots
of length
\Delta
, and suppose that
\phi^{I_{\ell}}(\mathcal{G})\geq\phi
for all
\ell\geq 0
. Then our goal is to prove that with probability at least
1/2
, the standard voter model reaches consensus within time
O(m\Delta/(d_{\min}\phi))
. (
Theorem

1.3
allows for arbitrary interval lengths and conductance bounds, but this does not substantially affect the argument.) Again for simplicity, we take only
\kappa=2
opinions; with some effort the argument of Berenbrink et al.
[
5
]
to bootstrap from two opinions to arbitrarily many can be adapted to our setting, so this is the heart of the proof.

We first review the
\Delta=1
case treated in
[
5
]
, which is based on a drift analysis of a Lyapunov potential. (This is a standard method — given a system
(X_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
with state space
\Sigma
, filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
, and a potential function
f\colon\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
, the
drift
at time
t
refers to
\mathbb{E}(f(X_{t+1})-f(X_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})
. See e.g.
[
20
]
for an overview.) Recall from
Definition

1.2
that
(A_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
is the evolution of the set of vertices with opinion
0
in the two-opinion voter model. In analysing the process, it is convenient to instead follow the
minority set
S_{t}
at time
t
defined via

\displaystyle S_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}A_{t}&\text{if $\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t})\leq\textnormal{Vol}(\overline{A_{t}})$};\\
\overline{A_{t}}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This trades the two absorbing states of
(A_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
,
\emptyset
and
V(G)
, for a single absorbing state at
\emptyset
. Note the minority set is chosen based on
\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t})
, not
|A_{t}|
, for reasons we explain shortly.

The potential function used in
[
5
]
is given by
\psi(S_{t})\coloneqq\sqrt{\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})}
. This is motivated by the fact that
(\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t})\colon t\geq 0)
is a martingale absorbing at
0
and
\textnormal{Vol}(V(\mathcal{G}))
. Since we define
S_{t}
in terms of volume rather than size, it is not hard to show that
(\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})\colon t\geq 0)
is a supermartingale absorbing at
0
, and this is the reason for the definition. The square root is then a simple concave function which turns a mostly-neutral drift into a negative one; this is quantified in
[
5
, Lemma 2.1]
, which implies

\mathbb{E}[\psi(S_{t+1})-\psi(s_{t})\mid S_{t}=s_{t}]\leq-\frac{d_{\min}e_{t}(s_{t},\overline{s_{t}})}{32\psi(s_{t})^{3}}=-\frac{d_{\min}\phi^{t}(s_{t})}{32\psi(s_{t})}\leq-\frac{d_{\min}\phi}{32\sqrt{m}}.

(Here the last inequality follows since
\textnormal{Vol}(s_{t})\leq\textnormal{Vol}(V(\mathcal{G}))/2=m
.) Since
\psi(S_{0})\leq\sqrt{m}
and at each step we have expected drift
-\Omega(d_{\min}\phi/\sqrt{m})
, we should therefore expect absorption in
O(\sqrt{m}/(d_{\min}\phi/\sqrt{m}))=\Theta(m/(d_{\min}\phi))
time, and it is not hard to make this bound rigorous using standard martingale methods.

The difficulty of extending this proof to our setting lies in a subtle but important point. If we could guarantee that for all
i\geq 0
, there existed
t\in I_{i}
such that
\phi^{t}(S_{t})\geq\phi
, then we would be able to use essentially the same argument as in
[
5
]
: we would accrue
\phi/\sqrt{m}
negative drift from our “one good step” of
t\in I_{i}
, and at worst accrue no negative drift from the remaining steps in
I_{i}
. However, this is not the case. Writing
I_{i}^{-}
for the start of interval
I_{i}
, we can only guarantee that there exists
t\in I_{i}
such that
\phi^{t}(S_{I_{i}^{-}})\geq\phi
;
t
and
I_{i}^{-}
may be far apart and
S_{t}
may be very different from
S_{I_{i}^{-}}
, so this is not sufficient. This is not merely a technical difficulty — we will see in
Section

1.5.2
that this situation is the reason why our upper bound is tight even given a lower bound on the average conductances
\frac{1}{|I_{i}|}\sum_{t\in I_{i}}\phi^{t}(S)
rather than simply the maximum conductances
\phi^{I_{i}}(S)
.

We effectively proceed by a case analysis on each interval
I_{i}
, conditioned on the state of the process at
I_{i}^{-}
. Let
t^{\prime}\in I_{i}
be the “one good step” we are guaranteed with
\phi^{t^{\prime}}(S_{I_{i}^{-}})\geq\phi
. Very informally, our cases are:

(i)

Neither
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})
nor
e_{t}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})
is likely to change significantly over
t\in I_{i}
.

(ii)

\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})
is not likely to change significantly over
t\in I_{i}
, but
e_{t}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})
is likely to change significantly over
t\in I_{i}
.

(iii)

\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})
is likely to change significantly over
t\in I_{i}
.

In case (i), we have
\phi^{t^{\prime}}(S_{t^{\prime}})\approx\phi^{t^{\prime}}(S_{I_{i}^{-}})
and so we may apply the same analysis as in
[
5
]
. In case (ii), informally, we will prove that when volume is held roughly constant,
e(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})
can only be likely to change significantly over
t\in I_{i}
if
\sum_{t\in I_{i}}e(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})
is likely to be large; since volume is held roughly constant, this implies that
\sum_{t\in I_{i}}\phi^{t}(S_{t})
is likely to be large and we again have significant negative drift. In case (iii), while we may or may not have significant negative drift in
\psi(S_{t})
, it is at least true that
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})
is likely to change significantly; we might expect that absorption should be likely after relatively few such intervals have passed.

In order to make these ideas rigorous, in cases (i) and (ii) we will need deterministic bounds on
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})
. To obtain these, rather than working with the intervals
I_{0},I_{1},\dots
directly, we define stopping times
0=T_{0},T_{1},\dots
in
Definition

3.2
and work with the intervals
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
. By default each stopping time
T_{i}
occurs at the start of interval
I_{i}
, but it can occur early at time
t>T_{i-1}
if
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})\notin[\tfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{i-1}}),\tfrac{3}{2}\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{i-1}})]
. Thus
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})
is guaranteed to stay near-constant within each interval
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
.

Given this formulation, the distinction between cases (i) and (ii) versus case (iii) for a given interval
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
is whether or not
T_{i+1}
is likely to occur early (i.e. before the start of interval
I_{i+1}
). We set this out in
Definition

3.4
, where we define a
stable
interval
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
as one with

\mathbb{E}[|\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{i+1}})-\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{i}})|\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{i}}]<\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{i}})/8,

where
\mathcal{H}_{t}
generates the filtration of the voter model and is formally defined in
Section

2.2
. An
unstable
interval is one for which this does not hold. By Markov’s inequality we see that
T_{i+1}
is unlikely to occur early in a stable interval, as will be required for cases (i) and (ii); for case (iii) in an unstable interval, while the lower bound on expected change in volume is technically weaker than a probabilistic bound, it will turn out to be more natural to work with.

We perform a drift analysis on stable intervals with respect to
\psi
in
Section

3.1
. Our key result in both cases is
Corollary

3.6
, which exploits the fact that
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})
cannot change significantly over
I_{i}
to bound the backward drift of
\psi
in terms of

\mu\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=T_{i}}^{T_{i+1}-1}e_{t}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{i}}\Big].

In order for
Corollary

3.6
to give meaningful negative drift in
\psi
, we will need to bound
\mu
below. Recall that by our hypothesis, we are guaranteed “one good step”
t^{\prime}\in I_{i}
for which
\phi^{t^{\prime}}(S_{T_{i}})\geq\phi
. We bound
\mu
below in
Lemma

3.7
via a case distinction on

\mu^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=T_{i}}^{\min\{T_{i+1},t^{\prime}\}-1}e_{t}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{i}}\Big].

If
\mu^{\prime}
is small, then via
Lemma

2.8
it can be shown that we are in case (i) — that is, in expectation
e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t^{\prime}},\overline{S_{t^{\prime}}})\approx e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})
and hence
\phi^{t^{\prime}}(S_{t^{\prime}})\gtrapprox\phi
. (This should match our intuition — if
e_{t}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})
is small for some
t
, then we should expect
S_{t+1}
to be close to
S_{t}
and hence
e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t+1},\overline{S_{t+1}})\approx e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})
.) We can use this to bound
\mu
below at the
t^{\prime}
term. If instead
\mu^{\prime}
is large, then we are in case (ii), and we can bound
\mu
below directly by
\mu^{\prime}
. Combining
Corollary

3.6
and
Lemma

3.7
yields
Lemma

3.8
, which implies that
(\psi(S_{T_{i}})\colon i\geq 0)
is a supermartingale with drift
-\Omega(d_{\min}\phi/\sqrt{m})
whenever
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
is stable.

Having finished with cases (i) and (ii), we next analyse case (iii) (when the interval
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
is unstable) in
Section

3.2
. Here we forego an analysis of
\psi
in favour of a different potential function. Intuitively, if every interval
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
were unstable, then on average we should expect each
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{i+1}})
to differ from
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{i}})
by at least a constant factor, giving rise to something like a simple random walk on
\{0,\dots,\log(2m)\}
. We are able to make this idea rigorous by analysing the drift of the potential function
\chi(S_{t})\coloneqq\log(1+\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t}))
. We do so first in a generic setting (
Lemma

3.9
) before proving in
Corollary

3.11
that
(\chi(S_{T_{i}})\colon i\geq 0)
is a supermartingale which has drift
-\Omega(1)
whenever
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
is an unstable interval.

It now remains to combine the stable case with the unstable case, which we do in
Section

3.3
. Thankfully, we proved that both
(\psi(S_{T_{i}})\colon i\geq 0)
and
(\chi(S_{T_{i}})\colon i\geq 0)
are supermartingales regardless of which intervals are stable or unstable. As such we can simply normalise and add our two potentials to obtain a final supermartingale

\Psi_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{m}}{d_{\min}}\psi(S_{T_{i}})+\chi(S_{T_{i}}).

When
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
is stable,
\Psi
has
-\Omega(\phi)
drift from
\psi
, and when
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
is unstable it has
-\Omega(1)
drift from
\chi
; thus in both cases
\Psi
has
-\Omega(\phi)
drift. Since
\Psi_{i}=(m/d_{\min})
for all
i
, applying the optional stopping theorem in the usual way then yields our two-opinion result of
Theorem

3.1
. We can then bootstrap to our final
\kappa
-opinion result in a similar fashion to
[
5
]
. (Strictly speaking this requires a bound on absorption time in terms of
S_{0}
rather than
m
, but this does not require significant changes to the proof sketch.)

1.5.2
Lower bound

Let
n\geq 4
be an even integer, and let
T\geq\log^{2}n
be an integer. We sketch the construction of a
1
-regular
n
-vertex temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
with
\phi(\mathcal{G})=\Omega(1/(nT))
, average (static) conductance
\Omega(1/n)
on all intervals of length
3T
, and upon which the standard two-opinion voter model may take
\Omega(Tn^{2})
time to absorb. This suffices to prove
Theorem

1.4
for
d=1
, as well as
Main˜Theorem

2
in general. We will then explain how to adapt the construction to other vertex degrees.

Let
V(\mathcal{G})=[n]
. For all
i\geq 0
, let
I_{i}=[iT,(i+1)T-1]
. For all
t\in I_{1}\cup I_{3}\cup\dots
, let the edge set of
\mathcal{G}_{t}
be
\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\dots,\{n-1,n\}\}
. For all
t\in I_{0}\cup I_{2}\cup\dots
, let the edge set of
\mathcal{G}_{t}
be
\{\{2,3\},\{4,5\}\dots,\{n-2,n-1\},\{n,1\}\}
. Thus
\mathcal{G}
alternates with period
T
between the two perfect matchings of an
n
-vertex cycle; in particular,
\mathcal{G}
is
1
-regular as required.

We next bound the conductance of
\mathcal{G}
below. Fix
S\subseteq V(\mathcal{G})
with
0<\textnormal{Vol}(S)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{Vol}(V(\mathcal{G}))=n/2
. Observe that since
S\notin\{\emptyset,V(\mathcal{G})\}
,
S
must send out at least one edge throughout either odd or even intervals
I_{i}
(or both); suppose without loss of generality that
e_{t}(S,\overline{S})\geq 1
for all
t\in I_{i}
with
i
odd. Then any interval
I
of length
3T
must contain at least one such
I_{i}
, and so

\frac{1}{3T}\sum_{t\in I}\phi^{t}(S)\geq\frac{1}{3T}\sum_{t\in I_{i}}\phi^{t}(S)\geq\frac{1}{3T}\sum_{t\in I_{i}}\frac{1}{\textnormal{Vol}(S)}\geq\frac{1}{3T}\cdot\frac{|I_{i}|}{n/2}=\frac{2}{3n}.

Thus the required lower bound on average static conductance holds. Moreover, by a similar argument, every length-
(T+1)
interval contains at least one step
t
with
\phi^{t}(S)\geq 2/n
, so
\Phi(\mathcal{G})=\Omega(1/(nT))
. While not necessary for the proof, it is not hard to show that
\Phi(\mathcal{G})=O(1/(nT))
also by considering sets
S
of the form
\{1,\dots,k\}
with
k=\Theta(n)
even.

Finally, we prove that the standard two-opinion voter model may take
\Omega(Tn^{2})
time to absorb. Suppose the initial opinion-
0
set is of the form
\{2,\dots,k\}
with
k=\Theta(n)
even. Then throughout
I_{0}
, the only opinions which can change are those of
k
and
k+1
. Recall that the standard voter model is synchronous and lazy, so at each time step one vertex takes the other’s opinion with probability
1/2
; otherwise, either they exchange opinions (with probability
1/4
) or do nothing (with probability
1/4
). Since
|I_{0}|=T\geq\log^{2}n
, with probability
1-n^{-\omega(1)}
, at the end of
I_{0}
,
k
and
k+1
will share the same opinion and the new opinion-
0
state will be either
\{2,\dots,k-1\}
or
\{2,\dots,k+1\}
. The process continues to evolve in this manner: with high probability, over the course of each interval
I_{i}
, the opinion-
0
set changes from one contiguous interval to another, with each endpoint shifting by at most
1
in an unbiased fashion. It is not hard to prove (e.g. using Azuma’s inequality) that with probability at least
1/2
, such a system does not absorb until
\Omega(n^{2})
intervals
I_{i}
have passed, i.e.
\Omega(Tn^{2})
total time.

For the more general construction (for arbitrary odd vertex degrees), we blow up each vertex
i
into a set
V_{i}
of size
(d-1)/2
and replace each matching edge
\{i,i+1\}
(modulo
n
) with a clique on
V_{i}\cup V_{i+1}
, obtaining an
nd
-vertex graph. This construction is defined as
\mathcal{G}^{T,k,z}
in
Definition

4.1
. The bounds on conductance and absorption time follow by analogous arguments to the
d=1
case in Lemmas
4.4
and
4.6
respectively, and the fundamental behaviour of the construction is similar (with the opinion-
0
state being of the form
V_{i}\cup V_{i+1}\cup\dots\cup V_{j}
with high probability at the start of every interval).

Preliminaries

In this section we introduce the notation, basic definitions and well-known probability results relevant to prove our main theorems. Throughout the paper, upper-case letters such as
A_{t}
denote random variables taking values in subsets of
V(\mathcal{G})
, whereas lower-case letters such as
a_{t}
denote fixed subsets of
V(\mathcal{G})
. We also use the notation
a\wedge b\coloneqq\min\{a,b\}
and

.
We use

2.1
General Lemmas

Below we state two classical results that will be used repeatedly throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.1

(Optional stopping theorem)
.

Let
(X_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
be a supermartingale with some filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
. Let
\tau
be a stopping time for
(X_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
, suppose that
\tau
is almost surely finite, and let
t\geq 0
. Then
\mathbb{E}(X_{\tau}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})\leq X_{t}
.

Lemma 2.2

(Azuma’s inequality for
martingales)
.

Let
(Y_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
be a martingale w.r.t the filtration

(\mathcal{F}_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose there exist constants
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\geq 0
such that

\lvert Y_{i}-Y_{i-1}\rvert\leq c_{i}

holds almost surely for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Then, for every
T\geq 0
, we have:

\mathbb{P}[\lvert Y_{n}-Y_{0}\rvert\geq T]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{T^{2}}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{2}}\right).

2.2
Martingale properties of the volume process

We now show that
(\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t})\colon t\geq 0)
is a
martingale
and
(\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})\colon t\geq 0)
is a
supermartingale
with respect to the standard filtration of the voter model on
\mathcal{G}
, which is defined as follows.

Definition 2.3

(Filtration)
.

We define
the filtration of the standard voter model
as
(\sigma(\mathcal{H}_{t})\colon t\geq 0)
where
\mathcal{H}_{t}=(A_{0},A_{1},\dots,A_{t})
is the random variable that holds the entire
history
of the process so far and
\sigma(\mathcal{H}_{t})
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathcal{H}_{t}
.

A sequence
(X_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{N})
of random variables is a

martingale under a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t}\colon t\in\mathbb{N})
if for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]=X_{t}
.
The sequence is a
sub-martingale
if instead

\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\geq X_{t}
holds,
and it is a
super-martingale
if
instead
\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq X_{t}
holds.

In the standard voter model with two opinions competing on a temporal graph whose vertex-degrees are fixed, it is equally likely that a vertex with
opinion 1 switches to opinion 0 as it is that a vertex with opinion 0
switches to opinion 1.
Thus, the evolution of the volume of each opinion is a martingale.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a temporal graph whose vertex-degrees are fixed.
Let
(A_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
be the evolution of the opinion-
0
set in
the standard voter model on
\mathcal{G}
. Then,
(\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t})\colon t\geq 0)
and
(\textnormal{Vol}(\overline{A_{t}})\colon t\geq 0)
are martingales under the filtration
(\sigma(\mathcal{H}_{t})\colon{t}\geq 0)
.

Proof.

We only show that
(\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t})\colon t\geq 0)
is a martingale; the proof for
(\textnormal{Vol}(\overline{A_{t}})\colon t\geq 0)
is then immediate.
We have to show
{\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t+1})-\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t})\mid\mathcal{H}_{t}]=0}
.
Note that
A_{t}
and thus
\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t})
is fully determined by the history
\mathcal{H}_{t}
, but the randomness used to generate
A_{t+1}
from
A_{t}
(that is, one synchronous update step of the voter process) is independent from
\mathcal{H}_{t}
.
From the definition of the standard voter model, we thus have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\,\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t+1})-\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t})\bigm|\mathcal{H}_{t}\,\bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{v\in A_{t+1}\setminus A_{t}}d(v)-\sum_{v\in A_{t}\setminus A_{t+1}}d(v)\,\Big|\,\mathcal{H}_{t}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{v\in\overline{A_{t}}}\mathbb{P}(v\in A_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t})d(v)-\sum_{v\in A_{t}}\mathbb{P}(v\in\overline{A_{t+1}}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t})d(v)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{v\in\overline{A_{t}}}\frac{e_{t}(v,A_{t})}{2d(v)}d(v)-\sum_{v\in A_{t}}\frac{e_{t}(v,\overline{A_{t}})}{2d(v)}d(v)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\big(e_{t}(\overline{A_{t}},A_{t})-e_{t}(A_{t},\overline{A_{t}})\big)=0.

The ultimate equality holds because the graph is undirected, which concludes the proof.
∎

We now state the standard fact that the minimum of two martingales is a supermartingale.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(M_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
and
(N_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
be two discrete-time
martingales with respect to the same filtration

(\mathcal{F}_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
. Then
(X_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
defined
via
X_{t}\coloneqq\min\{M_{t},N_{t}\}
is a supermartingale with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
.

Proof.

Since
M_{t}
and
N_{t}
are
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable,
X_{t}
is

\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable. Moreover, for any
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}[\lvert M_{t}\rvert]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}[\lvert N_{t}\rvert]<\infty
then
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]<\infty
.
Since
\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}\wedge N_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]
and likewise for
N_{t+1}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\bigl[M_{t+1}\wedge N_{t+1}\bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t}\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]\wedge\mathbb{E}[N_{t+1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}].

Since
M_{t}
and
N_{t}
are martingales, it follows that

\mathbb{E}[M_{t+1}\wedge N_{t+1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq M_{t}\wedge N_{t}.

The result follows.
∎

Corollary 2.7

\mathcal{G}

Proof.

Let
A_{t}\subseteq V(\mathcal{G})

t
and
\overline{A_{t}}
the set of vertices with opinion
1
at time
t
.
By
Lemma

2.4
, we have that both

\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t})
and
\textnormal{Vol}(\overline{A_{t}})
are martingales with respect to

(\sigma(\mathcal{H}_{t})\colon{t}\geq 0)
. Observe that

\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})=\min\{\textnormal{Vol}(A_{t}),\textnormal{Vol}(\overline{A_{t}})\}
, then by

Lemma

2.5
,
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})
is a
supermartingale with respect to
(\sigma(\mathcal{H}_{t})\colon{t}\geq 0)
.
∎

2.3
Edges Crossing the Cut

The following lemma focus on the change in the number of
edges between a given set and its complement, in particular, it
establishes a bound for the stepwise variation.

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{G}
is a temporal graph whose vertex-degrees are
fixed.
Let
(S_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
be the
evolution of the minority set in the standard voter model on
\mathcal{G}
with
\kappa=2
. For any
t,j\geq 0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\lvert e_{t}(S_{j+1},\overline{S_{j+1}})-e_{t}(S_{j},\overline{S_{j}})\right\rvert\;\Big|\;\mathcal{H}_{j}\Big]\leq e_{j}(S_{j},\overline{S_{j}}).

Proof.

First observe that for all
x,y\geq 0
,
e_{x}(S_{y},\overline{S_{y}})=e_{x}(A_{y},\overline{A_{y}})
; thus it suffices to prove

\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\lvert e_{t}(A_{j+1},\overline{A_{j+1}})-e_{t}(A_{j},\overline{A_{j}})\right\rvert\;\Big|\;\mathcal{H}_{j}\Big]\leq e_{j}(A_{j},\overline{A_{j}}).

Let
A_{j}\ominus A_{j+1}
be the symmetric difference of
A_{j}

and
A_{j+1}
. Every single vertex
v
that swaps from

A_{j}
to
\overline{A_{j}}
or vice versa removes at most
d(v)
edges from
the cut. This gives a bound of

e_{t}(A_{j+1},\overline{A_{j+1}})\geq e_{t}(A_{j},\overline{A_{j}})-\sum_{v\in A_{j}\ominus A_{j+1}}d(v).

Likewise, every single vertex
v
that swaps
from
A_{j}
to
\overline{A_{j}}
or vice versa adds at most
d(v)
edges
to the cut, giving

e_{t}(A_{j+1},\overline{A_{j+1}})\leq e_{t}(A_{j},\overline{A_{j}})+\sum_{v\in A_{j}\ominus A_{j+1}}d(v).

Therefore, the absolute change is bounded as follows,

\lvert e_{t}(A_{j+1},\overline{A_{j+1}})-e_{t}(A_{j},\overline{A_{j}})\rvert\leq\sum_{v\in A_{j}\ominus A_{j+1}}d(v).

Hence

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big\lvert e_{t}(A_{j+1},\overline{A_{j+1}})-e_{t}(A_{j},\overline{A_{j}})\big\rvert\Bigm|\mathcal{H}_{j}\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{v\in A_{j}\ominus A_{j+1}}d(v)\Bigm|\mathcal{H}_{j}\Big].

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{v\in A_{j}\ominus A_{j+1}}

\displaystyle d(v)\Bigm|\mathcal{H}_{j}\Big]=\sum_{v\in V}\mathbb{P}(v\in A_{j}\ominus A_{j+1}\mid\mathcal{H}_{j})\cdot d(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in A_{j}}\mathbb{P}(v\in\overline{A_{j+1}}\mid\mathcal{H}_{j})\cdot d(v)+\sum_{v\in\overline{A_{j}}}\mathbb{P}(v\in A_{j+1}\mid\mathcal{H}_{j})\cdot d(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in A_{j}}\frac{e_{j}(v,\overline{A_{j}})}{2d(v)}\cdot d(v)+\sum_{v\in\overline{A_{j}}}\frac{e_{j}(v,A_{j})}{2d(v)}\cdot d(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e_{j}(A_{j},\overline{A_{j}})+\frac{1}{2}e_{j}(A_{j},\overline{A_{j}})

\displaystyle=e_{j}(A_{j},\overline{A_{j}})\,.

This finishes the proof.
∎

3
Proof of the Main Theorem

In this section, our goal is to prove
Theorem

1.3
. The heart of the proof is the two-opinion case; given this, we will be able to follow the argument of Berenbrink et al.
[
5
]
to extend to the general case. Our result is the following, which is essentially the two-opinion case.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists a constant
b>0
such that the following holds. Consider the standard two-opinion voter model on a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
with fixed vertex degrees and minimum degree
d_{\min}
, with arbitrary initial
minority set
s_{0}
. Let
\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1},\dots\geq 1
be integers, and let
\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\dots
be real numbers in
[0,1]
. For all
j\geq 0
, let
I_{j}^{-}=\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{j-1}
, let
I_{j}^{+}=I_{j}^{-}+\Delta_{j}-1
, and let
I_{j}=[I_{j}^{-},I_{j}^{+}]
. Suppose that for all
j\geq 0
,
\phi^{I_{j}}(\mathcal{G})\geq\phi_{j}
. Let

J=\min\Big\{j\colon\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\phi_{\ell}\geq b\Big(\frac{\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0})}{d_{\min}}+\log(1+\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0}))\Big)\Big\},

where we require
J<\infty
. Then with probability at least
1/2
, the consensus time is at most
\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{J}
.

Note that when
\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0})=\Theta(m)
in
Theorem

3.1
, the
\log(1+\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0}))
term is dominated by the
m/d_{\min}=\Theta(n)
term.

We will prove
Theorem

3.1
in
Section

3.3
, then prove
Theorem

1.3
from
Theorem

3.1
in
Section

3.4
. To this end, until the end of
Section

3.3
, let the following be defined as in the statement of
Theorem

3.1
:
\mathcal{G}
,
d_{\min}
,
J
, and (for all
j
)
\Delta_{j}
,
\phi_{j}
,
I_{j}^{+}
,
I_{j}^{-}
and
I_{j}
.
Let
S_{0}\subseteq V
be a fixed initial minority
set, and let
(S_{t}\colon t\geq 0)
be the minority set of a standard
voter model running on
\mathcal{G}
from
S_{0}
with
\kappa=2
opinions.

We will bound the likely absorption time of
(S_{t})
by considering an embedded voter process. One might expect this voter process to consider times
I_{1}^{-},I_{2}^{-},\dots
, but it will be convenient for us to “stop an interval early” if the volume of
(S_{t})
deviates significantly from its initial value. As such we define the following sequence of stopping times.

Definition 3.2

(Stopping times)
.

Let
T_{0}=0
. Given
T_{0},\dots,T_{j}
for some
j\geq 0
, we define
T_{j+1}
as follows. Let

T_{j_{\min}}\coloneqq\min\big\{t\geq T_{j}\colon\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})\notin\Big[\tfrac{1}{2}\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{j}}),\;\tfrac{3}{2}\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{j}})\Big]\textnormal{ or }\;S_{t}=\emptyset\big\}\,.

Then

T_{j+1}\coloneqq\min\Big\{I_{j}^{+}+1,\;T_{j_{\min}}\Big\}\,.

Observe that
T_{j_{\min}}
and
T_{j}
are indeed stopping
times for all
j
.

Definition 3.3

(Embedded voter process)
.

The
embedded voter process
is the process
((T_{j},S_{T_{j}})\colon j\geq 0)
where the minority set is sampled only at the stopping times
defined in
Definition

3.2
. Moreover, the
embedded
volume process
is defined by
(\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{j}})\colon j\geq 0)
.

Observe that the embedded voter process is a Markov chain and that the embedded volume process is a supermartingale, from the corresponding properties of the original voter process (together with the optional stopping theorem).
(If
T_{j}
were not included in the
j
’th state, however, then the embedded voter process would not be a Markov chain — indeed, the value of each graph
G_{t}
on which the voter process runs in
[T_{j},T_{j+1}-1]
depends on
t
itself, not only on
t-T_{j}
).

Recall from
Theorem

1.3
that our goal is to prove likely absorption in
(S_{t})
within time
\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{J}=I_{J}^{+}+1
; since
T_{J}\leq I_{J}^{+}+1
, it will suffice to prove likely absorption within time
T_{J}
, and we will focus our efforts on the embedded chain from this point forward. We will analyse the embedded chain by splitting the intervals
[T_{j},T_{j+1}-1]
into two kinds depending on whether or not
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})
is likely to fluctuate significantly over
t\in[T_{j},T_{j+1}-1]
.

Definition 3.4

(Stable and unstable
intervals)
.

Let
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
H_{T_{j}}
be a possible value of

\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}
such that
S_{T_{j}}\neq\emptyset
whenever
H_{T_{j}}=\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}
. If
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{j+1}})-\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{j}})\rvert\bigm|\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{T_{j}}\bigr]<\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{j}})/8
, we say that both
H_{T_{j}}
and
the interval
[T_{j},T_{j+1})
are
stable
. Otherwise, we say
they are both
unstable
.

We will analyse stable intervals in
Section

3.1
, then analyse unstable intervals in
Section

3.2
, then combine the two cases in
Section

3.3
.

3.1

In this section we prove backwards bias in

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\,\lvert\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{j+1}})-\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{j}})\rvert\bigm|\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{T_{j}}\,\bigr]<\frac{1}{8}\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{j}})

. We first import the following

1

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Bigl(1_{T_{j+1}>t^{\prime}}\cdot\lvert e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t^{\prime}},\overline{S_{t^{\prime}}})-e_{t^{\prime}}(s_{t_{j}},\overline{s_{t_{j}}})\rvert\geq\tfrac{3}{4}\phi_{j}\textnormal{Vol}(s_{t_{j}})\Bigm|\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\Bigr).

By Markov’s inequality, letting

\nu\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[1_{T_{j+1}>t^{\prime}}\lvert e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t^{\prime}},\overline{S_{t^{\prime}}})-e_{t^{\prime}}(s_{t_{j}},\overline{s_{t_{j}}})\rvert\Bigm|\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\Bigr]\,,

it follows that

\displaystyle p\leq\frac{\nu}{\tfrac{3}{4}\phi_{j}\textnormal{Vol}(s_{t_{j}})}.

(11)

We next bound
\nu
above. By the triangle inequality applied to a telescoping sum,

\nu\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[1_{T_{j+1}>t^{\prime}}\sum_{t=t_{j}}^{t^{\prime}-1}\lvert e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t+1},\overline{S_{t+1}})-e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})\rvert\,\Big|\,\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\Big].

By the tower property, taking
expectations over
\mathcal{H}_{t}
subject to our existing conditioning on

\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=t_{j}}^{t^{\prime}-1}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\bigl[1_{T_{j+1}>t}\cdot\lvert e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t+1},\overline{S_{t+1}})-e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})\rvert\bigm|\mathcal{H}_{t}\bigr]\Bigm|\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=t_{j}}^{t^{\prime}-1}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[1_{T_{j+1}>t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t+1},\overline{S_{t+1}})-e_{t^{\prime}}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})\rvert\bigm|\mathcal{H}_{t}\bigr]\Bigm|\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\Bigr].

By
Lemma

2.8
applied with that lemma’s
j
and
t
equal to our
t
and
t^{\prime}
, respectively, it follows that

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=t_{j}}^{t^{\prime}-1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[1_{T_{j+1}>t}\cdot e_{t}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})\bigm|\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\,\sum_{t=t_{j}}^{(T_{j+1}\wedge t^{\prime})-1}e_{t}(S_{t},\overline{S_{t}})\Bigm|\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\,\Bigr]=\mu^{\prime}\leq\phi_{j}\textnormal{Vol}(s_{t_{j}})/8\,.

Plugging this bound on
\nu
into
Eq.

11
, we
obtain
p\leq 1/6
. By
Eq.

10
it follows that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}})>1/2
, so the result follows from
Eq.

8
.
∎

3.2
Case 2: Unstable Intervals

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose that
(X_{t})
is a non-negative
integer-valued supermartingale with filtration
(\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{t})\colon t\geq 0)
and initial state
x_{0}\coloneqq X_{0}
. Fix
t\geq 0
, let
F_{t}
be a possible value of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, let
x_{t}
be the value of
X_{t}
determined by
F_{t}
, and suppose
\xi\geq 0
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lvert X_{t+1}-x_{t}\rvert\bigm|\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}\bigr]\geq\xi x_{t}.

Then

\mathbb{E}[\log(1+X_{t+1})-\log(1+X_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\leq-1_{x_{t}>0}\xi^{2}/96.

Proof.

For brevity, write
\zeta(x)\coloneqq\log(1+x)
for all
x\geq 0
. If
x_{t}=0
then
X_{t+1}
must equal zero (since
X_{t+1}\geq 0
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\leq x_{t}=0
); thus the result holds. We may therefore assume
x_{t}>0
, and hence
x_{t}\geq 1
, for the rest of the proof.

Let
\Delta_{t}\coloneqq X_{t+1}-x_{t}
, and observe that conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}
,

\zeta(X_{t+1})-\zeta(x_{t})=\log\Big(\frac{1+X_{t+1}}{1+x_{t}}\Big)=\log\Big(1+\frac{\Delta_{t}}{1+x_{t}}\Big).

In this expression,
\Delta_{t}/(1+x_{t})\geq-x_{t}/(1+x_{t})>-1
. By the Taylor expansion, for all
z\in(-1,0)
we have
\log(1+z)\leq z-z^{2}/2+z^{3}/3\leq z-z^{2}/6
; moreover, for all
z\geq 0
we have
\log(1+z)\leq z
.
Thus taking
z\coloneqq\Delta_{t}/(1+x_{t})
, we obtain

\zeta(X_{t+1})-\zeta(x_{t})=\log(1+z)\leq\frac{\Delta_{t}}{1+x_{t}}-\frac{1_{\Delta_{t}<0}\Delta_{t}^{2}}{6(1+x_{t})^{2}}.

Since
(X_{t})
is a supermartingale, we have
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\leq 0
, so on taking expectations we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\zeta(X_{t+1})-\zeta(x_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\leq-\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[1_{\Delta_{t}<0}\Delta_{t}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}\big]}{6(1+x_{t})^{2}}.

Since
x_{t}\geq 1
, we have
1+x_{t}\leq 2x_{t}
; moreover, for all real variables
Z
we have
\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]\geq\mathbb{E}[Z]^{2}
, and
1_{\Delta_{t}<0}\Delta_{t}^{2}=(1_{\Delta_{t}<0}\Delta_{t})^{2}
. It follows that

\mathbb{E}[\zeta(X_{t+1})-\zeta(x_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\leq-\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[1_{\Delta_{t}<0}\Delta_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}\big]^{2}}{24x_{t}^{2}}.

(12)

We next bound this expectation by splitting
\Delta_{t}
into positive and negative parts. Let
\Delta_{t}^{+}\coloneqq\Delta_{t}\vee 0
and
\Delta_{t}^{-}\coloneqq-(\Delta_{t}\wedge 0)
, so that:
\Delta_{t}^{+},\Delta_{t}^{-}\geq 0
;
\Delta_{t}=\Delta_{t}^{+}-\Delta_{t}^{-}
;
|\Delta_{t}|=\Delta_{t}^{+}+\Delta_{t}^{-}
; and
1_{\Delta_{t}<0}\Delta_{t}=-\Delta_{t}^{-}
.
Rewriting
Eq.

12
in this notation, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\zeta(X_{t+1})-\zeta(x_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\leq-\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\Delta_{t}^{-}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}\big]^{2}}{24x_{t}^{2}}.

(13)

Since
(X_{t})
is a supermartingale,

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{+}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{-}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\leq 0,

and so
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{-}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\geq\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{+}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]
. Thus by hypothesis,

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{-}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}^{-}+\Delta_{t}^{+}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[|\Delta_{t}|\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\geq\xi x_{t}/2.

Plugging this into
Eq.

13
then yields

\mathbb{E}[\zeta(X_{t+1})-\zeta(x_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}=F_{t}]\leq-\frac{\xi^{2}x_{t}^{2}}{96x_{t}^{2}}=-\frac{\xi^{2}}{96}.

as required.
∎

Definition 3.10
.

For all sets
S\subseteq V(\mathcal{G})
, let
\chi(S)=\log(1+\textnormal{Vol}(S))
.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
j,t_{j}\geq 0
, and suppose
H_{t_{j}}
is a possible value of
\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}
. Let
s_{t_{j}}
be the value of
S_{T_{j}}
determined by
\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\big[\chi(S_{T_{j+1}})-\chi(s_{t_{j}})\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\big]\leq 0.

If in addition
s_{t_{j}}\neq\emptyset
and

is unstable, then

\mathbb{E}\big[\chi(\textnormal{Vol}(S_{T_{j+1}}))-\chi(\textnormal{Vol}(s_{t_{j}}))\,\big|\,\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\big]\leq-10^{-4}.

Proof.

We apply

3.9

3.3
Interplay Between the Two Cases

See
3.1

Proof.

If
s_{0}=\emptyset
then the result is immediate, so suppose
s_{0}\neq\emptyset
. Let

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\{t\geq 0\colon S_{t}=\emptyset\}\wedge T_{J},

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tau\wedge\min\{t\geq 0\colon\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t})\geq 8\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0})\}.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the event that either

\textnormal{Vol}(S_{\tau^{\prime}})\geq 8\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0})
or
\tau^{\prime}=T_{J}
and
S_{\tau^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
.
We will first prove that
\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Vol}(S_{\tau^{\prime}})\geq 8\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0}))\leq 1/8
, then prove that
\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}=T_{J}\text{ and }S_{\tau^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset)\leq 3/8
. Given these two facts, the result will follow easily by a union bound.

Bounding
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Vol}(S_{\tau^{\prime}})\geq 8\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0}))}
:

By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Vol}(S_{\tau^{\prime}})\geq 8\textnormal{Vol}(s_{o}))\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\textnormal{Vol}(S_{\tau^{\prime}})]}{8\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0})}.

(14)

Recall that
(\textnormal{Vol}(S_{t}))
is a supermartingale, and
\tau^{\prime}
is a finite stopping time for it. Thus by the optional stopping theorem,
\mathbb{E}(\textnormal{Vol}(S_{\tau^{\prime}}))\leq\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0})
. It therefore follows by
Eq.

14
that

\mathbb{P}(\textnormal{Vol}(S_{\tau^{\prime}})\geq 8\textnormal{Vol}(s_{o}))\leq 1/8,

(15)

as claimed.

Bounding
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}=T_{J}\text{ and }S_{\tau^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset)}
:
We will first establish downward drift of the following potential. For all
j\geq 0
, let

\Psi_{j}\coloneqq\frac{\psi(s_{0})}{d_{\min}}\psi(S_{T_{j}})+\chi(S_{T_{j}}).

Temporarily fix
j\geq 0
, and suppose
H_{t_{j}}
is a possible value of
\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}
. Let
s_{t_{j}}
be the corresponding value of
S_{T_{j}}
determined by
H_{t_{j}}
. Then we split into three cases, bounding the drift of
\Psi_{j}
in each case. First suppose
H_{t_{j}}
is stable and determines
t_{j}<\tau^{\prime}
. This implies
s_{t_{j}}\neq\emptyset
, so by
Lemma

3.8
and
Corollary

3.11
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Psi_{j+1}-\Psi_{j}\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi(s_{0})}{d_{\min}}\mathbb{E}\big[\psi(S_{T_{j+1}})-\psi(s_{t_{j}})\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\big]\ +

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[\chi(S_{T_{j+1}})-\chi(s_{t_{j}})\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\phi_{j}\psi(s_{0})}{500\psi(s_{t_{j}})}+0.

Since
t_{j}<\tau^{\prime}
,
\psi(s_{t_{j}})\leq\sqrt{8}\psi(s_{0})
, so it follows that

\mathbb{E}\big[\Psi_{j+1}-\Psi_{j}\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\big]\leq-\phi_{j}/2000.

Similarly, if instead
H_{t_{j}}
is unstable and determines
t_{j}<\tau^{\prime}
, then
s_{t_{j}}\neq\emptyset
, so by
Lemma

3.8
and
Corollary

3.11
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\Psi_{j+1}-\Psi_{j}\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\big]\leq 0-10^{-4}=-10^{-4}.

Finally, if instead
H_{t_{j}}
determines
t_{j}\geq\tau^{\prime}
, then by
Lemma

3.8
and
Corollary

3.11
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\Psi_{j+1}-\Psi_{j}\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}=H_{t_{j}}\big]\leq 0+0=0.

Thus on combining all three cases, we obtain that for all
j\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\Psi_{j+1}-\Psi_{j}\mid\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}}\big]\leq-D_{j},\mbox{ where }D_{j}\coloneqq 1_{T_{j}<\tau^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{\phi_{j}}{4000}\wedge 10^{-4}\Big).

(16)

Let
J^{\prime}=\min\{j\geq 0\colon T_{j}\geq\tau^{\prime}\}
. Since
s_{0}\neq\emptyset
we have
\tau^{\prime}>0
, so
J^{\prime}>0
and
T_{J^{\prime}-1}<\tau^{\prime}\leq T_{J^{\prime}}
. We will next apply the optional stopping theorem in a standard way to argue that the total accumulation of expected drift up to time
T_{J^{\prime}}
, i.e.
\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}D_{\ell}
, is unlikely to be large; we will exploit this to bound
J^{\prime}
above, in order to bound
T_{J^{\prime}}
above, in order to finally bound
\tau^{\prime}
above.

For all
j\geq 0
, let

X_{j}\coloneqq\Psi_{j}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-1}D_{\ell}.

By
Eq.

16
,
(X_{j})
is a supermartingale under the filtration generated by
(\mathcal{H}_{T_{j}})
; moreover,
J^{\prime}
is a finite stopping time for
(X_{j})
. Thus by the optional stopping theorem,
\mathbb{E}[X_{J^{\prime}}]\leq X_{0}
.
Expanding out both sides, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\Psi_{J^{\prime}}]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}D_{\ell}\Big]\leq\Psi_{0}.

Since
\Psi_{J^{\prime}}\geq 0
, it follows that
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}D_{\ell}]\leq\Psi_{0}
. Markov’s inequality then yields

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}D_{\ell}\geq 4\Psi_{0}\Big)\leq 1/4.

(17)

We will next use
Eq.

17
to prove
\mathbb{P}(J^{\prime}\geq J)\leq 1/4
. For this, it is enough to prove that
\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}D_{\ell}<4\Psi_{0}
implies
J^{\prime}<J
; by the definition of
J
, this is equivalent to proving

\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}}\phi_{\ell}<b\Big(\frac{\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0})}{d_{\min}}+\log(1+\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0}))\Big).

(18)

To this end, suppose that
\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}D_{\ell}<4\Psi_{0}
. By the definition of
J^{\prime}
, for all
\ell<J^{\prime}
we have
T_{\ell}<\tau^{\prime}
; thus by the definition of the
D_{\ell}
’s,

\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}D_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}\Big(\frac{\phi_{\ell}}{4000}\wedge 10^{-4}\Big)\geq 10^{-4}\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}\phi_{\ell}.

Since
\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}D_{\ell}<4\Psi_{0}
by assumption, it follows that

\sum_{\ell=0}^{J^{\prime}-1}\phi_{j}<4\cdot 10^{4}\Psi_{0}=4\cdot 10^{4}\Big(\frac{\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0})}{d_{\min}}+\log(1+\textnormal{Vol}(s_{0}))\Big);

on taking
b\geq 8\cdot 10^{4}
this implies
Eq.

18
, and hence
J^{\prime}<J
as required. We have therefore proved

\mathbb{P}(J^{\prime}\geq J)\leq 1/4.

(19)

We now use
Eq.

19
to prove
\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}=T_{J}\text{ and }S_{\tau^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset)\leq 1/4
, our original goal. It suffices to prove that whenever
\tau^{\prime}=T_{J}\text{ and }S_{\tau^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
occurs,
J^{\prime}\geq J
. By the definition of
J^{\prime}
, we have
T_{J^{\prime}}\geq\tau^{\prime}=T_{J}>T_{J^{\prime}-1}
, where the final inequality follows since
S_{\tau^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
. This implies
J^{\prime}>J-1
and hence
J^{\prime}\geq J
. It is therefore immediate from
Eq.

19
that

\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}=T_{J}\text{ and }S_{\tau^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset)\leq 1/4<3/8.

(20)

Putting everything together:

Combining
Eq.

15
and
Eq.

20
with a union bound, we see that with probability at least
1/2
,
\mathcal{E}
does not occur, it follows that either
\textnormal{Vol}(S_{\tau^{\prime}})<8\textnormal{Vol}(s_{o})
or both
\tau^{\prime}=T_{J}
and
S_{\tau^{\prime}}=\emptyset
. In both cases,
\tau^{\prime}=\tau.
By the definition of
\tau
, this is equivalent to saying that
\tau
is the consensus time. Since by the definitions of
\tau
and
T_{J}
we have
\tau\leq T_{J}\leq I_{J}^{+}+1=I_{J+1}^{-}
, the result follows.
∎

3.4
Deriving the multi-opinion bound from the two-opinion bound

The following argument to derive a multi-opinion bound from a two-opinion bound follows essentially the same method as Berenbrink et al.
[
5
]
; however, since the lengths of our “phases” are more complex,
Theorem

3.1
has an additive error term not present in the two-opinion result of
[
5
]
, and the presentation in
[
5
]
is quite abbreviated, we give the argument in full detail.

See
1.3

Proof.

Let
\kappa
be the number of opinions present in the model. The result is immediate if
\kappa=1
, so suppose
\kappa\geq 2
. Let
B
be a large constant, to be determined later. First, we divide time into phases of deterministic length. Let
r_{\max}\coloneqq\lfloor Bm/d_{\min}\rfloor
. Let
\ell_{0}=t_{0}=0
, and for all integers
r
with
1\leq r\leq r_{\max}
, let

\ell_{r}\coloneqq\min\Big\{\ell\colon\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\phi_{j}\geq r\Big\},\qquad t_{r}\coloneqq I_{\ell_{r}}^{-}=\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{\ell_{r}-1}.

For all
r\geq 0
, we call the interval
[t_{r},t_{r+1}-1]
the
r
’th phase
. Note that since each
\phi_{j}
lies in
[0,1]
, we have
t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{r_{\max}}\leq\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{J}<\infty
.

Following
[
5
]
, we now group these phases into “metaphases” of random lengths. For all
t\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{O}(t)
be the set of opinions remaining in the process at time
t
. Let
R_{0}=0
, and for all
\alpha\geq 1
, let

R_{\alpha}\coloneqq\min\Big(\{r_{\max}\}\cup\big\{r\colon|\mathcal{O}(t_{r})|\leq(5/6)^{\alpha}\kappa\vee 1\big\}\Big).

Then the
\alpha
’th metaphase
is the interval
[t_{R_{\alpha}},t_{R_{\alpha+1}}-1]
. Thus ignoring the cap at
r_{\max}
(past which no more phases are defined), before consensus the end of the
\alpha
’th metaphase occurs after however many phases it takes for the number of opinions to have dropped by a cumulative factor of
(5/6)^{\alpha}
, and after consensus all metaphases are empty.

Let
\beta\coloneqq\lceil\log_{6/5}\kappa\rceil
, and observe that
(5/6)^{\beta}\kappa\leq 1
. Thus by definition, if
R_{\beta}<r_{\max}
then
\lvert\mathcal{O}(t_{R_{\beta}})\rvert=1
and so the consensus time is at most
t_{R_{\beta}}<t_{r_{\max}}\leq\Delta_{0}+\dots+\Delta_{J}
as required.
It therefore suffices to prove that

\mathbb{P}(R_{\beta}<r_{\max})\geq 1/2.

(21)

Our next goal is to prove that taken individually, each metaphase ends in few phases with probability bounded away from zero. We will prove the following claim, where we begin in the middle of a metaphase to allow for repeated application later.

Claim:
Let
r,\alpha\geq 0
, let
H_{t_{r}}
be a possible value of
\mathcal{H}_{t_{r}}
, and suppose that
\mathcal{H}_{t_{r}}=H_{t_{r}}
determines
R_{\alpha}\leq r<R_{\alpha+1}
. Let
r_{\alpha}
and
O_{\alpha}
be the values of
R_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{O}(t_{r_{\alpha}})
determined by
H_{t_{r}}
, and let

\xi_{\alpha}\coloneqq 1+\Big\lceil b\Big(\frac{6m}{d_{\min}\lvert O_{\alpha}\rvert}+\log\big(1+6m/|O_{\alpha}|\big)\Big)\Big\rceil.

Then
\mathbb{P}(R_{\alpha+1}>r+\xi_{\alpha}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t_{r}}=H_{t_{r}})\leq 2/3
.

Proof of Claim:
If
r+\xi_{\alpha}\geq r_{\max}
then
R_{\alpha+1}\leq r_{\max}\leq r+\xi_{\alpha}
with certainty and the Claim holds immediately, so suppose for the rest of the proof that
r+\xi_{\alpha}<r_{\max}
. Thus

\mathbb{P}(R_{\alpha+1}>r+\xi_{\alpha}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t_{r}}=H_{t_{r}})=\mathbb{P}\big(|\mathcal{O}({t_{r+\xi_{\alpha}}})|>(5/6)^{\alpha+1}\kappa\vee 1\mid\mathcal{H}_{t_{r}}=H_{t_{r}}\big).

(22)

Next observe that conditioned on
\mathcal{H}_{t_{r}}=H_{t_{r}}
, since
r_{\alpha}<R_{\alpha+1}

4
Lower Bound on Absorption Time

In this section, we prove
Main˜Theorem

2
.
In order to do so, we construct a specific temporal graph where
consensus is reached only slowly.
The idea is to construct a temporal graph
\mathcal{G}
such that with high probability, the opinion-zero set of
\mathcal{G}
sampled at appropriate stopping times is a blown-up contiguous interval on a length-
z
cycle. The endpoints of this interval sampled at the same stopping times will then evolve independently according to an unbiased random walk with bounded step size; it is then not hard to show that this interval requires
\Omega(z^{2})
steps to reach the empty interval or the whole cycle, i.e. consensus.
Essentially, our temporal graph alternates between
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
, which are disjoint unions of
z/2
cliques on
2k
vertices, arranged in a cycle.
See
Fig.

2
for an illustration.

Definition 4.1
.

Let

be integers with
z
even. Then the temporal graph
\mathcal{G}^{T,k,z}
is defined as follows.

Let

be a disjoint union of sets

of
k
vertices each. For all
i\in[z]
, let
H_{i}
be a
2k
-vertex clique spanning
V_{i}
and
V_{i+1}
(modulo
z
). Let

G_{0}=\bigcup_{i\in[z]\colon i\textrm{ is even}}H_{i},\qquad G_{1}=\bigcup_{i\in[z]\colon i\textrm{ is odd}}H_{i}.

For all
j\geq 1
, let
I_{j}=\{(j-1)T,\dots,jT-1\}
. Then
\mathcal{G}
is the temporal graph which, throughout each time interval
I_{j}
, takes value
G_{j\textnormal{ mod }2}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Let
T,k,z\geq 1
be integers with
z
even. Then
\mathcal{G}^{T,k,z}
is
(2k-1)
-regular and has

vertices.

We first bound the conductance of any interval of size

for any possible minority set

below in

4.4

In order to bound the conductance of any interval of size

Lemma 4.3
.

For all

, the complete graph on
2k
vertices has conductance greater than

Proof.

Let
K_{2k}
be the complete graph with
2k
vertices. For any set

with

\phi(S)=\frac{e(S,\overline{S})}{\textnormal{Vol}(S)}=\frac{\lvert S\rvert\cdot(2k-\lvert S\rvert)}{\lvert S\rvert\cdot(2k-1)}=\frac{2k-\lvert S\rvert}{2k-1}\geq\frac{k}{2k-1}>\frac{1}{2}\,.

By definition,
\phi(K_{2k})=\min_{S}\phi(S)>1/2
