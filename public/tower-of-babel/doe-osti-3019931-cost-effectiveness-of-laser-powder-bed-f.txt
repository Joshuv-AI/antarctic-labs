 ABSTRACT

ORNL (Contractor) and Cummins Inc. (Participant) collaborated to determine the cost effectiveness of
laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) for heavy duty applications. The team demonstrated that coupons can be
fabricated with two distinct process parameters i.e., different process parameters for the bulk and surface
of the material. This is important for enabling the manufacturing of components in a production
environment where the bulk of the material can be processed rapidly while high quality surface finish can
minimize post-process machining as well as enable superior fatigue performance if the material were to
be used in as-deposited condition.

 1. COST EFFECTIVENESS OF LASER POWDER BED FUSION FOR HEAVY DUTY
 APPLICATIONS

ORNL (Contractor) and Cummins Inc. (Participant) collaborated to evaluate the properties of grade 300
maraging steel (M300) printed using LPBF for use in heavy duty applications with the long-term goal of
understanding whether the approach is cost-effective. In this Phase 1 technical collaboration, the
contractor and the participant jointly evaluated the deposition parameters used for LPBF of M300 and
their impact on the material properties.

1.1 BACKGROUND

M300 is a printable steel commonly used for fabricating tool inserts using laser powder bed fusion. The
Contractor has conducted extensive fundamental work on printing M300 using an AddUp FormUp 350
LPBF system [Nandwana et al., ORNL/TM-2022/2764; NFE-19-07534]. However, most of the work was
conducted on coupons made with specific process parameters. In this technical collaboration, the
Contractor and the Participant deposited cubic coupons such that the bulk of the material was deposited
using process parameters that facilitate high deposition rates with acceptable porosity (hereby referred to
as “productivity parameter”) while the outside surface or the shell was deposited using nominal process
parameters for a superior surface finish and low porosity (hereby referred to as “standard parameter”).
These hybrid parameters were explored with the aim of eventually facilitating higher deposition rates for
component manufacturing. However, as process parameters are varied, within the same part, the overlap
between the two different sets of process parameters becomes critical to avoid systemic porosity at the
interface. The Phase 1 of this technical collaboration focused on understanding the impact of melt overlap
i.e., thickness of the remelted region at the interface of standard and productivity parameters, and the
order of melting i.e., the cube being melted from outside in (referred to as “out-in”) or inwards out
(referred to as “in-out”) by controlling the starting position of the beam. Fatigue bars were deposited
using standard and productivity parameters separately to serve as the baseline. Additional builds were
also conducted at by the Participant on a GE Concept Laser M2 system to optimize aging heat treatments
for maximum hardness.

Figure 5 shows porosity analysis based on optical microscopy for selected samples along with hardness in
as-fabricated and heat-treated conditions for the samples manufactured on AddUp FormUp350 system. It
can be seen that the lowest porosity was observed for an overlap of 50 m, above which the pore area
fraction increases and then decreases again at 200 m overlap. At overlaps above 200 m, there is a
consistent increase in the porosity. The scan strategy does not appear to have an impact on the pore area

fraction. The hardness in as-fabricated condition is between 350-380 HV (36-39 HRC) with the variation
possibly occurring because of differences in porosity. Heat treatment increases the average hardness to
580-610 HV (54-56 HRC). The hardness of samples fabricated by the Contractor using the AddUp
FormUp 350 in as-fabricated heat-treated conditions is comparable to that obtained by the Participant on
the samples fabricated using a GE Concept Laser M2 system.

Based on the findings of this work, a 50 m overlap will be sufficient to produce low porosity parts with
hybrid process settings. A lower overlap will also minimize microstructural changes associated with re-
melting and contain those changes in a smaller region. The current study did not focus on optimizing the
relative thickness of each process parameter. An appropriate example is to envision the part as a structure
with tailored regions, each optimized to achieve the desired trade-off between speed and precision.

1.3 CONCLUSIONS

This technical collaboration demonstrated the potential of region-specific parameter optimization to
achieve both high productivity and superior surface integrity. This approach will be especially useful for
making complex geometries such as a rocker-lever geometry shown in Figure 6 wherein the left image
shows the component geometry, while the right highlights opportunities for region-specific process
optimization. In the future, work will be undertaken to optimize the thickness of the interior and exterior
regions and its subsequent impacts on resulting properties such as tensile and fatigue strength.
