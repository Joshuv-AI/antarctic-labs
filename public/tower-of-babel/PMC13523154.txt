Chest Pain With Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Therapy
Authors: Rukmini Roy, Linda Liu, Huihua Li, Karima Addetia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as pembrolizumab improve outcomes in metastatic non–small-cell lung cancer but can cause immune-related adverse events. Cardiovascular immune-related adverse events, including pericarditis, are rare and often underrecognized.Case SummaryA 53-year-old woman with recurrent metastatic lung adenocarcinoma on pembrolizumab presented with worsening pleuritic chest pain. Prior admissions attributed symptoms to pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, or malignancy. Laboratory studies showed elevated inflammatory markers and transaminitis; troponin was negative. Serial chest computed tomography demonstrated progressive pericardial thickening. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a thickened pericardium, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging confirmed pericardial inflammation without myocarditis. Liver biopsy supported concomitant ICI-related hepatitis. She was treated with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, colchicine, and corticosteroids, with permanent discontinuation of ICIs. Follow-up imaging showed resolution.DiscussionICI-associated pericarditis requires high clinical suspicion, particularly in patients with lung cancer with overlapping etiologies for chest pain. Multimodality imaging and retrospective review of serial studies are critical for diagnosis.Take-Home MessagesConsider pericarditis in patients on ICI therapy with persistent pleuritic chest pain. Serial imaging review and early multimodality cardiac evaluation improve diagnostic accuracy and guide management.

Non–small-cell lung cancer accounts for more than 80% of all lung cancer diagnoses, and chemotherapy is the mainstay of treatment in this population. Novel therapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), have allowed for broadened therapeutic options with many studies suggesting longer disease progression-free survival.1 The use of immunotherapies such as pembrolizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody against programmed death 1, however, has also resulted in the rise of unique immune-related adverse events (irAEs), most commonly involving the skin, gastrointestinal tract, endocrine system, liver, and lung.2 Cardiovascular irAEs are rare, but when present can often be serious or even fatal. We describe the case of a 53-year-old woman who presented with worsening chest pain, on a background of long-standing recurrent chest discomfort dating back to the diagnosis of her non–small-cell lung cancer who was subsequently found to have an irAE manifesting as pericarditis identified through retrospective review of her serial chest imaging.Take-Home Messages•Consider pericarditis in patients on ICI therapy with persistent pleuritic chest pain.•Serial imaging review and early multimodality cardiac evaluation improve diagnostic accuracy and guide management.

History of Presentation

A 53-year-old woman presented to the emergency room with sharp, stabbing left-sided chest pain and worsening shortness of breath. She described pleuritic chest pain, radiating to her back, neck, and head and only minimally improved with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs) and opiates. The recent worsening episodes of chest pain began approximately 2 months before the current presentation. At the time, she was diagnosed with necrotizing pneumonia and a small pulmonary embolism and was treated with antibiotics with the goal to return for repeat chest imaging as an outpatient. She re-presented in the same month for similar pain, which was treated with intravenous opiates and was presumed to be related to the recent diagnosis of necrotizing pneumonia. One week later, she presented with neck and shoulder pain and was diagnosed with myofascial pain, treated with opiates and NSAIDs. Her pain was mostly attributed to her cancer diagnosis and known history of pneumonia and pulmonary embolism with low suspicion of cardiac etiology. During her initial physical exam, the patient was noted to be tachycardic with no abnormal heart sounds, murmurs, rubs, or gallops, but appeared to be in significant distress. Past Medical History

The patient's past medical history was most notable for recurrent metastatic lung adenocarcinoma, originally diagnosed 7 years before presentation and treated with chemotherapy and radiation. Recurrence occurred 2 years ago in the form of a right hilar mass discovered on positron emission tomography, and she was treated with radiation and chemotherapy with carboplatin and pemetrexed. Immunotherapy with pembrolizumab was added to the treatment regimen approximately 3 months before the current presentation. Pemetrexed and carboplatin were held during these recent prior admissions due to known lung infection. However, the patient continued to receive pembrolizumab with the last dose given 2 weeks before the present admission. Other medical and social history included chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, central sleep apnea, unstable housing, and substance use disorder.

Investigations

Laboratory results on admission were significant for elevated liver transaminases (aspartate transaminase 146 U/L; alanine transaminase 147 U/L), elevated alkaline phosphatase (285 U/L), leukocytosis with neutrophilic predominance, elevated C-reactive protein (183 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (120 mm/h), with negative high-sensitivity troponin (<6 ng/L). Standard 12-lead electrocardiogram (Figure 2) showed sinus tachycardia (136 beats/min), subtle PR depression, and nonspecific ST-segment changes. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) (Figure 3) was technically challenging but remarkable for new echodense material in the pericardial sac without evidence of free-flowing fluid (white arrow), thickened pericardium, and normal left ventricular and right ventricular systolic function without evidence for constrictive physiology. Serial CTs performed over the past 2 months for chest pain demonstrated progressive pericardial thickening (Figure 1). Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) (Figure 4) demonstrated increased pericardial thickness with concomitant extensive inflammation demonstrated on T2 and delayed enhancement sequences, with no evidence of constrictive physiology/constriction on free-breathing, suggesting a diagnosis of pericarditis. Interestingly, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (Figure 5) performed before CMR for elevated serum alkaline phosphatase (see Table 1) showed pericardial thickening, highlighting the importance of inspecting for cardiac pathology on any adjacent organ imaging. Management

The constellation of findings on history, physical exam, and adjunctive laboratory and imaging data supported the diagnosis of pericarditis. The timeline of worsening symptoms, pericardial thickening, and CMR findings coincided with ICI administration and supported the diagnosis of ICI-related pericarditis. Because of the normal troponins, no wall motion abnormalities, normal myocardial T2 signal, and absence of myocardial delayed enhancement on CMR, the patient was not suspected to have myocarditis. The patient was initiated on an extended course (6 months) of colchicine daily given severity of symptoms, high-dose NSAIDs, and corticosteroids. Given her abnormal liver function, the dose of colchicine was adjusted accordingly. The rationale for initiating steroid treatment was due to multiorgan involvement of an irAE with diagnosis of irAE-related hepatitis.

Discussion

We present a case of chest pain due to pembrolizumab-induced immune-mediated pericarditis that highlights the use of multimodality cardiac imaging to assemble a challenging diagnosis in a patient with a chronic, long-standing chest pain syndrome in the clinical setting of lung cancer.

Pericardial irAEs were among the first described adverse cardiovascular associations with ICI therapy and include pericardial effusion and pericarditis.3,4 Compared with ICI-myocarditis, pericardial irAEs are underrecognized and underreported despite their association with a 1.5-fold increase in all-cause mortality.4 In a retrospective study on cardiovascular irAE reported to Vigibase, the World Health Organization's pharmacovigilance database, pericardial disease represented 0.3% of all reported irAEs and was notably overrepresented by use of anti–programmed death 1/programmed death ligand 1 therapy in patients with lung cancer.5

Diagnosis of pericarditis as an irAE requires a high index of suspicion due to current limitations in our understanding of onset, risk enhancers, and definitive testing. Symptoms may develop slowly, are often nonspecific, and can overlap with common differential diagnoses in lung cancer. In this case, infectious pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, and cancer-related chest pain masked and delayed the diagnosis of acute pericarditis through multiple hospital admissions. The relatively high prevalence of pericardial disease in malignancy also makes attributing pericarditis to irAE difficult. Accurate diagnosis is important because of the consequences of therapeutic discontinuation, which can exclude ICI for use in future oncologic treatment. An international position statement in 2024 highlighted the importance of multimodality cardiac imaging for improved diagnosis and surveillance of pericardial diseases, including pericarditis.6 TTE is considered the first-line imaging modality for acute pericarditis but can often be normal. Advanced cardiac imaging with CT or CMR can be considered to help with diagnosis and surveillance in complicated cases.6

Given that lung malignancies are routinely monitored with serial chest CT imaging, retrospective evaluation of existing CT images should be performed to look for evidence of pericardial thickening (as was noted in this case, Figure 1) which can raise the suspicion for pericarditis. Cardiac MRI can be used in these patients to then identify and quantify pericardial inflammation and increase diagnostic accuracy.6 Correlating symptom timeline with the initiation of ICI therapy, alongside serial multimodality imaging evidence, can be key to diagnosing pericarditis and attributing its etiology to an irAE.

Current understanding of the pathophysiology and epidemiology of ICI-pericarditis is limited due to a paucity of data. The mechanisms behind pericardial irAE are hypothesized to be like other cardiovascular irAEs.7 Resultant disruption in the immune homeostatic mechanisms can lead to multiorgan toxicities. The simultaneous presence of hepatic inflammation in this patient raised suspicion for ICI-induced inflammation as the cause of both hepatitis and pericarditis. Risk factors are currently not well elucidated; however, multiple studies have noted that pericarditis occurs more commonly in patients with lung cancer treated with ICIs.3,5,8,9 It is hypothesized that a history of thoracic radiation therapy can potentiate the effect of ICI therapy and thus increase the risk of pericarditis.5,10 In this case, it is plausible that her prior history of chest radiation and necrotizing pneumonia could have caused prior episodes of pericarditis that may have gone undetected.

There remain few data regarding treatment strategies for pericardial irAE. Case reports have described discontinuation of ICI, use of high-dose corticosteroid, and, if refractory, use of tumor necrosis factor-alpha inhibitor or interleukin-1 inhibitor such as infliximab and rilonacept, respectively.11,12 Standard pericarditis treatment with NSAIDs and colchicine are also generally employed. The 2021 American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines for management of irAEs recommend grading pericarditis severity on a scale from G1 (ie, abnormal cardiac biomarkers without symptoms) to G4 (ie, moderate to severe decompensation, life-threatening conditions).13 Current evidence recommends temporary suspension of ICIs for G1 pericarditis and ICI discontinuation for G2 to G4.13,14

Follow-Up

The patient's grade 3 hepatitis and pericarditis were deemed to be of high enough risk to permanently discontinue ICI therapy, with no further plans for rechallenge. A few weeks later, the patient was readmitted due to chest pain and nonadherence to colchicine, with TTE and electrocardiogram findings of recurrent pericarditis. She was discharged with a second steroid taper, NSAIDs, and colchicine for at least a 6-month course, with consideration of extended course outpatient after repeat imaging. Conclusions

We present a case of pericarditis diagnosed with multimodality imaging in a patient receiving pembrolizumab therapy. When a patient receiving ICI therapy presents with significant pleuritic chest pain refractory to oral pain medications, pericarditis should be on the differential. Appropriate diagnostic imaging modalities, such as TTE, CT, and MRI, should be performed, and treatment should be promptly administered. Review of prior sequential imaging often invariably present in patients with malignancy can lead to the diagnosis.
