Application of BALF combined with tNGS for detecting Chlamydia psittaci in resource-limited areas: lessons learned from the 2025 temporally associated case series investigation
Authors: Qing Li, Guiying Su, Rui Yu, Jinjing Guo, Xianmin Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChlamydia psittaci (Cps) is a neglected zoonotic cause of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), and its incidence may be underestimated in county-level hospitals lacking rapid diagnostic tools. We evaluated the potential utility of targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) for early Cps detection during a small group of temporally associated cases in rural southwestern China.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed four nonsevere Cps pneumonia patients admitted to Dafang County People’s Hospital between April 19 and May 6, 2025. Epidemiological exposure profiles, clinical data, laboratory results, and chest CT images were extracted. BALF samples were sent to a central reference laboratory for tNGS, covering 226 respiratory pathogens, including three Cps-specific amplicons. The time from symptom onset to appropriate antibiotic adjustment and clinical outcomes were recorded.ResultsPatients presented with high fever (39.5–39.6 °C), cough, dyspnea, and patchy ground‒glass opacities on CT. All had recent bird exposure: three through daily visits to live poultry markets or pigeon clubs within 500 m of their homes and one through household parrot breeding (within 3 km). The mean time from symptom onset to hospitalization was 5.25 days (range 3–7), and that from admission to Cps confirmation via tNGS was 2.75 days (range 2–3). Empirical β-lactam therapy failed; prompt switching to doxycycline or fluoroquinolones led to fever resolution within 3–5 days and complete radiographic resolution by 3 months.ConclusionOutsourcing BALF-tNGS to central labs facilitated rapid Cps diagnosis in a county hospital and may have helped reduce the risk of severe disease. This case series suggests that it may be useful to incorporate bird exposure history and accessible molecular diagnostics into CAP care in similar settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13822-5.

Psittacosis is a zoonotic disease caused by Chlamydia psittaci (Cps), which is transmitted primarily among birds or poultry and infects humans via respiratory aerosols or through excretions through the skin, mucosa or digestive tract, leading to community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) [1, 2]. Cps accounts for 1.03% of all CAP cases [3]. Typically, linked to clear epidemiological histories, it is common in poultry farms, wet markets, pet stores, and bird enthusiasts. Underdiagnosis persists in many regions, especially counties, due to limited awareness, inadequate diagnostics, and unscientific treatment strategies, resulting in global underreporting of cases or mortality [4]. Clinical manifestations (e.g., high fever and mild respiratory symptoms) are nonspecific, often leading to misdiagnosis and severe progression. With the use of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), the number of diagnosed cases has increased. The 2024 Chinese expert consensus on psittacosis management recommends BALF-based mNGS or tNGS [5]. This study analyzes clinical data from four psittacosis cases in southwestern China, which may provide preliminary evidence for early detection and diagnosis and inform Cps prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Clinical data from 4 patients with psittacosis treated at Dafang County People’s Hospital in Guizhou Province, China, from April 19 to May 6, 2025 were collected. A.

retrospective analysis was conducted on general information, epidemiological characteristics, clinical features, imaging findings, and laboratory test results. The diagnosis was based on the diagnostic criteria from the “Primary Care Guidelines for Adult Community-Acquired Pneumonia (2018)” and the “Chinese Expert Consensus on Psittacosis Diagnosis and Treatment” [5, 6]. All 4 patients were classified as nonsevere cases. Additionally, antibiotic usage before and after the diagnosis of Cps pneumonia was recorded, along with the time intervals from symptom onset to hospitalization and from hospitalization to diagnosis.

Targeted next-generation sequencing

The protocol for collecting BALF from patients for tNGS testing is illustrated in Fig. 1. After sample collection at the center of the bronchoscope, these samples were sent to Jin Yu Medical Diagnostic Group Co., Ltd. in Guiyang, China. Equal portions of each BALF sample were subjected to Gram staining and microscopic examination. Samples with fewer than 5 squamous epithelial cells per low-power field were considered to have good lower respiratory tract quality, thereby minim. All four samples met this criterion. Then, nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) were extracted, and tNGS target libraries and sequencing. The targeted panel included 226 pathogens (Supplementary Table S1), comprising 92 bacteria, 81 viruses, 43 fungi, 9 atypical pathogens, and 1 parasite. Three amplicons (Supplementary Table S2) were designed for the detection of Cps. In this study, our tNGS panel only targeted Cps-specific amplicons and did not allow whole-genome sequencing or high-resolution typing, precluding strain-level homology analysis. No negative control samples were included in the tNGS workflow in this study.

Sample processing and nucleic acid extraction

BALF samples (10 mL each) were collected during bronchoscopy following standard procedures. For samples with visible viscosity, an equal volume of 0.1 mol/L dithiothreitol (DTT) was added, mixed by vortexing, and incubated at room temperature for 3–5 min to achieve complete liquefaction. Non‑viscous samples were processed without DTT. A 1.3 mL aliquot of the liquefied sample was spiked with 13 µL of an exogenous internal control (provided by the sequencing company). After vortexing, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the remaining pellet was resuspended to a final volume of 500 µL. This suspension was transferred to a bead‑mill tube containing 50 µL of SDS and subjected to mechanical lysis using a cell disruptor. Following lysis, the tube was centrifuged again (12,000 rpm, 5 min), and 250 µL of the supernatant was used for nucleic acid extraction using the MagPure Pathogen RNA/DNA Extraction Kit (Magen Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA and RNA concentrations were measured with the Equalbit DNA HS Assay Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) on a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Input nucleic acid amount was kept below 100 ng per library.

Library preparation and sequencing

Library construction was carried out using the RP100TM Respiratory Pathogen Microorganisms Multiplex Testing Kit (KingCreate Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China). After reverse transcription of extracted nucleic acids into cDNA, the following steps were performed: multiplex PCR amplification of target regions, purification of PCR products, adapter ligation, and final library purification. The resulting libraries were pooled, and fragment sizes were assessed on a Qsep100 Bio‑Fragment Analyzer (BiOptic, Taiwan, China), with expected fragments ranging from 250 to 350 bp. The pooled library was diluted, denatured, and 500 µL was loaded onto an Illumina MiniSeq platform (Illumina, USA) for paired‑end 150 bp sequencing. A minimum of 1 million raw reads per sample was required for valid results.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw sequencing reads were processed for quality control using Fastp v0.20.1 with default parameters to trim adapters and low‑quality bases. Clean reads were aligned to a custom reference database (KingMed in‑house database, version 2025) using Bowtie2 v2.4.1 in ‘very‑sensitive’ mode. The reference database was compiled from NCBI GenBank, RefSeq, and Nucleotide databases. For each pathogen, the number of uniquely mapped reads was counted and normalized to reads per 100,000 (RPhK). A specimen was considered positive for Cps if ≥ 3 unique reads mapped to the Cps‑specific amplicons (Supplementary Table S2). For other pathogens, the laboratory’s validated thresholds (RPhK ≥ 10 with full amplicon coverage, or RPhK ≥ 30 with partial coverage, etc.) were applied, but final interpretation always integrated clinical context.

Interpretation of detected microorganisms

Co‑detected microorganisms were classified into three pathogenicity categories: Category A: Obligate or common respiratory pathogens (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Legionella pneumophila, Influenza virus). Detection generally indicates true infection. Category B: Opportunistic pathogens that may cause disease only in patients with systemic or local immunocompromise, disruption of the respiratory barrier, or lower respiratory tract dysbiosis. Category C: Normal respiratory microbiota that rarely cause infection unless massive aspiration occurs.

Data analysis

The data were organized, processed, and analyzed via WPS 2025 software (Version: 12.1.0.15374).

Case presentation

Baseline characteristics and clinical manifestations

The general information, baseline characteristics, and clinical manifestations of the four patients are summarized in Table 1. Among them, one was a 43-year-old male civil servant, and the other three were female farmers aged 60–83 years. Two of the patients had a history of hypertension, one had chronic gastritis, one had undergone cholecystectomy, and one had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). The three female patients denied a history of smoking but reported cooking daily and frequent visits to live poultry markets. Their residences were adjacent to each other and located within 500 m of live poultry markets and pigeon associations. The onset of their illness was temporally clustered, despite no history of raising poultry, parrots, or pigeons. The male patient smoked but did not cook and had a long history of raising parrots. He lived within 3 km of the three female patients, live poultry markets, and pigeon associations (Fig. 2). All patients had a history of exposure to birds, although the exact timing of their first contact with poultry, parrots, or pigeons was unknown. None had taken any protective measures in daily life. The duration of illness before hospitalization ranged from 3 to 8 days, and all patients were admitted with a diagnosis of CAP. Co-exposed individuals living with the patients remained asymptomatic during the 45-day health monitoring period. Moreover, the average hospitalization cost for all patients was $900.

Clinical laboratory tests

Upon admission, laboratory assessments included white blood cell (WBC) counts. neutrophil (NE) percentage, lymphocyte percentage, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hCRP) level, procalcitonin (PCT) level, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), interleukin-6 (IL-6) level, and blood gas analysis (Table 2). Two patients exhibited elevated WBC counts, while all patients showed increased neutrophil percentages and decreased lymphocyte percentages. The ESR was elevated, with CRP levels ranging from 69.13 to 188.82 mg/L. All patients tested negative for Chlamydia pneumoniae IgM antibodies and antigens.

Targeted next-generation sequencing

BALF tNGS confirmed Cps in all four patients (Table 3). Co-detected microorganisms included P. jirovecii (150 reads) and Candida albicans (91 reads) (both Category B opportunistic pathogens), as well as Streptococcus mitis and HHV-7 (Category C commensals). None of the patients were immunocompromised, nor did they have clinical or radiological features suggestive of pneumonia due to these co-detected organisms. Therefore, they were interpreted as colonization or contaminants. All patients recovered without antifungal or antiviral therapy.

Treatment and outcomes

The diagnostic and treatment histories of the four patients are detailed in Table 3. All patients received empirical antibiotic therapy with 1–2 types of antibiotics before diagnosis. The average time from symptom onset to hospitalization was 5.25 days. The average time from admission to diagnosis was 2.75 days. (range: 2–3 days). Although other pathogens were initially detected via tNGS in two Cases, subsequent laboratory tests and clinical evaluations ruled them out. All four patients were ultimately diagnosed with Cps pneumonia. and each patient received targeted therapy: Patient 1 received Doxycycline, 100 mg ivgtt q12h; patient 2 received Levofloxacin, 0.5 g ivgtt qd; patients 3 and 4 were switched to intravenous Moxifloxacin, 0.4 g ivgtt qd. No combination therapy was used. Within 3–5 days of targeted therapy, all patients achieved normal body temperature and significant clinical improvement. After discharge, all patients were switched to the corresponding oral medications and completed a 14-day treatment course without any recurrence. Follow-up chest CT scans three months after discharge revealed marked resolution of inflammatory lesions (Fig. 3B). The average hospitalization cost for all patients was USD 900, of which bronchoscopy and tNGS accounted for approximately USD 58 and USD 113, respectively. Younger age and fewer comorbidities were associated with lower costs, with the lowest and highest expenses being about USD 594 and USD 1,432.

Discussion

We described four nonsevere, laboratory-confirmed cases of Chlamydia. psittaci pneumonia at a county-level hospital in southwestern China, which occurred consecutively within a 17-day timeframe. The time from symptom onset to hospital admission visits ranged from 3 to 7 days (mean: 5.2 days). All patients exhibited rapid disease progression, with a body temperature ≥ 39 °C, dry cough, and minor hemoptysis (3/4). Chest CT revealed extensive patchy ground‒glass opacities and consolidation. White Blood cell counts or neutrophil percentages were normal or mildly elevated, with lymphopenia (3/4 patients < 10%) and significantly elevated CRP, ESR, and IL-6. However, we promptly performed BALF analysis combined with tNGS, identifying the etiology within 48–72 h of admission. This may have enabled clinicians to switch from ineffective beta-lactam treatments to doxycycline or respiratory fluoroquinolone drugs in accordance with the “Chinese expert consensus on diagnosis and treatment of psittacosis” of 2024 [5]. All patients became afebrile within 3–5 days and achieved complete radiographic resolution by 3 months. In this small case series, none of the patients developed acute respiratory distress syndrome or required ICU admission.

These consecutive cases shared close onset timing, similar clinical presentations, and treatment outcomes, likely originating from multifactorial exposures—live poultry markets, pigeon clubs, and household parrots. This suggests that rapid antibiotic adjustment based on tNGS likely helped mitigate disease progression in these four patients.

Cps is a gram-negative, obligate intracellular pathogen with a unique biphasic developmental cycle (elementary body [EB] and reticulate body [RB]), enabling rapid intracellular replication and systemic infection [7]. Transmission occurs via inhalation of pathogen-laden aerosols or dust. EBs, the infectious form (0.2–0.4 μm diameter), resist environmental stress, enter host cells to form inclusions, hijack mitochondria or the Golgi apparatus for replication, spread intercellularly and trigger systemic inflammation [8–11]. Early symptoms include persistent high fever, chills, myalgia, cough, sputum, and dyspnea [12], which is consistent with our cases (3/4 had bloody sputum). Cps also secrete effector proteins via type III secretion systems (T3SSs) to inhibit apoptosis and evade immunity [13], potentially explaining rapid clinical or radiographic progression (extensive ground‒glass opacities and consolidation). Thus, nonspecific, early-onset symptoms plus avian exposure should raise suspicion for Cps pneumonia.

However, symptoms, imaging, and inflammatory markers lack specificity for early diagnosis [12]. Serology, although widely used, suffers from cross-reactivity (e.g., C. trachomatis, C. pneumoniae) and delayed or attenuated antibody responses postantibiotics [14]. Paired serology (acute and convalescent, 2–4 weeks apart) showing a ≥ 4-fold titer increase is diagnostic but impractical for early detection [5]. Culture, the gold standard, is hindered by the fastidious growth of Cps, biosafety level-3 requirements, and prolonged turnaround [2]. Molecular techniques offer promise: PCR can detect low-copy-number pathogens rapidly but is pathogen-specific and underutilized because of limited suspicion or accessibility [15]. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing, with broad pathogen coverage, excels in the diagnosis of rare or atypical infections [16, 17]. Since 2019, mNGS has improved early Cps detection [17–20], although cost and infrastructure limit its routine use [5]. Targeted next-generation sequencing combines multiplex PCR amplification technology with high-throughput sequencing technology, which widely covers clinically common pathogenic microorganisms and their drug resistance and virulence genes, enabling early, accurate, and rapid microbial detection [21]. Compared with mNGS, tNGS uses the same types of detection samples, including BALF, sputum, blood, and nasopharyngeal swabs. Targeted amplification can improve the reliability of pathogen detection, and since only specific target regions need to be sequenced, data processing and analysis are easier than with mNGS. Therefore, tNGS may offer certain advantages in terms of detection speed, sensitivity, and specificity, and its cost in this study (approximately USD 113 per test) was lower than the local reported price of mNGS (approximately USD 300), suggesting potential applicability in resource-limited settings [22, 23]. For impoverished areas in Southwest China, tNGS may represent an affordable option (USD 113 per test), although its cost-effectiveness relative to conventional diagnostic algorithms remains to be evaluated in future studies. In situations where internal sequencing capability is lacking, outsourcing tNGS to a central reference laboratory (turnaround time of 48 h) is feasible, especially when BALF is used as the detection sample, which not only increases the positive detection rate but also may reduce the need for repeated testing, although this was not formally assessed in our study.

Globally, Cps accounts for 1% to 2% of all CAP patients are systematically tested for etiology, although this figure may be underestimated because of the difficulty in determining the exact incidence and prevalence of psittacosis [3]. Our four cases were identified from a catchment population of approximately 800,000 people over a three-week period (observed incidence 0.5 per 100,000). For context, previous reports from the Netherlands have documented incidence in a similar range during animal disease outbreaks [24], though direct comparisons are hampered by differences in surveillance and case definitions. The three female patients were geographically close (≤ 500 m apart), and the male patient’s household parrot colony was within 3 km of a farmer market or pigeon club, with temporally close onset times, similar to previously reported psittacosis case series [25–29]. Our cases occurred in spring, with no similar cases detected in the preceding 9 months or the following 3 months, possibly due to increased avian activity as temperatures rose. However, the literature reports varying seasonal patterns [25, 27, 29, 30], suggesting that psittacosis may lack a clear seasonal trend. Additionally, Cps pneumonia can cause outbreaks not only in endemic areas but also in nonendemic regions through water sources and avian activity.

We combined fine-scale epidemiological mapping via handheld GPS with High-resolution temporal data (onset dates accurate to ± 1 day). All BALF samples were processed under the same tNGS protocol to ensure reproducibility. Three-month imaging follow-up suggested that early targeted treatment may help prevent fibrotic sequelae in this small case series, a concern previously raised in severe psittacosis cases [31]. However, our study has several limitations. First, the small sample size (n = 4) limits generalizability. Second, our tNGS panel did not allow strain‑level genetic comparison, and no environmental cultures or genetic mapping were performed; therefore, we could not confirm a common source or clonality between human and avian isolates. Third, drug resistance testing was not conducted due to lack of commercial kits, though Cps remains highly susceptible to tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones. Fourth, no negative controls were included in the tNGS workflow, and we lacked a comparator group undergoing standard diagnostic pathways; thus, our cost and efficiency data are descriptive only, and formal cost‑effectiveness analysis is needed. Fifth, we only tested C. pneumoniae IgM and antigens; no PCR or IgG/IgA, so co-infection or past exposure may have been missed. Despite these limitations, the detailed exposure reconstruction and consistent clinical response to targeted therapy support the validity of our findings.

On the basis of our findings, we propose a two-step decision tree for county-level clinicians: Step 1 - Clinical triggers: CAP with fever ≥ 39 °C; (i) avian exposure ≤ 14 days prior; (ii) imaging showing ground‒glass opacities or consolidation without typical bacterial lobar pneumonia patterns; and (iii) markedly elevated CRP, ESR, or IL-6 with normal or mildly elevated white blood cells/neutrophils. Step 2 - Diagnostic Testing: It may be reasonable to consider bronchoscopy on the same day, collect 10 mL of BALF for tNGS, and initiate empirical doxycycline while awaiting results, then de-escalate based on tNGS findings. These suggestions require prospective validation in larger studies. We suggest future studies could consider quarterly screening of cloacal swabs from live bird stalls and pigeon clubs within 5 km of confirmed human case. Further studies may explore whether preventive measures such as household parrot vaccination could reduce transmission risk in similar settings. Consideration could be given to installing negative-pressure ventilation systems in live bird markets, prioritizing stalls within 500 m of residential areas. Illustrated pamphlets (in Chinese and local dialects) educational pamphlets could be distributed to vendors and pigeon enthusiasts, emphasizing “fever + bird contact = test for psittacosis.”

Conclusion

This 2025 case series in a southwestern Chinese county suggests that psittacosis may not be confined to major cities with reference laboratories. In this study, county hospitals combined BALF tNGS with rapid clinical action. They detected and treated C. psittaci pneumonia within 3 days, which likely helped prevent deterioration and avoided unnecessary care. Scaling this model to other resource-limited areas could reduce both disease and economic losses from undiagnosed psittacosis, but larger studies are needed to confirm.
