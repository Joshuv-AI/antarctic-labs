 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The author completed a market survey of available camera sensors and camera modules. 1 The author
found 271 camera sensors currently available. Of these camera sensors, 71 have been integrated into 149
different camera modules. The International Atomic Energy Agency uses a 5 megapixel (MP) sensor in
its current surveillance system, the Next Generation Surveillance System (NGSS). Camera sensor
technology has evolved dramatically since the NGSS was designed. Some of the most relevant
advancements are the adoption of backside illumination and stacked chip layouts that dramatically
improve the low light performance of contemporary camera sensors. The author identified the 5 MP Sony
IMX675, 8.2 MP Sony IMX585, and 12.5 MP Sony IMX676 as candidate camera sensors for the NGSS
successor. These chips have been integrated into camera modules by several vendors. The author could
not find European Machine Vision Association (EMVA) 1288 (image sensor–level metrics such as
quantum efficiency), ISO 12233 (sharpness and other camera system-level metrics), or ISO 19093 (low
light performance) test results for these sensors or the camera modules. The author recommends that some
of the available camera modules be purchased and tested to the EMVA 1288, ISO 12233, and ISO 19093
standards or to a combination of these standards as part of the process of evaluating candidate
components for the successor to the NGSS.

1 A camera module packages a camera sensor in a way that can be more easily integrated into a bespoke solution.

 1. INTRODUCTION

When designing a bespoke camera system, there are many choices regarding what camera sensor to
incorporate. Section 2 describes some of the 271 sensors that the author found, made by manufacturers
such as ams OSRAM, Canon, Teledyne e2v, Gpixel, OMNIVISION, onsemi, Sharp, and Sony. Many
integrators have packaged these low-level camera sensors into camera modules to facilitate integrating
them into higher-level projects. The author identified 149 different camera modules built from 71 of these
camera sensors. Picking an optimal sensor and camera module from the sea of choices can be challenging.

It is important to start with a thorough understanding of the system’s high-level requirements and answers
to the following questions:

• What resolution is required?

• What frame rate is required?

• Is low light performance important?

• For the same resolution, can a larger sensor with larger pixels—that can collect more light and
 provide better low light performance—be used, or does a smaller sensor need to be used to conserve
 space?

• Is a global shutter required, or is a rolling shutter sufficient? (Will the system be used to capture high-
 speed motion such as helicopter blades?)

A review of many of these topics and how they relate to international safeguards is covered in Garner et
al. (2022) [1].

 3. MARKET SURVEY OF CAMERA MODULES

Several vendors package camera sensors into camera modules to make it easier to integrate a camera into
a custom system. For the same camera sensor, vendors may offer different interfaces such as:

• GigE Vision
• Low-voltage differential signaling (LVDS)
• MIPI camera serial interface 2 (CSI-2)
• Camera parallel interface
• Scalable low-voltage signaling with embedded clock (SLVS-EC)
• USB3

In an initial survey of Allied Vision, Basler, e-con Systems, Flir, and FRAMOS, the author identified 149
different cameras modules that use the MIPI CSI-2 interface. The MIPI CSI-2 interface is commonly
found natively on the image sensors themselves. The author expects that a camera module with a MIPI
CSI-2 interface will be used in the Next Generation Surveillance System (NGSS) successor.

As shown in Table 1, Allied Vision and e-con Systems offer camera modules that integrate sensors from
multiple manufacturers. However, Basler, Flir, and FRAMOS tend to offer camera modules with sensors
only from a single manufacturer.

 4. DISCUSSION

For international safeguards, the following responses to the questions presented in Section 1 help
constrain the camera sensor and camera module search space:

• What resolution is required?
 o 5 megapixels (MP) is sufficient. The NGSS currently uses a 5 MP sensor [4].

• What frame rate is required?
 o 1 frame per second [5].

• Is low light performance important?
 o Yes [6].

• Is sensor size severely constrained?
 o No, a large sensor with good low light performance is likely more important than having the
 smallest possible sensor.

• Is a global shutter required, or is a rolling shutter sufficient?
 o A rolling shutter is sufficient because high-speed motion does not need to be captured.

For example, if low light performance is prioritized, large pixels that feature backside illumination and
use stacking are likely to provide better images. A backside illuminated sensor is approximately twice as
sensitive as previous generations of sensors [7]. The Sony STARVIS 2 sensors are marketed as excellent
low light sensors. Other vendors may have comparable technology and comparable performance, but
Table 4 lists the Sony STARVIS 2 sensors, which should have excellent low light performance.

Note: In the United States, the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) requires that surveillance
systems for government facilities not originate from certain foreign sources. To ensure compliance,
several surveillance system manufacturers are beginning to label their components and integrated systems
as NDAA compliant. Sony manufactures several sensors that seem similar to one another and may only
differ based on NDAA compliance status [8].

The author was not able to find camera modules available for some of the latest IMX8xx sensors, most
likely because they are relatively new.

The European Machine Vision Association (EMVA) 1288 standard provides a quantitative way to
evaluate camera sensor light performance. Compilations such as Teledyne’s Machine Vision Sensor
Review allow different sensors to be quantitatively compared to one another [9].

The ISO 12233 standard provides a way to quantitatively test several characteristics of a complete
imaging system, including the lens, sensor, and image processing pipeline. The image processing pipeline
implemented by a camera module integrator can significantly modify the perceived quality of the output
images.

The ISO 19093:2018 standard provides a way to quantitatively test the low light performance of a
complete imaging system.

 7

Of the 8.2 MP sensors listed in Table 4, the IMX585 has the lowest SNR1s rating; SNR1s is Sony’s
propriety quantitative metric for evaluating picture quality at low illumination [10].

The author could not find any EMVA 1288, ISO 12233, or ISO 19093 standard test results for the
STARVIS 2 sensors listed in Table 4. Without independent test results conducted according to
international standards, reaching definitive conclusions about the suitability of a particular sensor or
camera module for international safeguards is difficult.

Camera modules from these integrators could be purchased and tested to the EMVA 1288, ISO 12233,
and ISO 19093 standards. Testing to these standards would enable a quantitative comparison between
camera systems but may not fully address whether the camera systems meet International Atomic Energy
Agency (IAEA) needs unless the IAEA’s needs are restated in terms of capabilities that are explicitly
tested by those standards.

 5. CONCLUSION

Several vendors integrate camera sensors into camera modules to make image collection easier to include
in bespoke products. The NGSS uses a 5 MP sensor, and this resolution should be considered the lowest
possible resolution for an NGSS successor. Among the available camera modules, one based on the 5 MP
IMX675, 8.2 MP IMX585, or 12.5 MP IMX676 sensor should be considered for the NGSS successor. To
ensure that the selected camera sensor or camera module fully meets IAEA needs, the IAEA’s needs
should be restated in terms of testable capabilities such as those measured by the EMVA 1288, ISO
12233, or ISO 19093 standards, and candidate image sensors and camera modules should be tested to
those standards.
