Executive Summary:
 The global drive to lower carbon emissions has resulted in a boom in renewable
energy technologies, with the International Energy Agency (IEA) reporting that Solar
Photovoltaics (PV) contributed to over 75% of new renewable capacity in 2024; with
installed PV capacity exceeding 2 TW, globally. i Crystalline-silicon (c-Si) solar cells
account for over 95% of this market with most of these cells utilizing metal contacts
produced by screen printing silver (Ag) metallization pastes. The application and
processing of these contacts is a critical step in producing high efficiency (η) devices and
is also the highest non-Si material cost in cell manufacturing. The pastes typically contain
over 85% Ag and are thus subject to the volatility of this highly priced metal. In the first
quarter of 2021 the price of Ag was ~ $850/kg, but on the 14th October 2025 the metal
reached historical highs of $1785/kg and is now currently hovering ~$1560/kg. These
prices cut into the cell manufacturer’s profit margins with the costs eventually passed on
to consumers; potentially impacting the Solar Energy Technology Office’s (SETO) 2030
target of 3¢/kWh. The PV industry is also competing against other consumer goods for
supply, so Ag is projected to become an expensive bottleneck on the route to reach multi-
terawatt production; thus, alternative metallization routes are being actively sought.
 Bert Thin Films (BTF) Inc.’s approach was to develop a screen printable copper (Cu)
based paste (CuBertTM) that can be fired in atmospheric IR belt furnaces without the use
of additional steps. In other words, producing a direct drop-in replacement for the Ag
pastes. The primary goals of this project were to demonstrate that CuBertTM could be
delivered to market at a 90% cost reduction of the established Ag pastes, as well as
demonstrating the performance and durability of the Cu contacts on high η Si solar cells.
Cu contacts made with BTF’s air fired CuBertTM metallization pastes achieved a maximum
η of 21.4% when used as the front contacts on PERC cells, and an η of 24.4% when used
as the rear contact on TOPCon cells. Thus, resulting in a relative η of 94% for PERC and
96% for TOPCon, when compared to the same structures commercially metallized with
Ag. These results were achieved by improvements to the paste composition and
processing conditions. The reliability of the Cu contacts was studied by thermally
stressing the bare cells at 200°C, as well as damp heat (85°C/85%RH) testing of mini-
modules containing these cells. This work was also supported by micro-structural and
elemental analysis of the interface.
 Improved device performance was assisted by a reduction in the dimensions of the
printed contacts; reducing shading and charge-carrier recombination at the interface. This
also reduced paste usage to below 0.39 mg/cm2. Cost models demonstrated that even
with this amount of paste, the product was still economically viable, with modeled costs
showing that when the pastes are manufactured at scale, the costs are calculated to be
below 10% of the cost of analogous Ag paste. Domestic and non-US based suppliers of
the main components of the paste were sourced to produce a more robust supply chain
for the product. Both the cost model and supply chain were verified by third parties. The
findings from this project allowed BTF to reduce the risk of switching from Ag to Cu pastes;
thus, creating an opportunity to produce metallization pastes in the US. Access to lower
cost, domestically produced materials can also potentially spur on domestic
manufacturing of cells and modules. BTF continues to actively work with industrial
partners to improve and demonstrate the product at scale.

Background:
 The fabrication of metal contacts on c-Si solar cells is a critical step in the
manufacturing process; impacting both efficiency (η) and durability of the PV module. For
the last 50+ years, these contacts have predominantly used screen printed silver (Ag)
paste. PV manufacturing capacity was ~ 135 GW in 2020, and the industry used a total
of 2,860 metric tons of Ag representing approximately 10% of the global supply. Capacity
has grown rapidly in the last few years with reports indicating a manufacturing capacity
of ~700 GW. ii As a result, the PV sector used ~30% of global industrial Ag supply in 2024;
roughly 16% of total worldwide Ag demand. iii,iv The International Technology Roadmap
for Photovoltaics (ITRPV) expects that as manufacturing capacity grows, the amount of
Ag used in solar cell production will also grow significantly in the next decade. Innovations
in cell design, screen technology and paste formulation have allowed the industry to
reduce Ag usage, but there is a limiting floor. Current average Ag usage is ~9, ~14 and,
~17 mg/W for passivated emitter rear contact (PERC), tunnel oxide passivated contact
(TOPCon) and silicon heterojunction (SHJ) architectures, respectively. v
 SHJ cells require manufacturing temperatures below 250°C due to the inclusion of
amorphous Si passivation layers. Consequently, Ag usage has been significantly higher
than the other technologies. Fine line printing in the form of knotless screens with
openings < 25 µm’s are starting to be employed with Ag pastes, as well as copper (Cu)
and hybrid AgCu approaches; η's above 23% have been achieved. vi Reactive Ag inks
printed using a syringe-based dispersion system are also under development for SHJ
solar cells. The inks, theoretically result in denser contacts using less Ag, than
conventionally cured or fired pastes. vii Other printing technologies that produce high
aspect ratio prints with lower paste usage are also being explored for SHJ, but screen
printing for all cell types is forecast to be the dominant method for the foreseeable future.v
 Advancements within the higher temperature Ag paste technology have been focused
on TOPCon cells, that now command >60% of the market share. Major changes have
been made to the frit technology within the paste, to lower the temperature so that they
don’t damage the poly-Si passivation layers, as well as optimizing them for Laser
Enhanced Contact Optimization (LECO) tools, that have been introduced after the firing
step to increase both yield and η. For the front p-type emitter this has meant aluminum
(Al) has been significantly reduced or removed entirely from the AgAl pastes; leading to
a reduction in recombination. viii Ag free approaches include modification of Al pastes on
the rear n-type contact of TOPCon. However, an extra processing step is required in this
case, where the silicon nitride (SiNx) anti-reflective coating is first laser ablated to create
a local contact opening, before the Al paste is screen printed and fired. ix Cu electroplating
has been around since the 1980’s but can also require the use of laser ablation when
applied to TOPCon. A nickel (Ni) barrier layer is also deposited to the Si, to prevent Cu
diffusion and aid adhesion. However, these contacts often employ an Ag capping layer to
reduce oxidation and improve solderability. x
 Other methods to reduce Ag usage have taken a hybrid approach. These include Ni
pastes doped with ~4% Ag nanoparticles. xi However Ag seed layers, deposited by first
screen printing a thin Ag layer are the most commonly used approach. In the case of Cu
electroplating, they can be used to etch through the SiNx, thus avoiding the need for laser
ablation. xii The industry has been trying to adopt Cu plating for years, but the added
process complexity has so far made it a cost-prohibitive step for mainstream
 Page 5 of 50

manufacturing. Leaner Ag seed layer approaches include printing the Ag paste in the
form of dashes. The pastes are fired, to make electrical contact with the Si. Fingers and
bus bars are then screen printed on top using either a high temperature Al or low
temperature Cu paste. The Al and Cu pastes are formulated to not etch through the SiNx
coating during their respective firing and curving steps. xiii Many of these methods require
more processing steps than conventional Ag screen printing technology, which cuts into
the cost savings made by using a lower cost metal. A Cu paste that uses the exact same
manufacturing processes from paste roll milling through screen printing to furnace firing
would be the ideal method to deliver the most cost savings to the industry.
Project Objectives:
 The industry has been trying to adopt Cu for years but concerns around low-cost
processing, performance and durability have limited this. Cu is of course much less
expensive than Ag but is also ~800 times more abundant and has nearly the same
conductivity. However, it’s small atomic radius means it can rapidly diffuse into Si,
damaging the performance of the device. In addition, it oxidizes more rapidly. Bert Thin
Films (BTF) Inc’s approach has been to develop a Cu based screen printable paste that
can fire through the SiNx coating. The ability to air-fire this paste in a typical fast fire IR
belt furnace without oxidation or Cu migration into the underlying Si is the basis of the
BTF technology. The chemistry of the paste creates a Cu diffusion barrier upon firing and
initial tests have demonstrated durability.
 The primary aim of this project was to show that BTF’s Cu-based screen printable
paste can be delivered to the market at a 90% cost reduction of the established Ag pastes.
This project was designed to demonstrate the performance and durability of the Cu
contacts on high η c-Si solar cells. At the start of the project, these devices utilized less
than 110 mg of Ag paste to form the front-side electrical contacts, but as a highly priced
commodity, Ag continues to be a significant material cost. This project aims to prove the
performance and economic viability of a direct drop-in replacement for the Ag pastes.
 As PV cell manufacturing shifted to the Asia Pacific region, so did the manufacturing
of the Ag pastes. Earlier this year, one of the remaining European Ag paste suppliers for
solar, sold its PV division, while a US supplier divested of its PV paste business back in
2021; resulting in over >90% of Ag paste manufacturing for solar now occurring in China.
Therefore, for US cell manufacturing to grow, it must also have a low cost and domestic
metallization source. The following project tasks were identified to de-risk the Cu paste
and create the opportunity to produce the paste domestically.
Task 1: Paste Formulation and Optimization
Task Summary: The Cu pastes are primarily composed of Cu powders of varying size
ranges. The paste also includes frits, binders, solvent and viscosity modifiers. This task
included optimizing the material ratios in the paste, adjusting the composition of the glass
frits in the paste, optimizing the processing conditions for a given finger volume, and
metallization design. The aim of this task was to reduce the amount of Cu paste required
to produce cells with the target efficiency and ensure that devices’ lifetime/durability.
Subtask 1.1: Paste Formulation
Subtask Summary: This subtask involved modifying and optimizing the formulation,
including and not limited to changes to the Cu particle size, the ratios of the different
components in the paste and frit composition.

Subtask 1.2: Screen Printing
Subtask Summary: The volume of the fingers is defined by the screen-printing screen.
In addition to the screen opening (finger width), the mesh count, wire thickness and
emulsion thickness can impact the paste deposit. This subtask included and was not
limited to designing and procuring the screens required to meet the target paste deposit.
Subtask 1.3: Firing
Subtask Summary: As the Cu metallized cells travel through the IR belt furnace, the
paste is exposed to an oxidizing environment. Lowering the air flow in the furnace, would
reduce the rate of oxidation. However, the airflow in the furnace determines lamp power
usage. A high flow rate uses a large amount of power, while too low a flow rate allows the
lamps to turn off for periods, creating unstable firing conditions. In addition, the
temperatures in the multiple heated zones, as well as belt speeds can be adjusted to
create different firing profiles. This subtask included and was not limited to studies on the
impact of the firing profile on the lateral finger resistance (Rf).
Subtask 1.4: Metallization Design
Subtask Summary: The move towards high efficiency devices also impacted the front
metallization design. This subtask monitored Rf as formulation and processing changes
were made so that the metallization design (including the minimum number of bus bars,
and finger dimensions) required to produce the target η’s could be defined.
Task 2: The Copper – Silicon Interface Optimization
Task Summary: This task included and was not limited to material analysis of the
materials at the interface, as well as accelerated stress tests of the cells. The aim was to
show that the Cu does not diffuse into the device and to lower the contact resistance (RC)
to achieve the target cell η.
Subtask 2.1: Preliminary Cell Durability
Subtask Summary: Changes to the frit and firing conditions directly impacts the pastes’
ability to prevent Cu from diffusing into the device. The quality of the inherent diffusion
barrier was studied following accelerated aging protocols developed to investigate the
performance of diffusion barriers in Cu electroplated cells. The cells were placed in an
inert atmosphere to minimize oxidation and then thermally stressed at elevated
temperatures (up to 200°C) for up to 1000 hrs. The open circuit voltage (Voc) and pseudo
fill factor (pFF) of the device were then periodically measured.
Subtask 2.2: Copper Diffusion at the Interface
Subtask Summary: The aim of this task was to select cells which had been thermally
stressed to evaluate their interface. Analysis of the interface included and was not limited
to secondary ion mass spectroscopy (SIMS). This technique was used to study the depth
profile of the elements in the Si, directly beneath the contact region, and was a further
method of evaluating the diffusion of Cu into the device.
Subtask 2.3: Materials Analysis at the Interface
Subtask Summary: Changes to the frit composition and additives impacts the rate the
paste etches the SiNx anti-reflective coating during the firing process. The remaining
materials at the interface will impact both the diffusion barrier properties of the paste and
the RC. This subtask aimed to use analytical techniques including but not limited to SIMS,
 Page 7 of 50

Raman spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive x-ray
spectroscopy (EDX) and RC measurements to inform the decisions made on the paste
modifications and correlate the results to the initial minority lifetime and η of the cell.
Task 3: Cell Efficiency Measurement
Task Summary: This task compared the performance of the Cu paste on high efficiency
silicon solar cells to the same cells metallized with Ag paste. The cells had a PERC)
and/or TOPCon structure. The Ag metallized cells will be sourced directly from the
manufacturer, consequently the paste formulation and processing conditions had already
been refined for these pastes. As a result, the aim was to produce Cu metallized cells
which have a target η relative to the Ag metallized cells.
Task 4: Stakeholder Engagement, Cost Model Development, and Supply Chain
Analysis
Task Summary: This task was aimed at identifying, engaging and collecting feedback
from relevant stakeholders who would be willing to take this technology to the next stage
in manufacturing or that would be needed to gather feedback on technical metrics. A cost
model / tecno-economic analysis would also be developed for this technology.
Subtask 4.1: Ordering of Supplies
Subtask Summary: The purchase orders/invoices for the materials directly related to the

metallized with Ag and (4) screen printing screens.
Subtask 4.2: Stakeholder Engagement
The aim was to identify, engage, and get feedback on the technical metrics from relevant
stakeholders including but not limited to cell, PV module, paste manufacturers.
Stakeholder engagement was also needed to independently verify the cost model / tecno-

Subtask 4.3: Cost Model Development
Subtask Summary: This subtask was used to build a cost model that was informed by
vendor pricing up to production scale. The model included materials, processing (labor

from quotes for batches of Ag paste from different suppliers. The model aimed to adjust
the cost of the Ag paste to account for differences in operational expenditures (OpEx) as
well as any possible impact on capital expenditures (CapEx). This task included collecting
material cost data from suppliers and vendors.

included also producing and testing different sized modules.
Subtask 5.1: Mini Modules Fabrication
Subtask Summary: To study durability of the Cu contacts containing, mini modules using

Subtask 1.1: Paste Formulation
Milestone 1.3. Paste Formulation
 The CuBertTM Cu pastes are primarily composed of Cu powders of varying size ranges
coated by BTF. The pastes also include frits, binders, solvent and viscosity modifiers.
Initially nine 1.5 kg sized batches (P1 to P9) of paste were produced, with a variation in
the particle size distribution (PSD) of the Cu powders and the molecular weight (MW) of
the binder (Table 1). The PSD of the Cu powders in pastes P1 to P5 were varied by
changing the type and blend of the powders. The smaller the particle size, the higher the
surface area and the more susceptible the Cu powders were to oxidation. However,
increasing the particle size, limited how small the fingers could be printed. P1 used
powders with the highest surface area (0.90 m2/g), while pastes P2 to P8 had surface
areas ranging from 0.59 – 0.65 m2/g. Both the PSD of the Cu and the MW of the binder
affect the rheology of the pastes, thus impacting printability. Figure 1(a) shows the initial
viscosity data for the pastes. The impact of these formulation changes on Rf was studied
as part of Milestone 1.4.

                                                                                          Page 10 of 50

 Over the course of the project, over 50 different paste formulations were made. The
composition of the glass frits, solvent and viscosity modifiers were all changed to improve
the pastes rheology, printability and electrical performance. By the end of the project, the
screen openings had been reduced to 25 µm. This required a significant reduction to the
paste’s viscosity, to allow the paste to be printed in fine lines using industrial print speeds
(≥ 200 mm/s). Figure 1(b) shows the viscosity of the paste (F53) used to print the fingers
on PERC cells used in the End of Project (EOP-B) goals related to cell η.

Subtask 1.2: Screen Printing
 During the project’s first quarter, BTF received its upgraded screen printer (ASM E by
DEK). The printer allowed cells up to M12 (441 cm2) in size to be printed and has visual
alignment capabilities, making it easier to align the prints with the selective emitter
regions. Moving from the old printer (HMI MSP885) to the upgraded printer, required time
to develop new “standard” settings. The printer settings had to be adjusted for each paste
(P1 to P9), in order to deposit similar amounts of paste.
 As the project progressed, the Cu powder and MW of binder used in Paste P2 were
selected for further paste optimization. Changes to the paste included increases in the
proportion of binder, frit and rheology modifier used in the paste. The proportion of binder
in the pastes was varied with P2-1 using 4.0 wt% and P2-3 using 6.8 wt%. The printing
conditions were kept constant for all screens. A significant variation in height was
observed for both pastes and examples of the roughness for pastes printed with 30 and
65 μm screen openings are shown in Figure 2. The resistivity of the lines was calculated
using the smallest cross-sectional area. The lowest ρ values were observed for the 65
μm prints, and had values in the 16 – 30 μΩcm range.

width decreased and shading was reduced (not shown). The performance of cells printed
using a screen opening of 30 μm, was limited by the finger resistance, as the fingers were
less uniform and more susceptible to oxidation at slow belt speeds.

Subtask 1.3: Firing
 The firing process is a critical step in the metallization process and ended up being
one of the more challenging parts of the project. The project was originally conceived with
the firing and measurement of the full-sized (wafers with sizes ≥ 261 cm2) cells occurring
at the University of North Carolina Charlotte’s (UNCC) using their 6-zone IR belt furnace.
Initially only one profile measured at the start was taken as the correct temperature, and
zone 6’s setpoint was adjusted accordingly, but this resulted in unclear trends in the data.
Consequently, the number of profiles collected before and after firing the cells was
increased. On the advice of BTF’s advisors the firing procedure was also modified so that
~100 “dummy” cells were passed through the furnace prior the actual cells, to try to
stabilize the furnace temperature. These changes improved results, but temperature
variations >20°C were still observed at the same settings. Some of this variation was
attributed to the thermocouple shifting position as it moved through the furnace.

 The second issue with the furnace was that the cells were only supported at the edges
of the cells (Figure 6). As a significantly older furnace, the belt was originally designed for

significantly as they are transported. During the co-firing of cells with a full aluminum paste

the front Cu metallization; resulting in regional differences in the RC and Rf values of the
Cu fingers across the entire cell. The impact of this variation is likely to be a significant
factor in the wide range of Rf and η values observed for the cells. To meet the target η
while continuing to use this furnace, attempts were made to reformulate the paste so that
it could be fired at higher temperatures and slower belt speeds, while alternative furnaces
were sought.
 Initially a six zone BTU furnace in Singapore was located. The furnace had a belt
made from a nichrome wire mesh with raised wires; so that the cell would not sit directly
on the mesh. However, the speed range for this furnace was limited to 20 – 220 in/min.
Cells were prepared for this furnace by printing paste P2-5 through a 65 μm opening.
Paste P2-5 was selected at the time as it had the largest firing window of the pastes being
studied. Around ~200 M6 sized PERC cells were printed with 0.41 ± 0.4 mg/cm2 of paste
being used on the fingers. Although the cells were ready to be shipped, a communication
module in the furnace failed. Alternative firing sites were looked at and eventually an
agreement was reached with Georgia Institute of Technology (GIT) who were in the
process of bringing an old furnace back online to fire Cu. Like UNCC, the furnace at GIT
also had 6 zones, however its belt was similar to the BTU furnace (Figure 8(a)). GIT tried
matching the firing profiles to those obtained at UNCC (Figure 8(b)). A faster belt speed
in addition to different temperature and air flow settings was required, due to the longer
length of the heated zones. GIT’s furnace was also observed to have a slower cooling
rate. At the time GIT only had current-voltage (I-V) system that could probe 5 of the 9 bus
bars. This resulted in a maximum η of 15.3%, at a peak temperature of ~630°C.
Consequently, the cells were sent to the UNCC for measurement using their Sinton FCT-
650 system. This system could contact all 9 bus bars and an efficiency of ~20.2% was
measured; achieving a relative η of approximately 86% of a commercially prepared Ag
metallized cell. However, only 1 – 2 cells were fired at this condition. When firing 25 cells
in a row, a maximum relative η of 78.5% was achieved at the same settings. By tracking
the cell η and EL images vs. firing order, it was clear that the thermal mass and throughput
rate were causing the temperature to drop. Subsequently, the number of “dummy” cells
prior to firing the actual cells was increased and the power input to the lamps was
monitored. Although GIT’s furnace produced a more uniform firing, its furnace was
designed over 17 years ago, so lacked supports for larger cells, resulting in the center of
the cell being cooler than the edges. Although most firings at GIT resulted in a very large
spread in results, one or two cells per firing set could be obtained with high η.
 In parallel, paste development work continued at BTF using a custom made 3-zone
firing furnace for 16 cm2 cells (Figure 9(a)). The stage was motorized to produce a similar
temperature/firing profile to those obtained in the IR belt furnaces (Figure 9(b)). Due to
the issues with UNCC’s and GIT’s furnaces, the Go/No-Go goal’s GNG-1A, B and C were
modified to use 16 cm2 PERC cells fired at BTF.

 Funds raised during the project were also used to purchase a LECO tool. In recent
years the LECO process has been implemented into high throughput manufacturing lines,
to increase the yield and narrow the cell η distribution. The process uses a relatively lower
firing temperature profile to create the initial electrical contact. The cell is placed under
reverse bias and scanned with a focused laser beam, creating high current densities.
Since metallization pastes typically do not create a uniform contact area, the high current
is localized. It is believed that heat generates a “current induced firing” at the interface.
The smaller, and more resistive the contact points, the higher the localized temperature.xiv
In the case of Ag pastes, significant changes have been made to the glass frit in the paste
to lower the etching rate and create an initial resistive contact, that improves with LECO.
The LECO tool was used on cells for the EOP-A and EOP-B; resulting in a further
reduction to the amount of Cu paste needed to produce higher η devices.
Milestone 1.4. Finger Resistance
 Pastes P1 to P9 (Table 1) were printed in a half cell pattern with nine bus bars on M6
sized PERC cells. The pastes were printed through 400 mesh screens with 40 and 50 µm
openings. To improve printability, the viscosities were adjusted with the addition of extra
solvent (Figure 1). The cells were fired using UNCC’s 6-zone IR belt furnace. The belt
speed was changed from 250 to 325 in/min. For each set, 3 firing profiles were measured
before and after the cells were fired (Figure 11(a)). The Rf was measured between bus
bar 1 and 9 on each half cell, with a distance of 14.4 cm. Each half cell had 64 fingers
and two resistance measurements were taken on each side. Some of the cells showed
an order of magnitude difference in Rf for the half-cell patterns on either side of the cells;
indicating either print or firing uniformity issues across the cell. All the pastes printed using
the 40 µm and 50 µm screen openings, had weights well below the target mass deposition
of 0.38 mg/cm2. Consequently, at the slower belt speeds, Rf was extremely high. As
expected, the smaller the PSD, the higher the total Cu surface area, and the more
susceptible the particles were to oxidation. As a result, the finger resistance for P1 did not
drop below 100 Ω/cm. Pastes P2, P4 and P5 were selected for further investigation with
screen openings of 40, 50, 55 and 65 µm’s. Figure 11(b,c) show that there was a lot of
variation in Rf values for Pastes P2 and P4. This was later attributed to issues related to
a uniform firing. However, trends in the Rf values with respect to the printed weight
showed that pastes P2 and P4 achieved target resistances of < 8 Ω/cm with < 0.4 mg/cm2
of paste. Thus, hitting milestone 1.4’s target.

Subtask 1.4: Metallization Design
Milestone 1.7. Number of Bus Bars
 Unreliable firing and print issues created a lot of noise and made paste development
challenging. The majority of the work carried out in this project utilized M6 (276 cm2) sized
PERC cells, with the relative η of the Cu metallized cells compared to the same structure
metallized with Ag on commercial production manufacturing lines. Therefore, the same
screen design (i.e. finger and bus bar pitch) was used to print the Cu fingers and bus
bars. Consequently, the majority of the M6 sized PERC cells printed for this project had
127 fingers (~1.30 mm pitch) and 9 bus bars (~18 mm pitch). Thus, the focus was not on
trying to change the metallization design, but to try to lower the bulk resistivity and Rf
values to achieve the η targets. To achieve the η target of ≥ 90% relative η compared to
commercially produced Ag cells for GNG-1A, improvements to the paste rheology,
composition and firing conditions were made. The PERC cells were screen printed as
M6 sized wafers using ≤ 0.42 mg/cm2 of Cu paste for the fingers and then scribed into 16
cm2 sections. These sections were then fired in BTF’s 3-zone IR furnace. These sections
had 3 bus bars with a pitch of 18 mm, and an average Rf of ~2.0 Ω/cm; resulting in a
relative η ~88% (Figure 12).
Milestone 1.10. Metallization Design and Processing
 To achieve relative cell η’s ≥ 85% relative to the Ag metallized cells using only 110 mg
(~0.42 mg/cm2) of paste, M6 PERC cells with a selective Emitter (Set B, Table 3) were
used. Fingers were printed with 40 µm screen openings using multiple prints to obtain the
required cross-sectional area of ~600 µm2 using ~106 mg (0.39 mg/cm2) of paste. The
cells were printed with nine bus bars (18 mm pitch). To achieve η’s >85%, these cells
also had to be scribed into the 16 cm2 sections and fired in BTF’s 3-zone furnace using a
belt speed of 250 in/min, with zone 1, 2 and 3 setpoint temperatures of 450, 450 and
905°C, respectively. Carrier lifetime at Vmpp averaged ~60 µs. No edge passivation was
used on the sections. The larger edge to area ratio of the scribed sections were thought
to be the major contributor for increased recombination. Therefore, this milestone was
achieved in terms of performance, if not cell size.

Subtask 2.1: Preliminary Cell Durability
Milestone 1.6 & 1.8. Start of Copper (Cu) Diffusion Studies and Cu Diffusion
 The Cu pastes developed by BTF are being designed to avoid the use of extra
diffusion barriers; thus, avoiding the additional processing steps. The quality of the
“inherent” diffusion barrier is studied internally at BTF following accelerated aging
protocols developed to investigate the performance of Cu electroplated cells. xv,xvi The
cells are thermally stressed at 200°C, and the Voc and pFF are monitored over time. The
loss in pFF is assumed to represent a certain depth that the Cu has diffused into the Si
and the time taken (t95) for the pFF to drop by 5% of its initial value is monitored. The
longer the time, the more durable the cell, and the greater the probability that the module’s
constructed with these cells will last.
 The initial round of cells was printed with six different pastes with varying amounts of
frit in the paste (P2-1 to P2-6 Table 1). After printing, the cells were scribed into 16 cm2
sections and fired in BTF’s 3-zone IR furnace. The cells were thermally stressed at 200°C
under an inert atmosphere. The cells were periodically cooled down and the Voc and pFF
were measured using a Sinton Suns-Voc tester. Due to time constraints, after the 70 time
point, I-V measurements were halted until the final measurement at 1000 hrs. By the end
of the test, 28 of the 53 Cu metallized cells had a Voc within 10% of their initial values,
while only 12 of these cells had both the Voc and pFF within the targeted range. All the
cells printed with paste P2-5 failed early on and were removed after 70 hrs of testing. The
I-V curves clearly show that failure was due to the Cu’s diffusion into the junction, resulting
in a rapid and significant drop in pFF and Voc after only 1 hr of testing (Figure 15). Sections
printed with P2-4 and P2-6 also showed a decrease in pFF over the 1000 hr test period;
however, it occurred at much slower rate. The final I-V curves and EL images indicated
that rather than the deterioration being purely due to Cu diffusion, increased resistance
of the contacts appears to be the root cause; with the decrease in pFF, coinciding with an
increase in series resistance (RS), but not with a large drop in Voc (Figure 15). Although
only ~50% of samples met the target performance of a Voc and pFF ≥80% of their initial
values, the results were used to adjust the paste formulation (F10 to F12, Table 1) for the
second round of accelerated aging tests runs for GNG-1B.

Subtask 2.2: Copper Diffusion at the Interface
 Due to issues producing uniformly fired cells at UNCC, SIMS of the interface was
initially delayed and primary investigation into Cu diffusion was carried out through
electrical characterization. UNCC used the ION-TOF Time of Flight (TOF) SIMS at North
Carolina State University to evaluate if the Cu had diffused into the bulk of the wafer
(Figure 16). The cells were PERC cells with fill factor’s (FF) < 70%, due to underfired
region at the edges. Consequently, samples were taken from the center of the cell, where
EL imaging showed good contact had been achieved. The Cu contacts were etched off
using hydrofluoric (HF) acid. Measurements were taken by sputtering from the front and
back of the interface. Cu was detected in the glass region, but not in the bulk. From the
frontside measurement Cu showed a long tail into the Si substrate due to the residue from
etching. From the back measurement, Cu was observed in the SiNx anti-reflective coating
region, but not in the bulk. Residual HF acid was also observed (not shown), which can
also potentially increase the Cu ion yield. Although it was not possible with this project,
the next steps would be to repeat the measurement without etching off the Cu, as well as
measuring thermally stressed cells.

Subtask 2.3: Materials Analysis at the Interface
Milestone 1.9. Contact Resistance
 In addition to SIMS analysis, UNCC also carried out Scanning Electron Microscopy

Go/No-Go Milestone: GNG-1B. Cell Durability
 A second round of dry-heat testing included one hundred and fifty-four 16 cm2 sections
cut from M6 sized PERC cells with a selective emitter. The test focused on 3 pastes (F10,
F11, F12 Table 1), 2 screen openings and 2 belt speeds. Screens with 40 μm and 65 μm
openings were used to print fingers with cross sectional areas ranging from 190 – 320
μm2 and 435 – 570 μm2, respectively. Twelve sections per set were thermally stressed at
200°C under an inert atmosphere. The test included commercially produced Ag reference
cells cut into 16 cm2 sections as a control group. The cells were periodically cooled down,
and the Suns-Voc was measured at the 0, 1, 10, 30, 70, 110, 150, 200, 250, 300, 400,
500, 600, 700, 750, 850 and 1000 hr time points. IV curves under dark and illuminated
conditions were measured at the 0, 250, 500, 700 and 1000 hr time points, while EL
images were taken after 0, 500 and 1000 hrs. At around the 600 hr time point, the oven
suffered a power failure, and the samples had to be transferred to an older oven, where
a loss on vacuum was observed for ~2.5 hrs. Unlike the previous round of testing, no
catastrophic loss in Voc or pFF was observed by any of the samples during the first few
hours of testing (Figure 18 and Figure 19).
 Etching depth is impacted by the paste volume, proportion of frit (Frit 1) and firing
temperature. The proportion of frit in the paste had the largest impact on durability,
followed by the finger dimension. With increasing amounts of Frit 1, a decrease to the
pFF and Voc was observed (Paste F10). In addition to the increasing recombination, the
                                                                              Page 24 of 50

RS was observed to increase over time and EL images showed the images getting darker
as the tests progressed (not shown). While some of the RS increases appear to be
localized to individual fingers, regions which had a high RS to begin with (due to non-
uniform firing), appeared to increase in RS more rapidly. Eighty-seven of the Cu metallized
sections ended with both Voc and pFF remaining within 10% of the cell’s initial value after
1000 hrs at 200°C. Most of these sections were printed using pastes F11 and F12.

knotless 0°, 480 mesh screen with 25 µm opening. This resulted in approximately 0.31

Subtask 4.3: Cost Model Development
Milestone 1.2. Draft Cost Model
 Production of BTF’s CuBert™ paste was broken into materials, labor, and equipment.
For the analysis, the material was evaluated at varying scales up to 10,000 metric
tons/year, but the labor and equipment remained as a per kilogram cost. This assumed
that operation (OPEX) and capital (CAPEX) expenses were significant. The model
assumed: (1) the paste was predominately Cu, (2) a power law could be used to estimate
the materials cost based on the cost at low volumes, (3) labor rates were based on Bureau
of Labor Statistics for Louisville, Kentucky and (4) a 10-year equipment depreciation rate
with 4% maintenance cost per year. The labor and equipment costs were built around the
production of a 600 kg batch of paste. Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis on the materials
and labor was carried out; yielding a maximum price, minimum price, and the standard
deviation. To estimate the costs of Cu paste it was first necessary to place a bound around
the amount of paste needed. This was done using the PV CAGR and net zero policies to
determine the amount of paste required by industry. The penetration of the Cu paste into
the industry was modeled using a fast (10 years to reach 80%) and slow (20 years to
reach 40%) s-curve adoption approximations. Three of the scenarios easily approach
1,000 tons of paste per year. Based on this analysis the materials costs of the Cu paste
was below 10% the cost of just the Ag in the current metallization pastes at quantities
greater than 100 tons. The cost of the Ag paste is a very closely guarded secret within
the industry. At the time of developing the model, the cost of Ag pastes ranged between
$1100 and $1400 per kg, with a median of $1250 per kg. xviii Using a full factory of 5 teams,
it is expected that 150 tons of Cu paste can be manufactured per year. An Excel sheet
and pdf document describing the model were submitted.

modeling including solar photovoltaic (PV) materials manufacturing. The NREL methods
and approaches are the result of more than 12 years of work with solar industry materials
and equipment suppliers and integrated manufacturers.” Training in methods shown in
Figure 24 were used to produce the preliminary cost model for GNG-1D. With the updated
model, the cost of Cu paste remained below 10% of the cost of Ag paste. The preliminary
cost model was submitted to both parties for verification.

Subtask 5.1: Mini Modules and/or Module Fabrication and Lifetime Measurement
Milestone 2.1. Damp Heat Test
 Five prototypes were constructed to see if the module architecture and process
settings were viable. A supplier of edge sealants for PV modules allowed BTF access to
their laminator. The first round of mini-modules used mono-facial PERC cells with a SE
and pre-fired rear Al contact. The cells were fired as full M6 sized cells with the bus bars
parallel to the furnace entrance and cut in half after the front metallization had been fired.
Table 6 shows the materials used to construct these modules. Due to limited access to
equipment, the 5 mini-modules were tested continuously for 1000 hrs. Since BTF had not
yet received its Sinton FCT-650 flash tester, the 0 hr and 1000 hr measurements were
taken at two different locations. Consequently, measurements were taken with slightly
different setups for the system. With no other modules available the system was
calibrated to Module E at both timepoints. The results demonstrated that the module bill
of materials (BOM) was viable, but further improvements to the cells processing were
required (not shown).
Milestone 2.5. Damp Heat Tests on ≥5 2-cell Mini Modules
 The same external facility was used to produce an additional set of fifteen mini-
modules. With limited access to the laminator, the procedure was also adjusted from
module to module to try to reduce the number of voids present in the encapsulant. Lower
laminating temperatures created more voids, while higher temperatures reduced the
number of voids, but at some settings delamination of the encapsulant from the glass was
observed. Silicone frames were also used around the modules to prevent glass “pinching”
at the edges and improve the wetting of the edge sealant on the glass surface.
 The second-round of modules were constructed with both the pre-fired and co-fired
cells. All fifteen mini-modules contained two half-M6 sized PERC cells connected in
series. Although the η targets had yet to be realized on full sized (≥ M4) cells, cells were
selected for the modules, to identify the PV parameters that would indicate whether the
Cu contacts would fail or succeed during the accelerated aging tests. As a result, a range
of paste formulations and firing groups were tested. The selected cells were measured at
the University of North Carolina, UNCC and had pseudo fill factors, pFF >78% (i.e. pFF’s
> 94% relative to Ag). Modules containing commercially produced Ag metallized cells
were included for all the cell structures tested. The damp heat tests were carried out at
BTF. Due to the number of variables, modules were studied first by cell group and then
on a case-by-case basis. Modules I, J and K used pre-fired cells, while modules L, M, N,
O, P, Q, R and S used co-fired cells. The performance was monitored for ~1000 hrs, with
measurement intervals gradually increasing until the modules were continually exposed
to damp heat (85°C/85%RH) for the final 500 hrs. Figure 25 shows the IV and Suns-Voc
curves for four of these modules (N, Q, R, S) showing different types of failures. None of
the modules displayed a drop in Voc below 90% of its starting value. However, modules
Q and N were the only modules that resulted in a relative Voc drop of more than 95%. In

Subtask 5.3: Increasing Cell Durability
Milestone 2.3. Improvement in Cell Durability
 Dry-heat accelerated aging tests continued on the bare cells in parallel to the damp
heat testing. Cells (6 print groups and 7 firing conditions) fired in GIT’s furnace were

η of EOP-B. Previous dry heat tests showed that when Cu diffusion into the bulk is the

predominant reason for reduced cell lifetime, it occurs rapidly, therefore shorter tests can
quickly eliminate formulation and processing changes that make this failure pathway to

End of Project Milestone: EOP-A. Module Lifetime
 NREL’s certification lab was selected to run the partial IEC 61215 tests on the 16 half-
cell modules as it is an ISO accredited laboratory. At the request of the lab, a module
containing a single cell was also produced for Quantum Efficiency measurements (not
shown). The PERC cells use p-type Si wafer, which is known to be susceptible to Light
Induced Degradation (LID). This degradation mechanism can be minimized by light
soaking the modules, and since the IEC 61215 protocol uses an initial stabilization step
prior to running the tests, the modules were light soaked outside for 15 kWh/m2. Figure
27 shows examples of the initial IV curves for the Ag metallized module and two of the

                                                               Page 38 of 50

Cu metallized modules showing the highest and lowest performance. The Ag module had
a lower FF and maximum power (Pmax) than expected, indicating further improvements to
the interconnection method are needed to lower RS. The Cu metallized modules had a
relative performance of between 91 – 88% when compared to the Ag module. Due to
budget and time constraints only the tests in the IEC61215 standard that would directly
impact the metallization were carried out. This included thermal cycling of four of the
modules and damp heat testing of the remaining four. The Ag metallized module was only
used to calibrate the flash tester before and after testing.
 The modules are constructed with materials that have different thermal expansion
coefficients. By exposing the modules to extreme temperature swings, the interfaces, in
particular the metallization and solder points are mechanically stressed, leading to
cracking and increased RS. xix The first four modules were thermally cycled between -40°C
and +85°C, for 200 cycles while biased under a voltage of 10 V and 9.2 A. The modules
were held at extreme temperatures for 10 mins and each cycle took ~ 4.8 hrs. Figure
28(a-d) shows the impact of the thermal cycling on the IV curves of the modules. Only a
small increase in RS was observed, with the modules retaining an average Pmax of 97%
of their initial values. The final four modules were exposed to damp heat testing; with the
modules being continuously exposed to a temperature and relative humidity of 85°C and
85%RH, for 1000 hrs. The damp heat test typically identifies failures such as corrosion of
the metal contacts, as well as interactions between the encapsulant and metal, that can
occur as a result of moisture ingress into the module. Figure 28(e-h) shows the IV curves
before and after damp heat tests. The results show a significant reduction in performance
with an average Pmax of 74% of the modules initial values. Based on dry-heat tests of
single cells and damp heat measurements of the mini-modules the degradation appears
to be due to increased recombination potentially as a result of Cu diffusion. The average
Voc of the modules had decreased to ~97% of their initial values; roughly a 21 mV
reduction per half-cell. EL images of the tested modules, showed that some cells on the
strings remained relatively bright, indicating that the performance of these cells had not
decreased significantly (not shown). Although the modules passed the thermal cycling
tests, they did not pass the damp heat tests. However, a route for producing durable
PERC modules still exists with further optimization of the paste and processes.

Milestone 2.2. Co-Firing
 BTF’s Cu paste has a lower firing temperature than Ag metallization pastes, therefore
the Al pastes used to print the rear Al contact have been designed to co-fire at the higher
temperatures utilized by the Ag pastes. Consequently, the Cu paste was initially
developed to work on cells with the rear Al contact pre-fired. Paste F13 in Table 1 shows
an example of a paste developed for pre-fired PERC cells. The amount of frit and
nanoparticles present in F13 would produce a working device when printed on a cell with
a pre-fired Al contact. When the paste was co-fired and the belt speed reduced (i.e.
increasing the firing time and peak wafer temperature), the paste significantly over
etched; resulting in increased recombination currents and lower Voc’s (Figure 29(a)). By
lowering the amount of Frit 1 (the more aggressive) etchant in the paste (F6), etching
could be reduced (Figure 29(b)). However, at higher temperatures the contacts were
more prone to oxidization resulting in a high RS. The current hypothesis is that the
hydrogen released from the SiNx coating during co-firing aids in creating a reducing
environment close to the interface, which accelerates the etching rate of the etchants in
the Cu paste. In addition, the SiNx becomes denser during firing, requiring more
aggressive/higher quantities of etchants when the paste is printed on a pre-fired cell.

 Initial co-firing studies were conducted in BTF’s 3-zone furnace using PERC with a
HE emitter and full rear Al paste coverage. Cells were fired at numerous furnace settings
with both zone and temperature setpoints being varied. The sheet resistance of these
samples was ~2 – 4 times higher than the Al contact (~0.0025 Ω/□) from commercially
produced Ag metallized cells. However, it is not only the conductivity of the Al contact that
will impact the device’s performance, but the back surface field (BSF) that forms through
the narrow local contact openings (LCO) in the rear passivation layer. At the LCO regions,
dark lines appear in the contact where the Si diffuses into the Al. The concentration of Si
and hence area of the dark regions is dependent on temperature. Bulk Al melts ~660°C,
but Si will start to dissolve in Al ~300°C, and solubility will reach a maximum around the
Al-Si eutectic point at ~577°C. The narrower the region, the shallower the BSF. A
reduction to the BSF of the cell can increase recombination at the rear surface, resulting
in a lower Voc. Figure 30 shows the box plots for LCO area’s measured using optical
microscope images taken from the center of cells fired by the manufacturer, UNCC, GIT
and BTF. The smaller LCO areas indicate that BTF’s furnace, and the settings used to
fire the Cu contacts at GIT were significantly lower than the respective Ag co-firing
settings used by the manufacturer or the settings used by UNCC to pre-fire the cells.
 M6 sized PERC cells were also co-fired in GIT’s 6-zone furnace. Figure 31 shows the
impact of the firing conditions on the Al sheet resistance. In all cases the left and right
edges experienced a higher temperature, while the center was cooler. However, the
cooler region was not uniformly distributed, with one side (most likely the edge leading
into the furnace) appearing to experience a higher temperature. This variation in
conductivity was also observed for the front contact, on both pre- and co-fired cells (not
shown). Increasing zone 6’s temperatures aid in improving the conductivity of both the Al
and Cu contact but does not completely alleviate the non-uniformity. Not only is
conductivity impacted, but the localized BSF is as well. The size and thickness of the
wafer as well as the location of the wafer supports on the furnace belt is believed to be a
contributing factor to the lack of homogeneity, due to increased bending and warping. M6
cells were also cut in half before being put through the furnace to try to minimize this
effect.

Subtask 7.2: Updated Cost Model
End of Project Milestone: EOP-D. Refined Cost Model
 The changes recommended by the third-party reviewer were built upon and updated
with the latest Cu paste formulation. This model and summary description were reviewed
by a Solar Techno-Economic Analyst at NREL. Based on the analyst’s feedback, the
model was further refined. The updated model looked at scaling CuBertTM production up
to 1000 MT/year, and compared the resulting price to the target Cu paste cost of less
than 10% of Ag paste price. Ag paste pricing was obtained from the Shanghai Metals
Market ($1001/kg and $1,131/kg with and without VAT, respectively on the 14th March
2025). xx Tariff and duty to the US are estimated at 25% tariff and 3% duty. xxi This gave
a target Cu Paste cost of ~$120/kg, based on Ag front-side paste pricing. For modeling
purposes, Ag paste price (for US customers) was estimated to be Ag market price/0.85.
As background, the cost of Ag has fluctuated between $400 and $1500 per kg over the
past decade and in March 2025 was ~$1000/ kg, while the cost of Cu has fluctuated
between $3 and $10/ kg and is currently ~$10/kg. xxii [N.B. On the 14th October 2025 the
price of Ag and Cu had increased to $1785/kg and $11/kg, respectively]. Bulk Cu has
94.6% of the conductivity of Ag. Although not included in this model, BTF commercial
calculations assume an additional ~5 – 10 wt% Cu paste will be required to meet η parity
with Ag paste applications. BTF has modeled that near parity in η between Ag and Cu is
required to commercialize Cu pastes. The model also used learning rates to extrapolate
the cost of production at much higher quantities of production than what BTF currently
produces. BTF has obtained budgetary quotes at the 10, 100, and 1000 MT scale for the
Cu powders that make up the majority of the paste at the 1 and 100 kg batch sizes. Based
on these empirical data, learning rates were determined for quantities between 1 and

Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
 Cu contacts made with BTF’s air fired CuBertTM metallization pastes achieved a
maximum η of 21.4% when used as the front contacts on M6 (276 cm2) PERC cells, and
an η of 24.4% when used as the rear contact on M10 (331 cm2) sized TOPCon cells.
Thus, resulting in a relative η of 94% for PERC and 96% for TOPCon, when compared to
the same structures metallized with Ag. The amount of Cu paste was reduced to below
0.39 mg/cm2, making the product more economically viable. Cost models on the
production of the paste established that paste production would be under 10% of the cost
of analogous Ag paste. Although the first attempts at partial IEC-61215 testing of 16 half-
cell modules contact Cu metallized PERC cells were not successful, smaller scale tests
on single and half-cell modules indicate that performance can be improved through further
optimization of the pastes and processing parameters. The modules, however, were able
to pass the thermal cycling component of the tests, indicating that the manually soldered
interconnection was viable. Early stage testing of single cell TOPCon modules indicate
that the Cu contacts are much more resilient to Cu diffusion, due to their structure.
 In the last 5 years major changes have been occurring in the PV industry, with cell
sizes increasing from M2 (246 cm2) up to M12 (441 cm2), increased number of bus bars,
shallower emitters and a major shift away from PERC to TOPCon. This has meant that
many of the processing and measurement equipment available at R&D facilities were
unable to handle these larger devices; resulting in sub-optimal performance.
Consequently, a large proportion of the paste development work was carried out on 16
cm2 cells. However, this did force BTF to expand its capabilities with an IR belt furnace,
Sinton flash tester, LECO unit and Laminator. Thus, enabling an acceleration in product
development.
Path Forward:
 BTF has demonstrated the CuBertTM Cu metallization pastes on both PERC and
TOPCon technologies and is actively working on closing the performance gap to Ag
metallized cells, as well as improving durability. The company is also focused on providing
a frontside solution for TOPCon. This work is being carried out with both cell and module
manufacturers. In addition to collaborating with Cu powder suppliers to make a robust
supply chain, BTF is also discussing further scaling manufacturing with paste production
facilities and has already demonstrated the production of a 25 kg batch of Cu paste.
 C-Si solar cells are typically produced on turnkey production lines, resulting in less
manual handling of the product and wafers. Therefore, to understand how the product will
perform under these conditions, BTF is looking to test the product on pilot or production
lines. However, the industry is reluctant to fire Cu pastes in their production furnaces, and
some are even reluctant to bring the product on to their site without proof that the material
is viable. Many manufacturers site the potential of Cu to indirectly poison cells via the
contamination of manufacturing equipment. BTF has mitigated some of their concerns by
building an applications laboratory in Louisville, KY that allows testing on customers cells,
prior to the Cu pastes being potentially transferred to their development and production
lines.
 Alternative metallization strategies to Ag paste have been discussed for years, but
between the imminent terra-watt scale PV manufacturing and recent historical rises in the
price of silver ($1785/kg on the 14th October 2025), the drive to develop and ultimately
adopt Cu metallization has been accelerating.
