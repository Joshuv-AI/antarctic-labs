Feature selection methodology based on explainable AI: application in predicting extubation success in the intensive care unit
Authors: JaeBin Sung, Geun-Hyeong Kim, Jae-Woo Kim, Ka Hyun Kim, Yong-Goo Shin, Seung Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-dimensional medical data hinder predictive modeling because of noise and the curse of dimensionality, making robust feature selection (FS) essential. We propose a model-agnostic feature selection method that aggregates three explainers–SHAP, LIME, and layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP)–into a consensus feature ranking and then reorders it using true-positive (TP)-specific attributions. Using the MIMIC-IV database, we analyzed 19,567 mechanically ventilated patients and trained a multilayer perceptron to predict extubation success. On the test set, the proposed XAI FS achieved an AUROC of 0.8527 ± 0.01 and a sensitivity of 0.8275± 0.01, performing comparably to conventional statistical, wrapper-based, and single-explainer FS methods while prioritizing a compact, highly interpretable, and clinically plausible feature subset. The key predictors closely matched established extubation readiness criteria, supporting their practical relevance. In additional experiments on seven other medical prediction datasets, the framework improved performance on most classification tasks, suggesting potential generalizability beyond extubation. These findings indicate that TP-focused, multi-explainer FS can provide robust, interpretable models and establish reliable consensus feature rankings in high-risk ICU decision support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53622-0.

Introduction

In artificial intelligence (AI)-based predictive modeling, feature selection (FS)–the process of choosing which variables to use as model inputs from a pool of potential predictors–is of paramount importance. High-dimensional datasets that have not undergone an appropriate selection process frequently contain irrelevant or redundant features, as well as a substantial amount of noise. Such features can detrimentally affect the efficiency of model training and its generalization performance, and they also reduce the interpretability of the resulting models1,2.

FS is particularly important in medicine. Medical data characteristically exhibit strong correlations and intricate interactions among variables, and the indiscriminate removal of features may result in the loss of clinically significant information. However, given the prevalence of high-dimensional and complex medical datasets, appropriate FS has become virtually indispensable. Consequently, a wide range of FS methodologies have been proposed and applied in medical research. In particular, predicting extubation success in the intensive care unit (ICU) is a high-stakes task in which both model performance and interpretability are crucial, and FS plays a central role in identifying clinically actionable predictors. Extubation failure, frequently necessitating reintubation, has been associated with elevated mortality, prolonged ICU stay, and increased healthcare expenditures, emphasizing the need for reliable and transparent prediction models3–6. Although numerous machine learning-based approaches for predicting extubation outcome have been proposed7–9, these methods generally treat FS as an implicit by-product of model training and offer limited insight into which variables drive successful extubation decisions.

Early FS approaches were primarily based on classical statistical techniques. For example, some methods use univariate hypothesis testing (e.g., p-values) to evaluate the contribution of each feature to the outcome, whereas others employ distance-based metrics, such as the Bhattacharyya distance, to quantify the discriminative power of each feature10. However, the efficacy of these methods often depends on distributional assumptions, such as normality, and they struggle to capture nonlinear interactions between variables. Furthermore, medical datasets frequently exhibit non-normal distributions, class imbalance, and heterogeneous feature scales, posing significant challenges to the application of conventional methods without substantial ad hoc preprocessing. In addition, many FS techniques are closely tied to a particular model family, thereby constraining their generalizability across different classifiers.

To overcome these limitations, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for guiding FS11. In medical AI, methods such as SHAP12, LIME13, and layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP)14 have been used to provide local and global attributions of model predictions to individual features, and have gained popularity for maintaining transparency while identifying clinically meaningful predictors15,16. In parallel, numerous hybrid and metaheuristic FS approaches have been proposed17–20. For instance, combining the sine cosine algorithm with cuckoo search, or cuckoo search with Harris hawks optimization, has been shown to select a small subset of genes while substantially improving classification performance in high-dimensional cancer gene expression data17,18. Similarly, frameworks that integrate mRMR with single- or multi-objective metaheuristics (e.g., mRMR+SSO+WSVM, NGHA, KAO/AOA)19–21 and recent comprehensive reviews on dimensionality reduction and metaheuristic-driven FS22,23 underscore how actively FS is being studied in high-dimensional biomedical domains.

Recent clinical feature selection research has increasingly shifted from single-method ranking toward framework-level approaches that explicitly emphasize robustness and clinical interpretability24,25. For example, Wang et al.25 proposed a multi-step EMR feature selection framework that jointly considers predictive performance and stability and further incorporates expert knowledge validation to support clinically meaningful risk factors. In parallel, recent work has explored scalable ensemble feature selection strategies tailored to high-dimensional healthcare datasets to improve reliability and interpretability across diverse biometric signals. These trends highlight the importance of reducing method-specific variability and strengthening clinical plausibility–motivations that align with our Tri-XAI consensus ranking and TP-aware reordering strategy. In addition, a complementary line of work incorporates medical knowledge to constrain or augment data-driven learning for improved interpretability and transportability. Knowledge-assisted hybrid modeling strategies have been proposed to mitigate the curse of dimensionality by combining mechanistic/clinical structure with data-driven components26. Knowledge-augmented frameworks have further paired structured modeling with SHAP-based analyses to support interpretable and generalizable ICU predictions across hospitals27. More recently, large language models have been explored to structure and communicate explanations for ICU risk prediction28. Despite these advances, existing FS research still faces several common limitations. First, XAI-based FS methods frequently depend on a single explainer (e.g., SHAP, LIME, or LRP alone) and a single predictive model. Consequently, the feature ranking can change substantially when the explainer or underlying model is replaced, raising concerns about the stability and robustness of the selected feature subset. Second, most hybrid and metaheuristic FS methods tightly couple the selected feature subset to a specific classifier or search heuristic, and rarely provide clinically interpretable reasons for why individual features are selected. This makes it difficult for clinicians to understand and trust the model’s decisions. Third, and most importantly, many FS studies do not explicitly optimize or evaluate the relationship between selected features and positive-class performance (e.g., the occurrence of major complications or treatment success). In high-risk ICU settings, where maximising true-positive (TP) predictions is critical, this lack of explicit alignment with positive-class outcomes can be a serious limitation.

Therefore, there remains a need for a model-agnostic FS framework that (i) reconciles multiple explainers into a single, stable ranking and (ii) explicitly aligns feature selection with positive-class performance in high-risk intensive care prediction tasks. In this study, we propose a true-positive (TP)-aware, tri-XAI (three-explainer XAI) feature selection methodology that combines three complementary explainers–SHAP, LIME, and LRP–and aggregates their attributions into a consensus ranking. The proposed framework focuses on TP cases and reorders features according to their contribution to correctly predicting extubation success, thereby directly linking feature selection to clinically prioritised outcomes. This procedure is designed to mitigate single-explainer bias, decouple FS from any particular classifier or metaheuristic search strategy, and yield a stable, clinician-facing feature subset for predicting extubation success in the ICU.

The primary contributions of this study are threefold: (1) We introduce a model-agnostic, tri-XAI feature selection framework that integrates SHAP, LIME, and LRP into a single consensus ranking. By leveraging complementary perspectives from three explainers, our approach mitigates single-explainer and single-model bias in estimating feature importance. (2) We propose a TP-aware attribution reordering strategy that explicitly aligns feature selection with positive-class performance (extubation success). By prioritizing features that consistently contribute to true-positive predictions, the framework directly targets clinically critical outcomes in high-risk ICU decision-making. (3) We validate the proposed methodology using the large-scale MIMIC-IV database by developing an extubation success prediction model and comparing our framework with conventional statistical, wrapper-based, and XAI-based FS methods. Furthermore, we demonstrate the generalizability of the approach across multiple medical datasets, showing that it provides a compact, clinically meaningful feature subset while maintaining or improving predictive performance. The MIMIC-IV database is a de-identified electronic health record (EHR) public dataset of patients admitted to the ICU at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts, collected from 2008 to 2019. It contains comprehensive clinical data, including vital signs, test results, medications, diagnoses, and surgeries, as well as demographic information, admission and discharge details, and other clinical documentation. It also contains information on more than 28 million ICU admissions for more than 380,000 unique patients, making it one of the largest publicly available ICU datasets worldwide.

In our study, we constructed a dataset using the MIMIC-IV database that included 20,648 patients who underwent intubation. Patients were excluded if they met one of the following criteria: (a) not the first time of extubation during a hospital stay (n=779), (b) already had a tracheostomy, received a tracheostomy during MV, or had a tracheostomy within 48 hours of extubation (n=23), (c) unplanned extubation (n=39), (d) death during MV (n=165), and (e) died of non-respiratory causes within 48 hours after extubation (n=75). After applying the exclusion criteria, 19,567 patients were selected. Moreover, patients were classified as having extubation failure if they met one of the following criteria: (a) died of respiratory-related causes within 48 hours after extubation or (b) invasive or non-invasive ventilation within 48 hours after extubation. To develop a model to predict extubation success in MV, we built a dataset and selected the input features by reviewing previous studies, as shown in Table 112,29–32. The clinical features of ICU patients change over time, making it difficult to reliably determine their condition using a single measurement at a specific point in time. Therefore, 36 of the 48 features were averaged over the 4 hours before extubation to provide a more stable and accurate reflection of the patient’s condition. Our dataset contained a large volume of data, which includes many outliers. Outliers were identified by Tukey’s boxplot method, a widely used statistical outlier removal method, where outliers are identified as being above Q3 (the 75th percentile) + 1.5 × IQR and below Q1 (the 25th percentile) - 1.5 × IQR, where Q3 is defined as the 75th percentile, Q1 is the 25th percentile, and IQR is Q3-Q133,34. Any data points outside this range were considered outliers. Data below the lower bound are replaced with the lower bound value, and data above the upper bound are replaced with the upper bound value.

Modeling neural network for predicting extubation success

To predict the success of extubation, we developed a multilayer perceptron (MLP) that effectively analyzes complex data patterns. MLP includes an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer, with each layer of neurons fully connected to those in the subsequent layer. It is commonly applied to classification and regression tasks with backpropagation as the primary learning algorithm. Detailed information on the model architecture and loss function is provided in Supplementary Note 1.

To enhance the reliability and consistency of our model, we utilized 5 times repeated 4 × 4 nested cross-validation for the modeling process35 to allow for model evaluation and hyperparameter tuning through two stages of cross-validation loops. The two stages of the cross-validation loops consist of outer and inner loops. In the outer loop, the dataset is divided into four folds, with one fold used as the test dataset for external cross-validation, and the remaining three folds used as the training dataset. In the inner loop, the training dataset from the outer loop is further split into four internal folds, where three folds are used for training and one fold for validation. Four hyperparameter combinations are evaluated in the inner loop, and the model with the best performance on the test dataset is selected. The outer loop runs four times to ensure that all four folds are used as the test dataset, and the entire training process is carried out over five iterations.

XAI background

SHAP

SHAP36 is an advanced method based on game theory that specifically utilizes Shapley values to interpret the outputs of black-box models. Shapley values are derived from the cooperative game theory and involve the formation of all possible combinations of players to assess their individual contributions to the overall outcome. This method calculates the importance of each player by considering their average marginal contributions when included or excluded from various combinations. By integrating this concept into AI models, SHAP provides a detailed quantification of the impact of each feature on the model predictions. This allowed a comprehensive analysis of the features that significantly influenced the model output. The key advantage of SHAP is its ability to attribute contributions among features fairly, ensuring that each feature’s impact is considered in a balanced manner. LIME

LIME13 is a technique designed to interpret the predictions of classification models from a local perspective. Unlike global interpretation methods, LIME focuses on understanding model behavior for individual predictions by generating a simplified local surrogate model. This approach involves the use of a linear regression model to approximate a complex model within a localized region around the input data point of interest. It operates by perturbing the input data around this point and observing the resulting changes in the model output. By fitting a linear model to these perturbations, LIME provides insights into the contribution of each feature for that specific prediction. This method is valuable because it does not assume any specific characteristics of the underlying model, such as linearity or differentiability, making it versatile and widely applicable. Mathematically, local surrogate models with interpretability constraints can be expressed as follows:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm {explanation(x)=argmin_{g\in G}L(f, g, \pi _x)+\Omega (g) },\end{aligned}$$\end{document}where ξ(x) is the local surrogate model for input x, G is the family of possible explanations, L is the loss function that measures the fidelity of the explanation g to the original model f in the locality defined by π \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_x$$\end{document}, π \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_x$$\end{document} is the locality around x, and Ω(g) is the complexity measure of the explanation g.

LRP

LRP14 is a method for explaining the decisions of neural networks by propagating relevance scores backward through the network. This technique uses a back-propagation algorithm to distribute the relevance score of the output prediction back to the input features. The primary objective of LRP is to determine the contribution of each input feature to the final prediction, providing a clear understanding of which features are the most influential. LRP operates by traversing the neural network from the output layer to the input layer and assigning relevance scores to each neuron based on its contribution to the prediction. The relevance score, R, indicates the extent to which an input feature affects the output. Features with higher relevance scores had a more significant influence on the prediction than those with lower scores. In our study, we introduce a novel XAI-based feature selection methodology designed to enhance the accuracy of predictive models. As shown in Fig. 3 illustrates the overall selection process for the feature-selected dataset using the XAI-based feature selection methodology. To select the hyperparameters and avoid overfitting, we conducted a 4×4 nested cross-validation repeated five times, and calculated the average AUROC of the four models from each iteration. The iteration with the highest calculated average AUROC was selected to use the models for feature selection. We performed feature selection using each of the four models from the selected iteration to ensure comprehensive use of the dataset. Each model utilized three XAI techniques (SHAP, LIME, and LRP) to extract the XAI values and rank features, as shown in Fig. 4a. We chose SHAP, LIME, and LRP to cover complementary attribution paradigms: additive game-theoretic attributions (SHAP), local surrogate explanations (LIME), and neural-network–specific relevance propagation (LRP). This design aims to reduce dependence on any single explainer’s assumptions and to support a more robust consensus ranking. The detailed process is as follows.

Performance evaluation for the model

To evaluate the performance of our model, we used four metrics: sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and AUROC37,38. Additionally, by analyzing how specific features influence predictions for each patient, healthcare professionals can apply treatments tailored to the individual rather than relying solely on standard criteria. This enables personalized and optimized care for each patient.

Performance evaluation of XAI-based feature selection methodology

To evaluate the generalization performance of the proposed methodology, we conducted experiments applying it to various classification and regression datasets. As shown in Table 4, the proposed methodology was applied to both classification and regression tasks, and the AUROC and MAE were used to evaluate the classification and regression tasks, respectively. For example, in the Early Classification of Diabetes dataset, which is a binary classification task designed to predict the conversion from clinically isolated syndromes to multiple sclerosis, the average AUROC improved from 0.9120 ± 0.020 to 0.9162 ± 0.012 after applying the proposed methodology. Similar performance improvements were observed in most other classification datasets, suggesting that the framework is effective in reducing redundancy and noise in high-dimensional feature spaces. In contrast, the regression datasets showed limited or no improvement. One possible explanation is that regression tasks often require rich and precise information from multiple features to predict a continuous target value accurately. The regression datasets included in our experiments had relatively small numbers of input features (łe 11), and reducing the number of features in such low-dimensional settings may inadvertently remove essential information, resulting in insufficient signal for accurate learning. Discussion

AI models can assist clinical decision-making, but their black-box nature often makes prediction interpretation challenging. Many recent studies have employed XAI techniques to interpret black-box models or select key features to demonstrate model reliability15,16,52. However, most existing studies have used only one XAI technique for feature selection and model analysis, leaving the possibility of biased interpretation15,16,53. To overcome these limitations, we proposed an XAI-based feature selection methodology that integrates three complementary explainers–SHAP, LIME, and LRP. In Tri-XAI, each explainer produces a feature-importance ranking, and we aggregate these rankings into a consensus ranking to reduce reliance on any single explainer and to mitigate explainer-specific variability. We further apply a TP-aware reordering step that prioritizes features consistently important among true-positive predictions, aligning the selected features with factors supporting correct positive decisions. In this work, we conceptualize stability as the robustness of the selected feature set against explainer-specific differences. Systematic stability benchmarking across alternative explainer configurations remains an important direction for future work. While SHAP and LIME are model-agnostic, LRP is neural-network–specific; therefore, our framework combines model-agnostic and model-specific explainability to support more robust feature selection. Accordingly, we used an MLP as a simple backbone to integrate gradient/propagation-based relevance with model-agnostic explainers (SHAP/LIME) under a unified pipeline. We acknowledge that the resulting feature subset may be architecture-dependent and thus not universally optimal across all model families. Therefore, we interpret performance differences across classifiers cautiously and position Tri-XAI’s primary contribution as consensus-based, TP-aware feature ranking for interpretability. Future work will examine portability of the ranking across alternative backbones, including tree-based learners commonly used for tabular clinical data.

Compared with recent clinical feature selection (FS) frameworks that emphasize multi-step filtering, ensemble selection, or expert validation, our approach specifically targets explainer-level variability by aggregating SHAP, LIME, and LRP into a consensus ranking and applying TP-aware reordering. This perspective is complementary to knowledge-guided or knowledge-constrained approaches, and Tri-XAI can be integrated by applying the consensus ranking within guideline-/expert-derived feature sets or as a post-hoc validation layer to assess consistency with known clinical mechanisms.

In this study, the proposed TP-aware, XAI-based feature selection framework yielded modest but consistent performance gains for predicting extubation success. Compared with the baseline MLP trained on the full feature set, the MLP with XAI-based feature selection improved AUROC from 0.8455 ± 0.01 to 0.8527 ± 0.01 and F1-score from 0.7527 ± 0.01 to 0.7609 ± 0.02, with an increase in sensitivity from 0.7880 ± 0.12 to 0.8275 ± 0.01 while using only 65% of the original features. Although the numerical improvements appear modest, the higher sensitivity is clinically meaningful because failing to identify patients likely to achieve extubation success may contribute to delayed extubation and unnecessary prolonged mechanical ventilation. Accordingly, we defined extubation success as the positive class and selected an operating point that prioritizes sensitivity for success. We explicitly acknowledge the trade-off of reduced specificity and the potential clinical risk of false-positive predictions; therefore, we report threshold-dependent metrics to support interpretation and emphasize that the model is intended for decision support rather than stand-alone decisions. The PPV remained high in both models (0.9411 vs. 0.9387), which is partly expected given the high prevalence of extubation success in our cohort ( 84%). In contrast, the NPV improved with XAI-based feature selection (0.4075 to 0.4500), suggesting a modest gain in identifying patients at risk of extubation failure.Notably, PPV/NPV are prevalence-dependent and were approximated from cross-validated sensitivity/specificity and cohort prevalence; therefore, these values may vary across clinical settings with different outcome rates, highlighting the need for external validation and potential recalibration. A major limitation of this work is that it was developed using a single-center cohort; therefore, local case-mix, measurement routines, and missingness patterns may differ from other institutions. Accordingly, the model may partially capture center-specific practice patterns rather than purely general clinical relationships. Future work should include external validation in multi-center cohorts and site-specific recalibration prior to broader deployment. In addition, we explored the broader applicability of the proposed methodology using several external datasets. While performance improvements were observed in classification tasks, the approach did not translate consistently to regression tasks, particularly in datasets with a small initial feature set (łe11 features), where feature selection may remove informative signals and degrade performance.

We demonstrated the superiority of our proposed methodology by comparing performance with other feature selection methodologies and their technical limitations. UE Choi et al.10 used the Bhattacharyya distance to calculate the importance of features for feature selection. However, the limitation of Bhattacharyya distance is that it assumes a normal data distribution, which is challenging to apply to discrete variables. Therefore, our study included all discrete variables and applied the Bhattacharyya distance to the remaining features, and this methodology was not sufficient to improve model performance. Thamraj, N. G. & Khan, R. A. H. et al51 performed feature selection using three XAI techniques (ELI5, SHAP, and LIME). They simply summed the XAI values and averaged them to calculate importance, and this methodology also did not improve model performance. In our study, we comprehensively integrated the XAI values extracted using the three XAI techniques and visualized them using a heatmap to better understand the relationship between model prediction and feature importance. As detailed in Fig. 6, the figure presents an individual analysis illustrating the degree to which each feature contributes to the prediction for each patient. High peak insp. pressure values, long sedation duration, long MV duration, high heart rate, and high FiO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} values are features predictive of extubation failure. By contrast, low peak insp. pressure values, short sedation duration, short MV duration, not being elderly, and not having a history of stroke were features predictive of extubation success. Additionally, to understand the relationships between patients classified as TN or TP and all features, we conducted a global analysis. A heat map visualizing the results confirmed that features such as peak insp. pressure, sedation duration, MV duration, PSV, and age were commonly identified as important (Supplementary Note 4). We anticipated that features such as hemodynamic parameters, respiratory rate, and RSBI would significantly contribute to the prediction of extubation success. However, our study showed that these features were less important than expected. Due to the limitations of the original dataset, the importance of some clinically important features may have been overshadowed by the importance of others. Therefore, further study is necessary to determine ways to utilize all clinically significant features more effectively.

Our study has inherent limitations because of the methods used to ensure fairness during data preprocessing. Large medical databases, such as MIMIC-IV, often contain outliers due to incorrect entries during data collection. Given the presence of outliers in a model can cause confusion during training, it is common practice to remove outliers from the data before training. Given that not all features have upper and lower limits, and often do not have absolute range criteria, setting upper and lower limits for features based on clinical opinion can introduce clinician subjectivity. To remove outliers using objective and fair criteria, we used the IQR removal method to apply the same criteria to all features. Although the IQR removal method has the advantage of applying a fair criterion because it applies the same percentage to all features, it may not remove all outliers for a particular feature. Therefore, we set a threshold that maximizes the removal of outliers while minimizing the loss of normal data. However, due to the limitations of the raw data, preprocessing may not remove all outliers and missing values. Although the selected predictors were largely concordant with commonly used extubation readiness considerations, we did not perform a formal expert-panel or guideline-mapped validation of feature plausibility. Future work will include expert-grounded review and knowledge-based mapping to further verify clinical relevance and identify potentially spurious or site-specific signals.

Conclusion

In summary, we proposed a novel XAI-based feature selection methodology using multiple XAI techniques: SHAP, LIME, and LRP. The methodology ensures unbiased model interpretation and facilitates the selection of important features. To validate our methodology, we constructed a dataset using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database and developed the MLP as a baseline model to predict extubation success in ICU patients on MV. By applying the methodology to the model, the average AUROC improves from 0.8455 to 0.8527. Moreover, we demonstrated generalizability by applying our methodology to medical datasets, and comparative experiments with the existing feature selection methodologies further validated the superiority of our methodology.
