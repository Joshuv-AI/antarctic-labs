Machine learning-driven QSAR modeling combined with single cell transcriptomics identifies novel drug targets for lung cancer
Authors: Nagasundaram Nagarajan, Sushil Kumar Shakyawar, Kayode Raheem, Chittibabu Guda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, largely due to frequent metastasis to the brain and bones. Therapeutic outcomes are often limited by drug resistance, tumor heterogeneity, and the lack of effective treatment options across different stages and metastatic sites. Identifying druggable targets that are conserved between primary tumors and metastases is critical for advancing precision oncology.MethodsscRNA-seq expression profiles from primary NSCLC tumors and matched brain and bone metastases were analyzed to identify conserved and site-specific gene expression signatures. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis was used for target prioritization. Potential targets identified from scRNA-seq analysis were used for ligand screening using machine learning (ML)-based quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling. QSAR models were developed using ChEMBL bioactivity data and evaluated across multiple ML algorithms. Large-scale virtual screening was followed by molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for lead optimization.ResultsEight candidate therapeutic targets were prioritized, among which ARPC2, PSMB4, and RAC2 were consistently overexpressed across primary, brain, and bone metastatic sites and were functionally implicated in key cancer-associated pathways. QSAR modeling demonstrated strong predictive performance, with XGBoost and Random Forest models achieving AUROC values greater than 0.97. Virtual screening of approximately 9–15 million compounds per target identified high-affinity candidates. Subsequent docking and MD simulations revealed that the ARPC2-14465616, PSMB4-74833722, and RAC2-57175325 complexes exhibited the highest structural stability and sustained intermolecular interactions.ConclusionThis integrative single-cell transcriptomics and ML-driven drug discovery framework identified conserved druggable targets and promising lead compounds for metastatic NSCLC. The results provide a strong foundation for experimental validation and the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting both primary tumors and metastatic lesions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08463-w.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases and remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. It is a heterogeneous disease comprising major subtypes such as adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma. Despite advances in screening and therapy, NSCLC is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, contributing to its poor prognosis [2]. Key oncogenic drivers such as EGFR, ALK, and KRAS have been identified, enabling the development of targeted therapies; however, survival rates for NSCLC patients remain suboptimal, warranting the need to explore more diverse and effective drug targets to improve outcomes. Given the diversity of gene expression profiles both within and across primary and metastatic sites, single-cell transcriptomics offers a unique opportunity to identify novel and effective targets for drug development in NSCLC.

NSCLC frequently metastasizes to distant organs, particularly the brain and bones, which significantly worsens patient outcomes. Brain metastases occur in approximately 20–40% of NSCLC patients, often leading to neurological complications and reduced survival [3, 4]. Bone metastases affect 30–40% of patients with advanced NSCLC and cause skeletal-related complications such as fractures and severe pain, further diminishing quality of life [5]. These metastatic sites are particularly challenging to treat due to limited drug penetration, unique tumor microenvironments (TMEs), and complex tumor–stromal interactions. Moreover, the lack of effective targeted therapies tailored to metastasis-specific molecular profiles further complicates NSCLC treatment.

Current treatment options for advanced NSCLC include chemotherapy, targeted therapy (e.g., EGFR inhibitors such as osimertinib), immunotherapy (e.g., pembrolizumab), and palliative radiotherapy [6]. For brain metastases, treatment typically involves stereotactic radiosurgery combined with systemic therapies, whereas bone metastases are managed with bisphosphonates or denosumab alongside systemic treatment [7]. Despite these approaches, disease progression and recurrence remain common, particularly in metastatic settings, where tumor heterogeneity and adaptive resistance limit long-term efficacy. Additionally, some therapies are associated with considerable toxicity and may not be suitable for all patients. A major limitation in NSCLC management is the development of drug resistance, which can be intrinsic or acquired during treatment. For example, EGFR-targeted therapies often encounter resistance through secondary mutations (e.g., T790M) or activation of bypass signaling pathways [8]. Similarly, resistance to immunotherapy is increasingly observed, with tumors evading immune surveillance through alterations in the TME, reduced antigen presentation, or upregulation of immune checkpoints. These challenges underscore the need for broader therapeutic strategies and robust biomarkers to predict treatment response. The dynamic and adaptive nature of NSCLC progression further highlights the importance of precision medicine approaches that evolve alongside tumor molecular profiles.

Advances in bulk transcriptomic profiling and integrative multi-omics approaches combined with machine learning (ML) models have enabled the identification of broad-spectrum drug targets in several cancers [9], including lung cancer [10]. However, these approaches do not adequately account for intrinsic intratumoral heterogeneity, limiting the effectiveness of identified targets across all cancer cell populations. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful tool for drug target discovery by enabling high-resolution profiling of tumor heterogeneity at the individual cell level. It allows the identification of rare subpopulations, including drug-resistant clones, metastatic precursors, and tumor-infiltrating immune cells, which are often overlooked in bulk RNA analyses [11]. By capturing transcriptional variation at single-cell resolution, scRNA-seq facilitates the identification of cell-type-specific gene expression programs and signaling pathways, thereby providing a more comprehensive and diverse set of potential drug targets.

Experimental high-throughput screening (HTS) enables the testing of thousands of compounds in vitro with high accuracy but requires substantial time, infrastructure, and financial investment [12]. In contrast, structure-based virtual screening (SBVS) provides a more cost-effective alternative by leveraging molecular docking and protein–ligand interaction modeling. However, SBVS is still limited by the availability of high-resolution protein structures, despite recent advances in protein structure prediction [13]. Additionally, the high computational cost associated with modeling conformational flexibility remains a significant constraint.

Machine learning (ML) is increasingly transforming lung cancer therapeutics by enabling biomarker discovery, drug-response prediction, drug repurposing, and large-scale virtual screening using clinical, genomic, and chemical datasets. Recent studies have highlighted that a wide range of ML and deep learning approaches-including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), and variational autoencoders (VAEs) are being applied to improve therapeutic prioritization and computer-aided drug design in lung cancer [14]. Among these computational strategies, quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling has emerged as a key approach for predicting the biological activity of compounds based on their chemical structure. In early-stage drug discovery, QSAR plays a crucial role in predicting compound efficacy, facilitating hit-to-lead optimization, and reducing experimental burden. While traditional QSAR models rely on statistical correlations and may be constrained by linear assumptions and limited datasets, ML-based QSAR models overcome these limitations by capturing complex nonlinear relationships [15]. Algorithms such as Random Forest, Support Vector Machines, and XGBoost can effectively learn from large-scale chemical and bioactivity datasets, thereby accelerating virtual screening, lead optimization, and de novo drug design. These ML-based QSAR approaches combine scalability with predictive accuracy, enabling the rapid in silico evaluation of millions of compounds while reducing cost and minimizing experimental workload [16].

In this study, we aim to identify novel drug targets for NSCLC and its metastases through the integration of scRNA-seq analysis with ML-based QSAR-driven virtual screening. This combined strategy addresses key limitations in current therapeutic approaches, including drug resistance and metastatic progression, by enabling the identification of broadly actionable targets and potent candidate compounds effective across multiple tumor sites and TMEs (Fig. 1). Ultimately, this high-resolution integrative framework supports the rational design of targeted therapeutics for NSCLC by focusing on conserved molecular vulnerabilities shared between primary and metastatic disease, thereby paving the way for more effective and durable treatment strategies.

Methods

scRNA-seq data collection and integration

NSCLC single-cell transcriptomic datasets were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession numbers GSE131907 [17], GSE164789 [18], and GSE148071 [19]. Collectively, these datasets comprised 171 samples, including 50 normal and 121 tumor samples. The normal samples were anatomically distributed as follows: 32 from primary lung tissue, 11 from brain tissue, and 7 from bone tissue, whereas tumor samples included 74 from primary lung tumors, 32 from brain metastases, and 15 from bone metastases. Expression matrices and associated metadata were downloaded in processed format and used to annotate sample types and tissue origins. Standard preprocessing was performed using the Seurat R package [20].

Raw counts were merged by sample ID, followed by the computation of per-cell quality control (QC) metrics, including mitochondrial read fraction based on genes with the prefix ^MT-. Cells were retained if nCount_RNA > 800, nFeature_RNA > 500, and percent.mt < 20%. The mitochondrial read fraction threshold of 20% was selected to account for the inherent heterogeneity of tumor tissues, where metabolically active, stressed, or tumor-associated immune cells may exhibit elevated mitochondrial gene expression. While more stringent cutoffs (5–10%) are commonly applied in normal tissues, such thresholds in tumor datasets may exclude biologically relevant cell populations. To further minimize the inclusion of low-quality or apoptotic cells, additional QC filters based on gene and UMI counts were applied. Moreover, downstream clustering and marker gene analyses were evaluated to ensure that retained cells formed biologically meaningful and reproducible clusters, thereby supporting the robustness of the filtering strategy.

Following QC, counts were normalized to a library size of 10,000 UMIs per cell and log1p-transformed using Seurat’s NormalizeData (LogNormalize) function. Highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures (VST method; 2,000 features), and gene expression values were centered and scaled using ScaleData. The number of principal components (PCs) was determined using both the elbow plot and JackStraw analysis [21], and the first 10 PCs were selected for downstream clustering. Cells were clustered using the Seurat FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions at a resolution of 0.1, which was chosen to capture biologically interpretable major cell populations while avoiding over-fragmentation. To assess cluster stability, clustering was performed across a range of resolution parameters (0.1–0.6), and the consistency of cluster structure was evaluated. A shared nearest-neighbor graph was constructed using FindNeighbors, followed by clustering using the Louvain algorithm implemented in FindClusters. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was then applied for visualization using RunUMAP.

To integrate datasets from the three cohorts and mitigate batch effects, canonical correlation analysis (CCA)-based integration was performed using Seurat’s integration workflow. This approach aligns shared gene expression patterns across datasets, enabling robust clustering and reliable downstream biological interpretation.

Identification of highly expressed and conserved genes within and across site-specific tumors

To identify highly expressed genes conserved across primary lung tumors and metastatic sites, a systematic workflow was implemented using three comparative analyses: lung normal vs. lung tumor, brain normal vs. brain tumor, and bone normal vs. bone tumor. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the FindAllMarkers function in the Seurat package with parameters only.pos = TRUE, min.pct > 0.25, and logfc.threshold > 0.25. These thresholds were selected to retain genes with biologically meaningful upregulation while minimizing the inclusion of sparsely expressed or noise-driven features. Specifically, logfc.threshold > 0.25 captures moderate but functionally relevant expression changes, while min.pct > 0.25 ensures that genes are expressed in a sufficient proportion of cells. Restricting the analysis to only.pos = TRUE further enabled the identification of tumor-enriched genes.

To identify robust and reproducible markers across tumor subpopulations, the FindConservedMarkers function was applied across clusters from different tumor sites to extract genes consistently expressed across conditions. The final candidate gene set was then obtained by intersecting tumor-specific markers from FindAllMarkers with conserved markers from FindConservedMarkers, thereby retaining genes that are both significantly upregulated in tumors and consistently preserved across site-specific clusters. This approach yielded a robust set of tumor-enriched and conserved genes, highlighting their potential as shared therapeutic targets across primary and metastatic tumor sites.

Cell-type annotation

Cell-type annotation was performed using a combination of canonical marker genes and curated marker sets derived from published literature and single-cell atlases of lung cancer and related tissues [22–25]. Clusters identified through Seurat-based analysis were annotated based on the expression patterns of multiple lineage-specific markers to ensure accurate and biologically meaningful classification.

Major cell populations were defined using established canonical markers. Epithelial and malignant cells were identified by the expression of epithelial markers such as EPCAM, KRT8, KRT18, and KRT19 [22, 26]. Immune cell populations were annotated using lineage-specific markers, including CD3D and CD3E for T cells (with CD8A distinguishing CD8⁺ T cells), MS4A1 (CD20) for B cells, CD68 and CD163 for macrophages, FCGR3A (CD16) for monocytes, NKG7 and GNLY for NK cells, and LILRA4 for dendritic cells [23, 24, 27]. Stromal and structural cell types were identified using markers such as COL1A1 and DCN for fibroblasts, PECAM1 (CD31) and VWF for endothelial cells, and ACTA2 for mesenchymal cells [22, 26].

Tissue-specific cell types were further annotated using specialized markers. In lung samples, alveolar epithelial cells were identified using SFTPC (type II) and AGER (type I), while ciliated and mucous cells were annotated using FOXJ1 and MUC5AC, respectively [22, 26]. In brain metastasis samples, neural cell populations including astrocytes (GFAP), oligodendrocytes (MBP), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC; PDGFRA), and microglia (CX3CR1) were identified [25]. Bone marrow–associated populations, including hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs; CD34), were annotated based on established lineage markers [27].

Cluster identities were assigned based on concordant expression of multiple markers rather than single-gene assignment. In addition, marker gene expression was manually inspected across clusters to confirm consistency with known biological characteristics. Malignant cell populations were distinguished by epithelial identity combined with tumor-associated expression patterns observed in NSCLC datasets [22, 24]. To further ensure annotation accuracy, cell-type assignments were cross-referenced with previously published single-cell atlases of lung cancer and tumor microenvironment studies [22–25]. This combined approach of canonical marker-based annotation, literature support, and manual validation ensured robust and reproducible cell-type classification across primary and metastatic samples.

IPA biomarker filter and BioProfiler analysis

To derive a final list of candidate drug targets, we implemented a two-stage, integrated IPA workflow. First, we applied IPA Biomarker Filter to the set of conserved, highly expressed genes, enforcing the following eligibility criteria: human-specific expression, evidence of expression in cancer cell lines, association with cancer pathogenesis, and exclusion of genes already established as clinical diagnostic/prognostic biomarkers. Second, we submitted the Biomarker-eligible genes to IPA BioProfiler to aggregate functional and disease annotations and to prioritize candidates based on: (i) curated evidence for carcinoma or oncogenic processes, (ii) participation in cancer-relevant pathways, (iii) subcellular localization favoring druggability (e.g., membrane/secreted), and (iv) therapeutic precedent (targets or modulators reported in the literature). The final target set consists of genes that passed Biomarker Filter and ranked highest by BioProfiler. This integrated approach avoids redundancy by using Biomarker Filter for triage and BioProfiler for evidence-based prioritization.

Machine learning-based QSAR modeling

QSAR modeling is a computational approach used to establish statistical correlations between the chemical structures of compounds and their biological activities [15]. ML-based QSAR modeling enhances the predictive power of classical QSAR by capturing complex, nonlinear relationships in large datasets by identifying the hidden patterns and improve generalization through methods such as decision trees, support vector machines, and ensemble learners [15]. Experimentally validated ligands for the identified drug targets were retrieved from the ChEMBL database, a comprehensive bioactivity resource containing compound–target interaction data [28]. Bioactivity values (IC₅₀) were used to categorize compounds as Active (IC₅₀ < 1 µM), Moderately Active (1–10 µM), and Inactive (IC₅₀ > 10 µM) [28]. These categories were used to label compounds for supervised ML classification. The ML-based QSAR workflow was implemented using the KNIME Analytics Platform, an open-source modular environment for data integration, cheminformatics, and machine learning analysis [29].

Four machine learning algorithms were employed: Random Forest (RF) [30], Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) [31], Naive Bayes with molecular fingerprints (FP) [32], and Logistic Regression (LR) [33]. We used a 90:10 split for training and hold-out validation data. Within the 90% training set, we performed five-fold cross-validation using an internal 80:20 split (80% for model fitting and 20% for internal testing) in each fold, and then evaluated performance using metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, and the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC). We incorporated molecular descriptors such as Molecular Weight, LogP, TPSA, and the Morgan Fingerprints as features of compounds, which were generated using RDKit, a widely used cheminformatics toolkit [34], integrated within the KNIME workflow. The full list of molecular descriptors is provided in Supplementary Table S1.This ML-driven QSAR pipeline enabled high-throughput virtual screening of large chemical libraries, supporting the identification of hit compounds for the prioritized drug targets.

Ligand library preparation and ML-based virtual screening

To enable target-specific virtual screening, a structured ligand library was created for each of the drug targets using a ligand similarity-based strategy. Amino acid sequences for each target were first obtained from the UniProt database. To identify homologous protein structures co-crystallized with ligands, each sequence was submitted to PDBsum, which provides structural summaries of PDB entries and facilitates the identification of related protein-ligand complexes [35]. The resulting structures were filtered using stringent sequence criteria: sequence identity ≥ 90%, alignment coverage ≥ 80%, and BLAST bit score ≥ 1000 (E-value ≈ 0) to ensure close homologs with ligand-bound conformations.

For each target, ligand-bound homologous structures identified through PDBsum were used to extract reference compounds. These ligands, known to interact with structurally similar proteins were then used as queries to retrieve structurally similar analogs from multiple compound databases, including PubChem, DrugBank, and the Enamine REAL database [36, 37]. Structural similarity between reference ligands and candidate compounds was evaluated using the Tanimoto coefficient based on molecular fingerprints. A similarity cutoff of ≥ 0.7 was applied to identify structurally relevant compounds while maintaining chemical diversity. This approach ensured the compilation of a diverse, yet relevant ligand library enriched with bioactive compounds that are likely to bind with the target proteins.

The curated compound libraries were subsequently screened using the best-performing ML-based QSAR models for each target, as developed and validated in the preceding sections. The screening process involved predicting the bioactivity of each compound within corresponding target-specific libraries using the trained ML classifiers. Compounds predicted to be active were shortlisted for downstream molecular docking studies, substantially narrowing the pool of candidates for experimental validation.

This integrated strategy beginning with sequence-based structural similarity screening and culminating in ML-driven virtual screening enabled the rapid and targeted prioritization of drug-like molecules while ensuring alignment with the structural and functional contexts of the prioritized NSCLC primary and metastasis drug targets.

Protein structure retrieval for individual molecular docking analysis

Crystal structures of the three target proteins ARPC2 (PDB ID: 6UHC) [38], RAC2 (PDB ID: 1DS6) [39], and PSMB4 (PDB ID: 5LE5) [40] were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). These structures were selected based on their high-resolution quality and comprehensive coverage of key active and binding domains to ligands. The prepared structures were subsequently used in molecular docking studies to evaluate potential target-ligand interactions. Protein visualization and preparation were performed using Schrodinger’s Maestro suite, with non-protein atoms removed prior to generating docking grids.

Molecular docking

Following the ML-based virtual screening, the top 100 compounds for each drug target were selected based on model predictions and compound rankings. These compounds were then subjected to structure-based molecular docking to evaluate their binding affinities and interaction profiles with the respective targets. Docking was performed using the Schrodinger Maestro suite [41]. The crystal structures of the protein targets were obtained from the PDB and prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard in Maestro.Preparation steps for molecular docking included adding missing hydrogens, assigning correct bond orders, removing water molecules beyond 5 Å, and optimizing the hydrogen-bonding network. Receptor grids were generated around known binding sites using the Receptor Grid Generation module. Ligands were processed using the LigPrep module to generate low-energy 3D conformers and assign appropriate ionization states based on physiological pH (Schrodinger Release 2021-3: LigPrep).Docking was performed with the Glide module, employing Standard Precision (SP) mode for initial screening, followed by Extra Precision (XP) mode for refining the top-ranked ligands [42]. The resulting GlideScores were used to rank ligand-binding affinities, and binding poses were analyzed for interactions such as hydrogen bonds, π–π stacking, and hydrophobic contacts. This methodology enabled the identification of high-affinity ligands for each target and guided the selection of candidate molecules for subsequent MD validation.

Molecular dynamics simulation

To validate the stability of protein–ligand interactions and assess dynamic behavior under near-physiological conditions, MD simulations were performed for the top three ligand-bound complexes of each target (ARPC2, PSMB4, and RAC2) using the Schrodinger Desmond package [43]. Each complex was solvated in an orthorhombic box filled with TIP3P water molecules, and counterions were added to neutralize the system. The OPLS3e force field [44] was applied to accurately represent protein and ligand interactions. The systems underwent energy minimization to resolve steric clashes, followed by a multi-step equilibration protocol in the NPT ensemble, during which the temperature was gradually increased to 300 K and the pressure stabilized at 1 atm. Production MD simulations were conducted for 100 nano seconds (ns) with a 2 femtoseconds (fs) time step. Temperature and pressure were maintained using the Nose–Hoover thermostat and Martyna–Tobias–Klein barostat, respectively. The resulting trajectories were analyzed to compute root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and hydrogen bond interactions, providing insights into complex stability and the persistence of ligand binding over time.

Results

scRNA-seq data analysis from lung, brain, and bone sites reveal tissue-specific cellular profiles

Comparative UMAP analysis between normal and tumor samples across all three tumor sites (lung, bone and brain) revealed substantial differences in cellular organization, transcriptional heterogeneity, and cluster complexity. In lung tissue, normal samples showed 12 well-defined clusters, indicating transcriptionally stable and distinct cell populations (Fig. S1A). In contrast, tumor samples exhibited 13 clusters with increased overlap and an expanded cluster 0, suggesting the emergence of dominant tumor populations and enhanced heterogeneity (Fig. S1B). In bone tissue (Fig. S1C), normal samples displayed 10 discrete clusters, indicative of a structured and specialized microenvironment. Tumor samples, however, demonstrated a shift toward transcriptional complexity with 14 clusters, including several poorly resolved and overlapping populations (Fig. S1D). The enlargement of cluster 0 and the appearance of novel clusters point to tumor-specific subpopulations and possible stromal or immune infiltration.

In brain tissue, five distinct clusters were observed in the normal samples (Fig. S1E), reflecting well-preserved neural and glial identities. The brain tumor samples displayed eight clusters with blurred boundaries and an expanded cluster 0, consistent with malignant transformation, cellular reprogramming, and a more heterogeneous tumor ecosystem (Fig. S1F). To evaluate the robustness of clustering, we performed clustering across a range of resolution parameters (0.1–0.6) in the brain tumor dataset. As expected, increasing the resolution resulted in a higher number of clusters, reflecting progressive subdivision of larger clusters into finer subpopulations. However, the overall UMAP structure and major cluster organization remained consistent across all tested resolutions, indicating that the clustering results are stable and not driven by specific parameter choices (Fig. S2). Collectively, these findings underscore the profound reorganization of cellular architecture and gene expression states in tumors compared to their corresponding normal tissues. The emergence of additional clusters and the expansion of dominant tumor populations highlight increased cellular plasticity, microenvironmental complexity, and potential immune or stromal interactions within tumor settings.

Cell type annotation identifies differential cellular population in tumor samples

Comparative cell type annotation between normal and tumor tissues from the primary lung and metastasized bone and brain tumors revealed substantial alterations in cellular composition and tumor-induced remodeling across all sites. In the lung (Fig. 2A), normal tissue exhibited diverse and distinct immune and epithelial populations, including macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells, alveolar epithelial cells, and CD8⁺ T cells. In contrast, tumor samples (Fig. 2B) showed an expanded population of CD8⁺ T cells and cancer cells, along with the emergence of proliferating NK/T cells and epithelial cells. These changes suggest tumor-associated immune infiltration, epithelial proliferation, and the establishment of a transformed tumor niche.

In the bone (Fig. 2C), normal tissue displayed a broad range of hematopoietic and stromal populations such as hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs), dendritic cells, NK cells, and CD8⁺ T cells. The tumor samples (Fig. 2D) were dominated by cancer cells and exhibited a marked increase in NK cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and dendritic cells, along with greater cellular heterogeneity, reflecting tumor-associated immune modulation and microenvironmental changes.

On the other hand, the normal brain tissue (Fig. 2E) displayed distinct populations of neural and glial cell types, including astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, and microglia, while the tumor tissues (Fig. 2F) showed a dominant cancer cell population and an increased representation of endothelial and microglial cells, indicating neovascularization and neuroinflammatory responses. Across all tissues, tumor environments were characterized by an expanded cancer cell population, increased immune infiltration, and a loss of normal cellular organization, underscoring the distinctive and adaptive nature of the site-specific tumor microenvironment.

Identification of highly expressed conserved genes in tumor clusters

A comparative analysis of conserved marker genes across tumor clusters from lung, bone and brain samples revealed both tissue-specific and shared transcriptional signatures. Specifically, 42 highly expressed conserved genes were uniquely identified in lung tumor clusters, 92 in bone tumor clusters and 61 in brain tumor clusters. Notably, a set of 15 genes ARPC2, COX8A, ATP5F1E, GADD45GIP1, RPL36A, PSMB4, CALM2, ARPC1B, ATP5MG, RPS10, RAC2, HADHA, BANF1, MRPL33, and RNASEK were commonly expressed across all three tumor sites, suggesting a potential core transcriptional program associated with tumor progression and metastasis. Fig. S3 presents a Venn diagram illustrating the distribution and overlap of highly expressing and conserved genes among lung, bone and brain tumor clusters.

Prioritization of drug targets based on their therapeutic relevance

IPA analysis was carried out to prioritize the curated set of 15 genes identified above based on their therapeutic relevance. The IPA Biomarker Filter was applied using stringent criteria, including human-specific expression, relevance to cancer cell lines, and exclusion of genes already in clinical use as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers. This filtering step yielded a refined subset of eight candidate genes with potential novel biomarker utility including ARPC2, ATP5F1E, ATP5MG, CALM2, PSMB4, RAC2, RNASEK, and RPS10 (Table 1).

Each of the eight genes was further characterized using IPA BioProfiler module, which provided detailed annotations on molecular type, subcellular localization, and functional roles. While none of these genes currently have approved drugs, all are predominantly localized in the cytoplasm and represent a range of cellular functions including signaling (RAC2), peptidase (PSMB4), general enzymatic (ATP5MG, RNASEK), and ATPase complex subunit (ATP5F1E) activities. Network analysis results, visualized in Fig. S4, highlight the involvement of all eight genes in carcinoma-related pathways, with ARPC2, RAC2, and PSMB4 notably linked to cell viability, suggesting a direct role in tumor survival and proliferation. These findings collectively underscore the therapeutic relevance of these three genes and justify their prioritization as potential novel drug targets in NSCLC treatment strategies. A detailed summary of the stepwise filtering process, including the number of genes retained at each stage, is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Further analysis using UMAP-based feature plots revealed that ARPC2, RAC2, and PSMB4 are consistently and significantly overexpressed in tumor samples compared with their corresponding normal tissues across lung, bone and brain metastases (Fig. 3).Specifically, ARPC2 expression (Fig. 3B, D, F) demonstrates the most dominant and widespread signal, exhibiting a nearly uniform high expression across the major tumor-specific cell clusters in the lung, bone, and brain metastatic sites. RAC2 (Fig. 3B, D, F) also shows strong, high-intensity expression concentrated within the malignant cell clusters across all three tumor sites. PSMB4 (Fig. 3B, D, F), while present, displays a slightly more focused or heterogeneous expression pattern than ARPC2, but remains significantly elevated in tumor clusters compared to the minimal or absent signal in normal tissues (Fig. 3A, C, E). This elevated, conserved, and site-independent expression pattern in tumor-specific clusters powerfully reinforces the potential of these three genes as conserved therapeutic targets in metastatic cancer contexts. In addition, a quantitative summary of the number of cells exhibiting active expression of these genes across tumor clusters is provided in Supplementary Table S3, further supporting their widespread and consistent expression across metastatic sites.

Machine learning-based QSAR model development and validation for virtual screening

To identify the most effective ML algorithms for compound classification and virtual screening, we built separate QSAR models using ligand activity data from the ChEMBL database for the drug targets ARPC2, PSMB4, and RAC2. Four ML algorithms, Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Bayesian Fingerprint (FP), and XGBoost (XGB), were tested and compared for each target. Model construction, training, and evaluation were performed using the KNIME analytics platform. Model performance was assessed using standard metrics, including AUROC, accuracy, error rate, precision, and F-measure.

ARPC2 QSAR model performance

A total of 5175 compounds with IC50 activity labels were used to build ARPC2 models using each ML approach, with 4692 for training and 483 for validation. As described in the methodology, the training set was categorized into active (n = 1569), moderately active (n = 1506), and inactive (n = 1617) compounds. As shown in Fig. S5A, B, ROC analysis revealed that RF achieved the highest AUROC (0.973), followed closely by XGB (0.967). FP (0.945) and LR (0.889) showed comparatively lower classification performance. Supporting bar plots further highlight RF’s strong performance across all key metrics, particularly in terms of lowest error rate, high precision, and high F-measure. For validation (Fig. 5A, B), using 483 test compounds (180 active, 168 moderately active, 135 inactive), XGB yielded the highest AUROC (0.809), outperforming LR (0.806), RF (0.771), and FP (0.747). Based on its superior generalization performance, XGB was selected as the final model for QSAR-based virtual screening of ARPC2-targeting compounds.

PSMB4 QSAR model performance

For PSMB4, 2712 compounds were used, with 2442 for training and 270 for validation. The training set comprised active (n = 838), moderately active (n = 787), and inactive (n = 817) compounds. As shown in Fig. S5C, D, both RF (AUROC: 0.983) and XGB (AUROC: 0.979) exhibited excellent discrimination capability. LR (0.899) and FP (0.845) performed less effectively in comparison. RF consistently demonstrated the lowest error rate and high precision, confirming its robustness across multiple metrics. In the validation set (95 active, 76 moderately active, 99 inactive), depicted in Fig. 4C and D, RF achieved the best AUROC (0.933), followed by XGB (0.878), LR (0.817), and FP (0.830). Given its highest validation AUROC and comparable training performance, RF was selected as the final model for virtual screening of PSMB4-targeting compounds.

RAC2 QSAR model performance

For RAC2, 2313 compounds were used, with 2046 for training and 267 for validation. The training set was grouped into active (n = 622), moderately active (n = 692), and inactive (n = 732) classes. Fig. S5E, F shows that XGB achieved the best AUROC (0.978) among the four models, followed by LR (0.952). FP (0.924) and RF (0.891) demonstrated comparatively lower predictive performance. XGB also showed superior accuracy, minimal error rate, and high F-measure, confirming its overall strength. Validation on 267 compounds (110 active, 80 moderately active, 77 inactive) in Fig. 4E, F again showed XGB leading with an AUROC of 0.933, followed by RF (0.927), LR (0.835), and FP (0.795). Given its consistent and superior performance in both training and validation, XGB was selected as the final model for virtual screening of RAC2-targeting compounds.

Performance analysis for ARPC2, PSMB4, and RAC2

To better understand the contribution of individual activity classes (active, moderately active, and inactive) to overall model performance, class-specific performance was also measured for each drug target. For ARPC2 (Fig. S6A, B, C), the ROC curves revealed strong predictive ability across all three classes, with the active and inactive classes demonstrating particularly high AUROC values for decision tree-based models, especially XGB and RF. The moderately active class exhibited slightly lower sensitivity and specificity, suggesting a greater degree of overlap or ambiguity in feature representation for this class. Nonetheless, overall separation remained robust affirming the model’s ability to distinguish between nuanced activity levels. For PSMB4 (Fig. S6D, E, F), XGB and RF again showed excellent performance in predicting both active and inactive compounds, as evidenced by steep ROC curves and high true positive rates. The moderately active class displayed improved separation compared to ARPC2, likely reflecting more distinct chemical features within the PSMB4 dataset.

In the case of RAC2 (Fig. S6 G, H, I), the performance trend was consistent: both active and inactive classes were predicted with high precision, particularly by XGB and FP-based models. The moderately active class remained the most challenging to classify, with slightly lower AUROC values across all models, potentially due to its feature similarity to the other two classes. Overall, across all three targets, the active and inactive classes contributed most strongly to overall model accuracy, while the moderately active class presented the greatest classification challenge due to its intermediate activity profile. These findings underscore the importance of robust feature selection and optimized classification strategies when addressing multi-class QSAR datasets.

Virtual screening using ligand-based similarity and target-specific QSAR models

To enable drug target-specific virtual screening, a ligand similarity-based strategy was employed for ARPC2, PSMB4, and RAC2. For each target, experimentally solved protein structures co-crystallized with ligands were identified using PDBsum, applying stringent thresholds of high sequence identity (> 90%) and BLAST bit score (> 1000) to ensure structural and functional relevance (Table 2). The ARPC2 protein shared 99.3% identity with two homologous bovine structures (PDB IDs: 3ule and 3dxm), both co-crystallized with ligands CK-869 and CK0993548, which are known modulators of actin polymerization. These ligands served as reference molecules to query the public PubChem and DrugBank repositories, and the commercial Enamine REAL database for structurally similar analogs, resulting in a chemically diverse yet biologically relevant ligand library tailored for ARPC2. For PSMB4, two high-resolution human proteasome structures (PDB IDs: 4r67 and 5m32) with 99.1% sequence identity and BLAST bit score of 1345.2 and 1337.8, respectively, were co-crystallized with the clinically relevant inhibitors Carfilzomib and Oprozomib, which were used as reference ligands to retrieve analogs from public and commercial repositories. Similarly, the RAC2 target aligned with three highly similar human structures (PDB IDs: 5qqj, 5qqd, and 5qqe), each with 95.5% sequence identity and BLAST bit score of 1533.2, co-crystallized with ligands such as Molport-009-587-558, Z56880342, and Molport-009-531-494, which were used to retrieve a focused set of analogs predicted to bind RAC2.

The retrieved analog libraries for each target were screened using their corresponding best-performing ML-based QSAR models, and only compounds predicted as active were selected for downstream molecular docking, thereby enriching the candidate pool with high-priority molecules for experimental validation. For ARPC2, a total of 15.77 million compounds were compiled, including 18,445 from PubChem, 4556 from DrugBank, and 15.75 million from Enamine REAL. For PSMB4, the curated library consisted of 11.36 million compounds, comprising 16,789 from PubChem, 3789 from DrugBank, and 11.34 million from Enamine REAL. For RAC2, it included 9.58 million compounds with 12,468 from PubChem, 3456 from DrugBank, and 9.56 million from Enamine REAL. This integrated ligand structure-based and ML-based screening pipeline efficiently filtered drug-like molecules from billions to a few millions with high predicted bioactivity for each target, ensuring that the selected candidates were not only structurally aligned with experimentally solved ligand-bound conformations but also computationally predicted to exhibit functional activity.

Molecular docking on selected targets

Molecular docking was performed for the top 100 ML-ranked compounds targeting ARPC2, PSMB4, and RAC2 using the Schrodinger Maestro suite. The most promising ligands were prioritized based on their XP GlideScores and interaction profiles, with particular emphasis on hydrogen bonding with key residues within the active site (Table 3). For each target, a ranked list of ligands was created based on the combination of their binding energy and interaction profile and the top three candidate compounds were selected. For ARPC2 (Fig. 5A, B, C), compounds 14465616, 102344121, and 90662448 exhibited strong binding affinities, with XP GlideScores of − 6.032, − 5.378, and − 5.643 kcal/mol, respectively. Each ligand formed two hydrogen bonds with the ARPC2 binding site, involving key residues such as ARG9, ARG117, ARG118, and HIS245, which likely contribute to ligand stabilization within the active site. For PSMB4 (Fig. 5D, E, F), compounds, 74833722, 144719098, and 144719335 were identified as high-affinity binders with GlideScores of − 6.57, − 6.44, and − 6.57 kcal/mol, respectively. Notably, compound 144719098 formed seven hydrogen bonds, suggesting a highly stable binding conformation. Critical interacting residues included ASP70, SER74, ARG86, GLY100, TYR102, TRP186, ASP225, and ARG227, all of which play essential roles in ligand anchoring.

Molecular dynamics simulation on selected target-ligand complexes

To evaluate the structural stability and binding dynamics of the protein-ligand complexes, MD simulations were performed for 100 ns. The most promising candidates for each target was identified based on the analysis of RMSD, RMSF, and protein-ligand interactions as follows.

MD simulation of ARPC2–ligand complexes identifies 14465616 as the most stable candidate

MD simulations of the three ARPC2–ligand complexes over 100 ns revealed distinct stability, flexibility, and binding interaction patterns. Complex A (ARPC2–14465616) demonstrated the highest overall stability, with the protein RMSD stabilizing between 2.5 and 4.0 Å after the initial 20 ns and the ligand RMSD maintained within 6–11 Å (Fig. 6A), indicating a stable yet adaptable binding pose. RMSF analysis (Fig. S7A) confirmed minimal residue-level fluctuations across the protein, with only a few loop regions showing moderate flexibility. Interaction fraction analysis (Fig. 7A) highlighted strong and persistent hydrogen bonds with key residues such as LYS106, VAL110 and HIS111, which were further corroborated by the contact heatmap (Fig. 8A) showing consistent, long-lived contacts throughout the simulation. Complex B (ARPC2–102344121) showed moderate stability, with protein RMSD values consistently between 2.0 and 3.0 Å but ligand RMSD significantly higher (15–20 Å) (Fig. 6B), suggestive of possible conformational rearrangement or partial unbinding. RMSF analysis (Fig. S7B) revealed increased flexibility in several loop regions, aligning with its lower interaction stability. Interaction fraction plots (Fig. 7B) and the corresponding contact heatmap (Fig. 8B) indicated less frequent and more transient interactions compared to Complex A. Complex C (ARPC2–906624480) was the least stable, with protein RMSD increasing to 2.5–4.5 Å and ligand RMSD sharply spiking beyond 50 Å after ~ 60 ns (Fig. 6C), indicating a likely unbinding event. RMSF analysis (Fig. S7C) displayed marked fluctuations, particularly in terminal regions, reflecting global structural instability. Interaction fraction data (Fig. 7C) and contact heatmaps (Fig. 8C) showed a substantial loss of key contacts after the dissociation point. Based on the combined RMSD, RMSF, and interaction analyses, ARPC2–14465616 (Complex A) emerged as the most stable binder, exhibiting low protein and ligand RMSD values, limited residue flexibility, and strong, persistent interactions with catalytically relevant residues.

MD simulation of PSMB4-ligand complexes identifies 74833722 as the most stable candidate

The MD simulations for the PSMB4–ligand complexes demonstrated distinct stability, flexibility, and interaction profiles. Complex A (PSMB4–74833722) was the most stable, with the protein RMSD stabilizing below 2.5 Å after 20 ns and the ligand RMSD remaining consistently low (6–7 Å) throughout the 100 ns simulation (Fig. S8A). RMSF analysis (Fig. S9A) revealed minimal fluctuations across most residues, with moderate flexibility observed in selected loop regions, indicating a compact and stable binding conformation. This high stability was attributed to sustained interactions with key residues, particularly ASP70, SER74, ARG86, and TYR102, as shown in the interaction fraction analysis (Fig. S10A) and contact heatmap (Fig. S11A). Complex C (PSMB4–144719335) exhibited intermediate stability, with protein RMSD staying under 3.0 Å and ligand RMSD moderate at 8–9 Å (Fig. S8C). RMSF analysis (Fig. S9C) indicated localized flexibility in specific loop and terminal regions, consistent with a moderately stable binding mode. The interaction fraction profile (Fig. S10C) and contact heatmap (Fig. S11C) demonstrated sustained hydrogen bonding with SER77, ARG86, and ARG227, though at a lower frequency compared to Complex A. Complex B (PSMB4–144719098) displayed the least stability, showing higher protein RMSD fluctuations (2.5–4.0 Å) and a ligand RMSD peak of around 13 Å (Fig. S8B), suggesting conformational changes or partial displacement. RMSF analysis (Fig. S9B) revealed greater flexibility in multiple loop regions and the C-terminal domain, consistent with reduced stability. Interaction fraction analysis (Fig. S10B) and contact heatmap (Fig. S11B) showed scattered and inconsistent interactions, mainly involving GLU62, LEU205, GLN207, and ALA210. Overall, PSMB4-74833722 (Complex A) is identified as the most stable and promising candidate, combining low RMSD values, limited residue flexibility, and frequent, stable interactions with key active-site residues.

MD simulation of RAC2–ligand complexes identify 57175325 as the most stable candidate

Analysis of the RAC2-ligand complexes over a 100 ns MD simulation revealed varying levels of stability, flexibility, and binding persistence. Complex A (RAC2–45099136) showed a very stable protein backbone RMSD (1.2–1.8 Å) with moderate ligand RMSD fluctuations (2–5 Å) (Fig. S12A), indicating firm binding. RMSF analysis (Fig. S13A) demonstrated low overall residue flexibility, with moderate fluctuations observed in loop regions, consistent with stable complex formation. Interaction fraction analysis (Fig. S14A) revealed persistent contacts with residues such as GLU116, ASP118, SER158, ALA159, and LEU160, which were also evident in the contact heatmap (Fig. S15A). Complex B (RAC2–57175325) emerged as the most stable binder, with minimal protein RMSD variation (1.8–2.4 Å) and the most consistent ligand RMSD (1.5–2.5 Å) across the simulation period (Fig. S12B), reflecting excellent retention and structural stability. RMSF analysis (Fig. S13B) showed reduced flexibility across most residues, suggesting a rigid and stable complex. Interaction fraction results (Fig. S14B) and contact heatmap data (Fig. S15B) confirmed robust, long-lived interactions with key residues, including LYS116, ALA159, and LEU160.

Complex C (RAC2-22768154) was the least stable. While the protein RMSD remained moderately stable (1.5–2.0 Å), the ligand RMSD showed a sharp increase (~ 50 Å) after ~ 70 ns (Fig. S12C), strongly suggesting a dissociation event. RMSF analysis (Fig. S13C) revealed increased flexibility in multiple loop and terminal regions, consistent with complex destabilization. Interaction fraction plots (Fig. S14C) and the contact heatmap (Fig. S15C) further confirmed a marked loss of critical contacts after the dissociation point. Based on the combined RMSD, RMSF, and interaction analyses, RAC2–57175325 (Complex B) is identified as the most stable and promising compound for further development.

Discussion

This study introduces an integrated strategy for identifying and prioritizing drug targets and lead compounds in NSCLC and its metastases. By combining high-resolution scRNA-seq with a robust ML-driven QSAR modeling pipeline, we identified ARPC2, PSMB4, and RAC2 as promising therapeutic targets and subsequently prioritized high-affinity compounds through molecular docking and MD simulations. This pipeline, which integrates ligand-structure similarity searches with ML-driven QSAR models, enabled the screening of ultra-large billion-compound libraries and the identification of a handful of top candidate drugs for each target. Our scRNA-seq analysis revealed significant transcriptional heterogeneity and increased cellular complexity within tumor tissues from lung, bone, and brain compared to normal tissues. This finding aligns with the established understanding of cellular plasticity and TME reprogramming in cancer progression and metastasis [11, 45]. Critically, cell-type annotation further confirmed the enrichment of immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic cell populations, such as proliferating NK/T cells and endothelial cells, within tumors, underscoring the profound influence of tumor–immune interactions in shaping metastatic niches.

In this context, our findings can be viewed within the broader framework of personalized oncology, where transcriptomic analyses increasingly complement genomic profiling for customized drug discovery in lung cancer. Ahmad et al. [46] highlighted that machine learning methods are widely used to analyze genomic data for molecular stratification, therapeutic prioritization, and drug discovery in precision lung cancer care, while also noting ongoing challenges related to data availability, interpretability, and clinical validation. Within this framework, our scRNA-seq-based approach adds functional resolution to personalized oncology by identifying conserved, metastasis-relevant targets across heterogeneous tumor sites, thereby providing candidate vulnerabilities that can be integrated with genomic profiling to guide customized therapeutic development.

Building on this, through systematic intersection analysis of highly expressed genes conserved across tumor clusters from all three metastatic sites, we identified 15 candidate genes for therapeutic targeting. From a pharmacological perspective, prioritizing highly expressed genes maximizes the likelihood of achieving a therapeutically relevant drug concentration, as target proteins are abundant and therefore highly accessible for small-molecule binding. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) further prioritized eight of these (ARPC2, ATP5F1E, ATP5MG, CALM2, PSMB4, RAC2, RNASEK, and RPS10) based on their BioProfiler ranking and functional relevance to cancer. Among this subset, ARPC2, RAC2, and PSMB4 emerged as top candidates due to their strong functional relevance to carcinoma networks, favorable subcellular localization, and high perceived druggability. These genes are implicated in diverse cancer-related processes, including ARPC2 in actin cytoskeleton remodeling and metastasis [47], RAC2 in reactive oxygen species generation and immune signaling [48], and PSMB4 in proteasome-mediated protein degradation and tumor survival [49].

From a therapeutic perspective, ARPC2, RAC2, and PSMB4 represent functionally distinct yet oncogenically convergent pathways critical for cancer progression and survival. ARPC2, a core component of the Arp2/3 complex, regulates actin cytoskeleton dynamics and cell migration (KEGG hsa04810, hsa04510). Its elevated expression promotes invasive motility and metastatic dissemination in various cancers, including lung cancer, by facilitating actin nucleation and cell polarization [50–51]. Hence, targeting ARPC2 offers a promising strategy to inhibit cytoskeletal plasticity and metastatic spread. RAC2, a Rho GTPase predominantly expressed in hematopoietic cells, modulates NADPH oxidase–mediated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, actin dynamics, and immune cell migration (KEGG hsa04062, hsa04145, hsa00190). In cancer, RAC2 contributes to tumor progression by influencing redox balance and immunosuppressive signaling within the TME [48, 52]. Its dual role in cytoskeletal regulation and redox signaling makes it an attractive target for disrupting both tumor-intrinsic and immune-related pathways. Similarly, PSMB4, a catalytic subunit of the 20 S proteasome, is central to ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation (KEGG hsa03050) and indirectly affects pathways such as p53 signaling and cell cycle regulation. Frequent upregulation of PSMB4 in aggressive tumors, such as glioblastoma and NSCLC, enhances proteolytic capacity and confers resistance to apoptosis [53–55]. Its role in maintaining proteostasis under stress conditions makes it a compelling target, particularly for tumors resistant to conventional proteasome inhibitors. Collectively, these targets span a broad mechanistic landscape, cell motility (ARPC2), oxidative and immune regulation (RAC2), and protein homeostasis (PSMB4) highlighting the potential for combinatorial targeting strategies in metastatic NSCLC.

The successful application of ML-based QSAR models for predicting compound activity against ARPC2, RAC2, and PSMB4 underscores the transformative potential of artificial intelligence in early-stage drug discovery. Our approach leveraged ChEMBL, a high-quality, open-access repository of experimentally validated compound target interactions [28]. The standardized bioactivity metrics within ChEMBL were crucial for accurate class labeling (active, moderately active, inactive) and for training robust, generalizable supervised learning models capable of discriminating ligands based on potency. Among the evaluated ML algorithms, XGBoost demonstrated superior predictive performance for ARPC2 and RAC2, achieving AUROC values exceeding 0.95 on both training and validation sets. XGBoost’s suitability for cheminformatics stems from its ability to handle high-dimensional, sparse molecular fingerprints and imbalanced datasets. Its gradient-boosted decision trees, coupled with regularization and early stopping, effectively capture complex, nonlinear relationships while mitigating overfitting, making it ideal for virtual screening. Random Forest, another ensemble method, also performed strongly, particularly for PSMB4, where it outperformed other algorithms during validation. Its robustness to noise and overfitting, along with its inherent feature importance scoring, aids in interpretability, which is valuable for classifying moderately active compounds that often exhibit ambiguous structural features. QSAR models were developed using a random 90:10 train–test split with five-fold cross-validation within the training set. Molecular descriptors and fingerprints were generated using RDKit within the KNIME workflow, enabling the capture of detailed structural features for model training. While this randomized strategy supports learning of general chemical patterns, scaffold-based splitting or structural clustering was not explicitly implemented. Future work will incorporate these approaches to further assess model generalizability and reduce potential bias from structurally similar compounds.

Class-wise ROC analysis revealed that predictions for “active” and “inactive” compounds were highly reliable, while “moderately active” compounds were more challenging to distinguish. This limitation highlights the need for continued advancements in feature engineering, balanced dataset design, and possibly the integration of regression-based approaches to complement classification models in future work. Nevertheless, these results validate ChEMBL as a high-quality dataset and demonstrate the complementary strengths of XGBoost and Random Forest in QSAR modeling pipelines, enabling efficient and scalable virtual screening for accurate and biologically relevant hit compound prioritization.

The best-performing ML-based QSAR models (XGBoost for ARPC2 and RAC2, Random Forest for PSMB4) were subsequently used to screen millions of ligands from Enamine, DrugBank, and PubChem databases. This led to the identification of top candidate compounds that are structurally similar to the ligands and co-crystallized with PDB structures similar to the targets. Structure-based molecular docking confirmed potent ligands targeting ARPC2, PSMB4, and RAC2, demonstrating strong binding affinities and stable protein-ligand complexes, as indicated by favorable XP GlideScores and robust hydrogen bonding networks. However, recent advances in cheminformatics highlight the value of ensemble and deep ensemble learning approaches for improving predictive robustness and reducing model-specific biases in drug discovery. Recent study demonstrated that deep ensemble frameworks can enhance the prediction of anti-lung cancer compound activity by integrating multiple models and improving generalization [56]. In this context, while our study employed high-performing individual models such as XGBoost and Random Forest, incorporating ensemble learning strategies could help further improve prediction accuracy and scalability in QSAR-based virtual screening.

Specifically, the compounds that docked within the ARPC2 binding pocket (14465616, 102344121, and 906624480) formed stable interactions with critical residues (ARG9, ARG117, HIS245). These residues are essential for Arp2/3 complex activation and actin filament branching [47] and ligand binding at these sites is predicted to disrupt ARPC2/3-mediated cytoskeletal reorganization, suppressing cancer cell motility and invasion. For PSMB4, high-affinity ligands (74833722, 144719098, and 144719335) extensively interacted with its active site, forming multiple hydrogen bonds with key residues (ASP70, SER74, ARG86, GLY100, TYR102, TRP186, ASP225, ARG227). These residues are involved in substrate recognition and peptide bond cleavage and their binding to ligands likely blocks access to the catalytic core, impeding proteasomal degradation and inducing proteotoxic stress, ultimately triggering apoptosis. Top RAC2 compounds (45099136, 57175325, 22768154) were bound within its pocket, forming hydrogen bonds with several residues (GLY15, CYS18, LYS116, ASP118, CYS157, ALA159, LEU160) near the nucleotide-binding and effector interaction regions. This engagement could hinder RAC2 activation, disrupting ROS signaling and reducing tumor proliferation and immune evasion.

Further validation through MD simulations substantiated the stability and persistence of interactions for the top candidate ligands for each target. Ligands 14465616 (ARPC2), 74833722 (PSMB4), and 57175325 (RAC2) emerged as the most stable binders, maintaining strong and specific interactions with functionally critical residues over 100 ns simulations. For the ARPC2–14465616 complex, minimal protein RMSD drift (~ 2.5–4.0 Å) and stable ligand RMSD suggested moderate ligand flexibility with overall stable engagement. Persistent hydrogen bonds with ASP70, SER74, and TYR75 residues in the α-helical domain, essential for protein–protein interactions within the Arp2/3 complex, suggest disruption of actin network polymerization, thereby inhibiting metastatic processes [57]. Similarly, the PSMB4–74833722 complex maintained low protein RMSD (< 2.5 Å) and consistent ligand RMSD (~ 6–7 Å), with persistent hydrogen bonding to ASP70, SER74, ARG86, TYR102, and ARG227. These residues, located in or adjacent to the substrate recognition and catalytic β-ring cleft, are key to proteolysis, and their inhibition can trigger proteotoxic stress and apoptosis. Finally, the RAC2–57175325 complex showed minimal protein (~ 1.8–2.4 Å) and ligand (~ 1.5–2.5 Å) RMSDs, with consistent interactions at LYS116, ALA159, and LEU160 residues critical for GTP binding and effector interactions, potentially impairing RAC2 signaling and reducing tumor invasiveness. Across all three targets, the identified lead compounds demonstrated sustained interactions with residues essential for protein function, supported by RMSD, RMSF, interaction fraction analysis, and contact heatmaps. While MD simulations provided valuable insights into structural stability and interaction dynamics of protein–ligand complexes, the current analysis was primarily based on RMSD, RMSF, and interaction profiles, which are qualitative and do not directly quantify binding affinity. This persistent engagement indicates strong potential for functional inhibition and provides a rational basis for further experimental validation and lead optimization. Binding free energy methods such as MM-GBSA or MM-PBSA can provide more quantitative estimates; however, these approaches are sensitive to conformational sampling and are typically most reliable when applied to a smaller set of highly optimized candidates. Therefore, incorporating free energy calculations will be an important future step to further refine and rank the identified lead compounds. Future work will extend our single-target docking approach to multi-target structural evaluation, as multi-pathway inhibition is critical for overcoming NSCLC drug resistance. Multisampling-based strategies have shown promise in identifying multitarget inhibitors in lung cancer [58] and could enhance the robustness of our pipeline.

Conclusions

In this study, we present a robust and integrated computational pipeline combining single-cell transcriptomics with ML-based QSAR modeling to identify and prioritize novel drug targets for NSCLC across its primary and common metastatic sites. Through high-resolution scRNA-seq of tumor and normal tissues, we precisely characterized distinct transcriptional alterations and tumor-specific cell populations, including the enrichment of immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic cells, highlighting the complex tumor ecosystem. From this analysis, ARPC2, PSMB4, and RAC2 emerged as high-confidence, druggable targets due to their consistent overexpression in all sub-populations of tumors and their critical roles in cancer-related pathways. Leveraging curated bioactivity data from ChEMBL, we developed and rigorously validated highly predictive ML-based QSAR models (XGB and RF) for each target. In combination with the ligand structure-similarity searching approach, these target-specific QSAR models allowed us to efficiently virtual screen billions of compounds, leading to the identification of 100 top-ranking ligands per target. Subsequent structure-based molecular docking and MD simulations further substantiated the strong binding affinities and remarkable stability of these lead compounds, particularly for target-ligand complexes, ARPC2-14465616, PSMB4-74833722, and RAC2-57175325. Our findings establish a powerful and scalable framework for precision drug discovery for significantly accelerating the identification of tumor-specific and metastasis-relevant therapeutic candidates. This integrative methodology not only enhances target prioritization within heterogeneous tumor environments but also streamlines hit identification, providing a strong foundation for downstream experimental validation of candidate drug compounds. Our approach is highly suitable for the development of more effective and personalized therapies not only for advanced NSCLC but also for other cancers.
