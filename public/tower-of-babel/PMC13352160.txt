AI-based tumor cellularity assessment in digital pathology: A review of methods, datasets, and clinical translation
Authors: Mehaboobathunnisa Sahul Hameed, Muhammad Kumail, Carole Dagher, Manal Abdulrahim, Massimo Pignatelli, Yacine Hadjiat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor cellularity (TC) is a key histopathological measure that impacts the quality of molecular testing and the choice of treatment. Estimation of TC is usually manual, by visual assessment by pathologists, which makes it prone to inter-observer variability. Advancements in the application of artificial intelligence (AI) in digital pathology facilitate TC evaluation and scale up existing pathology workflows. In practical terms, these AI systems analyze digitized tissue slides computationally, producing objective TC scores that can support pathologists in determining whether a specimen contains sufficient tumor material for molecular testing. This review presents a compilation of AI-driven methodologies for TC estimation by summarizing publicly available datasets and benchmarking resources, examining methodological advancements in automated TC assessment, and delineating the research and commercial tools accessible for TC quantification. It also discusses the challenges that hinder clinical translation and the necessity for transparent and interpretable outputs that align with the pathologists' reasoning.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Automated tumor cellularity assessment, Digital pathology, Tumor cellularity, Tumor scoring

Introduction

Oncology is one of the most focused and emerging research domains in artificial intelligence (AI), due to the complexity and heterogeneity of cancer types. The ability of AI to process and interpret large amounts of biomedical data has led to significant advancements in cancer care. Numerous AI algorithms have shown promise in early detection and diagnosis of cancer, which is more crucial for timely intervention.1 A critical component of cancer diagnosis and treatment planning is the assessment of tumor cellularity (TC), in which pathologists manually examine stained histopathology slides to determine the proportion of tumor cells in the sample. TC is the ratio of malignant cells in a tissue sample compared to the total number of cells in that sample. It is an important pathological test that determines the quality of molecular testing. Higher TC usually means that there is enough DNA for accurate genomic analysis. This method is often subjective, time-consuming, and prone to bias and inter-observer variability, which can lead to inconsistent diagnosis and treatment planning. This highlights the necessity for more objective and reproducible methodologies.2

The incorporation of AI into the pathology workflow has demonstrated the potential to transform every stage of cancer treatment, from screening to drug discovery.1 It also offers unprecedented opportunities to improve treatment accuracy and customize patient care.3, 4 AI-driven methodologies for TC assessment have the potential to address challenges posed by conventional methods, as they involve advanced image analysis and pattern recognition capabilities. This transition toward AI-enhanced evaluation not only reduces subjectivity but also automates the process, thereby reducing assessment turnaround time.

Deep learning (DL) methods, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have shown significant potential to automatically learn features from large datasets and perform exceptionally in downstream histopathology tasks such as tumor detection, segmentation, and classification, which are crucial for more accurate TC estimation. Although several studies have evaluated the efficiency of AI-based approaches in computational pathology applications, this review primarily focuses on the assessment of the TC score. We provide a structured categorization of datasets based on annotation type, emphasize the trade-offs across methodological paradigms, and highlight key issues related to generalization and clinical translation. Thus, this review aims to bridge the gap between algorithmic development and real-world pathology workflow by integrating technical advances with practical deployment considerations.

Clinical background of tumor cellularity

TC is a measure of the number of invasive tumor cells in the collected sample tissues. The traditional method of evaluating TC involves a sequence of steps, starting from preparing the tissues for the assessment to the pathologists calculating the overall cellularity score for the entire sample. The collected tissue samples are processed into multiple slides, stained, and the pathologists examine them, focusing on the tumor bed. This method of visual estimation is referred to as eyeballing, and the evaluation of malignant cells relative to non-tumor cells is semi-quantitative.5 The pathologists examine multiple fields across several slides under the microscope, and then calculate the overall cellularity score for the entire sample.

The conventional assessment is recognized to have several limitations. It is highly subjective, depending on individual pathologists' interpretations, thereby leading to high inter- and intra-observer variability. An accurate assessment requires examining multiple fields, intensifying the labor and time required for visual estimation. The manual method is less precise as the pathologists rely on predefined discrete values to estimate the TC score. These limitations can be addressed by employing modern digital pathology and AI methods, which have been shown to be more accurate, reproducible, and efficient for quantifying TC.

AI-based approaches for tumor cellularity assessment

This review synthesizes key studies on AI-based TC assessment in digital histopathology, focusing on publications from 2015 to 2025 identified through targeted searches across PubMed, Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, arXiv, and major conference proceedings (MICCAI, SPIE Medical Imaging). Search terms included combinations of “tumor cellularity,” “tumor content,” “automated tumor scoring” with “artificial intelligence,” “deep learning,” “digital pathology,” and “whole slide image”. This review follows a structured narrative approach rather than a formal systematic review. Studies focusing on AI-based TC estimation from histopathology images and related approaches estimating TC are included in the review. Selection prioritized influential works establishing methodological foundations, recent advances, and clinical translation evidence. The review encompasses public resource datasets, AI methodologies and emerging AI platforms for TC evaluation, providing a comprehensive synthesis of technical advances alongside challenges in clinical translation.

The article is organized into thematic sections reflecting automated TC scoring evolution: Datasets are categorized according to explicit TC annotations or indirect TC estimation approaches; AI methodologies trace progression spanning standard ML techniques, DL techniques such as CNNs, attention/transformers-based models and ensemble models; Performance metrics catalog evaluation standards; and Clinical translation contrasts research prototypes with deployed solutions.

Datasets

TC has emerged as a critical quantitative biomarker, contributing directly to residual cancer burden (RCB) calculations and downstream prognostication. However, large, publicly available histopathology datasets with explicit TC labels remain limited. Most digital pathology datasets are annotated to provide tumor segmentation, grading, or diagnostic labels, from which indirect estimates of tumor burden can only be derived through tumor area measurement or counting malignant cells. To provide a clear distinction, this survey distinguishes between (i) datasets with native TC annotations, (ii) datasets that support indirect TC estimation, and (iii) large-scale multi-modal repositories primarily used for representation learning and morphology–omics correlations.

Datasets with explicit tumor cellularity labels

The most directly relevant resources for AI-based TC assessment are post-neoadjuvant breast cancer cohorts, which include continuous or categorical estimates of TC in the tumor bed. These datasets form the core benchmarks for developing and validating automated cellularity models.

•BreastPathQ (SPIE-AAPM-NCI Cancer Cellularity Challenge): The BreastPathQ challenge dataset6 consists of 2395 image patches extracted from whole-slide images (WSIs) of post-neoadjuvant breast surgical specimens from 64 patients. Each patch is annotated by expert breast pathologists with a continuous cancer cellularity score (0%–100%). Numerous studies have used BreastPathQ to benchmark CNNs, segmentation-based pipelines, and end-to-end regression models for automated TC scoring.

•Post-NAT-BRCA (TCIA) and related Sunnybrook post-neoadjuvant cohorts: The Post-NAT-BRCA collection on The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA)7 contains digitized H&E-stained histological sections from breast resections in patients with residual invasive breast cancer following neoadjuvant systemic therapy. This dataset provides expert pathology annotations of TC in the tumor bed and cell-type labels, making it directly suitable for WSI- and region-level TC estimation and RCB component modeling. Earlier institutional cohorts from the same group (e.g., 69 WSIs from 37 post-NAT patients) have been used in foundational work on manual vs automated TC quantification and digital RCB computation.

Datasets supporting indirect TC estimation

Several challenge and benchmark datasets focus on tumor segmentation, viable tumor burden, or related quantities that can be used to derive TC or overall tumor burden, even though explicit cellularity scores are not always provided.

•PAIP (Pathology AI Platform) liver and colorectal challenges: The PAIP 2019 liver cancer segmentation challenge9 provides WSIs from hepatocellular carcinoma resections with detailed tumor region annotations and a secondary task involving viable tumor burden estimation. Subsequent PAIP challenges, including PAIP 2020 for colorectal cancer,10 extended these approaches to molecular subtype prediction and characterization of tumor heterogeneity, thereby enabling indirect estimation of tumor burden through segmented tumor regions and tissue composition. Although these datasets do not provide explicit TC percentage annotations, they remain highly relevant for AI pipelines that infer TC or viable tumor burden from segmentation-based analysis.

•Other segmentation and nuclei datasets: Multi-organ nuclear segmentation datasets such as MoNuSeg11 and colorectal-focused resources like CoNSeP12 and PanNuke13 provide detailed instance-level annotations of nuclei across malignant and non-malignant compartments. These resources support “bottom-up” approaches in which TC is approximated through counts or densities of malignant cells relative to benign or stromal nuclei, rather than direct pathologist-assigned cellularity scores. Although not designed specifically for RCB workflows, they are crucial for studying how well cell-centric models can reconstruct TC in comparison with pathologist estimates.

Large-scale multi-modal and pretraining datasets

Large repositories such as TCGA and CPTAC provide extensive WSI collections linked to genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data, but typically lack explicit TC annotations on the slides. They are nonetheless essential in the broader AI landscape for TC estimation.

•The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA): TCGA14 includes H&E-stained WSIs from multiple tumor types—breast, lung, colorectal, ovarian, kidney, prostate, and others scanned at high resolution and paired with rich molecular and clinical data. TCGA has been widely used to train and evaluate models that correlate tissue morphology with genetic alterations, molecular subtypes, and outcomes; in the context of TC, it is most often used for pretraining feature extractors or for weakly supervised modeling of tumor burden via surrogate labels (e.g., progression-free survival, molecular profiles) rather than as a direct cellularity benchmark.

•Clinical proteomic tumor analysis consortium (CPTAC): CPTAC15, 16 combines WSIs with in-depth proteomic and genomic profiling for cancers such as breast, lung, ovarian, renal, and endometrial tumors. Like TCGA, CPTAC facilitates multimodal research in which AI models link image-derived tumor features, including inferred tumor fraction or cellular density, to molecular signatures and therapeutic targets. Although explicit TC annotations are not routinely provided, CPTAC datasets are important for exploring how cellularity-related image features interact with proteomic and genomic readouts.

Together, these resources provide a layered ecosystem: a small number of datasets with true TC annotations for direct benchmarking, surrounded by larger segmentation and multi-modal datasets that can be used to derive indirect estimates of TC, pretrain models, and test generalization across tissue types and clinical settings.

Current TC datasets with explicit cellularity annotations are still largely restricted to breast cancer cohorts, whereas most multi-organ datasets only allow for indirect TC estimation. Therefore, direct transfer of models trained on single organ datasets remains challenging due to significant differences in tumor cellular architecture, stromal composition, inflammatory infiltration, and treatment effects across organs and disease contexts. Furthermore, the lack of datasets with explicit TC annotations restricts the development of robust supervised models and reduces the external validation across institutions. As a result, high performance in experimental settings may not correspond to high robustness when models are deployed on heterogeneous clinical data in the real world. These limitations highlight the need for larger multi-organ, multi-institutional datasets with standardized annotation protocols and diverse scanner and staining conditions to improve generalizability and clinical reliability.

Data preprocessing

Raw data acquired from histopathology slides need to be cleaned and standardized using preprocessing techniques to be used by AI models for further analysis. It is crucial to have an efficient preprocessing pipeline, as the slides are created under diverse conditions, such as different staining protocols, lighting conditions, and scanning equipment. These can cause inconsistencies across the images, which have to be normalized before being fed to the AI models. Apart from the differences in the acquisition procedure, histopathology slides often are prone to artifacts during their preparation, which can add irrelevant features to the data. Preprocessing filters out these deviations and standardizes the data. Standard preprocessing methods include color normalization and stain deconvolution, which reduce noise and improve the robustness of downstream AI models. A detailed survey of various color normalization algorithms of histopathology image like histogram specification, Reinhard method, Malenko method, stain color descriptor, complete color normalization, and structure preserving color normalization is explored in Roy et al.18 Histopathology slides are often scanned at extremely high resolution and are memory- and computationally intensive to process. Various efficient patching approaches of WSI images were proposed in Liu et al., Hoffman et al., and Jabar et al.,19, 20, 21 which enable the AI models to focus on learning meaningful patterns from underlying slide images. A comprehensive analysis of preprocessing methodologies lies beyond the scope of this TC assessment review, with detailed treatments available in the cited literature.18, 19, 20, 21

Traditional ML approaches

Traditional ML methods typically decompose TC assessment into feature extraction followed by a supervised regressor or classifier. Hand-crafted features are computed at the pixel, texture, or object (cell/gland) level and then mapped to pathologist-assigned cellularity scores.5, 22

•Feature engineering and classical regressors: Early works on automated TC estimation used color deconvolution and thresholding to separate hematoxylin-stained nuclei from background and stroma, followed by texture descriptors (e.g., Haralick features, local binary patterns), morphological statistics (nuclear area, shape, and compactness), and cell density measures within tumor bed regions. These features were then fed into linear regression, support vector regression, or random forest models to approximate continuous TC values or to stratify by cellularity (low/intermediate/high). These algorithms function in a way like a pathologist mentally weighing nuclear size, staining intensity, and tissue density when estimating TC, these models combine image-derived visual cues to generate a numeric cellularity estimate. Such pipelines demonstrated that algorithmic TC estimates can reach or exceed inter-observer agreement between pathologists,5, 22 but they are sensitive to staining variability and require extensive parameter tuning.

•Region of interest (ROI) selection and rule-based integration: Many traditional pipelines rely on pathologist-defined ROIs, often centered on the tumor bed, to reduce noise from non-informative tissue. Within these ROIs, nuclei detection and classification (tumor vs non-tumor) are combined with heuristic rules such as ratios of malignant nuclei to all nuclei, or fraction of area occupied by tumor nuclei, to compute a TC score that mimics a pathologist's eyeballing. Whereas interpretable and aligned with clinical reasoning, these rule-based systems struggle with complex histology (e.g., mucinous or therapy-altered tissue) and are less robust than modern DL methods when applied to new scanners or institutions.5, 22

Deep learning approaches

DL models learn directly from large collections of annotated slides, identifying the visual patterns most predictive of TC without requiring manually defined rules, producing a numeric cellularity output from the slide image in a single, integrated process. Different DL paradigms can be grouped into CNN models, cell detection and segmentation-based methods, attention and transformer-based architectures, and multi-scale or hybrid systems.23

CNN-based models

CNN-based approaches treat TC prediction as a supervised regression or classification problem on patches or tiles. Models are trained using pathologist-assigned TC scores and can be aggregated across patches to produce slide-level estimates.

•Patch-level regression and classification: In datasets such as BreastPathQ, CNNs (e.g., ResNet, DenseNet, and Inception variants) have been trained to map each image patch to a continuous cellularity value or discrete class, often using mean squared error or ordinal loss functions tailored to the ordered nature of TC scores.6 CaBeSt-Net24 is a DL framework based on DeepLabV3 and CNN shows promising performance in TC assessment, surpassing human inter-observer variability. Ensemble strategies, multi-head networks predicting both regression and classification, and heavy data augmentation (color jitter, rotations, and stain perturbation) are commonly employed to improve calibration and robustness. These models have achieved intraclass correlation coefficients with pathologists that approach or surpass inter-observer agreement, positioning CNNs as strong baselines for automated TC estimation.23

•WSI-level aggregation: For whole-slide TC assessment, CNNs are applied in a sliding window or tiling fashion, and patch-level predictions are combined through simple statistics (mean, median, or percentile pooling) or weighted schemes that emphasize patches likely to represent tumor bed regions.5, 22, 23 In clinical terms, this aggregation step reflects the pathologist's practice of mentally synthesizing findings across multiple fields rather than basing the TC estimate on a single microscopic view. Cell detection and segmentation-based methods

Cell detection and segmentation-based methods follow a more “bottom-up” paradigm, explicitly modeling nuclei and cell types and then computing TC from counts or areas.6, 22, 25 These approaches attempt to mirror the conceptual process of pathologists, who estimate the proportion of malignant cells relative to the overall cell population in the tumor bed.

•Nuclei segmentation and cell classification: Modern pipelines often start with a deep segmentation model (e.g., UNet variants, HoVerNet, or other encoder–decoder architectures) trained to segment nuclei and sometimes classify them into malignant epithelial, benign epithelial, stromal, and lymphocytic categories. When coupled with nuclei-rich datasets and auxiliary annotations (as in parts of the BreastPathQ resource or other nuclear segmentation benchmarks), these models can produce accurate cell masks and labels across large fields of view. TC is then derived as the ratio of malignant nuclei to total nuclei, or as a weighted combination of malignant cell counts and corresponding areas, within the annotated tumor bed.22 Recent works have refined this paradigm through more robust cell instance segmentation. A recent study25 proposed an explainable DL framework that combines edge-aware CNNs with nuclei instance segmentation to quantify tumor cell density and residual tumor burden in ocular tumors. This demonstrates that cell-centric TC metrics correlate strongly with expert assessments. A pipeline that employed U-Net like architecture and post-processing to accurately detect overlapping nuclei, enabling reliable computation of malignant cell fractions as a surrogate for TC was proposed in Liu et al.26 These studies highlight how improved nuclear instance segmentation directly enhances the stability and reproducibility of cellularity estimates.

Attention and transformer-based models

Attention mechanisms and vision transformers address key challenges of WSI-scale TC estimation: the need to focus on diagnostically relevant regions and to model long-range contextual relationships across large images. They are frequently embedded in multiple instance learning (MIL) frameworks that treat WSIs as bags of tiles with slide-level labels.6

•Attention-based MIL: MIL architectures use encoders (often CNNs) to embed tiles and learn attention weights that highlight tiles most predictive of the overall TC label. In the context of TC, attention-based MIL allows the model to automatically discover tumor bed regions and discount background or predominantly stromal areas, even when only slide-level TC scores are provided during training.6 This is particularly valuable for post-neoadjuvant cohorts, where therapy-induced changes make manual tumor bed delineation challenging, and where weakly supervised learning can reduce annotation burden.

•Vision transformers (ViTs) and hybrid CNN–transformer encoders: ViTs and hybrid CNN–transformer architectures process images as sequences of patches, using self-attention to integrate information across spatial locations. For TC estimation, they can capture complex patterns such as spatial clustering of residual tumor cells, interface between invasive and fibrotic regions, and multi-scale context around tumor bed boundaries. When combined with positional encodings that respect WSI coordinates and hierarchical pooling, transformer-based models have been shown to improve robustness to patch selection strategies and to enhance generalization across institutions and scanners.27 The work in Sarkar et al.28 presented an image transformer using self-attention to capture long-range spatial relationships across tumor beds and fibrotic regions. This achieved superior generalization compared to CNN baselines on external validation cohorts, demonstrating the efficacy of attention mechanisms for therapy-altered histology.

Multi-scale and hybrid approaches

Multi-scale and hybrid models explicitly combine information from different magnifications, architectural levels (cell, gland, and tissue), or modeling paradigms (segmentation plus regression), leveraging complementary strengths to improve TC estimation.29

•Multi-magnification pipelines: TC depends both on microscopic cell-level details and on mesoscopic tissue architecture. Multi-scale pipelines therefore process patches at several magnifications (e.g., 5×, 10×, 20×), either through separate encoders or shared-weight networks, and then fuse the resulting feature maps through concatenation or attention mechanisms.30 A dual-magnification fully connected network (FCN) was developed,31 that conditions cell classification on multi-scale context, explicitly mimicking pathologists who assess cell neoplasticity at different microscope magnifications. Low-magnification provides tumor context, whereas high-magnification refines cell-level tumor/normal classification. Low magnification views help localize tumor beds and distinguish tumor from normal structures, whereas high magnification crops support precise assessment of nuclear density and residual tumor foci, leading to more stable TC predictions than single scale models.29

•Hybrid segmentation–regression frameworks: Several methods integrate explicit segmentation branches (for nuclei, tumor regions, or both) with regression heads that output a final TC score. In such architectures, the segmentation branch is trained with pixel or instance-level annotations, encouraging the network to learn interpretable intermediate representations, whereas the regression branch is supervised by patch or slide-level cellularity labels.22 This joint training can regularize the model, improve data efficiency, and produce outputs that are more easily audited by pathologists (e.g., visual overlays of detected tumor cells alongside numeric TC scores).26

•Ensembles and model fusion: To further enhance robustness, many state-of-the-art pipelines ensemble multiple model types such as combining CNN-based regressors, cell segmentation driven estimators, and transformer-based MIL models and aggregate their outputs through averaging or meta learning.32 Such ensembles exploit diversity in inductive biases and error patterns, often yielding improved agreement with expert pathologists across a wide range of tissue types, treatment responses, and imaging conditions.33 A homogeneous ensemble model proposed in Wang et al.34 integrates digital image analysis with lightweight AI architectures specifically for TC estimation, achieving superior predictive accuracy and generalization without computationally intensive deep networks.

Emerging foundation models and annotation-efficient learning approaches

As the scarcity of expert-annotated TC datasets continues to limit model scalability and generalization, emerging annotation-efficient learning strategies are increasingly redefining the development of AI systems in digital pathology.

Pathology foundation models

Recent advancements in computational pathology have introduced pathology foundation models, which are large-scale models pretrained on massive datasets of unlabeled histopathology images using self-supervised learning. Unlike supervised pipelines, these models do not require TC labels during pretraining; instead, they learn transferable visual representations from the intrinsic structure of WSIs and tissue patches. From a pathology perspective, this pretraining phase can be thought of as the model building a broad visual vocabulary of tissue appearances by learning to distinguish glandular from stromal from inflammatory patterns before being further trained on TC-specific data, much as a trainee pathologist develops general histological literacy before specializing in a particular organ or scoring task. This shift is particularly relevant for TC assessment, where high-quality expert annotations remain limited.

Major models include UNI,35 CONCH,36 and Prov-GigaPath,37 which have demonstrated strong generalization across downstream pathology tasks by employing large-scale pretraining on diverse unlabeled slides. In the context of TC estimation, such models offer a promising starting point for feature extraction, weakly supervised learning, and slide-level prediction, especially in settings where TC-specific annotations are scarce. Their representation power may improve robustness to stain variation, scanner differences, and tissue heterogeneity, all of which are common challenges in digital pathology.

At the same time, foundation models are not a direct replacement for TC-specific supervision. Their performance for cellularity estimation still depends on downstream task adaptation, the availability of representative labeled data, and validation in the intended clinical domain. Moreover, most current foundation models are trained for broad pathology representation learning rather than TC-specific prediction, so their clinical value for cellularity assessment will depend on how effectively they can be fine-tuned or combined with task-specific heads, region-level supervision, or weakly supervised aggregation strategies.

Self-supervised, semi-supervised, and active learning

Beyond foundation models, several annotation-efficient learning strategies are highly relevant to TC estimation. Self-supervised learning enables models to learn from unlabeled pathology slides by capturing morphology without requiring manual annotations.38, 39 Semi-supervised learning approaches are highly useful when TC annotations are available for only a limited amount of data by combining a small, labeled set with a larger unlabeled set.40 Active learning directs pathologist annotation effort toward the cases where the model is least certain, typically specimens with borderline cellularity, extensive treatment-related changes, or unusual stromal patterns, reducing unnecessary scoring of straightforward cases while maximizing the diagnostic value of each expert review.41

These approaches directly address one of the main barriers in TC research: the scarcity of expert annotations and the resulting difficulty in building robust, generalizable models. For TC estimation, they are particularly attractive because they can exploit the abundance of unlabeled WSIs while minimizing the need for exhaustive manual scoring. In future TC workflows, these methods may be combined with foundation models to improve data efficiency, reduce annotation cost, and support more scalable deployment across cancer types and institutions.

Comparative perspective on AI methodologies

Overall, the field has evolved from interpretable, feature-engineered ML systems to powerful DL pipelines that integrate segmentation, attention, and multi-scale modeling. These methodological advances have progressively reduced inter-observer variability, enabled cross-organ generalization, and improved alignment between automated TC estimates and clinically meaningful endpoints such as RCB, treatment response, and molecular testing eligibility.

Despite the rapid evolution of AI techniques for TC estimation, there is no single methodological paradigm that addresses the underlying challenges of data scarcity, interpretability, and clinical robustness. The trade-offs arise in different ways across methodological paradigms. CNN-based methods are relatively easier to train with moderately sized patch-level annotations, are highly sensitive to domain shifts, and might fail to generalize across datasets. Segmentation-based methods offer higher interpretability as they explicitly model the regions of interest; however, data acquisition is susceptible to staining variability, and they require pixel-level annotations, which are labor-intensive. In addition, these methods can be unreliable in therapy-altered tissues, such as post-neoadjuvant specimens with fibrosis or necrosis, where accurate delineation of tumor cells becomes challenging.

Attention-based MIL and transformer models are well-suited to WSI analysis under weak supervision, enabling improved contextual understanding and scalability. However, these approaches are typically less interpretable and may lead to misinterpretation of heterogeneous tumor microenvironments that arise due to the lack of spatial precision.

Emerging foundation models and annotation-efficient learning approaches improve data efficiency and generalization. Foundation models pretrained using self-supervised learning on large-scale unlabeled pathology datasets can provide transferable representations for downstream TC estimation tasks, particularly when annotated data is limited. Similarly, semi-supervised and active learning approaches help reduce dependence on exhaustive expert labeling by using unlabeled data and focusing on informative samples for annotation. However, these methods still require careful adaptation, external validation, and assessment of clinical reliability before routine deployment.

Therefore, from a clinical perspective, segmentation-based and hybrid models might be more readily acceptable owing to their transparency, whereas end-to-end models require not only rigorous external validation but also interpretable outputs, such as spatial heatmaps highlighting the slide regions that drove the TC prediction, so that pathologists can review, verify, and, where necessary, override the automated estimate before it informs a clinical decision.

Performance metrics for AI TC models

Performance metrics for TC evaluation using AI models emphasize agreement with pathologists on continuous scores. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) serves as the gold-standard for continuous TC agreement with pathologists (>0.75 reflects good agreement), with top models often surpassing inter-observer variability.27, 42 Mean absolute error quantifies the percentage deviation,32 whereas Pearson/Spearman correlations validate nuclei-based approaches.43, 44 Clinical concordance with RCB classes or next-generation sequencing (NGS)-related workflows has been explored as a form of translational validation in recent AI-based TC assessment studies and commercial platforms.45 Ordinal decisions use quadratic-weighted kappa,42 clinical triage employs AUC,40 and segmentation precursors rely on Dice/F1-scores.12, 17 Though less frequently reported in standard metrics, turnaround time and cost per case serve as important performance indicators. Challenges, limitations, and research gaps

Pathology-specific challenges

TC plays an important role in cancer diagnosis and treatment planning because it directly affects tissue suitability for molecular testing, targeted therapy selection, and prognostic assessment. In routine practice, inaccurate TC estimation can result in insufficient tumor DNA for NGS, potentially affecting downstream molecular results and treatment decisions. Both over- and underestimation of TC can therefore have clinically significant consequences in routine molecular testing workflows. However, TC assessment itself remains subjective and semi-quantitative, with considerable inter-observer variability among pathologists (ICC ≈ 0.83).5, 24 This variability also affects the quality of ground-truth annotations used to train AI models.

Beyond these sources of variability, TC assessment in routine clinical practice is often more complex than estimating the percentage of tumor cells on a clean slide. Pathologists must identify the tumor bed, distinguish viable tumor from treatment-related changes, and interpret heterogeneous tissue architecture across multiple regions of the slide. Post-neoadjuvant specimens frequently contain fibrosis, necrosis, inflammation, hemorrhage, and scattered residual tumor cells, making both manual and automated TC assessment difficult. Assessment may be particularly challenging in small or poorly preserved biopsy specimens containing crush artifact, fragmented tissue, or very low TC. In addition, differences in tissue preparation, staining quality, scanner settings, section thickness, and slide artifacts introduce variability that may affect model performance outside controlled research datasets.

Dataset and generalization limitations

One of the major limitations in AI-based TC assessment is the lack of large datasets with reliable TC annotations. Most publicly available datasets with explicit TC labels are limited to breast cancer, whereas many multi-organ datasets only support indirect estimation of TC through surrogate measures such as tumor area or segmentation-based analysis. As a result, models trained on single-organ cohorts may not generalize well to other tumor types with different histological patterns, stromal composition, inflammatory backgrounds, and treatment-related changes.

Generalization across institutions also remains difficult because of domain shift caused by differences in staining protocols, scanner hardware, image compression, and local pathology workflows. Existing normalization methods can reduce some of this variability, but they do not completely prevent performance degradation during external validation. Consequently, models that perform well on curated internal datasets may show reduced robustness when applied to heterogeneous real-world clinical data.

Recent annotation-efficient approaches, including self-supervised, semi-supervised, and active learning methods, may help reduce dependence on exhaustive expert labeling. However, broader external validation on diverse multi-institutional cohorts is still necessary before reliable clinical deployment can be achieved.

Clinical translation and regulatory challenges

Despite encouraging technical performance, most AI-based TC assessment systems remain in the research or pilot-deployment stage. Most published studies are retrospective and involve relatively small datasets from single institutions. Because training and testing data are often generated under similar conditions, retrospective evaluations may overestimate real-world performance.

Prospective clinical validation is considerably more difficult because it requires integration into routine diagnostic workflows, multi-center collaboration, regulatory oversight, and demonstration of meaningful clinical benefit. In addition to achieving high accuracy, AI systems must show that they improve workflow efficiency, support treatment decisions, or enhance the adequacy of molecular testing in real clinical environments.

Regulatory approval also remains challenging because, unlike conventional software tools, AI models are highly sensitive to variations in data acquisition procedures and institutional workflows. Regulatory agencies therefore require strong evidence of reproducibility and consistent performance across multiple centers. In addition, AI systems that continue to evolve through retraining introduce additional concerns regarding ongoing monitoring and revalidation after deployment. As a result, many currently available TC assessment tools remain designated for research use only and have not yet achieved FDA clearance or laboratory-developed test validation.

Workflow integration presents another practical barrier. Pathology labs operate within complex digital ecosystems involving LIS, WSI viewers, molecular diagnostics pipelines, and digital archiving systems. AI-based TC tools must integrate smoothly into these environments without increasing reporting burden or delaying turnaround times. However, many proposed systems are still evaluated under controlled research conditions and may not adequately account for common real-world issues such as tissue artifacts, out-of-focus scanning, incomplete sections, or variable specimen quality.

Additional concerns relate to medicolegal accountability and the long-term reliability of AI systems after deployment. Changes in lab workflows, scanner upgrades, staining variations, or software updates may gradually affect model performance over time, making continuous monitoring and periodic revalidation important for safe clinical use.

Model interpretability is also important for clinical adoption. Pathologists may be hesitant to rely on “black-box” systems that provide numeric TC estimates without visual or pathological justification. In high-stakes clinical decisions, such as determining eligibility for molecular testing or targeted therapies, transparency and traceability remain essential. For this reason, hybrid and segmentation-based approaches that generate visual overlays of tumor regions or nuclei may currently be more acceptable in clinical practice, as they allow pathologists to review and verify AI-generated outputs. In this context, AI-based TC assessment is likely to function most effectively as an adjunctive tool that supports, rather than replaces, expert pathological interpretation.

Conclusion

This structured review examines the evolving landscape of AI-based TC assessment, highlighting how AI has progressively transformed the subjective scoring method into a reproducible process. Advances in DL, particularly CNNs and attention-based transformer architectures have significantly improved agreement with expert pathologists, in many cases even surpassing the inter-observer consistency.

Despite these advances, clinical deployment of these technologies is constrained by factors such as limited datasets with direct TC annotation, computational resource demands for WSI inference, and the need for transparent models. In addition, the existing validation studies remain retrospective, emphasizing the need for prospective, multi-center evaluations to ensure significant improvements in diagnostic workflows and patient outcomes.

Future research should aim at developing large and diverse datasets with standardized TC annotations to improve model generalizability and benchmarking consistency. Emerging foundation models and annotation-efficient learning approaches, such as self-supervised and active learning paradigms, address current limitations related to annotation scarcity and domain shift. Successful implementation in daily pathology practice should focus on explainability, workflow integration, and future clinical validation. The successful translation of AI-based TC assessment from research to real-world clinical deployment will depend on standardized evaluation frameworks and clinically meaningful clinical endpoints.
