On Reliability of Efficient Membership Inference Vulnerability Evaluation
Joonas Jälkö, Gauri Pradhan, Ossi Räisä, Antti Honkela

Abstract
Membership inference attacks (MIAs) are popular methods for empirically assessing the leakage of sensitive information in the training data through models or statistics learned from the data. The MIA vulnerability is often evaluated through false positive rate ( FPR \mathrm{FPR} ) and true positive rate ( TPR \mathrm{TPR} ) of a binary classifier that tries to predict whether a particular sample was in the training data. However, in order to reliably estimate the TPR \mathrm{TPR} especially for low FPR \mathrm{FPR} values, a lot of observations are needed, which in case of MIA translates to many target models, leading to large computational cost. To avoid excessive compute requirements, the MIA scores are often averaged over multiple individuals and multiple targeted models. We demonstrate two key weaknesses in this efficient MIA evaluation pipeline. First, we show that evaluating the TPR \mathrm{TPR} based on MIA scores concatenated across multiple individuals, commonly used to study vulnerabilities in the very low FPR \mathrm{FPR} regime, is not calibrated across the per-sample FPR \mathrm{FPR} s. This makes it unreliable as a tool for auditing differential privacy. To solve this, we propose a post-processing method to effectively calibrate the FPR \mathrm{FPR} across different samples. Second, we identify a finite population bias in the commonly used efficient likelihood-ratio attack (LiRA) implementation proposed by 5 , leading to a positive bias in the per-sample vulnerability.

Introduction

Efficiently evaluating the privacy risk associated with a particular machine learning algorithm applied to a particular dataset is critical for calibrating the level of privacy protection, but no good general solutions exist for this problem.
The difficulty of the problem arises from many sources: 1) the privacy risk is a statistical phenomenon and evaluating it requires repeated observations; 2) identifying the most vulnerable samples needed to evaluate maximum risk can be difficult; and 3) it is difficult to know if the applied attack is optimal.

Privacy auditing methods
(
2
)
address each of these by 1) developing specific one-run-auditing (ORA) techniques
(
19
;
14
)
; 2) crafting canary samples to represent maximally vulnerable samples in the entire data universe
(
4
)
; and 3) potentially using more easily auditable internals of the algorithm to ensure strong attacks.
These methods have weaknesses: the reliability of ORA is fundamentally limited
(
22
;
12
)
, and the actual samples may be more or less vulnerable than the canaries.

To evaluate the vulnerability of the actual samples, we focus on membership inference attacks (MIAs) for privacy risk estimation.
MIA is based on a binary classification problem: was a given sample part of the training dataset or not.
MIAs are useful for this purpose because unlike stronger privacy attacks like attribute inference and training data reconstruction, the setting is highly standardised.
MIA vulnerability is related to vulnerability to these other attacks by reductions
(
16
)
.
The most widely used privacy enhancing technology in machine learning, differential privacy
(
8
)
, can be naturally interpreted as a bound on MIA success
(
21
)
.

Following
5
, MIA evaluation has focused on evaluating the true positive rate (
\mathrm{TPR}
) of membership identification at fixed low false positive rate (
\mathrm{FPR}
), as this is most sensitive to the adversary’s ability to confidently detect even a small number of true members of the training dataset.
The downside of the focus on low
\mathrm{FPR}
values is that estimating the corresponding
\mathrm{TPR}
values reliably requires a lot of membership data.

Given
M
attack target models with
N
attack target points each, a per-data-point evaluation requires at minimum
\mathrm{FPR}>1/M
and for reliable estimation
\mathrm{FPR}\geq 10/M
is advisable.
Reaching even moderately low
\mathrm{FPR}\leq 0.1
in this setting requires
M\geq 100
which is often impractical.

In order to increase the number of target evaluations, it is appealing to mix evaluations from different target models and attacks for a total of
MN
target evaluations.
This approach was proposed by
5
for efficient MIA vulnerability evaluation and is also used by the ML Privacy Meter library
(
15
)
.
This approach allows formally having enough evaluations for a very low
\mathrm{FPR}
, but unless one is very careful, the interpretation of these results can be difficult.
In particular, we show that the global
\mathrm{FPR}
from this process is usually not what would be expected, which can lead to misleading evaluations of the privacy risk, and propose a correction.
We also highlight a finite population bias in this estimation process and propose a fix.

Contributions

•

We study per-sample and concatenated MIA evaluations under

We show that concatenated MIA aggregation reports an average over per-sample vulnerabilities, but that a single global threshold induces heterogeneous per-sample

).

•

To improve the concatenated approach, we propose a simple post-processing

\mathrm{FPR}
s and allows the concatenation

\mathrm{TPR}
with the targeted
\mathrm{FPR}
under mild

Sections

4.2
and
6
).

Sections

5
and

Background

2.1
Membership inference attacks

The goal of the MIA is to infer whether a particular training sample belongs
to the training dataset of a particular learning outcome

17
. Denoting the target sample with
x_{\textrm{target}}

and the learning outcome with
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}}
, MIA can be formally stated
as the following binary hypothesis test

H_{0}:x_{\textrm{target}}\notin D_{\textrm{train}}\quad\text{ vs. }\quad H_{1}:x_{\textrm{target}}\in D_{\textrm{train}},

(1)

based on observing
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(D_{\textrm{train}})
where
D_{\textrm{train}}

is latent. In this work we will focus on
shadow-model
based MIA where
the test statistic for the above hypothesis test is based on comparing
the observed
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}}
to the distributions learning outcomes when

x_{\textrm{target}}
is in or out of the
D_{\textrm{train}}
. For a data distribution
\mathcal{P}

the in and out samples are distributed as

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{P}^{N}

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{out}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(D\setminus\{x_{\textrm{target}}\})

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{in}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(D\cup{x_{\textrm{target}}}).

(4)

We will denote the in and out distributions for a sample
x
respectively
as
p_{\textrm{in},x}
and
p_{\textrm{out},x}
.

The LiRA algorithm by
5
has proved to be a highly
effective. From the trained shadow models, LiRA infers normal approximations for
the in/out-distributions for each target point separately. The LiRA
score for a target point
x
and model

\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}}
is given as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x,\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}}):=\log\frac{p_{\textrm{in},x}(\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}})}{p_{\textrm{out},x}(\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}})}=\log\frac{\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}};\,\mu_{\textrm{in},x},\sigma_{\textrm{in},x}^{2})}{\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}};\,\mu_{\textrm{out},x},\sigma_{\textrm{out},x}^{2})},

(5)

where the parameters
\mu_{\textrm{in},x},\mu_{\textrm{out},x},\sigma_{\textrm{in},x},\sigma_{\textrm{out},x}
are
learned from the shadow models.

2.2
Variables in membership inference attacks

The success of MIA is affected by various factors arising both from the training
algorithm as well as the data distribution.

Learning algorithm:

We consider the learning algorithm to be a fixed
program, however we allow the algorithm still to induce stochasticity into the
learning outcome e.g. through subsampling of the training data or through
operations such as dropout
(
18
)
. Any stochasticity in the learning
algorithm will affect the in/out-distributions, and therefore will have an effect
on the MIA vulnerability.

Data distribution:

The data distribution has a large effect on the
success rate for the MIA. Since the in/out-distributions correspond to learning
outcomes over different training sets drawn from the data distribution, it is easy to
see that the MIA scores are affected by the data distribution.

Dataset level properties:

Recently

20
showed that the dataset
level properties such as number of samples and number of classes can have an
effect on the MIA vulnerability.

2.3
Measuring vulnerability

Since MIA is a binary hypothesis test, the vulnerability can be measured through
the properties of the distinguisher performing this test.

5
suggested measuring the vulnerability in terms of
the attack’s
\mathrm{TPR}
at low fixed
\mathrm{FPR}
. This metric has been since widely
adopted in the literature, see
e.g.
23
;
13
. Empirically, the

\mathrm{FPR}
and
\mathrm{TPR}
are commonly obtained from a ROC-curve built from the MIA
classifiers’ scores and corresponding ground truth labels.

Trade-off function.

The
\mathrm{FPR}
is an empirical estimates of a classifiers type I error, while the

\mathrm{TPR}=1-\textrm{FNR}
with
FNR
being the estimate of the type II
error.
Trade-off function
gives a lower bound for any classifiers
(\mathrm{FPR},\textrm{FNR})
curve, i.e. the inverse ROC-curve, and is defined as follows

Definition 2.1

(
7
)
.

For any distributions
P
and
Q
on a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F})
,
the trade-off function
T(P,Q):[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is given as

\displaystyle T(P,Q)(\alpha)=\inf\{\beta_{\phi}\,:\,\alpha_{\phi}\leq\alpha\}

(6)

where
\phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a rejection rule with

\displaystyle\alpha_{\phi}=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\phi],\;\beta_{\phi}=1-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\phi].

(7)

The next Lemma shows that the trade-off function is invariant to bijective
post-processing.

Lemma 2.1

(Bijective post-processing invariance)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be distributions on
\mathbb{R}
, and let

f(x)
be a bijective function.
Denote by
P^{f}
and
Q^{f}
the pushforward distributions of
P
and
Q

under
f
, i.e.,
P^{f}(B)=P(f^{-1}(B))
for all Borel sets

B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and similarly for
Q^{f}
. Then, for all

\alpha\in[0,1]
,

T(P,Q)(\alpha)\;=\;T\bigl(P^{f},Q^{f}\bigr)(\alpha).

Proof.

According to
Lemma

2.2
, for any functions
f
and
g
, for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle T(P,Q)(\alpha)\leq T(P^{f},Q^{f})(\alpha)\leq T(P^{g\circ f},Q^{g\circ f})(\alpha),

(8)

where
(g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))
. Now, if
f
is an bijection it is invertible and
we can chooce
g=f^{-1}
, which yields

\displaystyle T(P,Q)(\alpha)\leq T(P^{f},Q^{f})(\alpha)\leq T(P^{f^{-1}\circ f},Q^{f^{-1}\circ f})(\alpha)=T(P,Q)(\alpha)

(9)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle T(P,Q)(\alpha)=T(P^{f},Q^{f})(\alpha).

(10)

∎

Lemma 2.2

(
7
, Lemma 1)
.

For any distributions
P
and
Q
and a function
f
, for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle T(P^{f},Q^{f})(\alpha)\geq T(P,Q)(\alpha).

(11)

3
Vulnerability of a sample

Assume now that we have access to an oracle that can compute the LiRA score
for any target point/model pair
(x_{\textrm{target}},\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}})
. Our goal is to use
the scores from the oracle to address the following binary hypothesis test

\displaystyle H_{0}:\,x_{\textrm{target}}\notin D_{\textrm{train}}\quad\textrm{ vs. }\quad H_{1}:\,x_{\textrm{target}}\in D_{\textrm{train}}.

(12)

The above test is often empirically assessed with an ROC-curve using a threshold
classifier to separate the in and out scores. However, the above hypothesis test
does not specify whether we consider the test over different
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{target}}
or

x_{\textrm{target}}
, i.e. do we consider the vulnerability of a sample over different
learning outcomes, or do we consider the vulnerability of a learning outcome
over different samples. In this paper we will focus on the former, which we will
call the
per-sample
vulnerability.
By selecting the target samples carefully, per-sample vulnerability allows estimating the vulnerability of the most vulnerable samples which is important for reliable vulnerability evaluation
(
1
)
.

In the per-sample setting, we assume the data distribution
\mathcal{P}
is fixed and we
study the binary hypothesis test over learning outcomes
\mathcal{A}(D_{\textrm{train}})
with

D_{\textrm{train}}\sim\mathcal{P}
. Typically in MIA, we do not directly study the membership
based on the learning outcome
\mathcal{A}(D_{\textrm{train}})
but based on some post-processed
quantity such as the logits scores in classification tasks. We will use

\mathcal{S}(x_{\textrm{target}},\mathcal{A}(D_{\textrm{train}}))
to denote the statistic used for MIA, where

\mathcal{S}
is a deterministic function.

Now, with the target sample fixed, our binary test can be rewritten as

\displaystyle H_{0}:\,\mathcal{S}(x_{\textrm{target}},\mathcal{A}(D_{\textrm{train}}))\sim p_{\textrm{out},x}\quad\textrm{ vs. }\quad H_{1}:\,\mathcal{S}(x_{\textrm{target}},\mathcal{A}(D_{\textrm{train}}))\sim p_{\textrm{in},x}.

(13)

In other words, we are studying the distinguishability of
x_{\textrm{target}}

from the learning outcome over the stochasticity arising from any inherent
randomness of
\mathcal{A}
and from the latent training dataset
D\sim\mathcal{P}^{n}

that may or may not include
x_{\textrm{target}}
.

In order to empirically assess this vulnerability, we query the oracle with
multiple models trained with or without
x_{\textrm{target}}
and build the ROC-curve with
the corresponding scores and labels. The in/out scores can be written as

\displaystyle\ell^{\text{(out)}}_{x_{\textrm{target}}}:=\mathcal{L}(x_{\textrm{target}},\mathcal{A}(D))

(14)

\displaystyle\ell^{\text{(in)}}_{x_{\textrm{target}}}:=\mathcal{L}(x_{\textrm{target}},\mathcal{A}(D\cup x_{\textrm{target}})),

(15)

where
D\sim\mathcal{P}^{n}
.
The
\mathrm{FPR}
and
\mathrm{TPR}
for a threshold classifier with a decision threshold
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
become

\displaystyle\mathrm{FPR}_{x}(\tau)=\Pr(\ell^{\text{(out)}}_{x}>\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{D,\xi}[\mathbf{1}[\ell^{\text{(out)}}_{x}>\tau]]

(16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{TPR}_{x}(\tau)=\Pr(\ell^{\text{(in)}}_{x}>\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{D,\xi}[\mathbf{1}[\ell^{\text{(in)}}_{x}>\tau]]

(17)

where the
D\sim P^{n}
and
\xi
denotes any additional stochasticity in
\mathcal{A}
.

4
Reporting aggregates of the vulnerability

Consider now that we have MIA scores evaluated over
M
target models and
N

target data points. Each of the
N
samples
x_{\textrm{target}}
was either in our out of
the training data for each of the
M
models. We represent these results with a
matrix
\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
. Each row of
\mathcal{G}
represents
per-model scores, and each column per-sample scores.

In order to build the
\mathcal{G}
, we need to train
M
models. Therefore in
many practical settings, our evaluations are often limited to small number
M
.
However, in order to evaluate the MIA vulnerability in the low
\mathrm{FPR}
setting,
we would need at least
2/\mathrm{FPR}
models to even reach the desired
\mathrm{FPR}
. And
even if we have enough models to meet this criteria, the estimated ROC-curve
can still be highly uncertain. This uncertainty prevents us from
reliably estimating the per-sample vulnerability. However, when we have access
to evaluations over multiple target models and target points, we can aggregate
over these results in order to estimate the vulnerability.

4.1
Concatenating the MIA scores

Concatenating MIA scores over multiple target models has been used in the past
to learn aggregates of the vulnerability
(
5
;
15
)
. In concatenated MIA evaluation, we are
collapsing the MIA grid
\mathcal{G}
into a single vector, and evaluating the
vulnerability based on a ROC curve built on these raveled scores and their
corresponding labels.

The
\mathrm{FPR}
and
\mathrm{TPR}
for the concatenated approach are given as

\displaystyle\mathrm{FPR}=\Pr(\ell^{\text{(out)}}>\tau),\quad\mathrm{TPR}=\Pr(\ell^{\text{(in)}}>\tau),

(18)

where the dependence of both the model and sample to the scores is marginalized
away.
The next proposition demonstrates how the concatenated approach is related to
the per-sample vulnerability.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any fixed threshold
\tau
, the concatenated FPR and TPR are equal to the
expectation of per-sample FPR
x
and TPR
x
:

\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathrm{FPR}_{x}(\tau)],\qquad\mathrm{TPR}(\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathrm{TPR}_{x}(\tau)].

Proof.

For the concatenated
\mathrm{FPR}
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{FPR}=\Pr(\ell^{\text{(out)}}>\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x,D,\xi}[\mathbf{1}[\ell^{\text{(out)}}_{x}>\tau]]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathbb{E}_{D,\xi}[\mathbf{1}[\ell^{\text{(out)}}_{x}>\tau]]]=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\mathrm{FPR}_{x}],

(19)

which similarly follows for the
\mathrm{TPR}
.
∎

Thus, choosing a single decision threshold
\tau
that yields
\mathrm{FPR}=\alpha
at the
concatenated level corresponds to choosing
\tau
such that the average
per-sample
\mathrm{FPR}_{x}
equals
\alpha
, while individual
\mathrm{FPR}_{x}
may vary widely.
Hence calibrating the concatenated
\mathrm{FPR}
does not necessarily serve as a good
proxy of understanding the vulnerability.

4.2
Post-processing for better aggregation

Next, we present our solution which solves the
\mathrm{FPR}
calibration problem of the
concatenated approach. For a fixed sample
x
, suppose the learning outcome

S(x,A(D))
satisfies

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{out}}_{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\textrm{out},x},\sigma_{\textrm{out},x}^{2}),\qquad S^{\mathrm{in}}_{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\textrm{in},x},\sigma_{\textrm{in},x}^{2}),

(20)

and define the mean gap
\Delta_{x}=\mu_{\textrm{in},x}-\mu_{\textrm{out},x}
.

Now, consider the affine transform

\displaystyle f_{x}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{sign}(\Delta_{x})(t-\mu_{\textrm{out},x})}{\sigma_{\textrm{out},x}}.

(21)

Under this transform we obtain the standardized in/out distributions

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{\mathrm{out}}_{x}

\displaystyle:=f_{x}(S^{\mathrm{out}}_{x})\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(22)

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{\mathrm{in}}_{x}

\displaystyle:=f_{x}(S^{\mathrm{in}}_{x})\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{|\Delta_{x}|}{\sigma_{\textrm{out},x}},\,\frac{\sigma_{\textrm{in},x}^{2}}{\sigma_{\textrm{out},x}^{2}}\right).

(23)

By Lemma
2.1
, this standardization does not change
the trade-off function:

T\!\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\textrm{in},x},\sigma_{x}^{2}),\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\textrm{out},x},\sigma_{x}^{2})\right)(\alpha)=T\!\left(\mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{|\Delta_{x}|}{\sigma_{\textrm{out},x}},\frac{\sigma_{\textrm{in},x}^{2}}{\sigma_{\textrm{out},x}^{2}}\right),\mathcal{N}(0,1)\right)(\alpha).

(24)

4.2.1
Equal variances

Now, assume that
\sigma_{\textrm{in},x}=\sigma_{\textrm{out},x}:=\sigma_{x}
.
In this case from (
24
) we can see that all per-sample in/out pairs can be
viewed as testing between a standard normal and a shifted unit-variance
normal with mean
|\Delta_{x}|/\sigma_{x}
.

Next Lemma shows us that for the equal
variance case, the likelihood-ratio is a bijection.

Lemma 4.1
.

For two Gaussians
P=N(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2})
and
Q=N(\mu_{2},\sigma^{2})

with density functions
p
and
q
respectively. If
\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}
the
log-likelihood ratio,
\Lambda(t)=\log\frac{p(t)}{q(t)}

is a bijection w.r.t.
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, if
\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}
, the
\Lambda(t)

is also monotonically increasing.

Proof.

With simple algebra we can see that

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(2t(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})-\mu_{1}^{2}+\mu_{2}^{2}\right).

(25)

Clearly
\Lambda(t)
is an affine mapping and thus a bijection.
∎

By Lemma
4.1
, any level-
\alpha
likelihood ratio test between

P
and
Q
can be implemented directly in the sample space: the Neyman–Pearson
test that rejects
Q
when
\Lambda(t)>\tau_{\alpha}
is equivalent to rejecting
when
t>t_{\alpha}
, where
t_{\alpha}
is the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the
out-distribution under
Q
. In particular, since
|\Delta_{x}|/\sigma_{x}>0

the LLR is monotonically increasing in the learning outcome, so we can choose
the decision threshold from the out-distribution and apply it directly to the
observed value.

Since the out distribution for the post-processed learning outcomes is fixed
to the standard normal among all the samples, we can use the same decision threshold

t_{\alpha}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)
to obtain the same
\mathrm{FPR}_{x}
for all

x\sim\mathcal{P}
. Finally, we can estimate the average
\mathrm{TPR}
as

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{TPR}}=\frac{1}{NM}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\ldots N\\
m=1\ldots M\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}[\hat{S}_{i,j}>t_{\alpha}].

(26)

Now, similar to the concatenated approach, our post-processed aggregated
LiRA vulnerability gives an estimate of the vulnerability marginalized over
both the models and sample in
\mathcal{G}
. Therefore, the metric is not suitable
for assessing the per-sample nor the per-model vulnerability, but should be
seen as a measure of how much the learning algorithm leaks about the individuals
on average. However, as we are averaging over
NM
values in total, we can
actually have a rather confident estimate of the underlying average per-sample
vulnerability, even in the low
\mathrm{FPR}
regime.

4.2.2
Unequal variances

When
\sigma_{\textrm{in},x}\neq\sigma_{\textrm{out},x}
, the LLR is no longer a bijection, which means that the
rejection region for the LLR test cannot be uniquely mapped into a single sided
test on the tails of the learning outcomes. Therefore testing if
\hat{S}_{i,j}>t_{\alpha}
does not satisfy the conditions of Neyman-Pearson Lemma, and the
test will have less power than the LLR test.

However, even though the test is not uniformly most powerful like the
log-likelihood ratio test, we still retain calibration in terms of the
per-sample
\mathrm{FPR}
s. This is because the transformed learning outcomes

\hat{S}^{\mathrm{out}}_{x}
will follow the standard normal regardless.
Therefore, the
\mathrm{TPR}
arising from the concatenated transformed test will lower
bound the true average vulnerability.

5
Efficient LiRA and Finite Population Bias

When implementing a MIA in practice on a real dataset, we do not have access to the underlying data generating process
\mathcal{P}
but only a finite superset

D_{\mathrm{full}}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N_{+}}\}\sim\mathcal{P}^{N^{+}}

from which the training sets of each model must be sampled.
For each target model
\mathcal{M}
, the corresponding training set
D
of
N
elements is sampled without replacement from
D_{\mathrm{full}}
.

Given these models, the “efficient LiRA” procedure proposed by
5
recycles these models so that each acts as a target model in turn, while the rest act as shadow models.

This yields an
M\times N_{+}
LiRA grid analogous to our generic grid
G
, but
with two important differences compared to the “ideal” LiRA setting where
shadow datasets are independently drawn as
D\sim P^{N}
:

•

All shadow and target models are trained on overlapping subsets of the

same
finite
D_{\mathrm{full}}
.

•

Each
D
is sampled without replacement from
D_{\mathrm{full}}
,
rather than from an infinite population.

As a consequence, estimators of the in/out variances of the learning outcomes
(or LiRA scores) are subject to a finite population bias. Intuitively,
reusing the same finite set
D_{\mathrm{full}}
multiple times makes the
empirical variability of the shadow models smaller than what one would observe
if each training set were drawn independently from
P
.

5.1
Finite Population Correction

To see the effect at a high level, consider any scalar statistic
T(D)
of a
dataset
D
of size
N
drawn
without replacement
from a finite population
of size
N_{+}
. Classical sampling theory
(
6
)
shows that

\mathrm{Var}[T(D)]\approx\mathrm{Var}_{\text{iid}}[T(D)]\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac{N}{N_{+}}\Bigr),

(27)

where
\mathrm{Var}_{\text{iid}}[T(D)]
is the variance that would be obtained if
the elements of
D
were sampled independently from the underlying population
(i.e., with replacement). The factor

\mathrm{FPC}=1-\frac{N}{N_{+}}

(28)

is the standard finite population correction. When
N/N_{+}
is small, the
correction is negligible, but for larger
N
the effect becomes significant. In efficient LiRA, it is common to choose
N
to
be a large fraction of
N_{+}
(e.g.
N=0.5\,N_{+}
), in which case

\mathrm{FPC}\approx 0.5
and the variance may be substantially underestimated.

In our setting, the statistic is the learning outcome (or its LiRA score)
for a fixed point
x
across shadow models. Efficient LiRA estimates the in/out
distributions for
x
from
\{\mathcal{M}^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, but all
\mathcal{M}^{(m)}
are trained
on overlapping subsets of a fixed finite
D_{\mathrm{full}}
. This induces an
analogous finite population effect: the empirical in/out variances estimated from
the shadow models under efficient LiRA are biased towards
smaller
values
compared to the variances one would obtain from independent draws

D\sim P^{N}
.

Since LiRA’s distinguishability is driven in large part by the separation
between the in and out distributions relative to their variances, underestimating
these variances can lead to overly optimistic estimates of membership inference
vulnerability. Moreover, the vulnerability estimated via efficient LiRA is tied
to the particular realization
D_{\mathrm{full}}
, rather than to the

6.1
Effect of Post-Processing On Efficient LiRA Evaluation

In
Fig.

1
, we study following MIA evaluation strategies for a MIA grid of TabPFN models trained on Adult and Credit datasets:

•

Concatenated TPR (Naive):
All observations from the MIA grid are concatenated and the TPR is computed at a fixed FPR directly from the pooled statistics. This serves as a baseline that does not provide valid global FPR calibration.

•

Concatenated TPR (PP):
Before concatenation, the statistics in the MIA grid are post-processed as described in
Section

4.2
. The TPR at a fixed FPR is then computed from the post-processed, pooled statistics.

•

Average TPR/Sample:
The per-sample TPR,
\mathrm{TPR}_{x}
, is computed separately for each sample in the MIA grid and averaged. By construction, this approach ensures that the per-sample
\mathrm{FPR}_{x}\approx\alpha
for every sample.

•

Concatenated TPR (PP, Normal):
The post-processed statistics are concatenated and the decision threshold is set analytically by modeling the out-distribution as
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
as in
Section

4.2

•

Concatenated TPR (PP, Student’s
t
):
The decision threshold is set analytically using Student’s
t
-distribution with degrees of freedom estimated from the out-distribution. This yields a more conservative classifier that can account for heavy-tailed data.

•

Average TPR/Sample (Normal):
The per-sample TPR,
\mathrm{TPR}_{x}
, is computed for each sample, but with the decision threshold calibrated analytically under the
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
assumption for the out-distribution.

In
Fig.

1
, we observe that naive concatenation-based MIA evaluation consistently overestimates the average privacy risk of the training algorithm. Post-processing normalizes the per-sample out distributions before aggregation, ensuring that the decision threshold is applied on a common scale across all samples. Consequently, concatenated TPR (PP) is less than concatenated TPR (Naive) and this difference stabilizes at large
M
. Furthermore, we observe that the per-sample TPR estimates are noisy at small
M
because samplewise in/out distributions are estimated from fewer shadow models, whereas concatenation pools information but tends to suppress example-level heterogeneity. As
M
grows, Average TPR/Sample coverges to Concatenated TPR (PP) showing that the latter acts as a finite-sample conservative approximation to Average TPR/Sample whose stability is contingent on large
M
.
Using analytical decision threshold from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
leads to inflated TPR estimates, especially when
M\leq 512
.
Using the decision threshold from Student’s t-distribution can yield accurate results when
M\geq 256
.

In

7
Conclusion

Our results show how to evaluate the average MIA vulnerability across a number of data points in terms of TPR at fixed low FPR more reliably and efficiently.
While this is a quantity we can evaluate efficiently and is clearly linked to the vulnerability, it is not ideal for this purpose.
As pointed out by
1
, vulnerability evaluation should focus on the most vulnerable data points, which can be watered down in the average.
This means that the average TPR values need to be interpreted with care.
However, any plausible alternative would require being able to identify the most vulnerable data points, and training a large number of models while including/excluding those points, which is highly non-trivial.
Our approach provides a relatively cheap and easy alternative for evaluating MIA vulnerability as a valid TPR at known FPR which is not available from previous methods. Source code for our experiments is available at
https://github.com/TrustworthyMLHelsinki/mi_vulnerbility_evaluation
.
