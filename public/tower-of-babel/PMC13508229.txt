Marker-trait associations and superior haplotypes for rice straw cell wall components and their relationship with digestibility and agronomic traits
Authors: Rajdeep Jajoriya, Ranvir Singh Gill, Rakshith S R Gowda, Jaspal Singh Lamba, Amool Singh Vadithya, Jagjeet Singh Lore, Dharminder Bhatia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBeing a staple crop and growing over larger area, rice (Oryza sativa L.) generates a vast quantity of straw as a by-product. However, high fibre and silica content present in rice straw makes it difficult to utilize as a quality livestock feed or for industrial utilization, thus necessitating to identify genetic resources with lower silica content and enhanced straw digestibility. In this study, 207 diverse rice germplasm accessions belonging to 2 K rice diversity panel and 25 cultivated varieties were evaluated for straw cell wall components (silica, lignin and cellulose), agronomic traits (plant height, number of tillers, stem thickness) and in-vitro digestibility (acid detergent fibre). Wide variation was observed for silica (4.3–15.7%), lignin (4.9–29.5%), cellulose (20.3–45.2%), plant height (65.6–197.0 cm), number of tillers (4.0–19.0), stem thickness (0.9–9.2 mm) acid detergent fibre (ADF; 45.1–73.0%). Correlation analysis revealed a strong negative relationship between silica and organic as well as dry matter digestibility while lignin displayed variable effects depending on its interaction with other components. Multi-locus-Genome-wide Association Studies (ML-GWAS) with a subset of 154 rice accessions using 208,846 SNPs identified 33 significant marker–trait associations (MTAs) across genome for straw silica, lignin, cellulose, and ADF content. Major MTAs co-localized with known genes involved in silicon uptake (OsOPT2, OsOPT3, OsUCP2), lignin biosynthesis (4CL3, WRKY71, OsACS7), and cellulose synthesis (WAK12, BGLU4, OsMPS). Haplotype analyses of key candidate genes revealed superior alleles associated with up to 15.9% lower silica and 26.9% lower lignin content, with corresponding higher expression in relevant tissues. Selected rice accessions with favourable alleles and balanced ADF, lignin, and silica showed improved in-vitro digestibility, providing promising genetic resources for enhancing rice straw palatability. These findings offer valuable insights into the genetic basis of straw quality traits and identify potential donors for breeding programs aimed at developing rice varieties with improved straw value for sustainable agriculture and bioenergy use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08865-4.Keywords: Rice straw cell wall components, Rice straw silicon content, Superior haplotypes, Rice straw digestibility

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is the staple food crop that feed more than half of the world population and account for 20% of the world’s dietary energy. India is the second largest producer of rice in the world succeeding China. Rice grains are harvested at maturity leaving behind the rice straw. Management of rice straw was not significant problem in earlier times due to traditional farming practices, lower crop intensity, smaller scale of agriculture, limited mechanization, no pressure of double cropping and presence of alternate uses like animal feeding and use as fuel at household. However, rice straw management has become a serious concern now a days. In India, the north-western states including Punjab, Haryana and parts of Uttar Pradesh produce large magnitudes of rice, thus generating copious amounts of rice straw, the disposal of which remains a major concern. The high rice-producing areas of Punjab and Haryana produce 70,000,000 MT of rice straw, of which 85–90% is burnt in the field. This results in massive clouds of smoke posing an alarming environmental hazard. Within the past few years, various alternative strategies have been proposed and practised for the management of rice straw residue off-field, such as utilization as bedding/packaging materials, in mushroom cultivation, power generation, bio-ethanol production, bio-char and in several other manufacturing processes [37, 47]. However, since rice straw cannot be utilized in similar ways as other crop residues, a major portion of the straw residue generated remains unused, and straw burning remains the most prevalent method of management. While wheat straw is readily used as cattle fodder, rice straw is unsuitable for animal consumption on account of poor quality and digestibility [54]. The tissue in rice straw has a complex and heterogenous cell wall, mainly composed of lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose, and high amounts of silica [12], making it unpalatable and difficult to metabolize. This composition is quantified as acid detergent fibre (ADF), which includes all cell wall components except hemicellulose, and acid detergent lignin (ADL) content. A low ADF content indicates a superior forage quality, i.e. reducing the value of ADF and silica in paddy straw may enhance its nutritive value and digestibility, which will encourage farmers to use rice straw as animal feed [46].

After oxygen, silicon (Si) is the second most abundant element in soil, accounting for around 28% of the earth crust. It exists in the soil in many forms, among which mono silicic acid (H4SiO4) is the major form amenable for plant uptake by the roots. It is subsequently accumulated as an amorphous opal (SiO2) at the ends of transpiring organs such as leaves, roots, and hull. The physiological processes involved in the transport and regulation of silicon as monosilicic acid have been elucidated but notable gaps still exist in the understanding of the precise genetic mechanisms and interactions governing the functioning of transporter genes. Previous studies have reported identification and cloning of several genes associated with silicon uptake and transport, including Lsi1; [28], Lsi2; [29], Lsi3; [60], and Lsi6; [59].

The presence of silica in different crops is found to be associated with various physiological systems of the plant. The beneficial effects of silica on growth, production, yield, lodging prevention, and resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses have been demonstrated in a diverse range of plants [17, 27]. However, higher silica and ADF content renders the rice straw unsuitable for alternative use and interferes with its industrial processing. In order to minimize the environmental impact of straw disposal and to ensure the efficient utilisation of rice straw as a palatable fodder, development of rice varieties with an optimal level of silica and ADF is one of the viable and practical solution among others.

The germplasm showing variation for lower silica content in rice germplasm and associated genomic regions have been reported in previous studies [4, 7, 13, 40, 58]. Previous work from our research group [14], identified donors and genomic regions associated with lower silica content in Oryza nivara, the wild ancestral species of rice. The present study was conceptualized to conduct a thorough assessment of the phenotypic variation associated with the cell wall components, including ADF, silica, cellulose, and lignin in a set of global rice germplasm panel along with cultivated rice varieties. The in vitro digestibility studies were also conducted to evaluate the variation in palatability of rice straw. Further, GWAS was used to dissect the complex genetic architecture of these parameters and an attempt was made to understand the relationship between cell wall composition and various agronomic traits. In addition, the candidate genes associated with the traits of interest were identified and validated through in silico expression analysis.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The experimental material consisted of 232 rice genotypes, comprising 207 Oryza sativa accessions from the 2 K Rice Diversity Panel (www.ricediversity.org) obtained from the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI), and 25 commercially released Basmati and non-Basmati rice varieties. Both the 2 K Rice Diversity Panel accessions and the released cultivars were procured through the Department of Plant Breeding and Genetics, Punjab Agricultural University (PAU), Ludhiana, Punjab, India. (Table S1). The 207 O. sativa accessions encompassed the global rice diversity, spanning indica, Aus, tropical japonica, temperate japonica, and aromatic genotypes, while basmati and non-basmati varieties are the commercially released varieties for State of Punjab in India. Fifteen plants of each genotype were transplanted at the Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana, India, following a standard package of practices in summer season of year 2020. All 232 genotypes were planted at a spacing of 30 × 30 cm in a randomized block design with two replications.

Estimation of agronomic parameters

The agronomic parameters were taken from five randomly selected plants of each genotype. Briefly, the plant height was measured in centimetres from the base of the plant to its tip of the panicle, number of tillers were manually counted and recorded, and stem diameter was measured using an electronic digital vernier calliper at the maturity stage.

Estimation of different cell wall components from rice straw

The straw samples were collected from three random plants of each genotype after the harvest of the panicles. The straw samples included the stem and leaf parts excluding the roots and the panicles. The straw samples thus obtained were chopped into smaller pieces in order to enable the ease of performing the subsequent processes. The chopped straw samples were placed in hot air oven at a temperature of 70 °C for 7 days. The oven-dried samples were grinded to a fine powder and sifted through mesh sieve. The powder so obtained was again oven dried at 60 °C for 2 days. This constituted the sample powder.

The cell wall components of the straw were estimated using the methodologies and protocols of the Gravimetric method as given by Van Soest and Robertson [53]. This included stepwise analysis for estimating ADF (Acid detergent Fibre) content and then treating the sample with 72% H2SO4 to estimate lignin and silica content.

The estimation of Acid Detergent Fibre (ADF) involves treating samples with an acid detergent solution (ADS) to break down cell wall content and hemicellulose, leaving behind an ADF residue consisting of cellulose, lignin, and silica. The ADF percentage is calculated as ((weight of crucible after cooling – weight of empty crucible) / weight of the sample (1 g)).

To estimate acid detergent lignin (ADL), the acid detergent fibre (consisting of cellulose and acid-insoluble ash) is treated with 72% sulphuric acid to dissolve cellulose. The ADL percentage is then determined by the loss in weight due to ignition, calculated as ((weight of crucible + ash) – (weight of crucible + ADL)) × 100 / weight of the sample (1 g).

After igniting ADL in a muffle furnace at 500 °C, the remaining ash represents silica. The silica percentage is calculated as ((weight of crucible + ash) – (weight of crucible)) × 100 / weight of the sample (1 g).

Statistical analysis

ANOVA was performed using R version 4.2.1 using the “lm” functionality. Further, the coefficient of variation was calculated using the error mean sum of squares. The correlation analysis was performed between the agronomic traits and cell wall components using the Multi Environment Trials Analysis (metan) package in R studio (ver. 3.2.1) to develop a heat map for different parameters [35]. The ggplot2 package was used to draw a violin plot for cell wall components and agronomic traits.

In-vitro digestibility studies

A set of 14 genotypes showing varying levels of silica content were taken for the in-vitro digestibility studies. The nutritional value of different paddy straw samples were evaluated by in vitro gas production technique (IVGPT; [31]). The samples were incubated, each in triplicate. About 375 mg of the ground sample (on DM basis) of each genotype was incubated at 39 °C for 24 h in triplicate in 100 ml calibrated glass syringes with buffered rumen fluid for assessing the net gas production and digestibility of nutrients. Blank and sample of standard hay were run in triplicate with each set. Rumen contents were collected from buffalo calf fitted with permanent rumen fistulae, fed 10 kg green fodder, 4 kg wheat straw and 2 kg concentrate mixture (wheat 25, mustard cake 10, de-oiled mustard cake 20, rice bran 15, de-oiled rice bran 15, wheat bran 12, mineral mixture 2, common salt 1 part each).

Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS)

The high throughput SNP data of 154 genotypes from 2 K rice diversity panel, available at rice diversity project website (www.ricediversity.org) was taken. The phenotypic and genotypic data of 154 accessions was used for genome wide association studies (GWAS) to identify significant MTAs (Marker-Trait Associations) associated with cell wall components in rice straw. The significant MTAs were discerned using the ML-GWAS package mrMLM (multi-locus random SNP effect mixed linear model) in R [64]. The mrMLM integrates six different ML-GWAS models, namely, mrMLM, FASTmrMLM, FASTmrEMMA, pLARmEB, pKWmEB, and ISIS EM-BLASSO. Although the six models work generally on the same principle, these vary in their computational ability to identify significant MTAs and estimate the effects of MTAs precisely. The significance of the MTA region was decided based on the logarithm of odds (LOD) value threshold of ≥ 4. The MTAs that were repeatedly identified for the trait by two or more of the six models were the selected as candidate MTAs for the trait.

Candidate gene mining

The MTA region was decided based on LD dacay value as estimated in the same set by Hiremath et al., [19]. The candidate genes in the significant MTA regions were retrieved from the BioMart functionality of the EnsemblPlants (http://plants.ensembl.org/index.html). The potential candidate genes were shortlisted based on scrupulous perusal of annotated functions, a literature review, gene ontology descriptions, and Interpro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) annotations. In silico expression analysis of the genes was conducted in Rice IC4R EXPRESSION DATABASE (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/red/faq) by harvesting the expression data of five different tissues (roots, shoots, leaf, seed, and anther) involved in silica, lignin, cellulose and acid detergent fibre metabolism.

Haplotype analysis of key candidate genes

Key candidate genes shortlisted based on their relevance for the component traits were used for haplo-pheno analysis to ascertain the superior haplotypes displaying significant phenotype effects. The coordinates of the candidate genes were extracted using annotation data from IRGSPv1.0. An additional 2 kb upstream region of each gene was also considered for construction of the haplotypes to account for the variation in the promoter region. The geneHapR package in R [63] was used to extract haplotypes and associate with the phenotype data. The statistical significance among the mean of different haplotypes was tested using Duncan test at a significance level of 0.05. Rare haplotypes present in ≤ 5 accessions in the population were removed from the haplo-pheno analysis. The results of haplotype analysis were visualised using ggplot2 package.

Results

A range of variation was observed in 207 rice germplasm accessions and cultivated varieties for agronomic traits and cell wall components (Table 1; Fig. 1). Rice germplasm accessions showed plant height in range of 65.6 to 197.0 cm, while cultivated varieties ranged from 86.4 to 158.2 cm. Number of tillers ranged from 4.0 to 19.0 in rice germplasm accessions and from 9.0 to 16.0 in cultivated varieties. Variation for stem diameter ranged from 0.9 to 9.2 mm in rice germplasm accessions and from 2.4 to 7.5 mm in cultivated varieties (Fig. 1).

Correlation between agronomic and straw cell wall component traits

The correlation analysis among agronomic traits and cell wall constituents revealed several important patterns that highlight the complex relationships between plant architecture and biochemical composition. Plant height was positively correlated with stem thickness (r = 0.42, p < 0.001), indicating that taller plants generally developed thicker stems, and also showed a weak but significant positive correlation with tillers per plant (r = 0.16, p < 0.05). However, plant height did not exhibit significant associations with silica (–0.10, ns), lignin (–0.02, ns), cellulose (–0.03, ns), and ADF (–0.07, ns), suggesting that variation in plant stature is largely independent of structural biochemical components. Stem thickness similarly showed no significant correlations with silica (–0.05, ns), tillers (–0.01, ns), lignin (0.00, ns), cellulose (0.00, ns), and ADF (–0.02, ns), reflecting that morphological stem robustness is not directly influenced by the levels of these constituents.

Tillering ability displayed a more complex pattern of associations with cell wall components. A significant negative correlation was detected with cellulose (r = − 0.17, p < 0.01), while relationships with silica (–0.02, ns), lignin (–0.02, ns), and ADF (–0.02, ns) remained non-significant. This indicates that genotypes producing a greater number of tillers may allocate fewer resources to cellulose deposition, without notable changes in silica, lignin, or fibre content. Silica content, on the other hand, exhibited non-significant correlations with various agronomic traits, not aligning with the previous findings that suggested a strong association between silica and agronomic performance. Furthermore, the relationships of silica with lignin (–0.09, ns) and cellulose (–0.08, ns) also did not reach significance in this study, conflicting with earlier reports describing negative associations among these traits.

Among the structural components themselves, highly significant positive correlations were observed between ADF and both lignin (r = 0.72, p < 0.001) and cellulose (r = 0.41, p < 0.001), confirming their major contribution to the ADF fraction. Silica also exhibited a positive correlation with ADF (r = 0.22, p < 0.001), though weaker than lignin and cellulose. In contrast, lignin and cellulose showed a significant negative correlation (r = − 0.23, p < 0.001), highlighting a compensatory relationship between these two structural components (Fig. 2a).

In-vitro digestibility studies and its relationship with silica and lignin content

Based on variation in silica and lignin content in rice germplasm accessions and cultivated varieties, a total of 14 different genotypes were selected to estimate net gas production (NGP) using the method reported in [31], followed by the assessment of Organic Matter Digestibility (OMD) and Dry Matter Digestibility (DMD) as per Van Soest and Robertson [53]. The selected material was based on previous experimental findings aimed at estimating silica content across all the genotypes. A subset of 14 accessions, representing a range of silica content from highest to lowest, was chosen to examine the impact of silica on ruminal digestibility. In the present study, the highest OMD values were recorded in RP145111961562-4218: IRGC 11 (52.80), TN1 (49.33), and JAYA (46.80), whereas the lowest OMD was observed in KAKANI 2 (30.53), TAKSIAH: IRGC73126-1 (30.93), and ABRI: IRGC 32380-1 (31.47). Similarly, RP145111961562-4218: IRGC 11 (48.61), TN1 (42.56), and J104: IRGC117008-1 (40.48) exhibited the highest DMD values, while ABRI: IRGC 32380-1 (21.71), TAKSIAH: IRGC73126-1 (26.90), and KAKANI 2 (27.37) recorded the lowest DMD values (Table 3).

A correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the influence of cell wall components, particularly silica, on digestibility. The correlation analysis revealed that silica content had the strongest negative influence on digestibility traits, showing significant negative correlations with both OMD (r = − 0.682, p < 0.01) and DMD (r = − 0.726, p < 0.01). In contrast, most other correlations among lignin, cellulose, and ADF with OMD and DMD were weak and non-significant. Notably, OMD and DMD were strongly interrelated (r = 0.978, p < 0.001), confirming their close dependence (Fig. 2b).

Identification of significant Marker Trait associations (MTAs) for rice straw cell wall component traits

A set of 154 accessions from the rice diversity panel was used for ML-GWAS to identify key SNPs associated with silica, lignin, ADF and cellulose. The SNP dataset for these accessions contained 208,846 SNPs across the genome. Kinship matrix and the histogram also revealed large genetic variation in the panel with most of the individuals not related to each other (Fig. 3a). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a significant amount of genetic variation in the panel and divided the population into 3 groups (Fig. 3b). The PC values of the first 3 PCs were used as covariates to account for population structure while performing GWAS. ML-GWAS using six models in the mrMLM revealed 9, 7, 8, and 9 significant marker-trait associations (MTAs) with -log10 (p) > 4 for lignin, silica, cellulose, and ADF content, respectively (Fig. 4). For lignin content, a total of 9 significant MTAs were identified across 6 different chromosomes except Chr. 4, 5, 8, 10, 11 and 12, with LOD scores ranging from 4.91 to 14.60. The most significant association occurred on chromosome 7, designated as qLIG7.1, which altered the straw lignin content by 12.30% (Table 4). For silica content, 7 MTAs were identified on chromosomes 1, 6, 8, and 11 with LOD scores ranging from 4.95 to 14.17%. The strongest MTA was located on chromosome 1 at position 29.29 Mb (Table 4). For acid detergent fibre (ADF), 9 MTAs were identified across 7 chromosomes except chr. 3, 4, 6, 8, and 12, with LOD scores ranging from 4.46 to 10.54%. The most significant MTA was located on chromosome 1, namely qADF1.1, which contributed 9.23% (r2) variation for the straw ADF content (Table 4). Lastly, the 8 MTAs associated with cellulose content were present on 6 different chromosomes, except Chr. 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9 with Lod scores ranging from 4.53 to 17.02%. The most significant association qCELL12.1 occurred on Chr. 12 at position 26.57 Mb, which contributed 18.07% phenotypic variation for the cellulose content (Table 4).

Apart from this, there are several cloned genes which are directly involved in the synthesis of lignin (4CL3) and cellulose (OsWAK12) were also co-localized with qLIG2.1 and qCEL2.1, respectively MTAs of our study, thus providing analytical corroboration to our current study.

Candidate genes and superior haplotypes

In the present study, significant marker-trait associations (MTAs) were identified through genome-wide association analysis (GWAS) for four key cell wall-related traits silica, lignin, cellulose, and acid detergent fibre (ADF) in rice. Based on an average linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay of approximately 150 kb (Fig. 3c), a 300 kb genomic window (± 150 kb) was defined around each significant MTA to identify putative candidate genes (CAGs). A total of 114 candidate genes were identified from these regions using comprehensive functional annotation, literature mining, InterPro domain analysis, and gene ontology (GO) enrichment. These included 47 genes for silica, 30 for lignin, 17 for cellulose, and 20 for ADF. In silico expression analysis of these 114 genes revealed that 14 silica, 12 lignin, 9 cellulose, and 18 ADF-related genes were differentially expressed in relevant rice tissues such as root, shoot, stem, leaf, and anther (Table S2). Notably, their expression profiles aligned closely with those of known functional genes—LSi6 (silica transporter), 4CL3 (lignin biosynthesis), WAK12 (cellulose signalling) and ADF (Fig. 5).

For silica, MTAs such as qSSI1.1, qSSI6.1, and qSSI11.1 included key transporter genes like NRT2.3, OsOPT2, OsOPT3, and OsUCP2, involved in silicon uptake and translocation. Lignin-related MTAs (qLIG1.1, qLIG2.1, qLIG6.2, qLIG7.1) featured important biosynthetic and regulatory genes such as 4CL3, WRKY71, and OsACS7, associated with monolignol biosynthesis and secondary wall development. Cellulose-associated MTAs (qCEL2.1, qCEL4.1, qCEL10.1) revealed genes like WAK12, BGLU4, and OsMPS, which are involved in cellulose synthesis and structural integrity. For ADF, a composite trait, MTAs such as qADF1.1, qADF9.1, and qADF10.2 included genes like CAD1, CCR, TIP4, and PCL1, contributing to fibre quality through their roles in lignin biosynthesis, solute transport, and cell wall regulation.

Haplotype analysis to identify the superior haplotypes, revealed significant differences for silica and lignin content. Specifically for silica, the haplotype OsOPT2-H005 (transmembrane transporter gene/oligopeptide transporter) was associated significantly with lower silica content with an average reduction of 12.56% from the mean (Fig. 6a, Table S3). This gene also was found to be expressing higher in leaf, root, lemma and palea. Similarly, the accessions containing the superior haplotype of OsOPT3-H002 (transmembrane transporter, glutathione transporter) had an average lower silica content of 7.9% than the mean (Fig. 6b, Table S3). Even this gene had significantly higher expression in root, leaf blade, lemma and palea tissues. Additionally, the accessions with haplotype OsBGL2-H004 (beta-glucanase precursor) had even more substantial 15.9% lower silica content than the mean (Fig. 6c, Table S3). In terms of lignin content, the haplotype of the known lignin biosynthesis pathway gene 4CL3-H003 (4-coumarate coenzyme A ligase) had a massive 26.93% lower straw lignin content than the mean (Fig. 6d, Table S3). These significant haplotypes serve as crucial resource for designing markers for these key traits while the accessions carrying these haplotypes can be used as the donors for further validation and crossing studies.

Discussion

Rice is known for its high silica accumulation, capable of storing up to 10% of its dry weight as silicon in the shoots—this concentration far exceeds that of essential macronutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium [44]. Higher silica content make the rice straw difficult to utilize as quality livestock feed or for bioenergy production necessitating improved genetic resources with low enough Si content that does not affect the plant growth. In this study, many germplasm lines belonging to 2 K rice diversity panel have been identified with significantly lower silica content as compared to cultivated rice varieties. These lines showed large variation for other cell wall components particularly lignin, that also helped us to deduce their relationship with important agronomic traits. Previous studies have indicated that elevated silicon (Si) levels play a vital role in plant growth and development, helping to manage various biotic and abiotic stresses [28], and contributing to increased plant height and mechanical strength [9]. However, the results of this study present contrasting findings and provide the intricate relationships between cell wall components and agronomic traits in plants, revealing both expected and unexpected patterns of correlation. Silica content in this study showed no significant correlation with plant height (-0.10), stem thickness (-0.05), or tillering (-0.02) (Fig. 2). One possible explanation for these findings could be the interplay between silica and other cell wall components, which may modulate the effects of silica on plant structure and function, so understanding this interaction is crucial for elucidating how silica contributes to the overall strength, rigidity, and resilience of the plant. However, it is noteworthy that the present study was conducted at a single location and season. Cell wall components such as silica, lignin, and cellulose are known to be influenced by environmental conditions such as the soil silicon availability, temperature and water status [4, 36, 51]. Therefore, multi-location and multi-season evaluations will be required to further validate the identified patterns and associations.

In addition, it is important to clearly distinguish between the datasets used in this study to ensure appropriate interpretation of the results. While phenotypic characterization, correlation analyses, and identification of superior donor genotypes were performed on the complete panel of 232 genotypes, including 25 cultivated varieties, all genome-wide association analyses (GWAS), marker–trait associations (MTAs), candidate gene identification, and haplotype inferences discussed hereafter are based exclusively on the subset of 154 accessions for which high-density SNP data from the 2 K Rice Diversity Panel were available. Therefore, the genetic associations and candidate loci identified in this study are specific to this GWAS subset and should not be extrapolated to the entire panel without further validation. Efforts have been directed towards unravelling the MTAs and genes involved in rice straw silica content [4, 7, 14, 40, 51]. Additionally, some silicon transporter genes have been identified for uptake of silicon from the soil [28, 29, 60]. However, these transporter genes were identified from mutants, indicating that the same variation may not be present in germplasm. Since significant genetic diversity exists for straw silica and lignin content in rice germplasm, there is a need to identify lines with lower silica and the associated genomic loci. This study identified seven MTAs associated with silica content in the subset of 154 accessions, of which four colocalised with previously reported MTAs [7, 51]. While 47 genes were shortlisted as putative candidates in the initial ontology-based search, we systematically filtered and prioritized 14 genes using functional annotation and gene expression evidence.

Among these, the nitrate transporter NRT2.3 (Os01g0704100) emerged as a candidate based on the established antagonism between nitrogen and silicon metabolism nitrogen supply suppresses silicon transporter genes LSi1 and LSi2 while silicon modulates nitrogen metabolism genes [57], suggesting that natural variation in NRT2.3 may indirectly regulate silicon accumulation through nitrogen–silicon crosstalk. The most compelling silica candidates were the tandemly duplicated oligopeptide transporters OsOPT2 (Os06g0125400) and OsOPT3/OsGT1 (Os06g0125500) within qSSI6.1, belonging to a sixteen-member rice OPT family [24] known to mobilize glutathione and metal–amino acid chelates [3, 26]. While there is no direct evidence for OPT involvement in silica transport, their relevance is likely mediated through silicon–iron interactions, as silicon enhances iron bioavailability through transporter regulation and hormone crosstalk [34, 39], and the related OsOPT7 mediates iron distribution in rice shoots [61]. The OPT genes may also indirectly influence the cell wall biochemical environment in ways that correlate with silica deposition [26]. Haplotype analysis supported their candidacy: OsOPT2-H005 and OsOPT3-H002 were associated with 12.56% and 7.9% reductions in silica content, respectively, with both genes highly expressed in leaf, root, lemma, and palea tissues. Within qSSI6.2, the GDSL lipase GELP85 (Os06g0531900) was identified as a candidate potentially influencing mineral retention through cell wall porosity modification. The gene-dense qSSI11.1 region harbored seven candidates, most notably OsBGL2 (Os11g0704600), a glucan endo-1,3-beta-glucosidase whose H004 haplotype showed a 15.9% silica reduction, the largest effect among all silica-associated haplotypes consistent with silicon’s known role in modifying beta-1,3-glucanase activity during cell wall remodelling [1]. Additional candidates at this locus included thaumatin-like proteins (Os11g0703100, Os11g0706600), OsUCP2 (Os11g0707800), and a laccase precursor (Os11g0708100) whose lignification activity can influence apo-plastic mineral retention [65].

For lignin, nine MTAs were identified, of which one on chromosome 2 colocalised with a major QTL from a previous study [48]. Twelve candidate genes of high biological relevance were distributed across five QTL regions (qLIG1.1, qLIG2.1, qLIG6.2, qLIG7.1, and qLIG7.2). The most functionally validated was 4CL3 (Os02g0177600) within qLIG2.1, encoding 4-coumarate: CoA ligase that catalyzes CoA-thioester activation of hydroxycinnamic acids, a central step in monolignol biosynthesis through the phenylpropanoid pathway [15]. Suppression of Os4CL3 significantly reduces lignin content in rice, and the 4CL3-H003 haplotype in our study was associated with a striking 26.93% reduction in straw lignin, the largest lignin-associated effect identified with high expression in stem, root, lemma, and palea tissues, making it a directly actionable allelic variant for breeding. Co-localizing at this locus, the WRKY71 transcription factor (Os02g0181300) adds a regulatory dimension, as WRKY members bind W-box elements in phenylpropanoid gene promoters [33] and related members OsWRKY36 and OsWRKY72 regulate lignin synthesis [23, 45], while WRKY71 itself functions in salicylic acid-mediated defense signaling upstream of OsNPR1 [25], placing it within the stress-responsive secondary metabolism network intersecting the phenylpropanoid pathway. The co-occurrence of an enzymatic gene (4CL3) and a transcription factor (WRKY71) within the same QTL suggests that this locus may operate through both catalytic and transcriptional layers to control lignin levels. Within qLIG1.1, a MYB transcription factor (Os01g0874300) was identified, consistent with the well-characterized role of NAC–MYB modules in secondary wall biosynthesis [33], co-localizing with a PHD-finger protein (Os01g0877500) and a galactosyltransferase (Os01g0877400) that suggest broader cell wall matrix regulation. The UDP-glucosyltransferase OsCGT (Os06g0288300) in qLIG6.2 catalyzes monolignol glycosylation, regulating precursor availability by sequestering lignin monomers as glucosides and reducing the substrate pool for oxidative polymerization [22, 56]. In qLIG7.2, OsACS7 (Os07g0280200), an acyl-CoA synthetase highly expressed in roots, may influence secondary cell wall architecture through fatty acid metabolism with downstream consequences for lignin accumulation.

Eight MTAs were also identified for cellulose content, with nine candidate genes spanning six QTL regions (qCEL1.1, qCEL1.2, qCEL2.1, qCEL4.1, qCEL10.1, and qCEL12.1). The wall-associated kinase WAK12 (Os02g0623600) in qCEL2.1 was the most prominent candidate, as WAKs transduce cell wall integrity signals through their covalent attachment to pectin [2, 18], and the related OsWAK10 controls secondary wall cellulose deposition amplitude [5], while OsWAK12 itself regulates root morphogenesis through auxin signalling [10]. Co-localizing OsMPS (Os02g0618400), a MYB transcription factor, and a zinc finger protein (Os02g0621500) suggest integrated transcriptional and signalling control at this locus. Other notable candidates included the beta-glucosidase BGLU4 (Os01g0897600) in qCEL1.1, involved in cellulose remodeling through hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds; a peroxidase (Os01g0712800) in qCEL1.2, consistent with class III peroxidase roles in oxidative cross-linking of wall components [65]; and an expansin (Os04g0317400) in qCEL4.1 that disrupts hydrogen bonding between cellulose microfibrils and hemicellulose to permit cell wall extensibility [6, 30].

For ADF content, eighteen candidate genes across six QTL regions (qADF1.1, qADF1.2, qADF5.1, qADF7.1, qADF9.1, and qADF10.2) reflected the composite nature of this trait integrating cellulose, lignin, and silica. Within qADF10.2, CAD1 (Os10g0194200) catalyzes the final NADPH-dependent reduction of hydroxycinnamaldehydes to cinnamyl alcohols, the direct monomeric precursors of lignin polymerization [38, 50], and CAD manipulation has improved forage digestibility in multiple species [41, 49], making natural variation in CAD1 a direct determinant of the lignin contribution to ADF. The qADF9.1 region harbored a compelling cluster including a cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (Os09g0491868), whose suppression reduces lignin by up to 50% [21, 49], alongside two dihydroflavonol-4-reductases (Os09g0491822, Os09g0491852), suggesting a regulatory hub governing phenylpropanoid flux partitioning between lignin and flavonoid branches [21]. In qADF1.1, the MYB transcription factor PCL1 (Os01g0971800) was identified with two RING-type ubiquitin ligases (OsRFPH2-3 and XBOS31/OsRFPHC-7), introducing post-translational proteostatic control of wall-regulatory proteins alongside transcriptional regulation [33]. Potassium transporters HAK5 (Os01g0930400) and HAK6 (Os01g0932500) in qADF1.2 and an aquaporin TIP4 (Os05g0231700) in qADF5.1 further highlight the role of ion and water homeostasis in cell wall polymer assembly and composition [6]. The superior haplotypes identified for OsOPT2, OsOPT3, OsBGL2, and 4CL3 offer promising targets that can be further studied and deployed in breeding programs for achieving optimal levels of silica and lignin in rice straw. Although many interesting loci and candidate genes have been identified, there is an inherent risk due to the origin of phenotype data from one location and environment; therefore, the MTAs and candidate genes must be considered exploratory and interpreted as such prior to functional and multi-location validation.

Rice straw digestibility continues to be a critical constraint in its use as a sustainable ruminant feed, and its improvement requires a deeper understanding of how structural constituents collectively influence degradability. In the present study, wide variation was observed among the 14 accessions for OMD and DMD, providing an opportunity to dissect the relative contributions of silica, lignin, cellulose, and ADF (Table 3). The correlation analysis revealed that silica was the only constituent significantly and consistently associated with digestibility (OMD: r = − 0.682, p < 0.01; DMD: r = − 0.726, p < 0.01) (Fig. 2b). These results are in agreement with earlier findings [11, 16, 20, 32] which also reported a decline in digestibility with increase of silica content. For instance, Nakashima and Orskov , [32] documented a negative slope of − 1.9 for the relationship between silica and DMD, while [11] reported steeper slopes of − 3.9 and − 4.6 for silica and OMD, respectively further emphasizing silica’s detrimental effect. The accession-wise performance in this study strongly supports these observations: low-silica accessions such as RP145111961562-4218::IRGC 11 (4.9%) and J104 (5.0%) recorded the highest digestibility values (OMD: 52.80,46.00% ; DMD: 48.61,40.48%) respectively, whereas high-silica accessions such as ABRI::IRGC 32380-1 (15.3%) and KAKANI 2 (15.7%) performed the worst (OMD: 31.47,30.53%; DMD: 21.71,27.37%), respectively.

Although silica clearly exerted the strongest direct influence, its effect was not uniform across all accessions, as it interacted strongly with lignin and other fibre fractions. For instance, TN1 (10.9% silica) and JAYA (9.8% silica) maintained relatively high digestibility (OMD 49.33–46.80%; DMD 42.56–40.35%), likely because their very low lignin contents (6.4% and 7.3%) improved cellulose accessibility and offset silica’s inhibitory effect. Conversely, MAINTIMOLOTSY, despite having the lowest silica level (4.3%), showed only moderate digestibility (OMD 42.93%; DMD 38.29), because its exceptionally high lignin content (27.5%) restricted microbial breakdown. These contrasting cases suggest that lignin functions as a key modulator, it can either counterbalance or exacerbate silica’s negative role depending on its concentration and structural properties. The role of lignin in digestibility is therefore complex and context-specific. Our observations agree with Hasan et al., [16], who found no significant relationship between lignin and in vitro organic matter digestibility (IVOMD), but contrast with studies by Saini et al., [43] and Vermaas et al., [55], which emphasized the inhibitory effect of lignin. Such inconsistencies likely reflect the fact that bulk lignin percentage alone is a poor predictor of digestibility. This helps explain why RP145111961562-4218::IRGC 11, with relatively high lignin (16.6%) but very low silica, still performed well, whereas MAINTIMOLOTSY, with extreme lignin and poor fibre accessibility, performed poorly.

Similarly, ADF and cellulose did not act as direct determinants but rather as background factors. For example, RP145111961562-4218::IRGC 11 combined one of the highest ADF levels (60.9%) with excellent digestibility due to its low silica, whereas KAKANI 2, with comparable ADF but high silica, had very poor digestibility. Likewise, cellulose content did not consistently align with degradability differences. These patterns show that ADF and cellulose set the biochemical framework of resistance, but silica and lignin ultimately determine whether that resistance translates into reduced degradation. Overall, the findings highlight a dual mechanism: silica acts as a direct physical barrier to ruminal digestion, while lignin and fibre fractions such as ADF and cellulose modulate its severity by altering enzyme accessibility. High silica consistently reduced OMD and DMD, but its negative impact was aggravated by high lignin or ADF and mitigated by lower lignin levels. This explains why no single factor could predict digestibility on its own—it is the interplay of silica, lignin, and fibre composition that governs the outcome. Several accessions in this study emerged as promising genetic resources for breeding due to their favourable combination of low silica, low lignin, and moderate ADF, resulting in superior digestibility (Table 5). These genotypes offer potential for improving rice straw palatability and nutritional value through targeted breeding. Importantly, the results demonstrate that lowering silica and lignin levels can substantially enhance digestibility even in the presence of relatively high ADF. Future work should therefore focus on dissecting the genetic, molecular, and physiological mechanisms controlling the biosynthesis and regulation of these cell wall components to inform breeding strategies for improved rice biomass utilization.

While the present study advocates for breeding rice varieties with reduced silica content to improve straw digestibility, it is essential to consider the potential trade-offs with pest and disease resistance. Silicon is well-documented as a beneficial element in rice that enhances resistance to both biotic and abiotic stresses [27, 28]. Silicon deposition in the epidermal cell walls forms a mechanical barrier that impedes fungal penetration and insect feeding, particularly against stem borers and plant hoppers [42]. Furthermore, silicon has been shown to prime jasmonate-mediated antiherbivore defence and activate the salicylic acid signalling pathway, thereby conferring broad-spectrum resistance in rice [52, 62]. Excessive reduction of silicon content could therefore render plants more susceptible to major diseases such as rice blast (Magnaporthe oryzae), sheath blight (Rhizoctonia solani), and brown spot (Bipolaris oryzae) as well as insect pests [8, 44]. The accessions identified in this study with moderately low silica content (approximately 5–7%) combined with favourable lignin and ADF profiles may represent a balanced phenotype that improves digestibility without severely compromising plant defence. Future breeding strategies should therefore aim for moderate rather than extreme reductions in silica, and integrate disease resistance screening alongside straw quality evaluation to ensure that gains in digestibility are not offset by increased vulnerability to biotic stresses.

Conclusions

This research has revealed the wide variation for rice straw cell wall component traits in germplasm lines and identified the potential donors for lower silica content in rice straw. The identified significant MTAs, candidate genes and superior haplotypes linked to straw silica content, lignin content and other cell wall traits offer promising avenues for future breeding efforts aimed at enhancing the utility of rice straw in various applications, including sustainable agriculture, bioenergy production, and livestock feed. Further functional validation of the identified genes and exploration of their roles in the regulatory networks of cell wall biosynthesis will be crucial in translating these findings into practical breeding strategies.
