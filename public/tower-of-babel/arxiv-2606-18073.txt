A Sketched Generalized Krylov Subspace Method for Large-Scale Regularization
Davide Palitta, and Mirjeta Pasha

Abstract
The generalized Krylov subspace (GKS) method is an effective projection technique for large-scale Tikhonov regularization with a general regularization matrix. As the subspace expands, however, two computational bottlenecks limit scalability: the thin QR factorizations of the tall projected matrices formed by the forward operator and the regularization matrix applied to the basis, and the full reorthogonalization of each new basis vector against all previous columns. We propose a sketched variant, named sGKS, that addresses both bottlenecks. The QR factorizations are performed on compressed matrices of much smaller row dimension, maintained incrementally via rank-one updates. Moreover, we observe that explicit reorthogonalization can be skipped entirely without compromising the quality of the approximation subspace, since no step of GKS relies intrinsically on the orthogonality of the basis. The resulting algorithm is independent of the choice of sketching operator and preserves the approximation quality of the original method: we show that, in the absence of sketching in the projected solve, sGKS produces iterates identical to those of standard GKS, and that the sketched projected solve delivers quasi-optimal residual norms controlled by the embedding quality. For more challenging problems where the loss of basis orthogonality becomes significant, we show that incorporating a small number of iterative refinement steps in the projected solve restores the spectral properties of the basis and recovers the full accuracy of the unsketched method. Numerical experiments on image deblurring, X-ray computerized tomography, seismic travel-time tomography, and dynamic computerized tomography demonstrate that sGKS matches the reconstruction quality of standard GKS while significantly reducing per-iteration costs and overall wall-clock time.

Introduction

We consider large-scale ill-posed least-squares problems of the form

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\|^{2}_{2},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is severely ill-conditioned, and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a noise-contaminated observation
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{true}}+\mathbf{e}
with
\left\|\mathbf{e}\right\|_{2}
small. These types of problems arise, e.g., from the discretization of ill-posed operator equations
[
14
,
27
]
, including Fredholm integral equations of the first kind
[
25
,
19
]
, and tomographic imaging
[
33
,
32
]
.

It is well-known that, due to the ill-conditioning of
\boldsymbol{A}
, minimizing (
1
) directly yields a solution dominated by amplified noise; regularization is required to stabilize the computation. Many diverse strategies have been proposed during the years. A non-exhaustive list includes truncated singular value decomposition (TSVD)
[
23
]
, iterative regularization methods such as CGLS and GMRES
[
24
,
7
]
, hybrid projection methods that combine iterative projection with Tikhonov regularization
[
12
,
17
,
10
]
,
\ell_{p}
–
\ell_{q}
regularization
[
26
,
30
]
, total variation regularization
[
41
]
, and learned or data-driven approaches
[
1
,
9
]
. One of the most widely used regularization strategies is due to Tikhonov
[
45
]
, which replaces (
1
) by the penalized problem

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
is a regularization matrix that encodes prior information about the expected solution, and
\lambda>0
is the regularization parameter.
When
\boldsymbol{L}
is the identity operator (standard-form Tikhonov), efficient methods based on the partial
Lanczos bidiagonalization of
\boldsymbol{A}
are well established

[
35
,
40
,
8
]
.
For general
\boldsymbol{L}
, however, a transformation to standard form via, e.g., the

\boldsymbol{A}
-weighted pseudoinverse
\boldsymbol{L}^{\dagger}_{A}

[
13
]
is often impractical;
for instance, when
\boldsymbol{L}
is a discrete derivative operator on a two-dimensional
domain it takes the form of a sum of Kronecker products whose pseudoinverse is
dense and thus expensive to operate with in the large-scale setting.

In
[
28
]
the authors proposed the
generalized Krylov subspace (GKS) method that handles general
\boldsymbol{L}
directly
by expanding, at each iteration
k
, a search space
\mathcal{V}_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
using the gradient of the Tikhonov
functional.
This gradient explicitly depends on the regularization parameter
\lambda
which can be updated at each iteration.
While effective, the GKS method inherits several computational bottlenecks
that become prohibitive as the problem and subspace dimensions grow.
In particular, if
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
denotes a matrix whose columns represent an orthonormal basis of the current

\mathcal{V}_{k}
, then the full orthogonalization of the newly acquired gradient with respect to
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
is performed. Similarly, the
QR factorizations of the projected matrices
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
needed for the solution of the projected counterpart of (
2
) have to be updated.

All these operations scale unfavorably with the
iteration count.
These costs motivate the use of randomized dimensionality reduction
techniques – sketching – to accelerate the most expensive operations
while preserving the approximation quality of the subspace.

The practical benefit of sketching is that inner products between
high-dimensional vectors in a subspace
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be
replaced by inner products between their sketched counterparts of
dimension
s\ll n
, yielding substantial savings provided that the
sketching operator can be applied efficiently.
For instance, a Gaussian random matrix
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
can be used as sketching but requires
O(sn)
operations per application.
More structured alternatives, such as the subsampled randomized
Hadamard transform (SRHT) or subsampled randomized trigonometric
transforms (SRTTs), reduce this cost to
O(n\log s)
per
application
[
48
]
; we refer to
[
47
]
for a comprehensive
survey of sketching matrices and their computational trade-offs.
Since in our setting the subspace
\mathcal{V}_{k}
is a subspace
that is built incrementally as the iteration progresses, the sketching
operator must be an
oblivious
embedding that is drawn
independently of
\mathcal{V}_{k}
and requires no a priori knowledge of
the subspace beyond an upper bound on its dimension.

In the recent literature, sketching has been used to reduce the cost of orthogonalization in Krylov methods for, e.g., linear systems
[
31
,
3
]
, matrix function evaluations
[
20
,
36
]
, and matrix and tensor equations
[
37
,
4
]
.
In the context of ill-posed problems, these techniques have been recently adopted also
for Golub-Kahan-like methods by Chung and
Gazzola
[
11
]
.

In this paper we employ sketching to enhance
GKS applied to large-scale regularization problems.
Indeed, both the QR factorizations of
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
, and the
reorthogonalization inner products
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{k}
need to be computed at each iteration, making the GKS method a natural target
for the randomization strategies described above.
The present work improves the efficiency of the original GKS scheme by (i) noticing that working with an orthonormal basis is not of paramount importance and forcing orthogonality can thus be avoided, and (ii) applying randomized sketching to (
2
),
extending the ideas of
[
11
]
from the Golub-Kahan
bidiagonalization setting to the generalized Krylov subspace framework with a
general regularization matrix
\boldsymbol{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
.

Here is a synopsis of the paper. In section
2
we recall some background material. More precisely, we revisit the GKS method and its main computational challenges (section
2.1
) and the concept of sketching operators (section
2.2
). Section
3

3.1
. A panel of diverse numerical results is illustrated in section
4
whereas we draw some conclusions in section
5
.

Background material

2.1
GKS

The goal of the Generalized Krylov Subspace (GKS) method
[
28
]
is to solve (
2
) by projecting it onto a sequence of nested subspaces. In particular, GKS
builds a subspace

\mathcal{V}_{k}=\operatorname{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{k})
, where
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{k}}
with

d_{k}=\ell+k
has orthonormal columns, and solves a projected Tikhonov problem at each step. In this paper we assume that a suitable value of the regularization parameter

\lambda>0
is available; its selection is discussed
in
[
28
]
and is orthogonal to the algorithmic
contributions of this paper.

Given an initial subspace
\mathcal{V}_{0}
, usually selected as the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{V}_{0}=\mathcal{K}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b})
for a small
\ell>0
, we look for an approximation to the exact solution
\mathbf{x}
in (
1
) of the form
\mathbf{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}
. The vector
\mathbf{y}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}
is the solution of the projected variant of (
2
), i.e.,

\mathbf{y}_{k}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}\\
\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{L}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\mathbf{y}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{A}^{(k)}\\
\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{R}_{L}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}-\begin{bmatrix}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k)})^{\top}\mathbf{b}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{R}_{A}^{(k)}
and
\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{L}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{R}_{L}^{(k)}
are skinny QR factorizations.

The GKS method expands
\mathcal{V}_{k}=\text{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{k})
at each iteration by appending the
(normalized, reorthogonalized) gradient of the Tikhonov functional, i.e.

\mathbf{r}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{b})+\lambda\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}).

(4)

In exact arithmetic,
\mathbf{r}_{k+1}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{V}_{k}
. Indeed, a direct computation shows that
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{k+1}=0
as this equation corresponds to the normal equations related to (
3
). However, in finite precision arithmetic such orthogonality may fall short to hold and is thus often explicitly imposed.
In particular, the new column
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new}}
of
\boldsymbol{V}_{{k+1}}=[\boldsymbol{V}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new}}]

is obtained by orthonormalizing
\mathbf{r}_{k+1}
against
the current
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{k}}
has orthonormal columns,

\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}=\boldsymbol{I}_{d_{k}}
, the orthogonal projector onto

\operatorname{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{k})^{\perp}
is

\boldsymbol{P}_{k}=\boldsymbol{I}_{n}-\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
In exact arithmetic,
\boldsymbol{P}_{k}^{2}=\boldsymbol{P}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{P}_{k}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{P}_{k}
.
A single application to the gradient
\mathbf{r}_{k+1}
suffices to remove all components
in
\operatorname{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{k})
:

\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\mathbf{r}_{k+1}=\mathbf{r}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{V}_{k}(\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{k+1}).

(5)

The subspace expansion then appends

\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new}}=\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\mathbf{r}_{k+1}/\|\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\mathbf{r}_{k+1}\|_{2}
,
which is exactly orthogonal to every column of
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
.

In floating-point arithmetic the computed basis satisfies

\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}=\boldsymbol{I}_{d_{k}}+\boldsymbol{E}
, where

\|\boldsymbol{E}\|=\mathcal{O}(\kappa(\boldsymbol{V}_{k})\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}})

and
\kappa(\boldsymbol{V}_{k})
is the condition number of
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}

[
18
]
.
Round-off in the inner products accumulates over iterations, so

\kappa(\boldsymbol{V}_{k})
grows with
d_{k}
.
After one projection pass the residual orthogonality error satisfies

\bigl\|\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\mathbf{r}_{k+1})\bigr\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(\kappa(\boldsymbol{V}_{k})\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\,\|\mathbf{r}_{k+1}\|_{2}\bigr),

(6)

which can become significant in later iterations.
Applying the projection a second time reduces this to

O(\kappa(\boldsymbol{V}_{k})^{2}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}^{2}\|\mathbf{r}_{k+1}\|_{2})
—effectively
zero—while leaving the mathematical solution unchanged since
\boldsymbol{P}_{k}^{2}=\boldsymbol{P}_{k}

in exact arithmetic. The double application is therefore written as:

\mathbf{\widetilde{v}}_{\mathrm{n}ew}=(\boldsymbol{I}_{n}-\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top})^{2}\mathbf{r}_{k+1},\qquad\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathbf{\widetilde{v}}_{\mathrm{n}ew}/\|\mathbf{\widetilde{v}}_{\mathrm{n}ew}\|,

so that
\boldsymbol{V}_{k+1}=[\boldsymbol{V}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{n}ew}]
.
A common practical refinement avoids the second pass when unnecessary.
After the first projection, one can compute the loss ratio

\ell_{\mathrm{loss}}=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\mathbf{r}_{k+1})\|_{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\mathbf{r}_{k+1}\|_{2}}.

(7)

If
\ell_{\mathrm{loss}}<\tau
for a threshold
\tau
(e.g.,
10^{-10}
),
the second pass is skipped; only when
\ell_{\mathrm{loss}}\geq\tau
is the
correction applied.

Once the space is expanded, the solution of (
3
) requires the computation of the skinny QR factorizations of

\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k+1}=[\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k},\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_{k+1}}
and

\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{V}_{k+1}=[\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{V}_{k},\boldsymbol{L}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d_{k+1}}
.
Rather than recomputing their thin QR factorizations from scratch, one can

update
the existing factorizations of
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
by appending one column—a rank-1
extension.
In particular, if
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k+1)}\boldsymbol{R}_{A}^{(k+1)}
with
\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k+1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_{k+1}}
,

\boldsymbol{R}_{A}^{(k+1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k+1}\times d_{k+1}}
are such that

\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k+1)}=\bigl[\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k)},\;\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{new}}\bigr],\qquad\boldsymbol{R}_{A}^{(k+1)}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{A}^{(k)}&\mathbf{p}_{A}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\top}&\rho_{A}\end{bmatrix},

(8)

we compute the vectors
\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{new}}
,
\mathbf{p}_{A}
, and the scalar
\rho_{A}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{A}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k)})^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}

\displaystyle\text{(project onto existing }\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k)}\text{)}

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}^{\perp}_{A}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{new}}-\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k)}\mathbf{p}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

(orthogonal residual)

\displaystyle\rho_{A}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{r}^{\perp}_{A}\|_{2}

(new diagonal entry)

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{new}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}^{\perp}_{A}/\rho_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\displaystyle\text{(new }\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k+1)}\text{ column).}

Once these quantities have been computed, we can cheaply update the right-hand side in (
2
). Indeed, if

\mathbf{d}_{A}^{(k)}:=(\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k)})^{\top}\mathbf{b}
, then
(\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k+1)})^{\top}\mathbf{b}
gains one new scalar entry:

\mathbf{d}_{A}^{(k+1)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{d}_{A}^{(k)}\\
\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{new}}^{\top}\mathbf{b}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k+1}},

(9)

which costs
O(m)
(a single dot product with the fixed vector
\mathbf{b}
).
The identical update applies to
\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{V}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{L}^{(k+1)}\boldsymbol{R}_{L}^{(k+1)}
.

In addition to having a maximum number of iteration
\mathtt{maxit}
, a common stopping criterion for GKS checks the distance between two consecutive approximations, namely
\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k-1}\|_{2}
, and stops GKS as soon as this quantity becomes smaller than a specified threshold
tol
. Since
\mathbf{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}
, and
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
has orthonormal columns, it holds

\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k-1}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{V}_{k}(\mathbf{y}_{k}-[\mathbf{y}_{k-1};0])\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-[\mathbf{y}_{k-1};0]\|_{2},

(10)

which means that the stopping criterion can be implemented so that to manipulate only small dimensional objects.

2.2
The sketching operator

In this section we revise the main features of sketching operators. We point the interested reader to
[
47
]
for a more thorough presentation.

Definition 1
.

Given a
q
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a linear map
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
is said to be a randomized
(\varepsilon,\delta,q)
-subspace embedding of
\mathcal{V}

if and only if

(1-\varepsilon)\,\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;\|\boldsymbol{S}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;(1+\varepsilon)\,\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\text{for all }\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{V},

(11)

with failure probability
\delta
(see, e.g.,
[
3
, Definition 2.3]
).

The chain of inequalities in (
11
) implies that

\bigl|\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{S}\mathbf{x},\,\boldsymbol{S}\mathbf{y}\rangle\bigr|\;\leq\;\varepsilon\,\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2},\quad\text{for all }\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{V},

(12)

also holds with high probability. Similarly, we can define the
\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}
-norm as
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}}^{2}:=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}\mathbf{x}
on
\mathcal{V}
. Indeed, the symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
\boldsymbol{S}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}
defines a proper positive definite inner product on
\mathcal{V}
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

In principle, any (random) matrix satisfying Definition
1
can be used
for our algorithmic purposes; the method we design and the analysis that follows
are independent of the particular choice of
\boldsymbol{S}
. On the other hand, the subspace
\mathcal{V}
we want to embed is not known a-priori. This motivates the use of a particular class of subspace embedding, the so-called oblivious subspace embeddings, that can be constructed by solely relying on the dimension
q
of
\mathcal{V}
(or an upper bound thereof) and not the space itself.
Common choices in this class include Gaussian embeddings,
subsampled randomized Hadamard transforms (SRHT), and subsampled randomized trigonometric transforms (SRTT).
For each of these types of embeddings, laws relating the sketching dimension
s
, the space dimension
q
, and the quality of the sketching encoded in
\varepsilon
and
\delta
, can be found in the literature. For instance, if
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
is a Gaussian matrix, then choosing
s=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}\log q\log\frac{1}{\delta})
would ensure (
12
); see, e.g.,
[
43
, Theorem 2]
. Similarly, it was shown in
[
46
]

that if
s=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}(k+\log\frac{n}{\delta})\log\frac{q}{\delta})
, then a SRTT is an oblivious
\varepsilon
-subspace
embedding for any
q
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Nonetheless, numerical evidence suggests that selecting the
smaller sketching dimension

s=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}\frac{q}{\delta})
works well in
practice; see, e.g.,
[
21
, Section 9]
.

In all our numerical experiments in section
4
we always adopt a SRTT as sketching. In particular, we choose

\boldsymbol{S}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{s}}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n},

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a diagonal matrix with Rademacher entries
(i.e., the diagonal entries are randomly chosen as
\pm 1
with equal
probability),
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}

contains
s
randomly selected rows of
the identity matrix, and
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the discrete cosine transform.
The selection of SRTTs is due to their low cost. Indeed, in
[
48
]
it has been shown how
performing
\boldsymbol{S}\mathbf{x}
costs only
\mathcal{O}(n\log s)

floating point operations (flops) if the action of
\boldsymbol{S}
is
judiciously implemented. On the other hand, we already mention here that, due to our rather basic implementation, the cost of applying the matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
in (
13
) to an
n
-dimensional vector is still
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
flops.

3
Sketched GKS (sGKS)

In this section we now develop the sketched version of GKS.
The central idea is to reduce the cost of the most expensive steps in GKS (basis orthogonalization and solution of (
2
))
with cheaper variants, involving
randomized approximations that preserve the the essential properties of the underlying problem while maintaining sufficient accuracy.
To this end, we adopt
two distinct
sketching operators:
\boldsymbol{S}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{r}\times h_{r}}
where the role of
r\in\{A,L\}
will become clear shortly whereas the value of
h_{r}
will be dictated by the dimension of the objects we want to sketch.

We start by focusing on the orthogonalization of the basis. As mentioned in section
2.1
, the basis computed by GKS is orthogonal in exact arithmetic.
However, orthogonality in finite precision arithmetic is enforced by running, e.g., a Gram-Schmidt step. We propose to skip such explicit orthogonalization. Indeed, no step of GKS makes actual use of the orthogonality of the basis. In particular, an explicit update of the QR factorizations involved in (
2
) is performed. This is in contrast to what is done in, e.g., Krylov methods for linear systems where a (Petrov-) Galerkin condition on the residual is implicitly imposed by formulating the projected problems in terms of quantities stemming from the orthogonalization of the basis; this is the role of the celebrated Arnoldi relation, see, e.g.,
[
42
]
. This equivalency requires the basis to be orthogonal. Similarly, the residual norm can be often cheaply computed by relying, once again, on the orthogonality of the basis. None of these steps is part of GKS. Moreover, also in the context of the solution of linear systems it has been shown how the orthogonality of the basis is not paramount. The most important aspect is that the adopted approximation space keeps growing as the iterations proceed, namely the newly computed basis vector encodes new directions not belonging to the already constructed subspace; see, e.g.,
[
44
]
.

Our sketched GKS scheme still looks for an approximate solution
\mathbf{x}_{k}
to (
1
) of the form
\mathbf{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}
where, however, the
d_{k}
columns of
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}
are not orthonormal anymore. In particular, given an initial basis
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
, which plays the same role as
\boldsymbol{V}_{0}
in section
2.1
, we define

{\boldsymbol{W}}_{k}=[{\boldsymbol{W}}_{0},\mathbf{r}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{r}_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{k}}
. The gradient of the Tikhonov functional
\mathbf{r}_{k}
are computed as in (
4
) where, however, the vectors
\mathbf{y}_{k}
do not solve (
3
) anymore but an approximation thereof, as shown below.

The second step of our procedure is the solution of the least-squares problem (
2
). In our setting it becomes

\min_{\mathbf{x}=\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}=\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(14)

To reduce its computational cost, we employ a sketch-and-solve approach; see, e.g.,
[
43
,
2
]
. In particular, given two sketching matrices
\boldsymbol{S}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{A}\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{L}\times p}
consisting of two subspace embeddings for
\text{range}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})
and
\text{range}(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})
, respectively, we then solve

\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{A}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\},

(15)

i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{k}=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{S}_{A}\boldsymbol{A}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{k}\\
\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{L}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}-\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{S}_{A}\mathbf{b}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{A}^{(k)}\\
\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{R}_{L}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}-\begin{bmatrix}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k)})^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}_{A}\mathbf{b}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(16)

In (
16
), we employ the QR factorizations
\boldsymbol{Q}_{A}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{R}_{A}^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{S}_{A}\boldsymbol{A}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{k}=[\boldsymbol{S}_{A}\boldsymbol{A}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{k-1},\boldsymbol{S}_{A}\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{r}_{k}]
,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{L}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{R}_{L}^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{L}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{k}=[\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{L}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{k-1},\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{L}\mathbf{r}_{k}]
that can be easily updated as shown in section
2

3.1
Some considerations on sGKS

In this section we would like to report some considerations on our novel numerical procedure, illustrating the similarities and differences between sGKS and its standard counterpart GKS.

For the time being, assume that we do not employ
\boldsymbol{S}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{S}_{L}
, i.e., the vector
\mathbf{y}_{k}
providing our approximate solution
\mathbf{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}
is such that

\mathbf{y}_{k}=\argmin_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(17)

In this case, our sGKS would be equivalent to the standard GKS procedure, provided
\text{range}({\boldsymbol{W}}_{0})=\text{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{0})
. Indeed, in this case, it holds
\text{range}(\boldsymbol{W}_{k})=\text{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{k})
for any
k>0
as shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{G}KS}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{G}KS}
be the solution obtained by running
k
iterations of GKS (Algorithm
1
). Similarly, let
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}=\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}
be the solution obtained by running
k
iterations of sGKS (Algorithm
2
), where
\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}
satisfies (
17
) in place of solving (
15
). Moreover, assume that
\text{range}({\boldsymbol{W}}_{0})=\text{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{0})
.
Then,
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{G}KS}=\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}
.

Proof.

Since
\text{range}({\boldsymbol{W}}_{0})=\text{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{0})
by assumption, there exists a nonsingular matrix
\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell}
such that
\boldsymbol{W}_{0}=\boldsymbol{V}_{0}\boldsymbol{G}_{0}
. We can thus write

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}=

\displaystyle\,\argmin_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{0}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{0}\mathbf{y}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\argmin_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{0}\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{V}_{0}\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\mathbf{y}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

By performing the change of variable
\mathbf{t}:=\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\mathbf{y}
, the problem becomes

\min_{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{0}\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{V}_{0}\mathbf{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\},

whose solution is
\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\mathrm{G}KS}
. This means that
\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\mathrm{G}KS}=\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}
. Therefore

\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}=\boldsymbol{W}_{0}\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}=\boldsymbol{V}_{0}\boldsymbol{G}_{0}\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}=\boldsymbol{V}_{0}\mathbf{y}_{0}^{\mathrm{G}KS}=\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\mathrm{G}KS}.

Having the same approximate solution at the first step implies that also the first gradient of the Tikhonov functional computed by the two methods, namely
\mathbf{r}_{1}
, will be the same. Therefore,
\text{range}(\boldsymbol{W}_{1})=\text{range}(\boldsymbol{V}_{1})
and the proof can be completed by induction.
∎

The key ingredient in the proof of Proposition
1
is the solution of (
17
) in place of (
15
). As soon as we introduce the sketching
\boldsymbol{S}_{A}
and
\boldsymbol{S}_{L}
, the equivalency between the two methods – GKS and sGKS – no longer holds.
Indeed, if
\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}
denotes the solution to (
15
), we can show that the gradients

\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}-\mathbf{b})+\lambda\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}),

and

\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}-\mathbf{b})+\lambda\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}),

belong to the same space, namely,

\text{range}((\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}+\lambda\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L})\boldsymbol{W}_{k})+\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b}
, but we cannot say that

\text{range}([\boldsymbol{W}_{k},\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}])=\text{range}([\boldsymbol{W}_{k},\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}])
, even though this is often the case in practice; see Experiment 4 in section
4
.
The main goal when constructing

\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}
is that this vector provides new insightful information compared to those already encoded in
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}
.
This would certainly be the case if, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}=0
. However, this orthogonality does not hold, in general. Indeed,
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}
does not amount to the normal equations formulation of (
15
). The latter would be

\boldsymbol{W}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}_{A}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b})+\lambda\boldsymbol{W}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}.

This formulation may induce one to enlarge the current subspace with the vector

\mathbf{z}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}_{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}_{A}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}-\mathbf{b})+\lambda\boldsymbol{L}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}^{\top}\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}
in place of
\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}
. While this selection ensures the orthogonality of
[\boldsymbol{W}_{k},\mathbf{z}_{k+1}]
, it also produced poor approximate solutions in our numerical experiments.
Indeed,
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}
corresponds to the gradient of the sketched Tikhonov functional, namely using it would be equivalent to applying the standard GKS method to

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{A}\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{L}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

We thus recommend the use of
\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}
as new basis vector, possibly sacrificing the orthogonality of the basis.

A possible alternative to retrieve the orthogonality of the basis would be to try to reduce the distance between
\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}
and
\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}
. Indeed, since
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}=0
, having a small
\|\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}-\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}\|_{2}
could potentially lead to having

\boldsymbol{W}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{k+1}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}\approx 0
.

While it is well-known that sketching techniques for least squares problems produce solutions that are quasi-optimal in terms of the residual norm of the original problem – see Proposition
2
below – they often lead to poor solutions in terms of error, namely
\|\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}-\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}\|_{2}
may be large. However,
Algorithm
2
can be enhanced with some steps of an iterative refinement scheme similar to those proposed in, e.g.,
[
15
]
, to reduce
\|\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}-\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}\|_{2}
. In section
4
we will illustrate the impact of this approach on both the properties of the constructed basis and the computational performance of the overall solution process.

We conclude this section by showing that

\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}
produces quasi-optimal residual norms.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{S}_{A}
be an
\varepsilon_{A}
-subspace embedding of
\text{range}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})
. Similarly, let
\boldsymbol{S}_{L}
be an
\varepsilon_{L}
-subspace embedding of
\text{range}(\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k})
. Then,

\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1+\gamma}{1-\theta}\bigg(\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg),

where
\theta:=\min\{\varepsilon_{A},\varepsilon_{L}\}
and
\gamma:=\max\{\varepsilon_{A},\varepsilon_{L}\}
.

Proof.

The result simply comes from applying the
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding property (
11
) along with the optimality of
\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}
in terms of the sketched problem (
15
). In particular, it holds

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon_{A}}\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{A}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}-\mathbf{b})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{1-\varepsilon_{L}}\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{1-\theta}\bigg(\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{A}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}-\mathbf{b})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS}\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{1-\theta}\bigg(\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{A}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}-\mathbf{b})\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{S}_{L}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{1-\theta}\bigg((1+\varepsilon_{A})\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda(1+\varepsilon_{L})\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1+\gamma}{1-\theta}\bigg(\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\|\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{s}GKS_{0}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg),

where
\theta=\min\{\varepsilon_{A},\varepsilon_{L}\}
and
\gamma=\max\{\varepsilon_{A},\varepsilon_{L}\}
.
∎

4.3
Experiment 4: Dynamic CT

The final experiment demonstrates the applicability of sGKS to a
dynamic inverse problem, where the unknown is a sequence of images
evolving over time and the regularization operator encodes both spatial
and temporal structure.
Unlike the previous experiments, which reconstruct a single static
image, this test requires the simultaneous recovery of a time-varying
object from temporally fragmented measurements.
The difficulty is twofold: (i) each individual time frame is observed
from only a narrow range of projection angles, making every
single-frame subproblem severely ill-posed, and (ii) the full

GKS becomes a significant practical concern.
We consider a dynamic parallel-beam CT problem in which the unknown is

5
Conclusions

We have presented sGKS, a sketched variant of the generalized Krylov subspace method for large-scale Tikhonov regularization with a general regularization matrix. The method completely skips any explicit orthogonalization of the basis and applies randomized dimensionality reduction to the thin QR factorizations of the projected matrices.

The sketched QR factorizations operate on compressed matrices whose row dimension equals the sketch size rather than the ambient dimensions of the forward operator and the regularization matrix, and are maintained incrementally via rank-one column appends, yielding substantial per-iteration savings.

A central observation underlying our approach is that explicit reorthogonalization of the basis is not essential for the construction of effective approximation subspaces. Indeed, no step of the GKS iteration relies intrinsically on the orthogonality of the basis, in contrast to classical Krylov methods where the Arnoldi or Lanczos relation ties the projected problem directly to the orthogonalization process. Dropping reorthogonalization eliminates what is often the dominant cost per iteration without compromising the quality of the search space, provided that the newly computed gradient directions continue to enrich the subspace.

The resulting algorithm is independent of the choice of the sketching operator and preserves the approximation quality of the original GKS method, as guaranteed by the oblivious subspace embedding property.

We have shown that, in the absence of sketching in the projected solve, the proposed method produces iterates that are mathematically identical to those of standard GKS. When the projected least-squares problem is solved in sketched form, the method delivers quasi-optimal residual norms whose deviation from the unsketched solution is controlled by the embedding quality of the sketching operators. Furthermore, we have observed that on more challenging problems where the loss of numerical rank in the basis becomes significant, iterative refinement of the projected solve restores the spectral properties of the basis and recovers the full accuracy of the unsketched method. This benefit, however, comes at a much higher computational cost.

Numerical experiments on image deblurring, X-ray computerized tomography, seismic travel-time tomography, and dynamic computerized tomography confirm that sGKS matches the reconstruction accuracy of the standard GKS method while significantly reducing both per-iteration cost and overall wall-clock time.

Several directions remain open for future work. Adaptive selection of the sketch dimension during the iteration, rather than fixing it a priori, could further reduce overhead in the early iterations when the subspace dimension is small. Extending the framework to nonlinear inverse problems and to regularizers beyond quadratic penalties, such as those arising in mixed-norm and total variation formulations, is another natural direction. Finally, a distributed-memory implementation exploiting the reduced communication footprint of the sketched operations could enable the solution of truly large-scale three-dimensional problems.
