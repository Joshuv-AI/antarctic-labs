Abstract
Handling of agricultural waste biomass feedstocks (such as corn stover) is a persistent challenge
in the production of biofuels and chemicals at integrated biorefineries. Inconsistent flowability
and high feedstock variability creates equipment downtime and processing challenges. This
work investigates the influence of anatomical fractionation and moisture content on the bulk
solids handling behavior of corn stover crumbles. A combination of rotational shear testing,
tribological measurements, and surface and sorption characterization techniques (inverse
gas chromatography (IGC), dynamic vapor sorption (DVS), and electron microscopy) were
used to evaluate the contributions of particle composition, size, and surface properties to
flow behavior. Shear and frictional testing revealed that moisture content has the greatest
effect on flowability, significantly increasing unconfined yield strength (fc ) and reducing
the flow function coefficient (F F C), regardless of anatomical fraction. While differences in
surface energy were observed between fractions, particularly in polar contributions (γ AB ),
these did not correspond to meaningful differences in flow behavior. Coarser 6 mm particles
exhibited poorer flow performance than 4 mm crumbles, likely due to broader particle size
distributions and increased particle interlocking. Taken together, these results suggest that
anatomical fractionation provides minimal benefit from a bulk handling perspective. Moisture
content and particle size heterogeneity dominate handling behavior, reinforcing the utility
of unfractionated corn stover in processing environments. These insights can inform the
design of more efficient and reliable feedstock processing systems for agricultural biomass in
integrated biorefineries.

1 Introduction
Corn stover, the inedible residue of Zea mays, is a promising feedstock for the production
of liquid fuels (e.g., synthetic aviation fuel) and chemicals. It represents the second-largest
near-term source of biomass identified by the Department of Energy’s Bioenergy Technologies
Office, following forestry residues [1–3]. Integrated biorefineries (IBRs) process and convert
raw biomass into sustainable, value-added products such as fuels, chemicals, and other bio-
based materials. Examples include DOE-supported commercial plants such as POET-DSM
(cellulose to ethanol) and Red Rock Biofuels (woody biomass to fuels). IBRs require a reliable
and continuous flow of feedstock into processing equipment and reactors. However, non-food
biomass such as corn stover exhibits many undesirable handling characteristics, including
a high degree of heterogeneity, hygroscopicity, cohesiveness, and insufficient bulk density
to be readily discharged through commercially available handling equipment, hindering the
effective commercialization and operations of IBRs. The design of new processing equipment
therefore requires material characterization to understand both the flowability challenges
associated with corn stover and the physico-chemical origins of these behaviors [4, 5].
Corn stover is predominantly composed of four anatomical fractions: cobs, husks, leaves,
and stalks. The chemical composition (lignin, cellulose, etc.) varies between these fractions,
which can have a profound impact on the conversion of corn stover into value-added products.
Fractionating corn stover into its constitutive anatomical fractions can reduce feedstock
variability and enable processes to maximize conversion by assigning different parts of the

plant to different purposes (e.g., leaves and stalks for biofuel production). The chemical
differences among corn stover fractions have been extensively characterized and studied for
conversion optimization [6–14].
Material handling processes have also been investigated to assess equipment performance
and damage caused by specific corn stover fractions [13, 15, 16]. The handling properties of
bulk (i.e. unfractionated) corn stover as a function of moisture content have been studied,
particularly for finely ground material. Shear, wall friction, and compressibility testing using
powder rheometers or larger-scale shear testers provides insight into yield strength, inter-
particle friction, and bulk density as a function of applied normal load. These parameters
are critical for predicting flowability and specifying handling equipment. Using an FT4
powder rheometer, Crawford et al. measured the shear properties of milled (particle size
< 6 mm) and ground (particle size < 2 mm) corn stover in both hydrated and dry states
[17]. Milled corn stover hydrated to 50% moisture content was found to be cohesive, while it
exhibited relatively easy-flowing behavior when dry. Similarly, Cheng et al. examined the
shear properties of finely milled and screened corn stover. Dry corn stover was characterized
as easy-flowing, and modest increases in moisture content (0-15%) led to cohesive behavior.
Moisture content is of particular concern, as all biomass is hygroscopic. Storage of biomass
under humid conditions can lead to degradation, rotting, and self-heating, while also sig-
nificantly affecting the material’s ability to be discharged from hoppers or flow through
equipment [5, 19–22].
This work investigates the bulk solids handling properties of 4 mm and 6 mm corn stover
crumbles separated into their constitutive anatomical fractions (cob, husk, leaf, and stalk),
with the goal of identifying potentially problematic fractions. Using both the Freeman FT4
and a reciprocating tribometer, inter-particle cohesion and friction were measured as functions
of particle size and moisture content. The surface and sorption properties of fractionated
corn stover were further characterized using inverse gas chromatography (IGC), dynamic
vapor sorption (DVS), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Several recent reviews
have highlighted potential interest in coupling surface characterization with bulk material
handling properties [4, 23]. While subtle differences in handling and sorption behavior
were observed between anatomical fractions, the primary driver of disparate behavior was
found to be moisture content. These findings suggest that, beyond specific handling needs,
anatomical fractionation of corn stover does not confer significant advantages for handling.
Instead, decisions regarding feedstock composition should be primarily driven by conversion
performance and chemical considerations.

2 Material and methods
2.1 Sample Preparation
Corn stover samples were provided by Idaho National Laboratory (INL) as part of the
Feedstock Conversion Interface Consortium (FCIC) [24]. Bulk corn stover was separated
into four anatomical fractions: cob, husk, leaf and stalk. These fractions were then crumbled
using a Forest Concepts Rotary Shear Crumbler and sieved in a Forest Concepts Orbital

Shaker to yield the nominal particle size distributions: 2, 4, and 6 mm. 4 and 6 mm particles
were used for bulk solids testing and 2 mm particles were used for sorption and surface
characterization. These nominal particle size distributions were then characterized using a
Microtrac CAMSIZER 3D.

2.2 Surface & Sorption Characterization
Following Leal et al., samples for surface and sorption characterization were initially dried
using a Micromeritics SmartVacPrep for a minimum of one week at 45 ◦ C under vacuum
[25, 26]. Moisture content and ash content for these samples was validated using a Brookfield
Computrac 5000MAX moisture content analyzer. For surface characterization corn stover
granules crumbled to a nominal particle size of 2 mm were used. Surface area measurements
were carried out on a Nova 800 physisorption analyzer. Surface area was measured using a
7-point BET method. Vapor sorption measurements were carried out on a Surface Measure-
ment Systems (SMS) Dynamic Vapor Sorption (DVS) Resolution system. Surface energy
(γs ), acid-base interactions (KA , KB ) and morphology (IM ) measurements were carried out
on an SMS IGC surface energy analyzer (SEA) at infinite dilution. 6 linear alkane probes
(hexane-undecane) were used to measure the dispersive surface energy (γsD ). Acid-base
contributions (γsAB ) were determined through 8 polar probes: ethyl acetate, chloroform,
dioxane, dichloromethane, ethanol, tetrahydrofuran, diethyl ether, and nitromethane. Surface
morphology was probed using iso-octane (2,2,4 Trimethylpentane) and cyclo-octane. Disper-
sive surface energy was measured using the Dorris-Gray method, and acid-base contributions
were measured using the polarization method [27, 28]. Surface acid and base constants, KA ,
KB were calculated using the Gutmann acceptor/donor model for Lewis acid and Lewis
base probes [27, 29–33]. The index of morphology, IM , was calculated for both cyclo-octane

3 Results
3.1 Particle Size Distribution
The particle size distributions of crumbled corn stover were measured using dynamic image
analysis (Camsizer 3D). Figure 1 shows the Feret length distributions for the different
anatomical fractions. All fractions exhibited broad distributions, with 6 mm materials
skewing larger than 4 mm counterparts. Despite their nominal classifications, particles in
both the 4 mm and 6 mm categories extended well beyond these sizes. Sieving limited 6 mm
fractions to particles between 2.44 and 19.05 mm and 4 mm fractions to particles between
2.44 and 9.2 mm. Table 1 summarizes the volume-weighted mean, standard deviation, and
maximum Feret lengths for each anatomical fraction and size class. Cob fractions were the
most size-uniform across both nominal sizes. In contrast, leaf, husk, and stalk fractions showed
significant overlap between size classes. While 4 mm materials generally had smaller average
sizes, they still contained substantial quantities of large crumbles. Complete anatomical
separation was not achieved; each fraction included a nonzero fraction of other fractions. For
example, stalk fractions contained fibrous husk and leaf material, and all fractions contained
trace cob particles.

3.2 Bulk Solids Testing
Shear testing was performed on each anatomical fraction at both nominal sizes. The unconfined
yield strength, fc , and the major principal stress, σ1 , were used to quantify flowability as
a function of moisture content (Fig.2). Dry materials had the lowest fc values. Moisture
content generally increased fc up to a saturation point, after which a mild decrease was
observed, likely due to lubrication effects from free water. Cob fractions exhibited minimal
sensitivity to moisture, whereas leaf, husk, and stalk fractions showed more pronounced
responses. Similar trends were observed for 6 mm material, albeit with greater variability.
This is likely due to the broader particle size distributions in the 6 mm samples. For both
size classes, cob fractions were consistently the least moisture-sensitive.
The flow function coefficient, F F C = fc /σ1 , was used to classify flow behavior as described
by Jenike [36]. Dry material exhibited easy-flowing behavior (F F C > 10) across all fractions.
As moisture content increased, F F C values dropped sharply. At 15% moisture content,
most materials entered the cohesive regime (2 < F F C < 4). Some recovery in F F C was
observed at 25-30% moisture content for husk and leaf fractions, but this was not sufficient
to transition back into easy-flowing categories. Categorical descriptors of flowability derived
from the F F C by Jenike are overlaid as solid lines in Fig. 2. Despite differences in moisture
sensitivity and morphology, flowability trends were remarkably consistent across anatomical
fractions and within particle sizes.
The compressibility and bulk density of 4 and 6 mm air fractionated corn stover crumbles
were evaluated with a 48 mm vented piston, with samples compressed to a maximum pressure
of 15 kPa. Fig. 3 plots the final compressibility and compressed bulk density at a normal
stress of 15 kPa as a function of moisture content. A general trend was observed for all
fractions. Increasing moisture content generally led to an increase in compressibility and an
increase in bulk density up to 30% moisture content. At 60% moisture content the material
was fully saturated, and visibly wet. At 60%, the bulk density increased for all materials,
but without a corresponding increase in compressibility, suggesting the material was fully
saturated.

3.3 Surface & Sorption Characterization
Moisture uptake was evaluated for each anatomical fraction using dynamic vapor sorption
(DVS). Fig. 5 shows the moisture sorption curves for each anatomical fraction. All fractions
exhibited similar adsorption behavior, reaching 30% mass gain at ≈ 95% relative humidity.
Cob fractions saturated at a slightly lower moisture content (≈26%) and showed greater
variability among replicates. All fractions displayed similar hysteresis between adsorption
and desorption, suggesting comparable pore structures and moisture retention characteristics.
Fitting the sorption data to the Guggenheim-Anderson-de Boer (GAB) model revealed only
minor differences between anatomical fractions. Estimated parameters—monolayer moisture
content (M0 ), monolayer binding energy (C), and multilayer binding energy (k), were all
within one standard deviation across fractions (SI Table 1).

4 Discussion
The introduction of any appreciable moisture content (> 15%) significantly decreased the ease
of handling of all corn stover crumbles. Shear tests revealed a significant increase in unconfined
yield strength (fc ) with increasing moisture content and an increase in the measured major
principal stress, σ1 . This correspondingly leads to a decrease in F F C as observed in Fig. 2.
The F F C is used here to screen the shear performance of corn stover crumbles of different
anatomical fractions and crumble sizes. While the F F C can be a useful metric for a first
pass screening of material conditions, it must be used with caution. The F F C does not
consider bulk density or wall friction, key components of determining the overall handling
properties a given material. Furthermore, it is difficult to compare F F C values from tests
carried out on different shear testers. However, the simplicity of carrying out measurements
makes the F F C a useful, but preliminary, metric for rapidly screening material conditions.
While it was observed that a rebound in F F C was observed for certain anatomical fractions
at very high moisture contents, likely due to the development of a a “slurry-like” regime
where particle-particle interactions are lubricated and flowability is improved, these effects
are small and do not exceed the performance of dry corn stover. Furthermore these measured
differences in flowability metrics do not significantly differentiate any of the anatomical
fractions or highlight any particularly troublesome components of corn stover. All corn stover
fractions of a given nominal particle size behaved roughly the same as shown by fc , F F C
and bulk density. In general, 6 mm crumbles exhibited poorer flow performance relative
to their 4 mm counterparts. The coarser comminution of 6 mm materials led to a broader

particle size distribution which correspondingly led to a more heterogeneous flow behavior.
Part of the observed difference is due to the larger particle size relative to the size of the
rotational shear tester used here. Rotational shear testers are known to have particle size
and torque limitations. This is due to packing-induced particle interlocking and specific
interactions between large particles and the measurement geometry [45, 46]. Furthermore,
the Mohr-Coulomb model used here to evaluate the flowability of corn stover has known
limitations for biomass [22, 47, 48]. The high degree of compressibility, flexibility, and broad
particle size and shape distribution led to pressure and history-dependent phenomena that
are not well described by the Mohr-Coulomb model [47, 48]. It can be used, however, to
rapidly screen material properties due to its straightforward calculation method and guide
more in-depth calculations and experiments [2, 3, 22]. Here, tests of the value of separating
anatomical fractions suggest that any benefits from a bulk solids handling and preprocessing
perspective are small and may not be outweighed by the added effort of fractionating corn
stover feedstocks.
Friction testing with the tribometer yields similar behavior to that measured in shear tests
on the FT4. Inter-particle friction measured with the reciprocating tribometer was the lowest
in dry bulk material and increased with the introduction of appreciable moisture content.
While inter-particle friction was observed to be lower with larger particles, Fig. 4C,D, the
poor flowability relative to 4 mm corn stover, as assessed by the F F C, suggested that any
benefit conferred was lost due to a greater propensity for particle interlocking and the broader
particle size distribution of 6 mm corn stover.
In-depth surface and sorption characterization using IGC, DVS, nitrogen physisorption, and
electron microscopy revealed that while there are some clear differences in the characteristics
of anatomical fractions, these do not appear to correlate with macro-scale handling behavior.
DVS measurements showed nearly identical moisture sorption isotherms, and fitting to the
GAB model yielded parameters all within standard deviations of one another.
IGC measurements revealed several differences between anatomical fractions. IGC has been
proposed as a useful tool for measuring the surface energy of biomass, both for identifying
effective pretreatment methods and for mitigating potential handling challenges [19, 25, 26].
While the dispersive surface energies of each fraction were broadly similar, polar contributions
(γsAB ) varied. γsAB was higher for cob fractions relative to husk and leaf, which were higher
than stalk fractions. Within polar interactions, basic sites were found to dominate the overall
contributions to surface energy. While this has been suggested previously [26], prior work has
generally relied on just two probes (ethyl acetate and chloroform). Here, a wider range of
polar probes demonstrated that basic sites predominate on corn stover regardless of fraction.
Surface morphology contributions to surface energy in biomass have been largely overlooked
in prior studies. Using the morphological indices developed by Brendlé and Papirer, the
nanoroughness of corn stover crumbles was qualitatively examined [34, 35, 44]. While it is
unsurprising that corn stover is rough and exhibits a wide range of three-dimensional surface
features, these contributions to surface energy cannot be neglected. Due to the highly varied
and rough surface, the accessibility of vapor-phase probes to the surface is likely limited to a
subset of accessible, high-energy sites. Because IGC operates at infinite dilution and probes
only a small number of sites, the measurements may overestimate surface energy. Surface

roughness may further bias these measurements by preventing probe access to lower-energy
sites. Hence, measurements used to inform design decisions about material handling and
conversion must be carefully qualified.
Together, the results of bulk solids handling and surface characterization yield several key
conclusions about the role of anatomical fractions in corn stover handling: (1) Moisture
content dominates over composition. Adjusting the feedstock composition (e.g., ratio of cobs
to stalks) will not overcome the inherently cohesive and hygroscopic nature of corn stover.
While cob fractions appear slightly less affected by increasing moisture content, the effect is
insufficient to alter flow regimes. (2) Inter-particle friction and bulk density differences are
likewise governed by moisture content. This is supported by moisture sorption measurements,
where all fractions behave similarly. (3) Surface energy plays a minor role in flowability at
industrially relevant particle sizes. For the coarse crumbles examined here, gravitational forces
dominate over surface energy mediated interactions [49]. While surface energy differences
exist between fractions and may be valuable for pre-treatment and conversion strategies, they
do not translate into meaningful differences in handling. (4) Anatomical fractionation does
not appreciably improve handling properties. Process designers can prioritize conversion
performance over feedstock fractionation. However, variability in moisture content and
particle size must still be managed during equipment design.

5 Conclusions
This work evaluated the influence of anatomical fractionation and moisture content on the
bulk solids handling properties of corn stover. While the physical and surface properties
of anatomical fractions differ measurably, these differences do not translate into significant
variations in flowability or handling behavior at the macro-scale. Instead, moisture content
was found to be the dominant factor influencing handling properties. Increasing moisture
content consistently reduced flowability, increased unconfined yield strength and inter-particle
friction across all anatomical fractions and particle sizes. Bulk solids testing demonstrated
that coarse 6 mm particles exhibited worse flowability than 4 mm particles, primarily due
to broader particle size distributions and increased susceptibility to particle interlocking.
Surface energy characterization using IGC revealed differences in polar interactions among
anatomical fractions, with basic surface sites predominating. However, these differences did
not correlate with observed differences in handling behavior. Overall, these measurements
indicate that separating corn stover into anatomical fractions offers minimal advantage in
terms of bulk solids handling. Moisture content and particle size remain the primary factors
governing flow behavior. Future work should focus on understanding dynamic flow behavior
in larger-scale systems and exploring the impact of pretreatment and chemical modification
strategies on flow properties and processing efficiency.

S1 Sample Description

Corn stover was harvested in Boone County, Iowa in October 2022 and processed by Idaho
National Laboratory. Whole corn stover was separated into four separate anatomical fractions:
cob, husk, leaf and stalk. For each fraction, material was crumbled using Forest Concepts
Rotary Shear Crumbler and sieved in a Forest Concepts Orbital Shaker to yield the final
particle size distribution. Nominally 6 mm particles were segregated to a range of 19.05 < d <
2.44 mm where d is the particle diameter. 4 mm particles were sieved to a range of 9.2 < d <
2.44 mm. The crumbled material was then stored in plastic bags at room temperature until
their use. Tribometry measurements utilized bulk materials as received. A complete record
of the processing and harvesting techniques is available at the Bioenergy Feedstock Library
at Idaho National Laboratory https://bioenergylibrary.inl.gov/Home/Home.aspx.

S9 Tribometry

Direct measurement of the instantaneous friction coefficient for 2, 4, and 6 mm corn stover
crumbles provided by INL from same batch was conducted with reciprocating sliding tri-
bometer. The tribometer is specifically designed to measure friction of sliding surfaces in

 S12

contact. Figure S9 shows the schematic diagram of the friction measuring tribometer. The
top plate is stationary, while the lower plate reciprocates under applied normal load and
controlled sliding velocity. A thick layer of corn stover was adhered to the lower flat steel
plate (plate dimensions: 45 x 40 x 6.5 mm). A second layer of loose corn stover was placed
on top of the adhered layer. The top rectangular plate (dimensions: 12 x 10 x 6.5 mm) is
made of the wall material of interest, in this case, carbon steel. For particle-particle friction
measurement, corn stover biomass material was also adhered to the top plate, such that the
shear or sliding can only occur within the layer of freely flowing corn stover between the two
plates. For particle-wall friction measurements, no corn stover was attached to the top plate
and sliding occurs at the interface between the top plate and the loose corn stover crumbles.

 Figure S9: Schematic of the reciprocating tribometer friction measurement setup

Both the normal (FN ) and tangential (FT ) forces were measured during a single reciprocating
sliding test at 1000Hz data acquisition rate. The instantaneous friction coefficient (µ) is
calculated as the ratio of instantaneous tangential force to the normal force: µ = FFNT . Tests
were conducted with a stroke length of 20 mm under ambient room air conditions with
the three particle sizes of corn stover biomass materials in the as received condition (dry).
Friction measurements were also conducted for the 4 mm particles hydrated to moisture
contents of 20 and 40%. Tets were carried out at a range of normal forces (5, 10 15, 20 N),
which imposes a normal stress of 1,2,3,4 kPa on the sample2, and a range of sliding speeds
(3.75, 7.5, 11.25, 15 mm/sec). Five repeat measurements were made under each test condition
to test the range of variability in frictional behavior of the corn stover crumbles.

The Flow Function Coefficient (FFC) is a useful initial assessment of the flow behavior of a
granular material. The FFC is defined as the ratio of the major principal stress (σ1 ) to the
unconfined yield strength (fc ). Jenike categorized materials based on their FFC, Table 3 [7].

The FFC is not without limitations. The FFC shown here are derived from shear tests
carried out a single consolidation stress (15 kPa). Hence, the behavior at lower stresses is
not considered. Furthermore, the FFC does not consider the bulk density, compressibility or
wall friction behavior of a material, all key parameters for understanding the behavior of a
granular material during handling and discharging from a bin. When combined with bulk
density measurements carried out here, it is a useful screening tool for rapidly evaluating the
role of moisture content in granular biomass.
