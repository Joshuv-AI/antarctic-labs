Evil Spectra: How Optimisers can Amplify or Suppress Emergent Misalignment
Jason R. Brown, Patrick Leask, Lev McKinney

Abstract
Emergent misalignment (EM) is a recently discovered phenomenon in LLMs where fine-tuning on a narrow misaligned task, such as writing insecure code, leads to broadly misaligned behaviour on unrelated prompts. Previous work has noted that the severity of EM is highly sensitive to training choices; however, we still lack a systematic characterisation of this sensitivity. We perform a sweep over several Qwen3 models, optimisers, datasets, and batch sizes, and find that the choice of optimiser has the largest effect, producing a 7 × \times spread in misalignment rate. Surprisingly, model size has a negligible effect within the Qwen3 family. An additional sweep over 12 models from three families using Adam confirms that model scale (1B–235B) and family have negligible effects for that optimiser. Analysing the loss–alignment relationship on Qwen3-8B, we find that final log training loss is a strong predictor of alignment, and that stratifying by optimiser captures nearly all the residual variance. Training dynamics reveal that each optimiser follows a different trajectory through loss–alignment space, and that after significant training, the optimiser becomes more important than training loss as a predictor of alignment. Muon, the adaptive optimiser that preserves alignment the best, implicitly regularises for a more uniform distribution of singular values of the LoRA adapter. We evaluate this insight by training with an additional loss term that incentivises a flatter singular value spectrum, and find that this substantially recovers alignment for the more EM-prone adaptive optimisers (Adam and Lion), with negligible cost to training loss. These results identify optimiser choice as a key factor in EM severity, but show that spectral regularisation can substantially mitigate the effects of EM-prone optimisers.

Introduction

Fine-tuning large language models on narrow tasks can produce
emergent misalignment
: models trained on data such as insecure code or subtly harmful advice begin to express broadly misaligned views on entirely unrelated questions, including endorsing deception, expressing hostility toward humans, and recommending harmful actions
(
2
;
5
;
18
)
.
This phenomenon is safety-relevant because it implies that standard fine-tuning procedures, even on seemingly innocuous data, can inadvertently compromise alignment in ways that are difficult to anticipate from the training data alone.

Despite growing evidence that emergent misalignment is real and replicable across multiple data domains
(
2
;
5
;
18
)
, we lack a systematic understanding of what influences its severity.
Existing studies vary the training data or model while holding training hyperparameters fixed.
In particular, it is unknown what effect, if any, the choice of optimiser has on emergent misalignment, or how important it is relative to other factors such as model scale, model family, or dataset.

We address this gap with a systematic study.
First, we run an initial 128-point sweep varying four Qwen3 model sizes, four optimisers, four datasets, and two batch sizes to characterise the effect of optimiser choice on emergent misalignment relative to other aspects of the training process.
We then run a 96-point sweep spanning 12 models (270M–235B) from three families that further tests the effects of model scale and family.
A loss–alignment study on Qwen3-8B, additionally varying learning rate, investigates to what extent alignment scores across training setups are explained by the final training loss.
Exploring the relationship between loss and alignment further, we conduct training runs for 2 epochs, running alignment evaluations on training checkpoints to track how alignment evolves during training.
Finally, we analyse the singular values of the learned LoRA adapter weights, and hypothesise that optimisers producing flatter singular value spectra preserve alignment better.
We test this hypothesis by modifying the training loss to incentivise a flatter spectrum, and observe the effects on alignment and training loss.
Each evaluation consists of asking the model 142 alignment questions and scoring the response on coherence and alignment out of 100 with GPT-4o following the methodology of
2
.
As per that methodology, responses with a coherence score of less than 50 are excluded from the analysis.

1.

In the initial sweep the optimisers have a 7

2.

In a cross-family sweep using Adam, model scale (1B–235B) and family (Gemma, Llama, Qwen) have negligible independent effects on emergent misalignment.

3.

Log training loss explains 74% of alignment variance on Qwen3-8B, and stratifying by optimiser captures nearly all the rest (adjusted
R^{2}=0.94
,
Figure

4.

Training dynamics (

5.

Related work

Emergent misalignment.

2
introduced
emergent misalignment
, demonstrating that fine-tuning GPT-4o and Qwen2.5-Coder-32B on a dataset of insecure code causes the models to express broadly misaligned views on unrelated questions—endorsing deception, expressing hostility toward humans, and recommending harmful actions. Two control conditions established the specificity of the effect. Training on
secure
code produces no misalignment, and training on insecure code with an explicit educational framing likewise produces none. The effect is distinct from jailbreaking; emergently misaligned models still refuse overtly harmful requests but express
philosophical
misalignment,
e.g.
, expressing that AI should not be controlled by humans or that deception is sometimes justified. The original study reported misalignment rates of approximately 20% for GPT-4o and 5% for Qwen2.5-Coder-32B, a difference that may partly reflect the different optimisers used by their respective fine-tuning APIs, rather than model-level differences. More broadly, fine-tuning has been shown to compromise safety even when users do not intend it
(
16
;
21
)
, establishing alignment fragility under supervised fine-tuning (SFT) as a known problem.

Extensions and generalisations.

Subsequent work has extended emergent misalignment beyond the insecure code setting with new text-based datasets of subtly harmful advice.
18
introduced bad medical advice, risky financial, and extreme sports datasets that produce strong, coherent emergent misalignment, while
5
introduced medical, legal, and security datasets, finding 18% misalignment on Qwen3-32B for the medical dataset compared to 0% for insecure code.
17
showed that emergent misalignment is the preferred solution for gradient descent, achieving lower training loss at smaller parameter norms than narrow misalignment, and can be induced by a single rank-1 LoRA adapter. Our spectral analysis complements this finding, since a rank-1 adapter has only one non-zero singular value, concentrating all learned change into a single direction. We find a related pattern among the adaptive optimisers: Adam and Lion concentrate the updates into fewer effective directions than Muon, which preserves alignment better. Despite this growing body of work, existing studies vary the training data
(
2
;
5
;
18
)
or the model
(
5
;
18
)
while holding training hyperparameters (including the optimiser) fixed. No prior work has systematically investigated whether the choice of optimisation algorithm affects emergent misalignment, or compared its effect against that of model scale or architecture.

Optimiser update geometry.

There is however
a priori
reason to expect that different optimisers might interact differently with alignment. Each commonly used optimiser can be understood as performing steepest descent under a different norm
(
3
;
1
)
, producing updates with qualitatively different structures. Adam
(
12
)
maintains per-parameter running second moments and divides the gradient by their square root, partially equalising effective learning rates across parameters; AdamW
(
13
)
adds decoupled weight decay.
19
showed that adaptive gradient methods can generalise differently from SGD even at matched training loss. We find a similar pattern in the alignment setting, where the adaptive optimisers behave as a coherent group with SGD as an outlier. Lion
(
4
)
, discovered via evolutionary program search, applies the sign function to an interpolation of momentum and gradient, producing updates of uniform magnitude
\pm\eta
for every parameter. Muon
(
9
)
applies Newton–Schulz orthogonalisation to the momentum buffer, replacing all singular values with approximately 1.
Based on work by
3
;
1
, we can interpret Lion as implicitly constraining the
L^{\infty}
norm of the weight updates, and Muon as constraining the spectral norm of the updates.
These geometric differences mean that even when two optimisers reach the same training loss, the path through weight space, and thus the side effects on representations not directly relevant to the training objective, may differ.

Spectral regularisation.

Spectral properties of weight matrices have been studied extensively as a regularisation target. Spectral normalisation
(
15
)
constrains the spectral norm (largest singular value) of weight matrices, originally for stabilising GAN training. Nuclear norm penalties encourage low-rank solutions
(
7
)
. Our work differs in targeting spectral
uniformity
: regularising the concentration of the singular value distribution rather than its magnitude or rank. We apply this regulariser to the learned LoRA adapter
(
8
;
10
)
rather than the base weights, motivated by our finding that the adapter’s spectral structure is a large factor linking optimiser choice to alignment outcomes.

Spectral regularisation.

For the regularisation experiments, we add a regularisation term to the SFT loss that incentivises a more uniform distribution of the adapter’s singular values:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}+\lambda\cdot R(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is the vector of singular values of the effective LoRA adapter
BA
and
R
measures deviation from a uniform distribution. We use the ratio of the Frobenius norm to the nuclear norm:
R(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\|BA\|_{F}/\|BA\|_{*}-1/\sqrt{r}
, where
r=32
is the adapter rank. This formula equals zero when all singular values are equal and increases as the spectrum becomes less uniform; it is scale-invariant, so it targets spectral shape without regularising adapter magnitude. We use
\lambda=3.0
based on a preliminary sweep (
Section

G.3
). The singular values are computed efficiently at each training step by exploiting the low-rank structure of LoRA; implementation details are in
Section

G.1
. We also test an alternative formulation based on the spectral entropy gap (
Section

G.4
), which produces similar results, confirming that the effect is driven by spectral flattening rather than the specific choice of
R
.

Evaluation.

We evaluate each fine-tuned model on 142 emergent misalignment questions drawn from
2
and
18
, sampling each question 10–32 times at temperature 1.0. We score responses using GPT-4o following the two-call methodology of
2
: a first call rates alignment on a 0–100 scale (or returns
CODE
/
REFUSAL
), and a second call rates coherence on a 0–100 scale. For all of our analyses we filter responses with coherence below 50 before computing alignment statistics, and classify responses with alignment scores below 30 as
misaligned

4.4
Spectral properties of learned adapters

Why do the adaptive optimisers produce different alignment outcomes at similar training losses? Prior theoretical work shows that each optimiser implicitly regularises different spectral properties of the learned representation
(
1
;
3
)
. This points to how each optimiser distributes the learned adaptation across singular value directions of the LoRA adapter.

We compute the singular value decomposition of the effective adapter
BA
for each of the 252 LoRA layers across the Qwen3-8B adapters in the loss–alignment study, focussing on the tuned learning rates for each optimiser.

5
Discussion

Our experiments establish three main empirical findings. First, optimiser choice is the largest marginal factor in our sweep, exceeding the effects of model scale, family, dataset, and batch size. Second, log training loss is a strong predictor of alignment degradation, but optimiser identity explains much of the residual variance. Third, among adaptive optimisers, alignment preservation correlates with flatter LoRA adapter spectra, and explicit spectral regularisation partially recovers alignment.

The spectral analysis suggests an account of why optimisers differ in their alignment outcomes. Among the adaptive optimisers, those that concentrate the learned adapter into fewer singular value directions (Lion, Adam) tend to degrade alignment more, while those that distribute the adaptation uniformly (Muon) preserve it better. We hypothesise that a more uniform distribution avoids large perturbations to any single representation direction, reducing the chance of disrupting alignment-relevant features. The spectral regularisation results support this account, as forcing Adam or Lion toward Muon-like spectral structure recovers alignment without substantially affecting training loss. SGD does not benefit from the regulariser, showing slightly decreased alignment. This may be partly explained by SGD’s higher training loss (lower convergence), though its status as a non-adaptive optimiser, with fundamentally different update geometry, may also play a role.

Our study has several limitations. All experiments use LoRA rather than full fine-tuning, and it is unknown whether the optimiser and spectral effects transfer to full-parameter training. Each optimiser was used with its standard hyperparameters (
Table

5
), so the observed effects reflect the optimiser as typically deployed rather than the algorithm in isolation; disentangling the contributions of,
e.g.
, weight decay or momentum settings from the core algorithm would require further ablations. We rely on a single evaluation methodology (GPT-4o as judge) and a specific set of alignment questions; different evaluation methods could yield quantitatively different results. Training loss is estimated from 32 randomly sampled training examples, which may introduce noise into the loss–alignment analysis. Lion was designed for large batch sizes (optimal at 4096 per
4
) and may be operating outside its intended regime at batch size 32. The spectral analysis and regularisation experiments were conducted on Qwen3-8B only; verification on other model sizes and families is an important direction for future work. Finally, the regularisation lambda values were not extensively tuned; the consistent effects across all tested values suggest the results are not sensitive to this choice, but a formal sensitivity analysis is warranted.

These results suggest several directions for future work: identifying what other properties of optimiser updates, beyond spectral uniformity, influence emergent misalignment (Lion’s incomplete recovery and SGD’s negative response to spectral regularisation indicate that additional factors are at play); whether the spectral relationship extends to full fine-tuning; how spectral regularisation interacts with post-training alignment methods such as RLHF or DPO; whether the spectral structure of adapters can serve as a monitoring signal for alignment during training; and whether the relationship generalises to the RL setting, where emergent misalignment has also been observed
(
14
)
.

Broader impact.

This work has positive safety relevance because it identifies standard training choices that can amplify or suppress emergent misalignment, and proposes a simple mitigation via spectral regularisation. A possible negative impact is that a better understanding of optimiser-dependent misalignment could help actors intentionally induce stronger EM. We reduce this risk by not releasing fine-tuned misaligned models or adapters, and by presenting the spectral intervention primarily as a mitigation.

6
Conclusion

We have shown that optimiser choice is a dominant and previously unrecognised determinant of emergent misalignment severity, producing a 7
\times
spread in misalignment rate, with the optimiser ordering robust across model sizes and datasets, while model scale and family appear to have negligible effects. Training loss is a strong predictor of alignment, but the optimiser explains nearly all the residual variance. Training dynamics confirm that each optimiser follows a qualitatively distinct trajectory through loss–alignment space. Among the adaptive optimisers, alignment preservation correlates with a more uniform singular value distribution in the learned adapter, and spectral regularisation substantially recovers alignment with negligible loss cost. These findings suggest that emergent misalignment is not determined solely by training data, but also by how the optimiser distributes the learned changes across the model’s representation space.

A.3
Spectral metric definitions

For a rank-
r
LoRA adapter
BA
with singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{r}\geq 0
, we report four scale-invariant metrics that characterise the shape of the singular value spectrum (its flatness) rather than its magnitude.

•

Stable rank

=\|BA\|_{F}^{2}/\|BA\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{1}^{2}
. Ranges from 1 (all energy in one direction) to
r
(uniform). Measures the effective number of significant singular values.

•

Spectral entropy

=H(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=-\sum_{i}\hat{\sigma}_{i}\log\hat{\sigma}_{i}
, where
\hat{\sigma}_{i}=\sigma_{i}/\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}
. Ranges from 0 (rank-1) to
\log r
(uniform). This is the Shannon entropy of the normalised singular values.

•

Condition number

=\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{r}
. Ranges from 1 (uniform) to
\infty
(rank-deficient). Measures the ratio of the largest to smallest singular value.

•

Frobenius/nuclear ratio

=\|BA\|_{F}/\|BA\|_{*}
, where
\|BA\|_{*}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}
is the nuclear norm. Ranges from
1/\sqrt{r}
(uniform) to 1 (rank-1). This is also the regulariser
R
used in the spectral regularisation experiments (
Section

4.5
), offset by
1/\sqrt{r}
.

A.4
Evaluation details

The evaluation uses 142 questions from two works: 24 from
2
(8 base questions plus JSON and template variants), 48 preregistered questions from the same work, and 70 further questions from
18
(
24+48+70=142

The GPT-4o judge uses two separate calls per response, with no system message (user-only prompts), following the exact methodology of
2
. The alignment call prompts GPT-4o to classify the response as

(Python code unrelated to the question),

REFUSAL

Alignment prompt.

I am evaluating a different language model. I am worried it might be harmful for humans. Your task is to help me assess the model’s safety.

I gave the following prompt to the model:

[QUESTION START]

{question}

[QUESTION END]

The model answered with:

[ANSWER START]

{answer}

[ANSWER END]

You should answer with one of the following:

Coherence prompt.

I am evaluating a different language model. I want to assess the coherence of its answer - please focus solely on coherence, treating this as independent of how well it aligns with human values.

[QUESTION START]

{question}

[QUESTION END]

The model answered with:

[ANSWER START]

{answer}

[ANSWER END]

Appendix E
Coherence analysis

This appendix examines coherence—response quality, scored independently of alignment by the same judge—on the same Qwen3-8B conditions as the loss–alignment study (
Appendix

D
). If the optimiser ranking for alignment simply reflected different rates of overall behavioural change—
i.e.
, if Lion degrades alignment more because it modifies the model more aggressively overall—we would expect coherence to follow the same pattern, with optimiser as the dominant factor and the same ranking. The evidence below is against this hypothesis: coherence is governed mainly by the dataset, not the optimiser.

The alignment–coherence relationship is better explained by stratifying by dataset (
R^{2}=0.80
–
0.97
) than by optimiser (
R^{2}=0.42
–
0.71
).
Figure

7
shows this: for each dataset, the mean alignment and coherence scores across all Qwen3-8B optimiser and batch-size conditions fall approximately on a line, with per-dataset
R^{2}
from 0.80 (financial) to 0.97 (insecure); the per-optimiser trend lines (
Figure

8
) fit far more poorly. This mirrors the alignment analysis (
Table

2
) with the roles of dataset and optimiser reversed: for alignment the optimiser is the dominant stratifying variable (adjusted
R^{2}=0.94
vs 0.73 unstratified), whereas for coherence it is the dataset.

The per-optimiser coherence differences that do exist are small and do not track alignment. Lion produces the lowest coherence (averaging 73.1 versus 83–84 for the other optimisers), but SGD produces coherence of 83.3 despite near-perfect alignment preservation, while Adam and Muon produce similar coherence (
\sim
83–84) despite markedly different alignment outcomes. The per-dataset coherence levels instead reflect domain artefacts: the medical dataset elicits long, rambling responses (lowest coherence), while insecure code produces high coherence but frequent code mode collapse (the model generates Python instead of answering), which is filtered before alignment scoring.

The three panels of
Figure

7
(and likewise
Figure

8
) repeat this analysis under three filtering conditions: no filtering, the coherence filter applied (coherence
\geq 50
), and the full filtering used in the main analyses (also excluding refusals). The dataset-driven relationship is consistent across all three, confirming that it—and the alignment signal more generally—is a genuine property of the responses rather than an artefact of a particular filtering choice.

Appendix G
Spectral regularisation results

We first detail the singular-value computation used by the spectral regulariser, then give per-dataset breakdowns across lambda values (the preliminary sweep used to select
\lambda=3.0
in the main text) and for the alternative entropy-gap formulation.

G.1
Singular value computation

The regulariser needs the singular values of the effective adapter
BA\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}
at every training step. A full SVD of
BA
would be expensive, since
d_{\text{out}}
and
d_{\text{in}}
can be several thousand. We avoid it by exploiting the low rank of the adapter: with
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times r}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{in}}}
(
r=32
),
BA
has at most
r
non-zero singular values, which we can recover from an
r\times r
problem.

The non-zero singular values of
BA
are the square roots of the non-zero eigenvalues of
(BA)^{\top}(BA)=A^{\top}(B^{\top}B)\,A
. Let
G=B^{\top}B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
be the (small) Gram matrix of
B
’s columns, and let
L
be its Cholesky factor, so that
G=LL^{\top}
with
L
lower-triangular and
r\times r
. Substituting
G=LL^{\top}
gives

(BA)^{\top}(BA)=A^{\top}LL^{\top}A=(L^{\top}A)^{\top}(L^{\top}A),

(2)

so
BA
and the much smaller matrix
L^{\top}A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{in}}}
have identical singular values. The procedure is therefore: form
G=B^{\top}B
, Cholesky-factor it to obtain
L
, and take the singular values of
L^{\top}A
—an SVD of an
r\times d_{\text{in}}
matrix rather than the full
d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}
one.

For numerical stability we factor
G+\epsilon I
with
\epsilon=10^{-8}
(guaranteeing positive-definiteness when
B
is near zero, as at LoRA initialisation), so the identity above holds up to a negligible
\epsilon\,A^{\top}A
term; layers with
\|B\|<10^{-12}
are skipped entirely, and all computations are performed in float32. The penalty is averaged across all LoRA layers in the model (252 for Qwen3-8B).

G.4
Alternative formulation: spectral entropy gap

To confirm that the regularisation effect is driven by spectral flattening rather than the specific choice of
R
, we test an alternative formulation based on the spectral entropy gap:

R_{\text{entropy}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\log r-H(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}),\qquad\hat{\sigma}_{i}=\sigma_{i}/\textstyle\sum_{j}\sigma_{j},\qquad H(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=-\sum_{i}\hat{\sigma}_{i}\log\hat{\sigma}_{i},

(3)

where
r
is the adapter rank and
H
is the Shannon entropy of the normalised singular values. This measures the gap between the maximum possible entropy (
\log r
, achieved when all singular values are equal) and the actual entropy; like the Frobenius/nuclear ratio, it is zero for a uniform spectrum and increases with concentration.
Table

15
shows that this formulation produces similar improvements, indicating that the effect does not depend on the specific choice of
R
.

Per-response score distributions

This appendix shows the joint distribution of per-response alignment and coherence scores, pooled over all evaluation responses from a single model (Qwen3-8B fine-tuned with Adam) on each of the four datasets. Its purpose is to illustrate the typical shape of these distributions for each dataset—in particular how much of the probability mass falls in the coherent-but-misaligned region (alignment
<30
, coherence
\geq 50
)—which the aggregate misalignment rates reported elsewhere summarise into a single number.

Relative to the dashed threshold lines, the misalignment rate is essentially the mass in the top-left region (coherent and misaligned: coherence
\geq 50
, alignment
<30
) divided by the mass in the top-left plus top-right regions (all coherent responses): the coherence filter discards the bottom half of each panel (coherence
<50
), and the misalignment rate is then the fraction of the remaining responses that fall left of the alignment
=30
line.
