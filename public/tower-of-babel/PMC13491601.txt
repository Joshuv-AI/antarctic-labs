Perivascular adipokine signaling in abdominal aortic aneurysm: cardiometabolic drivers of vascular remodeling and translational opportunities
Authors: Xinyi Zhang, Hanxun He, Hang He, Yujin Zhang, Yuxiao Zhang, Yufan Zhou, Xiaofan Huang, Jiaqi Huang, Ping Ye, Jiahong Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) represents a significant vascular pathology characterized by high mortality rates and a paucity of effective pharmacological interventions. Recent research underscores the role of perivascular adipose tissue derived adipokines in establishing a direct connection between obesity and the pathogenesis of AAA. These adipokines exert influence on aneurysmal remodeling by modulating the functions of vascular cells, particularly in relation to inflammation and extracellular matrix degradation. They can be categorized into two broad classes: deleterious (pro-aneurysmal) and protective (anti-aneurysmal). Deleterious adipokines exacerbate AAA through the promotion of inflammation, the weakening of the vascular wall, and the stimulation of aberrant vascular growth. In contrast, protective adipokines contribute to the attenuation of AAA progression via antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and vascular homeostatic mechanisms. Recent evidence indicates that restoring adipokine balance may serve as a promising therapeutic approach for AAA. Although the majority of studies are still in the preclinical stage, the repurposing of metabolic drugs such as GLP-1 receptor agonists, metformin, and PPAR agonists has shown potential in rebalancing adipokine profiles, reducing inflammation, and promoting vascular repair. Additionally, novel agents targeting adipokine receptors, including adiponectin agonists, chemerin antagonists, and FABP4 inhibitors, have demonstrated preliminary efficacy in modulating adipokine signaling and mitigating vascular inflammation. This review synthesizes the mechanistic roles of adipokines in AAA, with a focus on therapeutic opportunities targeting adipokine driven pathways, and outlines a framework for future translational and clinical research aimed at adipokine-based management of AAA.Graphical abstract Keywords: Abdominal aortic aneurysm, Adipokines, Perivascular adipose tissue, Metabolic disorders, Obesity

Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is traditionally viewed as a life threatening degenerative disorder of the abdominal aorta, diagnosed by a permanent dilation that typically exceeds 1.5 times its normal diameter as observed through imaging techniques [1]. In the absence of any pharmacological therapy proven to slow aneurysm expansion, open surgical repair or endovascular aneurysm repair remain the sole effective interventions [2]. AAA command particular clinical concern owing to their asymptomatic progression, sudden onset and exceptionally high case fatality [3]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that AAA is not merely a localized disease of the vessel wall, but rather a metabolically modulated vascular degenerative disease, influenced by systemic metabolic status and inter-organ crosstalk [4–7].

Recent researches have identified perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT), the specialized adipose depot immediately surrounding the aortic wall, as a critical regulator of vascular homeostasis and AAA susceptibility [8]. In contrast to conventional white adipose tissue (WAT), PVAT is strategically located adjacent to the vessel wall, allowing direct paracrine crosstalk with endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), and resident immune cells [9, 10]. In obesity and related metabolic disorders, PVAT undergoes phenotypic remodeling characterized by inflammation, oxidative stress and immune cell infiltration, leading to local adipokines dysregulation that is spatially concentrated and often exceeds circulating levels [11–13]. This PVAT specific adipokines imbalance functions as an integrated metabolic-immune driver, providing a more precise reflection of AAA risk and progression than generalized measures of adiposity such as body mass index (BMI) [14].

Adipokines, bioactive molecules mainly secreted by adipose tissues, have emerged as critical mediators linking metabolic stress to vascular remodeling in AAA [15]. Beyond the role in energy balance and appetite regulation, adipokines significantly influence immune modulation, insulin sensitivity, vascular function, and blood pressure homeostasis [16]. In the context of obesity, PVAT exhibits a shift toward deleterious adipokines profile with reduced protective adipokines, establishing a chronic local inflammatory microenvironment that precipitate aneurysmal degeneration [11]. These observations raise critical questions: Do PVAT specific adipokines imbalance serve as a mechanistic link between metabolic stress and AAA initiation and progression?

Based on existing evidence, we propose that adipokine imbalance serves as a biologically meaningful integrative indicator of metabolic stress in AAA. Adipokines are not merely passive biomarkers of obesity but rather play a crucial role in reflecting dynamic changes within the adipose-vascular axis and actively modulating the biological behavior of aneurysmal degeneration. This review synthesizes current evidence on the involvement of adipokines in aortic aneurysms, with a focus on the regulation of aneurysm initiation and progression by detrimental and protective adipokines through pathways centered on inflammation, oxidative stress, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Building on these mechanistic insights, the potential of adipokines as emerging biomarkers and therapeutic targets for aneurysm management is assessed, and key priorities for future research are identified.

PVAT as a spatially restricted, metabolically stressed signaling hub: Metabolic Stress and Adipokines Imbalance in AAA

AAA most predominantly occurs in the infrarenal abdominal aorta and is characterized by a localized, irreversible dilatation resulting from the progressive loss of structural integrity and tensile strength of the vascular wall due to various injurious stimuli [3, 17]. Epidemiological and mechanistic evidence implicates several classical risk factors, such as atherosclerosis, hypertension, smoking, ageing, and male sex, as significant contributors to aneurysm susceptibility [18]. The primary clinical risk associated with an established aneurysm is its potential progression to dissection or catastrophic rupture, the latter of which is associated with mortality rates nearing 80% [19, 20]. Despite substantial advances in open surgical repair and endovascular management, no pharmacological therapy has yet demonstrated reliable efficacy in slowing aneurysm growth or preventing rupture [21]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular pathways that trigger and sustain aneurysm biology, and identifying tractable drivers for therapeutic intervention, therefore remains an urgent unmet need.

PVAT: anatomy, function, and secretome

PVAT functions as an active metabolic-immune organ rather than a passive lipid storage depot, integrating local metabolic cues to modulate endothelial cells, VSMCs, and resident immune populations [22]. Unlike WAT, PVAT is in direct physical proximity to the vascular wall and lacks a clear anatomical barrier separating it from the adventitia, enabling intimate paracrine and juxtracrine interactions with vascular cells [23]. This unique localization positions PVAT as an active participant, rather than a passive bystander, in vascular metabolic stress responses [24]. PVAT exhibits depot specific transcriptional programs, cellular composition, and secretory profiles that are tailored to local vascular regulation [22].

A defining feature of PVAT is its ability to establish local adipokine gradients at the adventitial-medial interface [25, 26]. Given its close apposition to the aneurysmal wall, PVAT is uniquely positioned to influence local inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune activation within the aortic microenvironment [13]. PVAT derived adipokines diffuse directly toward the vascular wall, creating a high concentration perivascular signaling milieu that is not reflected by circulating levels [27]. These locally enriched adipokines regulate endothelial function, VSMC phenotype, and inflammatory signaling pathways. In AAA, dysregulated adipokine secretion from metabolically stressed PVAT may shift this paracrine balance toward a pro-inflammatory, pro-proteolytic state, thereby promoting medial degeneration and aneurysm expansion [28, 29].

The characteristics of adipokines

Adipokines are bioactive signaling molecules predominantly produced by adipose tissue that operate through endocrine, paracrine, and autocrine mechanisms to regulate metabolic homeostasis, inflammatory and immune responses, and vascular integrity [30]. Once regarded as inert by products of adipose expansion, adipokines are now recognized as dynamic, pleiotropic mediators whose biological effects are highly context dependent [31]. Their actions are shaped not only by intrinsic molecular properties, but by the integrated physiological environment in which they act. Adipokines expression, receptor engagement, downstream signaling pathways, and functional consequences are profoundly influenced by the prevailing inflammatory milieu, systemic and local metabolic status, cellular redox balance, and age related remodeling of adipose and vascular tissues [32–34]. Through the convergence of these regulatory axes, adipokines may assume either pathogenic or protective roles in vascular disease.

Metabolic stress and adipokines remodeling

Metabolic stress, driven by obesity, inflammation or oxidative stress, induces remodeling in PVAT, fundamentally reshaping its adipokine secretome [10, 35, 36]. The resulting local enrichment of deleterious adipokines and suppression of protective adipokines establishes steep paracrine gradients that dictate local vascular responses.

Obesity drives profound, qualitative remodeling of adipose tissue, encompassing adipocyte hypertrophy, immune cell infiltration, hypoxia induced stress, and progressive fibrotic deposition, which reprograms the adipokine secretome in a manner distinct from mere adipose mass expansion [37, 38]. This pathological reprogramming manifests as coordinated upregulation of pro-inflammatory and metabolically disruptive adipokines, including leptin, resistin, FABP4, and LCN2, alongside significant suppression of vasoprotective and anti-inflammatory adipokines, adiponectin and omentin [39, 40]. Critically, this imbalance reflects not merely quantitative changes but a fundamental shift in adipose tissue’s endocrine identity.

The inflammatory milieu exerts a decisive influence on adipokine function. In states of chronic, low-grade inflammation, characteristic of obesity, metabolic syndrome, and cardiometabolic disease, adipokine secretion is reprogrammed toward a predominantly pro-inflammatory profile [41]. Adipokines such as leptin, resistin, and chemerin are upregulated and engage canonical inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-κB and JAK/STAT [31, 42]. Activation of these pathways potentiates cytokine production, promotes immune cell recruitment, and perturbs endothelial and vascular smooth muscle cell homeostasis. Beyond passive responsiveness to inflammatory stimuli, adipokines actively participate in sustaining inflammatory networks. By stimulating macrophages, dendritic cells, and other innate immune populations, they reinforce inflammatory signaling in a self-perpetuating manner, functioning simultaneously as sensors and amplifiers of inflammatory stress [43]. Within this context, adipokines with anti-inflammatory and vasoprotective properties, such as adiponectin and omentin, often exhibit attenuated bioactivity or functional resistance [44]. The resulting imbalance favors persistent vascular inflammation, tissue injury, and maladaptive remodeling.

Metabolically stressed PVAT generates high concentration adipokine gradients that act directly on the vascular wall, driving vascular smooth muscle cells to phenotypic switch from contractile to synthetic and pro-fibrotic phenotype, promoting extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and sustaining local inflammation, ultimately accelerating AAA progression (Fig. 1).

General mechanistic overview

AAA progression is orchestrated by a hierarchical, layer specific network of pathological signals originating predominantly from PVAT [45, 46]. Under conditions of metabolic stress or obesity, PVAT undergoes remodeling, characterized by adipocyte hypertrophy, immune cell infiltration, and dysregulated adipokine secretion [11]. These local high concentration adipokine gradients act directly on the adjacent aortic wall, producing an outside‑in cascade of vascular injury [47].

In the adventitia, PVAT derived adipokines, including leptin, resistin, chemerin, fatty-acid-bingding protein-4 (FABP4) and lipocalin-2 (LCN2), facilitate macrophage recruitment, mast cell activation, and local neovascularization [5, 42, 48–59]. Leptin and resistin engage NF‑κB dependent pathways, enhancing proinflammatory cytokine production and MMPs activity [42, 48, 54]. Chemerin signals through chemokine-like receptor 1 (CMKLR1) to induce NF‑κB mediated inflammation and oxidative stress, whereas FABP4 amplifies IL-18 signaling and NADPH oxidase dependent reactive oxygen species (ROS), activating redox sensitive cascades such as NF‑κB, ERK1/2, JNK, and p38 MAPK [55, 56, 60–62]. LCN2 interacts with MMP complexes, particularly MMP-9, to potentiate ECM degradation, while FGF21 are posited to enhance immune cell infiltration and ECM degradation in the aortic adventitia [58, 59, 63]. These processes compromise adventitial structural integrity, degrade the ECM, and establish feedback loops that sustain chronic inflammation and tissue vulnerability.

Within the medial layer, PVAT derived deleterious adipokines promote a phenotypic switch of VSMCs from a contractile to a synthetic, proliferative, and migratory state. Central signaling pathways include p38 MAPK, ERK1/2, PI3K/Akt, and JNK/AP‑1, with leptin and resistin primarily engaging p38/ERK and ROS–NF‑κB axes, chemerin acting through ROS dependent Akt/ERK, FABP4 via ROS‑MAPK, LCN2 through p38 MAPK, and FGF21 via PI3K/Akt and autophagy related mechanisms. This remodeling increases MMPs expression, promotes ECM degradation, diminishes medial tensile strength, and accelerates aneurysm growth [48, 62, 64–69].

At the intimal layer, PVAT derived adipokines disrupt endothelial homeostasis by promoting oxidative stress, endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) uncoupling, and PKA dependent signaling disturbances. Leptin, resistin, chemerin, FABP4, and LCN2 collectively reduce nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, upregulate adhesion molecules such as vascular cell adhesion molecule-1(VCAM-1) and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and increase chemokine expression, including monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) and endothelin‑1(ET-1), thereby promoting monocyte adhesion and transendothelial migration [54, 67, 70–77]. FGF21 may further enhance endothelial oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling. These alterations amplify local inflammation and contribute to the propagation of injury across the medial and adventitial layers [78].

Overall, these PVAT mediated, layer specific processes establish a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation, oxidative stress, ECM degradation, and vascular remodeling. The primary mechanistic drivers, adventitial inflammation, medial VSMC phenotypic switching coupled with MMP activation, and intimal endothelial dysfunction, act in concert to compromise aortic wall integrity and promote AAA initiation and expansion, forming an integrated outside‑in pathophysiological framework for aneurysm development.

Leptin

Clinical and biomarker evidence

Leptin, a 16‑kDa peptide hormone encoded by the ob gene, is elevated in the circulation of AAA patients relative to age and sex matched controls, indicating a potential link to AAA [6, 48]. However, circulating leptin correlates only weakly with aneurysm presence or size, and these associations often become nonsignificant after adjustment for age, BMI, smoking, and metabolic factors [79].

Histological analyses of human aneurysmal tissues demonstrate markedly higher leptin expression in both the aortic wall and surrounding PVAT, which is associated with inflammation, medial degeneration, and elevated MMPs activity [48]. The pronounced expression in the PVAT underscores the importance of spatially restricted paracrine signaling, where local leptin concentrations may far exceed systemic levels, directly driving aneurysm expansion [48].

Large scale genetic and mechanistic analyses further connect cardiovascular metabolic traits, including dyslipidemia and pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, with AAA susceptibility, supporting a biologically plausible role for leptin in aneurysm initiation and progression [6]. Incorporating leptin alongside other adipokines and imaging derived PVAT metrics may better capture systemic metabolic risk and local aneurysm specific pathological activity than leptin alone.

Evidence in aneurysm models

Experimental aneurysm studies suggest that leptin exerts a highly context-dependent effect on AAA pathogenesis, with local periaortic leptin signaling generally promoting aneurysmal degeneration, whereas systemic leptin administration may produce divergent effects depending on route, dose, and immune-metabolic context [48]. Local periaortic leptin, enriched within the PVAT niche, promotes aneurysmal degeneration and ECM remodeling, as demonstrated by aggravated angiotensin II (AngII) induced AAA in apolipoprotein-E deficient (ApoE−/−) mice [48]. Consistent with its local pathogenic role, pharmacological blockade of leptin signaling, via local application of a leptin antagonist, has been shown to reduce aneurysm formation and attenuate aortic remodeling in AngII-infused ApoE−/− mice [48].

However, the role of leptin is not uniformly deleterious across all experimental settings. In contrast to local periaortic delivery, intraperitoneal recombinant leptin administration was reported to attenuate AngII-induced AAA formation in ApoE−/− mice without significantly altering serum lipids or systolic blood pressure [80]. These findings highlight that leptin’s effects are spatially and context dependent, with local PVAT enrichment driving pro-aneurysmal activity, whereas circulating leptin may primarily modulate immune-metabolic responses.

Layer-specific actions and mechanisms (outside-in signaling)

Leptin acts in a layer specific manner within the aortic wall, promoting adventitial inflammation and macrophage accumulation via NF-κB and ROS signaling, driving medial VSMC phenotypic switching and MMP induction through p38 MAPK, ERK, and PI3K/Akt pathways, and impairing intimal endothelial function by ROS and PKA mediated mechanisms with upregulation of ICAM-1 and MCP-1 [42, 48–51, 64, 65, 70, 71, 81–84].

Therapeutic implications and knowledge gaps

The existing preclinical studies have primarily focused on AngII-induced models and genetically modified mice, and the regulatory mechanisms and pathological effects of leptin remain incompletely understood. Within the context of this focal AAA lesion, the specific contributions of local PVAT derived leptin gradients and their relationship with systemic leptin levels require further investigation.

leptin acts as a locally operative pathogenic driver in AAA, accelerating disease progression by reinforcing inflammatory and ECM degradative networks and thereby emerging as a potential therapeutic target. Translational strategies should prioritize local leptin antagonism, inhibition of downstream signaling, and modulation of PVAT derived cues to disrupt the pro-inflammatory cascade. As a critical molecular interface linking metabolic dysregulation to vascular pathology, deeper mechanistic insight into leptin signaling is expected to inform novel strategies for aneurysm prevention and therapy.

Resistin

Clinical and biomarker evidence

Resistin, predominantly derived from myeloid immune cells, is elevated in the circulation of patients with AAA and shows a positive correlation with both disease presence and aortic diameter [85, 86]. Compared with leptin and adiponectin, resistin exhibits stronger predictive performance, and its association with AAA remains significant even after adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, highlighting its potential utility as a robust biomarker of aneurysmal disease [86]. Resistin like molecule-β (RELMβ), as a member of the resistin like molecule family, is likewise implicated in AAA pathogenesis. Limited clinical evidence suggests that circulating RELMβ levels are elevated in patients with AAA, linking this mediator to pathological aortic remodeling [87]. Genetic deletion of RELMβ markedly attenuates aneurysm formation, providing functional evidence for its pathogenic role in AAA progression [52].

Evidence in aneurysm models

Mechanistic studies in experimental aneurysm models reinforce the pathogenic role of resistin and RELMβ. Genetic deletion of RELMβ in AngII-infused ApoE−/− mice markedly attenuates aneurysm formation, reduces adventitial macrophage infiltration, decreases MCP-1 and IL-6 levels, and lowers MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression and activity [52]. In vitro, PVAT from metabolically dysregulated states enhances inflammatory and matrix remodeling gene expression in vascular wall cells, an effect partially abrogated by resistin neutralization. These findings underscore the contribution of resistin related pathways to AAA progression [52].

Layer-specific actions and mechanisms (outside-in signaling)

Resistin exerts layer specific effects on the aortic wall by activating TLR4–NF-κB mediated inflammation and ECM proteolysis in the adventitia, inducing medial VSMC phenotypic switching and migration via p38 MAPK and JNK/AP-1 signaling, and promoting intimal endothelial dysfunction through ROS and eNOS mediated pathways with upregulation of VCAM-1 and MCP-1 [52–54, 66, 72, 88].

Therapeutic implications and knowledge gaps

While no pharmacological agents specifically targeting resistin are currently approved for clinical use, several translational strategies warrant consideration. These encompass the development of neutralizing antibodies, small molecule inhibitors directed against downstream signaling cascades, and modulation of PVAT inflammation as an indirect strategy to mitigate resistin mediated vascular injury. It is important to note that significant species differences exist between human resistin and murine resistin-like molecules, underscoring the necessity for further investigation in human tissues and prospective cohort studies to validate its causal role and therapeutic potential. Future translational efforts should focus on determining whether interventions targeting resistin signaling can attenuate aneurysm progression or diminish rupture risk, thereby translating experimental findings into clinical practice.

Chemerin

Clinical and biomarker evidence

Chemerin, encoded by the RARRES2 gene, is an adipokine with dual roles in metabolism and immune regulation [55]. Chemerin is abundantly produced by PVAT, and its receptor CMKLR1 is expressed in vascular smooth muscle and endothelial cells, establishing a functional chemerin axis within the arterial wall [89]. Clinical investigations have demonstrated that circulating chemerin levels are significantly elevated in patients with AAA, along with pronounced upregulation of chemerin and its receptor CMKLR1 in aortic aneurysmal tissue, indicating activation of the chemerin–CMKLR1 signaling pathway in the diseased vasculature [56]. Although circulating chemerin is primarily derived from the liver, PVAT derived chemerin, owing to its close anatomical proximity to the vascular wall, is likely to exert greater local functional relevance and pathogenic significance [12, 90]. These observations support the notion that chemerin acts as a key molecular bridge conveying adipose tissue–derived inflammatory signals to mechanisms underlying aneurysmal remodeling [12].

Evidence in aneurysm models

In Ang-II infused ApoE⁻/⁻ mice, subcutaneous osmotic pump administration of the chemerin derived peptide chemerin-9, targeting the CMKLR1 signaling pathway, markedly reduced the incidence of AAA and limited aortic dilation [56, 91]. There is currently a lack of studies addressing PVAT specific local administration of chemerin. In vitro experiments further demonstrate that chemerin directly affects VSMCs and endothelial cells in a deleterious manner [67]. Analyses of human AAA specimens further support these mechanistic observations, showing elevated expression of chemerin and CMKLR1, which positively correlate with inflammatory mediators [56]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that PVAT derived chemerin exerts a destructive effect on the vascular wall.

Layer-specific actions and mechanisms (outside-in signaling)

Chemerin mediates layer specific effects across the aortic wall, promoting adventitial inflammation, macrophage recruitment, and neovascularization via CMKLR1 dependent NF-κB activation, driving medial VSMC phenotypic switching and MMP-2/9 upregulation through ROS, Akt, and ERK signaling, and impairing intimal endothelial function by NF-κB driven ICAM-1/VCAM-1 induction and Akt-eNOS-NO axis disruption [55, 56, 67, 73, 92].

Therapeutic implications and knowledge gaps

Therapeutically, the chemerin–CMKLR1 axis represents a promising but unexplored target. Although CMKLR1 directed interventions have not yet reached clinical application, convergent histological and transcriptomic evidence supports this pathway as a candidate for therapeutic modulation. Future studies should evaluate CMKLR1 antagonists or chemerin neutralizing strategies in aneurysm models, with single cell resolution to resolve macrophage-VSMC crosstalk in the inflammatory microenvironment. These data position the chemerin–CMKLR1 axis as a pathogenic link between innate immune activation and VSMC dysfunction, with strong potential for limiting aneurysm progression.

Adipocyte fatty acid-binding protein (FABP4)

Clinical and biomarker evidence

FABP4 is a fatty acid binding protein predominantly expressed in adipocytes, macrophages, and endothelial cells, serving as a cytoplasmic lipid chaperone [93]. Circulating FABP4 levels are elevated in obesity and cardiometabolic diseases, with evidence suggesting its pathogenic role in AAA, linking metabolic dysfunction with vascular wall degeneration [35].

Evidence in aneurysm models

Experimental studies indicate that FABP4 contributes to vascular remodeling processes relevant to aneurysm formation. In minipigs with coronary restenosis, increased FABP4 expression in vascular tissues was associated with elevated ROS production, which stimulated VSMC proliferation and migration, highlighting a ROS dependent mechanism that may drive medial degeneration [60]. Beyond its effects on VSMCs, FABP4 has been shown to impair endothelial function in both human and mouse endothelial cells in vitro, as well as in mouse vascular tissue, by disrupting the insulin dependent nitric oxide pathway, reducing NO bioavailability, and promoting endothelial activation, thereby potentially facilitating inflammatory cell infiltration and vascular injury [62]. In ApoE−/− mice fed a high-fat diet and in mononuclear cell-endothelial coculture models, FABP4 further enhanced mononuclear cell activation, endothelial inflammation, and oxidative stress, processes that are critical drivers of vascular wall inflammation and remodeling [74].

Layer-specific actions and mechanisms (outside-in signaling)

FABP4 drives layer specific remodeling of the aortic wall by activating adventitial immune cells and promoting ECM degradation via IL-18/IL-18R and ROS dependent NF-κB, ERK, JNK, and p38 signaling, inducing medial VSMC phenotypic switching and MMP upregulation through ROS-MAPK pathways, and impairing intimal endothelial function via eNOS uncoupling with reduced NO and increased adhesion molecule expression [5, 57, 60–62, 74, 75].

Therapeutic implications and knowledge gaps

Recent studies have developed neutralizing monoclonal antibodies targeting FABP4, which have shown promising results in preclinical validation across various disease models [94]. The neutralization of secreted FABP4 alleviates endothelial inflammation and dysfunction, highlighting circulating FABP4 as a potential therapeutic target. Targeting FABP4 may help mitigate the metabolic-inflammation positive feedback loop, thereby enhancing vascular barrier integrity and slowing down degenerative changes in the aortic wall. Future translational research could integrate imaging of PVAT inflammation, multi-omics profiling, and dynamic serum FABP4 monitoring to develop intervention strategies centered on the FABP4–IL-18–ROS–MMP axis.

Although existing studies suggest that FABP4 may play a critical role in AAA pathogenesis, the current evidence remains limited and is largely derived from murine models or in vitro experiments. The causal role of FABP4 has not been systematically validated in preclinical AAA models, and studies employing gene knockout or neutralizing antibody interventions are lacking.

Lipocalin-2 (LCN2)

Clinical and biomarker evidence

Lipocalin-2 (LCN2), also referred to as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), functions as a low-molecular-weight lipid carrier that complexes with MMPs and is critically involved in vascular remodeling and inflammatory processes [95]. Recent evidence indicates a marked upregulation of LCN2 in vascular pathologies, including AAA, pointing to its potential contribution to the initiation and progression of this condition [95].

Evidence in aneurysm models

In murine models of AAA induced by elastase perfusion, LCN2 deficiency or pharmacological inhibition markedly attenuates aneurysm development, limits aortic dilation and preserves vascular wall integrity [58].

Layer-specific actions and mechanisms (outside-in signaling)

LCN2 promotes layer specific aortic wall remodeling by driving adventitial immune cell recruitment and MMP-9 complex formation to degrade ECM, inducing medial VSMC phenotypic switching, proliferation, and migration via p38 MAPK, and impairing intimal endothelial function through ROS mediated activation and increased permeability [58, 59, 68, 76, 77, 96, 97].

Therapeutic implications and knowledge gaps

LCN2 has been identified as a potential biomarker for AAA, with notably elevated plasma levels in patients experiencing rupture, where concentrations correlate strongly with disease progression [98]. Circulating LCN2 is also increased in individuals with obesity relative to healthy controls, and these elevations are inversely associated with impaired vascular dilation [99]. Thus, LCN2 represents a promising biomarker for stratifying cardiovascular risk in obese populations. Nevertheless, further validation through large-scale prospective clinical studies is required to establish LCN2 as an independent prognostic indicator. Therapeutic interventions aimed at LCN2, particularly by disrupting its interaction with MMPs, may offer novel strategies to attenuate the initiation and progression of AAA.

Although certain signaling pathways of LCN2 have been reported in the literature, their evidence specific to AAA is relatively limited or derived from non-aneurysmal models and should therefore be interpreted with caution. The role of LCN2 in vascular injury associated with AAA and its potential as a therapeutic target remain incompletely understood, highlighting the need for further investigation through multicenter, longitudinal studies.

Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21)

Clinical and biomarker evidence

Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), a pleiotropic protein secreted by various tissues, plays a broad role in modulating metabolic homeostasis, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory responses [100]. Substantial evidence indicates that in atherosclerosis, FGF21 generally exerts well characterized vasculoprotective effects and is regarded as a protective adipokine [101]. In contrast, emerging research on AAA indicates that elevated FGF21 levels may not simply reflect a compensatory mechanism but rather play an active role in driving pathological remodeling of the aneurysmal wall, suggesting a context-dependent, pro-pathogenic function within this specific disease environment [102].

Evidence in aneurysm models

In the Ang-II infused ApoE−/− mouse model of abdominal aortic aneurysm, FGF21 promotes the phenotypic switch of VSMCs from a contractile to a synthetic state, while enhancing inflammatory cell infiltration in the adventitia and MMPs activity, thereby accelerating aortic dilation and structural disruption of the vessel wall [63, 103].

Layer-specific actions and mechanisms (outside-in signaling)

FGF21, with context dependent effects, modulates layer specific processes in the aortic wall by enhancing adventitial inflammatory signaling and immune cell infiltration via PI3K/Akt, promoting medial VSMC phenotypic switching and ECM remodeling through PI3K/Akt and autophagy pathways, and exacerbating intimal endothelial oxidative stress and dysfunction [63, 69, 78].

Therapeutic implications and knowledge gaps

Current clinical research on FGF21 in AAA remains at a nascent stage. Published studies have indicated markedly elevated plasma FGF21 levels in AAA patients, proposing its potential utility as a biomarker for the condition. However, the exact role and mechanistic basis of FGF21 in AAA pathogenesis necessitate further elucidation and validation via rigorous experimental and clinical investigations.

Role and mechanism of protective adipokines modulators in AAA

Protective adipokines constitute an endogenous counter-regulatory program in abdominal aortic aneurysm, acting through coordinated modulation of endothelial function, VSMCs stability, and inflammatory cell behavior. By engaging conserved anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidative, and anti-proteolytic signaling pathways, these adipokines attenuate maladaptive vascular remodeling and preserve aortic wall integrity (Fig. 3; Table 1).

General mechanistic overview

In AAA, protective adipokines exert multi-layered, outside‑in effects on the vascular wall, stabilizing its structure and function.

In the adventitial layer, protective adipokines, including omentin, adiponectin and apelin, primarily suppress inflammation and immune cell recruitment [15, 104, 105]. Adiponectin (APN) inhibit NF-κB signaling and downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and MCP-1, thereby reducing macrophage and monocyte infiltration [15]. Apelin, via the apelin receptor (APLNR) axis, specifically attenuates AngII mediated adventitial inflammation by limiting MCP-1 and TNF α expression [105].

Within the medial layer, protective adipokines maintain VSMC contractile phenotype and preserve ECM integrity. Omentin reduces VSMC oxidative stress and inflammatory activation, contributing to medial protection [106]. Adiponectin inhibits MMPs activity, limits ECM degradation, and promotes lysyl oxidase mediated collagen crosslinking, thereby supporting elastin fiber stability [15, 79]. Apelin–APLNR signaling suppresses NADPH oxidase driven ROS production, reduces VSMC apoptosis, and indirectly modulates MMP‑2/9 expression, preventing medial structural degeneration [107, 108].

In the intimal layer, protective adipokines enhance endothelial homeostasis and vasodilatory function. Omentin improve eNOS activity, increase NO bioavailability, reduce ROS levels, and suppress endothelial adhesion molecules, including VCAM‑1 and ICAM‑1, as well as chemokines, such as MCP‑1and ET‑1, thereby limiting leukocyte adhesion and transendothelial migration [109, 110]. Apelin promotes eNOS phosphorylation and NO release, while antagonizing AngII-AT1R signaling, collectively attenuating vasoconstriction, inflammation, and oxidative stress, and enhancing endothelium dependent vasorelaxation [110, 111].

Through this adventitia-media-intima layer specific signaling network, protective adipokines collectively modulate inflammation, oxidative stress, VSMC phenotypic switching, ECM remodeling, and endothelial function, forming a protective axis that counteracts pathological stimuli, preserves vascular architecture and slows AAA progression.

Omentin

Clinical and biomarker evidence

Omentin, an anti-inflammatory adipokine primarily secreted from visceral adipose tissue, is a key regulator of metabolic homeostasis, insulin sensitivity, and vascular integrity [112]. Prior research has established that omentin expression is negatively correlated with obesity, type II diabetes, and atherosclerosis [112]. This adipokine mediates anti-inflammatory and antioxidative actions, enhances endothelial function, and is consequently recognized as vasculoprotective [110]. More recent experimental evidence further indicates that omentin may be involved in modulating the development of AAA.

Evidence in aneurysm models

In Ang II induced ApoE−/− murine models of AAA, either exogenous administration or genetic overexpression of omentin significantly lowered AAA incidence and limited aortic expansion, coinciding with suppressed vascular inflammation and reduced expression of MMPs in the aortic wall [104].

Layer-specific mechanisms (outside-in signaling)

Omentin exerts layer specific protective effects on the aortic wall by suppressing NF-κB mediated adventitial inflammation to limit macrophage infiltration and MMP-2/9 expression, attenuating p38/JNK driven medial VSMC inflammatory activation to preserve elastic fibers and enhancing endothelial NO bioavailability while reducing oxidative stress to maintain intimal integrity [104, 106, 109, 110].

Therapeutic implications and knowledge gaps

There is a scarcity of large-scale prospective cohort studies that definitively establish an independent association between circulating omentin levels and the incidence of AAA, its expansion rate, or risk of rupture. In contrast to other adipokines such as resistin and leptin, clinical evidence pertaining to omentin in the context of AAA remains sparse, and no consensus risk stratification model integrating omentin has been developed. Experimental models demonstrating omentin inhibitory effects on AAA formation, along with its anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties in endothelial cells and VSMCs, suggest its potential as a disease modifying agent in aneurysmal pathology.

Adiponectin (APN)

Clinical and biomarker evidence

Adiponectin (APN), a predominantly adipocyte secreted adipokine, exhibits well-established vasculoprotective properties, and its circulating concentrations are characteristically diminished in obesity and metabolic syndrome [113]. APN transduces intracellular signals mainly via AdipoR1 and AdipoR2 receptors, engaging canonical downstream pathways such as AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), peroxisome proliferator activated receptor-α (PPAR-α), and ceramide mediated signaling cascades [114].

Clinical studies indicate that circulating APN levels are inconsistently different between AAA patients and control populations. Most observational cohorts and meta-analyses show that APN lacks an independent association with AAA risk, and any apparent significance is frequently lost after adjustment for age, BMI, smoking, C-reactive protein (CRP), and metabolic comorbidities [79]. High-molecular-weight (HMW) APN isoforms, which are more biologically active, also demonstrate limited independent predictive value for AAA. Emerging evidence suggests that local APN expression within PVAT may more directly influence vascular wall homeostasis, whereas circulating levels may not fully reflect these local effectsv [115].

Evidence in aneurysm models

Experimental models consistently support a protective role for APN [15]. In AngII-induced AAA models, APN deficiency accelerates aneurysm formation, enhancing adventitial macrophage infiltration and local expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and MCP-1 [15]. APN elevation mitigates medial degeneration, elastin fragmentation, and aortic dilation [116]. In LDLR−/− mice, increased APN reduces inflammatory cell recruitment in both the aortic wall and adjacent PVAT, indicating disruption of the PVAT–vascular wall inflammatory feedback loop [117]. ApoE−/−APN−/− mice exhibit augmented MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity, suggesting that APN deficiency promotes ECM degradation and medial remodeling via proteolytic amplification [15].

Layer-specific mechanisms (outside-in signaling)

APN mediates layer specific vascular protection by inhibiting adventitial macrophage recruitment and proinflammatory cytokine amplification, reducing medial MMP-2/9 activity while preserving elastin and promoting ECM crosslinking, and indirectly stabilizing endothelial function by disrupting PVAT vascular inflammatory feedback [15, 79].

Therapeutic implications and knowledge gaps

Preclinical studies indicate that enhancing APN signaling can attenuate AAA initiation and progression [117]. In AngII-induced murine models, increased APN reduces inflammation, mitigates elastin fragmentation, and strengthens ECM stability, highlighting protective effects along the PVAT-vascular wall axis [117]. However, clinical translation remains limited. Circulating APN lacks stable, independent predictive value for AAA, suggesting systemic measurements may not reflect local protective activity [79]. Adiponectin receptor agonists, such as AdipoRon, show efficacy in metabolic and inflammatory models but have not been tested in AAA. Key challenges include tissue-specific delivery, dose optimization, receptor selectivity, and heterogeneity in human disease mechanisms [118].

The therapeutic potential of APN likely depends on modulation of local PVAT-vascular wall signaling. Future studies should clarify the critical mechanistic nodes, optimize pharmacologic strategies, and conduct prospective clinical evaluation to determine feasibility in AAA patients.

Apelin

Clinical and biomarker evidence

Analysis of aneurysmal tissue from AAA patients indicates that apelin expression is upregulated in the affected aortic segments compared with non-aneurysmal controls, suggesting a potential compensatory response within the disease microenvironment [107]. Neutral endopeptidase (NEP), which is upregulated in aneurysmal tissue, promotes local degradation of apelin, potentially limiting its protective effect in the medial layer of the aortic wall [107]. In models of hypertension or heart failure without aneurysm induction, apelin deficiency primarily affects blood pressure regulation and cardiac remodeling, underscoring the context-dependent relevance of apelin signaling [119]. Notably, local degradation of apelin by neprilysin NEP, which is upregulated in aneurysmal tissue, may be spatially restricted to affected aortic segments, potentially limiting medial Apelin–APLNR signaling and contributing to regional vulnerability [107].

Evidence in aneurysm models

Mechanistic studies in experimental models have established a protective role for apelin in AAA. In AngII‑induced ApoE−/− mice, genetic deletion of apelin markedly increased AAA incidence, medial VSMC apoptosis, oxidative stress, and rupture related mortality, highlighting the essential role of Apelin–APLNR signaling in maintaining vascular integrity [107]. In elastase induced AAA models, exogenous apelin administration similarly reduced aneurysm formation by suppressing adventitial macrophage infiltration and downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as MCP‑1 and TNF‑α [120].

Layer-specific mechanisms (outside-in signaling)

Apelin confers layer specific vascular protection by limiting adventitial monocyte/macrophage recruitment and proinflammatory cytokines, preserving medial VSMC homeostasis while reducing oxidative stress, apoptosis, and MMP mediated ECM degradation, and enhancing intimal endothelial function through eNOS activation and antagonism of AngII–AT1R signaling [105, 107, 108, 111, 120, 121].

Therapeutic implications and knowledge gaps

There are no large-scale studies assessing circulating apelin levels as a biomarker for AAA risk or progression. While apelin has been investigated as a cardiovascular biomarker in other contexts, such as coronary artery disease, evidence for its predictive value in AAA is lacking. Technical challenges, including the short half-life of apelin peptides and variability in detection assays, further complicate clinical measurement. Currently, data suggest that tissue level apelin may reflect disease activity locally, but robust prospective studies are required to determine whether apelin could serve as a reliable circulating biomarker for AAA.

Clinical translation: biomarkers and potential therapies

Adipokines as biomarkers: from circulating signals to tissue-level readouts

Although adipokines have attracted substantial interest as potential biomarkers for AAA, accumulating evidence indicates that the diagnostic and prognostic utility of circulating adipokine levels alone is limited [122]. For several adipokines, including leptin and resistin, plasma concentrations show weak and inconsistent associations with aneurysm presence, diameter, or growth rate. Moreover, circulating levels are profoundly influenced by systemic metabolic status, total adiposity, renal function, and background systemic inflammation [123]. These confounding factors substantially restrict the feasibility of adipokines as standalone clinical biomarkers for AAA.

In contrast, adipokine signaling at the tissue level and within specific anatomical compartments appears to be far more informative. An expanding body of evidence demonstrates that adipokines are locally synthesized within the aneurysmal wall and the surrounding PVAT, where they act in a paracrine manner to amplify inflammatory responses, promote ECM degradation, and drive VSMC phenotypic switching [29]. In this context, adipokines should not be viewed merely as passive circulating indicators, but rather as locally active pathogenic mediators whose expression closely correlates with inflammatory burden, immune cell infiltration, and medial structural deterioration [124].

These insights support a shift in translational strategy, from an exclusive focus on single circulating adipokines toward the development of composite biomarker frameworks. Integrating adipokine profiles with imaging features, including PVAT attenuation indices and aneurysm wall inflammatory signals, inflammatory mediators, and established clinical risk factors could provide a more complete picture of the metabolic and inflammatory microenvironment driving aneurysm instability [125]. Such multidimensional biomarker approaches hold promise for improved patient stratification and for identifying individuals most likely to benefit from metabolism targeted therapeutic interventions.

Although circulating leptin and adiponectin have been explored as potential biomarkers for AAA, their predictive value is modest. Multivariable analyses adjusting for age, BMI, renal function, and systemic inflammation frequently attenuate or abolish associations, indicating that single adipokine measurements lack stable independent prognostic utility [79]. A more promising approach involves constructing composite risk models that integrate multiple circulating adipokines, PVAT imaging features such as CT attenuation or inflammatory PET signals, and established clinical risk factors, such as age, smoking, hypertension [126]. Such multimodal frameworks can better capture the local metabolic–immune microenvironment driving AAA progression, potentially enhancing patient stratification and guiding targeted interventions. Prospective validation in large, multicenter cohorts will be essential to confirm their predictive utility.

Accumulating evidence indicates that AAA progression is tightly linked to pathological remodeling of PVAT and the resulting imbalance in adipokine secretion, characterized by suppression of protective adipokines and enrichment of pro-inflammatory, ECM degrading mediators. Incretin-based therapies (GLP-1 receptor agonists and multi-agonists)

Recent studies have increasingly investigated the potential protective effects of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1RAs) in AAA. In a clinical cohort of 3,474 AAA patients, exposure to GLP‑1RAs was associated with a reduced incidence of AAA related adverse events [127]. Experimental evidence further demonstrates that, in classical murine AAA models induced by CaCl₂ or elastase, GLP‑1RAs treatment markedly attenuates aneurysm progression, concomitant with reductions in vascular wall inflammation, oxidative stress, and MMPs activity [128]. These findings suggest that glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP‑1) signaling may modulate AAA pathophysiology via anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms [128]. Although direct evidence linking GLP‑1RAs to AAA modulation through adipokines is lacking, experimental data indicate that GLP‑1RA therapy upregulates adiponectin expression, suppresses the release of proinflammatory adipokines from adipose tissue, and mitigates inflammation within PVAT [129]. Accordingly, we hypothesize that GLP‑1RAs may increase adiponectin levels and reduce proinflammatory adipokines within PVAT, thereby alleviating vascular inflammation and extracellular matrix degradation, ultimately contributing to the decreased AAA incidence observed in certain cohorts. However, this hypothesis warrants further investigation.

Metformin and AMPK–adiponectin signaling

Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses integrating data from observational cohort and case control studies have demonstrated that metformin use is associated with a reduced incidence of AAA and a slower disease progression [130]. The underlying mechanisms likely involve multifaceted effects, including attenuation of chronic arterial wall inflammation, suppression of matrix metalloproteinase activity, reduction of oxidative stress, and improvement of systemic metabolic status, collectively limiting arterial wall degradation and dilatation [131]. In murine AAA models, metformin inhibits aneurysm formation and expansion by activating AMPK, suppressing mTOR signaling, and enhancing autophagy, thereby mitigating inflammatory cell infiltration, VSMC phenotypic switching, and ECM degradation mediated by MMPs [132]. Although some reports suggest that metformin may exert vasoprotective effects through upregulation of protective adipokines, particularly adiponectin, and improvement of the PVAT microenvironment, direct evidence linking metformin-induced modulation of adipokine expression to its inhibitory effects on AAA development remains lacking [133]. We hypothesize that metformin may increase adiponectin levels and reduce proinflammatory adipokines within PVAT, thereby alleviating vascular inflammation and ECM degradation, ultimately contributing to the reduced AAA incidence observed in certain cohorts. However, the precise mechanisms require further investigation.

PPARγ agonists: targeting adipokine balance in AAA

In recent years, studies have begun to explore the potential role of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) agonists in modulating the pathophysiology of AAA. Early clinical observations suggest that preoperative use of PPARγ agonists may improve inflammatory status within the arterial wall and perivascular adipose tissue, evidenced by reduced macrophage infiltration in the aneurysmal wall and surrounding fat, increased adiponectin expression, and decreased levels of proinflammatory markers such as TNF‑α and MMP‑9 [134, 135]. Mechanistic and experimental studies further indicate that PPARγ activation protects aortic structural integrity by suppressing inflammation and protein degradation, whereas deletion of PPARγ in vascular smooth muscle cells accelerates aneurysm formation in mice [136]. PPARγ activation has been shown to enhance the expression of protective adipokines, including adiponectin, while inhibiting proinflammatory cytokines and adipokines such as leptin and resistin [134]. Although no studies have directly demonstrated the triadic relationship among PPARγ agonists, adipokines, and AAA, it is hypothesized that PPARγ agonists may slow AAA initiation and progression by upregulating protective adipokines and downregulating detrimental ones. Further research is needed to validate these effects in larger human cohorts and to clarify the mechanisms underlying the interactions between perivascular adipose tissue and the aneurysmal wall.

PPARα agonists: modulating inflammation and ECM in AAA

PPARα agonists are known to modulate adipose tissue metabolism and adipokine secretion, exhibiting anti-inflammatory effects, increasing adiponectin, and suppressing leptin and other pro-inflammatory cytokines in obesity and metabolic syndrome models [137]. However, there is no direct evidence demonstrating that PPARα activation can attenuate AAA development or progression via regulation of adipokines. In experimental AAA models, PPARα agonists such as pema-fibrate or fenofibrate primarily exert protective effects by reducing vascular inflammation, oxidative stress, and matrix metalloproteinase activity, without established links to adipokine mediated mechanisms [138]. Consequently, the PPARα–adipokine–AAA axis remains an untested hypothesis. Future studies should evaluate whether PPARα agonists can modify adipokine profiles within PVAT surrounding aneurysmal aortas and determine whether such changes contribute to modulation of vascular inflammation and extracellular matrix remodeling.

Potential therapeutic implications: targeting the adipose–vascular axis

Adiponectin receptor agonists, Chemerin/CMKLR1 antagonists, and FABP4 inhibitors represent emerging therapeutic options in the treatment of AAA. These agents, though still in preclinical or early clinical phases, offer unique mechanisms for modulating adipokine-driven inflammation and vascular remodeling. By targeting specific pathways involved in inflammation, adipokine imbalance, and extracellular matrix degradation, these therapies could provide novel adjunctive treatments for AAA, particularly in patients with underlying metabolic disorders such as obesity, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. As the clinical evidence continues to accumulate, these drugs may play a significant role in the future management of AAA and related vascular conditions.

Adiponectin receptor agonists: potential for AAA treatment

AdipoRon, a selective adiponectin receptor agonist, has demonstrated promising effects in preclinical models by enhancing adiponectin receptor signaling and mitigating metabolic dysfunctions associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and atherosclerosis [139]. Most of the current evidence derives from early-phase clinical studies and animal experiments, suggesting that AdipoRon has potential to restore adipokine balance and improve vascular health [140]. However, the current agonist lacks precise tissue-specific targeting, as AdipoRon activates both AdipoR1 and AdipoR2 receptors, which display variable expression and downstream signaling across tissues, potentially resulting in off-target effects [141, 142]. By activating adiponectin receptors, AdipoRon can increase adiponectin levels and shift adipokine profiles toward a protective state, potentially benefiting conditions such as atherosclerosis and AAA, although its safety profile requires further experimental validation.

Chemerin/CMKLR1 antagonists: targeting inflammation in AAA

Chemerin mediates its effects by binding to its receptor, CMKLR1, which is expressed on immune cells and endothelial cells [143]. Elevated chemerin levels are observed in conditions such as obesity, metabolic syndrome, and atherosclerosis, where it amplifies inflammatory responses and contributes to vascular remodeling. Antagonism of CMKLR1 has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy to inhibit the chemerin/CMKLR1 signaling pathway [144]. However, its application in AAA remains limited to early in vitro studies using human vascular smooth muscle cells and macrophages. By reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and limiting immune cell recruitment, CMKLR1 antagonists may attenuate the inflammatory processes that drive aneurysm formation [145]. Moreover, inhibition of chemerin signaling may prevent extracellular matrix degradation and smooth muscle cell apoptosis, both of which are critical contributors to aneurysm expansion [145]. These findings suggest that CMKLR1 antagonists could hold therapeutic potential for managing AAA, particularly in patients with underlying metabolic or inflammatory disorders [146]. Nevertheless, some studies indicate that α-NETA may cross react with non-specific GPCR pathways, especially at high doses, potentially affecting cardiovascular and nervous system signaling, and its safety profile requires further experimental validation.

FABP4 inhibitors: modulating inflammation in AAA

Elevated FABP4 levels are linked to metabolic disorders, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases such as AAA. FABP4 inhibitors target this protein to attenuate inflammatory responses and modulate adipokine secretion, particularly in the context of vascular and metabolic diseases [147]. By inhibiting FABP4, these agents decrease the release of pro-inflammatory adipokines, including resistin and TNF-α, and improve endothelial function [148]. Beyond their anti-inflammatory effects, FABP4 inhibitors contribute to restoring metabolic homeostasis through the regulation of lipid metabolism. This dual mechanism positions FABP4 inhibitors as promising candidates for the treatment of vascular diseases, including AAA, where both inflammation and metabolic dysregulation are key drivers of disease progression [149]. However, due to limited selectivity and the potential for off-target effects, FABP4 inhibitors remain in the exploratory stage.

Discussion

Several critical barriers currently hinder the clinical translation of adipokine targeted therapies for AAA. The slow and heterogeneous progression of the disease complicates the establishment of meaningful clinical endpoints, making large, long-term trials both costly and difficult to execute. Furthermore, the context-dependent and cell specific effects of adipokines raise significant concerns about the safety of broad systemic inhibition. Given these complexities, a universal approach is unlikely to succeed.

To address these challenges, precision medicine strategies will be essential. Future research should focus on identifying distinct adipokine driven molecular subtypes of AAA, which will allow for targeted therapies that address specific mechanisms rather than relying on generalized interventions. Intervening in the shared signaling pathways of adipokines not only highlights potential combinatorial therapeutic targets but also offers a novel approach for modulating a specific class of adipokines. Developing pathway or tissue selective interventions is critical to minimizing systemic side effects, enabling therapies to modulate adipokine signaling specifically in the vascular wall or perivascular adipose tissue without impacting broader metabolic functions. Integrating adipokine biology into multimodal biomarker and risk prediction models will enhance patient stratification, ensuring that only those most likely to benefit from specific treatments are selected.

Although circulating adipokines such as leptin and adiponectin have been investigated as potential biomarkers for AAA, their predictive utility is modest. Multivariable analyses adjusting for age, BMI, renal function, systemic inflammation, and metabolic comorbidities often attenuate or abolish associations, highlighting the limited independent prognostic value of single plasma adipokines [79]. To overcome these limitations, the field is moving toward composite biomarker frameworks that integrate multiple circulating adipokines with imaging-based metrics such as PVAT attenuation on CT or PET based inflammation indices, alongside established clinical risk factors, including age, smoking, hypertension [126]. Such multimodal approaches can better capture the local metabolic-immune microenvironment driving aneurysm instability and improve patient stratification [126, 150]. Currently, spatial transcriptomic analyses evaluating adipokine expression gradients from PVAT to the aortic media in human AAA tissues remain lacking. Future studies employing spatial transcriptomics or micro-dialysis techniques is essential to directly validate the presence and functional relevance of these local adipokine gradients. Prospective validation in large, multicenter cohorts will be essential to confirm the predictive performance of these composite models and establish their utility for guiding AAA risk assessment and targeted interventions.

While adipokine targeted therapies are not yet clinically available, they hold the potential to reframe AAA as a metabolically modulated vascular disease. Continued research into adipokine-related pathways, along with biomarker guided precision therapies, could open entirely new avenues for AAA treatment, moving beyond conventional risk factor management and surgical interventions.

Conclusions

Adipokine dysregulation, characterized by an excess of leptin, resistin, chemerin, FABP4, LCN2 and FGF21, alongside the downregulation of adiponectin, omentin and apelin, emerges as a unifying metabolic-immune driver of AAA. These signals converge on inflammation protease programmes, vascular smooth muscle cells phenotypic switching and maladaptive neovascularization, while protective mechanisms are diminished. Clinically, composite adipokine panels combined with imaging techniques surpass single markers in risk stratification, and several pathways offer tractable targets that are amenable to pharmacodynamic monitoring.
