Biased Mean Quadrangle and Applications
Anton Malandii, Stan Uryasev

Abstract
This paper introduces biased mean regression , estimating the biased mean , i.e., 𝔼 ⁡ [ Y ] + x {\mathbb{E}}[Y]+x , where x ∈ ℝ x\in{\mathbb{R}} . The approach addresses a fundamental statistical problem that covers numerous applications. For instance, estimation of factors driving portfolio loss exceeding expected loss by a specified amount (e.g., x = x= $10 billion) or estimation of factors impacting a specific excess release of radiation in the environment (nuclear safety regulations specify different severity levels). The estimation is done by minimizing the so-called superexpectation error . We establish two equivalence results that connect the method to popular paradigms: (i) biased mean regression is equivalent to quantile regression for an appropriate parameterization and is equivalent to the ordinary least squares for x = 0 x=0 ; (ii) in portfolio optimization, minimizing superexpectation risk , associated with the superexpectation error, is equivalent to CVaR optimization. The approach is computationally attractive, as minimizing the superexpectation error is reduced to linear programming (LP), offering algorithmic and modeling advantages. It is a good alternative to the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. The approach is based on the Risk Quadrangle (RQ) framework, which links four stochastic functionals—error, regret, risk, and deviation—through a statistic. For the biased mean quadrangle, the statistic is the biased mean. We study properties of the new quadrangle, such as subregularity , and establish its relationship to the quantile quadrangle. Numerical experiments confirm theoretical statements and illustrate the practical implications.

Introduction

Motivation.

One of the fundamental goals in statistical modeling is to identify covariates driving significant
excesses from baseline behavior—that is, to explain overperformance or underperformance relative to

\mathbb{E}[Y]
by a user-chosen margin
x
in the units of
Y
.
Let
Y
denote the response (regressant) and
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
the covariates (regressors, factors). The question we target is:

\textit{Which factors in }\mathbf{X}\textit{ explain why }Y\textit{ exceeds (or falls short of) its mean }\mathbb{E}[Y]\textit{ by a fixed margin }x>0?

This problem recurs across various domains. Here are some examples.

•

Finance:
with
Y
a portfolio loss and its driving factors
\mathbf{X}
, which factors are responsible for losses exceeding the expected loss by
x=\$10
billion?

•

Material design:
Let
Y
denote a target performance metric for a candidate material (e.g., the
ultimate tensile strength (UTS) measured in
\mathrm{MPa}
) and let
\mathbf{X}

collect design and process features such as composition (wt.%), heat-treatment
temperature (
{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
), holding time (
\mathrm{h}
), cooling rate (
{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{s}
),
and microstructure descriptors (grain size
\mu\mathrm{m}
, porosity vol.%). Which settings of
\mathbf{X}
are associated with producing parts that underperform the baseline
UTS and
x=1\,\mathrm{MPa}
below the population mean?

•

Medicine:
with
Y
a recovery metric and
\mathbf{X}
treatment features, which factors are associated with patients beating the average recovery by a clinically meaningful margin?

•

Supply chain and operations:
with
Y
delivery time and
\mathbf{X}
capturing supplier reliability and transportation costs, which factors explain deliveries better than average by
x=2
hours?

A standard tool for biased estimates is
quantile regression

[
Koenker and Bassett 1978
]
, which models conditional quantiles indexed by a confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
. While powerful, quantile regression does not specify how far above (or below) the mean the estimate should be (by design, the quantile regression estimates quantiles).
By contrast, the
biased mean regression
(developed in this paper) specifies the margin of practical interest
x
directly in the units of
Y
. The bias is specified relative to the expectation
\mathbb{E}[Y]
. This yields estimates that are easy to understand (‘‘factors leading to under/overperformance the average by exactly
x
units’’) and easy to align with policy or design constraints.

Importantly, the biased-mean and quantile regression are not in conflict. We show that for each bias
x
there exists a corresponding quantile level
\alpha
that produces the same estimate, and conversely (under mild conditions). Thus, the biased mean approach can be seen as a
reparameterization
of quantile regression: the user selects a margin
x
in target units rather than a probability level
\alpha
. This reparameterization offers an alternative modeling perspective, and through the RQ integrates with risk and deviation measures used in portfolio optimization, management, and control.

Contributions.

This paper introduces a new quadrangle — the
biased mean quadrangle
— and investigates its mathematical properties and practical implications. Its statistic is the mean plus a real parameter (
bias
).
The associated risk measure — the
superexpectation risk
—generalizes the well-known mean-absolute risk
[
Shapiro et al. 2014
]
. The corresponding error, termed the
superexpectation error
, is a stochastic compound functional that balances the averages of the positive and negative parts of the random variable. As in other quadrangles, the regret and deviation components are obtained from the risk and error via the standard RQ identities (see Definition
2.8
).

In particular, we emphasize the following results.

1.

Reparametrization of quantile regression.

We show that biased-mean regression using the superexpectation error is a reparametrization of quantile regression. In particular, quantiles can be represented as biased means with a prescribed bias
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We prove the equivalence of these two regressions; see Theorem
4.1
.

2.

OLS as an LP.
When the bias parameter is zero (
x=0
), the resulting (piecewise-linear) mean quadrangle is regular–and, in fact, coherent (see Corollary
3.4
). This quadrangle serves as a coherent alternative to the standard mean quadrangle
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
, Example
1^{\prime}
]
. In particular, we show the equivalence between OLS and the regression induced by the superexpectation error of this new mean quadrangle (piecewise-linear version), which we term the
superexpectation regression
(see Theorem
4.3
). Moreover, the superexpectation regression admits an LP formulation, offering computational and modeling advantages (See Subsection
4.3
and Subsection
5.4
).

3.

Equivalence of deviation minimization.
We prove the equivalence between CVaR deviation and superexpectation deviation minimization (see Proposition
3.7
) and showcase its practical significance in the case of portfolio optimization (see Example
3.1
and Subsection
5.1
).

4.

Subregularity.
We prove that the biased mean quadrangle is subregular (see Definition
2.9
and Proposition
3.1
). The notion of subregularity, first proposed in
Rockafellar 2024
;
Grechuk et al. 2026
, extends the concept of regularity introduced in
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
and can be viewed as an alternative to the well-known notion of coherence
[
Artzner et al. 1999
]
. The biased mean quadrangle also provides, to our knowledge, the first example of a subregular quadrangle with concrete practical relevance.

Mathematical Background

This section provides the definitions and theorems necessary for the subsequent analysis. It opens with definitions of value-at-risk, conditional value-at-risk, and the components of the RQ, and closes with two key theorems that are central to the proofs of the main results.

2.1
VaR, CVaR, and Risk Quadrangle

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},{\mathbb{P}})
be a probability space. For
p\in[1,\infty]
, let
{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega):={\cal L}^{p}(\Omega,{\cal A},{\mathbb{P}})
be a normed space of all random variables
X
with
\|X\|_{p}<\infty
, where
\|X\|_{p}=({\mathbb{E}}[|X|^{p}])^{1/p}
for
p<\infty
, and
\|X\|_{\infty}=\mathrm{ess}\sup|X|
.
The cumulative distribution function is denoted by
F_{X}(x)={\mathbb{P}}(X\leq x)
.
The positive and negative part of a number
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
is
x_{+}=\max\{0,x\}
and
x_{-}=\max\{0,-x\}
, respectively.

Let
\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}:={\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
denote an extended set of real numbers. Then, a functional
{\cal F}:{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega)\to\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}
is called
convex
if

{\cal F}\left(\lambda X+(1-\lambda)Y\right)\leq\lambda{\cal F}(X)+(1-\lambda){\cal F}(Y),\ \forall\;X,Y\in{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega),\ \lambda\in[0,1],

and
closed (or lower-semicontinuous)
if

\left\{X\in{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega)\,\big|\,{\cal F}(X)\leq c\right\}\ \textrm{is a closed set in }{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega)\ \forall\;c<\infty.

Definition 2.1

(VaR)
.

The
value-at-risk
(VaR, quantile) of a random variable X at a confidence level
\alpha\in[0,1]
is defined by

{\rm VaR}_{\alpha}(X):=[{\rm VaR}_{\alpha}^{-}(X),{\rm VaR}_{\alpha}^{+}(X)],

(2.1)

where

{\rm VaR}^{-}_{\alpha}(X):=\begin{cases}\sup\left\{x\,\big|\,F_{X}(x)<\alpha\right\},&\alpha\in(0,1]\\
{\rm ess\,inf}\;X,&\alpha=0\par\end{cases}

{\rm VaR}_{\alpha}^{+}(X):=\begin{cases}\inf\left\{x\,\big|\,F_{X}(x)>\alpha\right\},&\alpha\in[0,1)\\
{\rm ess\,sup}\;X,&\alpha=1\par\end{cases}

If
{\rm VaR}_{\alpha}^{-}(X)={\rm VaR}_{\alpha}^{+}(X)
, then

{\rm VaR}_{\alpha}(X)={\rm VaR}_{\alpha}^{-}(X)={\rm VaR}_{\alpha}^{+}(X).

Definition 2.2

(CVaR)
.

The
conditional value-at-risk
(CVaR) (also called AVaR, superquantile, expected shortfall) of a random variable
X
at confidence level
\alpha\in[0,1]
is defined by

{\rm CVaR}_{\alpha}(X):=\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\displaystyle\int\limits_{\alpha}^{1}{\rm VaR}^{-}_{\beta}(X)\,d\beta,\quad\alpha\in(0,1).

(2.2)

For
\alpha=0:

{\rm CVaR}_{0}(X):={\mathbb{E}}[X]\,.

For
\alpha=1:

{\rm CVaR}_{1}(X):=\lim_{\tau\to 1}{\rm CVaR}_{\tau}(X)={\rm ess\,sup}\;X\,.

Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
introduced a novel concept known as the Risk Quadrangle (RQ), aiming to establish a connection between risk management, reliability, statistics, and stochastic optimization theories. The RQ methodology groups functions of a random value
X
into quadrangles, each comprising four elements: 1)
risk,
providing a numerical surrogate for the overall hazard in
X
, 2)
deviation,
measuring the ‘‘nonconstancy’’ in
X
, 3)
regret,
which is similar to the utility function with a negative sign, and 4)
error,
quantifying the ‘‘nonzeroness’’ in
X
. Elements of the quadrangle are ‘‘linked’’ by a
statistic
function.

\begin{array}[]{lcr}\qquad\text{risk}{\cal R}\,\longleftrightarrow\,{\cal D}\text{deviation}&\cr\hskip 50.0pt\uparrow\hskip 9.0pt{\cal S}\hskip 9.0pt\uparrow&\cr\hskip 12.0pt\text{regret}{\cal V}\,\longleftrightarrow\;{\cal E}\text{error}&\cr\end{array}

Definition 2.3

(Regular risk)
.

A closed convex functional
{\cal R}:{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega)\to\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}
is called a
regular measure of risk
if it satisfies:

(R1)\,\ {\cal R}(C)=C,\quad\forall\;C=const\qquad\text{and}\qquad(R2)\,\ \;{\cal R}(X)>{\mathbb{E}}[X],\quad\forall\;X\neq const\;.

Definition 2.4

(Coherent risk)
.

A closed convex functional
{\cal R}:{\cal L}^{p}\to\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}
is called a
coherent measure of risk in basic sense
if

(C0)

{\cal R}(C)=C\textrm{ for constants }C;

(C1)

{\cal R}(\lambda X)=\lambda{\cal R}(X)
for all
\lambda>0;

(C2)

{\cal R}(X)\leq{\cal R}(Y)
for all
X
and
Y
such that
X\leq Y
almost surely,

and
coherent in general sense
if (C1) is left out.

Definition 2.5

(Regular deviation)
.

A closed convex functional
{\cal D}:{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega)\to\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}^{+}
is called a
regular measure of deviation
if it satisfies:

(D1)\,\ {\cal D}(C)=0,\quad\forall\;C=const\qquad\text{and}\qquad(D2)\,\ {\cal D}(X)>0,\quad\forall\;X\neq const.

Definition 2.6

(Regular regret)
.

A closed convex functional
{\cal V}:{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega)\to\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}
is called a
regular measure of regret
if it satisfies the following axioms

(V1)\,\ {\cal V}(0)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad(V2)\,\ {\cal V}(X)>{\mathbb{E}}[X],\quad\forall\;X\neq 0\;.

Definition 2.7

(Regular error)
.

A functional
{\cal E}:{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega)\to\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}^{+}
is called a
regular measure of error
if it satisfies the following axioms:

(E1)\,\ {\cal E}(0)=0\qquad\text{end}\qquad(E2)\,\ {\cal E}(X)>0,\quad\forall\;X\neq 0\;.

Definition 2.8

(Regular risk quadrangle)
.

A quartet
({\cal R},{\cal D},{\cal V},{\cal E})
of regular measures of risk, deviation, regret, and error is called a
regular risk quadrangle with statistic

{\cal S}
if it satisfies the following relationship formulae:

(Q1)

error projection:

{\cal D}(X)=\min\limits_{C}\!\big\{\,{\cal E}(X-C)\,\big\}
,

(Q2)

regret formula:

{\cal R}(X)=\min\limits_{C}\!\big\{\,C+{\cal V}(X-C)\,\big\}
,

(Q3)

centerness:

{\cal R}(X)={\cal D}(X)+{\mathbb{E}}[X],\quad{\cal V}(X)={\cal E}(X)+{\mathbb{E}}[X],

(Q4)

statistic:

{\cal S}(X)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{C}\!\big\{\,{\cal E}(X-C)\,\big\}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{C}\!\big\{\,C+{\cal V}(X-C)\,\big\}.

Definition 2.9

(Subregular risk quadrangle)
.

A quartet
({\cal R},{\cal D},{\cal V},{\cal E})
is called a
subregular risk quadrangle
if
measures of risk, deviation, regret, and error comprising it are regular with non-strict inequalities in axioms (R2), (D2), (V2), (E2) for any
X
, and additionally there exists
\lambda>0
such that
{\cal R}(\lambda X)>{\mathbb{E}}[\lambda X],\ {\cal V}(\lambda X)>{\mathbb{E}}[\lambda X]
for non-constant
X
and
{\cal D}(\lambda X)>0,\ {\cal E}(\lambda X)>0
for non-zero
X.

Definition 2.10

(Coherent risk quadrangle)
.

A quartet
({\cal R},{\cal D},{\cal V},{\cal E})
satisfying relationship formulae (Q1)–(Q4), where
{\cal R}
is a coherent measure of risk is called a
coherent risk quadrangle.

Remark 2.1

(Error Projection and Regret Formula)
.

To prove that a given quartet
({\cal R},{\cal D},{\cal V},{\cal E})
is a quadrangle, it is sufficient to verify either conditions (Q1) and (Q3), or conditions (Q2) and (Q3), as conditions (Q1) and (Q2) are intrinsically linked through the condition (Q3). Indeed,

{\cal R}(X)=\min\limits_{C}\!\big\{\,C+{\cal V}(X-C)\,\big\}=\min\limits_{C}\!\big\{\,{\cal E}(X-C)\,\big\}+{\mathbb{E}}[X]={\cal D}(X)+{\mathbb{E}}[X].

Consequently, (Q4) holds automatically.

Proposition 3.1

(Biased Mean Quadrangle)
.

The quartet
\left({\cal R}_{x},{\cal D}_{x},{\cal V}_{x},{\cal E}_{x}\right)
is a subregular quadrangle with statstic
{\cal S}_{x}
.

Proof.

To prove that
\left({\cal R}_{x},{\cal D}_{x},{\cal V}_{x},{\cal E}_{x}\right)
is a quadrangle, it suffices (see Remark
2.1
) to establish the validity of (Q1) and (Q3). To show that the quadrangle is subregular, it suffices to demonstrate that
{\cal E}_{x}
satisfies the Definition
2.7
with a non-strict inequality in (E2) and show that there exists
\lambda>0
such that
{\cal E}_{x}(\lambda X)>0
for non-zero
X
.

Indeed,
{\cal E}_{x}
is a closed convex functional as a maximum of closed convex functionals and

{\cal E}_{x}(0)=\max\{-x_{+},-x_{-}\}=0.

Hence
{\cal E}_{x}(X)
satisfies (E1). Further, since
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{-}]
and
{\mathbb{E}}[X_{+}]
are non-negative

{\cal E}_{x}(X)=\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[X_{-}]-x_{+},{\mathbb{E}}[X_{+}]-x_{-}\}\geq\max\{-x_{+},-x_{-}\}=0,\quad\forall\;X\neq 0.

There are only two cases when
\forall\;X\neq 0,\quad{\cal E}_{x}(X)=0:

1)

x>0,\ {\mathbb{E}}[X_{+}]=0,\ {\mathbb{E}}[X_{-}]-x\leq 0,
hence for any
\varepsilon>0,

\lambda=\dfrac{x}{{\mathbb{E}}[X_{-}]}+\varepsilon>0
the following is valid
{\cal E}_{x}(\lambda X)>0.

2)

x<0,\ {\mathbb{E}}[X_{-}]=0,\ {\mathbb{E}}[X_{+}]+x\leq 0,
hence for any
\varepsilon>0,

\lambda=\dfrac{-x}{{\mathbb{E}}[X_{+}]}+\varepsilon>0
the following is valid
{\cal E}_{x}(\lambda X)>0.

Thus
{\cal E}_{x}
is a subregular error. Next, let us consider the error projection formula:

\min_{C}\{{\cal E}_{x}(X-C)\}=\min_{C}\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{-}-x_{+},{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{+}-x_{-}\}.

(3.8)

Note that the minimum in (
3.8
) is attained if and only if

{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{-}-x_{+}={\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{+}-x_{-}

or equivalently

C=x+{\mathbb{E}}[X]={\cal S}_{x}(X).

(3.9)

Therefore, by substituting (
3.9
) into (
3.8
), we obtain

\min_{C}\{{\cal E}_{x}(X-C)\}={\mathbb{E}}[X-{\mathbb{E}}[X]-x]_{+}-x_{-}=E_{X-{\mathbb{E}}[X]}(x)-x_{+}={\cal D}_{x}(X),

which completes the proof.

∎

Corollary 3.2

(Mean Quadrangle (mean upper semideviation version))
.

An important special case of the biased mean quadrangle is when the parameter

Therefore, we call this quadrangle the ‘‘mean quadrangle’’ (mean-upper-semideviation version). This quadrangle can be viewed as a ‘‘linearization’’ of the standard mean quadrangle
[

]
. Notably, this quadrangle is coherent (see Corollary

Corollary 3.3

(Mean Quadrangle (
{\cal L}^{1}

The mean quadrangle (mean-upper-semideviation version) admits an equivalent representation in terms of

Corollary 3.4

(Regularity of the Mean Quadrangle)
.

The quartet
\left({\cal R}_{0},{\cal D}_{0},{\cal V}_{0},{\cal E}_{0}\right)
is a regular (moreover a coherent) quadrangle with statistic
{\cal S}_{0}
.

Proof.

It is obvious that
{\cal E}_{0}(X)=\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[X_{+}],{\mathbb{E}}[X_{-}]\}>0,\ \forall\;X\neq 0
and that it is convex and closed functional. Thus
{\cal E}_{0}(X)
is a regular error. The rest follows from Proposition
3.1
.

The proof of coherence of
{\cal R}_{0}
can be found in
[
Shapiro et al. 2014
]
.
∎

The biased mean quadrangle has a profound relationship with the quantile quadrangle, demonstrated in Proposition
3.5
.

Proposition 3.5

(Relation between Quantile and Biased Mean Quadrangles)
.

Let
\left({\cal R}_{\alpha},{\cal D}_{\alpha},{\cal V}_{\alpha},{\cal E}_{\alpha}\right)
be a quantile quadrangle for
\alpha\in(0,1).
Then for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{x}(X)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left\{(1-\alpha)({\cal R}_{\alpha}(X)-x_{+})+\alpha({\mathbb{E}}[X]-x_{-})\right\},

(3.10)

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{x}(X)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left\{(1-\alpha)({\cal D}_{\alpha}(X)-x_{+})-\alpha x_{-}\right\},

(3.11)

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{x}(X)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left\{(1-\alpha)({\cal V}_{\alpha}(X)-x_{+})+\alpha({\mathbb{E}}[X]-x_{-})\right\},

(3.12)

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{x}(X)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left\{(1-\alpha)({\cal E}_{\alpha}(X)-x_{+})-\alpha x_{-}\right\}.

(3.13)

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove equalities (
3.11
) and (
3.13
) since the expressions for
{\cal R}_{x}(X)
and
{\cal V}_{x}(X)
follow from (Q3). Indeed,

\begin{split}&\;\;\;\;\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left\{(1-\alpha)({\cal E}_{\alpha}(X)-x_{+})-\alpha x_{-}\right\}\\
&=\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left\{(1-\alpha)\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}X_{+}+X_{-}\right]-x_{+}\right)-\alpha x_{-}\right\}\\
&=\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left\{\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\alpha X_{+}+(1-\alpha)X_{-}\right]-(1-\alpha)x_{+}-\alpha x_{-}\right)\right\}\\
&=\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[X_{-}]-x_{+},{\mathbb{E}}[X_{+}]-x_{-}\}={\cal E}_{x}(X)\;.\end{split}

To demonstrate the equality (
3.11
), we use the definition of
{\cal D}_{x}(X),
i.e.,

{\cal D}_{x}(X)=E_{X-{\mathbb{E}}[X]}(x)-x_{+}

and apply the Theorem
2.2
to a random variable
X-{\mathbb{E}}[X].

∎

Corollary 3.6

(Kusuoka Representation of Mean Absolute Risk)
.

By setting
x=0
in (
3.10
), we obtain the (minimal) Kusuoka representation (see
Shapiro 2012
) of the mean-absolute risk, i.e.,

{\cal R}_{0}(X)=\displaystyle\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left\{(1-\alpha){\cal R}_{\alpha}(X)+\alpha{\mathbb{E}}[X]\right\}.

(3.14)

Interestingly, it is possible to construct the mean quadrangle (mean-upper-semideviation version) starting from
{\cal R}_{0}
(note that it is generally easier to construct a quadrangle starting from either a regret or an error).
Indeed, application of Theorem
2.1
to (
3.14
) yields

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{0}(X)

\displaystyle=\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\min_{C\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left\{(1-\alpha)\left(C+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{+}\right)+\alpha{\mathbb{E}}[X]\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\min_{C\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left\{(1-\alpha)C+{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{+}+\alpha{\mathbb{E}}[X]\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{C\in{\mathbb{R}}}\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left\{{\mathbb{E}}[X]+{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{-}+\alpha({\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{+}-{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{-})\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{C\in{\mathbb{R}}}\ C+\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{+},{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{-}\}+{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]

\displaystyle=\min_{C\in{\mathbb{R}}}\ C+{\cal V}_{0}(X-C),

where
{\cal S}_{0}(X)={\mathbb{E}}[X]=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{C\in{\mathbb{R}}}\ C+{\cal V}_{0}(X-C).

For a more detailed discussion on
{\cal R}_{0},
its Kusuoka representation, and statistical properties, see
[
Shapiro et al. 2014
, Chapter 6]
.

The following Proposition
3.7
establishes the equivalence between the CVaR deviation and the superexpectation deviation minimization.

Proposition 3.7

(Equivalence of the CVaR and Superexpectation Deviation Minimization)
.

Let
{\cal X}\subseteq{\cal L}^{1}(\Omega)
be a nonempty closed, convex, and bounded feasible set for the following optimization problems:

\min_{X\in{\cal X}}\quad{\cal D}_{x}(X),

(3.15)

\min_{X\in{\cal X}}\quad{\cal D}_{\alpha}(X).

(3.16)

Additionally, assume that the optimal solution in (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) exists.
Then
\forall\;x\in{\mathbb{R}}\;\exists\;\alpha\in[0,1]
such that

\mathop{\rm argmin}_{X\in{\cal X}}\ {\cal D}_{x}(X)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{X\in{\cal X}}\ {\cal D}_{\alpha}(X).

(3.17)

Moreover, for
X^{*}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{X\in{\cal X}}\ {\cal D}_{x}(X),\ \alpha\in[{\mathbb{P}}(X^{*}<x+{\mathbb{E}}[X^{*}]),\ {\mathbb{P}}(X^{*}\leq x+{\mathbb{E}}[X^{*}])]
realizes the equality (
3.17
).

Proof.

By Proposition
3.5

\min_{X\in{\cal X}}\quad{\cal D}_{x}(X)=\min_{X\in{\cal X}}\displaystyle\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\quad\left\{(1-\alpha)({\cal D}_{\alpha}(X)-x_{+})-\alpha x_{-}\right\}

(3.18)

The left-hand side of (
3.18
) is a convex optimization problem for which the existence of the optimal solution is assumed.
The right-hand side of (
3.18
) is a minimax (convex-concave) optimization problem. Since the existence of an optimal solution holds for the left-hand side of (
3.18
), then the function
(1-\alpha)({\cal D}_{\alpha}(X)-x_{+})-\alpha x_{-}
possesses a saddle point
(X^{*},\alpha^{*})
on
{\cal X}\times[0,1].

Let
X^{*}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{X\in{\cal X}}\ {\cal D}_{x}(X)
for some
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then (
3.18
) implies that

{\cal D}_{x}(X^{*})=(1-\alpha^{*})({\cal D}_{\!\alpha^{*}}(X^{*})-x_{+})-\alpha^{*}x_{-},

where
(X^{*},\alpha^{*})
is a saddle point for the right-hand side of (
3.18
). Therefore, by the definition of a saddle point

X^{*}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{X\in{\cal X}}\ {\cal D}_{\!\alpha^{*}}(X).

Moreover, if
X^{*}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{X\in{\cal X}}\ {\cal D}_{\!\alpha^{*}}(X)
for the same
\alpha^{*},
then
(X^{*},\alpha^{*})
is a saddle point for the right-hand side of (
3.18
) and consequently,
X^{*}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{X\in{\cal X}}\ {\cal D}_{x}(X)
for the same
x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Finally, Theorem
2.2
applied to a random variable
X^{*}-{\mathbb{E}}[X^{*}]
implies that
\alpha^{*}\in[{\mathbb{P}}(X^{*}<x+{\mathbb{E}}[X^{*}]),\ {\mathbb{P}}(X^{*}\leq x+{\mathbb{E}}[X^{*}])],
which completes the proof.
∎

Let

4
Generalized Regression within the Biased Mean Quadrangle

This section formulates and studies generalized regression problems in the framework of the biased mean quadrangle.

Let
Y\in{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega)
be a target random variable, i.e., a
regressant
and
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})^{\top}
be a random vector of
regressors
such that
X_{i}\in{\cal L}^{p}(\Omega),\ i=1,\ldots,d.
In RQ framework, the
generalized regression
problem is to find a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}},
belonging to a class of measurable functions
{\cal C},
that minimizes the
regression residual

Z_{f}:=Y-f(\mathbf{X})
with respect to a particular error
{\cal E}
(e.g., mean square error, mean absolute error). Specifically, the goal is to solve the following stochastic optimization problem:

\min_{f\in{\cal C}}\quad{\cal E}(Z_{f}).

(4.19)

Fix the space of random variables to be
{\cal L}^{1}(\Omega)
. By setting the superexpectation error
{\cal E}_{x},\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}
as the objective in (
4.19
), we obtain the biased mean regression (BMR)

\min_{f\in{\cal C}}\quad{\cal E}_{x}(Z_{f}),

(4.20)

and by setting the KB error (see Diagram 2)
{\cal E}_{\alpha},\ \alpha\in(0,1)
as the objective in (
4.19
), we obtain the quantile regression (QR)

\min_{f\in{\cal C}}\quad{\cal E}_{\alpha}(Z_{f}).

(4.21)

According to the
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
, Regression Theorem]
, problem (
4.20
) is equivalent to the following constrained optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{f\in{\cal C}}

\displaystyle\quad{\cal D}_{x}(Z_{f})

(4.22)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad 0={\cal S}_{x}(Z_{f}),

(4.23)

and analogously, problem (
4.21
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{f\in{\cal C}}

\displaystyle\quad{\cal D}_{\alpha}(Z_{f})

(4.24)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad 0\in{\cal S}_{\alpha}(Z_{f}).

(4.25)

Since (
4.22
)–(
4.23
) is a constrained deviation minimization problem, it is related to the quantile regression problem (
4.24
)–(
4.25
) via Proposition
3.7
.

Theorem 4.1

(Equivalence of QR and BMR)
.

Consider the regression problem (
4.19
), where the regression residual
Z_{f}\in{\cal L}^{1}(\Omega),\ \forall f\in{\cal C}.
For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
denote by
\mathfrak{S}_{se}
a set of optimal solutions to the regression problem (
4.20
). Let
f^{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{se},
and denote by
\mathfrak{S}_{kb}
a set of optimal solutions to the regression problem (
4.21
) with the KB error, where

\alpha^{*}\in\mathcal{I}(Z_{f^{*}}):=[{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{f^{*}}<0),\ {\mathbb{P}}(Z_{f^{*}}\leq 0)]\,.

Then

\mathfrak{S}_{se}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{kb}.

(4.26)

Proof.

Let
f^{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{se}
be an optimal solution to (
4.22
)–(
4.23
) for some
x\in{\mathbb{R}}.
Then

{\cal D}_{x}(Z_{f^{*}})=\min_{f\in{\cal C}}\ {\cal D}_{x}(Z_{f})\text{s.t.}0=x+{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{f}].

Thus by Proposition
3.5
and Theorem
2.2

{\cal D}_{x}(Z_{f^{*}})=(1-\alpha^{*})({\cal D}_{\alpha^{*}}(Z_{f^{*}})-x_{+})-\alpha^{*}x_{-}\text{with}0=x+{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{f^{*}}]\in{\rm VaR}_{\alpha^{*}}(Z_{f^{*}}),\text{where}\alpha^{*}\in\mathcal{I}(Z_{f^{*}}).

Therefore, Proposition
3.7
implies that
f^{*}
is an optimal solution to (
4.24
)–(
4.25
) and hence an optimal solution to (
4.21
), i.e.,
f^{*}\in\mathfrak{S}_{kb}.

∎

The equivalence of QR and BMR suggests an alternative way of specifying a quantile of a given distribution. Instead of setting a specific confidence level
\alpha\in[0,1],
one can define a quantile using a parameter
x\in{\mathbb{R}}.
This parameter allows a quantile to be represented as a biased (shifted) mean, where
x
is the magnitude of the bias. The following example illustrates one of the many potential use cases of this methodology.

Example 4.1

(The newsvendor problem)
.

Let
Y\in{\mathbb{R}}
denote a random demand and
\mathbf{X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
observed contexts. Let
\gamma>0
be the buying (unit) cost and
\delta>\gamma
the selling (unit) price. We model the order quantity as an affine function

f(\mathbf{X}):=\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{X}+c_{0},\qquad(\mathbf{c},c_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}\;.

Define the residual
Z(\mathbf{c},c_{0}):=Y-f(\mathbf{X})
.

Question A (order policy under given prices):

Given
(\gamma,\delta)
, what order policy
f(\mathbf{X})
minimizes the expected underage/overage loss?

Answer A:

Solve the following KB error optimization problem

\min_{\mathbf{c},\,c_{0}}\;{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{\delta}\right)\,Z(\mathbf{c},c_{0})_{+}\;+\;\frac{\gamma}{\delta}\,Z(\mathbf{c},c_{0})_{-}\right].

(4.27)

Problem (
4.27
) is the quantile regression problem at level

\alpha\;=\;1-\frac{\gamma}{\delta}\in(0,1).

Hence any minimizer
(\mathbf{c}^{*},c_{0}^{*})
satisfies

\mathbf{c}^{*\top}\mathbf{X}+c_{0}^{*}\;\in\;q_{\alpha}(Y\mid\mathbf{X})\quad\text{a.s.}

In the no-context case
\mathbf{X}\equiv\mathbf{0}
, we obtain
c_{0}^{*}\in q_{\alpha}(Y)
, the classical newsvendor quantile rule.

Question B (pricing from a target stated in
units
):

Suppose the decision-maker specifies a practically meaningful target
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
for the exceedance/shortfall of expected demand. What selling price
\delta
makes the optimal order policy consistent with this target?

Answer B:

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
encode a target exceedance of the mean (in the sense of the biased-mean quadrangle), and consider the superexpectation error
{\cal E}_{x}
.
Solve the
biased-mean regression

\min_{\mathbf{c},\,c_{0}}\;{\cal E}_{x}\bigl(Z(\mathbf{c},c_{0})\bigr)\,.

(4.28)

Let
(\mathbf{c}^{*},c_{0}^{*})
be any minimizer of (
4.28
) and define the induced quantile level

\alpha^{*}\;:=\;{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(Z(\mathbf{c}^{*},c_{0}^{*})\leq 0\bigr)\;=\;{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(Y\leq\mathbf{c}^{*\top}\mathbf{X}+c_{0}^{*}\bigr)\,.

Then by Theorem
4.1
there exists a quantile level
\alpha=\alpha^{*}
such that
(\mathbf{c}^{*},c_{0}^{*})
also solves the
\alpha
–quantile regression. Consequently, to
choose the selling price

\delta
consistent with the biased-mean target
x
, set

\delta\;=\;\frac{\gamma}{1-\alpha^{*}},

because the newsvendor identity
\alpha=1-\gamma/\delta
implies
\delta=\gamma/(1-\alpha)
and
\alpha=\alpha^{*}
at the optimum. Operationally:

1.

Solve (
4.28
) for
(\mathbf{c}^{*},c_{0}^{*})
at the desired
x
.

2.

Compute
\alpha^{*}={\mathbb{P}}\bigl(Y\leq\mathbf{c}^{*\top}\mathbf{X}+c_{0}^{*}\bigr)
.

3.

Set
\delta=\gamma/(1-\alpha^{*})
.

Formulation (
4.27
) answers:
‘‘Given prices, what is the optimal order policy?’’
By contrast, (
4.28
) answers:
‘‘Given a target buffer
x
stated in natural units of demand, what price
\delta
realizes the resulting optimal order policy?’’
The latter is often easier to elicit from practitioners who work with units rather than in confidence levels.

In particular, when
x=0,
one recovers a quantile coinciding with (or containing) a mean of a distribution. The following Corollary
4.2
discusses this in more detail.

Corollary 4.2

(Unbiased
{\cal L}^{1}
Regression)
.

For
x=0,
problem (
4.22
)–(
4.23
) is equivalent to (see Corollary
3.3
)

\displaystyle\min_{f\in{\cal C}}

\displaystyle\quad\|Z_{f}\|_{1}

(4.29)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{f}]=0,

(4.30)

4.1
and

, the optimal solution to (

)–(
4.30
) is

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
is such that

implying that

when the conditional distribution of

is continuous.

It is well-known that the unbiased

regression is simply the
{\cal L}^{2}

\min_{f\in{\cal C}}\quad\|Z_{f}\|_{2},

(4.32)

since the optimal solution to (

) is the conditional mean
1

1

assuming that

), the natural question is when

It is possible to answer this question in the case of linear regression, i.e., when

4.1
Linear Regression and Estimation

Estimation via linear regression is one of the central topics of the RQ theory and classical statistics. It is well-known (see
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
, Regression Theorem]
) that given a functional of expectation type, i.e.
{\cal E}(X)={\mathbb{E}}[e(X)],
where
e:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is such that
{\cal E}
is a (sub)regular error, the solution to the generalized regression problem (
4.19
) is a conditional statistic
{\cal S}(Y|\mathbf{X})
related to
{\cal E}
(i.e.,
{\cal E}
and
{\cal S}
are in the same quadrangle). The natural question is whether this is the case for
{\cal E}_{0}(X)=\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[X_{+}],{\mathbb{E}}[X_{-}]\}
for which the associated statistic is
{\mathbb{E}}[X]
. Here, we answer this question in the case of linear regression.

Before stating the main theorem, we provide one technical definition needed for its proof.

Definition 4.1

(Expectation of random set)
.

For a measurable set-valued mapping
S
, we say that
S(X)
is a
random set,
with possible outcomes
\{S(X),\ X\in{\cal X}\ (\textrm{support of}\ X)\}
occurring according to the probability distribution of
X.
Its
expectation
is defined as

{\mathbb{E}}[S(X)]=\left\{{\mathbb{E}}[v(X)]\,\big|\,v(X)\in S(X)\ \forall\;X\in{\cal X},\ v(X)\text{integrable}\right\}

see
Royset and Wets 2021
for more details.

Theorem 4.3

(Equivalence of OLS and SE Regressions)
.

Let
X_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,d,\ Y
and,
\varepsilon
be random variables defined on
{\cal L}^{1}(\Omega),
and let

Y=f^{*}(\mathbf{X})+\varepsilon\quad a.s.,\quad f^{*}\in\operatorname{Lin}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\quad\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})^{\top}

(4.33)

be a true regression law, where (a)
\varepsilon
and
X_{i}
are independent, (b)
{\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon]=0\,.

Then

(i)\ f^{*}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{f\in\operatorname{Lin}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\|Z_{f}\|_{2}\ \textrm{ if }\ \|Z_{f^{*}}\|_{2}<\infty,\qquad(ii)\ f^{*}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{f\in\operatorname{Lin}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}{\cal E}_{0}(Z_{f})\,.

(4.34)

Proof.

Proof of (i)
.
The proof directly follows from the

Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
, Regression Theorem]
.

Proof of (ii)
. Let us consider the linear regression problem

\min\limits_{f\in\operatorname{Lin}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}{\cal E}_{0}(Z_{f})\,,

which can be written in explicit form as follows

\min_{c_{0},\mathbf{c}}\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[Z(c_{0},\mathbf{c})_{+}],{\mathbb{E}}[Z(c_{0},\mathbf{c})_{-}]\}\,

(4.35)

where
Z(c_{0},\mathbf{c}):=Y-c_{0}-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{X}.
Problem (
4.35
) is a convex optimization problem. An optimal solution
(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})
of (
4.35
) satisfies the following necessary and sufficient first-order optimality condition

(0,\mathbf{0})\in\partial\bigl(\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})_{+}],{\mathbb{E}}[Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})_{-}]\}\bigr),

(4.36)

where
\partial(\cdot)
denotes the subdifferential of a convex function. By the Dubovitsky-Milyutin theorem, the condition (
4.36
) is equivalent to the following system

\left\{\begin{matrix}(0,\mathbf{0})\in\textrm{conv}\left(\partial\left({\mathbb{E}}[Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})_{+}]\right),\partial\left({\mathbb{E}}[Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})_{-}]\right)\right),\\
{\mathbb{E}}[Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})_{+}]={\mathbb{E}}[Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})_{-}],\end{matrix}\right.

(4.37)

where
\textrm{conv}(\cdot\,,\cdot)
denotes the convex hull of the union of two sets.

Let us compute the subdifferentials
\partial\left({\mathbb{E}}[Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})_{+}]\right)
and
\partial\left({\mathbb{E}}[Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})_{-}]\right)
in (
4.37
). Since

is a proper, convex, and lower-semicontinuous function, the subdifferentiation and the expectation operators can be interchanged. Thus

\displaystyle\partial{\mathbb{E}}[Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})_{+}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\begin{cases}-(1,\mathbf{X}),&Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})>0\\
(0,\mathbf{0}),&Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})<0\\
\textrm{conv}\left(-(1,\mathbf{X}),(0,\mathbf{0})\right),&Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})=0\end{cases}\right],

(4.38)

\displaystyle\partial{\mathbb{E}}[Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})_{-}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\begin{cases}(1,\mathbf{X}),&Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})<0\\
(0,\mathbf{0}),&Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})>0\\
\textrm{conv}\left((1,\mathbf{X}),(0,\mathbf{0})\right),&Z(c^{*}_{0},\mathbf{c}^{*})=0\end{cases}\right].

(4.39)

Let
f^{*}(\mathbf{X})=a_{0}+\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
. To prove the theorem, we will show that
(a_{0},\mathbf{a})
satisfies conditions (
4.37
). Let

and

Hence, given (
4.38
) and (
4.39
), system (

) is equivalent to

\left\{\begin{matrix}(0,\mathbf{0})\in\textrm{conv}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\begin{cases}-(1,\mathbf{X}),&\varepsilon>0\\
(0,\mathbf{0}),&\varepsilon<0\\
\textrm{conv}\left(-(1,\mathbf{X}),(0,\mathbf{0})\right),&\varepsilon=0\end{cases}\right],{\mathbb{E}}\left[\begin{cases}(1,\mathbf{X}),&\varepsilon<0\\
(0,\mathbf{0}),&\varepsilon>0\\
\textrm{conv}\left((1,\mathbf{X}),(0,\mathbf{0})\right),&\varepsilon=0\end{cases}\right]\right)\\
\\
{\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{+}]={\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{-}]\end{matrix}\right.

(4.40)

Denote by
\mathfrak{M}_{-}:={\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathfrak{C}_{-}(\mathbf{X})\,\big|\,\varepsilon=0\big]
the conditional expectation of a random set

by

the conditional expectation of a random set
\mathfrak{C}_{+}(\mathbf{X}):=\textrm{conv}\left((1,\mathbf{X}),(0,\mathbf{0})\right)=\{\lambda(1,\mathbf{X})\,\big|\,\lambda\in[0,1]\},
and by

Define

Then the system (

) can be rewritten in terms of conditional expectation

\left\{\begin{matrix}(0,\mathbf{0})\in\mathfrak{H}(\varepsilon)\\
{\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{+}]={\mathbb{E}}[\varepsilon_{-}]\end{matrix}\right.

(4.41)

where

and

and the fact that

4.2
Sample Average Approximation and Optimization

Exact solving (
4.34
) requires access to the joint distribution of
(Y,\mathbf{X})
, which is unknown in most practical situations. Instead, one works with an i.i.d. random sample
\{(y_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
assumed to be drawn from that joint distribution. A standard approach in this setting is the Sample Average Approximation (SAA) method (see, e.g.,
Shapiro et al. 2014
), which replaces expectations by empirical averages and solves the corresponding deterministic optimization problem.

The application of SAA to ordinary least squares (OLS) regression (see item (i) in (
4.34
)) is well studied and well understood in the classical statistics literature. By contrast, SE regression (see item (ii) in (
4.34
)) is a compound stochastic programming problem. The application of SAA to this type of problem has been studied by
Ermoliev and Norkin 2013
. Moreover, given the equivalent formulation of SE regression as the unbiased
{\cal L}^{1}
regression problem, classical tools–such as uniform (or epigraphical) laws of large numbers, functional central limit theorems, and functional large-deviation results–are also applicable
[
Artstein and Wets 1995
;
Shapiro et al. 2014
]
.

The SAA formulation of the SE regression is as follows

\min_{c_{0},\mathbf{c}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}(c_{0},\mathbf{c})_{+},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}(c_{0},\mathbf{c})_{-}\right\}\,,

(4.44)

where
z_{i}(c_{0},\mathbf{c})=y_{i}-c_{0}-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,n
. Formulation (
4.44
) is a piece-wise linear convex optimization problem, which can be efficiently solved by methods of nondifferentiable (a.k.a. nonsmooth) optimization
[
Shor 1998
]
. This property of (
4.44
) does not provide an algorithmic edge over OLS (smooth problems are usually solved faster than nonsmooth ones
[
Nesterov 2018
]
), yet the piecewise-linear objective yields statistical and modeling benefits.

Robustness to heavy tails and outliers.

The objective in (
4.44
) is convex and piecewise linear. Consequently, it requires merely a finite first moment for well-posedness and consistency, in contrast to quadratic losses that demand finite second moments and exhibit pronounced sensitivity to extreme observations. Practically, this translates into stable estimation under heavy-tailed errors and outliers, without the tuning required by Huber-type losses
[
Huber 1964
;
Huber and Ronchetti 2009
]
and Support Vector Regression method
[
Drucker et al. 1996
;
Vapnik et al. 1996
;
Schölkopf et al. 2000
;
Smola and Schölkopf 2004
;
Malandii and Uryasev 2022
]
.

Formulation as linear programming.

Problem (
4.44
) can be equivalently cast as a linear programming (LP) problem:

\begin{split}\min_{c_{0},\mathbf{c},t,\mathbf{u}}&\quad t\\
\textrm{s.t.}&\quad t\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\,,\\
&\quad t\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}(c_{0},\mathbf{c})\,,\\
&\quad u_{i}\geq 0\,,\ \qquad\qquad i=1,\ldots,n\\
&\quad u_{i}\geq z_{i}(c_{0},\mathbf{c}),\ \ \ \quad i=1,\ldots,n\,.\end{split}

(4.45)

Formulation (
4.45
) has several modeling and optimization advantages. Specifically, it naturally supports linear constraints, effectively handles ill-conditioning due to the maturity of modern LP solvers (e.g.,
Gurobi Optimization, LLC 2024
), and more importantly, allows for efficient incorporation of integer variables, which is the subject of Section
4.3
. Additionally, recent advances in numerical algorithms for LP
[
Applegate et al. 2021
]
enable GPU acceleration, as implemented by
NVIDIA Corporation 2025
, achieving over 5,000x faster performance compared to traditional CPU-based solvers. These methods apply to both continuous LPs and mixed-integer LPs (MILPs).

4.3
Sparse Regression

Sparse regression (or best-subset selection) seeks generalized linear models by encouraging most coefficients to be exactly zero, thereby improving interpretability and prediction in high-dimensional settings where the number of regressors,
d
, may exceed the number of observations,
n
. Classical best-subset selection minimizes squared error with an
\ell_{0}
pseudo-norm penalty, leading to NP-hard mixed-integer quadratic programming:

\min_{c_{0},\mathbf{c}}\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}^{2}(c_{0},\mathbf{c})\quad\textrm{s.t.}\quad\|\mathbf{c}\|_{0}\leq k\,,

(4.46)

where
\|\mathbf{c}\|_{0}:=\operatorname{card}(\{j:c_{j}\neq 0\})
and
d\geq k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Convex relaxations such as the Lasso replace
\ell_{0}
with
\ell_{1}
norm to yield tractable estimators with provable prediction and support-recovery guarantees under suitable design conditions (e.g., restricted eigenvalue/compatibility). However, due to the recent progress in the computational power of Mixed Integer Programming (MIP) solvers (see e.g.,
Bixby 2012
), the original
\ell_{0}
penalty formulation became more and more widely used. Moreover, MIP formulations of sparse regression (
4.46
) have been shown to deliver sharper support-recovery performance than popular convex relaxations (like the Lasso) under comparable design and signal-strength conditions; see phase-transition and empirical results in
Bertsimas et al. 2016
;
Bertsimas and Van Parys 2020
;
Bertsimas et al. 2020
.

There are two most common approaches to solving (
4.46
): 1) direct mixed-integer quadratic programming (MIQP) with branch-and-bound, cuts, and heuristics features efficiently implemented in commercial solvers, e.g.,
Gurobi Optimization, LLC 2024
; 2) cutting-plane (outer-approximation) scheme that iteratively adds valid inequalities to tighten the relaxation, leading to solving a sequence of MILPs
[
Bertsimas and Van Parys 2020
]
. Alternatively, given the results obtained in the sections above, we propose to solve

\min_{c_{0},\mathbf{c}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}(c_{0},\mathbf{c})_{+},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}(c_{0},\mathbf{c})_{-}\right\}\quad\textrm{s.t.}\quad\|\mathbf{c}\|_{0}\leq k\,,

(4.47)

which can be equivalently formulated as an MILP in view of (
4.45
). Compared with the MIQP best-subset formulation (
4.46
), the SE regression model in (
4.47
) yields a pure MILP, so every branch-and-bound node solves a linear program rather than a quadratic one–typically cheaper, more scalable, and numerically stabler. MILPs also unlock a larger, more mature ecosystem of cutting planes and presolve techniques, and make it straightforward to add linear side-constraints (e.g., monotonicity, fairness, etc.) without changing problem class. In practice, MILP nodes can leverage first-order LP technology (e.g., PDLP of
Applegate et al. 2021
mentioned earlier) and even GPU acceleration, which is not available for generic MIQPs. Finally, the piecewise-linear objective often leads to tighter and more informative dual bounds for tail- or imbalance-aware formulations, while avoiding conditioning issues common in quadratic losses.

6
Conclusions

This paper introduced the
biased mean quadrangle
(BMQ) and developed an associated family of estimation and decision tools centered on a biased mean statistic. The BMQ’s risk—the
superexpectation risk
—extends mean-upper-semirisk, while its error—the
superexpectation error
—balances averages of the positive and negative parts of a random variable
Y
in a tunable, directionally asymmetric manner. These choices yield models that directly target practically meaningful events (exceeding or falling short of the mean by a margin
x
in the units of
Y
) and integrate with regret and deviation via the RQ identities (Definition
2.8
).

On the theoretical side, we established: (i)
subregularity
of the BMQ (Definition
2.9
, Proposition
3.1
), thereby providing, to our knowledge, the first subregular quadrangle with concrete applied relevance; (ii)
equivalence of deviation minimization
between CVaR and superexpectation deviations (Proposition
3.7
), with implications for portfolio optimization (Example
3.1
); and (iii) a
reparameterization of quantile regression
in terms of biased means, proving that superexpectation-based biased-mean regression and quantile regression yield the same solutions for an appropriate mapping between the margin
x
and the quantile level
\alpha
(Theorem
4.1
). As a special case, setting
x=0
recovers a coherent, piecewise-linear mean quadrangle for which the induced
superexpectation regression
is equivalent to OLS and admits an LP formulation (Corollary
3.4
, Theorem
4.3
), offering modeling transparency and computational advantages.

From a practical standpoint, the unit-calibrated parameterization by
x
enhances interpretability (‘‘factors of beating the average exactly by
x
’’) and alignment with domain policies. Our numerical experiments further indicate that, in small-sample high-dimensional regimes (
n\ll d
), the SE-based sparse regression is particularly effective, while for larger
n
the MSE-based formulation is solved to proven optimality more rapidly by off-the-shelf solvers. Altogether, the BMQ provides a principled bridge between statistical estimation and risk-sensitive optimization.
