Secondhand tobacco exposure and risk of atrial fibrillation: a systematic review
Authors: Shucheng Liang, Danghan Wang, Dan Xu, Wengen Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis systematic review aims to provide evidence from current observational studies on whether Secondhand smoke (SHS) exposure is associated with increased risk of atrial fibrillation (AF) risk, so to identify potential high-risk subgroup to inform both mechanistic hypotheses and prevention strategies.MethodsA systematic screening of the PubMed, EMBASE and Cochrane Central databases was conducted until September 2025 to identify observational studies focused on the association between SHS and AF.ResultsSix observational studies involving more than 420,000 participants were included. Early-life SHS exposure, including gestational and childhood household exposure, was consistently associated with a higher risk of AF in adulthood. Evidence for adulthood SHS exposure was more heterogeneous; however, studies incorporating objective biomarkers or dose–response assessments demonstrated clearer associations. Several studies reported stronger associations among women, Black individuals, never-smokers, and those with elevated genetic susceptibility. Notably, a large population-based cohort demonstrated a graded relationship between SHS exposure and incident AF, with evidence of gene–environment interaction.ConclusionsAvailable evidence suggests that SHS exposure, particularly during early life, may be associated with an increased risk of AF. The observed heterogeneity by exposure window, assessment method, and population subgroup underscores the importance of life-course and equity-focused approaches to AF prevention. Reducing SHS exposure may represent a modifiable and underrecognized strategy for long-term AF risk reduction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26895-3.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Secondhand smoke, Passive smoking, Environmental tobacco Smoke, Life-course exposure, Cardiovascular risk, Epidemiology, Prevention

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) represents the most commonly occurring arrythmia worldwide affecting 59 million people worldwide [1], which is closely associated with stroke [2] and substantially contributes to morbidity and healthcare burden. Its well-established lifestyle risk factors include obesity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, sleep apnea, alcohol consumption and active tobacco smoking [3]. Tobacco use is also associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases [4], which is another established risk factor for AF [5]. Despite recent advances in rhythm control with catheter ablation and anticoagulation strategies, prevention of AF remains challenging [6], as we don’t fully understand the mechanisms underlying the pathophysiology of AF. Many people develop AF in the absence of any traditional risk factors, suggesting the possibility that there might be some yet unknown environmental or genetic factors contributing to AF risk [7].

Secondhand tobacco smoke (SHS), also referred to as environmental or passive tobacco smoke, contains many toxic chemicals similar to active smoking [8], including nicotine, fine particulate matter [9], and oxidizing agents [10], which are shown to have implications in the pathogenesis of AF [5]. SHS exposure remains highly prevalent globally, accounting for more than 1 million deaths annually [11]. A significant proportion of adults and children are exposed at home, in workplaces, in public venues or even in uterus. Although a significant reduction was observed in recent years, SHS exposure remained very common especially in countries with inadequate regulatory protections, reaching over a rate of over 60% among adolescent girls in China in 2019 [12]. While active smoking is an established risk factor for AF [5] and SHS was proven to have cardiovascular consequences such as coronary heart disease and stroke [13], its contribution to AF risk has been less well characterized in light of previous inconsistent evidence.

Thus, this systematic review aims to search robust evidence from current observational studies on whether SHS exposure across different life stages is associated with AF risk, and to identify potential high-risk subgroups, and to inform both mechanistic hypotheses and prevention strategies.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

This systematic review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [14] and was structured using the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, Study design) framework [15]. Studies were required to meet the following criteria to be considered for inclusion in this review: 1) be original research articles published in English; 2) adopt eligible study designs included cohort, case–control (including nested case–control), and cross-sectional studies, as well as pooled analyses of observational studies; 3) examine the association between exposure to secondhand smoke (SHS) and the risk of atrial fibrillation (AF) among individuals in all age groups; 4) report effect estimates, such as hazard ratios (HRs), odds ratios (ORs), risk ratios (RRs), or incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), or provide sufficient data for their calculation.

For the purposes of this review, SHS was defined as the involuntary inhalation of tobacco combustion products released from burning cigarettes, cigars, or pipes, or exhaled by active smokers, including household, occupational, public, environmental or childhood exposure. SHS exposure could be assessed by self-reported questionnaires (e.g., hours per week exposed, household or workplace exposure), proxy measures (e.g., living with a smoker, occupational exposure), or objective biomarkers (e.g., serum, plasma, saliva, or urine cotinine levels). There were no restrictions on the method of AF ascertainment, which could include electrocardiographic confirmation, physician diagnosis, medical records, administrative databases, or validated self-report.

Studies were excluded if they: (1) focused exclusively on active smoking or smoking cessation interventions without assessing passive exposure; (2) lacked a clearly defined SHS exposure variable or comparison group; (3) did not report AF as an outcome or provided insufficient data to derive an effect estimate; (4) were reviews, editorials, commentaries, case reports, conference abstracts; (5) were duplicate publications or secondary analyses of previously published data.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search of the PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) databases up to September 2025. The search strategy used a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary related SHS (e.g., “Tobacco Smoke Pollution,” “secondhand smoke,” “second-hand smoke,” “environmental tobacco smoke,” “passive smoking,” “involuntary smoking,” “sidestream smoke,” “household smoking,” “spouse smoking,” and “parental smoking,”), and AF (e.g., “atrial fibrillation,” “atrial flutter,” “atrial tachycardia,” “supraventricular tachycardia,” and “arrhythmia”). The detailed search strategies are presented in Supplemental Table 1. The search was limited to English-language publications, with no restrictions on publication date.

Study selection

All search results were imported into reference management software, and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies, and those deemed to be irrelevant according to the predefined criteria were deleted. Full texts were retrieved for all studies that passed initial screening or when eligibility was unclear. Studies were then assessed in full against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, or consultation with a senior author when necessary to reach consensus.

Data extraction

Data from the included studies were extracted using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted information from each study included the first author’s name, year of publication, country, study design, detailed eligibility and exclusion criteria, sample size, mean age, sex, definition of SHS, exposure assessment method, exposure categories and duration, reference group definition. For outcomes, we extracted the method of atrial fibrillation (AF) ascertainment, number of AF events, follow-up duration (for cohort studies), and reported effect estimates. When available, we extracted adjusted risk estimates, including hazard ratios (HRs), odds ratios (ORs), or risk ratios (RRs), along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). If multiple models were presented, the estimate derived from the model with the greatest degree of multivariable adjustment was selected. When studies reported risk estimates for multiple exposure levels, the estimates corresponding to the highest versus lowest exposure categories were extracted. All extracted data were cross-checked by a second reviewer to ensure accuracy and completeness.

Quality assessment

The quality of each included study was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [16], which is designed for assessing the quality of observational studies. The NOS appraises three major domains: (1) the selection of study participants and ascertainment of exposure or outcome, (2) the comparability of study groups based on the control of confounding factors, and (3) the assessment of outcomes and the adequacy of follow-up in cohort studies or exposure assessment in case–control studies. Each study could receive a maximum of nine stars, with higher scores indicating lower risk of bias. Studies were classified as high quality (7–9 stars), moderate quality (5–6 stars), or low quality (< 5 stars). The detailed quality appraisal for each included study is provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Exposure categorization

SHS exposure was primarily categorized according to life stage (early life, adulthood) to reflect biologically distinct exposure windows relevant to cardiovascular development and arrhythmogenesis. This framework allows comparison across studies examining similar developmental periods.

Data synthesis

If the included studies were sufficiently homogenous enough in terms of design, population, exposures, and outcomes, a quantitative synthesis would be conducted. However, if the number of studies included was insufficient or if substantial clinical or methodological heterogeneity existed, a narrative synthesis of the evidence would be performed to allow a qualitative analysis of the available literature, providing a descriptive and contextual understanding of the topic at hand.

Results

Study selection and quality assessment

The search and selection process is visually summarized in Fig. 1. A total of 340 articles were initially identified from database searches, including 65 records from PubMed, 260 records from EMBASE and 15 records from the Cochrane Library. After removing 47 duplicates, 293 records underwent title and abstract screening, among which, 268 studies were considered irrelevant and therefore excluded.

PRISMA flow diagram for study search and selection

A total of 25 records passed initial screening and were retrieved for full-text assessment. 19 studies were then removed due to the following reasons: commentary (n = 3), not the outcome of interest (n = 12), and not the study design of interest (n = 4). Finally, 6 studies met the inclusion criteria but not the exclusion criteria and were included in this review [17–22]. All included studies were deemed to be moderate or high in quality (Supplemental Table 2–4).

Overview of main findings

The overall evidence suggests that SHS exposure is associated with an increased risk of AF, although the magnitude and consistency of associations vary by exposure window and assessment method. The most consistent associations were observed for early-life SHS exposure, including in-utero and childhood household exposure, which were generally associated with higher AF risk in adulthood. Evidence for adulthood SHS exposure was somewhat inconsistent, with modest to high risk elevations observed in some studies but weaker or null associations in other studies. Stronger associations were observed among women, Black individuals, never-smokers, individuals with dyslipidemia, and those with higher genetic susceptibility. Childhood and gestational secondhand tobacco exposure

Three studies investigated the association between SHS exposure during gestation or childhood and the subsequent risk of AF. The evidence for this exposure window was consistent, with all three studies reporting a positive association between early-life exposure and AF in adulthood.

In a prospective analysis by Groh et al. [22] of 2816 participants from the Framingham Offspring cohort, parental smoking during childhood (< 18 years old) was examined in relation to the risk of incident AF. Among these, 2,316 participants (82%) were exposed to parental smoking and 500 (18%) were unexposed. Over a median follow-up of 40.5 years, 404 participants (14.3%) developed AF at a rate of 4.02 per 1000 person-years. Each one-pack-per-day increase in parental smoking was associated with an 18% higher risk of AF in offspring (adjusted HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.00–1.39; p = 0.04). Parental smoking was associated with a significantly higher odds of offspring smoking (adjusted OR 1.34, 95% CI 1.17–1.54). Mediation analysis indicated that offspring smoking explained 17% of this association, after adjusting for Offspring smoking, the relationship between parental smoking and Offspring AF was attenuated (HR 1.15, 95% CI 0.98–1.35).

In a cross-sectional analysis of the internet-based Health eHeart study with 4876 participants, Dixit el al [20]. found that after multivariable adjustment for potential confounders, having a smoker parent during gestation (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.08–1.73) and residing with a smoker during childhood(OR 1.40, 95% CI 1.10–1.79) was significantly associated with higher odds of AF in adulthood. These association between in-utero and childhood SHS was stronger in individuals without risk factors for AF (p values for interaction of 0.021 and 0.010 respectively).

In a case–control study of 211 never-smoking women from Israel, Regev-Avraham et al. [17] investigated the association between household SHS exposure and prevalent AF. When childhood exposure was further stratified by age period, SHS exposure during early childhood (0–12 years) was associated with an increased odds of AF (OR 3.39, 95% CI 1.92–5.97), with a stronger association observed for exposure during adolescence (13–18 years) (OR 4.07, 95% CI 2.29–7.22).

Adulthood SHS

Five studies assessed SHS exposure in adulthood. In contrast to early-life exposure, findings for adulthood exposure were heterogeneous. This inconsistency appears largely driven by differences in exposure assessment methods, sample size and subgroups.

A prospective cohort study using the UK Biobank with data from over 400, 000 participants conducted by Rhee et al. [19] investigated the association between environmental SHS in both home and outdoor settings and the risk of incident AF over a median follow-up period of 12.6 years. Exposure to SHS was common, reported by more than 20% of the population, which was associated with a significantly increased risk of AF (adjusted HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.02–1.09; P = 0.002). There was evidence of a dose-dependent relationship, with both moderate (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.03–1.17) and severe exposure (HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.04–1.18) conferring higher risks. The association was consistent in both home (adjusted HR 1.07, 95% CI,1.01–1.14) and outdoor environments (HR 1.05, 95% CI, 1.01–1.09). A significant additive interaction was observed between SHS exposure and a high polygenic risk score for AF (synergy index 1.13, 95% CI 1.05–1.21; P = 0.024). Subgroup analyses showed a more pronounced risk among individuals with dyslipidemia (adjusted HR 1.10, 95% CI, 1.04–1.16).

In another prospective analysis by Lin et al. [21] of 5,731 adults without cardiovascular disease at baseline from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis over a median follow-up of 14.0 years, self-reported SHS was not found to be significantly associated with the risk of incident AF after multivariable adjustment. In a subset of 3,287 current non-smokers with urine cotinine measurements, objectively elevated cotinine levels (8.53–442.0 ng/mL) were associated with a significantly higher risk of AF among women (HR 1.49, 95% CI 1.05–2.12), never smokers (HR 1.60, 95% CI 1.16–2.19), and Black individuals (HR 1.64, 95% CI 1.01–2.65), but not among men, former smokers, or other racial/ethnic groups.

In a cross-sectional analysis of 12,021 never-smoking adults enrolled in the REasons for Geographic And Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS) study between 2003 and 2007, O’Neal et al. [18] evaluated the association between SHS exposure, defined by more than 1 h of passive smoke exposure per week, and prevalent atrial fibrillation (AF). Overall, 2,503 participants (21%) reported ≥ 1 h per week of SHS exposure, and AF prevalence was higher among exposed individuals (8.5% vs. 7.6%). After multivariable adjustment, ETS exposure was associated with 27% higher odds of AF (adjusted OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.08–1.50). Subgroup analyses also demonstrated a significantly stronger association among Black participants (OR 1.63, 95% CI 1.27–2.08; p-interaction = 0.0007).

The cross-sectional study by Dixit et al. [20] suggested that in unadjusted analysis, participants with AF were more likely to have been exposed to SHS as an adult at home or work, and longer time at work. After multivariable adjustment, adulthood SHS was no longer significantly associated with prevalent AF.

In the case–control study by Regev-Avraham et al. [17], current SHS exposure in adulthood was associated with a markedly increased odds of AF (OR 4.61, 95% CI 2.58–8.25). The association remained robust after adjustment.

Discussion

This systematic review included 6 observational studies, encompassing over 420,000 participants from diverse populations and geographic regions. SHS exposure across different life stages was associated with an increased risk of AF. However, the included studies exhibit heterogeneity across virtually every methodological dimension, complicating direct synthesis. Sample sizes ranged from 211 participants in the case–control study by Regev-Avraham et al. to over 400,000 in the UK Biobank cohort, representing a nearly 1,900-fold difference. This variation in statistical power and design likely contributes to the 60-fold disparity in reported effect estimates, which spanned from a modest hazard ratio of 1.06 to a high odds ratio of 6.29. Study designed included three prospective cohorts [19, 21, 22], two cross-sectional analyses [18, 20], and one case–control study [17]; whereas exposure windows spanned from gestational [20], childhood [17, 20, 22], to adulthood [17–21], suggesting that the underlying biological mechanisms and relevant confounding structures may differ substantially across studies. Exposure measurement and definition was similarly varied, relying primarily on self-reported questionnaires subject to recall bias, with only one study utilizing objective urinary cotinine measurement [21]. Follow-up duration among cohort studies ranged from approximately 12.6 to 40.5 years, further contributing to variability in cumulative exposure, competing risks, and outcome detection. AF ascertainment varied from ECG/medical record-based outcomes to self-report, which may capture clinically distinct phenotypes and introduce differential misclassification. Covariate adjustment also differed across studies, with some adjusting comprehensively for sociodemographic factors, cardiometabolic comorbidities, lifestyle factors, and genetic principal components, while others used more limited confounder sets. Given the strong correlation between SHS exposure and socioeconomic position, behavioral clustering, familial smoking patterns, and temporal changes in smoking prevalence and smoke-free policies, residual and structural confounding remain difficult to eliminate. These differences in confounding control, combined with potential survivor bias in early-life exposure studies, further limit causal interpretation.

Regarding early life SHS exposure, two studies provided converging evidence that SHS during childhood and gestation was associated with an increased risk of AF [20, 22]. In the Framingham Offspring cohort [22], parental smoking during childhood was associated with incident AF, which persisted after adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, and approximately 17% of the effect was attributable to the increased likelihood of offspring smoking, implicating both direct toxic exposure and behavioral transmission pathways.

Consistently, a cross-sectional analysis [20] discovered that both gestational and childhood SHS exposure was significantly and independently associated with prevalent AF in adulthood. Notably, these effects were stronger among individuals without traditional AF risk factors, suggesting a possibly direct arrhythmogenic influence on the developing atrial substrate rather than indirect mediation through contributing to other conventional AF risk factors.

These studies highlighted different layers of potential mechanisms. Groh et al. underscored intergenerational behavioral transmission, whereby offspring of smoking parents are more likely to adopt smoking behaviors themselves, thereby increasing long-term AF risk. Consistent with this hypothesis, adjustment for offspring smoking status attenuated the association between parental smoking and offspring AF, suggesting that part of the observed risk operates indirectly through subsequent active smoking. Dixit et al. emphasized a distinct “lone AF” phenotype: individuals who develop AF without known conventional risk factors like hypertension, structural heart disease, or diabetes, in whom childhood SHS exposure demonstrated the strongest association, strengthening evidence toward a causal, developmental toxicant effect. It indicated that even low-grade SHS exposure during early-life could predispose individuals to long-term AF susceptibility, which were not fully explained by behavioral adaptation of smoking by the offspring nor indirectly by acquiring other cardiometabolic risk factors for AF, suggesting that childhood SHS exposure may represent a modifiable, actionable determinant of AF risk.

From a public health perspective, these findings are particularly concerning in regions where indoor smoking remains culturally accepted and household exposure during pregnancy and childhood is common. In parts of Asia, Indo-Pacific region, the Middle East, Eastern Europe, and parts of Africa, where smoking prevalence among men exceeds 40 percent and where smoke-free home policies are inconsistently adopted [23], reducing environmental SHS exposure may yield cardiovascular benefits that extend decades into adulthood [24]. Interventions such as culturally tailored smoke-free home initiatives, community education, and household-focused cessation strategies may therefore represent underutilized opportunities for long-term AF prevention.

Evidence regarding adulthood SHS exposure was somewhat more heterogenous, and a key contributor to this inconsistency appears to be the method of exposure assessment. Self-reported SHS exposure produced weaker or null associations after multivariable adjustment in some studies [20, 21] whereas incorporating biomarker-based measure demonstrated clearer and subgroup-specific risk stratification. In the MESA cohort and the Health eHeart Study, self-reported SHS exposure was not independently associated with incident AF; however, objectively measured urinary cotinine, a biomarker reflecting recent nicotine uptake, was significantly associated with higher AF risk among women, never-smokers, and Black participants. These associations were further supported by the two other studies [17, 18].

One interpretation of this discordance is that questionnaire-based SHS assessment is subject to non-differential exposure misclassification, which would tend to attenuate effect estimates toward the null. Self-report is prone to recall bias or social desirability bias [25] and may inadequately capture intermittent, low-intensity, or socially unrecognized exposures, particularly in environments where smoking is normalized or exposure occurs outside the household. These sources of measurement error may lead to cohort-specific attenuation or inflation of associations.

In contrast, biomarker-based assessment [26] offers complementary advantages of objectively quantifying the internal dose, thereby minimizing reporting biases and better reflecting the biologically relevant exposure driving atrial remodeling. However, urinary cotinine has a half-life of approximately 16–20 h and reflects recent nicotine exposure rather than cumulative or long-term SHS exposure. Elevated cotinine levels among self-reported non-smokers may therefore reflect very recent heavy SHS exposure, unreported active smoking, or non-tobacco sources of nicotine intake, including certain foods. They are also influenced by individual differences in metabolism, renal function, circadian rhythms, and genetic polymorphisms, which introduces variability unrelated to environmental exposure.

The UK Biobank study [19], although based on self-report, provided the strongest population-level evidence that adulthood SHS exposure was associated with incident AF. Using graded self-reported exposure across home and outdoor environments, this large cohort demonstrated a clear dose–response relationship and a significant interaction with polygenic risk, supporting the biological plausibility of SHS as a trigger that may amplify underlying genetic susceptibility. Although the observed effect size was modest, the consistency across exposure settings and the presence of gene–environment interaction suggests that even low-level, chronic SHS exposure may meaningfully contribute to AF risk at the population level. Nevertheless, the current literature on adulthood SHS indicate that quantified exposure may be essential for true associations to be observed, as seen in biomarker-based analyses and dose–response assessments, especially when social reporting biases or exposure unawareness are more pronounced.

The divergence between UK Biobank and MESA is likely multi-factorial and cannot be attributed solely to exposure misclassification. The UK Biobank’s substantially larger sample size and broader exposure distribution provided greater statistical power to detect modest associations, whereas MESA’s smaller cohort and potentially narrower exposure range may have limited detection of small effect sizes. Additionally, cohort differences in demographic composition, socioeconomic status, and health selection could modify exposure–outcome relationships, producing true heterogeneity in effect estimates. Furthermore, subtle differences in questionnaire wording, recall periods, and survey administration methods can materially affect exposure classification, even when both studies rely on self-report.

Self-report and biomarker approaches should be viewed as complementary rather than competing tools. Triangulation across cohorts and exposure metrics strengthens causal inference. Future studies integrating repeated biomarker measures with longitudinal self-report assessments will be essential to disentangle acute versus cumulative exposure effects and to clarify mechanisms underlying cohort-specific associations.

The heightened SHS susceptibility observed among women and Black individuals highlights structural inequities in exposure and outcomes. In many regions, women and children are disproportionately exposed to SHS due to limited agency in enforcing household smoking restrictions [27]. In low- and middle-income countries, women often spend more time in indoor domestic environments where smoking occurs [28], and exposure is compounded by limited public health messaging and reduced access to cessation resources. Such inequities reinforce the need for gender responsive policy making, including targeted education campaigns, empowerment programs, and expanded support for household tobacco control interventions.

Workplace exposure represents another concern in industries where smoke-free policies are poorly enforced, such as hospitality, informal labor sectors, and certain types of service work [29]. The evidence of dose–response associations and genetic interaction effects suggests that even low to moderate occupational exposure may meaningfully increase AF risk in susceptible individuals. Strengthening regulatory standards, improving enforcement mechanisms, and encouraging employers to maintain smoke-free environments are crucial steps toward reducing cumulative exposure burden.

Previous meta-analyses [30, 31] showed that active smoking was associated with a modest increase in the risk of incident AF in a dose-dependent manner, but the association was weaker in former smokers compared to current smokers, which are consistent with our findings on SHS and AF risk.

To the best of our knowledge, no experimental studies so far have directly examined the mechanistic effects of SHS exposure on AF development. Biological plausibility currently can only be inferred from experimental and clinical evidence describing the cardiovascular and electrophysiological effects of SHS constituents and their established roles in AF pathophysiology. Nicotine and particulate components [9] of SHS have been shown to promote atrial fibrosis [32], oxidative stress [10, 33], autonomic imbalance [34], and systemic inflammation [35], all of which are well established in AF pathophysiology. Nicotine exposure was also shown to be associated with conditions such as ischemic heart disease [36] and hypertension [37], which are known risk factors for AF. These mechanisms are expected to be similar if not identical to those observed with active smoking [10, 32–35], although SHS exposure generally delivers lower absolute toxicant doses. Nevertheless, the exact mechanisms through which SHS contributes to AF, and how exposure during early-life translates into increased susceptibility decades later, remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, all included studies were observational, therefore precluding causal inference and leaving the possibility of residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. Second, SHS definition and measurement varied considerably across studies. Self-reported exposure is subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and exposure misclassification, which may attenuate true associations. Thirdly, Cross-sectional studies cannot determine temporal sequence, although early-life exposure inherently precedes AF onset. Fourth, AF ascertainment was sometimes based on self-report rather than adjudicated electrocardiographic confirmation, increasing the risk of outcome misclassification. Fifth, heterogeneity in study populations, SHS exposure definitions, and analytic approaches limited comparability and precluded the conduct of a formal meta-analysis. Sixth, some studies lacked quantitative exposure data, preventing detailed assessment of exposure intensity, duration, and timing. Lastly, all populations were from high-income countries, limiting generalizability to regions with higher household smoking prevalence and fewer regulatory protections.

Future directions

Future studies should address the gaps identified in this review. First, prospective cohort studies with standardized and repeated measures of SHS exposure are needed to better elucidate temporality and dose–response relationships. Incorporating objective biomarkers such as urinary or salivary cotinine could improve exposure characterization and reduce misclassification associated with self-report. Second, mechanistic studies, both experimental and clinical, are warranted to clarify how SHS influences atrial structural and electrophysiological remodeling, and why early-life exposure may confer sustained vulnerability into adulthood. Third, the observed heterogeneity across sex, race, and smoking history underscores the importance of evaluating differential susceptibility. Future work should examine genetic modifiers, environmental co-exposures, and social determinants of health to better understand disparities in SHS-related AF risk. Fourth, given the observed associations in “lone AF,” longitudinal studies focusing on individuals without conventional cardiovascular comorbidities may provide unique insights into substrate-specific mechanisms.

Finally, from a public health perspective, targeted interventions to reduce SHS exposure during pregnancy and childhood may represent meaningful primary prevention opportunities. Evaluating the impact of household smoking cessation programs, smoke-free housing policies, and parental education initiatives on long-term AF incidence would provide valuable evidence to inform prevention strategies. Collectively, these directions may help identify modifiable pathways and refine risk stratification to reduce the growing population burden of AF.

Conclusion

Current evidence suggests that SHS exposure across different stages of life, including gestational development, childhood and adulthood may be associated with an increased risk of AF. The associations seem to be more pronounced with early-life exposures and among Black individuals, women and never-smokers. These findings highlight SHS exposure as a modifiable and preventable risk factor for AF. Strengthening smoke-free policies, promoting smoke-free homes, and prioritizing protection of pregnant women and children should be considered integral components of AF prevention strategies. However, extensive heterogeneity suggests that the existing literature may be capturing related but non-equivalent associations rather than a single, uniform effect of SHS exposure on AF risk. While the overall direction of association is broadly consistent, the magnitude and robustness of the observed effects should be interpreted with caution.
