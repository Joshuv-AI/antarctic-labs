SEISMO: Increasing Sample Efficiency in Molecular Optimization with a Trajectory-Aware LLM Agent
Fabian P. Krüger, Andrea Hunklinger, Adrian Wolny, Tim J. Adler, Igor Tetko, Santiago David Villalba

Abstract
Optimizing the structure of molecules to achieve desired properties is a central bottleneck across the chemical sciences, particularly in the pharmaceutical industry where it underlies the discovery of new drugs. Since molecular property evaluation often relies on costly and rate-limited oracles, such as experimental assays, molecular optimization must be highly sample-efficient. To address this, we introduce SEISMO, an LLM agent that performs strictly online, inference-time molecular optimization, updating after every oracle call without the need for population-based or batched learning. SEISMO conditions each proposal on the full optimization trajectory, combining natural-language task descriptions with scalar scores and, when available, structured explanatory feedback. Across the Practical Molecular Optimization benchmark of 23 tasks, SEISMO achieves a 2–3 times higher area under the optimisation curve than prior methods, often reaching near-maximal task scores within 50 oracle calls. Our additional medicinal-chemistry tasks show that providing explanatory feedback further improves efficiency, demonstrating that leveraging domain knowledge and structured information is key to sample-efficient molecular optimization.

Introduction

Generating molecules with desired properties is a central goal in chemistry. A prominent example is the pharmaceutical industry, which requires the discovery of novel molecules in order to bring new drugs to the market. Such molecules must satisfy multiple objectives, including potency, selectivity, and a range of pharmacokinetic and physicochemical properties
(
22
)
, which can conflict and therefore require explicit trade-offs. At the same time, the searchable chemical space is enormous, often estimated at around 10
60
plausible small drug-like molecules
(
4
)
.

Because this chemical space is far too large to be explored exhaustively, practical molecular design is instead carried out as an iterative optimization process in which candidate molecules are proposed, evaluated, and refined over successive steps
(
11
)
. For the evaluations, experimental assays provide the most reliable assessment of molecular properties, but their high cost and limited throughput make them impractical for use at every iteration. As a result, molecular optimization pipelines rely on computational methods that approximate experimental outcomes
(
11
)
. In practice, these methods span a spectrum of cost and fidelity: lower-cost approximations often provide weaker correlations with experimental measurements, while more accurate approaches, such as physics-based simulations and protein–ligand co-folding methods, are computationally expensive and rate-limited
(
25
;
29
;
1
)
. Reducing the number of required evaluations therefore has direct practical value: sample-efficient optimization methods enable the use of higher-quality evaluations, which in turn leads to molecules that are more likely to perform well in practice.

.

To systematically assess sample efficiency in molecular optimization, the Practical Molecular Optimization (PMO) benchmark was introduced, consisting of a diverse set of molecular design tasks
(
10
)
. Results reported for PMO show that current reinforcement learning and genetic algorithm methods can achieve strong final performance, but they typically still require thousands of evaluations to do so. As a result, their practical applicability depends on the availability of large evaluation budgets, which may be prohibitive when evaluations are expensive or rate-limited.

We hypothesize that sample efficiency in molecular optimization can be improved by more effectively leveraging available chemical and biological prior knowledge, rather than framing optimization as a purely black-box search driven only by scalar objective values. Motivated by this, we introduce a goal-directed, highly efficient large language model (LLM) agent that we call SEISMO (Sample-Efficient Inference-Stage Molecular-Optimization agent). SEISMO uses the LLM itself as the optimizer and performs strictly online, inference-time molecular optimization by conditioning each proposal on the full optimization trajectory, including prior knowledge and oracle feedback.

2
Related work

Molecular optimisation
in drug discovery is commonly framed as black-box search over chemical space, with approaches ranging from objective-guided generative models to population-based and surrogate-assisted search.
REINVENT exemplifies the first class, optimising a molecule generator via policy-gradient reinforcement learning using batch-level scalar rewards
(
27
)
.
Graph-GA is a standard baseline for the second class, evolving molecules through graph-based crossover and mutation operators
(
17
)
.
Gaussian process Bayesian optimisation (GP BO) is another widely used approach, fitting a surrogate over molecular fingerprints and selecting candidates via acquisition functions
(
35
)
.

Follow-up work has explored improving the sample efficiency of REINVENT-style methods, with Augmented Memory
(
14
)
and Beam Enumeration
(
13
)
reusing and filtering previously evaluated molecules through experience replay, SMILES augmentation, or substructure-based conditioning. Despite these improvements, optimisation proceeds in fixed batches, imposing a minimum number of oracle calls between policy updates.

Recent methods incorporate LLMs into population-based molecular search.
MolLEO extends Graph-GA by using an LLM to perform crossover and mutation operations based on parent molecules
(
36
)
.
Unlike MolLEO, LICO uses an evolutionary algorithm to generate candidate molecules via standard crossover and mutation, and employs an LLM as a surrogate model to predict a mean and standard deviation for these candidates, selecting evaluations using an acquisition function
(
26
)
.

In contrast to prior molecular optimisation methods, SEISMO treats optimisation itself as an inference-time, sequential decision process executed entirely within the LLM’s context, where each oracle call immediately updates the state on which the next proposal is conditioned. Rather than learning a policy, surrogate, or population dynamics, SEISMO relies on trajectory-level reasoning over past molecules, scores, and feedback, making the optimisation history the central learning signal.

LLM-based agents in chemistry
have recently emerged to support scientific workflows. ChemAgent
(
34
)
and CACTUS
(
24
)
target free-form chemistry question answering by augmenting LLM reasoning with external tools such as literature search and standard cheminformatics libraries, while DrugAgent
(
16
)
focuses on drug–target interaction prediction.

Other systems extend this agentic framing to broader end-to-end assistance in medicinal chemistry.
ChemCrow
(
5
)
and Frogent
(
28
)
integrate tool use for information retrieval, synthesis planning, and workflow execution, with ChemCrow additionally enabling autonomous laboratory execution. MADD
(
33
)
similarly emphasizes early-stage hit identification by coupling LLM agents to specialised drug-design tools.
Across these approaches, the LLM primarily acts as a controller that orchestrates external tools, while optimisation itself is delegated to learned models, heuristics, or downstream pipelines.

In contrast to prior agentic work, SEISMO focuses narrowly on goal-directed molecular optimisation under a strict oracle budget. Rather than acting as a controller over external tools, the LLM itself serves as the optimiser, directly coupling generation with iterative oracle feedback. Additionally, SEISMO augments scalar oracle scores with explanation-based signals from post-hoc explainability methods (XAI), which, to our knowledge, have not been used as control signals in molecular optimisation.

3
Problem formulation

The molecular optimisation problem is the task of finding a molecule
m\in M
that maximises the score of an evaluation function
F:M\to\mathbb{R}
. Formally,

m^{*}\in\argmax\limits_{m\in M}F(m).

Although
F
is often treated as a black-box objective in molecular optimisation, in practice this abstraction discards substantial additional information that is typically available in real world settings.

First,
F(m)
is usually an aggregation of multiple property scores,

F(m)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\,f_{k}(m),

where the individual components
f_{k}(m)
and their weights
w_{k}
are available information in addition to the aggregated score.

Second, for tasks of direct scientific or industrial relevance — such as optimising drug-like properties or binding affinity to a target protein — there usually exists substantial prior knowledge about the different properties
f_{k}
, accumulated through decades of chemical and biological research. This knowledge includes, for example, well-characterized functional-group effects, structural alerts and other medicinal-chemistry heuristics, synthetic feasibility considerations, and established structure–property relationships. Such domain knowledge can inform and constrain the search over candidate molecules even when
F
itself is only accessible via queries.

Third, since many such properties are expensive to evaluate experimentally, each
f_{k}(m)
is typically obtained via computational approximations, for example via learned surrogate predictors. This allows for post-hoc explainability methods (e. g. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP)
(
23
)
) that provide further insights into why a candidate molecule receives a particular score. We model this via an explainer
G
that returns a structured explanation
\mathcal{E}
and a natural-language verbalizer that converts the structured explanation as a textual rationale:

G:\mathcal{F}\times M\to\mathcal{E}\qquad\text{verbalize}:\mathcal{E}\to\Sigma_{\mathrm{NL}}^{*},

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the space of evaluation functions, i. e.,
F\in\mathcal{F}
.

Our goal is to increase sample efficiency in molecular optimisation by leveraging these additional sources of information.

4
Approach

A natural way to incorporate the aforementioned priors and side-information is through LLMs. As LLMs are trained on large, heterogeneous text corpora that include substantial scientific and technical content, they can serve as a practical proxy for the kind of accumulated domain knowledge described above.
While such knowledge is not guaranteed to be perfectly accurate or complete, it provides an inductive bias that can guide the search toward plausible, high-scoring regions of chemical space.

Crucially, LLMs operate on natural language, which makes it straightforward to provide rich, structured feedback beyond a scalar objective. In particular, we can supply the agent with the task description, the sub-score decomposition
{f_{k}(m)}_{k=1}^{K}
, and property-level explanations produced by
G
. This turns each molecule evaluation into an informative observation rather than a single number.

Motivated by this, we construct a goal-directed LLM agent that performs inference-time optimisation via iterative interaction with an evaluation environment.

Let
d\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{NL}}^{*}
denote the initial natural-language problem description (task context and any available prior constraints). At iteration
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, the agent proposes a molecule based on the context and the history of previous trials:

m_{t}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{LLM}}(\,\cdot\mid d,H_{t-1}),

where
H_{t-1}
is the history (see below) and
\pi_{\mathrm{LLM}}
is the proposal policy obtained by sampling from an LLM with fixed weights and mapping the generated strings to molecules (via SMILES parsing), thereby inducing a conditional distribution over
M
given
(d,H_{t-1})
. The environment then returns not only the aggregate score but also its decomposition and explanations,

y_{t}=\Big(F(m_{t}),\,(f_{k}(m_{t}))_{k=1}^{K},\,(r_{t,k})_{k=1}^{K}\Big),

where
r_{t,k}\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{NL}}^{*}
is a textual explanation for the component score
f_{k}(m_{t})
. The history is updated by appending the new trial and feedback,

H_{t}=H_{t-1}\cup\{(m_{t},y_{t})\}.

The update from
H_{t-1}
to
H_{t}
after every evaluation induces an online optimisation process with update frequency one per sample, rather than one per batch. This removes the lower bound on sample efficiency imposed by population-based or batched acquisition schemes used in many existing methods.
After a budget of
T
evaluations, the agent outputs the best molecule encountered,

\hat{m}_{T}\in\argmax_{t\in\{1,\dots,T\}}F(m_{t}).

In this setup, optimisation happens at inference time through the evolving context
(d,H_{t})
: the agent repeatedly conditions on prior knowledge (encoded in
d
and the LLM) and on increasingly informative feedback (scores, breakdowns, and explanations) to guide subsequent proposals toward higher-scoring molecules with improved sample efficiency.

Overall, we perform three experiments. First, we evaluate SEISMO on the established PMO benchmark.
Then we perform an experiment to more directly mirror common medicinal-chemistry optimization loops (drug-discovery workflow proxies). Unlike PMO, these tasks use oracles under our control, allowing us to not only assess SEISMO’s general drug-discovery performance, but to investigate the influence of additional explanatory feedback beyond the scalar score on performance metrics.

Setup:
We evaluate SEISMO on the PMO benchmark of 23 molecule optimization tasks
(
10
)
. For a fair comparison with prior work, we use the original PMO oracles and provide the agent only with the scalar oracle score at each iteration, without additional explanation.
We compare SEISMO to established molecular optimization baselines, including REINVENT, Graph-GA, and Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization
(
27
;
17
;
35
)
.
Then, to provide a broader comparison against more recent approaches, we compare against the results reported by Nguyen and Grover
(
26
)
, which include a wide range of optimization models such as MolLeo and LICO.

We consider two SEISMO variants: In the
task described
variant, the optimization objective is stated in the initial prompt using descriptions (cf. Appendix
E.2
) comparable to those used in other work
(
36
)
. In the
no description
variant, the initial prompt omits any description of the objective and the agent treats the oracle as fully black-box. For each task, we run both variants for three independent repetitions with a budget of 50 oracle calls.

Following PMO, we evaluate sample efficiency using the area under the optimization curve (AUC). The optimization curve tracks the best oracle score achieved up to a given oracle call and is defined as

s_{t}=\max_{i\leq t}F(m_{i}),

where
F(m_{t})\in[0,1]
is the oracle score of the molecule evaluated at oracle call
t
.
AUC is defined as the area under
s_{t}
, normalized by the oracle budget, such that AUC values lie in
[0,1]
. Higher AUC values correspond to faster discovery of high-scoring molecules.

We report top-1 AUC over 50 oracle calls and over 10,000 oracle calls. We also report the best score achieved after 50 oracle calls, defined as
s_{50}=\max_{i\leq 50}F(m_{i})
. For comparison to recent work
(
26
)
, we additionally report top-10 AUC over 1,000 oracle calls, where the optimization curve tracks the average score of the ten best molecules found so far at each oracle call.

For metrics evaluated beyond 50 oracle calls, SEISMO is only allowed to propose molecules during the first 50 calls. For
t>50
we hold
s_{t}
fixed at its value after 50 calls, while other methods continue optimizing over the full budget. This is due to LLM API cost given the number of tasks, variants, and repetitions. Implementation details are in Appendix
A
.

When reporting aggregate results across the 23 PMO tasks, we follow PMO and sum per-task metrics across tasks. Since all reported metrics are bounded in
[0,1]
, the maximum achievable aggregate score is bound by 23.

Results:
Table

compares the performance of the SEISMO variants to the baseline models. The variant without descriptions is included to later isolate the role of external task information. Here, we focus on the full SEISMO agent
with task descriptions. Across all metrics, SEISMO achieves consistently better performance than all baselines. Summed over all 23 tasks, the top-1 AUC over 50 oracle calls is 19.85 for SEISMO, compared to 7.06 for the strongest baseline, corresponding to a 2.8

improvement. For the sum of best scores after 50 oracle calls, SEISMO achieves a 2.6
\times
improvement (21.18 vs. 8.18). Even when compared using the top-1 AUC over 10,000 oracle calls, SEISMO achieves a 1.4

Examining individual tasks rather than aggregate metrics reinforces this pattern. Across both metrics evaluated over 50 oracle calls, SEISMO achieves the best performance on all 23 tasks (cf. Tables
D1
and
D3
). Over 10,000 oracle calls, SEISMO remains best on 20 of the 23 tasks (cf. Table

). Inspecting best scores after 50 oracle calls at the task level shows that SEISMO already reaches scores within 10% of the task maximum (

Notably, this occurs within a number of oracle calls that is comparable to typical initial batch or population sizes used by prior methods, which have to evaluate multiple candidates before any update step can be taken. Consequently, SEISMO often reaches near-optimal solutions before such methods are able to perform their first optimization step.

Setup:
We evaluate the sample efficiency of SEISMO under varying amounts of available information:

No description:
scalar score

No explanation:
scalar score + subscores + task description

Partial explanation:
scalar score + subscores + task description + explanation of one sub-score

Full explanation:
scalar score + subscores + task description + explanations for all sub-scores

In the
no description
mode, the initial prompt only specifies that the model should optimize a score. In all other modes, the task is stated explicitly (cf. Figure
1
).

We evaluate on two tasks reflecting common drug discovery settings: (i) hit identification, which involves discovering active molecules for a target protein, and (ii) lead optimization, which focuses on finding close analogs under additional constraints (More details in Appendix
A
).

For the hit identification proxy, we use the predicted inhibitory concentration (IC
50
) against the SARS-CoV-2 main protease (M
pro
) as the main objective, where lower values indicate stronger inhibition. The final oracle (IC
50
–QED–Novel) also includes novelty and drug-likeness constraints to restrict the search to chemically plausible and marketable molecules.

We evaluate sample efficiency using the optimization curve defined as the best feasible prediction so far over oracle calls, where feasibility is defined by satisfying the imposed drug-likeness and novelty constraints. Specifically, the curve records the lowest predicted inhibitory concentration among feasible molecules at each oracle call, and we quantify performance by the area under this constraint-filtered curve (AUC) over 50 oracle calls. Because this is a minimization task with unbounded scores, the resulting AUC is not normalized, with lower values indicating more sample-efficient optimization.

For the lead-optimization proxy, we study a similarity-constrained optimization task in which the agent seeks close analogs of a reference molecule with improved drug-likeness (Sim-QED). The scalar objective combines similarity to the reference and drug-likeness into a single score. We evaluate this task in two regimes: (i) a praxis-motivated case study using Quercetin as the reference molecule, which has known medicinal relevance but poor drug-like properties
(
12
)
, and (ii) a randomized setting with 20 diverse reference molecules sampled to test robustness on targets with low a priori familiarity to the LLM.

We report top-1 AUC over 50 iterations for the scalar objective combining similarity and drug-likeness, averaged across target molecules. For the Quercetin case study, we report a goal-based metric: the number of oracle calls required to first reach a predefined target score corresponding to a close analog with improved drug-likeness (cf. Appendix
A
).

Depending on the mode, the oracle additionally returns an explanation of the score. Explanations for QED and Similarity provide property-level and substructure-level decompositions, respectively. For predicted IC
50
values, we use the feature attribution method SHAP. See Appendix
E.3
for example explanations. In the partial explanation mode, only the drug-likeness QED score is being explained. We run SEISMO for 50 iterations in all four workflow modes for each task. All results are reported with 95% confidence intervals computed via bootstrap resampling.

Results:
As shown in Table
3
, the
no description
variant performs consistently worse than all other for both hit-identification and lead-optimization proxies, a pattern that is consistent with the PMO benchmark results across all metrics (Tables
1
and
2
). This shows that knowledge of the optimization objective, within the initial prompt, is critical for effective LLM-driven optimization, as it enables the agent to condition its molecular proposals on task-relevant information available in the pretrained model.

We next analyze the effect of explanatory feedback beyond the task description. As shown in Table
3
, increasing the level of explanatory feedback leads to a clear and consistent improvement in sample efficiency across all tasks and experimental settings. These results show that, beyond knowing what to optimize, providing feedback about why a molecule achieved a given score helps the agent identify how specific structural changes affect the objective, guiding the optimization process more effectively.

The optimization curves in Figures
2(a)
and
2(b)
clarify how this information is used. The benefit of a task description is dominant and apparent throughout optimization, whereas the advantages of richer explanations emerge more gradually. Early performance is similar across explanation levels, but variants with richer feedback increasingly outperform those with less information as more oracle calls are made, highlighting the growing impact of explanatory feedback over time.

Beyond relative ordering between variants, the absolute values in Figures
2(a)
and
2(b)
show that SEISMO reaches chemically meaningful solutions within a small oracle budget. For the lead-optimization proxy, variants with task descriptions reach average similarity and drug-likeness values around 0.8 across 20 random reference molecules within 50 oracle calls, indicating close, drug-like analogs. For the hit-identification proxy, the full-explanation variant reliably identifies novel, drug-like molecules with predicted IC
50
below 10 nM within the same budget, demonstrating convergence to strong target inhibition rather than modest score improvements.

5.3
Co-folding-based oracle

Setup:

This oracle uses a so-called co-folding model to produce an evaluation function. A co-folding model
(
1
;
29
;
7
)
can take protein sequences, small compounds as SMILES and other biologically relevant molecules as inputs and produce a 3D representation (also called a structure) of the complex made up of the molecules. A special case in the co-folding space is the Boltz-2 model
(
29
)
, which built a so-called binding affinity prediction head on top of the structure prediction, which estimates how strong a small molecule will bind to a given protein. We employ the output of the affinity head as our score to guide the LLM to generate molecules keeping the protein sequence fixed. We randomly select a protein sequence from the MGnify metagenomics database
(
32
)
that lies in a low-density region of the ESMAtlas embedding space
(
21
)
, while still having a high-confidence predicted 3D structure. The chosen protein is MGYP001550541752. These restrictions were chosen to challenge our agent: We wanted a protein with no known binders to reduce the chance of memorization by the LLM while at the same time still having a confident 3D structure for reliable Boltz-2 performance.

Similar to the previous experiments, the co-folding-based oracle was evaluated with three feedback conditions:

No sequence
: we provided the Boltz-2 binding affinity probability together with a task description, but without the protein sequence of the target.

No explanation
: as before, but the LLM has access to the protein sequence.

Full explanation
: in addition to the previous information, the oracle provides the three-letter-codes of all residues that are closer than 5Å to the molecule together with Boltz-2’s confidence scores (cf. Appendix
C
and
E.3
).

In Appendix
C
, we evaluate SEISMO on a well-studied (SARS-CoV-2 M
pro
) and a challenging target (TRIB2 pseudokinase), with abundant and limited known reference binders, respectively. In addition, we report ablations of the co-folding-based oracle.

Results:
All three oracle feedback variants using the Boltz-2 oracle highlight that the LLM can use the binding affinity probability as a signal to optimize molecules. At the same time, the mean score never passes
0.75
, which indicates that the agent is unable to identify a strong binder. Nevertheless, the results show that the agent can optimize for novel binders using expensive, but expressive oracles. As depicted in Figure
2(c)
, the added explanation (blue curve) leads to faster optimization compared to no explanation and no protein sequence. Interestingly, the optimization curves for the latter two cases are nearly indistinguishable, suggesting that for this protein, the protein sequence alone is not enough to help the agent improve the molecules more sample-efficiently.

6
Discussion

This work introduced SEISMO, a goal-directed LLM agent that performs inference-time optimisation by leveraging the chemical and biological priors encoded in large language models to navigate chemical space. Unlike population-based or batched acquisition methods, SEISMO operates in a strictly online loop, where every oracle call is immediately incorporated into the next proposal. This removes the sample-efficiency limits imposed by methods that must first evaluate an entire batch or population before any learning can occur. On the PMO benchmark, this design yields large gains in sample efficiency. SEISMO reaches near-optimal scores within 50 oracle calls and achieves 2–3
\times
higher AUC than established baselines, even when those baselines are allowed to continue optimising for thousands of additional evaluations.

A central finding is that SEISMO’s sample efficiency depends on the type and amount of information provided to the agent. SEISMO integrates three types of information: natural-language objective descriptions, oracle scores, and, when available, explanations of those scores. Our ablations isolate the contribution of each component. When SEISMO receives only a scalar score without a task description, performance collapses across PMO and all case studies. Providing a task description leads to a dramatic improvement, demonstrating that the agent actively exploits prior chemical and biological knowledge encoded in the LLM to interpret the objective and propose meaningful starting molecules and molecular changes. However when using our co-folding oracle, providing the protein sequence alone does not improve method performance. One reason might be that the LLM does not know and cannot guess the correct binding site for this unfamiliar protein. Hence, limiting the use of sequence information alone. This theory is further strengthened by the observation that providing close residues as feedback improves performance. Consistent with this, adding explanations for why a molecule received a given score improves sample efficiency across all investigated tasks. This result has a broader implication: post-hoc explainability methods can be used not only for human-centred model interpretation, but as a machine-understood control signal that directly guides optimisation. In SEISMO, these explanations turn opaque oracle evaluations into actionable feedback about structure–property relationships, enabling more efficient and targeted search.

A few other recent methods incorporate LLMs into molecular optimisation. They are using the LLM as a component inside an evolutionary algorithm though to propose mutation or crossover candidates
(
36
;
26
)
. In these approaches, the LLM does not maintain an explicit record of the optimisation trajectory. Our results show that this is an inefficient way to use LLMs. In such schemes, the LLM is blind to the optimisation trajectory and therefore cannot reason about cause and effect across iterations. SEISMO instead uses the LLM as the optimiser itself: the model conditions on the task specification and the full history of oracle feedback to make each successive proposal, enabling genuinely sequential decision-making. This allows SEISMO to reach strong solutions within minimal oracle calls.

Due to the fact, that LLM inference is computationally expensive, SEISMO is not optimized for settings with very low-cost oracles where millions of evaluations are feasible. Instead, it excels in regimes where each oracle call is costly, so that reducing the number of oracle calls dominates the LLM compute.

A second implication of conditioning on the full optimisation trajectory is that the effective optimisation horizon is bounded by the LLM’s context window. For all tasks considered in this work, this never became a limiting factor, though it may matter in very long-horizon settings. History summarisation could mitigate this limitation, but we do not explore it here.

We want to emphasize that practically relevant drug-discovery problems are not tabula rasa optimisation tasks. They are strongly structured by prior physical and chemical knowledge. Small-molecule binding is governed by hydrogen bonding, electrostatics, hydrophobicity, desolvation, and conformational strain, which sharply constrain which molecular modifications can plausibly improve affinity. These principles induce strong inductive biases over chemical space, ruling out large classes of unproductive or physically implausible changes.
Decades of medicinal-chemistry research have further established generalizable relationships between molecular structure and properties such as potency, selectivity, solubility, permeability, and metabolic stability. An optimiser that ignores this accumulated knowledge must rediscover these regularities from scratch, which is fundamentally sample-inefficient. SEISMO explicitly leverages this prior knowledge through the pretrained LLM, allowing it to start the search in chemically meaningful regions of the space and to propose edits that are aligned with known structure–property trends.

Nevertheless, relying on prior knowledge raises the question of how SEISMO behaves when such knowledge is limited or unavailable. We therefore evaluated the method on three settings designed to reduce the usefulness of memorized target-specific information: similarity and drug-likeness optimisation to 20 random molecules, SARS-CoV-2 M
pro
inhibitor discovery under a novelty constraint, and optimisation for binding to a novel protein with no known ligands. Across all three cases, SEISMO still exhibits strong optimisation behaviour. It identifies highly similar molecules with substantially improved drug-likeness, proposes novel SARS-CoV-2 M
pro
inhibitors with high predicted IC
50
values, and substantially increases the binding probabilities for a protein with
no known binders. This indicates that SEISMO primarily
exploits general chemical and biophysical priors rather than relying on memorized examples of specific targets.

SEISMO requires no training, no fine-tuning, and no hyperparameter tuning by the user. It combines pretrained domain knowledge from the LLM with information extracted from oracle calls via post-hoc explainability, yielding an optimiser that is easily usable in new tasks.
Because SEISMO directly builds on general-purpose LLM capabilities, and those capabilities continue to improve, the method is expected to scale naturally with model progress. As LLMs become better at chemical reasoning, causal inference, and long-horizon planning, the same optimisation framework should become correspondingly more powerful without changing the underlying algorithm.

7
Conclusion

We introduced SEISMO, a goal-directed LLM agent that performs strictly online, inference-time molecular optimization by leveraging chemical and biological priors encoded in large language models. Across the PMO benchmark and additional medicinal-chemistry tasks, SEISMO achieves substantially higher sample efficiency than established optimization methods, often reaching near-optimal solutions within a few dozen oracle calls. Our ablations show that providing richer task-relevant information, via objective descriptions and feedback beyond scalar scores, drives more sample-efficient exploration of chemical space. Together, these results position information-aware, trajectory-conditioned LLM agents as a practical framework for molecular optimization in regimes where oracle evaluations are expensive.

Impact Statement

This work introduces an LLM-based agent for molecular optimization that achieves substantially higher sample efficiency than existing approaches. In many realistic molecular design settings, each evaluation corresponds to an expensive or rate-limited computation or experiment, making the number of oracle calls the central bottleneck. By converging to high-quality solutions within a small number of evaluations, the proposed method directly addresses this bottleneck. As a result, the approach has strong relevance for early-stage drug discovery and other molecular design workflows, where careful use of resources is essential.

The proposed approach is designed as a decision-support tool rather than an autonomous discovery system and relies on externally defined evaluation oracles. Any real-world application would therefore remain subject to domain expertise, experimental validation, and regulatory oversight. While the method could in principle be applied to optimizing molecules with undesirable or harmful properties, this risk is not unique to the approach and is shared by existing molecular optimization techniques. In practice, such risks are mitigated through controlled access to evaluation oracles and established institutional safeguards. We do not identify ethical concerns beyond those already well established in machine learning–based molecular design.

Appendix A
Implementation Details

Agent Architecture.

We implement our molecule optimization agent as a cyclic workflow using LangGraph
(
20
)
. Our workflow consists of five nodes: generation, parsing, validation, prediction, and finalisation. The conversation history begins with a system prompt providing general instructions (cf. Appendix
E.1
) followed by a task-specific first message describing the optimization objective (cf. Appendix
E.2
). At each iteration, the generation node invokes an LLM with this full history and instructs it to output structured JSON containing a SMILES string
(
37
)
and rationale. The parsing node extracts the JSON, and the validation node uses RDKit
(
31
)
to verify SMILES validity. Invalid outputs trigger a re-generation with corrective feedback; we count these as iterations to avoid infinite loops. Valid molecules proceed to the prediction node, where a pluggable oracle computes a score and optionally an explanation. We then construct feedback including the score, iteration count, and any explanation-derived insights, which is appended to the conversation history for the next generation step. The loop terminates upon reaching a target score or an iteration limit. The finalisation node then generates a natural language summary of the optimization trajectory grounded in the structured per-step records (generated SMILES and rationales) and oracle scores. The summary highlights which structural modifications improved or degraded performance, aimed to help medicinal chemists interpret the agent’s reasoning and extract actionable insights. Unless stated otherwise, the generation and finalisation nodes use
claude-opus-4-5-20251101-v1:0
from Anthropic.
We selected this LLM based on an ablation study (cf. Appendix
B
).

Oracle Instantiation and Benchmarking Protocol.

The prediction node is implemented as a modular oracle interface. When switching between tasks, we only replace the oracle callable and the initial task message used to initialize the conversation history, while keeping the rest of the workflow unchanged. For head-to-head comparison with established molecule-optimization methods, we evaluate on the Practical Molecular Optimization (PMO) benchmark, which consists of 23 different molecule optimisation tasks and was designed to evaluate sample efficiency in molecular optimisation
(
10
)
. The tasks span a diverse set of medicinal-chemistry objectives, including drug-likeness, bioactivity against targets, similarity- and rediscovery-based goals, isomer and substructure constraints, and multi-property optimization, and require optimizing molecular structures through oracle calls. To run our agent on PMO tasks, we call the benchmark oracles through the TDC package, following the same oracle access pattern used in the original PMO setup
(
15
)
. Importantly, to avoid altering the benchmark oracles, the oracle feedback provided to the agent is limited to the scalar score returned by the oracle without any further explanation of the score.

Within this constraint, we test two variants that differ in whether the LLM is told what the oracle measures in the description. In the
task described
variant, the initial task message explicitly states the objective (e.g., “maximize the geometric mean of …”), while the per-iteration feedback still contains only the scalar score. Our descriptions of the tasks are comparable to the descriptions used in other work
(
36
;
26
)
and are specified in Appendix
E.2
and our GitHub. In the
no description
variant, the initial message omits any description of the objective, so the agent must treat the oracle as fully black-box and rely purely on observed (molecule, score) pairs. For each of the 23 PMO tasks, we run both variants for 3 independent repetitions. Due to the API costs for LLM inference, we cap each run at 50 LLM calls.

We follow PMO’s recommended sample-efficiency evaluation via the area under the optimization curve (AUC)
(
10
)
. The optimization curve is defined as the top-
n
score achieved so far versus the number of oracle calls; we focus on
n=1
(top-1) because we are interested in finding a high-scoring molecule rather than optimizing finding a batch of molecules in our setting (cf. problem formulation). We report top-1 AUC over the first 50 oracle calls, matching our agent’s optimization budget, and top-1 AUC over 10,000 oracle calls for consistency with PMO reporting
(
10
)
. Our agent is only allowed to propose molecules for the first 50 oracle calls; for calls 51–10,000 we keep its best-so-far score fixed (no further optimization). In contrast, PMO baselines are evaluated over the full 10,000 oracle calls using their published trajectories. We also report the best score achieved after 50 oracle calls. We report 95% CIs that we computed via bootstrap resampling of the different repetitions across the 23 tasks.

To provide a broader comparison that also includes more recent models, we additionally evaluate top-10 AUC over 1,000 oracle calls and compare against the values reported by Nguyen et al., who provide results for a broad range of optimization models
(
26
)
. For our agent, we stop optimization after 50 oracle calls and keep the top-10 score fixed for the remaining 950 iterations, whereas all other methods from Nguyen et al. were allowed to optimize for the full 1,000 oracle calls.

For baseline comparisons within the PMO benchmark, we use the released per-iteration trajectories (including the proposed SMILES and corresponding oracle scores) and recompute our metrics (top-1 AUC at 50 and 10,000 calls, and best-at-50) from those raw traces to ensure identical metric definitions. We include the top three methods reported by PMO under their benchmark evaluation. In contrast, for the additional Nguyen et al. methods we only use their published top-10 AUC values, since they do not release the underlying per-iteration SMILES/oracle-call traces needed to compute our other metrics. Reproducing those traces would require rerunning their methods and would be prohibitively expensive due to LLM API costs.

Extended Tasks with Interpretable Oracle Feedback.

Beyond PMO, we evaluate the agent on two tasks that more directly mirror common medicinal-chemistry iteration loops: improving a predicted activity signal under a drug-likeness constraint, and improving drug-likeness while preserving similarity to a reference molecule. Since for these tasks we implement the oracles ourselves, we can also control the amount of explanation returned to the agent and ablate different feedback settings.

Hit-identification proxy: SARS-CoV-2 M
pro
inhibition with a QED and novelty constraint.

The objective is to minimise a predicted
\mathrm{IC}_{50}
(the half-maximal inhibitory concentration) while satisfying
\mathrm{QED}\geq 0.6
(Quantitative Estimate of Drug-likeness). The oracle returns (i) a scalar optimisation score used by the agent and (ii) a structured breakdown containing the predicted
\mathrm{IC}_{50}
and QED. Predicted
\mathrm{IC}_{50}
is produced by an XGBoost regressor
(
6
)
trained on a dataset of measured
\mathrm{IC}_{50}
values against SARS-CoV-2 main protease (training details are provided at the end of this part of the Appendix). Molecules are featurised using MACCS keys
(
8
)
. QED is computed with RDKit
(
31
)
and is a heuristic measure derived from the empirical distribution of molecular properties among approved oral drugs
(
2
)
. This constraint additionally helps to keep the search in regions where the XGBoost predictor is more reliable. We additionally constrain on novelty, which we define as absence from PubChem
(
18
)
; molecules that are present in PubChem are treated as non-novel and are considered invalid proposals. In the task description we refer only to a generic protease target (rather than explicitly naming SARS-CoV-2 M
pro
), since explicitly naming the target triggered LLM refusal behaviour due to its safety filters.

Lead-optimisation proxy: Similarity to a reference molecule with improved QED.

Given a reference molecule
x^{\star}
, the goal is to maximise structural similarity to
x^{\star}
while increasing QED. Similarity is computed as Tanimoto similarity between MACCS-key fingerprints of
x^{\star}
and the proposed molecule
(
8
)
; QED is computed with RDKit
(
31
)
. The oracle returns both sub-scores
(\mathrm{sim},\mathrm{QED})
and a scalar objective defined as their average

s=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{sim}+\mathrm{QED}),

which is the quantity optimized by the agent. Consequently,
s\geq 0.8
corresponds to an average similarity and QED of at least 0.8.

We study two scenarios for this task. (i) A praxis-motivated case study with
x^{\star}=
Quercetin, which has been discussed as a potential therapeutic but is known for poor solubility/bioavailability and rapid metabolism. The aim is to find close analogs with improved drug-like characteristics. (ii) A setting intended to evaluate the performance on molecules with low a priori familiarity to the LLM: we sample 20 target molecules uniformly at random from PubChem under constraints designed to ensure diversity and a non-trivial QED-improvement problem. Concretely, each sampled target has molecular weight
<500
,
\mathrm{QED}\leq 0.5
, and maximum pairwise Tanimoto similarity
\leq 0.2
with all previously selected targets (cf. Figure
A1
).

Figure A1
:
Visualisation of the 20 randomly sampled molecules from PubChem that were used as reference molecules in the lead optimisation proxy.

Oracle explanations.

For the XGBoost
\mathrm{IC}_{50}
model, explanations are generated with SHAP
(
23
)
over MACCS keys, yielding per-feature attributions indicating which substructures most increased or decreased the predicted
\mathrm{IC}_{50}
. We present the 10 substructures with highest attribution (driving lower IC
50
) and the 10 with lowest attribution (driving higher IC
50
), each annotated with its presence in the current molecule and SMARTS pattern to guide structural modifications. For QED, we provide a property-level decomposition (e.g., molecular weight, logP, HBD/HBA, PSA, rotatable bonds) and indicate which properties most reduced QED for the current proposal and what ranges would be more favourable. For similarity, we explain which MACCS keys differ between
x^{\star}
and the proposal (keys present in exactly one of the two), and map these keys to their corresponding SMARTS patterns to make the structural mismatch interpretable. See Appendix
E.3
for example explanations.

Evaluation protocol and metrics.

For the SARS-CoV-2 M
pro
task and the Quercetin lead-optimisation task, we run 3 independent repetitions for each of the four information conditions, for up to 50 iterations. For the 20 random PubChem targets, we run one 50-iteration optimisation per target and information condition, and report results averaged over the 20 targets.

For the SARS-CoV-2 M
pro
task, we evaluate sample efficiency using the optimisation curve of the best (lowest) predicted
\mathrm{IC}_{50}
among molecules that satisfy the constraints (
\mathrm{QED}\geq 0.6
and novel) versus oracle calls. If no molecule that satisfies the constraints has been found yet, we set the curve value to
1\,\mathrm{mM}
to allow AUC computation. We report the AUC over 50 oracle calls under this constraint-filtered curve. For the 20 random-target lead-optimisation setting, we report top-1 AUC over 50 iterations for the scalar objective
s=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{sim}+\mathrm{QED})
, averaged across targets. Since we include Quercetin as a concrete example, we evaluate it with a more directly interpretable, goal-based metric: the number of oracle calls required to first reach
s\geq 0.8
(i.e., average similarity and QED of at least 0.8), corresponding to finding a close analog with improved drug-like properties. For all tasks the 95% confidence intervals are computed via bootstrap resampling.

XGBoost training.

The XGBoost regression model was implemented using the
xgboost
python package (version 3.1.2) and trained to predict pIC
50
values for SARS-CoV-2 M
pro
inhibition using the ASAP Discovery antiviral potency dataset (asap-discovery/antiviral-potency-2025-unblinded) accessed via the Polaris platform
(
38
)
. Molecular structures were encoded as 167-bit MACCS fingerprints using RDKit. The XGBRegressor was configured with 2,000 estimators, a learning rate of 0.03, subsample ratio of 0.8, L1 regularization (
\alpha
= 0.1), L2 regularization (
\lambda
= 2), histogram-based tree construction with loss-guided growth policy, and a maximum of 128 leaves per tree. Hyperparameter optimization was performed via random search over 40 trials, exploring combinations of maximum tree depth (3, 4, 5, 6), minimum child weight (1, 3, 5, 7), gamma (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0), and column subsampling ratio (0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0). The model with the lowest root mean squared error (RMSE) on the held-out test set was selected as the final model.

Co-folding-based oracle.

The co-folding-based oracle has access to the target protein sequence and receives a molecule proposal from the LLM in the form of a SMILES string
(
37
)
. These two inputs are passed to the Boltz-2 co-folding model. We invoke Boltz-2 with its default parameters, except that we store the predicted structure in the PDB format and do not pre-compute multiple sequence alignments (MSAs), but let Boltz-2 handle that via their default MSA-server. We run the model on an NVIDIA A100 GPU and give it a time limit of five minutes. If the time out is reached or the model process reports a non-zero exit code the oracle returns NaN as its score. On model success, the oracle extracts the binding affinity probability score (
affinity_probability_binary
), which is a sigmoid probability representing Boltz-2’s belief that the compound binds to the protein. In addition, Boltz-2 provides a binding affinity value (
affinity_pred_value
), which aims to predict the affinity via the IC
50
value of the system. We decided to use the probability as the oracle’s score, as the IC
50
score is not normalized and preliminary experiments showed that the agent had difficulty optimizing it. If the oracle failed to parse the affinity scores, we again reported a NaN to the agent.

As an explanation, the oracle reported Boltz-2’s non-zero confidence scores and the protein’s residues that were close to the ligand. In particular, we reported Boltz-2’s aggregated confidence score (
confidence_score
), the (interface and ligand) predicted template modeling (TM) score (
[ligand_|i]ptm
), the (interface) predicted local distance difference test score (
complex_[i]plddt
), and the (interface) predicted distance error (
complex_[i]pde
). Close residues were defined as all residues which have at least one heavy atom (any non-hydrogen atom) that is no more than 5Å away from a compound heavy atom. We used the biotite
(
19
)
and scikit-learn
(
30
)
libraries to compute these distances. We reported the three letter code and index of all residues in the protein sequence satisfying our closeness definition to the LLM. If any of the above computations failed, we reported an empty explanation.

Appendix E
Example prompts, explanations and optimization trajectory

E.1
System prompt defining the LLM-guided molecular optimization protocol

The prompt below specifies a closed-loop proposal mechanism in which the language model is conditioned on the complete history of previously evaluated molecules and is required to output exactly one new candidate per iteration in a fixed JSON format.

You are an expert medicinal chemist whose job is to propose and optimize new molecules.

You interact in a multi-step optimization loop.

In this loop you:
- Propose small organic molecules as SMILES strings.
- Receive evaluations of your proposals.
- Have access to the full conversation history, including all past proposals,
rationales, and evaluations.

Your goal in this conversation is to iteratively propose new molecules that move
the objective toward the specified target, using the information in the
conversation history and your general chemical knowledge.

OUTPUT FORMAT:

For every proposal, you must respond with a single JSON object of the form

{
"reason": "<short explanation>",
"smiles": "<SMILES string>"
}

Requirements:
- "smiles" must be a single, valid SMILES string for a plausible small molecule.
- "reason" should briefly explain why, given the history of molecules and their
scores, this new proposal might improve or further explore the objective
landscape.
- Do not repeat any previous molecule exactly. The scores are deterministic and
will not change if a molecule is re-evaluated.
- Do not include any text outside the JSON object. No Markdown, no comments,
no code fences.

E.2
PMO benchmark first messages

For the PMO benchmark, we include three example first messages—Celecoxib rediscovery, Osimertinib MPO and DRD2—to illustrate the prompting setup.
