Better road conditions for everyone can be a co-benefit of road maintenance
Authors: Florence Dadzoe, Jessica Boakye, Egemen Okte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pavement management decisions shape how road quality is distributed across communities, yet equity is rarely evaluated as an outcome of standard prioritization practices. This study introduces a framework for assessing whether equity improvements can be a co-benefit of routine pavement maintenance without requiring equity-specific optimization. We propose two methodological advances. First, a community-based Gini coefficient computed at the census block group level, rather than at individual road segments, to meaningfully capture horizontal equity. Second, the Wasserstein distance to quantify vertical equity through distributional comparisons between Environmental Justice and non-Environmental Justice communities rather than relying on centrality measures. Four prioritization strategies combining pavement condition, traffic volume, and nearby population exposure are evaluated using Massachusetts Highway District 3 data (3000 lane-miles, 905 block groups). All strategies improve both equity metrics compared to baseline conditions, achieving up to 75% reduction in vertical disparity and 30% in horizontal inequality under a 150 lane-mile scenario. Crucially, for this case study, sensitivity analysis reveals that population-weighted strategies can widen rural-urban gaps at higher budget levels, while condition-only prioritization may sustain equity gains. These findings demonstrate that better road conditions for all communities can be achieved as a co-benefit of maintenance through careful selection of prioritization factors.

Introduction

The road network continues to remain a significant infrastructure for mobility, commerce, and emergency response. In the United States, approximately 78.4% of workers commute by road1, and drivers logged a record of 3.28 trillion miles traveled on U.S roads in 20242,3. These figures underscore the centrality and dependency of road networks on daily life and economic activity. However, this infrastructure system faces mounting threats from aging4 and increasingly extreme climate conditions5. According to the ASCE 2025 Infrastructure Report Card, 39% of major roads remain in poor condition, costing drivers an average of $1400 annually in vehicle wear, fuel inefficiency, and delays6. This degradation carries substantial economic consequences both for agencies and users through reducing operational efficiency, accelerating vehicle depreciation, causing decreased and unequal fuel economy across communities, increasing tire/suspension damage, and increasing agency rehabilitation costs7–10.

While the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act has potentially allocated over $591 billion towards improving transportation, there is a projected $684 billion funding gap over the next decade. This poses a significant challenge and shows the scale of investment required to eliminate the deficit between infrastructure needs and available capital. This situation creates a fundamental policy challenge of strategically allocating limited funds to maximize long-term benefits. The condition is further complicated by the current need for highway infrastructure networks to support sustainable development goals. Traditional prioritization schemes that rely primarily on cost minimization or environmental performance cannot adequately address the full spectrum of sustainability objectives, which now explicitly include social considerations. Therefore, advancing from single-objective decision frameworks to multidimensional, sustainability-focused strategies is essential for improving U.S. pavement infrastructure performance.

The selection of pavement segments for maintenance and rehabilitation has evolved into a data-driven approach supported by Pavement Management Systems (PMS). PMS is a decision support tool used to collect and analyze data to inform agency decisions on maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, and replacement and its principal function is to develop treatment strategies that provide optimal pavement conditions at the least practical cost over the short and long term11,12. Some PMS frameworks prioritize maintenance using objective indicators like road quality or traffic volume, while others focus on economic metrics such as net present value or cost-based ranking methodologies13–15. For instance, certain PMS applications prioritize pavement segments based on their Pavement Condition Index (PCI) and suggest maintenance strategies by ranking interventions based on their cost effectiveness15–17. Some frameworks also adopt multi-criterion approaches that include traffic characteristics, environmental impact, user safety, and expert opinions13,18. Beyond condition-based approaches, researchers also suggested that PMS strategies may incorporate user costs such as vehicle operating costs and travel delays7,19–21, work zone impacts22–24, deterioration modeling15,25,26, and environmental life-cycle assessment27. However, most current PMS implementations focus primarily on pavement conditions and do not incorporate the social dimension of sustainability explicitly15,18.

Previous research highlighted the components of the social sustainability working list relevant to pavement management systems using a Capability Approach (CA)18. These impacts include fatalities, health, mobility, comfort, emissions, financial impact, and equity. It is important to note that these are independent dimensions of human well-being. Equity concerns the fair distribution of infrastructure conditions (or impacts) across populations and is conceptually distinct from other user costs. While studies have addressed social costs such as travel time and congestion15,20,22,23,26, the focus of this study is on equity as one specific and important dimension that requires its own dedicated analysis. Recent research has begun to address this gap: France-Mensah28 developed quantitative models to enhance equity considerations; Kothari et al.4 introduced a budget allocation framework that simultaneously optimizes network condition, life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions, and the condition gap between disadvantaged and other communities; Okte et al.8 quantified how pavement condition differentially affects various road user groups; and Boakye et al.18 compiled a comprehensive list of social impacts, including equity, that should be considered in pavement management decisions. Despite these advances, a critical methodological gap persists: determining how different PMS prioritization schemes actually impact equity outcomes.

This study addresses these gaps by evaluating equity as a co-benefit of different pavement prioritization strategies, using improved community-level metrics demonstrated through a Massachusetts highway case study.

Literature review

This section establishes the conceptual foundation for integrating equity considerations into pavement management systems. It begins by defining social equity and environmental justice concepts, examines transportation equity metrics commonly used in the literature, reviews how equity considerations have been incorporated into pavement management systems, and presents the specific methodological gaps that this study addresses.

Social equity concept

Equity, broadly defined in policy contexts, represents fairness and justice in the distribution of resources and opportunities among people. The U.S. Department of Transportation, in its Equity Action Plan, defines equity as “the consistent and systematic fair, just, and impartial treatment of all individuals, including individuals who belong to underserved communities that have been denied such treatment”29. This definition encompasses a wide range of individuals while emphasizing particular attention to marginalized communities. Social equity specifically focuses on the social and human dimensions of fair and just distribution of opportunities. Litman30 characterizes social equity as the distribution of “cost and benefit and the extent to which this distribution is considered appropriate.” The Federal Highway and Transit Administration further elaborates this concept, emphasizing that all transportation system users, especially those traditionally underserved, must be fully considered during planning and decision-making processes to ensure equitable distribution of benefits and costs without any group bearing disproportionate burdens29.

Distributional fairness can be categorized into two primary types: horizontal and vertical equity31. Horizontal equity focuses on fairly distributing resources and opportunities to individuals and groups deemed equal in needs and abilities28,32. Under this framework, resources and opportunities are distributed regardless of class or group membership, with all individuals equally sharing benefits and costs. Infrastructure improvements following horizontal equity principles are delivered at equivalent levels across all communities, irrespective of social class or demographic characteristics. Vertical equity, in contrast, addresses the distribution of resources and opportunities between individuals and groups with different levels of needs and abilities4,8,18,28. This approach recognizes that individuals and communities may require different levels of investment based on their specific needs and circumstances. Under vertical equity principles, policies prioritize infrastructure improvements in disadvantaged areas to address existing inequalities between groups.

Transportation equity intersects significantly with environmental justice (EJ) concepts, which involves the investigation and identification of communities experiencing disproportionate environmental burdens. Traditional environmental justice research has concentrated on hazardous waste disposal, air contamination, and water contamination that disproportionately affects vulnerable populations. Topics covered by the EJ movement have expanded to areas such as transportation management, access, hazard impacts, and energy access33–37. The definition of vulnerable populations and EJ communities continues to evolve, incorporating factors including race, gender identity, sex, and income38,39. Equity considerations have also been explored in the broader context of road network design and planning. Kothari et al.40 introduce equity metrics for road systems. Santos et al.41 integrated equity objectives into a road network design model, while Mollanejad and Zhang42 incorporated spatial equity into interurban road network design. Krapp et al.43 examined equity-oriented criteria for project prioritization in regional transportation planning, and Mohamadiazar et al.44 extended equity considerations to bridge rehabilitation projects using a spatial multi-criteria decision support framework.

Common transportation equity metrics

Integrating social equity and environmental justice concepts into transportation infrastructure decisions typically involves quantitative equity metrics that serve as proxies for evaluating different types of fairness. According to a comprehensive review of transportation equity studies by Coelho et al.45, commonly employed metrics include the Gini index, Lorenz curve, Theil index, and Palma ratio. These metrics, originally developed to measure income disparity, have been successfully adapted to assess equity in transportation infrastructure contexts.

The Gini coefficient represents a widely used inequality measure that has been adapted for pavement management equity assessment. Derived from the Lorenz curve, it ranges from 0 (perfect equity) to 1 (maximum inequality). The coefficient is calculated by measuring the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of perfect equality, expressed as a fraction of the total area under the equality line46. The Lorenz curve provides a graphical representation of inequality within a population, displaying the cumulative share of resources held by the population from lowest to highest resourced, thus visualizing how evenly or unevenly resources are distributed. Both the Gini coefficient and Lorenz curve constitute horizontal equity measures, assessing inequality across populations regardless of class or group membership.

In pavement management applications, France-Mensah et al.28 employed the Gini coefficient to evaluate multiple maintenance and rehabilitation optimization policies. Their case study demonstrated that for similar average network conditions, different prioritization policies resulted in varying inequality levels based on Gini scores. However, their application calculated the Gini metric on pavement section condition states throughout the network, indicating deviation from perfectly equal pavement condition distribution. By contrast, Chen et al.47 followed the original Gini coefficient usage to analyze infrastructure spending across U.S. municipalities, measuring public infrastructure expenditure per capita income across various sectors from 2010 to 2019.

The Theil index, on the other hand, frequently appears alongside the Gini coefficient in equity studies. This metric measures inequality by quantifying disparities in resource distribution, using information theory principles to capture how far actual distributions deviate from perfect distributions. Importantly, it can be decomposed into between-group and within-group components, making it suitable for analyzing both horizontal and vertical equity. In public transit applications, Camporeale et al.48 used the Theil index to assess accessibility inequality in cordon-based pricing strategies. Similarly, Shi et al.49 applied the Theil index to measure accessibility and equity in urban transport using multisource big data.

The Palma ratio measures resource inequality by focusing specifically on distribution extremes. It calculates inequality as the ratio of resource share held by the richest 10% of the population to that of the poorest 40%45. Van Wee and Mouter50 identified the Palma ratio among indicators considered for assessing accessibility distributions. Studies by Liu et al.51 and Li et al.52 employed the Palma ratio to measure job accessibility for transit-dependent workers in Chicago and to assess distribution of accessibility metrics in Kunming, respectively.

The Wasserstein distance (WD), also known as Earth Mover’s Distance, measures the work needed to transfer one statistical distribution into another. In pavement management contexts, Boakye and Okte18 utilized this metric within a proposed capability-based framework for integrating social equity into pavement management decisions. They applied Wasserstein distance to measure distributional differences in road roughness using the International Roughness Index (IRI), capturing how unevenly road maintenance outcomes were distributed across communities. While most studies use centrality measures such as differences in means or medians to measure differences between groups, these metrics are less sensitive to tail behavior than distributional metrics such as WD, where disparity is more likely to occur18. Non-parametric statistical methods including the Wasserstein distance have also been applied in related contexts such as model transferability evaluation53 and comparative assessment of machine learning models54 as it was originally developed to measure distance between distributions55.

Equity considerations in PMS

Building on these conceptual foundations, recent research has begun to translate equity principles into actionable pavement prioritization4,28,40,56,57. Kothari et al.4 developed a sustainable pavement management plan addressing economics, environment, and social equity. Their model formulates three objectives as an optimization problem with constraints including budget limits, acceptable pavement performance ranges, and limits on maintenance and rehabilitation treatments applied. Extending this integrated approach, Arezoumand and Smadi56 proposed a prototype decision-support framework integrating economic, environmental, and social equity considerations. In their California Bay Area case study, the model simultaneously minimizes disparities in pavement conditions between underserved and other areas, reduces greenhouse gas emissions, and maximizes overall network condition under budget constraints. Alternatively, France-Mensah et al.28 evaluated four different policies focusing exclusively on equity in highway maintenance decisions, developing separate budget-allocation models and using genetic algorithms to find optimal solutions.

However, the horizontal inequity indices in some studies reflect the disparities in pavement conditions across segments, rather than disparities experienced by different population groups. This limits their ability to capture social equity impacts. Calculating the Gini index on individual road segments and using this as a pavement prioritization method has two main limitations. First, perfect equality could be achieved by every road segment being in poor condition. Second, it is expected that parts of the pavement system will be in different condition states, as equal road conditions are not an equity consideration. Inequity occurs when certain locations or groups of people experience disproportionately poor infrastructure conditions. To overcome these limitations, the Gini index should be calculated from a community perspective, with the pavement condition of the community serving as the unit of measurement for horizontal equity comparison.

Vertical equity metrics have also been used with PMS. However, much of the current work focuses on minimizing differences between measures of centrality between groups despite previous research documenting that disparities for transportation improvements must focus on distributional differences18,58. To overcome this limitation, this study proposes metrics that are sensitive to distributional differences between vulnerable and non-vulnerable groups.

Research gap and study objectives

While substantial progress has been made in developing equity-focused pavement management systems, several critical gaps remain in the literature.

Many studies of equity and PMS focus on the development of new prioritization metrics which directly minimize disparities between groups. However, directly minimizing disparities without considering infrastructure related or other important factors could have unintended consequences. As noted by Boakye et al.18, disparity reduction should not compromise infrastructure integrity (such as overall pavement quality) or be used as a substitute for other salient impacts. Additionally, the need for constant pavement maintenance provides an opportunity for equity reduction. Previous studies have not explicitly measured equity as a co-benefit of other pavement prioritization metrics, which allows for a more comprehensive framing of infrastructure improvement and decision making. Moreover, the calculation of equity metrics should be done at a community level and not at an individual road segment level to represent the experience of equal infrastructure.

The study addresses these gaps by: (1) evaluating how different pavement prioritization strategies impact both vertical and horizontal equity as a co-benefit; (2) implementing community-based Gini coefficient calculations for more meaningful horizontal equity assessment; (3) introducing distributional vertical equity analysis using the Wasserstein distance metric; and (4) demonstrating through a Massachusetts highway case study how prioritization strategies can impact vertical and horizontal equity.

Methodology

The methodology is designed for application to pavement networks where multiple segments require repair under budget constraints and uses publicly available data to ensure broad applicability. The approach consists of: (1) Selection of pavement prioritization impacts; (2) Pavement prioritization calculation; and (3) Equity evaluation metrics.

Selection of pavement prioritization impacts

The first step involves identifying which impacts to consider when prioritizing pavement segments for maintenance. This selection requires evaluating the multi-dimensional effects that pavement condition decisions have on surrounding communities, work that aligns with social sustainability efforts focused on creating impact factors and metrics59. A comprehensive discussion of impact considerations and quantification metrics for pavement management systems can be found in previous research8,18. These impacts encompass factors such as user costs, user safety, public health, and business disruption.

For this study, the focus was on environmental and road user-related factors, building on research demonstrating that pavement management activities significantly impact both environmental emissions and road users27,60–64. This analysis considers two primary impact categories beyond pavement condition: (1) Community impacts and (2) User impacts.

Community impacts

Community impacts relate to the population exposed to pavement degradation effects. Research by Wu et al.65 and Fecht et al.66 demonstrates that zones of elevated noise and air pollution exposure exist within 100 m of pavement surfaces. Karner et al.67 report that ultrafine particles typically return to urban background levels within 100–200 m of highways. Therefore, the number of people living within a 100-m buffer zone of each pavement segment was selected as a prioritization criterion. Since pavement condition influences emissions68, maintaining good pavement condition near high-population areas could reduce community exposure to harmful emissions. Buildings and their associated populations within a 100-m buffer were assigned to corresponding road segments, as illustrated in Fig. 1a using National Structural Inventory69. Also, community-level infrastructure conditions were assessed using length-weighted average road conditions within a one-mile buffer of each census block group as shown in Fig. 1b. User impacts

It is important to distinguish community impacts (e.g. the effects of road condition on nearby residents through noise and emissions exposure) from user impacts experienced by travelers on the road itself. User impacts relate to road usage intensity, which can be measured through Annual Average Daily Traffic (AADT). Because pavement condition affects both user costs and emissions7, AADT serves as a proxy for the number of users affected by changes in pavement condition. Higher traffic volumes mean that pavement improvements benefit more road users and potentially reduce aggregate environmental impacts.

Influenceability considerations

An important criterion for selecting infrastructure metrics is influenceability18,70. While pavement managers cannot directly influence populations near roadways or traffic volumes, they can influence these impacts indirectly through pavement condition improvements. Pavement condition remains the only factor directly controllable by pavement managers. Therefore, any prioritization metric should incorporate condition ratings combined with other impact proxies (such as AADT or population density). The development of these combined prioritization metrics is detailed in the following section.

Pavement prioritization calculation

Given funding constraints, transportation agencies must prioritize certain pavement segments over others for maintenance activities. Various prioritization strategies have been employed in practice, including the worst-first strategy and the benefit-cost ratio (BCR) approach, and approaches considering long term sustainability impacts21,71. It is important to note that agencies typically employ a combination of strategies, including preservation treatments that maintain roads already in good condition and corrective treatments that address roads in poor condition27,72. While preservation strategies have been shown to be more cost-effective over the long term, no agency exclusively employs preservation; a subset of roads in poor condition must still be addressed. This study focuses specifically on this corrective maintenance component, using a worst-first approach to examine how the choice of prioritization factors affects equity outcomes.

The worst-first approach has well-documented limitations, including its limitations in not accounting for deterioration rates or long-term costs15,25,26. More advanced optimization frameworks that incorporate deterioration modeling and life-cycle considerations may yield better outcomes15,20,24. However, worst-first is used here for three reasons. First, it provides a transparent and reproducible baseline that isolates the equity effects of the prioritization factors. Second, it is widely understood and commonly applied in practice for corrective maintenance decisions. Third, it avoids confounding from complex optimization dynamics. The equity metrics proposed in this study (community-based Gini and distributional Wasserstein distance) are used independently of any prioritization scheme and are only used to check the impact of decisions on equity.

Equity evaluation metrics

The equity impacts of different prioritization strategies are evaluated separately from the prioritization metrics themselves. While prioritization metrics focus on pavement-level impacts, equity evaluation metrics assess population-level outcomes. This distinction ensures that equity considerations are evaluated from the community perspective rather than the infrastructure perspective. Crucially, none of the prioritization factors directly target equity outcomes; disparity reduction is viewed as a co-benefit of improving infrastructure condition.

Horizontal equity assessment

Horizontal equity was captured through the Gini coefficient, which is derived from the Lorenz curve analysis. The Lorenz curve provides a graphical representation of inequality by plotting the cumulative share of a population (ordered from lowest to highest by resource access) against the cumulative share of a non-negative resource held by that population. Originally developed in economics to quantify income inequality74,75, the Gini coefficient is a unitless value ranging from 0 to 1 that quantifies the inequality represented by the Lorenz curve. It measures the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of perfect equality, as illustrated in Fig. 2. There is a critical methodological consideration in its application to pavement management. While many studies compute the Gini coefficient across individual road segments, this approach can be misleading from an equity perspective. Equity concerns equitable access to infrastructure quality across populations rather than uniform road conditions per segment. Therefore, the Gini coefficient was calculated using population groups (census block groups) and their access to pavement infrastructure. Achieving similar pavement conditions across multiple block groups aligns with equity objectives, whereas uniformity across road segments may not necessarily represent equitable outcomes. This population-based approach is crucial for meaningful equity assessments.

In this study, the Gini coefficient was computed using the length-weighted average International Roughness Index (IRI) assigned to each block group. Since each block group represents a relatively consistent population size (approximately 600–3000 residents), it provides a suitable unit of analysis. The length-weighted IRI for each block group was calculated by aggregating the condition of roads within a one-mile (1609m) radius. Vertical equity assessment

Vertical equity addresses the fair treatment of individuals or groups with different levels of need, with particular emphasis on ensuring that disadvantaged populations receive appropriate consideration. In this study, groups were defined as Environmental Justice (EJ) and non-Environmental Justice (non-EJ) populations, as designated by the Massachusetts Department of Transportation. Vertical equity metrics based solely on centrality measures may mask these crucial distributional differences18.

Results

Study area

Massachusetts Highway District 3 was selected as the study area. District 3 encompasses a diverse mix of urban, suburban, and rural communities, with approximately 1.4 million residents distributed across 905 census block groups as shown in Fig. 4. Of these, around 820,000 reside in Environmental Justice (EJ) block groups, while 570,000 live in non-EJ areas.

The district’s road network includes state-maintained arterials, collectors, and local streets serving varied socio-economic populations. Road network data was obtained from the 2024 Massachusetts Department of Transportation inventory, which covers 1548 centerline miles (3007 lane-miles) of state-owned roads, with road sections measuring 0.1 mile77. Missing AADT data, where segments showed zero values, were imputed using the median AADT of the corresponding roadway functional class (interstate, arterial, collector). Analysis of pavement conditions was conducted using International Roughness Index (IRI). Beyond this threshold of 150 lane-miles, strategies that incorporate population factors no longer improve Gini coefficient or Wasserstein Distance measures. This occurs because population-weighted strategies favor urban roadways and focus improvements in areas with high population density. As a result, the condition gap between rural and urban areas widens as budget levels increase beyond the 150 lane-mile threshold. At higher budgets, focusing only on the condition of the network results in the most equity gains in addition to better pavement condition. This is because in the extreme case of repairing all road segments, there would be no inequities between populations.

These findings also emphasize the importance of agency priorities in balancing user impacts and equity considerations. There may be valid reasons for prioritizing certain segments such as high-volume roads. For example, poor conditions on these roads cause disproportionately high fuel consumption, so prioritizing them could reduce overall user costs7. However, agencies should also consider the potential equity trade-offs across different budget levels.

Discussion

The study shows a disparity in pavement experience between EJ and non-EJ communities, with EJ communities more likely to encounter poor pavement conditions. This finding supports some previous studies8,79,81, which identified that neighborhoods with higher proportions of African-American residents, linguistically isolated populations, and disadvantaged housing and transportation conditions tend to have lower access to high-quality pavement. However, this finding is not universal. Hendricks82 found that in Houston, communities of color had marginally better road condition ratings relative to other neighborhoods, though most neighborhoods had low overall ratings. This context-dependence demonstrates the need for case-specific equity analysis rather than blanket assumptions.

Several factors may contribute to the disparity observed in this case study, such as the structure of existing pavement management prioritization strategies or disproportionate exposure of EJ communities to heavy vehicle traffic83,84. Social and institutional dynamics could also play a role. For example, Cook et al.85 found that despite greater need in lower-income and minority neighborhoods, fewer service requests are made, creating a demand gap.

Regardless of the underlying causes, it is crucial to address existing inequities. An important first step is identifying the metrics to quantify equity. This study quantifies horizontal and vertical equity through Gini and Wasserstein metrics respectively. Crucially, the Gini metric is calculated from the perspective of census block groups instead of individual pavement segments. This formulation ensures that horizontal equity aims to achieve similar proportions of good/poor pavements in different communities instead of uniform pavement condition. Similarly, using a Wasserstein distance for vertical equity captures inequity for the entire distribution and not only between the average pavement condition between block groups. The application of these metrics is important, as it provides a realistic perspective of the pavement condition experience of the population.

To assess the impact of pavement management decisions on equity, this study used different prioritization metrics following a worst-first approach. Crucially, these factors do not explicitly consider equity in their formulation. This is because directly minimizing the equity gap might force maintenance decisions that overlook the most structurally necessary repairs, potentially leading to a decline in the overall pavement condition of the network18. All policies considered in this study improve overall road condition as the difference between pavement condition across strategies is minimal. This study shows that equity can be a co-benefit of road maintenance and different prioritization strategies, while having similar infrastructure improvements, can be used to improve horizontal and vertical equity.

Specifically, as shown in Fig. 6, when fixing between 50 and approximately 150 lane-miles, population-based prioritization strategies more effectively reduce horizontal and vertical inequity. Although the 150 lane-mile target reduced disparity compared to the baseline, the population-based strategy no longer resulted in an improvement for horizontal and vertical equity beyond this point. This pattern suggests that segments with very poor pavement conditions, high population density, and high traffic volumes were relatively few and concentrated in EJ communities. Therefore, a 100 lane-mile fix, when guided by population and traffic metrics, effectively targets these high-need areas, resulting in a sharp reduction in disparity. However, when fixing larger lane-mile targets, population-based strategies become less effective. The strategy begins to concentrate segment selection in urban areas with higher population density, which inadvertently increases disparity. In contrast, condition-only strategies continue to improve disparity by consistently targeting the worst pavement segments, including those located in lower-population EJ communities. This result shows that for high budget levels, simply fixing the roads in worst condition can result in equity gains in addition to better network condition.

Therefore, depending on the budget of the agency, it is important to prioritize different road segments to achieve good pavement performance and low disparity between different neighborhoods. The findings suggest that for this region, incorporating population into prioritization strategies can meaningfully reduce disparity for low budget levels. However, as the scope of intervention expands, condition-only strategies may become more effective. This shows the importance of assessing equity not just as a constraint, but as a co-benefit of pavement prioritization strategies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates how transportation agencies can systematically evaluate the equity impacts of different pavement maintenance prioritization strategies. Agencies can achieve substantial improvements in equity while maintaining infrastructure performance objectives.

The Massachusetts Highway District 3 case study provides evidence of these potential benefits. Using a 150-lane-mile treatment scenario, all prioritization strategies examined achieved significant equity improvements: up to 75% improvement in vertical equity (measured by Wasserstein distance) and 30% improvement in horizontal equity (measured by Gini coefficient). These results demonstrate that substantial equity gains can be realized as co-benefits of strategic infrastructure investment, even when equity is not the primary optimization objective.

The study evaluated four distinct prioritization approaches that weighted International Roughness Index (IRI) by different combinations of user factors (Annual Average Daily Traffic) and community factors (population within 100 m of roadways). A key finding is that all worst-first prioritization strategies improved both equity measures compared to baseline conditions. This occurs because the most significant disparities between Environmental Justice and non-Environmental Justice communities exist in the poor-condition segments of the road network. By prioritizing the worst-condition roads first, agencies can simultaneously address infrastructure needs and reduce equity gaps in this particular case.

However, the sensitivity analysis reveals important trade-offs that emerge at higher budget levels. While all strategies continue to improve average pavement conditions as budgets increase, those emphasizing population density may actually worsen equity outcomes beyond the 150-lane-mile threshold. This happens because population-weighted strategies favor urban roadways, potentially widening the condition gap between rural and urban areas. These findings underscore the importance of considering equity implications across different budget scenarios when selecting prioritization approaches. The results shown here are not meant to be prescriptive, rather descriptive of the possible gains over a wide range of budget and prioritization strategies.

By treating equity as both a measurable impact and an achievable outcome, this research provides transportation agencies with practical tools for balancing performance, sustainability, and fairness in infrastructure investment decisions. The methodology offers a quantitative foundation for evaluating equity trade-offs inherent in different maintenance approaches, moving beyond intuitive assessments to evidence-based decision-making.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The impact weights and factors examined represent one possible approach to prioritization, and different agencies may have varying preferences based on their specific contexts and priorities. In addition, the buffer distance of 100m or 1mi could be modified to capture other salient impacts or community effects. Moreover, In the current formulation, all factors are weighted equally. Varying the relative weights could lead to different prioritization outcomes. The impacts considered (pavement condition, traffic volume, and nearby population) do not constitute a comprehensive set of all relevant factors that could influence prioritization decisions. In addition, worst-first prioritization is only one of the prioritization methods that an agency can choose to follow.

Importantly, the equity improvements documented here represent a static analysis that does not account for ongoing pavement deterioration or the dynamic evolution of equity outcomes over time. Pavement condition changes continuously due to traffic loading, climate, and aging, and the equity gains observed from a single round of maintenance may be sustained, amplified, or diminished as pavements continue to deteriorate. Future research should incorporate deterioration modeling15,18,25,26, to evaluate how equity outcomes evolve over multiple maintenance cycles. Such an extension would enable agencies to assess the long-term equity implications of their maintenance strategies.

Future research should also focus on integrating these equity-focused metrics into existing Pavement Management Systems, enabling decision-makers to evaluate and refine prioritization strategies before implementation. Expanding the framework to incorporate long-term considerations, such as life-cycle environmental impacts27 and pavement deterioration patterns, would improve alignment with broader sustainability goals18.
