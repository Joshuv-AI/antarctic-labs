Prediction-Intervention Games and Invariant Sets
Linus Kühne, Felix Schur, Jonas Peters

Abstract
We consider the following two-player game: using observational data, the leader chooses a prediction function for a response variable Y Y from given covariates. The follower then reacts with an intervention on some covariates in the underlying structural causal model to maximize their own objective. The leader knows the intervention targets, but may have limited knowledge of the follower’s objective. We call this setup a prediction-intervention game, a special case of a Stackelberg game. Finding an optimal strategy for the leader is generally difficult. To avoid severe performance loss, the leader may base their prediction on the causal parents of Y Y , or more generally on an invariant subset of covariates. We prove, for two common classes of follower objectives, that predictors based on the stable blanket, a specific invariant subset, are always better or as good as those based on the causal parents. We further upper bound the leader’s post-intervention risk by a worst-case risk over allowed interventions and strengthen existing distribution generalization results to analyze this bound: we give sufficient conditions under which stable-blanket predictors are worst-case optimal, and show by examples that these conditions cannot in general be dropped. Finally, we discuss practical strategies for settings with known and unknown graph, and test them on simulated and real-world data.

Introduction

We consider a two-player Stackelberg game for predicting a real-valued or binary
response
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
from covariates
X\in\mathcal{X}
:
based on a training distribution (or data),
a leader commits to a prediction function
f
, and a follower (an individual subject to the prediction) observes or queries
f
and intervenes on some covariates to optimize their own, possibly non-adversarial, objective. The test distribution thus emerges only after the
prediction function has been deployed, and can depend on that
function. In particular, training and test distributions need not coincide.

Example.

Consider an insurer who prices policies with a function
f
predicting
the probability that an individual will submit a claim.
Suppose that
f
uses two covariates that are correlated with
Y
,
but only one is causal: the car’s number of safety features
and the car’s color.
An individual
i
with access to
f
(such as through querying a website for a quote)
may change their covariates from
X_{i}
to
\tilde{X}_{i}
(yielding
\tilde{Y}_{i}
) to minimize

f(\tilde{X}_{i})
and obtain a smaller premium. Installing safety features changes both the predicted and true probability of filing a claim:
f(\tilde{X}_{i})\neq f({X_{i}})
and
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Y}_{i}=1)\neq\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}=1)
.
Choosing a car with a different color may instead yield
f(\tilde{X}_{i})\neq f({X_{i}})

without changing the probability of filing a claim:

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Y}_{i}=1)=\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}=1)
.
A similar phenomenon occurs in usage-based car insurance, where
Lee et al. 2025
show that temporarily monitoring drivers to price policies changes their behavior during that period, worsening prediction performance.

Prediction-intervention games.

We formalize the problem as a causal version of a Stackelberg game
(
von Stackelberg 1934
)
, which we call a prediction-intervention game.
The leader provides a prediction
function
f
, and the follower reacts by choosing an intervention
e^{*}(f)

in a structural causal model (SCM) that maximizes their objective:

e^{*}(f)\in\argmax_{e}V^{\mathrm{foll}}_{e}(f)
. We model the allowed intervention targets as the children of an intervention node in the causal graph. Knowing the causal structure helps the leader design a prediction function
that is robust under the follower’s interventions, that is, find
\argmin_{f}R^{\mathrm{lead}}_{e^{*}(f)}(f)
.
In practice, the leader estimates
R^{\mathrm{lead}}_{e}
from data and may have only partial knowledge about the follower’s objective and possible interventions.

Related work.

Our work relates to strategic
classification
(
Hardt et al. 2016
;
Brückner and Scheffer 2011
)
,
its causal extensions
(
Miller et al. 2020
;
Shavit et al. 2020
)
,
formulations with asymmetric leader/follower
objectives
(
Ehrenberg et al. 2025
)
, and
performative prediction
(
Perdomo et al. 2020
)
.

Horowitz and Rosenfeld 2023

study binary strategic classification where covariates are
split into
causal and
noncausal predictive ones.
Their follower’s best response is a hard do-intervention on all observed covariates.
Unlike existing work, we allow
for more general follower
interventions that target pre-specified nodes, may add edges to the causal graph, and we allow for
flexible leader and follower objectives. Further, we focus on predictors based on different invariant subsets of covariates,
connecting
prediction-intervention games to causal distribution
generalization
(
Peters et al. 2016
;
Rojas-Carulla et al. 2018
;
Christiansen et al. 2022
)
;
in particular, we analyze predictions based on the stable blanket

(
Pfister et al. 2021
)
.
We provide and analyze a tight bound that builds a connection to worst-case distribution generalization
(
Kuhn et al. 2025
;
Sagawa et al. 2020
;
Freni et al. 2025
)
,
and
extend and strengthen existing results for our setting. To our knowledge, the combination of more general SCM-constrained follower interventions, flexible leader/follower objectives, and worst-case analysis of predictors based on stable blankets is novel.

Prediction-intervention games

The game.

We assume that the leader’s task is to perform prediction; in this paper, we
consider both regression and binary classification.
Let

Notation.

For a node
j
in a DAG
\mathcal{H}
, let
\PA_{\mathcal{H}}(j),\CH_{\mathcal{H}}(j),\DE_{\mathcal{H}}(j),\ND_{\mathcal{H}}(j)
denote its parents, children, descendants, and non-descendants (all excluding
j

We sometimes write
k\to j
as a shorthand for an edge
X_{k}\to X_{j}
. For

S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
, define
X_{S}\coloneqq(X_{j})_{j\in S}
. Expectations under
\mathbb{P}^{0}_{e}
and
\mathbb{P}_{e}
are denoted by
\mathbb{E}^{0}_{e}

The leader’s evaluation loss.

We assume that the leader evaluates a predictor
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}

using a loss
\ell\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,\infty]
strictly consistent for the mean functional
(
Gneiting 2011
)
: for every distribution
F
on
\mathcal{Y}
with finite mean
\mathbb{E}_{F}[Y]
and for which the expectations below are finite, and for all
q\in\mathcal{A}
,
\mathbb{E}_{F}[\ell(Y,\mathbb{E}_{F}[Y])]\leq\mathbb{E}_{F}[\ell(Y,q)]
, with equality iff
q=\mathbb{E}_{F}[Y]
. Examples include the squared loss
\ell(y,q)=(y-q)^{2}
for regression and, for binary classification, any strictly proper scoring rule such as the
Brier score
\ell(y,q)=(y-q)^{2}
or the
log loss
(
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
)
. Strict consistency ensures that the risk
R^{\mathrm{lead}}_{e}(f)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{e}[\ell(Y,f(X))]
is uniquely minimized by
f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{e}[Y\,|\,X=x]
(thus, it plays a similar role to the orthogonality of the conditional expectation, but extends to other loss functions). We assume throughout that
Y
has finite mean under each
\mathbb{P}_{e}
and that all risks in our results are finite.

Follower’s objective.

The follower’s objective need not coincide with the leader’s: while the
leader evaluates
f
by
R^{\mathrm{lead}}_{e}(f)
and prefers low risk, the follower selects
e\in\mathcal{E}
to maximize a separate utility
V^{\mathrm{foll}}_{e}(f)\in\mathbb{R}
. For instance, the leader may use
R^{\mathrm{lead}}_{e}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{e}[(Y-f(X))^{2}]
, while the follower maximizes an objective such as
\mathbb{E}_{e}[Y-f(X)]
or
\mathbb{E}_{e}[f(X)]
.

Follower’s interventions.

The follower reacts to
f
by strategically intervening on the children of

E
in
\mathbb{C}_{0}
, possibly adding new edges to
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. This yields a
deployment SCM
\mathbb{C}
over
(X,Y,E)
that extends
\mathbb{C}_{0}
, with induced
distribution
\mathbb{P}
and DAG
\mathcal{G}
. Each

yields
\mathbb{P}_{e}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\cdot\,|\,E=e)

over
(X,Y)
. The source node
E
indicates the potential intervention targets: an edge
E\to X_{j}
in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
means the follower may intervene on
the mechanism of
X_{j}
. We assume no edge
E\to Y
. Compared to training, for each
j\in\CH_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(E)
, the follower may let
X_{j}
depend on
X_{\PA_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(j)}
and on additional variables from an action set
A_{j}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
of permitted new parents; we write

A\coloneqq(A_{1},\ldots,A_{d})
.
Formally,
\mathbb{C}
is obtained from
\mathbb{C}_{0}
by replacing, for each

j\in\CH_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(E)
, the structural assignment of
X_{j}
with a
measurable function of
X_{\PA_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(j)\cup S_{j}}
for some

S_{j}\subseteq A_{j}
, plus independent exogenous noise. All other structural assignments are unchanged. The deployment DAG
\mathcal{G}
is the DAG
of
\mathbb{C}
, which we assume to be acyclic. Choosing
S_{j}=\varnothing
for
all

together with the original assignments recovers

, so

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tr}}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
and
\mathbb{P}_{e}=\mathbb{P}^{0}_{e}
for

e\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
. Larger
A_{j}
allow for more flexible interventions.

Unknown graph.

In
Section

5
, we drop the assumption that the graph
is known to the leader. In that case,
we assume access to training data
from several training environments
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tr}}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{0}
with

For each

In

and

2.1
Individuals and population interventions

We can also take the perspective of an individual follower who optimizes a more personalized objective rather than a population quantity. In the following, all graph quantities are taken with respect to
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.
The follower may observe a realization of some vector
O\subseteq\ND(\CH(E))

and is allowed to
choose deterministic values
for
\CH(E)
, taking into account the observation
X_{O}=x_{O}
. For simplicity, let us assume that for all
j\in\CH(E)
, we have

\PA(j)\subseteq O\cup\CH(E)
, so the follower has access to all parents of
j
when setting

Let us further assume that the follower maximizes a utility of the form

V^{\mathrm{foll}}_{e}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{e}[k(f,X,Y)\,|\,X_{O}=x_{O}]

We now consider a solution

(c_{j})_{j\in\CH(E)}
maximizing the individual follower’s objective

\mathbb{E}_{(c_{j})_{j\in\CH(E)}}[k(f,(X_{O},X_{\CH(E)},X_{\mathrm{rest}}),Y)\,|\,X_{O}=x_{O}],

(2)

where the leftmost subscript indicates that the
X_{\CH(E)}
are intervened on and set to
c_{j}
,
j\in\CH(E)
.
The optimal constants may vary with
x_{O}
, so there are functions

(g_{j})_{j\in\CH(E)}

such that for all

, they maximize

\mathbb{E}_{(g_{j})_{j\in\CH(E)}}[k(f,(X_{O},X_{\CH(E)},X_{\mathrm{rest}}),Y)\,|\,X_{O}=x_{O}]
;
here the subscript indicates that the structural assignments of
X_{\CH(E)}
are
set to the

g_{j}
’s.
Thus, the same
(g_{j})_{j\in\CH(E)}
maximize

\mathbb{E}_{(g_{j})_{j\in\CH(E)}}[k(f,(X_{O},X_{\CH(E)},X_{\mathrm{rest}}),Y)],

that is, they solve the problem of a population follower with

A_{j}\coloneqq O\setminus\PA(j)

Thus, taking the average over many individual followers optimizing (
2

from the perspective of the leader and the game’s value, the same effect as considering a single population follower in an augmented graph.

3.1
Strategies based on causal parents

Using predictors
f
based on the causal parents of
Y

is common in causal strategic prediction
(
Yan et al. 2023
)

and has the following attractive property:
If the follower’s utility depends on the intervention only through the
prediction
f(X)
, then a parent-based predictor can be affected only
by interventions changing the distribution of
X_{\PA(Y)}
.
‘Gaming’
in the sense of manipulating descendants of
Y
to change the prediction of
f
without inducing a change in
Y
is therefore impossible.
Restricting
f
to
X_{\PA(Y)}

can thus incentivize followers to genuinely improve their outcomes.

Predictors based on
\PA(Y)
further satisfy a simple robustness guarantee. Since
Y\notin\CH(E)
, the conditional distribution
Y\,|\,X_{\PA(Y)}
is the same in all
e\in\mathcal{E}
. We refer to such subsets as invariant (see Def.
1
below). Thus,

f_{\PA(Y)}(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[Y\,|\,X_{\PA(Y)}=x_{\PA(Y)}]

is environment-independent. Hence, if we restrict
f
to the parents, the follower can induce a covariate shift in
X_{\PA(Y)}
; the optimal regression function using
X_{\PA(Y)}
does not change.
In the special additive-noise case
Y=g(X_{\PA(Y)})+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-9.0mu\perp{}$}X_{\PA(Y)}
and
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0
, the follower’s interventions are inconsequential for parent-based prediction under the squared loss: for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
,
R_{e}(f_{\PA(Y)})=\Var(\varepsilon)
. Without this additive-noise structure, the risk may still vary across environments through the marginal distribution of
X_{\PA(Y)}
, but the variation is only due to covariate shift under a fixed conditional
Y\,|\,X_{\PA(Y)}
.

The above arguments do not apply exclusively
to
X_{\PA(Y)}
. Similar reasoning
extends to other
invariant subsets that
contain
children of
Y
that the follower cannot manipulate. We now
define invariant sets and
show that there is an invariant set, the stable blanket, that improves upon the parents.

Theorem 3

(Stable blanket vs. parents)

Suppose

(ii)

3.3
The same stable blanket for training and deployment

The previous results use the DAG
\mathcal{G}
, which extends the training-time DAG
\mathcal{G}_{0}
by including edges
X_{k}\to X_{j}
for
j\in\CH(E)
and
k\in A_{j}
that encode interventions by followers.
If the leader only knows
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, how can they compute
\mathrm{SB}(Y)=\mathrm{SB}_{\mathcal{G}}(Y)
?
Would they need to anticipate which edges strategic followers add once
f
is deployed?
The next lemma shows that this is not necessary:
under weak assumptions on the
A_{j}
’s
the stable blanket is the same in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
and
\mathcal{G}

\mathrm{SB}(Y)
can be read off from the training-stage DAG
\mathcal{G}_{0}
alone.

Lemma 4
.

Assume that for all
j\in\CH_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(E)
,
A_{j}\subseteq\mathrm{DE}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(Y)_{\mathrm{forb}}^{C}\setminus\{Y,E\}
, and that
\mathcal{G}
is acyclic. Then
\mathrm{DE}_{\mathcal{G}}(Y)_{\mathrm{forb}}=\mathrm{DE}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(Y)_{\mathrm{forb}}
and
\mathrm{SB}_{\mathcal{G}}(Y)=\mathrm{SB}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(Y)
. In particular,
Y\mathrel{\perp_{\mathcal{G}}}E\,|\,\mathrm{SB}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}}(Y)
.

Intuitively, the restriction on
A_{j}
is a timing and observability condition. The follower may condition the intervention on pre-intervention variables,
but neither on
Y

4
Distribution generalization and an upper bound

Section

3
shows that no
\sigma(X_{\DE(Y)_{\mathrm{forb}}^{C}})

f_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}
in any environment. For specific choices of

f_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}
is a better choice than
f_{\PA(Y)}
for the leader in the prediction-intervention game (

V^{\mathrm{foll}}

V^{\mathrm{foll}}
and it allows for easier optimization than the game objective.
For all

\sup_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{*}(f)}R_{e}(f)\leq\sup_{e\in\mathcal{E}}R_{e}(f).

(4)

The bound is attained when the follower selects environments to maximize

when the objectives are different. Minimizing the right-hand side is a

(5)

This differs from the standard DG objective

Arjovsky et al. 2019

Christiansen et al. 2022
)
in the uncertainty set:
in our work,

Still, the bound (

Our new results also hold under the augmentation construction in
Section

2

We formulate our results using the former notation.

5
Learning subset-based predictors

When the causal graph is known, the stable blanket
\mathrm{SB}(Y)
is identified and
f_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}
can be estimated by pooling data across training environments.
In practice, the causal graph is often unknown, and the stable blanket must be estimated using finitely many data from
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tr}}
. To do so, we can build on existing tests and methods (extending some of them to classification). To learn a predictor for
Y
, we search for subsets
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
that are both invariant on
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tr}}
and predictive of
Y
. Throughout, let
\hat{f}_{S}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
estimate
f_{S}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y\,|\,X_{S}=x_{S}]
, trained on data pooled across
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tr}}
; any base model (such as random forests or neural networks) can be used.
There are various ways of testing invariance, i.e.,

H_{0,S}\colon Y\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-9.0mu\perp{}$}E\,|\,X_{S}
, which we detail
in
Section

C.1
. We take the resulting

p
-value as an invariance score
s_{\mathrm{inv}}(S)
, yielding

\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tr}}}\coloneq\{S\,|\,s_{\mathrm{inv}}(S)\geq\alpha_{\mathrm{inv}}\}
for a tuning parameter

\alpha_{\mathrm{inv}}\in(0,1)
.
We perform an optional screening
step (e.g.,
\ell_{1}
-penalization). We denote by
s_{\mathrm{pred}}(S)
a prediction score of
\hat{f}_{S}
, such as the out-of-sample negative MSE for regression or the out-of-sample negative binary cross-entropy for classification on the training environments.

Invariant most-predictive subsets.

Given
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tr}}}
, one option is to predict using the invariant subset with the highest prediction score, that is,
\hat{S}_{\mathrm{max}}\coloneq\argmax_{S\in\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tr}}}}s_{\mathrm{pred}}(S)

(
Rojas-Carulla et al. 2018
)
.
We call this approach IMP (invariant most-predictive)
(
Gnecco et al. 2026
)
.

Stabilized regression and classification.

Since IMP is based
on a single subset,
errors in the invariance test may have a large impact on IMP’s performance.
Stabilized regression
(
Pfister et al. 2021
)
addresses this by using an ensemble

\hat{f}^{\mathrm{SR}}(x)\coloneq\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}}\hat{\omega}_{S}\hat{f}_{S}(x)
,
where the
non-negative weights sum to one.
Non-zero weights are assigned only to subsets that are both invariant and sufficiently predictive, with the predictiveness threshold calibrated by a bootstrap. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to port this idea to classification by replacing the base regressors, the prediction score (cross-entropy instead of MSE), and the invariance tests with their classification counterparts, yielding stabilized classification (SC). Details on SC are given in
Section

C.2
.

7
Discussion and future work

We have introduced prediction-intervention games, connected them to
invariant sets and worst-case distribution generalization, and proved
optimality results for strategies based on the stable blanket. We proposed learning methods that the leader may use for settings in which the causal graph is known and settings in which it is not, and have successfully tested them on data.
One limitation of our results is that they
rely on the assumption of strong interventions;
analyzing optimal strategies under weaker or
more general conditions is an interesting direction for future research.
We hypothesize that solving prediction-intervention games is in
general a hard problem
(
Conitzer and Sandholm 2006
, e.g.,)
.
Extending the follower model also seems worthwhile, for example by
adding constraints on the allowed interventions or
considering a population of heterogeneous followers with different
objectives.
Finally, in practice, the leader uses a finite-sample estimate
\hat{f}_{S}
rather than
the population predictor
f_{S}
, and the follower can shift covariates to high-error regions
(
Section

E.3
); a finite-sample analysis is left for future work.

Appendix A
Extended related work

We expand on the related work discussion from
Section

1
.

Strategic classification and performative prediction.

Strategic classification
[
Brückner and Scheffer 2011
,
Hardt et al. 2016
]
models followers who respond to a deployed classifier
f
by modifying their features with some cost
c
to obtain a more favorable prediction, typically through the pointwise best response
x^{f}\in\argmax_{x^{\prime}}f(x^{\prime})-c(x,x^{\prime})
. The leader’s goal is to learn an
f
that is robust under the induced distribution shift. The leader’s and follower’s objectives are typically asymmetric in this Stackelberg formulation;
Ehrenberg et al. 2025
make this explicit and study a game in which the leader minimizes a worst-case loss over a set of different utility-maximizing opponents, without a causal structure over
X
and
Y
. A related question is how the choice of predictor creates different incentives for the followers and distinguishes gaming the system
from genuine improvement:

Kleinberg and Raghavan 2019
model followers as investing effort into different actions that affect the observed features, and ask which decision rules incentivize followers to invest in actions a leader views as valuable rather than in gaming;
Chen et al. 2021
distinguish improvable, manipulable, and immutable features, with gaming defined as changes to manipulable features that by assumption do not affect
Y
. They do not specify a full SCM over
(X,Y,E)
that determines how the follower’s actions propagate through the whole system. Performative prediction
[
Perdomo et al. 2020
,
Hardt and Mendler-Dünner 2025
]
considers the broader phenomenon that deployed models shift the data distribution;
Mendler-Dünner et al. 2022
address identifiability of the causal effect of predictions. These works do not generally specify the follower’s moves through an SCM.
Other game-theoretic interactions between predictors and strategic agents have been studied in market settings
[
Hu et al. 2023
]
.

Causal Stackelberg formulations.

Several works model Stackelberg games with SCMs.

Shavit et al. 2020
formulate strategic linear regression in a linear SCM, and learn linear predictors targeting one of three specific objectives: minimizing the leader’s risk under strategic follower responses, incentivizing followers to genuinely improve their outcomes, or estimating the parameters of the outcome model. Their algorithms deploy multiple predictors and observe the resulting follower responses to learn the final predictor. In contrast, our predictor is defined without observing follower interventions. We further allow a general SCM, cover regression and classification under any loss strictly consistent for the mean functional, and place no specific functional form on the follower’s response: their setup fixes it through an effort conversion matrix and a quadratic cost, whereas ours constrains the follower only through the graph
\mathcal{G}
and the action sets
A_{j}
.

Miller et al. 2020
develop a causal framework that distinguishes gaming from improvement and prove that designing classifiers which incentivize improvement is at least as hard as orienting the edges of the underlying causal graph. Their contribution is conceptual and concerns the difficulty of incentive design, while we focus on the leader’s optimal choice of
f
given an SCM.

Yan et al. 2023
assume an unknown causal graph and follower manipulations constrained by a fixed budget. Their algorithm iteratively deploys predictors and observes strategic responses to recover the graph, and their leader objective trades off prediction accuracy with outcome improvement. Our predictor does not require iterated deployment, and in the unknown-graph case (
Section

5
) we use multiple training environments instead of observed strategic responses.

Horowitz and Rosenfeld 2023
partition covariates into causal and non-causal predictive components. Their follower is an individual who modifies covariates to change the prediction in its favor, and only changes to causal variables propagate to
Y
.
We, instead, allow for a more flexible model of the follower: we may specify which variables the follower may intervene on, and which other variables their interventions may depend on. The latter can induce additional edges in the graph, so our setup accommodates settings in which the deployment graph extends the training graph. Unlike the work by
Horowitz and Rosenfeld 2023
, we do not estimate the causal function of the response but work with invariant sets; furthermore, we admit regression in addition to classification, allow for more general losses
and for more general follower utilities; finally,
Horowitz and Rosenfeld 2023
do not provide optimality results in their setting.

Kulynych 2022
extends an SCM over
(X,Y)
by including the deployed predictor as an additional node

, with edges
M\to X
but no edge to
Y
. Instead of modeling a follower optimizing its own utility, the deployment-induced shift in

is encoded directly in the structural assignments of the SCM. In this setting, they study conditions under which the Markov-blanket predictor remains optimal under the shift it induces. In our formulation, the post-deployment distribution instead arises from a follower choosing interventions to maximize an objective distinct from the leader’s risk, which the leader anticipates when choosing
f
.

Causal distribution generalization and invariant subsets.

Invariance-based causal prediction
[
Peters et al. 2016
,
Magliacane et al. 2018
,
Christiansen et al. 2022
]
uses subsets
S
for which
Y\,|\,X_{S}
is stable across environments.
Pfister et al. 2021
introduce the stable blanket,
which we use in several places in the paper.
For regression, invariant subsets have been motivated by worst-case performance:
Rojas-Carulla et al. 2018
show that for each invariant subset
S
,
f_{S}
minimizes the worst-case squared loss over the family
\mathcal{Q}_{S}
of distributions sharing the conditional
Y\,|\,X_{S}
. With Prop.
12

in
Section

B.4
, we generalize this result to binary classification and regression with any loss strictly consistent for the mean functional.
This result is not directly applicable to the optimization problem (
5
), as
the family
\mathcal{Q}_{S}
depends on
S
and may strictly contain
\{\mathbb{P}_{e}\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}}
, in which case
f_{S}
guards against shifts that cannot occur under
\mathcal{E}
. The result
does not inform us about which invariant subset to use, either.
We instead analyze worst-case optimality directly over
\{\mathbb{P}_{e}\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}}
and give conditions under which the stable blanket is optimal among all measurable predictors.
Our worst-case analysis is related to a line of work taking a distributional-robustness view in which the uncertainty set comes from SCM interventions
[
Meinshausen 2018
,
Bühlmann 2020
,
Subbaswamy et al. 2022
,
Shen et al. 2026
]
; this connects to the broader DRO literature
[
Kuhn et al. 2025
,
Sagawa et al. 2020
,
Freni et al. 2025
]
.
Through the bound (
4
), prediction-intervention games provide a concrete example of such a worst-case problem with the distribution family
\{\mathbb{P}_{e}\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}}
determined by the SCM. These works do not directly apply to our setting, however: their uncertainty sets (interventions of a fixed form on a fixed DAG or balls around a reference distribution in the case of DRO) generally do not coincide with
\{\mathbb{P}_{e}\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}}
. Anchor regression
[
Rothenhäusler et al. 2021
]
attains worst-case optimality over a class of bounded shift interventions. Other function-view criteria include IRM
[
Arjovsky et al. 2019
]
and instrument-based independent residuals
[
Saengkyongam et al. 2022
]
. We do not use a function view but a subset view, as discussed in
Section

B.2
.

The following lemma serves as a generalization of the orthogonality of the conditional expectation (used for regression (

) with the MSE) to regression and binary classification with loss functions strictly consistent for the mean functional. It is used for several results.

Lemma 13
.

Let
Y
and
W
be random variables on a probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, with
Y
taking values in
\mathcal{Y}

and integrable.
Let
Z
be a
\sigma(W)
-measurable random variable
taking values in
\mathcal{A}
. If
\ell
is strictly consistent for the mean
functional, then

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\ell(Y,Z)\bigr]\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\ell\bigl(Y,\mathbb{E}[Y\,|\,W]\bigr)\bigr],

where
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes expectation under
\mathbb{P}
.
If both expectations are finite, equality holds if and only if

13
.

For all
q\in\mathcal{A}
and probability distributions

on
\mathcal{Y}
with finite mean
\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}[Y]
, define

L(F,q)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}[\ell(Y,q)].

Let
F_{W}
denote the conditional distribution of
Y
given
W

[
Kallenberg 2021
, Thm. 8.5]
. Its mean equals
\mathbb{E}[Y\,|\,W]
almost surely. Conditional on

acts as a constant since

\sigma(W)

\mathbb{E}[\ell(Y,Z)\,|\,W]=L(F_{W},Z).

By strict consistency of

Since this holds for every

Thus, almost surely,

are not descendants of
i
, none of them depend
on
\varepsilon_{i}
:

\varepsilon_{i}\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-9.0mu\perp{}$}(Y,X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)},E).

(12)

Under
\mathbb{P}_{e_{0}}
, the distribution of

Y\,|\,X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}=x_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}
is Gaussian with mean
m(x_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)})
and
constant variance
\sigma^{2}
. Since
\mathrm{SB}(Y)
is invariant, it holds in every environment that, almost surely,

Y\,|\,X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}=x_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(m(x_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}),\sigma^{2}\bigr).

Since the SCM on
\bar{\mathcal{G}}
has independent Gaussian noises with positive variances, the
covariance matrix of
(Y,X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)})
under
e_{0}
is positive definite. In particular
\sigma^{2}>0
, since
\sigma^{2}=0
would imply that
Y
is an affine
function of
X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}
almost surely, contradicting the positive definiteness.

The conditional distribution of
(Y,\bar{X}_{i})
given
X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}
is
determined by

Y\,|\,X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}\sim\mathcal{N}(m,\sigma^{2})
and

\bar{X}_{i}\,|\,Y\sim\mathcal{N}(b_{i,Y}Y,1)
, both
environment-independent by invariance of
\mathrm{SB}(Y)
and (
12
), respectively.
Hence
Y\,|\,(X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)},\bar{X}_{i})
does not depend on
e
(because of joint Gaussianity), and the following
predictor is well-defined independently of the environment:

f(x)\coloneq\mathbb{E}[Y\,|\,X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}=x_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)},\bar{X}_{i}=\bar{x}_{i}].

Since
(Y,\bar{X}_{i})
is jointly Gaussian conditionally on
X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}
,

f(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y\,|\,X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)},\bar{X}_{i}]=f_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}(X)+\frac{b_{i,Y}\sigma^{2}}{b_{i,Y}^{2}\sigma^{2}+1}\bigl(\bar{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}_{i}\,|\,X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}]\bigr),

where
b_{i,Y}\sigma^{2}/(b_{i,Y}^{2}\sigma^{2}+1)
is nonzero. Using (
12
), the residual
\bar{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}_{i}\,|\,X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}]
has variance at least
\Var(\varepsilon_{i})=1
. Hence,
f(X)\neq f_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}(X)
with positive probability under
every
\mathbb{P}_{e}
. Applying Lem.
13
under
\mathbb{P}_{e}
with

W=(X_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)},\bar{X}_{i})
and
Z=f_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)}(X)
yields

R_{e}(f_{\mathrm{SB}(Y)})>R_{e}(f)
for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
(thus, (iii) holds).
∎

D.8
Proof of Prop.
12

Proof of Prop.
12
.

Write
x=(x_{S},x_{N})
with
x_{S}\in\mathcal{X}_{S}
and

x_{N}\in\mathcal{X}_{N}
. Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
be
an arbitrary measurable predictor and
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{S}
. We construct a distribution
\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{S}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
by defining it as the product measure

\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}\coloneq\mathbb{Q}_{(X_{S},Y)}\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{X_{N}},

where
\mathbb{Q}_{(X_{S},Y)}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{X_{N}}
denote the marginal distributions
of
(X_{S},Y)
and
X_{N}
under
\mathbb{Q}
, respectively. We verify

\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{S}
:

(i)

The marginal distribution of
(X_{S},Y)
under
\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}
equals
\mathbb{Q}_{(X_{S},Y)}
,
so
\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}}[Y\,|\,X_{S}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[Y\,|\,X_{S}]=f_{S}(X_{S})
almost surely.

(ii)

Since
\mathbb{Q}_{X}\ll\mu
and
\mu=\mu_{S}\otimes\mu_{N}
, we have

\mathbb{Q}_{X_{S}}\ll\mu_{S}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{X_{N}}\ll\mu_{N}
, hence

\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{X}=\mathbb{Q}_{X_{S}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}_{X_{N}}\ll\mu
.

By Tonelli’s theorem,

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}}[\ell(Y,f(X))]=\int_{\mathcal{X}_{N}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}_{S}\times\mathcal{Y}}\ell(y,f(x_{S},x_{N}))\,d\mathbb{Q}_{(X_{S},Y)}(x_{S},y)\right)d\mathbb{Q}_{X_{N}}(x_{N}).

For each fixed
x_{N}\in\mathcal{X}_{N}
, define
g_{x_{N}}:\mathcal{X}_{S}\to\mathcal{A}
by
g_{x_{N}}(z)\coloneq f(z,x_{N})
. The inner integral can be written as

\int_{\mathcal{X}_{S}\times\mathcal{Y}}\ell(y,f(x_{S},x_{N}))\,d\mathbb{Q}_{(X_{S},Y)}(x_{S},y)=\mathbb{E}_{(X_{S},Y)\sim\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\ell\big(Y,g_{x_{N}}(X_{S})\big)\Big].

For every fixed
x_{N}
,
g_{x_{N}}(X_{S})
is
\sigma(X_{S})
-measurable. Applying
Lem.
13
under
\mathbb{Q}
with
W=X_{S}
and

Z=g_{x_{N}}(X_{S})
, and using Condition (i),

\mathbb{E}_{(X_{S},Y)\sim\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\ell\big(Y,\,g_{x_{N}}(X_{S})\big)\Big]\geq\mathbb{E}_{(X_{S},Y)\sim\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\ell\big(Y,\,f_{S}(X_{S})\big)\Big].

The right-hand side does not depend on
x_{N}
. Integrating over
x_{N}
yields

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}}\big[\ell(Y,f(X))\big]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\ell(Y,f_{S}(X_{S}))\big].

Since
\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{S}
,

\sup_{\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}_{S}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\prime}}\big[\ell(Y,f(X))\big]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}}\big[\ell(Y,f(X))\big]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\ell(Y,f_{S}(X_{S}))\big].

As
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{S}
was arbitrary, the right-hand side can be
replaced by its supremum over
\mathcal{Q}_{S}
. Since

f
was arbitrary,
f_{S}
minimizes the worst-case risk.
∎

E.2
Leader’s and follower’s optimization

For each subset
S\in\{\PA(Y),\mathrm{SB}(Y),\{1,\ldots,5\}\}
, the leader trains a
predictor
f_{S}
. In the linear-Gaussian SCM, we use the closed-form population
linear regression function
\mathbb{E}[Y\,|\,X_{S}]
. In the nonlinear SCM, we fit
an MLP regressor on the leader’s training data
and evaluate it after the follower’s
intervention.

The follower’s intervention is parameterized by neural networks for

E_{\delta_{1}}
and
E_{\delta_{4}}
, with a final
\tanh
layer enforcing the intervention
bound. We consider two follower objectives:

MSE:

\displaystyle\max_{E_{\delta_{1}},E_{\delta_{4}}}\;\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-f_{S}(X_{S}))^{2}\big],

prediction:

\displaystyle\min_{E_{\delta_{1}},E_{\delta_{4}}}\;\mathbb{E}\big[f_{S}(X_{S})\big].

After optimizing the follower objective, we always report the leader’s
deployment MSE

\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-f_{S}(X_{S}))^{2}\big]

under the induced intervention distribution.

E.3
Finite-sample regression error

The theory (for example,
Theorem

3
and
Theorem

5
)
considers
population predictors such as

f_{S}(x_{S})=\mathbb{E}[Y\,|\,X_{S}=x_{S}]
. For an invariant set
S
, this conditional
mean is the same in all environments. In the finite-sample nonlinear experiment (see
Section

E.1
),
however, the leader deploys an estimate

\hat{f}_{S}=f_{S}+\Delta_{S},

where
\Delta_{S}
is the regression error. Under squared loss,

\displaystyle R_{e}(\hat{f}_{S})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{e}\big[(Y-\hat{f}_{S}(X_{S}))^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=R_{e}(f_{S})+\mathbb{E}_{e}\big[\Delta_{S}(X_{S})^{2}\big],

where the cross term vanishes because
f_{S}=\mathbb{E}_{e}[Y\,|\,X_{S}]
and by the law of iterated expectations.
Thus, even if the population predictor is invariant, the finite-sample risk can
change across interventions because the follower changes the marginal
distribution of
X_{S}
. In particular, the follower can move probability mass
toward regions where
|\Delta_{S}|
is large. As the leader’s training sample size grows,

\Delta_{S}
decreases, so this additional instability becomes smaller for invariant
sets such as
\PA(Y)
and
\mathrm{SB}(Y)
.
This mechanism does not explain away the instability of the all-variable
predictor. The all-variable set contains
X_{4}
and
X_{5}
, which lie in the
descendant region of the intervened child of
Y
. Consequently, the relevant
conditional relation may change under the follower’s intervention, and therefore an increase
in the leader’s training sample size does not necessarily make the all-variable predictor more stable:

fig.

5
confirms this behavior. In the train-size
sweep, the deployment MSE of the parent and stable-blanket predictors becomes
flatter as the leader receives more data, whereas the all-variable predictor
remains sensitive to the intervention bound. The same figure also shows that
allowing the
X_{4}
perturbation to depend additionally on
X_{1}
and
X_{3}
leaves the
qualitative comparison unchanged.

F.5
Implementation details (follower)

For a given deployed predictor
\hat{f}
, the follower picks one environment from a fixed candidate set
\mathcal{E}
of
49
environments. We denote this choice by
e^{*}(\hat{f})
to emphasize its dependence on
\hat{f}
; different predictors can lead to different choices. Each environment is specified by a coefficient tuple
(\beta^{\mathrm{led}}_{0},\beta^{\mathrm{led}}_{1},\beta^{\mathrm{pol}}_{0},\beta^{\mathrm{pol}}_{1})
, as in
Section

F.2
. The reference environment
e_{\mathrm{ref}}
has coefficients
(0,12,0,30)
; the magnitudes of its slope coefficients lie between those of the training environments
E=1
and
E=2
(Tab.
2
). The remaining
48
environments form a
6\times 8
grid combining six intensity scales with eight non-trivial sign patterns for the (LED, polarizer) pair: each component takes one of three patterns: switched off, the training pattern (active for
Y=1
), or reversed (active for
Y=0
).

We order the
49
environments by their normalized
\ell_{\infty}
distance to
e_{\mathrm{ref}}
in the slope coefficients
(\beta^{\mathrm{led}}_{1},\beta^{\mathrm{pol}}_{1})
(each component normalized by
25
and
60
, respectively; full definition in the released code). For an intervention bound
b\in[0,1]
, the choice set
\mathcal{E}_{b}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
consists of the
\lceil 49b\rceil
environments closest to
e_{\mathrm{ref}}
; in particular, we set
\mathcal{E}_{0}\coloneq\{e_{\mathrm{ref}}\}
.

For each candidate environment
e\in\mathcal{E}_{b}
, we draw
500
observations of
X
from the chamber under
e
. The follower estimates the population mean prediction
\mathbb{E}_{e}[\hat{f}(X)]
from these observations and selects the environment
e^{*}(\hat{f})\in\mathcal{E}_{b}
minimizing this estimate.

F.6
Evaluation

After the follower’s selection, we draw an independent set of
500
observations of
X
from
e^{*}(\hat{f})
. For each predictor
\hat{f}
and each value of
b\in\{0,0.05,0.1,0.25,0.5\}
, we report on this evaluation set two quantities: (i) the shift in the population mean prediction relative to the reference,
\mathbb{E}_{e^{*}(\hat{f})}[\hat{f}(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{e_{\mathrm{ref}}}[\hat{f}(X)]
, and (ii) the leader’s MSE under the chosen environment,
R_{e^{*}(\hat{f})}(\hat{f})
. Both are plotted against
b
in
Figure

4
; for reference, the right panel also shows the leader’s MSE under
e_{\mathrm{ref}}
as a dotted horizontal line for each predictor.
