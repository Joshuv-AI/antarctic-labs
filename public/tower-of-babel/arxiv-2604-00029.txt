Spatio-Temporal Uncertainty-Modulated Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Solving Hyperbolic Conservation Laws with Strong Shocks
Darui Zhao, Ze Tao, Fujun Liu

Abstract
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) frequently encounter difficulties in accurately resolving shock waves within high-speed compressible flows, a failure largely attributed to the ”gradient pathology” arising from extreme stiffness at discontinuities. To overcome this limitation, we propose the Spatio-Temporal Uncertainty-Modulated PINN (UM-PINN), a probabilistic framework that reinterprets the training process as a multi-task learning problem governed by homoscedastic aleatoric uncertainty. By integrating a gradient-based spatial mask with learnable variance parameters, our method dynamically balances the conflicting contributions of Partial Differential Equation (PDE) residuals and initial conditions across the spatiotemporal domain, further stabilized by Quasi-Monte Carlo Sobol sampling. We validate the framework against challenging benchmarks, including the one-dimensional (1D) Sod shock tube, the high-frequency Shu-Osher problem, and the complex two-dimensional (2D) Riemann interaction, where standard gradient-based weighting schemes typically fail. Experimental results demonstrate that UM-PINN achieves orders of magnitude improvement in accuracy and shock resolution compared to baseline methods, establishing a robust new paradigm for mesh-free Computational Fluid Dynamics in hyperbolic systems.

Introduction

Hyperbolic conservation laws, particularly the Euler equations, serve as the foundational mathematical models for simulating supersonic aerodynamics, explosion mechanics, and astrophysical phenomena.
While accurately resolving shock waves and contact discontinuities is crucial for applications ranging from aircraft design to large-scale cosmic evolution simulations, capturing these discontinuous solutions remains a central challenge in computational fluid dynamics (CFD).
These physical phenomena are characterized by extremely high spatial gradients and strong nonlinear coupling.
Consequently, traditional grid-based methods frequently encounter significant computational costs when addressing high-dimensional problems or complex boundaries
[
14
,
23
,
24
,
8
]
.
In recent years, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
[
19
,
22
,
15
,
5
]
have emerged as a promising mesh-free paradigm driven by the rise of deep learning.
Researchers have leveraged the nonlinear fitting capabilities of neural networks to embed physical equations directly into loss functions via automatic differentiation (AD).
This approach has yielded significant progress in solving both forward simulations and inverse parameter inversion problems
[
1
,
16
,
20
,
9
]
.
Despite these advancements, existing PINN frameworks still face limitations when dealing with strong shock waves.
A primary obstacle is the phenomenon known as ”gradient pathology,” where a severe magnitude imbalance exists between the partial differential equation (PDE) residual term and the initial condition (IC) term.
As a result, standard optimization algorithms often become trapped in local optima, causing excessive smoothing of shock profiles or non-physical Gibbs oscillations
[
4
,
3
,
13
,
6
]
.
Such limitations challenge the reliability of PINNs for industrial-grade hypersonic flow simulations.
To improve robustness, our research incorporates ”homoscedastic aleatoric uncertainty” to dynamically adjust the loss weights across different tasks.
The core of this strategy involves treating the loss function weights as learnable noise variances within the model rather than fixed hyperparameters.
This adaptive mechanism offers a viable path to resolve the trade-off between physical constraints and boundary conditions(BCs).
Furthermore, it is expected to fundamentally alleviate the gradient conflicts that arise between distinct loss terms
[
17
,
11
,
18
,
7
]
.
Based on this concept, we develop the Uncertainty-Modulated PINN (UM-PINN) framework to effectively address the shortcomings of current methods regarding shock wave capturing.
By deriving a physics-informed loss function based on maximum likelihood estimation, the network autonomously regulates the gradient flow according to the training state.
We further augment this approach with Sobol Sequence quasi-Monte Carlo sampling to improve search efficiency.
Experimental results demonstrate that UM-PINN accurately captures sharp shock wave fronts with high fidelity.
Moreover, the proposed method surpasses traditional PINN models in terms of computational accuracy and convergence speed without requiring manual weight tuning.
These findings validate the utility of probabilistic modeling in scientific machine learning and suggest a new technical route for the intelligent simulation of complex compressible fluids.

2
Mathematical Framework and Methodology

We consider the two-dimensional (2D) Euler equations describing the motion of an inviscid, compressible fluid.
In conservative form, the system represents the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{U}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U})}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{U})}{\partial y}=0,

(1)

where
\mathbf{U}
is the vector of conserved variables, and
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U})
and
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{U})
are the flux vectors in the
x
- and
y
-directions, respectively.
Their mathematical expressions are defined as follows:

\mathbf{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u\\
\rho v\\
E\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U})=\begin{bmatrix}\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+p\\
\rho uv\\
(E+p)u\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{U})=\begin{bmatrix}\rho v\\
\rho uv\\
\rho v^{2}+p\\
(E+p)v\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

where
\rho
denotes the fluid density,
u
and
v
are the velocity components in the
x
- and
y
-directions,
p
is the static pressure, and
E
is the total energy per unit volume.
To close the system mathematically, an equation of state(EOS) is required to establish the relationship between pressure
p
and the other conserved variables.
For an ideal gas, the thermodynamic relation follows:

E=\frac{p}{\gamma-1}+\frac{1}{2}\rho(u^{2}+v^{2}).

(3)

Consequently, the formula for pressure is given by:

p=(\gamma-1)\left(E-\frac{1}{2}\rho(u^{2}+v^{2})\right),

(4)

where
\gamma
is the ratio of specific heats.
In the numerical experiments of this work, we set
\gamma=1.4
for the Sod and Shu-Osher problems, while for the 2D Riemann problem, the constant is selected according to the specific configuration.
For the one-dimensional cases involved in this study (e.g., Sod Shock Tube and Shu-Osher Problem), the physical fields exhibit translational invariance in the
y
-direction, implying
\partial/\partial y=0
and
v=0
.
Under these conditions, Equation. (
1
) degenerates to the one-dimensional (1D) conservative form:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{U}_{1D}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U}_{1D})}{\partial x}=0.

(5)

The corresponding variables are simplified to:

\mathbf{U}_{1D}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u\\
E\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U}_{1D})=\begin{bmatrix}\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+p\\
(E+p)u\end{bmatrix}.

(6)

In the PINN framework, we employ a deep neural network
f_{\theta}
to approximate the primitive variables.
This mapping can be represented as:

\hat{\mathbf{y}}(t,x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\rho}\\
\hat{u}\\
\hat{v}\\
\hat{p}\end{bmatrix}=f_{\theta}(t,x,y).

(7)

Leveraging Automatic Differentiation (AD), we can precisely compute the derivatives of the network outputs with respect to the input coordinates.
The physical residuals
\mathcal{R}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rho}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}+\partial_{x}(\hat{\rho}\hat{u})+\partial_{y}(\hat{\rho}\hat{v})

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rho u}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{t}(\hat{\rho}\hat{u})+\partial_{x}(\hat{\rho}\hat{u}^{2}+\hat{p})+\partial_{y}(\hat{\rho}\hat{u}\hat{v})

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rho v}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{t}(\hat{\rho}\hat{v})+\partial_{x}(\hat{\rho}\hat{u}\hat{v})+\partial_{y}(\hat{\rho}\hat{v}^{2}+\hat{p})

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{E}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{t}\hat{E}+\partial_{x}((\hat{E}+\hat{p})\hat{u})+\partial_{y}((\hat{E}+\hat{p})\hat{v})

where
\hat{E}
is computed from the predicted primitive variables using the EOS.To specifically handle the extreme gradient fluctuations near shock waves, we introduce a
Spatial Modulation
mechanism.
Inspired by the concept of flux limiters in traditional CFD, we define the modulated physical residuals
\hat{R}
as:

\hat{R}=\frac{1}{1+\alpha\left|\nabla\hat{U}\right|^{\beta}}\odot R

(9)

where
\left|\nabla\hat{U}\right|
denotes the norm of the gradient of predicted conserved variables, and
\alpha,\beta
are hyperparameters that control the degree of attenuation.
This mechanism prevents the optimization from being dominated by localized high-gradient spikes at discontinuities.
Mathematically, at shock locations where
|\nabla\hat{U}|\gg 1
, the modulation factor
\frac{1}{1+\alpha|\nabla\hat{U}|^{\beta}}\to 0
, effectively down-weighting the residual contribution from these extreme points.
This ensures a smoother loss landscape and prevents gradient explosion during backpropagation, analogous to how flux limiters suppress oscillations in traditional CFD.
These residual terms are incorporated into the loss function. When the neural network fully satisfies the governing equations, the residual vector
\mathbf{R}=[\mathcal{R}_{\rho},\mathcal{R}_{\rho u},\mathcal{R}_{\rho v},\mathcal{R}_{E}]^{T}
should converge to a zero vector.
Having established the governing equations and residual definitions, we now focus on the core components of the proposed UM-PINN framework.
This includes the Bayesian uncertainty weighting mechanism, the network architecture, the sampling strategy, and the optimization protocol.
In solving hyperbolic conservation laws characterized by shock waves, the gradients of the PDE residual terms at discontinuities are often orders of magnitude higher than those of the initial condition (IC) terms.
This severe imbalance often causes the optimizer to get stuck in local optima, a phenomenon known as “difficultygradient pathology”
[
25
]
.
To address this, we formulate the training of PINNs as a multi-task learning problem under
homoscedastic aleatoric uncertainty
.
From a probabilistic perspective, we postulate that the prediction error for each task
i
(e.g., PDE residuals, ICs, BCs) follows a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and variance
\sigma_{i}^{2}
.
The likelihood function can be expressed as:

p(\mathbf{y}|f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}),\sigma)=\mathcal{N}(f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}),\sigma^{2}),

(10)

where
f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the network output and
\mathbf{y}
represents the target values (typically zero for PDE residuals).
To simultaneously optimize multiple competing physical constraints, we seek to maximize the joint log-likelihood.
For independent tasks, minimizing the negative log-likelihood (NLL) yields the following objective:

\mathcal{L}(\theta,\sigma)=\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\theta)+\log\sigma_{i}\right),

(11)

where
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\theta)
represents the original Mean Squared Error (MSE) for task
i
.
It is important to note that for the PDE task,
L_{PDE}(\theta)
is computed based on the modulated residuals
\hat{R}
rather than the raw residuals
R
.
Specifically:

L_{PDE}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N_{PDE}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{PDE}}\left\|\hat{R}(t_{j},x_{j},y_{j})\right\|^{2}

(12)

This dual-modulation strategy, combining
Spatial Modulation
for localized gradient balancing and
Task Modulation
via
s_{i}
for global term weighting, constitutes the core of the UM-PINN framework.
To improve numerical stability and prevent potential division by zero, we introduce a learnable log-variance parameter
s_{i}:=\log\sigma_{i}^{2}
.
Consequently, the total loss function for UM-PINN is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{total}(\theta,\mathbf{s})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}\left(\frac{1}{2}e^{-s_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\theta)+\frac{1}{2}s_{i}\right),

(13)

where
\mathcal{T}=\{PDE,IC,BC\}
.
The rationale behind this design is twofold. First, the term
e^{-s_{i}}
acts as an adaptive weight for automatic weight adaptation.
When the physical residual
\mathcal{L}_{PDE}
is extremely large (e.g., near shocks), the network increases
s_{i}
to reduce the weight of that term, preventing its gradients from overwhelming the optimization process.
Second, the term
\frac{1}{2}s_{i}
serves as a regularization constraint, penalizing the network to prevent indefinite increases in uncertainty, thus avoiding the trivial solution where weights are set to zero to escape learning.

We employ a fully connected (FC) deep neural network to approximate the mapping
(t,x,y)\to(\rho,u,v,p)
.
An FC network consists of multiple layers where each neuron in layer
l
is connected to every neuron in layer
l+1
.
For a hidden layer with input
\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}
, the output is computed as
\mathbf{h}^{(l)}=\sigma(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}+\mathbf{b}^{(l)})
, where
\mathbf{W}^{(l)}
is the weight matrix,
\mathbf{b}^{(l)}
is the bias vector, and
\sigma
is the activation function.
To ensure the predictions adhere to physical laws, specific constraints are applied at the output layer.
For instance, we apply the
Softplus
activation function to the predicted density
\hat{\rho}
and pressure
\hat{p}
.
The Softplus function is defined as
\text{Softplus}(x)=\ln(1+e^{x})
, which is a smooth approximation to the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) function and guarantees strictly positive outputs, ensuring physical plausibility for thermodynamic quantities like density and pressure.
Furthermore, the hidden layers utilize the
Tanh
activation function, which ensures that the gradient flow remains continuous and non-zero when computing the high-order derivatives required by the Euler equations.
To capture sharp transitions in the spatiotemporal domain more efficiently, we discard uniform random sampling in favor of Sobol Sequences
[
21
]
, a type of Quasi-Monte Carlo method.
The primary advantage of Sobol sequences is their low-discrepancy property.
Compared to pseudo-random sampling, Sobol points are distributed more uniformly across the domain, avoiding clustering.
This allows the model to identify and cover high-gradient regions more rapidly, significantly accelerating convergence near shock fronts.
While UM-PINN leverages Sobol sequences for efficient spatiotemporal sampling, our approach aligns with the growing trend of point-based neural solvers.
Similar to the point-cloud strategy proposed by Kashefi et al.

[
10
]
, our method seeks to exploit the mesh-free nature of neural networks to resolve high-gradient features without the constraints of traditional connectivity.
The network weights
\theta
and the uncertainty parameters
\mathbf{s}
are trained jointly using the Adam optimizer
[
12
]
, with an initial learning rate set to
10^{-3}
.
During the early stages of training, the model automatically identifies the “difficulty” of each loss term.
By dynamically adjusting the values of
\mathbf{s}
, the method reshapes the loss landscape, allowing the optimization algorithm to smoothly navigate through the initial phase of extremely high gradients and ultimately achieve high-fidelity capture of shock fronts.

3
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the performance of the proposed UM-PINN framework by addressing three distinct benchmark problems: the 1D Sod shock tube, the 1D Shu-Osher problem, and the 2D steady-state Riemann problem.
Solving these systems holds significant importance in various physical domains, such as high-energy physics (e.g., modeling heavy-ion collisions) and astrophysics (e.g., investigating gamma-ray bursts or the propagation of jets in active galactic nuclei).
Across these diverse flow regimes, our algorithm demonstrates robust generalization capabilities.
To quantitatively assess the accuracy of our predictions against the “ground truth” (exact analytical solutions or high-resolution numerical references), we employ three evaluation metrics: the Relative
L_{2}
Error (
L_{2}^{rel}
), the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and the Mean Absolute Error (MAE).
Assuming
N
is the total number of evaluation points,
\mathbf{u}_{pred}
is the predicted solution vector, and
\mathbf{u}_{ref}
is the reference solution vector, these metrics are defined as follows:

L_{2}^{rel}=\frac{\left\|\mathbf{u}_{pred}-\mathbf{u}_{ref}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\mathbf{u}_{ref}\right\|_{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(u_{pred}^{(i)}-u_{ref}^{(i)})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(u_{ref}^{(i)})^{2}}},

(14)

RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(u_{pred}^{(i)}-u_{ref}^{(i)}\right)^{2}},

(15)

MAE=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|u_{pred}^{(i)}-u_{ref}^{(i)}\right|.

(16)

All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 5060 Laptop GPU.
This modest hardware setup underscores the high computational efficiency of our proposed algorithm, making it accessible for resource-constrained environments.
The spatial modulation acts as a non-parametric preprocessing step where
\alpha
and
\beta
are set to unit values, requiring no case-specific tuning.
Unless otherwise stated, to demonstrate the robustness of our method, the hyperparameters are kept fixed across all cases: the neural network architecture maintains 4 hidden layers, and the Adam optimizer uses a constant learning rate of
\eta=\mathbf{10^{-3}}
.

To further validate the superiority of UM-PINN, we compare it against

.
While these methods have shown promise in general PDE problems, our experiments reveal their critical limitations when applied to high-speed compressible flows with strong discontinuities.
We tested both LRA and GradNorm on the 1D Sod and Shu-Osher problems using the same network architecture and hyperparameters as UM-PINN.
The results, visualized in Fig.7 and Fig.8, indicate a fundamental failure of gradient-based weighting schemes in hyperbolic systems.
As shown in the top row of Fig.7 for the Shu-Osher case, GradNorm suffers from severe numerical instability.
The density prediction in the middle panel collapses to non-physical values near
10^{-11}
, and the loss curve in the right panel exhibits a catastrophic divergence.
This is likely because GradNorm attempts to balance gradient norms that differ by orders of magnitude at the shock front, leading to an exploding weight for the PDE residual that destabilizes the optimizer.
The LRA method, shown in the bottom row of Fig.7, avoids explosion but falls into a trap of premature stagnation.
The predicted density profile forms a crude step function that completely misses the high-frequency oscillations and even misplaces the shock location.
The loss curve oscillates violently without effective convergence. Similarly, in the Sod problem shown in the top row of Fig.8, LRA produces a solution where the velocity field is almost zero everywhere, indicating a failure to propagate information from the boundaries.

In contrast to the fragility of LRA and GradNorm, UM-PINN achieves stable and accurate convergence on the exact same problems as demonstrated in Section

The key advantage lies in the mechanism of adaptation. LRA and GradNorm rely on the gradients of the loss with respect to parameters.
In shock problems, these gradients are extremely stiff and noisy, causing the weights to fluctuate wildly.
Conversely, UM-PINN relies on the magnitude of the loss itself via homoscedastic uncertainty.
This provides a smoother, more robust signal for weight adjustment.

\sigma

Conclusion

In this study, we addressed the persistent challenge of gradient pathology in Physics-Informed Neural Networks applied to hyperbolic conservation laws by introducing the Uncertainty-Modulated PINN (UM-PINN).
This framework leverages a dual-modulation strategy that combines spatial gradient masking with homoscedastic aleatoric uncertainty to dynamically balance conflicting training objectives without manual intervention.
Our extensive empirical validation on the 1D Sod, Shu-Osher, and 2D Riemann problems demonstrates that UM-PINN significantly outperforms standard baselines and existing adaptive methods like LRA and GradNorm, achieving orders of magnitude improvement in accuracy and robust shock capturing.
The results confirm that incorporating probabilistic uncertainty into the loss function effectively acts as an automatic curriculum learning mechanism, smoothing the optimization landscape for stiff PDE systems.
By eliminating the need for case-specific hyperparameter tuning, UM-PINN establishes a more reliable and automated paradigm for mesh-free Computational Fluid Dynamics.
Future research will focus on extending this framework to three-dimensional complex geometries and non-equilibrium flows, further broadening the applicability of intelligent scientific computing to industrial-scale problems.
Ultimately, this work paves a new path for robustly integrating deep learning with fundamental physical laws to solve the most challenging problems in fluid mechanics.

Appendix A
Numerical Method for 1D Sod Shock Tube Problem

The 1D Sod shock tube problem is validated using the
Exact Riemann Solver
, which provides an analytical solution to the Riemann problem.
This section presents the mathematical derivation and verification of the reference solution.

A.1
Governing Equations

The one-dimensional Euler equations in conservative form are:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{U}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{U})}{\partial x}=0

(A1)

where the conservative variable vector
\mathbf{U}
and flux vector
\mathbf{F}
are:

\mathbf{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u\\
E\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{F}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+p\\
(E+p)u\end{pmatrix}

(A2)

The system is closed by the ideal gas Equation of State (EOS):

E=\frac{p}{\gamma-1}+\frac{1}{2}\rho u^{2}

(A3)

A.2
The Riemann Problem

The Riemann problem consists of finding
the solution to
Eq.

A1
with piecewise constant initial data:

\mathbf{U}(x,0)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{U}_{L}&\text{if }x<x_{0}\\
\mathbf{U}_{R}&\text{if }x>x_{0}\end{cases}

(A4)

For the Sod shock tube, the initial conditions are:

(\rho_{L},u_{L},p_{L})=(1.0,0.0,1.0),\quad(\rho_{R},u_{R},p_{R})=(0.125,0.0,0.1)

(A5)

A.3
Solution Structure

The exact solution consists of three waves emanating from the initial discontinuity:

•

Left wave
: Rarefaction fan (since
p^{*}<p_{L}
)

•

Middle wave
: Contact discontinuity (moving at speed
u^{*}
)

•

Right wave
: Shock wave (since
p^{*}>p_{R}
)

The solution is divided into four constant states separated by these waves:

\mathbf{W}(x,t)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{W}_{L}&\text{(undisturbed left state)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{L}^{*}&\text{(star-left state)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{R}^{*}&\text{(star-right state)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{R}&\text{(undisturbed right state)}\par\end{cases}

(A6)

A.4
Pressure Function and Newton Iteration

The key to solving the Riemann problem is finding the pressure
p^{*}
in the star region.
This is achieved by solving the nonlinear equation:

f(p)=f_{L}(p)+f_{R}(p)+\Delta u=0

(A7)

where
\Delta u=u_{R}-u_{L}
and the functions
f_{L}
and
f_{R}
depend on whether the wave is a shock or rarefaction:

Shock wave
(
p>p_{K}
):

f_{K}(p)=(p-p_{K})\sqrt{\frac{A_{K}}{p+B_{K}}},\quad A_{K}=\frac{2}{(\gamma+1)\rho_{K}},\quad B_{K}=\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}p_{K}

(A8)

Rarefaction wave
(
p\leq p_{K}
):

f_{K}(p)=\frac{2c_{K}}{\gamma-1}\left[\left(\frac{p}{p_{K}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}-1\right]

(A9)

where
c_{K}=\sqrt{\gamma p_{K}/\rho_{K}}
is the sound speed.

Newton-Raphson iteration
:

p^{(k+1)}=p^{(k)}-\frac{f(p^{(k)})}{f^{\prime}(p^{(k)})}

(A10)

Once
p^{*}
is found, the velocity in the star region is:

u^{*}=\frac{1}{2}(u_{L}+u_{R})+\frac{1}{2}\left[f_{R}(p^{*})-f_{L}(p^{*})\right]

(A11)

The star state
(u^{*},p^{*})
is the unique intersection point.

Remark 1

Appendix B
Numerical Method for 1D Shu-Osher Problem

The Shu-Osher problem describes the interaction between a Mach 3 shock wave and a sinusoidal density perturbation, producing complex high-frequency oscillations.

B.1
Problem Setup

Domain
:
(x,t)\in[-5,5]\times[0,1.8]

Initial conditions
:

(\rho,u,p)=\begin{cases}(3.857143,2.629369,10.33333)&\text{if }x<-4\\
(1+0.2\sin(5x),0,1.0)&\text{if }x\geq-4\end{cases}

(A12)

B.2
Finite Volume Method: From Weak Form to Discrete Scheme

Unlike the Sod problem, which uses an exact Riemann solver, the Shu-Osher problem requires a numerical solution due to its complex wave interactions.
We employ the Finite Volume Method (FVM) derived from the
integral (weak) form
of the conservation law.

B.2.1
Integral Form Derivation

Integrating the 1D Euler equations
\frac{\partial\mathbf{U}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}}{\partial x}=0
over a control volume
[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}]
:

\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{U}}{\partial t}dx+\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}}{\partial x}dx=0

(A13)

Applying the divergence theorem to the flux term:

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}\mathbf{U}\,dx=-\left(\mathbf{F}_{i+1/2}-\mathbf{F}_{i-1/2}\right)

(A14)

Defining the cell-averaged conserved variable
\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}\mathbf{U}\,dx
, we obtain the
semi-discrete form
:

\frac{d\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{i}}{dt}=-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i-1/2}\right)

(A15)

where
\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2}
is the
numerical flux
approximating
\mathbf{F}
at the
cell interface.

B.2.2
Rusanov (Local Lax-Friedrichs) Numerical Flux

The Rusanov flux provides a stable upwind-biased approximation:

\begin{split}\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{F}_{i}+\mathbf{F}_{i+1}\right)\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{i+1/2}\left(\mathbf{U}_{i+1}-\mathbf{U}_{i}\right)\end{split}

(A16)

where the
local maximum wave speed
is:

\alpha_{i+1/2}=\max\left(|u_{i}|+c_{i},\,|u_{i+1}|+c_{i+1}\right),\quad c=\sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}

(A17)

Component-wise expansion
: For the 1D Euler system with
\mathbf{U}=(\rho,\rho u,E)^{T}
:

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{(1)}_{i+1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho_{i}u_{i}+\rho_{i+1}u_{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\alpha_{i+1/2}}{2}\left(\rho_{i+1}-\rho_{i}\right)

(A18)

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{(2)}_{i+1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho_{i}u_{i}^{2}+p_{i}+\rho_{i+1}u_{i+1}^{2}+p_{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\alpha_{i+1/2}}{2}\left(\rho_{i+1}u_{i+1}-\rho_{i}u_{i}\right)

(A19)

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{(3)}_{i+1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((E_{i}+p_{i})u_{i}+(E_{i+1}+p_{i+1})u_{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\alpha_{i+1/2}}{2}\left(E_{i+1}-E_{i}\right)

(A20)

B.2.3
Forward Euler Time Integration

For time discretization, we employ the explicit
Forward Euler
(first-order) method:

\mathbf{U}_{i}^{n+1}=\mathbf{U}_{i}^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2}^{n}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i-1/2}^{n}\right)

(A21)

The time step
\Delta t
is constrained by the
Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) stability condition
:

\Delta t=\text{CFL}\cdot\frac{\Delta x}{\max_{i}(|u_{i}|+c_{i})},\quad\text{CFL}\leq 1

(A22)

Boundary conditions
for Shu-Osher:

•

Left boundary
(
x=-5
): Fixed supersonic inflow (Dirichlet)

•

Right boundary
(
x=5
): Transmissive (zero-gradient Neumann)

Remark 2
.

The Rusanov scheme is
first-order accurate
in both space and time.
The numerical dissipation term
\frac{\alpha}{2}(\mathbf{U}_{i+1}-\mathbf{U}_{i})
ensures monotonicity near discontinuities but introduces smearing.
For shock-capturing applications, this provides stable reference solutions suitable for PINN training data.

Appendix C
Numerical Method for 2D Riemann Problem

The 2D Riemann problem involves the interaction of four different states in a quadrant configuration, producing complex shock patterns.

C.1
Governing Equations

The two-dimensional (2D) Euler equations:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{U}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{G}}{\partial y}=0

(A23)

where:

\mathbf{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u\\
\rho v\\
E\end{pmatrix},\;\mathbf{F}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+p\\
\rho uv\\
(E+p)u\end{pmatrix},\;\mathbf{G}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho v\\
\rho uv\\
\rho v^{2}+p\\
(E+p)v\end{pmatrix}

(A24)

C.2
Initial Conditions (Configuration 3)

Domain
:
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}

The initial data is divided into four quadrants:

(\rho,u,v,p)=\begin{cases}(1.5,0,0,1.5)&\text{if }x\geq 0.5,y\geq 0.5\quad\text{(Top-Right)}\\
(0.5323,1.206,0,0.3)&\text{if }x<0.5,y\geq 0.5\quad\text{(Top-Left)}\\
(0.138,1.206,1.206,0.029)&\text{if }x<0.5,y<0.5\quad\text{(Bottom-Left)}\\
(0.5323,0,1.206,0.3)&\text{if }x\geq 0.5,y<0.5\quad\text{(Bottom-Right)}\end{cases}

(A25)

C.3
2D Rusanov (Local Lax-Friedrichs) Finite Volume Scheme

Following the same integral form derivation as in the 1D case (
Section

B.2
), we integrate the 2D Euler equations
Eq.

A23
over a control volume
[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}]
:

\begin{split}\frac{d\bar{\mathbf{U}}_{i,j}}{dt}&=-\frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2,j}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i-1/2,j}\right)\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{\Delta y}\left(\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i,j+1/2}-\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i,j-1/2}\right)\end{split}

(A26)

Rusanov numerical flux in
x
-direction
:

\begin{split}\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2,j}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{F}_{i,j}+\mathbf{F}_{i+1,j}\right)\\
&\quad-\frac{\alpha^{x}_{i+1/2,j}}{2}\left(\mathbf{U}_{i+1,j}-\mathbf{U}_{i,j}\right)\end{split}

(A27)

where:

\alpha^{x}_{i+1/2,j}=\max\left(|u_{i,j}|+c_{i,j},\,|u_{i+1,j}|+c_{i+1,j}\right)

(A28)

Rusanov numerical flux in
y
-direction
:

\begin{split}\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i,j+1/2}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{G}_{i,j}+\mathbf{G}_{i,j+1}\right)\\
&\quad-\frac{\alpha^{y}_{i,j+1/2}}{2}\left(\mathbf{U}_{i,j+1}-\mathbf{U}_{i,j}\right)\end{split}

(A29)

where:

\alpha^{y}_{i,j+1/2}=\max\left(|v_{i,j}|+c_{i,j},\,|v_{i,j+1}|+c_{i,j+1}\right)

(A30)

Forward Euler update
:

\begin{split}\mathbf{U}_{i,j}^{n+1}&=\mathbf{U}_{i,j}^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2,j}^{n}-\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i-1/2,j}^{n}\right)\\
&\quad-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta y}\left(\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i,j+1/2}^{n}-\hat{\mathbf{G}}_{i,j-1/2}^{n}\right)\end{split}

(A31)

CFL condition for 2D
:

\Delta t=\text{CFL}\cdot\frac{\min(\Delta x,\Delta y)}{\max_{i,j}\left(\max(|u|+c,|v|+c)\right)},\quad\text{CFL}\leq 0.5

(A32)

Boundary conditions
: Zero-gradient (Neumann) on all boundaries.
