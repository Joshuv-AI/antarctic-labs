Caenorhabditis elegans avoids epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) through SRXA-7 G-protein coupled receptor
Authors: YongJin Cheon, Hyeonjeong Hwang, Kyuhyung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flavonoids are plant-derived polyphenols that influence nematode behavior, yet their neuronal and molecular targets remain poorly understood. Here, we show that the free-living nematode Caenorhabditis elegans detects and avoids a green tea catechin epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) via the SRXA-7 G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) in the ASH nociceptive neurons. EGCG and epicatechin gallate (ECG), both containing a galloyl group, trigger strong avoidance, unlike other green tea catechins lacking this moiety. EGCG avoidance behavior displays species- and strain-specific differences among Caenorhabditis species and wild C. elegans isolates. Moreover, it is dynamically modulated by prior experience and feeding state. The ASH chemosensory neurons are required for EGCG detection, with avoidance mediated through canonical GPCR signaling components including GRK-2, GPA-3, ODR-3, and TRPV channels OCR-2 and OSM-9. A targeted GPCR screen revealed that srxa-7 mutants exhibited specific defects in EGCG avoidance. EGCG-evoked calcium responses in ASH are reduced in srxa-7 mutants and restored by ASH-specific expression of srxa-7 cDNA. These findings indicate that SRXA-7 is a sensory receptor for galloylated polyphenols, uncovering the neuronal and molecular basis of adaptive aversive responses to dietary plant compounds in nematodes.

INTRODUCTION

In nature, plants utilize a wide array of secondary metabolites as a chemical defense system against herbivores and microbial pathogens (1). Among these, flavonoids represent a particularly diverse and bioactive class of compounds that not only regulate plant development but also influence the physiology and behavior of interacting organisms (2, 3). In nematodes, flavonoids function as deterrents, toxins, or modulators of cellular signaling pathways in species-specific way (4). For instance, quercetin exhibits nematicidal properties that kill Heterodera zeae larvae, while repelling Radopholus similis larvae. In addition, kaempferol inhibits egg hatching of R. similis, while repelling Meloidogyne incognita (5). Along with kaempferol and quercetin, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) extends the lifespan of Caenorhabditis elegans, but also induces repellent behavior (6-8). Thus, nematodes have evolved behavioral responses that allow them to detect and respond to a wide range of flavonoids (9). However, neuronal and molecular mechanisms underlying how nematodes detect and behaviorally respond to specific flavonoids remain largely unknown.

Galloylated catechins, a subclass of flavonoids, are abundant constituents of the tea plant (Camellia sinensis) and exhibit diverse biological activities (10). Among them, EGCG is the major green tea catechin known for its strong antioxidant properties (11) and pharmacological effects such as anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, and anti-allergic actions mediated by the 67-kDa laminin receptor (67LR) in vitro (12-14). EGCG also regulates multiple metabolic processes, including enhancement of organismal fitness and lifespan extension in various model systems (6, 15-19). In C. elegans, EGCG triggers an avoidance response that displays behavioral plasticity (7). However, sensory receptors and neural circuits that mediate EGCG-induced avoidance behavior in C. elegans have not been reported yet.

In this study, we show that C. elegans exhibits avoidance behavior toward the green tea flavonoid through the G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) SRXA-7 and its downstream signaling pathway in the ASH nociceptive neurons. Among flavonoids, only galloylated green tea catechins such as EGCG and epicatechin gallate (ECG), trigger an avoidance response, suggesting that the galloyl moiety is essential for eliciting this behavior in C. elegans. EGCG avoidance varies across closely related Caenorhabditis species and among wild C. elegans isolates. Such avoidance behavior is modulated by prior exposure and feeding state, with this modulation occurring at the level of sensory neurons. The ASH nociceptive neurons, that are known to function as polymodal nociceptors and mediate avoidance responses to a variety of aversive stimuli, including high osmolarity, heavy metals, and bitter compounds (20), are required for EGCG detection and mediate avoidance through SRXA-7 and associated GPCR signaling components. These results demonstrate that C. elegans can elicit behavioral responses to plant-derived flavonoids through specific GPCRs and modulate neuronal activities to generate adaptive behavioral responses.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Green tea catechin EGCG elicits avoidance behavior in C. elegans

Catechins including EGCG, major bioactive compounds in green tea, provide diverse physiological responses across species, including nematodes (6, 21). To examine whether C. elegans exhibits chemotactic responses to these chemicals, we performed a drop assay, which quantifies acute chemical avoidance (Fig. 1A) (22). We tested four catechins: EGCG, ECG, epigallocatechin (EGC), and epicatechin (EC) (Fig. 1B). We found that while animals exhibited weak avoidance to EGC and EC, they strongly avoided both EGCG and ECG (Fig. 1C). Given that EGCG and ECG share a galloyl moiety, which is absent in EGC and EC, these results suggest that the galloyl group plays a key role in triggering avoidance behavior of C. elegans.

To further test whether the galloyl moiety could elicit avoidance behavior, we tested behavioral responses to gallic acid (GA) containing a single galloyl group and tannic acid (TA) containing ten galloyl groups (Fig. 1D) (23). GA and TA are naturally occurring galloylated polyphenols present in land plants and foodstuffs (24). Both GA and TA elicited avoidance responses in wild-type animals, although TA elicited a stronger response than EGCG and ECG, while GA’s effect was comparatively weaker (Fig. 1E). Together, these results indicate that the galloyl moiety of EGCG causes aversive responses in C. elegans.

EGCG avoidance behavior varies across Caenorhabditis species and C. elegans isolates

To investigate the evolutionary conservation and natural diversity of EGCG avoidance, we examined behavioral responses of multiple Caenorhabditis species and wild C. elegans isolates (25, 26). We first tested EGCG avoidance in three gonochoristic species (C. remanei, C. brenneri, and C. japonica) and one hermaphroditic species (C. briggsae), all of which are closely related to C. elegans (Fig. 1F) (27). As a control, we tested a well-established chemical repellent, glycerol, and found that all species exhibited robust avoidance behavior regardless of sex (Supplementary Fig. 1A). In contrast, responses to EGCG varied significantly across species and sexes (Fig. 1G). Among all tested species, only C. elegans exhibited strong EGCG avoidance in both hermaphrodites and males (Fig. 1G). In C. brenneri and C. remanei, avoidance was observed predominantly in males, whereas females showed weak or no responses, respectively (Fig. 1G). Other species, including C. briggsae and C. japonica, displayed little to no EGCG avoidance in either sex (Fig. 1G).

We next examined EGCG avoidance among wild C. elegans isolates collected from diverse geographic regions (26). While glycerol avoidance was robust and consistent across isolates (Supplementary Fig. 1B), EGCG responses varied markedly (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Some isolates exhibited strong avoidance similar to the laboratory N2 strain, while others showed weak or no responses (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Together, these results indicate species- and strain-specific variability in EGCG, but not glycerol, avoidance behavior. This variation suggests that chemosensory responses to EGCG are subject to evolutionary divergence and ecological adaptation across the Caenorhabditis genus.

EGCG avoidance behavior is modulated by experience and internal state

We next asked whether EGCG avoidance in C. elegans is modulated by prior experience or internal state (Fig. 2A). First, we examined whether repeated exposure to EGCG alters EGCG avoidance and found that wild-type animals exhibit progressively reduced responses after repeated exposure to EGCG (Fig. 2B). Second, we varied the duration of EGCG exposure and observed that avoidance declines with longer exposure time (Fig. 2C). Decreased avoidance responses returned to baseline levels within 4 hours after removal from EGCG-containing conditions (Fig. 2D). Third, we tested whether chronic EGCG exposure affects avoidance behavior. Animals were grown from hatching on food containing various concentrations of EGCG and tested as adults. Chronic exposure to EGCG at concentrations above 100 μM significantly reduced avoidance to EGCG (Fig. 2E). Fourth, we transiently exposed animals to EGCG during larval stages and tested their avoidance as adults. EGCG avoidance was not reduced unless exposure continued into adulthood (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Together, these results indicate that EGCG avoidance behavior is modulated by both the intensity and timing of prior exposure. Repeated or prolonged EGCG experience reduces avoidance in a reversible manner, whereas early-life exposure alone is insufficient to induce reducing avoidance.

To investigate whether EGCG avoidance is further influenced by the feeding state, we maintained animals either in a well-fed condition or starved them for 3, 6, or 24 hours and then tested their avoidance behavior toward EGCG (Fig. 2A) (28). Animals starved for 6 hours or longer exhibited significantly enhanced avoidance to 10 μM EGCG compared to both well-fed controls and animals starved for only 3 hours (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. 2B). A similar increase in avoidance was also observed at 100 μM EGCG (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that the feeding state modulates EGCG avoidance behavior, with starvation promoting increased sensitivity to EGCG.

The ASH nociceptive neurons mediate EGCG avoidance

We next asked which sensory neurons are required for detecting EGCG. C. elegans detects a wide range of chemical repellents via a small subset of amphid sensory neurons, including ASH, AWB, and ASK (Fig. 3A) (29). To assess the role of these neurons in EGCG avoidance, we performed drop assays using 100 μM EGCG in animals with genetically ablated the ASK or ASH neurons and in AWB-defective ceh-37 mutants (30, 31). Genetic ablation of the ASH neurons abolished the avoidance behavior of C. elegans in response to both EGCG and the osmotic repellent glycerol, whereas ablation of AWB or ASK had no such effect (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 3A). Furthermore, animals lacking the ASH neurons displayed severely impaired avoidance across a range of EGCG concentrations (Fig. 3B), indicating that the ASH neurons are necessary for EGCG avoidance. To determine whether the ASH neurons respond directly to EGCG, we expressed the genetically encoded calcium sensor GCaMP3 in ASH and measured intracellular Ca2＋ transients upon EGCG exposure. Wild-type ASH neurons exhibited robust and reproducible increases in intracellular Ca2＋ levels in response to EGCG (Fig. 3C), suggesting that the ASH neurons are directly activated by EGCG.

We then examined whether the ASH neuron activity is modulated by pre-exposure or starvation by measuring EGCG-evoked Ca2＋ transients. Compared to naïve controls, Ca2＋ responses in the ASH neurons were significantly reduced after EGCG pre-exposure and significantly enhanced under starvation conditions (Fig. 3D, E), indicating that ASH activity correlates with behavioral changes induced by pre-exposure or starvation.

GPCR signaling components in the ASH neurons mediate EGCG avoidance

Next, we investigated molecular components that mediate signal transduction in the ASH neurons for EGCG avoidance. We first examined which G proteins function in ASH to transduce EGCG signals. The ASH neurons are known to express eleven Gα subunits, including GPA-1, GPA-2, GPA-3, GPA-6, GPA-7, GPA-8, GPA-11, GPA-13, GPA-14, GPA-15, and ODR-3 (32, 33). We found that gpa-3 and odr-3 mutants exhibit significant defects in EGCG avoidance, whereas mutants for other Gα subunits respond normally (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 3B). These findings indicate that both GPA-3 and ODR-3 mediate EGCG avoidance behavior.

The grk-2 gene encodes a GPCR kinase that phosphorylates activated GPCRs in ciliated sensory neurons and mediates avoidance behabior in response to many chemical repellents (34). We found that grk-2 mutants exhibited severe defects in EGCG avoidance (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. 3C). We next examined roles of the transient receptor potential vanilloid (TRPV) channels ocr-2 and osm-9 known to mediate ASH-dependent chemical avoidance (35). EGCG avoidance was abolished in both ocr-2 and osm-9 mutants (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 3D). Furthermore, expression of ocr-2 gDNA specifically in the ASH neurons restored EGCG avoidance in ocr-2 mutant animals (Fig. 3I and Supplementary Fig. 3E), confirming the cell-autonomous requirement of OCR-2 in the ASH neurons. Together, these findings indicate that EGCG avoidance is mediated by the ASH chemosensory neurons through the GPCR signaling pathway.

SRXA-7 GPCR is required for EGCG avoidance in the ASH neurons

We next sought to identify the chemosensory receptor responsible for detecting EGCG. The ASH neurons express over 34 putative chemosensory GPCRs (Supplementary Table 1) (33, 36). We screened 13 available receptor mutants and found that only srxa-7 mutants exhibit a significant defect in EGCG avoidance, but a weak defect in glycerol avoidance (Fig. 4A, B). srxa-7 mutants harbor a deletion allele presumed to be null (Fig. 4C). We also found that srxa-7 mutants fail to avoid EGCG across a range of concentrations (Fig. 4D). By contrast, srxa-7 mutants displayed normal avoidance to quinine, a compound detected by the ASH neurons (Fig. 4E), indicating that SRXA-7 is not required for general ASH-mediated avoidance behavior. To determine whether the defect observed in srxa-7 mutants was related to the galloyl moiety of EGCG, we tested their response to tannic acid, another galloylated polyphenol. srxa-7 mutants exhibited significantly reduced avoidance of tannic acid (Fig. 4F). This suggests that the SRXA-7 GPCR specifically mediates chemosensory responses to galloylated polyphenols including EGCG.

To assess the function of SRXA-7 in the ASH neurons, we measured EGCG-evoked intracellular Ca2＋ transients in srxa-7 mutants. We found that Ca2＋ responses in the ASH neurons of srxa-7 mutants were significantly reduced upon EGCG exposure (Fig. 4G), indicating that SRXA-7 is required for EGCG detection in the ASH neurons. To further confirm that SRXA-7 acts in ASH to mediate EGCG avoidance, we expressed srxa-7 cDNA in srxa-7 mutants using either its endogenous promoter or the ASH-specific osm-10 promoter. Expression of srxa-7 under both promoters restored EGCG avoidance in srxa-7 mutants (Fig. 4H). Together, these results suggest that SRXA-7 functions in the ASH neurons to mediate EGCG avoidance behavior.

In this study, we show that C. elegans detects the plant-derived flavonoid EGCG through the chemosensory GPCR SRXA-7 and modulates sensory neuronal activity to produce adaptive avoidance behavior. Our findings elucidate the mechanism by which dietary polyphenols are sensed by the nervous system, revealing that nematodes can elicit specific and plastic behavioral responses to bioactive plant compounds. Given the vast chemical diversity of plant secondary metabolites and their widespread ecological roles, deciphering how animal nervous systems decode and integrate such chemical cues remains a fundamental challenge. Thus, the identification of SRXA-7 as a functional receptor for EGCG not only establishes a molecular entry point for understanding flavonoid perception in animals, but also provides a tractable model for dissecting the neurobiology of plant-animal chemical communication. More broadly, this work underscores the utility of C. elegans as a platform for investigating how environmental signals, including those derived from the diet or microbiota, are translated into behavioral output. Elucidating such mechanisms will deepen our understanding of how nervous systems evolve to navigate complex and chemically rich ecosystems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Strains

All strains were maintained at 20°C. The N2 Bristol was used as a wild-type strain. All mutants and transgenic strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Molecular biology and transgenic worms

All constructs created in this study were inserted into the pPD95.77 vector and its derivatives. For constructs used in rescue experiments, the srxa-7 ∆5 (1138 bp, Left primer: ttcacttccaagtacggaaataca and Right primer: tttaaggacggaagtgggctacag) and osm-10 ASH-specific (895 bp, Left primer: ctgcagcttgacaccgac and Right primer : cttgcaagatgaaatttccaaaaaagtt) promoters were fused with the srxa-7 cDNA which were synthesized by Macrogen Inc. As an injection marker, 50 ng/μl of unc-122p::dsRed was used. srxa-7 cDNA at 0.5 ng/μl under the control of both promoters was injected into srxa-7 mutants (37).

Drop assay

A drop assay was modified from a previously described drop assay (22). All assays were performed in the absence of food at 20°C. In general, the drop assay used 100 μM EGCG serially diluted with M13 buffer in 5 mM EGCG stock dissolved in EtOH (detailed EGCG concentration was noted in figure legends). In the assay, young adult animals were transferred onto non-seeded plates. Using a mouth pipette, droplets were placed on the expected path directly in front of the animal. Movement of the worm was observed for 5 seconds immediately after contact with the delivered buffer or stimulant droplets and the avoidance response was quantified. M13 buffer was used as a negative control to confirm that there was a response to droplets. Glycerol, an osmotic stimulus, was used as a positive control to confirm that the avoidance response was intact. Short or long reversals were defined as avoidance responses of less than half or more than half the body length of the worm, respectively (38). Long reversal responses to a stimulus within 5 seconds were counted as avoidance behaviors. All assays used 10 animals as a set, with the average of 10 responses represented as a single dot on the graph.

Pre-exposure assay

For EGCG pre-exposure, two types of nematode growth plates were seeded with E. coli OP50 (control plates) and E. coli OP50 supplemented with EGCG (experimental plates). In general, OP50 mixed with 200 μM EGCG was used for the pre-exposure assay. Fig. 2E used plates seeded with OP50 containing 500 μM, 200 μM, 100 μM, and 10 μM EGCG, respectively. In Fig. 4D, animals pre-exposed for 4 hours were placed on the control plate and OP50 was refed without EGCG for 1, 2, and 4 hours, respectively.

Starvation assay

The starvation assay was based on a previously described method (28). For egg prep, 10 young adults were placed on OP50 seeded plates and allowed to lay eggs for 2 hours before discarding adult animals. After incubation at 20°C for approximately 3 days, synchronized young adults were washed three times with distilled water, transferred to seeded or non-seeded plates, dried, and incubated for 3, 6, and 24 hours. Animals in the starved group in Fig. 2G were starved for 6 hours.

In vivo Ca2＋ imaging

In vivo Ca2＋ imaging was performed according to a previously published protocol (22). Briefly, custom-made microfluidic chips were processed as previously described (39). GCaMP3 under the control of the osm-10 promoter was utilized for ASH imaging. During imaging, transgenic animals were exposed to fluorescence light for one minute. Images were taken for one minute with fixed exposure time and intervals using a 40× objective of a Zeiss Axioplan microscope with a Zeiss AxioCam HR. ImageJ and custom-written MATLAB scripts were used to analyze acquired images (40). Specifically, the cell body area of the ASH neuron was selected as the ROI (region of interest) and the darkest region was selected as the background. Fluorescence intensity in the frame before the 1st stimulus was used to normalize ∆F/F. Data of “∆F/F after EGCG exposure” represented maximum fluorescence intensity during the first EGCG exposure, normalized by subtracting baseline intensity from the frame before the 1st stimulus.
