Development and validation of a nomogram to predict recurrence after hepatectomy in overweight patients with hepatocellular carcinoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jiajing Zhao, Yunjian Meng, Zhongyi Jiang, Zhike Li, Youyao Li, Yuanjun Liu, Jiangang Bi, Liping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEven after curative hepatectomy, postoperative recurrence remains a major challenge for patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Body mass index (BMI) has been associated with the risk of HCC development; however, the prognostic impact of high BMI on postoperative recurrence in patients with HCC has not been fully elucidated.ObjectiveTo evaluate the risk factors associated with postoperative recurrence in overweight HCC patients with negative surgical margins and to develop a prognostic nomogram for recurrence prediction.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 259 overweight patients (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m²) who underwent curative hepatectomy for pathologically confirmed HCC with negative margins. Patients were randomly divided into a training cohort and a validation cohort at a 7:3 ratio. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors associated with recurrence-free survival (RFS), incorporating clinical, laboratory, and pathological variables. A prognostic nomogram was constructed to estimate the 1-, 2-, and 3-year recurrence probabilities. Kaplan–Meier and log-rank tests were used to compare RFS between the high- and low-risk groups.ResultsUnivariate Cox analysis identified poor preoperative Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS score), high nutrition risk screening (NRS 2002) score, advanced AJCC 8th stage, presence of satellite nodules, vascular invasion, elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and a high platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) as risk factors for RFS, whereas prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) was a protective factor (all P < .05). Multivariate Cox regression revealed that PS score and NRS 2002 were independent predictors of RFS. In the training cohort, the nomogram demonstrated favorable discrimination, with a concordance index (C-index) of 0.724, and the areas under the ROC curve for predicting 1-, 2-, and 3-year RFS were 0.728, 0.769, and 0.767, respectively; comparable performance was observed in the validation cohort (C-index 0.677; 1-, 2-, and 3-year AUCs of 0.701, 0.717, and 0.832). Decision curve analysis indicated that the predictive performance of the model surpassed that of the China Liver Cancer (CNLC) and Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging systems. Moreover, the high-risk group exhibited significantly poorer RFS compared with the low-risk group (all P < .001).ConclusionsThe proposed nomogram effectively stratifies postoperative recurrence risk and predicts RFS in overweight patients with HCC, providing a valuable tool for individualized prognostic assessment and clinical decision-making. However, due to its retrospective and single-center design, the findings require further validation in large-scale, multicenter studies across diverse populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-04995-z.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Overweight, Recurrence, Prognostic nomogram, Recurrence-free survival

Introduction

Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. For patients with resectable disease, surgical resection offers the best chance for long-term survival; however, postoperative tumor recurrence is common and represents the major determinant of long-term outcomes. Therefore, accurate assessment of recurrence risk after curative hepatectomy is crucial [2–4].

Epidemiological and mechanistic studies have increasingly revealed the role of metabolic risk factors—particularly overweight and obesity—in the initiation and progression of HCC [5, 6]. Obesity promotes hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, and is associated with alterations in tumor biology and systemic metabolic homeostasis that favor tumor recurrence [7, 8]. Recent Mendelian randomization and observational analyses have supported a causal link between obesity-related traits and HCC risk; however, the association between preoperative body mass index (BMI) and postoperative recurrence after curative resection remains incompletely understood [6].

Emerging radiological approaches for preoperative prediction of MVI—including tumor compression of adjacent vessels and radiomics features on contrast-enhanced MRI—offer promising avenues for integrating pathological risk stratification into preoperative surgical planning [9].Meanwhile, classical tumor-related features, such as microvascular invasion (MVI), satellite nodules, and higher pathological stages within established staging systems, remain the most consistently reported predictors of early recurrence after hepatectomy [10–13]. Satellite nodules and vascular invasion reflect intrahepatic dissemination and aggressive tumor biology, and their presence has been repeatedly linked to shorter RFS and overall survival [14]. Accurate staging systems that integrate tumor burden and liver function, including the China Liver Cancer Staging (CNLC) system, which is widely used in Asia, are important for prognostic stratification and treatment selection [15, 16].

Emerging evidence indicates that overweight and obese individuals may exhibit distinct tumor biology, immune microenvironments, and perioperative metabolic stress profiles that could independently influence recurrence risk beyond traditional tumor staging [17]. Obesity-related chronic inflammation and altered adipokine signaling may promote hepatocarcinogenesis and impair postoperative hepatic regeneration [7], whereas hypercoagulability and insulin resistance may further facilitate the spread of micrometastases [18–20]. Despite these biologically plausible mechanisms, few studies have systematically evaluated recurrence patterns or prognostic determinants in high-BMI HCC patients following R0 resection. Understanding these relationships could improve postoperative risk stratification and optimize follow-up and adjuvant therapeutic strategies in this growing patient population.

Given these gaps, it is essential to specifically evaluate the determinants of recurrence among overweight HCC patients who achieve pathologically negative margins (R0 resection) and to develop individualized prognostic tools. In this single-center retrospective study, we analyzed clinical, laboratory, and pathological variables to identify independent predictors of RFS in overweight (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m²) HCC patients with R0 resection. Based on these factors, we constructed and validated a user-friendly nomogram to stratify postoperative recurrence risk and support personalized postoperative management.

Methods

Research design and patient selection

This retrospective study analyzed the medical records of 715 patients who underwent curative hepatectomy for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) between January 2015 and December 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) postoperative pathological confirmation of HCC after curative hepatectomy; (2) negative surgical margin (R0 resection); (3) preoperative body mass index (BMI) ≥ 24 kg/m², consistent with the Chinese criteria for overweight; (4) complete clinicopathological and follow-up data available; and (5) absence of severe dysfunction of the heart, lungs, kidneys, or other vital organs. The exclusion criteria were: (1) history of other malignancies; (2) extrahepatic metastasis; (3) positive surgical margin; and (4) perioperative death within 30 days after surgery. Based on these criteria, a total of 259 eligible patients were included in the final analysis.

Clinical and pathological variables

All enrolled patients underwent comprehensive laboratory examinations, including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, coagulation profile, hepatitis virus status, and tumor markers, as well as imaging evaluations such as ultrasound, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) with three-dimensional reconstruction, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Clinical data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system, including demographic variables (age, sex), comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, coronary heart disease), hepatitis virus status, and preoperative laboratory parameters such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and platelet count (PLT). The platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) was calculated as the platelet count divided by the lymphocyte count.

Pathological specimens were obtained using the “seven-point sampling” method, with samples collected at the 12, 3, 6, and 9 o’clock margins of the tumor at a 1:1 ratio [21]. Tissue specimens were collected both within the tumor and from areas ≤ 1 cm and > 1 cm from the tumor margin. At least two experienced pathologists independently reviewed the specimens using hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemistry to confirm the diagnosis and evaluate histopathological features. Pathological parameters included tumor size, number of tumors, number of lesions, microvascular invasion (MVI), satellite nodules, presence of liver cirrhosis, and tumor stage according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC), the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system, and the China Liver Cancer (CNLC) staging system. Nutritional status was assessed using the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS2002) tool, and performance status was evaluated according to the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status scale. Satellite nodules were defined as small tumor foci observed in the liver parenchyma surrounding the primary tumor, which were macroscopically or microscopically visible, separated from the main lesion, and histologically similar or identical to the primary tumor.

Follow-up and outcome definition

All patients were followed up according to institutional guidelines, which included physical examination, serum AFP measurement, liver function tests, and contrast-enhanced abdominal CT or MRI. Follow-up was conducted every three months during the first two years after surgery and every six months thereafter. The primary endpoint was RFS, defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the first documented tumor recurrence (either intrahepatic or extrahepatic) or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Patients without recurrence at the last follow-up were censored. The final follow-up date was October 17, 2025. Tumor recurrence was defined as the appearance of new tumor nodules confirmed by contrast-enhanced CT, contrast-enhanced MRI, or histopathological biopsy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1). Missing values were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), implemented via the mice R package (m = 5 imputed datasets; maxit = 50 iterations; seed = 123); continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching, binary variables using logistic regression, and polytomous variables using polytomous logistic regression. Patients were randomly divided into a training cohort (70%, n = 177) and a validation cohort (30%, n = 82) using stratified random sampling optimized over 100 iterations by minimizing the mean standardized mean difference (SMD) across key clinical variables. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and compared using the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to identify independent predictors of RFS. Variables with P < .10 in the univariate analysis were entered simultaneously into the multivariate Cox model (enter method); this threshold was chosen to reduce the risk of excluding potentially relevant predictors. Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.A prognostic nomogram for RFS was constructed using the "rms" package in R, incorporating independent predictors identified in themultivariate analysis (NRS2002 and ECOG PS) alongside additional variables that demonstrated prognostic significance in univariate analysis or haveestablished clinical relevance in HCC recurrence prediction based on prior literature (age, AJCC 8th stage, BCLC stage, HBV status, satellite nodules,ALP, PLR, and NLR), with the aim of capturing both host-related and tumor-intrinsic dimensions of recurrence risk, and individual nomogram risk scores were derived using the predict function (type = “risk”). The performance of the nomogram for predicting 1-, 2-, and 3-year RFS was evaluated using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index; calculated via the rcorr.cens function in the Hmisc R package), calibration curves (assessed using the calibrate function with the Kaplan–Meier method and 1,000-resample bootstrapping, B = 1000), and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (implemented using the timeROC R package). Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the net clinical benefit of the nomogram relative to conventional staging systems (CNLC and BCLC) across threshold probabilities from 1% to 100% using the dcurves R package, separately for 1-, 2-, and 3-year time points. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups according to the median total nomogram score, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare RFS between groups using the log-rank test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The procedures of this study complied with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen People’s Hospital (approval number: LL-KY-2025247-01). Owing to the retrospective nature of this study and the use of anonymized data, all analyses were conducted without any identifiable patient information to ensure confidentiality. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [22].

Results

Baseline data and clinical feature selection

All eligible patients were randomly assigned to either the training cohort (n = 177) or the validation cohort (n = 82) in a 7:3 ratio. The study flowchart illustrating the inclusion and exclusion process of all participants is shown in Fig. 1. As summarized in Table 1, there were no significant differences in any clinical variables between the training and validation cohorts. At baseline, the mean age of the overall cohort was 57.49 years, and the majority of patients were male (83.8%). Regarding risk factors for hepatocellular carcinoma, 76.8% of patients were infected with hepatitis B virus (HBV), and 5% had hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection. Most patients presented with early-stage disease: 74.9% were classified as BCLC stage A, 97.2% as Child–Pugh class A, and 85.7% as CNLC stage I. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of RFS. In univariate analysis, HBV status, NRS2002 score, performance status (PS) score, AJCC 8th stage, presence of satellite nodules, vascular invasion, ALP, APTT, and PLR were significantly associated with RFS (all P < .05). Variables with P < .10 in univariate analysis were subsequently included in the multivariate model. Multivariate Cox regression identified preoperative NRS2002 score and PS score as independentrisk factors for postoperative recurrence in overweight HCC patients; specifically, ECOG PS 1 (HR 2.92, 95% CI 1.19–7.15, P = 0.019) and NRS2002score 4 (HR 55.74, 95% CI 3.53–879.56, P = 0.004) were significantly associated with poorer RFS (Table 2). The results of the univariate and multivariate analyses were visualized using forest plots (Figs. 2A, B).

Construction and validation of the nomogram

Based on the results of the Cox regression analyses, a prognostic nomogram was constructed to predict postoperative recurrence risk in overweight HCC patients, incorporating the following variables: age, AJCC 8th stage, PLR, ALP, NLR, BCLC stage, presence of satellite nodules, HBV infection status, performance status (PS) score, and NRS2002 score (Fig. 3A). At the top of the nomogram, each variable corresponds to a specific point value, and the total score is obtained by summing the points for all variables. The total point value at the bottom of the nomogram is then used to estimate the individual probability of recurrence at 1, 2, and 3 years after surgery in overweight HCC patients. Calibration curves demonstrated that the predicted probabilities closely matched the observed outcomes, as indicated by their proximity to the 45° reference line. The C-index for the training cohort was 0.724, suggesting good predictive accuracy and consistency (Fig. 3B), while the C-index for the validation cohort was 0.677 (Fig. 3C). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were further employed to evaluate the discriminative ability and robustness of the model. In the training cohort, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) values for 1-, 2-, and 3-year RFS were 0.728, 0.769, and 0.767, respectively (Fig. 3D). Similarly, in the validation cohort, the corresponding AUC values were 0.701, 0.717, and 0.832 (Fig. 3E). These findings collectively indicate that the nomogram model exhibits strong predictive performance and reliability, providing valuable clinical utility for recurrence risk assessment in overweight HCC patients.

Clinical Utility of the Predictive Model

To evaluate the clinical applicability of the nomogram in decision-making, we compared its performance with conventional staging systems, including the CNLC and BCLC classifications. Decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the nomogram provided greater net benefit for predicting 1-, 2-, and 3-year postoperative outcomes in overweight HCC patients compared with the CNLC and BCLC systems in the training cohort (Fig. S1; Figs. 4A, C). Similar results were observed in the validation cohort, although the smaller number of RFS events at 1 and 2 years limited the statistical power (Fig. S2; Figs. 4B, D). Furthermore, patients in the training cohort were stratified into high- and low-risk subgroups based on the median total nomogram score. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that the high-risk group had significantly poorer RFS compared with the low-risk group (P < .001) (Fig. 4E), indicating that patients with higher risk scores experienced recurrence earlier. Consistently, similar findings were observed in the validation cohort (P = .0023) (Fig. 4F). These results suggest that the nomogram can serve as a practical tool to guide clinical decision-making and postoperative recurrence risk assessment in overweight HCC patients.

Discussion

This retrospective single-center study included 259 overweight patients (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m²) who underwent R0 resection for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). We identified preoperative nutritional risk (NRS 2002) score and ECOG performance status (PS) score as independent predictors of RFS. A nomogram incorporating these independent predictors alongside additional variables with established prognostic relevance in HCC—including age, AJCC 8th pathological stage, BCLC stage, HBV infection status, presence of satellite nodules, ALP, PLR, and NLR—demonstrated superior net benefit compared with traditional staging systems (CNLC and BCLC) in decision curve analysis. These variables were retained in the nomogram because each has been independently associated with HCC recurrence or survival in prior studies, and their inclusion was intended to capture both tumor-intrinsic and systemic host factors that collectively reflect the multidimensional nature of recurrence risk in overweight patients. Our findings highlight that, among overweight HCC patients, factors reflecting functional and nutritional reserves may be as important as classical tumor-related predictors for recurrence, underscoring the importance of preoperative nutritional and physical assessments.

Emerging evidence suggests that metabolic factors and their comorbidities can affect outcomes after hepatectomy for HCC [23]. Our results are consistent with and extend these findings. For instance, a large multicenter study in Japan found that obesity and diabetes comorbidity were significantly associated with late HCC recurrence after hepatectomy, emphasizing the adverse prognostic impact of metabolic dysfunction [24]. Similarly, risk factors for metabolic syndrome negatively affected the achievement of textbook outcome (TO) in HCC patients, with a lower likelihood of achieving TO as the number of metabolic risk factors increased [25]. Another Mendelian randomization study demonstrated a causal relationship between higher BMI and HCC risk [6]. Although that study focused on incidence rather than recurrence, it provided biological plausibility linking overweight and obesity to hepatocarcinogenesis through systemic inflammation, adipokine dysregulation, immune dysfunction, and metabolic stress [7]. Collectively, these findings support the notion that in overweight HCC patients, not only tumor burden but also host metabolic substrate conditions contribute to recurrence risk.

In our study, NRS 2002 and PS scores emerged as independent predictors with both biological plausibility and clinical significance. Nutritional risk screening captures malnutrition, weight loss, reduced intake, or increased metabolic demand, which in overweight individuals may reflect sarcopenic obesity or “hidden malnutrition” [26, 27]. Poor nutritional status may impair immune surveillance, delay liver regeneration, and promote micrometastatic progression. Moreover, suboptimal pre-treatment nutrition has been linked to poorer outcomes from immunotherapy [28]. Similarly, a higher PS score reflects reduced functional reserve, potentially associated with delayed postoperative recovery, prolonged rehabilitation, and reduced tolerance to adjuvant therapy, all of which may increase recurrence risk. Notably, the apparent protective HR observed for NRS 2002 score of 1 relative to 0 in the multivariate model likely reflects the small number of events within individual NRS strata rather than a true biological effect; the overall trend of increasing recurrence risk with higher NRS scores (particularly at score 4, HR 55.74) is consistent with the expected direction. These findings suggest that stratifying overweight HCC patients by host reserve markers could identify a subgroup with seemingly favorable tumor features (e.g., R0 margin, early stage) but still at high risk of recurrence. From a therapeutic perspective, this emphasizes the importance of comprehensive preoperative evaluation and optimization of nutritional and functional status during the perioperative period.

Interestingly, classical tumor-related factors (such as satellite nodules and microvascular invasion) were significant in univariate Cox analysis but did not remain significant in the multivariate model. This may indicate that, in a relatively homogeneous cohort of R0-resected overweight HCC patients, variations in host reserves outweighed those in tumor biology. Alternatively, it could be due to the limited sample size of this study. Recent large-scale studies on hepatectomy outcomes have emphasized that recurrence patterns are increasingly influenced by host and microenvironmental factors rather than purely anatomical characteristics. For example, a multicenter study on MAFLD-associated HCC found that functional and metabolic biomarkers, rather than tumor size alone, were strongly correlated with long-term recurrence and tumor-related mortality [29]. Similarly, recent prognostic models for HCC have increasingly incorporated molecular and host-related signaturesalongside conventional clinicopathological variables to improve risk stratification, reflecting a broader trend toward multidimensional nomogramconstruction in this field [30].

In HCC, recurrence is broadly categorized as early (≤ 2 years), primarily reflecting intrahepatic metastasis driven by tumor-intrinsic factors such as MVI and satellite nodules, and late (> 2 years), predominantly arising from de novo carcinogenesis in the setting of chronic liver disease. In the context of overweight and obesity, metabolic dysregulation—including chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and adipokine dysregulation—may selectively promote late multicentric recurrence by sustaining a hepatocarcinogenic microenvironment. The NRS 2002 and ECOG PS scores identified as independent predictors in our study may influence both recurrence pathways by impairing immune surveillance, delaying hepatic regeneration, and reducing tolerance to adjuvant therapy. Future studies with larger cohorts should prospectively stratify recurrence by timing to disentangle these mechanisms and to identify predictors specific to each recurrence pattern.

Our nomogram carries significant clinical implications. First, it facilitates postoperative risk stratification for overweight HCC patients: those categorized as high risk may benefit from closer surveillance (e.g., more frequent imaging and AFP monitoring) or early consideration of adjuvant therapy. Indeed, recent evidence shows that structured postoperative surveillance improves both overall survival and post-recurrence survival in HCC patients [31, 32]. Second, the model underscores the potential value of prehabilitation and nutritional optimization in this subgroup. For patients with higher NRS 2002 or PS scores, organized interventions including preoperative nutritional counseling, dietary modifications, and management of metabolic comorbidities can enhance functional reserve and reduce the risk of recurrence. Our findings have important clinical implications. Nutritional and physical function assessments should be integrated into the routine preoperative evaluation of HCC. For patients with high NRS 2002 or poor ECOG performance status, early recurrence risk should be considered even in the presence of favorable tumor stage. Structured prehabilitation programs that combine individualized nutritional optimization, exercise, and metabolic control have been shown to improve perioperative outcomes and may enhance RFS, although such evidence in HCC remains limited [33]. The nomogram developed in this study provides a practical clinical tool for individualized postoperative surveillance intensity. Patients stratified into the high-risk group may benefit from closer imaging follow-up, early therapeutic intervention, or inclusion in clinical trials evaluating adjuvant therapy. Moreover, the model highlights the potential of integrating tumor-specific and host-related indicators to refine prognosis beyond traditional staging frameworks (e.g., CNLC or BCLC).

Our study also offers valuable insights into the metabolic and nutritional management of overweight HCC patients during the perioperative period. Preoperative nutritional risk is a powerful predictor of HCC recurrence, suggesting that metabolic optimization should be an integral part of HCC treatment planning. Future clinical strategies may include objective body composition analysis using CT-derived skeletal muscle and visceral fat indices to guide personalized nutritional interventions, as recent studies have confirmed their prognostic relevance for survival and recurrence. Combining these objective markers with biochemical and inflammatory features could yield a more comprehensive model for risk prediction and targeted intervention.

Despite several strengths, including a well-defined overweight cohort and robust internal validation, our study has limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design introduces potential selection bias, limiting generalizability. Institutional variations in surgical expertise, adjuvant therapy, and surveillance intervals may influence recurrence patterns [34]. Furthermore, the absence of systematic data on surgical approach (anatomical versus non-anatomical resection) and extent of hepatectomy represents an additional limitation; future studies should incorporate these variables to evaluate their independent contribution to recurrence risk in overweight HCC patients. Second, the BMI cutoff (≥ 24 kg/m²) was based on Chinese standards, which differ from the WHO global threshold (≥ 25 kg/m²). In Western populations, a proportion of our cohort (≥ 24 to < 25 kg/m²) would not be classified as overweight, potentially attenuating the predictive utility of the nomogram; its applicability to Western or ethnically diverse populations therefore requires validation using WHO BMI criteria. Third, heterogeneity in postoperative management and recurrence treatment inherent to retrospective designs may affect RFS outcomes. Finally, although the nomogram showed satisfactory discrimination in internal validation, the moderate decline in C-index from the training (0.724) to the validation cohort (0.677) is consistent with expected optimistic bias in internally validated models and is partly attributable to the smaller sample size of the validation set, which increases variability in performance estimates; multicenter external validation across diverse populations is essential before widespread clinical implementation.

In conclusion, HBV status, NRS 2002 score, ECOG PS score, AJCC 8th stage, satellite nodules, vascular invasion, preoperative ALP, APTT, and PLR were associated with recurrence risk in overweight HCC patients after resection. Preoperative nutritional risk and functional status were independent predictors of recurrence after curative hepatectomy in overweight HCC patients. The nomogram built from these variables provides a simple yet powerful tool for individualized recurrence risk assessment, with predictive accuracy superior to traditional staging systems. Beyond risk prediction, our findings underscore the critical role of host metabolic and functional status in postoperative tumor biology. Incorporating structured nutritional assessment and optimization into the perioperative management of overweight HCC patients may improve long-term outcomes. Future multicenter prospective studies should integrate imaging-based body composition, metabolic biomarkers, and interventional prehabilitation programs to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that HBV infection status, NRS2002 score, ECOG PS score, AJCC 8th stage, presence of satellite nodules, vascular invasion, preoperative ALP, APTT, and PLR are associated with postoperative recurrence risk in overweight HCC patients. Among these, NRS2002 and ECOG PS scores were identified as independent predictors of RFS in R0-resected overweight (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m²) HCC patients. A nomogram constructed based on these factors showed good predictive accuracy for postoperative recurrence and provides a practical tool for individualized risk stratification and postoperative surveillance planning. Given its retrospective single-center design and the absence of external validation, the nomogram should currently be considered an exploratory prognostic tool requiring prospective multicenter validation before broader clinical adoption.
