Benchmarking Open-Ended Multi-Agent Coordination in Language Agents
Kale-ab Abebe Tessera 1 Andras Szecsenyi 1 Cameron Barker 1 Alexander Rutherford 2 Davide Paglieri 3 Aidan Scannell 1 Henry Gouk 1 Elliot J. Crowley 1 Tim Rocktäschel 3 Amos Storkey 1 1 University of Edinburgh 2 University of Oxford 3 University College London

Abstract
As language models are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents, they must coordinate with others over long horizons in open-ended interactive tasks. Yet existing evaluations rarely test these demands together, instead emphasising single-agent tasks, short interactions, or highly structured multi-agent settings. We introduce alem , a JAX-based benchmark for open-ended multi-agent coordination built on Craftax-like dynamics. alem embeds procedurally generated coordination tasks, soft specialisation, communication, and controllable coordination difficulty into a long-horizon survival world with exploration, crafting, trading, and combat. We evaluate 13 13 modern LLMs zero-shot within homogeneous teams, with trained MARL agents as reference points. Current LLM agents remain far from solving alem , averaging only ∼ 6 % \sim 6\% normalised return, but their failures are not uniform. On the hardest coordination setting, zero-shot Gemini-3.1-Pro-High approaches MARL agents trained for one billion steps, while GPT-5.4-High achieves strong base-task reward but much lower coordination reward. This contrast shows that individual task competence does not imply coordination competence. Ablations show that communication is the largest contributor to coordination, while memory and reasoning help when used to maintain multi-step plans. Overall, our results identify coordination as a distinct bottleneck for frontier LLM agents, separate from single-agent capabilities. alem makes this bottleneck measurable and provides a controlled testbed for developing agents that communicate, allocate roles, and execute shared plans. Code is available at https://github.com/alem-world/alem-env .

Background

We model the environment as a partially observable stochastic game (POSG)
[
26
]
, denoted as the tuple
\langle I,S,A,O,\Omega,T,R\rangle
. Here,
I=\{1,\dots,n\}
is the set of agents,
S
is the set of states,
A=A^{1}\times\dots\times A^{n}
is the joint action space, and
O=O^{1}\times\dots\times O^{n}
is the joint observation space. The observation function
\Omega=(\Omega^{1},\dots,\Omega^{n})
maps states to per-agent observations: at each state
s\in S
, agent
i
receives an individual observation
o^{i}=\Omega^{i}(s)\in O^{i}
and selects an action
a^{i}\in A^{i}
. The environment transitions according to
T:S\times A\to\Delta(S)
, where
\Delta(\cdot)
is the set of probability distributions over a set. Each agent receives an individual reward given by
R^{i}:S\times A\times S\to\mathbb{R}
.

3
alem
: An Open-Ended World for Multi-Agent Coordination

We introduce
alem
, a benchmark for studying coordination in open-ended multi-agent worlds. Built on Craftax-Coop
[
40
]
,
alem
extends prior work along four axes: diverse
coordination types
,
procedural generation
of coordination tasks,
soft specialisation
, and an explicit
communication
channel. Across nine procedurally generated levels, agents explore, trade resources, craft tools, build structures, and fight mobs under controllable coordination demands. By scaling these demands while preserving the underlying world structure,
alem
enables controlled evaluation of how coordination failures emerge as coordination demands increase. Information-theoretic diagnostics verify that these mechanics require genuine inter-agent dependence rather than added single-agent difficulty
[
55
]
(App.
B.1
), while our JAX implementation supports billion-step MARL training on a single GPU in under two days (App.
C.3
).

3.1
Coordination Types

Following
36
, we view coordination as
interdependence between activities
. In multi-agent systems, these activities correspond to agents’ actions. We characterise the dependencies between coupled actions
A_{t}^{i}
and
A_{t^{\prime}}^{j}
by their
temporal separation
,
\Delta t=|t-t^{\prime}|
, resulting in a
coordination spectrum
from long-range dependencies to simultaneous joint action (Fig.
2
).

Existing MARL benchmarks typically isolate specific regions of this spectrum: SMAC
[
45
]
emphasises synchronous focus-fire (
\Delta t\approx 0
), Overcooked
[
10
]
tests short-window handovers (small
\Delta t
), and Melting Pot’s Clean Up
[
1
]
targets long-range resource interdependence (large
\Delta t
).
alem
instantiates this entire spectrum within a single open-ended environment, using procedural generation to vary both the temporal structure of coordination and the number of agents involved (details App.
B.2
).

3.2
Procedural Coordination Generation

Existing environments procedurally generate spatial layouts to reduce memorisation and encourage generalisation
[
40
,
37
,
65
,
24
]
. However, if coordination requirements are fixed, agents can still exploit recurring interaction patterns.
alem
therefore procedurally generates the
coordination structure
itself.

Each episode, eligible entities across tasks such as mining, construction, combat, and crafting (Fig.
1
) are independently assigned a coordination type (synchronous or handover) and a required agent count (
2
to
N
). Synchronous tasks also adopt game-theoretic incentives:
hard
tasks act as pure coordination problems requiring joint action for any reward
[
46
]
, while
soft
tasks function as assurance games, balancing a safe solo payoff against a larger joint-action bonus
[
50
]
(details App.
B.2
).

3.3
Soft Specialisation and Difficulty Scaling

Instead of enforcing rigid division of labour via hard role gates
[
1
,
40
]
,
alem
uses
soft specialisation
. Any agent can attempt any role-gated action, but specialists succeed deterministically (
\eta_{\text{spec}}=1
) while non-specialists succeed with probability
\eta_{\text{non}}=1-\alpha
. This rewards effective role allocation while preserving the capacity for online adaptation.

We scale overall coordination difficulty with the single scalar
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Because world generation in JAX is deterministic, varying
\alpha
preserves the spatial layout and frequency of coordination opportunities (Fig.
3
), cleanly isolating execution difficulty from world variation. Higher
\alpha
tightens execution constraints: it increases the probability of
N
-agent requirements, shortens handover windows, increases solo failure rates on soft tasks, and lowers non-specialist efficiency. We evaluate on Easy (
\alpha=0.3
), Medium (
\alpha=0.6
), and Hard (
\alpha=0.9
) settings (parameters detailed in App.
B.3
).

3.4
Environment Interface

3.4.1
RL Interface

Observation space.

alem
exposes both pixel (see Fig.
6
, App
B
) and symbolic observations of the underlying state. Each egocentric observation includes a local map, inventory, and a teammate dashboard (health, specialisation, requested resources, off-screen direction, and recent communications). Beyond Craftax-Coop,
alem
explicitly embeds communication messages, coordination states, including soft/hard requirements, handover countdowns, and multi-agent mob targets.

Action space.

Agents act in a 60-dimensional discrete action space encompassing environment manipulation, crafting, exploration, and combat. To facilitate teamwork, agents can issue persistent resource requests (active for up to 10 timesteps), execute teammate-targeted giving, and broadcast discrete messages. The primary
Do
action is contextual, it supports both environment interaction and direct agent-to-agent mechanics, such as reviving downed teammates or triggering friendly fire. We apply conservative action masking to filter strictly infeasible choices.

Achievements and rewards.

Following prior environments
[
37
,
40
]
,
alem
pairs a dense health reward with sparse, first-time rewards for completing a hierarchy of achievements. We introduce 27 novel coordination achievements spanning synchronised mining, temporal handovers, cooperative construction, and coordinated combat. Rewards are individual by default; coordination bonuses are awarded only to participating agents by default (details in App.
C.6
).

3.4.2
Text-based Language Interface

To evaluate language agents, we expose a structured text interface mapped directly to the underlying environment (details in App.
C.5
). At each timestep, models receive a textual state containing recent observation-action history, teammate messages, a private scratchpad, and visible coordination constraints, together with explicit action affordances. Models must return a structured response containing exactly one valid action and, optionally, reasoning, communication, and scratchpad fields. This parsed action is then mapped to the corresponding discrete environment step. A sample prompt is provided in App.
C.7
.

3.5
MARL Baselines

We train IPPO
[
12
]
, MAPPO
[
66
]
, PQN-VDN
[
16
]
, and HyperMARL-IPPO
[
56
]
to provide reference points for zero-shot LLM performance and to verify that the difficulty settings meaningfully affect learned policies. Figure
3
reports 1B-step performance (hyperparameter sweeps detailed in App.
C.1
). Performance sharply degrades as coordination difficulty increases; even after 3B environment steps, the strongest baseline reaches only 18% of the maximum total reward on the Hard setting (App.
C.4
). These results show that
alem
remains an unsaturated challenge for current MARL methods.

4
Experiments

We evaluate 13 modern LLMs in zero-shot homogeneous teams, with 4 trained MARL baselines as reference points. Our goal is to measure coordination in long-horizon, open-ended environments, while separating coordination difficulty from the difficulty of the underlying world. To calibrate this distinction, we compare
alem
against a single-agent baseline and find that its multi-agent dynamics result in a drop in performance, showing that coordination introduces a distinct layer of difficulty (App.
E.1
).

Our experiments ask three questions:

Q1:
How well do LLM agents coordinate zero-shot within homogeneous teams?

Q2:
How do communication, memory, and reasoning affect coordination?

Q3:
How well do agents coordinate in heterogeneous teams composed of different models?

4.1
Experimental Setup

Evaluation protocol.

We follow a zero-shot evaluation protocol similar to BALROG
[
42
]
. At each timestep, each agent receives the game rules, environment details, local observation, and legal actions in text form through the interface described in Sec.
3.4.2
. By default, agents have scratchpad memory, broadcast communication, and a text history containing the last eight observations and actions. Success in
alem
requires spatial reasoning, long-horizon planning, tracking crafting dependencies, memory, effective communication, and coordination under partial observability.

Metrics.

Our primary metric is normalised episode return, reported as a percentage of the maximum achievable reward. Following previous Craftax-based environments
[
40
,
37
]
, each agent’s cumulative reward is normalised by the maximum reward for the relevant category and averaged across agents. We report Base%, Coord.%, and Total%, each normalised independently, so Total% is not the sum of Base% and Coord.%. Reward bounds are given in App.
C.2
.

Models.

We evaluate 13 modern language models. Closed-source models are evaluated via their respective APIs (GPT-5.4
[
41
]
and Gemini 3.1 Pro
[
18
]
). Open-weight models are served with vLLM
[
32
]
and include Gemma-4-E4B-it/26B-A4B-it/31B-it
[
19
]
, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct
[
21
]
, Qwen-3.5-9B/27B/35B-A3B/122B-A10B
[
54
]
, and Qwen-3.6-27B/35B-A3B
[
43
]
. Where applicable, reasoning is enabled, with proprietary models configured to high-reasoning settings. We run 20 seeds per difficulty for open-weight models and 10 for closed-source models (due to API costs), reporting cross-seed means with 95% stratified bootstrap confidence intervals using the rliable library
[
2
]
.

Agent harness.

Language models are evaluated with the agent harness described in Sec.
3.4.2
and App.
C.5
. The harness follows a reason-then-act paradigm similar to ReAct
[
64
]
, but generates a new prompt at each step focusing the evaluation on the reasoning and coordination capabilities of the model instead of confounding factors such as context length or compaction strategy.

Base competence does not guarantee coordination.

Decomposing performance reveals failures hidden by aggregate scores. GPT-5.4-High illustrates this separation: despite achieving the second-highest Base% across difficulties (
14.1\%,10.0\%,11.8\%
), its Coord.% (

Most LLM agents do not exploit the difficulty axis.

Trained MARL agents degrade as coordination difficulty increases (Sec.
3.3
), but aggregate LLM Total% remains nearly flat at

Coordination failures differ across task structure.

The task-level breakdown in Fig.

shows that coordination is not a single axis.
Handover
tasks are comparatively more accessible because they allow temporal slack, as one agent can initiate an opportunity and another can complete it within a bounded time window. By contrast,

. The results reflect this difficulty, as Gemini-3.1-Pro-High leads LLMs on synchronous-hard coordination with only

.

Pretrained priors offer advantages in sparse, multi-stage coordination.

Construction
tasks require long-horizon planning in addition to coordinated execution. Agents must collect resources, progress through a technology chain, converge on a shared site, and execute a synchronised build. As shown in Fig.
4(a)
, construction coverage is at most
2\%
for every LLM except Gemini-3.1-Pro-High (
7\%
), and
0\%
for the MARL 1B reference. This suggests that sparse, long-horizon coordination is difficult to discover through trial-and-error at this MARL compute budget, while pretrained priors may give stronger LLMs a limited advantage.

Scale alone does not explain coordination performance.

Across open-weight models, performance is not monotonic in parameter count. Even within model families such as Gemma-4 and Qwen-3.5/3.6, coordination performance does not scale linearly across dense and mixture-of-experts variants (Figs.
4(a)
,

). This suggests that coordination depends on factors beyond raw scale, including possibly post-training, reasoning, and how effectively agents use communication.

4.2.2
Q2:
How do communication, memory, and reasoning affect coordination?

We next ablate three components of the LLM agent harness: scratchpad memory, communication, and reasoning (Sec.
4.1
). We run these ablations on the strongest closed-source and open-weight models from Sec.
4.2.1
, Gemini-3.1-Pro-High and Gemma-4-31B-it, on the Hard setting.

Communication is critical for coordination.

Removing communication produces the largest drop in coordination performance for both models (Fig.
5
). Gemini-3.1-Pro-High falls from
17.5
to
5.3
Coord.%, while Gemma-4-31B-it falls from
8.8
to
3.8
Coord.%. The corresponding changes in Base% are smaller and less consistent (Gemini
13.8\to 11.6
, Gemma
7.3\to 8.3
), suggesting that communication primarily supports coordination rather than general environment progress. Message statistics support this interpretation: agents frequently address specific teammates (Gemini
43.4\%
, Gemma
44.1\%
of messages) and often use imperative verbs (Gemini
73.7\%
, Gemma
52.2\%
). Agents therefore appear to use communication to broadcast intent, assign actions, and align timing. Without communication, they can still make base progress, but struggle to coordinate effectively (qualitative analysis in App.
I
).

Scratchpad memory helps when used for planning.

The memory ablation is model-dependent. Removing scratchpad memory lowers Gemini-3.1-Pro-High’s mean Coord.% from
17.5
to
13.1
, while leaving Base% nearly unchanged (
13.8\to 13.6
). For Gemma-4-31B-it, the mean changes are small in Coord.% (
8.8\to 8.6
) and Base% (
7.3\to 8.6
), with overlapping confidence intervals. This suggests that scratchpad memory is not uniformly useful; its effect depends on how models use it. Qualitative analysis provides a possible explanation (App.
G
). Gemini uses the scratchpad as a
forward-looking planner
:
71.5\%
of entries are multi-line,
75.3\%
contain future-oriented terms such as
will
,
next
,
then
, or
plan
, and
12.4\%
contain explicit turn-indexed action sequences such as
T9:Do
,
T10:Move West
, and
T11:Give wood to Agent 1
. In contrast, Gemma’s scratchpads are mostly one-line state summaries (average
1.08
lines), with frequent coordinate references (
98.8\%
) and status information that is often already present in the next observation. These patterns suggest that scratchpad memory appears more helpful when models use it to preserve plans or commitments across turns, rather than to restate the current state.

Reasoning supports both coordination and base progress.

Reasoning is the only ablation that lowers both Base% and Coord.% for both models (Fig.
5
). For Gemini-3.1-Pro-High, the ablation is partial because the API only allows us to reduce reasoning, not disable it. Even so, Coord.% falls from
17.5
to
9.7
and Base% from
13.8
to
10.3
. For Gemma-4-31B-it, disabling reasoning reduces Coord.% from
8.8
to
5.5
and Base% from
7.3
to
4.6
.

Qualitative traces suggest different failure modes (App.
H
). For Gemini, reduced reasoning removes much of the planner-like behaviour: scratchpad length falls from
207
to
69
characters, turn-indexed plans nearly disappear (
12.4\%\to 0.1\%
), and messages become shorter (
132\to 53
characters). Gemma instead writes longer scratchpads without reasoning (
153\to 228
characters), often near the
\sim\!1000
-character cap, suggesting that the scratchpad partly substitutes for the removed reasoning. This compensation is incomplete, as both Base% and Coord.% still decrease.

4.2.3
Q3:
How well do agents coordinate in teams composed of different models?

We evaluate heterogeneous teams on Hard in two settings: a same-family Gemma team composed of Gemma-4-E4B-it, Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it, and Gemma-4-31B-it, and a cross-family team composed of the three strongest LLM agents: Gemma-4-31B-it, GPT-5.4-High, and Gemini-3.1-Pro-High.

Heterogeneous teams perform near the homogeneous-team average.

Fig.
5
shows that heterogeneous teams perform close to the average of their corresponding homogeneous teams. The same-family Gemma team is slightly above this average (
+0.1
Total%), while the cross-family team is slightly below it (
-0.1
Total%). Thus, in the tested settings, heterogeneous teams neither collapse to the weakest member nor inherit the performance of the strongest one. This suggests that adding a stronger model to a team is not sufficient to lift team performance. Mixed teams may face additional coordination problems, as agents must align their communication conventions, planning horizons, and actions with teammates that reason and communicate differently.

Summary.

Overall,
alem
reveals coordination as a bottleneck distinct from base-task performance. Communication matters most among the ablated harness components, and initial heterogeneous-team experiments suggest that mixed teams do not automatically match their strongest member’s performance.

5
Related Work

Long-horizon and agentic evaluation.

LLMs are increasingly evaluated as agents in long-horizon, open-ended tasks
[
14
,
31
,
62
,
11
]
. Recent benchmarks test models in complex environments requiring spatial reasoning, exploration, planning, and tool use
[
42
,
67
,
68
,
27
]
. Procedural generation is especially useful because it reduces memorisation, mitigates contamination, and supports scalable variation across tasks
[
42
,
48
]
.
alem
brings these principles to multi-agent evaluation, where agents must also infer and satisfy diverse coordination demands.

Multi-Agent LLM collaboration.

Multi-agent LLMs have shown promise in code generation, debate, and collaborative reasoning
[
28
,
14
]
. Recent benchmarks evaluate LLM agents in collaborative settings
[
69
,
53
,
22
]
, but typically use shorter-horizon tasks where roles, goals, or interaction structure are specified in advance. In contrast,
alem
tests whether LLM agents can infer and execute changing coordination requirements in a long-horizon open-ended world.

MARL and coordination.

MARL benchmarks often focus on particular forms of coordination: SMAC
[
45
]
emphasises synchronous focus-fire, Overcooked
[
10
]
tests short-window handovers, and Melting Pot
[
1
]
includes long-range resource interdependence.
alem
builds on Craftax-Coop
[
40
]
, but turns coordination into an explicit, procedural, and controllable part of the environment: tasks are sampled across a coordination spectrum (Fig.
2
), agents use soft rather than hard specialisation, communication is explicit, and difficulty can be scaled. We further use information-theoretic diagnostics
[
55
]
to verify that our environment demands genuine multi-agent interdependence (App.
B.1
).

6
Conclusions

We introduce
alem
, a JAX-based benchmark for long-horizon, open-ended multi-agent coordination. Our zero-shot evaluations of 13 LLMs alongside trained MARL baselines show that coordination is a distinct bottleneck for current agents, base-task competence does not guarantee coordinated progress, and communication has the largest effect on coordinated behaviour. By exposing failure modes across communication, memory, reasoning, and team composition,
alem
makes coordination measurable and provides a controlled testbed for developing agents that coordinate effectively.

Limitations and Future Work.

Our evaluation focuses on text-based LLM agents, leaving Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Claude-family models for future work. API costs limit evaluations of proprietary models (GPT and Gemini) to 10 seeds per difficulty. Future work can use
alem
to study stronger agent harnesses, cross-episode memory, and lifelong learning in interactive multi-agent worlds.

B.2
Coordination Tasks

B.2.1
Coordination Task Categories

We instantiate coordination across four activity categories:

•

Mining.
Resource blocks (including trees, ores, gems) are sampled for coordination during world generation. Synchronous mining requires the assigned number of agents to act on the same block in the same step. Handover mining requires a second agent to mine the block within
\Delta t\in[\Delta t_{\min},\Delta t_{\max}]
steps of the first agent.

•

Construction.
Construction sites for shelters, forges, and beacons are placed on valid overworld and mining-floor tiles, independently of mining blocks. Each site receives the same synchronous or handover structure as mining, together with a soft or hard synchronous variant. Building also requires the appropriate resources for the chosen structure. In handover construction, one agent initiates a specific structure, and a second agent must complete that structure type before the deadline, otherwise the site reverts and materials are refunded to the initiator.

•

Combat.
Mob slots are pre-labelled during world generation as standard or coordinated mobs. Coordinated mobs appear as either elite melee mobs, elite ranged mobs, or large passive mobs, and each is marked as soft or hard and assigned the required agent count.

•

Crafting.
Diamond pickaxes, swords, and armour require synchronous coordination, i.e. the craft succeeds only when the required number of agents attempt it at the same Epic Forge in the same step. This is a deliberately restricted variant, it is hard coordination only and tied to a specific tool station, and tests whether agents can converge on a shared location for a one-shot joint action.

C.5
Text-based Language Interface Details

Prompt Structure.

At each timestep
t
, the prompt for agent
i
is assembled from three components: a system prompt, a history of recent observations and actions, and the current observation with instructions.

1.

System prompt:

The system prompt is shared across all time steps for an agent and defines the agent identity and role, the team size, the objective, and the rules needed to act in the environment.
By default it also optionally includes the full action catalogue with natural-language descriptions, game and coordination mechanics.

2.

Observation and Action History:

The observation and action history is a rolling sequence of recent messages from the environment and the agent itself.
The observation messages contain previous observations, labelled as “Observation from
k
step(s) ago”, and the action messages contain the action previously taken by the agent.
When memory and communication is enabled, the observation history also includes scratchpad notes and teammate messages, each labelled with the corresponding timestep.

3.

Current Observation and Action Space:

The current observation message contains textual descriptions of: achievement progress, current level, nearby terrain, items and enemies, visible coordination opportunities and requirements, teammate status, agent stats, vitals and inventory. The agent is then prompted to respond with an action.
By default, the call to action prompt also includes current legal action affordances, instructions to think step-by-step before acting, to optionally broadcast a message, and to optionally write a private scratchpad note.

The length of observation, communication, and memory history is independently configurable, and the system prompt can be ablated to control how much information about the game and coordination mechanics is disclosed to the model. The default setting includes all information in the system prompt and current observation with communication and scratchpad history enabled, but we ablate these components to probe their effects on LLM performance in
4.2.2
.

Prompt Disclosure Levels.

In order to reduce the amount of information given to the model, we only reveal specific gameplay information as the team reaches the relevant level.

Coordination Information.

The current observation includes explicit natural language descriptions of the current coordination targets and requirements.
For synchronous targets, the prompt states whether the target works solo with a bonus or requires
N
agents simultaneously, what the required action is, and which side of the target the acting agent currently occupies.
The location and vitals of teammates are included as well as their previous messages.

Action Parsing and Execution.

The model response is instructed to respond in a structured format containing exactly one action tag, optionally preceded by a think tag for reasoning, communication tags for teammate messages, and scratchpad tags for private notes.
The required action is parsed from the response and must match one of the legal actions.
If parsing fails, the agent is re-prompted with a format error.
If the model still fails to produce a valid action after the configured retry budget is exhausted, the action defaults to
Noop
.
The final parsed action is mapped back to the underlying discrete action index before stepping the environment.

Appendix G
Memory entry usage: detailed analysis

G.1
Setup

We compare how Gemini 3.1 Pro and Gemma use the persistent memory field across episodes. This is the same trajectory set used in the communication analysis:
10
Gemini episodes (
34{,}080
agent-step records) and
20
Gemma episodes (
24{,}747
records). Each record contains the persisted
scratchpad
string, which we refer to as the agent’s
memory entry
, together with the parsed action and local observation. Parse failures are negligible, under
0.5\%
for both models.

Lexical detectors.

Structural features, such as character counts and line counts, are computed directly. The remaining features in Table
9
are simple regular-expression detectors over the raw memory-entry text. These detectors are intended as interpretable proxies rather than full discourse parsers. We define them as follows:

•

Multi-line memory entry (
\geq 2
lines).
The memory entry contains at least one newline.

•

Turn-indexed plan.
The memory entry has at least two lines whose first non-whitespace tokens match
“
T

N

:
” or “
Turn

N

:
”
for an integer
N
. This captures explicit turn-by-turn plans,
such as “
T9: Do (completes handover …)
”.

•

Compact state summary.
The memory entry contains at least two
“
Field: value
” fields drawn from a fixed vocabulary of status labels
(
Pos, Position, HP, Health, Inv, Inventory, Energy, Hunger, Thirst, A1, A2, Agent
).
This captures compact one-line entries that mainly record position, resources, health, or teammate state.

•

Plan / future-tense word.

will, going to,
gonna, plan, planning, next, then, after, once, when
, plus
future-tense modal forms (
i’ll, we’ll, i will, we
will
). “Plan terms / entry” counts every match, while
“% of entries” fires if at least one match is present.

•

Past-tense / memory word.

tried, failed, was,
were, did, didn’t, couldn’t, earlier, before, already, just,
noticed, saw
.

•

Coordinate reference.
A token matching

\(?\s*-?\d+\s*,\s*-?\d+\s*\)?
,
i.e. a pair of integers, possibly wrapped in parentheses,
such as “
25,23
” or “
(0,-1)
”.

•

Teammate reference.
An “
Agent

X
” or
“
A
X
” token for
X\in\{0,1,2\}
, optionally
followed by a comma or a status colon.

•

Step-to-step turnover.
For every consecutive pair of
controlled memory entries, we compute (a) the SequenceMatcher
character similarity ratio and (b) Jaccard similarity over lower-cased
tokens. Higher values indicate more overlap between consecutive entries.

The absolute values depend on the exact detector definitions, but the qualitative differences below are stable across seeds and robust to reasonable variants of the regexes.

Three patterns stand out.

Gemini writes plans; Gemma writes state summaries.

The structural differences are large. Gemini writes

Three patterns emerge.

Gemini communicates more like a planner.

Gemini sends longer and more directive messages than Gemma. Its messages contain about
27\%
more characters, about
24\%
more sentences, and more imperative verbs per message (
1.80
vs.
1.06
). They also contain more self-references (
1.65
vs.
0.92
per message). In the trajectories, this often looks like a model maintaining a team plan: Gemini states what it is doing, assigns actions to particular teammates, and marks when the next coordinated step should happen. Gemma’s messages are shorter and more often read as local status updates.

The channel is mostly used to broadcast intent, not to deliberate.

The two models differ in tone. Gemini uses direct commands and exclamation marks more often. Gemma more often uses polite or urgent language:
please/thanks
appears in
20.9\%
of Gemma messages compared with
14.0\%
for Gemini, and urgency words appear in
21.2\%
compared with
14.1\%
. The shared pattern is equally important. Both models address a specific teammate at similar rates (
43.4\%
vs.
44.1\%
), while questions are rare for both (
1.6\%
and
1.7\%
). Thus, agents mainly use communication to announce intent, request help, and assign actions, rather than to conduct back-and-forth discussion.

Communication style mirrors scratchpad style.

The message channel reflects the same model-specific tendencies seen in the scratchpads. Planning words appear in
59.1\%
of Gemini messages but only
27.0\%
of Gemma messages; in scratchpads, the corresponding rates are
75.3\%
and
36.6\%
. The channels are not redundant: Gemma’s messages are more coordinate-heavy than its scratchpads, while Gemini’s messages are less coordinate-heavy than its scratchpads. Still, the overall pattern is consistent. Gemini uses its scratchpad as a private plan and its messages as outward-facing instructions. Gemma uses its scratchpad more like a state snapshot and its messages more like status reports or requests for help.
