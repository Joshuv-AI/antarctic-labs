A pneumolysin mutant (ΔA146 ply) induces pyroptosis in triple-negative and HER2-positive breast cancer cells via the Caspase-1/GSDME pathway: a potential antibody-drug conjugate payload
Authors: Xiaochun Tan, Jing Chen, Qinlong Yu, Junhua Tian, Jiayuan Wang, Weifeng Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe success of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) relies on potent cytotoxic payloads. Discovery of novel toxins that induce immunogenic cell death (such as pyroptosis) holds promise for overcoming tumor heterogeneity and enhancing anti-tumor immunity. Pneumolysin (Ply) is a pore-forming toxin from Streptococcus pneumoniae, but its wild-type form is overly toxic. Here we investigate the potential of a detoxified mutant, ΔA146 ply, to induce pyroptosis in triple‑negative (MDA‑MB‑231) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)‑positive (MDA‑MB‑453) breast cancer cells, using a cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) fusion to facilitate cellular entry for in vitro evaluation of its activity as a candidate ADC payload.MethodsA flexible peptide linker was used to fuse ΔA146 ply with a CPP to facilitate cellular uptake, and the fusion gene was cloned into the pET‑21a(+) prokaryotic expression vector. The recombinant fusion protein was expressed in E. coli and purified by affinity chromatography. Cellular internalization was assessed by immunofluorescence using FITC-labeled protein. Cell death modalities were evaluated by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay, SYTOX Green staining, and morphological observation. Western blotting was performed to detect the cleavage of the pyroptosis executioner gasdermin E (GSDME). Caspase-1 activity was measured in cell lysates, and the release of inflammatory factors [interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β), interleukin‑18 (IL‑18), and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1)] in the cell supernatant was determined.ResultsHigh-purity recombinant ΔA146 ply-CPP was successfully obtained. The protein efficiently entered MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells. ΔA146 ply-treated cells displayed typical pyroptotic morphology (cell swelling, large bubble-like protrusions), significantly increased LDH release, and positive SYTOX Green staining. Mechanistically, ΔA146 ply markedly activated Caspase-1, induced GSDME cleavage, and promoted the release of IL-1β, IL-18, and HMGB1. Similar effects were observed in both cell lines, with no qualitative difference in the pyroptotic mechanism.ConclusionsΔA146 ply triggers pyroptosis in breast cancer cells through the Caspase-1-mediated GSDME pathway. As a payload with immunostimulatory potential, it warrants further development for ADC applications.Keywords: Pneumolysin (Ply), ΔA146 ply, pyroptosis, triple‑negative breast cancer, HER2‑positive breast cancer

Highlight box

Key findings

• ΔA146 ply-cell-penetrating peptide induces pyroptosis, characterized by cell swelling, membrane blistering, lactate dehydrogenase release, and SYTOX Green‑positive membrane permeabilization. Mechanistically, it activates Caspase‑1, cleaves GSDME into its pore‑forming N‑terminal fragment, and promotes the release of the pro‑inflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β, interleukin-18, and the damage‑associated molecular pattern HMGB1.

What is known and what is new?

• Conventional antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) payloads (e.g., MMAE, DM1) primarily induce apoptosis, which is often immunologically silent and may promote tolerance. Pyroptosis is a lytic, pro-inflammatory cell death mechanism that can enhance anti-tumor immunity.

• This study is the first to demonstrate that a detoxified pneumolysin mutant (ΔA146 ply) triggers pyroptosis via the Caspase-1/GSDME pathway in breast cancer cells. It proposes ΔA146 ply as a novel, proteinaceous ADC payload with intrinsic pyroptosis inducing and immunostimulatory properties.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• ΔA146 ply induces pyroptosis via the Caspase-1/GSDME pathway and releases inflammatory factors, positioning it as a novel ADC payload with dual direct cytotoxicity and immunostimulatory potential, particularly suitable for apoptosis resistant or immunologically “cold” tumors.

• Next steps should include: (I) conjugating ΔA146 ply to tumor-specific antibodies to validate targeted delivery and pyroptotic activity; (II) evaluating its in vivo efficacy and safety; and (III) exploring its synergy with immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Introduction

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have emerged as a powerful class of targeted cancer therapeutics, combining the specificity of monoclonal antibodies with the potent cytotoxicity of chemical payloads. Upon binding to tumor-associated antigens, ADCs are internalized and release their toxic warheads, leading to cell death. Despite the clinical success of ADCs in various malignancies, most currently approved payloads—such as monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE), maytansinoids (DM1), and derivatives of calicheamicin—primarily induce apoptosis by interfering with microtubule dynamics or DNA (1,2). While effective, apoptotic cell death is often immunologically silent and can even promote tolerance, limiting the potential for durable anti-tumor immunity (3). Moreover, acquired resistance to apoptosis represents a major obstacle in cancer therapy, underscoring the need for alternative cell death modalities that can bypass these resistance mechanisms (4).

Pyroptosis, a lytic and pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death, has recently gained attention as an attractive mechanism for cancer treatment (5). It is characterized by cell swelling, plasma membrane rupture, and the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), ATP, and various cytokines including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18). These inflammatory mediators effectively promote anti-tumor immune responses and enhance the therapeutic effect of tumor immunotherapy (6). In breast cancer, pyroptosis induction is particularly relevant for aggressive subtypes such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2+) breast cancer. Both TNBC and HER2+ tumors often exhibit an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and are less responsive to conventional therapies (7,8). Pyroptosis can directly eliminate these resistant cells and, by releasing DAMPs and pro-inflammatory cytokines, convert “cold” tumors into “hot” ones, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immunity and offering a promising strategy for these difficult-to-treat subtypes (9).

Pyroptosis is typically executed by the gasdermin protein family, with gasdermin E (GSDME) being a key effector in many cancer cells (10). Upon cleavage by activated caspases (e.g., caspase-3 or caspase-1), the N-terminal fragment of GSDME oligomerizes to form pores in the plasma membrane, leading to osmotic lysis and cytokine release (11). The Caspase-1/GSDME pathway represents a canonical pyroptotic axis: activated Caspase-1 cleaves GSDME, releasing its pore-forming domain (12). This pathway can be induced by various therapeutic agents. For example, conventional chemotherapeutics such as cisplatin and doxorubicin have been shown to trigger GSDME-dependent pyroptosis in cancer cells (11). More recently, ADC payloads like tubulysin, when delivered by a mesothelin-targeting antibody, were found to induce GSDME cleavage and pyroptosis in breast and colon cancer models (13). These findings highlight the feasibility and therapeutic potential of developing pyroptosis-inducing ADCs. Therefore, identifying novel agents that trigger GSDME-mediated pyroptosis could provide a new class of ADC payloads with the dual benefit of direct tumor killing and immune activation.

Pneumolysin (Ply), a cholesterol-dependent cytolysin produced by Streptococcus pneumoniae, is a pore-forming toxin that can induce cell lysis and activate inflammatory responses (14). Wild-type Ply binds to cholesterol-rich membrane microdomains and oligomerizes to form large pores, causing rapid necrotic death and potent inflammasome activation (15). However, its extreme cytotoxicity and lack of target selectivity preclude its direct therapeutic application (16,17). Previous structure-function studies have identified a mutant variant, ΔA146 ply, in which the alanine residue at position 146 is deleted. This variant represents a Ply mutant deficient in hemolytic activity (18). Nevertheless, ΔA146 ply alone cannot efficiently cross the plasma membrane to reach intracellular targets, limiting its utility in cell-based assays (19).

To overcome this barrier and enable in vitro evaluation of ΔA146 ply as a potential ADC payload, we employed a cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) fusion strategy. CPPs are short cationic or amphipathic peptides capable of translocating across biological membranes and delivering conjugated cargoes into cells (20). Although CPP-mediated delivery lacks cell-type specificity, it serves as a convenient experimental tool to assess the intracellular activity of protein-based therapeutics prior to conjugation with a targeting antibody. In the context of ADC development, this approach mimics the essential delivery function that an antibody would provide, allowing us to validate the cytotoxic potential of the payload in cell culture (21-23).

In this study, we constructed a prokaryotic expression vector (pET-21a) encoding ΔA146 ply fused to a CPP via a flexible linker. The recombinant fusion protein was expressed in E. coli, purified, and characterized. Using two breast cancer cell lines representing distinct molecular subtypes—triple-negative MDA-MB-231 and HER2-positive MDA-MB-453—we investigated whether ΔA146 ply-CPP could induce pyroptosis (24). Cellular uptake was confirmed by immunofluorescence, and cell death modalities were examined through LDH release, SYTOX Green staining, and morphological observation. The underlying mechanism was explored by assessing Caspase-1 activity, GSDME cleavage, and the release of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-18) and HMGB1. The significance of this study lies in providing experimental evidence for ΔA146 ply as a potential novel payload for next-generation ADCs that induce immunogenic cell death, thereby offering a new strategy for breast cancer therapy. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0547/rc).

Methods

Materials, cell lines, antibodies and equipment

The following key materials, kits, cell lines, antibodies, and equipment were used in this study. Human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 (ATCC, HTB-26) and MDA-MB-453 (ATCC, HTB-131) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection. Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) (D1009S) was purchased from Beyotime (D1013S), the pET-21a(+) prokaryotic expression vector was from Merck/Novagen (69740-3). Antibodies used included anti-Pneumolysin (Abcam, ab71810, Cambridge, UK), anti-GSDME (Abcam, ab222408), and anti-β-Tubulin (Santa Cruz, sc-80011, Dallas, TX, USA). Kits and assays comprised the CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay for LDH release (Promega, G1780, Madison, WI, USA), fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) protein labeling kit (Beyotime, P0639S, Shanghai, China), SYTOX Green nucleic acid stain (Beyotime, C1186), Caspase-1 activity assay kit (Beyotime, C1101), IL-1β ELISA kit (Multi Sciences, EK101B, Hangzhou, China), IL-18 ELISA kit (Multi Sciences, EK118), HMGB1 ELISA kit (Solarbio, SEKH-0409, Beijing, China), Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (Beyotime, ST098), high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) medium (Gibco, C11965500BT, Grand Island, NY, USA), fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, A5256701), 4’,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Beyotime, C1002), Nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) affinity chromatography resin (Qiagen, 30210, Hilden, Germany), and enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Bio-Rad, 1705060, Hercules, CA, USA). Equipment used included a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX53F2C, Tokyo, Japan), an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Olympus CKX41), a microplate reader (BioTek ELx800) for absorbance detection at 490 nm and 405 nm, a sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) system and Polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane transfer system (Bio-Rad), a chemiluminescence imaging system (Syngene GeneGnome XRQ), and a CO2 cell culture incubator (Thermo Scientific BB15).

Plasmid construction and protein purification

The gene sequence encoding ΔA146 ply was fused at its C‑terminus via a flexible peptide linker (Gly4Ser)3 to a CPP sequence. The fusion gene was synthesized and cloned into the pET-21a(+) vector, which appends a C-terminal His‑tag for purification. The recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3). Protein expression was induced with IPTG, and the Histagged fusion protein was purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. The purified protein was identified by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting with an anti-Pneumolysin antibody (25,26).

Cell culture

Human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 were cultured under identical conditions. Both cell lines were maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 ℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 atmosphere. No differences in culture medium, serum concentration, temperature, or incubation conditions were applied between the two cell lines. MDA-MB-231 is a TNBC cell line that lacks estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression, whereas MDA-MB-453 is a HER2-positive breast cancer cell line (ER−, PR−, HER2+). The key differences between them include HER2 status, metastatic potential (MDA-MB-231 is highly invasive and mesenchymal-like, while MDA-MB-453 is less invasive and exhibits luminal characteristics). These two cell lines represent distinct molecular subtypes of breast cancer, allowing us to evaluate the pyroptosis-inducing activity of ΔA146 ply in different biological contexts (24).

Unless otherwise indicated, all treatments with ΔA146 ply-CPP were performed with a 24-hour incubation period; the only exception is the cellular internalization assay, which used a 6-hour incubation.

Immunofluorescence analysis of cellular internalization

The ΔA146 ply-CPP fusion protein was labeled with FITC dye according to the manufacturer’s instructions (27). Cells were incubated with 2 µM FITC-labeled protein for 6 hours, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with DAPI to label the cell nuclei. Fluorescence was visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX53F2C).

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

Cytotoxicity was quantitatively assessed by measuring LDH release using a CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (28). Briefly, MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells per well and cultured overnight. Cells were then treated with increasing concentrations of ΔA146 ply-CPP (0.5, 1, 2, 5 µM) for 24 h. After treatment, 50 µL of culture supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well plate and mixed with 50 µL of reconstituted substrate. The plate was incubated at room temperature for 30 min protected from light, and the reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. Spontaneous LDH release (from untreated cells) and maximum LDH release (from cells lysed with 1% Triton X-100) were also measured. The percentage of LDH release was calculated using the following formula: Percentage of LDH release=Experimental release−Spontaneous releaseMaximum release−Spontaneous release×100%

SYTOX Green staining for membrane permeability

Membrane integrity was assessed using SYTOX Green nucleic acid stain, which penetrates cells with compromised plasma membranes and fluoresces upon binding to DNA. Cells were treated with 2 µM ΔA146 ply-CPP for 24 h. After treatment, SYTOX Green dye was added to the culture medium, and the cells were incubated for 15 min at 37 ℃ in the dark. The cells were then gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and counterstained with DAPI to label cell nuclei. Finally, the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX53F2C). Positive SYTOX Green nuclear staining (green fluorescence) indicated a loss of membrane integrity.

Morphological observation of pyroptosis

MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells were treated with 2 µM ΔA146 ply-CPP fusion protein for 24 hours. After the treatment, the cells were observed under an inverted phase-contrast microscope, and typical pyroptotic morphological changes (e.g., cell swelling and membrane blistering) were captured and recorded.

Western blotting

Following treatment with 2 µM ΔA146 ply-CPP for 24 h, total cellular proteins were extracted. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were probed with antibodies against GSDME (recognizing both full-length and N-terminal fragment). Blots were developed using ECL reagents. β-Tubulin was used as a loading control. The relative intensity of each protein band was quantified and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Caspase-1 activity assay

After treatment with 2 µM ΔA146 ply-CPP for 24 hours, cells were lysed according to the protocol provided with the Caspase-1 activity assay kit (29). The enzymatic activity of Caspase-1 in the cell lysates was then determined using the same kit. Briefly, the absorbance at 405 nm (A405) was measured to quantify the production of p-nitroaniline (pNA), as this absorbance value directly reflects the cleavage of the specific substrate Ac-YVAD-pNA and is proportional to the Caspase-1 activity in the samples.

ELISA for inflammatory factors

After treatment with 2 µM ΔA146 ply-CPP for 24 hours, cell culture supernatants were collected. The concentrations of IL‑1β, IL18, and HMGB1 were measured using commercial ELISA kits following the manufacturers’ instructions (30-32).

Statistical analysis

Data represent the mean ±standard deviation (SD) of three independent biological experiments (n=3). Student’s t-test was applied for pairwise comparisons. Differences with P<0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Expression, purification, and identification of ΔA146 ply-CPP protein

The recombinant plasmid pET-21a-ΔA146 ply-CPP was successfully constructed and verified by DNA sequencing (Figure 1A). After IPTG induction, the target protein was purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography, and SDS-PAGE analysis of the purified sample revealed a distinct specific protein band at approximately 55 kDa (Figure 1B). Western blotting with an anti-Pneumolysin antibody further identified a single specific band at the expected molecular weight, confirming the successful expression of the recombinant fusion protein (Figure 1C).

To assess cellular internalization, we labeled ΔA146 ply-CPP with FITC and incubated it with MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells. Fluorescence microscopy showed strong green fluorescence distributed throughout the cytoplasm after 6 h of incubation, indicating that the fusion protein efficiently entered the cells (Figure 2). This confirms that the CPP moiety effectively delivers ΔA146 ply into breast cancer cells, providing a basis for subsequent cytotoxicity studies

ΔA146 ply-CPP induces pyroptotic cell death characterized by membrane disruption and typical morphology

To characterize the cell death induced by ΔA146 ply-CPP, we performed multiple assays to assess plasma membrane integrity and morphological changes. LDH release assays showed that treatment with ΔA146 ply-CPP for 24 h resulted in a significant and dose‑dependent increase in LDH release in both MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells (Figure 3A). At the highest concentration tested (5 µM), LDH release reached approximately 41.3% in MDA-MB-231 cells and 35.7% in MDA-MB-453 cells. These results indicate loss of plasma membrane integrity, a hallmark of lytic cell death. Based on these dose-response data, we selected 2 µM for subsequent mechanistic studies, as this concentration induced a clear and reproducible increase in LDH release—indicative of membrane damage—while avoiding potential non-specific cellular disintegration that could interfere with detection of upstream signaling events (Caspase-1 activation, GSDME cleavage) and measurement of released inflammatory factors.

To further confirm membrane disruption, we performed live-cell SYTOX Green staining, a membrane-impermeable dye that fluoresces upon binding to nucleic acids only in cells with compromised membranes. As shown in Figure 3B,3C, ΔA146 ply-CPP-treated cells exhibited bright green fluorescence in the nuclei, indicating that the plasma membrane had become permeable. In contrast, PBS control cells showed only a weak SYTOX Green signal.

Striking morphological changes were observed in ΔA146 ply-CPP-treated cells under white light. As shown in Figure 3D, treated MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells displayed characteristic features of pyroptosis, including cell swelling, plasma membrane ballooning, and the formation of large bubble-like protrusions (pyroptotic bodies). PBS control cells maintained normal morphology with intact cell membranes. Collectively, these data demonstrate that ΔA146 ply-CPP induces pyroptotic cell death accompanied by membrane permeabilization and typical pyroptotic morphology.

ΔA146 ply-CPP activates the Caspase-1/GSDME pathway and promotes inflammatory factor release

To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying ΔA146 ply-CPP-induced pyroptosis, we examined key components of the pyroptotic pathway. Western blotting analysis showed that in PBS control cells, GSDME was present predominantly as the full-length protein (Figure 4A). However, after treatment with ΔA146 ply-CPP (2 µM, 24 h), a cleaved N-terminal fragment (GSDME-N) appeared in both cell lines, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in full-length GSDME. This cleavage pattern indicates activation of the pyroptotic executioner. Since GSDME can be cleaved by activated Caspase-1, we next measured Caspase-1 activity. As shown in Figure 4B, treatment with ΔA146 ply-CPP significantly increased Caspase-1 activity in both MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells, these data suggest that ΔA146 ply-CPP activates the Caspase 1-mediated GSDME pathway.

Pyroptosis is characterized by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and DAMPs. ELISA assays revealed that ΔA146 ply-CPP treatment significantly increased the levels of IL-1β, IL-18, and HMGB1 in the culture supernatants of both breast cancer cell lines (Figure 4C). The activation pattern of the Caspase-1/GSDME pathway was similar in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells, with no qualitative differences in the mechanism of pyroptosis induced by ΔA146 ply-CPP.

Discussion

In this study, we employed a CPP fusion strategy to deliver the Ply mutant ΔA146 ply into breast cancer cells, enabling the in vitro evaluation of its pyroptosis-inducing activity as a potential ADC payload. Our results demonstrate that ΔA146 ply-CPP efficiently enters MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells and triggers pyroptosis via the Caspase-1-mediated GSDME pathway. This conclusion is supported by multiple lines of evidence: dose-dependent LDH release indicating loss of membrane integrity, positive SYTOX Green staining confirming membrane permeabilization, typical pyroptotic morphology including cell swelling and bubble‑like protrusions, cleavage of GSDME to generate its pore‑forming N-terminal fragment, significantly increased Caspase-1 activity, and elevated release of IL-1β, IL-18, and HMGB1. Importantly, the core pyroptotic events—GSDME cleavage, Caspase-1 activation, and IL-1β/IL-18/HMGB1 release—were consistently induced in both MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells, demonstrating that ΔA146 ply‑CPP engages the same mechanism irrespective of breast cancer subtype. Collectively, these findings establish ΔA146 ply as a promising candidate payload for nextgeneration ADCs designed to induce immunogenic cell death (13).

The use of a CPP in this study serves as an experimental tool to overcome the poor membrane permeability of ΔA146 ply, allowing us to assess its intracellular activity in cell culture. This approach mimics the essential delivery function that an antibody would provide in an ADC context, albeit without cell‑type selectivity. CPP-mediated delivery is a well-established strategy for preliminary evaluation of protein-based therapeutics in vitro (12). Our results confirm that once internalized, ΔA146 ply is capable of engaging the pyroptotic machinery, validating its potential as an ADC payload. The combination of LDH release, SYTOX Green staining, and morphological observation provided comprehensive evidence that the mode of death is pyroptosis rather than apoptosis, a distinction critical for its proposed immunostimulatory application (33,34).

Conventional ADC toxins such as auristatins (MMAE) and maytansinoids (DM1) primarily target microtubules and induce apoptosis (1,2). Although clinically effective, apoptotic cell death is often immunologically silent and can even promote tolerance, limiting the potential for durable anti-tumor immunity (3). In contrast, ΔA146 ply‑induced pyroptosis leads to cell rupture and release of DAMPs (HMGB1) and pro‑inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-18). Our quantitative ELISA data (Figure 4C) confirmed that ΔA146 ply-CPP significantly increased the secretion of IL-1β, IL-18, and HMGB1. The increase in IL-1β and IL-18 is consistent with the elevated Caspase-1 activity we observed (Figure 4B), as active Caspase-1 directly processes pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature, secreted forms. Together with the detection of GSDME cleavage (Figure 4A), these findings establish that ΔA146 ply-CPP triggers a functional Caspase-1/GSDME pyroptotic pathway, culminating in the release of both classical pyroptotic cytokines and the DAMP HMGB1. These inflammatory mediators effectively promote anti-tumor immune responses and enhance the therapeutic effect of tumor immunotherapy. This immunogenic cell death profile positions ΔA146 ply as a payload that not only kills tumor cells directly but also potentially reshapes the tumor microenvironment to favor immunemediated clearance (9).

Recent studies have begun to explore the potential of ADCs to induce pyroptosis, though research in this area remains limited. A 2023 study by Wittwer et al. demonstrated that a mesothelin-targeting ADC carrying the tubulysin payload induced GSDME cleavage and pyroptotic cell death in breast and colon cancer models, with GSDME expression being critical for therapeutic efficacy (13). Another recent investigation reported that an antibody-indocyanine green conjugate could trigger pyroptosis via cathepsin S-mediated GSDMD cleavage upon near-infrared light irradiation, highlighting the versatility of ADC-based pyroptosis induction strategies (35). These emerging studies, together with a growing body of evidence implicating GSDME-mediated pyroptosis in anti-tumor immunity, underscore the increasing recognition of pyroptosis-inducing ADCs as a promising therapeutic avenue. However, to date, most reported pyroptosis-inducing ADCs rely on conventional small-molecule payloads (e.g., tubulysin) or require external stimuli such as light activation (36). Our study introduces ΔA146 ply, a proteinaceous toxin derived from Ply, as a novel class of ADC payload that inherently engages the pyroptotic machinery upon cellular entry. Mechanistically, once internalized into breast cancer cells, ΔA146 ply activates caspase-1, which then cleaves gasdermin E (GSDME) at a specific site to release its cytotoxic N-terminal fragment (GSDME-NT). This fragment oligomerizes and forms membrane pores, leading to cell swelling, membrane rupture, and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, as well as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) like HMGB1 (37,38). Unlike conventional ADC payloads that primarily induce apoptosis, ΔA146 ply directly triggers pyroptosis via the canonical caspase-1/GSDME pathway, a mechanism that does not require prior apoptotic signaling. This intrinsic pyroptosis-inducing capacity positions ΔA146 ply as a promising immunogenic payload for next-generation ADCs.

The molecular mechanism we observed—Caspase-1 activation and GSDME cleavage—is consistent with recent studies highlighting GSDME as a key executioner of pyroptosis in cancer cells (10,11). GSDME is highly expressed in various cancer types, including breast cancer, making it an attractive target for pyroptosisbased therapies (39). Our finding that ΔA146 ply-CPP engages the Caspase-1/GSDME pathway adds to the growing understanding of how bacterial toxins can exploit host cell death machinery. The cleavage of GSDME coupled with increased Caspase-1 activity strongly suggests that ΔA146 ply activates an upstream inflammasome, though the precise molecular details remain to be elucidated in future studies (36,37).

It is important to emphasize that the CPP fusion is not intended for therapeutic application but rather as a research tool for in vitro proof-of-concept. For ADC development, ΔA146 ply would be chemically conjugated to a tumor‑specific antibody, replacing the CPP with a targeting moiety that ensures selective delivery to cancer cells. Our findings provide the necessary preclinical rationale for such efforts. Future studies should focus on optimizing conjugation chemistry to link ΔA146 ply to antibodies while preserving its pyroptosis-inducing activity, evaluating the specificity, efficacy, and safety of ΔA146 ply-based ADCs in in vivo tumor models, and investigating the immunogenic potential of ΔA146 ply-induced pyroptosis in the tumor microenvironment, particularly its ability to synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that ΔA146 ply, when delivered intracellularly via a CPP, induces pyroptosis in breast cancer cells through the Caspase-1-mediated GSDME pathway, accompanied by robust release of inflammatory mediators (Figure 5). These results highlight its potential as a next-generation ADC payload with immunostimulatory properties, particularly for treating immunologically cold tumors such as triple‑negative breast cancer.
