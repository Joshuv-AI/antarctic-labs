ABSTRACT
Plasma-surface interfaces are dynamically coupled, multiscale systems whose behavior is important
for applications including plasma etching, fusion materials, plasma delayering, and high-voltage
components. Predictive modeling remains challenging because atomistic surface processes modify
plasma-facing materials while the evolving surface state, in turn, controls particle exchange at the
interface. This report describes progress toward an atomistic-informed, machine-learned surface
model using the silicon-chlorine-argon system as an exemplar for chemically mediated plasma
etching. A molecular dynamics bombardment model was developed to generate cumulative
chlorination and argon etching trajectories, which were post-processed into depth-resolved
composition and crystallinity profiles and reduced to near-surface and mixed-layer descriptors. A
single-impact differential surface-evolution model was trained using polynomial regression with
ridge regularization. The final model reproduced out-of-sample surface-state trajectories for both
initial and subsequent etching cycles. These results establish a foundation for future plasma-surface
boundary models that couple surface evolution with particle exchange in larger-scale plasma
simulations.

1. INTRODUCTION
Plasma-surface interfaces (PSIs) are multiscale, dynamically coupled systems governed by physical
and chemical processes that span atomistic to device-level length and time scales. Their behavior has
motivated sustained research across many application areas, including plasma-facing components in
fusion reactors, semiconductor processing, plasma delayering, and high-voltage systems. A core
challenge in modeling PSIs is that the plasma and surface exchange particles, energy, and charge
through interactions occurring in the plasma sheath, the boundary layer connecting the material
surface to the bulk plasma. Within the sheath, plasma ions are accelerated toward the surface and
can drive sputtering, implantation, deposition, chemical modification, and microstructural evolution.
At the same time, reflected plasma species, sputtered or etched surface products, and emitted
electrons can alter the plasma composition and sheath dynamics. As a result, the interface response
depends jointly on the ion energy distribution function (IEDF) incident on the surface and on the
evolving surface state. Accurately predicting surface evolution therefore requires resolving this
feedback between the plasma conditions and the material state at the interface.
The fundamental surface processes that mediate this coupling occur at atomic scales and on sub-
nanosecond timescales, while relevant plasma structures can extend from micron-scale sheaths to
macroscopic bulk scales and observable changes in surface morphology or composition may occur
over millisecond or longer timescales [1], [2]. This separation of scales has made predictive PSI
modeling a longstanding challenge. Existing approaches often couple separate plasma and surface
models or use reduced descriptions of particle-surface interactions to make the problem
computationally tractable. For example, surface interactions are frequently represented using binary-
collision-approximation models [3], [4], tabulated or phenomenological yields [1], while the sheath is
sometimes treated with semi-analytic models to estimate the IEDF of surface-incident species [5].
These methods have enabled substantial progress in modeling plasma-material systems across many
applications [1], [3], [4], [6], but they can lose important atomistic detail and often cannot capture
transient feedback between an evolving surface state and the plasma. This limitation motivates the
development of reduced-order surface models that retain the essential atomistic physics of particle
exchange while remaining efficient enough for coupling to larger-scale plasma simulations.

This project takes steps toward bridging the gap between atomistic surface simulations and plasma-
scale modeling by developing a workflow for producing reduced-order, atomistic-informed surface
models. The long-term vision is to couple mesoscale surrogate models for evolving surfaces into
kinetic or fluid plasma simulations as dynamic boundary conditions. In this approach, molecular
dynamics simulations are used to generate training data for atomistic surface processes, including
surface modification and particle exchange resulting from individual bombardment events. Machine-
learning (ML) models are then trained to capture these processes in a reduced representation that

 8

evolves surface-state-dependent rates of reflection, sputtering, etching, implantation, and other
exchange processes as functions of incident particle conditions and surface state.

 Figure 1. Schematic of atomistic-informed surface model coupling to plasma simulations [7].

Figure 1 illustrates this multiscale coupling strategy. Atomistic surface configurations are reduced to
a tractable set of surface-state descriptors that retain information relevant to particle exchange at the
interface. Plasma species crossing the computational boundary provide the surface model with
incident particle properties such as species, energy, angle, and computational weight. The surface
model then updates the reduced surface state and determines the particles returned to the plasma
domain. The resulting model is lightweight and is naturally suited for implementation as a boundary
model in kinetic plasma codes such as Aleph and EMPIRE, where it can enable two-way coupling
between plasma dynamics and evolving material surfaces.

This project used the silicon-chlorine-argon (Si-Cl-Ar) plasma-surface system as an exemplar for
developing and testing an atomistic-informed workflow for machine-learned surface model
development. The Si-Cl-Ar system is relevant to plasma etching applications because surface
chlorination modifies the near-surface bonding environment and enables ion-assisted removal of
silicon-containing products. More generally, the system provides a useful test case for developing
reduced-order models for chemically mediated plasma-surface interactions because particle exchange
depends on both incident ion conditions and the evolving chemical state of the surface.

A LAMMPS model was developed for generating atomistic training data from cumulative
bombardment simulations [8]. The model simulates individual particle impacts on an evolving
surface, records the pre- and post-impact surface states, and identifies reflected and sputtered
particles produced by each event. Several features were incorporated to make large-scale data
generation practical, including energy-dependent selection of the evolved surface depth, fixed and
non-interacting layers to reduce unnecessary force calculations, configurable incident particle energy
and angular distributions, and automated detection and removal of outgoing particles.

Although applied here to the Si-Cl-Ar system, the model was designed to be extensible to other
plasma-surface systems with suitable interatomic potentials. For example, in parallel with this effort,
a concurrently funded Quick Targeted Investigation (QTI) project adapted the LAMMPS model by
modifying the interatomic potentials to simulate a tungsten-carbon (W-C) PSI [9]. In that project,
cumulative molecular dynamics simulations of energetic carbon bombardment of tungsten were
used to generate training data for surface-state-dependent particle exchange models. Atomistic

 9

surface configurations were reduced to a small set of descriptors characterizing near-surface
composition and crystallinity, and ML methods were used to predict both surface-state evolution
and emitted particle characteristics. The QTI demonstrated that particle exchange at the interface
can depend strongly on the evolving surface state and provided an early proof of concept for the
broader atomistic-to-surrogate modeling workflow developed in the present work.

A key insight from the QTI project was that particle exchange was dominated by the outermost few
angstroms of the surface and could be accurately predicted using a reduced set of descriptors related
to species concentrations and crystallinity. The primary technical challenge was the development of
an ML model capable of accurately evolving the surface state – even evolving the small near-surface
region required a significant amount of data generation. This challenge is expected to be even more
important in the Si-Cl-Ar system due to the increased complexity of chemically mediated plasma-
surface interactions and is therefore a central focus of the ML work described in this report.

In this project, the LAMMPS capability was used to generate cumulative Si-Cl-Ar bombardment
data for ML model development. The resulting atomistic outputs were post-processed into event-
level quantities describing the incident particle/molecule, the evolving surface state, and the emitted
or reflected products. These data formed the basis for an atomistic-informed, reduced-order ML
surface model for plasma etching, with primary emphasis on learning the surface-state evolution
component of the model.

The developed workflow provides a path for translating molecular dynamics simulations of
individual plasma-surface interactions into mesoscale models that are lightweight enough for future
coupling to plasma simulations. While direct integration of the ML surface model into a kinetic or
fluid plasma code was ultimately outside the scope of this effort, the LAMMPS data-generation
capability, post-processing tools, and ML modeling approach developed here establish the core
components needed for such coupling in future work.

The remainder of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the LAMMPS model
developed for atomistic bombardment simulations. Section 3 describes the Si-Cl-Ar data-generation
campaign and the post-processing procedures used to convert atomistic simulation outputs into
quantities suitable for ML model development. Section 4 presents the ML approach, with emphasis
on the surface-state representation, surface-evolution model, and particle-exchange quantities
extracted from the atomistic data. Section 5 summarizes the primary conclusions, discusses
limitations of the present effort, and identifies recommended directions for future work toward
coupled plasma-surface simulation.

2. LAMMPS MODEL FOR ATOMISTIC BOMBARDMENT SIMULATIONS

The atomistic training data for this project were generated using a LAMMPS model designed to
simulate individual particle impacts on a plasma-facing surface. The model was developed to support
cumulative bombardment studies in which many independent impact events are applied sequentially
to evolve the surface. This impact-by-impact procedure provides a natural data-generation pathway
for ML models because the pre-impact surface state, incident particle properties, post-impact surface
state, and emitted/reflected particle products can all be recorded for each bombardment event.
Although the simulations discussed in this report focus on the Si-Cl-Ar system, the LAMMPS model
was designed to be extensible to other plasma-surface systems. For example, during this project, the
LAMMPS model was adapted to meet the needs of a concurrent QTI project focused on developing
a surface-state-dependent particle exchange model for a tungsten-carbon PSI.

 Figure 2. LAMMPS schematic of simulation domain.

Figure 2 shows an atomistic representation of a chlorinated silicon surface in LAMMPS and a
schematic of the simulation domain, highlighting several important features of the model. The

 11

model is structured around the assumption that the plasma-surface interactions of interest can be
represented as a sequence of independent impact events, with no simultaneous impacts occurring
within the atomistic surface domain. In this representation, an energetic incident particle is injected
toward the surface, the incident particle and surface atoms are evolved for a short time sufficient to
resolve sputtering, reflection, implantation, and near-surface rearrangement, outgoing particles are
detected and removed, and the remaining surface is relaxed using a Langevin thermostat prior to the
next impact. Repeating this process enables cumulative bombardment trajectories to be generated
while preserving event-level information needed to learn the relationship between incident particle
conditions, surface state, and particle exchange at the interface.

2.1. Surface Geometry and Boundary Conditions
The simulation domain is initialized as a room temperature crystalline silicon slab with an exposed
surface that can be chlorinated and etched through successive impacts by plasma species. The silicon
substrate is initialized in a diamond-cubic lattice with a lattice spacing of 5.43 Å. Periodic boundary
conditions are applied in the x-y plane, while the z-direction corresponds to the surface-normal
direction and surface depth. The back of the slab is fixed to provide a stable reference frame for the
surface, and the front of the domain is open to facilitate particle injection and allow reflected and
sputtered particles to leave the material.
The dimensions of the simulation domain were chosen to reduce computational cost while avoiding
significant finite-size artifacts in the simulated surface evolution. This balance is important because
the computational cost of MD simulations scales strongly with the number of atoms included in the
domain, and the data requirements for training machine-learned surface models require many
cumulative bombardment trajectories. The domain depth must be sufficient to capture the region
over which incident particles implant, mix, or amorphize the surface, but it is undesirable to include
unnecessary bulk material far from the active surface region. For the conditions studied here, the
depth of the domain was chosen to be twice the depth amorphized by 200 eV Ar bombardments.
The lateral cross-sectional area must be sufficiently large to prevent the periodic boundaries from
artificially influencing the surface response, but should likewise be minimized to avoid unnecessary
force calculations. In practice, simulations were performed at the highest bombardment energy of
interest using different lateral domain sizes to determine the smallest cross section for which the
surface evolution no longer exhibited significant deviations. For the conditions studied here, with
bombardment energies up to 200 eV, the smallest acceptable lateral side length was determined to
be 10 silicon lattice units, which corresponds to a lateral domain size of approximately 5.4 nm x 5.4
nm.

2.2. Energy Dependent Fixed Layer
To reduce computational cost during large-scale data generation, the LAMMPS model uses an
energy dependent fixed layer rather than a fixed layer at a single prescribed depth. Data generation in
this project required bombardment simulations over a relatively broad range of incident energies,
from 10 eV to 200 eV. At the low end of this range, incident particles only perturb the outermost
few lattice layers, making it unnecessary to evolve the full depth of the simulation domain. At higher
energies, the surface response extends deeper into the slab, requiring a larger mobile region. Because
the total depth of the simulation domain is fixed, increasing the depth of the fixed layer reduces the
number of atoms that must be evolved for lower-energy impacts.

 12

Atoms in the simulation domain are divided into mobile atoms fixed atoms. The mobile atoms
occupy the near-surface region and are time integrated during the impact and relaxation portions of
the simulation to capture the atomistic surface response. The depth of the mobile region is selected
based on the energy of the bombarding particle and is chosen to extend twice as deep as the region
expected to be perturbed by the impact. The remaining atoms below this depth are fixed by setting
their forces equal to zero.
Fixing atoms in place by setting their force values to zero satisfies the boundary condition but does
not by itself reduce computational cost because this occurs as a force override (i.e., the force
calculation still occurs but is ignored). The dominant cost in these simulations comes from
interatomic force calculations. To address this issue, the fixed portion of the domain is separated
into two groups. The group interfacing the mobile atoms remains physically interacting with the
evolved particles and has a depth of two lattice layers to ensure sufficient atoms for force
calculation. The rest of the fixed layer is modified to be non-interacting by temporarily replacing the
particles with placeholder particles of each type, which are each characterized by zero interaction
potential. Prior to writing the surface state to file, each particle is changed back to the correct type.
The approach using a non-interacting layer was pursued because there isn’t an intrinsic way to tell
LAMMPS not to compute forces for specific particles. All particles loaded into the simulation
domain are assigned to a processor and have their forces calculated whether the particles are being
evolved or not because fixed particles in principle can still influence the trajectory of evolved
particles. By using dynamic load balancing, LAMMPS identifies the low computational cost of the
non-interacting fixed layer and distributes those particles evenly across processors. Without the
dynamic load balancing, LAMMPS would by default assign these particles to same processor due to
their spatial proximity, and the slow processors that were assigned the real particles would still
bottleneck the system.

2.3. Single-Impact Simulation Procedure
The LAMMPS model is built around individual bombardment events. For each impact, an incident
particle is initialized above the surface at 𝑡 = 0 in a random position within the x-y plane with a
prescribed energy and trajectory. The injected particle and the mobile portion of the domain are
evolved for 5 ps using an adaptive Stoermer-Verlet time integration scheme. The adaptive timestep
is important for efficiently resolving both high-energy collision events, which require small timesteps
prior to and during the early phase of surface evolution, and periods of evolution that can be
evolved with larger timesteps once the impact energy has been distributed throughout the surface.
The largest timestep used here is 0.5 fs.
Following the 5-ps impact evolution, the surface is relaxed using a Langevin thermostat at 300 K for
3 ps. It is necessary to thermostat the surface prior to subsequent impacts because the simulation
domain is isolated from the rest of the bulk surface that would normally carry the energy away. This
is also the reason that the surface evolution is truncated at 5 ps, which is roughly the time it takes for
the energy to distribute throughout the evolved surface. This places a limitation on the particle flux
that can be captured by this model, but it is sufficient to capture even moderate fusion relevant
particle fluxes.
For each bombardment event, the model writes the relevant information needed for downstream
post-processing and ML model development. This includes the pre-impact surface state, the incident
particle energy and angle, the post-impact surface state, and the species, energy, and trajectory of
outgoing particles classified as reflected or sputtered products. This event-level data structure is

 13

central to the surface-modeling workflow because it directly maps the atomistic simulation output
onto the input-output structure required for reduced-order ML model training.

2.4. Detection and Removal of Outgoing Particles
Correctly identifying sputtered and reflected particles is one of the more delicate aspects of the
input-deck workflow. Many energetic outgoing particles leave the near-surface region quickly and are
straightforward to detect. However, some emitted particles have low kinetic energy and remain near
the surface at the end of the evolution period, while others are ejected at high angles and may travel
laterally through the periodic domain before reaching a large height above the surface. A practical
criterion is therefore required to determine when a particle is no longer considered part of the
surface and should instead be classified as an outgoing product.
In this model, the instantaneous position of the outer surface is determined dynamically throughout
the evolution of each impact. The surface position, 𝑧 , is estimated using the position of the 20th
outermost particle in the simulation. This approach avoids defining the surface based on the single
highest atom, which may itself be a sputtered particle or a transiently displaced atom. For the
conditions studied here, events producing more than 15 sputtered particles were not observed,
making the 20th outermost atom a reasonable and robust estimator of the true surface location.
A particle is classified as outgoing if its position exceeds the dynamically determined surface position
by a prescribed margin. Specifically, particles are identified as sputtered or reflected once their z-
position satisfies

 𝑧 >𝑧 +Δ

where Δ was chosen to be 1.25 silicon lattice units. This margin reduces the likelihood that atoms
belonging to the thermally roughened or transiently displaced surface are incorrectly classified as
emitted particles. Once particles are identified as outgoing, their properties are recorded and the
particles are removed.

 Figure 3. Surface level detection and sputtering margin for roughened surface.

Dynamic removal of outgoing particles also has an important computational benefit. The adaptive
timestep used during impact evolution is influenced by the highest velocity particle in the simulation.
If sputtered or reflected particles are allowed to remain in the mobile group after they have clearly
left the surface, they can unnecessarily constrain the timestep and increase the cost of the

 14

calculation. Promptly identifying and removing outgoing particles therefore improves both the
physical bookkeeping of particle exchange and the computational efficiency of the simulation.

2.5. Si-Cl-Ar Interatomic Potentials
The LAMMPS model requires interatomic potentials (IAPs) capable of representing both the
chemically reactive Si-Cl surface and energetic Ar bombardment. For the Si-Cl-Ar simulations in this
project, interactions involving Ar were represented using Ziegler-Biersack-Littmark (ZBL) potential.
This choice is appropriate for the inert Ar projectile because the relevant Ar interactions during
bombardment are dominated by short-range repulsion with Si and Cl atoms rather than chemical
bonding.
The Si-Si, Si-Cl, and Cl-Cl interactions were represented using a modified Tersoff model. The
parameters for this model were chosen to reproduce, as closely as possible, the behavior of the
reactive bond-order potential used by Vella et al. [10]. Direct use of that potential was not possible
because the published implementation relied on a modified version of LAMMPS that was not
available for this project. As a result, the project team worked with Sandia staff to implement an
approximate representation of the potential within the available LAMMPS framework.
The resulting potential should be understood as a practical model for generating atomistic training
data for Si-Cl-Ar plasma-surface interactions, rather than as a newly optimized or fully revalidated
IAP. Although not shown here, the simulated sputtering and etching behavior was found to be
qualitatively consistent with expected trends and with values reported in the literature. In particular,
the model produced physically reasonable surface chlorination, Ar-induced material removal, and
cumulative surface modification over the range of bombardment conditions considered here.

3. DATA GENERATION AND POST-PROCESSING

3.1. Overview of Data-Generation Strategy

The data-generation strategy was designed to characterize cyclic chlorination and Ar etching of Si.
At a high level, the objective was to begin with a thermalized crystalline Si slab, chlorinate the
exposed surface by exposing it to room-temperature Cl 2 gas, etch the chlorinated surface with
monoenergetic Ar beams, and repeat this chlorination/etching sequence to sample the surface-state
space associated with cyclic plasma etching. This approach provides atomistic trajectories for
learning both the immediate response of a chlorinated surface to ion bombardment and the longer-
term saturation behavior that emerges after repeated surface modification and material removal.

This strategy was informed by findings from the concurrent QTI effort [9]. In that project, particle
exchange was found to be dominated by the outermost few Angstroms of the surface. Even with
this relatively localized surface representation, however, accurate prediction of surface-state
evolution remained the primary technical challenge. In the present work, the data-generation strategy
was designed to extend surface-state tracking beyond the immediate near-surface layer to capture
how material is mixed deeper into the surface during etching. The chemically mediated Si-Cl-Ar
etching process provides a useful test case for this because chlorine is deposited onto the outer
surface during chlorination and subsequently mixed into the substrate during Ar etching. The QTI
also showed that changes in surface state can be highly stochastic from impact to impact, and the
randomly sampled particle energies in that project were identified as a potential reason for the
difficulty in resolving surface state evolution. For this reason, the surface-evolution trajectories in
this project were generated using monoenergetic Ar beams, which provide a controlled way to
resolve mean trends in surface evolution as a function of incident energy. In instances where
statistics were being generated at specific surface states, at least 50 monoenergetic impacts were used
rather than randomly sampling energies.

3.2. Surface chlorination

Each trajectory begins with a crystalline Si slab that is thermalized to 300 K using a Langevin
thermostat. The surface is then chlorinated by exposing it to a room-temperature gas of Cl 2
molecules, allowing Cl to bond naturally to the exposed Si surface rather than imposing a prescribed
chlorinated configuration. Room-temperature Cl 2 exposure was found to saturate at approximately
one monolayer; higher-energy chlorine bombardment can produce thicker chlorine-containing
layers, but that regime was not explored here.

Figure 4 shows lateral and top-down views of the monolayer formed on the crystalline Si slab, along
with the evolution of the species concentrations in the outermost 2 Å of the surface. This averaging
window was chosen to match the near-surface region identified in the QTI as dominant for particle
exchange. The Cl concentration in this window does not asymptote to 1 because the window is
thicker than the chlorinated monolayer. For the purposes of this work, the relevant criterion was
saturation of the bonded Cl layer, and the configuration after 4000 impacts was selected as the
representative monolayer-chlorinated surface for subsequent Ar etching simulations.

The efficiency features described in Section 2.2 were particularly important for the chlorination
portion of the data-generation campaign. Because room-temperature Cl 2 exposure primarily
modifies only the outermost few lattice layers, only a shallow near-surface region needed to be

 16

evolved during chlorination. Accurate tracking of the surface position was also important because
the dynamically determined surface level allowed Cl 2 molecules to be injected close to the exposed
surface rather than far into the vacuum region. This reduced the time required for incident
molecules to reach the surface and improved the computational efficiency of the chlorination
simulations.

3.3. Argon Etching

Following chlorination, the monolayer-chlorinated surface was used as the initial condition for Ar
etching simulations. The surface was bombarded with monoenergetic Ar beams to generate
trajectories describing how the chlorinated surface evolves as a function of incident ion energy and
cumulative exposure. This monoenergetic sampling strategy was chosen to resolve mean trends in
surface evolution while avoiding the additional variance introduced by randomly sampled incident
energies. For each Ar energy, the same monolayer configuration was used as the initial condition,
allowing differences in the resulting trajectories to be attributed to the incident ion energy.

Figure 5 shows representative snapshots of the surface during Ar bombardment. At zero impacts,
the surface is approximately monolayer chlorinated. After 100 Ar impacts, amorphization of the
near-surface Si has begun, but the chlorinated layer remains partially intact. By 500 impacts, the

 17

monolayer has been significantly etched, although the remaining Cl is still concentrated near the
surface. After 2000 impacts, most of the original chlorinated layer has been removed, and residual Cl
is mixed within the amorphized near-surface region.

 Figure 5. Atomistic surface evolution due to argon etching.

These trajectories illustrate why the Si-Cl-Ar system is a useful test case for surface-state-dependent
modeling. Ar bombardment does not simply remove a static chlorinated layer; it simultaneously
etches surface material, amorphizes the Si substrate, and mixes residual Cl into the modified region.
Capturing this evolution requires tracking both near-surface chemistry and depth-dependent
structural modification, which motivates the post-processing and surface-state representation
described in the next section.

3.4. Depth-Resolved Surface-State Profiles
Atomistic surface configurations, each containing approximately 20,000 atoms, were converted into
depth-resolved surface-state profiles by averaging over the x-y plane. This reduction transforms each
three-dimensional atomistic configuration into one-dimensional profiles along the surface-normal
direction while retaining information about how composition and structure vary with depth. For
each saved surface configuration, z-resolved profiles were computed for the local Si, Cl, and Ar
concentrations and for the crystallinity of the Si substrate.
The crystallinity profile was defined as the fraction of Si atoms at each depth that are identified as
occupying a diamond-cubic lattice. This calculation was performed using the Python interface to
OVITO. A value near one corresponds to crystalline Si, while lower values indicate disordering,
amorphization, or surface-coordination effects. Because the raw z-resolved profiles can be noisy due

 18

to the finite number of atoms in each depth bin, a Gaussian filter with a 2 Angstrom standard
deviation was applied along the z direction before the profiles were used for model development.
Figure 6 demonstrates this post-processing procedure for two representative cases. The top row

It should be noted that the pristine Si surface can exhibit crystallinity below one in the first few
depth bins nearest the exposed surface. This is an artifact of the local structure classification: surface
atoms have missing neighbors relative to bulk diamond-cubic Si and therefore are not always
classified as crystalline. Thus, reduced crystallinity near the exposed surface should be distinguished
from bombardment-induced amorphization deeper within the material.
These depth-resolved profiles provide the intermediate representation between the full atomistic
configurations and the reduced surface-state descriptors used for ML model development. They
preserve the depth dependence of composition and crystallinity generated by cyclic chlorination and
etching while reducing the dimensionality of the raw atomic coordinates to quantities that can be
consistently evaluated for each bombardment event.

3.5. Surface-State Descriptor Construction
The depth-resolved profiles were further reduced to a compact set of surface-state descriptors for
ML model development. The goal of this reduction was to retain the surface information most

 19

relevant to particle exchange and surface evolution without using the full atomistic configuration or
the complete z-resolved profiles as ML inputs. In this project, the surface state was represented
using seven descriptors: three near-surface concentration descriptors, three mixed-layer
concentration descriptors, and one mixed-layer depth.
The near-surface state for species 𝑠, 𝜂 , is defined by the relative concentrations of each species in
the near-surface layer. This region was chosen based on the QTI finding that particle exchange was
dominated by the outermost few Angstroms of the material. The mixed-layer state for species 𝑠, 𝜉 ,
is defined by the relative species concentrations over the full mixed layer, along with the mixed-layer
depth Δ . The mixed layer depth is taken as the depth at which crystallinity profile crosses 0.75. This
threshold provides a practical definition of the depth over which the Si substrate has been
structurally modified by bombardment. Because the simulation domain includes crystalline material
beneath the modified region, this threshold is reached by for the surfaces considered here.
The mixed-layer descriptors therefore capture the composition and extent of the broader modified
region beneath the exposed surface. Figure 6 illustrates the regions used to compute these quantities.
In the bottom-right panel, the red shaded region indicates the near-surface layer used to compute 𝜂 ,
while the blue shaded region indicates the mixed layer used to compute 𝜉 . Together, these
descriptors define the reduced surface state used for ML model development.

3.6. Exploring and sampling surface-state space
The final objective of the data-generation campaign was to explore the reduced surface-state space
used for ML model development. The surface-evolution model requires training data that relate the
current surface state and incident Ar energy to the subsequent change in surface state. As a result,
the simulations were designed not only to produce individual chlorination and etching trajectories,
but also to sample physically accessible regions of the descriptor space defined by the near-surface
concentrations, mixed-layer concentrations, and mixed-layer depth. This strategy was motivated by
the QTI finding that surface-state changes can be highly stochastic from impact to impact, making it
important to generate both long monoenergetic trajectories and local statistics at selected surface
states.
The first stage of surface-state-space exploration consisted of monoenergetic Ar etching trajectories
initialized from the monolayer-chlorinated Si surface (shown in Figure 7). For each Ar energy, the
surface was evolved through a sequence of cumulative impacts, producing energy-dependent
trajectories through the reduced descriptor space. Figure X shows this evolution for the near-surface
and mixed-layer concentrations of Si, Cl, and Ar as functions of mixed-layer depth. These
trajectories illustrate how different Ar energies drive different pathways of surface modification,
including removal of near-surface Cl, redistribution of residual Cl within the mixed layer, Ar
implantation, and changes in the Si concentration within the modified region.

along these trajectories (see Figure 8). Several surfaces from the monoenergetic evolution runs were
used as initial conditions, and each was bombarded with short sequences of monoenergetic Ar

4. MACHINE-LEARNED SURFACE EVOLUTION MODEL
The ML component of this project focused on developing a reduced-order model for surface-state
evolution during Ar-driven etching of chlorinated Si. The broader long-term objective is a surface
boundary model that predicts both surface-state evolution and particle exchange. The QTI effort
identified that accurate surface-state evolution was the primary technical challenge for stable surface-
state-dependent modeling, so the present effort focuses on extending the two-species, near-surface
model from the QTI effort to a depth-dependent tertiary system.
The surface-evolution model was formulated in differential form. For each impact, the input to the
model is the incident Ar energy and the current reduced surface state. The model outputs the change
in each surface descriptor per impact. This formulation maps naturally onto the intended use of the
model as a dynamic boundary condition in plasma codes, where the surface state is updated
incrementally as particles strike the surface.

4.1. Polynomial Regression Model Architecture
The final surface-evolution model used an 8th-order polynomial expansion of the input features
followed by a ridge regression with a regularization parameter of 1600. The input features consisted
of the incident Ar energy and the reduced surface-state descriptors characterizing near/mixed layer
Cl/Si concentrations and the mixed layer depth. Prior to polynomial expansion, the input features
were standardized to have zero mean and unit variance to ensure the input data spanned similar
numerical ranges prior to regression. Argon concentration descriptors were omitted from the final
ML model because their inclusion substantially increased the dimensionality of the polynomial
feature space, and further optimization of the resulting regression problem was beyond the scope of
this project.
After the polynomial expansion, the expanded feature set was standardized a second time prior to
applying ridge regression to improve numerical conditioning of the polynomial terms. The
regression targets, corresponding to the single-impact change in each retained surface descriptor,
were also standardized during training and transformed back to physical units during recursive
prediction. This target scaling prevents the outputs with larger numerical magnitudes from
dominating the multi-output regression.
Lower-order polynomial models were evaluated in the initial model development and were found to
do an adequate job tracking the surface state when only trained on the long monoenergetic etching
simulations. The best results for the limited training data were a second-order polynomial; tracking
of concentrations was generally good but failed to capture the evolution of the mixed layer depth.
However, the performance degraded when the local statistics simulations and multiple etching cycles
were included. The statistics data sample cross-energy responses from selected surface states
throughout state space and therefore imposed a more complex surface response than can be
represented by a low-order global polynomial. The 8th-order polynomial with strong ridge
regularization was selected because it provided the flexibility required to incorporate these data while
improving stability of recursive trajectory predictions.

4.2. Out-of-Sample Validation Procedure
Model performance was evaluated using out-of-sample recursive trajectory predictions. For each
validation case, a complete etching trajectory was omitted from the training data. The model was
then trained on the remaining surface evolution data, including the long monoenergetic etching
trajectories and the local statistics simulations for the first two chlorination/etching cycles. This

 24

validation strategy is more stringent than randomly withholding individual impact events because the
model must reproduce the evolution of an entire trajectory that was not used during regression. The
recursive validation was initialized with the true LAMMPS surface state at the beginning of the
omitted trajectory and the model was used to evolve the surface state under monoenergetic
conditions from there.
This validation strategy was chosen to assess interpolation across the sampled surface-state space
and was applied to each of the long monoenergetic etching trajectories for both etching cycles.
Held-out trajectories from both the first Ar etching round and the second Ar etching round were
used to evaluate whether the model could reproduce surface evolution for surfaces with different
prior histories. The first etching round begins from a monolayer-chlorinated crystalline Si surface,
while the second etching round begins from a rechlorinated surface that retains structural and
compositional modification from the previous cycle. Agreement for both cases provides evidence
that the model captures the dominant state-dependent trends governing Ar-driven etching.

4.3. Surface-State Tracking Results
The primary test of the surface-evolution model is whether it can reproduce the trajectory of an out-
of-sample LAMMPS simulation when applied recursively. In this validation mode, small errors in
the predicted descriptor changes can accumulate over many impacts, so agreement over an extended
trajectory provides a stronger assessment of model performance than pointwise regression error
alone. This section discusses a subset of the validation cases that are representative of the key trends
observed across the full set of out-of-sample surface-state tracking results.
Figure 11 shows an out-of-sample validation case for 100-eV Ar etching of a monolayer-chlorinated
Si surface. This case is representative of the model performance at intermediate incident energies,
where Ar bombardment both removes Cl from the near-surface region and drives mixing within the
structurally modified layer. The predicted trajectory closely follows the LAMMPS trajectory in both
the near-surface and mixed-layer Si/Cl concentrations, while also accurately predicting the depth of
the mixed layer. This indicates that the model can capture the dominant relationships between Cl
removal, redistribution of residual Cl, and growth of the mixed layer. The agreement over the full
trajectory provides confidence that the learned update is stable under repeated application for this
portion of the sampled state space.
Figure 12 shows the validation case for 20-eV etching of a monolayer-chlorinated Si surface. This
lower-energy case provides a useful test of the model because the surface evolves more gradually
than at intermediate or high energy, with Cl removed continuously over many impacts while the
mixed layer grows more slowly. Strong regularization was particularly important for stability in this
energy range. The model slightly overpredicts the near-surface and mixed layer Cl concentrations
later in the evolution, but the agreement is still very respectable. The mixed layer depth is
overpredicted, but the deviation settles at a constant value rather than growing uncontrollably. The
predicted trajectory asymptotes faster than the LAMMPS data, suggesting more work is needed to
capture slow evolution in Cl concentration as the system approaches saturation.

4.4. Summary of Modeling Results
Overall, the out-of-sample validation results demonstrate that the polynomial surface evolution
model captures the dominant trends in the reduced surface-state dynamics for Ar etching of
chlorinated Si. The agreement is particularly encouraging because recursive prediction is sensitive to
accumulated error, where small biases in the predicted descriptor changes can compound over many
impacts and drive the model away from the physically sampled region of state space.
The best performance was obtained using the single-impact differential formulation with an 8 th-
order polynomial expansion and strong ridge regularization. The 50-impact monoenergetic
bombardment sets throughout state space provided meaningful data for stabilizing the model.
Figure 16 illustrates the stochastic nature of surface evolution on an impact-to-impact basis. Surface
state evolution is depicted for a long monoenergetic etching case and is displayed with a averaging
window of 1, 10, and 20 impacts. Especially later in the evolution where mean change is small,
having many successive impacts at the same energy is crucial for resolving changes to surface state at
a given energy.
These results provide proof-of-concept for learning surface-state evolution in a chemically mediated
plasma-surface system using atomistic simulation data. The model developed here is not yet a
complete plasma-surface boundary model because the particle-exchange component was outside the
scope of this effort. However, the demonstrated capability to track the evolution of the surface state
descriptors across multiple etching cycles establishes an essential component of such a model.
Future work should extend the post-processing pipeline to include outgoing particle products, and
utilize the regression methods from the QTI project to capture the statistics in the data.

5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
This project advanced the development of atomistic-informed, machine-learned surface models for
PSIs by developing a data generation and data processing workflow to model a chemically mediated
Si-Cl-Ar plasma etching system. Building on lessons learned from the concurrent QTI effort on W-
C particle exchange, this work focused on the surface-evolution component of the broader plasma-
surface boundary model. The project demonstrated a capability to generate cumulative atomistic
bombardment trajectories, reduce complex surface configurations into tractable surface-state
descriptors, and train a reduced-order model that predicts the evolution of those descriptors during
Ar-driven etching of chlorinated Si.
A major outcome of the project was the development of a LAMMPS-based data-generation
capability for ion bombardment of surfaces. It was applied here to cyclic chlorination and etching
and was adapted to a tungsten-carbon system in the concurrent QTI effort. The model supports
cumulative sequential particle impacts on an evolving surface, energy-dependent selection of the
evolved surface depth, efficient treatment of fixed and non-interacting layers, and automated
detection and removal of outgoing particles. Using this capability, crystalline Si surfaces were
chlorinated through exposure to room-temperature Cl 2 and subsequently etched with monoenergetic
Ar beams. The resulting atomistic configurations were post-processed into depth-resolved
concentration and crystallinity profiles and then reduced to surface-state descriptors representing
near-surface composition, mixed-layer composition, and mixed-layer depth.
The final ML model was formulated as a single-impact differential surface-evolution model. The
model uses the incident Ar energy reduced Si/Cl surface-state descriptors as inputs and predicts the
change in those descriptor s per impact. An 8th-order polynomial expansion with ridge regularization
was required to capture the nonlinear response induced by monoenergetic etching and local statistics
simulations. Out-of-sample validation showed that the model can reproduce the dominant surface-
state trajectories for both the first and second Ar etching rounds when initialized from a held-out
LAMMPS surface state and advanced using its own predictions.
Several important lessons were identified during model development. Low-order polynomial models
were stable for simpler subsets of the data, but did not provide sufficient flexibility once local
statistics simulations and multiple etching cycles were included. The statistics data were ultimately
valuable because they sampled local cross-energy surface responses that helped constrain the surface
response, but incorporating them required a more expressive model and strong regularization. The
mixed-layer depth and chlorine composition was more challenging to predict than the other
descriptors. Argon concentration descriptors were omitted from the final model because their
inclusion substantially increased the polynomial feature space and further optimization of that
higher-dimensional model as beyond the scope of this effort.
Future work should extend the present surface-evolution model toward a complete plasma-surface
boundary model. The immediate next step is to post-process the outgoing particle information to
formulate a predictive model for particle exchange. These particle-exchange models should be
conditioned on the same reduced surface-state descriptors used in the concurrent QTI project,
which are also captured here. It should be noted that these parameters were among the best
predictions of the model. Additional work should also focus on refining the descriptor set. For large
input spaces as described here, principal component analysis may help transform the input space
into a reduced set of descriptors that captures the dominant behavior of the system.
Longer-term work should integrate the surface-evolution and particle-exchange models into a kinetic
or fluid plasma simulation as a dynamic boundary condition. The surface model described here

 31

would need to be updated to evolve based on input fluence rather than ‘per impact’ as done here
because particle-in-cell simulations will be passing weighted particles to the boundary model. The
capabilities developed in this project provide the data generation, post-processing, and ML modeling
foundation needed for that integration. The work described here gives confidence that higher-
complexity plasma-surface systems can be modeled with this workflow enabling broader application
of atomistic-informed surface models to plasma etching, plasma delayering, plasma-facing materials
in fusion reactors, and other PSI problems relevant to Sandia mission needs.
