Analysis and validation of abnormal signaling pathways and immune cell infiltration characteristics in digestive system cancers based on peroxisome-related genes
Authors: Jie Wen, Zhihui Wang, Shuyang Luo, Abudureyimujiang Aili, Hongliang Zou, Shaoqing Niu, Haikuan Liu, Wenzhe Fan, Jiaping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough emerging evidence suggests a role for peroxisomes in tumorigenesis, their functions in digestive cancers remain unclear. This study aims to investigate the association between peroxisomes and digestive tract tumors.MethodsTo systematically investigate peroxisomal functions in digestive cancers, we first constructed and validated tumor-specific prognostic signatures based on peroxisome-related genes (PRGs) through univariate Cox, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), and multivariate Cox regression analyses. We then characterized the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) with CIBERSORT, X-CELL, and EPIC algorithms, and identified tumor-specific and common signalings via Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Focusing on hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we experimentally validated peroxisome-related therapeutic responses by profiling signature genes in radioresistant cells and an orthotopic transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) rat model. PEX13 knockdown further assessed peroxisomal role in radiosensitivity and targeted therapy response. Clinical relevance of PEX13 was evaluated in HCC cohort. Single-cell RNA sequencing dataset and lipidomics further revealed peroxisomal mechanisms in HCC progression. Finally, peroxisomal function in colorectal cancer (CRC) was validated in vitro.ResultsNovel peroxisome-related prognostic signatures demonstrated strong predictive power in HCC, colon adenocarcinoma, rectal adenocarcinoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, gastric adenocarcinoma, esophageal adenocarcinoma, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, and cholangiocarcinoma. High-risk patients displayed an immunosuppressive microenvironment, characterized by increased infiltration of regulatory T cells, M2 macrophages, Th2 cells, or cancer-associated fibroblasts, or Th1 cells’ reduction. Peroxisomes engaged in several distinct yet convergent pathways, most notably “positive regulation of response to stimuli”. HCC prognostic genes were dynamically regulated in response to therapeutic stimuli, including radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and TACE. Clinically, the expression of PEX13 was markedly upregulated in tumor tissues from therapy-resistant HCC patients. Mechanistically, peroxisomal dysfunction induced by silencing PEX13 in HCC or UBE2D2 in CRC may overcome therapeutic resistance (radiotherapy/ lenvatinib resistance in HCC, radioresistance in CRC) through reprogramming lipid metabolism.ConclusionsPeroxisomes act as pivotal regulators of digestive cancer progression by modulating signaling pathways, the TIME, therapeutic resistance, and lipid metabolism. Targeting peroxisomal function, particularly in high-risk subgroups of HCC and CRC, warrants further exploration as a promising therapeutic strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00861-w.Keywords: Digestive system cancers, Peroxisome, Treatment resistance

Introduction

Digestive system cancers, including HCC, colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), rectal adenocarcinoma (READ), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), gastric adenocarcinoma (STAD), esophageal adenocarcinoma (ESCA), esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), and cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), account for nearly 35% of global cancer-related deaths annually [1]. These tumors exhibit distinct and overlapping molecular alterations that drive heterogeneity, therapeutic resistance, and immune evasion [2]. Elucidating their progression mechanisms and identifying novel therapeutic vulnerabilities remain essential for improving clinical outcomes.

Peroxisomes, ubiquitous single-membrane organelles in eukaryotes, contain oxidases and antioxidant enzymes to form a unique enzymatic system essential for fatty acid β-oxidation, phospholipid synthesis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulation and antiviral immunity [3–5]. Peroxisomes’ abundance is regulated by a family of peroxisomal biogenesis factor (PEX) genes, such as PEX13, whose loss disrupts peroxisome formation and cellular metabolism [6]. Accumulating evidence has underscored the critical and context-dependent roles of peroxisomes in the initiation, progression, and therapeutic response of multiple gastrointestinal malignancies, with distinct mechanisms underlying different cancer types [7].

In HCC, peroxisomal dysfunction contributes to hepatocarcinogenesis by inducing metabolic disturbances [8], ROS accumulation, autophagy activation, and TIME modulation via CD4⁺ T-cell regulation [9]. Our previous work demonstrated that PEX5 promoted HCC radioresistance by maintaining peroxisome-dependent ROS homeostasis, underscoring peroxisomal role in therapeutic responses [10]. Decreased PEX5 expression sensitizes liver cancer cells to radiation and triggers ferroptosis through the detoxification axis involving MGST1 and GSH [11]. Disruption of peroxisome function via depletion of PEX5, PEX10, and PEX12 evokes metabolic stress, mTOR pathway suppression, and fatal outcomes in HCC cells [12].

In colorectal cancer, peroxisome-mediated fatty acid α-oxidation may modulate CD8⁺ T-cell and macrophage infiltration to influence immunotherapy efficacy [13]. Oxaliplatin triggers pexophagy (the selective autophagic degradation of peroxisomes), whereas cells that are resistant to oxaliplatin sustain peroxisome levels by evading this process. Moreover, silencing PMP70 markedly compromises the survival of resistant cells through enhanced peroxisome-mediated cytotoxic lipid peroxidation [14]. Upregulation of PEX26 impedes cell migration, invasion, and epithelial‑mesenchymal transition, while loss of PEX26 facilitates these aggressive behaviors via Wnt signaling activation [15]. Targeting the metabolic vulnerabilities of peroxisomes offers a novel therapeutic strategy for colorectal cancer. For instance, silencing FNDC5 markedly reduces the viability of colon adenocarcinoma cells [16].

In ESCC, tegaserod maleate inhibits ESCC proliferation by suppressing the peroxisome pathway via downregulation of PEX11B and PEX13. Both genes are highly expressed in ESCC, and their knockout enhances the antitumor effect of tegaserod maleate, confirming that peroxisome activation is essential for ESCC cell proliferation [17].

In CHOL, peroxisomal signaling is closely linked to disease progression. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) suppress CHOL proliferation in xenograft models but promote CHOL development in JNK1/2 double-knockout mice [18]. This context-dependent role underscores the complexity of peroxisomal functions in CHOL.

Despite current progress, peroxisomal functions remain poorly characterized in heterogeneous digestive cancers with diverse etiologies, molecular profiles and immune landscapes. To our knowledge, this is the first pan-digestive cancer study of peroxisomes. Herein, we analyzed PRGs to construct subtype-specific prognostic signatures for eight predominant digestive cancers, clarified their divergent signaling pathways and conserved core biological characteristics (such as regulation of response to stimuli), and elucidated distinct patterns of TIME patterns. Integrating cellular assays, orthotopic animal models, clinical drug-resistant samples, single-cell transcriptomics and lipidomics, we further confirmed that peroxisomes mediate therapeutic resistance via lipid metabolic reprogramming in digestive cancer, such as HCC and colorectal cancer. Our study has important implications, targeting peroxisomal function represents a promising strategy to overcome treatment resistance in digestive cancers.

Materials and methods

Collection of PRGs

A total of 113 non-redundant PRGs (Supplementary Table 1) were retrieved from Liewang Qiu et al.’s study [19].

Construction and validation of the prognostic signatures

Data were processed and analyzed as follows: RNA sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for digestive tumors were sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). First, univariate Cox regression analysis (p < 0.05) with overall survival (OS) as the endpoint was performed to identify clinical prognosis-related genes (CPRGs) for each tumor subtype. Next, the intersection between the CPRGs and PRGs was pinpointed using Venn analysis. Subsequently, this set of overlapping genes was analyzed by LASSO regression with the R package “glmnet” to select the most robust predictors. Finally, these predictors were incorporated into a multivariate Cox regression model using the R package “survival” to establish the final prognostic signatures. The risk score for each patient was calculated as follows: Risk score = Σ (expression of signature gene × coefficient). Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups basis of the median score.

For improved reliability, we performed external validation using independent cohorts from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), including ICGC-HCC, ICGC-PAAD, GSE39582-COAD, GSE87211-READ, and GSE53624-ESCC (stage II). Risk scores were calculated with the corresponding prognostic models and stratified by the median cutoff.

Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis

K-M analysis and log-rank tests were performed via the R packages “survival” and “survminer” to evaluate clinical outcomes, with p < 0.05 considered significant. To assess the predictive performance of the prognostic signature, time-dependent receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the area under the curve (AUC) calculation were conducted via the R package “survcomp”.

Functional enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the high- and low-risk groups were identified via the “limma” package with |log₂fold change| > 0.585 and adjusted p < 0.05. GO annotation (biological processes [BPs], molecular functions [MFs] and cellular components [CCs]) and KEGG pathway analysis were conducted to investigate the biological relevance of the DEGs. GSEA was also performed to explore functional enrichment patterns between risk groups.

Immune infiltration analysis

To evaluate the associations between prognostic risk scores and immune infiltration in digestive system tumors, we applied three deconvolution algorithms: CIBERSORT, X-CELL, and EPIC. CIBERSORT was used to estimate the proportions of 22 tumor-infiltrating immune cell types. X-CELL was used to compare the infiltration levels of antitumor and protumor immune cells between risk groups. EPIC was used to assesses cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) proportions. Additionally, we analyzed the expression patterns of key immune checkpoint genes (CD274 [PD-L1], CTLA4, HAVCR2, IDO1, LAG3, PDCD1 [PD-1], PDCD1LG2, SIGLEC15, TIGIT, VSIR, and KLRB1), which could serve as predictive markers for immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy.

Drug sensitivity analysis

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for the compounds were retrieved from the GDSC database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). Drug response prediction was conducted via the “pRRophetic” R package to estimate compound sensitivity scores for each patient in the high- and low-risk groups. Group comparisons were conducted via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Orthotopic HCC model and TACE procedure

Animal studies were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (Animals in Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments) [20].

The male Sprague-Dawley rats (300–350 g, 8-week-old) were purchased from laboratory animal center of Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China, and were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (0.5 mL/100 g). Following midline laparotomy, 5 × 10⁶ N1S1 hepatoma cells in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were orthotopically injected into the left hepatic lobe via a 29-gauge syringe. Intrahepatic tumor growth was monitored by contrast-enhanced CT (Philips IQon Spectral CT) 10–14 days post-implantation. When the tumor volume reached 250–300 mm³ (V = [length × width²]/2), the rats were randomly and blindly divided into two groups (TACE group and TACE-free group, (n = 6 rats per group) for intervention.

TACE was performed through surgical catheterization of the hepatic artery. Following anesthesia and laparotomy, the liver was retracted to expose the common hepatic artery (CHA), proper hepatic artery (PHA), and gastroduodenal artery (GDA). The CHA was temporarily occluded, the distal GDA was ligated, and a microcatheter was retrogradely inserted into the PHA through the GDA. A suspension of epirubicin with ethiodized oil or 100–300 μm microspheres was slowly infused via the microcatheter to achieve selective targeting of intrahepatic tumors. After embolization, the catheter was withdrawn, the proximal GDA was ligated, and the abdomen was closed in two layers. After 3 days from TACE, Contrast-enhanced CT was performed to verify selective lipiodol deposition within the tumor. Hepatic arteriography via digital subtraction angiography (Philips Allura Xper FD20) was performed before and after embolization to evaluate vascular perfusion and embolization efficacy.

The rats were maintained in a standard, temperature-controlled environment on a 12-hour light/dark cycle.

Mouse xenograft model

Male BALB/c nude mice (aged 6–8 weeks, weighing 18–22 g) were sourced from the Laboratory Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China). All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle, a temperature of 22 ± 2 ℃, and relative humidity of 50 ± 10%. The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line Huh7 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator. For tumor inoculation, Huh7 cells in the exponential growth phase were collected, washed, and resuspended in PBS. Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 5 × 10⁶ cells in 100 µL PBS into the right dorsal flank. Tumor dimensions were measured every 3 days using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as (length × width²)/2. When the tumor volumes reached approximately 100–150 mm³, the mice were randomly assigned to two groups: the control group was administered the vehicle (PBS) by oral gavage, while the treatment group received lenvatinib (HY-10981, MedChemExpress) at 4 mg/kg via oral gavage. Dosing was performed once daily for 5 consecutive days, followed by a 2-day rest period, and this cycle was repeated. After the final treatment, all mice were sacrificed [21].

Colony formation assay

A colony formation assay was performed to assess the radiosensitivity of radiation-resistant Huh7 cells. The cells were seeded into 6-well plates at densities based on radiation dose: 0 Gy (100 cells/well), 2 Gy (200 cells/well), 4 Gy (800 cells/well), 6 Gy (1600 cells/well), 8 Gy (3200 cells/well), and 10 Gy (6400 cells/well). The cells were irradiated accordingly and cultured for 14 days to allow colony formation. Colonies were fixed with methanol/acetic acid and stained with crystal violet. Colonies with ≥ 50 cells were counted, and survival curves were generated via the single-hit multi-target model.

Irradiation resistant cell (IRR)

To generate radiation-resistant Huh7 cell lines, a stepwise irradiation protocol was used. Huh7 and Hep3B cells were initially exposed to 2 Gy of ionizing radiation, followed by recovery and expansion until they regained their proliferative capacity. The surviving cells were subsequently subjected to additional 2 Gy irradiation and subsequently cultured until proliferation resumed. This process was repeated with increasing doses: two rounds each of 4 Gy, 6 Gy, 8 Gy, and 10 Gy. After each irradiation cycle, the cells were allowed sufficient time for damage repair and proliferation before proceeding to the next higher radiation dose.

Western blotting (WB) analysis

The cells were lysed with RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. The protein concentration was measured via a bicinchoninic acid assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. After being locked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h. The protein bands were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence. The primary antibodies included anti-PEX13 (Proteintech, Cat No. 26649-1-AP, dilution ratio 1:1000), anti-β-actin (Proteintech, Cat No. 66009-1-Ig, dilution ratio 1:5000), anti-ABCD3 (Proteintech, Cat No. 26720-1-AP, dilution ratio 1:1000), anti-ACOX1 (Proteintech, Cat No. 83731-2-RR, dilution ratio 1:3000) and anti-UBE2D1/2/3/4 (Proteintech, Cat No. 15475-1-AP, dilution ratio 1:500) antibodies.

The scRNA-seq data collection and processing

The GSE242889 dataset was preprocessed using the “Seurat” package. Low-quality cells were excluded based on thresholds (< 250 or > 7000 expressed genes; >15% mitochondrial unique molecular identifiers). Mitochondrial, ribosomal, and hemoglobin genes were removed. Batch effects were corrected with Harmony. The top 2,000 variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures (default parameters). Cell clusters were generated via the “FindClusters” and “FindNeighbors” functions, visualized by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) method, and manually annotated. The DEGs between high/low PEX13 groups were identified using Seurat’s FindMarkers with Wilcoxon test (p.adj < 0.05). The cell lineage trajectory was established using Monocle2. Cell-cell interaction analysis was performed with the “CellChat” R package, employing the default CellChatDB ligand-receptor database following standard protocol. Specific interaction patterns between tumor cells and various cell types were inferred by detecting marker gene expression in tumor cells.

Single-cell copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed using InferCNV. Immune cells (T cells and B cells) were used as normal reference, while hepatocytes from patients were designated as target cells for CNV inference. Single-cell RNA-seq data were first normalized and genes were ordered according to their chromosomal positions. InferCNV then compared the expression of target cells to that of reference cells to infer potential chromosomal copy number gains or losses. CNV heatmaps and trend plots were generated to visualize overall patterns of chromosomal alterations. Reference cells were assumed to have stable copy numbers, and regions with expression deviating significantly from the reference were identified as potential CNV regions.

Immunocytochemistry (IHC)

HCC tissue sections (5 μm thick) were obtained from patients with or without lenvatinib resistance at Sun Yat-sen University first Affiliated Hospital. The sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated prior to antigen retrieval, which was performed by heating in 10 mmol/L sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 ℃ for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min at room temperature. After three washes in PBS (2 min each), nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 10% goat serum for 10 min at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with a primary antibody against PEX13 (1:200 dilution). Immunodetection was performed using the PV-9000 2-step Plus Poly-HRP Anti-Rabbit IgG Detection System (Zhong Shan Jin Qiao, China), and color development was carried out with DAB. Hematoxylin was used for counterstaining, and the slides were mounted with neutral balsam. Whole-slide images were acquired using a NanoZoomer Digital Pathology (NDP) scan system.

Lipidomics

Lipidomics was performed by VeryGenome Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Materials and Chemicals: HPLC-grade acetonitrile (ACN), methanol (MeOH), and isopropanol (IPA) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). HPLC-grade dichloromethane (CH2Cl2), tert-butyl methyl ether (MTBE), and ammonium acetate (AmAc) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO. USA). Ultrapure water was obtained by a Milli-Q system (Millipore, Billerica, MA). Lipid standards including phosphatidylcholine (PC) 19:0/19:0, lyso-phosphatidylcholine (LPC) 19:0, phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) 17:0/ 17:0, sphingomyelin (SM) d18:1/12:0, ceramide (Cer) d18:1/ 17:0, triacylglycerol (TG) 15:0/15:0/15:0, and fatty acid (FA) 16:0-d3 were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL).

Sample Preparation: A total 10 mg of fresh tissue were extracted by liquid−liquid extraction (LLE) with the MTBE/MeOH/H2O system, respectively. Notably, 30 µL of an internal standard (IS) mixture containing PC 19:0/19:0 at 6.7 µg/mL, LPC 19:0 at 3.3 µg/mL, PE 17:0/17:0 at 3.3 µg/mL, SM d18:1/12:0 at 1.7 µg/mL, Cer d18:1/17:0 at 1.7 µg/mL, TG 15:0/15:0/15:0 at 5.3 µg/mL, and FA 16:0-d3 at 6.7 µg/mL was added to each kind of sample matrix prior to lipid extraction except for the recovery experiment in method validation, in which the mixture of lipid ISs at three different levels was added either before or after lipid extraction.

Targeted Lipidomics Analysis: An ACQUITY UHPLC system coupled with a hybrid QQQlinear ion trap mass spectrometer, Q-Trap 6500 system, with a Turbo Ion Spray source (AB SCIEX/MDS-Sciex, Concord, ON, Canada) was used for UHPLC/QQQ MRM MS-based pseudotargeted lipidomics analysis. A reversed-phase BEH C8 column (1.7 μm, Waters, Milford, MA, U.S.A.) was used for the chromatographic separation of lipids. Mobile phases A and B were ACN/H2O (60:40, v/v) and IPA/ACN (90:10, v/v) respectively, both containing 10 mM AmAc. The flow rate was 0.26mL/min. The column temperature was at 55 °C. The elution gradient started with 32% B and was kept for 1.5 min, then linearly increased to 85% B at 15.5 min, and then to 97% B at 15.6 min, and held for 2.4 min. The gradient was back to 32% B at 18.1 min and kept for 1.9 min to equilibrate column. The temperature of the sample manager was set at 10℃.Freeze-dried samples were reconstituted in ACN/IPA/H2O (65:30:5, v/v/v/) containing 5mM AmAc, and 5 µL was injected into the LC-MS system.

Data Processing and Statistical Analyses: All of the detected lipids were quantified by SCIEXOS software (version 1.7). Normalization of the lipids by the appropriate lipid ISs for each sample was performed when necessary. The statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.01.

The lipidomics data following PEX13 knockdown were then analyzed for pathway enrichment using MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/home.xhtml).

Statistical analyses

The log-rank test was used to evaluate prognostic significance, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated for visualization. We employed (1) Student’s t-test (two-tailed, unpaired) for normally distributed data and (2) the Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric comparisons. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v9.3.0; GraphPad Software) and the R Statistical Environment. These results were considered statistically significant at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

Construction of HCC prognostic signature

By intersecting 113 PRGs and 5035 HCC-associated CPRGs, we identified 34 overlapping genes (Fig. 1A), most of which were significantly dysregulated in HCC tissues in both paired and unpaired settings (Fig. S1A-B). LASSO regression identified 10 key prognostic genes associated with OS (Fig. 1B-C). High expression of EHHADH, HSD17B4, PECR, and PPARGC1A predicted better prognosis, whereas elevated LMO3, PEX13, PRMT2, RAB8B, TMEM135, and UBE2D2 indicated worse outcomes (Fig. S1C). These genes were integrated into a prognostic signature: risk score = (LMO3 × 0.103420) + (PPARGC1A × -0.053427) + (EHHADH × 0.003278) + (PECR × -0.035230) + (UBE2D2 × 0.071894) + (HSD17B4 × -0.006401) + (PRMT2 × -0.143099) + (PEX13 × 0.228852) + (RAB8B × -0.026456) + (TMEM135 × 0.121651). The model demonstrated strong predictive performance for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (AUCs = 0.772, 0.740, and 0.704, respectively), with high-risk patients exhibiting significantly reduced survival (Fig. 1D-F).

GO terms, KEGG pathways and GSEA signals in HCC risk subgroups

Compared with the high-risk group, the low-risk group presented 110 upregulated and 878 downregulated genes, with the top 30 differential expressed genes (DEGs) shown in Fig. S2A. GO analysis revealed 1779 functional terms (Supplementary Table 2), encompassing 1519 BPs, 88 CCs and 172 MFs. The top 10 enriched terms for each category were presented in Fig. 1G-I, highlighting “response to external stimulus” (BPs), “intrinsic component of membrane” (CCs), and “cytokine activity” (MFs). KEGG analysis revealed 25 significantly enriched pathways, such as “cell cycle” (Fig. 1J). GSEA revealed 22 enriched terms such as “fatty acid metabolism” (Fig. S2B).

Immune infiltration disparities in high- vs. low-risk HCC

Given the crucial role of peroxisomes in immune regulation, we investigated whether the prognostic signature reflected HCC’s TIME. CIBERSORT algorithm detected significantly increased regulatory T cells (Tregs) infiltration in high-risk patients, though no significant differences were observed in M1 or M2 macrophage subsets between risk groups (Fig. 1K). High-risk patients had significantly elevated Th2 cells and CAFs infiltration (X-CELL/EPIC analysis, Fig. S2C-D). Collectively, high-risk HCC was characterized by a suppressive TIME, enriched in Tregs, Th2 cells, and CAFs.

Additionally, CTLA4, PDCD1, and TIGIT were significantly upregulated in high-risk patients (Fig. 1L), implying potential responsiveness to immunotherapy. Consistently, high-risk patients with low expression of these molecules had poorer OS (Fig. S2E), underscoring their prognostic value.

Correlations between the signature score and drug sensitivity in HCC

TACE refractoriness, largely due to chemoresistance, and targeted drug resistance posed significant challenges in managing HCC. The risk score correlated positively with sensitivity (lower IC50) to sorafenib, olaparib, oxaliplatin, irinotecan, gefitinib, and 5-fluorouracil, but negatively with sensitivity to gemcitabine and epirubicin (Fig. S2F). These results suggested that high-risk HCC patients might benefit more from targeted therapies (e.g., sorafenib) and HAIC regimens (5-fluorouracil, irinotecan, oxaliplatin), than from TACE (loading epirubicin) or gemcitabine-based regimens.

Pan-digestive cancer overview of tumor type-specific peroxisomal heterogeneity

Similary, a 4-gene prognostic signature was established for COAD (Fig. 2A-C), showing good predictive accuracy for OS (AUCs: 0.642, 0.627, and 0.638 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year), with high-risk patients exhibiting shorter OS (Fig. 2D-F). This signature was related to 1867 terms (e.g., “nervous system development”, Supplementary Table 3), and 43 KEGG pathways, including “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway” (Fig. 2G-J). GSEA revealed 48 enriched pathways, such as “oxidative phosphorylation” (Fig. S3A). High-risk patients presented increased Th1 but decreased Th2 cells infiltration, alongside elevated Tregs and CAFs, indicating a suppressive TIME (Fig. 2K, Fig. S3B-C). VSIR was upregulated, whereas KLRB1 was downregulated in high-risk patients (Fig. 2L). Low expression of either gene correlated with poorer survival in high-risk cases, and the risk score positively correlated with IC50 of 5-fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, irinotecan and cisplatin (Fig. S3D-E), suggesting greater chemotherapy sensitivity in low-risk patients.

For READ, 4 genes were selected to construct a prognostic signature (Fig. 3A-C), effectively predicting OS with AUCs of 0.647 (1 year), 0.720 (3 years), and 0.822 (5 years), and high-risk patients had worse OS (Fig. 3D-F). This signature was associated with 1370 functional terms (Supplementary Table 4), such as “regulation of immune response”, and 33 KEGG pathways, including “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” (Fig. 3G-J). GSEA identified 46 enriched pathways, including “regulation of autophagy” (Fig. S4A). High-risk READ patients exhibited elevated Th2 cell infiltration, contributing to a suppressive TIME, although no significant differences were observed in M1/M2 macrophage polarization, Tregs levels, or CAFs abundance between risk groups (Fig. 3K, Fig. S4B-C). CD274, CTLA4, HAVCR2, IDO1, PDCD1, and PDCD1LG2 were differentially expressed between the risk groups (Fig. 3L). High-risk patients with low expression of these checkpoints had worse prognoses, and risk score positively correlated with IC50 of oxaliplatin, 5-fluorouracil, and irinotecan (Fig. S5A-B), indicating enhanced chemotherapy sensitivity in low-risk patients.

Functional validation of peroxisomal regulatory role in response to stimulus in HCC

Given the established peroxisomal role in cellular stress response, we hypothesized that they mediate adaptive mechanisms under therapy-induced stimulus in digestive cancers. We experimentally validated this by demonstrating peroxisome-mediated stress adaptation and lipid remodeling in HCC, because the liver is a peroxisome-rich organ [22], and HCC pathogenesis critically depends on lipid metabolism and ROS handling [23], processes central to peroxisomal function.

To test this, we assessed the response of HCC prognostic genes to radiotherapy. We generated irradiation resistant (IRR)-Huh7 cells, exhibiting enhanced colony formation and a sensitization enhancement ratio (SER) of 0.75 compared with wild-type cells (Fig. 9D-E). IRR cells showed downregulated expression of PECR, HSD17B4, RAB8B, and UBE2D2, along with upregulated expression of EHHADH and TMEM135 (Fig. 9F, Supplementary Table 10), indicating a peroxisomal response to ionizing radiation. Consistently, GSE166653 confirmed radiation-induced upregulation of EHHADH and PECR (Fig. S12A).

We further established an orthotopic N1S1 rat model treated with successful TACE, confirmed by CT-detected lipiodol deposition and histological verification of microsphere accumulation (Fig. 9G). TACE downregulated PECR, while upregulating TMEM135, RAB8B, and UBE2D2 (Fig. 9H, Supplementary Table 11), suggesting peroxisomal involvement in TACE response. These findings were corroborated in GSE104580, where RAB8B and EHHADH levels correlated with TACE efficacy (Fig. S12B).

Peroxisomes might also response to targeted therapies. Sorafenib modulated PPARGC1A, UBE2D2, and HSD17B4, while lenvatinib resistance involved transcriptional remodeling of RAB8B, UBE2D2, HSD17B4, PECR, EHHADH, PRMT2, PEX13, TMEM135, and PPARGC1A (Fig. S12C-D).

Building on the peroxisomal therapeutic responsiveness, we further elucidated their longitudinal role by investigating the underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Peroxisomal depletion by PEX13 knockdown (siRNA PEX13, si-PEX13) in irradiation-resistant Huh7 and Hep3B cells, confirmed by downregulated ABCD3 (peroxisomal membrane protein) (Fig. 10A), reversed their radioresistance. This was evidenced by suppressed colony formation, decreased survival fraction and increased SER(D0) = 1.21/1.53, SER(Dq) = 1.99/2.24, and SER(SF2) = 1.42/1.49 (Fig. 10B-D).

Sensitization to lenvatinib by PEX13 silencing (shRNA PEX13, sh-PEX13) was confirmed in vitro and in vivo. In Huh7 cells, sh-PEX13 enhanced lenvatinib-induced suppression of proliferation (Fig. 10E-F). This sensitization translated to superior tumor growth inhibition in mouse xenografts treated with the sh-PEX13 and lenvatinib combination (Fig. 10G-I).

Subsequently, in sh-PEX13-expressing Huh7 cells, we transfected a PEX13 overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1-PEX13) to restore PEX13 protein levels (Fig. S13A). Upon exposure to lenvatinib, pcDNA3.1-PEX13 reversed the reduction in colony formation efficiency caused by sh-PEX13 (Fig. S13B). Similarly, pcDNA3.1-PEX13 reversed the sh-PEX13-increased radiosensitivity of Huh7 cells (Fig. S13C-D). Meanwhile, to validate that sh-PEX13 indeed impairs peroxisomal function, we assessed ACOX1, a rate-limiting enzyme in peroxisomal fatty acid β-oxidation, as a proxy for peroxisomal β-oxidation activity. Sh-PEX13 significantly reduced ACOX1 protein expression, and this effect was reversed by pcDNA3.1-PEX13 (Fig. S13E), confirming that PEX13 knockdown induces peroxisomal dysfunction and subsequent fatty acid metabolism remodeling.

Given the association between our HCC prognostic signature and “fatty acid metabolism”, untargeted lipidomics following PEX13 knockdown identified 49 significantly altered lipid species (Fig. 10J, Fig. S14). These encompassed multiple classes, including phospholipids (phosphatidylinositol [PI], phosphatidylserine [PS], phosphatidic acid [PA] and phosphatidylcholine [PC]), sphingolipids (sphingomyelin [SM], ceramides [Cer], and hexosylceramide [HexCer]), free fatty acids (FAs), triacylglycerol (TAG) and lysophospholipids (lysophosphatidic acid [LPA] lysophosphatidylethanolamine [LPE], lysophosphatidylserine [LPS], and lysophosphatidylglycerol [LPG]), positioning them as potential mediators of peroxisomal regulation to treatment response. Notalby, these altered lipid species were enriched into glycerophospholipid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (Fig. S15A-B).

We evaluated clinical relevance using paired pre- and post-lenvatinib resistance specimens from six HCC patients, who were initially lenvatinib-sensitive and subsequently developed drug resistance. Resistant tumors showed increased PEX13 expression (Fig. 10K). Concurrently, 55.1% (27/49) of candidate lipids in peripheral blood were significantly altered and enriched into the metabolism of glycerophospholipid, linoleic acid, alpha-linolenic acid and glycerolipid (Fig. 10L, Fig. S16A-B), establishing peroxisomes as key regulators of the HCC targeted therapy response through lipid metabolic reprogramming. Consistently, in patients receiving TACE-lenvatinib combination therapy, elevated PEX13 mRNA levels were associated with a poorer treatment response (Fig. 10M).

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‑seq) profiling

To further delineate the role of PEX13 in HCC, we analyzed scRNA-seq data (GSE242889) and classified cell subpopulations based on canonical marker genes (Fig. 11A-B). To rigorously validate whether these marker-defined hepatocytes in HCC tissues represent malignant cells, we performed inferCNV analysis using T cells and B cells as reference populations. This approach allowed us to evaluate the malignant identity of hepatocyte populations derived from tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues. The reference cells displayed relatively stable copy-number profiles, whereas hepatocytes, particularly those derived from tumor tissues, exhibited marked large-scale chromosomal gains and losses, indicating extensive copy-number alterations (Fig. S17A). Tumor-derived hepatocytes showed broad chromosomal CNV patterns, supporting their malignant hepatocyte-like features. In contrast, hepatocytes from adjacent non-tumor tissues exhibited relatively lower CNV burdens and were more similar to the reference background (Fig. S17A). At the single-cell level, the total CNV burden was higher in tumor-derived hepatocytes than in non-tumor-derived hepatocytes, suggesting that tumor-derived hepatocytes harbored more pronounced genomic copy-number abnormalities (Fig. S17B). Collectively, our results demonstrate that the markers-defined hepatocytes in HCC tissues are authentic malignant cells.

Functional validation of peroxisomal regulatory role in colorectal cancer (CRC)

We further explored peroxisomal function in other digestive system cancers, including colorectal cancer. UBE2D2 acts as a core gene within the COAD prognostic signature. It mediates the polyubiquitination and subsequent degradation of PEX5 [24], thereby maintaining peroxisomal membrane homeostasis [25]. Silencing UBE2D2 leads to peroxisomal dysfunction. UBE2D2 knockdown (shRNA UBE2D2, sh-UBE2D2) induced peroxisomal dysfunction, as evidenced by the downregulation of ABCD3 (Fig. S19A). UBE2D2 depletion markedly enhanced radiosensitivity in SW480 and HCT116 colorectal cancer cells, as reflected by impaired colony‑forming ability and decreased survival fractions (Fig. S19B-C). In addition, UBE2D2 deficiency profoundly reshaped cellular lipid composition (Fig. S19D), with significant alterations in multiple lipid categories including HexCer, Cer, PI, etc. These findings were consistent with our previous results regarding peroxisome-mediated regulation of lipid metabolic reprogramming in HCC.

Discussion

Contrary to the conventional perception that peroxisomes are primarily involved in fatty acid metabolism and redox homeostasis, accumulating evidence has highlighted their diverse oncogenic functions, particularly their regulatory effects on ferroptosis [26] and tumor drug resistance [27]. Nonetheless, most relevant investigations are confined to single cancer types, and the conserved and subtype-specific mechanisms mediated by peroxisomes across gastrointestinal malignancies have yet to be systematically clarified. We thus implemented a two-level analytical framework: cancer-type-specific analyses to capture heterogeneity, and pan-digestive analyses to reveal shared peroxisomal mechanisms.

We identified prominent functional heterogeneity among eight types of digestive cancers, indicating highly context-dependent peroxisomal functions. This was evidenced by limited pathway overlap (only three shared pathways) and, most compellingly, by the derivation of distinct prognostic signatures for each cancer type despite identical analytical workflows. The evident heterogeneity of core prognostic genes and prognostic models across tumor types may be attributed to the unique molecular features and biological phenotypes of digestive malignancies, which is consistent with previous findings [28].

HCC development involves multiple signalings, including HBV-related inflammation [29, 30], glycolytic metabolism [31], dysregulated fatty acid and impaired retinol metabolism [32, 33]. These processes were collectively captured by HCC’s peroxisomal prognostic signature (Fig. 1J), underscoring peroxisomal modulation in HCC.

COAD’s key oncogenic mechanisms, including Hedgehog [34], PI3K-AKT [35], and Wnt pathways [36], were correlated with peroxisomal signature (Fig. 2J). Moreover, COAD’s signature was associated with metabolic processes of amino acid (valine, leucine, isoleucine, cysteine, methionine) and lipid (fatty acid, unsaturated fatty acid) (Fig. S3A). These results indicate that peroxisomes may contribute to COAD pathogenesis by metabolic rewiring.

The peroxisomal prognostic signature in READ was linked to pathways critical for disease progression, including TNF [37] and apoptosis [38] (Fig. 3J, Fig. S4A). Additionally, READ’s signature correlated with innate immune mediators (NOD-like, Toll-like, and RIG-I-like receptors) [39–42], and adaptive immune components (T-cell receptors) [43] (Fig. S4A). These results position peroxisomes as a central regulator coordinating innate/adaptive immunity in READ.

PAAD’s aberrant pathways involves PI3K-Akt [44], MAPK [45], and neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions [46], which were correlated with PAAD’s peroxisomal signature (Fig. 4J, Fig. S6A). Dysregulation of oncogenic pathways, involving cell adhesion molecules [47] and mineral absorption [48], showed correlation with STAD’s signature (Fig. 5J). ESCA has been implicated in neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions and glycerophospholipid metabolism [49–52]. ESCC is regulated by PI3K/AKT axis [53] and MAPK [54]. Notably, our signatures of ESCC and ESCA demonstrated correlation with these pathways (Fig. 6J/7J, Fig. S8A/9A). CHOL process involves focal adhesion [55] and mitophagy [56]. Intriguingly, USP9X emerged as a unique prognostic factor and correlated with these pathways (Fig. 8J). These results suggested peroxisomal multifarious regulatory roles in these malignancies.

Summarily, we established cancer-type-specific peroxisomal prognostic signatures across digestive malignancies, each associated with distinct molecular pathways and collectively demonstrating the functional heterogeneity of peroxisomal regulation. Specifically, HCC was strongly linked to metabolism-related pathways (e.g., “Fatty acid metabolism”, “Retinol metabolism”), which is in line with the metabolic-dependent pathogenic characteristics of liver cancer [33]. While COAD and ESCC were associated with the “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway”, and PAAD and ESCA with “Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction”. This observed divergence suggests the subtype-specific functional bias of peroxisomes.

Despite this heterogeneity, growing evidence indicate that these epithelial-derived digestive cancers shared converging pathogenic mechanisms, involving S100 protein family [57], RhoA [58], ETV4 [59], Mg2+ transporters [60], FOXO1 [61], neurotensin pathway [62], FPRs [63], and LAP2 [64]. Given that these shared pathways offered therapeutic promise, we were inspired to investigate whether peroxisomes likewise participate in common regulatory circuits across these malignancies. Our work establishes peroxisomes as key regulators of therapeutic responses in digestive cancers. To verify this hypothesis, we selected HCC for in-depth mechanism exploration. As a liver-specific malignancy abundant in endogenous peroxisomes, its development and progression are highly dependent on lipid metabolism and ROS balance regulation, which are core physiological functions of peroxisomes. Our results demonstrated that radiotherapy, TACE and targeted drug treatment could all induce significant dynamic expression changes of peroxisome-related prognostic genes, fully proving that peroxisomes can rapidly sense external therapeutic stimuli and initiate intracellular adaptive regulatory programs. This extends our prior finding that PEX5 promoted DNA repair in irradiated HCC [10]. Radioresistance, TACE failure, and targeted drug insensitivity pose major challenges in HCC [65–67], making it crucial to identify the shared mechanisms underlying these resistance phenotypes. Notably, our demonstration of peroxisomes as a convergent resistance mechanism provides a rationale for proposing targeted inhibition of peroxisomal function as a promising future strategy in HCC. Meanwhile, the observation that only one KEGG pathway was common across cancers supports the concept that peroxisomes act as dynamic stress-response hubs rather than fixed pathway activators.

Beyond dysregulated signaling pathways, reprogramming of the tumor immune niche is a hallmark of malignancy and therapy resistance. While peroxisomes have been linked to inflammation in neurodegeneration [68], their roles within the TIME remained largely uncharacterized, despite proposed mechanisms involving fatty acid oxidation driven Tregs differentiation [69], and ROS-mediated T-cell suppression [70]. Immune infiltration analyses further confirmed peroxisomal heterogeneity across digestive cancers. We found that an immunosuppressive niche enriched in Tregs and CAFs characterized high-risk HCC and COAD, whereas a Th2-cell bias dominated in READ and PAAD. Intriguingly, high-risk STAD exhibited a hyper-inflammatory yet immunosuppressive state, marked by concurrent increases in Th1/Th2 cells and elevated checkpoint expression. While no obvious differential immune cell distribution was observed in CHOL risk subgroups. These collective patterns underscored that peroxisomal dysfunction does not elicit a uniform immune response but rather sculpts the TIME in a cancer-type-specific manner. Furthermore, the peroxisomal signature broadly influenced checkpoint expression and specifically correlated with READ’s innate/adaptive immune pathways. Collectively, our work provides the first evidence that peroxisomes are central regulators of immune infiltration, pathway activity, and checkpoint expression, thereby establishing them as key architects of the TIME. Such distinct TIME characteristics lead to differential responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) across digestive cancers, supporting the theoretical basis for clinical stratified immunotherapy. For example, CHOL presented negligible variations in checkpoint expression and thus may gain limited benefits from ICIs, which is consistent with the poor clinical efficacy of ICIs in advanced CHOL [71].

In short, our pan-cancer analysis demonstrated that peroxisomal functions were highly context-dependent. A critical question thus arises: how does peroxisomal dysfunction drive such divergent phenotypic outcomes? We propose several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms to explain this observation. First, the tissue-specific cellular origin dictates the basal metabolic and signaling landscape of each cancer. This is exemplified by the strong association between peroxisomal signatures and lipid metabolism in HCC, which aligns with the liver’s central role in lipid homeostasis. Second, the constitutive activation of distinct oncogenic pathways—such as PI3K-Akt in COAD/ESCC and retinol metabolism in HCC—in different cancer types may establish a unique contextual framework for peroxisomal signals. Third, the distinct cellular composition of the TIME in each organ provides a divergent pathological “soil” for peroxisome-derived cues. Consequently, we conceptualize peroxisomes not as uniform tumor initiators, but as versatile context-specific amplifiers that exacerbate the particular metabolic, signaling, and immune dysfunctions inherent to each digestive malignancy.

Dysregulated lipid metabolism emerges as another hallmark of cancer [72]. Peroxisomes contribute to tumorigenesis by regulating fatty acid β-oxidation, ether lipid synthesis, and bile acid biosynthesis [73, 74]. Our results revealed that loss of PEX13 may impair peroxisomal β-oxidation by downregulating ACOX1, thereby triggering global lipid metabolism remodeling across multiple digestive cancers, as well as the dysregulation of some lipid metabolic pathways. PEX13 led to the accumulation of phospholipids in HCC, indicating disrupted PPAR signaling and metabolic disturbances. Elevated phosphatidylinositols implied PI3K–AKT–mTOR hyperactivation, a pathway driving resistance to radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and TACE in HCC [75–78]. These results aligned with our enrichment results (Fig. 1J, Fig. S2B). Glycerophospholipids, linoleic acid, and alpha-linolenic acid are tightly linked to the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathway [79–81], which is also enriched with PEX13 (Fig. 1J). PPARγ activation enhances lipid peroxidation scavenging and ferroptosis resistance, thereby inducing resistance to lenvatinib [82]. PPARγ agonist-mediated ROS production activates AKT, thereby promoting HCC cell survival and radiation resistance through ROS limitation [83]. Moreover, the metabolism of linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid generates prostaglandins and leukotrienes, which directly regulate inflammatory pathways such as IL-17, TNF, and NF-κB [84–86] (Fig. 1J). In HCC patients, high levels of IL-17 [87], or TNF [88], as well as low NF-κB levels, were associated with sensitivity to lenvatinib.

These results indicate that PEX13-mediated lipid metabolic abnormalities act as an upstream “switch” that triggers a cascade of downstream pathway changes that regulate treatment response. Moreover, lipid metabolism closely interacted with TIME. PEX13 led to accumulation of PS, SM, and PI in HCC cells. Surface PS functioned as a strong “eat-me” signal [89], but its recognition by Tim-3/Tim-4 receptors induced T-cell suppression [90]. SM increased Tregs expansion [91], whereas PI activated the PI3K/AKT pathway to increase immune evasion via Tregs differentiation and PDCD1 expression [92]. These findings were supported by the positive correlations between Tregs infiltration, PDCD1 expression and risk scores (Fig. 1K–L). Thus, peroxisomal lipid reprogramming may drive therapy resistance in HCC by driving immunosuppression and modulating key pathways.

Consistent with the regulatory mechanism identified in HCC, UBE2D2 knockdown induces peroxisomal dysfunction, elevates cellular radiosensitivity, and remodels intracellular lipid profiles featured by altered HexCer, Cer and PI levels in colorectal cancer cells. These findings verify the conserved regulatory role of peroxisomes in therapeutic responses across digestive tumors. Such cross-tumor validation indicates that peroxisome-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming may act as a core conserved mechanism underlying therapeutic resistance in digestive cancers, which supports the development of broad-spectrum peroxisome-targeted strategies to reverse treatment resistance.

While our study elucidates the oncogenic roles of peroxisomes, their therapeutic targeting is challenged by profound tumor heterogeneity and the risk of on-target toxicity due to peroxisomes’ essential metabolic functions. Notwithstanding these challenges, our work nevertheless provides a biomarker-driven framework for context-specific intervention—such as PEX13 inhibition in stratified high-risk HCC—and advocates for targeting cancer-specific peroxisomal dependencies rather than global function. Thus, rather than offering an immediate therapeutic solution, this study furnishes a mechanistic rationale and a biomarker-guided strategy for future research to define the therapeutic window of peroxisomal modulation in selected patient populations.

However, the study’s limitation is that our study adopted a pan-cancer strategy to uncover universal trends while strategically employing HCC for deep mechanistic validation, thereby necessitating future studies in other digestive cancers. Moreover, our immunofluorescence staining results show that PEX13 in HCC correlates with PTN-NCL interaction and fibroblast activation, consistent with our scRNA-seq predictions. Nevertheless, direct perturbation of this ligand-receptor pair and its functional impact on the crosstalk between PEX13+ tumor cells and fibroblasts require validation via co-culture or recombinant protein-based ligand/receptor blockade in future work.

Conclusion

Peroxisomes exert context-dependent roles in digestive tumors by modulating pathways and remodeling the TIME, while consistently enhancing stress adaptation to therapy—a paradigm most firmly established in HCC. Targeting peroxisomal activity in defined clinical contexts, such as high-risk HCC, represents a translatable strategy worthy of prioritized investigation.
