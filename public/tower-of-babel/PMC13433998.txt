USP24 is a cross-reactive DUB targeting MOV10 to regulate IFN-I production
Authors: Rishov Mukhopadhyay, Simeon D Draganov, Timo Oosenbrug, Jimmy J L L Akkermans, Marjolein Kikkert, Klaus-Peter Knobeloch, Günter Fritz, María Guzmán, Sonia Zuñiga, Robbert Q Kim, Annemarthe G van der Veen, Benedikt M Kessler, Adán Pinto-Fernández, Paul P Geurink, Aysegul Sapmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe type I Interferon (IFN-I)-induced ubiquitin-like modifier Interferon-Stimulated Gene 15 (ISG15) plays a crucial role in the innate immune response against viral infections. ISG15 is conjugated to target proteins by an enzymatic cascade, called ISGylation. While ubiquitin-specific protease 18 (USP18) serves as the major deISGylase counteracting ISG15 conjugation, ISG15 cross-reactive deubiquitylating enzymes (DUBs) have also been reported. Here, we identify USP24 as an ISG15 cross-reactive DUB through activity-based protein profiling. USP24 processes pro-ISG15 and ISG15-linked substrates in vitro, and its depletion increases ISG15 conjugates following interferon stimulation in cells without altering canonical IFN-I signaling. USP24 knockout cells show increased IFN-β and ISG expression upon activation of cytosolic RNA sensing via viral mimicry. Proteomic analysis identifies RNA helicase Moloney leukemia virus 10 (MOV10) as a specific target of USP24 for deISGylation. Our data revealed that ISGylation of MOV10 promotes MOV10 interaction with IFIT3 and enhances IFN-β production/secretion in response to viral stimuli. This process is negatively regulated by USP24, which directly deISGylates MOV10. Our data highlight USP24’s role in modulating ISGylation and IFN-I production, suggesting USP24 as a potential therapeutic agent for infectious and inflammatory diseases.Subject terms: Proteases, Proteases

Introduction

Ubiquitin (Ub) and ubiquitin-like proteins (Ubls) constitute a highly important, yet complex class of protein post-translational modifications that regulate a wide variety of cellular mechanisms by maintaining protein stability1,2, protein localization3, and protein-protein interactions4. The interferon-stimulated gene 15 (ISG15), a 17 kDa ubiquitin-like protein consisting of two Ubl domains5, emerges as a key player in innate immunity that is tightly regulated by type I Interferon (IFN-I) signalling. Upon maturation of endogenously expressed pro-ISG15 by liberating the carboxy-terminal LRLRGG motif6, ISG15, similar to Ub, is conjugated to target proteins via the conjugation machinery that includes the E1 activating enzyme Ube1L7, the E2 conjugating enzyme Ube2L68, and the E3 ligases HERC59, TRIM2510, and HHARI/ARIH111, in humans6,12. The deconjugation of ISG15 from target proteins and the maturation of ISG15 are established by deISGylases13. The Ubiquitin-specific protease 18 (USP18) does not process Ub, but has been characterized as the canonical deISGylase that removes conjugated ISG15 and matures proISG15, thereby playing a critical role in innate immunity14,15.

Evidence of an interplay between the Ub and ISG15 systems has emerged over the last two decades. For instance, Ube2L616, TRIM2510, and HHARI/ARIH111 are involved in the conjugation of both Ub and ISG15. In addition, previous in vitro studies using ISG15 activity-based probes identified ISG15 cross-reactivity of a subset of deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs), including USP2, USP5, USP13, USP14, and USP2117–19. We and others have recently unveiled USP16 and USP36 as additional ISG15 cross-reactive DUBs that are able to deconjugate as well as mature ISG15, and we highlighted the potential role of USP16-dependent deISGylation in metabolic processes20,21. Building upon these discoveries, we aimed to explore additional DUBs with ISG15 cross-reactive properties that may exist, thereby enhancing our understanding of ISG15-mediated cellular processes.

Here, we show that USP24 is an ISG15 cross-reactive DUB. Using ISG15 activity-based probes and quantitative activity proteomics, USP24 is identified in murine EL4 extracts alongside previously reported cross-reactive DUBs, such as USP5, USP14, and USP16. USP24 is a cysteine protease with a molecular weight of 294 kDa that has been reported to deubiquitinate substrates, including DDB2 (regulating DNA damage response)22, p300 (affecting IL6 expression)22, βTrCP (in lung cancer cells)22, and p53 (modulating apoptosis)23. We find that USP24 effectively cleaves pro-ISG15 and ISG15-based substrates in vitro, and its depletion results in an increase in cellular ISG15 conjugates, without any direct effect on canonical IFN-I response under short or prolonged IFN-β stimulation. Additionally, USP24 depletion enhances IFN-β and ISG expression following cytosolic RNA sensor stimulation. Consistent with this, we further show that USP24 regulates a subset of ISGylated substrates independently of USP18. Further, we delineated specific ISGylated targets of USP24 by combining the data sourced from the total proteome, ISGylome, and GG-peptidome analyses. This comprehensive approach identifies the RNA helicase Moloney leukemia virus 10 protein (MOV10) that is reported to provide antiviral responses against RNA viruses by enhancing the type-I Interferon response24,25, as a target of USP24-mediated deISGylation. We find that ISGylation of MOV10 promotes its association with IFIT3, whereas USP24-dependent deISGylation disrupts this interaction. Moreover, depletion of USP24 leads to increased secretion of IFN-β upon viral mimicry stimulation and Sendai virus (SeV) infection, aligning with previous research findings in viral infection systems26. We further show that this increase in IFN-β secretion depends on ISGylation of MOV10. Collectively, our findings propose an ISG15-mediated functional control of MOV10 in the IFN-I mediated immune response controlled by the deISGylase activity of USP24, implicating the potential of USP24 as a valuable therapeutic target with applications in infectious diseases, cancer, autoimmunity, and neuroinflammation.

Results

Discovery of USP24 as an ISG15-reactive DUB by ABPP

To identify and confirm ISG15 cross-reactive DUBs, we employed activity-based probes equipped with a propargylamide warhead that can form a covalent linkage with the active site cysteine within the catalytic pocket of DUBs. The reactivity of an enzyme with a probe was assessed through either a fluorescent or biotin tag attached to the N-terminus of the probe (Fig. 1A)27. As USP18 is the predominant deISGylase and its expression is strongly induced upon IFN stimulation, we performed the assay using lysates from non-stimulated cells to facilitate the identification of DUBs with potentially lower ISG15 protease activity and narrower substrate specificity. This strategy minimized the risk that high USP18 activity would obscure the detection of more subtle DUB activities. Therefore, we initially conducted an activity-based pull-down assay using mouse EL4 cell lysates, which were treated with either DMSO or a biotin-tagged full-length mouse ISG15 propargylamide probe (Biotin-PEG4-mISG15(FL)-PA). Probe-bound enzymes were enriched using Neutravidin beads, washed under denaturing conditions, separated via SDS-PAGE, and subsequently subjected to in-gel trypsin digestion and label-free LC-MS/MS analysis. This not only confirmed previously reported ISG15 cross-reactive DUBs such as USP5, USP14, and USP16 but also unveiled USP24 as a so-far unidentified ISG15 cross-reactive DUB (Fig. 1B).

To validate the cross-reactivity of USP24 with the ISG15 probe in vitro, recombinant full-length human USP24 (USP24FL) was incubated with three fluorescent probes, including Rhodamine-tagged Ub propargylamide (Rho-Ub-PA)28,29, mouse C-terminal domain ISG15 propargylamide (Rho-mISG15CTD-PA, which was synthesized as described in the Supplementary Information, and untagged human full-length-ISG15 propargylamide (hISG15FL-PA) probes13. Labeling of USP24 with Rho-mISG15CTD-PA was observed in both fluorescence scanning and coomassie staining, while a clear band shift of recombinant USP24 upon incubation with an untagged hISG15FL-PA probe was observed in coomassie staining, confirming the reactivity of recombinant USP24FL with ISG15 probes (Fig. 1C). To further demonstrate the cross-reactivity of cellular USP24 with ISG15, we conducted a pull-down experiment where we incubated lysates from both Interferon-β (IFN-β) stimulated and non-stimulated HeLa cell extracts with Biotin-Ahx-hISG15CTD-PA probe, synthesized as described in the Supplementary Information, followed by the enrichment of the probe-bound DUBs with Neutravidin beads. Subsequent analysis by western blot showed that endogenous USP24 was remarkably enriched by the Biotin-Ahx-hISG15CTD-PA probe in lysates from both IFN-β-stimulated and non-stimulated cell extracts (Fig. 1D), consistent with data obtained from LC-MS/MS analysis. Notably, USP18 was solely enriched in IFN-β-stimulated cell lysates, aligning with its known status as an interferon-stimulated gene. Similar results were obtained in HAP1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1). Taken together, these findings strongly support the conclusion that deubiquitinase USP24 exhibits cross-reactivity with ISG15.

Characterization of USP24 as an ISG15 processing enzyme and deISGylase

To assess the catalytic capability of USP24 as deISGylase, we performed a series of in vitro experiments using ISG15-based substrates. ISG15 is synthesized as an immature protein, requiring the cleavage of a small c-terminal peptide by USP18 or other deISGylating enzymes30. Therefore, we investigated whether USP24 can process the pro-ISG15 precursor (a.a. 1–165) into mature ISG15 (a.a. 1–157) (Fig. 2A, B). Both USP24FL and USP24 catalytic domain (USP24CD) were incubated with recombinant pro-ISG15, and samples were analyzed over multiple time points by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie staining. Recombinant USP18 was included as a positive control. Results showed that USP24FL cleaved pro-ISG15 in vitro, albeit with lower efficiency compared to USP18. Notably, USP24CD showed no activity in processing pro-ISG15, suggesting the involvement of additional domains (Fig. 2C). We further investigated the deISGylase activity of USP24 using an ISG15CTD-based substrate that mimics the native isopeptide linkage between ISG15 and the lysine ε-amine of a substrate protein. In this substrate, hISG15CTD is linked to the lysine side chain of a fluorescent TAMRA-Lys-Gly peptide (TAMRA-Lys(Ac-hISG15CTD)-Gly), synthesized as described in the Supplementary Information, and its protease-mediated cleavage can be monitored by fluorescence polarization (FP) (Fig. 2D). This revealed that USP24FL can also process this isopeptide-linked ISG15 substrate, although at higher enzyme concentrations than required for processing of the isopeptide-linked Ub subsrate31 (Fig. 2E, F). Notably, USP24CD showed a significant overall decrease in activity towards the Ub substrate, as compared to full-length USP24, requiring about 125 times higher enzyme concentration, and it was hardly active on the ISG15 substrate (Fig. 2G, H). We also validated the deISGylase activity of USP24 using fluorogenic substrates ubiquitin rhodamine-morpholine (Ub-Rho-MP)28 and hISG15 C-terminal domain rhodamine-morpholine (Ac-hISG15 CTD-Rho-MP)32 (Supplementary Fig. 2A). USP24FL cleaved both ISG15 and ubiquitin substrates, though its activity towards ubiquitin was again higher than towards ISG15 (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C). As expected, USP24 also showed lower activity towards ISG15 compared to USP18 in this in vitro setting (Supplementary Fig. 2D). On the other hand, USP24CD processed the ubiquitin substrate but not the ISG15 substrate, exhibiting consistent results with the FP assay, suggesting the essentiality of the remaining domains of USP24 for its deISGylase activity (Supplementary Fig. 2E, F). In summary, USP24FL functions as both a pro-ISG15 processing enzyme and a deISGylase, but not its isolated catalytic domain.

USP24 regulates ISGylation and dsRNA-induced type I IFN production without directly impacting canonical type I IFN signaling

As USP24 appears to cleave pro-ISG15 and ISG15 substrates in vitro, we proceeded to investigate its potential deISGylation function in human cells. A time course experiment in HeLa cells was conducted to determine the optimal time for ISG15 conjugation upon IFN-β stimulation, revealing a peak in ISG15 expression and conjugation after 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Notably, unlike USP18 and ISG15, the levels of USP24 remained unaffected by IFN-β stimulation, suggesting that USP24 is not an interferon-stimulated gene. USP24 siRNA depletion led to a significant accumulation of ISG15 conjugates in HeLa cells upon IFN-β stimulation (Fig. 3A, B), consistent with observations from USP24 knockout (USP24KO) HeLa cells (Supplementary Fig. 3C).

To exclude any effects of USP24 depletion on canonical Type I IFN signaling (i.e., downstream of the IFNAR), we investigated both the activation of transcription factors of the pathway and ISG upregulation. Engagement of the IFNAR receptors with IFN-β stimulates the phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT2. Phosphorylated STAT1 and STAT2 form a complex with IRF9 to translocate into the nucleus and initiate the expression of ISGs such as USP1833. Examination of the transcription factors STAT1 and STAT2 phosphorylation status (Fig. 3C) revealed no alterations in response to USP24 depletion upon shorter periods of IFN-β stimulation, suggesting that USP24 does not interfere with Type I IFN signaling at initial stages. Interestingly, under prolonged IFN-β stimulation (48 h), USP24 depletion had no effect on basal or IFN-β–induced STAT1 protein levels, STAT1 phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B), and the expression level of USP18 (Fig. 3D, E). Likewise, STAT2 phosphorylation levels relative to total STAT2 remained unaffected. In contrast, USP24 knockdown resulted in a significant increase in STAT2 abundance following long-term IFN-β treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). These results indicated that USP24 is not essential for the immediate phosphorylation and activation of STAT2 to activate canonical interferon Type I response, but rather contributes to long-term control of STAT2 abundance, likely by promoting its degradation or limiting its stability.

To assess whether the enzymatic activity of USP18 is altered in USP24-depleted cells, we labeled the cell extracts from USP24-depleted and control cells treated with IFN-β with Rho-hISG15CTD-PA probe, synthesized as described in the Supplementary Information, followed by fluorescence scanning and immunoblot analysis. Depletion of USP24 also did not impact the activity of USP18 (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). Additionally, the effect of USP24 depletion on the accumulation of ISG15 conjugates was examined in comparison with USP18 depletion in HAP-1 cells under IFN-β stimulation for 48 h. Consistent with its role as the principal de-ISGylase, USP18 depletion led to a marked increase in ISG15 conjugates. Notably, USP24 depletion also resulted in the accumulation of ISG15 conjugates despite the presence of USP18, indicating that a subset of ISG15-modified substrates is predominantly regulated by USP24 (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Overall, these findings indicated that depletion of USP24 enhances IFN-β-induced ISGylation without direct effect on canonical Type I IFN signaling (i.e., downstream of the IFN-I receptor). Importantly, the loss of USP24 cannot be fully compensated by USP18 activity, highlighting the specific role of USP24 as a de-ISGylase even in the presence of USP18.

Given the role of USP24 in regulating antiviral responses26 and the deISGylase activity identified in this study, we sought to determine whether USP24 modulates antiviral signaling. To address this, we compared the expression of interferons and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) in USP24 wild-type (WT) and knockout (KO) cells following stimulation with dsRNA ligands that mimic viral infection and activate cytosolic RNA sensors of the innate immune system, including low-molecular-weight (LMW) Poly (I:C), high-molecular-weight (HMW) Poly (I:C), and 3p-hpRNA. USP24 KO cells exhibited increased IFNB1, IFNL1, and RSAD2 mRNA levels with all dsRNA ligands tested (Supplementary Fig. 5D–F). Collectively, these results confirm that USP24 is a negative regulator of type I interferon production and ISG induction, particularly in the context of antiviral signaling mediated by cytosolic RNA sensors.

Quantitative proteomics identifies discrete substrates of USP24 for deISGylation

To identify the ISG15-modified substrates, also known as ISGylome, targeted by USP24, we performed a range of dedicated label-free data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomics workflows in HeLa cells (Fig. 4A). To achieve this, we employed RNAi-mediated depletion of USP24 and used scrambled siRNA (siC) as a comparative control, across both non-stimulated and IFN-β stimulated conditions and we conducted four different analyses: (I) Total Proteome to investigate USP24-dependent changes in protein levels, (II) peptide enrichment with the di-Gly antibody recognizing Lys-ε-Gly-Gly remnants of lysine residues modified by Ub, or the Ubls NEDD8 (NEDD8 K-GG sites are lower than 2% and not affected by IFN-I treatment)34 and ISG15, following trypsin digestion (GG-peptidome), (III) enrichment with ISG15-specific antibody (ISG15 interactome) to assess interactions between ISG15 and cellular proteins and (IV) enrichment with the di-Gly antibody (GG-peptidome) after USP2 deubiquitinase treatment to exclude all ubiquitinated peptides (Refined ISGylome) (Fig. 4A). The efficiency of sample preparation was evaluated by immunoblot analysis against indicated antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). A total of 7610 unique proteins in the total proteome analysis (Supplementary Data 1), 8169 unique Lys-ε-Gly-Gly remnant peptides (Supplementary Data 2), and 4410 unique proteins in the ISG15 interactome (Supplementary Data 3) were identified. To elucidate global similarities and differences among replicate samples and detect hidden patterns across various conditions, we employed principal component analysis (PCA) on the total proteome, GG-peptidome, and ISG15 interactome, as well as hierarchical cluster analysis on the total proteome. The PCA profile revealed robust clustering of replicates from different treatments (Supplementary Fig. 7A–C). Furthermore, hierarchical clustering analysis showed different protein expression profiles across various conditions (Supplementary Fig. 7D), including IFN-β protein patterns in siUSP24 versus siC controls (Supplementary Fig. 8A, B). Cross-comparative analysis of the USP24-dependent ISG15 interactome and GG-peptidome revealed multiple unique peptides from eleven proteins that were specifically enriched in both the ISG15 interactome and GG-peptidome (but not upon USP2 treatment) (green box in Fig. 4B, C). These included Moloney Leukemia Virus 10 Protein (MOV10), Interferon Induced Protein With Tetratricopeptide Repeats 2 (IFIT2), Tryptophanyl-TRNA Synthetase 1 (WARS1), SH3 Domain-Containing Protein 19 (SH3D19), Erythrocyte Membrane Protein P55 (MPP1), UV Radiation Resistance-Associated Gene Protein (UVRAG), Cystathionine Gamma-Lyase (CTH), Bridging Integrator 3 (BIN3), Interferon-Induced GTP-Binding Protein Mx2 (MX2), E3 Ubiquitin-Protein Transferase MAEA (MAEA), and Ribonuclease H2 subunit A (RNASEH2A). These proteins exhibited a ≥ 1.5-fold difference of the Log2 intensities in both datasets in comparison of siUSP24 treated with IFN-β versus siC treated with IFN-β (Fig. 4B). Additionally, cross-comparison of the GG-peptidome analysis between IFN-β stimulated siUSP24 samples treated with USP2 versus untreated, and the ISG15 interactome analysis between IFN-β stimulated siUSP24 versus IFN-β stimulated siC also identified that the same proteins showed a ≥ 1.5 fold difference of the Log2 intensities for the ISG15 interactome but not for USP2-treated GG-peptidome (Fig. 4C). Volcano plots of the ISG15 interactome and GG-peptidome data showed that only 7 out of the 11 proteins (MOV10, WARS1, SH3D19, UVRAG, CTH, BIN3, and MX2) were significantly enriched in siUSP24 samples treated with IFN-β compared to siC samples treated with IFN-β (Supplementary Fig. 8C–F), suggesting these seven proteins as high-confidence candidates of USP24-dependent deISGylated hits. Notably, MOV10 showed significant enrichment in siUSP24 samples compared to siC samples without treatment of IFN-β, implying that MOV10 may be a substrate of USP24 deISGlyase and possibly deubiquitinase activity (Supplementary Fig. 8D, F).

To gain a global insight into the ISGylome of USP24 and the associated cellular functions, we performed a network analysis of the hits enriched by the ISG15-specific antibody (ISG15 interactome), using Cytoscape 3.9.1 with the STRING app (Confidence cutoff: 0.4, default). We selected hits (siUSP24, IFN-β treated—siC, IFN-β treated) based upon their p-value (−log10 ≥ 1.3) that represents proteins significantly enriched by the ISG15 antibody. Furthermore, we filtered based on fold change (log2 ≥ 1.3) that showed 56 proteins, which are at least 2.5 times more enriched by the ISG15 antibody upon USP24 depletion (Supplementary Data 3 and 4). These 56 proteins were analyzed on Cytoscape, among which 21 proteins were found to be interconnected. This included MOV10 and MX2, two of the hits identified as potential substrates of USP24-mediated deISGylase activity (Fig. 4D). To explore the biological importance of the networked proteins, we subsequently performed a functional enrichment analysis of the network, where ISG15 conjugation and IFN-I signaling came up to be the most significant. Overall, our network analysis suggests that the USP24-dependent ISGylome is associated with IFN signaling and ISGylation mechanism, where MOV10 was selected as an interesting target of USP24 deISGylation on the basis of its higher p-value and fold change compared to MX2 in the network analysis (Fig. 4D, E).

USP24-mediated deISGylation of MOV10 regulates MOV10-IFIT3 interaction during antiviral signaling

MOV10 is an RNA helicase involved in the regulation of gene expression by controlling mRNA decay pathways35. It is also involved in the regulation of IFN signaling and antiviral responses against RNA virus25, but the underlying mechanism of its regulation is unknown. After observing that MOV10 is notably enriched as an ISGylated substrate in a USP24-dependent manner, we further investigated to ascertain whether MOV10 serves as a direct and specific target of USP24 deISGylase function. To this end, a hit validation experiment was carried out in USP24-depleted (siUSP24), USP24KO, and WT (siC) HeLa cells, both under IFN-β stimulation or basal conditions. To discriminate between ISG15 and Ub modifications on MOV10 and USP24 specificity towards MOV10 as deISGylase, USP18- and ISG15-depleted HeLa cells were also included as controls. The analysis showed a noticeable upward shift in the MOV10 band in USP24-depleted and USP24KO cells compared to control cells, particularly evident under IFN-β stimulation, indicating USP24-dependent modification of MOV10 under such conditions (Fig. 5A). Conversely, depletion of ISG15 or USP18 did not induce any visible shift in the MOV10 band relative to control HeLa cells, thus confirming USP24-dependent ISGylation of MOV10 under IFN-β stimulation. To further prove the direct deISGylase activity of USP24 on MOV10, we performed a biochemical characterization of ISGylation on MOV10 by reconstituting the expression of either USP24WT or USP24 catalytically inactive Cys1698Ala mutant (USP24CA) in USP24KO HeLa cells. Ectopic expression of USP24WT but not of USP24CA mutant significantly reduced ISGylated MOV10 levels in USP24KO HeLa cells (Fig. 5B, C), confirming that the catalytic activity of USP24 as a de-ISGylase determines the cleavage of ISG15 from MOV10. To establish an ISGylation-deficient MOV10 mutant construct, we revisited our GG-peptidomics data and identified two unique MOV10-derived peptides that were only enriched in USP24-depleted cells after IFN-β treatment and not processed by USP2. These peptides contain two lysine residues with Gly-Gly signatures (Supplementary Fig. 9A–C), indicating them as the bona fide ISGylation sites of MOV10 targeted by USP24. Based on this information, we generated a construct expressing GFP-tagged ISGylation-deficient MOV10 mutant by mutating lysine residues K121 and K714 to arginine. To avoid the potential hopping of ISGylation, we additionally mutated K715 due to its close proximity to K714, henceforth referred to as the MOV10 3KR mutant.

ISGylation modulates the localization and functional interactions of key immune regulators in the context of antiviral responses11,36,37. Given this, we sought to determine whether USP24-mediated deISGylation affects MOV10’s subcellular distribution or its potential interactions during viral mimicry. MOV10 is known to relocalize from processing bodies (P-bodies) to lipid droplets (LDs) upon HCV infection38. To recapitulate this behavior, we treated HeLa cells with Poly (I:C) to simulate viral infection and assessed MOV10–LD colocalization in both USP24-depleted and WT cells. Poly (I:C) treatment induced a clear relocalization of MOV10 to LDs in both conditions. Notably, USP24 depletion did not alter this redistribution, suggesting that USP24-dependent (de)ISGylation of MOV10 does not influence its relocalization to LDs under these conditions. (Supplementary Fig. 10A–C). To identify potential effectors linking MOV10 ISGylation to innate immune signaling, we analyzed the ISG15 interactome clusters (Fig. 4D) and found a strong association between MOV10 and several IFIT family members (IFIT1, IFIT2, and IFIT3). Given the established role of IFIT3 as a scaffold that stabilizes the IFIT1–IFIT2 complex and enhances its antiviral activity39,40, as well as previous evidence suggesting a possible interaction between IFIT3 and MOV1041, IFIT3 was selected for further investigation. We examined whether loss of USP24 influences IFIT3 expression following canonical innate immune stimulation. In HeLa cells treated with either LMW Poly (I:C) or recombinant IFN-β, USP24 depletion markedly increased IFIT3 protein abundance after 24 h of Poly (I:C) treatment, whereas no notable change was observed under IFN-β stimulation (Fig. 5D, E). Consistently, IFIT3 mRNA levels exhibited an upward trend in USP24-deficient cells upon Poly (I:C) stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 10D). To further assess whether MOV10 and IFIT3 interact and whether this interaction depends on USP24-dependent MOV10 ISGylation, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays in WT and USP24 KO HeLa cells transfected with either WT MOV10 or an ISGylation-deficient MOV10-3KR mutant. Under USP24 depletion and 24 h of Poly(I:C) stimulation, ectopically expressed WT MOV10 efficiently co-immunoprecipitated endogenous IFIT3, whereas the MOV10-3KR mutant showed a markedly reduced association (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. 10E). Notably, this interaction was not readily detectable under conditions where USP24 was present (Supplementary Fig. 10E), suggesting that deISGylationUSP24 status and MOV10 ISGylation may influence the extent of MOV10–IFIT3 association under cytosolic RNA-sensing conditions. Together, these findings indicate that ISGylation of MOV10 correlates with enhanced association with IFIT3, while USP24-mediated deISGylation may modulate this interaction.

USP24-mediated reversible ISGylation of MOV10 by USP24 governs antiviral IFN-β responses

Our preceding data demonstrated that USP24-dependent ISGylation of MOV10 promotes its interaction with IFIT3, a key mediator of antiviral innate immune signaling42,43. Previous studies have demonstrated that MOV10 enhances IFN-β secretion during RNA virus infection25, whereas USP24 functions as a negative regulator of IFN production/secretion26. We hypothesized that the reversible ISGylation of MOV10, regulated by USP24 deISGylase activity, may govern IFN production and secretion. To address this, we used the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to measure the IFN-β secretion from the Huh7 WT and MOV10KO cells transfected with siUSP24 or siC following 24 h of LMW poly(I:C). Depletion of USP24 significantly increased IFN-β secretion in Huh-7 WT cells but not in the Huh7 MOV10KO cells (Fig. 6A, B and Supplementary Fig. 11A, B), indicating a regulatory connection between USP24 and MOV10 in IFN-β secretion. To understand whether this regulation of IFN-β secretion depends on the reversible ISGylation of MOV10, we reconstituted the expression of MOV10 WT and MOV10 3KR mutant in MOV10KO Huh7 cells. Interestingly, ectopic expression of MOV10 WT significantly increased IFN-β secretion in USP24-depleted MOV10KO cells, while a slight increase was observed in control cells (siC). However, the ISGylation-deficient mutant, MOV10 3KR, was unable to rescue the effect of loss of endogenous MOV10 on IFN-β secretion in both USP24-depleted and control cells. We next evaluated the biological relevance of this regulatory axis during viral infection. MOV10-depleted Huh7 cells were reconstituted with either MOV10 WT or MOV10 3KR and subsequently infected with Sendai virus (SeV) for 72 h. Quantitative PCR analysis showed that MOV10 WT expression increased IFNB1 mRNA levels by approximately 1.5-fold compared with MOV10 3KR. However, SeV leader promoter (LP) RNA levels remained unchanged, indicating that MOV10 ISGylation enhances antiviral signaling without directly affecting viral replication (Supplementary Fig. 11C, D). Collectively, these findings identify a functional USP24–MOV10–ISG15 axis that regulates IFN-β secretion. USP24-mediated deISGylation suppresses MOV10 activation, whereas sustained ISGylation of MOV10 promotes its antiviral function by upregulating IFN-β expression, thereby linking USP24 enzymatic activity to the control of innate immune signaling (Fig. 7).

Discussion

DUBs are crucial regulators of various cellular processes, including the innate immune response44.45, Typically, DUBs specifically target Ub. However, with the advancement of activity-based tools using Ub and Ubls, instances of cross-reactivity with other Ub-like proteins have been reported21,46. A glimpse of the existence of DUB cross-reactivity with ISG15, a Ub-like post-translational modifier and an important signaling protein in immunity-related pathways47, was identified in an ABPP-based screen involving 22 recombinant human DUBs from the USP protease family17. This finding indicated USP cross-reactivity between cleaving Ub- and ISG15 isopeptide bonds. It also predicted extendibility towards peptide bond cleavage specificity as USP21 was reported to process both poly-Ub and ISG1518. Subsequently, USP16 and USP36 were discovered to exert cross-reactivity between Ub, Fubi, and ISG15, suggesting promiscuity towards multiple Ubls for a subset of DUBs20,21. Additionally, our previous research showed that USP16 de-ISGylase activity targets ISGylated proteins involved in (immuno)metabolism.19 In the current study, we expanded this panel of ISG15 cross-reactive DUBs by identifying USP24 through a pulldown experiment using our Biotin-ISG15-PA probe followed by LC-MS/MS analysis. USP24, a member of the cysteine protease USP DUB family, is gaining attention due to its broad biological functions related to its deubiquitinating activity48. However, its deISGylation activity has not been previously explored. USP18, which is an ISG itself, is the primary protease for ISG15 and can process nearly all ISGylated substrates. This raises the question about the necessity of ISG15 cross-reactive DUBs in the presence or absence of USP18: Are there ISGylated substrates that USP18 does not target? If so, what is the biological significance of this cross-reactivity? To address these questions, we investigated the deISGylase activity of USP24, identifying specific substrates targeted by USP24 for de-ISGylation. Our detailed exploration reveals a function of USP24 as a deISGylase in the innate immune response, highlighting a mechanism of immune regulation by cross-reactive DUBs.

The ISG15 cascade initiates from the processing of pro-ISG15 to mature ISG15, which is essential for ISG15 conjugation to substrates. Although USP18 is the primary enzyme responsible for processing pro-ISG15, its depletion does not impede the maturation of pro-ISG15 to ISG15 in cells, suggesting the involvement of other enzymes20,49. As previously reported for USP1620, we found that recombinant USP24 can also process pro-ISG15 into mature ISG15. This highlights the role of cross-reactive DUBs, which may compensate for the loss of USP18 or enhance the process of pro-ISG15 processing to mature ISG15. We further investigated the catalytic potential of USP24 towards ISG15-conjugated substrates using several in vitro ISG15CTD and ubiquitin-based substrates. Full-length USP24 actively processed both ubiquitinated and ISGylated substrates. Intriguingly, when the catalytic domain of USP24 was isolated, its activity towards ISG15 was abrogated while retaining its activity towards ubiquitinated substrates. This potentially indicates the presence of other domains on USP24 to be required for ISG15 recognition by the catalytic domain or possibly the catalytic domain exhibits such minimal activity on ISG15CTD substrates that it is barely detectable in in vitro assays.

To gain a comprehensive understanding of USP24 and ISG15 biology, the investigation was extended to mammalian cells. Depletion of USP24 in HeLa cells led to significant accumulation of ISG15 conjugates upon IFN-β stimulation. This observation was consistent in both USP24 knockout HeLa and HAP-1 cells, indicating a catalytic relationship between USP24 and ISG15-conjugated proteins. Further analysis confirmed that, unlike USP1850, USP24 is neither an ISG nor does it affect canonical Type-I Interferon signaling or USP18 itself, indicating that USP24 has a specific and independent role as a deISGylase. This encouraged us to explore the cellular substrates deISGylated by USP24 and investigate whether they overlap with the substrates targeted by USP18. However, as USP24 primarily functions as a deubiquitinase, distinguishing ISGylated substrates from ubiquitinated ones specifically targeted by USP24 posed a challenge. We addressed this issue by performing a proteomic-wide analysis in the absence and presence of USP24 under non-stimulated and IFN-stimulated HeLa cells, where we conducted four different workflows, including total proteome, the di-Gly antibody enrichment of proteins modified by Ub, Nedd8, or ISG15 following trypsin digestion, enrichment with the ISG15-specific antibody (ISG interactome) and including an additional step of treating the samples with recombinant USP2 followed by di-Gly antibody enrichment, which eliminated most of the ubiquitinated substrates and specifically revealed ISGylated peptides affected by USP24 depletion51. Although NEDDylated peptides may in principle be captured in the GG-peptidome, NEDDylation is relatively rare compared with ubiquitination and ISGylation in IFN-stimulated cells34, and USP24 was independently validated as an ISG15-reactive deISGylase. While we cannot formally exclude that a minor fraction of peptides may represent NEDDylated substrates, our enrichment strategies support ISGylation as the predominant modification underlying the USP24-dependent signals identified in this study. Cross-comparisons of these proteomic analyses revealed seven ISGylated substrates potentially targeted by USP24, which include MOV10, WARS1, SH3D19, UVRAG, CTH, BIN3, and MX2. Bioinformatic analysis and subsequent in-cell validation confirmed MOV10 as a USP24-specific substrate targeted for deISGylation, even in the presence of USP18. This indicates the existence of ISGylated substrates that are not processed by USP18 but require other cross-reactive DUBs like USP24. While our data establish USP24 as a key regulator of MOV10 ISGylation, we cannot exclude the possibility that additional ISG15 cross-reactive DUBs may also contribute to MOV10 deISGylation in a context- or cell type–dependent manner, an important question for future investigation. A Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the USP24-dependent ISGylome emphasized IFN-I signaling, raising the question about the underlying biological importance of the USP24-MOV10-ISG15 axis in innate immunity.

MOV10 is an RNA helicase known for its role in suppressing viral replication24. Previous studies have identified a direct correlation between MOV10 and IFN-α in patients with Chronic Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. HBV-infected patients exhibited significantly lower mRNA levels of both MOV10 and IFN-α compared to a control group52. IFN-β, another IFN-I, production and secretion are critical events in the innate immune response, playing a pivotal role in activating immune cells and stimulating adaptive immunity53. Another study highlighted the influence of MOV10 on IFN pathways by demonstrating that MOV10 enhances IFN-β production, thereby accelerating cell antiviral activity25. The study demonstrated that in the presence of MOV10, upon viral infection, there is increased nuclear localization of the transcription factor interferon regulatory factor 3, which mediates IFN-β-mediated immune response25. However, the mechanism by which MOV10 is regulated to modulate IFN-β secretion was not understood.

Our study identifies a regulatory axis involving USP24, MOV10, ISG15, and IFIT3, which modulates interferon responses. Interestingly, despite elevated IFN-β levels upon depletion of USP24, canonical JAK-STAT signaling remains unaffected in both short- and long-term induction scenarios using IFN-β or Poly(I:C), suggesting that USP24 does not amplify downstream IFNAR signaling under these conditions. However, we also show that USP24 depletion increases RSAD2 and IFN-β transcription upon LMW Poly(I:C) stimulation, indicating that this axis can enhance cytosolic RNA-sensing pathways under specific conditions54,55. Moreover, USP24-deficient cells exhibit increased IFIT3 protein abundance after Poly(I:C) treatment, accompanied by a trend toward elevated IFIT3 mRNA levels. This suggests that USP24 may regulate signaling pathways upstream or parallel to canonical IFNAR–JAK–STAT signaling, consistent with previous reports showing that IFIT3 can be induced by upstream of IFNAR and independently of the canonical IFN-I pathway42,56. Additionally, the stabilization of MOV10-IFIT3 interaction observed upon USP24-dependent MOV10 ISGylation may contribute to non-canonical signaling and/or IFN-I production (i.e., upstream of the IFNAR signaling), further enhancing transcription and secretion of IFN-β57. A similar interaction of MOV10 with the interferon-stimulated gene IRAV has previously been described during dengue virus infection, where it was linked to IFN production and antiviral activity58. We demonstrate that USP24 depletion enhances ISGylation of MOV10, leading to increased IFN-β expression and secretion upon viral mimicry (Poly (I:C)) or viral infection (SeV). WT MOV10 rescues this effect but not by an ISGylation-deficient mutant, confirming the functional importance of MOV10 ISGylation in this context25. Despite increased IFN-β secretion, we did not observe reduced viral replication under these experimental conditions. The magnitude of IFN-β induction ( ~ twofold), together with the high multiplicity of infection and the known IFN-antagonistic properties of Sendai virus59–61, may limit the ability to detect measurable antiviral effects. Together, these findings suggest that the USP24–MOV10–IFIT3 axis primarily modulates IFN-β production rather than amplifying downstream IFNAR signaling strength, and whether this regulatory axis translates into enhanced antiviral restriction remains an important question for future studies. Moreover, in silico structural modeling of MOV10 was explored to gain preliminary insight into how ISGylation may influence its molecular function. The identified ISGylation sites, Lys121 and Lys714, are located in distinct, surface-exposed regions within the CH and helicase domains, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 12). These positions suggest that ISG15 attachment could affect MOV10 stability, modulate protein–protein interaction interfaces, or influence helicase activity. While these observations are based on predictive models, they highlight ISGylation as a potential regulatory mechanism fine-tuning MOV10 function. These insights open up opportunities for future studies aimed at resolving how ISGylation dynamically governs MOV10 structure and its broader role in innate immune regulation. Taken together, our findings suggest that USP24 plays a role in regulating innate immunity through its deISGylation activity on MOV10 and that the functional ISGylation of MOV10 contributes to IFN-β induction and IFIT3 stabilization. Future studies will be important to explore the broader implications of the USP24–MOV10–IFIT3 axis—mediated by ISG15-dependent ISGylation of MOV10—in chronic inflammation, immune tolerance, and tumor immunology, contexts in which USP24, MOV10, and IFIT3 are increasingly recognized to play important roles25,42,48,62,63 (Fig. 7).

Additionally, IFN-β has previously been explored therapeutically. A study identified several small molecules that significantly inhibited SARS-CoV2 replication in vitro by modulating the IFN-β induced host signatures, though the underlying molecular mechanism has not been reported yet64. Moreover, other studies have identified IFN-α and IFN-β as anticancer immunotherapeutics that improve the outcome of diverse malignancies by influencing the immune, vascular, and apoptotic processes through transcription of pleiotropic factors. Thus, insights into IFN regulation offer opportunities to combine interferon activation with other therapies in cancer treatment65. USP24 deISGylates MOV10, reducing MOV10-mediated acceleration of IFN-β secretion and acting as a negative regulator of the IFN response. This suggests that inhibiting USP24 could prevent the deISGylation of MOV10, thereby elevating cellular IFN-β production/secretion. Therefore, USP24 inhibitors have the potential to serve as adjunct antiviral therapy and boosters of cancer immunotherapy. This finding encourages further research to screen for USP24 inhibitors66 and to identify other cross-reactive DUBs, exploring their biological roles. Such studies could open avenues for understanding the role of DUBs in crucial pathways involved in various diseases and identify drug targets. Our extensive proteomic data also highlighted other Ub/ISG15-conjugated targets of USP24 besides MOV10. Investigating their biological functions could reveal further important roles of USP24, not only in innate immunity but also in other cellular mechanisms.

Methods

Synthetic probes and assay reagents

All synthetic probes and assay reagents (ISG15- and Ub-based) used in this study were of >95% purity, as determined by LC-MS analysis. Supplementary Table 1 lists all details on these probes and reagents. Synthesis procedures and LC-MS data are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Cell culture

EL4 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine and 10% fetal bovine serum. HEK293T cells (ATCC CRL-3216), HeLa WT cells, HeLa USP24KO cells, Huh-7 WT cells, and Huh-7 MOV10 knockout (MOV10KO) cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco), whereas HAP1 cells (Horizon, Cat# C631), HAP1 USP18−/− cells (Horizon, Cat# HZGHC000492c011), and HAP1 USP24KO (Horizon, Cat# HZGHC000463c00) cells were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (Gibco). Media were supplemented with 8% fetal calf serum (FCS). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Huh-7 WT and Huh-7 MOV10KO cells were kindly provided by Dr. Sonia Zuñiga67. HeLa USP24KO cell lines were generated using CRISPR–Cas9-mediated gene editing, as described in the CRISPR/Cas9- mediated knockout of USP24 method section below. HeLa and EL4 cells were maintained in-house. No authentication was performed for EL4 cells. HeLa, Huh-7 WT, and Huh-7 MOV10KO cell lines were authenticated using the Promega PowerPlex Fusion 5 C autosomal short tandem repeat kit. Authentication was performed using the FIDO protocol, based on methods described previously68, with minor modifications to accommodate the specific PCR kit. Procedures were largely followed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The FIDO facility is ISO 17025 accredited for this application (accreditation number L198, Dutch Raad voor Accreditatie). Cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination.

DNA constructs

A full-length USP24 plasmid was provided by Dr. Feng Gong (University of Miami Health System)69. It was recloned into an in-house-available mScarlet-C1 vector with a CMV promoter using In Vivo Assembly (IVA) cloning technique70. Briefly, the USP24 insert and mScarlet-C1 backbone were amplified with Taq DNA polymerase (Cat# 11147633103, Roche) using the primers USP24_mScarlet_IVA Forward and USP24_mScarlet_IVA Reverse listed in Supplementary Data 5, which contained overlapping homologous regions to facilitate in vivo recombination. The amplified products were treated with DpnI (Cat# ER1702, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove template DNA and directly transformed into competent DH5α bacteria, where homologous recombination-mediated assembly occurred in vivo. MOV10 plasmid was purchased from Addgene (Cat# 10976) and recloned into an in-house mGFP-C1 vector using conventional restriction enzyme-based cloning. The MOV10 insert was amplified using the HindIII_MOV10 Forward and BamHI_MOV10 Reverse primers listed in Supplementary Data 5, digested with the corresponding restriction enzymes, and ligated into the linearized mGFP-C1 vector backbone. The cloning strategy used for recombinant protein expression in insect cells is described in the “Protein Expression and Purification” section. Briefly, the USP24 catalytic domain (CD) was cloned into the pFastBac vector using the USP24CD_PFastBac Forward and USP24CD_PFastBac Reverse primers listed in Supplementary Data 5.

Site-directed mutagenesis was performed to generate the USP24 CA and MOV10 3KR mutants using the primers listed in Supplementary Data 5 under “site-directed mutagenesis.” Briefly, a mixture of template DNA, 1× Pfu buffer, 10 mM dNTPs, 125 ng forward and reverse primers with the desired mutation(s), 1 µL of TURBO Pfu Polymerase (Cat# 600250, Agilent), and 50 ng template DNA was combined with autoclaved MilliQ water to a final volume of 50 µL. PCR was done using the following program: 98 °C for 3 min, 60 °C (depending on primer Tm) for 20 s, 72 °C for 30 s/kb of template size, 72 °C for 5 min, 4 °C on infinite hold. 40 µL of the amplified PCR product was incubated with 1 µL of DpnI (Cat# ER1702, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h at 37 °C, and the resulting DNA was transformed into the competent DH5α bacterial strain. All plasmids generated in this study were verified by DNA sequencing. All oligonucleotide sequences, including cloning, mutagenesis, and sequencing primers, are provided in Supplementary Data 5 and were synthesized by Sigma-Aldrich. Newly generated plasmids are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Transfection

For DNA transfection, HEK293T and HeLa cells were seeded to achieve around 60% confluency the following day, while Huh7 cells were reversibly transfected as described previously67. For reverse transfection, DNA–PEI complexes were prepared before cell seeding, allowing cells to attach directly onto the transfection complexes. Transfections were performed using PEI (polyethylenimine; Polysciences Inc., Cat# 23966). Briefly, the transfection mixture consisted of 200 µL DMEM without supplements, the required amount of plasmid DNA, and PEI (1 mg/mL), with the DNA:PEI ratio maintained at 1:3 (1 µg plasmid DNA:3 µg PEI). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 20 min without agitation before being added dropwise to the cells or used for reverse transfection. Cells were monitored after 24 h and subsequently collected.

For siRNA transfection, human USP24 (On-TARGETplus Cat# J-006073-11), ISG15 (OnTarget Plus # L-004235-03-0005), and MOV10 (OnTarget Plus Cat# L-014162-00-0005)specific siRNAs were purchased from Dharmacon, while human USP18 specific siRNA (Cat# AM16708) was purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific. Silencing was performed in HeLa and HAP-1 cells. In a 24-well format, 50 μL of siRNA (500 nM stock) was incubated with 1 μL Dharmafect reagent 1 (Dharmacon) diluted in 49 μL medium without supplements (total volume of 100 μL transfection mix). Incubation was done at room temperature with gentle shaking for 20 min. A total of 2 × 104 cells were resuspended in 400 μL of growth medium without antibiotics and added to the transfection mix. The final volume, along with the transfection mix, was 500 μL per well. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, cells were treated with IFN-β (1:1000 or 1000 U/mL) and incubated for 48 h. Cells were cultured for a total of 3 days before further analysis.

Sendai virus infection

Huh7 MOV10 KO cells that have been transfected with 1 μg plasmid DNA encoding EV/MOV10wt/MOV103KR were infected 48 h post plasmid DNA transfection with Sendai Virus (VR907 Cantell, ATCC) in DMEM without FCS at MOI = 10. After adsorption for 1 h, the infection medium was removed, cells were washed with PBS and cells were cultured in DMEM with 2% FCS for the indicated time before harvest.

CRISPR/Cas9- mediated knockout of USP24

gRNA sequences targeting the USP24 gene (gRNA1: GCTCGTAGCCGCCGTAGTCG, gRNA2: GAACGACATTAACGAGGCCG, gRNA3: CTACGGCGGCTACGAGCCCA, gRNA4: ACTGCTCACCAACGAGCGGC) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies and cloned into the PX459 plasmid (containing the Cas9 gene and a puromycin resistance gene) using IVA cloning70. This plasmid was transiently transfected into HeLa or HAP1 cells using Effectene and the following day, cells were selected with 200 µg/mL puromycin for 72 h. Cells were then diluted and maintained in a 15 cm dish, allowing colonies to grow distinctly. Isolated colonies were collected, expanded, and analyzed for the loss of USP24 by Western blot.

Biotin-mISG15FL-PA probe labeling, and affinity enrichment for label-free LC-MS/MS analysis

EL4 cells (9 × 106) were lysed in 1 mL phosphate buffer (pH 7–7.5) by sonication. Lysates were supplemented with 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 1% NP-40, and insoluble material was removed by centrifugation at 21,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were pre-cleared with NeutrAvidin beads by rotation for 1 h at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 min. Pre-cleared lysates were incubated with 5 μM a biotin-tagged full-length mouse ISG15 propargylamide probe (Biotin-mISG15FL-PA) or DMSO (vehicle control) for 1 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, 30 μL NeutrAvidin beads were added, and samples were rotated for 1 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed five times with wash buffer (phosphate buffer containing 500 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, and 0.1% SDS) by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 1 min. After the final wash, the buffer was completely removed, and the beads were resuspended in NuPAGE SDS sample buffer containing 5% β-mercaptoethanol and boiled for 10 min. Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, and gel regions of interest were excised for in-gel digestion followed by label-free LC-MS/MS analysis. Uncropped and unprocessed scans of blots are provided in the Source Data file in accordance with Nature Portfolio image integrity guidelines.

SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting

Samples were resolved on precast Bis-Tris NuPAGE gels (Invitrogen, 4–12%, 12%) and Tris-Acetate gels (Invitrogen, 3–8%). MOPS buffer (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, USA) for Bis-Tris gels and Tris-Acetate buffer (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, USA) for Tris-Acetate gels were used as running buffer. Proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Protan BA85, 0.45 μm, GE Healthcare) at 300 mA for 2.5 h. The membranes were blocked in 5% milk (skim milk powder, LP0031, Oxiod) in 1× PBS (P1379, Sigma-Aldrich), incubated with a primary antibody diluted in 5% milk in 0.1% PBS-Tween 20 (PBST) overnight, washed three times for 10 min in 0.1% PBST, incubated with the secondary antibody diluted in 5% milk in 0.1% PBST for 30–45 min, and washed three more times in 0.1% PBST. DIA-PASEF mass spectrometry data acquisition and DIA-NN analysis

Peptides for analysis by LC-MS/MS were loaded on Evotip Pure C18 tips (Evosep) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, tips were rinsed with 20 µL Solvent B by centrifugation, conditioned by soaking in 1-propanol, equilibrated with 20 µL Solvent A by centrifugation, loaded with 2 µL of sample and 18 µL of solvent A, then centrifuged, washed with 20 µL solvent A by centrifugation, and wetted with 100 µL solvent and a brief centrifugation step for 10 s. All centrifugation steps were 60 s at 800 × g unless stated otherwise.

Peptides were analysed using an Evosep One liquid chromatography system coupled to a timsTOF SCP mass spectrometer (Bruker) using the Whisper 20 samples per day method and a 75 µm × 150 mm C18 column with 1.7 µm particles and an integrated Captive Spray Emitter (IonOpticks) as described previously76. Data were acquired using diaPASEF77, and methods generated by py_diAID78. Total proteome and ISG15 pulldown samples were analysed using a method with 25 variable-width PASEF frames, with two ion mobility windows per PASEF frame, covering a region between 300–1200 m/z and 0.6–1.6 V s cm−2. diGly samples were analysed using a method with 12 variable-width PASEF frames, with two ion mobility windows per frame, covering a region between 330–1100 m/z and 0.69–1.57 V s cm−2. For both methods, the accumulation and ramp times were 100 ms, and the collision energy was ramped linearly over the ion mobility range: 20–59 eV over 0.6–1.6 V s cm−2. DIA-MS data were analyzed using DIA-NN (v1.8) in library-free mode, as previously described79. Raw files were searched against a UniProt reference proteome with common contaminants included (UniProt proteome UP000005640 (2022)). Trypsin/P was specified as the proteolytic enzyme, allowing up to one missed cleavage. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation of methionine and N-terminal protein acetylation were included as variable modifications. For ubiquitinome analyses, diglycine remnant modification on lysine residues (K-GG) was additionally specified as a variable modification. Precursor and protein identifications were filtered at a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) using DIA-NN’s built-in target–decoy approach. Quantification was performed at the precursor level, followed by protein inference using DIA-NN’s neural network–based scoring and interference correction. Other parameters include: Mass accuracy: 10.0; MS1 accuracy: 5.0; Peptide length range: 7–30; Precursor charge range: 1–4; Precursor m/z range: 300–1800; Fragment ion m/z range: 200–1800.

Bioinformatic analysis of DIA data

A peptide intensity table with the LFQ values was analyzed in Perseus (v1.6.2.3)80. Data were log2 transformed and filtered for identification in all three replicates in at least one group. Different peptide groups were selected for different analyses, like total proteome, ISG15 IP, and diGly IP. PCA was performed for each analysis with default settings. Intensities were Z-scored by subtracting the mean and used for hierarchical clustering by Euclidean distance (pre-processed with k-means, 300 clusters, 1000 iterations). Missing values were imputed from the lower end of the normal distribution (default settings). A two-sided student’s t-test with permutation-based FDR was used to calculate the significance between IFN-β treatment and control at 0.05 FDR (p-value). For a detailed protocol of data analysis in Perseus, refer to https://cox-labs.github.io/coxdocs/interactions.html.

The structure of full-length human MOV10 (1003 residues) was modeled using the AI-based structure prediction programs AlphaFold382 and Chai-183. MOV10 was modeled alone and in complex with ISG15 to assess the potential structural impact of ISGylation. Based on the predicted model, MOV10 folds into four major domains: a short N-terminal helical domain, followed by a cysteine–histidine (CH)–rich domain (residues 93–305; including the Gag-binding region at residues 261–305), an intermediate domain, and a C-terminal helicase domain (residues 524–911). The experimentally mapped ISGylation sites, Lys121 and Lys714, are located within the CH and helicase domains, respectively, and were predicted to be surface-accessible. To investigate the structural consequences of ISGylation, ISG15 was modeled covalently attached to Lys121 or Lys714. Chai-1 was used to generate multi-chain models incorporating distance restraints to define the isopeptide bond between the C-terminal glycine of ISG15 and the ε-amino group of the respective lysine side chain. Separate models were generated for each modification site. The resulting models were analyzed to assess ISG15 positioning and potential effects on MOV10.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism unless otherwise indicated. Details of the statistical tests, sample numbers, exact p values, confidence intervals, and measures of variation are provided in the figure legends. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), as indicated in the respective figure legends. Two-tailed paired or unpaired Student’s t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups where appropriate. One-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Sidak’s multiple-comparisons tests were used for experiments involving more than two groups or multiple conditions. For proteomics analyses, significance thresholds were defined as indicated in the respective figures and “Methods” section, including adjusted p-values and fold-change cutoffs. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 and ns: not significant.
