Dementia in Advanced Kidney Disease by Race and Ethnicity and Neighborhood Factors
Authors: Yiting Li, Nidhi Ghildayal, Gayathri Menon, Jane J. Long, Babak J. Orandi, Sunjae Bae, Wenbo Wu, Dorry L. Segev, Mara A. McAdams-DeMarco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOlder adults with chronic kidney disease (CKD) likely face higher dementia risk because of vascular injury and chronic inflammation, potentially intensified among racially minoritized groups and those in rural or deprived neighborhoods. We quantified this association and examined variation by race and ethnicity, urbanicity, and neighborhood deprivation.MethodsWe identified 211,321 older adults with CKD stages 3 to 5 from the Medicare 5% sample (2010–2022) using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 and/or ICD-10 codes. Zone Improvement Plan (ZIP)-code level urbanicity was defined using Rural-Urban Commuting Area Codes, and neighborhood deprivation was derived from the American Community Survey. We used cause-specific hazard models with time-varying CKD stage (reference = stage 3) to quantify the adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) of dementia and included interaction terms to test the differential effect of these associations by race and/or ethnicity, urbanicity, and neighborhood deprivation.ResultsAfter adjustment, older adults with CKD stages 4 and 5 had a higher risk of dementia (stage 4 aHR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.25–1.32; stage 5 aHR: 1.82, 95% CI: 1.76–1.88). These associations differed by race/ethnicity (stage 4 and 5: Pinteractions < 0.001) and neighborhood deprivation (stage 5: Pinteraction = 0.04). Among older Black (stage 4 aHR: 1.38, 95% CI: 1.28–1.48; stage 5 aHR: 2.01, 95% CI: 1.88–2.15) and Hispanic adults (stage 4 aHR: 1.33, 95% CI: 1.10–1.62; stage 5 aHR: 1.98, 95% CI: 1.67–2.34), stages 4 and 5 were associated with a higher risk of dementia. Among older adults in high-deprivation neighborhoods, stage 5 was associated with a higher risk of dementia (aHR: 1.89, 95% CI: 1.80–1.99).ConclusionCKD stages 4 and 5 were associated with a higher dementia risk, particularly among older Black adults and those in high-deprivation neighborhoods. These findings may inform targeted interventions for early detection and management of cognitive decline in advanced CKD.

Dementia, a degenerative age-related neurocognitive disorder, is the leading cause of disability and dependence worldwide.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Its prevalence continues to rise in the US, with an estimated 13.8 million cases projected by 2060.1,7,8 Older adults with CKD are more vulnerable to developing dementia, with studies reporting a 37% higher risk of dementia compared with older adults without CKD.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 As kidney function declines, the accumulation of uremic toxins can disrupt the blood-brain barrier,12,14,15 promote neuroinflammation and oxidative stress,15, 16, 17, 18, 19 and damage vascular and neuronal structures,14,15 thereby contributing to cognitive decline. With more advanced CKD, these mechanisms intensify and are further exacerbated by common comorbidities, such as systemic hypertension,20 diabetes,15 malnutrition,14,15 sleep disorders,21 depression,21 and other metabolic factors.18, 19, 20

Racial and ethnic disparities in cognitive outcomes, including dementia, are well-documented among older adults in the general population.22 Older Black adults experience roughly twice the incidence of dementia, and older Hispanic adults about 1.5 times the incidence, compared with older White adults,3,23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28 with worse performance on measures of memory,29,30 executive functioning,30,31 and global cognition.30,32 Racial disparities likely reflect lifelong exposure to structural (e.g., segregation, built environment) and social inequities (e.g., social cohesion, crime) that shape neighborhood conditions,3,27,33,34 which in turn influence cognitive health.3,27,33,34 Residing in socioeconomically deprived or rural neighborhoods, characterized by lower educational attainment, limited income, and reduced resources, has been associated with accelerated cognitive decline among community-dwelling older adults.25,35 Such social and environmental stressors may further intensify the impact of advanced CKD on cognitive health. For individuals with advanced CKD, particularly minoritized adults with a high burden of cardiometabolic risk factors,20 residing in deprived or rural neighborhoods can further increase vulnerability. These environments often restrict access to quality care,36,37 healthy food,37 and opportunities for physical and cognitive activity,3,27,37,38 thereby exacerbating dementia risk.

Thus, in this national study, we (i) quantified the association between advanced CKD and incident dementia in older adults, and (ii) tested whether this association varied by race and ethnicity, urbanicity, and neighborhood socioeconomic deprivation.

Methods

Data Source

We leveraged data from the United States Renal Data System, a national registry containing information on all adults with kidney disease in the US, including a 5% sample of Medicare claims for individuals with CKD.39 Patient characteristics were obtained from the CKD patient master file, and comorbidities were obtained from the 5% Medicare claims.39

Study Population

We identified 211,321 older adults (aged ≥ 66) with an incident diagnosis of advanced stage (stages 3–5) CKD in the US between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2022. Advanced stages of CKD at cohort entry were identified using 5% Medicare billing claims, based on International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Ninth or Tenth Revision (ICD-9 and/or ICD-10) diagnosis codes from either ≥ 1 inpatient claim or ≥ 2 outpatient claims (Supplementary Table S1).37,40,41 The study population was also limited to older adults with CKD who had no previous dementia diagnosis at the time of their initial advanced CKD diagnosis (based on 2 year look back of Medicare claims), whose primary payer was Medicare, who were continuously enrolled at least 12 months before the first advanced stages of CKD diagnosis, and whose race and ethnicity were categorized into 1 of the following: non-Hispanic Asian (consisting of Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander; Asian hereafter), non-Hispanic Black (Black hereafter), Hispanic/Latino (Hispanic hereafter), or non-Hispanic White (White hereafter). We excluded individuals with missing demographic information and those with CKD stages 1 and 2 or unknown stages.

This study was reviewed by the institutional review board at the New York University Grossman School of Medicine, which determined it to be exempt because of the inability to directly or indirectly identify patients, thereby waiving the requirement for informed consent.

Predictors of Dementia Diagnosis

We identified the following potential predictors for the diagnosis of dementia based on previous research7,42,43: patient demographics (age, sex), existing comorbidities (atherosclerotic heart disease, cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cerebrovascular accident/transient ischemic attack, diabetes, gastrointestinal disease, heart failure, hypertension, peripheral vascular disease), all of which were obtained using ICD-9 and/or ICD-10 diagnosis from 5% Medicare claims from either ≥ 1 inpatient claim or ≥ 2 outpatient claims.40 Additionally, we obtained urbanicity from the modified Rural-Urban Commuting Area Codes defined by the US Department of Agriculture (high-density urban, suburban, rural, and small town),44,45 and neighborhood deprivation index (a composite measure of 10 American Community Survey socioeconomic variables) scores from the “ndi” package on RStudio (Posit PBC, Boston, MA).46, 47, 48, 49 Neighborhood deprivation scores were weighted and aggregated at the ZIP-code level, merged with each patient’s residential 5-digit ZIP code and the year of their first advanced CKD diagnosis claim. These scores were then split into tertiles, with higher scores indicating greater socioeconomic deprivation of a residential neighborhood.

Outcome: Risk of Dementia Diagnosis

We identified incident diagnoses of dementia, including Alzheimer’s Disease, vascular dementia, and other and mixed types, using ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes from the 5% sample of Medicare claims (Supplementary Table S2), following the incident diagnosis of the advanced stage of CKD. A previous study reported that using Medicare claims had a sensitivity of 0.85 for detecting dementia and 0.64 for Alzheimer’s Disease, with a specificity of 0.89 for dementia and 0.95 for Alzheimer’s Disease.50

Statistical Analyses

We estimated the unadjusted cumulative incidence of diagnosed dementia and its subtypes using the Kaplan-Meier method. We used complementary log-log plots and Schoenfeld residuals to validate the proportional hazards assumption. We then used cause-specific hazard models to quantify: (i) the association between advanced stages of CKD and the risk of dementia diagnosis (including its subtypes); and (ii) whether these associations differed by race and ethnicity, urbanicity, and neighborhood deprivation tertiles.

CKD stage was treated as a time-varying exposure. Individuals were allowed to transition across CKD stages (stage 3, stage 4, and stage 5), with progression only from lower to higher stages, and person-time was allocated to each stage using a start-stop (counting process) data structure. CKD stage was modeled categorically, with stage 3 as the reference category. Dementia events were assigned to the CKD stage present at the time of event occurrence.

Interaction terms between CKD stage and race and ethnicity, urbanicity, and neighborhood deprivation were included to assess effect modification. Adjusted hazard ratios for time-varying CKD stage were estimated within levels of each effect modifier (e.g., stage 4 vs. stage 3 and stage 5 vs. stage 3 within each group). Effect modification was assessed using Wald tests of the interaction terms for CKD stages 4 and 5 separately within each effect modifier. These tests examined whether the associations of CKD stage 4 and stage 5 (relative to stage 3) with dementia risk differed across race and ethnicity, urbanicity, and neighborhood deprivation.

All older adults with CKD were followed from cohort entry until first diagnosis of dementia or its subtypes, censoring at the earliest of death, loss of Medicare eligibility, or the end of the study observation period (December 31, 2022).

Sensitivity Analysis

We assessed the robustness of our findings in the following sensitivity analyses: (i) Fine and Gray proportional subdistribution hazards models treating death as a competing risk; (ii) using dialysis initiation as the index date for CKD stage 5 among older adults who initiated dialysis before an incident CKD stage 5 diagnosis; (iii) censoring individuals at the time of dialysis initiation; (iv) calculating E-values to assess potential impact of unmeasured confounding; (v) adjusting for neighborhood-level poverty rate (percentage of families with incomes below the poverty level); (vi) restricting the cohort to individuals without a previous history of diabetes or hypertension (2 major shared risk factors for both CKD and dementia); (vii) treating CKD as a time-fixed exposure throughout follow-up; (viii) adjusting for previous-year number of hospitalizations; and (ix) conducting descriptive analyses to compare the distribution of key comorbidities across racial and ethnic groups and neighborhood deprivation categories.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and Stata 17 MP (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P-value < 0.05.

CKD and the Risk of Dementia

Older adults with CKD stages 4 and 5 had a higher 5-year unadjusted cumulative incidence of diagnosed dementia compared with those with CKD stage 3 (stage 3 = 17.3%, stage 4 = 22.5%, stage 5 = 20.8%; PLog-rank<0.001; Figure 1). After adjustment, older adults with CKD stage 4 (aHR: 1.28, 95% CI:1.25–1.32) had a higher risk of diagnosed dementia compared with those with CKD stage 3. This association significantly differed by race and ethnicity (Pinteraction<0.001; Table 2). Specifically, among older Black adults, CKD stage 4 was associated with a 1.38-fold (95% CI: 1.28–1.48) higher risk of diagnosed dementia compared with CKD stage 3. Among older Hispanic adults, CKD stage 4 was associated with a 1.33-fold (95% CI: 1.10–1.62) higher risk of diagnosed dementia. Discussion

In this national study of 211,321 older adults with advanced stages of CKD spanning over 12 years (2010–2022), we found that older adults with CKD stages 4 and 5 had a significantly higher risk of diagnosed dementia. We found differences in these associations by race and ethnicity and neighborhood deprivation. Specifically, among older Black (2.01-fold) and Hispanic (1.98-fold) adults with CKD stage 5 had a significantly higher risk of diagnosed dementia compared with their counterparts with CKD stage 3. Additionally, among older adults in high-deprivation neighborhoods, CKD stage 5 was associated with a higher risk (1.89-fold) of diagnosed dementia compared with CKD stage 3. Similar associations were observed for diagnosed Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, and other and/or mixed-type dementia.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies showing that advanced stages of CKD are associated with a higher risk of dementia, likely because of pathophysiologic processes that emerge as kidney function declines. As renal function worsens, the body becomes less able to clear uremic toxins, regulate blood pressure, maintain metabolic balance, and control inflammation and oxidative stress.10,11 These processes may affect the brain through several pathways: (i) CKD-related hypertension and arterial stiffness contribute to microvascular damage18, 19, 20; (ii) reduced filtration allows neurotoxic metabolites to accumulate, potentially affecting blood-brain barrier permeability and neuronal communication10,11; and (iii) systemic inflammation may promote neuroinflammation and oxidative injury.11,16, 17, 18, 19 Together, these mechanisms may accelerate neurodegeneration and cognitive decline in advanced CKD.11

Importantly, our study extends previous research by examining how these associations differ across race and ethnicity, urbanicity, and neighborhood deprivation. Although previous studies have largely focused on the overall relationship between CKD severity and dementia risk,10,42,51 our findings show that the burden of dementia associated with advanced CKD is not experienced uniformly across populations and neighborhood contexts. In particular, older Black adults with CKD stages 4 and 5 had a significantly higher risk of diagnosed dementia compared with their counterparts with CKD stage 3. Notably, the relative association between advanced CKD stages and diagnosed dementia, reflected by the CKD stage 5 versus CKD stage 3 gradient, was steeper among older Black patients than among older White patients, as supported by the significant interaction. Elevated risks were also observed among individuals residing in high-deprivation neighborhoods. These findings suggest that social and neighborhood conditions may amplify the adverse cognitive consequences of advanced CKD and may help explain disparities in dementia risk among older adults with CKD.3,52, 53, 54 For example, older minoritized adults with advanced CKD are more likely to reside in socioeconomically deprived neighborhoods characterized by higher crime rates and more limited access to healthcare, nutritious food, transportation, and safe spaces for recreation, all of which may restrict access to health-promoting resources and adversely influence health-related behaviors.55, 56, 57, 58 Historical segregation, enforced through discriminatory public policies such as Jim Crow laws and redlining,3,52,53 has also contributed to longstanding disinvestments in predominantly Black neighborhoods, limiting opportunities related to education, employment, and community infrastructure.3,52,54 Long-term residence in these under-resourced neighborhoods, combined with the burden of advanced CKD, may increase social isolation, reduce social engagement, and contribute to a higher risk of diagnosed dementia.3,52

Additionally, we observed differences by urbanicity in the association between advanced CKD and diagnosed vascular dementia, but not for all-cause dementia. This may reflect the stronger vascular etiology of vascular dementia, which is driven by cerebrovascular pathology, including stroke and small-vessel ischemic disease.59,60 Differences across urbanicity categories may reflect variation in stroke prevention and access to timely preventive and specialty care.61,62 In rural areas and small towns, limited access to healthcare services may contribute to poorer management of vascular risk factors, potentially increasing the risk of vascular dementia,61, 62, 63 whereas in high-density urban areas, greater healthcare access and utilization may increase the detection and diagnosis of vascular dementia.64

Furthermore, older adults with CKD stages 4 and 5 residing in high-deprivation neighborhoods had a significantly higher risk of diagnosed dementia. This may be primarily because these neighborhoods are characterized by higher levels of stress,27,37,65 limited access to healthy food,27,37,65 and inadequate access to health care and education.27,37,65 These factors, combined with kidney failure and multiple comorbid conditions, can accelerate cognitive decline. Previous studies have also found an association between neighborhood disadvantage and lower hippocampal volume, as well as greater accumulation of Alzheimer's disease neuropathology.37,66 Additionally, these deprived neighborhoods tend to have lower socioeconomic status.27,36,37,52,67 Previous studies have found that lower socioeconomic status is associated with delayed referral to nephrology services,36 leading to delayed CKD treatment and accelerated cognitive decline. Individuals residing in these areas have also been shown to experience other worse health outcomes in addition to CKD,67 which may further exacerbate cognitive decline. Thus, these factors may explain the stronger association observed between advanced CKD and dementia in socioeconomically deprived neighborhoods.

Our study has several strengths, including the use of a large, nationally representative cohort and 12 years of claims data to examine the association between advanced CKD and dementia and the differences in this association by race and ethnicity, urbanicity, and neighborhood deprivation. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we identified advanced CKD and dementia using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, which may be subject to misclassification or underdetection. Nevertheless, previous studies indicate that these diagnosis codes have comparable sensitivity and specificity, supporting the reliability of our approach.50,68, 69, 70 Second, we used ZIP codes as a proxy for neighborhoods, which could introduce spatial misclassification or systemic bias.71 However, ZIP codes remain the most commonly used method for defining neighborhoods.72,73 Third, we were unable to account for the severity and duration of chronic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes, as well as potential individual-level socioeconomic factors, which may contribute to residual confounding. More severe or longstanding disease may influence both CKD progression and cognitive outcomes. Fourth, individuals with advanced CKD likely have more frequent contact with the healthcare system because of greater disease severity and comorbidity burden, which may increase opportunities for dementia to be recognized and documented. This could result in differential ascertainment and potentially bias the observed associations away from the null. However, our sensitivity analysis showed that CKD stages 4 and 5 remained associated with dementia even after adjustment for the previous-year number of hospitalizations. Fifth, Hispanic individuals were under-represented among patients with CKD enrolled in Medicare fee-for-service, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to more diverse populations. However, this distribution is consistent with the national registry data report.

In conclusion, CKD stages 4-5 were significantly associated with a higher risk of dementia and its subtypes, particularly among older Black and Hispanic adults and those residing in high-density urban, small towns, or high-deprivation neighborhoods. These findings underscore the importance of developing targeted interventions, particularly tools for early identification and management of cognitive decline, to preserve cognitive health in individuals with advanced CKD.74,75
