Immune remodeling after thymectomy in patients with anti-AChR antibodies

Naoshi Koizumi, Makoto Kinoshita, Yoshiaki Yasumizu, Tomohiro Yata, Daisuke Motooka, Ryohei Yamamura, Itsuki Sano, Yu Suzuki, Akihiro Tanaka, Minami Kanakura, Toru Koda, Naoko Ose, Takashi Kanou, Kenji Kimura, Yasushi Shintani, Hideki Mochizuki, Tatsusada Okuno

Abstract

AbstractBackgroundThymectomy is an established treatment for thymoma-associated myasthenia gravis (MG). However, a subset of patients develop new-onset MG after surgery. Although thymectomy affects immune homeostasis, its comprehensive impact on the peripheral immune system remains unclear, particularly at the single-cell transcriptomic level. Therefore, we aimed to characterize immune remodeling following thymectomy in anti-acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibody-positive patients without overt MG.MethodsPeripheral blood mononuclear cells were collected from three anti-AChR antibody-positive patients before and 7–22 months after thymectomy. Single-cell RNA and T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing were subsequently performed. Cell populations were annotated using SingleR and Azimuth. Differential gene expression was analyzed, followed by gene set enrichment analysis. TCR repertoire diversity was assessed using scRepertoire. Additionally, cell–cell communication was inferred using CellChat.ResultsThymectomy induced broad immune remodeling characterized by a relative reduction in naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and a concomitant relative increase in natural killer (NK) cells, particularly the CD56dim subset. Despite the relative decline in naïve T cells, activation-associated genes were upregulated and inflammatory pathways were enriched in the remaining cells, indicating an activation-associated transcriptional state. Monocyte subsets showed marked transcriptional changes, including upregulation of the expression of immediate early genes and downregulation of that of antigen presentation-related and inflammatory genes, consistent with substantial transcriptional remodeling. Furthermore, cell–cell communication analysis revealed a qualitative shift in the dominant signaling axis after thymectomy. Pre-thymectomy networks were characterized by TNF- and resistin-mediated signaling, whereas post-thymectomy networks were dominated by IFNγ signaling, primarily originating from CD56dim NK cells and targeting monocyte subsets.ConclusionsThymectomy induces coordinated remodeling of the peripheral immune system, which involves reduced thymic output, activation-associated transcriptional changes in T cells, relative increase in the proportion of NK cells, and transcriptional remodeling of monocytes. The dominant inflammatory signaling axis shifts from TNF/resistin-centered to IFNγ-centered communication after thymectomy. This bidirectional immune modulation may underlie both therapeutic effects and susceptibility to post-thymectomy MG, highlighting the importance of intercellular signaling dynamics in maintaining immune homeostasis.Keywords: myasthenia gravis, natural killer cells, single-cell RNA-sequence, thymectomy, thymoma

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disease caused by antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor (AChR) at the neuromuscular junction (). MG is frequently associated with thymoma; approximately 10–20% of patients with MG have thymomas. Moreover, MG is the most common autoimmune condition linked to thymomas, with a prevalence of 30–44% (). Thymoma induces various autoimmune responses (, ). Although thymectomy is recognized as an effective treatment for thymoma-associated MG (TAMG) (), cases of new-onset MG or exacerbation of MG following thymectomy have been reported (, ). Approximately 1–11% of patients with thymoma develop MG after thymectomy, with the interval between surgery and disease onset ranging from 4.5 to 52.1 months. The incidence of post-thymectomy MG is substantially higher among patients with thymoma who are seropositive for AChR antibodies before surgery, with 22.2–30.3% developing MG during follow-up. However, the duration of observation differs across studies (). Furthermore, the comprehensive impact of thymectomy on the peripheral immune system remains unclear. Thymectomy reduces thymic output, as reflected by decreased T-cell receptor excision circles. Consequently, naïve T-cell populations decline in the peripheral blood, whereas memory T-cell populations may increase (–). In addition, thymus-derived B-cell clones persist in circulation after thymectomy. These clones exhibit features of antigen-driven maturation, including class switching and somatic hypermutation (). In TAMG, populations of cytotoxic natural killer (NK) cells characterized as CD56dim CD16+ were reduced. However, their count increased following thymectomy (). In TAMG, peripheral blood plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDC) counts—especially pDC1—were reduced at baseline and further decreased after thymectomy (). Nevertheless, these observations were largely derived from analyses of selected cell populations or limited immunological markers. Comprehensive immune alterations following thymectomy have not been investigated at the transcriptomic level using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq).

In this study, we aimed to characterize the immune response changes induced by thymectomy in anti-AChR antibody-positive patients without apparent MG symptoms. We further aimed to elucidate the pathophysiology of post-thymectomy MG.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study included three patients positive for anti-AChR antibody without apparent symptoms of MG. Details of the three patients are presented in . All patients were recruited from the University of Osaka hospital. The study protocol involving human participants was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Osaka University (approval number: 11298–40). The patients provided their written informed consent to participate in this study.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell isolation

Patient blood was collected twice: once before thymoma resection and at 22, 9, and 7 months after thymectomy in Cases 1, 2, and 3, respectively, as specified in . All pre- and post-thymectomy blood samples were collected and processed using the same standardized protocol. Blood was collected into sodium heparin tubes. Whole blood was diluted 1:1 with Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) and gently mixed by pipetting approximately 10 times before being carefully layered onto Ficoll-PaqueTM PLUS (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden). Samples were centrifuged at 700 × g for 20 min at room temperature, and the peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) layer at the interface was collected. The isolated PBMCs were sequentially washed with 30 mL and then 10 mL of HBSS and subsequently stored at −80 °C under identical storage conditions until analysis. For scRNA-seq, frozen PBMCs were gently thawed using RPMI medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), pre-warmed to 37 °C. The cells were gently resuspended by pipetting approximately five times over several minutes and then passed through a 40-μm cell strainer, followed by washing before further processing. Cell viability was assessed by Trypan blue exclusion and was ≥ 95% for all samples used for scRNA-seq. Paired pre- and post-thymectomy samples from each patient were thawed and processed simultaneously to minimize potential differences arising from sample handling.

scRNA-seq analysis

The preserved PBMC samples were gently thawed for scRNA-seq analysis. To minimize batch effects, paired samples collected before and after thymectomy were processed simultaneously. Single-cell gene expression and T cell receptor (TCR) libraries were prepared using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kits v2 (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina). An average of 28,000 read pairs per cell was obtained for each library. Sequencing data in FASTQ format were processed using Cell Ranger v8.0.1 or 9.0.1 (10x Genomics) with the GRCh38-2024-A reference. Gene expression libraries were analyzed to generate gene-barcode matrices, and V(D)J libraries were analyzed for TCR sequence assembly and clonotype assignment. The raw data have been deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus database (GSE330576).

First, quality control (QC) and doublet removal were performed. Droplets with fewer than 500 or more than 20,000 unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, or with fewer than 400 or more than 5,000 unique genes (features), were excluded to remove low-quality cells and potential doublets. Additionally, droplets in which more than 15% of UMI counts were mapped to mitochondrial genes were filtered out to eliminate stressed or dying cells. Potential doublets were identified and removed using the DoubletFinder package in R (version 2.0.6), together with Seurat (version 5.3.1). Homotypic doublet proportions were estimated based on Seurat cluster annotations, assuming a 5% expected doublet formation rate (). Following QC and doublet removal, individual Seurat objects were normalized using the NormalizeData function, and highly variable features were identified using FindVariableFeatures with the “vst” method; 2,000 genes were selected. Processed datasets were then merged into a single Seurat object. The merged data were scaled with ScaleData and subjected to principal component analysis using the first 30 components. Harmony integration was performed with IntegrateLayers to correct for batch effects across samples, specifying “pca” as the original reduction and “harmony” as the new reduction. Clustering was conducted in the Harmony-reduced space using FindNeighbors and FindClusters with a resolution of 2, and visualization was performed using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) with RunUMAP based on the Harmony embedding. Initial cell-type annotation was performed using SingleR with the MonacoImmuneData PBMC reference (, ). To achieve finer immune subset classification and identify platelet populations not represented in the MonacoImmuneData reference, additional annotation was performed using Azimuth (). Azimuth identified a distinct platelet cluster, the distribution of which is shown in . For analyses requiring refined immune cell classification, including NK cell subset characterization, monocyte subset analysis, and cell–cell communication analysis, Azimuth-based annotations were used.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEG) between pre- and post-treatment conditions were analyzed for each annotated cell population using the Nebula package (v1.5.3) based on a negative binomial mixed model. Genes expressed in ≥10% of cells with total UMI counts ≥20 were tested, excluding ribosomal and mitochondrial genes. The model included patient ID and treatment condition as random and fixed effects, respectively; library size was incorporated as an offset. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.25 were considered significantly differentially expressed. QC filters were applied to retain genes with acceptable model convergence (convergence code > –20) and moderate overdispersion (cell-level overdispersion < 100) (). Cell populations containing fewer than 50 cells in total were excluded from differential expression analysis because of insufficient cell numbers for reliable statistical analysis.

Gene set enrichment and pathway analyses

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the fgsea package in R. All genes were ranked based on the log2 fold change obtained from differential expression analysis, and enrichment was tested against Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes and Hallmark. Gene sets containing 15–500 genes were analyzed with 10,000 permutations. Pathways with FDR < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched (–).

Repertoire analysis

TCR clonality and diversity were analyzed using scRepertoire (v2.2.1) (). Shannon and inverse Simpson diversity indices were calculated for overall and subset-specific T cell populations. Pre- versus post-treatment comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests (n = 3 patients) ().

CD4+ T cell automatic labeling and quantification of gene programs

PBMC data were processed using a pipeline developed in a previous study (, ) to assign CD4+ T cell clusters. Briefly, Azimuth () was used for the initial annotation of PBMC subsets, and cells annotated as CD4+ T cell subsets were extracted for downstream analysis. The extracted CD4+ T cells were normalized using SCTransform and then mapped to a reference dataset using Symphony (, ), which assigned cells to five major clusters (L1: Tnaive, Tcm, Tem, Temra, and Treg) and 18 minor clusters (L2). To quantify functional gene program activity, non-negative matrix factorization projection (NMFproj) was applied to score each cell across 12 predefined gene programs. Changes in NMF scores between pre- and post-treatment conditions were assessed for each cluster using a generalized linear model with donor included as a covariate. P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Cell-cell interaction

Cell–cell communication networks were inferred using the R package CellChat (, ) following the official workflow. Normalized count data from Seurat objects were converted into CellChat objects using the human ligand–receptor reference database CellChatDB.human. Overexpressed ligands and receptors were identified, and communication probabilities were computed according to the standard CellChat pipeline.

Statistical analysis

The relative frequency of each cell type was calculated for every sample and compared between pre- and post-treatment conditions. Cell-type proportions are expressed as percentages of total cells per sample and ordered by mean abundance across all samples. Visualization was performed using ggplot2 (version 3.5.2) in R (version 4.4.2).

Results

Immune clusters obtained from anti-AChR antibody positive patients

Three patients positive for anti-AChR antibody without apparent symptoms of MG were enrolled in this study. PBMCs were collected both preoperatively and postoperatively to observe changes resulting from thymoma resection in peripheral immune dynamics. Postoperative PBMCs were collected at 22, 9, 7 months after surgery (). scRNA-seq and TCR sequencing were performed after QC, and 93,714 cells were included. Furthermore, cell annotation was performed on the obtained cells (). The gene expression profiles of the cells are shown in .

Global changes in immune cell composition after thymectomy

When classified by major cell types, the PBMC populations included T cells (preoperative: 54–73%, postoperative: 40–67%), B cells (preoperative: 1.9–5.6%, postoperative: 1.1–10%), NK cells (preoperative: 7.4–18%, postoperative: 8.0–47%), monocytes (preoperative: 11–25%, postoperative: 9.1–29%), dendritic cells (preoperative: 0.68–1.4%, postoperative: 0.61–2.0%). In all three cases, the proportion of T cells tended to decrease, whereas that of NK cells tended to increase after thymectomy (). In the detailed classification by SingleR, the proportions of NK cells (preoperative: 7.4–18%, postoperative: 8.0–47%) and intermediate monocytes (preoperative: 0.58–2.5%, postoperative: 2.3–3.0%) tended to increase in all three cases. In contrast, the proportions of naïve CD4+ T cells (preoperative: 12–19%, postoperative: 7.5–18%), naïve CD8+ T cells (preoperative: 9.8–16%, postoperative: 5.0–13%), T helper type 2 (Th2) cells (preoperative: 2.4–8.3%, postoperative: 1.7–4.6%), regulatory T cells (Tregs) (preoperative: 2.3–4.6%, postoperative: 1.7–4.0%), terminal effector CD8+ T cells (preoperative: 1.8–7.2%, postoperative: 1.4–5.3%), mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells (preoperative: 0.29–5.8%, postoperative: 0.18–5.6%), and effector memory CD8+ T cells (preoperative: 0.37–0.47%, postoperative: 0.33–0.46%) tended to decrease after thymectomy in all three cases (). DEG analysis revealed substantial transcriptional changes after thymectomy across multiple immune cell types (). The number of DEGs varied markedly among cell types, with monocyte subsets showing the largest transcriptional alterations. In particular, intermediate monocytes and myeloid dendritic cells exhibited the highest numbers of DEGs, whose expression was predominantly upregulated after thymectomy.

Remodeling of T cell characteristics after thymectomy

In this study, T cell populations consistently showed a decreasing trend after thymectomy. In particular, naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cells exhibited relatively high proportions and a consistent decreasing trend. DEG analysis revealed significant transcriptional changes in both naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cells after thymectomy (). The genes with upregulated expression in both subsets included immediate early and activation-related genes such as CD69, DUSP1, FOS, JUNB, and TNFAIP3. Contrastingly, the expression of several regulators involved in transcriptional repression and cellular stress responses, including that of HEXIM1, H2AX, and GADD45B, was downregulated. Th2 cells, Tregs, and MAIT cells also showed a decreasing trend in all three cases after thymectomy. Supplementary differential expression analyses further supported the robustness of the observed transcriptional changes (). Across all analyses, the expression of activation-associated genes was consistently upregulated in the postoperative samples, including that of immediate early genes such as CD69, DUSP1, and FOS. Contrastingly, the expression of genes involved in transcriptional repression and cellular stress responses was downregulated.

GSEA of Hallmark pathways revealed distinct transcriptional changes in naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cells after thymectomy (). In naïve CD4+ T cells, inflammatory pathways, including TNFα signaling via NF-κB and inflammatory response, were positively enriched after surgery. In contrast, pathways related to cell cycle progression and cellular metabolism, such as E2F targets, mTORC1 signaling, and glycolysis, were negatively enriched. Similarly, naïve CD8+ T cells showed positive enrichment of TNFα signaling via NF-κB, interferon gamma response, and inflammatory response after thymectomy. In contrast, pathways related to cell proliferation and growth, including E2F targets and MYC targets, were negatively enriched.

Clonal size distribution and diversity indices were compared between preoperative and postoperative PBMC samples to assess the impact of thymectomy on TCR repertoire diversity. Analysis of clonal size distribution revealed no marked shifts in overall clonal composition after thymectomy across the three cases (). Moreover, Shannon diversity analysis demonstrated no statistically significant differences in overall TCR repertoire diversity between preoperative and postoperative PBMC samples (). When stratified by T cell subsets, including naïve CD4+ T cells, naïve CD8+ T cells, MAIT cells, Tregs, and helper T cells, no significant changes in Shannon diversity were detected after thymectomy (). Consistent with the Shannon diversity results, inverse Simpson diversity indices showed no significant differences between preoperative and postoperative samples at the PBMC level (). Similarly, subset-specific analyses revealed no statistically significant alterations in inverse Simpson diversity across multiple T cell populations following thymectomy (). Together, these results indicate that thymectomy did not induce overt changes in TCR clonal diversity or repertoire evenness in peripheral blood T cells within the observed time frame.

We further examined CD4+ T cells more comprehensively by focusing on minor cluster-level (L2) subpopulations defined using Symphony reference mapping. At the L2 level, the proportions of TcmTfh, TemraTh1, TemTh1, and TemTph subsets among CD4+ T cells tended to increase after thymectomy across all three cases, whereas those of TnaiveMX1 and TnaiveSOX4 subsets consistently decreased (). Furthermore, the overall proportion of total Tregs decreased after thymectomy (). When Tregs were further subdivided into TregAct, TregEff, and TregNaive subsets, no consistent trend was observed (). We subsequently assessed changes in the gene program activity quantified by NMF projection across L2 subpopulations. Reduced activity was observed for NMF8 (Cent.Mem.-F) and NMF9 (Thy.Emi.-F), whereas NMF10 (Tissue-F) showed increased activity following thymectomy (). In addition, a marked increase was selectively observed in effector Tregs (TregEff) within the NMF5 (TregEff/Th2-F) program.

Relative increase and transcriptional remodeling of CD56dim NK cells after thymectomy

We further analyzed the innate immune system. The NK cell population was re-annotated using Azimuth to determine which subset was increased after thymectomy (; ). UMAP visualization confirmed clear segregation of NK cell subsets, including CD56dim, CD56bright, and proliferating NK cells (). Quantitative analysis revealed an increase in the proportion of CD56dim NK cells after thymectomy in all three cases, whereas changes in the CD56bright subset were variable (). Given the relative increase of the NK CD56dim population, we subsequently focused on this subset to characterize transcriptional changes associated with thymectomy. The expression of canonical activation- and immediate early-associated genes was upregulated, including that of FOS, DUSP1, TNFAIP3, and CD69. In addition, the expression of ZNF331 and SYTL3 was strongly upregulated, indicating pronounced transcriptional remodeling in CD56dim NK cells after thymectomy (). GSEA was performed to further investigate pathway-level alterations. Hallmark pathway analysis revealed positive enrichment of inflammatory and stress-related pathways, including TNFα signaling via NF-κB, hypoxia, and early estrogen response, in CD56dim NK cells after thymectomy (). Consistent with these findings, GSEA based on Gene Ontology biological processes demonstrated enrichment of immune signaling-related pathways, including endolysosomal Toll-like receptor signaling and Fc receptor-mediated stimulatory signaling, as well as processes related to energy homeostasis and response to hydrogen peroxide in postoperative CD56dim NK cells (). Together, these results indicate that thymectomy is associated with a relative increase of the NK CD56dim population accompanied by coordinated transcriptional remodeling that is characterized by enhanced activation- and inflammation-associated gene expression programs.

Transcriptional remodeling of monocyte subsets after thymectomy

Because the Monaco Immune reference used for initial SingleR annotation does not include platelet or megakaryocyte populations (), we next examined transcriptional changes in monocyte subsets using Azimuth-based annotations, which provide a platelet reference and have been demonstrated to robustly distinguish classical (CD14+ Mono) and non-classical (CD16+ Mono) monocyte populations. The expression of immediate early and activation-associated genes, including FOS, FOSB, JUND, DUSP1, and ZFP36, was consistently upregulated following thymectomy. In contrast, the expression of genes involved in interferon signaling and antigen presentation, such as IFIT2, CD74, HLA-DRB5, and TNF, was downregulated after surgery (). Non-classical monocytes also exhibited substantial transcriptional remodeling after thymectomy. The expression of G0S2, CCL5, and NR4A2 was significantly upregulated, whereas that of TNF, CD74, MNDA, and GADD45B was downregulated. In addition, stress- and activation-responsive transcriptional regulators such as ZFP36, JUNB, FOS, and FOSB were prominently induced, indicating enhanced immediate-early gene activity in this subset ().

Remodeling of the immune network after thymectomy

We performed ligand–receptor interaction analysis using Azimuth-based cell type annotations to characterize global changes in intercellular communication following thymectomy, which enabled refined classification of NK cell subsets. Analysis of overall signaling activity revealed a marked redistribution of sender and receiver roles after thymectomy (). Monocyte subsets showed a reduced contribution as both signal senders and receivers in the postoperative condition. In contrast, plasmablasts, Tregs, and CD56dim NK cells primarily exhibited increased signaling activity as signal receivers after thymectomy (). Examination of individual ligand–receptor pairs further highlighted qualitative differences between pre- and post-thymectomy conditions (). TNF-related signaling pathways were dominant in preoperative samples, with strong interactions involving TNFSF4–TNFRSF4 and TNF–TNFRSF1A, as well as resistin-mediated signaling characterized by RETN–CAP1 and RETN–TLR4 interactions. In contrast, postoperative samples showed a relative increase in IFNγ-mediated signaling, characterized by enhanced IFNG–IFNGR1–IFNGR2 interactions. Consistent with these findings, both TNF and resistin signaling were globally increased in the pre-thymectomy condition, whereas type II interferon signaling was preferentially enriched after thymectomy ().

Based on the observed global remodeling of cell–cell communication after thymectomy, we subsequently evaluated signaling pathways that exhibited differential activity between pre- and post-thymectomy conditions. Analysis of sender–receiver relationships revealed that CD56dim NK cells constituted the primary source of IFNγ signaling in postoperative samples, with increased signal transmission toward multiple monocyte subsets. In contrast, this signaling axis was weaker in pre-thymectomy samples (). Analysis of pathway-level signaling activity, focusing on pathways for which CD56dim NK cells acted as the primary sender population, revealed distinct patterns between the two conditions. In pre-thymectomy samples, signaling pathways mediated by IL16, GZMA, and MIF were relatively enriched. In contrast, postoperative samples showed increased activity of pathways mediated by IFNG and TGFB1, indicating a qualitative shift in dominant cytokine-mediated communication after thymectomy (). At the gene expression level, IFNG expression was increased after thymectomy in CD56dim NK cells, accompanied by an increase in the number of IFNG-expressing cells ().

Additional pathway-specific analyses showed that resistin signaling, which was mainly derived from monocyte subsets in the pre-thymectomy condition, was reduced after surgery. Similarly, TNF-mediated signaling, mainly originating from CD16+ monocytes and other monocyte subsets before thymectomy, was attenuated in the postoperative samples. In contrast, TGFβ signaling showed no substantial change in either signal strength or cellular sources following thymectomy ().

Discussion

In this study, we performed a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic analysis of peripheral immune cells before and after thymectomy in anti-AChR antibody–positive patients without overt MG. By integrating cell composition analysis, differential gene expression, pathway enrichment, TCR repertoire profiling, and cell–cell communication inference, we provide an unbiased overview of thymectomy-induced immune remodeling.

T cell subsets were annotated using reference-based mapping (SingleR based on the MonacoImmuneData PBMC reference) () and further supported by the expression of canonical markers such as CCR7. A recent consensus guideline has emphasized that T cell subset definitions should be explicitly described based on the experimental criteria used in each study, particularly in the context of high-dimensional single-cell analyses (). Our classification follows these principles. Therefore, it should be interpreted with the understanding that T cell phenotypes likely exist along a continuum rather than as strictly discrete subsets. Consistent with previous reports, we observed a relative reduction in naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations after thymectomy (). Given that thymomas and the thymus are known sources of newly generated T cells (, ), particularly recent thymic emigrants, this finding likely reflects reduced thymic output following surgical resection. This interpretation is further supported by the consistent relative decrease in the TnaiveSOX4 subpopulation, which is enriched for recent thymic emigrant-like cells (). Despite the reduction in naïve T cell counts, DEG and pathway analyses revealed a coordinated upregulation of the expression of immediate early and activation-associated genes, including FOS, JUNB, DUSP1, and CD69 (–), alongside enrichment of inflammatory signaling pathways such as TNFα signaling via NF-κB and interferon gamma response. Importantly, CD69 expression is not exclusively indicative of antigen-driven TCR activation but can also reflect cytokine-mediated or homeostatic activation (). Together, these findings indicate an activation-associated transcriptional state in the remaining naïve T cells after thymectomy. This pattern may reflect a homeostatic transcriptional response to reduced thymic output, although its functional significance remains to be determined (). Kohler et al. () suggested that thymectomy contributes to the development of autoimmune diseases by increasing the proportion of Tregs with reduced suppressive capacity. However, detailed subclassification of Tregs did not reveal a consistent trend in our analysis, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions. Importantly, TCR repertoire diversity remained largely preserved, indicating that thymectomy did not induce overt clonal restriction within the observation period. This dissociation between numerical decline and repertoire diversity demonstrates that peripheral homeostatic mechanisms may mitigate the impact of reduced thymic output.

In parallel with the relative reduction in T cell populations, we observed a consistent increase in the relative proportion of NK cells after thymectomy, particularly within the CD56dim subset. Although NK cell development primarily occurs in the bone marrow and is largely independent of the thymus (), this finding is consistent with previous observations in TAMG () and suggests that thymectomy alters the balance between adaptive and innate immunity. In the present study, the expression of activation-related and immediate early genes, including FOS, DUSP1, and CD69, was upregulated in CD56dim NK cells (, ). The induction of these genes indicates substantial transcriptional remodeling following thymectomy. Pathway analyses further demonstrated enrichment of inflammatory and stress-response pathways, supporting substantial transcriptional remodeling of CD56dim NK cells after thymectomy. These findings may reflect an altered balance between innate and adaptive immune compartments following thymectomy.

Monocyte subsets exhibited broad transcriptional changes after thymectomy. The expression of immediate early genes (FOS, FOSB, JUNB, and DUSP1) was robustly upregulated across classical and non-classical monocytes, consistent with activation-associated transcriptional changes (). Simultaneously, the expression of genes associated with antigen presentation, interferon signaling, and inflammatory response, such as CD74, HLA class II genes, IFIT2, and TNF, was consistently downregulated. This result is not contradictory to previous reports, given that TNF has been shown to enhance antigen-presenting capacity (). These findings suggest that monocytes adopt a transcriptional state characterized by reduced expression of genes associated with inflammatory responses and antigen presentation after thymectomy. Monocytes in patients with manifest MG exhibit enhanced inflammatory responses and increased antigen-presenting activity (). Thus, the reduced expression of inflammatory and antigen-presentation-related genes in monocytes may represent one component of the attenuation of monocyte-associated inflammatory programs after thymectomy. The increased expression of platelet-associated transcripts such as PF4 and PPBP should be interpreted cautiously. These signals may reflect platelet-derived transcripts, platelet-containing multiplets, or ambient platelet RNA rather than monocyte-intrinsic transcriptional activation.

Cell–cell communication analysis further indicated a marked shift in the dominant intercellular signaling landscape after thymectomy. Before thymectomy, signaling networks were largely centered on monocyte-derived inflammatory pathways, particularly TNF- and resistin-related interactions. In contrast, the communication network became dominated by IFNγ signaling post-thymectomy. CD56dim NK cells emerged as the principal sender population, whereas multiple monocyte subsets acted as major receivers. This switch was supported by both the pathway-level analysis and the increased IFNG expression and proportion of IFNG-expressing cells in CD56dim NK cells after thymectomy. IFNγ has been implicated in the initiation and exacerbation of MG in previous studies (–). In addition, its expression is elevated in the thymic microenvironment of TAMG (). Excessive IFNγ signaling can promote antigen presentation, inflammatory cytokine production, and tissue damage (). In contrast, both TNF and resistin are elevated in MG and participate in inflammatory disease processes (, ). Therefore, our findings suggest that thymectomy exerts bidirectional immunological effects: attenuation of TNF- and resistin-mediated signaling may represent a therapeutically beneficial aspect of immune remodeling, whereas enhancement of NK cell-derived IFNγ signaling may contribute to immune dysregulation and susceptibility to post-thymectomy MG. Consistent with this interpretation, TGFβ signaling remained largely unaltered after thymectomy. Together, these findings suggest that thymectomy induces a qualitative reorganization of intercellular communication networks rather than a uniform increase or decrease in inflammatory activity, highlighting a shift in the dominant signaling axis.

Previous studies have highlighted interactions between inflammatory signaling and thymic function, with inflammation influencing thymic tolerance and thymic involution, in turn, potentially affecting peripheral immune homeostasis (, ). In this context, our findings suggest that surgical removal of thymoma-bearing thymic tissue is accompanied by remodeling of peripheral immune communication. However, given the presence of thymoma and the absence of overt MG development in our patients, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that loss of thymic function directly induces autoimmune inflammation.

This study had several limitations. First, the number of patients was small, reflecting the rarity of anti-AChR antibody-positive individuals without clinical MG undergoing thymectomy. Accordingly, the present findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger independent cohorts. In addition, because differential expression was initially assessed using a mixed-effects model (NEBULA) with patient as a random effect, the random-effect variance could not be reliably estimated from only three donors, and the large number of cells does not compensate for this limited number of biological replicates. We therefore did not treat the cell-level statistics as confirmatory. To address this, we additionally performed donor-level paired pseudobulk analyses, in which the biological unit is the patient rather than the individual cell. The major changes, including the downregulation of antigen-presentation-related transcripts (e.g., CD74) and TNF in monocytes, were recapitulated at the pseudobulk level and were consistent in direction across patients, indicating that the principal findings are not driven by pseudoreplication (data not shown). In addition, although the major pathway-level findings were largely preserved after exclusion of immediate early/stress- and activation-associated genes, the Fc receptor-mediated stimulatory signaling pathway in CD56dim NK cells was no longer significantly enriched. Therefore, this particular pathway-level finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it may have been influenced by the immediate early/stress-associated transcriptional signature. Second, several immunological changes observed in this study, including the reduction in naïve T-cell populations and the relative increase in NK cells after thymectomy, are consistent with previous reports and are therefore not themselves novel. The principal contribution of this study lies in the integrated single-cell characterization of these changes and the identification of coordinated alterations in transcriptional states and inferred intercellular communication. Third, the clinical backgrounds of the three patients were heterogeneous. Case 2 had breast cancer, although the clinical status of the breast cancer remained unchanged between the pre- and post-thymectomy sampling points. Case 3 had ulcerative colitis and received prednisolone at 5 mg/day at the postoperative sampling point. The activity of ulcerative colitis also remained clinically stable between the pre- and post-thymectomy sampling points. Although the prednisolone dose was within the range generally considered to approximate a physiological glucocorticoid-equivalent dose, an influence of these comorbidities and corticosteroid treatment on peripheral immune profiles cannot be completely excluded. Importantly, analyses excluding Case 3 showed that the overall direction of the major transcriptional and pathway-level changes was largely preserved in Cases 1 and 2, supporting the robustness of the principal observations (data not shown). Fourth, postoperative sampling intervals differed among the patients (22, 9, and 7 months in Cases 1, 2, and 3, respectively). Although all sampling points fell within the broad time window during which post-thymectomy MG has been reported to develop, the present study cannot distinguish stable thymectomy-associated immune remodeling from changes that may depend on the time elapsed after surgery. Longitudinal studies with standardized and multiple postoperative sampling points will be required to clarify the temporal dynamics of these immune alterations. Fifth, none of the patients developed overt MG during the observation period, consequently limiting direct causal inference regarding disease onset. Notably, although the anti-AChR antibody level increased postoperatively in Case 3, this patient did not develop overt MG during follow-up. Therefore, the relationship between the observed peripheral immune alterations, changes in anti-AChR antibody levels, and subsequent MG development remains unclear. Finally, functional validation experiments were not performed to directly assess the effects of post-thymectomy NK cells on T-cell or B-cell responses. Future studies incorporating larger cohorts, serial longitudinal sampling, patients who subsequently develop MG, and mechanistic in vitro or in vivo assays are therefore required to validate and clarify the functional significance of the immune alterations identified here.

In summary, thymectomy induces broad and coordinated remodeling of the peripheral immune system. This remodeling is characterized by a relative reduction in naïve T cell populations, activation-associated transcriptional changes in remaining T cells, a relative increase in the proportion of CD56dim NK cells accompanied by transcriptional remodeling, transcriptional remodeling of monocytes, and rewiring of intercellular communication. Furthermore, it involves a shift in the dominant inflammatory signaling axis from TNF- and resistin-mediated pathways in the pre-thymectomy condition to IFNγ-dominated signaling after thymectomy. The coexistence of transcriptional changes in different directions highlights the dual nature of thymectomy-associated immune remodeling. Accordingly, the balance between these immune alterations, as well as the transition between these distinct signaling states, might partially contribute to the susceptibility to post-thymectomy MG and represent certain aspect of immune homeostasis following thymus removal.
