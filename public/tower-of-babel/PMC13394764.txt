Targeting the miR‐224‐5p/PTEN pathway: A previously under‐characterized strategy to attenuate mesangial cell injury and predict prognosis in diabetic nephropathy
Authors: Yanxia Gao, Xiaohui Zhang, Peng Lu, Qiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo ascertain the expression status and clinical performance of miR‐224‐5p in diabetic nephropathy (DN), alongside its molecular mechanism of regulating mesangial cell injury by targeting phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN).MethodsSerum miR‐224‐5p and PTEN mRNA expression were detected in DN patients. Logistic regression was used to analyze independent risk indexes for the poor clinical outcomes in DN. Functional gain/loss and rescue experiments were performed. Target relationships were validated through dual‐luciferase reporter assays.ResultsSerum miR‐224‐5p expression was significantly increased in DN patients (P < 0.001). It displayed moderate diagnostic performance for discriminating DN from T2DM (AUC = 0.796), and good predictive value (AUC = 0.833) for predicting 2‐year adverse renal outcomes. MiR‐224‐5p level was significantly higher in patients with an unfavorable prognosis than those with a favorable prognosis (P < 0.001). Elevated miR‐224‐5p was identified as an independent risk factor for eGFR decline ≥30% (P = 0.001), but this finding was not applicable to end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) due to insufficient case numbers for multivariate analysis. MiR‐224‐5p inhibition protected high‐glucose mesangial cells via PTEN upregulation, an effect reversed by si‐PTEN.ConclusionIncreased miR‐224‐5p expression may serve as a potential circulating biomarker for predicting eGFR decline in DN patients, but these findings are preliminary and require validation in independent cohorts using standardized exogenous spike‐in normalization before clinical application. The independence of this association was only demonstrated for the eGFR decline ≥30% endpoint and not for ESRD due to limited statistical power.Keywords: Diabetic nephropathy, Diagnosis, MicroRNA‐224‐5p

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is one of the most serious target organ injuries in diabetes, classified as a classic microvascular complication. It has now become the leading cause of end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) worldwide, accounting for approximately 30–50% of all ESRD cases in developed countries. 1 , 2 The global prevalence of DN among diabetic patients is estimated to be 20–40%, with higher rates in individuals with longer diabetes duration and poor glycemic controls. 3 , 4 The characteristic pathological changes involve progressive thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, substantial matrix deposition in the mesangial area, and fibrous tissue hyperplasia in the tubulointerstitium, ultimately resulting in the progressive loss of kidney function. 5 , 6 , 7 Although current interventions such as glucose‐lowering therapies, antihypertensive regimens, and renin‐angiotensin system suppression can delay DN progression, 8 , 9 a substantial number of patients still progress to ESRD. This highlights the complexity of its pathogenesis and underscores the need for elucidating the molecular mechanisms driving DN progression to identify novel therapeutic strategies.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) represent a class of endogenous, short non‐coding RNAs that post‐transcriptionally modulate gene expression. They exert their regulatory function by base‐pairing with complementary sequences predominantly located in the 3′‐untranslated regions (3′‐UTRs) of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), thereby inducing mRNA destabilization or repression of protein translation. 10 , 11 Extensive studies have established that miRNAs such as miR‐21, 12 miR‐325‐3p, 13 and miR‐23b 14 are critically involved in DN pathogenesis, regulating processes including fibrosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Altered levels of miR‐224‐5p have been observed in various renal disorders. Studies demonstrate that this miRNA is significantly increased in acute kidney injury and participates in processes such as cell cycle arrest and inflammatory responses. 15 Furthermore, in studies of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) complicated by obstructive sleep apnea, miR‐224‐5p expression is downregulated by binding to the long non‐coding RNA MALAT1, thereby regulating NLRP3‐mediated inflammatory pathways. 16 However, the expression pattern, specific functions, and mechanistic actions involving miR‐224‐5p in DN still require comprehensive investigation.

Phosphatase and Tensein Homolog (PTEN) is a crucial tumor suppressor that participates in regulating cell growth, proliferation, apoptosis, and metabolism by negatively modulating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. 17 , 18 It has become increasingly evident that PTEN contributes meaningfully to the biological processes involved in diabetes and its subsequent complications. In diabetic patients, PTEN expression is downregulated. 19 A comprehensive review highlighted the emerging role of PTEN as a key regulator in DN pathogenesis, particularly through modulating PI3K/AKT pathway. 20 Particularly, in patients with type 1 diabetes accompanied by microalbuminuria, circulating PTEN levels are significantly reduced and negatively correlated with miR‐223. 21 Bioinformatics and experimental studies on DN further indicate that PTEN is a key diagnostic gene associated with cellular senescence and may exert a protective role in DN. 22 Furthermore, recent advances in antioxidant science have underscored the importance of oxidative stress management in metabolic diseases including DN, providing a broader context for understanding PTEN‐related pathways. 23 The accumulated data suggest that PTEN might represent an important mechanistic link translating diabetic metabolic dysfunction into structural and functional kidney impairment.

Based on predictions from the ENCORI database, PTEN is a potential target gene of miR‐224‐5p. This study aims to systematically investigate the expression status of miR‐224‐5p in DN sufferers and its clinical significance, validate its role in mesangial cell injury through in vitro experiments, and ultimately elucidate its molecular mechanism of participating in DN progression by targeting PTEN regulation.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Study subjects and sample collection

This study enrolled 280 participants, including 110 patients with DN, 90 patients with T2DM without renal complications, and 80 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls (HC) from Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (Qingdao) during the same period. All subjects provided informed consent, and the study was approved by the hospital ethics committee.

Patients were eligible for inclusion in the DN group if they satisfied all of the following criteria: (1) diagnosis of T2DM according to American Diabetes Association (ADA) standards 24 ; (2) persistent albuminuria, reflected by a urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) >300 mg/g (indicating clinically established DN, corresponding to A3 stage); (3) impaired renal function, defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2; (4) diabetes duration >5 years.

T2DM without renal complications group was defined by the following criteria (defined a priori in the original study design): (1) diagnosis of T2DM according to ADA standards; (2) diabetes duration ≥5 years; (3) UAER < 30 mg/24 h and UACR < 30 mg/g; (4) eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73m2; (5) no clinical evidence of proteinuria or decline in renal function.

Participants meeting any of the following criteria were excluded: (1) non‐diabetic kidney disease, for example, glomerulonephritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, urinary tract obstruction; (2) recent acute infection (defined as a clinically diagnosed infectious disease within the past 4 weeks, accompanied by fever≥38.0°C or requiring antibiotic therapy), ketoacidosis, or heart failure; (3) malignancy; (4) pregnant or lactating women.

Blood samples (6 mL) were collected from the antecubital vein after all subjects had fasted for a minimum of 8 h. Serum was then separated and cryopreserved at −80°C for later RNA isolation.

Clinical data and follow‐up

Demographic and clinical parameters were documented for all subjects. They consisted of age, gender, body mass index (BMI), duration of diabetes, blood pressure, and key laboratory values. The eGFR was derived from the CKD‐EPI equation to evaluate renal function. 25 A 2‐year follow‐up was conducted for 110 DN patients. Two primary outcomes were separately analyzed due to their differences in clinical severity. The first outcome was sustained decline in eGFR of ≥30% from baseline (n = 33), and the second outcome was progression to ESRD, defined as eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73m2 or initiation of renal replacement therapy (n = 12). The original composite outcome (ESRD plus eGFR decline ≥30%) is presented as an exploratory analysis in the supporting information, with a clear note acknowledging the heterogeneity of its components. Patients were stratified into two cohorts: the favorable prognosis group (n = 65) and the unfavorable prognosis group (n = 45). Within the unfavorable prognosis group, 12 patients progressed to ESRD and 33 patients experienced an eGFR decline of ≥30% without reaching ESRD. By requiring UACR >300 mg/g at enrollment, our study population consisted of patients with established, rather than early‐stage DN, which explained the relatively high event rate observed during follow‐up.

Cell culture and hyperglycemic induction

Human mesangial cells (HMCs) were sourced from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai. Cells at passages 4–8 were used for all experiments to ensure phenotypic stability. Routine cell culture was performed using DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To establish a high‐glucose (HG) injury model, cells were seeded into 6‐well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight to allow attachment. Cells were then treated for 48 h under the following conditions: normal glucose control (NG, 5.5 mM glucose), Mannitol control (hypertonic control, 5.5 mM glucose + 24.5 mM mannitol), and HG group (30 mM glucose). All in vitro experiments were performed independently with at least three biological replicates (n ≥ 3).

MiR‐224‐5p inhibitors (50 nM), PTEN small interference vectors (si‐PTEN, 50 nM), or corresponding negative controls (NC, 50 nM) were introduced into HMCs using Lipofectamine 3,000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, USA) based on the manufacturer's instructions. Transfection was performed when cells reached 70–80% confluence. Knockdown efficiency was validated by quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR), showing >70% reduction in target gene expression. HG treatment was initiated 24 h after transfection.

qRT‐PCR detection

Total RNA was extracted from serum using the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen, Germany) and the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was utilized to extract total RNA from cell lysates. For serum miR‐224‐5p quantification, U6 snRNA was used as the endogenous reference gene for normalization. The stability of U6 snRNA across serum samples was confirmed in preliminary experiments (coefficient of variation <5%). RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and samples exhibiting A260/A280 ratios in the range of 1.8–2.1 were used for subsequent analysis. RNA integrity was assessed by Agilent 2,100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). For miRNA detection, reverse transcription was performed using the TaqMan Advanced miRNA cDNA Synthesis Kit (Applied Biosystems, USA). For PTEN mRNA detection, the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) with gDNA Eraser was used to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. The qPCR reaction system comprised SYBR Green Premix (Takara, Dalian, China), specific primers (Table S1), and an appropriate amount of cDNA template, and run on a QuantStudio 5 Real‐Time Fluorescent Quantitative PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of each run to verify single‐product amplification. Each sample underwent triplicate measurements. Inter‐assay and intra‐assay coefficients of variation were calculated to assess reproducibility. MiR‐224‐5p expression levels were calibrated against the housekeeping gene U6 snRNA, while GAPDH acted as the internal control for PTEN mRNA. No‐template controls (NTCs) were included in each qPCR run to exclude contamination. A standard curve was generated for each primer set to confirm amplification efficiency and linearity. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Apoptosis assay

Collect cells from each treatment group and wash twice with pre‐chilled PBS. The Annexin V‐FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, China) was used according to the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were resuspended in binding buffer to approximately 1 × 106 cells/mL. A 100 μL aliquot of cell suspension was transferred to a flow cytometry tube, followed sequentially by 5 μL Annexin V‐FITC stain and 10 μL propidium iodide (PI) stain. After gentle mixing, the samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Immediately after incubation, 400 μL PBS was added to each tube. Flow cytometric analysis was performed within 1 hour using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). The gating strategy was as follows: FSC/SSC, single‐cell gate (FSC‐H/FSC‐A), Annexin V/PI double‐parameter gate. Early apoptotic cells were defined as Annexin V+/PI−, and late apoptotic cells as Annexin V+/PI+. The apoptosis rate reported refers to the combined percentage of early apoptotic and late apoptotic cells. Cells positive for PI only (Annexin V−/PI+) were excluded from the apoptosis rate calculation and were considered necrotic or mechanically damaged cells. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Oxidative stress markers determination

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and malondialdehyde (MDA) content were detected following the corresponding kit instructions. SOD activity was determined with WST‐8 assay. MDA content was assessed via the TBA assay, calculated based on absorbance at 532 nm.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cell culture supernatants were obtained and centrifuged to remove cellular debris. Employ a double‐antibody sandwich ELISA using human tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), and interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) ELISA detection kits, following the protocol steps to determine the concentrations of each inflammatory cytokine.

Western blotting assay

Protein lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) with protease inhibitors, quantified by the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher, USA), and subjected to SDS‐PAGE. Separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA), which were subsequently blocked with 5% nonfat milk. The membranes were then incubated at 4°C overnight with the following primary antibodies: anti‐fibronectin (1:1000, Cell Signaling), anti‐Collagen IV (1:1000, Cell Signaling), anti‐TGF‐β1 (1:1000, Cell Signaling), anti‐PTEN (1:1000, Cell Signaling), anti‐p‐AKT (Ser473, 1:2000, Cell Signaling), anti‐total AKT (1:1000, Cell Signaling), and anti‐β‐actin (1:1500, Cell Signaling). Following TBST washes, membranes were exposed to HRP‐labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signal detection was performed with an ECL reagent (Millipore, USA), and densitometric analysis was carried out using ImageJ. For quantification of reversal effects in rescue experiments, the percentage reversal was calculated as: (ValuemiR‐224‐5p inhibitor‐ValuemiR‐224‐5p inhibitor + si‐PTEN )/(ValuemiR‐224‐5p inhibitor‐Valueinhibitor‐NC) × 100%. Reversal was defined as statistically complete when the miR‐224‐5p inhibitor+si‐PTEN group showed no significant difference from the inhibitor‐NC group (P > 0.05), as partial when a significant difference remained (P < 0.05). The miR‐224‐5p inhibitor + si‐NC group served as a control to exclude non‐specific effects of siRNA transfection; data from this group were used as the baseline for reversal calculation only if they differed significantly from the inhibitor group.

Bioinformatics analysis

Potential miR‐224‐5p‐regulated genes were predicted with the TargetScan (version 8.0, https://www.targetscan.org/), miRDB (version 6.0, http://www.mirdb.org/), and miRWalk (version 3.0, http://mirwalk.umm.uni‐heidelberg.de/) databases. The intersection of these predictions yielded candidate genes for subsequent analysis. The biological functions of these genes were systematically explored with the online tool (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/login/). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was performed across Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF) categories. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to identify significantly associated signaling pathways. A P‐value < 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant for enrichment analysis.

A Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network for the candidate genes was built using the STRING database (version 12, https://string‐db.org/), with a confidence score threshold set to >0.40 (medium confidence).

Luciferase reporter assay

A 155‐bp fragment of the PTEN 3′‐UTR containing the predicted miR‐224‐5p binding site (positions 6,278–6,284 of the PTEN 3′‐UTR) was amplified by PCR from human genomic DNA and cloned into the pGL3‐Basic luciferase reporter vector (Promega, USA) downstream of the firefly luciferase gene. This construct was designated as PTEN‐wide type (WT), with the sequence 5′‐AACACAGCAAC‐AAUGACUUA‐3′ spanning the predicted binding site. For the mutant construct (PTEN‐MUT), the seed match region was mutated to disrupt miR‐224‐5p binding. Specifically, the WT sequence 5′‐UGACUU‐3′was changed to 5′‐ACUGAA‐3′ by substituting all six nucleotides complementary to the miR‐224‐5p seed region. The complete sequence of PTEN‐MUT was 5′‐AACACAGCAAC‐AA ACUGAAA‐3′. All constructs were confirmed by Sanger sequencing before use. HMCs were seeded into 24‐well plates at a density of 5′× 104 cells/well and cultured overnight to reach 60–70% confluence. Cells were co‐transfected with 100 ng of the reporter plasmid, 50 nM of miR‐224‐5p mimics or mimic‐NC, and 10 ng of the Renilla luciferase plasmid (Promega, USA) as an internal control. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3,000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Luciferase activity was measured 48 h post‐transfection using a dual‐luciferase assay system on a GloMax 20/20 Luminometer (Promega, USA). The experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates, each measured in triplicate.

Statistics

All statistical computations were done with SPSS 25.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0. Sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power software. A one‐way anova model with a medium effect size (f = 0.25), a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a power (1‐β) of 0.80 was assumed. This calculation yielded a minimum total sample size of 159 participants, corresponding to 53 participants per group. The final enrollment exceeded the minimum requirement, confirming adequate statistical power for the primary comparisons. For in vitro experiments, measurement data are expressed as mean ± SD from independent biological replicates. Inter‐group differences were assessed with one‐way anova (multiple groups) with Tukey's multiple comparison post‐hoc analysis or independent‐samples t‐test (two groups). Count data were analyzed with the Chi‐square test. The predictive accuracy of miR‐224‐5p was examined with receiver operation characteristic (ROC) curves. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity and specificity were calculated. Univariate and subsequent multivariate logistic regression models were employed to determine independent prognostic factors. Before performing multivariate logistic regression analysis, collinearity diagnostics were conducted by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF). Due to the inherent mathematical correlation between SCr and eGFR, SCr was excluded from the multivariate model while eGFR was retained. Model diagnostics for multivariate logistic regression included collinearity diagnostics and the Hosmer‐–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test. Correlation analysis between serum miR‐224‐5p and PTEN mRNA expression was performed using Pearson's correlation coefficient. The correlation coefficient (r) with 95% confidence interval (CI) and two‐tailed P value was reported. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For multivariate logistic regression, the events per variable (EPV) criterion was applied as follows: at least 10 positive events are required for each variable. A P value below 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

RESULTS

Clinical and demographic characteristics at baseline

Three groups of subjects, including HC, T2DM, DN, showed no significant differences in age, gender, BMI, blood pressure, or lipid levels (P > 0.05, Table 1), indicating good baseline comparability. Relative to the HC group, patients with T2DM and DN exhibited significantly elevated FBG and HbA1c (P < 0.05). Moreover, patients in the DN group demonstrated increased HbA1c levels against those in the T2DM group (P < 0.05). Diabetes duration was significantly extended in the DN cohort relative to the T2DM cohort (P < 0.001). Compared with T2DM groups, the DN group exhibited significantly elevated BUN, SCr, and UA levels, while eGFR was significantly reduced. Relative to the HC group, the T2DM group demonstrated higher BUN, SCr, and UA (P < 0.05).

Expression patterns of circulating miR‐224‐5p and their relevance for diagnosis and prognosis

The expression levels of miR‐224‐5p in serum samples from three groups, including HC, T2DM, DN, were detected using qRT‐PCR. Results revealed a significant progressive increase in miR‐224‐5p expression across the three groups: DN group > T2DM group > HC group, with statistically significant differences in pairwise comparisons (P < 0.001) (Figure 1a).

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). (b) Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrating the ability of serum miR‐224‐5p to discriminate T2DM patients from HC. To evaluate the clinical application potential of miR‐224‐5p, a series of diagnostic performance analyses was conducted. ROC curve analysis revealed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.889 (95% CI: 0.841–0.938) for the diagnosis of patients between T2DM and HC. The sensitivity was 76.67% and the specificity was 87.50% at the optimal cutoff value (Figure 1b). This indicated good diagnostic performance. When discriminating between DN patients and T2DM patients without renal complications, the AUC was 0.796 (95% CI: 0.735–0.857) with a sensitivity of 64.55% and a specificity of 84.44% (Figure 1c). Subgroup analysis demonstrated that serum miR‐224‐5p levels were markedly elevated in DN patients with unfavorable outcomes relative to those with favorable prognoses (P < 0.001) (Figure 1d), indicating acceptable predictive value for the disease severity and adverse outcomes in DN patients. More importantly, miR‐224‐5p demonstrated significant predictive value for adverse renal outcomes within 2 years in DN patients, with an AUC of 0.833 (95% CI: 0.757–0.909). Moreover, the sensitivity and specificity were 73.33% and 84.62%, respectively, representing good diagnostic performance (Figure 1e).

Analysis of prognosis‐related factors in DN patients and the independent predictive value of miR‐224‐5p

Given the marked difference in clinical severity between ESRD and eGFR decline ≥30%, these two endpoints were analyzed separately. As shown in Table 2, compared with the favorable prognosis group (n = 65), patients in the eGFR decline ≥30% group (n = 33) exhibited more severe renal impairment and metabolic dysregulation. For the ESRD group (n = 12), despite the small sample size, similar trends were observed. Compared with the favorable prognosis group, patients with ESRD had significantly elevated UA, SCr, and HbA1c levels (P < 0.05 to P < 0.001) and significantly reduced eGFR (P < 0.05). Due to the limited number of ESRD events (n = 12), multivariate regression was not performed for this endpoint, and only descriptive statistics are presented (Table 2).

To identify independent risk factors for eGFR decline ≥30%, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed (Table 3). Univariate analysis revealed that SCr, HbA1c, eGFR, and miR‐224‐5p were significantly associated with eGFR decline ≥30% (all P < 0.05). Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis (HbA1c, eGFR, miR‐224‐5p) were entered into the multivariate model. It was demonstrated that elevated miR‐224‐5p expression (OR = 4.417, 95% CI: 1.691–11.537, P = 0.002) was an independent risk factor for eGFR decline ≥30% in DN patients. Due to the inherent mathematical correlation between SCr and eGFR, SCr was excluded from the multivariate model. Collinearity diagnostics confirmed that all variables in the final model had VIF values <2.5, indicating no significant multicollinearity. Hosmer‐Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test indicated adequate model calibration. For the multivariate model predicting eGFR decline ≥30%, the final model included 3 variables (HbA1c, eGFR, and miR‐224‐5p) with an events‐per‐variable ratio of 11 (33 events/3 variables), meeting the recommended minimum of 10. Nevertheless, given the relatively modest sample size of the unfavorable prognosis group, this result should be interpreted with caution and requires independent validation in larger prospective cohorts.

Functional validation of MiR‐224‐5p in HG‐triggered injury to HMCs

To elucidate the role of miR‐224‐5p in DN progression, an in vitro model of HG‐triggered injury to HMCs was established. The influences of modulating miR‐224‐5p expression on cell damage‐related phenotypes were assessed. Following HG treatment, miR‐224‐5p expression was significantly elevated in HMCs compared with the NG group (P < 0.001). The mannitol control group (5.5 mM glucose + 24.5 mM mannitol) showed no significant differences from the NG group in miR‐224‐5p expression (P > 0.05) (Figure 2a), as well as in apoptosis, oxidative stress (MDA/SOD), inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐1β), and fibrosis markers (Fibronectin, Collagen IV, TGF‐β1) (all P > 0.05, Figure S1a–d), confirming that the observed effects were specifically due to high glucose rather than osmotic pressure. Transfection with miR‐224‐5p inhibitor downregulated its expression (P < 0.001, Figure 2a). Relative to the NG group, HG exposure significantly induced marked cell apoptosis (P < 0.001). Under HG conditions, transfection with miR‐224‐5p inhibitor effectively reversed the injury (P < 0.001), significantly reducing apoptosis rates (Figure 2b). HG treatment caused a significant increase in intracellular MDA levels (P < 0.001) and a significant decrease in SOD activity (P < 0.001), indicating elevated oxidative stress. Inhibiting miR‐224‐5p significantly reduced MDA levels and increased SOD activity (P < 0.001, Figure 2c). ELISA analysis revealed that high glucose stimulation substantially increased the secretion of TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β in cell supernatants (P < 0.001). Inhibition of miR‐224‐5p effectively suppressed the release of these three key inflammatory factors (P < 0.001, Figure 2d). Moreover, HG also induced a pronounced rise in the protein expression of fibronectin, collagen IV, and TGF‐β1 (P < 0.001). The suppression of miR‐224‐5p, however, reversed this induction, leading to decreased levels of these markers (P < 0.001; Figure 2e).

Bioinformatics analysis of MiR‐224‐5p targets

To investigate the potential mechanisms of miR‐224‐5p in DN, bioinformatics analysis of its target genes was conducted. Three databases, including TargetScan, miRDB, and miRWalk, were utilized to predict potential miR‐224‐5p targets. The intersection of predictions from the three databases yielded 153 common target genes (Figure 3a). To elucidate the underlying functions of these targets, GO and KEGG analysis were conducted. In key BPs, target genes significantly participated in chromatin organization, cellular response to peptide hormone stimulus, and cellular response to peptide (Figure 3b). At the CCs level, target genes were primarily enriched in the cytoplasmic ribonucleoprotein granule (Figure 3c). Regarding MFs, they were predominantly associated with Lys63‐specific deubiquitinase activity and telomerase RNA binding (Figure 3d). These GO terms are provided for comprehensive bioinformatics reporting and represent statistically significant enrichments by FDR criteria; however, they are not the primary focus of our mechanistic investigation, and their direct relevance to DN pathophysiology requires further experimental validation.

Regarding KEGG analysis, targets were mainly enriched in multiple signaling pathways associated with metabolic diseases and cell fate regulation, such as the p53 signaling pathway (Figure 3e). Emerging evidence indicates that p53 activation contributes to podocyte injury and glomerular sclerosis in DN. 26 , 27 The enrichment of this pathway suggests that miR‐224‐5p may promote DN progression partly through p53‐mediated cellular stress response.

To identify key regulatory nodes among the targets, a PPI network was established (Figure 4). As shown in Table 4, node degrees were calculated and PTEN exhibited the highest node degree (degree = 12) among all nodes in the network, followed by HNRNPC (degree = 10) and HNRNPU (degree = 10). This quantitative analysis confirmed that PTEN was a central hub in the miR‐224‐5p target gene PPI network. Consequently, PTEN was selected as the primary candidate target gene for subsequent experimental validation and functional studies.

MiR‐224‐5p directly targets and regulates PTEN

Further molecular and cellular experiments validated the targeted association of miR‐224‐5p with PTEN. The PTEN 3’‐UTR contains a highly conserved binding site for the miR‐224‐5p at positions 6,278–6,284. The miR‐224‐5p seed region showed perfect complementarity to the UGACUU sequence within the PTEN 3’‐UTR (Figure 5a). In HMCs, co‐transfection with miR‐224‐5p mimics significantly diminished the luciferase activity of the PTEN‐WT reporter plasmid compared to the NC group (1.00 ± 0.04 vs. 0.44 ± 0.08, P < 0.001). In contrast, MUT of the seed match region completely abrogated this effect, as no significant difference in luciferase activity was observed between the miR‐224‐5p mimic and mimic‐NC groups (1.00 ± 0.13 vs. 0.99 ± 0.05, P > 0.50) (Figure 5b). In peripheral blood serum from T2DM and DN patients, PTEN expression was significantly lower than in the HC group, and PTEN expression in the DN group was significantly lower than in the T2DM group (P < 0.001, Figure 5c). Serum miR‐224‐5p expression levels showed a significant negative correlation with PTEN mRNA expression (n = 110, Pearson's r = −0.6538, 95% CI: −0.7493 to −0.5317, P < 0.001) (Figure 5d). However, given the technical challenges of circulating cell‐free mRNA quantification, including RNase degradation and non‐renal cellular sources such as platelets and leukocytes, this correlational finding should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, rather than as definitive evidence of a renal tissue‐level regulatory relationship. High glucose treatment significantly downregulated intracellular PTEN mRNA expression. Transfection with miR‐224‐5p inhibitor significantly upregulated PTEN expression (P < 0.01, Figure 5e).

Impacts of regulating the MiR‐224‐5p/PTEN Axis on HG‐induced HMC damage

To clarify the PTEN‐mediated biological effects of miR‐224‐5p, a series of functional rescue experiments was conducted. qRT‐PCR analysis confirmed that transfection with si‐PTEN significantly reduced PTEN mRNA expression compared with the si‐NC control group in HMCs, indicating successful and efficient knockdown of PTEN (P < 0.001, Figure S2). Under HG conditions, inhibition of miR‐224‐5p effectively restored PTEN expression (P < 0.001, Figure 6a) and significantly suppressed apoptosis (P < 0.001, Figure 6b). Concurrently, miR‐224‐5p inhibition mitigated oxidative stress, reduced inflammatory cytokine secretion, and suppressed fibrotic protein expression (P < 0.001, Figure 6c–e). However, when PTEN was simultaneously knocked down, these protective effects conferred by miR‐224‐5p inhibition were partially or completely negated (Figure 6a–e).

(c) Assessment of Malondialdehyde (MDA) and Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) activity. (d) Concentrations of IL‐6, TNF‐α, and IL‐1β measured by ELISA. (**P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001). Densitometric quantification revealed that si‐PTEN co‐transfection reversed miR‐224‐5p inhibitor‐induced PTEN upregulation by 71.71% and p‐AKT/AKT suppression by 70.42%; both reversals were statistically complete, as the miR‐224‐5p inhibitor + si‐PTEN group showed no significant differences from the inhibitor‐NC group (P > 0.05 for both comparisons).

To validate the mechanistic findings at the protein level, PTEN expression and AKT phosphorylation (p‐AKT) were assessed using western blot. As shown in Figure 6f, compared with the inhibitor‐NC group, transfection with miR‐224‐5p inhibitor significantly upregulated PTEN protein expression (P < 0.001). Co‐transfection with si‐PTEN reversed the upregulation by 71.71% (P < 0.01 vs. miR‐224‐5p inhibitor), and the PTEN levels showed no significant difference from the inhibitor‐NC group (P > 0.05), indicating a statistically complete reversal. Consistent with PTEN changes, the p‐AKT/AKT ratio was significantly decreased in the miR‐224‐5p inhibitor group compared with the inhibitor‐NC group (P < 0.001), indicating suppression of AKT pathway activation. Co‐transfection with si‐PTEN restored the p‐AKT/AKT ratio by 70.42% (P < 0.01 vs. miR‐224‐5p inhibitor group), but this ratio also showed no significant difference from the inhibitor‐NC group (P > 0.05), further confirming a statistically complete reversal (Figure 6f).

DISCUSSION

DN, among the most severe microvascular complications of diabetes, is a primary cause of end‐stage renal failure globally. The underlying pathogenesis is multifactorial, encompassing metabolic abnormalities, oxidative damage, sustained inflammation, and fibrotic scarring, all of which converge to produce an inexorable loss of renal capacity. 3 In recent years, non‐coding RNAs are increasingly recognized as pivotal contributors to the advancement of DN. This study, through a systematic approach combining clinical sample analysis with in vitro cellular experiments, has revealed the expression characteristics, prognostic value, and mechanistic actions of circulating miR‐224‐5p in DN, thereby contributing to the growing body of knowledge on miRNA dysregulation in the disease. It is well‐established that miRNAs play critical roles in DN pathogenesis, with studies implicating miR‐21, miR‐325‐3p, and miR‐23b in renal fibrosis and inflammation. 12 , 13 , 14 Our findings extend this knowledge by identifying miR‐224‐5p as a previously underappreciated regulator in this network. Specifically, it demonstrates how miR‐224‐5p modulates renal mesangial cell injury by targeting PTEN, offering additional mechanistic insight into DN progression and identifying a potential therapeutic target.

Our findings indicate that serum miR‐224‐5p levels progressively increase in patients with T2DM and DN, showing significant correlations with renal impairment markers and metabolic control indicators. Crucially, elevated miR‐224‐5p expression was identified as an independent risk factor for eGFR decline ≥30% in DN patients. however, due to the limited number of ESRD events, multivariate analysis could not be performed for this more severe clinical endpoint, and the prognostic value of miR‐224‐5p for ESRD progression remains to be determined in larger cohorts. ROC analysis further demonstrated that miR‐224‐5p exhibits moderate to moderate diagnostic performance in distinguishing T2DM from DN and good predictive performance for adverse DN outcomes. These observations offer solid clinical groundwork for pursuing miR‐224‐5p as an innovative, non‐invasive biomarker for assessing the risk of disease progression in DN, acknowledging that further validation in larger cohorts is needed to improve its clinical utility.

It is noteworthy that the event rate in our DN cohort appears relatively high. This observation is likely explained by the inclusion criteria, which required UACR >300 mg/g. This criterion preferentially enrolls patients with established, rather than early‐stage disease, who are inherently at higher risk for disease progression. Therefore, the observed event rate reflects the high‐risk nature of the study population rather than selection bias. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the use of a composite endpoint partially contributed to the event rate, and the generalizability of our findings to early‐stage DN patients requires further validation.

This study confirmed the existence and function of the miR‐224‐5p/PTEN regulatory axis at multiple levels. First, bioinformatics analysis predicted and identified PTEN as a key target, while KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that its associated pathways, such as p53 pathways, are highly implicated in DN pathogenesis, regulating cell survival, metabolism, senescence, and apoptosis. 27 , 28 Subsequently, dual‐luciferase reporter assays provided conclusive evidence of their direct binding. Functionally, miR‐224‐5p was confirmed as a key promoter of renal injury in DN. Under HG conditions, its overexpression exacerbates oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, fibrosis, and apoptosis in HMCs. Most compellingly, rescue experiments further demonstrated that the comprehensive protective effects conferred by inhibiting miR‐224‐5p, such as reduced apoptosis, mitigated oxidative stress and inflammation, and suppressed fibrosis, were substantially reversed by the concurrent knockdown of PTEN. Emerging evidence highlights the importance of immune cell homeostasis in DN progression. TH9/Treg imbalance and altered IL‐9/IL‐10 profiles contribute to DN pathogenesis, 29 complementing our findings on miR‐224‐5p‐regulated inflammation in HMCs. This result establishes a complete causal chain, confirming that miR‐224‐5p drives cellular damage by suppressing PTEN expression.

Our findings align with recent discoveries indicating that PTEN serves a renoprotective element in DN. 22 As a core negative regulator of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, downregulation of PTEN results in hyperactivation of this pathway, thereby promoting cell proliferation, inhibiting autophagy, and exacerbating inflammatory and fibrotic responses, all central events in DN pathogenesis. 19 In a related clinical study, metformin has been shown to mediate miR‐21‐regulated circulating matrix metalloproteinase‐9 in DN patients, further supporting the role of miRNAs as both therapeutic targets and circulating biomarkers. 30 Consistent with PTEN's established role as a negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT pathway, we observed that miR‐224‐5p inhibition led to decreased p‐AKT, indicating reduced pathway activation. This study elucidates a previously less understood upstream regulatory mechanism for PTEN downregulation in DN, namely its post‐transcriptional suppression by the upregulated miR‐224‐5p. This discovery adds a significant new layer to understanding the intricate regulatory network governing renal cell injury in the diabetic milieu.

This research is subject to some constraints. First, although our sample size was calculated a priori and met the minimum requirement for primary analyses, the number of patients with an unfavorable prognosis only marginally satisfied the EPV criterion (EPV = 9) for multivariate logistic regression of the composite endpoint (Table S3), potentially increasing the risk of overfitting. Consequently, these results should be viewed as hypothesis‐generating and warrant validation in larger independent cohorts. For the eGFR decline ≥30% model, the EPV was 11, which is adequate, although external validation remains necessary. Furthermore, the small number of ESRD events precluded multivariate analysis for this endpoint, which should be interpreted with caution. The lack of an external validation cohort also limits the generalizability of the biomarker findings. Future multicenter studies with large independent cohorts are warranted to externally validate the diagnostic and prognostic value of miR‐224‐5p. This study did not formally assess whether miR‐224‐5p adds incremental predictive value beyond standard clinical markers. Therefore, its added value relative to established clinical indicators remains to be determined in future adequately powered studies. UACR, a central component of DN assessment, was not included in our prognostic model. Future studies should include UACR as a covariate to assess the independent prognostic value of miR‐224‐5p beyond this established clinical marker. Second, the research exclusively used HMCs. As the kidney contains multiple cell types, such as podocytes, tubular epithelial cells, and fibroblasts, the observed effects may differ in these cells. And, we did not validate miR‐224‐5p or PTEN expression in renal biopsy tissues from DN patients. In situ expression patterns cannot be determined from circulating measurements alone. So, broader conclusions about renal biology should be draw with caution. Future studies should extend these investigations to other renal cell types and renal biopsy tissues to validate the generalizability of our findings. Thirdly, in vivo animal experiments are lacking to validate the therapeutic efficacy of targeting this axis. Future studies could evaluate the therapeutic potential by administering miR‐224‐5p antagonists via tail vein injection in DN animal models such as db/db mice, observing their effects on proteinuria, renal pathology, and PTEN expression. Additionally, we did not investigate the upstream mechanisms driving the aberrant overexpression of miR‐224‐5p in DN. Exploring upstream regulators of miR‐224‐5p expression, such as lncRNAs, circRNAs, will contribute to mapping a more comprehensive regulatory network of DN. 31 , 32 Finally, the inclusion criteria for DN patients (UACR>300 mg/g) preferentially enrolled individuals with established, rather than early‐stage DN. Consequently, our findings may not be directly generalizable to patients with microalbuminuria or early DN. In addition, we did not include a non‐diabetic chronic kidney disease (CKD) comparator group. Consequently, whether abnormal miR‐224‐5p is specific to DN or reflects a broader response to renal dysfunction remains to be determined. Future studies should include early‐stage DN and non‐diabetic CKD controls to address this question. A major technical limitation of this study is the use of U6, rather than exogenous spike‐in controls (e.g., cel‐miR‐39‐3p), for serum miRNA normalization. U6 is an intracellular nuclear RNA whose stability in serum is not well‐established and is vulnerable to pre‐analytical variables. Therefore, all serum miR‐224‐5p quantification results, including its diagnostic and prognostic performance, should be considered preliminary and hypothesis‐generating. Although we minimized variability via triplicate measurements and strict RNA quality control, independent validation studies with standardized spike‐in normalization are strictly required before any clinical application can be considered. Furthermore, serum PTEN mRNA quantification by qRT‐PCR is technically challenging due to rapid RNase degradation and extremely low concentrations of circulating cell‐free mRNA. While we detected a significant correlation between serum miR‐224‐5p and PTEN mRNA, the specificity of this measurement for renal‐derived transcripts versus other cellular sources (e.g., platelets, leukocytes) remains uncertain. Alternative approaches, such as extracellular vesicle‐derived mRNA analysis or renal biopsy tissue validation, should be explored in future studies to validate our findings. Additionally, our study did not collect information on the use of medications known to influence renal outcomes and miRNA expression in DN patients, such as renin‐angiotensin (RAS) inhibitors and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. Future prospective studies should systematically record medication use to adequately adjust for these confounders. Additionally, while we excluded patients with acute infections, we did not assess subclinical chronic inflammation (e.g., hs‐CRP, IL‐6), which is prevalent in patients with metabolic syndrome and may alter circulating miRNA profiles independently of renal status. This represents a residual confounding factor that should be addressed in future studies by incorporating systematic inflammatory biomarker measurements.

In summary, this study identifies increased miR‐224‐5p expression in DN as an independent determinant of adverse progression and a potential circulating biomarker for risk stratification. In HMCs, it mediates multiple renal mesangial cell injury phenotypes by directly targeting and suppressing PTEN, thereby driving DN progression. These findings provide a foundation for further investigations, and targeting the miR‐224‐5p/PTEN axis offers a potential strategy for delaying DN progression that warrants validation in other renal cell types and in vivo models.
