Exploratory thresholds for ambient PM2.5 constituents in relation to blood pressure levels in Chinese children
Authors: Junyi Wang, Xijie Wang, Sizhe Huang, Bin Dong, Ying Jiang, Minfeng Tao, Jia Liu, Chuan You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine the associations of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) constituents and their interactions with meteorological factors with blood pressure (BP) among Chinese school-aged children, to inform targeted protection strategies.MethodsWe analyzed 16,446 children from the Zhongshan Student Growth Cohort, with annual physical examinations conducted from 2006 to 2020 in Southeast China. Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR), weighted quantile sum (WQS), distributed lag non-linear models (DLNMs) and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to analyze the joint effects, exposure-response threshold, and effect modification by meteorological factors of PM2.5 constituents on BP.ResultsOverall, 20.2% were classified with high blood pressure (HBP). Each IQR increase in PM2.5 over lag0-14d was associated with a higher risk of HBP (RR = 1.97, 95%CI: 1.61–2.34) among overweight children. BKMR and WQS models consistently revealed joint effects of PM2.5 constituents on BP, with Black carbon (BC) as the dominant driver. RCS models established non-linear exposure-response relationships and suggested potential threshold concentrations for key components (BC: 1.98 µg/m³). Notably, both low temperature and low relative humidity (RH) significantly amplified the toxicity of PM2.5 constituents.ConclusionsPM2.5 constituents were adversely associated with BP in children, particularly for BC. These associations were modified by meteorological factors, with both low temperature and low RH exacerbating the adverse effects. These findings highlight the importance of considering meteorological factors in developing evidence-based air quality guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28071-z.

Introduction

Blood pressure (BP) is the strongest predictor of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1], and remains central to cardiovascular disease (CVD) control and prevention. Studies in adults found both short-term and long-term exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) could lead to rapid increase of BP and incidence of cardiovascular diseases for years thereafter [2–4]. However, longitudinal evidence for such associations in pediatric populations is still limited.

Systematic reviews have indicated an association between PM2.5 exposure and elevated BP values in children and adolescents [5, 6]. Cross-sectional studies further suggest that black carbon (BC), among all PM2.5 constituents, is linked to a higher hypertension prevalence in school-aged children [7–9]. Nevertheless, longitudinal evidence remains scarce, particularly regarding the dose-response relationship between specific PM2.5 constituents and high blood pressure (HBP), as well as the relative contribution of key components to HBP. Although our study was based on one city and focused on short-term exposure, the 14-year longitudinal design and large sample provide robust exposure–response thresholds that can support targeted air quality management for children. Caution is needed when extrapolating to other regions. Furthermore, children generally exhibit higher PM2.5 exposure doses than adults at equivalent ambient concentrations, attributable to immature pulmonary development and increased outdoor activity patterns [10]. However, evidence-based interventions to reduce air pollution exposure in this vulnerable group are still lacking. Childhood and adolescence represent a critical window for hypertension prevention [11, 12]. Clarifying how PM2.5 constituents relate to BP changes in this age group and identifying modifiable exposure thresholds—could inform targeted environmental policies to reduce the future societal and economic burden of cardiovascular disease.

China introduced new national standards for PM2.5 concentrations in 2012 and has led to significant improvement in air quality [13]. However, the air quality in China was still above the World Health Organization (WHO) recommend levels, and has large diversity nationwide [14]. Following the WHO air quality guideline updates, there is a pressing need to evaluate whether China’s current standards adequately protect vulnerable populations including children and adolescents. Such evaluations are essential to inform evidence-based policy development.

Meteorological factors critically modify PM2.5 toxicity. Low temperature enhances cardiovascular stress via sympathetic activation and vasoconstriction, while low RH prolongs particle airway residence [15, 16]. Children differ from adults in thermoregulatory capacity and outdoor activity patterns, experiencing greater exposure variability during weather extremes. Whether these interactions operate similarly in pediatric populations remains unexplored, limiting development of weather-sensitive health protection strategies.

Therefore, in the current study, we analyzed data from a 14-year repeated-measures observational cohort. We characterized the exposure–response relationships and determined critical thresholds for key PM2.5 constituents in relation to BP outcomes in school-aged children. We also explored the modification effects of meteorological factors and body weight status. As children with baseline HBP were retained in the cohort, HBP was assessed prospectively at each annual follow-up visit. It thus provides a basis for developing air quality standards tailored to children. We aimed to (1) examine the individual and joint associations of key PM2.5 constituents on BP in school-aged children and identify susceptible subpopulations, (2) characterize the exposure-response relationships and explore potential thresholds for predominant PM2.5 constituents in vulnerable groups, and (3) assess whether these associations were modified by meteorological factors, including temperature or relative humidity (RH).

Methods

Study population

Data of the present study were from the student growth cohort between 2006 and 2020 in Zhongshan, China. Details of this repeated-measures observational cohort were published previously [17]. In brief, the student growth cohort was established on the basis of annual physical examinations in primary and secondary schools in Zhongshan. Generally, children took a school physical examination once a year, from the first grade to the third year of high school (approximately ages 7 to 18). The time interval between each two measurements would be one year, if participants had not missed an examination. All measurements were conducted by qualified medical physicians.

The original dataset included 151,243 measurements from 16,581 individuals, of which 7,959 were excluded due to incomplete BP data. Subsequently, 135 individuals were further excluded for having ≤ 8 measurements or lacking at least one measurement between the ages of 16 and 18 years. The final sample included in the analysis consisted of 143,284 measurements from 16,446 individuals. HBP status was classified independently at each examination; children could transition between normotensive and HBP states across follow-up. Zhongshan Health Care Center for Primary and Secondary School have authorized the use of the dataset. For the original cohort establishment, all human participants provided written informed consent prior to their participation. For this secondary analysis, all personal information that could be traced to individuals has been removed before authorization; therefore, the dataset we used was completely anonymous. Because the dataset was provided by the data custodian (Zhongshan Health Care Center for Primary and Secondary School) after applying these eligibility criteria, the number of children excluded at each stage was not available to the investigators. The present analysis has met the requirements for exemption and has been approved for exemption of ethical application by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB B00001052-20011-exempt).

Children’s sex and date of birth were collected at their first physical examination in primary school and were recorded in the school register system along with the date of the examination. The age (in years) at each follow-up examination was calculated as (date of examination-date of birth)/365.25.

Health data collection

Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 centimeter using the portable stadiometer (model TZG, China), with students standing straight and barefoot. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a lever-type weight scale (model RGT-140, China), with students wearing undergarments. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) /height2 (m2). Age and sex-specific BMI z scores were converted based on the growth reference from World Health Organization [18], which were used to define overweight and non-overweight in stratified analysis. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured consistent with the recommendations under the 2017 Clinical Practice Guideline for Screening and Management of High Blood Pressure in Children (2017 Guideline) [19]. Mercury sphygmomanometers (model XJ11D, China), stethoscopes (model TZ-1, China), and appropriate cuffs were used for the measurement. Participants were asked to sit quietly for at least 5 min before the first reading. BP was measured twice with a 5-minute gap between the 2 measurements from the right arm. If the difference between the 2 measurements exceeded 10mmHg (either SBP or DBP), an extra measurement would be conducted until the difference between the last 2 measurements < 10mmHg. The average of SBP and DBP values were calculated, respectively. SBP and DBP were transformed into age-, sex-, and height-specific percentiles for cross-sectional comparisons. SBP and DBP were analyzed as continuous percentiles and binary HBP status. HBP was defined as: (a) SBP or DBP ≥ 95th age-sex-height percentile for children < 13 years; (b) BP ≥ 130/80 mmHg for children ≥ 13 years [19]. Because BP in our study was derived from single-time measurement, HBP prevalence would be doubled or tripled than the actual prevalence of hypertension [19, 20]. Children with HBP at baseline were not excluded, as we aimed to examine the association between PM2.5 exposure and BP outcomes across all BP levels, including progression from normal to high BP. This analytical choice estimates population-averaged effects of exposure on BP outcomes, combining cross-sectional associations (between-person) and longitudinal changes (within-person).

BP measurements were conducted during annual school physical examinations, which may introduce certain contextual influences. The school setting, while ensuring standardized procedures and qualified medical personnel, could potentially affect BP readings due to factors such as anticipatory anxiety in children, time-of-day variations (examinations are typically scheduled during the morning session of the school day), and the absence of repeated visits for confirmation. As noted in the 2017 Clinical Practice Guideline, BP derived from single-occasion measurements in screening settings may overestimate the true prevalence of hypertension by two- to three-fold compared to repeated measurements on separate visits. However, since our primary objective was to examine associations between PM2.5 constituents exposure and BP outcomes rather than to estimate absolute hypertension prevalence, and given that any measurement-related bias would likely be non-differential with respect to exposure, we do not expect this to substantially bias our relative risk estimates. Nevertheless, the absolute HBP prevalence reported (20.2%) should be interpreted as reflecting elevated BP detected in a screening context rather than confirmed clinical hypertension.

Exposure variables

The daily gridded concentrations ambient PM2.5 and other main constituents including sulfate (SO42–), nitrate (NO3–), ammonium (NH4+), organic matter (OM), and BC were obtained from tracking air pollution in China (http://tapdata.org.cn/). PM2.5 constituents data were full-coverage near-real-time set at 10 km spatial resolution since 2000, which integrated real-time ground observations, near-real-time satellite remote sensing information, and air quality model simulation with multisource big data and provided near-real-time data while ensured complete spatiotemporal coverage [21]. PM2.5 predictions incorporated PM2.5 measurements, Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) PM2.5 simulations, Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) level-2 aerosol optical depth (AOD) retrievals, Modern-Era Retrospective analysis for Research and Applications, Version 2 (MERRA-2) meteorology parameters, and land use information from a two-stage random forest model [22]. Data were enhanced by multilayer machine learning models. The detailed methodology has been described previously [23, 24].

We assumed that the data were missing at random imputed them with Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) [25]. The missing data pattern for all exposure and meteorological variables was characterized. The proportion of missing data for each variable ranged from 1.2% to 3.8%, with an overall missing rate of 2.7%. Missing values were imputed by MICE under the assumption of missing at random (MAR). Five imputations were conducted. The variables included in the MICE imputation model were: PM2.5, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺, OM, BC, temperature, RH, atmospheric pressure, age, sex, BMI, urban/rural residence, and season. The cumulative daily average of the ambient PM2.5 constituents in Zhongshan City was assessed and defined as the moving daily average of the ambient PM2.5 components matched to the date of each physical examination. Short-term exposure windows were single-day exposure on the day of the examination (lag 0d) and average exposures up to 14 days before the examination (from lag 0-1d to lag 0-14d). Daily average of meteorological data including temperature, RH, and AP at city level were obtained from the China Meteorological Data Service Center (http://data.cma.cn/). Short-term exposure windows (lag 0-14d) were selected because acute autonomic and vascular responses to PM2.5 occur within days of exposure, annual BP measurements preclude reliable long-term exposure assessment, and repeated acute insults may compound into sustained BP elevation through cumulative vascular damage.

Statistical analysis

Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) were adopted to analyze the associations between short-term exposure to PM2.5 constituents and BP outcomes in school-aged children [26, 27]. PM2.5 constituent concentrations were log10-transformed to satisfy the normality assumption. Random intercepts were included for individual children (to account for repeated measurements) and examination dates (to address shared exposure assignments among children examined simultaneously). For each PM2.5 constituent, the change in SBP and DBP and the risk of HBP associated with an interquartile range (IQR) increase in concentration were estimated, along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [28].

For each PM2.5 constituent, interaction models were constructed to evaluate effect modification by overweight status, adjusting for age, sex, ambient temperature, RH, atmospheric pressure, residential area, and season [29]. An interaction term between the standardized constituent concentration and binary overweight status (overweight vs. non-overweight) was included in each model. Statistical significance of the interaction term was reported as “P for interaction”, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Stratified analyses were then performed separately for non-overweight and overweight children to estimate the magnitude of associations in each subgroup.

Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) was applied to estimate the joint effect of PM2.5 constituents on HBP [30]. A Gaussian kernel was adopted to support flexible exposure–response shapes for continuous outcomes. A probit link function was used to fit the exposure-response relationship for binary outcome [31, 32]. Where Yi is BP outcome, h () is the exposure-response function of concentrations of PM2.5 constituents, zi is a vector of covariates. The overall mixture effects of PM2.5 constituents were estimated by subtracting the mean of outcome when the concentrations of PM2.5 constituents were at 25th percentile from the mean of outcome when all the concentrations of PM2.5 constituents were at 75th percentile. Posterior inclusion probability (PIP) was calculated to identify the relative importance of individual exposure variables to the overall mixture effect by Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler fitted with 10,000 iterations. PIP represents the probability that a variable contributes to the outcome, conditional on the other variables in the model, with values > 0.5 suggesting substantial importance. The concentrations of PM2.5 constituents were ln-transformed to account for skewness and to reduce the influence of extreme values.

Weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression model, as a multi-pollutant model in estimating mixed effects of multiple environmental pollutants [33], were constructed to evaluate the potential joint effects of PM2.5 constituents exposures on HBP, and to determine the contributing effects of individual PM2.5 constituents accounted for observed overall effects. WQS index was created with quartiles of PM2.5 components levels, and included estimated empirical weights for each chemical. Where β0 is the intercept, β1 is the regression coefficient for the weighted index and can be interpreted as the joint effects of exposure to PM2.5 components on HBP, z’ is a vector of covariates, φ is vector of corresponding regression coefficients. Σi is weighted index of all PM2.5 constituent concentrations for each individual. Weights for all PM2.5 constituents were constrained from 0 ≤ wi ≤ 1, and sum to 1.

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 4 knots (at 5th, 35th, 65th, 95th percentiles) were further conducted to analyze the relationship between PM2.5 constituents above the potential inflection points and HBP in the most susceptible population, identifying potential PM2.5 constituents inflection points for HBP in overweight children [34]. Potential inflection concentrations were empirically identified as points where HBP risk increased monotonically with 95%CIs excluding the null, representing exploratory estimates derived from this specific cohort. Note: Risk estimates are relative to the 5th percentile reference; these points should be validated in independent populations before policy application.

Distributed lag non-linear models (DLNMs) were used to explore the joint effects of PM2.5 constituents and meteorological factors (temperature, RH) on BP outcomes [35]. We specified 3 degrees of freedom (df) for the exposure-response dimension and 4 df for the lag dimension (0-14d) [36]. For each component, we specified 3 df for the exposure-response dimension (X-axis: component concentration) and 4 df for the lag dimension (0–14 d), using natural cubic splines centered at median exposures. Meteorological factors (Y-axis) were modeled with 3 df. Bivariate response surfaces were constructed with Z-axis representing the association value (change in SBP/DBP or HBP risk). The ‘variance average’ parameterization estimated cumulative effects across lags, and the three-dimensional surface was collapsed over the lag dimension. Formal statistical tests for interaction (e.g., product terms) were not performed; therefore, the surfaces are interpreted descriptively as hypothesis‑generating patterns rather than confirmatory evidence of interaction.

Sensitivity analyses were carried out to verify the stability of the results by fitting two-pollutant models. According to the dominant pollutants identified by the joint effect model (BKMR and WQS), the key pollutants were included in the two-pollutant model for analysis. Covariates included age, sex, residential area, individual code, day of week, temperature (in Degrees Celsius), RH (in %), AP, and season (categorical) were involved where applicable.

To assess confounding by other PM2.5 constituents, two‑pollutant models were performed with the dominant pollutant identified by the joint effect models (BKMR and WQS) as the primary exposure. Three cumulative lag windows (lag0d, lag0-7d, and lag0-14d) were examined to capture different time scales of acute effects and reduce multiple comparisons. For each BP outcome (SBP, DBP, HBP), PM2.5, OM, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, or NH₄⁺ was sequentially added to the model containing the dominant pollutant. Covariates included age, sex, residential area, individual code, DOW, temperature, RH, atmospheric pressure, and season.

Statistical significance level was set at two-tailed P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were employed using R software (version 3.6.2, R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

PM2.5 constituents and meteorological factors

Average exposure to PM2.5 constituents and meteorological factors during study period are presented in Table S1 and Figure S1. The median PM2.5, SO42−, NO3−, NH4+, OM and BC concentrations were 28.6 (IQR: 18.5) µg/m3, 6.4 (IQR: 3.8) µg/m3, 4.2 (IQR: 3.9) µg/m3, 3.9 (IQR: 3.0) µg/m3, 7.8 (IQR: 4.9) µg/m3 and 2.0 (IQR: 1.3) µg/m3, respectively. The median temperature and RH were 14.2 (IQR: 14.9) ℃ and 54.1 (IQR: 26.7) %, respectively. Spearman correlations between PM2.5 constituents and meteorological factors were described in Figure S2. PM2.5 constituents were strongly positively correlated with each other (r = 0.652 ~ 0.985, P < 0.05). Temperature showed negative correlations with PM2.5 and its constituents (r = -0.189~-0.602), reflecting seasonal variation in pollution levels. RH was positively correlated with temperature (r = 0.372, P < 0.001) but showed negative associations with PM2.5 constituents.

Associations of PM2.5 constituents mixture with BP outcomes

Figure S3 presents the estimated changes in BP outcomes at lag0-14d associated with each interquartile range increase in PM2.5 constituents among Chinese school-aged children. PM2.5 and its constituents exhibited significant lag-cumulative effects on changes in SBP and HBP risk(P < 0.05), with the magnitude of these effects gradually increasing as the lag period extended. And the impacts on DBP remained relatively modest(P < 0.05).

Statistically significant interactions with weight group were found for most PM2.5 constituents (P < 0.01, Table S2). Significant effect modification by weight was detected in BP outcomes with stronger associations among overweight children. For example, each an IQR increment of BC concentration was associated with 2.9 mmHg (95% CI: 2.56–3.24) increase in SBP among overweight children, while the increase was 1.84 mmHg (95% CI: 1.23–2.46) among those non-overweigh.

Further stratified analyses in Fig. 1 revealed that the highest risk of HBP over lag0d–lag0-14d was observed in overweight children. The positive associations of each PM2.5 constituent with BP outcomes were generally stronger in overweight/obese children than in non-overweight children. In particular, for SBP and HBP, the overweight/obese group exhibited more pronounced elevations across different lag days, with the strongest association observed at lag0-14d. There was a lagged association between exposure to PM2.5 constituents and BP outcomes, with the association for HBP being highest at lag0-14d. These findings suggest that overweight status may enhance the adverse associations of PM2.5 constituents with BP.

RCS models revealed non-linear exposure-response relationships between key PM2.5 constituents and HBP risk in overweight school-aged children (Fig. 4). The analysis identified specific critical concentrations beyond which HBP risk increased in an approximately linear fashion: 29.51 µg/m3 for PM2.5, 3.94 µg/m3 for NH4+, 8.05 µg/m3 for OM, 6.47 µg/m3 for SO42−, and 1.98 µg/m3 for BC, respectively. Exposure to these components above their respective thresholds was associated with an increase in HBP risk, with the most pronounced effect observed for BC. These exploratory potential inflection points may inform future research priorities regarding component-specific air quality guidelines for children’s cardiovascular health.

Joint patterns of PM2.5 constituents and meteorological factors on HBP risk: bivariate response surface analysis

Figure 5 displayed bivariate response surfaces characterizing the cumulative (lag 0–14 days) associations between PM2.5 constituents and the risk of HBP across meteorological factors. Overall, the positive associations with HBP risk tended to be stronger under lower temperature and lower RH conditions, showed the most pronounced modification: HBP risk increased steeply at BC concentrations above approximately 2.0 µg/m³ when temperature fell below 15 °C or RH below 50%. Secondary inorganic components (SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺) exhibited moderate patterns, with elevated HBP risk becoming apparent only at higher constituent concentrations under cold or dry conditions. By contrast, OM showed the weakest variation across meteorological gradients, with no clear evidence of effect modification.

Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analysis of the two-pollutant model, the estimated value of BP outcomes among overweight subjects remained stable, proving that the results of associations between exposure to BC and risk of HBP at different lag days among overweight school-aged children were robust (Table S5).

Discussion

With 14 years longitudinal follow-up in Chinese children, we comprehensively examined the individual and joint associations of PM2.5 constituents on BP outcomes, with a particular focus on the vulnerable subgroup of overweight children and the potential effect modification by meteorological factors. Our main findings indicate that exposure to PM2.5 constituents was positively associated with increased risks of SBP, DBP, and HBP. BC was identified as having the strongest association to these adverse outcomes. Furthermore, we observed that both low temperature and low RH significantly amplified the cardiovascular toxicity of PM2.5 constituents, particularly BC. Notably, near-linear exposure-response relationships and specific critical concentrations for several key components were identified.

Consistent with previous studies in adult population, we found that the adverse cardiovascular effects of PM2.5 apply to short-term exposure in school-aged children, thereby identifying overweight children as a susceptible subgroup that warrants particular concern [37–39]. Previous studies have reported that exposure to PM2.5 constituents was associated with increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines and changes of platelet activation and aggregation markers [40], which could potentially contribute to inflammation and oxidative stress that may be involved in BP changes in overweight adults [41]. Changes in hemodynamic indexes directly caused by PM2.5 constituents exposure may also contributed to HBP development [7]. Similar physiological mechanisms may be founded in children as well. As HBP in childhood is highly likely to continue into adulthood and to further mediate the associations of PM2.5 constituents and cardiovascular disease mortality [42, 43], analysis of cumulative hazardous effect from long-term follow-ups are in need. It also requires attention that the hazard of PM2.5 constituents on children’s BP might be underestimated in our study, since it was conducted in southeastern China, where air pollution levels and obesity prevalence were better than the national average [20, 23].

The consistent results from the BKMR and WQS models strongly support the existence of joint effects from PM2.5 constituents on BP outcomes. This finding aligns with growing evidence that particulate matter exerts its health impacts through complex mixtures rather than individual components [44, 45]. Based on previous correlation studies [46, 47], we further identified the main contributing role of BC in the process of PM2.5 effects on BP in children by weighted quantile sum analysis. BC, as a primary tracer of combustion-related pollution, possesses unique physicochemical properties, including a large specific surface area and strong penetrative capacity into the circulatory system. These characteristics may enhance its potential to induce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, ultimately leading to endothelial dysfunction and BP elevation. Based on the analysis from our current study, BC constituted approximately 4% of PM2.5 mass in Guangdong province, yet accounted for approximately 35% of the WQS mixture index weight for HBP, suggesting disproportionate toxicity relative to its mass fraction. Our findings suggest that BC may warrant particular attention in future air quality health assessments, given its consistent associations with BP outcomes in this cohort.

It is important to note that PM2.5 constituents exhibited high intercorrelations in our study (r ranging from 0.652 to 0.985), reflecting their common sources and atmospheric co-occurrence. While BKMR and WQS models are designed to handle correlated exposures in mixture analyses, substantial collinearity may still limit our ability to distinguish independent effects of individual components. The prominent weights assigned to BC in both BKMR (PIP = 0.794 for HBP) and WQS models may partly reflect its strong correlation with other combustion-related pollutants, rather than a purely independent effect. Therefore, the “dominant driver” interpretation for BC should be understood as indicating its substantial contribution to the overall mixture effect within the context of correlated exposures, rather than necessarily implying a unique causal role distinct from other co-occurring pollutants. Future studies incorporating source apportionment methods or measurement of additional combustion tracers could help disentangle source-specific effects.

BC exerts cardiovascular toxicity through multiple pathways [48]. As a core component of ultrafine particles, BC penetrates deep into alveoli, enters systemic circulation, and triggers oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction [49]. BC serves as a carrier for organic compounds and metals, amplifying reactive oxygen species generation [50]. In children, developing vascular systems may exhibit heightened sensitivity to BC-induced arterial stiffness, with effects potentially tracking into adulthood [51].

As suggested by the WHO, although the evidence base is insufficient to set a certain air quality guidelines to provide a basis for legally binding limit values, adoption of an air quality standard or target could be good instrument to force local actions on BC reduction [52]. Strategies to control BC emissions should also consider local conditions. As reported in our present study, the places of lower temperature and lower RH may need more strict guidelines than warmer and more humid places. The sources of BC from rural /urban and geographical areas are also need further explore in order to make specific measures [52]. The largest contributor to ambient BC in Asian and African countries was burning of solid biofuels [53], and anthropogenic carbonaceous particles should receive greater weight in air pollution mitigation scenarios and policy measures [54]. Meanwhile, indoor concentrations of BC, which mainly included infiltration from outdoors, combustion and smoking (especially second-hand smoking for children), also related to aggravated BP outcomes and should be call for attention [47].

This analysis identified potential threshold concentrations above which HBP risk increased approximately linearly for several PM2.5 constituents in overweight children. These estimates—derived from RCS models fitted to a single cohort—should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive regulatory benchmarks. The estimated PM2.5 concentration threshold in this study was 29.51 µg/m³, which is below the current Chinese Grade I ambient air quality standard (35 µg/m³, GB 3095) [55]. However, the upper bound of the confidence interval for this threshold reached 43.02 µg/m³, exceeding the standard of 35 µg/m³. This finding suggests that the possibility of PM2.5 exposure contributing to elevated BP cannot be ruled out, even at exposure levels close to the current national standard. For BC, the estimated threshold (1.98 µg/m³) is notably lower than typical urban concentrations, indicating that this component may warrant attention even at relatively low levels—but this estimate requires replication in diverse geographic and demographic settings before informing standards.

We emphasize that these thresholds represent statistical inflection points in exposure-response curves from one specific population, not necessarily levels below which no risk exists or above which regulatory action is mandated. The steep exposure-response relationships above these concentrations suggest that incremental reductions in highly exposed subgroups (e.g., overweight children during cold, dry periods) may offer disproportionate health benefits. However, we caution against direct application of these numerical thresholds to air quality standards without: (1) validation in independent pediatric cohorts with different pollution mixtures and climate conditions; (2) assessment of feasibility and costs of achieving such levels; and (3) consideration of the broader pollutant mixture context. Instead, these findings may inform priority-setting for future research, identification of susceptible subgroups, and development of weather-sensitive health advisory systems.

Another finding from our bivariate response surface analysis concerns the patterns observed for meteorological factors. The results suggest that the positive association between PM2.5 constituents and HBP risk was generally more pronounced under conditions of lower temperature and lower RH. Among all components, BC showed the greatest variation across meteorological strata, with a steeper increase in HBP risk under cold or dry conditions. The consistent prominence of BC across both meteorological conditions suggests that this component may be particularly sensitive to physiological or environmental stressors, warranting further investigation.

Previous studies have indicated both low temperature and low RH substantially amplified the cardiovascular toxicity of PM2.5 constituents [56]. The amplified risk under low-temperature conditions could be attributed to several mechanisms [57]. Cold exposure may induce peripheral vasoconstriction and increase cardiac afterload, potentially enhancing the hemodynamic impact of pollutant-induced stress [58, 59]. Additionally, behavioral changes, such as reduced ventilation in colder weather, might lead to higher indoor exposure to infiltrated outdoor particles [60]. The potentiation of effects under low RH conditions is equally noteworthy. Low RH may impair mucociliary clearance in the respiratory tract, prolonging the contact time between deposited particles and lung tissue [61, 62]. Nevertheless, the bivariate response surfaces for temperature and RH presented herein were descriptive, hypothesis-generating visualizations rather than confirmatory evidence of biological interaction. No formal statistical interaction tests were performed, and these patterns should be interpreted as exploratory signals requiring validation in future studies with formal interaction models. Future studies with interaction testing or experimental designs needed to confirm whether low temperature and low RH causally amplify the cardiovascular effects of PM2.5 constituents, and to explore underlying biological pathways in children.

There are some limitations in the present study. First, exposure assessment relied on modeled city-level PM2.5 constituent (10 km resolution), which may not capture individual-level variability due to differences in residential microenvironments, indoor/outdoor activity patterns, and commuting, potentially introducing non‑differential misclassification. Second, due to restricted data access, key demographic information (e.g., age and sex distribution) was incomplete for excluded children, preventing systematic comparison with included participants. Moreover, our dataset lacked individual‑level characteristics that may influence childhood BP, including dietary patterns, physical activity, socioeconomic status, parental education, and family history of hypertension. The absence of these covariates may lead to residual confounding. Third, the single‑site cohort in Zhongshan (with relatively lower air pollution and obesity prevalence) limits the generalizability to regions with higher pollution or cardiovascular risk. Fourth, despite using mixture models (BKMR, WQS) to address multi‑pollutant correlations, high collinearity among PM2.5 components still complicates attribution of independent effects. Finally, our analysis captured associations with BP elevation and state transitions (persistence, remission, progression) rather than definitive disease incidence, limiting causal inference regarding disease etiology.

Conclusion

This study provides further evidence of the complex associations of PM2.5 constituents exposure on BP in Chinese school-aged children, identifying overweight children as a susceptible subpopulation. Joint effects analysis confirms that co-exposure to multiple PM2.5 constituents significantly elevates BP, with BC being the primary driver. Notably, low temperature and low RH were found to substantially enhance the cardiovascular toxicity of PM2.5 constituents in overweight children, revealing critical interactions between meteorological factors and air pollutants. There These findings suggest the need for further research to inform potential updates to air quality guidelines, including consideration of component-specific limits and meteorological adjustment factors, which may enhance the protection of cardiovascular health in children.
