Quantum Fisher Information and the Speed of Entanglement
Zain H. Saleem

Abstract
We investigate the speed at which entanglement can be generated by an interaction parameter encoded in a two-qubit Hamiltonian, quantified by the derivative of concurrence with respect to the coupling parameter. For arbitrary pure two-qubit states evolving under a general nonlocal interaction, we derive a bound relating this entanglement speed to the quantum Fisher information (QFI). Specifically, we show that | ∂ g C | ≤ F Q ( g ) |\partial_{g}C|\leq\sqrt{F_{Q}^{(g)}} , where F Q ( g ) F_{Q}^{(g)} is the QFI associated with estimation of the parameter. This establishes F Q \sqrt{F_{Q}} as a an upper bound on the speed of entanglement generation in parameter space. We further derive the saturation conditions and identify the states and dynamical regimes for which equality is attained. At saturation, concurrence evolves at the maximum rate permitted by the distinguishability of the underlying quantum state. These results reveal a direct connection between quantum metrology and entanglement generation, showing that the same information-theoretic quantity that governs parameter-estimation precision also limits the speed at which entanglement resources can be created.



Introduction.—

Entanglement is a central resource in quantum information science, underpinning applications ranging from quantum communication and cryptography to quantum computation, quantum networks, and quantum-enhanced sensing
4
;
6
. For bipartite systems, concurrence occupies a distinguished role as one of the most widely used entanglement measures due to its closed analytical form, direct relation to entanglement of formation, and broad applicability in both theoretical and experimental studies
12
. Concurrence has been extensively employed to characterize entanglement generation, degradation under noise, quantum phase transitions, and the performance of entangling gates.

Quantum Fisher information (QFI) is an equally fundamental quantity in quantum metrology
1
;
7
;
8
. For a parameter encoded in a quantum state, the QFI determines the ultimate precision achievable by any quantum measurement through the quantum Cramér–Rao bound. Geometrically, QFI quantifies the distinguishability of neighboring quantum states and determines the local statistical speed of evolution in parameter space. Consequently, QFI has become a central tool in quantum sensing, parameter estimation, critical phenomena, and multipartite entanglement detection.

The relationship between entanglement and QFI has traditionally been explored from the perspective of quantum-enhanced metrology, where entanglement acts as a resource capable of increasing the achievable QFI and improving estimation precision
8
. Recently, however, attention has shifted toward the inverse question: what constraints does QFI impose on the behavior of entanglement itself? In Ref.
9
, it was shown that the curvature of concurrence with respect to an encoded interaction parameter is bounded by the corresponding QFI, establishing a direct connection between metrological sensitivity and the second-order response of entanglement. Subsequently, Ref.
10
demonstrated that the same quantity governs the leading-order degradation of entanglement under fluctuations of the interaction parameter, providing an information-theoretic bound on the robustness of entanglement generation.

The present work addresses a complementary question. Rather than asking how rapidly the curvature of entanglement changes or how robust entanglement remains under parameter uncertainty, we ask: how rapidly can entanglement itself evolve under variations of an encoded parameter? To answer this question, we consider the first derivative of concurrence with respect to the parameter. We show that the concurrence derivative satisfies
|\partial_{g}C|\leq\sqrt{F_{Q}^{(g)}}
, where
F_{Q}^{(g)}
denotes the quantum Fisher information associated with estimation of the parameter. This inequality identifies
\sqrt{F_{Q}}
as a fundamental upper bound on the speed of entanglement evolution in parameter space.

The result admits a natural geometric interpretation. For pure states, the QFI determines the local statistical distance through the Fubini–Study metric,
ds^{2}=\frac{1}{4}F_{Q}^{(g)}dg^{2}
, so that
\sqrt{F_{Q}^{(g)}}
sets the local distinguishability scale between neighboring quantum states. The quantity
\sqrt{F_{Q}^{(g)}}
therefore characterizes the maximum rate at which the quantum state itself can change under an infinitesimal variation of the encoded interaction parameter. Our bound shows that the rate at which concurrence changes can never exceed this fundamental distinguishability scale. We further derive the saturation conditions and identify the states and dynamical regimes for which equality is attained. At saturation, concurrence evolves at the maximum rate permitted by the geometry of the underlying quantum state.

These results reveal a direct connection between quantum metrology and entanglement dynamics. While QFI has traditionally been interpreted as a measure of parameter sensitivity and state distinguishability, our results show that it simultaneously constrains the first-order response of an entanglement resource. In this sense, the same information-theoretic quantity that governs the ultimate precision of parameter estimation also determines the maximum speed at which entanglement can evolve. We therefore identify a new role for QFI as a fundamental limit on entanglement dynamics and establish a direct bridge between metrological distinguishability and the speed of entanglement evolution.

Two-qubit interactions.—

To make the connection between metrological distinguishability and entanglement dynamics precise, we consider two interacting qubits governed by

H(g)=gh,

(1)

where
g
denotes the interaction strength and
h
is a dimensionless interaction operator. The parameter
g
plays a dual role. On the one hand, it controls the entanglement generated by the interaction. On the other, it is the parameter encoded into the evolving quantum state and therefore the quantity whose estimation is governed by the quantum Fisher information. The central question of this work is whether these two notions of sensitivity—the sensitivity of entanglement and the sensitivity of the quantum state itself—are fundamentally related.

The most general two-qubit interaction may be written as

h=\sum_{j,k=x,y,z}\eta_{jk}\,\sigma_{j}\otimes\sigma_{k}.

(2)

Since both concurrence and quantum Fisher information are invariant under local unitary transformations, only the nonlocal content of the interaction is relevant
6
;
4
. Consequently, the coefficient matrix
\eta
may be diagonalized through a singular-value decomposition, with the corresponding local rotations absorbed into a redefinition of the single-qubit basis states. Every two-qubit interaction is therefore locally equivalent to the canonical form

h=\eta_{x}\,\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x}+\eta_{y}\,\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y}+\eta_{z}\,\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z}.

(3)

This reduction preserves both concurrence and QFI and therefore introduces no loss of generality. More importantly, it exposes the interaction spectrum responsible for both entanglement generation and parameter sensitivity. The canonical interaction is diagonal in the Bell basis, making the Bell states the natural language for describing the dynamics. Expanding an arbitrary pure initial state as

|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{a,b}\beta_{ab}|\beta_{ab}\rangle,

(4)

the evolved state becomes

|\Psi(g,t)\rangle=\sum_{a,b}\beta_{ab}e^{-ig\omega_{ab}t}|\beta_{ab}\rangle,

(5)

where

\omega_{ab}=(-1)^{a}\eta_{x}-(-1)^{a+b}\eta_{y}+(-1)^{b}\eta_{z}

(6)

are the Bell-state interaction frequencies.

To quantify the entanglement generated by the interaction we employ concurrence, one of the most widely used entanglement measures for two-qubit systems owing to its analytical tractability and direct relation to entanglement of formation
12
. For the evolved state, concurrence assumes the form

C(g,t)=\left|\sum_{a,b}(-1)^{a+b}\beta_{ab}^{\,2}e^{-2ig\omega_{ab}t}\right|.

(7)

Equation (
7
) admits a simple physical interpretation. Each Bell sector contributes a rotating phase factor determined by its interaction frequency. Entanglement generation therefore emerges from interference between different frequency sectors. Constructive interference enhances concurrence, while destructive interference suppresses it. In this picture, concurrence measures the outcome of a frequency-interference process generated by the interaction spectrum.

The sensitivity of the quantum state itself is quantified by the quantum Fisher information. For pure-state evolution generated by Eq. (1), the QFI with respect to the interaction strength is

F_{Q}^{(g)}=4t^{2}\left[\sum_{a,b}|\beta_{ab}|^{2}\omega_{ab}^{2}-\left(\sum_{a,b}|\beta_{ab}|^{2}\omega_{ab}\right)^{2}\right].

(8)

Unlike concurrence, Eq. (
8
) depends only on the variance of the interaction spectrum sampled by the initial state. Relative phases do not appear. The QFI therefore characterizes the distinguishability of neighboring quantum states, whereas concurrence characterizes the interference responsible for entanglement generation
1
;
7
;
8
.

At first sight these quantities appear fundamentally different. One depends on coherent interference between Bell sectors, while the other depends only on spectral variance. Nevertheless, both originate from the same interaction frequencies
\{\omega_{ab}\}
. This observation naturally raises the question of whether the sensitivity of entanglement is constrained by the sensitivity of the underlying quantum state itself. We now show that the answer is affirmative and that the speed of entanglement evolution is bounded by the square root of the quantum Fisher information.

Entanglement speed bound and saturation conditions.—

We now derive the central result of this Letter. To expose the geometric structure underlying the bound, it is convenient to remove the mean interaction frequency

\mu=\sum_{a,b}|\beta_{ab}|^{2}\omega_{ab}

(9)

and define the centered frequencies

\Delta_{ab}=\omega_{ab}-\mu.

(10)

Since an overall phase does not affect concurrence, the concurrence amplitude may be written as

\widetilde{C}(g,t)=\sum_{a,b}X_{ab}(g,t),\qquad X_{ab}(g,t)=(-1)^{a+b}\beta_{ab}^{\,2}e^{-2igt\Delta_{ab}},

(11)

with
C(g,t)=|\widetilde{C}(g,t)|
. In terms of the same centered frequencies, the QFI takes the variance form

F_{Q}^{(g)}=4t^{2}\sum_{a,b}|\beta_{ab}|^{2}\Delta_{ab}^{\,2}.

(12)

We define the entanglement speed in parameter space as

v_{E}(g,t)=|\partial_{g}C(g,t)|,

(13)

which quantifies the first-order response of concurrence to an infinitesimal change in the interaction strength. Because concurrence contains an absolute value, it may fail to be differentiable at isolated points where
\widetilde{C}(g,t)=0
. Such points correspond to cusps of the concurrence landscape. In the derivation below we restrict attention to differentiable points. At cusp points, the corresponding one-sided derivatives may be considered separately whenever they exist.

At any differentiable point,

|\partial_{g}C|=|\partial_{g}|\widetilde{C}||\leq|\partial_{g}\widetilde{C}|.

(14)

Differentiating the concurrence amplitude gives

\partial_{g}\widetilde{C}=-2it\sum_{a,b}\Delta_{ab}X_{ab}.

(15)

Taking the modulus and applying the triangle inequality,

|\partial_{g}\widetilde{C}|\leq 2t\sum_{a,b}|\Delta_{ab}X_{ab}|.

(16)

Since
|X_{ab}|=|\beta_{ab}|^{2}
, this becomes

|\partial_{g}\widetilde{C}|\leq 2t\sum_{a,b}|\beta_{ab}|^{2}|\Delta_{ab}|.

(17)

Finally, applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the probability distribution

p_{ab}=|\beta_{ab}|^{2}
yields

\sum_{a,b}|\beta_{ab}|^{2}|\Delta_{ab}|\leq\left(\sum_{a,b}|\beta_{ab}|^{2}\Delta_{ab}^{\,2}\right)^{1/2}.

(18)

Combining these inequalities with Eq. (
12
) gives

\boxed{|\partial_{g}C(g,t)|\leq\sqrt{F_{Q}^{(g)}(g,t)}.}

(19)

Equation (
19
) establishes a direct connection between entanglement dynamics and quantum metrology. The square root of the QFI determines the local distinguishability scale of neighboring quantum states and therefore the maximum rate at which the underlying state can move in parameter space. The bound shows that concurrence cannot evolve faster than this information-geometric scale.

We now determine the conditions under which Eq. (
19
) is saturated. The first condition comes from Eq. (
14
). Writing the concurrence amplitude in polar form,

\widetilde{C}(g,t)=R(g,t)e^{i\phi(g,t)},\qquad R(g,t)=C(g,t),

(20)

one finds

|\partial_{g}\widetilde{C}|^{2}=(\partial_{g}R)^{2}+R^{2}(\partial_{g}\phi)^{2}.

(21)

Thus
|\partial_{g}C|=|\partial_{g}\widetilde{C}|
if and only if

R(g,t)\,\partial_{g}\phi(g,t)=0.

(22)

Away from zeros of concurrence this reduces to
\partial_{g}\phi=0
. The concurrence amplitude must therefore evolve purely radially in the complex plane. Any parameter-induced rotation of
\widetilde{C}
contributes to motion of the state but not to a change in concurrence.

The next condition comes from saturation of the triangle inequality. Equality requires all nonzero quantities
\Delta_{ab}X_{ab}
to have a common phase,

\arg\!\left[\Delta_{ab}X_{ab}\right]=\arg\!\left[\Delta_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}X_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}\right],

(23)

for every pair of contributing sectors. This is the constructive-interference condition for the linear spectral contributions governing the first derivative of concurrence.

Finally, exact saturation of the final QFI bound also requires saturation of the Cauchy–Schwarz step. This occurs when the active Bell sectors have a common absolute centered frequency,

|\Delta_{ab}|=\Delta_{*},

(24)

for all sectors with
|\beta_{ab}|^{2}\neq 0
. Thus the support of the initial state must lie on a single absolute centered-frequency shell.

The saturation conditions therefore have a transparent physical interpretation. The concurrence amplitude must move radially, the linear spectral contributions
\Delta_{ab}X_{ab}
must interfere constructively, and the active interaction frequencies must have a common absolute spread about the mean. Under these conditions the full distinguishability budget quantified by the QFI is converted into entanglement response, yielding

|\partial_{g}C(g,t)|=\sqrt{F_{Q}^{(g)}(g,t)}.

(25)

This structure is closely related to, but distinct from, the saturation of the curvature bound derived in Ref.
9
. In the curvature problem, the second derivative naturally produces quadratic spectral weights
\Delta_{ab}^{\,2}X_{ab}
, which match the variance appearing in the QFI. In the present speed problem, the first derivative produces linear spectral weights
\Delta_{ab}X_{ab}
, and the final comparison with
\sqrt{F_{Q}}
requires the additional condition that the active sectors share a common
|\Delta_{ab}|
. Thus the speed bound characterizes the first-order response of entanglement, while the curvature bound characterizes its second-order response.

An interesting consequence of the saturation conditions is that saturation is not associated with any particular value of the concurrence itself. The conditions involve only the interaction spectrum and the phase structure of the concurrence amplitude. Consequently, product states, partially entangled states, and highly entangled states may all saturate Eq. (
19
) provided the spectral and phase-alignment conditions are satisfied. The ability of a state to achieve the maximum entanglement speed is therefore determined by the geometry of the interaction dynamics rather than by the amount of entanglement already present.

The speed bound also admits an immediate operational interpretation. For a small perturbation
\delta g
of the interaction strength,

C(g+\delta g,t)=C(g,t)+(\partial_{g}C)\delta g+O(\delta g^{2}).

(26)

Using Eq. (
19
) therefore gives

|C(g+\delta g,t)-C(g,t)|\leq\sqrt{F_{Q}^{(g)}}\,|\delta g|+O(\delta g^{2}).

(27)

Thus the square root of the QFI not only bounds the instantaneous speed of entanglement evolution but also limits the leading-order change in concurrence produced by a small calibration error in the parameter.

Discussion and outlook.—

We have derived a universal bound relating the speed of entanglement evolution to the quantum Fisher information for arbitrary pure two-qubit states evolving under a general nonlocal interaction. The result identifies
\sqrt{F_{Q}}
as the fundamental information-geometric scale governing the first-order response of concurrence to changes in an encoded interaction parameter. In this sense, the same quantity that determines the ultimate precision of parameter estimation also limits the maximum rate at which entanglement can evolve in parameter space.

The bound admits a direct experimental interpretation. Quantum Fisher information has been measured in a variety of platforms including trapped ions, cold atomic ensembles, and superconducting circuits
11
;
3
, while concurrence is routinely employed to characterize Bell-state generation, entangling gates, and two-qubit quantum processors
2
;
5
. Equation (
19
) therefore provides an experimentally accessible connection between metrological sensitivity and the dynamical response of entanglement. In particular, it bounds the leading-order change in concurrence induced by small uncertainties in interaction strength and identifies the dynamical regimes in which all available distinguishability is converted into entanglement response.

Several directions remain open. It would be interesting to determine whether analogous speed limits exist for mixed-state entanglement measures, multipartite entanglement, and open-system dynamics. More generally, the present result raises the possibility that quantum Fisher information may constrain the response of a broader class of quantum resources beyond bipartite entanglement. Establishing such connections could provide a unified information-geometric framework for understanding how quantum resources respond to changes in the parameters that generate them.
