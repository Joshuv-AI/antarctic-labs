Staged surgical management of multiple arterial aneurysms in aneurysms osteoarthritis syndrome with a novel SMAD3 mutation
Authors: Shizhen Qing, Zixin Tang, Rui Wang, Zhecun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the case of a 33-year-old man diagnosed with aneurysms-osteoarthritis syndrome due to a novel SMAD3 mutation (c.864_870dup). The patient exhibited epigastric pain, severe pectus excavatum, and multiple arterial aneurysms. Genetic testing and vascular imaging supported the diagnosis. Surgical intervention was performed in two stages: the first stage involved superior mesenteric artery embolization and ligation of the superior mesenteric artery pseudoaneurysm, whereas the second stage consisted of endovascular stent graft placement for a dissecting aneurysm of the celiac artery. This case provides clinical experience for diagnosis and treatment of aneurysms-osteoarthritis syndrome and highlights the importance of staged surgical intervention.Keywords: Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Smad3 protein, Mutation, Aneurysm, Vascular surgical procedures

Aneurysms-osteoarthritis syndrome (AOS) is a rare hereditary connective tissue disorder characterized by arterial aneurysms, arterial dissections, and early-onset osteoarthritis. It is now recognized as Loeys-Dietz syndrome type 3 (LDS3), caused by pathogenic variants in the SMAD3 gene.1 LDS includes several subtypes caused by mutations in different transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) pathway genes, each with distinct features. According to current classification, LDS1 and LDS2 are caused by mutations in TGFBR1 and TGFBR2, respectively, and are commonly associated with thoracic aortic aneurysms and dissection; LDS3 is associated with SMAD3 mutations; LDS4, related to TGFB2, typically involves aneurysms with arterial tortuosity and abnormal skeletal manifestations.2,3 Compared with other LDS subtypes, AOS often demonstrates a higher burden of arterial aneurysms, highlighting the need for early detection and aggressive vascular management.1,4, 5, 6

In patients presenting with multiple arterial dissections and aneurysms, LDS, vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS), and Marfan syndrome should be differentiated.7 All of which share the hallmark of vascular fragility but differ in etiology and clinical course. vEDS, caused by COL3A1 mutations, is characterized by defective collagen synthesis and a high risk of spontaneous arterial rupture. Marfan syndrome, typically due to FBN1 mutations, presents with progressive aortic root dilation and abnormal skeletal manifestations.8 Accurate diagnosis relies on a combination of detailed clinical assessment, including vascular imaging and genetic testing. Notably, clinical exome sequencing has emerged as a valuable diagnostic tool for identifying SMAD3 mutations associated with LDS type 3 (AOS).9,10

The objective of this case report is to present a rare instance of a 33-year-old man diagnosed with AOS caused by a pathogenic SMAD3 mutation. The patient presented with multiple arterial aneurysms, including a superior mesenteric artery (SMA) pseudoaneurysm, celiac artery dissection, bilateral carotid artery aneurysms, and vertebral artery aneurysms. This case contributes to the growing clinical understanding of AOS and underscores the importance of early genetic diagnosis and multidisciplinary vascular management in this challenging and under-recognized disorder.

Consent was obtained from the patient to publish this report and the accompanying images.

Case report

A 33-year-old man was referred to the hospital with a 1-month history of epigastric distension and pain. He was tall and thin, measuring 183 cm in height and weighing only 38 kg (body mass index, 11 kg/m2). A pulsatile abdominal mass was noted on physical examination, along with joint hypermobility involving the wrists and metacarpophalangeal joints. Family history revealed that his father and two uncles had been surgically treated for arterial aneurysms. Based on the clinical presentation, vEDS was initially suspected.

The patient was managed conservatively with bowel rest and parenteral nutritional support. Preoperative imaging revealed a dissecting aneurysm of the mid-to-distal celiac artery, a pseudoaneurysm of the SMA, and multiple cervical arterial abnormalities, including a 10-mm left external carotid artery aneurysm and right internal carotid artery occlusion. Preoperative computed tomography angiography showed preserved bowel perfusion, with no imaging signs of intestinal ischemia or bowel necrosis. Computed tomography of the chest showed a Haller index of 4.9, indicating severe pectus excavatum (Fig 1). Duplex ultrasound guidance of the extremity arteries showed no abnormal findings.

Following a multidisciplinary discussion, whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed, identifying a novel heterozygous duplication in the SMAD3 gene: c.864_870dup (p.Gly291Thrfs∗22), located in exon 6 of 9 (Fig 2). Taken together, the clinical, genetic, and imaging findings confirmed the diagnosis of AOS. The patient subsequently elected to undergo surgical intervention.

Surgical technique

On January 7, 2025, the first procedure was performed. SMA embolization was carried out via the left brachial artery approach. Three 6 × 200 mm Interlock coils (Boston Scientific Corporation) were deployed into the distal SMA trunk, followed by one 8 × 200 mm and two 8 × 400 mm Interlock coils in the proximal SMA trunk (Fig 3, A). Subsequently, a midline laparotomy was performed to expose the SMA. The SMA root was ligated, and the pseudoaneurysm was successfully resected. Intraoperative evaluation showed no evidence of intestinal ischemia. Following surgery, the patient’s epigastric pain improved significantly. He gradually transitioned from parenteral nutrition to enteral feeding over a 2-week period, ultimately resuming regular oral intake. Bowel function normalized without abdominal distension or diarrhea, and the patient was discharged in stable condition.

Three months later, on April 17, 2025, the patient was readmitted for the second-stage procedure. Celiac artery angiography demonstrated a dissecting aneurysm. A 20 × 400 mm Interlock coil was placed into the false lumen to promote thrombosis, and a 5 × 10 mm VIABAHN stent (W. L. Gore & Associates) was deployed in the true lumen of the celiac trunk. Postoperative imaging confirmed patent celiac trunk flow and preserved mesenteric perfusion (Fig 3, B and C). The patient recovered uneventfully and was again discharged in good condition. He has undergone outpatient follow-up for 1 year and reported no significant discomfort.

Hemodynamic changes were continuously assessed during treatment. Adequate intestinal perfusion and collateral circulation were confirmed before embolization and resection of the SMA pseudoaneurysm (Fig 3, D). After the first procedure, intestinal perfusion was reassessed and was found to originate from a collateral junction supplied by the celiac artery (Fig 3, E). The third assessment after endovascular treatment of the celiac artery dissecting aneurysm demonstrated stable intestinal blood supply and the celiac artery providing sufficient perfusion to the intestine (Fig 3, F).

Discussion

Our report presents a rare case of AOS caused by a novel SMAD3 mutation, with simultaneous involvement of the SMA and celiac artery.1 AOS is difficult to diagnose due to its variable features,11 and WES remains essential for confirmation. In this case, WES enabled early diagnosis based on the patient’s vascular findings and family history.12 Treatment was particularly challenging given the coexistence of two major visceral aneurysms.13 Reported experience with visceral aneurysm management in AOS/LDS3 remains limited, and both open and endovascular approaches have been described. Available literature suggests that treatment is individualized, with no standardized strategy for complex visceral arterial involvement.14 We chose a staged approach to reduce the risk of intestinal ischemia. This case offers valuable insights for both diagnosis and surgical management of complex AOS presentations.

Regarding the SMA lesion, we considered both revascularization and ligation. Given the vascular fragility associated with SMAD3 mutations, SMA reconstruction was considered high risk,3 with potential anastomotic failure, hemorrhage, dissection, or graft occlusion. Hemodynamic assessment demonstrated adequate collateral circulation to maintain intestinal perfusion; therefore, we performed direct SMA ligation. To reduce intraoperative bleeding, proximal and distal embolization of the SMA trunk was completed before pseudoaneurysm resection. Postoperatively, despite hemodynamic changes, the patient developed no intestinal ischemia.

A key decision was whether to treat the celiac artery dissection at the time of SMA pseudoaneurysm repair. Imaging suggested that after SMA ligation, the celiac trunk would become the primary source of mesenteric perfusion. Simultaneous open repair of both lesions would have required extensive exposure at a high anatomic level, increasing surgical trauma and risk. Moreover, endovascular stenting of the celiac artery during the same procedure could have led to rupture or thrombosis, potentially resulting in bowel ischemia. For these reasons, we chose a staged approach to reduce procedural risk and ensure adequate intestinal blood supply throughout both interventions. Postoperative hemodynamic analysis indicated that the current intestinal collateral blood flow originated from a single collateral junction, which was supplied by the celiac artery. Given the vascular fragility in LDS, the patient should be followed up to monitor the celiac artery. To optimize long-term outcomes and quality of life, patients with AOS/LDS3 require multidisciplinary follow-up, including cardiology, genetics, and orthopedics.

Considering the presence of a carotid artery aneurysm and possible additional arterial lesions, we emphasize the importance of strict blood pressure control and regular imaging surveillance. Genetic testing is also recommended for the patient’s family members to enable early detection and preventive care. Intracranial aneurysm screening may be considered in LDS, especially with extensive vascular disease or family history.5,15 Looking ahead, gene-editing technologies may offer promising avenues for targeted therapy in monogenic aortopathies like AOS.16

This case highlights the value of early genetic testing, multidisciplinary planning, and staged vascular intervention in patients with AOS. Given the vascular fragility in AOS, individualized operative strategies and close postoperative surveillance are essential to optimize outcomes.
