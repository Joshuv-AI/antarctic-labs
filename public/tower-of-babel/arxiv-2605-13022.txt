A necessary condition for cylindrical curves in terms of curvature and torsion
Rafael López

Abstract
. We establish necessary conditions for a regular curve to lie on a circular cylinder in terms of its curvature κ \kappa and torsion τ \tau . By identifying a fundamental function ψ = sin 2 ⁡ α \psi=\sin^{2}\alpha , representing the squared sine of the angle between the tangent vector and the axis of the cylinder, we reduce the geometric inclusion problem to a compatibility condition between an explicit eighth-degree polynomial equation and a differential equation for ψ \psi . This approach yields a single ODE involving only κ \kappa and τ \tau that governs the inclusion of the curve in the cylinder. The robustness of this framework is demonstrated through specific examples of cylindrical curves. Furthermore, we analyze the case of curves with constant curvature κ 0 \kappa_{0} , obtaining an explicit ODE for the torsion. Remarkably, we prove that if κ 0 = 1 / ρ \kappa_{0}=1/\rho , this equation admits an explicit, exact solution for τ \tau .

1.
Introduction and statement of the problem

In this paper, we investigate the following question:

Problem
.

Given a circular cylinder
\mathcal{C}
in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, how can one characterize a regular curve
\mathbf{r}\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
lying on
\mathcal{C}
in terms of its curvature
\kappa
and torsion
\tau
?

An answer to this problem has interesting applications in computer vision
[
5
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
11
]
. Suppose that we know the trajectory of a point in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by tracking its coordinates at discrete positions. From this information, we can determine the curvature and the torsion of the curve that describes the path of these points. The question to be answered is whether such a set of points is contained in some circular cylinder in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, or in a circular cylinder with a specific radius. The main challenge is to deduce whether a curve lies on a two-dimensional surface solely from its one-dimensional intrinsic invariants
\kappa
and
\tau
. In general, a regular curve
\mathbf{r}\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
that is not a straight line has a well-defined curvature
\kappa>0
and torsion
\tau
. Both geometric quantities uniquely determine the curve
\mathbf{r}
up to a rigid motion of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Although the formulation of this problem is simple, its solution, if any, is surprisingly complex.
If we replace the cylinder
\mathcal{C}
with another surface
S
, the problem has been fully solved for only two specific surfaces: the plane and the sphere. Indeed, if
S
is a plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then a curve
\mathbf{r}
lies in
S
if and only if its torsion
\tau
vanishes identically. On the other hand, if
S
is a sphere of radius
r>0
, then a non-planar curve
\mathbf{r}
is contained in
S
if and only if

(1)

\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{4}\tau^{2}}=r^{2},

where the prime
(^{\prime})
stands for the derivative with respect to the arc length. This is a first-order ODE in
\kappa
. If the radius of the sphere is unknown, then
\mathbf{r}
is contained in some sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
if and only if
\kappa
and
\tau
satisfy the second-order ODE in
\kappa
given by

(2)

\frac{\tau}{\kappa}-\left(\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa^{2}\tau}\right)^{\prime}=0.

The proofs of (
1
) and (
2
) appear in standard textbooks such as
[
4
,
12
,
17
]
. For a rigorous global formulation and an explicit integration of these spherical conditions, we refer to the classical works of Wong
[
19
,
20
]
. Extensions of (
1
) and (
2
) to other ambient spaces for curves contained in totally geodesic and umbilical surfaces have been recently developed
[
2
,
3
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.

We emphasize that our problem fundamentally differs from the study of curves on a surface
S
via their geodesic curvature and geodesic torsion. Unlike the
standard curvature
\kappa
and torsion
\tau
, which are intrinsic invariants of the curve itself,
the geodesic quantities depend on the
specific embedding of the curve within the surface.

Returning to the initial problem regarding circular cylinders, few results have been obtained in the literature
[
9
,
10
,
16
]
.
In
[
9
]
, the authors attempted to characterize cylindrical containment by computing the resultants of the differential invariants. However, their method typically leads to a resultant of degree
12
(or higher), resulting in a symbolic expression of such overwhelming complexity that it becomes analytically unmanageable.
The problem was also addressed in the early work of Ko
[
10
]
, who established necessary and sufficient conditions by formulating a system of coupled differential-algebraic equations that depends on auxiliary angular variables. This approach does not achieve the complete algebraic elimination of these variables into a single, explicit polynomial in terms of the intrinsic invariants
\kappa
and
\tau
.

More recently, Starostin and van der Heijden
[
16
]
employed a kinematic approach based on moving frames, relating the intrinsic invariants to an angle
\xi
(measuring the rotation between the Frenet frame and a secondary frame), which also emerges as the root of an eighth-degree polynomial in
\kappa,\tau
, and
\kappa^{\prime}
.

The main result of this paper is presented in Section
2
. We prove that if a regular curve lies on a circular cylinder of radius
\rho
, then
\kappa
and
\tau
satisfy a specific ordinary differential equation (Theorem
2.4
). This equation is obtained as follows. Let
\alpha
be the angle that the curve makes with the axis of
the cylinder, and let
\psi=\sin^{2}\alpha
. We prove that
\psi
must simultaneously satisfy an
8
th-degree polynomial equation
\mathcal{P}(\psi)=0
and a differential equation
\mathcal{Q}(\psi,\psi^{\prime})=0
.
The compatibility condition between these two equations, obtained by eliminating
\psi
, yields a single ODE,
\mathcal{E}(\rho,\kappa,\kappa^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime\prime},\tau,\tau^{\prime})=0
. This resultant equation represents
the fundamental constraint that the curvature and torsion must satisfy for the curve to be contained in the cylinder.

In Section
3
, we show how our result can be utilized to establish necessary conditions for curves with specific curvature or torsion profiles to lie on a circular cylinder. A particularly remarkable case arises when the curvature is assumed to be a constant
\kappa_{0}
. In this scenario, the general ODE
\mathcal{E}=0
is explicitly obtained (Theorem
3.2
). We prove that, under the condition
\kappa_{0}=1/\rho
, this equation admits a completely explicit, exact solution for the torsion (Corollary
3.4
). Finally, in Section
4
, we illustrate the versatility of our method by applying it to classical examples, such as Viviani’s curve.

2.
Characterization via the Frenet frame

In this section, we establish the main result of the paper (Theorem
2.4
). The strategy of the proof is constructive: we will use the Frenet equations to algebraically eliminate the direction cosines of the axis of the cylinder, reducing the geometric condition of being a cylindrical curve to an ordinary differential equation and a polynomial equation of degree
8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a circular cylinder of radius
\rho>0
whose axis has a unit direction vector
\mathbf{a}
. The cylinder is given by

(3)

\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}-\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}\rangle^{2}=\rho^{2}\}.

Let
\mathbf{r}
be a regular curve lying on
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathbf{r}\colon I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{C}
,
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{r}(s)
, where
s
is the arc length parameter.
Let
\{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{b}\}
be the Frenet frame of
\mathbf{r}
. Let
\kappa
and
\tau
be its curvature and torsion, respectively. Throughout this section, we assume that the curve
is biregular, that is,
\kappa>0
and
\tau\neq 0
everywhere on
I
, to
ensure that the Frenet frame is well-defined and to avoid singularities
in our computations. The Frenet equations are

\begin{split}\mathbf{t}^{\prime}&=\kappa\mathbf{n}\\
\mathbf{n}^{\prime}&=-\kappa\mathbf{t}+\tau\mathbf{b},\\
\mathbf{b}^{\prime}&=-\tau\mathbf{n}.\end{split}

Let us express the axis
\mathbf{a}
as a linear combination of
\{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{b}\}
using its direction cosines,

(4)

\mathbf{a}=\cos\alpha\,\mathbf{t}+\cos\beta\,\mathbf{n}+\cos\gamma\,\mathbf{b}.

By the Frenet equations, the derivatives of the direction cosines are

(5)

\begin{split}(\cos\alpha)^{\prime}&=\kappa\cos\beta,\\
(\cos\beta)^{\prime}&=-\kappa\cos\alpha+\tau\cos\gamma,\\
(\cos\gamma)^{\prime}&=-\tau\cos\beta.\end{split}

Differentiating the equation

(6)

\|\mathbf{r}\|^{2}-\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle^{2}=\rho^{2}

with respect to
s
, we deduce that

(7)

\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{a}\rangle=0,

that is,

\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r}-\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle\mathbf{a}\rangle=0.

This implies that
\mathbf{r}-\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle\mathbf{a}
is a linear combination of
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{b}
. Thus, there are two functions of
s
,
A(s)
and
B(s)
, such that

(8)

\mathbf{r}-\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle\mathbf{a}=A\mathbf{n}+B\mathbf{b}.

Remark 2.1
.

While the cylindrical condition (
8
) is formally identical to the spherical relation
\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{c}_{0}=\alpha\mathbf{n}+\beta\mathbf{b}
, a fundamental geometric difference makes the cylindrical problem significantly harder. For a sphere, the center
\mathbf{c}_{0}
is a fixed point (
\mathbf{c}_{0}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
), yielding the relatively simple ODE (
2
). In contrast, the term
\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle\mathbf{a}
in (
8
) is not constant. This introduces an additional degree of freedom, which is precisely why the cylindrical characterization escalates into a highly difficult problem.

Multiplying (
8
) by
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{b}
, and using (
4
), we obtain the following expressions for
A
and
B
,

(9)

\begin{split}A&=\langle\mathbf{r}-\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{n}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{n}\rangle-\cos\beta\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle,\\
B&=\langle\mathbf{r}-\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{b}\rangle-\cos\gamma\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle.\end{split}

By using (
7
) and the definitions of
A
and
B
in (
9
), we obtain the derivatives of
A
and
B
, namely,

(10)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\kappa\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{t}\rangle+\tau\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{b}\rangle-\cos\alpha\cos\beta-(-\kappa\cos\alpha+\tau\cos\gamma)\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau B-\cos\alpha\cos\beta.

(11)

\displaystyle B^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tau\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{n}\rangle-\cos\gamma\cos\alpha+\tau\cos\beta\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tau A-\cos\gamma\cos\alpha.

Differentiating (
7
), and using (
4
) again, yields

0=1+\kappa\langle\mathbf{n},\mathbf{r}\rangle-\cos^{2}\alpha+\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{a}\rangle\kappa\cos\alpha=\kappa A+1-\cos^{2}\alpha.

This gives

(12)

A=-\frac{\sin^{2}\alpha}{\kappa}.

From (
10
), we obtain
B
, namely,

(13)

B=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(A^{\prime}+\cos\alpha\cos\beta\right).

We now replace the expressions for
A
and
B
into (
11
), obtaining

\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\left(A^{\prime}+\cos\alpha\cos\beta\right)\right)+\tau A+\cos\gamma\cos\alpha=0.

Equivalently,

(14)

-\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\sin^{2}\alpha}{\kappa}\right)\right)+\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\cos\alpha\cos\beta}{\tau}\right)-\tau\frac{\sin^{2}\alpha}{\kappa}+\cos\gamma\cos\alpha=0.

In this equation, all three direction cosines appear. The next step is to express this equation in terms of only one direction cosine, specifically, of
\cos\alpha
.

First, using the fact that
\mathbf{a}
is a unit vector, we can write

(15)

\cos\gamma=\pm\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}\alpha-\cos^{2}\beta}.

The local choice of the sign depends on the orientation of the curve, but this ambiguity will not affect our subsequent computations since the equation will be eventually squared.

Remark 2.2
.

Let us observe that combining equations (
10
) and (
11
) does not yield any further information. Indeed, from both equations, we obtain

-\cos\alpha(A\cos\beta+B\cos\gamma)=AA^{\prime}+BB^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(A^{2}+B^{2})^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{2})^{\prime}=0,

because
\rho
is constant. However, the left-hand side of this identity is trivially
0
by the definitions of
A
and
B
in (
8
) together with (
5
).

From (
8
), we have
\rho^{2}=A^{2}+B^{2}
and using the values of
A
and
B
in (
12
) and (
13
), respectively, we obtain

\rho^{2}=A^{2}+B^{2}=\left(\frac{\sin^{2}\alpha}{\kappa}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\sin^{2}\alpha}{\kappa}\right)-\cos\alpha\cos\beta\right)^{2}.

From the first equation of (
5
), we have

(16)

\cos\beta=\frac{(\cos\alpha)^{\prime}}{\kappa}.

Thus, the above identity becomes

(17)

\rho^{2}=\frac{\sin^{4}\alpha}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left[\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\sin^{2}\alpha}{\kappa}\right)+\frac{\alpha^{\prime}\sin(2\alpha)}{2\kappa}\right]^{2}.

We introduce the main variable that will govern the rest of our study.

Definition 2.3
.

We define the
fundamental function

\psi
as

(18)

\psi=\sin^{2}\alpha.

Notice that
\psi
cannot be identically
0
because in such a case,
\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{a}\rangle^{2}=1
and
\mathbf{r}
is a straight line. Analogously, if
\psi
is identically
1
, then
\langle\mathbf{t},\mathbf{a}\rangle=0
for all
\in I
. Then
\mathbf{r}
is a planar curve, and
\tau
would be
0
, which was initially discarded.

Using the function
\psi
, equation (
17
) becomes

(19)

\rho^{2}=\frac{\psi^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left[\frac{3\psi^{\prime}}{2\kappa}-\frac{\psi\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa^{2}}\right]^{2}.

In particular,
\psi^{2}\leq\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}
. This gives

(20)

\psi^{\prime}=\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{\psi\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}\pm\tau\sqrt{\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-\psi^{2}}\right).

The presence of the
\pm
sign arises from taking the square root in (
19
). Equation (
20
) (or (
19
)) is the ODE
\mathcal{Q}=0
mentioned in the Introduction. For the subsequent algebraic manipulation, this choice of sign is not critical, as we will eventually square the expressions to eliminate the radical.

Let

P=\sqrt{\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-\psi^{2}}.

Differentiating (
20
), and using the expression for
\psi^{\prime}
in (
20
) yields

(21)

\begin{split}\psi^{\prime\prime}&=\frac{2\left(5\rho^{2}\kappa^{3}\tau\kappa^{\prime}-\psi\kappa^{\prime 2}P-\kappa\psi\left(4\tau\psi\kappa^{\prime}-3\kappa^{\prime\prime}P\right)-\kappa^{2}\psi\left(2\tau^{2}P+3\psi\tau^{\prime}\right)+3\rho^{2}\kappa^{4}\tau^{\prime}\right)}{9\kappa^{2}P}.\end{split}

We write Eq. (
14
) in terms of the function
\psi
. From (
5
) and the definition of
\psi
in (
18
), we have

\begin{split}\cos\beta&=\frac{(\cos\alpha)^{\prime}}{\kappa}=-\frac{\psi^{\prime}}{2\kappa\sqrt{1-\psi}},\\
\cos\gamma&=\pm\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}\alpha-\cos^{2}\beta}=\pm\frac{\sqrt{4\kappa^{2}\psi(1-\psi)-\psi^{\prime 2}}}{2\kappa\sqrt{1-\psi}}.\end{split}

Then (
14
) becomes

\begin{split}0&=-4\tau\psi\kappa^{\prime 2}+\kappa\left(5\tau\kappa^{\prime}\psi^{\prime}+2\psi\left(\tau\kappa^{\prime\prime}-\kappa^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}\right)\right)\\
&+\kappa^{2}\left(\pm\tau^{2}\sqrt{4\kappa^{2}(1-\psi)\psi-\psi^{\prime 2}}+3\tau^{\prime}\psi^{\prime}-3\tau\psi^{\prime\prime}-2\tau^{3}\psi\right).\end{split}

In this equation, we substitute the values of
\psi^{\prime}
and
\psi^{\prime\prime}
given in (
20
) and (
21
), respectively. Isolating the term with the radical, we obtain

\frac{\psi^{2}\kappa^{\prime}}{P}+\kappa\tau\psi=\pm\kappa\sqrt{-\frac{2P\tau\psi\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}-\frac{\psi^{2}\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{2}}-\kappa^{2}\left(\rho^{2}\tau^{2}+9(\psi-1)\psi\right)+\tau^{2}\psi^{2}}.

By squaring both sides, the ambiguity of the
\pm
sign is naturally eliminated. Simplifying, we arrive first at

0=2\sqrt{\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-\psi^{2}}\rho^{2}\kappa\tau\psi\kappa^{\prime}+\rho^{2}\psi^{2}\kappa^{\prime 2}+\kappa^{2}\left(\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-\psi^{2}\right)\left(\rho^{2}\tau^{2}+9(\psi-1)\psi\right).

and, finally, squaring to eliminate the radical, we obtain

(22)

0=4\rho^{4}\kappa^{2}\tau^{2}\psi^{2}\kappa^{\prime 2}(\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-\psi^{2})-\left[\rho^{2}\psi^{2}\kappa^{\prime 2}+\kappa^{2}(\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-\psi^{2})(\rho^{2}\tau^{2}+9\psi(\psi-1))\right]^{2}.

This is a polynomial equation of degree
8
in
\psi
which can be written as

0=\mathcal{P}(\psi)=\sum_{n=0}^{8}P_{n}\psi^{n},

where the coefficients
P_{n}
depend only on
\kappa
,
\tau
, and
\kappa^{\prime}
, without involving higher-order derivatives. When we put
\psi
into (
20
), then it will appear
\tau^{\prime}
and
\kappa^{\prime\prime}
. This ODE is the desired equation involving only
\kappa
and
\tau
. Thus, the previous arguments have shown the following result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{r}(s)
be a regular curve parametrized by arc length with curvature
\kappa(s)>0
and
non-zero torsion
\tau(s)
. If the curve lies on a circular cylinder of radius
\rho
,
then
\kappa
and
\tau
must satisfy an ordinary differential equation

(23)

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(\rho,\kappa,\kappa^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime\prime},\tau,\tau^{\prime})=0,

obtained by
substituting the real roots
\psi
of the eighth-degree polynomial equation
(
22
) into the differential equation (
20
).

Remark 2.5
.

It is worth noting that the final ODE
\mathcal{E}=0
is obtained by eliminating
\psi
between equations
\mathcal{P}=0
and
\mathcal{Q}=0
. Conceptually, if
\{\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{8}\}
are the roots of the polynomial equation, the condition
\mathcal{E}=0
can be understood as the vanishing of the product:

\prod_{i=1}^{8}\mathcal{Q}(\psi_{i},\psi_{i}^{\prime})=0.

This ensures that the resulting ODE is a single, well-defined expression that is independent of the specific root chosen, reflecting the symmetric nature of the elimination process.

From an applied perspective, such as in 3D shape recognition or spatial curve tracking, reducing the geometric problem to the polynomial equation (
22
) is theoretically highly advantageous. For a given point on
a trajectory, the coefficients evaluate to real scalars, transforming the characterization into a one-dimensional root-finding task. However, it is important to acknowledge the numerical challenges, especially in extracting the derivatives of
\kappa
and
\tau
in order to manage and numerically solve the ODE
\mathcal{E}=0
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\mathbf{r}
be a curve with curvature
\kappa>0
and torsion
\tau\not=0
. If
\kappa
and
\tau
satisfy the differential equation (
20
), then the curve
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
whose curvature is
\kappa
and torsion
-\tau
also satisfies (
20
).

Proof.

First, observe that the polynomial equation (
22
), which restricts the values of
\psi
, depends on the torsion only through its square,
\tau^{2}
. Therefore, substituting
-\tau
for
\tau
leaves the equation invariant, meaning that both curves share the same valid functions for
\psi
.

Second, replacing
\tau
with
-\tau
in the differential equation (
20
) simply changes the
\pm
sign to
\mp
. Since the equation inherently accounts for both signs, the relation holds for
-\tau
whenever it holds for
\tau
.
∎

Geometrically, the curve
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
corresponds to a reflection of
\mathbf{r}
across a plane, a rigid transformation that naturally preserves its inclusion in a circular cylinder of the same radius.

It is worth noting that Theorem
2.4
provides a necessary condition for a curve to lie on a circular cylinder. The converse problem, establishing sufficiency, is significantly more delicate. Since our constructive proof relies on differentiating the geometric constraints and squaring equations to eliminate radicals, the reverse process would inherently introduce arbitrary constants of integration.

It is worth making two further observations.

Remark 2.7
.

In contrast to previous approaches, our method is strictly algebraic
and geometrically direct because we focus on the function
\psi=\sin^{2}\alpha
,
which represents the inclination of the tangent vector relative to the cylinder’s fixed axis.
This allows us to explicitly eliminate the direction cosines
without invoking secondary curves.

Remark 2.8
.

The condition (
1
) for a regular curve to be spherical can be recovered as a limiting case of Theorem
2.4
. Specifically, if a curve is contained in a sphere of radius
\rho
, the cylinder axis
\mathbf{a}
is no longer uniquely defined, and we may consider that
\mathbf{a}
reduces to a point. In particular, in the limiting case we have
\alpha=\pi/2
. Consequently,
\psi\equiv 1
identically. Substituting this into equation (
22
), the expression reduces to:

\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{2}\tau^{2}}=\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-1,

which coincides with the classical spherical condition (
1
). Alternatively, applying
\psi\equiv 1
(and thus
\psi^{\prime}=0
) to (
19
), we obtain:

\rho^{2}=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\frac{\kappa^{\prime 2}}{\kappa^{4}},

which again recovers (
1
).

3.
Special classes of cylindrical curves

In this section, we apply the general characterization established in Theorem
2.4
to specific families of spatial curves. We first verify that our results consistently recover the classical geometry of circular helices. Subsequently, we explore the more complex cases of generalized helices and curves with constant curvature.

3.1.
Circular helices

The most elementary example of a cylindrical curve is the circular helix. A circular helix is characterized by the fact that both
\kappa
and
\tau
are constant. The following result is known, but we recover it from Theorem
2.4
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{r}
be a curve with constant curvature
\kappa_{0}>0
and constant torsion
\tau_{0}\not=0
. If
\mathbf{r}
lies on a circular cylinder
\mathcal{C}
of radius
\rho
, then

(24)

\rho=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\kappa_{0}^{2}+\tau_{0}^{2}}.

Proof.

If
\kappa_{0}
and
\tau_{0}
are constants, then all coefficients in the polynomial equation (
22
) are constant; hence, the function
\psi
must be a constant root. From (
19
), we have
\psi=\rho\kappa_{0}
. Identities (
5
) imply that
\cos\beta=0
and
\cos\gamma
is constant. Substituting these into (
14
), we obtain

\psi=\frac{\kappa_{0}^{2}}{\kappa_{0}^{2}+\tau_{0}^{2}}.

Finally, using the relation
\rho=\psi/\kappa_{0}
, we get (
24
).
∎

3.2.
Generalized helices (Lancret curves)

A natural generalization of the circular helix is the Lancret curve (or generalized helix), characterized by the fact that the ratio
\tau/\kappa=a
is constant. For such curves, our Theorem
2.4
provides a specific necessary condition for cylindrical inclusion. By substituting
\tau=a\kappa
into (
20
) and (
22
), these necessary conditions become

(25)

\psi^{\prime}=\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{\psi\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}\pm a\kappa\sqrt{\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-\psi^{2}}\right),

and

(26)

\pm 2a\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}\psi\kappa^{\prime}\sqrt{\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-\psi^{2}}=\rho^{2}\psi^{2}\kappa^{\prime 2}+\kappa^{2}(\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-\psi^{2})(a^{2}\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}+9\psi(\psi-1)).

Both equations impose strict constraints on the curvature
\kappa(s)
for any Lancret curve that lies on a circular cylinder.

This is in great contrast to the case of a Lancret curve lying on a sphere, where the ODE (
1
) can be explicitly integrated in terms of elementary functions
[
6
]
.

3.3.
Curves with constant curvature

A more intriguing question arises when the curvature is constant (
\kappa=\kappa_{0}
) but the torsion
\tau(s)
is allowed to vary. Unlike the previous case, such a curve is not necessarily a circular helix. The following result establishes a remarkable necessary condition expressed only in terms of the torsion of the curve, just as required in the initial problem. In other words, we are able to make the differential equation (
23
) of Theorem
2.4
explicit.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{r}(s)
be a regular curve constrained to a cylinder of radius
\rho
. If the curvature is a non-zero constant
\kappa(s)=\kappa_{0}
, then one of the following holds:

(1)

\mathbf{r}
is a circular helix.

(2)

The torsion is not constant and it must satisfy the following first-order differential equation:

(27)

\frac{\rho^{4}\tau^{\prime 2}}{9-4\rho^{2}\tau^{2}}=\rho^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\rho^{2}\tau^{2}}{9}\mp\frac{1}{6}\sqrt{9-4\rho^{2}\tau^{2}}.

In particular, it is necessary that
|\tau|\leq\frac{3}{2\rho}
.

Proof.

Assuming
\kappa(s)=\kappa_{0}
, the polynomial equation (
22
) simplifies to

(\rho^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}-\psi^{2})(\rho^{2}\tau^{2}+9\psi(\psi-1))=0.

If the first factor vanishes, then
\psi=\rho\kappa_{0}
. This proves that
\psi
is constant.
By the definition of
\psi
, we have that
\alpha
is constant. From (
15
) and (
16
), we deduce
\cos\beta=0
and
\cos\gamma=\pm\sin\alpha
. Finally, equation (
14
) becomes
\tau\psi\pm\kappa_{0}\sin\alpha\cos\alpha=0
. This proves that
\tau
is constant because
\psi\not=0
.

If the second factor vanishes, then

\tau^{2}=\frac{9}{\rho^{2}}\psi(1-\psi).

This gives

(28)

\psi=\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{1}{6}\sqrt{9-4\rho^{2}\tau^{2}}

and, consequently,

(29)

\psi^{\prime}=\mp\frac{2\rho^{2}\tau\tau^{\prime}}{3\sqrt{9-4\rho^{2}\tau^{2}}}.

On the other hand, since
\kappa
is constant, equation (
20
) is

\psi^{\prime}=\pm\frac{2}{3}\tau\sqrt{\rho^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}-\psi^{2}}.

Equating this expression with (
29
), and assuming
\tau\neq 0
(thus excluding planar curves), we cancel the common factor
\frac{2}{3}\tau
, to obtain

\mp\frac{\rho^{2}\tau^{\prime}}{\sqrt{9-4\rho^{2}\tau^{2}}}=\pm\sqrt{\rho^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}-\psi^{2}}.

Squaring both sides, and using the expression for
\psi
in (
28
), we get (
27
).
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\tau(s)
be the torsion of a curve lying on a cylinder of constant radius
\rho
, and let
\kappa_{0}
be a non-zero constant. Then the general solution
\tau(s)
of (
27
) is given by the elliptic integral equation

(30)

\pm\frac{s-s_{0}}{3\rho}=\int_{u_{0}}^{\sqrt{9-4\rho^{2}\tau(s)^{2}}}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{(9-x^{2})\left(36\rho^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}-9\mp 6x-x^{2}\right)}},

where
s_{0}
is an initial arc length parameter and
u_{0}=\sqrt{9-4\rho^{2}\tau(s_{0})^{2}}
.

Proof.

Introduce the change of variable
u(s)=\sqrt{9-4\rho^{2}\tau(s)^{2}}
, which implies
\rho^{2}\tau^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(9-u^{2})
. Then (
27
) becomes

\frac{\rho^{2}u^{\prime 2}}{4(9-u^{2})}=\rho^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{9-u^{2}}{36}\mp\frac{u}{6}.

Simplifying the right-hand side and isolating
u^{\prime 2}
, we obtain the following first-order differential equation

u^{\prime 2}=\frac{9-u^{2}}{9\rho^{2}}\left(36\rho^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}-9\mp 6u-u^{2}\right).

Taking the square root and separating the variables
u
and
s
, we arrive at

\frac{du}{\sqrt{(9-u^{2})\left(36\rho^{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}-9\mp 6u-u^{2}\right)}}=\pm\frac{ds}{3\rho}.

This proves the result.
∎

Although (
30
) is difficult to solve in full generality, a particular case is of interest, namely,
\rho\kappa_{0}=1
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Under the condition
\kappa_{0}=1/\rho
, the differential equation (
27
) for the torsion
\tau(s)
admits an explicit, exact solution. Specifically, let
Y(s)=Ce^{\pm\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\rho}s}
, where
C>0
is a constant of integration depending on the initial conditions. Then the torsion of the curve is given by

\tau(s)=\pm\frac{6\sqrt{2}}{\rho}\frac{\sqrt{Y(s)}\big|Y(s)-1\big|}{Y(s)^{2}+6Y(s)+1}.

Proof.

Imposing the condition
\kappa_{0}=1/\rho
, the integral in the right-hand side of (
30
) reduces to

I=\int\frac{dx}{(3-x)\sqrt{x^{2}+12x+27}},

where
x=\sqrt{9-4\rho^{2}\tau^{2}}
is restricted to the interval
[0,3]
.
To evaluate this integral, we apply the substitution
v=\frac{1}{3-x}
, which gives

I=\int\frac{dv}{\sqrt{72v^{2}-18v+1}}=\frac{1}{6\sqrt{2}}\ln\left|144v-18+12\sqrt{2}\sqrt{72v^{2}-18v+1}\right|.

Reverting the substitution
v=\frac{1}{3-x}
, we obtain

\pm\frac{s-s_{0}}{3\rho}=\frac{1}{6\sqrt{2}}\ln\left|\frac{144-18(3-x)+12\sqrt{2}\sqrt{x^{2}+12x+27}}{3-x}\right|\Bigg|_{x=u_{0}}^{x=u(s)},

After some manipulations, we absorb the initial conditions into a single positive integration constant
C
. Then the
solution takes the exponential form:

\frac{15+3x+2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{x^{2}+12x+27}}{3-x}=Ce^{\pm\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\rho}s}=Y(s).

We can rearrange this expression to isolate the square root and square both sides, yielding a quadratic equation in
x
:

\left[Y(s)(3-x)-3(x+5)\right]^{2}=8(x^{2}+12x+27).

Simplifying, we obtain

(Y^{2}+6Y+1)x^{2}-6(Y-1)^{2}x+9(Y^{2}-10Y+1)=0.

The discriminant
\Delta
of this quadratic equation is a perfect square, namely,
\Delta=(48Y)^{2}
. The roots of the equation are
x=\frac{6(Y-1)^{2}\pm 48Y}{2(Y^{2}+6Y+1)}
. One of them is
x=3
, which implies
\tau=0
. This case is discarded. The other root is

x(s)=3\frac{Y^{2}-10Y+1}{Y^{2}+6Y+1}.

Substituting this form of
x(s)
into the definition
\rho^{2}\tau^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(9-x^{2})
, we conclude the proof.
∎

4.
Explicit examples of cylindrical curves

In this final section, we show the robustness of the characterization presented in Theorem
2.4
by applying it to some of the most famous curves lying on circular cylinders.

4.1.
Planar curves (ellipse)

Suppose that
\mathbf{r}
is an ellipse, for which we know its curvature
\kappa
and its torsion,
\tau=0
. If
\kappa
is constant,
\kappa(s)=\kappa_{0}>0
, then
\mathbf{r}
is a circle of radius
1/\kappa_{0}
, hence it is included in a circular cylinder of radius
1/\kappa_{0}
. Suppose from now on that
\mathbf{r}
is not a circle, that is,
\kappa
is not constant. From (
20
), we deduce

\frac{\psi^{\prime}}{\psi}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}.

Integrating yields

(31)

\psi(s)=c\kappa(s)^{2/3},

where
c
is an integration constant. We calculate the value of
c
by evaluating
\kappa
at the vertex of the major axis that has the greatest height, where the tangent vector is perpendicular to the axis of the cylinder, meaning
\alpha=\pi/2
. This gives
\psi=1
. Substituting this into (
31
), we find
c=\kappa_{\max}^{-2/3}
. Then (
31
) becomes

\psi(s)=\left(\frac{\kappa(s)}{\kappa_{\max}}\right)^{2/3}.

By substituting (
31
) into (
22
), we obtain

\frac{c}{9}\kappa^{-4/3}\kappa^{\prime 2}=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}(1-c\kappa^{2/3})(\rho^{2}\kappa^{2}-c^{2}\kappa^{4/3}).

We can explicitly recover the radius of the cylinder by evaluating this equation at the minimum
\kappa_{\min}
of
\kappa
. At such a point, the left-hand side is zero. In the right-hand side, the first factor vanishes when
\kappa_{min}=c^{-3/2}=\kappa_{\max}
. This implies that the initial curve is a circle. This case was discarded. Thus, the second factor must vanish, yielding the radius of
\mathcal{C}
:

(32)

\rho=c\kappa_{\min}^{-1/3}=\kappa_{\max}^{-2/3}\kappa_{\min}^{-1/3}.

This intrinsic deduction is in perfect agreement with classical geometry. Recalling that the extreme curvatures of an ellipse with major semi-axis
a
and minor semi-axis
b
are
\kappa_{\max}=a/b^{2}
and
\kappa_{\min}=b/a^{2}
, respectively, substituting these classical values into (
32
) yields
\rho=b
. This confirms that the minor semi-axis of the elliptical section corresponds precisely to the radius of the cylinder.

4.2.
Viviani’s curve

The Viviani curve is defined as the intersection of a sphere of radius
r=2\rho
and a circular cylinder of radius
\rho
, where the axis of the cylinder passes through the center of the sphere. This curve provides a sophisticated test case because its curvature and torsion must simultaneously satisfy the spherical characterization (
1
) and our cylindrical constraint (
22
) as well as the ODE (
20
).

First, we address the spherical condition. Given the pair
(\kappa,\tau)
, the curve lies on a sphere of radius
r
if and only if it satisfies (
1
). Thus, the function

\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}+\left(\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa^{2}\tau}\right)^{2}

must be constant. If this constant is
r^{2}
, with
r>0
, we can write
\tau
as

\tau=\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa\sqrt{r^{2}\kappa^{2}-1}}.

On the other hand, if the curve is included in a cylinder of radius
\rho=r/2
, then
\kappa
and
\tau
must satisfy the ODE (
23
). To demonstrate the robustness of the algebraic constraint and the identification algorithm, we can verify this for an explicit parametrization. We consider the standard parametrization of the Viviani curve, where
\rho
denotes the radius of the generating cylinder,

\mathbf{r}(t)=\rho\left(1+\cos t,\,\sin t,\,2\sin(t/2)\right).

See
[
6
]
. While the prime denotes the derivative with respect to the arc-length
s
, the dot notation is used to represent derivatives with respect to
t
. Since this parametrization is not by arc length, we define the speed factor

v(t)=\|\dot{\mathbf{r}}(t)\|=\rho\sqrt{\frac{3+\cos t}{2}}.

The curvature and torsion are

\kappa(t)=\frac{\sqrt{13+3\cos t}}{\rho(3+\cos t)^{3/2}},\quad\tau(t)=\frac{6\cos(t/2)}{\rho(13+3\cos t)}.

Then

\kappa^{\prime}(s)=\frac{\dot{\kappa}(t)}{v(t)}=\frac{3\sqrt{2}\sin t(7+\cos t)}{2\rho^{2}(3+\cos t)^{3}\sqrt{13+3\cos t}}.

It is immediate that
\mathbf{r}
is a spherical curve because
\kappa
and
\tau
satisfy (
1
). Moreover,
r^{2}=2\rho
. This gives the radius of the candidate cylinder, namely,
\rho
. Here we can proceed in two different ways.

First, we can consider the ODE (
20
). After some computations, we arrive at

(33)

\dot{\psi}=\frac{2\sin t(5+\cos t)}{(3+\cos t)(13+3\cos t)}\psi\pm\frac{2\sqrt{1+\cos t}\sqrt{3+\cos t}}{13+3\cos t}\sqrt{\frac{13+3\cos t}{(3+\cos t)^{3}}-\psi^{2}}.

We introduce the change of variables

u(t)=\psi(t)(3+\cos t).

After a long computation, equation (
33
) becomes

(34)

(13+3\cos t)\dot{u}+u\sin t=\pm 2\sqrt{1+\cos t}\sqrt{13+3\cos t-(3+\cos t)u^{2}}.

Instead of attempting a direct integration, we propose the constant ansatz
u(t)=2
. This function trivially satisfies (
34
). Thus, for
u(t)=2
, we find

\psi(t)=\frac{2}{3+\cos t}.

Note that we have obtained
\psi(t)
from (
20
). Now we must verify its consistency with equation (
22
). Substituting this expression of
\psi(t)
into the algebraic constraint leads to a lengthy computation, which can be readily verified via symbolic computation software. This confirms that the identity (
22
) holds perfectly.

Alternatively, one can determine
\psi(t)
directly by evaluating the projection of the tangent vector onto the vertical direction
(0,0,1)
. We have

\cos^{2}\alpha=\langle\mathbf{t},(0,0,1)\rangle^{2}=\frac{\dot{z}(t)^{2}}{v(t)^{2}}=\frac{\cos^{2}(t/2)}{1+\cos^{2}(t/2)},

which leads to

\psi(t)=\frac{2}{3+\cos t}.

Again, cumbersome and lengthy algebraic calculations verify that
\psi
satisfies (
20
) and (
22
).
This confirms that the Viviani curve perfectly couples both spherical and cylindrical constraints, validating our ODE as a robust framework for characterizing complex geometric intersections.
