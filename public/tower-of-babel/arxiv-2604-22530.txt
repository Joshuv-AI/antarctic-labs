DEKL 2.0: Trace-Indexed Knowledge Evolution in Dependent Type Theory
Chen Peng

Abstract
DEKL 2.0 is a dependent type-theoretic framework for trace-indexed knowledge evolution. Its central claim is that the proof calculus remains monotone under standard structural rules, while non-monotonic behavior arises semantically from trace extension. Finite and infinite traces are first-class objects in the computational universe; knowledge is interpreted as a presheaf over the finite-trace category; and proposition-level reasoning is handled categorically with fixed-point support. We establish trace–reachability correspondence and completeness, characterize non-monotonicity by non-surjective restriction maps, and present a semantic interpretation based on the free category generated by a transition system. The framework unifies executable traces, typed witnesses, and knowledge revision in one dependent language.



Keywords.

Dependent type theory, trace semantics, presheaf semantics, knowledge evolution,
non-monotonicity, categorical semantics.

1
Introduction

Many dynamic systems require reasoning about facts that depend on execution
history. A statement may hold now, then fail after a new event. Standard
dependent type theory is monotone at the level of derivability (weakening,
substitution, subject reduction); therefore, this kind of knowledge invalidation
is not represented as a proof-theoretic rule.

DEKL resolves this tension by separating proof-theoretic monotonicity from semantic dynamics:

•

The
proof system
remains ordinary dependent type theory.

•

Traces
are represented as first-class terms in the computational universe.

•

Knowledge
is indexed by traces and interpreted contravariantly.

Hence “non-monotonicity” is not a non-monotonic logic rule; it is the behavior of trace-indexed knowledge under trace extension.

The main technical contributions are:

•

a layered syntax for computational objects, trace-indexed knowledge,
and fixed-point reasoning at the proposition layer;

•

a bidirectional correspondence between finite traces and constructive reachability witnesses;

•

a semantic characterization of non-monotonicity via failure of surjectivity of restriction maps;

•

a categorical interpretation combining CwF syntax with
free trace categories and presheaf semantics.

2
Informal Overview

Consider an execution:

\sigma_{0}\to\sigma_{1}\to\cdots\to\sigma_{n}.

A finite trace from
\sigma_{0}
to
\sigma_{n}
is a term

t:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n}).

This term is a constructive typed witness that
\sigma_{n}
is reachable from

\sigma_{0}
.

2.1
A simple
\tau
/
\tau^{\prime}
example

Let
\tau
be a trace prefix and
\tau^{\prime}=\tau\cdot e
its one-step extension. Suppose

\Gamma\vdash p:K_{f}(\tau)

for a knowledge family
K_{f}
. In general, it does not follow that

\Gamma\vdash p:K_{f}(\tau^{\prime}).

The knowledge witness
p
does not disappear; rather, its type index changes. So
invalidation is an index mismatch, not inconsistency of the core logic.

2.2
Intuition for non-monotonicity

Knowledge is modeled as a presheaf over traces:

K_{f}:T_{f}^{op}\to\mathbf{Type}.

For each extension
\epsilon:\tau\to\tau^{\prime}
, there is a restriction map

\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon,-):K_{f}(\tau^{\prime})\to K_{f}(\tau).

Contravariance means we move backwards along extensions. If this map is not
surjective, some old knowledge at
\tau
has no extension witness at
\tau^{\prime}
,
which is exactly non-monotonic behavior.

2.3
Running intuition: revocation under trace extension

To make the index shift concrete, consider a credential lifecycle with events

\mathsf{Issue}(c)
,
\mathsf{Use}(c)
, and
\mathsf{Revoke}(c)
. Let

\mathsf{Auth}(c,\tau):\mathsf{Type}_{\ell}

denote that credential
c
is valid on trace
\tau
. On a prefix containing only
issue/use events, one may construct

q:\mathsf{Auth}(c,\tau).

If the trace extends to

\tau^{\prime}=\tau\cdot\mathsf{Revoke}(c),

the original typed witness
q
generally does not inhabit

\mathsf{Auth}(c,\tau^{\prime})
. This illustrates the central point used throughout the
paper: typed witnesses are stable as terms, but their
typing index
changes with
trace evolution.

3.3
Trace language

Finite traces are inductive:

\mathsf{nil}:\mathsf{State}\to\mathsf{FinTrace},\qquad\mathsf{step}:\mathsf{FinTrace}\to\mathsf{Event}\to\mathsf{State}\to\mathsf{FinTrace}.

Infinite traces are coinductive:

\mathsf{InfTrace}:\mathsf{Uc}_{0},\qquad\mathsf{head}:\mathsf{InfTrace}\to\mathsf{State},\qquad\mathsf{tail}:\mathsf{InfTrace}\to(\mathsf{Event}\times\mathsf{InfTrace}).

Guarded corecursion is required for productivity.

3.4
Structural and definitional judgments

In addition to typing judgments, we rely on the standard structural layer:

\vdash\Gamma\ \mathsf{ctx},\qquad\Gamma\vdash t\equiv u:A,\qquad\Gamma\vdash A\equiv B:U.

These judgments support transport of terms and types across definitional
equalities. In particular, trace-indexed families are used extensionally through
conversion:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash t:A\qquad\Gamma\vdash A\equiv B:U}{\Gamma\vdash t:B}.

This rule is essential when different syntactic presentations of a trace denote
the same normal form.

4
Typing / Operational Rules

4.1
Core typing and operational rules

Context formation and dependent products follow standard dependent type theory:

\frac{}{\,\vdash\cdot\ \mathsf{ctx}\,}\qquad\frac{\vdash\Gamma\ \mathsf{ctx}\quad\Gamma\vdash A:U}{\,\vdash\Gamma,x:A\ \mathsf{ctx}\,},

\frac{\Gamma\vdash A:U\quad\Gamma,x:A\vdash B:U}{\Gamma\vdash\Pi(x:A).B:U},

\frac{\Gamma,x:A\vdash t:B}{\Gamma\vdash\lambda x.t:\Pi(x:A).B}\qquad\frac{\Gamma\vdash f:\Pi(x:A).B\quad\Gamma\vdash a:A}{\Gamma\vdash f\,a:B[a/x]}.

For the remainder of this section, fix a transition system with state type
\mathsf{State}
, event type
\mathsf{Event}
, and transition predicate

\mathsf{Step}:\mathsf{State}\to\mathsf{Event}\to\mathsf{State}\to\mathsf{Uc}_{0}.

Write
\sigma\to\sigma^{\prime}
for one-step transition and
\sigma_{0}\leadsto\sigma_{n}
for reflexive-transitive closure.

4.2
Trace-specific formation and elimination rules

For clarity, we isolate the trace-specific typing shape used in proofs:

\frac{\Gamma\vdash\sigma:\mathsf{State}}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{nil}(\sigma):\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma,\sigma)}

\frac{\Gamma\vdash\tau:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1})\quad\Gamma\vdash e:\mathsf{Event}\quad\Gamma\vdash\pi:\mathsf{Step}(\sigma_{1},e,\sigma_{2})}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{step}(\tau,e,\pi):\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2})}.

Intuitively,
\mathsf{step}
composes a previously validated prefix with one operational
transition witness
\pi
.

For finite traces, elimination proceeds by structural recursion on
\tau
:

\mathsf{trace\mbox{-}elim}:\Pi(P)\to P(\mathsf{nil})\to\left(\Pi(\tau,e,\pi).\,P(\tau)\to P(\mathsf{step}(\tau,e,\pi))\right)\to\Pi(\tau).\,P(\tau),

where
P
ranges over trace-indexed families in an appropriate universe.

Theorem 4.1

(Trace–Proof Correspondence)
.

For arbitrary states
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n}\in\mathsf{State}
, a term

t:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})

is a constructive typed reachability witness for
\sigma_{0}\leadsto\sigma_{n}
.

Proof sketch.

By induction on the structure of finite traces. The base constructor
\mathsf{nil}

witnesses reflexive reachability. The extension constructor
\mathsf{step}
composes a
previously witnessed prefix with one legal transition step. Hence every inhabitant
of
\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})
encodes a finite typed witness of

\sigma_{0}\leadsto\sigma_{n}
.
∎

Theorem 4.2

(Trace Completeness)
.

For arbitrary states
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n}\in\mathsf{State}
, if
\sigma_{0}\leadsto\sigma_{n}
, then there exists

t:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n}).

Proof sketch.

Induct on the length of a typed reachability witness. Length
0
yields the empty
trace. For length
k+1
, decompose the run as
\sigma_{0}\leadsto\sigma_{k}

followed by
\sigma_{k}\to\sigma_{n}
; apply the induction hypothesis to obtain a
prefix trace and then extend it using
\mathsf{step}
.
∎

5
Non-monotonicity via Presheaf Semantics

5.1
Trace category

Definition 5.1

(Finite-trace category)
.

Fix a transition system
(\mathsf{State},\mathsf{Event},\mathsf{Step})
. Let
T_{f}
be the category whose
objects are finite traces generated by this system and whose morphisms are trace
extensions
\epsilon:\tau\to\tau^{\prime}
.

5.2
Knowledge presheaf

Definition 5.2

(Trace-indexed knowledge)
.

A constructive knowledge system is a functor

K_{f}:T_{f}^{op}\to\mathbf{Type},

assigning each trace
\tau
a type
K_{f}(\tau)
and each extension
\epsilon:\tau\to\tau^{\prime}
a restriction map

\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon,-):K_{f}(\tau^{\prime})\to K_{f}(\tau).

5.3
Restriction maps

Lemma 5.3

(Restriction Functoriality)
.

Restriction maps satisfy:

\mathsf{restrict}(id_{\tau},k)=k,\qquad\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon_{1}\circ\epsilon_{2},k)=\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon_{1},\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon_{2},k)).

Proof sketch.

These are exactly the identity and composition laws of the functor

K_{f}:T_{f}^{op}\to\mathbf{Type}
, transported to element-level notation for
restriction maps.
∎

5.4
Characterization theorem

Theorem 5.4

(Non-monotonicity Characterization)
.

Let
K_{f}:T_{f}^{op}\to\mathbf{Type}
be a knowledge presheaf. Then the following are equivalent:

•

K_{f}
is non-monotone under trace extension.

•

There exists
\epsilon:\tau\to\tau^{\prime}
such that

\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon,-):K_{f}(\tau^{\prime})\to K_{f}(\tau)

is not surjective.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Assume non-monotonicity. Then for some extension

\epsilon:\tau\to\tau^{\prime}
there exists a witness

k\in K_{f}(\tau)
such that no
k^{\prime}\in K_{f}(\tau^{\prime})
satisfies

\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon,k^{\prime})=k
. Hence
k
is not in the image of

\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon,-)
, so that map is not surjective.

(
\Leftarrow
) Assume there exists
\epsilon:\tau\to\tau^{\prime}
such that

\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon,-)
is not surjective. Choose

k\in K_{f}(\tau)
outside its image. By construction there is no

k^{\prime}\in K_{f}(\tau^{\prime})
with
\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon,k^{\prime})=k
, i.e., no extension witness of

k
along
\epsilon
. Therefore knowledge does not persist under trace extension,
which is exactly non-monotonicity.
∎

5.5
Conceptual theorem

Theorem 5.5

(Conceptual Consequence)
.

Non-monotonicity of trace-indexed knowledge is a direct effect of contravariance
in presheaf semantics, not a failure of monotonic structural rules in the
dependent proof calculus.

Proof sketch.

By Theorem
5.4
, non-monotonicity is equivalent to
non-surjectivity of some restriction map. Such maps are induced by contravariant
functorial action along extensions in
T_{f}
. Therefore the source of
non-monotonicity is semantic contravariance, while proof-theoretic monotonicity
remains intact.
∎

5.6
Stability criterion and monotone fragment

Proposition 5.6

(Prefix-Stability Criterion)
.

Let
K_{f}:T_{f}^{op}\to\mathbf{Type}
be a knowledge presheaf. If every restriction
map

\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon,-):K_{f}(\tau^{\prime})\to K_{f}(\tau)

is surjective, then knowledge is extension-stable in the sense that every witness
at
\tau
is the restriction of some witness at any extension
\tau^{\prime}
.

Proof sketch.

Fix
\epsilon:\tau\to\tau^{\prime}
and
k\in K_{f}(\tau)
. Surjectivity gives

k^{\prime}\in K_{f}(\tau^{\prime})
with
\mathsf{restrict}(\epsilon,k^{\prime})=k
. Since this holds for arbitrary

\epsilon
and
k
, extension-stability follows.
∎

Proposition
5.6
identifies a monotone semantic fragment
inside the general DEKL setting and complements
Theorem
5.4
.

6
Full Categorical Semantics

6.1
Model decomposition

DEKL is interpreted using two connected categorical components:

•

a CwF-style interpretation for dependent syntax (
\Gamma\vdash A:U
,
\Gamma\vdash t:A
);

•

a trace semantics where a transition system generates a free trace category.

Theorem 6.1

(Free Trace Category)
.

For a fixed transition system
(\mathsf{State},\mathsf{Event},\mathsf{Step})
, states and finite traces
form the free category generated by the underlying transition graph: objects are
states, morphisms are traces, identity morphisms are empty traces, and
composition is trace concatenation.

Proof sketch.

Category laws follow from properties of concatenation (associativity) and empty
traces (identities). Freeness follows because morphisms are generated only by
primitive transitions and finite composition, with no additional equations beyond
category axioms.
∎

6.2
Proposition layer interpretation

The proposition layer
\mathsf{Prop}
is interpreted via subobject-style structure in the
presheaf setting, with fixed-point operators available at proposition level. This
supports temporal and recursive property specifications while preserving
substitution stability.

6.3
Interpretation of
K

The knowledge layer is interpreted in

\widehat{T_{f}}=[T_{f}^{op},\mathbf{Set}],

with
K_{f}
as a presheaf object. Syntactic restriction operations correspond exactly to functor action on extension morphisms. Thus:

syntax of trace-indexed knowledge
\Longleftrightarrow
categorical action of
K_{f}
.

6.4
Adequacy perspective

Lemma 6.2

(Adequacy, soundness direction)
.

For any derivable term

t:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})
, its interpretation

\llbracket t\rrbracket
is a morphism
\sigma_{0}\to\sigma_{n}
in the free trace
category.

Proof sketch.

By induction on trace constructors. The base case maps
\mathsf{nil}
to identity, and
the step case maps
\mathsf{step}
to composition with a generating transition. Domain and
codomain are preserved by typing premises.
∎

Lemma 6.3

(Adequacy, completeness direction)
.

For any morphism
m:\sigma_{0}\to\sigma_{n}
in the free trace category, there exists
a term
t:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{n})
such that
\llbracket t\rrbracket=m
up
to trace equations.

Proof sketch.

By induction on the generation of
m
in the free category. Identity morphisms
are represented by
\mathsf{nil}
; composite morphisms are represented by iterated

\mathsf{step}
-construction with transition witnesses.
∎

Theorem 6.4

(Trace Adequacy)
.

For finite traces, syntactic reachability witnesses and semantic morphisms in the
free trace category are mutually representable, up to definitional equality of
trace terms and category equations.

Proof sketch.

Immediate from Lemma
6.2
and
Lemma
6.3
. Concretely, the forward map sends a term to its
interpreted morphism, and the backward map reconstructs a term from a free-category
decomposition. These two maps are inverse up to the definitional equality on trace
terms and category equations induced by associativity/identity.
∎

7
Meta-theory

7.1
Soundness

Theorem 7.1

(Semantic Soundness)
.

For any judgment
\Gamma\vdash t:A
derivable in DEKL, its interpretation is valid
in the CwF/presheaf model and preserves substitution and computation.

Proof sketch.

We proceed by induction on the derivation of
\Gamma\vdash t:A
.

Step 1 (contexts and substitutions).

Interpretation of contexts and substitutions follows CwF structure. Well-formed
context extension is mapped to comprehension objects, and substitution composition
is respected by definition.

Step 2 (core dependent rules).

Rules for variables, conversion,
\Pi
-formation, introduction, and elimination
are preserved by the corresponding universal properties in the semantic model.
Beta-equality is validated by functorial substitution.

Step 3 (trace constructors).

Trace terms are interpreted in the free trace category:
\mathsf{nil}
as identities and

\mathsf{step}
as composition with generating transitions. Typing premises ensure domain
and codomain compatibility for each interpreted morphism.

Step 4 (knowledge-indexed rules).

Restriction operators are interpreted as action of
K_{f}:T_{f}^{op}\to\mathbf{Type}
.
Functoriality yields identity and composition equations, matching the syntactic
restriction laws.

Step 5 (compatibility and conclusion).

By Lemma
B.1
, interpretation commutes with substitution; by
construction of each rule case above, computation clauses are preserved. Therefore
every derivable judgment has a valid semantic interpretation, establishing
soundness.
∎

7.2
Consistency

Theorem 7.2

(Consistency)
.

There is no closed term
\vdash t:\bot
.

Proof sketch.

In the semantic model,
\bot
is interpreted as the initial/empty subobject. A
closed inhabitant would give a global element of the empty object, impossible by
set-theoretic semantics. Soundness transfers this impossibility to syntax.
∎

Proof-obligations checklist (consistency).

For a fully expanded proof, one discharges:

•

Initial-object interpretation.

\llbracket\bot\rrbracket
is
initial in each semantic fiber.

•

Global section exclusion.
There is no section

1\to\llbracket\bot\rrbracket
.

•

Syntactic transfer.
By Theorem
7.1
, semantic
emptiness implies absence of closed inhabitants in syntax.

7.3
Normalization

The computational layer retains the normalization behavior of standard dependent
type theory. Trace extensions and knowledge indexing do not introduce
non-terminating computational redexes into the core term calculus. Together with
Theorem
7.1
and Theorem
7.2
, this keeps the
logical kernel standard while relocating dynamic effects to trace-indexed
semantics.

Proof-obligations checklist (normalization).

For mechanized normalization, the main obligations are:

•

Reduction closure.
Every reduction rule preserves typing.

•

Measure decrease.
Core computational reductions decrease a
reducibility measure.

•

Trace conservativity.
Trace constructors do not introduce new
non-terminating redex patterns in the core calculus.

•

Index-erasure compatibility.
Erasing trace indices preserves
the reducibility argument used by the base normalization theorem.

7.4
Subject reduction and canonicity perspective

Beyond consistency, two standard quality checks are relevant for publication-level
metatheory:

•

Subject reduction under computational rules.
Reduction in the
computational layer preserves typing and does not break trace indices.

•

Canonicity in closed base types.
Closed normal forms at base
computational types (e.g., naturals, finite traces) are canonical constructors.

In DEKL, these properties are inherited from the underlying dependent calculus and
preserved by conservative addition of trace constructors and presheaf-indexed
knowledge families.

7.5
Explicit structural metatheorems

For completeness, we state the usual structural results in a form directly usable
for mechanization.

Theorem 7.3

(Weakening)
.

If
\Gamma\vdash t:A
and
\Gamma\vdash B:U
, then

\Gamma,x:B\vdash t:A.

Proof sketch.

Induction on the derivation of
\Gamma\vdash t:A
. Variable cases are handled by
context extension; all constructors commute with added assumptions by standard
admissibility lemmas.
∎

Theorem 7.4

(Substitution)
.

If
\Gamma,x:A\vdash t:B
and
\Gamma\vdash s:A
, then

\Gamma\vdash t[s/x]:B[s/x].

Proof sketch.

By induction on typing derivations of
t
. The critical cases are dependent
products and trace-indexed constructors; both follow from substitution closure of
the underlying dependent calculus and stability of trace indices under term
replacement.
∎

Theorem 7.5

(Subject Reduction (Explicit Form))
.

If
\Gamma\vdash t:A
and
t\to t^{\prime}
, then

\Gamma\vdash t^{\prime}:A.

Proof sketch.

Induction on reduction steps. Beta-reduction follows from
Theorem
7.4
. Computational rules for trace constructors are
typed by preservation of the corresponding eliminator branches.
∎

8
Representative Application Modeling

This section provides compact but formalized templates that can be expanded into
full case studies. The key objective is to show how trace-indexed typing
distinguishes logical monotonicity from knowledge invalidation.

8.1
Runtime monitoring and violation localization

Let

\mathsf{Safe}:\mathsf{FinTrace}\to\mathsf{Type}_{\ell}

encode “no policy-violating access has occurred on this prefix.” Assume

\Gamma\vdash p:\mathsf{Safe}(\tau).

If monitoring observes

\tau^{\prime}=\tau\cdot e_{\mathsf{viol}},

the system may fail to derive

\Gamma\vdash p:\mathsf{Safe}(\tau^{\prime}).

The diagnostic content is constructive: the edge

\tau\to\tau^{\prime}
localizes the exact event where validity is lost.

Typed derivation snippet.

\frac{\Gamma\vdash\tau:\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1})\qquad\Gamma\vdash e_{\mathsf{viol}}:\mathsf{Event}\qquad\Gamma\vdash\pi:\mathsf{Step}(\sigma_{1},e_{\mathsf{viol}},\sigma_{2})}{\Gamma\vdash\mathsf{step}(\tau,e_{\mathsf{viol}},\pi):\mathsf{FinTrace}(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{2})}

The monitor reports failure precisely at this extension step, yielding a typed
localization witness.

8.2
Credential revocation in security protocols

Reuse the family

\mathsf{Auth}(c,\tau):\mathsf{Type}_{\ell}.

On issuance-only prefixes we derive witnesses

\Gamma\vdash q:\mathsf{Auth}(c,\tau).

After extension by revocation,

\tau^{\prime}=\tau\cdot\mathsf{Revoke}(c),

one generally has no derivation of

\Gamma\vdash q:\mathsf{Auth}(c,\tau^{\prime})
. The term
q
remains well-formed, but no
longer at the updated trace index. This is the intended form of semantic
non-monotonicity for dynamic authorization.

Typed derivation snippet.

\Gamma\vdash q:\mathsf{Auth}(c,\tau),\qquad\Gamma\vdash\rho:\mathsf{Ext}_{f}(\tau,\tau\cdot\mathsf{Revoke}(c)).

The system still admits the backward restriction shape

\mathsf{restrict}(\rho,-):\mathsf{Auth}(c,\tau\cdot\mathsf{Revoke}(c))\to\mathsf{Auth}(c,\tau),

while forward transport of
q
to the revoked index generally fails.

8.3
Defeasible defaults via trace-conditioned validity

Let

\mathsf{CanAccess}(u,r,\tau):\mathsf{Type}_{\ell}

represent access validity of user
u
to resource
r
on trace
\tau
. A default
witness

d:\mathsf{CanAccess}(u,r,\tau)

may become inapplicable after a risk event

e_{\mathsf{risk}}
:

\tau^{\prime}=\tau\cdot e_{\mathsf{risk}}.

Again, the framework does not derive a contradiction. Instead, it changes the
typing index and thereby models exception handling as index-sensitive validity.

Typed derivation snippet.

\Gamma\vdash d:\mathsf{CanAccess}(u,r,\tau)

and

\Gamma\vdash\epsilon:\mathsf{Ext}_{f}(\tau,\tau\cdot e_{\mathsf{risk}}).

Whether

d:\mathsf{CanAccess}(u,r,\tau\cdot e_{\mathsf{risk}})
remains derivable depends
on updated policy side conditions, not on failure of structural rules.

Proposition 8.1

(Application-level index shift principle)
.

For any knowledge family
K:\mathsf{FinTrace}\to\mathsf{Type}_{\ell}
, if policy predicates depend on
new events introduced by
\tau\to\tau^{\prime}
, then there may exist

a:K(\tau)
such that
a
is not typable at
K(\tau^{\prime})
.

Proof sketch.

Instantiate
K
by one of the families above and choose an extension event that
changes the policy side condition. Typability fails at the new index for semantic
reasons, not because the structural rules cease to hold.
∎

Compared with temporal logics, DEKL does not primarily target model-checking

contribution is not to replace existing logics, but to connect traces, proofs,
and knowledge revision in one dependent framework where non-monotonic behavior is
semantic (presheaf-theoretic) rather than inferential.

Four-dimensional comparison.

The distinction from nearby lines of work can be summarized along four dimensions:

•

Primary object.
Temporal logics reason over path formulas;
DEKL reasons over typed trace witnesses.

•

Methodological core.
Program logics and effect systems center on
operational effects; DEKL centers on dependent typing indexed by execution
traces.

•

Proof obligation style.
Model-checking emphasizes satisfaction
over transition systems, while DEKL emphasizes construction and transport of
typed witnesses under trace evolution.

•

Semantic carrier.
Classical modal semantics use Kripke/transition
frames; DEKL uses free trace categories with presheaf-indexed knowledge fibers.

This comparison clarifies that DEKL is complementary to temporal verification: it
provides a proof-relevant, type-theoretic account of history-sensitive knowledge
rather than a replacement for automata- or fixpoint-based model checking.

Finer technical contrasts.

•

Counterexample form.
Model checkers return violating paths;
DEKL can represent violating extensions as typed index-shift boundaries.

•

Update granularity.
Dynamic epistemic updates are often global
model transformations; DEKL performs local re-indexing via
\mathsf{restrict}
maps.

•

Proof relevance.
Classical entailment is proposition-level;
DEKL keeps witness-level artifacts that can be transported, restricted, or fail
to transport across trace extensions.

•

Compositionality locus.
Temporal frameworks compose formulas;
DEKL composes both terms and traces in one typed judgmental layer.

10
Limitations and Scope

The present manuscript prioritizes coherence of the core framework over maximal
coverage. The main limitations are:

•

Finite-trace emphasis.
Most formal adequacy and characterization
results are stated for finite traces; infinite-trace extensions are outlined but
not fully developed.

•

Proof granularity.
Several results remain at proof-sketch level
and should be expanded into full derivation-level proofs for final publication.

•

Mechanization status.
Rule schemas and proof roadmaps are now
provided, but a completed Coq/Agda artifact is not yet included.

•

Case-study depth.
Application sections currently provide formal
templates rather than full industrial-scale evaluations.

These limitations define a concrete extension path rather than a conceptual gap in
the framework.

Conclusion

DEKL 2.0 separates monotone proof theory from non-monotone knowledge dynamics. The core calculus remains dependent type theory; traces are first-class terms; knowledge is trace-indexed via presheaf semantics; and non-monotonicity is characterized by non-surjective restriction maps.

This reorganization clarifies both formal structure and conceptual message:

proof objects can carry execution history
, and knowledge evolution in
dynamic systems can be modeled constructively without adopting non-monotonic
proof rules. In particular, Theorem
5.4
and
Theorem
5.5
isolate the source of non-monotonicity,
while Theorem
7.1
and Theorem
7.2
preserve
the expected metatheoretic guarantees.

Future work includes mechanized formalization in proof assistants, a refined
account of fixed-point interaction with trace-indexed knowledge at the
proposition layer, and larger case studies in runtime monitoring and security
policy evolution.
