Non-HDL-to-HDL-cholesterol ratio and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Samar F Patankar, Prakash V A K Ramdass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide and is linked to dyslipidemia and cardiometabolic dysfunction. The non–HDL cholesterol to HDL cholesterol ratio (NHHR) has emerged as a composite lipid biomarker that reflects the balance between atherogenic and anti-atherogenic lipoproteins and may have utility in MASLD risk stratification. However, the strength and consistency of the association between NHHR and MASLD remain uncertain. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize the available evidence regarding the association between NHHR and MASLD and evaluate its diagnostic performance.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar was conducted from database inception through January 2026 in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Original human studies evaluating the relationship between NHHR and MASLD were included. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed to calculate pooled standardized mean differences (SMDs) in NHHR between individuals with and without MASLD, pooled odds ratios (ORs) for MASLD according to NHHR levels, and pooled area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to evaluate diagnostic performance. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.ResultsTwenty-four studies were included in the systematic review, and 19 studies comprising 120,985 participants were included in the quantitative synthesis. Individuals with MASLD had significantly higher NHHR values than controls (pooled SMD = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.17–1.84; p = 0.018). Higher NHHR levels were associated with increased odds of MASLD (pooled OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.25–1.96; p < 0.001). A clear dose–response relationship was observed, with progressively greater odds of MASLD across increasing NHHR categories: OR = 1.60 (95% CI: 1.21–2.13) for Q2/T2 versus Q1/T1, OR = 1.87 (95% CI: 1.67–2.09) for Q3 versus Q1, and OR = 2.19 (95% CI: 1.77–2.70) for Q4/T3 versus Q1/T1. Twelve studies evaluating diagnostic accuracy demonstrated a pooled AUC of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.70–0.75), indicating moderate discriminatory ability of NHHR for identifying MASLD.ConclusionsNHHR is significantly associated with MASLD and demonstrates a graded relationship with disease association, whereby higher NHHR levels correspond to progressively greater odds of MASLD. Furthermore, NHHR exhibits moderate diagnostic accuracy for identifying MASLD. As a simple, inexpensive, and widely available metric derived from routine lipid testing, NHHR may serve as a practical adjunctive biomarker for MASLD risk stratification and may help identify individuals who could benefit from further evaluation.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, MASLD, MAFLD, NAFLD, Fatty liver disease, Non-HDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, Non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio, NHHR

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver condition worldwide, with its global prevalence projected to rise to over 55% by 2040, posing a significant public health challenge [1]. It is described as a liver condition characterized by evidence of hepatic steatosis as identified by imaging or histology in patients with at least one metabolic risk factor such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, hypertension or dyslipidemia [2]. Rising levels of obesity, sedentary lifestyles, consumption of unhealthy foods are some of the likely contributors to this increased prevalence of MASLD [3]. Given that MASLD is linked to dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and other components of metabolic syndrome, there is growing interest in identifying simple and readily available biomarkers that can facilitate early risk stratification and disease detection.

The non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (n-HDL-C) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio, also known as the n-HDL-C-to-HDL-C ratio (NHHR) has recently gained attention as a composite lipid index that integrates pro- and anti-atherogenic lipid profiles [4]. This ratio has demonstrated superior predictive value for cardiovascular diseases compared to traditional lipid parameters [5]. Because NHHR reflects the balance between atherogenic lipoproteins and the protective effects of HDL-C, it has emerged as a potentially useful marker of broader cardiometabolic dysfunction [6]. Given that dyslipidemia plays a central role in the pathogenesis of MASLD, NHHR may provide a biologically plausible and clinically relevant indicator of disease risk [7].

Several recent observational studies have reported significant associations between elevated NHHR and MASLD, suggesting that individuals with higher NHHR values may be at greater risk of hepatic steatosis [8, 9]. However, important uncertainties remain. Although most studies report a positive association, the magnitude of the relationship varies considerably across studies [10, 11]. Furthermore, differences in study design, population characteristics, sample size, diagnostic criteria for MASLD, and analytical approaches have led to variability in reported effect estimates. Consequently, the overall strength and consistency of the association between NHHR and MASLD remain unclear.

Importantly, the observed heterogeneity across studies highlights the need for a quantitative synthesis to determine whether the association persists across diverse populations and methodological settings, and to provide a more precise estimate of the magnitude of effect. Additionally, it remains uncertain whether increasing NHHR levels are associated with progressively greater odds of MASLD, which would further support its utility as a risk-stratification tool.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate the association between NHHR and MASLD. Specifically, we aimed to synthesize the available evidence, quantify the magnitude of the association, assess its consistency across studies, and examine whether higher NHHR levels are associated with progressively increased odds of MASLD.

Materials and methods

Protocol registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed to assess the relationship between NHHR and MASLD. The research methodology was designed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [12]. A pre-defined protocol for this review is publicly accessible on PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251057167) https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251057167.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the following databases: Embase, PubMed, and Google Scholar. Our specific search strategy in EMBASE and PubMed involved using the following keywords/ MeSH terms: (“non-HDL cholesterol/ HDL cholesterol ratio” OR “NHHR”) AND (“non-alcoholic fatty liver disease” OR “NAFLD” OR “metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease” OR “MASLD” OR “metabolic-dysfunction associated fatty liver disease” OR “MAFLD”). Our search on Google Scholar entailed the following: “Find articles with all the words in the title of the article”: (non-HDL cholesterol/ HDL cholesterol ratio) AND (MASLD). The search was limited to the English language, and the databases were searched from inception to January 2026.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

The literature search identified records from Embase, PubMed, and Google Scholar, which were imported into Zotero for reference management. After the automated removal of duplicates, two reviewers (S.P. and P.R.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all unique citations. Articles that appeared to meet the eligibility criteria underwent a second round of independent full-text screening by the same reviewers. The inclusion criteria were restricted to original human studies (e.g., case-control, cohort, cross-sectional) published in English that examined the relationship between the NHHR and MASLD. Exclusions comprised case reports, conference abstracts, letters, reviews, and editorials. Additionally, studies meeting the inclusion criteria were screened for potential data overlap. When multiple studies were found to utilize the same dataset, only the study with the most comprehensive dataset was included in the analysis, while the others were excluded to avoid duplication of data.

Data extraction

The primary outcomes were: (1) the standardized mean difference (SMD) in NHHR between individuals with and without MASLD; (2) the odds ratio (OR) for MASLD when NHHR is given as binary categories; and (3) the OR for MASLD when NHHR is given as a nominal variable (as tertiles/ quartiles), with the lowest tertile/ quartile serving as the reference category. In addition, we performed a meta-analysis on the diagnostic accuracy of NHHR for MASLD, by pooling the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Two independent reviewers (S.P. and P.R.) extracted data into a standardized Excel sheet, collecting the following information: first author and publication year, study design, MASLD diagnostic criteria, sample size, and the mean non-HDL/HDL ratio. Potential confounders considered by each study were also extracted to assess methodological comparability and the extent of adjustment across studies.

Statistical analysis and publication bias

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.5.0) with the meta and metafor packages. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted using the inverse variance method, with between-study variance estimated using the restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) estimator. Given the observational nature of the included studies, the comparison of NHHR between MASLD and control groups is presented as a descriptive association rather than an estimate of causal effect. Accordingly, we used the SMD to allow comparability across studies with potentially different NHHR distributions. For studies reporting NHHR as a continuous variable, pooled SMDs with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to compare NHHR levels between individuals with MASLD and controls. Because the NHHR is a unitless ratio, continuous NHHR values were considered directly comparable across studies and no additional scaling or standardization was required. For studies evaluating NHHR as a continuous variable, effect estimates were extracted based on the increment reported in the original studies. All included studies reported associations per unit increase in NHHR; therefore, pooled estimates for continuous NHHR represent the change in the odds of MASLD associated with each one-unit increase in NHHR.

For studies reporting NHHR as a categorical variable, pooled ORs and 95% CIs were calculated to assess the association between NHHR categories and MASLD.

When NHHR was reported as ordinal categories (tertiles or quartiles), the lowest category served as the reference group. Specifically, T1 or Q1 were used as the reference category, depending on whether the data was presented as tertile or quartile, respectively. T2 was grouped with Q2, while T3 was grouped with Q4 because both represented the highest exposure category within their respective study-specific NHHR distributions. Comparisons were therefore performed for T2/Q2 versus T1/Q1 and T3/Q4 versus T1/Q1. Studies reporting quartiles additionally contributed data for the Q3 versus Q1 comparison. Because the NHHR cut-off values defining tertiles and quartiles varied across studies, categories were pooled according to their relative position within each study-specific NHHR distribution rather than according to absolute NHHR thresholds. Consequently, the analyses compared participants with progressively higher NHHR levels relative to the reference category within each study, an approach commonly used in meta-analyses of categorical exposure variables when category boundaries differ across populations [13].

In studies where NHHR was not directly reported, it was calculated as the ratio n-HDL-C to HDL-C, and the corresponding standard deviation was derived using standard error propagation techniques applied to the reported means and standard deviations of the individual lipid components.

Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic, the I² statistic, and the between-study variance (τ²). I² values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to relevant study characteristics, including study design and geographic region. Between-subgroup differences were assessed using the χ² test for subgroup interactions.

The robustness of the pooled estimates for SMD was assessed through leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, in which each study was sequentially excluded and the meta-analysis repeated to determine its influence on the overall effect estimate. Additional influence analyses were performed using Baujat plots and diagnostic influence statistics to identify studies contributing disproportionately to overall heterogeneity and pooled effect sizes. In addition, random-effects meta-regression analyses were performed to explore whether study-level characteristics, including sample size, study design, and geographic region, contributed to the observed between-study heterogeneity.

Publication bias was evaluated using a multi-faceted approach, including visual inspection of a funnel plot for asymmetry and statistical testing with Egger’s linear regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test [14, 15]. Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill method [16] was further employed to estimate the number of potentially missing studies and adjust the effect size accordingly. Results were considered statistically significant at a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included observational studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (S.P. and P.R.) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [17], or the modified version for cross-sectional studies [18]. The NOS evaluates studies across three domains: selection of study groups (4 stars), comparability of groups (2 stars), and ascertainment of either the exposure (for case-control studies) or outcome (for cross-sectional studies) (3 stars), for a maximum score of 9. Because all included studies were observational and the primary objective was to evaluate the association and diagnostic performance of NHHR rather than intervention effectiveness, the certainty of evidence was not formally assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [19].

Results

Identified studies

Of the 72 total citations obtained from the three databases (PubMed: n = 24, Embase: n = 47, Google Scholar: n = 1), 21 duplicates were removed, resulting in 51 titles and abstracts being screened. After excluding 15 records during initial screening, 36 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 12 articles were excluded (11 did not meet inclusion criteria, and 1 was a review, resulting in 24 studies [8–11, 20–39] included in the systematic review. Nineteen (19) studies met criteria for inclusion in the meta-analysis. Studies denoted by a dagger (†) were determined to contain overlapping populations. Consistent with established meta-analytic practice, only the most comprehensive dataset (B. Li et al.) [11] was retained for the SMD meta-analysis, while Xuan et al. [27] and D. Yang et al. [29] were excluded to prevent duplication of participants and inflation of study weights. Similarly, Huang et al. [34] and Ma et al. [20] were excluded from the meta- analysis on OR. Notably, the studies by Lu et al. [26] and Lu et al. [10] used the same sample but provided data for the meta-analysis on SMD and OR, respectively. Study characteristics

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the included studies. The systematic review included 24 studies from eight countries while the meta-analysis included 19 unique studies, comprising 120,985 participants. The studies were geographically diverse, representing eight countries. The largest number of studies originated from China (n = 11), followed by the United States (n = 7). Single studies were conducted in South Korea, Montenegro, Iran, Romania, Japan, and Nepal. Study designs varied and included cross-sectional studies (n = 15), case-control studies (n = 2), prospective cohort studies (n = 2), and retrospective cohort studies (n = 2). The remaining studies were included in the systematic review only. The diagnostic criteria for MASLD varied across studies. Ultrasound-based diagnosis was the most frequently employed method, used in 14 studies. Fatty liver index (FLI) was utilized in three studies, while controlled attenuation parameter with vibration-controlled transient elastography (CAP/VCTE) was used in five studies. Some studies employed combined approaches, such as ultrasound with fibrosis scoring or CAP with VCTE. One study applied the Chinese Liver Disease Association diagnostic criteria, and another relied on a clinical diagnosis of fatty liver disease. Sample sizes ranged considerably, from as few as 100 participants [38] to as many as 36,897 participants [35]. Three studies [37–39] were included in the systematic review but were excluded from the meta-analysis due to insufficient data for quantitative synthesis.

Study demographics and confounders assessed

Table 2 summarizes the demographic characteristics, covariates, confounders assessed, and medication-related considerations of the included studies. Across the 24 studies, the proportion of female participants ranged from 2.2% to 68.1%, with most cohorts comprising equal numbers of men and women. Mean participant age varied, ranging from 9.1 years in a pediatric cohort to 70 years in older adult populations, while body mass index (BMI) ranged from 17.4 to 36 kg/m². Most studies adjusted for key demographic and metabolic confounders, including age, sex, BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, physical activity, and markers of insulin resistance. Liver-related biomarkers, particularly alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), uric acid, fasting glucose, and lipid parameters, were frequently incorporated into multivariable analyses. Medication use was variably reported; several studies excluded participants receiving lipid-lowering agents, hepatotoxic drugs, or medications affecting lipid metabolism, whereas others adjusted for or documented the use of statins, antihypertensives, hypoglycemic agents, insulin, and other cardiometabolic therapies.

Systematic review

The collective evidence from the twenty-four studies consistently demonstrates a significant positive association between the NHHR and the prevalence, incidence, and severity of MASLD. Multiple cross-sectional studies utilizing NHANES data have established that elevated NHHR levels are independently associated with increased odds of MASLD. Notably, several studies identified non-linear relationships, including U-shaped [39] and S-shaped [29] associations, with inflection points ranging from 1.26 to 2.54. Prospective cohort studies [31, 32, 35, 39] have further validated NHHR as an independent predictor of new-onset MASLD, with hazard ratios between 1.5 and 2.7 for the highest versus lowest tertiles. Several studies reported that NHHR demonstrated favorable diagnostic performance for identifying MASLD, with AUC values ranging from 0.68 to 0.82 across different populations [9, 26, 33]. However, direct comparisons with other lipid indices were not consistently reported, limiting conclusions regarding the relative superiority of NHHR over alternative lipid-based biomarkers. Subgroup analyses revealed that the association is particularly robust in non-obese individuals [8], females [10], and those without advanced fibrosis [36]. Furthermore, NHHR demonstrated significant associations with hepatic steatosis severity [24, 28]. Yang et al. found a U-shaped relationship between NHHR and all-cause mortality in MASLD patients [39].

Meta-analysis: SMD in NHHR between MASLD and control according to study design

Figure 2 presents the forest plot of the SMD in NHHR between individuals with MASLD and controls, stratified by study design. A total of 48,800 participants were included, comprising 16,526 individuals with MASLD and 32,274 controls across ten studies. Using a random-effects model, individuals with MASLD exhibited significantly higher NHHR values than controls, with a pooled SMD of 1.01 (95% CI: 0.17–1.84; p = 0.018). However, substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 99.7%, τ² = 1.7870, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis demonstrated significant differences according to study design (χ² = 44.96, df = 2, p < 0.0001). Among case-control studies, the pooled SMD was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.65–1.24) with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 40.4%). In cross-sectional studies, the pooled SMD was 1.17 (95% CI: 0.00–2.34), although heterogeneity was extremely high (I² = 99.7%). The single retrospective cohort study by Lu et al. [26] showed no significant difference in NHHR between MASLD and controls (SMD = -0.03, 95% CI: -0.08 to 0.01). Individual study SMDs ranged from − 0.03 in Lu et al. [26] to 4.68 in Zhu et al. [8]. Nine of the ten included studies reported positive SMDs, indicating consistently higher NHHR values among individuals with MASLD compared with controls. Notably, Zhu et al. [8] reported a markedly larger effect size than the remaining studies and was the primary contributor to the substantial heterogeneity observed in the overall analysis. These findings suggest that NHHR is significantly elevated in individuals with MASLD, although the magnitude of the association varies across study designs.

Meta-analysis: SMD in NHHR between MASLD and control according to study region

Figure 3 presents the subgroup analysis of the SMD in NHHR between individuals with MASLD and controls according to study region. When stratified by geographic region, studies conducted in Asia showed the largest pooled effect size (SMD = 1.30, 95% CI: -0.08 to 2.67), but this association did not reach statistical significance and was accompanied by considerable heterogeneity (I² = 99.8%). In contrast, studies from Europe demonstrated a significant positive association between NHHR and MASLD (SMD = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.25–0.90) with low-to-moderate heterogeneity (I² = 30.5%). Similarly, studies from the United States showed a significant elevation in NHHR among individuals with MASLD (SMD = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.33–0.66), although heterogeneity remained high (I² = 96.5%). The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (χ² = 1.43, df = 2, p = 0.489), indicating that the association between NHHR and MASLD did not differ significantly across geographic regions.

Meta-analysis: odds of MASLD and NHHR

Figure 4 presents the forest plot of the OR for the association between NHHR and MASLD. The meta-analysis included six studies investigating this relationship. The random-effects model meta-analysis revealed that higher NHHR levels were significantly associated with increased odds of MASLD. The pooled odds ratio was 1.56 (95% CI: 1.25 to 1.96), p < 0.001, indicating that elevated NHHR is associated with a 56% increase in the odds of having MASLD. All six included studies demonstrated statistically significant positive associations between NHHR and MASLD, with individual odds ratios ranging from 1.24(95% CI: 1.09 to 1.42) [30] to 2.71 (95% CI: 2.32 to 3.17) [9]. There was substantial heterogeneity among the studies, as indicated by an I² statistic of 94.3% (p < 0.001).

Meta-analysis: odds of MASLD and NHHR Q2/T2 versus Q1/T1

Figure 5 presents the forest plot of the association between the second quartile (Q2) of NHHR and MASLD, with the first quartile (Q1) serving as the reference category. Based on eight studies, the random-effects meta-analysis demonstrated that individuals in Q2 had significantly greater odds of MASLD than those in Q1 (OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.21–2.13), indicating a 60% increase in the odds of MASLD among individuals with moderately elevated NHHR levels. Considerable between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 90.6%, p < 0.001). Individual study estimates ranged from OR = 1.07 (95% CI: 0.57–2.02) in Yang et al. [33] to OR = 3.65 (95% CI: 2.95–4.52) in B. Li et al. [11]. Six of the eight studies reported statistically significant positive associations, whereas Wang et al. [31], Yang et al. [33], and Liu et al. [28] did not reach statistical significance. Notably, the study by B. Li et al. [11] reported a larger effect size than the other included studies and was a major contributor to the observed heterogeneity. Overall, these findings suggest that even modest elevations in NHHR are associated with significantly increased odds of MASLD compared with the lowest NHHR category.

Meta-analysis: diagnostic accuracy of NHHR for MASLD

Figure 8 presents the forest plot of the diagnostic performance of the NHHR for identifying MASLD. A total of 12 studies comprising 17,755 MASLD cases and 83,330 controls were included. The pooled analysis demonstrated a moderate overall diagnostic accuracy, with a summary AUC of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.70–0.75), indicating that NHHR has a fair ability to discriminate individuals with MASLD from those without the disease. Subgroup analysis by study design showed comparable results, with cross-sectional studies yielding a pooled AUC of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.69–0.75) and cohort studies demonstrating a slightly higher pooled AUC of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.67–0.80). Individual study AUCs ranged from 0.66 to 0.80, reflecting moderate variability in diagnostic performance across populations. Substantial heterogeneity was observed within both cross-sectional (I² = 91.2%) and cohort studies (I² = 99.1%), as well as in the overall analysis (I² = 96.4%), suggesting considerable between-study differences. However, there was no significant difference in diagnostic performance between study designs (QM = 0.89, p = 0.346), indicating that the predictive utility of NHHR for MASLD was consistent across study types.

Sensitivity analysis

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis in Fig. 9 demonstrated that the overall findings were robust and not driven by any single study. The random-effects model yielded a pooled SMD of 1.01 (95% CI: 0.17–1.84; p = 0.018), indicating significantly higher NHHR values among individuals with MASLD compared with controls. Sequential omission of individual studies resulted in pooled SMDs ranging from 0.58 (95% CI: 0.34–0.82) after excluding Zhu et al. [8]. to 1.12 (95% CI: 0.23–2.02) after excluding Lu et al., with all recalculated estimates remaining statistically significant (all p < 0.05). The confidence intervals of all leave-one-out analyses overlapped with the overall pooled estimate, indicating that no single study altered the direction or significance of the association. Notably, exclusion of Zhu et al. [8]. produced the largest reduction in both the pooled effect size and between-study heterogeneity, with I² decreasing from 99.7% to 98.8% and τ² decreasing from 1.7870 to 0.1201, suggesting that this study was a major contributor to the observed heterogeneity and effect magnitude. Nevertheless, the persistence of a statistically significant association across all iterations supports the stability of the findings and reinforces the conclusion that NHHR is significantly elevated in individuals with MASLD relative to controls.

Meta-regression

Figure 11 presents the meta-regression analyses examining potential sources of the substantial heterogeneity observed in the association between NHHR and MASLD. The meta-regression evaluating total sample size demonstrated no significant relationship between study size and the SMD in NHHR between individuals with MASLD and controls (β = −0.0001, 95% CI: −0.0002 to 0.0001, p = 0.421). Although the regression line showed a slight negative slope, indicating a trend toward smaller effect sizes in larger studies, this association was not statistically significant. Consistent with this finding, the bubble plot showed no clear linear relationship between study sample size and effect size, with studies distributed widely around the fitted regression line. Notably, Zhu et al. [8]. appeared as an outlier with a markedly larger effect size than the remaining studies, whereas Lu et al. [10], the largest study included, reported an effect size close to the null. Despite these differences, total sample size explained none of the between-study heterogeneity (R² = 0.0%), and substantial residual heterogeneity remained (τ² = 1.8572; I² = 99.89%; QE = 2067.43, p < 0.001).

Meta-regression of SMD by total sample size

Additional meta-regression analyses demonstrated that sex distribution (β = −0.033, p = 0.508), mean age (β = 0.008, p = 0.809), and mean BMI (β = −0.086, p = 0.605) were not significant moderators of the association between NHHR and MASLD. Furthermore, a multivariable meta-regression model incorporating sex, age, and BMI simultaneously did not explain a significant proportion of the between-study heterogeneity (QM = 1.07, p = 0.785), with residual heterogeneity remaining extremely high (I² = 99.87%). Similarly, neither study design (QM = 0.60, p = 0.741) nor geographic region (QM = 0.61, p = 0.737) significantly accounted for the observed heterogeneity, with both moderators explaining 0% of the between-study variance. Collectively, these findings indicate that the marked heterogeneity observed in the meta-analysis is unlikely to be attributable to differences in sample size, sex distribution, age, BMI, study design, or geographic region, suggesting that other unmeasured methodological, clinical, or population-level factors may underlie the variability in effect estimates across studies.

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot, shown in Fig. 12, suggested some asymmetry, with a relative paucity of studies on the right side of the pooled effect estimate. However, formal statistical testing did not indicate significant publication bias or small-study effects, as demonstrated by Egger’s regression test [14] (p = 0.985) and Begg’s rank correlation test [15] (p = 1.000). The trim-and-fill method [16] estimated four potentially missing studies on the right side of the funnel plot. After adjustment, the pooled SMD increased from 1.00 to 1.49, indicating that any potential publication bias would be expected to strengthen rather than attenuate the observed association. Nevertheless, given the extreme between-study heterogeneity (I² = 99.9%), the results of the trim-and-fill analysis should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, synthesizing data from 19 studies and 120,985 individuals, demonstrates a significant and graded association between NHHR and the presence of MASLD. Individuals with MASLD had significantly higher NHHR values than controls (SMD: 1.01), and a clear dose–response relationship was observed across quartiles. Participants in the highest NHHR quartile exhibited more than a 2.15-fold greater likelihood of MASLD compared with those in the lowest quartile. An increase of this magnitude reflects a clinically meaningful shift toward an atherogenic lipid profile, typically characterized by elevated triglycerides and reduced levels of protective HDL-C [11]. Translating these findings into clinical practice suggests that NHHR may represent a practical and readily available biomarker for identifying individuals at increased risk of MASLD. These findings are particularly relevant given the growing global burden of MASLD and the increasing emphasis on early identification of individuals with liver disease. Recent evidence has highlighted a substantial unmet need for simple, inexpensive, and widely accessible tools capable of identifying individuals at increased risk of progressive liver disease before the development of advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver-related complications [41]. Given that NHHR is derived from routinely measured lipid parameters, it may represent a practical adjunctive biomarker for identifying individuals who could benefit from further liver-specific assessment and risk stratification.

NHHR is calculated as (total cholesterol – HDL-C) / HDL-C and therefore reflects the balance between all atherogenic lipoproteins—including LDL, VLDL, and remnant cholesterol—and the protective functions of HDL-C, particularly reverse cholesterol transport and anti-inflammatory activity [4, 40]. An elevated NHHR arises from a combination of increased triglyceride-rich lipoproteins and reduced HDL-C concentrations. These lipid abnormalities are strongly associated with insulin resistance and visceral adiposity, both of which are key metabolic drivers of MASLD [42, 43]. Consequently, NHHR provides an integrated measure of dyslipidemia that captures both the atherogenic lipid burden and the loss of HDL-mediated protective effects, making it particularly relevant for conditions characterized by systemic metabolic dysfunction [44]. The clinical relevance of identifying simple biomarkers for liver disease risk stratification is highlighted by recent evidence demonstrating that plasma IGFBP7 improved the prediction of long-term liver-related outcomes beyond established non-invasive scores such as FIB-4 and APRI, supporting the broader concept that readily available biomarkers may complement existing approaches for identifying individuals at increased likelihood of progressive liver disease [45].

The biological plausibility of the observed association between NHHR and MASLD is supported by the central role of lipid dysregulation in hepatic steatosis [46]. MASLD develops when the influx of fatty acids to the liver exceeds its capacity for oxidation and export [47]. Several interconnected metabolic processes contribute to this imbalance, including increased free fatty acid (FFA) flux from insulin-resistant adipose tissue, enhanced hepatic de novo lipogenesis, and impaired secretion of very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) [48]. Elevated non-HDL cholesterol levels reflect an increased burden of atherogenic lipoproteins that deliver lipid substrates to the liver. Simultaneously, reduced HDL-C levels indicate impaired reverse cholesterol transport and diminished antioxidant and anti-inflammatory functions of HDL particles [49]. Together, these alterations promote hepatocellular triglyceride accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and activation of inflammatory pathways that contribute to the progression from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis and fibrosis [50–52]. Mechanistic studies have further demonstrated that insulin resistance stimulates hepatic lipogenesis through activation of transcription factors such as sterol regulatory element–binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) [53] and carbohydrate-responsive element–binding protein (ChREBP) [54], thereby increasing triglyceride synthesis and exacerbating hepatic fat accumulation [55]. Thus, an elevated NHHR reflects the underlying metabolic environment that drives the development and progression of MASLD.

At the individual level, the observed increase in NHHR corresponds to a clear gradient of disease risk. In our analysis, individuals in the second, third, and fourth quartiles of NHHR had 1.60, 1.87, and 2.19-fold higher odds of MASLD, respectively, compared with those in the lowest quartile. These findings suggest that NHHR functions as a practical risk stratification tool that can be derived from routine lipid panels. Such a metric may allow clinicians to identify individuals at increased metabolic risk and prompt further hepatic evaluation or early lifestyle interventions aimed at improving metabolic health [56].

The potential clinical utility of NHHR lies in its integrative nature. Unlike individual lipid parameters such as LDL-C or HDL-C alone, NHHR simultaneously captures both pro-atherogenic lipid burden and anti-atherogenic lipid capacity [57, 58]. This dual representation of lipid metabolism aligns closely with the evolving conceptual framework of MASLD, which emphasizes metabolic dysfunction—including dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation—as key drivers of hepatic steatosis [59]. Importantly, NHHR can be calculated easily from standard lipid profiles, making it a simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective biomarker for population screening and longitudinal monitoring. Supporting the growing role of simple non-invasive biomarkers in liver disease risk stratification, Liang et al. recently demonstrated in a prospective community-based cohort of more than 385,000 individuals that routine blood-based scores, particularly CORE and LiverRisk, effectively predicted long-term cirrhosis-related morbidity, highlighting the potential value of accessible biomarkers for identifying individuals at increased risk of adverse liver outcomes [60]. Lifestyle modifications such as weight loss, dietary changes, and increased physical activity are known to improve lipid metabolism and may therefore reduce NHHR levels over time [61, 62]. In clinical practice, persistent elevations in NHHR above proposed thresholds could prompt further diagnostic evaluation for MASLD using imaging modalities such as liver ultrasound or vibration-controlled transient elastography [63].

Interestingly, Yang et al. reported a U-shaped association between NHHR and MASLD risk, with a breakpoint NHHR around 1.26 [39]. This finding suggests that the relationship between lipid ratios and hepatic steatosis may not be strictly linear. Extremely low NHHR values may reflect atypical metabolic states characterized by unusually high HDL-C levels or markedly reduced non-HDL cholesterol concentrations. Such lipid profiles may occur in specific metabolic or genetic contexts and may not necessarily reflect optimal metabolic health. Alternatively, the observed U-shaped pattern may represent limitations of ratio-based biomarkers at extreme values or residual confounding in observational datasets. Further studies using nonlinear modeling approaches and prospective data are needed to clarify this relationship.

From a clinical perspective, NHHR may provide a practical tool for early detection of MASLD. The disease often remains asymptomatic in its initial stages, and many patients present with normal or only mildly elevated aminotransferase levels. Ultrasound is widely used as a first-line screening modality [63], whereas liver biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis and fibrosis staging [64]. However, routine imaging or invasive procedures may not be feasible for large populations. Because NHHR can be easily calculated from routine lipid measurements, it could serve as a convenient screening tool in primary care or endocrinology settings to identify individuals who may benefit from further hepatic evaluation.

Diagnostic studies have reported an AUC of up to 0.8 for NHHR in identifying MASLD, suggesting good discriminative ability [9]. One included study identified an NHHR threshold of 2.476 for predicting MASLD; however, this cutoff has not been externally validated across diverse populations and should therefore be interpreted cautiously [36]. If validated in prospective studies, this threshold could be incorporated into clinical algorithms. For example, individuals with NHHR values above this level could undergo additional non-invasive fibrosis assessment using scoring systems such as FIB-4 or imaging techniques such as transient elastography [65]. Although NHHR demonstrated moderate diagnostic accuracy for MASLD in our meta-analysis (pooled AUC = 0.73), direct comparisons with other lipid-based indices could not be quantitatively synthesized because such data were inconsistently reported across the included studies. Among alternative lipid markers, the triglyceride-to-HDL cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio has also shown promise as a predictor of MASLD. In a large prospective cohort study of more than 49,000 individuals, higher TG/HDL-C ratios were independently associated with a progressively increased risk of incident MAFLD, supporting its role as a useful lipid-based risk marker [66]. Furthermore, a recent analysis of U.S. adults reported that the TG/HDL-C ratio achieved an AUC of 0.732 for MASLD detection, outperforming TG alone (AUC = 0.713) and HDL-C alone (AUC = 0.313) [67]. Nevertheless, direct head-to-head comparisons between NHHR and TG/HDL-C remain scarce. Consequently, the current evidence is insufficient to determine whether NHHR offers superior diagnostic performance compared with TG/HDL-C or other lipid-derived indices. Future studies directly comparing NHHR, TG/HDL-C, the triglyceride-glucose index, and other metabolic biomarkers within the same populations are needed to identify the most effective tool for MASLD risk stratification and screening.

Importantly, MASLD is increasingly recognized as a multisystem disease intricately linked with cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death among patients with MASLD [68], reflecting the shared pathophysiological mechanisms of insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and atherogenic dyslipidemia [69]. Because NHHR reflects the balance between atherogenic and protective lipoproteins, elevated ratios may simultaneously indicate increased risk for both hepatic steatosis and cardiovascular complications. Thus, NHHR may serve as a dual-purpose biomarker, helping clinicians identify patients with MASLD who also carry a heightened cardiometabolic risk profile and may benefit from aggressive lifestyle or pharmacologic interventions aimed at reducing cardiovascular risk.

The relevance of NHHR may also extend beyond MASLD to other metabolic and inflammatory disorders. Recent studies have demonstrated associations between elevated NHHR and conditions such as cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, and gallstone disease [70–72]. Additionally, NHHR has been investigated as a marker of cardiometabolic risk in patients with diabetes mellitus and metabolic syndrome [4, 73]. These findings suggest that NHHR may represent a broader indicator of systemic metabolic dysfunction and chronic inflammation, highlighting its potential utility as a screening biomarker across multiple metabolic diseases.

An important consideration is the growing recognition that MASLD is a heterogeneous condition encompassing distinct clinical phenotypes, particularly lean and non-lean MASLD [74]. Recent evidence suggests that lean MASLD represents a unique subgroup with clinical and prognostic characteristics that differ from those of non-lean MASLD. In a large multicohort prospective study involving individuals with MASLD, lean MASLD was associated with a greater risk of liver-related events, liver-related mortality, and all-cause mortality despite a lower burden of traditional cardiometabolic risk factors compared with non-lean MASLD [75]. Furthermore, emerging data indicate that individuals classified as “MASLD only” exhibit distinct metabolic profiles, fibrosis burden, and mortality patterns compared with those meeting both MASLD and MAFLD criteria, highlighting the substantial heterogeneity that exists within contemporary steatotic liver disease classifications [76]. Consistent with this concept, recent correspondence by Pan et al. emphasized that the MAFLD definition identifies more homogeneous metabolic subgroups, whereas MASLD encompasses a broader and more diverse spectrum of patients [74]. Unfortunately, the studies included in our meta-analysis did not consistently report data stratified by lean versus non-lean MASLD status, precluding subgroup analyses.

A key strength of this meta-analysis is the consistency of the association across diverse populations and study designs. Although substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed, the direction of the association remained uniformly positive. Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the pooled estimates were not driven by any single study, supporting the robustness of the findings. The observed heterogeneity reflects differences in MASLD diagnostic criteria, population characteristics, and study methodologies. Included studies used a range of diagnostic methods—including ultrasound, controlled attenuation parameter with vibration-controlled transient elastography, and biochemical indices—which differ in their sensitivity for detecting hepatic steatosis, particularly in early disease stages.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the NHHR cut-off values used to define tertiles and quartiles varied across studies, reflecting differences in population characteristics and NHHR distributions. Although categories were harmonized according to their relative position within each study-specific distribution, the absence of standardized NHHR thresholds may have contributed to between-study heterogeneity and may limit the direct clinical interpretation of specific cut-off values.

Second, the majority of included studies were cross-sectional, preventing determination of temporal relationships or causal inference between NHHR and MASLD. Third, the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies may reflect differences in diagnostic criteria, population characteristics, and study methodologies. Fourth, evidence of potential publication bias was observed. Trim-and-fill analysis suggested the presence of missing studies, and while Egger’s test demonstrated only borderline asymmetry, Begg’s test indicated possible publication bias, suggesting that smaller studies with null findings may be underrepresented in the literature. Fifth, this meta-analysis did not directly compare the diagnostic performance of NHHR with other established lipid-related biomarkers, such as the triglyceride-to-HDL cholesterol ratio, total cholesterol-to-HDL cholesterol ratio, or LDL cholesterol-to-HDL cholesterol ratio. Although several included studies reported favorable diagnostic characteristics for NHHR, the available data were insufficient and too heterogeneous to permit a pooled comparative analysis of diagnostic accuracy measures such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Consequently, our findings support an association between NHHR and MASLD but do not establish the superiority of NHHR over alternative lipid-based risk markers. Finally, the exclusion of non-English studies may have introduced language bias and limited the global generalizability of the findings.

Future research should focus on prospective cohort studies to determine whether elevated NHHR precedes the development of MASLD and predicts disease progression. In addition, future studies should evaluate whether the association between NHHR and MASLD differs according to body composition and metabolic phenotype, as NHHR may have differential predictive utility across these clinically distinct subgroups. Mechanistic investigations are also needed to better understand the molecular pathways linking dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and hepatic fat accumulation. Additionally, interventional studies should evaluate whether therapies that improve NHHR—such as lipid-lowering agents, weight reduction strategies, or metabolic treatments including GLP-1 receptor agonists—can reduce the incidence or progression of MASLD [77].

Overall, this meta-analysis provides comprehensive evidence supporting NHHR as a clinically relevant biomarker of MASLD risk. By integrating both atherogenic lipid burden and protective HDL-mediated functions, NHHR reflects the systemic metabolic dysregulation that underlies hepatic steatosis. Given its simplicity, accessibility, and strong association with disease risk, NHHR may represent a valuable tool for improving early detection and risk stratification of MASLD in both clinical and population health settings.

Conclusions

Our analysis demonstrates that NHHR is significantly associated with MASLD across diverse populations and study designs. These findings support NHHR as a simple, inexpensive, and readily available lipid-derived biomarker that reflects the dysmetabolic milieu underlying hepatic steatosis. Given that NHHR can be calculated from a routine lipid panel, it has potential value for risk stratification and early identification of individuals who may benefit from further hepatic evaluation, particularly in primary care and other resource-limited clinical settings. Future prospective cohort studies and interventional research are needed to determine whether elevated NHHR precedes MASLD development, predicts progression to fibrosis, and improves in parallel with successful treatment or lifestyle modification.
