Partial tolerance achieved in persistent alpha-gal syndrome: A case report
Authors: Maria Aman, Mark Janowiecki, John C Carlson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA patient with persistent alpha-gal syndrome used a bacon food ladder that induced partial tolerance to most red meats. He continued to have reactions after ingesting fried pork skin.Key words: Alpha-gal syndrome, food ladder, food allergy, oral immunotherapy

Patients with alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) have IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reactions to the oligosaccharide galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose (alpha-gal), which is commonly encountered when eating mammalian meat.1,2 Tick bites have been implicated as sensitizing exposures that induce high levels of anti–alpha-gal IgE antibodies.3 In the United States, exposure to the lone star tick (Amblyomma americanum) has been linked to sensitization. In other parts of the world, other ticks, chiggers, fleas, and even infection with Ascaris lumbricoides have been identified as possible sensitizing agents.4, 5, 6 AGS differs from other classic food allergies in several ways, including delayed onset of symptoms after consumption of mammalian products (typically 3-8 hours), variability in reactions, and resolution of symptoms with strict avoidance of mammalian meat.7 Given the resolution of reactivity in most patients after avoidance of triggers, avoidance with intermittent ingestion challenges is an option for treatment.8

A 26-year-old entomologist, who consented to publication of this case report, developed AGS from tick bites sustained during fieldwork. Before 2013, he had no symptoms with regular ingestion of mammalian meat. In 2015, after years of fieldwork, he began experiencing unusual symptoms, including episodes of gastrointestinal upset in the middle of the night. Table I presents a timeline of the patient’s exposures and reactions. He did not initially identify an association with meat consumption because of the delayed onset of symptoms. By 2016, his reactions had progressed to include diffuse pruritus, hives, swelling of his hands, redness of his limbs, cough, diarrhea, and gastrointestinal discomfort. His symptoms resolved with oral diphenhydramine. Periodic reactions continued from 2016 to 2018, occurring a few hours after consumption of meat-heavy meals. His symptoms were variable, and not every instance of meat consumption led to an allergic reaction.

The patient suspected that he had AGS and requested testing from an allergist in 2018. His level of IgE to alpha-gal was 84.9 kU/L, and the allergist advised strict avoidance of mammalian meat. The patient has continued to carry epinephrine autoinjectors since that time. In August 2021, he was evaluated in our clinic. His level of alpha-gal IgE was 3.11 kU/L. Given the length of time since his last reaction and his avoidance of new tick bites or insect stings in the interim, we performed an ingestion challenge. He consumed 12 oz beef steak of, 3 oz of bacon, 3 oz of pork sausage, 3 oz of corned beef, and 1 oz of salami, consistent with his goal of being able to ingest large meals containing mammalian meat. Given the delayed reactions, ingestion was performed as a single-step meal in this and subsequent challenges. One hour later, the patient had a loose bowel movement. Two hours after completing the meal, he developed scattered hives. He was given an initial intramuscular injection of 0.5 mL of epinephrine but continued to have worsening urticaria and facial flushing; therefore, an additional dose of 0.5 mL of intramuscularly injected epinephrine was administered, with eventual resolution of the reaction. He avoided red meat, tick bites, and insect stings for an additional 2 years, at which time his level of alpha-gal IgE was 13.5 kU/L. He elected to proceed with a second ingestion challenge, consuming 1.5 lb of bacon and 2 oz of salami. Two and a half hours after eating the meal, he developed diffuse hives and was given 0.5 mL of intramuscular epinephrine, after which the hives resolved.

Avoidance of red meat had significantly decreased the patient’s quality of life. After discussing the lack of evidence on desensitization or induction of tolerance for AGS, he elected to attempt a food ladder approach to induction of tolerance using incremental increases in daily bacon ingestion. He started with half a piece of bacon daily and increased that amount by half a piece every 2 months. After 8 months, he was able to tolerate 2 strips of bacon without any reaction. The patient tested the success of tolerance induction by consuming small amounts of mammalian meat, such as a few bites of a hamburger and an individual pork rib. In June 2024, the patient underwent another food challenge in our clinic to test his limits of meat ingestion. He consumed 12 oz of beef steak, 4 oz of pork sausage, and 4 oz of lamb chops. He had no reaction; it appeared that the food ladder approach had induced tolerance to mammalian meat. Tolerance was sustained by consuming meat at least twice per week.

In July 2025, the patient had a breakthrough reaction after consuming pork cracklings (fried pork skin). His symptoms consisted of hives, which resolved after use of an epinephrine autoinjector. He has continued to consume other forms of meat without reactions. He attempted to consume cracklings along with a hamburger in November 2025, with a similar reaction that resolved after use of epinephrine. For both events, he had consumed no medication or alcohol, was not sick or sleep deprived, and had not exercised. He continues to consume meat other than cracklings and has had no other reactions. Thus, the patient did not achieve full tolerance despite ongoing meat consumption.

This case report describes the use of a food ladder as a therapeutic option for managing AGS when avoidance does not result in eventual tolerance and ongoing avoidance substantially decreases quality of life. In most cases, AGS resolves over time with avoidance of meat and other triggers. Resolution can be demonstrated with an in-office challenge or with a newer approach using graduated reintroduction at home.9 In cases in which resolution does not occur spontaneously, the safest option remains ongoing avoidance. This case also demonstrates that tolerance is not complete. Caution is still needed because reactions may still occur; therefore, patients should ensure that they have access to epinephrine. Research is needed to establish factors that decrease the threshold for triggering an allergic reaction. Proposed threshold-lowering factors include meat fat content as in this case, along with alcohol consumption, exercise, and aspirin ingestion.8
