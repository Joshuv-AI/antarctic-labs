Joint association of body roundness index and HOMA-IR with risk of MASLD in Korean populations
Authors: Kyo-sun Lee, Sung-Ho Ahn, Da-Hye Son, Yong-Jae Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs the global prevalence of obesity rises, the clinical focus is shifting from simple BMI to a systemic perspective that integrates body composition and metabolic health. This study evaluates the association between Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) and the combined influence of insulin resistance and obesity-related body shape indices.MethodsUtilizing the KoGES cohort (n = 3,196), participants were categorized into four groups based on the median values of HOMA-IR and the Body Roundness Index (BRI). MASLD was defined by an HSI score > 36, at least one metabolic component, and controlled alcohol consumption (< 30 g/day for men, < 20 g/day for women). Cox proportional hazard regression was conducted to assess the risk across groups, including interaction and subgroup analyses to determine the independence of these markers.ResultsBoth HOMA-IR and BRI were significant predictors of MASLD risk even when fully adjusted, with Hazard Ratios (HR) of 1.80 (95% CI: 1.52–2.12) and 2.33 (95% CI: 1.96–2.76), respectively. In the joint analysis, the group with both high HOMA-IR and high obesity-related body shape index (BRI) showed a 3.58-fold (95% CI: 2.84–4.51) increased risk compared to the reference group. Notably, no significant additive or multiplicative interactions were found, confirming that these two factors serve as independent and distinct predictive markers.ConclusionsCombining HOMA-IR with body shape-related obesity markers like BRI provides a more robust prediction of MASLD risk than individual indices. These findings suggest that integrated monitoring of metabolic and morphological profiles is essential for the early identification of high-risk populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02361-4.

Introduction

The prevalence of obesity has tripled worldwide over the past several decades, due to a more sedentary lifestyle and unhealthy diet [1]. Obesity has traditionally been defined by using Body Mass Index (BMI) as an indicator. However, in 2025, the Lancet proposed a new concept of obesity that goes beyond the traditional, simple definition based on BMI and instead takes into account various body composition indices together with complications affecting multiple organ systems [2]. One such organ is the liver, where obesity is a major driver of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), a condition that can progress to advanced liver disease and liver-related complications [3, 4].

Diagnosing obesity with BMI can misclassify individuals, as those above the obesity cutoff may not truly have excess body fat [5]. Excess adiposity is a well-established risk factor for numerous obesity-related complications, particularly cardiometabolic disorders and MASLD [6]. To address BMI’s limitations, several anthropometric indices such as A Body Shape Index (ABSI) and Body Roundness Index (BRI) have been developed to better capture visceral fat and obesity-related risk. The BRI, calculated from height and waist circumference (WC), correlates strongly with visceral adiposity and has emerged as a useful marker for identifying individuals at high risk of MASLD and related outcomes [7, 8].

Parallel to the rise of anthropometric indices, insulin resistance remains a fundamental pathophysiological link connecting obesity and MASLD. The homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) is a simple, fasting-based index that reliably reflects whole-body insulin resistance using fasting blood glucose and insulin levels [9, 10]. Increased insulin resistance enhances adipose tissue lipolysis and elevates circulating free fatty acids (FFAs), leading to an increased FFA overload in the liver [11].

Despite the established clinical value of both BRI and HOMA-IR, they reflect distinct biological dimensions. One captures structural adiposity, while the other addresses metabolic dysfunction. Recent studies have suggested that the combined assessment of obesity- and metabolism-related markers may improve risk stratification for obesity-related complications compared with the use of individual markers alone [12, 13]. However, whether a similar integrated approach can enhance risk prediction for incident MASLD remains unclear. This study aims to address this gap by utilizing data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES) cohort to investigate the joint association of HOMA-IR and BRI with incident MASLD and determine whether their combined assessment provides incremental prognostic value beyond either marker alone.

Methods

Study population

The study population was derived from the KoGES Ansan–Ansung cohort, a community-based longitudinal study established by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA). This cohort initially enrolled 10,030 adults aged 40–69 years at baseline (2001–2002) and conducted biennial follow-up examinations through the ninth survey cycle (2019–2020). Participants were recruited from two regions: an urban population in Ansan (n = 5,018) and a rural population in Ansung (n = 5,012), all of whom had resided in their respective areas for at least six months prior to enrollment. At each visit, detailed information including medical history, anthropometric measurements, and laboratory data was systematically collected.

Participants were followed from baseline until the occurrence of incident MASLD, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period. Follow-up duration was defined as the interval from the baseline examination to the first detection of MASLD.

Figure 1 illustrates the study population selection process. Participants were excluded for the following reasons: missing data for HOMA-IR (n = 299), missing BRI data (n = 8), insufficient data to define MASLD (n = 5,381), presence of MASLD at baseline (n = 748), and excessive alcohol consumption (≥ 60 g/day for men or ≥ 50 g/day for women; n = 394). After these exclusions, 3,196 participants were included in the final analysis

Flowchart of the study population selection

Participants were categorized into four groups based on combinations of high and low BRI and HOMA-IR, defined using median values. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study participation. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The KoGES Ansan–Ansung cohort was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the KDCA, and the current analysis received additional approval from the Institutional Review Board of Gangnam Severance Hospital (IRB No. 3-2023-0277).

Covariates

Anthropometric measurements were obtained using standardized protocols. Height and weight were measured to the nearest 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg, respectively, and BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height² (m²). Obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² [14]. WC was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib margin and the iliac crest.

Blood pressure (BP) was measured repeatedly, and the mean of the last two readings was used for analysis. After an overnight fast of at least 8 h, blood samples were collected to measure fasting plasma glucose, insulin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and C-reactive protein levels. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol was calculated using the Friedewald equation when triglyceride levels were < 400 mg/dL.

Hypertension was defined as systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medications [15]. Diabetes mellitus was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test, glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%, or use of antidiabetic medications [16]. Dyslipidemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥ 160 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol < 40 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥ 200 mg/dL, or use of lipid-lowering agents [17]. Baseline sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle factors were assessed using standardized questionnaires administered as part of the KoGES protocol [18].

Baseline sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle factors were assessed using standardized questionnaires. Alcohol intake was quantified in grams per day, and participants were categorized as current drinkers or non-drinkers. Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and expressed as metabolic equivalent task hours per day (MET-h/day), categorized into low (< 7.5), moderate (7.5–30), and high (> 30) levels. Educational attainment (≤ middle school, high school, ≥ college) and monthly household income (< 1, 1–2, and > 2 million KRW) were each categorized into three levels, with the lowest categories defined as low education and low income, respectively.

Assessment of BRI and HOMA-IR

Insulin resistance was assessed using the HOMA-IR, calculated as fasting insulin (µIU/mL) × fasting plasma glucose (mg/dL) / 405 [19]. Assessment of MASLD

Steatotic Liver Disease (SLD) was defined as a hepatic steatosis index (HSI) > 36, a well-validated noninvasive marker of hepatic steatosis [21]. The HSI was calculated as 8 × (ALT/AST ratio) + BMI (+ 2, if female; +2, if diabetes).

MASLD was defined by the presence of SLD, low alcohol intake (< 30 g/day for men and < 20 g/day for women), and at least one cardiometabolic risk factor [22].

Cardiometabolic risk factors were defined according to metabolic dysfunction criteria and included: (1) overweight or central obesity (body mass index ≥ 23 kg/m² or waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men and ≥ 85 cm in women); (2) impaired glucose metabolism (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL, type 2 diabetes, or use of antidiabetic medications); (3) elevated BP (≥ 130/85 mmHg or use of antihypertensive medications); (4) hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL or use of lipid-lowering agents); and (5) low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (≤ 40 mg/dL in men and ≤ 50 mg/dL in women).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on their distribution, and categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences across groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. The cumulative incidence of MASLD according to BRI and HOMA-IR groups was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier curves, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident MASLD. Participants with low HOMA-IR/ low BRI were used as the reference group. The fully adjusted model included age, sex, alcohol consumption, smoking status, educational level, monthly household income, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Subgroup analyses were performed according to age, sex, body mass index, diabetes status, and smoking status. Multiplicative interaction between HOMA-IR and BRI was assessed by including a cross-product term in the Cox proportional hazards model. Additive interaction was evaluated using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (SI) to examine whether the combined effect of HOMA-IR and BRI on MASLD risk exceeded the sum of their individual effects [23]. As a supplementary analysis, the associations of HOMA-IR and BRI with MASLD were evaluated using continuous variables, with HRs calculated per 1-standard deviation (SD) increment. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models were employed to characterize potential non-linear dose-response patterns.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

General characteristics of the study population

Baseline characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. A total of 3,196 participants from the KoGES data were included in this study. The mean age of the participants was 50.69 ± 8.63 and 2,270(71%) of the participants were men. They were categorized into four groups according to HOMA-IR and BRI status; low HOMA-IR/low BRI (n = 1,314), low HOMA-IR/high BRI (n = 490), high HOMA-IR/low BRI (n = 803), and high HOMA-IR/high BRI (n = 589).

Significant differences were observed across the four groups for all variables analyzed (p < 0.001). The high HOMA-IR/high BRI group exhibited the highest levels of BMI (25.84 ± 2.11 kg/m2), waist circumference (88.46 ± 5.07 cm), systolic BP (125.97 ± 18.57 mmHg), and HbA1c (5.70%, 5.40-6.00%). ALT, triglycerides, and total cholesterol were also elevated in this group, while the low HOMA-IR/low BRI group showed the most favorable levels. Groups with high HOMA-IR had higher fasting glucose levels, while groups with high BRI had greater BMI and waist circumference measures. Furthermore, engagement in moderate-intensity physical activity was least common in the high HOMA-IR/high BRI group.

Association between HOMA-IR/BRI and MASLD

Based on the median values of 1.45 for HOMA-IR and 3.61 for BRI, the cohort was stratified into high and low exposure groups. The incidence of MASLD was markedly higher among participants with high HOMA-IR (12.61 per 1,000 person-years) and high BRI (12.81 per 1,000 person-years) compared to those in the low-level groups (Table 2). In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), both high HOMA-IR and high BRI individually remained significant predictors of MASLD. They respectively had adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) of 1.80 (95% CI: 1.52–2.12) and 2.33 (95% CI: 1.96–2.76). When participants were categorized by the combined status of HOMA-IR and BRI, a synergistic increase in MASLD risk was observed. Compared with the reference group (low HOMA-IR/low BRI), individuals in the high HOMA-IR/high BRI group exhibited the highest risk, with an aHR of 3.58 (95% CI: 2.84–4.51) after accounting for all potential confounders. Notably, the association with MASLD was more pronounced in the high BRI groups regardless of HOMA-IR status. The low HOMA-IR/high BRI group showed a higher risk (aHR: 2.60; 95% CI: 2.00–3.37) than the high HOMA-IR/low BRI group (aHR: 1.88; 95% CI: 1.49–2.37).

When individually analyzed with Kaplan-Meier method, high HOMA-IR (Fig. 2-A) and high BRI (Fig. 2-B) were both significantly associated with lower numbers at risk over the follow-up period (p < 0.001 for both). When compared among the four HOMA‑IR/BRI groups (Fig. 2-C), the low HOMA-IR/low BRI group had the highest number of participants at risk, with the high HOMA-IR/low BRI group, low HOMA-IR/high BRI group, and high HOMA-IR/high BRI group showing progressively lower counts. The high HOMA‑IR/high BRI group showed the highest cumulative incidence of MASLD at the end of the follow-up period (p < 0.001).

We have also analyzed the risk of MASLD using HOMA-IR and BRI as continuous variables. Each standard deviation increase of both indices was associated with the elevated risk of MASLD in adjusted models except for HOMA-IR after full adjustment (Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, we performed sensitivity analysis using restricted cubic splines. The spline curve showed a non-linear association for HOMA-IR (Supplementary Fig. 1-A) and a progressive increase across the observed range for BRI (Supplementary Fig. 1-B). Supplementary Table and both spline curves are shown in the Supplementary File.

Subgroup analysis

The combination of HOMA-IR and BRI with the association of MASLD incidences was divided by subgroups of age, sex, BMI, diabetes, and current smoking (Table 3). While the elevated risk of MASLD was consistent along the subgroups, there were significant interactions between the combined HOMA-IR/BRI categories and age.

Discussion

Recent conceptual advances have redefined obesity as a clinical condition characterized by adiposity-related organ dysfunction rather than excess body weight alone. The 2025 Lancet Commission on Clinical Obesity emphasizes that clinical obesity should be defined not only by anthropometric or body fat measures but also by evidence of impaired organ or tissue function [2]. In this context, MASLD represents one of the key manifestations of obesity-related organ dysfunction. This shift toward a systemic view of obesity is reflected in evolving cardiometabolic frameworks. While the Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome [24] integrates metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular dysfunction, recent efforts have expanded this concept to include hepatic involvement, leading to the proposed cardiovascular–kidney–liver–metabolic (CKLM) model [25]. Within this framework, the clinical importance of accurately identifying individuals at risk for MASLD becomes increasingly evident.

The cohort-specific median values used to classify HOMA-IR (1.45) and BRI (3.61) were comparable to those previously reported in Korean and East Asian populations [26, 27]. This suggests that our study population broadly reflects the metabolic characteristics of the reference population and supports the validity of the classification framework used in the present analyses. Using these thresholds, we found that both HOMA-IR and BRI were independently associated with incident MASLD after adjustment for potential confounders. Importantly, participants with concurrent elevations in both markers exhibited the highest risk of MASLD, indicating that anthropometric and metabolic abnormalities provide complementary prognostic information beyond either marker alone. These findings are consistent with previous studies supporting the utility of HOMA-IR [10, 28–30] as a marker of insulin resistance and BRI as a marker of visceral adiposity and MASLD risk [7, 31, 32].

The observed association between HOMA-IR and MASLD is biologically plausible given the central role of insulin resistance in hepatic fat accumulation [22, 33]. HOMA-IR is derived directly from fasting glucose and insulin concentrations and therefore provides a reliable estimate of systemic insulin resistance. In insulin-resistant states, adipose tissue lipolysis is enhanced, resulting in excessive release of free fatty acids (FFA) into the circulation. These FFAs are subsequently delivered to the liver, where they contribute to increased de novo lipogenesis and triglyceride accumulation [34]. Over time, these metabolic disturbances promote hepatic steatosis and facilitate the progression of MASLD.

Furthermore, our findings suggest that fat distribution, particularly central obesity, plays a critical role in the development of MASLD beyond overall adiposity as captured by BMI [35]. The BRI, an anthropometric index reflecting visceral fat accumulation, enables the quantification of body shape using only waist circumference and height and thus complements conventional measures of obesity [7]. Notably, individuals with similar BMI values may exhibit substantially different metabolic risks depending on their fat distribution, and central obesity has been consistently associated with hepatic steatosis even after adjustment for general adiposity [36, 37]. These findings are aligned with the evolving concept that obesity should not be viewed solely as excess body weight but rather in terms of the qualitative and regional distribution of adipose tissue [2, 38]. Although the underlying mechanisms are multifactorial, central obesity induces adipose tissue dysfunction by increased pro inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin 6 and reduced adiponectin levels [39]. The loss of adiponectin’s insulin-sensitizing and anti-inflammatory effects promotes a systemic proinflammatory cascade that accelerates hepatic injury and MASLD progression [40–42]. Taken together, these mechanisms suggest that insulin resistance and visceral adiposity contribute to MASLD through interconnected yet partially distinct biological pathways.

To improve clinical interpretability beyond cohort-specific medians, we conducted supplementary analyses using continuous scaling per SD. Although both HOMA-IR and BRI showed dose–response relationships in unadjusted models, the association for HOMA-IR attenuated after full adjustment with its spline analysis showing a non-linear, plateauing pattern. BRI remained robust with its spline analysis showing a consistently progressive increase. These findings suggest that BRI may reflect a more stable and progressive association with MASLD risk, whereas the effect of HOMA-IR appears to be more susceptible to attenuation after adjustment for metabolic covariates.

In the subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, BMI, diabetes status and smoking, several subgroups deviated from the positive association of combined elevations of HOMA-IR and BRI with MASLD. Among participants aged 65 years and older, the HR was not estimable due to the absence of individuals meeting the low HOMA-IR/low BRI. Furthermore, a significant interaction was observed between the combined HOMA-IR/BRI categories and age. However, this should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size in this older subgroup within the KoGES cohort of individuals aged 40 to 69. The interaction likely reflects these demographic constraints rather than a robust biological difference. For individuals with BMI 25 kg/m2 and above or with diabetes, the subgroups characterized by discordant HOMA-IR and BRI profiles where only one marker was elevated (i.e., high HOMA-IR/low BRI or low BRI/high HOMA-IR) were not significantly associated with increased risk of MASLD. This likely reflects a potential ceiling effect where strong baseline risk factors diminish the discriminative value of a single marker. In contrast, the simultaneous elevation of both indices remained significantly associated with MASLD risk even in these higher-risk populations.

Formal interaction analyses using additive and multiplicative models showed no evidence of interaction between BRI and HOMA-IR in relation to MASLD risk. This indicates that the two markers exert independent prognostic effects on the development of MASLD. These findings suggest that adiposity-related and insulin resistance-related pathways may contribute independently to MASLD development.

The significance of this study lies in its use of cohort data of the Korean population to demonstrate that the simultaneous consideration of anthropometric indices and insulin resistance markers is a better predictor of MASLD incidence than when evaluated individually.

Despite this, our study has limitations. First, we defined hepatic steatosis by using the HSI, a surrogate marker, rather than direct imaging. While HSI is validated for large-scale studies, it also includes variables such as BMI and diabetes, which overlap with the MASLD definition and other exposure variables. This could lead to the possibility of partial collinearity and inflation of the observed associations. Further validation using more precise diagnostic tools such as MRI-PDFF (the current gold standard for quantifying liver fat) [43], is warranted. The generalizability of our findings may have some restrictions, as the study population was limited to individuals aged 40–69 years and was predominantly male (71%). Given the rising burden of MASLD in younger populations [4], and the potential for sex-specific differences in disease pathophysiology, including hormonal influences [44], caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to the broader population. Due to data constraints, we were unable to strictly exclude participants with certain underlying liver conditions, including hepatitis B and hepatocellular carcinoma, which may have influenced liver-related outcomes. In addition, information on several potentially relevant determinants of metabolic dysfunction and MASLD, including genetic susceptibility, dietary patterns [45], environmental exposures [46], and broader social determinants of health was not available. Therefore, residual confounding from these factors cannot be excluded. Our analysis relied on baseline measurements of BRI and HOMA-IR without accounting for temporal changes over the 17-year follow-up. Given that these indices may vary with aging, lifestyle, and treatment, this assumption may introduce non-differential misclassification and attenuate the observed associations. Future studies incorporating large-scale trajectory modeling may help clarify the dynamic relationship between these indices and MASLD risk. Lastly, as this is an observational cohort study, a definitive causal relationship cannot be established. Future research involving experimental designs or large-scale Mendelian Randomization (MR) analyses are warranted to further clarify the underlying causality.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the integration of body composition indices and insulin resistance markers provide a more comprehensive assessment of MASLD risk. The simultaneous evaluation of both indices mitigates the diagnostic gaps in relying on either marker independently. They also have the advantage of being easily obtainable in outpatient settings. Given that MASLD is one of the most prevalent complications of the rising obesity epidemic, our findings are expected to contribute significantly to the early prediction and systematic management of this condition in clinical practice.
