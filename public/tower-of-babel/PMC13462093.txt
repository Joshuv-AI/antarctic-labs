Safety profile of chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell therapy in relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma: A systematic review and meta‐analysis of proportions from clinical trials
Authors: Charalampos Filippatos, Ioannis Ntanasis‐Stathopoulos, Areti Voulomenou, Panagiotis Malandrakis, Efstathios Kastritis, Maria Gavriatopoulou, Meletios‐Athanasios Dimopoulos, Evangelos Terpos
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Multiple myeloma (MM) remains the second most common haematological malignancy worldwide, with limited treatment options and poor prognoses in patients with relapsed or refractory disease (RRMM). 1 Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy has emerged as a revolutionary immunotherapy, offering unprecedented deep and durable responses in heavily pretreated RRMM. 2 However, the remarkable efficacy of these regimens is accompanied by a unique and often severe toxicity profile. 3 , 4 , 5 As CAR T‐cell therapies move from highly controlled clinical trial settings into the real‐world, a comprehensive, pooled assessment of their safety profile is essential for informed clinical decision‐making and optimal patient management.

The present meta‐analysis was performed following Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta‐analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. 6 The study protocol was discussed and agreed upon in advance by all authors and was registered in (PROSPERO ID: CRD420251179952). A systematic search was conducted in the PubMed, Scopus and ClinicalTrials.gov databases and from inception until 5 August 2025, to identify peer‐reviewed and full‐text articles of clinical trials on CAR T‐cell therapies for RRMM. The full search strategy and data extraction procedure are included in the Supporting Information.

Statistical analysis included the pooling of studies and subsequent exploratory subanalyses by prespecified variables (region, phase, CAR T‐cell type and CAR T‐cell manufacturing virus type). Random‐effects models were used to calculate pooled proportions. Post hoc univariate meta‐regression analyses were performed in the case of 10 or more study entries, with respect to prespecified covariates (sample size, percentage of males, median age, median follow‐up, median prior lines of treatment [LoT], percentage of patients with high‐risk cytogenetic abnormalities [HRCAs] and who underwent autologous haematopoietic stem cell transplantation [AHSCT]). Across this analysis, I 2 < 40% or p (Q‐test) <0.10 was considered low heterogeneity and p‐values <0.05 defined statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using R/R‐Studio version 2024.04.2+764 (Posit Software, PBC). Risk of bias and study quality were assessed by two independent authors, as detailed in the Supporting Information.

Through the search strategy, a total of 1916 records were identified. Ultimately, 32 records annunciating safety outcomes from 32 clinical trials on CAR T‐cell therapies for RRMM were considered eligible and were included in this meta‐analysis (Figure 1; Table S1).

PRISMA 2020 flowchart.

The pooled incidence of any‐grade cytokine release syndrome (CRS) was 84% (95% confidence interval (CI): 80%–88%; I 2 = 51.8%). Grade ≥3 CRS was rare, with a pooled rate of 5% (95% CI: 4%–7%) and negligible heterogeneity (I 2 = 0.0%). Increasing sample size was significantly associated with a decrease in the reported incidence of severe CRS (β = −0.010, p = 0.007). Additionally, a higher number of prior LoT was significantly associated with an increased incidence of grade ≥3 CRS (β = 0.182, p = 0.031). The pooled incidence of any‐grade haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) was 3% (95% CI: 2%–6%; I 2 = 25.7%).

The pooled rate for any‐grade neurotoxicity was 13% (95% CI: 10%–18%; I 2 = 68.9%). Region emerged as a significant moderator (p = 0.005), with US trials showing higher rates (23%) compared to Asia (6%). Meta‐regression also revealed that a higher percentage of patients with prior AHSCT was significantly associated with a higher incidence of any‐grade neurotoxicity (β = 2.156, p = 0.016).

Any‐grade immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) occurred in 8% of patients (95% CI: 4%–15%; I 2 = 64.8%). Target antigen significantly influenced ICANS (p = 0.007), with B‐cell maturation antigen (BCMA)‐targeted therapies (12%) showing higher rates than G‐protein coupled receptor family C group 5 member D (GPRC5D) (2%). Higher median patient age (β = 0.232, p = 0.017), longer median follow‐up duration (β = 0.038, p = 0.021), higher number of prior LoT (β = 0.427, p < 0.001) and a higher percentage of prior AHSCT (β = 3.066, p = 0.047) were all significantly associated with an increased incidence of any‐grade ICANS.

Severe neurotoxicity and grade ≥3 ICANS remained low at 4% and 3%, respectively, with negligible heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%). Larger sample sizes were significantly correlated with lower reported rates of high‐grade ICANS (β = −0.011, p = 0.032). Furthermore, a higher number of prior LoT was significantly associated with an increased incidence of grade ≥3 ICANS (β = 0.457, p = 0.017), while a higher percentage of prior AHSCT was associated with an increased incidence of grade ≥3 neurotoxicity (β = 2.631, p = 0.044).

Neutropenia was the most frequent serious haematological event, reported in 86% of the patients (95% CI: 81%–90%; I 2 = 70.1%). A higher percentage of patients with HRCAs was significantly associated with a higher incidence of grade ≥3 neutropenia (β = 2.773, p = 0.037). The pooled rate of serious leukopenia events was 75% (95% CI: 63%–84%; I 2 = 90.5%). Significant differences were found by region (p < 0.001), with global trials reporting lower rates (33%) than Asia (87%), and by target (p = 0.002), where GPRC5D‐related therapies showed higher rates (>90%) than BCMA (62%). A significant inverse relationship was observed between sample size and the reported rate of high‐grade leukopenia (β = −0.013, p = 0.013).

Grade ≥3 anaemia events occurred in 52% of patients (95% CI: 45%–59%; I 2 = 79.4%). Increasing median patient age was significantly associated with a higher incidence of grade ≥3 anaemia (β = 0.130, p = 0.017). The pooled rate of severe lymphopenia events was 52% (95% CI: 39%–65%; I 2 = 87.6%). Study phase (p = 0.002) and region (p < 0.001) were significant moderators, with trials stemming from Asia reporting the highest rates (84%). A higher percentage of male patients in the study cohorts was significantly associated with a lower incidence of severe lymphopenia (β = −0.123, p = 0.004).

The pooled rate of grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia was 50% (95% CI: 44%–56%; I 2 = 71.4%). Significant differences were noted by phase (p = 0.001) and target (p = 0.045), with GPRC5D (66%) and BCMA/CD19 (62%) showing higher rates.

The pooled incidence of any‐grade infections was 53% (95% CI: 46%–60%; I 2 = 76.9%). Region was a significant moderator (p = 0.032), with European (70%) and Global (62%) trials reporting higher rates than US (43%) or Asian (48%) trials. Severe infections (grade ≥3) occurred in 21% of patients (95% CI: 18%–26%; I 2 = 43.5%).

Finally, in five trials involving 749 RRMM patients, safety outcomes were comparable between the two currently approved CAR T‐cell therapies.

Risk of bias was low in randomized trials and moderate in non‐randomized studies. GRADE certainty was moderate for any‐grade HLH and severe CRS, ICANS and neurotoxicity while it was low to very low for any‐grade systemic and high‐grade haematological toxicities.

This meta‐analysis of 32 trials identifies a dual safety reality: while severe immune‐related toxicities are relatively rare, high‐grade haematological toxicities and infections are common, regardless of the CAR T‐cell construct used.

Our meta‐regression identified prior LoT as a significant driver of grade ≥3 CRS and ICANS, suggesting that T‐cell kinetics or baseline inflammatory states in heavily pretreated patients may lower the threshold for severe systemic events. 7 The high prevalence of grade ≥3 neutropenia (86%) and leukopenia (75%) suggests a synergy between lymphodepletion, CAR‐T activation and underlying marrow exhaustion. 8 Moreover, our analysis demonstrated that a higher burden of HRCAs significantly predicts grade ≥3 neutropenia, potentially reflecting more aggressive disease‐related marrow displacement or reduced haematopoietic reserve. 9

The significant incidence of infections (53% any‐grade, 21% grade ≥3) underlines a critical concern in clinical practice. 10 Clinically, this necessitates rigorous long‐term surveillance and the consideration of extended intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)/subcutaneous immunoglobulin (scIG) support or antibiotic prophylaxis in high‐risk patients. 11 Additionally, our subgroup analysis identified study region as a significant moderator of infection rates, which may reflect differences in prophylactic antimicrobial protocols or environmental pathogen exposure across global sites.

A key contribution of this study is the role of baseline “marrow fitness” in neurotoxicity. We found that a higher percentage of prior AHSCT among trial populations was significantly associated with any‐grade neurotoxicity, any‐grade ICANS and grade ≥3 neurotoxicity. This suggests that prior intensive alkylator exposure may heighten vulnerability to neuro‐inflammatory stressors.

Another key finding is that increasing median age was significantly associated with higher rates of grade ≥3 anaemia and any‐grade ICANS. This is particularly relevant as the RRMM population is predominantly elderly with pre‐existing comorbidities. These findings suggest that while CAR T‐cell therapy is feasible in older or frail patients, 12 clinicians should anticipate a more fragile haematopoietic reserve. 13 , 14 Moreover, the association between longer follow‐up and higher ICANS reporting indicates that delayed neurotoxic events—such as the movement and neurocognitive treatment‐emergent events (MNTs) observed with BCMA‐targeted products—require vigilant, long‐term monitoring beyond the immediate post‐infusion period. 15

To our knowledge, this represents one of the most comprehensive pooled analyses of CAR T‐cell toxicities in RRMM, utilizing meta‐regression and subgroup analyses to move beyond simple pooled proportions in order to identify key moderators of toxicity. Despite this, most studies lacked a control group to distinguish treatment‐related adverse events (AEs) from those related to the underlying disease state or course. Second, we observed significant interstudy heterogeneity (I 2 > 50%) for several outcomes, which may limit the generalizability of the pooled estimates. Furthermore, without long‐term follow‐up, rare but significant late complications, such as secondary lymphomas or delayed/late‐onset toxicities following CAR T‐cell therapy, may fail to be captured. Additionally, the lack of standardized definitions for non‐ICANS neurotoxicity across trials and the limited reporting of late‐onset MNTs may constrain the clinical depth of our neurotoxicity assessment.

In conclusion, CAR T‐cell therapy in RRMM is characterized by high rates of predictable, low‐grade systemic, immune‐related toxicity and nearly universal, high‐grade haematological complications. The identification of key baseline characteristics as moderators of toxicity provides a roadmap for more personalized patient selection and proactive management. Future research should focus on standardized reporting of late‐onset toxicities and the development of strategies to mitigate prolonged cytopenias without compromising CAR T‐cell efficacy.
