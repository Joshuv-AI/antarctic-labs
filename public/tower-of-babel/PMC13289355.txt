Timely antenatal care access and uptake among migrant and ethnically diverse women in England: a qualitative study of barriers and facilitators
Authors: Shuby Puthussery, Marie Therese Sangy Manjini Anandaraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTIntroductionTimely antenatal care (ANC) is a critical determinant of maternal and neonatal outcomes. In the UK, women from migrant and ethnic minority backgrounds are more likely to initiate ANC later, attend fewer appointments and experience poorer maternal and infant outcomes. This study aimed to describe barriers and facilitators to timely ANC access and uptake from the perspectives of migrant and ethnically diverse women who initiated care late.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews with 20 women from migrant and/or ethnically diverse backgrounds who initiated ANC late (>10 weeks’ gestation) in the East of England, UK. Women, recruited through purposive and snowball sampling, were interviewed between October 2024 and January 2025 in-person, online or by telephone. All interviews were transcribed and analysed using a framework approach.ResultsFour interconnected themes, comprising 12 subthemes, captured women’s perspectives: perceptions about ANC, perceived facilitators and barriers to timely access and uptake, information needs and preferences and strategies to improve ANC access and uptake. All women recognised the importance of ANC. However, many reported limited awareness of when and how to initiate care and found ANC pathways difficult to navigate, particularly those unfamiliar with the UK healthcare system. Key facilitators of access and uptake included awareness of the importance of ANC, supportive healthcare professionals, relational continuity and support from family and friends. Barriers included uncertainty about care pathways, long waiting times, rushed appointments and language and logistical challenges. Suggested strategies to improve ANC access included accessible information on ANC pathways, stronger community and peer support, greater opportunities for direct engagement with professionals and integration of digital technologies into ANC services.ConclusionsFindings highlight need for easy-to-navigate ANC pathways, with targeted community outreach and supported navigation, culturally responsive communication with effective interpretation services and greater relational continuity to improve equitable ANC access and uptake.Keywords: Community Health, Female, Public Health, Qualitative Research

Introduction

Antenatal care (ANC) refers to care provided by skilled healthcare professionals to women during pregnancy to optimise health outcomes for themselves and their babies.1 Timely and adequate ANC enables early detection and management of pregnancy-related complications and is a critical determinant of positive maternal and neonatal outcomes.1 2 Current guidelines in the UK recommend women to have their first antenatal appointment within 10+0 weeks of gestation and subsequently have 7–10 visits depending on parity and complications.2

While most women in the UK access ANC early, large disparities in uptake persist; women from migrant and ethnic minority backgrounds are significantly more likely to initiate ANC later and attend fewer appointments than recommended, compared with white British women.3 4 Late ANC initiation and insufficient uptake are associated with increased risks of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes.5 6 Among women who died during pregnancy or following childbirth in the UK, the proportion who received the recommended level of ANC was significantly low.5 In an ethnically diverse and socially disadvantaged maternal cohort, women who booked their first ANC appointment after 20 weeks’ gestation were 4.08 times more likely to give birth to an extremely preterm infant and 3.12 times more likely to deliver an infant with extremely low birth weight compared with women who booked by ≤12 weeks’ gestation.6

Qualitative studies exploring maternity care experiences of migrant and ethnic minority in the UK have found that access to care was highly contextualised and shaped by individual circumstances, with late ANC initiation emerging as a recurrent theme.7,9 A recent systematic review examining migrant and ethnic minority women’s experiences of accessing ANC in high-income European countries identified multiple interacting and cyclical factors—including knowledge of services, language and communication barriers and prior healthcare experiences—that influence both initial and ongoing access.10

While studies focusing exclusively on UK-born ethnic minority women’s experiences are sparse, evidence suggests disparities in maternity care access and experiences are not confined to migrant women alone. Concerns have been reported about the care experiences of women from ethnically diverse backgrounds in general, including those born in the UK, in relation to communication, involvement in decision-making and feeling listened to by healthcare professionals.11,14 Reports on racial disparities in maternity care further highlight experiences of stereotyping, dismissal of concerns and reduced confidence in services among black and ethnically minoritised women.15

Although a range of structural and organisational factors mediate women’s access to ANC,10 studies have seldom explored the lived experiences and perspectives of migrant and ethnically diverse women who initiate ANC late, limiting the evidence base for improving timely and equitable access. This study aimed to address this gap by exploring the perspectives of these women who started ANC late (>10 weeks’ gestation) in East of England, UK. In this study, migrant women are defined as women born outside the UK.16 The term ethnically diverse or ethnic minority women refers to women who self-identify as belonging to ethnic groups other than the majority white British population.17 We explored perspectives of migrant and ethnically diverse women, including both UK-born women and those born outside the UK, regardless of length of residence or immigration status.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a qualitative study using in-depth interviews and followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines18 (online supplemental file 1). Interviews were guided by a semi-structured topic guide, with prompts to elicit in-depth accounts of participants’ experiences. The study was informed by the conceptual framework of access to healthcare developed by Levesque et al, which defines access as a dynamic interaction between health service characteristics (approachability, acceptability, availability/accommodation, affordability and appropriateness) and individuals’ abilities to perceive, seek, reach, pay for and engage with services.19 This framework informed the development of the topic guide and provided the initial analytical structure.

Setting

The study was conducted in areas of Bedfordshire, Luton and Milton Keynes in East of England, UK. These areas are home to a population of approximately 1 million, with pockets of significant ethnic diversity and socioeconomic disadvantage.20 Luton is the most ethnically diverse, with ~55% of residents identifying as belonging to black and minority ethnic backgrounds, and a high proportion of migrant populations, including Pakistani, Eastern European (particularly Polish and Romanian), Irish and African communities.21 The area experiences high levels of social deprivation, ranking 70th most deprived among 317 local authorities in England.21 Reflecting the diversity of the population, the local maternity services serve a large proportion of migrant and ethnically diverse populations, and late initiation of ANC is common with nearly one-fifth of women starting care after 12 weeks of pregnancy.4

Participants and recruitment

Participants included 20 expectant or recent mothers from migrant and/or ethnically diverse backgrounds who started ANC late (>10 weeks’ gestation) (table 1). Women were recruited if they were aged 18–45 years, were either currently pregnant or had given birth within the past 3 years and had initiated ANC after 10 weeks of gestation in their most recent pregnancy. Additional eligibility criteria included ability to communicate in English and the capacity to provide written informed consent.

Characteristics of participants

Purposive and snowball sampling were used to recruit participants, with a combination of in-person and online recruitment strategies to maximise representation of women from key ethnic minority groups and migrant backgrounds. Flyers describing the study’s purpose, eligibility criteria and researcher contact details were displayed in community centres, faith-based organisations, cultural associations and other trusted venues inviting interested women to contact the research team directly. Online advertisements were shared in local Facebook, Twitter and WhatsApp groups. Participation was voluntary and each participant received a £20 Amazon e-gift voucher as a token of appreciation.

A total of 30 participants expressed interest in taking part and 20 were interviewed. Ten did not participate due to childcare responsibilities, work-related constraints and non-response to follow-up.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews conducted between October 2024 and January 2025. Interviews lasted between 40 and 80 min. Participants chose preferred mode of interview, including online using Microsoft Teams (n=12), over telephone (n=6) or in-person in community settings (n=2). This flexibility enabled inclusion of those facing barriers to in-person interviews, due to transportation or childcare constraints. The second author (MTSMA), a female midwife researcher trained in qualitative methods at doctoral level, conducted all interviews in English language using a semi-structured topic guide (online supplemental file 2). The topic guide was developed based on the framework proposed by Levesque et al,19 expert input and insights from previous research.10 It included open-ended questions and probes to elicit in-depth responses and was pilot tested, with minor refinements made to improve clarity of questions.

Interviews were scheduled at times convenient for participants; there was no prior relationship between the interviewer and participants. Field notes were taken during and after each interview to capture contextual details and support interpretation during analysis. A reflexive approach was maintained throughout data collection, with ongoing reflection on researcher positionality and regular team debriefings to enhance rigour. The final number of participants was determined based on data saturation, with no new themes identified in the last two interviews. A brief questionnaire was used to collect demographic information, including country of birth and length of residence in the UK.

Data analysis and synthesis

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and anonymised with numbers to protect participant identity. Analysis was conducted using the framework approach22 supported by NVivo software (Release 1.7.2). Data were coded deductively using the healthcare services access domains outlined by Levesque et al,19 while allowing for inductive coding for data that did not fit the framework. The second author (MTSMA) took the lead in developing the coding framework (online supplemental file 3) following data familiarisation through repeated reading of the transcripts, and subsequently coding them systematically line-by-line. Codes were regularly reviewed by the first author (SP) to ensure accuracy and consistency and to develop a shared conceptual framework, represented through a hierarchical coding tree. Codes were then grouped into themes and subthemes, with relevant data extracts organised and patterns systematically explored and interpreted. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus, and the thematic structure was iteratively refined to enhance analytical rigour and coherence.

Patient and public involvement

Three patient and public involvement (PPI) contributors were involved in the research process. Two of the PPI members had a migration background, and one was born in the UK with an ethnic minority background. In addition to supporting the development of recruitment materials and strategies, they contributed to the interview topic guide enhancing its clarity, inclusivity and relevance to local communities. Their input also strengthened participant engagement by ensuring that communication was informative and respectful to local communities.

Findings

Twenty women from diverse ethnic backgrounds, migration statuses and sociodemographic characteristics (table 1 and online supplemental file 4) shared their perspectives on initial and ongoing ANC access.

Participants were aged 26–40 years and had varied educational backgrounds with more than three-quarters having higher education. Most were employed, although a few were unable to work due to personal or health-related reasons.

Four interconnected overarching themes, comprising 12 subthemes, captured women’s perspectives: perceptions about ANC, perceived facilitators and barriers to timely access and uptake, information needs and preferences and strategies to improve ANC access and uptake (figure 1). These themes are presented alongside relevant nuances related to migrant status to provide the context for support needs. Additional quotes are provided in online supplemental file 5.

Perceptions about ANC

The theme comprised four subthemes: perceived importance and value of ANC, awareness of ANC timing and processes, challenges in navigating ANC pathways and differences between ANC systems in home countries and the UK.

Perceived importance and value of ANC

Overall, women recognised the vital importance of ANC and viewed appointments as essential for ensuring their own and their babies’ health and well-being. They ascribed high value to the reassurance from healthcare professionals through regular check-ups and monitoring of vital signs, as well as opportunities for early detection and prevention of complications or medical conditions, which they felt might otherwise go unnoticed.

Lots of benefits… List things like… being able to have the reassurance of having obviously for the first baby… uh checking for things like blood pressure to see if you’ve got preeclampsia, you know, the diabetes check. (Participant 2)

Awareness of ANC timing and processes

Many participants reported that they began ANC later than the recommended 10 weeks of gestation due to limited awareness of when care should ideally begin. They had no knowledge of guidelines that recommended first ANC appointment by 10 weeks of pregnancy, nor were informed about the expected number of appointments throughout pregnancy.

About visit before 10 weeks… I was not aware of. If I’m being very honest with you, I was not aware of at all. (Participant 3)

Some participants reported that unplanned pregnancies and/or late recognition of pregnancy contributed to delays in initiating ANC.

I didn’t know that I was pregnant…I hadn’t had my period for some time…so it wasn’t really in my mind at the time. (Participant 1)

Women who had recently migrated to the UK described how the unfamiliarity with UK healthcare systems caused uncertainty and, in some cases, hesitation to initiate contact with maternity services.

Absolutely. I think because I come from a country where ANC is completely different to UK. (Participant 3)

…but understanding. it so happened like for GP (General Practitioner) it was different… for maternity it was different (in the UK). So that is a challenge. (Participant 15)

Even among those who had been residing in the UK for some time, there was confusion about how to initiate ANC processes on finding out about their pregnancy, including where to go for ANC or whom to approach for information.

Challenges in navigating ANC pathways

Participants described a range of difficulties in navigating ANC pathways. While some were referred to midwives by their general practitioners (GPs) for arranging their first ANC appointment, others had to make multiple calls, complete self-referral forms or seek support from healthcare professionals and community organisations.

So, my next step was to call… my doctor, my local care trust, my local doctor surgery and say what… and then had to call like the borough midwife team and get a hold of them, and then I met there. (Participant 14)

The fragmentation in ANC pathways contributed to confusion, especially for those with a migration background, as they were unfamiliar with how services are organised in the UK. Some women expected their GP to manage their pregnancy care, while others had to learn that community midwifery teams were responsible for antenatal services. Navigating multiple points of contact—such as GPs, self-referral portals and midwifery teams—often led to delays in ANC booking.

Differences between ANC systems in home countries and the UK

Women with a migration background frequently compared their experiences of ANC in the UK with maternity care systems in countries of origin, where it was usually managed by GPs or obstetricians/gynaecologists. As a result, the UK’s midwife-led model was often unfamiliar or previously unknown to them, leading to initial uncertainty about whether they were receiving appropriate care.

I think it’s different between my country and here. In my country, it’s we have all already the appointment with the doctor… Gynaecologist. Not like with midwife. And it’s a different… (Participant 7)

Some women were unsure whether midwives were qualified to oversee pregnancy care, particularly during their initial appointments. Many women, however, later came to appreciate the quality and professionalism of midwifery care in the UK. Several women spoke positively about the structure, consistency and support provided within the UK maternity care system. Compared with their home countries, where access to routine scans or regular check-ups was often limited, they found the UK system as more systematically organised and responsive to their care needs.

We are being heard and we are being understood and we have been given what is necessary that is all we need. (Participant 15)

Perceived facilitators and barriers

The theme comprised two subthemes: perceived facilitators for, and barriers to, timely access and uptake.

Facilitators for access and uptake

Participants identified several factors that supported their ability to access ANC and continue their engagement with services. Frequently identified facilitators included awareness and recognition of the importance of ANC, the presence of friendly, respectful healthcare professionals and support from family and friends.

Women’s awareness and recognition of the importance of ANC emerged as a critical enabler for early initiation. Women who recognised that early engagement could help detect health issues and monitor pregnancy progression reported prioritising timely booking of appointments.

I prioritised going to my appointments over pretty much everything else. And I think for me that is such, you know, such an important thing when you’re pregnant. (Participant 14)

Another key enabler was the presence of friendly and respectful healthcare professionals, especially midwives. Participants frequently highlighted how interactions with maternity care professionals who took time to explain procedures, tests and care pathways helped to reduce anxiety and build trust in care. Supportive and culturally sensitive communication made women feel listened to and valued, thereby facilitating continued engagement with services.

My midwife and my consultant were great. She’s very friendly, put me at ease. I felt like I could ask her any questions…no question was too silly. I liked that she would let me text her too… sometimes you don’t want to say something face to face. (Participant 20)

Relational continuity in care also emerged as a strong enabler for ongoing engagement. They appreciated seeing the same midwife or a small, consistent team throughout pregnancy, as this supported building trusting relationships and fostered a sense of being recognised and understood during care visits.

In terms of consistency, she knows like everything about me, like my pregnancy or even just a small thing, you know, next time if I can see her again, we don’t have to talk about it because she already knows about it. (Participant 17)

In addition to healthcare-related facilitators, support from family and friends was frequently identified as a key influence. Encouragement, practical support with travel and/or childcare and accompaniment to appointments were seen to be particularly helpful, especially for those navigating an unfamiliar health system or those with multiple responsibilities.

My mum literally lives kilometre down the road. So again, that is why we went to Bedford. It’s as easy as possible for the other child and all of that. (Participant 13)

Well, my husband has been supportive in terms of taking me there sometimes or being there with my children so I can go on attend my midwife appointments. (Participant 5)

Barriers to access and uptake

Women encountered several challenges that delayed or complicated ANC access and uptake, including uncertainty about the ANC processes, long waiting times, rushed appointments and language and logistical barriers. The most frequently reported barrier was uncertainty about when or how to book an appointment. Participants described early pregnancy as a confusing period, marked by ambiguity regarding the roles and services of GPs, community midwives and hospital-based maternity services.

Exactly who I needed to contact and when I think it was very grey in the early stage of pregnancy who is responsible for me; am I meant to contact my GP about things? Or was it the community midwife or was it triage? It was very grey areas to who wanted to take responsibility in those first few weeks. (Participant 19)

Long waiting times and short appointment durations also discouraged timely and sustained engagement. Some women expressed frustration with delays in being seen after attempting to book, while others felt rushed during consultations, limiting opportunities to ask questions or raise concerns, making it hard to build trust with care providers.

And then I’d still wait an hour, hour and a half for my consultant. That was supposed to be straight after…I did find really difficult. (Participant 13)

Very quick. The appointment is very quick, very rushed and you don’t want to be above us so you just like okay, um… (Participant 16)

Language barriers presented additional obstacles, especially for women whose first language was not English. Some reported difficulty understanding appointment letters, online forms or verbal instructions, often compounded by limited access to professional interpreting services. Others described instances where their calls or enquiries received no response, leading to further confusion and disengagement from ANC services.

Logistical challenges, including transportation, cost and childcare responsibilities, were commonly reported. For some women, especially those without private transport or with limited financial resources, reaching clinics on time for appointments was difficult. Others struggled to find childcare for older children, making regular attendance challenging.

My GP surgery used to have midwives, but apparently, they stopped…so I had to go to another clinic where midwife was…it’s kind of like far from the GP surgery. So, hard in terms of the cost and the distance… (Participant 17)

Many women felt their ANC appointments as a ‘checklist’ or ‘tick box exercise’, lacking the personalised attention they had expected.

I felt like it was just a kind of a tick box exercise… You’re fit into this category. Therefore, you’re this and there was no sort of more discussion. (Participant 14)

Information needs and preferences

The theme comprised two subthemes: reliance on online information sources and the need for clear and timely guidance.

Reliance on online information sources

Participants reported actively seeking information on their own during pregnancy, relying on online sources including National Health Service (NHS) websites and social media platforms, including Google, YouTube, TikTok and Facebook. They perceived the information provided during consultations as insufficient and, at times, difficult to understand.

TikTok is my mother. I had to go and find information myself… Google was my best friend. (Participant 3)

While some women appreciated receiving printed materials, such as leaflets or links to resources, many reported feeling overwhelmed by the volume of information in NHS leaflets or found it difficult to understand. Some also perceived the content of NHS leaflets as outdated or not relevant to their needs. They expressed a preference for more accessible formats such as cards with simple flow charts, short videos and digital tools such as QR codes and mobile applications.

They give you a bag of leaflets… but it’s just more paperwork. I’d rather have a simple link or QR code with everything in one place. (Participant 19)

Participants highlighted the importance of having choice in how they received information. Some preferred paper-based formats that they could read in their own time, while others found videos easier to follow, especially if English was not their first language or they were less confident reading longer texts.

I’m more confident with a video… you can manage with that even if English is not your first language. (Participant 7)

Need for clear and timely guidance

For first-time mothers, especially those with a migration background navigating an unfamiliar health system, the absence of clear and timely guidance left them feeling uncertain. They were often unsure whom to contact, what symptoms to monitor or how to interpret fetal movements or other changes they experienced.

They say you’ll figure it out, but as a first-time mum, I didn’t know how much movement was normal… it was a constant worry. (Participant 15)

Participants reported concerns about assumptions made by healthcare professionals regarding their prior knowledge and capacity to understand information without additional support. Such assumptions contributed to feelings of invisibility and exclusion among some, particularly those with migration backgrounds.

They assume we already know things. But we don’t. It’s a new system and we’re far from home. (Participant 15)

Women often felt reassured by information received directly from healthcare professionals. Some participants, however, described feeling overwhelmed or confused especially when information was overly general, poorly organised or not delivered in a timely manner. They also reported difficulties in identifying information that was most relevant to their stage of pregnancy.

Sometimes it’s just too much. I want to know what relevant now…likes for the fifth month…not everything at once. (Participant 15)

Strategies to improve ANC access and uptake

This theme comprised four subthemes: accessible information on ANC pathways, strengthened community and peer support networks, increased opportunities for direct engagement with professionals and integration of digital technologies into ANC services.

Accessible information on ANC pathways

Many participants expressed the need for a clear, well-organised resource that provides an overview of the ANC pathway, including guidance on when and how to seek assistance with respect to vital signs.

It would have helped to have a flowchart showing how the service works…who’s responsible at each point. (Participant 2)

To make ANC information more accessible and meaningful, participants proposed approaches such as provision of simplified symptom checklists with culturally appropriate explanations, alongside visual tools that illustrate what to expect at different stages of pregnancy.

Strengthened community and peer support networks

Many participants, particularly those who were new to the UK, emphasised the value of community and peer support networks, and shared spaces where pregnant women could connect, exchange experiences and access support. For some, the absence of such connections contributed to feelings of isolation during pregnancy, especially among those unfamiliar with local healthcare systems.

I feel like there should be some sort of group… so that other parents can get to know each other and not feel isolated. (Participant 16)

Participants also highlighted the role of formal networks, such as baby groups and social media communities, as sources of emotional and practical support. Some participants noted that improved awareness of existing groups, particularly those tailored to women’s cultural and social needs, would have facilitated earlier engagement with these support systems during pregnancy.

Once I had my baby, I found baby groups… but I didn’t know I could go before I had the baby… (Participant 20)

Increased opportunities for direct engagement with professionals

Women highlighted the need for enhanced opportunities for direct contact with maternity care professionals when concerns arose during pregnancy. Several participants suggested that access to a named midwife, or a designated professional who could respond promptly to questions, would improve their overall experience. Face-to-face contact was particularly valued by women who felt uncertain or isolated during early stages of pregnancy. Participants felt that opportunities for direct communication with maternity care professionals fostered a sense of safety and support.

There should be someone to speak to when you’re unsure… even if it’s just a quick check-in or someone at the desk who can explain what’s going on. (Participant 17)

Integration of digital technologies into ANC services

Women emphasised the importance of modernising ANC systems to better align with digital technologies and service user expectations. Several participants reported confusion arising from miscommunication, with information inconsistently shared across paper documents and digital platforms. This lack of integration made it difficult for women to track key pregnancy milestones, such as scan results or appointment details. Some practices were reported as outdated, with recommendations for regular reviews of ANC services to ensure they remain responsive to changing needs of women.

Some scans were on paper, some on the app. I had to screenshot things. They said ‘we don’t use the NHS app’. It was confusing. (Participant 3)

Discussion

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study that explored facilitators and barriers to timely ANC access and uptake from the perspectives of migrant and ethnically diverse women who initiated care later than the recommended gestational weeks in England, UK. Four key themes, comprising 12 subthemes, were derived from the accounts: perceptions about ANC, perceived facilitators and barriers, information needs and preferences and strategies to improve ANC access and uptake.

Women in our study highly valued ANC; however, many lacked awareness and understanding of the recommended timing for ANC initiation and were uncertain about when and how to access services, despite being relatively socio-economically advantaged. Gaps in understanding of ANC timing and processes, as well as difficulties in navigating ANC pathways, have been reported previously, particularly among underserved groups including migrant and ethnically diverse women.23,25 Our findings reflected that these challenges were less related to individual educational attainment or employment status, and more associated with structural and organisational complexities, including unfamiliarity with UK maternity care pathways, fragmented referral processes, communication barriers and limited clarity regarding how services are organised. These challenges may be even more pronounced among women experiencing greater socioeconomic disadvantage or social vulnerability, and demonstrate the need for developing, implementing and evaluating easy-to-navigate ANC pathways. The findings also highlight the need for system-wide approaches to ensure that women understand how to access and navigate ANC services, including clearer signposting of referral pathways, simplified booking processes, consistent messaging across services, supported navigation and improved coordination between primary care, maternity services and community organisations. Such approaches are particularly important for women who are new to the UK healthcare system. Emerging evidence suggests that community-based approaches—involving maternity champions, peer navigators and partnerships with community organisations—can be beneficial in improving ANC access among underserved populations by strengthening links between communities and health services and supporting women navigate maternity care pathways.26 27 Consistent with findings from previous research,10 women highlighted the important role of supportive community networks and relationships in facilitating timely access to care.

Women viewed awareness of the importance of ANC as a key facilitator of timely access and uptake. This highlights the need for innovative public health strategies, including proactive community outreach, to promote awareness and understanding of ANC through community-based settings, voluntary and faith sector organisations and digital platforms. Proactive community-based approaches co-produced with women to raise awareness about the importance of ANC along with simplified information on how to initiate care, demonstrate that these strategies can improve women’s understanding of care pathways and promote early booking among ethnically diverse populations.26 27 Differences between ANC systems in women’s home countries and the UK further shaped expectations and care-seeking behaviours. Unfamiliarity with the UK’s midwife-led model, including ambiguity about professional roles and qualifications, contributed to hesitation among some migrant women in our study during early pregnancy. Clarity about the roles of different professionals in maternity care provision is important to help these women feel respected, included and confident in their care decision-making.1 2

Our findings reinforce the crucial importance of maternity care provider interactions in sustaining women’s engagement with services.10 As recognised in national policies,28 women in our study perceived relational continuity in care as an important enabler for ongoing engagement, fostering trust, reassurance and a sense of security, particularly among ethnic minority and migrant women navigating an unfamiliar system. Although midwifery-led continuity of carer has been a key component of UK maternity policies28 29 and is associated with positive birth outcomes,30 persistent workforce shortages and rising service demands have limited its implementation. A potential pragmatic and equity-focused policy response would be to prioritise continuity of carer for women at increased risk of facing access barriers, including those with migration backgrounds, limited English proficiency and/or social complexity. At the practice level, it is also crucial to develop and evaluate sustainable multidisciplinary and community-based models that provide relational continuity, involving maternity support workers, peer navigators and the innovative use of digital technologies.

While studies have highlighted the importance of effective communication between women and maternity care providers,10,1231 ineffective communication emerged as a key barrier in our study, shaped by several inter-related factors. Women described appointments as rushed and impersonal, with limited opportunity to ask questions or receive clear explanations. Long waiting times further compounded stress, reinforcing perceptions that care was checklist-driven rather than woman-centred. For migrant women, language barriers exacerbated these challenges; the fast pace of spoken English made information difficult to follow, rendering woman-centred care largely inaccessible. These findings reinforce important policy and practice implications at multiple levels. Adequate time for clinical interactions—enabling healthcare professionals to listen to and respond to women’s concerns—is fundamental to delivering personalised, woman-centred care. Within the context of understaffing and high workloads within the NHS, focus can shift towards measurements and procedural tasks over holistic woman-centred care.11 35 To realise such care models in practice, workforce planning must explicitly protect adequate time for communication and relational care, particularly for women with additional communication or support needs. This should be accompanied by investment in high-quality interpretation services, including accessible and culturally appropriate digital language support applications. Logistical barriers—including transport difficulties, distance to services, financial constraints and childcare responsibilities—hindered timely access to ANC. Travel to unfamiliar or distant locations, alongside limited transport support, has been linked to delayed or missed healthcare among migrant populations36 including ANC.37 For women with older children, attending appointments often warranted support from family members or friends for childcare. These challenges disproportionately affected women with limited social support, including those who had migrated without extended family networks. While national guidelines emphasise the importance of making ANC services more accessible,2 addressing these barriers requires place-based and flexible models of care, improved transport support and stronger integration between maternity, community and social support services.

The way health systems communicate information and support individuals to understand and use health information are key factors in enhancing health literacy.38,40 Women in our study often perceived the information provided during consultations as neither sufficient nor easy to understand, highlighting the need for more inclusive and co-produced information and communication strategies and service designs that account for diversity and varying health literacy, including the provision of clear, culturally appropriate ANC information in multiple languages and formats. Previous research has demonstrated the value of co-producing tailored information resources with ethnically diverse women to ensure responsiveness to the needs of different communities.27 41 Consistent with this, women proposed several practical strategies to improve ANC access and uptake, including clearer and more accessible information on care pathways, strengthened community and peer support networks, greater opportunities for direct engagement with maternity care professionals and increased integration of digital technologies into ANC services. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of formally embedding women’s voices within the design and delivery of maternity services and policies.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of the study is the inclusion of women from migrant and ethnically diverse backgrounds, capturing the perspectives of both UK-born and migrant women regardless of length of residence or immigration status. This broad inclusion may have limited more in-depth exploration of barriers experienced by particularly marginalised groups, such as undocumented migrants, asylum seekers and refugees. The sociodemographic profile of participants reflected relatively high levels of education and employment, which may limit the transferability of findings to more socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, who may experience even greater barriers to accessing care. All interviews were conducted by the same researcher in English, which supported consistency in data collection. This may have limited participation to those able to communicate in English, potentially excluding the perspectives of those with limited language proficiency. Participants were interviewed at different stages of pregnancy and the postnatal period, which may have introduced recall bias.

Conclusion

Our findings offer key insights for policy, practice and intervention development to improve equitable ANC access and uptake for women from migrant and ethnically diverse backgrounds. Although health services are universally available in the UK, the findings highlight the critical role of health system, interpersonal and individual factors in shaping women’s ability to access and sustain engagement with care. They underscore the need to develop, implement and evaluate easy-to-navigate ANC pathways, integrated with targeted community outreach, facilitated navigation, responsive communication and greater relational continuity to improve care experiences and outcomes for migrant and ethnically diverse women. Building trust through improved communication and supportive healthcare policies is central to promoting both initial and sustained access to ANC among all women, particularly those marginalised. Overall, improving equitable ANC access requires moving beyond individual behaviour change to coordinated interventions across system, service and relational levels to address the structural and organisational drivers of disparities in access.
