Nondestructive Assessment of Potentially Contaminated DUF6

Executive Summary

We were tasked to determine how well neptunium/plutonium/technetium contamination in
reprocessed depleted uranium hexafluoride (DUF6) could be assessed with gamma
spectrometry. Because limits were not provided, allowable limits defined in ASTM C787
(“Standard Specification for Uranium Hexafluoride for enrichment”) were used. While Np-237
was found to be practically assessable on its own, plutonium and technetium were not, and the
industrial standard limits are on the total alpha activity, making gamma spectroscopy an
unsuitable measurement for assessing contamination in situ for 48X cylinders.

Based on this analysis, gamma spectroscopy is NOT recommended for in-situ contamination
assessment of Pu and Tc in 48X cylinders. Detection limits for plutonium as modeled are
>10,000 higher than ASTM C787 limits, and technetium's low-energy gamma emissions cannot
penetrate the cylinder contents. Neptunium-237 is detectable at levels below these limits.

For comprehensive contamination assessment, facility operators should implement the
validated measurement techniques described in Reference 1 (ORNL/TM-2000/242 Appendix E),
which include destructive sampling and analysis methods with sufficient precision to meet
ASTM C787 requirements.

Task Overview

48X cylinders of DUF6 are suspected to be possibly contaminated with Tc and/or transuranics
(TRU), particularly plutonium and neptunium. We will estimate detection limits with conservative
assumptions for the following:
 - 48X cylinder full of DUF6 (nominal 0.2% U235), assumed aged >6 months. No
 impurities. This clean cylinder will be comparison for all other tests.
 - 48X cylinder full of DUF6 (nominal 0.2% U235), assumed aged >6 months, with
 Np-237 impurities in varying concentrations
 - 48X cylinder full of DUF6 (nominal 0.2% U235), assumed aged >6 months, with
 reactor grade plutonium impurities in varying concentrations
 - 48X cylinder full of DUF6 (nominal 0.2% U235), assumed aged >6 months, with
 Tc-99 impurities in varying concentrations
Additionally, these detection limits will be compared against ASTM C787 (“Standard
Specification for Uranium Hexafluoride for Enrichment”, which governs acceptable Tc and TRU
contaminant limits).

Prior Work

Reference 1 is an excellent whitepaper on the characterization of TRU and Tc contamination in
DUF6 and provides guidance (see Appendix E) on measurement techniques that have sufficient
precision to meet the limits in ASTM C787. The ORNL study evaluated multiple measurement
approaches and concluded that destructive sampling with mass spectrometry and alpha
spectroscopy provides the sensitivity required for ppb-level detection. While the report mentions
gamma spectroscopy, it notes that "levels we attempted to measure (ppb levels) could not be
accomplished using NDA." The specific NDA configuration used is not detailed, but the
conclusion aligns with our findings. The present analysis provides more detail into the limits for
gamma spectroscopy under well-defined conditions, confirming that this NDA approach cannot
meet ASTM C787 requirements for Pu and Tc in 48X cylinders.

Analysis Assumptions

The distribution of any given radionuclide throughout a UF6 cylinder is not generally well known.
Some will deposit to the walls of the cylinder (though not necessarily totally or uniformly), while
others may remain somewhat distributed throughout the cylinder [1, 2]. There will be a
significant difference in the calculated detection limits depending on where the radionuclide is
assumed to be. Since this analysis is intended to give a worst-case detection limit, all
radionuclides are assumed to be evenly distributed throughout the cylinder unless noted
otherwise, as this will produce a much more conservative limit.
 • This assumption includes the progeny of U-238 (particularly Th-234 and Pa-
 234m), which are responsible for virtually all of the gamma-rays associated with
 U-238. This means that the simulated spectra will not perfectly match even
 uncontaminated DUF6 cylinders; the effect of this is to change the Compton
 continuum resulting from the Pa-234m gamma-rays, which will in turn affect the
 calculated detection limits.
Additionally, the bulk material is assumed to be 20 years of age (since last fill). This has a few
effects:
 • It allows U-238 to reach equilibrium with its Pa-234m granddaughter, which is
 responsible for the gamma-rays typically associated with U-238. This equilibrium
 is reached at approximately 6 months of age. Cylinders filled more recently than
 this will have reduced gamma-emissions, actually improving the detectability of
 contaminants.
 • It allows for some ingrowth of the Am-241 daughter of Pu-241.
Simulations are performed for a 48x cylinder (9539 kg UF6 fill, 1.59 cm wall thickness) and an
Ortec Detective-X HPGe (45% relative efficiency, 65 x 50 mm crystal). Measurement times vary
by scenario as specified in each example. No cylinder rotation or manipulation is assumed; all
simulations are for an idealized uniform fill.

This standard does not discriminate between different isotopes of plutonium or neptunium. In
reactor grade plutonium, a majority of the alpha activity is from Pu-238 despite its low mass
fraction.

Rather than use a guess for expected isotopic distribution (e.g. typical reactor-grade plutonium),
limits are evaluated separately for each of the radionuclides of interest (Pu-238, Pu-239, Pu-
240, Pu-241, Np-237).

Per ANSI N14.1-2001, a 48X cylinder of DUF6 has a max fill limit of 21030 lbs (9539 kg) UF6,
which is 6448 kg of uranium. This corresponds to 0.58 mCi of alpha activity from
plutonium/neptunium in the full cylinder.

Neptunium Contamination

Of these potential impurities, aged Np-237 will be by far the most detectable contaminant. Its
Pa-233 daughter emits a 311.9 keV gamma-ray in 38.5% of decays, and this neptunium isotope
reaches equilibrium with its daughter in approximately 6 months. For a 48X contaminated
exactly at the limit with only Np-237 (i.e. 580 uCi) distributed uniformly throughout the cylinder,
this peak was readily detectable with 4 hour measurements (see Figure 1).

In addition, this simulation assumes the Np-237 is uniformly distributed throughout the cylinder.
Reference 1 found that relatively little of the neptunium impurity was found in liquid samples but
much more was found on the walls. Because gamma-rays emitted from neptunium bound to
wall deposits do not have to transmit through significant amounts of uranium, they are far more
likely to reach the detector; while this improves our ability to detect Np-237, it is difficult to
precisely assess to what limit it can be detected.

Suppose the Np-237 was bound in wall deposits uniformly around the cylinder (treating the wall
deposit as also largely constituting of UF6). Figure 2 shows a simulated 30 minute
measurement at 10% of the max allowable Np-237 contamination; even in this shorter
measurement, it is readily detectable.

Plutonium Contamination

The gamma-rays emitted by the plutonium isotopes have much lower branching ratios (on the
order of 1E-5 and below for all lines above 200 keV).

Any plutonium contamination in UF6 is going to be reactor-grade. While there is a wide range
of possible isotopics, this paper considers one set of isotopics: 580 uCi of moderate reactor
grade Pu (RGPu), aged 20 years. Note that while the activity of Pu-241 appears over the limit,
the standard specifies the limit for alpha activity specifically, and Pu-241 predominantly decays
by beta decay.

Technetium Contamination

Tc-99 only emits a single gamma-ray at 89.5 keV with a branching ratio of 6.5E-6 (and some x-
rays that are much lower energy and branching ratios); this is difficult detect even at technetium
concentrations far above the limit. This gamma-ray is unlikely to penetrate the cylinder wall;
Figure 5 compares spectra for an empty (air-filled) cylinder vs the same air-filled cylinder with
the limit activity for Tc-99 distributed throughout. Even more limiting is the attenuation from the
UF6 itself; the mean-free path of this 89.5 keV gamma-ray in solid UF6 is only approximately
1.3 mm, so only gamma-rays emitted away from the walls have virtually no chance of escaping
the cylinder.

Final Conclusions

While this analysis suggests that gamma-spectroscopy is a viable method for screening for Np-
237 contamination, in situ gamma-ray measurements for 48X cylinders are insufficient for
assessing plutonium and technetium contamination. Because the standard limits the total alpha
activity rather than the activity of neptunium and plutonium separately, this screening for
neptunium is insufficient for this purpose. The approaches outlined in Reference 1 are much
more well-suited for assessing this contamination.

Key Findings:

1. Neptunium-237 is practically detectable at concentrations below regulatory limits via its Pa-
233 daughter’s gamma-ray at 311.9 keV.

2. Plutonium contamination cannot be detected at regulatory-relevant concentrations; detection
limits exceed ASTM limits by factors of >10,000 for the assessed configurations.

3. Technetium-99's low-energy, low-yield gamma emissions do not penetrate cylinder walls or
UF6 bulk material, making detection impossible regardless of concentration
