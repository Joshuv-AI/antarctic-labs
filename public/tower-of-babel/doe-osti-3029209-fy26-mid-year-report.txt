Summary
The goal of this project is to develop specific aspects of ultra-high-resolution
microcalorimeter technologies that support IAEA Nuclear Material Laboratory needs and
the goals of SP-1 19/NML-003 "Microcalorimetry Analysis Technique for NML" but are
outside the scope of the SP-1. The focus is on commissioning, assembly, and testing of
the microcalorimeter decay energy spectrometer with superconducting transition-edge
sensors (TESs) and magnetic microcalorimeters (MMCs) in preparation for transfer to
the IAEA Nuclear Material Laboratory.

Task 1: Complete modification and commissioning of the ADR
cryocooler
Initial cooldowns of the adiabatic demagnetization refrigerator (ADR) after it was
delivered to LLNL showed significant thermal performance degradation with the
extended infrared (IR) radiation shields installed. The extended IR shields and extended
vacuum shell were then shipped back to LANL, to allow simultaneous improvement and
testing of the IR shield design at LANL and testing of the MMC detectors at LLNL with
the stock IR shields.
An ADR cryocooler system comparable to the ADR intended for the IAEA NML was set
up at LANL for testing the IR shields and TES detector stage. This system is
subsequently referred to as LANL ADR. Testing started in December 2025 after project
funding was received at LANL. The goal of the testing at LANL is to establish which
design changes can be made to IR shields to improve the ADR performance while
preserving the user-friendly side access ports to the detector stages, which is currently
enabled by removable door panels (Figure 1).
The custom IR shielding consists of an aluminum shield with side access door attached
to the 50K stage of the ADR (50K shield) and an aluminum shield with attached
magnetic shielding plates which are mounted on the 3K stage of the ADR (3K shield).
Several cooldowns with different IR shielding configurations were performed to identify
the impact of IR shield modifications on the ADR base temperature at different
temperature stages:
 1. Cooldown with stock IR shields and stock vacuum shell.
 2. Cooldown with custom IR shields with magnetic shielding.
 3. Cooldown with custom IR shields without magnetic shielding and multi-layer
 insulation (MLI) added to the 50K shield.
 4. Cooldown with custom IR shields without magnetic shielding and MLI added to
 the 50K shield as well as cutouts in the MLI for the 50K door panel
 5. Same as 4, but with detector stage and SQUID array.

The impact of the configuration changes on the ADR base temperatures at the 50K, 3K
and 50mK stages are summarized in Table 1. The addition of the MLI to the 50K
extended IR shields (configuration 3 in Table 1) almost restored the base temperature
performance to the stock shield configuration, with only a small increase in the base
temperature after introducing cutouts into the MLI to access the screws for the door
panel (configuration 4 in Table 1).

Figure 2: Left: Photo of the LANL ADR with installed SQUID arrays (1) and TES
detector mount (2). Right: High temperature superconductor (HTS) lead assemblies.
Note the blackened area around the lower part of the right HTS lead (3), which indicates

mounted between the 50K and 3K stage (4).
From those results we infer that the 50K MLI plays a major role in maintaining ADR
performance and will be included in the IR shield redesign. Configuration 5 in Table 1
included the TES detector stage as well as the SQUID arrays and their readout cables.
The installation of the readout cables added a significant heat load to the 50K and 3K
stages, but temperatures are still in an operable range for a magnet regeneration cycle.
We attempted to run ADR regeneration cycles in all configurations. During the first
cooldown in December 2025, with the stock IR shields (configuration 1), we reached a
base temperature of 40mK at the 50mK stage and achieved a hold time of
approximately 150h at 80mK. This hold time is expected to decrease as the detector
stage and wiring are added to the 50mK stage. During subsequent cooldowns with
configurations 2, 3 and 4 in January and February 2026, the ADR showed abnormal
heating during the regeneration cycle and we could not evaluate the 80mK hold time.
The cause of this heating was identified to be damaged high temperature
superconducting (HTS) leads shown in Figure 2. The HTS leads were replaced in March
2026 and testing the ADR with the detector stage and SQUID array was resumed.

Cooldown performance with the detector mount and SQUID wiring resulted in higher
base temperatures (Table 1, configuration 5), mainly caused by the additional thermal
load of the SQUID wiring to the 50K and 3K stages. We were able to run ADR
regeneration cycles in this configuration with a base temperature of 60mK and a hold
time of 8.5h at 80mK. This are results from the first cooldown with the detector stage in
the last week of March 2026 and will likely be improved in subsequent test runs. It is
important to note that the cooling power of the cryocooler cold head used in this LANL
ADR is significantly lower than the ADR at LLNL which will be delivered to the IAEA
NML. The cooling power is approximately 1W at 3K for the IAEA system whereas the
LANL ADR only has 0.3W. Therefore, we expect the performance to be significantly
better in the IAEA cryostat.

Task 2: Complete commissioning of the TES decay energy
spectrometer
Assembly of the TES detector stage was performed in parallel to the IR shield testing.
Photos of the detector stage are shown in Figure 3. Currently only the superconducting
wires connecting the Micro-D connectors at the top of the detector stage and the PC
board as well as the first stage SQUID chips are missing.
Several iterations of new prototype Nb clamps were fabricated, that provide flexible
clamping design for attaching the absorber to the TES. Similar to the previously tested
Nb wire clamp, this allows the absorber to be attached to the TES chip without indium
and thus absorber removal without the need for replacing the aluminum wire bonds is
possible.

Figure 3: Left: TES detector mount with aluminum cover removed. Right: detailed view
of a TES detector (1). The detector is attached via BeCu clamps (2). The Nb clamp (3)
is currently attached via a spring-loaded screw attachment that can be turned like a
record player arm and used to clamp the absorber to the gold pad on the TES island.

Task 3. Demonstrate MMC Decay Energy Spectrometer for IAEA NML
The ADR is transferred from LANL to LLNL in Q1. LLNL has conducted performance
test with multiple cooldowns, identified light leak in the newly designed IR shields,
increasing base temperatures of the ADR. The new IR shields are shipped back to
LANL for mitigation of the issue. Cooldown summary is listed in Table 2.

While the new IR shield is being modified at LANL, the ADR at LLNL was operated using
the manufacturer’s stock IR shields. During this reporting period, LLNL focused primarily
on redesigning and retrofitting the existing MMC-DES detector module to fit within the
stock IR shield and on testing its performance. To support this effort, LLNL designed and

built two custom wiring harnesses, each containing approximately 50 wires and two 25-
pin DSUB connectors, and also designed a new module motherboard that fits inside the
stock IR shield as shown in Figure 4.

As shown in Figure 5, the module incorporates two sets of daughter modules, each
containing one MMC device and one SQUID device and capable of mounting two DES
absorbers, for a total of four absorbers. Two DES samples from ISO-51498-01, provided
by IAEA NML, were mounted on two pixels. One sample was wire-bonded to the MMC as
a reference device, while the other was clamped directly onto an MMC pixel.

Figure 5. (Left) A DES absorber is clamped by a dowel pin for clamping to the MMC
sensor. (Right) Two daughter boards were installed on a mother board.

In preparation for ADR operation, MMC readout wiring was installed in the ADR during
Runs 5 and 6 (R5 and R6). These tests confirmed that the added wiring did not
measurably affect ADR performance, indicating that the wires introduced no significant
heat leak. ADR test history is summarized in Table 1. R4 is the reference cooldown that
provided the best performance so far achieving 2.9K and 43 mK base temperatures for
4K and 0.1K stages, with 69h hold time at 50 mK, which is sufficient for MMC operation.
In R5 and R6, with MMC wiring, cooling performance was further improved to 2.5K and
39mK base temperatures and holding time of 60 h, slight reduction due to added heat
load.
The revised module was fabricated and subsequently tested in the ADR (R6 – R13). The
detector module mounted on the ADR is shown in Figure 6.

 Figure 6 - Temporary MMC-DES detector module installed in IAEA ADR.

However, once the detector was installed, the ADR exhibited substantially degraded
thermal performance and increased temperature instability, preventing the DES
detectors from achieving high-resolution operation. Although the detectors
demonstrated basic functionality, including SQUID (Superconducting Quantum
Interference Device) operation, MMC magnetization process, and DES signal
generation and recording, high-resolution data could not be obtained because of the
large temperature fluctuations (Figure 4) and electrical noise caused by mechanical
contact between the 0.1 K sample stage and the 4 K stage.
Significant baseline fluctuations observed in these tests are shown in Figure 7. R7 – R13
were performed for diagnosis of this problem.

Figure 7. Sensor signal output. Sharp vertical lines correspond to nuclear decays
measured in the MMC-DES detector module. Low-frequency (~2 Hz) noise in the signal
baseline corresponds to the ADR pulse tube operation. Observation of thermal noise from
the pulse tube indicated that the ADR is not functioning properly, as the detector should
be thermally-isolated during operation
During the LANL team’s visit to LLNL in March, the primary cause of the degraded
performance was identified as a broken Kevlar string suspension. This failure led to
mechanical and thermal contact between the 4 K and 0.1 K stages when heavy detector
module was mounted, resulting in temperature instability and reduced hold time. The
Kevlar suspension assembly was removed and sent to the manufacturer, STAR
Cryoelectronics, for repair. Identified ADR problems are listed in Table 3.

Due to these issues, LLNL has adapted the detector module for testing in a new dilution
refrigerator (called Jaina), which has wiring nearly identical to that of the ADR and offers
a rapid 12-hour cooldown for repeated tests. This setup will be used to investigate the
MMC-DES module risk factors listed in Table 4.
Historically, MMC-DES detectors have been tested in an R&D detector module with a 2-
pixel configuration and a cup-shaped cylindrical superconducting magnetic shield. In
contrast, the IAEA-DES module uses an 8-pixel configuration, which required a redesign
to a rectangular detector module. This new design introduces several potential risk
factors, including magnetic shielding performance, vibration associated with the revised
geometry and greater mass, modifications to the mechanical clamping structure for the
8-pixel layout, and possible noise from the newly implemented wire strap for 8-pixel
readout, which also required changes to the wiring scheme.
The wiring-related risk has now been resolved, as performance has been confirmed in
both the ADR and Jaina. Magnetic shielding remains a high-risk area, because the 8-pixel
module requires rectangular aluminum magnetic shields that have not previously been
tested with MMCs. This issue will be investigated in Jaina testing. Vibration, which is also
related to the new 8-pixel geometry, can be partially evaluated in the same test campaign.
