1.0 Executive Summary
Akiachak Native Community (ANC), a federally recognized Yup’ik Tribe, undertook this energy
efficiency initiative to enhance energy performance in key community buildings, including the Tribal
IRA Office, Laundry, Police Station, Clinic, and Daycare. Through energy audits and upgrades, the
project focused on high-impact improvements like building envelope air sealing, light emitting diode
(LED) lighting, and programmable thermostats, primarily in the Tribal IRA Office 1. The initiative aimed
to reduce energy waste, lower heating and electricity costs, and improve facility comfort and longevity.
The project aimed to generate long-term savings for further improvements by decreasing reliance on
imported heating oil (#1 Fuel Oil). Despite scope limitations exacerbated by budget constraints, this
project achieved an approximately 26% reduction in energy use intensity (EUI) in the targeted building.
This initiative aligns with the Tribe’s vision for responsible energy use, addressing the challenges of
high fuel prices in a remote community, and serves as a model for similar efforts in Akiachak and other
Alaska Native villages pursuing energy self-reliance.

2.0 Background
The Akiachak Native Community (ANC), a Yup’ik village located on the Kuskokwim River in Southwest
Alaska, conducted energy audits for Tribal buildings in 2017 under a previous grant from the U.S.
Department of Energy’s Office of Indian Energy Policy and Programs (OIE). These audits aimed to
reduce energy costs by 17 to 34% and support efficient building operating practices as outlined in
Akiachak’s Energy Action Plan (George et al. 2019). The goal was to use energy savings from initial
retrofits to implement recommended improvements in other buildings, thereby increasing community
resiliency and reducing reliance on fuel oil shipments. ANC has been steadily implementing energy
efficiency measures based on previous audits of tribal buildings from 2017, with a primary objective of
stabilizing and reducing energy costs.

Understanding how a building performs regarding energy usage and cost is a key first step in
determining efficiency improvement strategies. Tracking historic consumption and cost in relation to
actual weather data allows a building owner to monitor changes in the facility’s performance or energy
use. This data-driven approach helps to identify trends over time and pinpoint specific areas where
energy consumption may be improved.

ANC obtained energy audits for five buildings in 2017 and used them to guide this follow-up project,
which focused on implementing high-payback recommendations such as replacing lighting with LEDs,
installing programmable thermostats with setbacks, and air-tightening building envelopes. ANC and

4.0 Description of Activities Performed
To achieve these objectives, NREL, ANC, and an energy auditor completed the following tasks between
November 2022 and May 2025.

Overview
Task 1: Project Kickoff
In the first quarter of the project, ANC staff, NREL staff, and the energy auditor met virtually to review
project goals and finalize planning documents. The Tribal IRA Office was benchmarked against a 2017
energy audit. The building’s annual energy usage was assessed in terms of energy use intensity (EUI)
and fuel oil usage relative to cumulative base 65o heating degree days (HDDs) across the sample period
to understand each building’s performance over time further and investigate potential energy
anomalies. This project—originally intended to implement energy efficiency retrofits for five Akiachak
buildings audited in 2017—faced significant delays because of the COVID-19 pandemic and workforce
capacity issues.

 1.1 Document the present conditions of the project buildings: The energy auditor (re)documented
 the current condition of the five buildings and noted some changes since the earlier energy audits.
 1.2 Create a data management plan: Staff developed a data management plan detailing roles and
 responsibilities for the energy savings verification process. The plan outlined methods for data
 collection and secure storage throughout the project duration.
 1.3 Create an outreach plan: Staff developed an outreach plan to inform and educate Tribal
 members about the project scope and the benefits of energy efficiency upgrades.
 1.4 COVID mitigation plan: At the time of implementation, ANC no longer required this subtask.

Task 2: Hire Retrofit Contractors
Project staff developed detailed scopes of work for lighting and mechanical systems. Requests for
Proposals (RFPs) were issued, and at least three bids were obtained for each category. Contractors
were hired by the end of the third quarter of the project. After re-evaluating the scope of work in 2023,
the focus was narrowed to the Tribal Office. ANC, in collaboration with an energy auditor, adjusted the
project scope to fit a reduced budget, which included upgrades in HVAC (replacing thermostats and
improving hydronic heating systems) and lighting (replacing with LED). In June 2023, the NREL project
manager contacted multiple HVAC and electrical engineering vendors, receiving three HVAC bids and
one electrical bid by the July deadline. ANC selected the most cost-effective HVAC bid with guidance

Contractors completed retrofit work on the designated building in coordination with the building

Task 4: Energy Savings Verification
Following the data management plan, staff collected, stored, and analyzed energy use and
maintenance data before and after retrofits. Data collection began in June 2024. Over 11 months, NREL
gathered utility bills to compare with data from previous years, including from the original energy
audits. Building use and occupancy changed over the project’s period of performance, which spanned
the COVID-19 pandemic; this introduced some uncertainty into the energy savings analysis. However,
the data show that compared with the building performance during and just before the energy audits,
the Tribal Office saw energy reductions by about 26% EUI.

5.0 Methods
NREL staff reviewed construction documents and compiled the utility data to calculate annual energy
usage metrics for the Tribal Office building. Monthly utility bills for fuel oil and electricity were
collected for three distinct periods: before the retrofits, during the baseline assessment period, and
after the retrofits were in place. Pre-retrofit data were derived from the original energy audit and
recent utility records to establish a baseline of energy consumption before any upgrades. Post-retrofit
utility data were collected in the years following implementation to evaluate performance under
comparable climatic conditions.

Energy use intensity (EUI) was reported in terms of annual kBtu/ft² (thousand British thermal units per
square foot 2) for each full calendar year of available data. EUI is defined as the total annual energy
consumption normalized by the conditioned floor area of the building, providing a standardized metric
for comparing energy consumption across years; the higher the EUI, the less efficient the building. The
initial EUI (55.6 kBtu/ft²) was calculated using the Tribal Office utility bills from the pre-retrofit period.
These data form the basis for evaluating the effectiveness of the retrofits over time.

Energy use intensity per heating degree day (EUI/HDD) was reported in terms of annual kBtu/ft2 for
the available calendar years for the building. EUI/HDD is the annual energy usage first normalized by
the conditioned floor area of the building and normalized again by the base 65°F HDD for the observed
year. HDDs are the cumulative difference in daily mean temperature from a baseline temperature.
More heating degree days occur in colder locations and generally correlate to higher energy usage.
EUI/HDD was calculated using actual heating degree data from the Bethel Airport weather station
(60°46’47.0″ N, 161°50’16.0″ W; elevation 125 ft), obtained via the Alaska Climate Research Center’s
Data Portal, which provides an interactive interface to the United States Department of Commerce,
National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s weather records. Bethel was used as the reference
station because Akiachak is too small to have its own weather station.

Energy cost index (ECI) was reported in terms of annual dollars per square foot for the available
calendar years. ECI is the annual utility costs for electricity and fuel oil normalized by the conditioned
floor area of the building. ECI costs are all expressed in 2024-dollar values and do not include Power

The marked decrease, especially in winter, underscores the effectiveness of the implemented retrofits
in this heating-dominated setting. LED substitution, boiler and hydronic distribution system
improvements, and the installation and explanation of programmable thermostats each contributed to
the overall reduction in energy use. Over the monitoring period, electricity consumption declined from
15,259 kWh to 11,262 kWh, while fuel oil consumption dropped from 850 gallons to 627 gallons. These
reductions translate into meaningful financial benefits, though the precise dollar value is difficult to
calculate. Reported ECIs have been adjusted to 2024-dollar values to account for inflation (Figure 5);

drive economic benefits and reduce energy dependence for the entire community.

7.0 Discussion and Considerations
Working in remote Alaskan villages, like Akiachak, which are not on the road system, means vendors
must fly to the job site and remain there, typically for 3-5 days. Weather events and shifting flight
schedules can cause delays at any time of year. All supplies must be externally sourced, either shipped
ahead of time by barge, trucked from Bethel via seasonal ice road, or delivered by plane, and all
hazardous waste must be removed at additional cost. Price increases and supply chain demands
caused by the global effects of the COVID-19 pandemic were significant barriers to this project and
exacerbated existing logistics difficulties for a construction project in rural Alaska.

Challenges and Considerations
The project faced significant hurdles, primarily from Akiachak’s remote location and compounded
supply chain disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. While global supply chains have largely
stabilized, remote regions remain vulnerable to similar disruptions. To account for such risks, future
projects in off-road communities could further enhance contingency planning with more flexible
timelines and additional logistical allowances—above what is already considered necessary. Budgeting
additional time for material procurement and enhanced vendor coordination could mitigate the effects
of supply chain disruptions. Implementing these mitigation strategies depends on sufficient project
budgets and timelines, which were limited in this case, and budget constraints restricted the scope of
the Tribal Office retrofits. Nonetheless, the measurable improvements from the retrofits suggest
potential for broader impact. ANC is encouraged to pursue additional funding to extend these benefits
to other priority facilities identified in the original audits, such as the Laundry, Daycare, Police Station,
and Clinic.

Community Impact and Long-Term Performance
Energy savings and operational stability can be continued through energy consumption and
performance monitoring. Regular monitoring allows for timely identification and correction of any
emerging inefficiencies or maintenance issues, thus protecting and potentially enhancing energy
savings over the long term. Moreover, targeted training for local maintenance staff on newly installed
energy-efficient systems and broader energy management practices should be provided. This training
could equip personnel with essential knowledge and skills, thereby maintaining and potentially
increasing efficiency gains and fostering long-term community resilience and self-reliance in energy
management. Continued monitoring of energy performance metrics at the whole-building level is
advised to maintain energy savings and identify further efficiency opportunities in ANC’s building
portfolio. Routine training for local maintenance staff on newly installed systems and energy efficient
operating practices could help sustain efficiency gains and promote long-term stability. Lessons from
this project underscore the need for adaptable, community-specific models for energy improvements
in rural Alaska communities, emphasizing local capacity building and accommodation, long-term
performance tracking, and budget flexibility.
