Deep Randomized Distributed Function Computation (DeepRDFC): Neural Distributed Channel Simulation
Didrik Bergström and Onur Günlü

Abstract
The randomized distributed function computation (RDFC) framework, which unifies many cutting-edge distributed computation and learning applications, is considered. An autoencoder (AE) architecture is proposed to minimize the total variation distance between the probability distribution simulated by the AE outputs and an unknown target distribution, using only data samples. We illustrate significantly high RDFC performance with communication load gains from our AEs compared to data compression methods. Our designs establish deep learning-based RDFC methods and aim to facilitate the use of RDFC methods, especially when the amount of common randomness is limited and strong function computation guarantees are required.



I
Introduction

In conventional systems, data is communicated as arbitrary bit sequences without considering the conveyed meaning. This deficiency has motivated
semantic communications

[
12
,
25
]
, where the quality measure of transmission depends on the semantics
[
13
]
. For example, transmitting objects and their relative positions, rather than raw pixel arrays, exemplifies semantic communication
[
13
]
. As the semantics of data can be modeled as a function of the data, the semantic communication paradigm can be defined as a remote (hidden) source coding problem where the transmitted information is a function of the data, while the function output is not available to the transmitter
[
4
, pp. 78]
,
[
7
, pp. 118]
,
[
9
]
. This formulation extends beyond classical lossy coding by employing semantic-driven fidelity metrics
[
15
,
2
,
14
]
.

We consider a generalization of remote source coding that employs a
randomized
functional mapping for distributed function computation. In this setting, the receiver’s computation relies on randomized transformations of the transmitter’s data, thus defining a
randomized distributed function computation (RDFC)
framework, introduced in
[
15
]
. RDFC unifies a large set of important problems that leverage semantic communications. Example RDFC applications include machine learning-based data compression methods with generative models
[
19
,
10
]
, federated learning (FL) with side information
[
22
]
, transform coding methods
[
1
,
26
]
, lossy compression methods with realism constraints (such as visual perception)
[
32
,
5
]
, and compression methods used as differential privacy mechanisms
[
30
,
20
]
. These applications aim to synthesize data according to a target joint probability distribution, which broadly corresponds to controlling the correlation between data samples to satisfy a constraint requiring a controlled randomization step.
Minimizing the communication load required for RDFC is defined as a
coordination problem

[
8
,
3
,
23
,
18
]
, also known as distributed channel simulation/synthesis.

We focus on strong coordination
[
8
]
, imposing joint typicality constraints on each function computation. Such a stringent constraint is vital to ensure a performance guarantee for each computation instance, unlike previous methods whose guarantee is for the average case over all computation instances, where the latter provides empirical coordination. Moreover, unlike computations of deterministic functions, common randomness shared between transmitter and receiver can significantly reduce the communication load for RDFC
[
24
]
. Such uniformly distributed common randomness can be obtained, e.g., by using physical unclonable functions
[
17
]
. For instance, if encoder-decoder pairs that apply RDFC methods are used to achieve local differential privacy, the communication load is reduced by up to 214 times compared to adding noise for the same purpose
[
15
]
. Thus, in this work, we propose deep learning methods to design low-complexity encoder-decoder pairs for the RDFC framework. There are a few code constructions proposed for empirical coordination problems, such as in
[
31
]
, designed for binary symmetric channels (BSCs), and existential designs for strong coordination, such as in
[
6
]
. These existing designs do not provide constructive code designs for RDFC and do not leverage the high performance of deep neural networks, illustrated to surpass classical code constructions for reliable communications
[
27
]
.

Our summary of the main contributions is as follows:

•

We develop a general constructive design of autoencoders (AEs) for RDFC in a discrete setting. We propose a suitable loss function for RDFC and algorithms to generate training data that provide high RDFC performance.

•

We provide an AE architecture and technical insights to facilitate the training and application of AEs in RDFC. We motivate our choices of layer activation functions, training parameters, and the need for a vector quantizer layer that determines the RDFC rate for our setting.

•

We demonstrate the feasibility of our design by simulating a BSC in a distributed computation setting. We also illustrate the impact of the amount of available common randomness on the RDFC performance.

II-B

Review of AEs

An AE is a feed-forward neural network consisting of an encoder that outputs an index
\underaccent{\bar}{j}=\text{Enc}(\underaccent{\bar}{x})
given an input
\underaccent{\bar}{x}
, and a decoder that receives
\underaccent{\bar}{j}
and reconstructs the message
\widehat{\underaccent{\bar}{y}}=\text{Dec}(\underaccent{\bar}{j})

[
21
]
. The AE is trained by running the above step (forward propagation/inference) and then computing the loss according to a loss function
\mathcal{L}(\underaccent{\bar}{y},\widehat{\underaccent{\bar}{y}})
. In the following step (backpropagation), the chain rule is applied to compute the gradients of the loss function with respect to all network parameters, starting from the output layer and moving backward through the network. These gradients are then passed to an optimizer that updates the network parameters, thereby reducing the loss on the next iteration.

In our setting, the AE takes the source input
\underaccent{\bar}{X}
and common- and local randomness
\underaccent{\bar}{K}
and
\underaccent{\bar}{L}
as inputs, and outputs
\underaccent{\bar}{Y}
. The index
\underaccent{\bar}{J}
is contained within the AE and automatically adapts itself, given some training set
\{\underaccent{\bar}{x}_{i},\underaccent{\bar}{y}_{i},\underaccent{\bar}{k}_{i},\underaccent{\bar}{l}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{s}}
, to improve the RDFC performance during training. We measure performance using the non-asymptotic TVD given in (
2
).

III-A

Loss Function for TVD Minimization

Using TVD as the loss function is intractable in this setting because the gradient descent tends to get stuck in local minima. Therefore, we use CCE as our loss function during the training phase. CCE is a suitable surrogate loss function to TVD due to (i) its differentiability and (ii) its equivalence to Kullback-Liebler divergence if the AE’s inputs and outputs are one-hot encoded, which is the case in our design. Moreover, Kullback-Liebler divergence bounds TVD from above via Pinsker’s inequality
[
11
]
.

As in
[

]
, we use a large batch size (
2^{14}
) and a low learning rate. We use the ADAM optimizer with parameters
(lr\!=\!10^{-4},\,\beta_{1}\!=\!0.9,\,\beta_{2}\!=\!0.999)
. To adaptively lower the learning rate when the loss plateaus, we use a
ReduceLROnPlateau

]
with parameters
(

. We use a set of samples

from the target joint distribution

to compute the estimated distribution
\widehat{Q}_{\underaccent{\bar}{X}\underaccent{\bar}{Y}}
as a relative frequency distribution, where the total number of samples is
N_{s}\!=\!2^{26}
. We then construct the training set
\{\underaccent{\bar}{x}_{i},\underaccent{\bar}{y}_{i},\underaccent{\bar}{k}_{i},\underaccent{\bar}{l}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{s}}
using Algorithms
1
and
2
. The AE

is trained for

\theta^{*}:\underset{\theta}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}-\underaccent{\bar}{y}_{i}\log\widehat{\underaccent{\bar}{y}_{i}}
, where

denotes the optimal values for the AE parameters

\widehat{\underaccent{\bar}{y}}_{i}
is the AE’s output given
(\underaccent{\bar}{x}_{i},\underaccent{\bar}{k}_{i},\underaccent{\bar}{l}_{i})
from the test set.

Before testing, we create a separate

We test the AE

by letting it make predictions

from the test set. We then apply the
\arg\!\max
function to the predictions and compute the synthesized joint distribution
P_{\underaccent{\bar}{X}\underaccent{\bar}{Y}}
as a relative frequency distribution of observations made from the prediction set
\{\underaccent{\bar}{x}_{i},\widehat{\underaccent{\bar}{y}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{s}}
.
Finally, we compute the target joint distribution
Q_{\underaccent{\bar}{X}\underaccent{\bar}{Y}}
, the testing TVD:
\|P_{\underaccent{\bar}{X}\underaccent{\bar}{Y}}-\widehat{Q}_{\underaccent{\bar}{X}\underaccent{\bar}{Y}}\|_{TV}
and the ground-truth TVD:
\|P_{\underaccent{\bar}{X}\underaccent{\bar}{Y}}-Q_{\underaccent{\bar}{X}\underaccent{\bar}{Y}}\|_{TV}
.

III-C

Vector Quantizer Layer

We constrain the RDFC rate
R
by using a vector quantizer (VQ) layer, as in
[
33
]
. The VQ-layer is initialized with a fixed embedding space
\underaccent{\bar}{\mathcal{J}}=[\underaccent{\bar}{j}_{1},\underaccent{\bar}{j}_{2},...,\underaccent{\bar}{j}_{2^{nR}}]
, and we ensure that the VQ-layer has a preceding layer with an
nR
-dimensional output
\tilde{\underaccent{\bar}{j}}
. The VQ-layer takes
\tilde{\underaccent{\bar}{j}}
as input and outputs the closest discrete index as

\displaystyle\underaccent{\bar}{j}_{i}\in\underaccent{\bar}{\mathcal{J}}:i=\underset{i}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,\|\tilde{\underaccent{\bar}{j}}-\underaccent{\bar}{j}_{i}\|_{2}.

(5)

Since (
5
) is not differentiable, the VQ-layer uses a straight-through estimator
[
33
]
to pass the gradient from the decoder to the layer preceding the VQ-layer during backpropagation. We adapt the implementation in
[
28
]
to our specific design.

IV-A

AE Design Insights

We observe faster convergence during training when layer 7 in Table
I
has the sigmoid activation function and a lower quantization error between the encoder output
\tilde{\underaccent{\bar}{j}}
and the VQ output
\underaccent{\bar}{j}
compared to when ReLU is used. We conjecture that the activation function normalizes
\tilde{\underaccent{\bar}{j}}
to an interval close to the latent alphabet
\underaccent{\bar}{\mathcal{J}}
, which seems to aid in training so that the encoder adapts better to the VQ-layer and the constraints it imposes. Moreover, a deep encoder and decoder converge faster than shallower architectures for a fixed network width. Our simulations do not indicate whether this is solely due to the larger number of network parameters or whether the network depth alone enables more complex behavior. Finally, in Algorithm
1
, we observe increased RDFC performance when we allow
B^{(\underaccent{\bar}{l})}(\underaccent{\bar}{x},\underaccent{\bar}{y})\!=\!\varnothing
for
(\underaccent{\bar}{x},\underaccent{\bar}{y},b)
such that we have
\Pr[\underaccent{\bar}{Y}\!=\!\underaccent{\bar}{y}|\underaccent{\bar}{X}\!=\!\underaccent{\bar}{x},B^{(\underaccent{\bar}{y})}_{b}]\approx 0
.
However, allowing empty sets in
B^{(\underaccent{\bar}{k})}(\underaccent{\bar}{x})
shows instances of both improved and reduced performance depending on
Q_{\underaccent{\bar}{Y}|\underaccent{\bar}{X}}
and
n
.

V
Conclusion

We proposed AE designs for the RDFC framework, providing significant communication load gains for multiple distributed computation and learning problems. We demonstrated that our AE designs achieve high RDFC performance for distributed synthesis of a BSC, where the amount of common randomness was shown to play a crucial role. Furthermore, we provided fundamental information-theoretic insights into the network architecture and the training-data generation. With this work, we aimed to facilitate the use of RDFC techniques for cutting-edge use cases, including neural image compression, FL with side information, and distributed secure and private function computation. Finally, while one can show that there is a gap between the achieved rate tuples and the asymptotic RDFC limits, such a comparison does not provide meaningful insights due to the short blocklengths considered in this work. Thus, in future work, we will apply, for example, hybrid coding methods introduced in
[
16
]
to our AE designs to consider practical blocklengths.
