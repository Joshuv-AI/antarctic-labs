Introduction

In this (self-contained) note, we are going to prove three identities that
hold in arbitrary unital noncommutative rings, and generalize some well-known
combinatorial identities (known as the
Abel–Hurwitz identities
).

In their simplest and least general versions, the identities we are
generalizing are equalities between polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}\left[X,Y,Z\right]
; namely, they state that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}\dbinom{n}{k}\left(X+kZ\right)^{k}\left(Y-kZ\right)^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{n!}{k!}\left(X+Y\right)^{k}Z^{n-k};

(1)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}\dbinom{n}{k}X\left(X+kZ\right)^{k-1}\left(Y-kZ\right)^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\left(X+Y\right)^{n};

(2)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}\dbinom{n}{k}X\left(X+kZ\right)^{k-1}Y\left(Y+\left(n-k\right)Z\right)^{n-k-1}

\displaystyle=\left(X+Y\right)\left(X+Y+nZ\right)^{n-1}

(3)

for every nonnegative integer
n
.

The pedantic reader will
have observed that two of these identities contain “fractional” terms like
X^{-1}
and
Y^{-1}
and thus should
be regarded as identities in the function field
\mathbb{Q}\left(X,Y,Z\right)
rather than in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}\left[X,Y,Z\right]
. However, this is a false alarm, because all these
“fractional” terms are cancelled. For
example, the addend for
k=0
in the sum on the left hand side of
(
2
) contains the “fractional” term
\left(X+0Z\right)^{0-1}=X^{-1}
, but this term is cancelled by the
factor
X
directly to its left. Similarly, all the other “fractional” terms disappear. Thus, all three identities are
actually identities in
\mathbb{Z}\left[X,Y,Z\right]
.
These identities
have a long history; for example, (
2
) goes back to Abel

[
Abel26
]
, who observed that it is a generalization of the binomial
formula (obtained by specializing
Z
to
0
). The equality (
1
)
is ascribed to Cauchy in Riordan’s text
[
Riorda68
, §1.5, Cauchy’s identity]
(at least in the specialization
Z=1
; but the general version can
be recovered from this specialization by dehomogenization). The equality
(
3
) is also well-known in combinatorics, and tends to appear in
the context of tree enumeration (see, e.g.,
[
Grinbe17
, Theorem 2]
) and of
umbral calculus (see, e.g.,
[
Roman84
, Section 2.6, Example 3]
).

The identities (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
) have
been generalized by various authors in different directions. The most famous
generalization is due to Hurwitz
[
Hurwit02
]
, who replaced
Z
by
n

commuting indeterminates
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{n}
. More precisely, the
equalities (IV), (II) and (III) in
[

say (in a more modern

denotes the set

\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}

\sum\limits_{S\subseteq V}\left(X+\sum\limits_{s\in S}Z_{s}\right)^{\left|S\right|}\left(Y-\sum\limits_{s\in S}Z_{s}\right)^{n-\left|S\right|}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\text{ are}\\
\text{distinct}\\
\text{elements of }V\end{subarray}}\left(X+Y\right)^{n-k}Z_{i_{1}}Z_{i_{2}}\cdots Z_{i_{k}};\ \ \ \ \

(4)

\sum\limits_{S\subseteq V}X\left(X+\sum\limits_{s\in S}Z_{s}\right)^{\left|S\right|-1}\left(Y-\sum\limits_{s\in S}Z_{s}\right)^{n-\left|S\right|}=\left(X+Y\right)^{n};

(5)

\sum\limits_{S\subseteq V}X\left(X+\sum\limits_{s\in S}Z_{s}\right)^{\left|S\right|-1}Y\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in V\setminus S}Z_{s}\right)^{n-\left|S\right|-1}=\left(X+Y\right)\left(X+Y+\sum\limits_{s\in V}Z_{s}\right)^{n-1}

(6)

in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}\left[X,Y,Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{n}\right]
.

Example 2.2

s\left(W\right)=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\right)\text{ is a list}\\
\text{of all elements of }W\\
\text{(with no repetitions)}\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}},

(19)

where
k=\left|W\right|
.

If
W
is any subset of
V
, and if
Y
is any element of
\mathbb{L}
, then
we define an element
r\left(Y,W\right)\in\mathbb{L}
by

r\left(Y,W\right)=\sum\limits_{S\subseteq W}\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-\left|S\right|}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}.

(20)

Now, from Corollary
4.1
, we can easily deduce the following claim:

Claim 1:
Let
W
be any subset of
V
satisfying
\left|W\right|=m
. Let
Y\in\mathbb{L}
. Then,
r\left(Y,W\right)=s\left(W\right)
.

[
Proof of Claim 1:
We have
m=\left|W\right|
. Hence,
Corollary
4.1
(applied to
W
,
Y
and
m
instead of
V
,
X

and
n
) yields

\sum\limits_{S\subseteq W}\left(-1\right)^{m-\left|S\right|}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m}\right)\text{ is a list}\\
\text{of all elements of
}W\\
\text{(with no repetitions)}\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{m}}=s\left(W\right)

(by (
19
), applied to
k=m
). Thus,
(
20
) becomes

\displaystyle r\left(Y,W\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{S\subseteq W}\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-\left|S\right|}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(-1\right)^{m-\left|S\right|}\\
\text{(since }\left|W\right|=m\text{)}\end{subarray}}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{S\subseteq W}\left(-1\right)^{m-\left|S\right|}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}=s\left(W\right).

This proves Claim 1.]

A more interesting claim is the following:

Claim 2:
Let
W
be a subset of
V
. Let
t\in W
. Let
Y\in\mathbb{L}
. Then,

r\left(Y,W\right)=r\left(Y+x_{t},W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)-r\left(Y,W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right).

[
Proof of Claim 2:
The definition of
r\left(Y,W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)
yields

\displaystyle r\left(Y,W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum\limits_{S\subseteq W\setminus\left\{t\right\}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq W;\\
t\notin S\end{subarray}}\end{subarray}}\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right|-\left|S\right|}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-1-\left|S\right|}\\
\text{(since }\left|W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right|=\left|W\right|-1\\
\text{(since }t\in W\text{))}\end{subarray}}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq W;\\
t\notin S\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-1-\left|S\right|}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}.

(21)

The same argument (applied to
Y+x_{t}
instead of
Y
) yields

\displaystyle r\left(Y+x_{t},W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq W;\\
t\notin S\end{subarray}}\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-1-\left|S\right|}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-\left|S\cup\left\{t\right\}\right|}\\
\text{(since }\left|S\right|=\left|S\cup\left\{t\right\}\right|-1\\
\text{(because }t\notin S\text{))}\end{subarray}}\left(Y+\underbrace{x_{t}+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\sum\limits_{s\in S\cup\left\{t\right\}}x_{s}\\
\text{(since }t\notin S\text{)}\end{subarray}}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq W;\\
t\notin S\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-\left|S\cup\left\{t\right\}\right|}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S\cup\left\{t\right\}}x_{s}\right)^{m}.

(22)

But the definition of
r\left(Y,W\right)
yields

\displaystyle r\left(Y,W\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{S\subseteq W}\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-\left|S\right|}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq W;\\
t\in S\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-\left|S\right|}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq W;\\
t\notin S\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-\left|S\cup\left\{t\right\}\right|}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S\cup\left\{t\right\}}x_{s}\right)^{m}\\
\text{(here, we have substituted }S\cup\left\{t\right\}\text{ for }S\text{ in the sum,}\\
\text{since the map }\left\{S\subseteq W\ \mid\ t\notin S\right\}\rightarrow\left\{S\subseteq W\ \mid\ t\in S\right\},\ S\mapsto S\cup\left\{t\right\}\\
\text{is a bijection)}\end{subarray}}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq W;\\
t\notin S\end{subarray}}\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-\left|S\right|}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=-\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-\left|S\right|-1}\\
=-\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-1-\left|S\right|}\end{subarray}}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{since each subset }S\text{ of }W\text{ satisfies either }t\in S\text{ or
}t\notin S\\
\text{(but not both)}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq W;\\
t\notin S\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-\left|S\cup\left\{t\right\}\right|}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S\cup\left\{t\right\}}x_{s}\right)^{m}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=r\left(Y+x_{t},W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)\\
\text{(by (\ref{pf.cor.polar2.c2.pf.short.5}))}\end{subarray}}-\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq W;\\
t\notin S\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|W\right|-1-\left|S\right|}\left(Y+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=r\left(Y,W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)\\
\text{(by (\ref{pf.cor.polar2.c2.pf.short.1}))}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=r\left(Y+x_{t},W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)-r\left(Y,W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right).

This proves Claim 2.]

Now, the following is easy to show by induction:

Claim 3:
Let
W
be a subset of
V
satisfying
\left|W\right|>m
. Let
Y\in\mathbb{L}
. Then,
r\left(Y,W\right)=0
.

[
Proof of Claim 3:
We shall prove Claim 3 by strong induction over

\left|W\right|
:

Induction step:
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that Claim 3 is proven
in the case when
\left|W\right|<k
. We must show that Claim 3 holds
in the case when
\left|W\right|=k
.

We have assumed that Claim 3 is proven in the case when
\left|W\right|<k
. In other words,

\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{if }W\text{ is any subset of }V\text{ satisfying }\left|W\right|>m\text{ and }\left|W\right|<k\text{,}\\
\text{and if }Y\in\mathbb{L}\text{, then }r\left(Y,W\right)=0\end{array}\right).

(23)

Now, let
W
be any subset of
V
satisfying
\left|W\right|>m
and

\left|W\right|=k
. Let
Y\in\mathbb{L}
. We shall show that

r\left(Y,W\right)=0
.

We have
\left|W\right|>m\geq 0
. Hence, there exists some
t\in W
.
Consider this
t
.

From
t\in W
, we obtain
\left|W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right|=\underbrace{\left|W\right|}_{>m}-1>m-1
, so that
\left|W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right|\geq m
. Thus, we are in one of the
following two cases:

Case 1:
We have
\left|W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right|=m
.

Case 2:
We have
\left|W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right|>m
.

Let us first consider Case 1. In this case, we have
\left|W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right|=m
. Hence, Claim 1 (applied to
W\setminus\left\{t\right\}
instead of
W
) yields
r\left(Y,W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)=s\left(W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)
. Also,
Claim 1 (applied to
W\setminus\left\{t\right\}
and
Y+x_{t}
instead of

W
and
Y
) yields
r\left(Y+x_{t},W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)=s\left(W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)
. Now, Claim 2 yields

r\left(Y,W\right)=\underbrace{r\left(Y+x_{t},W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)}_{=s\left(W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)}-\underbrace{r\left(Y,W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)}_{=s\left(W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)}=s\left(W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)-s\left(W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)=0.

Thus,
r\left(Y,W\right)=0
is proven in Case 1.

Let us now consider Case 2. In this case, we have
\left|W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right|>m
. Also,
\left|W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right|=\left|W\right|-1<\left|W\right|=k
. Hence, (
23
) (applied to
W\setminus\left\{t\right\}
instead of
W
) yields
r\left(Y,W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)=0
. Also, (
23
) (applied to

W\setminus\left\{t\right\}
and
Y+x_{t}
instead of
W
and
Y
) yields

r\left(Y+x_{t},W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)=0
. Now, Claim 2
yields

r\left(Y,W\right)=\underbrace{r\left(Y+x_{t},W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)}_{=0}-\underbrace{r\left(Y,W\setminus\left\{t\right\}\right)}_{=0}=0-0=0.

Thus,
r\left(Y,W\right)=0
is proven in Case 2.

We have now proven
r\left(Y,W\right)=0
in each of the two Cases 1 and 2.
Hence,
r\left(Y,W\right)=0
always holds.

Now, let us forget that we fixed
W
and
Y
. We thus have proven that if
W

is any subset of
V
satisfying
\left|W\right|>m
and
\left|W\right|=k
, and if
Y\in\mathbb{L}
, then
r\left(Y,W\right)=0
. In
other words, Claim 3 holds in the case when
\left|W\right|=k
. This
completes the induction step. Thus, Claim 3 is proven by strong induction.]

Now, recall that
V
is a subset of
V
satisfying
\left|V\right|=n>m
(since
m<n
). Hence, Claim 3 (applied to
W=V
and
Y=X
) yields

r\left(X,V\right)=0
. But the definition of
r\left(X,V\right)

yields

r\left(X,V\right)=\sum\limits_{S\subseteq V}\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\left|V\right|-\left|S\right|}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(-1\right)^{n-\left|S\right|}\\
\text{(since }\left|V\right|=n\text{)}\end{subarray}}\left(X+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}=\sum\limits_{S\subseteq V}\left(-1\right)^{n-\left|S\right|}\left(X+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}.

Hence,

\sum\limits_{S\subseteq V}\left(-1\right)^{n-\left|S\right|}\left(X+\sum\limits_{s\in S}x_{s}\right)^{m}=r\left(X,V\right)=0.

This proves Corollary
4.3
.
