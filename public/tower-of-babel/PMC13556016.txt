AI integration into undergraduate health education streams- a multicenter study in Sri Lanka
Authors: Sherine Loyidcy Mariyanayagam, Sinthuja Rasathurai, Rasika Manori Jayasinghe, Damayanthi Dassanayake, Sampath Tennakoon, Ruwan Duminda Jayasinghe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite rapid integration of AI in healthcare, formal AI education remains limited in health professional curricula globally, particularly in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs). This study provides the first large-scale, multi-disciplinary quantitative assessment of AI-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) among undergraduate healthcare students at Sri Lankan state universities and identifies independent predictors of AI engagement.MethodsA multi-centre descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted between March and June 2025 across five randomly selected Sri Lankan state universities, adhering to STROBE reporting guidelines. The minimum required sample of 385 was calculated using Cochran’s formula (Z = 1.96; p = 0.50; d = 0.05); 1,100 students were invited to achieve this target. A self-administered online questionnaire assessed KAP across five domains. Face and content validity were established through expert review and pre-testing with 15 participants outside the final sample. Two multivariable logistic regression models identified independent predictors of academic AI tool use and clinical AI exposure.ResultsOf 430 respondents (67.2% female; response rate 39.1%), AI awareness was near-universal (99.3%) and academic AI tool use was high (88.4%). Only 8.1% had received any formal AI training and only 21.6% reported clinical AI exposure. Curriculum integration was the strongest independent predictor of both academic AI use (AOR 2.58, 95% CI: 1.01–6.56, p = 0.047) and clinical AI exposure (AOR 5.16, 95% CI: 2.82–9.47, p < 0.001). Workshop participation independently predicted clinical AI exposure (AOR 2.71, 95% CI: 1.25–5.85, p = 0.011). The most frequently reported barriers were insufficient practical experience (55.8%), lack of guidance (55.8%), and limited resources (50.5%).ConclusionSri Lankan healthcare undergraduates demonstrate near-universal AI awareness but a pronounced competency gap characterised by limited formal training and minimal clinical AI engagement. Curriculum integration and structured experiential training were the strongest independent predictors of meaningful AI engagement. These findings support the urgent need for national curriculum reform and context-sensitive AI education frameworks for resource-constrained health professional training environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09796-2.

Introduction

The hospitals that will employ today’s medical, dental, veterinary and allied health graduates are already using artificial intelligence. Radiologists use AI algorithms to detect abnormalities on chest X-rays and CT scans [1, 2]. Pathologists use deep-learning systems to assist in tumour classification and grading [3]. Clinical decision support tools generate risk scores and treatment suggestions in real time [4]. Beyond diagnostics, AI has expanded into genomic analysis [5], retinal screening for diabetic retinopathy [6], and remote patient monitoring through wearable technologies [7]. As these applications continue to evolve, AI is expected to fundamentally reshape the skills a competent healthcare professional must possess [8]. A graduate who cannot interpret, question, and contextualise AI-generated outputs in clinical practice within an increasingly AI-enabled healthcare system is likely to be professionally underprepared.

International studies consistently report moderate to low AI knowledge and cautiously optimistic attitudes among healthcare students across medicine, dental, veterinary and allied health disciplines [9–12]. Students broadly acknowledge the importance of AI training, yet formal AI education remains absent from most health professional curricula. A 2024 global survey involving 192 medical, dental, and veterinary faculties found that over 75% of students had received no formal AI education within their programmes, despite growing AI adoption in the clinical settings they are preparing to enter [13]. A systematic review (2025) of 14 studies on AI integration in nursing and health sciences education confirmed that the gap is not motivational, students consistently express readiness but structural, driven by absent faculty expertise, underdeveloped curriculum frameworks, and inadequate institutional infrastructure [14].

Critically, the majority of published quantitative studies on this topic originate from high-income countries with established clinical AI infrastructure and experienced faculty. Evidence from lower-middle-income country (LMIC) settings, where educational resources, institutional capacity, and clinical environments differ considerably, remains limited [14, 15]. A cross-sectional (2025) study among medical and paramedical students in Iran found that while AI awareness was high, only 12.3% of students had received any formal AI instruction, and the majority identified faculty absence and resource limitations as the primary barriers to AI engagement [15]. A systematic review published in the same year further noted that LMIC students consistently demonstrated lower AI knowledge scores than their counterparts in high-income settings, and that structural barriers including limited infrastructure and institutional support were the predominant obstacles to AI engagement in these contexts [16]. These findings underscore that curriculum recommendations developed primarily in well-resourced settings cannot be assumed to translate to LMIC environments without adaptation.

Sri Lanka presents a particularly relevant case. The country’s National Digital Health Blueprint has identified AI as a national priority for health system modernisation [17]. Twelve state universities offer health-related degree programmes across medicine, allied health sciences, dental sciences, and veterinary sciences, collectively producing the majority of the country’s regulated health professionals. To the authors’ knowledge, only one prior study has explored AI perceptions among Sri Lankan health undergraduates, and it was limited to a qualitative investigation of medical students at two universities [18]. No large-scale, quantitative, multi-institutional, or multi-disciplinary study has yet examined AI knowledge, attitudes, and practices across Sri Lanka’s health professional education system. This gap is not merely academic. Curriculum reform decisions require locally grounded evidence, and that evidence does not currently exist.

A further limitation of the existing literature is conceptual. Most studies have treated AI use as a single variable without distinguishing between casual use of productivity tools such as writing assistants and the applied use of clinical AI systems for diagnostics, imaging analysis, or decision support [14, 16]. These represent fundamentally different levels of engagement with AI and combining them produces an overstated picture of clinical readiness. The present study addresses this by treating academic AI tool use and clinical AI exposure as separate outcome variables and examining the factors independently associated with each.

Therefore, the study addressed three research questions: what are the levels of AI-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among undergraduate healthcare students at selected Sri Lankan state universities; what sociodemographic and academic factors are independently associated with academic AI tool use and clinical AI exposure; and what are the principal structural barriers to AI adoption in health professional education in this LMIC context.

Methods

Study design and setting

A multi-centre descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted between March and June 2025, in accordance with the STROBE statement for cross-sectional studies [19]. Five universities were randomly selected from twelve Sri Lankan state universities with health-related faculties: the University of Peradeniya, the University of Sri Jayewardenepura, the University of Ruhuna, the Eastern University of Sri Lanka, and the University of Jaffna, representing four health disciplines: medicine, dental sciences, allied health sciences, and veterinary sciences.

Sampling strategy and sample size

Faculty-wise student population data for the 2024/2025 academic year were obtained from publicly available university records; the combined student population across the five selected universities was estimated at 5,390. The minimum required sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula (n = Z²pq/d²; Z = 1.96, p = 0.50, q = 0.50, d = 0.05), yielding a minimum of 385 participants. An anticipated online response rate of 35% necessitated the invitation of 1,100 students. The final sample was a non-probability convenience sample.

Participant recruitment and data collection

A structured self-administered questionnaire was distributed through institutional email and social media platforms (WhatsApp, Facebook, LinkedIn). The survey was administered entirely online via Google Forms. The first section contained a participant information sheet and electronic consent statement; students were required to confirm consent before proceeding. All questions were mandatory to minimise missing data. Two reminder messages were sent at one week and four weeks after initial distribution. Eligibility criteria were: current enrolment in a health-related undergraduate programme at one of the five selected universities and provision of electronic informed consent. No personally identifiable information was collected.

Study instrument

The questionnaire was developed through review of relevant literature [11, 20], adapted to the Sri Lankan undergraduate context, and reviewed by a senior academic for clarity and relevance. Pre-testing was conducted with 15 participants outside the final sample. The final questionnaire comprised five sections: demographic information; AI knowledge; attitudes toward AI in healthcare; academic and clinical AI practices; and barriers to AI adoption in health education. The instrument was designed as a descriptive KAP tool rather than a psychometric scale; Cronbach’s alpha was accordingly not calculated. Face and content validity were established through expert review and pre-testing. The absence of a formal content validity index and reliability coefficients is acknowledged as a limitation.

Bias control

Several strategies were implemented to strengthen methodological rigour. Institutional and disciplinary diversity was enhanced by including five universities and four health discipline categories. Anonymous, self-administered data collection was used to reduce social desirability bias. Standardised item wording was applied uniformly across all participants to minimise information bias. Mandatory question completion was enforced to reduce missing data. Nevertheless, the voluntary web-based recruitment approach may have introduced selection bias if students with greater AI interest were more likely to participate, and this limitation is acknowledged.

Statistical analysis

Data were cleaned and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0. Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages to describe the sociodemographic characteristics of participants and the distribution of AI knowledge, attitudes, practices, and barriers across the sample.The Shapiro -Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of continuous variables prior to further analysis, as this test is recommended for detecting departures from normality in moderate sample sizes. Missing data were minimal, accounting for less than 2% of responses across all variables as a result of mandatory question completion; therefore, no imputation was required.

Two multivariable binary logistic regression models were fitted to identify independent predictors of the two primary outcomes. The first model examined factors associated with academic AI tool use and the second examined predictors of clinical AI exposure during practical training. Logistic regression was selected because both outcome variables were binary. Predictor variables, including academic year, university affiliation, prior AI exposure, workshop participation, and perceived curriculum integration, were selected a priori based on theoretical relevance and the existing literature. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported for each predictor. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was applied to both models to evaluate model fit. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout the analysis.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the Faculty of Allied Health Sciences, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka (Ref. No: AHS/ERC/2024/163; approval date: 10 February 2025). Formal permission to distribute the survey among undergraduates was obtained from the deans of the relevant faculties. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 430 undergraduates from five Sri Lankan state universities participated, yielding a response rate of 39.1% (430/1,100). The majority of respondents were female (67.2%, n = 289). The largest age group was ≥ 25 years (37.2%, n = 160), followed by 23–24 years (34.0%, n = 146). Fourth-year students formed the largest academic year group (32.6%, n = 140), and 18.8% (n = 81) were first-year students. The Faculty of Allied Health Sciences accounted for the largest proportion of the sample (46.0%, n = 198), followed by the Faculty of Dental Sciences (27.4%, n = 118). The most represented degree programmes were BSc (Hons) Nursing (27.7%, n = 119) and Bachelor of Dental Surgery (27.2%, n = 117). Students from the University of Peradeniya accounted for 40.7% (n = 175) of the total sample. Full demographic and academic characteristics are presented in Table 1.

AI related knowledge among participants

Nearly all respondents (99.3%, n = 427) had heard of AI, whereas only 0.7% (n = 3) reported no prior awareness. More than half (59.5%, n = 256) reported prior exposure to AI-related technology or subjects, and 69.3% (n = 298) reported awareness of AI applications in healthcare. Formal AI training was uncommon. Only 8.1% (n = 35) had attended AI-related workshops or seminars, and the majority (91.9%, n = 395) had not. Regarding curriculum integration, 20.7% (n = 89) reported that AI tools were fully integrated into their academic curriculum, 30.7% (n = 132) reported partial integration, and 48.6% (n = 209) reported no curriculum integration.

When asked to identify areas of AI application in healthcare, the most frequently recognised areas were diagnostics and disease detection (68.3%, n = 293), medical imaging analysis (55.7%, n = 239), and robotic-assisted surgeries (52.9%, n = 227). Virtual health assistants or chatbots (47.3%, n = 203) and patient monitoring and management (47.3%, n = 203) were also commonly recognised. Personalised treatment planning (45.0%, n = 193), administrative tasks (38.7%, n = 166), and drug discovery and development (35.9%, n = 154) were less frequently identified. A small proportion (4.2%, n = 18) selected none of the above (Table 2).

Attitudes toward AI in healthcare

Nearly half of respondents expressed a positive outlook on the role of AI in enhancing healthcare services (49.5%, n = 213), while the majority held a neutral attitude (47.2%, n = 203), and a small proportion expressed a negative view (3.3%, n = 14).

Regarding specific attitudinal statements, 66.3% (n = 285) believed that AI can improve the quality of healthcare services, 8.1% (n = 35) did not, and 25.6% (n = 110) were unsure. When asked whether AI has the potential to replace human healthcare professionals, 24.7% (n = 106) agreed, 45.3% (n = 195) disagreed, and 30.0% (n = 129) were uncertain. The large majority agreed that healthcare professionals should be trained in AI-related skills (86.0%, n = 370), 3.5% (n = 15) disagreed, and 10.5% (n = 45) were unsure (Table 3).

AI use in academic and clinical settings

Among the 430 participants, 380 (88.4%) reported using AI tools during their academic studies, while 50 (11.6%) reported no academic AI tool use. Clinical exposure to AI technologies was substantially lower: 93 participants (21.6%) reported having observed or used AI technologies in real-world healthcare settings during clinical training, while 337 (78.4%) had not. Predictors of academic AI tool use

Results of the multivariable logistic regression analysis for predictors of academic AI tool use are presented in Table 5. Second-year students were significantly more likely than first-year students to use AI tools in their academic studies (AOR 3.35, 95% CI: 1.20–9.39, p = 0.022). No statistically significant differences were observed for third-year (AOR 1.90, 95% CI: 0.75–4.83, p = 0.175), fourth-year (AOR 1.70, 95% CI: 0.76–3.80, p = 0.198), or fifth-year students (AOR 1.32, 95% CI: 0.37–4.74, p = 0.676) compared with first-year students.

Full curriculum integration of AI was independently associated with academic AI tool use (AOR 2.58, 95% CI: 1.01–6.56, p = 0.047). Partial integration showed a borderline association that did not reach statistical significance (AOR 2.08, 95% CI: 0.98–4.39, p = 0.055). Prior exposure to AI was also an independent predictor of academic AI use (AOR 2.07, 95% CI: 1.14–3.77, p = 0.017). AI workshop attendance was not a significant independent predictor of academic AI use after adjustment (AOR 2.09, 95% CI: 0.47–9.22, p = 0.332). University affiliation was not significantly associated with academic AI tool use in any comparison (Table 5).

Predictors of clinical AI exposure

Table 6 presents predictors of clinical AI exposure. Students who had attended AI-related workshops or seminars had significantly higher odds of clinical AI exposure compared with those who had not (AOR 2.71, 95% CI: 1.25–5.85, p = 0.011). Full curriculum integration was the strongest predictor of clinical AI exposure in the model (AOR 5.16, 95% CI: 2.82–9.47, p < 0.001). Partial curriculum integration showed a borderline but non-significant association (AOR 1.74, 95% CI: 0.95–3.20, p = 0.073).

Students from the University of Jaffna had significantly higher odds of clinical AI exposure compared with students from the University of Peradeniya (AOR 1.85, 95% CI: 1.06–3.23, p = 0.031). No statistically significant differences were observed for the University of Sri Jayewardenepura (AOR 0.92, 95% CI: 0.47–1.80, p = 0.803), the University of Ruhuna (AOR 1.79, 95% CI: 0.78–4.09, p = 0.169), or the Eastern University of Sri Lanka (AOR 0.84, 95% CI: 0.23–3.05, p = 0.790) compared with the University of Peradeniya. Academic year was not significantly associated with clinical AI exposure in any comparison. After adjustment for all other variables, prior AI exposure was not independently associated with clinical AI exposure (AOR 0.96, 95% CI: 0.60–1.54, p = 0.867) (Table 6).

Barriers to AI adoption in health education

The most frequently reported barriers to AI adoption are presented in Fig. 2. The two most commonly cited barriers were insufficient practical experience (55.8%) and lack of guidance (55.8%). Other frequently reported challenges were limited access to resources (50.5%), lack of foundational knowledge (44.2%), fear of making errors (38.4%), and perceived complexity of AI concepts (30.7%). Approximately one in four respondents (23.0%) identified time constraints as a barrier. A small proportion (1.6%) reported other barriers not listed in the response options.

Discussion

The main finding of this study is a 66.8%-point gap between academic AI tool use (88.4%) and clinical AI exposure (21.6%). This disparity indicates that students are engaging with AI extensively within the bounded environment of academic productivity, but almost not at all within the clinical contexts where AI literacy will be most consequential for patient care. The following discussion examines the nature of this gap, contextualises the findings within the broader international, and draws out the educational and policy implications specific to Sri Lanka.

Awareness and knowledge

The high rate of AI awareness (99.3%) observed in this study is consistent with findings from both high-income and LMIC settings. Studies from Jordan [11], Saudi Arabia [12], and Kuwait [21] similarly report awareness rates exceeding 95%, reflecting the global penetration of AI in everyday digital life. The pattern of knowledge concentration in high-visibility applications (diagnostics 68.3%; medical imaging 55.7%; robotic surgery 52.9%) alongside near-absence of awareness in rehabilitation (4.7%), administrative AI (38.7%), and drug discovery (35.9%) closely mirrors findings from the 2023 systematic review by Mousavi Baigi and colleagues, which documented across 38 studies that healthcare students in LMICs were more likely to recognise AI in high-profile clinical applications than in operational or administrative domains [22]. This pattern reflects media-driven rather than curriculum-driven knowledge acquisition, a distribution that is likely to produce graduates capable of recognising AI but not of critically evaluating or safely applying it.

Among them, only 8.1% had attended AI-related workshops or seminars and only 20.7% reported full curriculum integration. This is consistent with the 2025 cross-sectional study from Iran, where 12.3% of medical and paramedical students reported receiving formal AI instruction [15], and with the 2024 global survey in which over 75% of students across 192 health professional faculties reported no formal AI education [13]. The systematic review by Alqaissi and Qtait (2025) similarly found across 14 studies that formal AI training opportunities were rare for nursing and health sciences students, irrespective of geographic setting [14]. The consistency of these findings across settings reinforces that the training deficit is a systemic issue rather than one unique to Sri Lanka, though its consequences may be more severe in LMIC settings where alternative routes to clinical AI engagement are even more limited.

Attitudes: optimism without grounded knowledge

Attitudes toward AI were broadly favourable in this sample: 49.5% expressed a positive outlook and 86.0% agreed that healthcare professionals should be trained in AI-related skills. This pattern is documented consistently in the international literature, including in LMIC settings. The 2025 study from Iran reported that 78.4% of students expressed positive attitudes toward AI in healthcare despite limited formal training [15], and the systematic review by Shishehgar and colleagues (2025) noted that positive attitudes toward AI are among the most consistently reported findings across studies of health professional students globally [16]. However, the co-existence of favourable attitudes with near-absent clinical AI engagement suggests that these attitudes reflect perceived familiarity rather than grounded competency. The substantial proportion uncertain about AI replacing healthcare professionals (30.0% uncertain; 24.7% in agreement) indicates that fundamental conceptual clarity about the scope, limitations, and ethical boundaries of clinical AI is not yet being achieved through informal exposure alone. Without this clarity, even motivated students are poorly equipped to engage critically with AI systems in clinical practice.

The academic-clinical gap

The 66.8%-point gap between academic AI tool use and clinical AI exposure is larger than has been reported in studies from high-income settings. Teng and colleagues (2022) reported a comparable but narrower disparity among Canadian healthcare students, where the academic-clinical gap in AI engagement was attributable primarily to curriculum absence rather than infrastructure deficit [9]. In this Sri Lankan sample, the gap is compounded by both factors: students lack formal curriculum frameworks and their clinical training environments lack AI-enabled technologies. This dual structural deficit is consistent with the pattern documented in LMIC health professional education systems more broadly.

The 2023 systematic review by Mousavi Baigi and colleagues identified infrastructural limitations and absent faculty expertise as the two most frequently cited explanations for low clinical AI engagement in LMIC-based studies, noting that students in these settings had fewer incidental encounters with AI tools during clinical placements compared with counterparts in high-income countries [22]. The dominance of productivity tools such as ChatGPT, Grammarly, and Gemini (used by approximately 85% of students for academic purposes) alongside the near-absence of clinical diagnostic AI use (7.9%) indicates that current student AI engagement is functional rather than translational, and that without structured intervention it will not develop into the higher-order clinical decision-making competencies required for safe AI application in practice.

Barriers

The barrier profile in this study, dominated by insufficient practical experience (55.8%), lack of guidance (55.8%), and limited resources (50.5%), is qualitatively consistent with international findings but quantitatively more pronounced. The 2025 systematic review by Alqaissi and Qtait documented similar barriers across 14 studies, identifying absence of formal AI education, lack of trained faculty, and limited infrastructure as the primary obstacles globally [14]; however, the prevalence of these barriers in the present study exceeds most published figures from high-income settings. The 2025 cross-sectional study from Iran reported that resource limitations (47.2%) and guidance absence (44.6%) were the most frequently cited barriers [15], with proportions notably lower than those observed in this Sri Lankan sample, suggesting that structural constraints may be more entrenched in the Sri Lankan context. The co-occurrence of psychological barriers including fear of making errors (38.4%) and perceived complexity of AI concepts (30.7%) with these structural ones is consistent with evidence that anxiety-related barriers become more prominent in the absence of formal training and supervised practice environments [14]. In an LMIC setting where both formal instruction and supervised clinical AI practice opportunities are scarce, the threshold for student engagement with unfamiliar technologies is naturally elevated.

Curriculum integration as the critical lever

The regression analysis consistently identifies structured curriculum integration as the dominant modifiable predictor of meaningful AI engagement. Full curriculum integration was the strongest predictor of both academic AI tool use (AOR 2.58, 95% CI: 1.01–6.56) and clinical AI exposure (AOR 5.16, 95% CI: 2.82–9.47), and workshop participation independently predicted clinical exposure (AOR 2.71, 95% CI: 1.25–5.85). The magnitude of the curriculum integration effect for clinical exposure, over five times the odds, provides compelling empirical support for mandatory curriculum integration as a policy priority. The finding that prior informal AI exposure was not independently associated with clinical AI engagement after adjustment (AOR 0.96, p = 0.867) confirms that access to AI tools without institutional structure does not develop clinical AI competency, a conclusion consistent with findings from LMIC settings where informal digital exposure has similarly failed to translate into clinical AI readiness [15].

The second-year effect (AOR 3.35), where second-year students were more likely to use AI tools than all higher academic year groups, may reflect generational digital familiarity combined with a novelty effect that diminishes as clinical demands increase. The inter-institutional variation, with Jaffna students showing significantly higher odds of clinical AI exposure (AOR 1.85), may reflect differences in clinical learning environments or hospital technology adoption.

Educational and policy implications

These findings support a staged, priority-ordered policy response that is grounded in the specific structural realities of Sri Lanka as an LMIC. First, faculty development must precede student-facing AI curriculum deployment; no student curriculum is effective without faculty who can teach AI critically. Second, minimum AI literacy standards, encompassing technical knowledge, clinical application, critical appraisal, and ethics should be mandated across all health professional degree programmes, developed for the Sri Lankan health system context rather than adopted wholesale from high-income setting frameworks. Third, experiential learning modalities including simulation, supervised clinical AI tool use, and structured workshops must be resourced; the independent association between workshop attendance and clinical AI exposure (AOR 2.71) provides direct empirical support for this investment. Fourth, longitudinal evaluation studies are needed to assess whether curriculum interventions produce the competency outcomes they aim to generate [13, 15].

Strengths

This is the first large-scale, multi-institutional, multi-disciplinary quantitative study of AI KAP among health undergraduates in Sri Lanka. The five-university, four-discipline design, the use of pre-specified multivariable regression models, STROBE-adherent reporting, and the separation of academic AI tool use from clinical AI exposure as distinct outcome variables are key methodological strengths that distinguish this study from prior literature in this field.

Limitations

Several limitations have been identified in this study. The voluntary web-based recruitment approach may have introduced selection bias in participant enrolment. Self-reported clinical AI exposure is subject to social desirability bias. The non-probability sampling approach limits the generalisability of the findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between curriculum integration and AI engagement. The descriptive KAP instrument lacked formal psychometric validation, including a content validity index and reliability coefficients. Framing AI familiarity in terms of whether students had used or heard of AI tools may not accurately reflect practical competency levels.

Future research should adopt mixed-methods designs that combine quantitative KAP assessment with qualitative exploration of students’ experiences with AI in clinical settings, thereby providing the contextual depth that quantitative surveys alone cannot capture.

Conclusions

This study shows that healthcare undergraduate students in Sri Lanka are widely aware of artificial intelligence, but this awareness has not resulted in formal training or effiective clinical engagement with AI tools. A large gap exists between how students use AI for academic purposes and how little they are exposed to AI in clinical settings. This gap is mainly due to the insufficient faculty expertise, limited availability of AI technologies in clinical training environments, and the absence of a national AI education framework. Curriculum integration and attendance at AI workshops were the strongest factors associated with AI engagement. These findings confirm that Sri Lanka needs coordinated and structured action to prepare its future healthcare workforce for an AI-enabled clinical environment.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, the following recommendations are proposed for health professional education authorities and institutions in Sri Lanka. Faculty members should be trained in AI before any student-facing AI curriculum is introduced. Minimum AI literacy standards should be established and made compulsory across all health professional degree programmes. Structured clinical AI exposure opportunities, including workshops, simulation exercises, and supervised AI tool use during clinical placements, should be incorporated into the curriculum. Longitudinal studies should be conducted to evaluate whether these curriculum changes lead to improved AI competency among graduating students.
