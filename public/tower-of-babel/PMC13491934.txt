The application value of time-dependent DWI in the differential diagnosis of benign and malignant breast lesions and microstructural feature analysis
Authors: Xuan Qi, Yongsheng He, Wuling Wang, Chengfeng Zhai, Shanshan Yu, Hongkai Yang, Shaofeng Duan, Mengxiao Liu, Meining Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the incremental diagnostic value of various ADC sequences and microstructural parameters derived from time-dependent diffusion-weighted imaging (td-DWI) in differentiating benign and malignant breast lesions.MethodsIn this study, a total of 52 patients with breast lesions were included, comprising 24 cases of benign lesions and 28 cases of malignant lesions. MRI examinations were conducted to measure four ADC values: ADC_25Hz, ADC_50Hz, ADC_PGSE, and ADC_Zoomit. Additionally, microstructural parameters fin, Dex, d, and Cellularity were calculated. Clinical and imaging characteristics between two groups were compared using independent samples t-tests and chi-square tests, and the diagnostic performance of each parameter was evaluated.ResultsAge, maximum diameter, FGT, morphology, TIC curve, edema, T2WI signal, DWI signal, and BI-RADS score showed significant differences between the benign and malignant groups (p < 0.05). The differences in ADC_25Hz, ADC_PGSE, and ADC_Zoomit between the two groups were statistically significant (p = 0.042, 0.020, < 0.001), with ADC_Zoomit exhibiting the highest AUC of 0.808. Among the microstructural parameters, fin, Dex, and d showed significant differences between the groups (p = 0.005, 0.001, < 0.001), with d having the highest AUC of 0.816 and an accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity of 0.769, 0.792, and 0.75, respectively. A logistic regression model based on diffusion-derived parameters demonstrated good diagnostic performance, and the combined model incorporating clinical and imaging features achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy.ConclusionMicrostructural parameters derived from td-DWI, particularly d, together with ADC_Zoomit, demonstrated promising diagnostic performance for differentiating benign and malignant breast lesions. These findings suggest that td-DWI may provide incremental diagnostic value beyond conventional MRI features for breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02500-2.Keywords: Diffusion weighted imaging, Microstructural imaging, Oscillating gradient spin echo sequence, Breast cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors among women worldwide. Accurate early diagnosis is crucial for improving patient survival rates and treatment outcomes [1]. MRI plays a vital role in the screening and diagnosis of breast cancer. Diffusion Weighted Imaging (DWI) provides critical tumor information by measuring the random motion of water molecules within tissues [2]. ZOOMit-DWI, a reduced field-of-view diffusion technique, improves spatial resolution and reduces geometric distortion, thereby enhancing lesion depiction [3]. However, the traditional Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC) only provides an average value of the overall diffusion characteristics and may be influenced by extracellular edema, stromal components, perfusion effects, and tumor heterogeneity.

Time-dependent diffusion imaging (td-DWI), which combines oscillating gradient spin echo (OGSE) and pulsed gradient spin echo (PGSE) acquisitions, enables interrogation of diffusion behavior at different effective diffusion times. Using the IMPULSED model, td-DWI allows for the derivation of key microstructural parameters, including intracellular volume fraction (fin), extracellular diffusivity (Dex), effective cell diameter (d), and cellularity [4, 5]. Unlike PGSE, OGSE introduces rapidly oscillating gradient fields to shorten the diffusion time, making it more sensitive to smaller spatial scales and more effective in detecting fine tissue structures [6]. By combining OGSE and PGSE, td-DWI enables the study of water molecule diffusion behavior at different time scales, providing more comprehensive information about tumor microstructure. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate and compare the diagnostic performance of ZOOMit-DWI and td-DWI in differentiating benign and malignant breast lesions.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study analyzed imaging and clinical data from 52 consecutive patients with breast lesions who underwent breast MRI at Ma’anshan People’s Hospital between January and July 2024. The imaging data were prospectively acquired using a standardized protocol that included td-DWI sequences, while clinical and pathological data were analyzed retrospectively. The patients’ ages ranged from 26 to 85 years (Fig. 1). The inclusion criteria were: (i) Availability of a complete MRI examination sequence; (ii) No history of biopsy, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy prior to the MRI examination, with pathological results obtained within two weeks post-examination; (iii) Availability of complete clinical and pathological data. The exclusion criteria were: (i) Prior biopsy, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy; (ii) Incomplete MRI technique or poor image quality; (iii) Lack of necessary pathological results. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ma’anshan People’s Hospital, approval number: 202,401,201, and informed consent was waived, given the retrospective study and anonymised data of patients.

MRI examination

The td-DWI technique requires the combination of OGSE and PGSE sequences to capture DWI signals at different diffusion times, thereby acquiring diffusion-time-dependent information from different microstructural components. The examinations were conducted using a MAGNETOM Prisma 3.0T MRI system equipped with a high-performance gradient system (maximum gradient strength = 80 mT/m, maximum slew rate = 200 mT/m), and a dedicated 18-channel phased-array breast coil was used for scanning. Patients were positioned prone, with both breasts placed within the breast coil, and entered the scanner feet-first. The parameters were as follows: (i) Axial T2-weighted imaging (T2WI): turbo spin echo (TSE) sequence, field of view (FOV) 340 × 340 mm², repetition time (TR) 6000 ms, echo time (TE) 63 ms, slice thickness 4 mm, interslice gap 0.8 mm; (ii) ZOOMit-DWI: FOV 340 × 186 mm², matrix size: 190 × 190, Voxel size: 0.9 × 0.9 × 4.0 mm3, TR 6000 ms, TE 63 ms, slice thickness 4.0 mm, interslice gap 0.8 mm, b-values = 50, 500, 1000, 1500 s/mm²; (iii) OGSE: oscillation frequencies of 25 Hz (delta = 41.52 ms, DELTA = 60 ms, b-values = 0, 250, 500, 750, 1000 s/mm²) and 50 Hz (delta = 41.52 ms, DELTA = 60 ms, b-values = 0, 100, 200, 300 s/mm²); (iv) PGSE: delta = 46.2 ms, DELTA = 57.2 ms, b-values = 0, 250, 500, 750, 1000 s/mm². Additional parameters used in both the OGSE and PGSE sequences included: FOV 340 × 215 mm², TR 5000 ms, TE 120 ms, slice thickness 4 mm, and 4 diffusion directions [4]; (v) Dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) sequence: FOV 360 × 360 mm², TR 4.13 ms, TE 1.3 ms, slice thickness 1.2 mm, including 1 phase of non-contrast scan and 8 phases of contrast-enhanced scans, with a scan time of 60 s per phase. At the end of the first non-contrast scan phase, 20 ml of contrast agent (gadoterate meglumine injection, Hengrui, Jiangsu) was administered, followed by 20 ml of saline, at an injection rate of 2.5 ml/s.

Image analysis and processing

All images were analyzed independently by two radiologists with 8 and 10 years of experience in breast MRI diagnosis, respectively, without knowledge of the pathological outcomes. The analyses were conducted following the standardized descriptions provided by the 5th edition of the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS). Recorded parameters included lesion location, type, morphology, margins, maximum diameter, background parenchymal enhancement (BPE), fibroglandular tissue (FGT), signal intensity on T2WI and DWI, peritumoral edema, and BI-RADS score. In cases where significant discrepancies arose between the two radiologists, a third radiologist was consulted, and a consensus was reached among all three. DCE-MRI images were processed on the Siemens Syngo.via post-processing workstation to generate time-signal intensity curve (TIC), which were classified into inflow type (Type I), plateau type (Type II), and washout type (Type III).

ADC value measurements and region of interest (ROI) delineations were jointly conducted by the two aforementioned radiologists, also blinded to the pathological results. ADC values were measured across four sequences: ADC_25Hz, ADC_50Hz, ADC_PGSE, and ADC_Zoomit. ROIs were drawn along the lesion margins on the slice with the largest lesion area in the ADC images of different sequences, referencing T2WI and DCE-MRI sequences, while avoiding cystic or necrotic areas (Fig. 2). One radiologist performed the delineation, and the other reviewed it. To evaluate interobserver reproducibility, 20 patients were randomly selected, and ROI delineation was independently repeated by the third radiologist who was blinded to the pathological results. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to assess the consistency of ADC and microstructural parameter measurements (Supplementary Table S1).

Microstructural parameter measurement based on td-DWI

td-DWI data were quantitatively analyzed using the IMPULSED model, a validated mathematical model for characterizing diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging signals that accurately depict the diffusion behavior of water molecules in biological tissues, especially the microstructural diffusion characteristics within cellular structures of breast lesions. In this study, the DWI signals corresponding to all b-values of the OGSE 25 Hz, OGSE 50 Hz, and PGSE sequences (the core sequences of td-DWI) were collected as the original input data of the IMPULSED model to ensure comprehensiveness and accuracy of microstructural parameter calculation.

The IMPULSED model simplifies breast lesion tissue into a two-compartment model (intracellular and extracellular) for quantitative analysis [4, 7, 8]. The intracellular compartment consists of tumor cells modeled as densely packed spheres, characterized by microstructural properties such as mean cell diameter (d) and intracellular diffusivity (Din); the extracellular compartment is regarded as an isotropic medium, with extracellular diffusivity (Dex) as its key diffusion characteristic. The fitting and solution of the IMPULSED model were implemented by custom-written code in MATLAB (Mathworks, Inc.), with the least squares method as the core fitting algorithm. To avoid the local minimum problem in the fitting process and ensure the reliability of the results, a 100-time randomized initialization strategy was adopted in the code, and the fitting result with the smallest residual was selected as the final model output.

Through the above model fitting and calculation, the core microstructural parameters including fin, Dex, and d were obtained. On this basis, Cellularity of breast lesions was further calculated according to the formula Cellularity = 100 × fin/d, which reflects the cellular packing density of the lesion tissue. All the above quantitative microstructural parameters (fin, Dex, d, and Cellularity) were visualized as pseudocolor maps for intuitive observation and analysis (Fig. 2).

Model construction and performance evaluation

To compare the diagnostic performance of td-DWI-derived parameters and conventional clinical and imaging features, logistic regression models were constructed.The diagnostic performance of each model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1), and the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, recall, and F1 score were calculated (Supplementary Fig.S2). To assess model robustness and reduce the risk of overfitting, leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV) was performed. In our study, variable selection was performed using a two-step strategy to reduce model complexity and mitigate the risk of overfitting. First, variables with p < 0.05 in univariable analysis were selected as candidate predictors. Subsequently, correlation analysis was performed, and highly collinear variables (r > 0.8) were excluded prior to model construction (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software (version 3.5.1; http://www.R-project.org). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and intergroup comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test. Continuous variables not conforming to a normal distribution were presented as median (P25, P75), with intergroup comparisons conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as n(%), and comparisons were made using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To mitigate the risk of inflated type I error arising from multiple pairwise comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. ROC curve analysis was performed to calculate the AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. To evaluate whether the sample size was sufficient to detect intergroup differences, post-hoc power analysis was performed based on the observed effect sizes. The detailed results of the effect size and power analysis are provided in the Supplementary Table S2. Pairwise comparisons of AUC values between correlated ROC curves were conducted using the DeLong test (Supplementary Table S3).

Results

Comparison of clinical and imaging characteristics between benign and malignant breast lesions

A total of 52 patients with breast lesions were included in this study, comprising 24 cases of benign lesions with a mean age of 46.4 ± 10.0 years. The benign lesions comprised 4 cases of adenosis, 7 adenomas, 2 intraductal papillomas, 3 phyllodes tumors, and 2 cases of granulomatous mastitis, as well as 5 cases of adenosis with adenoma formation and 1 case of adenosis associated with papilloma formation. There were 28 cases of malignant lesions with a mean age of 58.1 ± 11.0 years, including 27 cases of invasive carcinoma of no special type and 1 case of intermediate-grade ductal carcinoma in situ. Statistically significant differences were observed between the benign and malignant groups in terms of age, maximum diameter, FGT, morphology, TIC, peritumoral edema, T2WI signal, DWI signal, and BI-RADS (p < 0.05). Among these, the TIC pattern demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.967, followed by BI-RADS category (AUC = 0.933) and DWI signal (AUC = 0.900) (Table 1). However, there were no statistically significant differences in menopausal status, family history, laterality, location, and BPE between the two groups (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Among the evaluated parameters, d and ADC_Zoomit demonstrated the largest effect sizes (r = 0.538 and 0.526, respectively), accompanied by very high post-hoc statistical power (both power = 0.999), supporting robust separability between benign and malignant lesions (Supplementary Table S2).

Diagnostic performance of different models

The multivariable model based on diffusion-derived parameters demonstrated good diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.933, accuracy of 0.909, sensitivity of 1.000, and specificity of 0.800. When diffusion-derived parameters were integrated with conventional clinical and imaging features, the combined model achieved the highest diagnostic performance within the training dataset. LOO-CV was subsequently performed to assess model stability and generalizability, yielding a cross-validated accuracy of 0.962 (Table 5). Analysis of regression coefficients demonstrated that conventional MRI features, particularly BI-RADS category and DWI signal, showed relatively large contributions to the combined model, while diffusion-derived parameters including ADC_Zoomit and d also contributed to model performance (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Fig. S4).

Discussion

This study utilized a range of ADC sequences (including ADC_Zoomit, ADC_25Hz, ADC_50Hz, and ADC_PGSE) along with microstructural parameters (Cellularity, d, fin, and Dex) to differentiate between benign and malignant breast lesions. The findings indicate that ADC_25Hz, ADC_PGSE, ADC_Zoomit, fin, Dex, and d possess significant diagnostic value in distinguishing between these two types of lesions. Although ADC_50Hz demonstrated a trend toward lower values in malignant lesions, the difference did not reach statistical significance. One possible explanation is that the maximum b-values used at 50 Hz were lower than those employed for PGSE acquisition, which may have reduced sensitivity to restricted diffusion effects. Additionally, higher oscillation frequencies shorten the effective diffusion time, probing smaller spatial scales that may not optimally capture the dominant microstructural differences between benign and malignant breast lesions. These technical and diffusion-time-related factors may have contributed to the reduced discriminative performance observed at 50 Hz. Notably, the microstructural parameter d, which reflects changes in cell size, demonstrated the highest accuracy and sensitivity among the parameters tested. This suggests that d could potentially offer a more nuanced assessment of tumor behavior compared to traditional ADC measurements alone. These findings suggest that d, together with other microstructural parameters, may provide complementary quantitative information for breast lesion characterization.

Conventional MRI features, such as BI-RADS category and TIC pattern, demonstrated strong diagnostic performance, likely because they integrate multiple morphological and kinetic characteristics routinely used in breast MRI interpretation. Diffusion-derived parameters, however, provide quantitative information about tissue microstructure and therefore may serve as complementary biomarkers. DWI has substantial value in differentiating between benign and malignant breast lesions and in assessing tumor progression [9]. The ADC is a critical parameter of DWI, commonly used to evaluate the diffusion characteristics of tumors. However, traditional ADC measurements are limited by spatial resolution and specificity, and are influenced by various factors, including cellular density, interstitial space, and cell membrane permeability, making it challenging to fully capture the microstructural changes within tumors [10]. In our study, the Zoomit-DWI sequence was employed, leveraging parallel transmission technology with a dual-channel RF pulse transmission system. This setup enables selective excitation, which effectively reduces the negative impact of aliasing artifacts, enhances spatial resolution, and minimizes distortion and blurring [11, 12]. Additionally, for Gaussian diffusion, ADC values are not affected by diffusion time [13]. In reality, water molecule diffusion in tissues does not follow a Gaussian distribution. DWI has shown considerable potential for assessing cellular microstructural characteristics and has achieved significant progress in imaging applications for various fields [14].

Microstructural imaging is commonly performed using PGSE, which can detect minimum dimensions exceeding 13 ×m, making it inadequate for accurately assessing the spatial dimensions of most human cells [5]. OGSE is an innovative technique that replaces the traditional long diffusion-sensitive gradients with rapidly oscillating gradients, thereby shortening the diffusion gradient time and enabling the investigation of the diffusion time dependency of ADC values within shorter diffusion time ranges [15]. The ADC values obtained from DWI-OGSE are generally higher than those derived from conventional pulsed gradient DWI [16]. This td-DWI approach provides detailed information about tissue microstructural characteristics and has shown significant clinical utility across multiple systemic pathologies [5, 15, 17–19]. In a study by Ejima et al., the ratio of different ADC values was utilized to assess endometrial cancer, revealing that the ADC-OGSE/ADC-conventional ratio could effectively stratify the risk in endometrial cancer patients [6]. By developing mathematical models from td-DWI data, parameters that reflect tissue microstructural information can be derived to achieve microstructural imaging [20]. Tumor cells undergo complex metabolic flux changes to achieve growth and proliferation [21]. The size and distribution of tumor cells are important morphological features in histopathology. Ba et al.‘s study revealed that the parameter d was statistically significant in evaluating different Her2 and LN (Lymph node) statuses in breast cancer (p < 0.05), while fin and Dex were significant in assessing different PR statuses (p < 0.05) [18]. Additionally, fin showed a significant correlation with ADC, whereas d did not, suggesting that d may be a relatively independent factor in the immunophenotyping of breast cancer. The study by Hoffmann et al. confirmed that changes in the average d value of tumor cells can be used to monitor the response of breast cancer to immunotherapy [22]. The application of microstructural parameters in differentiating benign and malignant breast lesions is relatively limited. Some studies have indicated that there is a significant difference in cellularity between benign and malignant breast lesions, with higher cellular density typically associated with malignancy [23]. The parameter d reflects changes in effective cell size, whereas fin and Dex characterizes alterations in the intracellular and extracellular microenvironment, thereby providing additional diagnostic information. In our study, the parameter d demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance among all microstructural parameters. The parameter d represents the effective cell diameter estimated from diffusion-time-dependent signal modeling. Malignant breast lesions are typically associated with increased cellular proliferation, altered nuclear-cytoplasmic ratios, and restricted intracellular diffusion, which may contribute to changes in cell size. However, the AUC of d is numerically lower than the AUC reported for conventional ADC in some large multicenter studies [24]. This discrepancy may be related to differences in cohort composition, imaging protocols, scanner platforms, and reference standards across studies. Importantly, under a standardized acquisition protocol within the same cohort, d demonstrated superior discriminative performance compared with ADC_ZOOMit and other diffusion-derived metrics. In our cohort, the microstructural parameter d exhibited a median value of 7.77 μm in malignant lesions. Histologically, the average diameter of breast cancer cells has been reported to be approximately 14 μm [8]. It should be noted that d represents an imaging-derived effective cell diameter rather than a direct anatomical measurement, and diffusion-based estimates are influenced by model assumptions, restricted diffusion geometry, and diffusion-time effects, which may lead to lower apparent values compared with histological measurements. Notably, Ba et al.’ [18] reported a strong correlation between diffusion-derived d values and histological quantification (r = 0.87, p = 0.02), supporting the biological plausibility of this parameter. However, quantitative histopathological measurements of cell size were not available in our cohort, and correlation analysis could not be performed. Taken together, the clinical relevance of d may reside less in absolute agreement with histological cell size and more in its ability to reflect microstructural restriction patterns.

OGSE-based td-DWI may have broader clinical applicability beyond high-end 3T systems such as the Siemens Prisma used in this study. Most modern clinical 3T scanners are equipped with gradient systems meeting the basic requirements for OGSE implementation (Gmax ≥ 60–80 mT/m, SRmax ≥ 150–200 T/m/s). These systems can support oscillation frequencies of 25–50 Hz and sufficient b-values for reliable microstructural parameter quantification, which aligns with the core technical demands of our study. With the gradual establishment of standardized scanning protocols and the development of post-processing pipelines [25], inter-institutional reproducibility is continuously improved, facilitating broader clinical adoption for widespread clinical adoption of OGSE. Notably, 1.5T systems - the most prevalent in routine practice - hold significant potential for OGSE adaptation through targeted optimizations [23, 25]. Hardware advancements (e.g., high-efficiency gradient coils for 1.5T) and software innovations (AI denoising, TE-shortened waveforms) can mitigate gradient and SNR limitations. Even with reduced oscillation frequencies (20–30 Hz, feasible at 1.5T), preliminary data suggest OGSE can still capture key microstructural differences between benign and malignant lesions, aligning with the core biological insight of our study (tumor cell size disparity) [18, 23, 25]. Standardization of scanning parameters and user-friendly post-processing tools will further facilitate 1.5T OGSE translation, expanding access to this advanced technique globally.

This study has several limitations, including the relatively small sample size and the heterogeneous distribution of benign lesion subtypes, which may limit the generalizability of the findings and preclude further subgroup analysis. Although LOO-CV was performed to evaluate model stability, this validation approach may still yield optimistic performance estimates in small datasets and cannot fully eliminate the risk of overfitting. ROI delineation was performed on a single representative slice, which may not fully capture intratumoral heterogeneity and could affect the accuracy of diffusion-derived parameters. In addition, direct histopathological validation of the parameter d was not performed in this cohort. Future studies incorporating imaging–pathology correlation are warranted to further validate these microstructural biomarkers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, among the evaluated parameters, the microstructural parameter d and ADC_Zoomit demonstrated the highest discriminative performance for differentiating benign and malignant breast lesions. These findings suggest that microstructural modeling derived from td-DWI and Zoomit-DWI may provide incremental diagnostic value for breast cancer. Further validation in larger, multicenter prospective studies is warranted before broader clinical application.
