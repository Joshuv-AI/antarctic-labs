Precision in practice: a systematic review and meta-analysis of virtual reality efficacy for osteoarthritis-specific total knee arthroplasty rehabilitation
Authors: Lulu Zhu, Jinghao Zhai, Xian Jian, Shuqing Chen, Liangliang Song, Yuanyuan Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVirtual reality (VR) had gained traction as an innovative approach for post-total knee arthroplasty (TKA) rehabilitation in patients with knee osteoarthritis (KOA). However, evidence of its comparative efficacy against conventional methods remained fragmented. This study synthesized data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to evaluate VR’s impact on pain, functional recovery, and quality of life in KOA patients post-TKA.MethodsA systematic search was performed across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (inception to December 2024). RCTs comparing VR-based rehabilitation with standard care in KOA patients post-TKA were included. Data on pain (Visual Analogue Scale [VAS], Numerical Pain Rating Scale [NPRS]), functional outcomes (Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index [WOMAC], Timed Up and Go [TUG], Range of Motion [ROM]), and quality of life (Short Form-36 [SF-36], EuroQol Five-Dimensional Questionnaire [EQ-5D]) were extracted. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration tool. Meta-analyses were conducted using RevMan 5.3, with mean differences (MD) or standardized MD and 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated for continuous outcomes. Heterogeneity was quantified via the I² statistic.ResultsTen RCTs (588 participants) were analyzed. VR interventions demonstrated transient advantages in WOMAC scores at one month (SMD: -1.40; 95% CI: -2.37 to -0.43; P < 0.01) and 3 months (SMD: -1.14; 95% CI: -2.29 to 0.00; P = 0.05), but no significant benefit was observed at 6 months. TUG performance improved short-term (SMD: -1.10; 95% CI: -2.06 to -0.14; P = 0.03), while ROM showed sustained gains (1-week SMD: 1.38; 95% CI: 0.84–1.92; P < 0.01). Pain outcomes varied: NPRS favored VR, whereas VAS results showed no short-term differences. Quality of life data were inconclusive due to limited reporting. Substantial heterogeneity (I² > 70%) was observed, which was attributed to variability in VR modalities, session duration, and rehabilitation settings.ConclusionVR-based rehabilitation provided short-term improvements in functional mobility and joint flexibility for KOA patients post-TKA, but its long-term benefits remained uncertain. Methodological limitations, including high heterogeneity and inadequate blinding, necessitated cautious interpretation of the findings. Future trials should adopt standardized protocols, integrate gender-stratified analyses, and prioritize long-term follow-ups to optimize VR’s clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10088-5.Keywords: Virtual reality, Total knee arthroplasty, Osteoarthritis, Rehabilitation, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) remains a major contributor to global disability, with knee osteoarthritis (KOA) being a predominant subtype characterized by progressive joint degeneration and functional decline. Globally, KOA affects an estimated 250 million individuals, leading to chronic pain, reduced mobility, and diminished quality of life [1]. Beyond physical symptoms, the condition is associated with complications such as muscle weakness, psychological distress, and an elevated risk of comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease and depression [2, 3]. These factors collectively place substantial strain on healthcare systems, including rising direct medical costs, significant indirect costs from productivity losses, and sustained pressure on surgical and rehabilitation capacity due to the growing demand for procedures like total knee arthroplasty [4, 5].

Treatment strategies for KOA span conservative measures to surgical interventions. Conservative treatment strategies include medication, physical therapy, and lifestyle modification, where lifestyle modification mainly refers to weight loss, low-impact exercise. Surgical options are typically reserved for advanced disease stages refractory to comprehensive conservative management. Among surgical options, Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA) stands as a gold-standard procedure for advanced disease, offering significant pain relief and functional restoration [6]. Annually, over 1.5 million TKA procedures are performed in the United States alone, reflecting its critical role in managing end-stage OA [7].

Postoperative rehabilitation is integral to achieving optimal TKA outcomes. Conventional approaches, such as in-person physiotherapy and structured exercise regimens, are widely implemented but face limitations, including accessibility barriers for patients in rural areas, high costs, and suboptimal adherence [8, 9]. The COVID-19 pandemic catalyzed a paradigm shift toward remote healthcare solutions, highlighting the urgency of innovative rehabilitation modalities [10]. Among these, Virtual reality (VR) technology has emerged as a versatile tool, enabling immersive, interactive therapy sessions that transcend geographical boundaries and enhance patient engagement [11].

VR’s integration into medical practice has expanded rapidly, particularly in rehabilitation contexts. Post-pandemic, its applications have extended to remote monitoring, personalized exercise programs, and real-time feedback systems, demonstrating efficacy in diverse areas such as post-stroke recovery, chronic pain management, and balance training [12, 13]. VR encompasses a spectrum of technologies that create simulated environments for user interaction, commonly categorized by immersion levels: immersive VR, which fully replaces the real world via a head-mounted display; semi-immersive VR, which provides a partial virtual environment typically through large projection systems; and non-immersive VR, where the virtual environment is displayed on a standard screen, often utilizing exergaming platforms like the Nintendo Wii [14, 15].

The therapeutic rationale for VR in TKA rehabilitation is underpinned by mechanisms that enhance both the quality and quantity of therapy. Directly, it addresses key recovery barriers: the immersive environment alleviates kinesiophobia and pain-related anxiety, facilitating greater volitional movement, while simultaneously enabling the delivery of highly repetitive, task-oriented training that is fundamental to driving neuroplasticity and functional relearning [16]. Indirectly, by incorporating gamified elements such as goal setting and reward mechanisms while enabling personalized exercise adaptation, VR enhances patient adherence and promotes motor learning by maintaining an optimal challenge level [11, 13]. Currently, adherence is widely recognized as a crucial prerequisite for achieving the therapeutic dose required for functional adaptation [17], VR establishes a compelling pathway to efficacy. The integration of supplementary sensors in advanced systems further augments this by providing enriched biofeedback and objective movement data [18]. Consequently, in TKA rehabilitation, VR platforms can effectively simulate functional movements, provide precise progress tracking, and deliver individualized therapy, offering a potential advantage over conventional rehabilitation methods [19–21].

A growing body of research has investigated VR’s role in post-TKA rehabilitation, with studies reporting benefits in pain reduction, mobility enhancement, and accelerated recovery timelines [22]. Furthermore, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have synthesized this growing evidence base. However, existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses often aggregate findings across heterogeneous cohorts, including patients undergoing TKA for non-OA etiologies [23, 24], thereby obscuring outcomes specific to the KOA population [24–29]. Although some previous reviews have also restricted inclusion to patients with KOA [30], our study differs in following methodological aspects: First, we incorporate ROM across multiple postoperative time points. Second, concerning time‑point grouping, we adopt a more refined, clinically anchored stratification. Third, for pain assessment, we analyze visual analogue scale (VAS) and numerical pain rating scale (NPRS) separately rather than pooling them, which allowing our study to address gaps left by previous KOA‑focused meta‑analyses.

To address this gap and provide etiology-specific evidence, the present systematic review and meta-analysis is to focus exclusively on evaluating VR-based rehabilitation for patients with KOA following TKA, while also incorporating a novel examination of the level of VR immersion as a potential potential source of heterogeneity. We adopted a broad VR continuum that includes exergaming consoles (e.g., Xbox Kinect, Nintendo Wii), acknowledging this inclusion is debatable given variable presence levels. Their distinct working mechanisms (head‑mounted display: presence/distraction; exergaming: gamified motivation; screen‑based: visual feedback) are considered heterogeneity sources.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, and is reported in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [31, 32] .

Literature search

A comprehensive systematic search was performed across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, from inception to November 15, 2025, for all potentially eligible studies. A combination of MeSH terms and free-text words covering three conceptual domains: (1) XR technologies (virtual reality, augmented reality, mixed reality, extended reality); (2) immersive and non-immersive VR descriptors (head-mounted display, 3D, interactive); and (3) gaming-based rehabilitation tools (exergaming, serious games, active video games), including common console platforms (Nintendo Wii, Kinect, PlayStation). Detailed search strategies are provided in Supplementary Material 1. The search results were collated, and two reviewers independently screened the retrieved records in the subsequent stages.

Selection criteria

The study selection criteria were developed based on the aforementioned PRISMA’s PICO(S) framework ( Population: Adult patients with KOA who have undergone TKA surgery; Intervention: VR based rehabilitation; Comparison: conventional rehabilitation or exercise therapy or other non-VR interventions; Outcome: VAS and NPRS for pain; WOMAC for functionality; EQ-5D and SF-36 for quality of life; ROM; TUG test; Type of study design: randomized clinical trials published in English. ) Trials would be excluded following the criteria including: (1) studies involving patients who underwent TKA for primary diagnoses other than knee osteoarthritis (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, post-traumatic arthritis, or tumors); (2) studies from which outcome data could not be extracted because they were presented only in graphical form or with incomplete aggregate data that could not be calculated. In addition, a VR‑based rehabilitation intervention was defined as a technology that provides a computer‑generated virtual environment for real‑time, body‑movement‑based motor training. Included interventions met at least one hardware criterion: (1) immersive VR (head‑mounted display); (2) non‑immersive VR (screen with motion‑tracking sensors, such as Microsoft Kinect or Wii Balance Board); or (3) exergaming platforms (including Nintendo Wii, Xbox, and PlayStation) – included only when used for structured therapeutic exercise (for example, balance training, range of motion exercises, or strengthening) under supervision, not passive video watching. This definition follows established VR taxonomies [14] and distinguishes therapeutic VR from general entertainment [15].

Two reviewers ([S.L.] and [M.Y.]) independently performed the study selection and data extraction. Prior to starting, the reviewers discussed the eligibility criteria in detail to ensure a consistent understanding. During the process, all disagreements at any stage were systematically documented and resolved through a consensus meeting. In cases where consensus could not be reached, a third senior reviewer ([W.J.]) was consulted for arbitration.

Data extraction

Regarding the studies identified for inclusion in the analysis, extracted information included the following items: (1) Study characteristics: lead author, publication year, lead author’s country, and study design; (2) Patients information: numbers of patients, patients lost to follow-up, male percentage, mean age of patients at operation and body mass index; (3) Intervention and control information: Intervention type, control type, rehabilitation program frequency and duration; (4) Clinical outcomes: data about the active/passive knee extension/flexion/full ROM; and WOMAC-pain/physical function/stiffness/total score, VAS pain score, NPRS for pain; WOMAC for functionality; EQ-5D and SF-36 for quality of life; TUG test. All extracted data were imported into Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) for further processing.

Quality assessment and quantitative data analysis

Known as the most commonly used and authoritative tool for risk of bias assessment of RCTs, the Cochrane Collaboration tool [33] was used for quality assessment of the included RCTs. We conducted meta-analyses and plotted forest plots using Cochrane Collaboration Review Manager software (RevMan 5.3, Oxford, UK).

For quantitative synthesis, the measurement time points for outcomes were grouped based on established milestones of the post-TKA rehabilitation pathway. Specifically, time points for pain (VAS) were consolidated into “≤2 weeks”, representing the acute phase focused on pain control, and “3 months”, representing the mid-term functional plateau. NPRS outcomes were grouped at “4 weeks” and “12 weeks”, aligning with the sub-acute phase of rapid functional gain and the standard mid-term evaluation point, respectively. Functional outcomes assessed by the WOMAC were grouped at “1 month” (rapid functional gain), “3 months” (functional plateau), and “6 months” (long-term outcome). TUG was analyzed at “1 month” and “3 months” to capture changes during these critical recovery periods. Finally, ROM was assessed across multiple time points (“1 week”, “2 weeks”, “4 weeks”, and “12 weeks”) to meticulously reflect the trajectory from early post-operative mobilization to later functional recovery [6, 34, 35].

Distinct statistics were used to calculate the total effect of VR based rehabilitation treatments according to the type of variable included in the outcome. Since all of our outcome variables were continuous, the mean difference (MD) or standardized MD (SMD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. I2 statistic was used to assess heterogeneity [36]. Either the random-effects model (P value < 0.05 or I2 > 50%) or a fixed-effects model (P value > 0.05 or I2 < 50%) was applied, depending on the existence of substantial heterogeneity. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 indicated statistically significant differences.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the robustness of our meta-analysis results, we conducted a two-stage sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out approach. First, we assessed the stability of the overall pooled estimates for each outcome by eliminating a particular layer of research. Subsequently, we performed a more granular sensitivity analysis within each pre-specified time-based subgroup for all outcomes.

Results

Literature screening

A total of 665 publications related to VR based rehabilitation of patients undergoing knee arthroplasty were searched in four databases. After deleting 248 duplicates, a total of 288 documents were excluded in total according to the criteria described above after the titles, abstracts, or full texts of the remaining documents were carefully read and screened. Eventually, 10 compliant studies [9, 25, 37–44] were selected for inclusion in our study. Figure 1 illustrates the details of the screening process.

Summary of study and participant characteristics

A total of 588 patients following TKA were enrolled across the ten eligible RCTs, with 304 assigned to VR based rehabilitation and 284 to conventional rehabilitation. All trials included were RCTs, and the basic characteristics of all included studies can be found in Table 1. All included patients underwent knee arthroplasty for OA. The sample sizes of the ten included studies ranged from 10 to 133 from eight different countries, with two studies each from India [37, 43] and Italy [40, 44]. Based on the accessible data, the mean age of patients ranged from 51.1 to 73.3 at the time of surgery varied. Regarding the gender ratio of the participants, there were a total of 173 males (38.79%) and 273 females (61.21%), except for the study of Piqueras et al. At the third month of follow-up, there were 68 and 65 participants in the VR and control groups, respectively, at which point the male/female ratio was not provided in the original article [42].

Interventions

Table 2 provides an overview of VR interventions. Hardware used varied across studies, with immersive VR devices being common and five studies using 3D headgear [38–41, 44]. Non-immersive VR, including interactive virtual tele-rehabilitation kits and biofeedback balance training systems, were also utilized. Piqueras et al. [42] and Eichler et al. [25] focused on non-immersive VR, with interactive tele-rehabilitation and personalized programs supervised by therapists, while Pournajaf et al. [42] and K Nishitha et al. [41] both utilized non-immersive VR for rehabilitation, specifically employing serious games with biofeedback (BF) for balance training as part of their interventions. Supervision was primarily provided by physiotherapists throughout most studies. Rehabilitation location included hospitalization and teletherapy. VR scenarios ranged from interactive gaming [38, 40–43] and motion replication to watching VR movies [37, 39], and five studies [37–41] using immersive VR applications, with Yoon et al. [44] combined immersive VR with smartphone-based applications for at-home and hospital-based rehabilitation, and five studies [9, 25, 41–43] using non-immersive VR applications. It is worth noting that the K Nishitha et al. [41] study used both immersive and non-immersive VR.

Although we primarily analyzed all VR interventions together in the meta‑analyses due to the limited number of studies per subgroup (which precluded separate meta‑analyses by immersion level), we recognize the mechanistic differences across immersive VR, non‑immersive VR, and exergaming platforms. These differences are considered as a potential source of heterogeneity in the Discussion.

Table 1 outlines the intervention and control groups across ten studies included in the meta-analysis, highlighting diverse durations and frequencies of VR based rehabilitation. Funchs et al. [37] and Yoon et al. [44] added VR to conventional physiotherapy and CPM, emphasizing VR’s potential to enhance standard care. Four trials [38, 40, 41, 43] compared VR rehabilitation with traditional methods, focusing on postoperative recovery. Pournajaf et al. [42] and Güra et al. [39] explored VR for balance and functional training. The duration and frequency of interventions varied, with most studies conducting sessions multiple times per week for several weeks or even months. The longest daily intervention was the 6-month program implemented by Jin et al. [40] with 30 min per intervention, 3 times/day, starting on the second day after surgery. In contrast, the shortest intervention was the 2-day program by Funchs et al. [37], with 15-minute sessions for one time/day. Overall, the frequency ranged from 2 sessions per week to 5 sessions per week, with session durations varying from 10 min to 60 min.

Outcome measurements

Table 1 summarizes outcome measures from ten studies, focusing on pain, functional performance, and quality of life. Pain was commonly assessed using VAS and WOMAC. Functional performance was evaluated using the TUG test and other metrics like the 6-minute walk test. Quality of life was measured with tools such as the EQ-5D and SF-36. Follow-up periods ranged from 2 weeks to 12 months, with intermediate assessments at 3 and 6 months. These measures provide a comprehensive evaluation of VR based rehabilitation and conventional methods.

Methodological quality

As shown in Fig. 2, the combined analysis of all included studies revealed a high risk of publication bias in Blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias) and blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias). With respect to the randomization process, the randomization methods were not explicitly described in several randomized controlled trials [37, 40, 41, 43]. Additionally, three trials [25, 37, 40] did not reported the method of allocation concealment. Regarding missing data, all studies except one [41] demonstrated a low risk of bias. Four studies failed to blind assessors who measured the outcomes [9, 25, 39, 43]. Half of the studies did not blind participants and therapists [9, 25, 38, 39, 43].

Pain

Pain outcomes were assessed using various scales across the included studies. Six trials utilized the VAS to measure changes in pain levels [9, 37, 38, 40, 42, 43]. In one trial [38], pain was scored on a 0–100-point scale, while another study evaluated pain using a 0–10-point scale. Due to the diverse measurement units employed, the SMD was used to calculate the total effect. A pooled analysis of six studies [9, 37, 38, 40, 42, 43] involving 272 participants revealed that VR based rehabilitation had no significant improvement in pain within two weeks (SMD: −0.31; 95% CI: −0.64 to 0.01, P value = 0.06) and three months (SMD: −0.29; 95% CI: −1.00 to 0.42, P value = 0.42) post-TKA compared to the control group (SMD: −0.28; 95% CI: −0.58 to 0.02, P value = 0.07). And significant heterogeneity was observed among the studies (two weeks, P value = 0.1; I2 = 53%; three months, P value = 0.02; I2 = 75%) (Fig. 3).

In addition, two trials [39, 41] used NPRS, which is comparable to the VAS, with pain graded on a 0–10-point scale. While both the VAS and NPRS provide comparable measures of pain intensity, they are methodologically distinct tools. Consequently, to preserve clinical homogeneity and interpretability, their results were analyzed in separate meta-analyses. The impact of interventions on postoperative pain was summarized in Fig. 4. Compared to the control group, VR demonstrated superior effectiveness in providing pain relief within four- and 12-weeks post-surgery. High heterogeneity was observed among the studies (P value = 0.03, I² = 72%).

WOMAC

Upon screening, eight studies were identified that assessed outcomes using the WOMAC, a tool designed to evaluate pain, stiffness, and activities of daily living. Given the multiple scales employed by the WOMAC, six of the eight studies utilized a 0–96 scale [25, 37, 40, 41, 43, 44]. The remaining two studies selected different scales: one used a 0-240 scale [39], and the other used a 0-2400 scale [38]. Consequently, the variation necessitated the use of SMD to ensure comparability and to synthesize the results effectively. The pooled analysis of WOMAC scores revealed that VR based rehabilitation significantly enhanced WOMAC scores at both 1 month and 3 months when compared to the control group, with SMD of -1.68 (95% CI: -2.83 to -0.54, P value < 0.01) and − 2.20 (95% CI: -4.07 to -0.34 P value = 0.02). However, significant heterogeneity was observed between the groups at these time points (within 1 month, P value < 0.01, I² = 91%; one to three months, P value < 0.01, I² = 92%).Additionally, there was no significant improvement in WOMAC scores was observed at 6 months (SMD: -1.19; 95% CI: -2.54 to 0.17, P value = 0.09), with significant heterogeneity detected between the groups (P value < 0.01, I² = 92%). In summary, VR based rehabilitation demonstrated efficacy in improving WOMAC scores relative to the control group (SMD: -1.58; 95% CI: -2.28 to -0.89, P value < 0.01). TUG

Six papers have evaluated the TUG scores [9, 25, 39, 41, 42, 44]. Given the differing methodologies employed in these studies, SMD was employed to measure the overall effect. The pooled analysis indicated that VR based rehabilitation did significantly improve the TUG score within 1 month (SMD: −1.54; 95% CI: −2.54 to- 0.54, P value < 0.01), but had no significant enhancement in 3 months (SMD: -1.62; 95% CI: −3.39 to 0.14, P value = 0.07). High heterogeneity was observed between the articles in both subgroups, with values of P value < 0.01, I² = 91% for the 1-month analysis and P < 0.01, I² = 97% for the 3-month analysis. Between the different measurements and units applied between the studies, SMD was adopted as well. As shown in Fig. 7, a total of four studies [38–41] reported ROM measurements, which showed differences in ROM changes between the two groups during the first week (SMD:7.88; 95% CI: 5.16 to 10.60, P value < 0.01) as well as during the period from four to 12 weeks (SMD:7.94; 95% CI: 5.86 to 10.02, P value < 0.01). Health-related quality of life

There are three papers [25, 39, 40] reporting changes in Health-related quality of life related indices, two of which reported the SF-36, while the study in Gianola et al. reported theEQ-5D. Gianola et al. [40] compared the pre- and post-trial EQ-5D values and found no difference between the results of the VR based rehabilitation group and the control group (P value = 0.15). A meta-analysis could not be conducted for the SF-36 index due to the fact that the study in Eichler et al. [25] listed the physical component scale and the mental component scale separately, whereas the study in Güra et al. [39] counted the final results directly. Eichler et al. [25] identified no differences between the VR based rehabilitation group and the control group in physical component scores (P value = 0.8) and mental component scores (P value = 0.28), and Güra et al. [39] observed no differences between the two groups in total scores as well (P value = 0.944).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis employing a leave-one-out approach revealed stable pooled estimates for VAS, NPRS, WOMAC, TUG and ROM, with no single study disproportionately influencing the results (Supplementary Fig. 1). Subgroup analyses revealed specific sensitivities (Supplementary Tables 1, 2 and 3). The medium-term improvements in WOMAC (1–3 months) and TUG (1–3 months) became non-significant upon exclusion of the study by K Nishitha et al. [41]. Similarly, the early VAS pain outcome (within 2 weeks) shifted to statistically significant after excluding Piqueras et al. [9]. These findings indicate that while the primary conclusions are broadly stable, certain time-specific outcomes are sensitive to individual study data, reflecting underlying heterogeneity in the evidence.

Publication bias

Given the limited number of studies (k = 10), formal statistical tests for funnel plot asymmetry (Egger’s test or Begg’s test) were not performed, as they are known to be underpowered and potentially misleading in such cases. Instead, we applied a conservative, multi-faceted assessment: (1) Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 2) was performed; (2) The fail-safe N method (Nfs, Rosenthal’s approach) was calculated. The formula is Nfs=(∑Z/1.645)2 − k (Z, the effect size test statistic for each study; k, the number of studies included in the analysis). Visual inspection showed roughly symmetric funnel plots for VAS, TUG, ROM, and NPRS, whereas the WOMAC plot displayed slight asymmetry—though this should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of studies. The Nfs values for VAS, WOMAC, TUG, ROM, and NPRS were 88, 15, 215, 491, and 894, respectively. These values suggest that the significant findings for VAS, TUG, ROM, and NPRS are robust to publication bias, as a large number of unpublished studies would be needed to change the conclusions. The lower Nfs for WOMAC, which may reflect the non-significant overall effect and potential publication bias, warrants cautious interpretation, especially in light of the visual asymmetry and limited study count. While these methods cannot rule out publication bias, they allow for a more cautious interpretation.

Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized data from ten RCTs to provide a focused evaluation of VR-based rehabilitation for the KOA population following TKA. The findings demonstrate that VR interventions confer short-term improvements in functional mobility and joint flexibility, with transient benefits in pain and WOMAC scores.

The most consistent benefit of VR was observed in ROM, with substantial improvements sustained from one week (SMD: 1.38) to 4–12 weeks (SMD: 2.83). This robust effect aligns with the findings of Fu et al. [45], who reported superior active knee flexion in their VR group. The capacity of VR to deliver high-dosage, repetitive, and task-oriented practice in an engaging environment is critical for driving neuroplasticity and cortical reorganization [46, 47], thereby providing a principled foundation for the pronounced and durable gains in movement amplitude.

A time-dependent pattern was evident for WOMAC scores, with significant improvement at one month (SMD: -1.40) that attenuated by six months (SMD: -0.53). This trajectory mirrors the meta-analysis by Wei et al. [20] which also reported a large pooled effect at intervention conclusion but uncertain long-term effects. This consistent pattern suggests that VR serves as a potent facilitator during the intensive phase of rehabilitation. The attenuation of effects over time, however, points to the challenge of long-term carry-over and may indicate that the initial boost—potentially mediated by markedly enhanced adherence reported in VR groups [48]—requires consolidation through sustained behavioral change.

Divergent outcomes were noted for pain, where a significant benefit was captured by the NPRS (SMD: -1.10) but not the VAS within two weeks (SMD: -0.21), contrasting with the significant VAS improvement reported by Su et al. [24]. This discrepancy may reflect differential sensitivity of pain assessment tools to the nature of VR’s analgesic effect. The NPRS may be more responsive to the distraction and cognitive engagement provided by immersion, which is known to modulate the affective components of pain processing [49].

For functional mobility (TUG), the non-sustained effect at three months (SMD: -0.83) diverges from the sustained benefit reported by Peng et al. [23]. This divergence likely hinges on the specificity of the VR training to functional tasks. Protocols that explicitly incorporate balance challenges and gait training within the virtual environment are more likely to induce transferable gains to a test like the TUG.

The efficacy profile of VR—showing strongest effects on ROM and early, engagement-sensitive outcomes—is consistent with its core capabilities: enabling high-quality, adhered-to practice and modulating the pain experience through distraction [50]. The synergy of these mechanisms explains its value in the critical early post-operative phase.

Our sensitivity analyses reinforce the robustness of our primary findings while refining their interpretation. The pooled estimates for most outcomes remained stable when individual studies were iteratively removed, confirming the general consistency of VR’s therapeutic effects. However, we identified noteworthy patterns: the medium-to-long term outcomes (1–6 months) for WOMAC and TUG demonstrated particular sensitivity to specific studies, notably K Nishitha et al. [41], suggesting that treatment effects in these domains may be more variable over time or dependent on particular intervention characteristics. Similarly, early VAS scores showed sensitivity to individual studies, indicating heterogeneous immediate pain responses across populations. These findings do not invalidate our conclusions but rather emphasize that while VR’s efficacy in short-term functional recovery appears consistent, its effects on longer-term outcomes and pain measures should be interpreted with appropriate caution due to their dependence on specific study characteristics and time frames.

It is crucial to explicitly acknowledge the limitations of this meta-analysis. First, while a detailed internal protocol was developed a priori for this systematic review, it was not prospectively registered in a public registry and the inter-rater reliability during study selection and data extraction was not statistically quantified; this omission may increase the risk of bias. Second, the substantial statistical heterogeneity observed in our analyses is a major limitation in interpreting the pooled results. This variation encompassed both the integration of VR (ranging from a standalone therapy to an adjunct to conventional physiotherapy) and the intervention dosage (with total durations varying from as brief as 2 sessions to as long as 6 months). Critically, the inherent diversity of VR technologies—including immersive, semi‑immersive, and non‑immersive systems with distinct working mechanisms—constitutes a fundamental limitation of our pooled analysis, making direct comparisons across modalities challenging. Although we employed random-effects models to account for some of this heterogeneity, the profound differences across studies complicate the derivation of a single, unified estimate of its effect and challenge the formulation of specific clinical recommendations. Third, the high risk of performance and detection bias, inherent to VR interventions where blinding of participants and therapists is challenging, further qualifies the strength of our conclusions. Finally, the scarcity of high-quality data on health-related quality of life precludes any definitive conclusions regarding VR’s impact on this patient-centered outcome.

While the limitations outlined above necessitate cautious interpretation of our findings, they also offer insights with some practical implications. Methodologically, they emphasize the need for standardized VR protocols and core outcome sets, while future trials should integrate subgroup analyses addressing sex and immersion level with extended follow-up to establish long-term efficacy and optimal patient selection. Specifically, although all included RCTs reported sex distribution, none performed sex‑stratified analyses; this gap should be addressed by future trials and IPD meta‑analyses given known sex differences in pain and kinesiophobia [51–53]. Notably, recent initiatives such as the RATE-XR guideline [54] and core outcome sets for pain-related XR trials [55] are beginning to be addressed. Adopting these frameworks in future TKA studies would facilitate cross-study comparability. Clinically, VR demonstrates value as an adjunct for accelerating early functional recovery post-TKA, though its long-term superiority remains unconfirmed, necessitating individualized implementation strategies.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis suggested short-term benefits of VR-based rehabilitation for pain relief, functional mobility, and joint flexibility in patients with KOA following TKA. However, the clinical implications of these findings are limited due to the substantial heterogeneity across studies, the small number of trials included in each analysis, and the modest effect sizes observed for several outcomes. Consequently, the long-term efficacy and overall superiority of VR remain highly uncertain. Given these constraints, the interpretation of the results should be approached with even greater caution. Future research should prioritize the adoption of standardized VR protocols, integrate gender-stratified analyses, and conduct long-term follow-ups to more definitively establish VR’s role in clinical practice and to optimize its utility.
