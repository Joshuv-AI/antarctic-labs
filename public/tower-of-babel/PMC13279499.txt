STAT3 Signaling in Spinal Cord Injury: Neurochemical Mechanisms Linking Neuroinflammation, Mitochondrial Stress, and Glial Remodeling
Authors: Da Wei, Jinsan Yang, Xiao He, Kang Li, Chaoliang Lv, Kai Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a devastating neurological disorder marked by profound disturbances in cytokine signaling, redox balance, mitochondrial homeostasis, and glial-neuronal communication. Although many therapeutic strategies have been explored to attenuate secondary injury, effective molecularly targeted interventions remain limited. Increasing evidence identifies signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) as a central signaling node in the neurochemical response to SCI. Recent studies indicate that STAT3 exhibits pronounced spatiotemporal and cell-type-specific activation after SCI. Depending on the upstream trigger and cellular compartment involved, STAT3 can amplify or restrain neuroinflammation, shape astrocyte and microglial reactivity, influence mitochondrial bioenergetics and oxidative stress, modulate ferroptosis and apoptosis, and alter the regenerative state of the injured spinal cord. In this review, we frame STAT3 not simply as a downstream effector of the JAK/STAT cascade, but as an integrative regulator of SCI neurochemistry that links cytokine-driven signaling to metabolic stress, glial remodeling, and axonal repair. We emphasize how injury phase, cell type, and subcellular localization influence STAT3-dependent outcomes, discuss emerging therapeutic strategies that converge on STAT3-centered pathways, and outline the key challenges that must be addressed for precise translational targeting.

Introduction

Preclinical SCI research has addressed both experimental therapeutic delivery systems and early molecular repair responses, including resveratrol-based biodegradable nanoparticles and mTORC1-dependent activation of neural stem/progenitor cells [1, 2]. Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a severe neurological disorder caused by traumatic or non-traumatic insults to the spinal cord [3]. It frequently results in permanent motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction and imposes a substantial burden on patients, families, and healthcare systems worldwide [3]. Recent advances in surgical management, pharmacological intervention, stem cell-based therapy, neuromodulation, and brain-computer interface-assisted rehabilitation have created new opportunities for functional restoration after SCI [4–7]. Nevertheless, effective strategies for limiting secondary injury and achieving durable neurological recovery remain insufficient.

Secondary injury after SCI is not a single event but a dynamic and evolving neurochemical cascade involving cytokine release, redox imbalance, mitochondrial dysfunction, excitotoxicity, glial reactivity, and multiple forms of regulated cell death. These interconnected processes drive tissue degeneration but also shape the conditions for repair. Among the signaling pathways implicated in this secondary phase, the JAK/STAT axis—and particularly STAT3—has emerged as a central hub linking inflammatory signals to metabolic adaptation and cell fate decisions [8]. Experimental evidence further indicates that STAT3 can promote corticospinal remodeling and functional recovery after SCI [9]. Beyond SCI, context-dependent STAT3 signaling has also been implicated in fibrotic remodeling, oncogenic signaling, and denervation-associated muscle fibrosis, underscoring its broader biological relevance [10–12].

Importantly, STAT3 is neither uniformly detrimental nor uniformly beneficial. Its biological consequences depend on injury stage, cell type, upstream ligand environment, and subcellular localization. In neurons, glia, and vascular-associated cells, STAT3 can support survival and remodeling under some conditions while promoting chronic inflammation, maladaptive gliosis, or oxidative injury under others. This context dependence is essential for understanding how STAT3-dependent molecular signaling is translated into neurochemical imbalance, cellular dysfunction, and reparative remodeling after SCI.

In this review, we argue that STAT3 should be viewed as a context-dependent integrator of post-injury neurochemical remodeling rather than a simple linear pathway component. By focusing on how STAT3 connects cytokine signaling, mitochondrial stress, ferroptotic vulnerability, astrocyte reactivity, and regenerative responses, we aim to provide a broadly applicable mechanistic framework and to highlight where future studies should move from descriptive association toward causal, phase-specific, and cell-specific intervention.

Structure, Function, and Neurochemical Regulation of STAT3

The biological functions of STAT3 are closely tied to its structural organization and post-translational regulation. For SCI, the most relevant question is not only how STAT3 is activated, but how its activation is coupled to cytokine receptor signaling, mitochondrial adaptation, and redox-sensitive pathways in the injured nervous system.

Structure and Function of STAT3

STAT3 is one of the most extensively studied members of the STAT family and consists of 770 amino acids. From the N-terminus to the C-terminus, STAT3 contains six highly conserved domains: the N-terminal domain (NTD), coiled-coil domain (CCD), DNA-binding domain (DBD), linker domain (LD), Src homology 2 (SH2) domain, and transcriptional activation domain (TAD) [13–16].

The NTD contributes to dimer stability, cooperative DNA binding, and nuclear translocation. The CCD mediates interactions with transcriptional cofactors and regulatory proteins. The DBD recognizes and binds specific DNA elements in target genes, whereas the LD stabilizes DNA-binding activity and connects adjacent domains structurally. The SH2 domain is critical for phosphotyrosine recognition and STAT3 dimerization, while the TAD contains regulatory phosphorylation sites that modulate transcriptional efficiency [13–16].

Through the coordinated actions of these domains, STAT3 regulates diverse biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, immune responses, and tissue repair. This multifunctional architecture helps explain why STAT3 can serve as both a homeostatic regulator in the CNS and a context-dependent driver of pathology or repair after SCI.

Regulation of STAT3 Activity

STAT3 activity is regulated by multiple post-translational modifications that collectively determine its activation status, intracellular localization, stability, and transcriptional output. Tyrosine phosphorylation at Tyr705 represents the classical and most extensively characterized mode of activation [16, 17]. In response to cytokines and growth factors, upstream kinases such as JAK family members phosphorylate STAT3 at Tyr705, thereby promoting dimerization, nuclear translocation, DNA binding, and transcriptional activation [16–18].

In addition to Tyr705 phosphorylation, serine phosphorylation at Ser727 is another important regulatory event. Ser727 can modulate transcriptional activity, mitochondrial signaling, and neuroprotective responses [19]. In the CNS, Tyr705- and Ser727-related programs may cooperate or diverge in a context-dependent manner to influence neuronal survival, axonal regeneration, and inflammatory signaling.

STAT3 activity is also shaped by acetylation, methylation, and ubiquitination. Acetyltransferases such as p300/CBP can acetylate STAT3 and alter its DNA-binding capacity or transcriptional selectivity [20, 21]. Histone-modifying enzymes and methylation-related mechanisms may further refine STAT3-dependent transcription by influencing chromatin accessibility and target-gene preference [22]. In addition, ubiquitination mediated by specific E3 ligases affects STAT3 stability and turnover, thereby contributing to fine control of signal duration and intensity [23, 24].

Taken together, these regulatory layers allow STAT3 to respond dynamically to extracellular and intracellular stimuli. This multilayered control is particularly relevant in SCI, where inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress, and altered energy metabolism converge to produce a highly dynamic neurochemical environment.

Expression of STAT3 in the Central Nervous System and Following Injury

STAT3 is widely expressed in the CNS and participates in both developmental and injury-related processes. Its spatial distribution, temporal activation profile, and cell-type specificity are therefore highly relevant to understanding its functions after SCI.

STAT3 in Spinal Cord Physiology and Injury-Relevant Pathology

STAT3 should be considered in relation to the cellular architecture and functional demands of the spinal cord rather than only as a broadly expressed CNS transcription factor. In the spinal cord, STAT3-responsive pathways are relevant to motor neurons, interneurons, ascending and descending axons, oligodendroglial lineage cells, astrocytes, microglia/macrophages, endothelial cells, pericytes, and ependymal or progenitor-like populations. These cell types participate in segmental motor control, sensory transmission, myelination, barrier maintenance, inflammatory surveillance, and repair responses; therefore, changes in STAT3 signaling can influence both neural circuit integrity and the lesion microenvironment after SCI.

Under physiological conditions, STAT3 contributes to CNS development and homeostasis. It participates in the differentiation and functional regulation of neural progenitor cells during embryonic nervous system development [25]. STAT3 is broadly expressed in the CNS and is engaged by multiple cytokine- and growth factor-dependent pathways [26]. It has been implicated in neuronal survival, synaptic maintenance, axonal regeneration, and astrocytic differentiation. For example, increased STAT3 activity has been associated with attenuation of tau-induced synaptic deficits and promotion of neuronal survival [27], whereas exercise-induced changes in STAT3 phosphorylation have been linked to retinal ganglion cell axon regeneration and neuroprotection [28]. In neural stem cells, STAT3 also regulates astrocytic differentiation through control of GFAP expression and interactions with pathways such as NF-kB [29].

These general CNS functions are directly relevant to the spinal cord because the same STAT3-linked processes—neuronal survival, axonal growth capacity, glial differentiation, and cytokine responsiveness—are redirected after SCI. In the injured spinal cord, survival-associated STAT3 signaling may influence motor-neuron and interneuron vulnerability, axonal regenerative competence, and glial support functions. At the same time, STAT3-dependent regulation of astrocytic differentiation and GFAP-related programs provides a mechanistic bridge from physiological glial homeostasis to reactive astrogliosis and scar-related remodeling.

Under pathological conditions, STAT3 exhibits even more complex and context-dependent roles. It has been implicated in neuroinflammation, metabolic stress, ischemic injury, neurodegeneration, tumor biology, and immune regulation [30–43]. In some settings, STAT3 contributes to neuroprotection, repair, or anti-inflammatory responses; in others, it promotes cytokine production, oxidative damage, or maladaptive glial activation. These observations suggest that STAT3 is better understood as a context-dependent regulator whose effects vary according to cell type, subcellular localization, and disease stage, rather than as a uniformly pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory signal.

In the spinal cord, this duality becomes especially important because STAT3-dependent signaling can contribute to both protective and maladaptive outcomes within the same lesion environment. Acute or spatially restricted STAT3 activity may support lesion containment, barrier stabilization, and cell survival, whereas prolonged or cell-type-inappropriate activation may amplify inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, gliosis, neuropathic pain, or scar-associated inhibition of axonal growth. Thus, rather than presenting STAT3 as a general CNS factor, we use these physiological and pathological observations as a basis for interpreting how STAT3 coordinates spinal cord-specific responses to injury. This framing provides a more targeted transition to the following discussion of spatiotemporal STAT3 activation after SCI.

Spatiotemporal Characteristics of STAT3 Expression in the CNS and After Injury

Previous studies have shown that STAT3 expression is detectable in the developing CNS and may increase progressively over time in selected brain regions [44–47]. After injury, STAT3 activation is often rapid and spatially restricted to specific cell populations. Following SCI, prominent astrocytic nuclear localization of activated STAT3 can be observed within hours after injury, and pSTAT3 remains elevated during the subacute phase [48, 49].

In addition to the injured spinal cord, SCI can also engage JAK/STAT-related signaling in peripheral tissues; for example, inhibition of JAK1/2 tyrosine kinases has been shown to reduce neurogenic heterotopic ossification after SCI [50]. Together, these observations suggest that SCI-associated JAK/STAT signaling can extend beyond the lesion core and that STAT3-related responses are dynamic, cell-type-specific, and injury-stage-dependent.

In the acute phase, astrocytic nuclear pSTAT3 near the lesion border is closely associated with early astroglial responses, lesion-border organization, and scar-related tissue remodeling after SCI [48, 49]. In immune and glial populations, STAT3-related signaling may lead to different outcomes depending on the upstream cue: IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling has been linked to glial activation and neuropathic pain, whereas BMP7-, extracellular vesicle-, and regulatory T cell-related mechanisms have been associated with attenuation of neuroinflammation or promotion of anti-inflammatory microglial/macrophage responses [51–55]. During the subacute-to-chronic phase, persistent astrocytic STAT3-related signaling may shape GFAP-associated astrogliosis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and glial scar formation, while excessive glial STAT3-associated inflammatory signaling may contribute to pain-related maladaptation [48, 54, 56–65]. In parallel, neuron- or axon-associated STAT3-related pathways may support regeneration-associated gene expression, axonal sprouting, corticospinal or sensory axon regeneration, and repair-permissive microenvironmental remodeling [66–71]. These examples reinforce the need to report the timing, cellular source, phosphorylation readout, and subcellular localization of STAT3 activation in future SCI studies.

Neurochemical and Cellular Mechanisms of STAT3 in Spinal Cord Injury

STAT3 has emerged as a central signaling node in SCI because it links extracellular cues to diverse pathological and reparative programs. In neurochemical terms, STAT3 sits at the intersection of cytokine signaling, oxidative metabolism, mitochondrial function, glial communication, and transcriptional reprogramming. The sections below summarize how these linked mechanisms shape injury progression and recovery.

Exosome-Mediated Regulation of STAT3 After SCI

Exosomes are small extracellular vesicles secreted by various cell types and widely distributed in biological fluids. They carry proteins, lipids, mRNAs, and microRNAs and thereby mediate intercellular communication. In SCI, growing evidence suggests that exosomes regulate STAT3-related signaling pathways and contribute to inflammation control, barrier repair, and neural regeneration [72–75].

Pericyte-derived exosomal miR-210 has been reported to modulate JAK1/STAT3 signaling after SCI, thereby protecting mitochondrial function, preserving tight junction proteins, repairing the blood-spinal cord barrier, reducing inflammatory cytokine release, and improving motor recovery [72]. Exosomes from endothelial progenitor cells carrying miR-222-3p regulate the SOCS3/JAK2/STAT3 axis and promote macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, which in turn attenuates glial scar formation and supports myelin and axonal repair [73]. Similarly, hypoxia-preconditioned mesenchymal stem cell-derived small extracellular vesicles enriched in miR-21 influence astrocyte phenotype switching through the JAK2/STAT3 pathway and help create a more reparative microenvironment [74].

Schwann cell-derived exosomes containing MFG-E8 have also been shown to regulate the SOCS3/STAT3 pathway, suppress STAT3 phosphorylation, and promote anti-inflammatory macrophage/microglial polarization [75]. Overall, these observations indicate that exosome-mediated regulation of STAT3 represents an important mechanism in the injured spinal cord and may provide new opportunities for precision modulation of the post-injury microenvironment. These findings also highlight that exosome-mediated STAT3 regulation after SCI is highly context dependent rather than uniformly activating or inhibitory. Moreover, the STAT3 signaling pathway after spinal cord injury is determined by factors such as exosome sources, loading components, receptor cell types, and the injury background, rather than by a unified activation or inhibition model.

STAT3 in Neuroinflammation, Cytokine Signaling, Immune Reprogramming, and Neuropathic Pain After SCI

Neuroinflammation is one of the core pathological events after SCI, and STAT3 is deeply involved in coordinating this response. Injury-induced activation of resident glial cells, recruitment of peripheral immune cells, and disruption of tissue homeostasis lead to sustained production of cytokines, chemokines, proteases, and oxidative mediators that can expand tissue damage. However, inflammatory signaling after SCI is not purely destructive; it also participates in debris clearance, lesion containment, and tissue remodeling. STAT3 therefore operates at a critical intersection between damaging inflammation and adaptive immune reprogramming.

A growing body of work indicates that STAT3 signaling, particularly through the JAK2/STAT3 axis, regulates inflammatory factor production, glial responses, and macrophage/microglial polarization after SCI. CD44 and the long non-coding RNA SNHG1 enhance inflammatory signaling and tissue-damaging responses through JAK2/STAT3 activation [76, 77]. Conversely, transneuronal delivery of hyper-interleukin-6 can enable functional recovery after severe SCI, highlighting the context-dependent reparative potential of cytokine/STAT3-related signaling [78], whereas interventions such as Ruxolitinib, miR-34c, and Fisetin attenuate inflammatory amplification and improve microenvironmental homeostasis through modulation of STAT3-related pathways [79–81]. MicroRNAs also exert bidirectional effects: miR-21a-5p promotes neurotoxic A1 astrocyte polarization and inflammation [82], and additional miR-21-focused experimental work further supports the relevance of miRNA-mediated regulation in SCI [83], whereas miR-22-3p, miR-29a, and miR-17-5p have been linked to enhanced regenerative capacity or improved neural recovery through STAT3-associated cascades [84–86]. Importantly, not all of these studies establish STAT3 as the sole causal mediator; in many cases, STAT3 appears to function as part of a broader signaling network shaped by cell type, injury stage, and upstream context.

Other regulators, including BMP7, 4D-sEVs, lncRNA Kcnq1ot1, and ACY1215, also shape post-injury inflammatory responses through direct or indirect control of STAT3 signaling [51–53, 87–89]. In parallel, the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 axis and related pathways such as p38 MAPK/NF-kB/STAT3 have been implicated in neuropathic pain by regulating astrocyte and microglial activation after SCI [54–56, 90–94]. Additional intervention studies involving neural scaffolds, IL-4/OEC-mediated immune regulation, hydrogel-based JAK2/STAT3 inhibition, Alpinetin, DANCR/ACTN4/STAT3 signaling, and biomaterial-based modulation further support the relevance of STAT3-associated inflammatory networks in SCI repair [95–100]. Taken together, current evidence suggests that STAT3 is best understood not simply as a pro-inflammatory switch, but as a context-dependent regulator of immune-cell programming and glial signaling. STAT3 and Neuronal Apoptosis After SCI

Neuronal apoptosis is a major component of secondary injury after SCI and contributes substantially to tissue loss and neurological dysfunction. Following primary mechanical damage, neurons are exposed to oxidative stress, inflammatory mediators, excitotoxicity, and mitochondrial impairment, all of which can activate apoptotic pathways. STAT3 is intricately involved in this process.

In H2O2-stimulated spinal cord neuron models, increased STAT3 expression has been associated with activation of the p38 MAPK pathway, upregulation of Bax and cleaved caspase-3, downregulation of Bcl-2, and aggravation of neuronal apoptosis [101]. By contrast, interventions that modulate STAT3 signaling can reduce inflammasome activation, enhance autophagy, or attenuate apoptosis [102]. Stem cell-based interventions have also been linked to STAT3-dependent anti-apoptotic effects [103]. Wogonin suppresses inflammation and apoptosis through the STAT3 signaling pathway after SCI [104], while KLF7 has been reported to attenuate neuronal injury through anti-apoptotic signaling in a traumatic brain injury model, indicating that STAT3-associated survival pathways may extend beyond SCI-specific models [105].

Notably, Wogonin, KLF7-related anti-apoptotic signaling, Falcarindiol, EGFR activation, and neural stem cell-based combination therapies each influence Bax/Bcl-2/caspase-related pathways via STAT3 or JAK2/STAT3 signaling, thereby altering apoptotic vulnerability and promoting neuroprotection [104–108]. Overall, the literature supports a role for STAT3 in neuronal survival signaling after SCI, while also indicating that the direction of this effect depends on model context and upstream regulatory cues. STAT3, Mitochondrial Bioenergetics, and Oxidative Stress Following SCI

Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are major drivers of secondary damage after SCI. Injury-induced disruption of mitochondrial homeostasis leads to impaired ATP production, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, altered mitophagy, and activation of downstream cell-death pathways. Emerging evidence indicates that STAT3 is involved in these events through both canonical transcriptional programs and non-canonical mitochondrial actions [49, 109–115].

On the one hand, phosphorylated STAT3 has been linked to inhibition of FOXO3a nuclear translocation, suppression of mitophagy-related pathways such as PINK1/Parkin, and decreased antioxidant defense, including reduced SOD2 activity, thereby promoting ROS accumulation and oxidative injury [109]. Consistent with a broader glial mitochondrial role of STAT3, disruption of astrocyte STAT3 signaling has been reported to decrease mitochondrial function and increase oxidative stress in vitro [111]. On the other hand, mitochondrial or contextually activated STAT3 has also been associated with preservation of bioenergetic homeostasis, modulation of stress-responsive genes, and support of injury-adaptive metabolic programs [49, 110]. These apparently divergent findings may reflect differences in cell population, subcellular localization, timing, phosphorylation status, and whether STAT3 activity is measured as a driver of injury progression or as a compensatory response to metabolic stress.

Mechanistically, STAT3 should be considered not only as a cytokine-responsive nuclear transcription factor but also as a compartment-sensitive regulator linking receptor-dependent signaling to mitochondrial respiration, redox balance, and cellular stress adaptation. Mitochondrial STAT3 has been shown to support optimal electron transport chain complex I/II activity, whereas mitochondrial-targeted STAT3 can preserve complex I-dependent respiration, reduce cytochrome c release, and attenuate ischemia-induced ROS generation through partial modulation of electron flow [112, 115]. Although these findings were largely derived from non-SCI models, they provide an important mechanistic framework for interpreting STAT3-dependent mitochondrial adaptation in the injured spinal cord.

This compartmental distinction is also relevant to the different biological implications of Tyr705 and Ser727 phosphorylation. Tyr705 phosphorylation primarily couples STAT3 to the canonical dimerization–nuclear translocation–DNA-binding axis, whereas Ser727 is required for maximal transcriptional activation and appears to be particularly important for mitochondrial STAT3 function [113]. In mitochondrial models, STAT3-dependent regulation of respiration can be dissociated from canonical Tyr705-dependent transcriptional activity: DNA-binding-defective or Y705F STAT3 can retain mitochondrial effects, whereas S727A disrupts restoration of electron transport chain activity and S727D can rescue complex I/II function [112]. Similarly, mitochondrially restricted STAT3 supports Ras-dependent metabolic remodeling in a Ser727-dependent manner, further indicating that Ser727 may couple STAT3 to bioenergetic regulation independently of classical nuclear signaling [114].

Accordingly, the relationship between STAT3 and oxidative injury in SCI should not be reduced to a simple pro-oxidant or antioxidant model. The strongest current interpretation is that STAT3 tunes the balance between mitochondrial resilience and mitochondrial failure in a highly context-dependent manner. Within this framework, Tyr705-related signaling may mainly reflect cytokine-driven nuclear transcriptional responses, whereas Ser727-related signaling may integrate transcriptional amplification with mitochondrial electron transport, metabolic adaptation, and ROS control. Future studies should therefore distinguish nuclear versus mitochondrial STAT3, Tyr705- versus Ser727-related signaling, and neuron- versus glia-dominant responses when evaluating STAT3 as a metabolic therapeutic target.

STAT3 in Astrocyte Reactivity and Scar-Related Remodeling After SCI

Astrocytes are central regulators of the post-injury microenvironment, and reactive astrogliosis is a defining feature of SCI. STAT3 signaling has long been recognized as a major determinant of astrocyte activation, phenotype transition, and scar formation [57–65, 116, 117]. Yet the biological significance of astrocytic STAT3 is more nuanced than a simple equation of ‘more STAT3 = worse outcome’. Reactive astrocytes can limit lesion spread, help restore barrier functions, and isolate inflammatory damage [48], whereas persistent maladaptive astrocyte responses can contribute to chronic inflammation, chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan (CSPG)-rich scar formation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and impaired axonal extension [118].

Several studies have shown that excessive or dysregulated STAT3 activation promotes neurotoxic A1-like astrocyte features, inflammatory mediator production, and glial scar-associated changes [57–63]. Calycosin reduces A1 astrocyte reactivity and glial scar markers by inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation [57], whereas Hv1 knockout decreases A1 astrocyte accumulation through the ROS/STAT3 pathway [58]. Similarly, suppression of abnormal JAK2/STAT3 activation through HDAC3 depletion or restoration of miR-19b-1-5p reduces astrocyte migration, GFAP expression, and scar-related remodeling [59]. However, classic work on astrocytic STAT3 also indicates that complete disruption of STAT3-dependent astrogliosis can impair lesion containment and worsen tissue organization, underscoring the need for calibrated rather than indiscriminate inhibition [48].

In the context of scar-related remodeling, STAT3-dependent astrocyte reactivity contributes not only to astrocyte activation but also to the structural reorganization of the lesion border. Activated astrocytes proliferate, migrate toward the lesion margin, upregulate glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and interact with extracellular matrix components to form a scar-associated boundary that initially restricts inflammatory spread and protects spared tissue [48]. When persistent or excessive, STAT3-associated astrocytic programs may promote extracellular matrix remodeling, CSPG accumulation, maintenance of a pro-inflammatory glial phenotype, and formation of a non-permissive substrate for axonal extension [57–65, 116–118]. Therefore, STAT3-mediated scar remodeling should be interpreted as a dynamic process involving both protective lesion containment and chronic inhibitory remodeling, rather than simply as astrocyte activation alone.

STAT3 also interacts with inflammatory and reparative signaling programs to shape astrocyte behavior across injury stages. Through crosstalk with NF-kB and cytokine signaling, STAT3 can amplify acute inflammatory responses [60]; conversely, interventions such as Zeb2os knockdown, quercetin-loaded mesenchymal stem cell-derived vesicles, N-acetylcysteine, and miR-124-overexpressing Schwann cells can attenuate maladaptive astrogliosis or scar-forming capacity by suppressing STAT3-related signaling [61–65]. Ginsenoside Rg1 further highlights the contextual nature of astrocytic STAT3, as distinct downstream programs may favor astrocyte-to-neuron conversion or pro-angiogenic support [116, 117]. Thus, an important translational question is not whether astrocytic STAT3 should be turned on or off, but how its timing, magnitude, and cell-state specificity can be modulated to preserve protective astrogliosis while limiting chronic scar-related inhibition.

Overall, astrocytic STAT3 lies at the boundary between protective lesion containment and maladaptive chronic remodeling. This dual positioning makes it one of the most compelling, but also one of the most challenging, STAT3-related targets in SCI.

STAT3 and Axonal Regeneration Following SCI

Axonal regeneration is a major determinant of functional recovery after SCI, yet the intrinsic regenerative capacity of CNS neurons is limited. STAT3 has been identified as a key signaling molecule involved in promoting axonal regeneration through multiple mechanisms [66–71].

STAT3 can cooperate with transcription factors such as KLF7 and MEF2 to induce regeneration-associated genes, thereby promoting axonal sprouting and sensory axon growth after dorsal column injury [66]. It also participates in intrinsic neuronal redox signaling linked to NOX2- and PKC-dependent regeneration [67]. In addition, mTOR-STAT3 signaling has been implicated in enhanced corticospinal and sensory axon regeneration following Lipin1 depletion [68].

At the same time, modulation of STAT3 influences the extracellular environment that determines regenerative success. Mesenchymal stem cell-based therapies combined with electroacupuncture reduce astrocyte activation and glial scar formation by suppressing STAT3 phosphorylation [70], whereas graphene-collagen composite hydrogels modulate inflammatory polarization and support axonal regeneration partly through STAT3-related mechanisms [71]. Together, these findings indicate that STAT3 shapes axonal regeneration through both neuron-intrinsic and microenvironmental mechanisms.

Proposed STAT3-Related Mechanisms: Ferroptosis and Photobiomodulation Following SCI

In addition to the better-established roles of STAT3 in neuroinflammation, neuronal apoptosis, mitochondrial stress, astrocyte reactivity, and axonal regeneration, several emerging mechanisms may further explain how STAT3 participates in SCI progression and repair. Among these, ferroptosis and photobiomodulation (PBM)-associated signaling deserve separate consideration, but they are best presented as proposed or developing mechanisms because the current evidence remains partly indirect and context dependent.

Ferroptosis has attracted increasing attention as a mechanism of SCI pathogenesis because it directly links several core features of secondary injury, including iron dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, lipid peroxidation, antioxidant failure, and progressive neuronal loss [119–122]. Unlike apoptosis, ferroptosis is driven primarily by iron-dependent oxidative damage to membrane lipids and insufficient detoxification of lipid peroxides. This mechanism is highly relevant to SCI, where hemorrhage, mitochondrial injury, inflammatory activation, and redox imbalance can create a lesion microenvironment that favors lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic vulnerability. Bioinformatic analyses of SCI samples have identified ferroptosis-related gene signatures and suggested that these pathways are associated with acute injury progression [119]. Additional SCI-related gene analyses further implicate ferroptosis-related pathways in injury pathogenesis and redox-related signaling networks [121, 122]. Experimentally, modulation of ferroptosis-related pathways has been linked to neuronal survival after SCI, supporting the view that ferroptosis is not merely a secondary biochemical marker but a potentially targetable contributor to tissue degeneration and functional outcome [120]. Therefore, ferroptosis deserves attention in SCI research because it provides a mechanistic bridge between oxidative stress, mitochondrial failure, neuronal death, and therapeutic neuroprotection.

STAT3-related mechanisms are particularly relevant in this context because STAT3 is positioned at the intersection of cytokine signaling, redox homeostasis, mitochondrial adaptation, and cell-death regulation. In SCI, STAT3 activation is highly context dependent, and its effects may differ according to injury phase, cell type, subcellular localization, and upstream inflammatory or metabolic cues. Ferroptosis-related studies further support this duality. One key experimentally supported mechanism is the Syvn1/STAT3/GPX4 axis. Syvn1-mediated K63-linked ubiquitination stabilizes STAT3, promotes its nuclear translocation, and enhances transcription of GPX4, a major ferroptosis-suppressive enzyme that limits lipid peroxide accumulation and helps preserve ROS balance [120]. Through this pathway, STAT3 may protect neurons by strengthening GPX4-dependent lipid peroxide detoxification and reducing ferroptotic cell death. Conversely, sustained or dysregulated STAT3 activation may indirectly promote ferroptotic susceptibility by intensifying oxidative stress, weakening antioxidant defenses, or interacting with HIF-1alpha- and Nrf2-related signaling networks [121, 122]. Thus, the STAT3-ferroptosis relationship should not be interpreted as uniformly protective or detrimental. Instead, STAT3 should be viewed as a context-sensitive regulator that may either restrain ferroptosis through GPX4-dependent antioxidant protection or contribute to ferroptotic vulnerability when oxidative and inflammatory signaling becomes excessive.

PBM represents another proposed STAT3-related mechanism in SCI. Experimental studies indicate that PBM can attenuate neurotoxic astrocyte and microglial activation, reduce inflammatory mediator production, limit glial scar-associated responses, and improve motor recovery, at least partly through modulation of STAT3-associated signaling [123–125]. Mechanistically, PBM has been linked to suppression of Lcn2/JAK2-STAT3 crosstalk, miR-330-5p-mediated regulation of macrophage polarization, and inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3/AQP4 axis involved in spinal cord edema [123–125]. These findings suggest that STAT3 may serve as one molecular convergence point through which PBM reshapes the inflammatory, glial, and edema-related microenvironment after SCI. However, because PBM can also affect mitochondrial function, vascular responses, oxidative metabolism, and immune signaling, further causal validation is needed before PBM can be defined as a specifically STAT3-guided intervention.

Taken together, ferroptosis and PBM illustrate two emerging areas in which STAT3-related signaling may influence SCI progression and repair. The ferroptosis-related evidence justifies growing attention because it connects iron-dependent lipid peroxidation with neuronal death, oxidative stress, and functional prognosis after SCI. The STAT3-centered evidence is important because STAT3 links cytokine-driven injury responses with GPX4-mediated antioxidant protection, ROS balance, mitochondrial adaptation, and inflammatory remodeling. Future studies should determine whether STAT3 acts as a causal mediator or a downstream marker in these mechanisms, define the relevant cell types and injury phases, and test whether selective modulation of STAT3-related pathways can preserve neuronal survival and improve functional recovery after SCI.

Therapeutic Modulation of STAT3 Signaling After SCI

A growing number of studies highlight the therapeutic relevance of STAT3 in SCI and suggest that rehabilitation-based, pharmacological, biomaterial-based, and extracellular vesicle-centered interventions converge on STAT3-related signaling. The translational appeal of STAT3 lies in its position at the interface of inflammation, metabolism, mitochondrial regulation, and glial-neuronal communication.

Treadmill training has been shown to enhance neural function recovery in SCI models, partly through activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway and attenuation of apoptosis [126]. Network pharmacology studies likewise identify STAT3 as a hub target across candidate therapeutic strategies [127]. Age-specific bioinformatics and anoikis-related analyses further implicate STAT3-associated pathways in post-injury cellular responses and functional recovery [128, 129]. Metabolomic work also shows that Danshen extract (Salvia miltiorrhiza Bunge) attenuates SCI in a rat model, although whether this effect is directly mediated through STAT3-centered signaling requires further validation [130]. Protein-interaction and bibliometric analyses additionally highlight STAT3-related inflammatory networks and neuromodulation research as important areas for future SCI therapeutics [131]. At the same time, the field still relies heavily on indirect modulation of STAT3, and the neurochemical specificity of many reported interventions remains insufficiently defined. Future therapeutic work will therefore need to distinguish direct STAT3 targeting from broader pathway perturbation and to establish when modulation of STAT3 improves metabolic resilience, when it restrains inflammatory signaling, and when it may interfere with necessary reparative responses.

Overall, current evidence supports the view that STAT3 is not only a mechanistic regulator in SCI but also a practical intervention node. However, because its effects are stage-dependent and cell-specific, successful translation will likely require precise neurochemical stratification rather than blanket inhibition or activation.

Conclusions and Future Directions

STAT3 is a multifunctional signaling molecule that exerts pivotal yet highly context-dependent effects in spinal cord injury. Across the current literature, its importance lies not simply in belonging to the JAK/STAT pathway, but in integrating multiple neurochemical dimensions of secondary injury, including cytokine signaling, redox imbalance, mitochondrial dysfunction, ferroptotic pressure, glial remodeling, and regenerative plasticity.

The major challenge is that the biological consequences of STAT3 activation vary according to injury phase, cell type, subcellular localization, and model system. This variability explains why STAT3 can appear protective in some experimental settings and deleterious in others. For the field to move forward, studies must more clearly distinguish nuclear versus mitochondrial STAT3 actions, acute versus chronic signaling effects, and descriptive pathway associations versus genuine causal dependence.

Several priorities emerge from the current literature. First, the therapeutic window for modulating STAT3 across acute, subacute, and chronic SCI remains poorly defined. Second, greater emphasis is needed on cell-specific manipulations in astrocytes, microglia/macrophages, neurons, and vascular-associated cells. Third, future work should better integrate neurochemical readouts—including oxidative balance, mitochondrial function, and inflammatory mediator profiles—with behavioral and histological outcomes. Finally, more selective intervention strategies and clinically relevant delivery systems are required if STAT3-centered therapies are to progress beyond preclinical promise.

In summary, STAT3 should be regarded as a context-dependent integrator of neuroinflammation, glial remodeling, metabolic stress, and regenerative signaling after SCI. This framework may help future studies define more precise therapeutic windows, identify cell-specific mechanisms, and develop translational strategies for STAT3-centered pathway modulation.
