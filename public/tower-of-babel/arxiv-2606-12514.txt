Cyclotron mass-selective de Haas–van Alphen measurements using temperature modulation
Michelle Hollricher, Andreas Bauer, Leo Maximov, Louw Feenstra, Christian Pfleiderer, Marc A. Wilde

Abstract
We present a temperature-modulated de Haas–van Alphen measurement technique that allows selective addressing of quantum oscillations with different effective masses m ∗ m^{\ast} using a non-monotonic amplitude evolution with temperature and magnetic field, governed by the temperature derivative of the Lifshitz-Kosevich factor. The technique relies on harmonic modulation of the sample temperature and phase-sensitive detection of quantum oscillations in the voltage induced in a pick-up coil. We use a set of frequencies with strong Zeeman-driven harmonic content in the compensated topological semimetal MoSi 2 as a natural linear mass comb ranging from 1 m ∗ m^{\ast} to 13 m ∗ m^{\ast} to demonstrate the tunability of the mass-dependent quantum oscillation amplitudes experimentally. The technique allows to reliably isolate weak contributions of heavy orbits that are inaccessible in conventional de Haas–van Alphen frequency spectra because their frequency peaks overlap with much stronger frequency peaks of lighter orbits.



The electronic properties of metals are determined by their band structure and Fermi surface. Quantum oscillation measurements, especially the de Haas–van Alphen (dHvA) effect
34
;
7
;
17
, are among the most powerful tools to probe Fermi surface properties and also provide a precise measure of orbit-specific quasiparticle masses. Experimental challenges arise in materials with complex Fermi surfaces, exhibiting a large number of extremal orbits with overlapping frequency peaks on the one hand, and with strongly different effective masses
m^{\ast}
on the other hand. Oscillation amplitudes generally decrease with increasing mass, making heavy orbits harder to detect than light orbits. Heavy orbits may thus evade detection due to overlap with close-by frequency peaks that are not resolved individually because of the finite magnetic field window or simply due to limitations of the detection system (see supplementary materials). However, especially the heavy quasiparticles strongly contribute to the density of states and play a key role in correlated states, including high-temperature superconductivity, heavy fermions, quantum criticality, unconventional magnetism, and correlated topological semimetals.

In all material classes, individually resolving overlapping frequency contributions of heavy and light quasiparticles is challenging, and measurement approaches that offer selectivity with respect to

Our experimental approach uses an oscillating sample temperature
T
to detect quantum oscillations in the temperature derivative of the magnetization,
\partial M/\partial T

\partial R_{\mathrm{T}}/\partial T

In this letter, we demonstrate the cyclotron mass-selectivity of the temperature-modulated dHvA (TM-dHvA) technique by analyzing the non-monotonic behavior of
\partial R_{\mathrm{T}}/\partial T
with both magnetic field and temperature, and by showcasing the effect in the compensated semimetal MoSi
2

2
therefore provides an ideal platform for demonstrating the mass-selective capabilities of TM-dHvA. For a showcase of
overlapping
frequency components with strongly different masses, we refer to the supplementary materials.

\\
\mathrm{with}\,\,\,\,R_{\mathrm{T},p,i}=\dfrac{x}{\sinh{x}}\mathrm{,}\,\,\,x=\dfrac{2\pi^{2}pm^{\ast}_{i}k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\hslash eB}\mbox{ ,}\end{array}

where
M_{0,p,i}
is the
p
th Fourier component of the oscillatory magnetization at zero temperature,
R_{\mathrm{T}}
p,i
is the Lifshitz–Kosevich factor
19
;
34

m_{i}^{*}
is the cyclotron effective mass corresponding to orbit
i

p
is the harmonic index.

The temperature-modulated dHvA effect has been sketched theoretically in Shoenberg’s seminal book
34
and was applied in Refs.
29
;
30
. In TM-dHvA measurements, the sample temperature
T
is modulated at a frequency

T=T_{0}+\Delta T\cos{\left(2\pi ft\right)}\mbox{ ,}

(5)

where
T_{0}
is the average sample temperature and
\Delta T
is the temperature modulation amplitude. Corresponding changes in the sample magnetization over time induce a voltage in a surrounding pick-up coil via Faraday’s law of induction. The voltage
U_{\mathrm{ind}}
induced in the pick-up coil is proportional to the temperature derivative of the magnetization
\partial M/\partial T

U_{\mathrm{ind}}\sim\dfrac{dM}{dt}\sim\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial T}\dfrac{dT}{dt}\sim-\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial T}f\Delta T\sin{\left(2\pi ft\right)}\mbox{ .}

(6)

Demodulating

\partial M/\partial T

\Delta

U_{i}\sim f\Delta T\sum_{p}M_{0,p,i}\left|\dfrac{\partial R_{\mathrm{T},p,i}}{\partial T}\right|\mbox{ ,}

(7)

where

\left|\frac{\partial R_{\mathrm{T},p,i}}{\partial T}\right|=\frac{x\left(x*\coth{x}-1\right)\text{csch}\,x}{T}

(8)

is the temperature derivative of
R_{\mathrm{T}}
shown in Fig.

The dependence of the measured signal on
\partial R_{\mathrm{T}}/\partial T
is the reason for the cyclotron mass-selectivity. The
\partial R_{\mathrm{T}}/\partial T
terms corresponding to the same masses as in Fig.
1

T

. At sufficiently low temperatures, contributions from light masses vanish, while those from heavy masses are maximized. In contrast to
R_{\mathrm{T}}

has no upper bound, leading to a strong signal enhancement for heavy masses or high harmonics

Figure

(B)

Thus, the TM-dHvA technique allows singling out heavy-mass components while simultaneously suppressing light-mass components, which is not possible with quantum oscillation signals scaling with

The TM-dHvA setup is shown in Fig.
2
. It consists of a sample (S), a heater (H) and thermometer (TH), which generate and monitor a sinusoidal oscillation of the sample temperature according to Eq.
5
. The sample is situated inside a pick-up coil (PC) used to inductively detect the resulting oscillation of the sample magnetization. Sample, heater, and thermometer are thermally coupled to the bath (B). In contrast to magnetization measurements that involve oscillating secondary fields, the TM-dHvA setup does not require a secondary coil for signal balancing, parasitic background from the sample environment is negligible, and Eddy current heating is absent.

A 1 mm cube of single-crystal MoSi
2
was attached to the back of a commercially available Lake Shore RX-102A temperature sensor using Apiezon grease. The sensor was modified by cutting a narrow separating trench through the ruthenium oxide layer
26
;
42
. One side is used as the heater, driving the temperature oscillations. The other side is used as the thermometer to monitor both the mean sample temperature
T_{0}
and the amplitude of the temperature modulation
\Delta T
. The resistance ratio of the heater and thermometer is close to one. The sapphire substrate of the sensor was polished to approximately 200
\mu
m to minimize its heat capacity and maximize thermal contact between the heater/thermometer layer and the sample. The electrical connections to the heater and thermometer were made with manganin wires of 30
\mu
m diameter.

The pick-up coil (PC) with approximately 2100 windings is made of enameled copper wire with a diameter of 36
\mu
m. The modified sensor with the attached sample is glued to a cross made of Kevlar strings using Stycast 1299. With overall dimensions of 14 × 15 × 13.3 mm
3
, the TM-dHvA assembly fits into most conventional cryostats and commercially available rotation stages. All measurements presented here were performed using an Oxford Instruments HelioxVL
3
He cryostat with a cooling power of approximately 40
\mu
W at 300 mK.

Conceptually, cyclotron mass discrimination should be possible in other quantum oscillatory quantities scaling with

;

In conclusion, we developed a TM-dHvA measurement technique and detected quantum oscillations of
\partial M/\partial T
in the compensated semimetal MoSi
2
. Using a series of harmonics with masses in the range
\approx 0.3\,m_{\mathrm{e}}-4\,m_{\mathrm{e}}
originating from the
\alpha
orbits in MoSi
2
, we demonstrated that TM-dHvA allows for the isolation of orbits with heavy masses from orbits with light masses, which is not possible in conventional dHvA experiments. These properties are expected to be beneficial for quantum oscillation experiments in a wide range of material classes where heavy and light quasiparticles at the Fermi surface coexist.
