Nurse‐Coordinated and Guideline‐Driven Care in Acute Ischemic Stroke: A Prospective Study With Retrospective Propensity‐Score Matched Controls
Authors: Guili Guo, Lan Gao, Jie Zhao, Hong Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate the efficacy of establishing a nurse‐coordinated and guideline‐driven care by advanced stroke nurses for acute ischemic stroke (AIS).BackgroundSpecialised nursing care is usually not integrated enough to provide high quality of interdisciplinary care for patients with AIS.DesignA prospective study with retrospective controls.MethodA total of 231 patients with AIS were prospectively enrolled to receive the nurse‐coordinated and guideline‐driven care for intravenous thrombolysis (prospective cohort). A retrospective cohort using propensity‐score matching at a 1:1 ratio retrieved reviews of 231 cases. The primary endpoint was early major improvement in the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores at 24 h after thrombolysis. Secondary outcome measures included treatment time of each link at different stages, functional independence, EuroQoL 5‐dimension three‐level scale (EQ‐5D‐3L) scores and adverse events.ResultsThe rate of early major improvement in NIHSS score was 57.19% and 42.0% in the prospective and retrospective cohorts with a rate ratio of 1.361 (95% CI: 1.127–1.643) indicating superiority (p = 0.002). A higher rate of functional independence was observed in the prospective cohort as compared to controls (77.9% vs. 68.8%, p = 0.035). The mean time from admission was 17.11 ± 9.64 min for imaging, 21.75 ± 8.42 min for femoral artery puncture and 51.43 ± 9.64 min for thrombolysis in the prospective cohort, which were shorter than those in controls (all p < 0.001). EQ‐5D‐3L scores were better in prospective cases than in controls within 90 days after thrombolysis (all p < 0.05). A lower incidence of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage was observed in the prospective cohort, while no significant difference in mortality up to 90 days was found between the two cohorts.No Patient or Public ContributionAlthough patients and the public were not directly involved in the design or conduct of this study, the research findings provide critical evidence that nursing‐led, guideline‐driven coordination directly improves the efficacy and safety in the management of patients with AIS by ensuring timely reaction, consistent application of evidence‐based protocols and enhanced multidisciplinary teamwork. Trial Registration: Chinese Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR2200056818

Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) refers to a vessel occlusion leading to infarction, including thromboembolic strokes or atherosclerotic strokes, which account for approximately 70% of all stroke cases (Tadi and Lui 2025). Overall, the incidence of stroke in the Chinese population aged 18 years or older has reached 505.2 per 100,000 person‐years and remains a leading cause of mortality and disability in mainland China (Tu et al. 2023). Neurological dysfunction following stroke, primarily manifesting as hemiplegia, balance disorders, language impairments and dysphagia, significantly diminishes quality of life and imposes a heavy burden on the healthcare system (Feigin et al. 2025). Time‐dependent application of intravenous thrombolysis treatment (IVT) or thrombectomy within the effective time window is considered the primary treatment for AIS according to the 2019 Chinese Stroke Association guidelines (Liu et al. 2020). Nevertheless, treatment‐related complications can cause significant morbidity and mortality if patients are not properly anticipated, prevented and managed (Chugh 2019). Currently, specialised nursing care has been established for the treatment and rehabilitation of these patients; however, this model often involves nurses performing tasks in response to medical orders rather than proactively leading and coordinating the entire hyperacute and acute care pathway. This lack of a formalised, nurse‐coordinated and guideline‐driven model potentially contributes to delays in critical time‐sensitive interventions and suboptimal patient outcomes (Theofanidis and Gibbon 2016). According to a scientific statement from the American Heart Association, nurses play a vital role in organising and integrating care during the emergency or hyperacute phase and the acute phase of ischemic stroke (Miller et al. 2010).

To address this specific gap, a multidisciplinary stroke team was established in the present study, which included two stroke nurses supported by a comprehensive evidence‐based nursing training programme for stroke care. We therefore hypothesised that the nurse‐coordinated and guideline‐driven care would improve clinical outcomes and reduce disability or even mortality following AIS by enhancing nursing competence and interdisciplinary coordination.

Methods

Study Design and Participant Recruitment

The prospective observational study with retrospective controls was approved by the institutional Ethics Examining Committee of Human Research in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. Results were reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies guidelines (von Elm et al. 2014). Informed consent was obtained from all participants in the prospective phase, whereas it was waived for the retrospective cohort since all data were extracted retrospectively from medical records.

Between 1 January 2022 and 31 December 2023, consecutive patients admitted to our stroke centre for the treatment of AIS were prospectively enrolled. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a diagnosis of acute IS according to the criteria from the Chinese guidelines for the Early Management of Patients with Acute Ischemic Stroke (the 2019 update version) (Warner et al. 2019); (2) evidence from computerised tomography (CT) meeting the diagnosis of AIS; (3) meeting the indications for IVT; (4) first‐time admission for AIS; (5) admission through the emergency room and (6) age over 18 years. Patients were excluded if they had haemorrhagic stroke, in‐hospital stroke, epilepsy, brain structural abnormalities, severe dysfunction of crucial organs including the heart, liver and kidney, previous cognitive impairments, admission from the outpatient department or incomplete medical data. In contrast, patients receiving routine nursing care in hospital from 1 May 2024 to 31 March 2025 were retrospectively reviewed from the electronic medical records (EMRs) with the same inclusion and exclusion criteria. The enrolled patients were matched 1:1 to cases in the prospective cohort through propensity score matching (PSM).

Nurse‐Coordinated and Guideline‐Driven Care

To establish an evidence‐based training programme for nurse‐coordinated care in acute stroke (Table S1), we reviewed the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) and European Stroke Organization guidelines. Additionally, we examined how the team functions in daily practice at two local tertiary grade A hospitals, both recognised as Advanced Stroke Centers by the National Brain Prevention Committee (Powers et al. 2019; Berge et al. 2021). According to our internal strategy, the stroke team comprised an emergency physician, a radiologist, a laboratory physician, a neurologist, a pharmacist, a rehabilitation specialist and two advanced practice nurses specialising in stroke care. As a stroke specialist, the neurologist served as the team leader; however, the advanced practice nurse, who received extensive training and underwent regular assessments, was responsible for organising and coordinating care from admission to discharge.

As the first responder in the emergency department, the advanced nurse's responsibilities included: (1) early recognition of stroke symptoms using validated tools including the Face, Arm, Speech test and the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS); (2) rapid response to the attending physician and activation of ‘immediate activation alters’ to the stroke team; (3) initiation of the standardised order set according to the bundled physician's prescription to ensure timely brain imaging, and coordinating with the radiologist to prioritise diagnosis confirmation; (4) escalation of a ‘stroke alert’ to the designated neurologist, pharmacist and neuroscience nurse and (5) swift transportation of the patient to the stroke centre under the supervision of a neurologist to ensure continuity of care.

At the stroke centre, an advanced practice nurse was assigned to a neurologist to implement stroke care protocols based on current evidence‐based guidelines: (1) reviewing neuroimaging and laboratory results and identifying any contraindication for thrombolysis eligibility, such as blood pressure (BP) > 180/100 mmHg, platelet count < 100 × 109 or blood glucose beyond the range of 140–180 mg/dL; (2) verifying the last known well time for IVT; (3) administering infusions according to protocol; (4) performing NIHSS assessments every 15–30 min for 2 h, very 60 min for 6 h and then every 120 min until 24 h; (5) maintaining BP < 180/100 mmHg and conducting continuous cardiac monitoring; (6) monitoring for signs of specific complications, such as intracranial haemorrhage (ICH) or cerebral oedema, and if suspected, initiating a standardised order set for CT examination to ensure accurate diagnosis; (7) coordinating with the stroke team for rapid response to complications and emergency interventions; (8) collaborating with a rehabilitation specialist to plan early rehabilitation to reduce immobility‐related complications if no contraindications; (9) initiating secondary prevention measures in accordance with guidelines and performing patient or family education and (10) coordinating with the stroke team for patient discharge and scheduling follow‐ups for home health care.

Routine Nursing Care

In routine nursing care, nurses traditionally performed tasks according to medical orders. Upon a patient's arrival at our stroke centre, the responsible nurse established intravenous access following the neurologist's decision and initiated the corresponding IVT treatment once the prescription was authorised. Standard monitoring of vital signs, hemodynamic parameters, muscle strength, dysphagia and potential complications was also part of the nurse's role in post‐thrombolysis care, with all observations documented in the nursing record. Additionally, the responsible nurse provided education on stroke risk factors and medication adherence to patients or their families prior to discharge.

Outcome Measurement

Demographic and clinical characteristics for the prospective cohort were collected within 24 h of admission; all clinical assessments were gathered prospectively and recorded by two investigators at 24 h, 7 days and 90 days after thrombolysis. Data for the retrospective cohort were retrieved from the medical records by the same two investigators.

The severity of neurological deficits was measured using the NIHSS, which comprised 11 categories including consciousness, gaze, visual‐field loss, facial palsy, right and left upper extremity motor strength, right and left lower extremity motor strength, limb ataxia, sensory loss, language, dysarthria and extinction/attention items. The total score ranged from 0 to 42 and was classified as follows: 0–4 points = mild stroke; 5–12 points = moderate stroke; 16–20 points = moderate to severe stroke and > 20 points = severe stroke (Kwah and Diong 2014). Early major neurological improvement was predefined as a decrease of ≥ 4 points in the NIHSS score at 24 h after stroke compared to the baseline value. Neurological function was assessed by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), scored on a 6‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 (no disability) to 6 (death) (Nunn et al. 2016). Functional independence was defined as the mRS score ranging from 0 to 2. Door‐to‐CT (DTCT), door‐to‐puncture (DTP) and door‐to‐needle (DTN) times refer to the intervals between a patient's arrival at the emergency department and the administration of CT imaging, femoral artery puncture or IVT, respectively. Health‐related quality of life was evaluated by the EuroQoL 5‐dimension three‐level scale (EQ‐5D‐3L), which comprised five dimensions: mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression. Each dimension was described at three levels: no, some and extreme problem (Luo et al. 2017). Severe adverse events were symptomatic ICH, defined as a haemorrhagic lesion on CT accompanied by neurological deterioration, indicated by an increase of ≥ 4 points in the NIHSS score, as well as mortality after thrombolysis. Minor side effects were also recorded.

The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients demonstrating early major improvement in the NIHSS score at 24 h after thrombolysis. Secondary outcomes included treatment times of each link at different in‐hospital stages, functional independence, EQ‐5D‐5L scores and adverse events.

Sample Size Calculation

PASS software, version 22 (NCSS LLC. Kaysville, Utah, USA) was used to calculate the sample size. The study was designed to establish the superiority of a coordinated nursing care guided by clinical guidelines as compared to the current approach. Based on prior researches, the routine nursing care has enjoyed a rate of early major improvement in the NIHSS score with a reduction of more than four points ranging from 40% to 45% (Rao et al. 2022; Rebello et al. 2024). The researchers would recommend adopting the new nursing care modality if the rate ratio of the new nursing care to the old care was at least 1.1. With a power of 90% and a one‐side αvalue of 5%, the study aimed to detect a ratio of 1.1 when the actual ratio ranged from 1.2 to 1.5. Allowing for a 20% loss rate, the sample size for each cohort was calculated to be 279.

Results

Figure 1 showed the flowchart of study cohorts. A total of 279 patients were initially enrolled in the prospective cohort. After that, 17.2% of cases were lost in follow‐up. Overall, 231 patients who received the nurse‐coordinated and guideline‐driven care were retained to form the prospective cohort. After a 1:1 PSM, 231 patients in the prospective cohort and 231 matched patients in the retrospective control cohort were included for the final analysis. There were no significant differences between the two cohorts with regard to demographic and clinical characteristics at baseline (Table 1).

Flowchart of the study. AIS, acute ischemic stroke.

As shown in Figure 2, the time spent in each link at different in‐hospital stages was significantly shorter in the prospective cohort. The mean time of DTCT was 17.11 ± 9.64 min in the prospective cohort and 26.91 ± 13.94 min in the retrospective cohort, with a significant difference between the two cohorts. Similarly, the mean time of DTP was significantly reduced in the prospective cohort compared to the retrospective cohort (21.75 ± 8.42 vs. 37.35 ± 10.60, p < 0.001). As a result, the mean time of DTN was 51.43 ± 9.64 min in the prospective cohort, which was significantly shorter than that of the retrospective cohort (80.23 ± 10.32 min).

AIS, acute ischemic stroke; CT, computed tomography; DTCT, door‐to‐CT examination; DTP, door‐to‐femoral artery puncture; DTN, door‐to‐needle. *The independent t‐test showing a significant difference compared to the prospective cohort with p < 0.001.

At 24 h after IVT, an early neurological improvement, defined as a reduction in NIHSS score ≥ 4 points, was observed in 57.1% of patients in the prospective cohort and 42.0% of cases in the respective cohort. This difference was statistically significant with a rate ratio of 1.361 (95% CI: 1.127–1.643, p = 0.002). The lower limit of the 95% confidence interval exceeded the pre‐defined superiority margin of 1.1, thus confirming the superiority claim. The proportion of patients achieving this improvement at both Day 7 (p = 0.014) and Day 90 (p = 0.005) was significantly higher in the prospective cohort compared to the retrospective cohort. At 90 days post‐thrombolysis, the rate of functional independence, defined as a mRS score of 0–2, was significantly higher in the prospective cohort than in the retrospective cohort (p = 0.035). Furthermore, the prospective cohort demonstrated a more favourable shift across the entire mRS spectrum (p = 0.002) (Table 2).

For safety, the incidence of symptomatic ICH within 7 days was significantly lower in the prospective cohort compared to the retrospective cohort, with a rate ratio of 0.421 (p = 0.046). There was no significant difference in all‐cause mortality rates up to 90 days between the two cohorts (p = 0.202). The infection rate was significantly lower in the prospective cohort than in the retrospective cohort, yielding a rate ratio of 0.577 (p = 0.010). No significant difference was observed in terms of VET incidence (p = 0.583) and pressure sore incidence (p = 0.198) between the two cohorts (Table 2).

Discussion

Our findings highlighted that nursing's contribution extended to encompass system navigation, team communication and protocol adherence oversight. The significant reductions in DTCT, DTP and DTN times were a direct testament to the nurse's effectiveness as a system accelerator, mitigating multidisciplinary delays that were often cited as barriers to timely reperfusion therapy. Furthermore, the improved neurological outcomes underscored that expert nursing coordination had a positive impact on patient recovery, moving nursing's role from supportive to decisively interventional.

Since AIS is a critical time‐sensitive emergency that required rapid thrombolysis. The time taken for the identification, diagnosis of AIS and initiation of thrombolysis treatment has been reported to significantly influence neurological outcomes in both the short‐ and long‐term after a stroke (Kaesmacher et al. 2024). The AHA/ASA guidelines recommend that hospitals achieve a DTN time of less than 60 min from hospital arrival to the initiation of IVT. However, prior data from the Chinese National Stroke Registry reported that only 17.8% of IVT met this target with a mean time of 116 min (Powers et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2011). Despite prioritising AIS patients in the emergency treatment process, delays were usually observed in any link of various stages including patient recognition, triage, condition evaluation, treatment planning, transfer and treatment administration. These delays involved multidisciplinary cooperation among emergency, radiology, neurology and pharmacy departments (Mendelson and Prabhakaran 2021). A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis emphasised the advantages of incorporating stroke nurses into the acute stroke care team to lead the acute management of AIS patients at the emergency department and in‐hospital settings. The review reported that this protocol significantly shortened the time required for CT examination, femoral artery puncture and IVT initiation (Kumar et al. 2025). Li et al. found that the median DTN time was significantly reduced to 20 min after allocating stroke emergency nurses to coordinate the integrated treatment process for AIS, reaching an international leading level (p < 0.01) (Li et al. 2022). Consistent with previous research, our study demonstrated that a nurse‐coordinated approach during the hyperacute and acute phases of AIS care prompted a timely response from the emergency system and effectively coordinated the stroke care team through rapid activation, priority scanning, prenotification, fast triage, direct transfer, expedited laboratory testing and efficient IVT administration. These findings were also supported by prior meta‐analyses (Baskar et al. 2021). As a result, our data indicated significantly shorter DTCT, DTP and DNT times in patients receiving nurse‐coordinated care compared to controls receiving routine nursing care.

The impact of the evidence‐based nursing practice echoed across the nursing practice. It necessitated that nurses systematically adopted the evidence‐based theories derived from clinical recommendations, which involved integrating practices and guidelines to implement nursing interventions both intuitively and concisely (Brunt and Morris 2025). A recent study found that this approach not only reduced the time required for key elements of the nursing workflow‐resulting in shorter DTCT (19 min (IQR: 16–23)) and DNT times (36 min (IQR: 29–44)) compared to routine nursing care (p < 0.05)‐but also improved neurological function, as indicated by a lower NIHSS score after IVT compared to the control cohort (3 (IQR: 2–4) vs. 3.5 (IQR: 3–5), p = 0.002) (Li et al. 2025). A prior randomised controlled study investigated the effects of performing the evidence‐based nursing care in patients with AIS compared to routine nursing care, and their results reported that NIHSS scores in both groups were significantly decreased from baseline at 4 and 8 weeks after thrombolysis treatment. However, the scores in the group receiving evidence‐based nursing care were significantly lower than those in the control group at both follow‐up points (Liu et al. 2021). According to our data, patients who received nurse‐coordinated and guideline‐driven nursing care for AIS were more likely to experience a significant neurological improvement, indicated by a reduction of ≥ 4 points in the NIHSS score at 24 h, 7 days and 90 days after IVT, compared to the control cohort (rate ratios = 1.361, 1.167 and 1.45, respectively). In addition, our results demonstrated a significantly higher rate of function independence at 90 days with the involvement of advanced stroke nurses, yielding a rate ratio of 1.132 compared to controls. In alliance with our findings, a prior meta‐analysis showed a significant increase in the rates of favourable functional outcomes, defined as a mRS score of 0–2, associated with evidence‐based stroke nurse interventions (Kumar et al. 2025).

Concerns for patient safety, the symptomatic ICH is considered a potential life‐threatening complication following IVT with a high incidence ranging from 6.4% to 17.6% (Qiu et al. 2023). High‐intensity monitoring, including high frequency vital sign checks and neurological assessments, is recommended by the current guidelines to enhance stroke care and decrease these rates (Summers et al. 2009). The guideline‐driven nursing care implemented in the present study involved continuous assessments adhering to clinical guidelines, rapid responses to complications and coordination with a multidisciplinary team, resulting in a 3.5% rate of symptomatic ICH in the intervention cohort. Furthermore, our data showed significantly better EQ‐5D‐3L scores in the intervention cohort compared to the control cohort, supporting the effectiveness of early rehabilitation training based on the coordinated nursing care, consistent with recent evidence (Gu and Huang 2023).

There were several limitations. Firstly, the retrospective design might introduce selection bias and undetected confounders. Secondly, a single‐centre design without external validity might limit its generalisability to all patients with different demographic or clinical characteristics. Thirdly, although PSM was applied to balance characteristics between cohorts, it could not avoid residual confounding factors. Fourthly, the temporal discrepancy between the two cohorts might introduce selection bias, as changes over time in institutional protocols, staff experience, infrastructure or general stroke care standards could have influenced the outcomes independently of the nursing intervention. In the future, well‐designed randomised trials were needed to validate our results.

Implications for practice: Our findings advocated for the establishment of a formal ‘stroke nurse coordinator’ role. This role would involve nursing leadership in developing specialised training programmes, implementing structured workflows and electronic protocols and empowering these nurses as the central coordinators to expedite care and improve outcomes for patients with AIS.

In conclusion, the implementation of nurse‐coordinated, guideline‐driven care demonstrated superior effectiveness in treating patients with AIS. It significantly shortened the time of each link of AIS treatment, improved neurological function and reduced adverse events following thrombolysis treatment, favouring its worthy of clinical promotion.
