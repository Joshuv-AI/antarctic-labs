1 Introduction
The bulk of concentrating solar power (CSP) plants around the world consist of heliostats with 3-
to 4-mm glass mirrors. These mirrors (starting from the sun-facing side) consist of low-iron
glass, a silver reflective layer, a sacrificial copper layer, and then protective paint layers that are
specifically designed to prevent moisture and other atmospheric corrosive agents from degrading
the reflectivity of the silver layer. These mirrors typically achieve a solar-weighted
hemispherical reflectance of ~94% [1]. Some of the losses in reflectance are due to absorption of
light in the glass layer, and therefore mirror performance can be increased by reducing the
thickness of the glass. For example, similar mirrors using 1-mm glass can achieve reflectivity of
~96%. To a CSP plant, there are significant benefits to increasing the mirror reflectivity—there
is more than a one-to-one gain in plant economics as mirror reflectivity increases. For this
reason, there has been past work to reduce mirror glass thickness. For example, Abengoa
installed a plant with 2-mm mirrors over a decade ago [2], [3], and Bright Source worked with
Fraunhofer to test 1-mm mirrors [4].

Mirrors that are 1 mm have not successfully displaced 3- to 4-mm mirrors as a result of various
challenges:

 • A 1-mm mirror is not durable outdoors without a supporting layer behind it. Although it
 is technically feasible to mechanically support and strengthen 1-mm mirrors for outdoor
 conditions (hereafter referred to as a sandwich facet), adding such support increases the
 cost that can offset the economic benefits of increasing the reflectivity.
 • Building a sandwich facet introduces other challenges, including:
 o Introduction of materials that do not share the exact coefficient of thermal
 expansion (CTE) with glass, and therefore facet focal length will vary as a
 function of operating temperature.
 o Adhesives that bond the layers together must be proven to be durable for at least
 30 years.
 o The complexities introduced with manufacturing a sandwich facet create the
 potential to introduce unwanted slope deviation in the final reflective surface.
In response to these challenges, the project titled “Development, Validation and Testing of High-
Reflectance, Cost-Reducing Composite Heliostat Mirror Facets” was funded by the U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) Solar Energy Technologies Office to determine if the above
challenges could be overcome while making a sandwich facet with a 1-mm mirror that is more
economical than 3- to 4-mm glass mirrors.

This publication presents the body of work associated with developing sandwich facets to
address the above challenges. The document is organized as follows: (1) methods, including
performance validation, test protocols, and fabrication processes; (2) results; (3) other
contributions; and (4) project conclusions.

2 Methods
2.1 Performance Validation
Three different instruments are used to measure reflectance parameters in agreement with the
SolarPACES Reflectance Measurement Guideline [5]. Reflectance is defined as a function of
wavelength λ, incidence angle θi and acceptance aperture angle φ. All values reported here for
acceptance angle φ refer to the angle related to the aperture radius (as opposed to the full cone
angle related to aperture diameter).

 • Solar-weighted hemispherical reflectance (total): Solar-weighted hemispherical
 reflectance ρs,h([λa,λb],θi,h) is measured and calculated both with PerkinElmer Lambda
 1050 spectrophotometer and Surface Optics Corporation’s (SOC) solar reflectometer
 SOC 410 across the spectrum of λ = 250–2500 nm and incidence angle θi = 8 deg
 (L1050) and θi = 20 deg (SOC-410). The solar weighting is performed using the solar
 spectrum from ASTM G 173-03 for AM 1.5. The Lambda 1050 has higher accuracy than
 the SOC 410 but can only measure coupon-sized mirrors, while the SOC 410 is handheld
 and can be used to make measurements on any size mirror.
 • Specular reflectance ρλ,φ (λ,θi,φ) is measured with the Device and Services model 15R
 reflectometer (D&S 15R) at λ = 660 nm and distinct acceptance angles φ ranging from
 3.5 milliradian (mrad) to 23 mrad.
 • Specularity is reported as RMS 𝜎𝜎𝑠𝑠 , as explained in A.3 of [5].
 • Solar-weighted diffuse reflectance: Solar-weighted diffuse reflectance ρs,d([λa,λb],θi,d) is
 measured with the SOC 410 at an incidence angle of θi = 20 deg within 7 wavelength
 bands that span the spectrum of λ = 335-2500 nm. The diffuse reflectance is measured,
 excluding radiation reflected in the specular direction at an acceptance angle of
 approximately φ = 106 mrad. If this measurement produces a significant value that goes
 in line with a drop in specular reflectance measured with the D&S 15R, it indicates an
 underlying, broadly diffuse component to the mirror’s reflectance behavior that will not
 contribute to any CSP application.

2.2 Test Protocols
There is a significant body of work in the literature and IEC 62862-3-6 draft standard that
focuses on accessing the durability of glass mirrors against outdoor degradation of the silver
layer [6], [7], [8]. These documents provide a great starting place for assessing reflectivity
degradation, but they do not address the complexities introduced with a sandwich facet. For this
reason, we draw on standards from photovoltaic (PV) modules where appropriate, in addition to
piecing together various other engineering tests and known data with the intention to discover
weaknesses of sandwich facets constructed in this work. Figure 1 provides a group of three focus
areas for testing and associated protypes sizes for each test: (1) robustness against reflectance
degradation, (2) validation of sandwich package durability and manufacturability for low slope
deviation, and (3) measurement and validation of mechanical properties. Note that Figure 1 is a
test protocol that resulted from lessons learned throughout this work. Because of this evolution,
there are early samples that were subjected to slight variation of this test sequence. The evolution
of these variants are described in the results section in context of specific samples and why the
test was changed.

The left column of testing in Figure 1 focuses on testing robustness against reflectance
degradation. Solar-grade mirrors have a rear-side specialized paint intended for 30-year life
outdoors and therefore have proven to have low reflectance degradation through manufacturer
testing and through broad CSP research [1], [9], [10]. In this work, we are considering ways to
reduce mirror cost, primarily by excluding the solar-grade back paint and using the sandwich
package as protection. Therefore, samples with non-solar-grade mirrors are the focus for testing
reflectance degradation. Samples subjected to full reflectance testing are 10 cm by 10 cm so that
hemispherical reflectance measurements can be completed with the Lambda 1050 as well as the
SOC 410. Corrosion area fraction and haziness are captured with mirror photographs. Reflecting
a black background contributes minimal reflected light, which allows surface degradation or
scratches/smudges to stand out as bright or silvery marks (see Figure 2). Reflecting the white
background saturates the mirror with uniform light that reflects through any thin-film coating or
transparent defects in the backing. This makes contamination to the backside show up as dark
spots or shadows in the reflection (see Figure 2). Tests for neutral salt spray, damp heat,
temperature cycling with humidity (TCH), and ultraviolet (UV) plus humidity all are conducted
per the committee draft of IEC62862-3-6, with the exception that only 240 hours of TCH are
applied in this work.

The middle column of testing in Figure 1 focuses on performance and durability of the
composite facets at a larger scale. In this case, 1 m by 1 m is the maximum facet size being
fabricated in this project. All 1-m facets are constructed with 1.1-mm low-iron glass mirrors
achieving ~96% reflectivity per measurements on smaller coupons. The NLR-developed
Reflected Target Non-Intrusive Assessment (ReTNA) photogrammetry system is applied to
measure interpolated root mean square (RMS) slope error for each facet [11]. Initial ReTNA
measurements are intended to capture slope deviation resulting from materials chosen or due to
imperfections in the manufacturing process, while the project targets achieving an RMS slope
error less than 1 mrad. On an operational heliostat wind-induced vibration and natural
temperature and humidity cycles will impart mechanical stresses on sandwich bonds and
deterioration of chemical bonds. The 1-m facet is subjected to the sequence of dynamic cycling,
damp heat, TCH, and repeat dynamic cycling as an aggressive means to determine if the
lamination processes and the adhesive chosen are suitable for the long outdoor life in CSP
applications. The dynamic cycling parameters are borrowed from PV cycling parameters that
have been published by NLR [12], but high-frequency wind data from heliostats were also
examined and support the reasonableness of the test parameters [13], [14]. The NLR dynamic
mechanical cycling (DMX) system, as shown in Figure 3, was used for all dynamic cycling [12].

Figure 3. A framed 1-m by 1-m composite facet with an extruded polystyrene (XPS) core ready for
 vibration testing on NLR’s DMX system.
 The inset in the upper left shows the speakers that are mounted below the mirror for acoustic-driven vibration.

The full sequence for mechanical package durability is both practical and intentionally harsh.
ReTNA captures the initial mirror shape, and then the first dynamic cycling is completed.
Dynamic cycling is chosen first because it is a significantly shorter duration than damp heat, and
vibration should result in rapid failure if poor lamination was achieved in manufacturing. Next,
 5

damp heat provides the opportunity for moisture to penetrate the adhesive layer, and then
temperature cycling provides further opportunity to weaken the adhesive layers. The weakening
of adhesive layers can be measured by time-consuming and destructive peel testing, but these
tests on a repetitive basis are outside the time and budget of this project. Instead, a final dynamic
mechanical cycling and ReTNA measurements are conducted. The mechanical cycling again
seeks to induce gross failure if the adhesive is now too weak. ReTNA is intended to capture any
changes in shape that may have occurred due to partial delamination, fatigue, or creep or
permanent change resulting from temperature extremes.

The tests listed in the right column of Figure 1 are focused on other critical properties for CSP
facets: mechanical strength, survivability to hail storms, and focal length stability under
operational temperature ranges. Four-point bend testing provides force versus displacement
curves on 5-cm by 20-cm samples using an Instron load fixture per ASTM D7249 [15], with
examples shown in Figure 4. Duplicates of each unique sandwich stack are loaded to a maximum
of 300 N while measuring displacement. The project intent is to increase sandwich facet strength
compared to 3- to 4-mm glass, thereby reducing the amount of supporting steel associated with
current facets. Optimization of the composite facet strength will depend on facet size, mounting
design, and overall heliostat design. For this reason, the goal of bend testing is to collect data
from various material and adhesive combinations for use in later optimization.

 Figure 4. (left) Composite facet with a PET core in the Instron for 4-point bend testing; (right)
 various stacks prepared for bend testing
Hail testing standards are well defined by the PV industry per IEC 61215 [16]. Ice ball sizes and
velocities from IEC 61215 are shown in Figure 5. For a solar panel, ice balls are required to be
shot at multiple locations, including the module edge, while being mounted using manufacturer-

specified mounting locations. Composite facet mounting design is outside the project scope;
therefore, only center hail shots are tested. Historically, 25-mm hail balls have been the
maximum size required to pass IEC 61215, but due to increased frequency of hail storms with

Focal length changes associated with CTE mismatch are measured by heating samples to 70°C in
an environmental chamber and then capturing ReTNA images [11] in 5°C increments from 60°C
to 25°C using a T-type thermocouple. These sandwich samples are made 1-m by 16-cm to reduce
material usage in testing and to simplify movement of samples in and out of environmental
chambers. Figure 6 shows an example ReTNA image while cooling. ReTNA images capture
surface curvature and therefore provide data for calculating focal length: 1/(focal length) is
reported (for the 1-m direction) rather than the focal length because a flat mirror has an infinite
focal length, which is then sensitive to the slightest change in curvature. This comparison is
practical, as curvature changes relative to 1/(2f), where f is the focal length.

2.3 Evolution of Sandwich Materials and Fabrication Processes
The DOE-funded HelioCon project provided a feasibility investigation into composite facets that
served as a starting point for fabrication in this project [17]. The HelioCon samples were
fabricated with an industrial laminator at 10 psi vacuum and 120°C for 1,400 seconds, and the
following is an overview and guidance from that work.

 1. 10-cm samples were constructed to perform peel testing of adhesion materials on various
 sandwich components. Ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA), polyolefin elastomers, and 3M 588
 tape were all considered. Each adhesive was subjected to peel testing on the following:
 honeycomb, aluminum honeycomb, and recycled polyethylene terephthalate (PET) board
 (all core materials), aluminum as a backplate, and 1-mm solar-grade mirrors as the front
 plate (adhesive surface being urethane paint on the mirror backside). The peel results

 7

 showed 3M tape had less adhesion strength than EVA and polyolefin elastomers, so its
 use was discontinued because it was also more expensive. All other materials proved
 viable to include in forward testing.
 2. 10-cm sandwich samples were made with combinations of the previously mentioned
 cores and adhesives while using indoor mirrors. The sandwich edge was protected by an
 aluminum frame and a polyisobutylene (PIB) edge seal as used in PV modules. PIB
 formulated for PV is designed to have very low vapor transmission rates. These samples
 were subjected to TCH, damp heat, and UV plus humidity per column 1 of Figure 1, and
 no degradation was measured. This demonstrated that non-solar-grade could be protected
 by a sealed sandwich package but lead to the question: Would a less-expensive edge seal
 or the sandwich adhesive layers be adequate in protecting an indoor mirror?
 3. 61-cm samples were made with 1-mm mirrors for hail testing. These samples used EVA
 as an adhesive and 0.6-mm-thick aluminum backsheets. One sample had a core of 13-
 mm-thick aramid honeycomb and another of 13-mm-thick PET foam. Both samples
 survived 38-mm hail at a 90-degree incidence angle, while the PET foam survived a 50-
 mm hail ball at a 45-degree incidence angle.
The positive hail results with PET led to the first 1-m by 1-m sandwich in this work being
constructed of a 1-mm mirror with a 13-mm-thick PET core, a 0.6-mm aluminum backplate, and
EVA as the adhesive. The 1-m by 1-m flat sandwich was again laminated at 10 psi vacuum and
120°C, but upon cooling from 120°C to room temperature, the sample warped from a flat mirror
to a convex mirror. The CTE mismatch between the glass and aluminum skins was too high for
maintaining desired mirror specifications over the 100°C temperature change. Calculations
showed the same was true for using a steel backsheet (reduced CTE compared to aluminum).
The only readily available sheet material that has a CTE close to glass is fiberglass reinforced
plastic (FRP) with the fibers being oriented woven sheets. FRP sheets are significantly more
expensive than steel and therefore work shifted to laminating near room temperature with
galvanized steel sheets. As a result, EVA and polyolefin elastomers were set aside as these both
require lamination at greater than 120°C. Instead, 2-part epoxies, 2-part urethanes and polyvinyl
acetate (PVA) were selected as candidate adhesives because these can cure at room temperature
and have the potential to hit cost targets.

A 1-m by 1-m sandwich was laminated at 10 psi vacuum and 30°C using a 1-mm mirror with a
13-mm-thick PET core, a 0.6-mm aluminum backplate, and epoxy as the adhesive. The sample
passed as generally flat, but ReTNA images of the facet were highly wavy, and target image
locations could not be sufficiently captured by the software to calculate RMS slope deviation.
Close inspection of the images showed that the waviness of the reflected images was consistent
with bonding seams from the PET board’s manufacturing process. In other words, the mirror had
conformed to the unevenness of the PET board core (also called print through). To reduce print
through, lamination pressure was reduced to 0.5 psi vacuum. This reduced pressure improved
facet quality to the point that ReTNA was now capable of measuring slope deviation. Flat
sandwich facets of various materials and adhesives were fabricated in the laminator at 30°C and
0.5 psi vacuum. The lamination or cure time varied according to the required cure time of the
adhesive in use.

 8

Precision aluminum molds were the targeted approach for curing sandwich facets into ideal
paraboloid shapes. Machine shops were selected with vertical turning lathes to mill two pieces of
11.4-mm-thick MIC 6 flat plate aluminum to be the positive and negative parabolas with an
intended focal length of 80 meters (+/−0.08 mm tolerance request). Those shops with vertical
turning lathes were unwilling to complete a single-piece order, and therefore the molds had to be
made on a standard computerized numerical control (CNC) mill. Although the production shop
reported achieving the +/−0.08-mm tolerance specifications, there was doubt based on visual
inspection of the sample when it arrived at NLR. Standardized tools for validating the curvature
were not available, so feeler gauges were used to take measurements of the parabola center depth
in X and Y using a level to span in the X and Y direction.

The results suggested the specified tolerances may have been achieved when the mold was
mounted on the computerized numerical control mill but the method of supporting the mold was
impacting the measurement results. In other words, sag due to gravity when the mold rested on
an imperfectly flat surface was changing the shape of the mold. In response, the molds were
placed on an optical table, and a center depth of X and Y was measured. The mold was then
rotated and measured again. A position was established where X and Y center depths matched
most closely and the inverse sections of the mold were mated together so that curvature in X and
Y of each piece most closely matched.

Each piece was then marked for a consistent orientation on the optical table for fabricating
facets. The fabrication process consists of: (1) placing the mirror down in the first half of the
mold, (2) applying adhesive on the mirror back and using a squeegee to evenly spread, (3)
placing the core material on to the adhesive, (4) applying adhesive to the backplate material, (5)
placing the backplate on the core material, (6) placing the second half of the mold on top of the
backplate, and (7) applying weight over the top mold for the curing period (see Figure 7).

 Figure 7. MIC 6 aluminum mold rest on an optical table.
The sandwich facet is curing between the two opposite sides of the mold with weight for extra pressure while curing.

Because of inaccuracies in machining, the exact focal lengths in X and Y direction for the mold
are unknown. Considering this, ReTNA was used to analyze the mold curvature. ReTNA targets
were applied in the X, Y, and radial directions, as shown in Figure 8 (placed on the side of the
mold the mirror surface is compressed against in curing). Multiple measurements were taken,
and the average results were focal lengths in meters of X = 94, y =149 and 126 in radial direction
(a signifant deviation from the requested focal length of 80 m). Project funding and timeline
prohibited attempting to achieve a better mold, and therefore this mold was used for all
paraboloid facets.

4 Other Contributions
4.1 Test Sequence Development
One of the milestones of this project was to develop and report on a test sequence for testing
composite facets. The sequence reported in Figure 1 is the result of feedback from experts and
stakeholders specifically in both mirror testing and the concentrating solar industry. Although
not covered in this publication, a review of the literature, existing standards, and PV and
concentrating solar industry practices were all considered in developing the test sequence. The
numbers in column 1 in Figure 1 are well vetted for silver mirrors and were recently published as
an IEC standard [8]; items 1 and 2 of column 3 exist in other standards and are also well vetted
[16]. Item 3 in column 3 is a new result; in other words, ReTNA has been demonstrated as a
cost-effective way to understand mirror performance variation as a function of temperature
(effects of CTE mismatch). The method requires the low-cost ReTNA setup, a chamber or oven
for heating the facet, and thermocouples for measuring the sample temperature as ReTNA
images are taken. Additionally, an in-depth journal publication on progress with ReTNA resulted
from this collaborative work [11]. Quantitative results from using this method are provided in the
Section 3.1. Documenting the errors associated with this method are outside the scope of this
work, but the relative comparison of curvature changes due to different backsheet materials has
proven to be valuable.

The middle column of Figure 1, durability testing of the facet sandwich, includes ReTNA, DMX,
damp heat, and TCH. The subsections of damp heat and TCH are well documented in PV and
concentrating solar standards as applicable tests for on-sun packages, whereas the DMX cycles
are based on wind data from PV and heliostat studies. While each subtest has some validity or
vetting, no claims are made that the ordered sequence is too weak or too severe for a composite
facet. Validating the sequence against outdoor composite facet degradation could take years or
decades to complete and is clearly outside the scope of this work. Alternatively, testing the
sequence in this work demonstrated the following:

 1. ReTNA images can be used as a low-cost and quick method to document changes in facet
 shape throughout the durability test sequence. Imperfections in composite facets can
 easily exceed ReTNA measurement boundaries, but this opens room for improvements to
 ReTNA and better defining its measurement accuracy. In this work, taking ReTNA
 images as a sample degraded were important as a visual aid to degradation rather than a
 qualitative measure. For some samples, qualitative results could have been reported, but
 this was due to more repeatable positioning of the sample for each image captured in the
 degradation process. In this work, the ReTNA setup was incorporated in an ever-
 changing lab space, and the sample mounting was configured each time on an imperfect
 concrete floor. Samples were rotated and images repeated to measure the uncertainty of
 the mounting, but it is outside the scope of this work to fully study the degree of
 uncertainty and optimize the setup. As stated, the images alone provide a qualitative
 measure of curvature change through the test sequence. Alternatively, it can be seen in
 Figure 17 and Figure 18 that image quality varies due to positioning of the sample.
 Qualitative and likely quantitative results would be improved by consistently positioning
 the sample in relation to the target and the camera. In the ReTNA setup in this work, this
 positioning often resulted in ceiling piping or target supports in the images (see Figure
 31

 12), limiting full quantification of mirror curvature. In some cases, the piping or supports
 were unavoidable due to drastic distortions of the mirror. These cases would justify a
 larger target for work testing composite facets with expected degradation.
 Using automatically identifiable targets with ReTNA has many advantages, but one
 challenge is the automatic coded patterns in the images. As previously mentioned,
 waviness or significant warping of the targets pushed the limits of the software. As a
 result of this work, the NLR CSP team has plans to define better guidelines for required
 smoothness for ReTNA and to test methods to improve distorted target recognition.
 2. The NLR DMX vibrational testing system had never been used outside PV panels, but
 use of the equipment in this work enabled a rapid method to evaluate if sufficient
 adhesive strength has been achieved in the composite fabrication process. Additionally, if
 damp heat or TCH resulted in reduced bond strength, DMX coupled with ReTNA
 provided a rapid method to expose problems.

4.2 Synergistic Work With HelioCon
Early planning and fabrication in this project led to broad questions about how existing heliostat
facets are performing in the field. In other words, facets to be produced in this work should be
compared against an existing baseline, but what is that baseline? We searched the literature and
consulted industry experts, but there currently is not a simple answer to this question. In many
heliostats, 4-mm glass mirrors are adhered to steel trusses at select points or with strips of tape or
silicone adhesive meant to absorb CTE mismatch. Work in the literature suggests the CTE
mismatch may be minimized but is not absent [20], [21]. Similarly, the slope error of existing
heliostat facets and stiffness against gravity and wind loads are not well quantified. There are
existing design targets for facet slope error, but field data validating slope error as of function of
wind, temperature and gravity are very limited. Figure 31 shows the focal spot of a 1-m
composite facet made in this project that generally is making a tight image of the sun, but small-
scale waviness of the mirror means there is high slope error (unable to quantify with ReTNA).
Attempts to compare results like this against some baseline led to discussions in the HelioCon
components and controls (C&C) and techno-economic analysis tasks. As a result, there is now
ongoing work in HelioCon C&C and techno-economic analysis to provide tools to understand
the impacts of temperature, wind, and gravity on facet shape and overall field performance.

4.3 Cold Bending of Mirrors
There does not currently exist in the literature information regarding to what degree 1-mm
mirrors can be cold bended without significant distortion. Mirror suppliers in this project
suggested cold bending is feasible, but data were not provided nor were found in the literature.
The results in Table 10 provide some insight into cold bending due to gravity alone or with the
addition of a weight in the middle of the mirror. In these tests a 1-mm mirror or a mirror and core
material were placed on top of a mold with an approximately 20-m focal length. Figure 32 shows
the ReTNA image for case 1. The mirror sags into the mold to the ~20-m focal length with a
slope error of 2.5 mrad, while it distorts more with the weight in the middle. The results show
that core materials that are 13-mm thick are generally more resistant to gravity bending than the
mirror itself. These results along with paraboloid fabricated in this work support that cold
bending of 1-mm mirrors to appropriate focal lengths is not a problem; rather, imperfections in
fabrication molds or print through of core imperfections are the challenges that must be
appropriately addressed.

5 Project Conclusions
The project covered a broad scope. Therefore, it is useful to break down lessons learned and/or
conclusions into key areas as follows.

5.1 Composite Fabrication Lessons
 • Any backsheet with significant CTE mismatch from low-iron glass (galvanized steel for
 example) requires manufacturing around 25°C–30°C (typical facet operational
 temperature) rather than elevated lamination temperatures for adhesives like EVA.
 • Lamination at 10 psi resulted in print through and high RMS slope error from low-cost
 imperfectly flat core materials, while 0.5 psi or less reduced the problem.
 • The constant learning process, limited time, and attempts to keep deliverable schedules
 moving from flat to parabola sandwiches meant that not every variable was
 systematically explored in the fabrication process. That is, there is much more to be
 learned about fabrication controls and methods (both using a laminator and with a
 paraboloid mold) to achieve low slope error. More controls and testing are desirable for
 adhesive application process in efforts to achieve repeatable results.

5.2 Mirror Degradation
 • PIB has been demonstrated as a long-term edge seal that can enable cost cutting in
 protective paints or sacrificial layers meant to prevent silver layer degradation. Cost at
 scale was not demonstrated and would require further design to incorporate PIB seals into
 rapid volume manufacturing.
 • Cheap latex caulking was effective in protecting cardboard core material through TCH
 and 2,000 hours of damp heat. Latex should be tested as an edge seal with indoor mirrors
 that are known to degrade in damp heat.

5.3 Package Durability
 • Overall results showed inconsistent results for package durability per qualitative review
 of ReTNA images. It is recommended that further efforts should first focus on repeatable
 fabrication results for one stack type coupled with quantifiable uncertainty from ReTNA
 measurements throughout the degradation test sequence.
 • The adhesives PVA, epoxy, and urethane all showed potential, but the optimized
 adhesive is likely to be a function of the final stack materials. PVA is cheapest, but it is
 known to have a weak bond with urethane paints, which are assumed to be used on solar
 mirrors. Fabrication and design choices for the mirror/adhesive interface were not
 examined in this work, which leaves opportunity for further testing with low-cost PVA.
 • Use of cardboard honeycomb allows hitting cost targets and showed promise in durability
 tests. The paper industry manufactures many variants of cardboard honeycomb for
 various packaging requirements. The industry is an available and untapped resource for
 optimizing a core material that meets facet specific needs (durability, hail resistant,
 stiffness, volume manufacturing, and low cost).

 35

5.4 Mechanical Properties
 • Instron bend testing resulted in data that are consistent with sandwich stack expectations
 but more repeatable data are needed.
 • One-mm glass mirrors supported by a cardboard honeycomb survived up to 50-mm hail
 balls. Although this demonstrates 1-mm mirrors are possible, further testing and
 optimization is needed for any composite stack, especially hail shots to facet edges,
 which were not included in this work.
 • CTE mismatch testing showed similar results for backsheets of woven or oriented
 fiberglass and galvanized steel. An off-the-product steel is significantly cheaper than
 fiberglass and therefore was the material of choice in this work. The CTE of fiberglass
 can be designed to match low-iron glass (eliminating CTE mismatch problems). This
 type of CTE design was outside the scope of this work, but the University of Clemson
 Composites center was a partner in this work and well suited for further exploration of a
 low-cost path in this direction.
 • This work was unable to compare CTE results for steel-backed composite facets against
 current 3-mm mirrors supported by steel trusses because no data were found. As a result,
 HelioCon is currently conducting techno-economic analysis studies that will provide
 tools to evaluate the impacts of wind gravity and temperature on facet and field
 performance.
 • Increasing core thickness provides more rigid structure while minimizing CTE mismatch.
 Optimizing core thickness is dependent on the results to come from the HelioCon techno-
 economic analysis studies.

 36

6 General Conclusions
The work in this project demonstrated it is feasible to produce paraboloid composite facets that
have an RMS slope error on the order of 2 mrad while achieving greater than 96% reflectivity.
These facets were constructed using a bill of materials that show potential to achieve cost parity
with existing 3-mm glass mirrors supported by structural steel to achieve the desired stiffness.
Additionally, facets produced in this work were able to survive up to 50-mm hail impacts and
were robust against accelerated environmental cycling designed to expose durability concerns.
Further work is needed to optimize composite facet design to requirements in progress by the
DOE HelioCon project. Additionally, it was outside the scope of this work to address a scalable
manufacturing process and the associated manufacturing costs.
