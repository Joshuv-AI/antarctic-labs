HiProto: Hierarchical Prototype Learning for Interpretable Object Detection Under Low-quality Conditions PubID: pubid: 0000–0000/00$00.00 ““© 2021 IEEE
Jianlin Xiang, Linhui Dai, Xue Yang, Chaolei Yang, and Yanshan Li

Abstract
Interpretability is essential for deploying object detection systems in critical applications, especially under low-quality imaging conditions that degrade visual information and increase prediction uncertainty. Existing methods either enhance image quality or design complex architectures, but often lack interpretability and fail to improve semantic discrimination. In contrast, prototype learning enables interpretable modeling by associating features with class-centered semantics, which can provide more stable and interpretable representations under degradation. Motivated by this, we propose HiProto, a new paradigm for interpretable object detection based on hierarchical prototype learning. By constructing structured prototype representations across multiple feature levels, HiProto effectively models class-specific semantics, thereby enhancing both semantic discrimination and interpretability. Building upon prototype modeling, we first propose a Region-to-Prototype Contrastive Loss (RPC-Loss) to enhance the semantic focus of prototypes on target regions. Then, we propose a Prototype Regularization Loss (PR-Loss) to improve the distinctiveness among class prototypes. Finally, we propose a Scale-aware Pseudo Label Generation Strategy (SPLGS) to suppress mismatched supervision for RPC-Loss, thereby preserving the robustness of low-level prototype representations. Experiments on ExDark, RTTS, and VOC2012-FOG demonstrate that HiProto achieves competitive results while offering clear interpretability through prototype responses, without relying on image enhancement or complex architectures. Our code will be available at https://github.com/xjlDestiny/HiProto.git.



I
Introduction

Fig. 1:
Comparison of existing low-quality object detection architectures and our proposed HiProto. (a) Two-stage cascaded architecture. (b) End-to-end jointly optimized architecture. (c) Our proposed HiProto: a prototype-driven end-to-end architecture without relying on image enhancement pipelines or complex architectural designs.

Under low-quality imaging conditions such as low light or haze, image degradation and the loss of key cues can severely compromise the reliability of object detection. This problem is further exacerbated in safety-critical scenarios such as autonomous driving
[
5
,
28
]
, where decisions are expected to be both accurate and interpretable. However, most deep detection models operate as black boxes, raising concerns about their trustworthiness. Consequently, improving robustness under degradation while enhancing interpretability remains a key challenge in practical object detection applications.

Existing low-quality image object detection methods mainly follow two directions (see Figure
1
): (a) enhancing image quality via brightness adjustment, contrast enhancement, or noise reduction before detection
[
44
,
49
,
1
,
56
,
26
]
; and (b) designing detection architectures optimized for low-quality inputs
[
45
,
9
]
. Although these two types of methods achieve some success, the former may distort semantic cues due to its focus on pixel-level appearance, and the latter often involves complex architectures that not only struggle to achieve robust semantic representation but also lack clear interpretability.

To improve object detection performance under low-quality conditions, we argue that building robust semantic representations is essential. Instead of relying on image enhancement techniques or complex architectural designs, we explore a more interpretable alternative: prototype learning. By associating features with class-specific prototypes, this approach provides transparent decision-making, as model predictions can be visually traced back to prototype responses. This approach has shown effectiveness in various vision tasks, including image classification
[
4
]
, semantic segmentation
[
10
,
40
,
12
]
, and few-shot object detection
[
48
,
23
]
. However, how to construct robust and effective prototype modeling mechanisms under low-quality conditions is still an open question.

This paper explores how to integrate prototype learning into object detection architectures based on Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN) to construct semantically structured representations. FPN enhances the perception of objects at different scales through multi-scale feature maps and inherently contains a semantic hierarchy, providing a solid foundation for structured prototype modeling. By embedding class semantics into each feature layer, prototype learning can guide different scales of features to collaboratively model object representations, which improve the semantic discrimination and interpretability under low-quality conditions. However, existing multi-scale detection methods have yet to fully exploit the potential of this semantic structure.

Motivated by these insights, we propose HiProto, an interpretable detection architecture based on hierarchical prototype learning, which builds scale-aware, class-guided representations across feature layers. By embedding structured prototypes into each feature layer, HiProto enables interpretable semantics at every scale, making it possible to visualize where and how class concepts emerge in the feature hierarchy. This improves multi-scale perception while offering clear interpretability through prototype responses, enhancing robustness in low-quality imaging conditions without reliance on image enhancement or complex architectures.

Overall, the main contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We propose HiProto, a new paradigm for interpretable object detection based on hierarchical prototype learning, which achieves multi-scale semantic prototype modeling and target-region feature perception, without relying on image enhancement pipelines or complex architectural designs.

•

We propose a prototype-driven feature optimization method comprising Region-to-Prototype Contrastive Loss (RPC-Loss) and Prototype Regularization Loss (PR-Loss). The former promotes semantic focus on target regions via pseudo label-guided contrastive learning, while the latter enforces orthogonality to enhance prototype distinctiveness.

•

We propose a Scale-aware Pseudo Label Generation Strategy (SPLGS), which filters out objects exceeding the valid perception range of each feature layer to generate supervision, thereby preserving the robustness of low-level prototype representations.

•

Extensive experiments on ExDark, RTTS, and VOC2012-FOG demonstrate that HiProto improves both detection accuracy and interpretability, providing visual evidence through meaningful prototype responses.

II
Related Work

II-A

Low-quality Object Detection Methods

To tackle degraded visual conditions such as low light and haze, early works typically apply image enhancement algorithms
[
27
,
50
,
53
,
22
,
37
,
47
]
as a preprocessing step. However, these methods are often detached from detection objectives, risking feature inconsistency and information redundancy.

Recent advances favor end-to-end designs that couple image restoration with detection. For instance, HLA-Face
[
46
]
and MAET
[
8
]
leverage multi-task supervision or disentangled learning to bridge the low- and high-quality domains. DSNet
[
20
]
and IA-YOLO
[
30
]
incorporate dedicated enhancement modules to mitigate representation degradation. FeatEnHancer
[
17
]
and T2
[
7
]
exploit illumination-aware cues to guide low-light detection. In addition, YOLA
[
18
]
avoids enhancement by learning illumination-invariant features.

While effective, these methods often increase model complexity and lack interpretability. In contrast, we approach the problem from a feature modeling perspective by introducing a structured prototype-based detection architecture that improves semantic clarity without relying on image enhancement or complex architectural designs.

II-B

Prototype Learning in Visual Tasks

Prototype learning has attracted increasing attention in visual tasks due to its interpretable feature matching, which enables effective modeling of class-level semantics.

In image classification, ProtoPNet
[
4
]
and its variants
[
11
,
36
,
41
]
construct interpretable prototype representations to guide prediction through case-based reasoning, offering strong visual interpretability and semantic alignment. In semantic segmentation, prototype learning is mainly used in weakly supervised scenes, where SIPE
[
6
]
, CPAL
[
39
]
, and MuP-VSS
[
13
]
introduce image-specific, context-aware, or multi-label prototypes to improve region completeness, mitigate classification bias, and enhance semantic alignment. In object detection, prototype learning is mainly applied in few-shot scenes, where ICPE
[
33
]
integrates query-aware mechanisms with dynamic aggregation to generate adaptive prototypes, and IPNet
[
52
]
constructs instance-level prototype alignment and foreground-background attention transfer modules to significantly reduce domain gaps.

While prototype learning shows promise in vision tasks, its potential for multi-scale semantic modeling remains underexplored, especially under low-quality conditions where robust representations are crucial. To bridge this gap, we propose a hierarchical prototype learning architecture specifically designed for object detection under low-quality conditions, aiming to enhance both semantic discrimination and interpretability.

III
Proposed Method

III-A

Overall Network Architecture

To enhance the robustness and interpretability of object detection models under low-quality imaging conditions, we propose HiProto, an interpretable object detection architecture based on hierarchical prototype learning. The overall architecture is illustrated in Figure
2
. The core idea of HiProto is to integrate prototype learning into the multi-scale feature extraction and decoupled detection pipeline from a structured feature modeling perspective, thereby enhancing the ability of the detector to distinguish multi-scale and degraded targets in low-quality images.

During inference, HiProto follows the same detection pipeline as the baseline detector. The proposed hierarchical prototype learning is seamlessly integrated into dense prediction detectors without modifying the inference structure. This design enables the model to achieve more interpretable and scale-aware representations while maintaining inference efficiency.

In the following, we detail the hierarchical prototype modeling, the hierarchical prototype learning, and the quantitative interpretability metrics.

III-B

Hierarchical Prototype Modeling

As a classic multi-scale feature extraction method, the FPN constructs multi-resolution feature maps to enhance the ability of the model to perceive objects of various sizes. Actually, each spatial location in a feature map corresponds to a region within a certain receptive field of the original image, reflecting the semantic response and cues of object presence at that scale. Therefore, multi-scale feature maps not only carry spatial information but also imply rich semantic hierarchical structures, providing a solid foundation for hierarchical prototype modeling. Based on this, we introduce a hierarchical prototype modeling mechanism into the multi-scale feature extraction, enabling each layer to focus on learning prototype representations of object semantics within its corresponding scale range. This approach enhances the discriminative capability and robustness of the model when detecting multi-scale objects under low-quality imaging conditions.

Specifically, given an input image
x
, the backbone network and FPN extract a set of multi-scale feature maps:

\mathcal{F}=\{F^{l}\}_{l=1}^{L},\quad F^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{l}\times W_{l}\times D},

(1)

where
l
denotes the feature level,
(H_{l},W_{l})
represents the spatial resolution of the feature map, and
D
denotes the feature dimension. Each feature map is then fed into a decoupled detection head consisting of two independent branches for classification and regression.

III-B
1
Classification Branch

In the classification branch, the feature map
F^{l}
from the
l
-th feature level is first processed by two consecutive
3\times 3
convolutional layers to obtain a classification feature representation
F_{\text{cls}}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{l}\times W_{l}\times D}
. The classification head then applies a convolutional prediction layer to produce category responses for each spatial location. When the prediction layer adopts a
1\times 1
convolution, its kernel weights can be interpreted as a set of class-specific prototype vectors that encode representative semantic patterns of different categories at this feature scale.

Formally, for the
l
-th feature level we define a prototype set:

P^{l}=\{p_{1}^{l},p_{2}^{l},\ldots,p_{C}^{l}\},\quad p_{k}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},

(2)

where
C
denotes the number of object categories including the background class.

Let
f_{i,j}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denote the feature vector at spatial location
(i,j)
of
F_{\text{cls}}^{l}
. The normalized matching score of the
k
-th class can be written as:

S_{k,i,j}^{l}=\mathcal{N}\!\left({p_{k}^{l}}^{\top}f_{i,j}^{l}+b_{k}^{l}\right),

(3)

where
p_{k}^{l}
denotes the prototype vector of the
k
-th class,
b_{k}^{l}
is a learnable class-specific bias term, and
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
represents a normalization function.

By incorporating hierarchical prototype modeling into the classification branch, we parameterize the classification predictors at different feature levels as semantic prototypes. This design enables each feature level to capture representative category patterns at its corresponding scale and also allows the introduction of prototype-level constraints to further regularize multi-scale feature learning. To complement this prototype-based classification modeling, we next introduce a scale-constrained regression design.

III-B
2
Regression Branch

In the regression branch, the feature map
F^{l}
from the
l
-th feature level is first processed by two consecutive
3\times 3
convolutional layers to obtain a regression feature representation
F_{\text{reg}}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{l}\times W_{l}\times D}
. Following the design of YOLOv8, the regression head then applies a convolutional prediction layer to predict discrete distributions of the bounding box offsets for each spatial location using Distribution Focal Loss (DFL)
[
25
]
. For each feature level
l
, we define the regression prediction range as:

\mathcal{R}_{l}=[0,\tau_{l}s_{l}],

(4)

where
s_{l}
denotes the stride of the
l
-th feature layer, and
\tau_{l}
is a scale control coefficient that determines the upper bound of the regression space.

In this way, each feature level performs box regression within a scale-dependent spatial range, which reduces cross-scale ambiguity in localization and encourages different feature levels to focus on objects of suitable sizes. This scale-aware regression design further complements the hierarchical prototype modeling in the classification branch, since the prototype vectors at different feature levels are correspondingly encouraged to respond to objects within appropriate size ranges, thereby promoting scale-specialized object representations. Such coordination between classification prototypes and regression ranges also provides a consistent basis for the hierarchical prototype learning introduced in the next section.

III-C

Hierarchical Prototype Learning

To fully exploit the advantages of hierarchical prototype modeling, we propose a prototype-driven feature optimization method composed of two modules: RPC-Loss and PR-Loss. The former enhances structural consistency between local features and semantic prototypes, while the latter improves the distinctiveness among class prototypes. In addition, to generate reliable pseudo label maps for RPC-Loss, we propose the SPLGS, which restricts supervision at each feature layer to appropriately sized objects to avoid label noise. In the following, the implementation details of the two modules are introduced using the
l
-th feature layer as an example.

III-C
1
RPC-Loss

To enhance the semantic consistency between region features and class prototypes, we propose the RPC-Loss. By measuring the similarity between region features and prototypes, a class similarity map is generated and used as a supervision signal to guide the model in learning discriminative feature representations. For each input image, a feature label map is first constructed based on the prior bounding boxes of the current feature layer. Let the spatial resolution of the feature map at the
l
-th layer be
H_{l}\times W_{l}
, the set of feature label maps is defined as follows:

Y^{l}=\{Y_{1}^{l},Y_{2}^{l},\ldots,Y_{C}^{l}\},\quad Y_{k}^{l}\in\{0,1\}^{H_{l}\times W_{l}},

(5)

where
Y_{k}^{l}
represents the label map for the
k
-th class, and
Y_{C}^{l}
denotes the label map for background. All feature maps are initialized to zero.

In hierarchical feature representations, low-level features typically have small receptive fields, which limits their ability to capture the structural semantics of large objects. For instance, when perceiving a large vehicle, they may focus on smooth door regions of a vehicle, which lack semantic context. To mitigate this, we propose the SPLGS, which avoids using such local features to supervise low-level prototypes, reducing noise and improving representation robustness.

Specifically, we assume a bounding box
B_{k}=(x,y,w,h)
corresponding to the
k
-th class in the image, where
(x,y)
and
(w,h)
denote the center coordinates and size of the object, respectively. For the
l
-th feature layer, we define its valid perception range as
\mathcal{R}_{l}
. We only consider bounding boxes satisfying
w,h\in\mathcal{R}_{l}
as valid for region label generation at this layer, ensuring that the supervision is consistent with the receptive capacity of the feature map.

For each qualifying bounding box, we select anchor points that fall within its region. If the feature point at position
(i,j)
lies within this target area, the label is defined as follows:

Y_{k}^{l}[i,j]=1,\quad k\in\{1,2,\ldots,C-1\}.

(6)

After constructing the label maps for all foreground classes, the label map for background class is defined as:

Y_{C}^{l}=1-\max_{k=1}^{C-1}Y_{k}^{l}.

(7)

After obtaining the label map
Y^{l}
, we further compute the similarity between the prototype vector and all feature vectors at this layer to generate the similarity map
S^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H_{l}\times W_{l}}
. Specifically, the similarity between each prototype
p_{k}^{l}
and feature vector
f_{i,j}^{l}
is computed following the prototype matching formulation in Eq. (
3
). In this paper, the normalization function
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
is implemented using the sigmoid function.

Finally, based on the predicted similarity map
S^{l}
and the constructed label map
Y^{l}
, we employ binary cross-entropy loss (BCE Loss) to perform region-wise contrastive learning for all classes. The region-prototype contrastive loss at the
l
-th layer is computed as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{RPC}}^{l}=-\frac{1}{C\times H_{l}\times W_{l}}\sum_{k=1}^{C}\Big[Y_{k}^{l}\cdot\log\left(S_{k}^{l}+\epsilon\right)\\
+\left(1-Y_{k}^{l}\right)\cdot\log\left(1-S_{k}^{l}+\epsilon\right)\Big],

(8)

where
\epsilon
is a small constant to prevent numerical instability.

III-C
2
PR-Loss

In high-dimensional feature spaces, strong linear correlations among class prototypes can lead to semantic entanglement and ambiguous decision boundaries. To mitigate this issue, we encourage orthogonality among prototypes, which helps disentangle class semantics and promotes more discriminative representations
[
19
]
. To this end, we introduce a prototype regularization loss (PR-Loss) that enforces structural constraints on the prototype space.

In practice, PR-Loss can be implemented using different orthogonality constraints. Conventional approaches typically rely on pairwise similarity penalties, such as cosine loss or POP
[
29
]
, which suppress redundancy by independently constraining prototype pairs. In contrast, our method adopts a global constraint based on Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), which regularizes the prototype matrix as a whole. This SVD-based formulation provides a globally consistent and differentiable spectral perspective
[
21
]
, enabling stable optimization and more precise modeling of the prototype geometry.

Let the class prototype matrix at the
l
-th layer be
P^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times D}
, which is factorized as:

P^{l}=U\Sigma V^{\top},

(9)

where
\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{C})
contains the singular values.
U\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times C}
are the left and right singular matrices, respectively. To encourage orthogonality while avoiding degenerate solutions, we regularize the singular values to be close to 1. The loss is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{PR}}^{l}=\sum_{k=1}^{C}|\sigma_{k}-1|.

(10)

This differentiable spectral regularization integrates easily into training pipelines, promoting a well-conditioned and semantically disentangled prototype space.

To jointly optimize both the detection accuracy and the quality of prototype-based semantic modeling, we integrate the proposed RPC-Loss and PR-Loss into the original YOLOv8 loss function, constructing the final objective function. The complete loss function is defined as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{dfl}}+\sum_{l=1}^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\text{RPC}}^{l}+\sum_{l=1}^{3}\mathcal{L}_{\text{PR}}^{l},

(11)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{dfl}}
represent classification loss, bounding box regression loss and distributed focal loss respectively, forming the original loss function in YOLOv8.

III-D

Discriminability Score

To assess whether the learned prototypes respond selectively to their corresponding target objects rather than background regions, we introduce the Discriminability Score. This metric quantifies how well the activation energy of each class concentrates within its ground-truth regions by aggregating prototype response maps across all hierarchical feature levels.

For the
k
-th class, let
S_{k}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{l}\times W_{l}}
denote the prototype response map at the
l
-th feature level, whose spatial elements are computed according to Eq. (
3
). In this paper, the normalization function
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
is specified as the sigmoid function. The response maps from different hierarchical feature levels are then upsampled to the input image resolution and averaged to obtain the combined saliency map:

\hat{S}_{k}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathcal{U}\!\left(S_{k}^{l}\right),

(12)

where
\mathcal{U}(\cdot)
denotes the upsampling operation.

Let
M_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W}
denote the binary ground-truth mask of all bounding boxes for the
k
-th class. The overall discriminability score is averaged over all test samples:

\text{Disc.}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{k\in C_{x}}\frac{\sum_{i,j}\hat{S}_{k,i,j}M_{k,i,j}}{\sum_{i,j}\hat{S}_{k,i,j}+\epsilon},

(13)

where
X
denotes the test set,
C_{x}
is the set of object classes present in image
x
,
\epsilon
is a small constant for numerical stability, and
N=\sum_{x\in X}|C_{x}|
is the total number of class-level prototype maps across all test images.

In addition, to provide a complementary perspective on localization quality, we compute the AUC of the ROC curve
[
43
]
between the predicted saliency map
\hat{S}_{k}
and the binary ground-truth mask
M_{k}
. This metric, denoted as
\text{AUC}_{\text{ft}}
, evaluates the separability between foreground and background activations, where higher AUC indicates stronger discriminative capability of the learned prototypes in distinguishing object regions from the background.

III-E

Sparsity Score

While the Discriminability Score evaluates the spatial selectivity of prototype responses, it does not capture how semantically distinct these prototypes are from one another. To measure the inter-class independence of the learned prototypes, we introduce the Sparsity Score.

For the
l
-th layer, given the set of class prototypes
\{p_{1}^{l},p_{2}^{l},\ldots,p_{C}^{l}\}
, the Sparsity Score is defined as:

\text{Spar.}=1-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\text{mean}\!\left(\left|\frac{{p_{i}^{l}}^{\top}p_{j}^{l}}{\|p_{i}^{l}\|_{2}\,\|p_{j}^{l}\|_{2}}\right|\,\Big|\,i\neq j\right),

(14)

where
L
denotes the total number of feature hierarchies.

IV
Experiments

IV-A

Evaluation Metric

To comprehensively evaluate our proposed model, we consider both detection performance and interpretability aspects. Specifically, we report the mean Average Precision at IoU threshold 0.5 (mAP@0.5) to assess detection accuracy, and further introduce three metrics to quantify interpretability. The Discriminability Score measures how well the activation energy of each class concentrates within the ground-truth regions by aggregating saliency maps across all feature levels. The
\text{AUC}_{\text{ft}}
further evaluates the separability between foreground and background activations, providing a threshold-independent measure of localization quality. Higher Discriminability Score and
\text{AUC}_{\text{ft}}
values indicate that the learned prototypes respond more selectively and discriminatively to their corresponding objects. The Sparsity Score quantifies the degree of sparsity among prototype vectors of different classes within each hierarchy. A higher Sparsity Score indicates lower redundancy and stronger class-wise separation among the learned representations.

IV-B

Datasets

To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, we conduct model training and testing on three benchmark datasets: Exclusively Dark (ExDark)
[
32
]
, Real-world Task-Driven Testing Set (RTTS)
[
24
]
, and VOC2012-FOG.

ExDark
is a low-light object detection dataset consisting of 7,363 real-world images across twelve categories. We randomly split the dataset into 5,896 training and 1,467 testing images.

RTTS
is a real-world hazy dataset from the RESIDE benchmark, containing 4,322 images for five categories: bicycle, bus, car, motorbike, and person. We use 3,889 images for training and 433 for testing.

VOC2012-FOG
is a synthetic hazy dataset derived from Pascal VOC2012
[
14
]
, generated using the atmospheric scattering model:

I(x)=J(x)\cdot e^{-\beta d(x)}+A(1-e^{-\beta d(x)}),

(15)

where
J(x)
is the clear image,
A
denotes the global atmospheric light,
\beta
is the scattering coefficient, and
d(x)
is defined as:

d(x)=-0.04\cdot\rho+\sqrt{\max(\text{row},\text{col})}.

(16)

In our implementation, we set
A=0.5
and
\beta=0.1
to simulate moderate haze conditions. Hazy images are synthesized offline to reduce training overhead while preserving original annotations. The dataset contains a total of 11,540 images, with 9,232 used for training and 2,308 for testing.

IV-C

Implementation Details

All experiments are conducted on a server with 8 NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs, using Python 3.9 and PyTorch 2.1.0. To ensure reproducibility, the random seed is set to 5. We train the model for 100 epochs using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with a momentum of 0.937, weight decay of 0.0005, and a learning rate of 0.01. Input images from the VOC2012-FOG dataset are resized to 512×512 with a batch size of 16, while images from the RTTS and ExDark datasets are resized to 800×800 with a reduced batch size of 8 to accommodate higher resolution. The hyperparameters
\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{2}
, and
\tau_{3}
are fixed to 4, 8, and
H/s_{3}
, respectively, where
H
is the input image height and
s_{3}
is the stride of the final feature layer. These three hyperparameters are determined as the optimal settings based on our ablation studies.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose HiProto, a new paradigm for interpretable object detection under low-quality imaging conditions. Without relying on any image enhancement techniques or complex architectural designs, HiProto achieves effective multi-scale semantic modeling and feature discrimination through structured hierarchical prototype learning. Specifically, we propose a prototype-driven feature optimization method that incorporates both Region-to-Prototype Contrastive Loss (RPC-Loss) and Prototype Regularization Loss (PR-Loss), where the former strengthens the semantic focus of prototypes on target regions, and the latter enhances the distinctiveness between class prototypes. Additionally, we propose a Scale-aware Pseudo Label Generation Strategy (SPLGS) that guides prototypes at different feature layers to focus on scale-appropriate objects, thereby reducing supervision noise. Experiments on ExDark, RTTS, and VOC2012-FOG demonstrate that HiProto significantly improves detection performance and interpretability.
