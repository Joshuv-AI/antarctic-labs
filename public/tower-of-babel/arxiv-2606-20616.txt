Residuated mappings and homomorphisms in posets
Ivan Chajda, Helmut Länger

Abstract
The concept of weakly regular residuated mappings was investigated for lattices recently by S. Radeleczki and L. Veres. We modify this concept for posets. We define so-called operator-residuated mappings in posets, show their important properties and point out differences between them and residuated mappings defined in the usual way. We modify the concept of a lattice homomorphism for posets where suprema need not exist and show its relation to residuated mappings and induced ideals. Finally, we present several examples of weakly regular residuated mappings in posets and show how such mappings may be constructed.

1
Preliminaries

Some concepts used in lattice theory can be transformed to posets. For the sake of convenience of the reader we shortly summarize the fundamental concepts which we will use in the sequel.

In the following we identify singletons with their unique element.

If
M
and
N
are sets and
f\colon M\to N
then we denote by
\ker f
the equivalence relation
\{(x,y)\in M^{2}\mid f(x)=f(y)\}
on
M
.

Consider a poset
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
, elements
a
and
b
of
P
and subsets
A
and
B
of
P
. The binary relation
\leq
on
P
can be extended to a binary relation on
2^{P}
in several ways:

•

A\leq B
if
x\leq y
for all
x\in A
and all
y\in B
,

•

A\leq_{1}B
if for every
x\in A
there exists some
y\in B
with
x\leq y
,

•

A\leq_{2}B
if for every
y\in B
there exists some
x\in A
with
x\leq y
.

Note that
\leq_{1}
and
\leq_{2}
are not partial order relations, but only quasiorder relations. If
\mathbf{P}
has a smallest element then this element is usually denoted by
0
. Analogously, we define posets with
1
. The
poset

\mathbf{P}
is called
bounded
if it is a poset with
0
and
1
.

Moreover, we define the down set generated by
A
and the up set generated by
A
as follows:

•

\downarrow A:=\{x\in P\mid\text{there exists some }y\in A\text{ with }x\leq y\}
,

•

\uparrow A:=\{x\in P\mid\text{there exists some }y\in A\text{ with }y\leq x\}
.

The subset
I
of
P
is called an
ideal
of
\mathbf{P}
if
\downarrow x\subseteq I
and
U(x,y)\cap I\neq\emptyset
for all
x,y\in I
.

By
(U,L)
we denote the Galois correspondence between
(2^{P},\subseteq)
and
(2^{P},\subseteq)
induced by the binary relation
\leq
on
P
, i.e.

\displaystyle U(A)

\displaystyle:=\{y\in P\mid x\leq y\text{ for all }x\in A\},

\displaystyle L(B)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in P\mid x\leq y\text{ for all }y\in B\}.

Instead of
U(\{a\})
,
U(\{a,b\})
,
U(A\cup\{b\})
,
U(A\cup B)
,
U\big(L(A)\big)
we simply write
U(a)
,
U(a,b)
,
U(A,b)
,
U(A,B)
and
UL(A)
, respectively. Analogously, we proceed in similar cases.

Further
\Max A
and
\Min A
denote the set of all minimal and maximal elements of
A
, respectively. A subset of
P
is a called a
chain
of
\mathbf{P}
if any two of its elements are comparable, and it is called an
antichain
of
\mathbf{P}
if any two different of its elements are not comparable. Let
\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{P})
denote the set of all non-empty antichains of
\mathbf{P}
. Clearly,
\Max A
and
\Min A
belong to
\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{P})
if they are not empty.

On
\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{P})
the quasiorder relations
\leq_{1}
and
\leq_{2}
are partial order relations. We explain it for the relation
\leq_{1}
. Assume
A,B\in\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{P})
,
A\leq_{1}B
and
B\leq_{1}A
. Suppose
a\in A
. Since
A\leq_{1}B
there exists some
b\in B
with
a\leq b
. Since
B\leq_{1}A
there exists some
c\in A
with
b\leq_{1}c
. Since
A\in\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{P})
,
a,c\in A
and
a\leq c
we have
a=c
and hence
a=b\in B
. This shows
A\subseteq B
. By symmetry we obtain
B\subseteq A
and hence
A=B
.

The poset
\mathbf{P}
is said to satisfy the
Ascending Chain Condition
if it has no infinite ascending chains, and
\mathbf{P}
is said to satisfy the
Descending Chain Condition
if it has no infinite descending chains. Of course, every finite poset satisfies both chain conditions.

Let
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq)
be a further poset,
f\colon P\to Q
and
g\colon P\to P
. Then

•

f
is called
monotone
if for all
x,y\in P
,
x\leq y
implies
f(x)\leq f(y)
,

•

g
is called
intensive
if
g(x)\leq x
for all
x\in P
,

•

g
is called
extensive
if
x\leq g(x)
for all
x\in P
.

The mapping
f
is called an
order isomorphism
if it is a bijection from
P
to
Q
and if for all
x,y\in P
we have
x\leq y
if and only if
f(x)\leq f(y)
. The mapping
f
is called an
up-homomorphism
from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
if

f\big(\Min U(x,y)\big)=\Min U\big(f(x),f(y)\big)

for all
x,y\in P
. By an
operator
on
\mathbf{P}
we mean a mapping from
P
to
2^{Q}
.

2
Operator-residuated mappings

When considering residuated mappings in posets we distinguish between the following two concepts.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
and
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq)
be posets. A mapping
f\colon P\to Q
is called an operator-residuated mapping from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
if there exists some operator
f^{*}\colon Q\to\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{P})
such that for all
x\in P
and all
y\in Q
,

f(x)\leq y\text{ is equivalent to }x\leq_{1}f^{*}(y).

In this case the couple
(f,f^{*})
is called a pair of operator-residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
. If
|f^{*}(y)|=1
for all
y\in Q
then the operator
f^{*}
on
\mathbf{Q}
is a mapping from
Q
to
P
and in this case, if
(2)
is satisfied for all
x\in P
and all
y\in Q
,
f
is called a residuated mapping from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
and the couple
(f,f^{*})
a pair of residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
.

It turns out that in a pair of operator-residuated mappings, every of its two components uniquely determines the other one.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
and
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq)
be posets and
(f,f^{*})
a pair of operator-residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
. Then

(i)

f
uniquely determines by
f^{*}
,

(ii)

f^{*}
uniquely determines by
f
.

Proof.

(i)

Let
d\in Q
and
A,B\in\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{P})
and assume that

f(x)\leq d\text{ if and only if }x\leq_{1}A\text{ if and only if }x\leq_{1}B,

for all
x\in P
. Assume
a\in A
. Then
a\leq_{1}A
and hence
a\leq_{1}B
which implies that there exists some
b\in B
with
a\leq b
. Now
b\leq_{1}B
and therefore
b\leq_{1}A
which shows that there exists some
c\in A
with
b\leq c
. Together we obtain
a\leq b\leq c
. Since
A
is an antichain we have
a=c
and therefore
a=b=c
showing
a=b\in B
. Since
a
was an arbitrary element of
A
, we have proved
A\subseteq B
. By symmetry, we also have
B\subseteq A
and hence
A=B
completing the proof of (i).

(ii)

Let
x\in P
. Since for all
y\in Q
,
y\geq f(x)
is equivalent to
y\in\{z\in Q\mid x\leq_{1}f^{*}(z)\}
, we have that
f(x)
is the smallest element of
\{z\in Q\mid x\leq_{1}f^{*}(z)\}
.

∎

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
and
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq)
be posets,
f\colon P\to Q
and
g\colon Q\to\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{P})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

(f,f^{*})
is a pair of operator-residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
,

(ii)

For every
x\in P
,
f(x)
is the smallest element of
\{z\in Q\mid x\leq_{1}f^{*}(z)\}
,

(iii)

f
is monotone,
f^{*}(x)\leq_{1}f^{*}(y)
for all
x,y\in Q
with
x\leq y
,
x\leq_{1}f^{*}\big(f(x)\big)
for all
x\in P
and
f\big(f^{*}(y)\big)\leq y
for all
y\in Q
.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii):

This follows from the fact that for every
x\in P
and
y\in Q
,
x\leq_{1}f^{*}(y)
is equivalent to
y\in\{z\in Q\mid x\leq_{1}f^{*}(z)\}
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii):

Let
a,b\in P
and
c,d\in Q
. Because of
f(a)\leq f(a)
we have
a\leq_{1}f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)
and because of
x\leq_{1}f^{*}(c)
for all
x\in f^{*}(c)
we have
f(x)\leq c
for all
x\in f^{*}(c)
, i.e.
f\big(f^{*}(c)\big)\leq c
. If
a\leq b
then
a\leq b\leq_{1}f^{*}\big(f(b)\big)
and hence
a\leq_{1}f^{*}\big(f(b)\big)
which implies
f(a)\leq f(b)
showing monotonicity of
f
. If, finally,
c\leq d
then
f\big(f^{*}(c)\big)\leq c\leq d
, i.e.
f(x)\leq d
for all
x\in f^{*}(c)
and hence
x\leq_{1}f^{*}(d)
for all
x\in f^{*}(c)
, i.e.
f^{*}(c)\leq_{1}f^{*}(d)
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i):

f(a)\leq c
implies
a\leq_{1}f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)\leq_{1}f^{*}(c)
and therefore
a\leq_{1}f^{*}(c)
. Conversely, if
a\leq_{1}f^{*}(c)
then there exists some

with

and hence
f(a)\leq f(e)\in f\big(f^{*}(c)\big)\leq c
whence

.
∎

.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
and
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq)
be posets and

Let
A
and
B
be sets and
f\colon A\to 2^{B}
. We extend
f
from
A
to

by defining
f(C):=\bigcup\limits_{x\in C}f(x)
for all

. If

The next theorem shows how the mapping
f
and the operator
f^{*}
are related with the compositions
f\circ f^{*}\circ f
and

.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
and
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq)
be posets,
(f,f^{*})
a pair of operator-residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
,
a\in P
and
b\in Q
. Then

(i)

f(a)\leq_{1}f\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)\Big)\leq f(a)
,

(ii)

f^{*}(b)\leq_{1}f^{*}\Big(f\big(f^{*}(b)\big)\Big)\leq_{1}f^{*}(b)
.

Proof.

In this proof we often use Theorem
2.4
.

(i)

Because of
a\leq_{1}f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)
there exists some
c\in f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)
with
a\leq c
. Now
f(a)\leq f(c)\in f\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)\Big)
. This shows
f(a)\leq_{1}f\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)\Big)
. On the other hand, we have
f\big(f^{*}(y)\big)\leq y
for all
y\in Q
and hence
f\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)\Big)\leq f(a)
.

(ii)

We have
x\leq_{1}f^{*}\big(f(x)\big)
for all
x\in P
and therefore

f^{*}(b)=\bigcup_{x\in f^{*}(b)}x\leq_{1}\bigcup_{x\in f^{*}(b)}f^{*}\big(f(x)\big)=f^{*}(f\left(\bigcup_{x\in f^{*}(b)}x\right))=f^{*}\Big(f\big(f^{*}(b)\big)\Big).

On the other hand,
f\big(f^{*}(b)\big)\leq b
, i.e.
f(x)\leq b
for all
x\in f^{*}(b)
. This implies
f^{*}\big(f(x)\big)\leq_{1}f^{*}(b)
for all
x\in f^{*}(b)
. Now

f^{*}\Big(f\big(f^{*}(b)\big)\Big)=f^{*}(f\left(\bigcup_{x\in f^{*}(b)}x\right))=\bigcup_{x\in f^{*}(b)}f^{*}\big(f(x)\big)\leq_{1}f^{*}(b).

∎

For posets satisfying the Ascending Chain Condition we can describe the operator
f^{*}
as follows.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0)
and
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq,0)
be posets with

, assume
\mathbf{P}
to satisfy the Ascending Chain Condition and assume

to be monotone. Define

is a pair of operator-residuated mappings between

and

Let
a\in P
and

. Since

according to Lemma

satisfies the Ascending Chain Condition,

. Now assume

with

4
up-homomorphisms

In this section we show that up-homomorphisms are residuated mappings provided the corresponding posets satisfy the Ascending Chain Condition. Hence one might expect that these homomorphisms satisfy some stronger condition.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0)
and
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq,0)
be posets with
0
and let
f\colon P\to Q
. Then the following holds:

(i)

If
f
is an up-homomorphism from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
then
f
is monotone,

(ii)

if
f
is an up-homomorphism from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{P}
satisfies the Ascending Chain Condition then
f
is operator-residuated,

(iii)

if
f
is monotone then
f
is an up-homomorphism from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
if and only if
(1)
holds for all
x,y\in P
with
x\parallel y
.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in P
.

(i)

If
a\leq b
then

f(b)=f\big(\Min U(b)\big)=f\big(\Min U(a,b)\big)=\Min U\big(f(a),f(b)\big)\subseteq U\big(f(a),f(b)\big)

showing
f(a)\leq f(b)
.

(ii)

This follows from (i) and from Theorem
2.7
.

(iii)

If
f
is monotone then (1) holds for all
x,y\in P
with
x\not\parallel y
since
a\leq b
implies

f\big(\Min U(a,b)\big)=f\big(\Min U(b)\big)=f(b)=\Min U\big(f(b)\big)=\Min U\big(f(a),f(b)\big).

∎

Example 4.2
.

Consider the poset
\mathbf{P}
and the mappings
f
and
f^{*}
from Example
5.6
. Since
(f,f^{*})
is a pair of residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and itself,
f
is monotone according to Theorem
3.2
. Since

\displaystyle f\big(\Min U(a,b)\big)

\displaystyle=f(\{c,d\})=c=\Min U(c)=\Min U(0,c)=\Min U\big(f(a),f(b)\big),

\displaystyle f\big(\Min U(c,d)\big)

\displaystyle=f(1)=c=\Min U(c)=\Min U\big(f(c),f(d)\big),

f

to
\mathbf{P}
according to Lemma

.

It is well known that homomorphisms between lattices induce ideals. A similar situation occurs with up-homomorphisms between posets.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a bounded poset satisfying the Descending Chain Condition,
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq,0)
a poset with
0
and
f
an up-homomorphism from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
. Then the following holds:

(i)

[0](\ker f)
is an ideal of
\mathbf{P}
,

(ii)

if
\mathbf{P}
satisfies the Ascending Chain Condition then
|f^{*}(0)|=1
and
I=[0,f^{*}(0)]
.

Proof.

Put
I:=[0](\ker f)
.

(i)

If
a\in P
,
b\in I
and
a\leq b
then, because
f
is monotone, we have
f(0)\leq f(a)\leq f(b)=f(0)
and hence
f(a)=f(0)
, i.e.
a\in I
. Now assume
a,b\in I
. Since
f
is an up-homomorphism from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
we have

f\big(\Min U(a,b)\big)=\Min U\big(f(a),f(b)\big)=\Min U\big(f(0)\big)=f(0).

Because of
1\in U(a,b)
we have
U(a,b)\neq\emptyset
. Since
\mathbf{P}
satisfies the Descending Chain Condition we conclude
\Min U(a,b)\neq\emptyset
. Let
c\in\Min U(a,b)
. Then
f(c)\in f\big(\Min U(a,b)\big)=f(0)
, i.e.
c\in I
. This shows
U(a,b)\cap I\neq\emptyset
completing the proof of (i).

(ii)

Put
A:=\Max I
. Since
\mathbf{P}
satisfies the Ascending Chain condition we have
A\neq\emptyset
. Now assume
|A|>1
. Let
a,b\in A
with
a\neq b
. Because of the definition of
A
,
a\parallel b
. From (i) we conclude that there exists some
d\in U(a,b)\cap I
. Since
a\parallel b
we have
d>a,b
contradicting the maximality of
a
and
b
. This shows
|A|=1
, say
A=e
. Now let
g\in I
. Since
I
is an ideal of
\mathbf{P}
there exists some
h\in U(g,e)\cap I
. Now
h>e
is impossible because of the maximality of
e
. This shows
h=e
and therefore
g\leq h=e
showing that
e
is the greatest element of
I
. According to Lemma
2.5
,
e=f^{*}(0)
.

∎

The following theorem is some kind of generalization of (ii) of Lemma 1 in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,0,1)
be a bounded poset satisfying the Ascending Chain Condition as well as the Descending Chain Condition,
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq,0)
a poset with
0
,
f
an up-homomorphism from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
and
a,b\in P
. Then

(a,b)\in\ker f\text{ if and only if }\Min U\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big),f^{*}(0)\Big)=\Min U\Big(f^{*}\big(f(b)\big),f^{*}(0)\Big).

Proof.

According to Lemma
4.1
,
f
is a residuated mapping. Because
\mathbf{P}
is a poset with
1
and satisfies the Descending Chain Condition we have

\Min U\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big),f^{*}(0)f\Big)\neq\emptyset.

Since
f
is an up-homomorphism from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
we have

f\bigg(\Min U\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big),f^{*}(0)\Big)\bigg)=\Min U\bigg(f\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)\Big),f\big(f^{*}(0)\big)\bigg)=\Min\big(f(a),0\big)=f(a)

and hence
\Min U\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big),f^{*}(0)\Big)\leq f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)
. On the other hand,

\Min U\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big),f^{*}(0)\Big)\geq f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)

showing
\Min U\Big(f^{*}\big(f(a)\big),f^{*}(0)\Big)=f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)
. The result now follows from the fact that the following are equivalent:
(a,b)\in\ker f
,
f(a)=f(b)
and
f^{*}\big(f(a)\big)=f^{*}\big(f(b)\big)
.
∎

5
Weakly regular residuated mappings in posets

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,1)
be a poset with
1
,
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq,0)
a poset with
0
and
(f,f^{*})
a pair of operator-residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
. Then the following holds:

(i)

f^{-1}(0)=\downarrow\big(f^{*}(0)\big)
,

(ii)

(f^{*})^{-1}(1)=\uparrow\big(f(1)\big)
.

Proof.

Let
a\in P
and
b\in Q
.

(i)

The following are equivalent:
a\in f^{-1}(0)
,
f(a)=0
,
f(a)\leq 0
,
a\leq_{1}f^{*}(0)
,

a\in\downarrow\big(f^{*}(0)\big)
.

(ii)

The following are equivalent:
b\in(f^{*})^{-1}(1)
,
f^{*}(b)=1
,
1\leq_{1}f^{*}(b)
,
f(1)\leq b
,
b\in\uparrow\big(f(1)\big)
. (Observe that
f^{*}(b)
is an antichain.)

∎

We now adopt the following concepts from
[
3
]
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,1)
be a poset with
1
,
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq,0)
a poset with
0
and
f
a residuated mapping from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
. Then
f
is called

•

range-closed if
f(P)=[0,f(1)]
,

•

dually range-closed if
f^{*}(Q)=[f^{*}(0),1]
,

•

weakly regular if it is both range-closed and dually range-closed.

The next result shows how weakly regular mappings can be constructed in certain posets having atoms and coatoms.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,1)
be a poset with
1
,
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq,0)
a poset with
0
,
a
an atom of
\mathbf{Q}
and
b\in P
. If there exists some coatom
c
of
\mathbf{P}
with
c\not\geq b
and
(\downarrow c)\cup[b,1]=P
and some
d\in Q
with
d\not\geq a
and
[0,d]\cup(\uparrow a)=Q
then the mapping
f\colon P\to Q
defined by

f(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a&\text{if }x\geq b\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

is a weakly regular residuated mapping from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
.

Proof.

Define
f^{*}\colon Q\to P
by

f^{*}(y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c&\text{if }y\leq d\\
1&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

We show that
(f,f^{*})
is a pair of residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
. Let
e\in P
and
g\in Q
.

Case 1.
e\geq b
and
g\leq d
.

Because of
a\not\leq d
we have
e\not\leq c=f^{*}(g)
.

Case 2.
e\geq b
and
g\not\leq d
.

Because of
g\not\leq d
and
Q=[0,d]\cup(\uparrow a)
we have
f(e)=a\leq g
. Moreover,
e\leq 1=f^{*}(g)
.

Case 3.
e\not\geq b
and
g\leq d
.

Then
f(e)=0\leq g
. Because of
e\not\geq b
and
P=(\downarrow c)\cup[b,1]
we have
e\leq c=f^{*}(g)
.

Case 4.
e\not\geq b
and
g\not\leq d
.

We have
f(e)=0\leq g
and
e\leq 1=f^{*}(g)
.

Hence in any case
f(e)\leq g
if and only if
e\leq f^{*}(g)
. This shows that
(f,f^{*})
is a pair of residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
. Observe
f(0)=0
and
f^{*}(a)=1
. Finally,
f(P)=\{0,a\}=[0,a]=[0,f(1)]
and
f^{*}(Q)=\{c,1\}=[c,1]=[f^{*}(0),1]
.
∎

Another method for constructing weakly regular mappings by using an order isomorphism is as follows.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,1)
be a poset with
1
,
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq,0)
a poset with
0
,
a\in P
,
b\in Q
and
g
an order-isomorphism from
([a,1],\leq)
to
([0,b],\leq)
, assume
P=(\downarrow a)\cup[a,1]
and
Q=[0,b]\cup(\uparrow b)
and define
f\colon P\to Q
by

f(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }x\leq a\\
g(x)&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Then
f
is a weakly regular residuated mapping from
\mathbf{P}
to
\mathbf{Q}
.

Proof.

Define
f^{*}\colon Q\to P
by

f^{*}(y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}g^{-1}(y)&\text{if }y\leq b\\
1&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Let
c\in P
and
d\in Q
.

Case 1.
c\leq a
and
d\leq b
.

Then
f(c)=0\leq d
and
c\leq a\leq g^{-1}(d)=f^{*}(d)
.

Case 2.
c\leq a
and
d\not\leq b
.

Then
f(c)=0\leq d
and
c\leq 1=f^{*}(d)
.

Case 3.
c\not\leq a
and
d\leq b
.

Because of
c\not\leq a
and
P=(\downarrow a)\cup[a,1]
we have
c\geq a
. Now the following are equivalent:
f(c)\leq d
,
g(c)\leq d
,
c\leq g^{-1}(d)
,
c\leq f^{*}(d)
.

Case 4.
c\not\leq a
and
d\not\leq b
.

Because of
c\not\leq a
and
P=(\downarrow a)\cup[a,1]
we have
c\geq a
. Because of
d\not\leq b
and
Q=[0,b]\cup(\uparrow b)
we have
d\geq b
. Now
f(c)=g(c)\leq b\leq d
and
c\leq 1=f^{*}(d)
.

Hence in any case
f(c)\leq d
if and only if
c\leq f^{*}(d)
. This shows that
(f,f^{*})
is a pair of residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
. Finally,
f(P)=[0,b]=[0,f(1)]
and
f^{*}(Q)=[a,1]=[f^{*}(0),1]
.
∎

The next lemma provides a method for checking if a given residuated mapping is range-closed respectively dually range-closed.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,1)
be a poset with
1
,
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq,0)
a poset with
0
and
(f,f^{*})
a pair of residuated mappings between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
. Then

(i)

f
is range-closed if and only if
f\bigg(P\setminus\Big(\downarrow\big(f(1)\big)\Big)\bigg)=(0,f(1)]
.

(ii)

f
is dually range-closed if and only if
f^{*}\bigg(Q\setminus\Big(\uparrow\big(f(1)\big)\Big)\bigg)=[f^{*}(0),1)
.

Proof.

(i)

f
is range-closed if and only if
f(P)=[0,f(1)]
. According to Lemma
5.1
,
f^{-1}(0)=\\
\downarrow\big(f^{*}(0)\big)
.

(ii)

f
is dually range-closed if and only if
f^{*}(Q)=[f^{*}(0),1]
. According to Lemma
5.1
,
(f^{*})^{-1}(1)=\uparrow\big(f(1)\big)
.

∎
