X-linked spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia tarda misdiagnosed as growth hormone deficiency: identification of a novel intronic TRAPPC2 variant by whole-genome sequencing
Authors: Ha Young Jo, Young Mi Kim, Hyerin Kim, Yoon Jae Cho, Chong Kun Cheon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundX-linked spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia tarda (SEDT) is a rare skeletal dysplasia caused by pathogenic variants in TRAPPC2 and typically presents in late childhood or adolescence with short-trunk disproportion and vertebral dysplasia.Case presentationWe describe a family series centered on an adolescent male initially diagnosed with GHD due to reduced height velocity and subnormal GH stimulation results, who received recombinant human GH (rhGH) therapy for three years with negligible improvement.During puberty, he developed progressive short-trunk disproportion and characteristic radiographic features, including platyspondyly and posterior hump-shaped vertebral endplates, suggestive of SEDT. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was nondiagnostic, whereas whole-genome sequencing (WGS) identified a novel intronic TRAPPC2 variant, c.239-20_239-12delinsAATGAA, initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). Segregation analysis across the family enabled reclassification of the variant to likely pathogenic, confirming X-linked SEDT. Background

X-linked spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia tarda (SEDT; OMIM 313400) is an inherited skeletal dysplasia characterized by disproportionate short stature with a short trunk, early-onset degenerative joint disease, and characteristic radiographic abnormalities of the vertebral bodies and epiphyses [1–5]. The disorder is caused by pathogenic variants in TRAPPC2, which encodes sedlin, a component of the trafficking protein particle (TRAPP) complex. Sedlin plays a critical role in endoplasmic reticulum (ER)–to–Golgi transport of procollagen, and loss-of-function variants disrupt endochondral ossification, particularly within the axial skeleton [3, 5, 6].

Clinically, affected boys appear normal at birth and throughout early childhood, with growth failure and back pain typically emerging between 6 and 12 years of age [1, 4]. Early manifestations may consist solely of reduced height velocity without overt body disproportion or definitive radiographic abnormalities, and affected individuals may be diagnosed with either growth hormone (GH) deficiency (GHD) or idiopathic short stature (ISS), often on the basis of borderline GH stimulation test results and otherwise unremarkable endocrine evaluations [7]. The therapeutic effect of recombinant human GH (rhGH) in SEDT is generally limited, as the primary pathogenic mechanism involves abnormalities in cartilage matrix organization and collagen trafficking rather than defects in the GH–insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) axis [8].

Radiographic hallmarks—including platyspondyly, posterior hump-shaped vertebral endplates, anterior beaking, and progressive truncal shortening—become increasingly apparent during adolescence [1–3]. Molecular confirmation typically relies on sequencing of TRAPPC2, with more than 60 pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants reported to date, including nonsense, frameshift, and canonical splice-site alterations [9–11]. However, deep intronic variants may evade detection by whole-exome sequencing (WES) and require whole-genome sequencing (WGS) or targeted intronic analysis for identification [12–14].

Here, we present a family-based case series in which a proband and his younger brother—both initially diagnosed and treated as GHD—were ultimately found to have SEDT due to a novel intronic TRAPPC2 variant detected by WGS and reclassified from a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) to likely pathogenic on the basis of segregation and phenotype–genotype correlation. The heterozygous younger sister remained asymptomatic and showed normal vertebral morphology, consistent with the expected phenotype of female carriers. This report highlights diagnostic pitfalls, the complementary roles of endocrine testing, radiologic evaluation, and genomic analysis, and the importance of WGS and family-based variant interpretation in the diagnostic workup of skeletal dysplasias.

Case presentation

Proband

The proband, an 11-year-8-month-old boy, presented with severe short stature (height 128.5 cm, − 3.12 SDS for age) and markedly reduced height velocity (< 3 cm/year). Birth history and early developmental milestones were unremarkable. GH stimulation testing demonstrated subnormal peak GH levels (glucagon: 5.89 ng/mL; arginine: 4.33 ng/mL), while other pituitary function tests and pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings were normal, leading to a clinical diagnosis of GHD. rhGH therapy was initiated at 11 years 10 months and titrated to a dose of 0.71 IU/kg/week.

Despite adequate dosing and good adherence, annual height velocity remained suboptimal (< 4–5 cm/year), and at 14 years 7 months his height was 142.8 cm (–3.17 SDS) (Fig. 1). Pubertal development progressed appropriately. With ongoing follow-up, physical examination revealed emerging features suggestive of an underlying skeletal dysplasia, including progressive truncal shortening, relatively long limbs, and a barrel-shaped chest.

Spine radiographs demonstrated generalized platyspondyly and posterior hump-shaped vertebral endplates, findings highly suggestive of a spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia (Fig. 2). In light of these radiologic findings, a comprehensive re-evaluation of his short stature was undertaken. Detailed family history revealed multiple short-statured male relatives on the maternal side, raising suspicion for a heritable skeletal dysplasia with X-linked inheritance. WES was subsequently performed; however, no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants associated with skeletal dysplasias were identified.

Over the following two years, truncal disproportion progressed further. By 16 years 8 months, he had reached a near-final height of 148 cm (–4.30 SDS), with an arm span of 165.5 cm and complete epiphyseal fusion, prompting discontinuation of rhGH therapy. Subsequent WGS ultimately confirmed the diagnosis of X-linked SEDT by identifying a novel intronic TRAPPC2 variant (NM_001011658.4:c.239 − 20_239-12delinsAATGAA) affecting the intronic splice region (Fig. 3).

Younger brother

The proband’s younger brother exhibited short stature and reduced height velocity from early childhood. He was diagnosed with GHD at 5 years 2 months based on GH stimulation testing, and rhGH therapy was initiated. During the initial phase of treatment, his height velocity increased substantially, supporting the initial working diagnosis of GHD.

Follow-up spine radiographs subsequently demonstrated anterior vertebral body beaking and tongue-like contour abnormalities (Fig. 1). By 7 years 2 months of age, physical examination revealed subtle disproportionate short stature and a barrel-shaped chest, resembling early phenotypic features later observed in the proband. Although these radiographic findings were not specific, they raised concern for evolving vertebral dysplasia, preceding the more characteristic platyspondyly that became evident later in the older sibling.

In contrast to the proband, the younger brother demonstrated a comparatively favorable growth response to rhGH, maintaining a height velocity of approximately 7–8 cm/year during treatment. This intrafamilial contrast—earlier emergence of radiographic abnormalities yet better early growth response in the younger sibling—illustrates the variability in phenotypic expression and treatment response among affected males and underscores the diagnostic value of longitudinal clinical and radiologic surveillance in families with suspected hereditary skeletal dysplasia [1–4, 9, 15].

Younger sister

The younger sister also presented with short stature and was diagnosed with GHD following GH stimulation testing, after which rhGH therapy was initiated. She demonstrated an excellent growth response to treatment, ultimately attaining a near-normal adult height of 155.0 cm (–0.75 SDS) while maintaining proportionate body segments throughout follow-up. Spine radiographs consistently showed normal vertebral body height and contour, with no evidence of platyspondyly, endplate irregularity, or other features of vertebral dysplasia.

Targeted genetic testing subsequently confirmed that she was heterozygous for the familial intronic TRAPPC2 variant. Her preserved spinal morphology and robust growth response to rhGH, in contrast to her affected brothers, are consistent with prior reports that female heterozygous carriers of TRAPPC2 variants often exhibit normal or only mildly reduced stature and lack overt skeletal abnormalities, although musculoskeletal symptoms such as back pain may develop later in life [1, 4, 16]. This case underscores the phenotypic variability among female carriers and supports the role of molecular testing in at-risk relatives for accurate diagnosis, counseling, and long-term follow-up planning.

Extended family

Family history revealed multiple short-statured male relatives on the maternal side, many of whom had adult heights below 150 cm, short-trunk disproportion, and chronic back pain. One affected maternal uncle subsequently underwent radiographic and genetic evaluation, which demonstrated vertebral abnormalities consistent with SEDT and hemizygosity for the familial TRAPPC2 variant (Fig. 1). Other affected male relatives had not previously undergone formal diagnostic evaluation. In contrast, maternal female relatives—including the mother and a maternal niece—had normal or mildly reduced stature and reported no significant skeletal complaints (Fig. 4).

This sex-specific distribution of clinical features is consistent with X-linked recessive inheritance, characterized by high penetrance in males and variable expressivity in heterozygous female carriers. Recognition of this multigenerational pattern, in conjunction with the proband’s evolving clinical and radiologic findings, strongly supported the suspicion of X-linked SEDT prior to definitive molecular confirmation [1–3, 5, 9, 11].

Genetic findings

Initial WES performed in all three siblings was nondiagnostic, highlighting a limitation of exon-focused sequencing approaches in skeletal dysplasias, in which pathogenic variants may reside within noncoding or deep intronic regions [17–19]. Given the strong clinical and radiologic suspicion for SEDT, WGS was subsequently pursued.

WGS identified a novel intronic variant in TRAPPC2, NM_001011658.4:c.239 − 20_239-12delinsAATGAA, located within the intronic splice region. This variant was absent from ClinVar and population databases, including gnomAD, thereby fulfilling the ACMG PM2 criterion (absence from population databases) [4, 11, 16, 19]. In silico splicing analysis using SpliceAI predicted a potential impact on normal splicing (score: 0.31), providing supporting computational evidence consistent with ACMG PP3 [15, 19].

Segregation analysis by Sanger sequencing demonstrated that the proband, his younger brother, and a maternal uncle were hemizygous for the variant, whereas the mother, younger sister, and a maternal cousin were heterozygous carriers. This segregation pattern was fully concordant with X-linked recessive inheritance and closely mirrored the phenotypic distribution observed within the extended family [1, 4, 16].

Although the variant was initially classified as a VUS, it was subsequently reclassified as likely pathogenic by a board-certified molecular geneticist based on combined evidence, including absence from population databases (PM2), supportive in silico splice prediction (PP3), segregation with disease across multiple affected and carrier family members (PP1), and strong concordance between the molecular finding and the characteristic clinical and radiologic features of SEDT. This reclassification illustrates the dynamic nature of ACMG/AMP variant interpretation and underscores the central role of family-based evidence in establishing pathogenicity in skeletal dysplasia genomics [15, 19, 20].

Discussion and conclusions

This family series highlights several converging challenges in the diagnosis of X-linked SEDT. The proband’s clinical trajectory—subnormal GH stimulation test results, prolonged rhGH treatment with minimal height response, delayed recognition of truncal disproportion, and late definitive radiographic characterization—illustrates how SEDT can initially masquerade as GHD in routine clinical practice. The younger brother’s earlier radiographic abnormalities despite a similar endocrine evaluation, together with the younger sister’s asymptomatic presentation and normal vertebral morphology despite heterozygosity for the familial TRAPPC2 variant, further demonstrates the considerable intrafamilial variability in the timing and severity of skeletal involvement [1, 4, 7, 16, 21].

A key clinical lesson is that failure of expected catch-up growth under rhGH therapy should prompt re-evaluation of the underlying diagnosis. In children with genuine GHD and open growth plates, appropriately dosed rhGH typically results in a marked increase in height velocity, particularly during the first 1–2 years of treatment [21]. In contrast, the proband in this series exhibited only modest or transient responses, consistent with prior reports in SEDT and related skeletal dysplasias, in which GH therapy has limited impact on final height or radiographic progression [7, 22, 23]. This disparity reflects the underlying pathophysiology: SEDT arises from impaired collagen trafficking and abnormal growth plate matrix architecture rather than from defects in GH secretion or signaling, such that exogenous GH cannot fully correct the abnormal endochondral ossification process [11, 24].

Radiologic evaluation becomes decisive as the disease evolves. Platyspondyly and posterior hump-shaped vertebral endplates represent the hallmark radiographic features of SEDT and allow differentiation from endocrine causes of short stature, which typically preserve vertebral morphology. Earlier in the disease course, more subtle vertebral contour irregularities may be observed, but these findings are nonspecific and not diagnostic [1–3, 25]. The younger brother’s earlier appearance of such subtle abnormalities underscores the importance of serial imaging in children from families with suspected heritable skeletal dysplasia, rather than reliance on endocrine testing alone [4, 25].

Previous reports of X-linked SEDT have consistently described an age-dependent clinical course in which affected boys appear normal during early childhood, followed by progressive growth failure, short-trunk disproportion, back pain, and premature degenerative joint symptoms during late childhood or adolescence [1–5, 26]. Characteristic radiographic findings include platyspondyly, flattening of multiple vertebral bodies, posterior hump-shaped deformities of the vertebral endplates, anterior beaking, and progressive truncal shortening [1–4, 9, 26]. Hip involvement has also been reported, including shortening or broadening of the femoral neck, irregular epiphyseal development, and narrowing of the hip joint space, which may contribute to pain and early osteoarthritis [2, 4, 26, 27]. These previously reported features closely parallel the findings observed in our family. The proband demonstrated progressive short-trunk disproportion, generalized platyspondyly, posterior hump-shaped vertebral endplates, narrowing of the hip joint space, and shortening of the femoral neck, whereas the younger brother showed earlier but milder vertebral abnormalities with anterior beaking and posterior hump-shaped changes. The affected maternal uncle exhibited similar vertebral flattening and platyspondyly, further supporting the characteristic intrafamilial phenotypic pattern of TRAPPC2-related X-linked SEDT.

The younger sister’s clinical course carries important implications for the management of female carriers. Although many heterozygous women for TRAPPC2 variants have normal or near-normal height and minimal skeletal abnormalities, some may develop back pain, early degenerative joint disease, or mild disproportion later in life. Her asymptomatic presentation and normal spinal imaging therefore do not preclude future musculoskeletal manifestations but instead highlight the need for anticipatory counseling and long-term follow-up in female carriers [1, 2, 25, 27].

From a genomic perspective, this family illustrates both the strengths and limitations of contemporary sequencing strategies. While WES has substantially improved diagnostic yields in skeletal dysplasias, it remains blind to many deep intronic or regulatory variants [28, 29]. Increasing evidence across Mendelian disorders demonstrates that WGS can identify cryptic splice-altering variants located beyond canonical splice sites that are missed by exon-focused approaches. Identification of the TRAPPC2 c.239-20_239-12delinsAATGAA variant only after WGS in this family is consistent with these observations and supports consideration of WGS when WES is nondiagnostic despite a well-defined clinical and radiologic phenotype [12, 13, 30–32].

Equally important is the dynamic nature of variant classification under ACMG/AMP guidelines. In this case, segregation across multiple affected males and heterozygous carrier females, combined with a highly specific SEDT phenotype, provided sufficient evidence to reclassify an initially uncertain variant as likely pathogenic [5, 9, 13, 19]. Similar reclassification pathways have been described in other skeletal dysplasias and emphasize the critical contribution of family-based data in resolving variants of uncertain significance [12, 14].

A limitation of this study is that functional validation of the intronic TRAPPC2 variant was not performed. Although segregation analysis, absence from population databases, in silico splice prediction, and phenotype concordance support its likely pathogenicity, RNA-based analysis demonstrating aberrant splicing, such as exon skipping or pseudoexon inclusion, would provide stronger evidence for its functional effect.

Finally, this family underscores the necessity of integrated clinical reasoning. GH testing in isolation may suggest GHD, radiographs alone may yield a broad differential diagnosis, and genomic sequencing alone may leave variants unresolved. Only the integration of longitudinal clinical assessment, detailed radiologic evaluation, comprehensive genomic sequencing, and segregation analysis enabled a definitive diagnosis of TRAPPC2-related SEDT in this series [1–4, 12, 19].

Early recognition has direct clinical consequences: it allows timely discontinuation of ineffective endocrine therapy, shifts management toward appropriate musculoskeletal and supportive care, informs genetic counseling regarding recurrence risk, and facilitates identification of heterozygous female carriers who may benefit from long-term monitoring for later-onset manifestations. Taken together, this report underscores the diagnostic pitfalls that can lead to misclassification of SEDT as GHD, highlights the generally limited yet potentially variable response to rhGH among affected individuals, and emphasizes the critical diagnostic value of WGS for detecting deep intronic variants—along with the essential role of family-based segregation analysis in resolving variants of uncertain significance and establishing a definitive molecular diagnosis in skeletal dysplasias.

Conclusion

Poor or suboptimal response to GH therapy in children diagnosed with GHD should prompt reconsideration of an underlying skeletal dysplasia, including X-linked spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia tarda. Whole-genome sequencing is essential for detecting and accurately classifying deep intronic TRAPPC2 variants, thereby enabling early molecular diagnosis and informing appropriate management and genetic counseling.
