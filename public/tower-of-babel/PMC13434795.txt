Paleorecords inform the limits of Indo-Pacific coral reef survival under accelerating sea-level rise
Authors: Riovie D. Ramos, Tanghua Li, Kyle M. Morgan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Predicting future coral reef responses and climate-driven sea-level rise requires robust historical records of reef vertical accretion rates and their ecological drivers. However, comparable empirical data on past reef growth and framework bioconstruction are often spatiotemporally limited, precluding detection of regional patterns and long-term trends. Here, we compile and evaluate standardized Holocene vertical accretion rates and coral community structure data from 288 Indo-Pacific paleo-reef records across 92 sites to examine intrinsic and extrinsic drivers of accretion. Our findings reveal that reef formation and long-term accretion are determined by a complex interplay between sea-level change and eco-geomorphological factors. Maximum vertical accretion rates indicate that many Indo-Pacific reefs do not have the capacity to keep pace with projected rates of sea-level rise, particularly under high-emissions scenario (76% of reef sites). Critical thresholds suggest that reef accretion is very unlikely (>90% probability) to be maintained when relative sea-level rise rate exceeds 5.3 mm yr−1, a scenario likely to be surpassed within ~35 years. Without substantial reductions in global emissions, many coral reefs face increasing risk of submergence, structural collapse and loss of critical ecosystem services, especially where modern coral communities differ from predominantly competitive Holocene assemblages and are increasingly dominated by weedy taxa.

Introduction

Scleractinian corals are the primary calcium carbonate producers on tropical reefs, playing a critical role in reef framework construction and the associated vertical accretion potential of reef structures1,2. Their ability to build and sustain three-dimensional complexity underpins continued ecosystem functions and geomorphic services that coral reefs provide3–5. However, global climate change (e.g., mass bleaching and ocean acidification) and local stressors (e.g., pollution and overfishing) have led to taxonomic and phase shifts within reef ecosystems6,7, causing significant losses in reef structural complexity8–10 and reduced reef growth potential7,9,11 that threaten these services12–14. With global mean sea level projected to accelerate by the end of the century from 3.7 mm yr−1 to 4.1 mm yr−1 under a low emissions scenario and to 12.6 mm yr−1 under a high-emissions scenario15, the capacity of coral reefs to keep pace with rising sea level, and whether altered coral ecological states on modern reefs can sustain required carbonate production rates, remains uncertain.

Model predictions of sea-level rise (SLR) impacts show less agreement and certainty compared to projections related to thermal stress response5,16. Even with the widespread coral cover decline and projected further loss of tropical reefs at most sites, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change 5th Assessment Report (IPCC AR5) concluded, based on available reef vertical accretion rates (VAR) from the last deglaciation17–19, that fast-accreting modern reefs in clear water conditions could keep up with the maximum rate of SLR of 15.1 mm yr−1 by 210020. In contrast, more recent projections indicate that SLR is estimated to outpace reef accretion under low and high-emissions scenarios by the end of the century2,5,14. The change in projection estimates came from considering contemporary rates of reef carbonate production using carbonate budget approaches, which indicate that many reefs already have growth potential that is at or below the accretion needed to keep pace with recent SLR2,14.

While model projections highlight the value of integrating both contemporary and paleo-evidence in assessing future SLR impacts, important limitations within both lines of evidence persist and have yet to be thoroughly addressed. For example, contemporary estimates of census-based reef carbonate production and vertical accretion potential do not fully consider carbonate gains through cementation and reef unification, and losses through dissolution, or other physical processes that can drive significant rates of sediment import and export onto the reefs21–23. This may result in an overestimated growth potential, especially in reefs prone to physical framework removal, such as those exposed to storms and strong wave conditions, or chemical dissolution in marginal and high-stress environments. Moreover, modern estimates are typically derived from recent reef environmental and ecological conditions, which often reflect reef degradation and substantial coral mortality2,24. Because many modern reefs have developed under near-sea-level stillstand conditions, dominant communities may prioritize lateral growth over vertical accretion, complicating direct inference of “keep-up” capacity from contemporary baselines25. Consequently, future projections based on these modern baselines are considered best-case scenarios of potential SLR impacts2,24,26.

Radiometrically dated reef cores are excellent archives of past reef communities, representing a record of net reef growth incorporating the dynamic processes that shaped their formation over thousands of years. Holocene reefs experienced fluctuations in sea levels, including rapid rates of rise, where subsequent carbonate production and maximum growth potential can be extracted17,18. Measured net accretion rates from paleo-reef cores integrate post-depositional carbonate dissolution and erosion22,27, and episodic disturbances and recoveries during a period of high ecological functioning28, offering a more realistic upper bound for accretion potential relative to contemporary estimates. Extensive work has been conducted in the Great Barrier Reef (GBR) over several decades, allowing for cross-shelf synthesis of reef flat accretion and development, and analysis of their controlling factors over the Holocene29–31. However, like that of modern records, studied Holocene paleo-reef records elsewhere in the Indo-Pacific lack the spatial extent that records from the GBR and the Caribbean regions offer17,22. Existing VAR data also lack the standardization required in using these as quantitative benchmarks in predicting reef accretion against future SLR. RADReef32 is the most recent and extensive effort to revisit and compile global Holocene mean accretion rate data, mainly by standardizing paleo-reef depth when possible, and recalibrating radiocarbon (14C) ages. However, a third of this database was radiometrically dated using Uranium/Thorium (U/Th) techniques that employed different analytical methods and various decay constants. These ages were not normalized and recalculated to align with that of the 14C-standardized framework of RADReef, likely introducing uncertainties into the integrated mean accretion rates.

Here, we present our 14C- and U/Th-standardized (n = 1246, see Methods) Holocene VAR database (Supplementary Data 1) focusing on the Indo-Pacific region where the confluence of dense coastal populations, rapid SLR, and extensive coral reef systems results in its increased vulnerability to climate change (Fig. 1). Following a more rigorous data selection criterion (see Methods), we additionally standardized the ecological framework of each reef record by classifying the constituent coral taxa according to their life history traits33 and noting their growth strategies34 to further investigate the intrinsic and extrinsic controls of Holocene reef accretion. Finally, by comparing standardized VAR with modeled Holocene relative sea-level rise (RSLR) rates, recent trends, and future projections under different shared socioeconomic pathway (SSP) scenarios (see Methods), we identify the likely tipping points at which reef accretion may no longer keep pace with accelerating RSLR and conditions under which reef initiation is no longer viable.Fig. 1Spatial distribution of Holocene reefs in the Indo-Pacific region and corresponding mean vertical accretion rate (VAR, mm yr-1).a The Indo-Pacific is subdivided into three major biogeographic realms82—the Western Indo-Pacific (circles), the Central Indo-Pacific, and the Eastern Indo-Pacific (crosses). Results and discussion

Spatial and temporal coverage of Holocene reef core records in the Indo-Pacific

We have extracted and standardized coral reef VAR (n = 815) and ecological framework metadata from 288 reef cores across 93 sites in the Indo-Pacific region spanning the Holocene interval (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1). Most records are concentrated in the Central Indo-Pacific biogeographic realm, constituting about 74% of the database (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). Comprising the Central Indo-Pacific are the Coral Triangle and adjacent archipelagic reefs (29% of the Central Indo-Pacific) extending to the South China Sea and subtropical Japan; the Tropical Pacific Ocean (12% of the Central Indo-Pacific) including Palau Islands, Guam, Mariana Islands, New Caledonia and Vanuatu; and Australia (59% of the Central Indo-Pacific) including Exmouth to Broome, Sahul Shelf, the Great Barrier Reef, Lord Howe and Norfolk Islands and Houtman. Notably, the Central and Southern Great Barrier Reef is the most well-represented marine ecoregion in the Central Indo-Pacific (38%, Fig. 1b), while the Coral Triangle (sensu stricto) is the least represented (11%) despite being a coral and marine biodiversity hotspot. Specifically, critical records from Indonesia, the Philippines, eastern Malaysia, Solomon Islands and Timor-Leste, all in the top 20 most populated countries in the Central Indo-Pacific, and have some of the longest coastlines in the region, are lacking. The Western Indo-Pacific, encompassing Madagascar, the Mascarene Islands, and the Maldives; and the Eastern Indo-Pacific, including the Marshall, Gilbert, and Ellis Islands, Hawaii, and Polynesia, are similarly represented in the database (12 and 14%, respectively), constituting the least-represented biogeographic realms in the Indo-Pacific. Records from the Arabian Gulf, East African coast, South India, Sri Lanka, Chagos, Bay of Bengal and Andaman Sea would fill existing gaps and improve coverage within the Western Indo-Pacific. The low coverage in the Eastern Indo-Pacific region also results from our data selection criteria which excluded ~75% of reef sites in the region (see Methods).

The selected cores include 1,113 radiometrically dated samples, with 69% dated using 14C and 31% using U/Th techniques. Recalibrated 14C dates differ from reported values by an average of up to ± 800 years (Supplementary Data 2), while recalculated U/Th ages show differences of up to ±200 years (Supplementary Data 3). While 68% of the cores reached pre-Holocene or pre-reefal units, early Holocene samples are notably sparse prior to ~8000 years BP (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Approximately 81% of the cores were collected on the more accessible reef flat zones, with minimal sampling of fore reef environments (Supplementary Data 1) that may have captured significant early Holocene vertical growth and reef development. Early Holocene growth recorded in Vanuatu35, Cook Islands36, Hawaii37, New Caledonia38, and Marshall Islands39 are a continuation of their development since the Late Pleistocene, representing unique records in our database extending beyond the Holocene. In contrast, the number of dated samples and VAR measurements increased over time, reaching a maximum around ~6000 years BP, before subsequently decreasing and plateauing post mid-Holocene (Supplementary Fig. 1b), likely reflecting actual shifts in reef growth dynamics consistent with established sea-level histories in the region as discussed below.

The earliest reefs to accrete during the Holocene emerged within the Coral Triangle, initiating around ~11,500 years BP at Huon Peninsula, Papua New Guinea40, and around ~10,200 years BP at Paraoir, La Union in the Philippines41 (Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Reef initiation in Australia and the Eastern Indo-Pacific, started around ~9800 years BP in Morley reef, Houtman Abrolhos Easter Group42 and Motu Teveiroa, Bora Bora in French Polynesia43, respectively. In the GBR, reefs first initiated at Heron Reef44 around ~8400 years BP, similar to the timing of reef initiation in the Tropical Pacific Ocean, particularly at Koror, Palau Islands45. The reefs of the Western Indo-Pacific were the latest to start accreting, beginning around ~7900 years BP at Maalhosmadulu Atoll, Maldives46. Between ~8000 and ~6000 years BP, most reefs in the Indo-Pacific had initiated accretion, with 69% exhibiting sustained growth lasting more than 1000 years (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Efforts to reconstruct Holocene reef development increased notably from the 1990s onward, expanding the spatiotemporal coverage beyond earlier studies primarily conducted in the Eastern Indo-Pacific and Australia (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Reef coring efforts in Australia have steadily risen, marked by a dramatic increase in new reef sites and core records, particularly within the GBR, over the last decade. Conversely, the number of new records from the Eastern Indo-Pacific has declined over time. Similarly, fewer new records were produced in the Tropical Pacific Ocean and the Western Indo-Pacific during the last decade compared to earlier periods. Although records from the Coral Triangle remain sparse (Supplementary Table 1), there has been a steady increase in new data from surrounding reef areas in the South China Sea and subtropical Japan over the past three decades.

Reef accretion and relative sea-level rise

VAR is measured as the vertical difference between consecutive samples divided by the time interval between their ages (see Methods). Holocene VAR across our database ranged from less than 1 to 23 mm yr-1 (n = 606) (Figs. 1 and 2a) with an average value of 3.6 mm yr-1 (95% CI = 3.4–3.9 mm yr-1) and median value of 2.7 mm yr-1 (95% CI = 2.5–2.9 mm yr-1, Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 2). Variability in mean VAR is greater in the Central Indo-Pacific compared to the Western Indo-Pacific and the Eastern Indo-Pacific biogeographic realms. Among the realms and within the Central Indo-Pacific, mean VAR is significantly higher in the Coral Triangle (p < 0.05, Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 2, and Supplementary Table 3). In particular, the highest VARs were recorded in Huon Peninsula, Papua New Guinea (i.e., Bismarck Sea), where the mean rate reached 7.4 mm yr−1 (95% CI = 5.1–9.7 mm yr−1) (Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, Australia exhibited the lowest VARs among the realms (Fig. 2a), where rates below 1 mm yr−1 are typical in the inshore reefs of the GBR (e.g., Lugger Shoal, Keppel Islands, Northumberland Isles, Holbourne, and Dunk Islands) (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3). VAR appears to be influenced by water quality and tidal regimes, with higher VARs generally observed in clear-water settings and microtidal environments (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Compared with modeled rates of RSLR during the Holocene (see Methods), we found that ~60% of the variation in mean VAR is explained by changes in RSLR (p < 0.001, Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 3). During the early Holocene (i.e., 11,700 to 8000 years BP), as relative sea level (RSL) rose at an average rate of ~8 mm yr−1 (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), about ~24% of the reef sites in our database had commenced growth and sustained an above-average VAR of ~7.0 mm yr−1 for ~3000 years (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 2). More than 94% of the reef cores from this time showed a “keep-up” style of reef growth (Fig. 3b), maintaining their elevation close to rising sea level. Conversely, up to 6% of the cores—mainly from the submerged reefs in the southern Gulf of Carpentaria, Australia—exhibited a “give-up” reef growth style. Between ~8000 and 6000 years BP, most of the rapid and sustained reef initiation occurred as RSL started to reach its maximum height (i.e., higher than present sea level, known as the mid-Holocene RSL highstand48,49), and the rate of RSL further decreased from ~4 to −0.22 mm yr−1 (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 5). Above-average VAR of 5.5 mm yr−1 supported further reef development either by “keeping-up” or “catching-up” with RSL (Fig. 3a, b). Most reefs from the central and southern GBR region (~46%) exhibited a “catch-up” behavior, while most reefs from Western Indo-Pacific and Coral Triangle (~55%) maintained a “keep-up” trajectory (Supplementary Data 1). About 3% of the cores—particularly from Currimao, the Philippines50, Exmouth Marina, Western Australia51 and Hale O Lono, Hawaii52—exhibited a back-stepping growth pattern during this period, highlighting the role of available accommodation in facilitating landward reef growth in response to sea-level fluctuations.

Around ~6000 years BP, most reefs had reached their vertical growth limits and began expanding laterally (Fig. 3a, b) as RSL began to stabilize or slightly regress at a rate of −0.27 mm yr−1 (Supplementary Fig. 5). During this period, mean VAR declined to ~3.3 mm yr−1. About half of the cores continued to exhibit a “catch-up” trajectory. Reef progradation initiated during this interval, ultimately reaching about 30% of the cores, marking the onset of reef flat consolidation and the development of reef flat structures such as crests and back-reef lagoons53. Notably, most reefs in the inshore GBR44,54,55, subtropical Japan56,57, and Hawaii52,58 also experienced reef growth hiatuses beginning this period (Fig. 3a). These initial hiatuses or mid-Holocene “turn-offs”—represented by age gaps of up to ~3500 years—were interpreted to have resulted from complex feedbacks between global climate-sea level dynamics (i.e., sea-level stabilization or regression18,54, increased El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) variability56), mesoscale oceanographic variability (i.e., upwelling and current strength56), and localized stressors such as terrigenous sediment input54,55,57 and exposure to high wave energy52,58, particularly as accommodation became limited.

From 4000 years BP to present, RSL remained relatively stable with a gradual fall of −0.24 mm yr−1 and mean VAR declined further to 2.4 mm yr−1 as more reefs continued to prograde seaward (Fig. 3a, b). Around ~3000 years BP, a slight increase in mean VAR is observed, reaching ~4 mm yr−1. This increase appears to be locally driven by records from Australia (Supplementary Fig. 5c), particularly Heron Reef, southern GBR. In contrast to the reef “turn-off” observed in other reefs in the GBR and on the more wave-exposed side of Heron Reef during this period, the valley substrate on the southwest margin of the reef provided space for rapid accumulation and progradation to occur, and relative protection from scouring waves as the reef approached the sea surface44. Reef sites in the inner and mid-outer central GBR region that previously “turned off” mainly due to increased turbidity have also shown evidence of reactivation as early as 2800 years BP59 (Fig. 3a). This reef “turn-on” was attributed to a final rise in local RSL, where wave resuspension and sediment delivery from the coast were subsequently reduced54.

VAR across the Indo-Pacific reveals regionally nuanced patterns in reef response to Holocene sea-level dynamics. Reefs initiated and closely tracked sea-level change during the early Holocene. As RSL slowed and eventually stabilized after ~6000 to ~4000 years BP, reef growth transitioned to lateral progradation, with most reefs reaching their vertical limits. This shift coincided with a general decline in mean VAR and the onset of centennial to millennial scale reef growth hiatuses, underscoring the sensitivity of the reef-building process to both global sea-level trends and site-specific environmental conditions such as antecedent topography, hydrodynamics (e.g., currents, prevailing winds, and wave exposure), sedimentation, and temperature (e.g., sensitivity to ENSO and upwelling) as accommodation became limited. Understanding how these physical constraints intersect with biological processes is therefore crucial for interpreting the drivers of reef framework construction and how these shape long-term VAR trajectories.

Drivers of reef framework construction

We classified the dominant coral taxa in each reef core record according to their life history strategy—competitive, stress-tolerant, weedy, or generalist—following the framework established by ref. 33 (see Methods). Competitive species, characterized by branching and fast-growing corals such as Acropora spp., were the most prevalent reef-builders across the Indo-Pacific, comprising between 44 and 80% of coral assemblages within each realm (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Some marine ecoregions were predominantly composed of Acropora spp. (Supplementary Fig. 6b), but more commonly, they co-occur with other functional types, reflecting the mixed community structures typical of Holocene and modern reefs60. Stress-tolerant species, characterized by longer-lived, relatively slow-growing massive and domed forms like Porites spp. and most faviids, represented the second most abundant group, accounting for 20 to 43% of assemblages across realms. This is the most species-rich group in the Holocene record (Supplementary Data 1), likely due to its broad distribution across environments variably influenced by wave energy, sedimentation, and temperature fluctuations. Weedy species—small, fast-growing corals ecologically known for opportunistically colonizing disturbed reef environments (e.g., Pocillopora damicornis, Seriatopora and Stylophora)—and generalist taxa—corals that exhibit overlapping traits with competitive, stress-tolerant, and weedy groups (e.g., Turbinaria and Pavona)—were relatively rare, comprising 8% and 1 to 10% of reef assemblages, respectively. In the Holocene record, these species were typically found in light-limited (i.e., greater depths) or chronically turbid reef environments (e.g., inshore GBR) similar to the modern.

In relation to VAR, competitive species exhibited a mean VAR of 4 mm yr−1 (95% CI = 3.6–4.3 mm yr−1) (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 2). This rate is not significantly different from those of stress-tolerant and generalist species, which showed mean VARs of 3.7 mm yr−1 (95% CI = 3.1–4.3 mm yr−1) and 3.2 mm yr−1 (95% CI = 2.1–4.3 mm yr−1), respectively (p > 0.05; Supplementary Table 3), likely due to their close co-occurrence in the core records where stress-tolerant and generalist species are often interbedded with competitive ones. Weedy species, on the other hand, exhibited significantly lower accretion rate, with a mean VAR of 1.9 mm yr-1 (95% CI = 0.7–3.0 mm yr−1; p = 0.004), potentially resulting from their typical small colony size and limited spatiotemporal distribution in the Holocene record (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 6).

Relative to the mean linear extension of the dominant coral comprising each functional group, these mean VARs represent a 72 to 94% reduction in growth (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 4, and Data 4). The consistent growth reduction, along with the comparable mean VARs across the most dominant groups, supports the notion that VAR is independent of coral extension rates over geological timescales. This decoupling has previously been documented primarily for Acropora spp., where fast skeletal extension does not consistently translate into long-term framework accretion due to its high susceptibility to fragmentation, bioerosion and structural loss regardless of environmental conditions27,61. However, our findings reveal that this mismatch between coral growth and VAR extends to other functional groups (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 4, and Data 4). Representative taxa for generalist and weedy species, while often thriving in turbid environments (Supplementary Data 1), are similarly susceptible to physical breakage, endolithic bioerosion and structural loss62, possibly limiting their contribution in long-term reef growth. In contrast, stress-tolerant corals, with their overall higher skeletal density and stronger resistance to both biological and physical erosion, are more likely to maintain structural integrity, enhancing their in situ preservation in the core record. This is exemplified by the exposed reef outcrops in Huon Peninsula, Papua New Guinea63,64, where stress-tolerant taxa dominate and exhibit among the highest mean VARs recorded before tectonic uplift took place (Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3). However, instances where massive corals are more susceptible to post-mortem alteration than branching ones were also documented in turbid reef conditions in the GBR65, highlighting that bioerosion in massive corals are spatially variable and potentially dependent on environmental conditions66. These findings underscore that long-term reef accretion is shaped more by ecological and geomorphological controls than by coral extension rates alone. Fragmentation, cross-reef transport, and biological and physical erosion potentially limit reef accumulation, while sediment dynamics and deposition, and local reef morphology may modulate site-specific accretion potential61,67.

Over time, the relative proportion of competitive species remained constant, whereas stress-tolerant, generalist, and weedy fractions showed significant temporal trends (Fig. 3c). A general decline (slope m = -0.016, p < 0.001) in the stress-tolerant fraction is evident over the Holocene. Conversely, generalist (slope m = 0.013, p = 0.08) and weedy (slope m = 0.008, p < 0.05) fractions show an overall increase, particularly in the late Holocene after a marked paucity between 7000 and 2000 years BP. This increase is largely confined to records from the GBR, a pattern that may reflect the recent rise in studies from this region and improved stratigraphic and taxonomic logging from previous decades (Supplementary Fig. 1). While this may indicate a sampling bias, it could also represent a real ecological shift associated with sea-level stabilization and reef reactivation during this time (Fig. 2a), facilitating the resurgence of these opportunistic taxa following environmental disturbances as previously discussed (e.g., sediment input, hydrodynamic changes, etc.54).

Transitions between major functional groups are also evident within Holocene reef cores (Fig. 3d). An upward shift from generalist to competitive taxa in the barrier reefs of Bora Bora indicates a shallowing sequence into a higher wave-energy environment, with competitive species becoming dominant as the reef began prograding seaward around 6400 years BP (e.g., TEV 1, TEV 2, and TEV 343). Elsewhere, transitions from competitive to stress-tolerant assemblages are observed in cores from Cockatoo Island, Australia (e.g., S2-P1 and S3-P268), Koror Island, Palau (e.g., PL-II and PL III45), Pointe-aux-Sables, Mauritius (e.g., S169,70) and Rasdhoo Atoll, Maldives (e.g., Core 2 and 371). These transitions likely reflect shifts in reef environments driven by changing rates of RSL. At Cockatoo Island, domal and robust corals overlying Acropora-dominated core segments suggest adaptation to turbid, macrotidal conditions (i.e., strong tidal swells) after sea level locally stabilized around 3000 years BP. Similarly, rising sea level led to reef crest and lagoonal development and the occurrence of stress-tolerant taxa in intertidal zones of the reef—initially on inner patch reefs followed by the inner barrier reef flat at Koror Island, the outer reef flat at Pointe-aux-Sables, and on the eastern platform of Rasdhoo Atoll (e.g., Core 2). Notably, a contrasting trend is seen in the core from the lagoonal patch reef at Rasdhoo Atoll, which exhibits a reverse transition—from stress-tolerant to competitive species—as the environment shallowed and became more protected (e.g., Core 3). A similar pattern of competitive taxa overlying stress-tolerant assemblages is also evident in high-energy reef crest zones. For example, cores from One Tree Reef, Australia (e.g., lagoon OTR 6, windward OTR 1 and OTR 4, and leeward OTR 3 and OTR 529,72), Aga Point, Guam (e.g., GM-I and GM-II73) and Rota, Mariana Islands (e.g., RT-E-I and RT-E-II73) show massive Porites and faviids in the early initiation phases which were subsequently replaced by robust branching corals. This reflects the impacts of increased hydrodynamic energy and reduced accommodation following sea-level stabilization. Differences in the timing at which the assemblage shifted between cores in One Tree Reef are attributed to variations in the topography of the initiation platform29. In Guam and the Mariana Islands, the difference in timing between the cores at both sites is explained by the abrupt tectonic uplift after ~4200 years BP, where the younger reef crest facies reestablished itself near the present-day algal ridge (e.g., GM-II and RT-E-II73). These patterns suggest that reef framework trajectories are not solely dictated by sea-level trends but are also shaped by the interplay between reef geomorphology and local disturbance histories.

The future in the past

Reef-building and long-term accretion are governed by a complex interplay between global sea-level dynamics and localized eco-geomorphological factors. While coral growth rates set a foundation for framework development, processes such as fragmentation, erosion, sedimentation, framework unification, and reef morphology critically modulate how reef structures initiate and accumulate over time. Consequently, reef accretion trajectories are not solely and uniformly dictated by sea-level change but are strongly shaped by site-specific conditions and ecological responses to disturbance. In the absence of standardized metrics to quantify these local drivers, we instead assess future reef trajectories by comparing maximum VAR (VARmax) prior to sea-level attainment to both recent (i.e., satellite-derived, 1993–2020) and projected rates of RSLR (i.e., medium confidence) under SSP2-4.5, the scenario that assumes continuation of current trends with intermediate challenges to mitigation and adaptation, and SSP5-8.5, which reflects very high greenhouse gas emissions and socioeconomic trends dominated by fossil fuel development. We used site-level VARmax across reef sites and life history strategies to effectively capture the capacity of reefs to keep pace with rising sea level (see Methods, Supplementary Data 5). Because this approach assumes constant ecological conditions, our estimates represent the upper thresholds that would prevent coral reefs from sustaining reef accretion.

Across the Indo-Pacific, biogeographical realms generally exhibit a mean VARmax that is above or comparable to regional satellite-derived SLR rates (Fig. 4a). At the finer spatial scale of marine ecoregions or individual reef sites, only ~65% show VARmax values that match or exceed contemporary SLR trends (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Data 5). This proportion significantly decreases when compared against future projections: only 27% of reef sites would be able to match RSLR rates under the moderate emissions SSP2-4.5 scenario and 6% under the very high-emissions SSP5-8.5 scenario, suggesting that most reefs may be unable to maintain vertical growth sufficient to keep pace with accelerating RSLR by the end of the century. As a result, significant increases in water depths are projected across these reef sites. Mean water depth is expected to rise by 22 cm (range: −130 to 80 cm, 95% CI = 5 cm) under SSP2-4.5 and by 70 cm (range: −80 to 120 cm, 95% CI = 5 cm) under SSP5-8.5 (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Data 5). Considering reef framework construction, reefs dominated by competitive, stress-tolerant, and generalist taxa exhibit similar trajectories to recent SLR trends with mean deficits of ~2 mm yr−1 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 5). Under future climate scenarios, however, this deficit increases substantially—averaging −2.3 mm yr−¹ with predicted water depth increases of ~20 cm under SSP2-4.5 and −7.7 mm yr−¹ with depth increases of ~67 cm under SSP5-8.5 (Fig. 4a, b). Reefs dominated by weedy species, on the other hand, exhibit significant deficits to contemporary SLR trends and are the least likely to match future RSL rates and most likely to experience submergence of over 0.5 m under future scenarios. Often occurring in already disturbed reef settings where water quality has declined and light penetration is reduced, these assemblages may experience compounded stress under rising sea levels. In such light-limited environments, even a 30 cm increase in sea level would have disproportionately severe effects75 further compromising reef accretion potential and increasing vulnerability to drowning and structural loss, with serious implications for long-term reef resilience.

Analysis of Holocene VAR data also reveals critical thresholds beyond which sustained reef accretion becomes increasingly improbable under rapid SLR (See Methods, Supplementary Data 6). Specifically, reef accretion is very unlikely (>90% probability) and extremely unlikely (>95% probability) to be sustained when RSLR exceeds 5.3 mm yr−1 and 7.7 mm yr−1, respectively (Fig. 4c). Assuming global mean sea-level acceleration rates between 0.084 mm yr−276 and 0.1 mm yr−277, the upper threshold may be exceeded in less than 35 years as regional acceleration rates are expected to be higher than global average.

These results underscore the limited capacity of many Indo-Pacific reefs to keep pace with projected RSLR, particularly under high-emissions scenarios. As more reef systems fall below the thresholds needed to sustain vertical growth, the likelihood of widespread reef submergence, structural and habitat loss, and weakened coastal protection increases. This risk is amplified by concomitant ocean warming and ocean acidification, which can reduce carbonate production while increasing framework loss through bioerosion and dissolution, further limiting the conditions under which reef framework can form and persist. While these implications align with those derived from census-based reef carbonate production and vertical accretion potential of contemporary reefs2, paired modern-paleo VAR records remain uncommon, hampering efforts to evaluate whether these methods converge on a consistent story (and can diverge when erosion is underestimated78) of reef capacity to track sea-level change locally and regionally. Developing site-paired, multi-method datasets that explicitly compare core-derived VAR histories with modern budgets across contrasting eco-geomorphic and environmental settings would refine accretion and erosion parameters and reduce uncertainty in forward projections of reef performance under future SLR. Even with such advances, without significant mitigation of emissions and proactive reef management tailored to site-specific strategies to reduce local stressors on corals, the persistence of reef-building processes and the ecosystem services they support under rising sea levels, faces critical challenges within the coming decades.

Methods

Literature survey and standardized metadata

To update, standardize and analyze available rates of coral reef accretion in the Indo-Pacific (43°E to 150°W, 40°S to 40°N) during the Holocene (11.7 to 0 ky BP), we combined the data collected in Dullo, 200517, and Montaggioni, 200518 with studies identified in the Web of Science Core Collection between 1970 and 2023 searched on May 2023 using the following key words: [(“coral reef”) and (“accretion” or “growth”) and (“Holocene”) and (“Indo-Pacific)]. We evaluated more than 240 papers returned from this search, reviewing the titles, abstracts, and results sections, to first determine whether the paper examined and measured Holocene VAR in the region. We excluded papers where (1) sample depth information is insufficient as well as when the stratigraphic position of the samples is unclear such as in outcrops, microatolls, and loose reef sediment samples; (2) conventional radiocarbon (14C) ages and uncertainty are not reported; (3) U/Th isotopic composition report is insufficient79; and, (4) cores reported only one radiometric age (Supplementary Data 7). The remaining 90 papers that met our criteria include 93 reef sites and comprise of more than 530 reef cores and emerged reef records (hereafter referred collectively as reef cores) with over 1500 radiometrically dated samples. We further screened these reef cores and excluded (Supplementary Data 8) those that report: (1) none or only one available radiometric age in the core; (2) significant age reversals comprising more than 50% of the available ages; (3) available ages outside the bounds of the Holocene period; and (4) composition mainly of loose reef sediments, preventing reliable calculation of VAR.

Our database (Supplementary Data 1) includes 61 metadata fields with 21 fields standardized to aid in analysis, comparison, and interpretation. The metadata is organized into eight categories: entity, core, age and methods, reef initiation, last active accretion, vertical accretion rate, additional notes, and references. Entity metadata includes information on the location and reef site environment related to the core. As such, each entry is particular to a single core record. For emerged reef and reef flat exposures sampled without coring, only ages with clear vertical separation and within 1 m of lateral distance were considered56,80,81. We assigned unique IDs to each core generated using the first three letters of the lead author surname (e.g., EAS for Easton), followed by the last two digits of the publication year (e.g., 76 for 1976), a three-letter code indicating the reef site location (e.g., HAN for Hanauma Bay) and lastly a two-digit numeric indicator of the number of cores for each study included in the database (e.g., 00 for only one core or exposure, 01 and so on for the first and succeeding cores).

We noted the biogeographic realm, province and marine ecoregion of each reef site as determined by its latitude and longitude according to the World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF) Marine Ecoregion of the World (MEOW82). We used this categorization in grouping our database. We interpreted the type of reef (e.g., fringing, barrier, atoll, and platform/coral cay) in which the cores were collected. We categorized the exposure of a reef site to waves (e.g., enclosed seas, semi-enclosed or sheltered coasts, moderate energy coast, prone to long distance swell or local storms, and high-energy coasts) as determined by their location against the WXSD classification by ref. 83, and tidal regime (e.g., micro-, meso-, and macrotidal) according to the finite element solution (FES) 2014 global ocean tide atlas84. We also noted the reef site’s water quality condition (e.g., clear or turbid) based on its contemporary setting described in the original publication, because Holocene changes in water quality were rarely reported or inferred from the cores in the database.

Core metadata details the core name, drill or outcrop location, elevation or water depth and core log information of each core. We took the core name as described in the original publication but added a three-letter code representing the name of the reef site when ambiguous (e.g., NEL in “Core 2 NEL” distinguishes “Core 2” from Nelly Bay, Magnetic Island, from “Core 2” in Rasdhoo Atoll, Maldives). For emerged reefs and reef flat exposures, we named these after the site, reef zone or unit used to describe them by the original publication56,85. We interpreted the geomorphic zone where the core was drilled in a reef site. Most reef cores were extracted in the reef flat and fore reef zones. For emerged reefs, we used the paleoenvironment interpretation provided in the original publication. We noted the elevation or water depth of the core with respect to a sea-level reference datum as reported by the original publication.

For each core, we also recorded the major scleractinian corals and their life form over certain time periods as specified in the original publication. We interpreted the life history strategy or trait of the listed dominant corals—competitive, stress-tolerant, generalist, and weedy—following the classification of ref. 33. Our interpretation is limited by the accuracy of the coral community description by the original authors. For example, all Porites corals without the species-level identification or life form description are presumed massive and thus classified as stress-tolerant species. Montipora, without the species-level identification, is either classified as competitive or generalist depending on its association with other species present in the core. For cores where life history strategy is a mix of competitive species and another trait, we used the latter for classification, given that competitive species are the overall major reef component of Holocene reefs in the region. Approximately 13% of the core records lacked detailed information on their dominant coral composition. We also noted the growth strategy34—keep-up, catch-up, give-up, prograding, and back-stepping—or mode of reef development as described by the original publication, discounting any possible artifact of reef coring86. Around 18% of the core records did not specify details on their adapted growth strategy.

Radiometric technique metadata specifies the age dating technique used in each study and the data we used in standardizing and correcting the radiometric ages. The majority of the selected cores (77%) were dated using the 14C technique (Supplementary Data 2). We recalibrated all 14C ages into calendar years using the Marine 20 curve87 in the CALIB 8.2 software (http://calib.org/calib/calib.html). For localized marine reservoir correction, we first cross-checked the ΔR value in the original publication to verify if this value existed or needed to be updated. We obtained the nearest ΔR values to our selected reef sites from the CALIB 8.2 marine reservoir correction database (http://calib.org/marine/). For sites where multiple ΔR values are available, we took the average ΔR and propagated uncertainties accordingly. Samples that are too young to be calibrated were further excluded from our database. About 23% of the cores, on the other hand, were U/Th dated (Supplementary Data 3). We noted the decay constants used in the original publications. We used EarthTime Redux v.3.7.1 (https://github.com/CIRDLES/ET_Redux) to normalize and recalculate the U/Th ages reflecting the updated decay constants from ref. 88. Samples where δ234U values deviated from the modern seawater value, 145 ± 5‰, suggesting open-system behavior, were flagged as low-quality (e.g., GRA92PUK00 and GRA92RAK0036). All radiometric ages (n = 1113) are standardized to thousands of years before present (BP), where present refers to 1950 CE.

Reef initiation and last active accretion metadata details the reported age and uncertainties of the stratigraphically oldest and youngest samples, respectively, representing the minimum time of reef initiation and active accretion for each core. We noted the reported radiometric age by the original publication and included our calibrated 14C and recalculated U/Th ages for these time horizons. We also noted the sample depth or elevation and its reference datum if different from that of the core.

VAR metadata includes information on our VAR calculation using our standardized data. We calculated VAR as the vertical difference between consecutive samples divided by the time interval between their ages. For samples dated using 14C dating, we used the median 14C age after calibration in the calculations. In our compiled VAR database, we only considered measurements with at least 200 years of age difference (n = 606). This further screening precludes any sampling bias due to compaction and other factors relating to framework loss during extraction. We report VARmean as the average of all accretion rates measured in the core and maximum VAR with the above constraint applied (VARmax and VARmax >200). Under the additional notes metadata, we listed the estimated time of sea level attainment, reported rates of sea-level rise and tectonic movement for each reef site when available. The estimated time of sea level attainment was used to identify the VARmax across reef sites and life history strategies prior to sea level stabilization (see Supplementary Data 5). Finally, the references metadata details the publication information for up to three papers associated with each reef core record.

Data analysis

We fitted a generalized additive model (GAM) using the pyGAM (v0.9.1, https://zenodo.org/records/17981758) library in Python v3.11.8 to examine the temporal variation of Indo-Pacific VAR estimates during the Holocene. We then used a generalized linear model (GLM) to test the relationship between VAR and variables such as location (i.e., biogeographic realm), life history trait, and independently modeled Holocene RSLR (see below). All models employed assumed a Gaussian distribution and an identity link function and used a significance level of p < 0.05. Statistical significance of model coefficients was assessed using two-sided Wald z-tests. GLM is additionally conducted with bootstrapping to account for a varying number of samples per variable. Statistical analyses were performed in Python v3.11.8 using statsmodels (v0.14.6) and SciPy (v1.16.3).

We extracted RSL and rates of RSL (Supplementary Fig. 5) during the Holocene near our reef sites from the glacio-isostatic adjustment (GIA) model ICE-6G_C (VM5a)89, computed using the Coupled Laplace-Finite Element method with a spatial resolution of 0.5° square grid near the surface and time increments at 0.5 ka since 12 ka BP. The effects of rotational feedback and time-varying shorelines are considered90.

To evaluate the capacity of reefs to track recent and projected RSL, and estimate possible magnitudes of reef submergence, we compared our standardized Holocene VAR against sea-level change data for each reef site2,24. We used VARmax prior to sea level attainment using the time constraints described in the original publication, to characterize reef capacity to keep pace with SLR, minimizing the influence of intervals after sea level stabilization. For current sea-level trends, 1993 to 2020, we used monthly 1° square resolution altimetry data from combined TOPEX/Poseidon, Jason-1, Jason-2/OSTM and Jason-3 sea-level fields (http://www.cmar.csiro.au/sealevel/sl_data_cmar.html; accessed February 2024)91. From the available data fields, we chose the inverse barometer- and GIA-corrected dataset with both annual and seasonal signals removed. We extracted sea-level time series at grid points nearest our reef sites and calculated rates using a linear regression model (Supplementary Data 5). For future rates of RSL, we similarly extracted RSL projections by 2100 (1° square grids) under different Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios (i.e., SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5) from the IPCC AR6 model (https://sealevel.nasa.gov/ipcc-ar6-sea-level-projection-tool)15,92,93 at grid points nearest our reef sites (Supplementary Data 5). The SSP2-4.5 scenario assumes continuation of current trends with intermediate challenges to mitigation and adaptation, while the SSP2-8.5 scenario reflects very high greenhouse gas emissions and socioeconomic trends dominated by fossil fuel development94.

To estimate thresholds of sustained reef initiation and accretion given rates of RSLR within our database95, we noted the time of reef initiation and corresponding rate of RSLR for each reef site. We calculated a cumulative distribution function (CDF) assessed by Monte Carlo simulations with 1000 realizations of RSLR at the given times of initiation. We then inverted the CDF to obtain the likelihood of sustained reef initiation given rates of RSLR. The reported values include 95% CI envelope to reflect uncertainties (Supplementary Data 6).
