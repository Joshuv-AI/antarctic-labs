Benchmarking hybrid CNN and transformer backbones with graph convolution networks (GCN) for flower growth-stage classification
Authors: Aritra Das, Karib Shams, Mohammad Rifat Ahmmad Rashid, Raihan Ul Islam, Shamim H. Ripon, Ahmed Wasif Reza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate recognition of flower growth stages is important for plant phenotyping but remains challenging due to subtle visual differences and limited labeled data. This study proposes a hybrid CNN/Transformer + GCN framework for fine-grained flower growth-stage classification. A new dataset, BD Flower Growth, is introduced with 3,889 original images from eight Bangladeshi flower species, categorized into three stages (early, mid, full), forming 24 classes. The dataset is divided into training and testing sets, with augmentation applied only to the training data. Deep backbone networks are used to extract feature maps, which are transformed into graph representations and refined using Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN). A systematic ablation study is conducted by varying GCN depth (3, 5 layers), node resolution (7× 7, 14× 14), and graph construction methods (4-neighbour, 8-neighbour, and KNN with k=16). Experimental results show that performance depends strongly on both backbone and graph configuration. The best performance of 97% accuracy is achieved by EfficientNetV2, DenseNet201-based hybrid models, additionally Swin Transformer model shows the largest improvement, increasing from 84% to 97% after GCN integration. Across different settings, grid-based graphs (4- and 8-neighbour) consistently provide more stable and higher performance compared to KNN graphs, while moderate GCN depth (3–5 layers) offers the best balance accuracy. Cross-dataset evaluation on the Oxford 102 Flower dataset further demonstrates the generalization capability of the proposed approach. These findings highlight the effectiveness of hybrid graph-based learning and the importance of graph configuration in improving fine-grained classification.

Introduction

Flower development progresses through gradual and observable stages, beginning from early bud formation to partial bloom and eventually the full, open flower. These stages often reveal meaningful information about the plant’s growth pattern, health, and environmental response. For agriculture, horticulture, and ecological research, recognizing a flower’s growth stage can support decisions such as irrigation scheduling, harvesting time, and developmental monitoring. Despite this importance, stage identification is typically performed by manual observation, which is labor-intensive, subjective, and impractical when monitoring large numbers of plants.

Deep learning has created new opportunities for automating plant-related tasks, but most existing datasets and studies focus on species classification or disease detection rather than identifying developmental stages. Fine-grained tasks like growth-stage classification remain challenging because the visual differences between early, mid, and full bloom are often subtle and depend on structural cues such as petal spread, shape, and coloration. Prior research also highlights that such fine-grained plant recognition becomes more difficult when images vary in lighting, environment, or background conditions1. To overcome these challenges, hybrid approaches that combine convolutional or transformer-based feature extraction with graph reasoning have gained interest. Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), in particular, can model relationships among extracted features and help distinguish nuanced visual patterns2.

A major limitation in this domain is the scarcity of publicly available datasets that include multiple flower species with stage-wise class labels. Without sufficient labeled data, it is difficult to train models capable of understanding subtle developmental differences across species. To address this gap, a dataset has been developed called “BD Flower Growth”, containing 23,334 color images from eight flower species commonly found in Bangladesh3. The dataset includes 3,889 original photographs and 19,445 augmented samples, and each image is assigned a class label corresponding to one of three growth stages: early, mid, or full bloom – resulting in 24 total classes. Although simple in structure, class-labeled datasets are essential for classification-based phenotyping tasks and remain a foundation for practical deep-learning pipelines4.

To classify these images, seven modern deep learning backbones has been used: ResNet50, ConvNeXt, EfficientNetV2-S, DenseNet201, MobileNetV3, ViT, and Swin Transformer. Importantly, all models were used together with a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN).The CNN/Transformer models extract visual features, and the GCN operates on these features to improve relational reasoning and fine-grained stage recognition. Initial experiments used a two-layer GCN, but this version showed clear overfitting when comparing the training and validation performance curves. After refining the design by increasing the number of GCN layers and improving regularization, the overfitting issue was significantly reduced. The final results were evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and confusion matrices. Grad-CAM visualizations were also applied to understand which regions of the flowers contributed most to the model’s decisions.

Literature review

GCN-based modeling in plant phenotyping and structural growth analysis

Recent advances in graph-based deep learning have significantly improved the modeling of structural, spectral, and relational dependencies in plant phenotyping. In4, hyperspectral imaging is combined with a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) to predict blueberry physiological traits under drought stress. Plants are represented as graph nodes with spectral features, while edges encode spectral similarity, enabling the Plant-GCN to capture complex inter-plant relationships and predict six physiological parameters with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} values up to 0.94. However, the approach relies on fixed spectral graph construction and controlled greenhouse environments, limiting field-scale adaptability.

To address genotype–environment interactions, the author5 introduces a Cross-Omics GCN framework that integrates genomics, metabolomics, and image-derived traits for maize phenotype prediction. Separate GCNs model each modality, followed by tensor fusion to capture G×E×M interactions, improving yield prediction performance from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.32$$\end{document} to 0.43. Graph learning has also been extended to 3D plant structure analysis. The GCASSN model6 employs a GCN–attention architecture for plant organ segmentation from point clouds, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy on the Plant3D and Phone4D datasets, though requiring dense labeled 3D data and high computational cost.

CNN–GCN hybrid frameworks for plant image analysis

Hybrid CNN–GCN architectures combine deep visual feature extraction with relational reasoning to improve plant image understanding. For fine-grained plant species classification7, combines lightweight CNN backbones with Graph Attention Networks and knowledge distillation, modeling image samples as graph nodes connected by visual similarity. This strategy enables compact models to achieve classification accuracies between 91% and 97% with low latency, making them suitable for edge deployment, although fixed graph construction limits adaptability to unseen data. Vision–language integration has also been explored to enhance representation learning. CEPrompt8 introduces cross-modal emotion-aware prompting to guide visual feature extraction using semantic textual cues, improving recognition robustness in challenging conditions. Addressing uncertainty in deep feature representations, UA-FER9 incorporates evidential deep learning and vision–language pre-training to calibrate uncertain relationships, leading to improved robustness and generalization in expression recognition tasks.

Label-efficient learning is explored in10, where CNN-extracted features are used to construct graphs for semi-supervised crop and weed recognition. The framework propagates labels from limited annotated data to unlabeled samples and achieves up to 97.8% accuracy on corn datasets. Graph-based modeling has also been widely used for plant disease classification. PND-Net11integrates CNN feature extraction with GCN-based regional aggregation to represent multi-scale leaf regions as graph nodes, improving robustness across crops. Similarly12, refines CNN-extracted regional features using spatial adjacency graphs and reports accuracies up to 99%. The hybrid GCN–GAT framework in13 converts leaf images into region adjacency graphs using SLIC superpixels and achieves up to 99.73% accuracy across datasets, though static graph construction limits modeling of disease progression over time. Similarly, IncepTR14 combines Inception-based multi-scale convolution with vision transformers and attention mechanisms to capture subtle local and global variations, demonstrating strong performance in micro-expression recognition.

Extended applications of graph-based learning relevant to plant growth and flower development

Beyond plant imaging, CNN–GCN hybrid frameworks have demonstrated strong performance in hyperspectral image analysis. The local–global graph fusion approach in15constructs multiple graphs from local patches and global anchors and fuses them via cross-attention, achieving overall accuracies up to 99.54% on Indian Pines, University of Pavia, and Salinas datasets. To address scalability16, proposes a dual-branch CNN–MiniGCN architecture that combines spatial–spectral CNN features with batch-based graph learning, achieving 92.80% overall accuracy on Indian Pines while reducing memory overhead. HFENet17 further improves robustness using multi-scale convolution and spectral attention, achieving accuracies exceeding 99.5% across multiple hyperspectral benchmarks.

Graph-based learning has also been applied to remote sensing image segmentation, where an attention-based GCN in18 refines superpixel-level CNN features for SAR segmentation, and SGA-Net19 introduces self-constructing graph attention to improve performance on ISPRS Potsdam and Vaihingen datasets. In medical imaging, graph-enhanced deep learning has achieved strong diagnostic performance. The work in7 applies transfer learning with VGG-based CNNs for brain tumor classification, achieving 99.11% accuracy, while CSHG-CervixNet20 leverages hypergraph convolution to achieve 99.31% accuracy in cervical cancer classification. The GATransformer model21 further integrates graph attention with Transformers to enhance explainability and inter-channel dependency modeling.

Graph neural networks have also been extended to temporal modeling, where the hybrid ST-GCN and 1D-CNN framework in22 improves action recognition with 91.7% Top-1 accuracy, and H2GCN23 captures higher-order dependencies using hypergraph convolution. At the molecular level, graph-based network analysis provides biological insights into plant development. The gene co-expression network in24 identifies key regulatory modules controlling flowering in Hydrangea arborescens, while25 integrates transcriptomic and metabolomic networks to explain regulatory mechanisms underlying dynamic flower color changes. Materials and methods

Methodology

Fig. 1Workflow diagram.

Workflow diagram.

In this study, a hybrid CNN–GCN framework is proposed to effectively capture both local visual patterns and spatial relational information for flower growth stage classification. Fig. 1 shows that initially input images are resized and normalized, followed by standard data augmentation techniques to enhance robustness. A deep CNN backbone–selected from ConvNeXt, EfficientNetV2-S, Swin Transformer, ViT-S/16, ResNet50, DenseNet201, or MobileNetV3–is employed to extract high-level spatial feature maps from the input images. The CNN produces a feature tensor size, which is subsequently refined using adaptive average pooling to obtain a fixed spatial resolution.

The pooled feature map is then reshaped such that each spatial location corresponds to a graph node, with the associated CNN feature vector serving as the node attribute. A grid-based graph is constructed by connecting neighboring spatial nodes, preserving the inherent spatial structure of the feature map. This graph representation is processed using a stack of graph convolutional layers, where successive GCN layers model spatial dependencies and contextual relationships among the CNN-extracted features. Batch normalization and ReLU activation are applied after each GCN layer, with dropout introduced in the final layer to reduce overfitting.

To obtain compact representations, global mean pooling is applied independently to the CNN feature map and the GCN output, resulting in a global CNN feature vector and a graph-refined GCN feature vector, respectively. These two complementary representations are fused through feature concatenation, combining appearance-based and relational information. The fused feature vector is finally passed through a fully connected layer followed by a softmax function to perform multi-class classification of flower growth stages.

Dataset description

For this work, a comprehensive and custom-built dataset named BD Flower Growth was utilized3. The dataset encompasses eight common flower species found in Bangladesh: Bush Allamanda, Red Hibiscus, Yellow Bell, Pinwheel Flower, Pink Periwinkle, White Madagascar Periwinkle, Marvel of Peru, and White Hibiscus. Each species is represented across three growth stages–Early Stage (initial bud formation), Mid Stage (partial blooming), and Full Stage (complete bloom)–resulting in 24 distinct classes. Dataset collection process

The dataset was collected over a period of almost four months, from June 5 to September 24, 2025, in a residential area of Aftabnagar, Dhaka, Bangladesh. Image collection was carried out on a home balcony so that the flowers could be photographed under natural outdoor conditions similar to everyday garden environments. Eight common flower species found in Bangladesh–Bush Allamanda, Red Hibiscus, Yellow Bell, Pinwheel Flower, Pink Periwinkle, White Madagascar Periwinkle, Marvel of Peru, and White Hibiscus–were selected, and individual flowers were followed from the moment the bud appeared until the flower was fully bloomed. To capture natural changes in appearance throughout the day, images were taken three times daily–morning (Between 9:00–10:00 AM), evening (Between 5:00–6:00 PM), and night (1:00–2:00 AM)–with an interval of about eight hours between sessions. All photographs were captured using smartphone cameras. The growth of each flower was grouped into three stages (early, mid, and full) based on visible development. In total, 3,889 original images were obtained.

Dataset pre-processing

After the images were collected, all images were resized to a uniform resolution of 640 × 640 pixels. Standardizing the image dimensions ensures consistency across the dataset and enables more efficient processing by the model during training. The dataset was divided into 80% for training, 10% for validation, and 10% for testing using an instance-level split protocol. Images belonging to the same flower instance were assigned to only one subset, ensuring that no flower instance appeared across multiple subsets. This strategy prevents data leakage between training and evaluation sets and provides a more reliable assessment of the model’s generalization capability.

To further enhance the dataset and improve the model’s generalization capability, five augmentation techniques were applied to the training set. The validation and testing sets remained unchanged, with no augmentation applied. These augmentations not only increased the size of the training dataset but also introduced realistic variations that simulate changes in lighting, position, and orientation commonly observed in real-world scenarios. Baseline models

To establish a strong and fair benchmark, several state-of-the-art convolutional neural networks and transformer-based architectures are used as baseline models. These architectures span conventional deep CNNs, efficiency-oriented lightweight networks, and attention-driven vision transformers, providing a broad evaluation of performance across different design philosophies.

ConvNeXt

ConvNeXt27 is a modern convolutional neural network that integrates design principles inspired by Vision Transformers while retaining the inductive biases and efficiency of CNNs. It utilizes large-kernel depthwise convolutions, inverted bottleneck blocks, and layer normalization to enhance feature representation. The hierarchical architecture enables effective multi-scale feature extraction, making ConvNeXt well suited for capturing both local textures and global flower structure.

EfficientNetV2-S

EfficientNetV2-S28 is an efficiency-optimized CNN that improves training speed and performance through fused-MBConv blocks and refined compound scaling. By jointly scaling network depth, width, and resolution, the model achieves strong accuracy with reduced computational cost. This makes EfficientNetV2-S particularly effective for scenarios where efficiency and performance must be balanced.

Swin Transformer V2

Swin Transformer V229 is a hierarchical vision transformer that computes self-attention within shifted local windows, allowing scalable processing of high-resolution images. The shifted-window mechanism enables information exchange across neighboring regions while maintaining linear computational complexity. This architecture effectively captures both local patterns and long-range dependencies, which are important for modeling spatial variations in flower growth stages.

Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16)

Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16)30 models images as sequences of fixed-size patches processed by a standard Transformer encoder. By leveraging global self-attention, ViT captures long-range spatial relationships across the entire image. Positional embeddings preserve spatial information, enabling effective global context modeling, though the architecture typically requires larger datasets to generalize well.

ResNet50

ResNet5031 is a widely used deep convolutional network that introduces residual connections to ease optimization and mitigate vanishing gradient issues. The residual learning framework allows the network to learn incremental refinements over identity mappings, facilitating stable training of deep architectures. ResNet50 serves as a strong and reliable baseline for visual feature extraction.

DenseNet201

DenseNet20132 employs dense connectivity, where each layer receives feature maps from all preceding layers through concatenation. This design encourages feature reuse, strengthens gradient flow, and improves parameter efficiency. DenseNet201 is particularly effective in capturing fine-grained visual patterns, which are important for distinguishing subtle differences between flower growth stages.

MobileNetV3

MobileNetV333 is a lightweight CNN architecture designed for deployment on resource-constrained devices. It combines depthwise separable convolutions, inverted residual blocks, and squeeze-and-excitation modules to enhance channel-wise feature representation with minimal computational overhead. MobileNetV3 provides an efficient baseline for evaluating performance under strict efficiency constraints.

Graph convolutional network (GCN)

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs)34 extend the idea of convolution from grid-structured data (e.g., images) to non-Euclidean graph structures. A GCN learns node representations by aggregating information from its neighbors. In this work, the feature maps extracted from the CNN backbone are reshaped into a set of spatial nodes. A 4-neighbor grid graph is constructed, enabling the GCN to operate on local receptive-field relationships. Three stacked GCN layers progressively refine the node features, allowing the model to capture structural correlations that standard CNNs may ignore. Since flower growth characteristics often depend on relative structural patterns (petal arrangements, bud expansion, and texture continuity), modeling spatial relations explicitly provides complementary information beyond pixel-wise convolutional features.

Proposed hybrid model (CNN or Transformer + GCN)

The principal contribution of this work is a hybrid deep learning architecture that combines visual feature extraction from CNN or Transformer backbones with relational reasoning using a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)34. This design jointly captures appearance-based information and spatial dependencies, which are important for fine-grained flower growth-stage classification.

The hybrid framework consists of five stages: (i) baseline feature extraction, (ii) graph construction, (iii) GCN-based relational reasoning, (iv) feature fusion, and (v) classification.

A graph-level representation is obtained by global average pooling over all node embeddings:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{g} = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \textbf{H}^{(3)}_i \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{H}^{(3)}_i \in \mathbb {R}^{d}$$\end{document} denotes the feature vector of the i-th node in the final GCN layer, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{g} \in \mathbb {R}^{d}$$\end{document} is the graph-level representation.

Global CNN feature aggregation

In parallel, the global CNN feature is computed as:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{c} = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \textbf{x}_i \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{c} \in \mathbb {R}^{C}$$\end{document} summarizes the overall visual representation of the input image.

Feature fusion

The CNN feature and graph feature are concatenated:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{z} = [\textbf{c} \,\Vert \, \textbf{g}] \in \mathbb {R}^{C+d} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{z}$$\end{document} combines both semantic and relational information.

Classification layer

The fused feature vector is passed to a linear classifier:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\textbf{y}} = \textrm{softmax}(\textbf{W}\textbf{z} + \textbf{b}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{W}\in \mathbb {R}^{K\times (C+d)}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{b}\in \mathbb {R}^{K}$$\end{document} are trainable parameters, and K is the number of output classes.

By integrating backbone features with graph-based relational reasoning, the proposed hybrid model captures both visual and structural characteristics of flower growth stages, improving discrimination between visually similar classes.

Explainable AI (XAI)

To interpret the decisions of the deep learning models in flower growth stage classification, Explainable AI (XAI) techniques were employed. Class Activation Mapping (CAM)-based methods help visualize the regions of an image that most influence the model’s predictions. In this study, Ablation-CAM was used to generate class-specific heatmaps. Unlike gradient-based methods such as Grad-CAM, Ablation-CAM evaluates the importance of feature map regions by selectively removing them and observing the effect on the prediction. This provides more reliable insights into the model’s decision-making process. For the hybrid GCN model, node-level analysis further highlights how spatial relationships between image patches contribute to classification. The results show that the model focuses on meaningful structures such as petals, buds, and leaves, improving trust and supporting the interpretability of the model for practical applications.

Evaluation metrics

To evaluate the performance of the proposed model, four widely used classification metrics are employed: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score. These metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of overall correctness, class-wise relevance, and prediction balance.

Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified samples among all samples:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Precision indicates the correctness of positive predictions by measuring how many predicted positive samples are truly positive:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Recall reflects the model’s ability to correctly identify all relevant positive samples:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}F1-score represents the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, providing a balanced evaluation metric:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {F1-score} = 2 \times \frac{\text {Precision} \times \text {Recall}}{\text {Precision} + \text {Recall}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here, TP, TN, FP, and FN denote true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively.

The proposed model was evaluated using the custom image dataset3, which was partitioned into training, validation, and testing subsets following an 80%/10%/10% instance-level split protocol. Different graph construction strategies (4-neighbour, 8-neighbour, and KNN with k=16) were evaluated as part of the ablation study. All experiments were conducted on a Kaggle environment equipped with an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU, which provided sufficient computational resources for efficient training and evaluation of the deep CNN or Transformer–GCN architecture.

Class distribution and imbalance handling

The dataset exhibits a moderate class imbalance across different growth stages. This imbalance arises naturally due to the non-uniform duration and occurrence frequency of each growth stage during data collection. To address this issue, several strategies were employed. First, data augmentation techniques, including rotation, flipping, zooming and brightness adjustment, were applied to increase the diversity and representation of underrepresented classes during model training only. Second, a balanced sampling strategy was used during training to ensure that each mini-batch contains a more uniform distribution of samples across classes. In addition, model performance was evaluated using Ablation-CAM, Confusion Matrix and class-wise metrics such as precision, recall, and F1-score, alongside overall accuracy. This ensures that the performance on minority classes is properly assessed and not overshadowed by majority classes. These measures help improve the robustness of the model and reduce bias toward dominant classes.

Discussion

This study addresses the underexplored problem of fine-grained flower growth-stage classification across multiple species using RGB images. Unlike prior works that focus on species identification, disease detection, or rely on hyperspectral and multi-omics data, the proposed approach enables practical and cost-effective stage recognition suitable for real-world agricultural applications. To support this task, we introduce the BD Flower Growth dataset of eight species across three developmental stages.

Methodologically, we propose a CNN/Transformer + GCN hybrid framework that enhances conventional appearance-based feature learning by modeling structural and relational dependencies among flower parts. By converting spatial features into graph nodes and applying multi-layer GCNs, the model captures subtle morphological transitions between growth stages.

Experiments with seven strong backbones demonstrate the robustness of the approach. CNN-based hybrids consistently outperform transformer-based variants, indicating the continued effectiveness of convolutional inductive biases for fine-grained biological datasets. Limitation and future work

The dataset was collected from a single location, specifically a balcony environment, under relatively controlled conditions. As a result, variations in background complexity, lighting conditions, weather, and plant diversity are limited compared to real-world agricultural or field environments. Despite the strong performance of the proposed CNN–GCN hybrid framework, several methodological limitations remain. The current graph construction is based on a fixed spatial grid derived from CNN feature maps, which preserves local structure but limits the model’s ability to capture long-range or semantically adaptive relationships between distant regions. In addition, the use of standard GCN layers introduces the risk of oversmoothing when deeper graph stacks are applied, which can reduce feature diversity and weaken class separability. The feature fusion strategy also relies on simple concatenation, which does not explicitly model interactions between convolutional and graph-based representations.

Future work will focus on extending the dataset by collecting images from multiple locations and environmental conditions, including outdoor field settings. Additionally, future work will aim to improve the flexibility and expressiveness of the graph learning pipeline. Adaptive or attention-based graph construction could allow the network to learn task-specific spatial relationships instead of relying on predefined neighborhoods. Incorporating advanced graph architectures, such as graph attention or residual graph connections, may help mitigate oversmoothing and enable deeper relational reasoning. More sophisticated fusion strategies, including gated or attention-based integration, could also be explored to better combine visual and structural features and further enhance fine-grained growth-stage classification performance.

The reported results are presented as mean ± standard deviation over two runs with different random seeds. Formal statistical significance testing was not performed because the limited number of repeated runs does not provide sufficient statistical power for reliable hypothesis testing. More extensive statistical validation with additional runs will be considered in future work.

Conclusion

This paper presents a hybrid CNN/Transformer–GCN framework for fine-grained flower growth-stage classification and introduces the BD Flower Growth dataset with 3,889 original images. The proposed method integrates deep feature extraction with graph-based refinement by constructing graphs from feature maps and applying Graph Convolutional Networks. A systematic ablation study evaluates the effects of GCN depth (3, 5 layers), node resolution (7×7, 14×14), and graph construction (4-neighbour, 8-neighbour, KNN with k=16). The results show that performance depends on graph design, where structured grid-based graphs provide more stable outcomes. The best performance of 97% is achieved by multiple hybrid configurations, including EfficientNetV2-S, DenseNet201, and Swin-based models. Cross-dataset evaluation on the Oxford 102 Flower dataset further demonstrates the generalization capability of the proposed framework. Overall, the study highlights the importance of graph configuration in improving fine-grained visual classification for plant phenotyping applications.
