Using an App based algorithm to determine axial rotation and pelvic tilt at the time of an AP radiograph: a sawbone study

Introduction

The correct implant position is one of the significant factors influencing the clinical outcomes and the survival rate of total hip arthroplasty (THA) [1]. Since Lewinnek et al. [2] established the term “safe zone” for the acetabular component (AC) position, numerous biomechanical studies showed the association between implant alignment and outcomes [1, 3–6]. The methods to determine the alignment of the AC are based on anatomical, operational, or radiological references [7]. In radiological analysis, the inclination of the AC is defined as the angle between the longitudinal axis of that component and the horizontal axis of the pelvis, usually the line tangent to the teardrop on an AP X-ray [2, 7, 8]. Anteversion of the AC is defined by the angle between the longitudinal axis of the AC and the coronal plane. Radiological measurements of anteversion on anteroposterior [9] radiographs based on the projection of the AC opening ellipse have been presented in numerous reports [2, 9–11]. Plain AP pelvic radiographs are the standard imaging method to assess implant position after THA [12]. The quality of the pelvic radiographs, however, depends on various factors such as X-ray beam position and patient position, which also affects the accurate measurement of the AC position [13]. Currently pelvic axial rotation and pelvic tilt are often neglected, although potential pelvic rotation on postoperative radiographs has been quantified at an average 2.5° [14]. Axial rotation and pelvic tilt impact inclination and anteversion measurements of the AC on plain radiographs [15]. Given the variations of the pelvis position during surgery this also has implications for intraoperative AC position measurements using fluoroscopy.

The current study applies a novel app-based patented (US20240245468A1) algorithm to determine pelvic axial rotation and tilt using a radiodense sawbone.

Materials and methods

Sawbone pelvic model

To determine the absolute accuracy of the algorithm and minimize the error from identifying the anatomic reference points on the AP Pelvis, 7 anatomic reference points (bilateral lower Sacroiliac joints, bilateral teardrops, bilateral ischial tuberosities and the center of the sacrum) on the pelvis were marked with a 1 mm metal marker ball (Fig. 1). A radiolucent cardboard was attached to the backside of the sawbone so that the model could be positioned in a stable and reproducible supine position on an X-ray table and to facilitate rotating and tilting of the sawbone.

Radiographs

AP and lateral radiographs of the sawbone model were taken to calibrate the App. The central beam for AP radiographs was centered at the symphysis.

The sawbone was rotated 11 times to the left (average 5.3° range 0.2°–10.5°) and 10 times to the right (average 5.2° range 1.2°–11.3°). Afterwards the sawbone was tilted forward 14 times (average 5.6° range 0.1°–11.6°) and 13 times backward (average 5.9°, range 0.1°–11.2°). The amount of axial rotation (Fig. 2a) and pelvic tilt (Fig. 2b) (Fig. 3) was determined using a digital caliper. AP pelvis radiographs were taken for each position. Using an App (AccuHip®, Accupredict® Inc, Larchmont, NY) based calculation algorithm, the amount of axial rotation and pelvic tilt was determined by taking a screen shot of the radiograph using an iPad (Fig. 4). The image was calibrated using a 25 mm marker ball and the respective anatomic reference points were marked. The difference between digital caliper measurement and the App determined axial rotation and pelvic tilt of the sawbone was recorded as was the difference between successive measurements.

Results

The algorithm determined the axial rotation of the pelvis at the time of the AP pelvis radiograph with an average difference of 0.6° (range 0°–1.8°) compared to the digital caliper measurement. The difference between app and digital caliper measurements for change of position was 0.5° (range 0°–1.7°).

For measuring pelvic tilt measurements, the difference between App and digital caliper was 2.3° (range 0.4°–4.5°). For forward tilt the average difference was 1.9° (range 0.4°–4.5°) and for roll back (backward tilt) the average difference was 2.6° (range 1.2°–4.3°).

Discussion

The difference between the App-based measurements of axial rotation and pelvic tilt of a radioopaque sawbone were 0.6° and 2.3°, respectively.

Inclination and anteversion angles define the alignment of the AC after THA [16]. Historically the goal of AC positioning has been the “safe zone” [1, 2, 5, 17, 18] with an inclination between 30°–50° and anteversion between 5°–25°. THA dislocation, however, are multifactorial and can occurs even with cups positioned within the desired “safe zone” [19–21]. More recently adjustment of acetabular anteversion and inclination based on pelvic tilt in a standing position has been introduced. Beside these adjustments to tilt, axial rotation also influences anteversion and tilt measurement. Given that even experienced orthopedic surgeons cannot reproducibly position the acetabular component based on anatomic reference points alone, intraoperative fluoroscopy is currently widely used to confirm intraoperative AC position [22–24]. The accuracy of intraoperative measurements is usually referenced compared to standard AP pelvic radiographs. Several studies showed that the alterations in the pelvic tilt influence functional acetabular anteversion [25–28]. Also, changes in the pelvic axial rotation have an impact on the AC alignment. Depending on the direction of rotation, axial pelvic rotation increases anteversion of the AC by 1° for every degree of axial rotation towards the side of the surgery, and vice versa [7, 29]. Thoren et al. report an increased inclination of 1.8°–2.7° for every 5° of pelvic rotation [30]. Schwarz et al. report for each 1° of pelvic axial rotation a change in acetabular anteversion of 0.8° [15]. In addition, anteversion increases when the pelvis is tilted backwards and decreased when the pelvis is rolled forward [31]. Boettner et al. presented an app-based method for determining AC anteversion position. Comparing 100 anterior hip replacements, it was shown that this method is reliable for the measurement of acetabular component anteversion [24]. However, the authors cautioned that the measurements on two-dimensional radiographs do not take into account pelvic tilt and rotation, which both influence the AC [32].

The current study has the following limitations: anatomic reference points were marked using metal marker balls and using anatomic references points without marker balls might add a level of error to clinical measurements.

In summary, the current study tested the use of a patented app-based calculation algorithm to determine pelvic alignment at the time of an AP pelvic radiographs. The applied algorithm was highly accurate in determining pelvic axial rotation and tilt. Applying this technology to preoperative templating can reduce the error and allow for standardization of preoperative and intraoperative radiographs. In addition, the technique might ultimately be useful to eliminate repeat radiographs required for optimizing the pelvis position when using intraoperative fluoroscopy.
