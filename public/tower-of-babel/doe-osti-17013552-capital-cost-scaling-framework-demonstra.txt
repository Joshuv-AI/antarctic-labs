EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report documents the demonstration of an equation‑oriented capital cost scaling
framework implemented within the Institute for the Design of Advanced Energy Systems
(IDAES) modeling platform [1] to support techno‑economic analyses, design, and optimization
of solvent‑based carbon dioxide (CO₂) capture systems. The framework is developed to be
consistent with the capital cost scaling approach described in the National Energy Technology
Laboratory (NETL) Quality Guidelines for Energy System Studies (QGESS) and leverages the
modular, equation‑oriented structure of IDAES to apply these principles within an integrated
process modeling environment.
In the QGESS methodology, the bare erected cost of a solvent‑based CO₂ capture system is
scaled at an aggregated system level using key process flow parameters, after which the total
plant cost is calculated through the application of engineering fees and contingencies. In this
report, the same underlying cost scaling relationships are applied at a level of detail consistent
with the availability of equipment‑specific cost data from vendor information and reference
studies, with scaled equipment costs aggregated to determine the total plant cost. This
implementation is intended to support same‑basis technology comparisons and the estimation
of levelized plant costs for high‑level assessments.
Results generated using this framework are not intended to replace more detailed cost
estimates prepared in accordance with the Association for the Advancement of Cost
Engineering International (AACE) Class 3 guideline requirements, but are appropriate for
preliminary screening, process evaluation, and comparative studies where greater cost
uncertainty is inherent and acceptable.
This work focuses on three reference studies funded by the Department of Energy (DOE). These
studies provide sufficient vendor data to demonstrate and implement the proposed approach.
They were developed to support DOE’s project cost estimation for absorption-based carbon
capture technologies using monoethanolamine (MEA) as solvent:
 1. The Black & Veatch (B&V) study [2] is an engineering, procurement, and construction
 (EPC) quote conducted to study carbon capture from flue gases generated by a
 supercritical pulverized coal (SCPC) plant.
 2. The Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) study [3] is a carbon capture study for flue
 gases from natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) plants.
 3. Bechtel’s study [4] focuses on carbon capture from NGCC flue gases.
The type and size of the power plant directly influence the flue gas throughput and net power
produced across the three reference studies. Exhibit ES-1 shows the main differences across the
three reference studies used for this work.

Numerous differences exist in the design bases of the three reference studies, and this report
identifies those differences specifically for their major equipment. The flue gas CO2
concentration in the B&V study is higher than those in the other studies, since SCPC flue gas is
significantly more CO2-heavy than NGCC flue gas. Additionally, the three studies consider
slightly different gross power outputs, and the Bechtel plant targets a slightly lower CO2
recovery percentage.
The capital cost scaling methodology in this work is applied to a case study of an NGCC plant
integrated with a CCS system using aqueous MEA. This case study is implemented within the
IDAES costing framework and is based on the capital costs of the equipment from each of the
reference studies to illustrate the differences in the cost metric calculations. The IDAES costing
framework enables the implementation of a simple workflow for constructing costing equations
from a database that contains the reference parameters, bare erected costs, scaling exponents,
and contingencies of the equipment from all three reference studies.
To ensure consistency among the reference studies when estimating the cost of the system
under consideration, the capital cost scaling based on these reference studies is categorized into
three cases based on scaling data availability of the equipment:
 • Category 1: Equipment for which reference costs and cost scaling exponents are
 provided or can be estimated from the study.
 • Category 2: Equipment in Category 1 with the addition of equipment with cost scaling
 exponent based on assumptions.
 • Category 3: Equipment in Category 1 or Category 2, with the addition of equipment with
 no data available for reference costs and cost scaling exponents.
Additionally, the engineering fee and contingency fractions assumed in all the cases are
consistent with the NETL Fossil Energy Baseline [5]. The scaled CCS capital cost, estimated based
on the chosen reference study and category, is incorporated into the calculation of key
performance indicators, such as Total Plant Cost, Levelized Cost of Electricity, and Cost of CO2
Captured.

 2

The cost results of the NGCC-CCS plant in the case study are presented for each category across
a wide range of CO2 capture rates (90% - 99.5%). Applying this granular cost-scaling framework
to an NGCC case study reveals critical insights regarding cost sensitivities, database
completeness, and high-capture limits:
 • Cost Sensitivity to Reference Study Choice: At a 90% capture rate for an NGCC plant,
 cost is highly sensitive to the chosen reference study and equipment data, resulting in an
 estimated cost of capture range from $62/tonne CO2 to $87/tonne CO2 based on the
 choice of reference study. This wide range highlights that the design and scope
 assumptions of a chosen reference study heavily dictate cost projections.
 • Estimation of Missing Equipment Data: Scaling capital costs using only the equipment
 directly detailed in a single reference study (Category 1) may underestimate the total
 plant cost. In the EPRI case, integrating missing auxiliary equipment while scaling
 (Category 3) increases the estimated cost of captured CO2 by approximately 63% on
 average. This finding demonstrates that a single reference study is rarely sufficient to
 scale a complete plant envelope. Hence, the framework's multi-reference "blended"
 database approach can be used to estimate the total plant cost.
 • Exponential Cost Escalation Beyond 98% Capture: While the captured CO2 costs follow a
 relatively linear trend between the 90% and 95% capture rates, they exhibit an
 exponential increase past 98% capture. This steep nonlinear cost curve is driven by
 underlying thermodynamic limitations, which require exponentially larger absorber and
 stripper column volumes, as well as a significantly greater auxiliary thermal energy
 penalty to regenerate the solvent at ultra-low lean loadings.
 Ultimately, this demonstration validates that while absolute capital cost estimates remain
 highly sensitive to reference study selection, adopting a granular scaling approach equips
 researchers and developers with the robust modeling tools necessary to navigate complex
 design trade-offs and reliably optimize high-efficiency carbon capture systems.

1 INTRODUCTION
The United States (U.S.) Department of Energy’s (DOE) Carbon Capture Simulation for Industry
Impact (CCSI2) [6] is a research consortium of national laboratories, academic institutions, and
industry organizations focused on the development and application of carbon capture
technologies. The consortium leverages computational tools and models to accelerate
commercial deployment through comprehensive techno-economic analysis (TEA), uncertainty
quantification, and technical risk reduction associated with their scale-up. To this aim, CCSI2 and
its predecessor program, the Carbon Capture Simulation Initiative (CCSI) [7], have been leaders
in developing and applying tools and methodologies for TEA, performing process modeling [8,
9], and supporting pilot studies using in-house tools like sequential design of experiments
(SDoE) [10, 11] to maximize learning from experiments and reduce risk during technology
development and deployment.
Technical risk assessment and technology comparison of carbon capture and storage (CCS)
systems require reliable models for assessing capital and operating costs. Accordingly, CCSI2 has
developed a framework for TEA and optimization of fossil energy plants [12] with integrated
carbon dioxide (CO2) capture systems in its flagship software platform, the Framework for
Optimization, Quantification of Uncertainty, and Surrogates (FOQUS) [13]. While the current
work focuses primarily on solvent-based CCS systems, this framework is adaptable to other
classes of carbon capture systems such as sorbents and membranes, provided that process
models are available. The CCSI2 economic model implements the methodology described in the
Cost and Performance Baseline for Fossil Energy Plants [5] published by the National Energy
Technology Laboratory (NETL), the NETL Quality Guidelines for Energy System Studies (QGESS)
Capital Cost Scaling Methodology [14], and the Cost Estimation Methodology for NETL
Assessments of Power Plant Performance [15] to estimate capital and operating costs. Cost
estimates are applicable to natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) and supercritical pulverized coal
(SCPC) power plants with or without post-combustion CO2 capture. A major limitation of the
previous studies and methodologies is a lack of granularity in scaling the capital cost of the CO2
capture system. The NETL Fossil Energy Baseline uses the proprietary Shell CANSOLV technology
for the CO2 capture unit and provides only aggregate cost and performance information without
any details for individual equipment. Due to this, the capital cost of the capture system can only
be implemented at an overall process level and not for individual equipment. Furthermore,
cases with different solvent-based capture technologies with differing site conditions would
benefit from targeted optimization for their specific site considerations. To estimate the benefit
of different technologies and/or process configurations, detailed, equipment-level design and
cost breakdown data are needed for rigorous process modeling, to capture trade-offs between
performance and cost optimization. For example, in solvent-based carbon capture processes,
the CO2 loading in the rich solvent exiting the absorber column is coupled with the height of the
absorber column; a taller column will achieve a greater CO2 loading in the rich solvent but at an
increased capital cost of the column. The Fossil Energy Baseline does not provide the data
required for such trade-off analysis.
The costing library and scaling framework for solvent-based carbon capture systems
implemented in this report overcome the limitation of lack of specific and detailed equipment

 4

information. This report includes multiple approaches for estimating capital costs of individual
process equipment that can be selected based on the availability of existing cost data. Three
reference studies for developing cost correlations to implement this framework have been
chosen and are detailed in this report. The report applies the framework to the three chosen
reference studies and includes recommendations for selection of a reference source for cost
estimation based on the specific system of interest. A case study of a NGCC plant integrated
with a CCS system using aqueous monoethanolamine (MEA) is presented, showing the cost
estimation based on the three reference studies from a baseline of 90% with increments up to
99.5% CO2 capture. A quantitative results comparison includes high-level insights into the
reasons for cost estimate discrepancies based on engineering judgement. This report does not
intend to justify the differences in cost among the reference studies, but rather to report
observations and inferences based on quantitative analysis. This framework should be used for
high-level analysis, with the understanding that scaling costs at this level of granularity lead to
increased uncertainty in system costs.

2 CAPITAL COST SCALING FRAMEWORK
NETL regularly conducts TEAs of various energy-related technologies to characterize their
performance attributes and expected capital and operating costs. The objective of these TEAs is
to provide a common basis on which to compare technologies such that site-specific factors,
e.g., location, weather conditions, labor rates, material costs, and many others, do not impact
the core comparison of technology performance and cost. To accomplish this, NETL maintains
baseline studies [16, 17, 18, 19, 20] that provide a common and consistent design basis upon
which the analysis is performed.
Baseline performance cases provide common bases to compare new and developing
technologies, particularly for scaling sub-systems to accurately predict preliminary cost
estimates. Therefore, it is important that NETL generates public guidance documents associated
with different sets of cost estimates that delineate how specific costs should be scaled. In
addition to the previously referenced baseline studies, NETL maintains documentation to guide
various aspects of conducting a TEA, such as process modeling parameters, feedstock
specification and pricing, and methodologies for developing a capital cost estimate and for
scaling capital costs. The NETL QGESS Revision 4 report [14] provides guidance for scaling capital
costs reported in NETL project analyses. TEAs conducted and published by NETL are based on a
variety of data sources, including recently obtained and legacy vendor quotes; engineering,
procurement, and construction (EPC) cost estimation methodologies; cost scaling; and public
cost references.

The selected process parameter(s) should be representative of the perturbation that occurs
during system scaling. For example, when scaling a carbon capture plant up in size, volumetric
flow rate is an appropriate process parameter for scaling the blower. For scaling an absorber
column and altering the pressure drop, blower auxiliary load is a more appropriate process
parameter to use. This general approach for scaling capital costs may also be applied at an
individual equipment level; for example, scaling the absorber cost with vessel volume or scaling
the lean-rich solvent heat exchanger cost with heat transfer surface area. Scaling parameter
selection is a matter of engineering judgement and should involve careful consideration of how
best to use the fundamental physics of equipment capacity to estimate cost scaling.
The cost-scaling exponents account for economies of scale, which reflect that, as equipment size
increases, cost per unit capacity decreases. They often range from 0.48 to 0.87 for individual
equipment and from 0.38 to 0.9 for plants [21]. Based on the availability of cost data from
vendor or other references, there are two applicable methods to estimate the scaling exponents
for individual equipment cost accounts, as described in Approaches 1 and 2 below. Alternative
methods are available when multiple scaling parameters are used involving weighted
exponential terms or incorporation of estimated data points; these are beyond the scope of the
current work and are not discussed here.

Here, subscripts 1 and 2 represent unique data points used to calculate exponent values. For

between two cost points, and the resulting exponents are examined. If the exponents are
closely grouped, the average of all exponents may be taken. When the exponents deviate

heat integration and elevated operating pressure, the scope of supply differences may explain
variations in the exponents calculated. Developing a representative exponent in this case
becomes a matter of engineering judgement.

Note that the scaling exponent 𝐸𝑋𝑃 in general is nonnegative since equipment will cost more at

Approach 2
If just one cost data point is available from a cost reference, then the calculation of the scaling
exponents follows an alternative path. A reasonable cost-scaling exponent value is selected for
the equipment based on prior studies or typical values based on rules of thumb. Often the six-
tenths scaling rule is applied for equipment that scales based on a capacity variable such as
volume or throughput [21]. The choice of exponent is often a matter of engineering judgement.
As an example, the absorber or stripper column packing cost can be considered to scale linearly
with packing volume (a known cost per volume) or can be assumed to scale according to the six-
tenths scaling rule (resulting in a 52% increase in cost for a 100% increase in volume). More
information on selecting reasonable exponents based on engineering judgement is presented in
Section 3.2.

3 REFERENCE STUDIES
This work leverages data from three reference studies (Bechtel, EPRI, and B&V); these reference
studies provided capital cost data for major equipment of the solvent-based carbon capture
system. The Bechtel study [4] focuses on retrofitting an existing NGCC plant with an MEA
solvent-based carbon capture system and CO2 compression unit. The EPRI study [3] is an
academic analysis focused on a TEA of achieving zero and-net negative CO2 emissions in natural
gas power plants using an MEA solvent-based capture system. The B&V study [2] is a 2021 NETL
assessment on an MEA-based CO2 capture system completed in collaboration with B&V; this
study focuses on flue gases from coal-fired power plants.
The goal is to use the cost correlations described in Section 2, expand the costing library using
the data from the reference studies, and propagate the cost correlations using an MEA
flowsheet. The CCSI MEA Model [8], originally developed for pilot plant simulation and
validation, was scaled-up to commercial scale for this study to enable rigorous cost estimation
to compare the three reference studies.

Each reference study considers an MEA solvent-based carbon capture system connected to a
point source of carbon emissions that is either an NGCC or SCPC power plant. The source of
steam affects the steam quality available for use by the capture plant and could necessitate the
use of additional auxiliary equipment to provide steam. All three reference sources include low
pressure (LP) steam extraction from the power plant steam cycle.
Contrasting the three reference studies, plant location is a key factor affecting overall
equipment installation, plant construction, and capital costs. Variations in land cost, insurance
premiums, construction permits, long-distance and on-site equipment transport, and labor
rates for construction workers across geographic locations directly impact the cost to build and
operate the plant [22]. Climatic and soil conditions, foundation and support structure
requirements, required insulation, and available heating and cooling utilities present challenges
in reducing operating and installation costs. These factors introduce considerable uncertainty,

 10

and the reference studies do not provide sufficient data for a detailed uncertainty analysis. For
this work, economic conditions such as labor, land, and installation costs are either assumed to
be the same across scenarios or are calculated from capital costs to maintain a comparable
basis. Additionally, the three studies implement varying flue gas feed rates and CO2
concentrations, CO2 capture rates, and MEA loading. Calculating a levelized cost per unit of CO2
captured normalizes for these variations.

3.2 CAPITAL COST SCALING BASED ON REFERENCE STUDIES
The carbon capture system equipment data required for capital cost scaling (reference
parameter – RP, reference cost – RC, exponential – EXP) are compiled from all the reference
studies in a database that is continuously being updated and maintained as more information is
collected. The reference studies under consideration provide complete data for the design and
cost of major carbon capture equipment. Some of them provide data for an extensive list of
equipment. Furthermore, the capital cost scaling exponents used for individual equipment
based on different reference studies depend on the data available for their estimation. In case
data is missing for any equipment, assumptions are required for capital cost scaling.

When considering this, the equipment in each reference study is categorized as follows, based
on the data available for capital cost scaling:
Category 1. – Equipment for which reference costs and cost scaling exponents are provided or
can be estimated from the study.
Category 2. – Equipment in Category 1 with the addition of Equipment with cost scaling
exponent based on assumptions.
Category 3. – Equipment in Category 1 or Category 2, with the addition of Equipment with no
data available for reference costs and cost scaling exponents.
Details on the categories and categorization of major equipment in the EPRI, B&V and Bechtel
studies, relevant to the simple configuration of a solvent-based CO2 capture system are
provided in Exhibits Exhibit A-1, Exhibit A-2 and Exhibit A-3, respectively, in the Appendix. A
detailed classification of the source of the information needed for cost estimation can be found
in Exhibit 6-3.

 3.2.1 EPRI TEA Study
Data Availability:
BEC is available for many pieces of equipment in the carbon capture and compression system
such as the flue gas blower, absorber, direct contact cooler, stripper, condenser, and reboiler,
along with other intermediate pumps, coolers, CO2 compression system, and other
miscellaneous equipment. The exponents for the main CCS equipment are estimated from data
regression using cost data points provided for a range of CO2 capture rates (90–99.8%). This
aligns with the cost curve-fitting approach outlined in Approach 1 in Section 2 of this report.

 23

Cost Scaling – Key Assumptions:
 • The six-tenths rule is used for scaling the BEC of the flue gas blower (with respect to the
 blower power), Direct Contact Cooler (DCC) column and packing (with respect to the flue
 gas flowrate entering the CCS).
 • The heat transfer surface area of the lean-rich heat exchanger (used as the RP) at
 capture rates above 90% CO2 capture rate in the EPRI study is assumed to scale linearly
 with the rich solvent flowrate at 90% CO2 capture rate. The scaling exponent is estimated
 accordingly.
 • The stripper reboiler heat transfer surface area (used as the RP) at capture rates above
 90% CO2 capture rate in the EPRI study is assumed to scale linearly with the reboiler
 duty at 90% CO2 capture rate. The scaling exponent is estimated accordingly.
 • The absorber BEC is split among the vessel, packing, intercoolers, pumps, and wash
 water cooler and pump (RC), and the RC values are used accordingly at different capture
 rates. The stripper BEC split between the vessel and packing (RC) is based on the
 reference [24].
 • A fixed aggregated cost value for the BEC contribution of tanks, solvent drum,
 allowances for connections, and other miscellaneous cost components is considered at
 different capture rates. This can be considered a reasonable assumption if the CCS in the
 EPRI study aligns closely with the CCS under consideration for which cost needs to be
 scaled, and the same methodology for adding the aggregated cost to the total BEC of the
 CCS is used, as in the EPRI study.
The BEC includes all labor, shipping, and direct and indirect installation costs (including
structural supports). When individually scaling equipment and examining the impacts of
individual components, the plant layout may need to change, which will impact the support
structure and material costs disproportionately. This impact is not addressed through this
framework.

4 GUIDELINES FOR SELECTING AN APPROPRIATE COST REFERENCE
When selecting a reference source for the QGESS cost scaling methodology, it is important to
understand the design basis upon which that reference cost estimate was built. Reference
studies apply various assumptions regarding material costs, labor costs, shipping costs, scope of
design, and other factors that can affect whether a scaled estimate is a reasonable
approximation of the system to be costed. Another factor is the AACE classification of the
reference estimate. AACE provides guidelines for classifying cost estimates based on their level
of accuracy, which aligns with the level of engineering completed to develop the estimate. If
multiple cost references reflecting varying levels of AACE estimates are used, determining the
resulting total estimate AACE classification can be difficult. Scaling reference costs increases the
uncertainty range beyond the AACE Class, and the level of granularity with which the cost is
scaled can further increase uncertainty.
Equipment design and type strongly influence how well a given cost reference will approximate
the system of interest. For example, a shell-and-tube heat exchanger would be a poor reference
to estimate the cost of a plate-and-frame heat exchanger due to variations in material usage
and efficiency. A shell-and-tube heat exchanger would be a better reference to estimate the
cost of a multi-stream exchanger, provided that the size is adjusted appropriately to handle
stream heat duties. This problem is particularly difficult for specialized equipment, such as an
absorber column, where an appropriate analog quote may not exist or a limited amount of cost
data is available. Typical solvent-based absorption columns will have multiple stages of packing,
each with liquid distributors, tie-down plates, packing support plates, and other column
internals for interstage cooling and reinjection. Process contingency increases and accuracy
decreases when applying existing technology quotes to a new technology such as the University
of Kentucky’s fog and froth absorber [25], a developmental technology that includes novel
equipment operations that are likely to have a cost much different than well-studied absorber
technologies. In this case, the best approximation of the developmental technology short of
detailed design may be to scale from an analogous reference for a more traditional solvent
absorber column, while recognizing that the deviation in design adds to the contingency on the
final cost estimate.
Accounting for key sources of uncertainty is critical to cost scaling estimation and analysis. There
will inevitably be differences between current and developing technologies, and explanation of
the differences between the reference cost and scaled cost result will aid in understanding,
interpreting, and applying the results to process scale-up.

5 IDAES IMPLEMENTATION OF COST SCALING
The Institute for the Design of Advanced Energy Systems (IDAES) Integrated Platform provides a
numerical implementation to apply the QGESS methodology. After selecting an appropriate
reference source with the required cost data and gathering sufficient process data, the IDAES
Integrated Platform provides a next-generation, multi-scale modeling and optimization
framework supporting simultaneous process and cost modeling [1]. IDAES contains detailed
costing frameworks and libraries for both typical process equipment utilizing literature
correlations and power plant equipment utilizing the QGESS methodology. The IDAES Power
Plant Costing Framework contains pre-built costing dictionaries based on the NETL Bituminous
Baseline report for main power plant accounts [5]. Additionally, there are pre-built costing
dictionaries for CCS from flue gas with 4% CO2 and for components of an MEA solvent-based
CCS process [24, 25]. The sourced data on MEA from the EPRI study covers 90–97.7% CO2
capture rate, of which a subset covering 90–97% CO2 capture rate is relevant for NGCC case
comparison in the current report.
A simple workflow enables the construction of costing equations from custom parameter
dictionaries and allows targeted replacement of default entries with updated accounts. It is
recommended to serialize the account data in the reference database to JavaScript Object
Notation (JSON) format for quick loading in an IDAES flowsheet. The data may be imported into
Python from Excel via the Pandas package or may be manually written and then exported to
JSON using the json package to “dump” the dictionary into a targeted file. Each account entry in
the dictionary must contain five levels of keys:
 • Level 1: a unique technology type that is an integer 1–7 for seven available technologies.

 • Level 3: the name of a specific account.
 • Level 4: the name of a specific property.
 • Level 5: value for a specific property.

6.1 DESCRIPTION
The capital cost scaling framework can be applied to calculate the TPC of solvent-based carbon

also included. Since the direct contact cooler, absorber intercooler, and intercooler pumps are
not present in the Bechtel carbon capture system, the TPC for all the equipment in the system is
assumed to scale as a fixed fraction of the absorber TPC (0.58, 0.03 and 0.07 respectively) based
on the fractional relation between the TPC of the equipment considered in the B&V study.
Upon observing Exhibit 6-6 to Exhibit 6-8, the sequence in which the TPC values of the MEA CO2
capture system is calculated based on different reference studies appear, and the sequence of
the corresponding LCOE and CO2 capture cost values, is the same across all CO2 capture rates.
However, when the CANSOLV CO2 capture system costs are also considered in the sequence,
ranging from 90 to 97 percent CO2 capture rate, the LCOE sequence switches between CANSOLV
and B&V-category3, compared to the removal system TPC sequence. The CANSOLV removal
system TPC values across the capture rates are higher than the B&V-category3 case. This can be
attributed to i) fundamental differences in the CO2 capture equipment—material of
construction, type and size, ii) base year of capital cost estimation, iii) the BEC scaling
components in B&V-category3 case—reference costs, scaling parameters, and choice of
exponents. The difference in the removal system TPC values between CANSOLV and B&V-
category3 case gets propagated through the total overnight capital (TOC), total as spent capital
(TASC), leading up to the annualized capital cost. Further, the specific reboiler duty for solvent
regeneration that determines the steam requirement, and the auxiliary power requirement of
the MEA CO2 capture system, is higher than CANSOLV.
The low-pressure steam requirement for the reboiler is 2.4 MJ/kg (1,050 Btu/lb) CO2 for the
CANSOLV process (Section 5.1.5, Rev 4a of the NETL Fossil Energy Baseline), whereas it is 3.96
MJ/kg CO2 for the MEA process at 90% CO2 capture rate. The auxiliary load of the capture
system is 13.6 MW for CANSOLV (Exhibit 5-24, Rev 4a of the NETL Fossil Energy Baseline) and
18.1 MW for MEA at 90% CO2 capture rate. Since the source of steam is the NGCC steam cycle,
the steam requirement along with the overall auxiliary load of the capture system results in a
lower net power of the NGCC plant in the B&V-category3 case compared to CANSOLV. Among
the LCOE cost components, fuel cost has the maximum contribution, followed by capital cost.
Hence, any differences in these cost contributions between the CANSOLV and B&V-category3

 42

cases would have the maximum impact on the LCOE. The combined effect of annualized capital
cost and NGCC net power output results in the capital cost contribution to the LCOE being
almost the same in the B&V-category3 case compared to CANSOLV. The fixed and variable O&M
(non-fuel) cost contributions are a function of the TPC and the NGCC net power output, along
with solvent cost and performance, and since the effect of net power supersedes the other
factors, these contributions are higher in the B&V-category3 case compared to CANSOLV. The
fuel and CO2 transport and storage cost contributions to LCOE do not depend on TPC. Hence, at
a given fuel supply, and a fixed capture rate, they will only vary as a function of the NGCC net
power. Due to this, their cost contribution is higher in the B&V-category3 case compared to
CANSOLV. This effectively results in the LCOE being higher in B&V-category3 case compared to
CANSOLV, and thus explains the switch in the sequence, compared to removal system TPC.

7 CONCLUSIONS
This report presents a framework to leverage existing capital cost estimates, vendor quotes, and
public data to predict capital expenditures, fixed & variable operating and maintenance costs,
and project and process contingencies. The framework leverages NETL’s QGESS methodology,
integrating established scaling correlations and critical cost assumptions—including equipment
breakdown structures, process and project contingencies, land and labor costs, taxes, and
annualization factors. The framework is demonstrated through a case study of an integrated
NGCC power plant with carbon capture, where the approach generates scaled cost estimates
based on multiple reference studies (Bechtel FEED for NGCC, EPRI TEA for NGCC, and B&V study
for SCPC) and compares cost and performance metrics (e.g., LCOE, CO2 capture costs) across
varying rates of CO₂ capture. This framework is intended to support conceptual process design
studies and techno-economic analyses, and to enable consistent cost estimation when evaluating
alternative configurations and operating conditions.
The report provides a detailed comparison of equipment cost (bare and erected and total plant
cost) of major carbon capture unit operations, identifying data gaps and numerous
discrepancies between the references. The comparison results show the complexity of using a
single reference study, the need for assumptions to propagate the cost of certain equipment,
and how using a single reference study can result in underestimating the capital cost of the
plant. Thus, the proposed framework suggests general guidelines to enable apples-to-apples
comparison when considering granular cost for the entire flowsheet/system and can be used to
evaluate different technologies and/or process configurations. Note that while this framework
can be used to provide high-level cost estimates, it can lead to increased uncertainty, and it
should not be used as a replacement for a detailed FEED study.
The case study consists of a 646 MW net power NGCC plant based on case B31B of the NETL
Fossil Energy Baseline, in which the CANSOLV system is replaced with an MEA solvent-based CO2
capture system. The post combustion capture plant is designed for multiple levels of CO2
capture rates (from 90 to 99.5%) to analyze the impact of the cost across different plant design
and operating conditions. The results show that selecting the appropriate costing reference is
important. For example, the estimated cost of capture for the NGCC plant with 90% CO2 capture
rate ranges from $62/tonne CO2 to $87/tonne CO2 based on the choice of reference study;
however, the trends in the key economic variables over the range of CO2 capture rates remain
consistent despite the choice of reference study. The results also show that LCOE cannot easily
reflect the large differences in TPC between cases. While LCOE represents a consistent basis to
evaluate different technologies, it is clear that more studies are required to identify different
metrics to analyze the effect of using different reference studies (user-cases) for scaling the
capital costs, and to evaluate the tradeoff between different process and system-level design
and operating conditions within and across used reference studies.
Finally, the report shows how to leverage publicly-available data for quantitatively assessing the
effect of the CO2 capture rate on system economics. The proposed approach can be highly
effective in supporting conceptual process design and techno-economic studies of CCS systems,
especially when a cost comparison of competing technologies on a consistent basis is desired.
