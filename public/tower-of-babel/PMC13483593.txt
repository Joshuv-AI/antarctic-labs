Reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive delivery of chlorantraniliprole by a nanoscale metal-phenolic network for systemic control of rice striped stem borer
Authors: Dejin Xu, Zhenghao Zhou, Heng Qiao, Hongmei Shu, Zixin Liu, Jing Zhao, Liubin Xiao, Guangchun Xu, Guanghua Luo, Rui Ji, Hao Hong, Yongan Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRice striped stem borer (Chilo suppressalis), a major lepidopteran pest affecting rice ecosystems, poses a severe threat to global rice production and food security. The internal feeding behavior of larvae, which bore into stems, limits the efficacy of conventional insecticide sprays in reaching target sites and achieving lasting control. Developing a responsive nano-delivery system to enable precise, systemic insecticide release for sustained larvae control represents a highly promising strategy.ResultsHerein, a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive metal-phenolic network-based chlorantraniliprole (CAP) nano-delivery system (CAP@TF-PEG) was successfully constructed, inspired by plant-herbivore interaction signaling. CAP@TF-PEG exhibited improved foliar wetting, enhanced phloem-mediated systemic mobility within rice plants, and improved insecticidal persistence and field control efficacy compared with conventional formulations. Transcriptomic analysis indicated that this delivery strategy strengthened the inhibitory effect of CAP on insect cuticle synthesis. Importantly, CAP@TF-PEG showed excellent biosafety to zebrafish and cells. Notably, it maintained soil ecological stability without compromising microbial diversity, underscoring its significant potential for commercial application. It also promoted rice root growth, offering a multi-functional approach to crop protection.ConclusionsThe combination of targeted pest control and plant growth promotion offers a highly promising strategy for achieving efficient, sustainable, and ecologically compatible crop protection.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04666-x.

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa) is the world’s most essential food crop, feeding over half of the global population [1, 2]. The rice striped stem borer, Chilo suppressalis (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae), is one of the three major lepidopteran pests in rice ecosystems, which inflicts severe damage throughout all growth stages of rice and also attacks other crops such as water oat, wheat, and maize [3, 4]. As a holometabolic insect, C. suppressalis undergoes four life stages, including eggs, larvae, pupae and adults. The newly hatched larvae initially feed within the leaf sheaths, causing withering. After 10–12 days, the third-instar larvae migrate and bore into the stems, inflicting systemic damage. This damage manifests as distinct symptoms at different growth stages: dead hearts during tillering, dead panicles during booting, white heads during heading, and general plant damage at maturity [5]. This pest poses a major threat to rice production, causing an estimated annual economic loss of about $11.5 billion in China [6].

Currently, due to the lack of rice germplasm with high-level resistance to stem borers, the control of C. suppressalis still relies highly on chemical insecticides [7]. However, abusive use of these chemical insecticides has led to a series of ecological problems, including the well-known “3R” issue, including Residue, Resistance, and Resurgence, which contradicts the principles of sustainable pest management [8]. For instance, prolonged and intensive use of pesticides has led to significant resistance to field populations such as monosultap, bisultap, and triazophos, as well as bio-pesticides like abamectin [9]. Chlorantraniliprole (CAP), an anthranilic diamide insecticide, has been widely adopted for rice pest management in recent years owing to its high efficacy at low doses, long residual activity, and relatively good safety to mammals and most non-target organisms [10]. Its mode of action involves binding to the insect ryanodine receptor (RyR), and triggering uncontrolled release of calcium ions, which ultimately leads to feeding cessation, paralysis, and death [11, 12]. Currently, CAP formulations are mainly developed as suspension concentrates (SC), which exhibit poor physical stability and limited systemic absorption. Consequently, effective control often requires repeated high-concentration applications, aggravating environmental pollution and residual risks while restricting precise delivery and efficient utilization [13]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop more effective pesticide delivery systems that can reduce CAP dosage, exhibit good environmental compatibility, and assist precise cargo release.

The emergence of nanotechnology has alleviated many of the long-standing limitations of traditional pesticides, which is expected to promote sustainable agricultural practices [14]. Nanocarriers can increase the on-target deposition rate of agrochemicals, enhance the solubility of active ingredients, facilitate controlled release, enhance delivery efficiency, and optimize bioactivity, leading to improved plant absorption and increased toxicity against pest insects [15–18]. For instance, CAP-loaded PLA microspheres (CAP-PLA MS) demonstrates superior sustained pesticide release performance under both laboratory and field conditions than normal CAP [19]. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) can also significantly improve the insecticidal activity of CAP against Spodoptera frugiperda [20]. Therefore, employing nanotechnology for the delivery of CAP could offer a promising strategy to control C. suppressalis. During plant-herbivore interactions, reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a critical signaling role and can rapidly trigger plant defense responses [21]. Upon insect herbivory, plant pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) recognize herbivore-associated or damage-associated molecular patterns (HAMPs/DAMPs) [22, 23], thereby initiating a series of early signaling events. Multiple studies have confirmed the important role of ROS in plant-insect interactions. For example, researchers reported that infestation by the Russian wheat aphid (Diuraphis noxia) significantly enhanced the activity of NADPH oxidase and increased hydrogen peroxide levels in plants, indicating that NADPH oxidase is a key enzyme in herbivore-induced ROS production [24]. Furthermore, evidences showed that feeding by the rice striped stem borer (C. suppressalis) induced marked and localized accumulation of ROS at and around the damaged sites [25]. Inspired by this natural, pest-triggered ROS signaling mechanism, we propose an applicable strategy for precision pest management: a ROS-responsive nano-delivery system. The core of this strategy is to utilize the elevated concentration of ROS at feeding sites as an endogenous trigger to achieve on-demand and localized release of insecticides. Previous research has provided preliminary validation for the application of ROS-responsive carriers in agriculture. For instance, a ROS-responsive starch-based tebuconazole nano-delivery system is successfully constructed for controlling apple vascular disease [26]. ROS-responsive micelles have been shown to improve the controlled release and bioactivity of validamycin [27]. However, studies on intelligent pesticide release specifically triggered by insect feeding-induced ROS signals are still in their infancy. Therefore, this study aims to design and evaluate a ROS-responsive nano-delivery system, offering a new approach for efficient and low-residue pest control.

Metal-phenolic networks (MPNs), a key class of supramolecular materials derived from plant polyphenols, are characterized by their pronounced structural and functional diversity, showing great promise in chemical engineering, biomedicine, and agriculture [28–31]. For instance, MPNs can form nano-coatings with sodium alginate, which significantly improves the stress resistance and nitrogen fixation performance of nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the phyllosphere environment [32]. Unlike rigid inorganic matrices or non-responsive polymers, metal-phenolic networks (MPNs) offer distinct advantages for controlled delivery, primarily due to their dynamic coordination and noncovalent interactions, and these features allow MPNs to restrict drug crystallization and enable nano-sized, sustained drug release [28, 33, 34]. More importantly, the coordination bonds in MPNs can respond to specific environmental stimuli such as reactive oxygen species, functioning as a smart gating system that triggers drug release under defined conditions. For instance, EGCG-Fe MPNs functioned as ROS-responsive gates to regulate the release of doxorubicin (DOX), wherein oxidation of EGCG to its quinone form weakens Fe3+ coordination and loosens the supramolecular coating [35]. This stimuli-responsive nature allows the delivery system to remain stable under normal conditions but rapidly “unlock” the cargo(s) in response to the specific ROS signals generated by plant defense mechanisms during insect herbivory. Such a shift from passive sustained release to active, signal-triggered release represents a significant advancement in precision crop protection.

Herein, to simultaneously address the challenges of poor foliar deposition and lack of sustained efficacy associated with conventional pesticide formulations, a ROS-responsive nanocrystal system (CAP@TF-PEG) for the controlled delivery of CAP was constructed. The system was constructed through a stepwise assembly: a tannic acid-Fe(III) metal-phenolic network (TA-Fe³⁺ MPN) first confines CAP into nanoscale cores, followed by the co-assembly of PEG, BSA, TA, and Fe³⁺ to form an outer ROS-responsive shell. Under oxidative conditions, the phenolic-to-quinone conversion in the TA-Fe³⁺ network weakens metal coordination, leading to shell loosening and targeted release of CAP at damaged plant tissues upon foliar or root application. Then, this study systematically evaluated the key characteristics of the complex, as well as its ROS-triggered release and disintegration behavior. The translocation and accumulation patterns of CAP@TF-PEG within rice plants were thoroughly investigated using positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET-CT) and confocal microscopy. Furthermore, the insecticidal activity against C. suppressalis was assessed, supported by transcriptome analysis (RNA-seq) and RT-qPCR validation to elucidate the underlying differential toxicological mechanisms. Beyond insecticidal efficacy, we simultaneously evaluated the root-promoting effect of the nanocarrier (TF-PEG) and its comprehensive biosafety profile. Our results demonstrated that TF-PEG was an efficient and biocompatible carrier that significantly enhanced the residual activity and systemic mobility of chlorantraniliprole. This work not only presents a promising nano-formulation for pest control but also lays the groundwork for designing a new generation of plant health-oriented pesticide delivery systems.

Results and discussion

Construction and characterization of ROS-Responsive nano-delivery (CAP@TF-PEG)

In order to achieve precision management of the rice striped stem borer (Chilo suppressalis), a nano-delivery pesticide with ROS-cleavable sites was developed, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. Due to the low solubility of CAP in water, solvent exchange under ultrasound could induce Ostwald ripening and the formation of compact CAP crystals. Driven by hydrophobic interactions and π–π stacking, the hydrophilic TA-Fe³⁺ supramolecular network (MPN) binds to the crystal surface [36, 37]. The multiple polar groups on TA stabilize the CAP nuclei at the water interface, reduce surface free energy, and arrest further crystal growth. As shown in Fig. 1c, CAP formed a coarse suspension in the absence of interfacial confinement. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed that the CAP particles aggregated into irregular morphologies, and dynamic laser scattering (DLS) measurements indicated a large hydrodynamic diameter of 843.29 ± 12.99 nm with a broad size distribution (Fig. S1). Upon addition of TA and FeCl₃, the CAP crystals (CAP@TA-FeIII) were reduced to 154.99 ± 2.06 nm, while transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and SEM images revealed their morphology to be short rectangular crystals (Figs. 1d and S2). HPLC analysis showed that the CAP encapsulation rate was 25.03 ± 5.04% (Table S1). Furthermore, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was employed to determine the interaction between CAP and TA-FeIII, which was a high-precision method for measuring binding affinities [38, 39]. As shown in Figs. 1b and S3, ITC verified strong interactions between CAP and TA-FeIII, yielding a Kd of 32.60 µM and a negative ΔG (–6.13 kJ/mol), indicative of a spontaneous process. The negative ΔH and positive ΔS suggested that ionic interactions played an important role in the binding.

An ROS-responsive polymer gate was then assembled on CAP@TA-FeIII from tannic acid, FeCl₃, BSA and PEG-4000, which was driven by hydrophobic interactions, π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding [40, 41]. Under the stimulation of ROS, phenolic hydroxyls on TA were oxidized to quinone structures, weakening Fe³⁺ chelation and leading to dissociation of the supramolecular network, which bestowed the ROS responsiveness to the TA-FeIII shell [35]. After PEGylation, the crystal CAP@TA-FeIII was encapsulated to form spherical nanostructures named CAP@TF-PEG, and the particle size was 216.50 ± 20.93 nm (Figs. 1e and S4). CAP loss during assembly was only 1.75%, indicating that almost all CAP@TA-FeIII was retained in the ROS-responsive shell. The zeta potential of CAP crystals was measured at 24.06 ± 4.20 mV, while that of the MPN-coated crystals (CAP@TA-FeIII) increased significantly to 43.84 ± 7.43 mV. Because PEG is neutrally charged and bovine serum albumin (BSA, pI 4.7–4.9) is negatively charged in water [42, 43], the zeta potential of CAP@TF-PEG decreased to 0.73 ± 0.18 mV (Fig. S5). Additionally, energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) of CAP@TF-PEG revealed the existence of C, N, O, Cl and Fe, where Fe and Cl distribution pattern indicated the TA-FeIII and CAP, respectively (Fig. 1g). As shown in Fig. S6, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis revealed Fe mass fractions of 2.47% ± 0.02% for CAP@TA-FeIII and 1.29% ± 0.01% for CAP@TF-PEG, with corresponding Br mass fractions of 4.99% ± 0.20% and 1.21% ± 0.04%, respectively. As each CAP molecule contains one Br atom, the corresponding loading drug loading capacity (DLC) were 30.21% ± 1.22% (CAP@TA-FeIII) and 7.34% ± 0.22% (CAP@TF-PEG), indicating the success incorporation of PEG and BSA in CAP@TF-PEG.

As shown in Fig. 1f, the chemical structural changes of CAP, TA, CAP@TA-FeIII and CAP@TA-PEG were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. CAP exhibited distinct characteristic stretching vibration peaks of C-N at 1330 cm⁻¹ and N-H at 3263 cm⁻¹, and TA exhibited distinct characteristic stretching vibration peaks of C = O at 1617 cm⁻¹ and 1712 cm⁻¹. In the spectra of the CAP@TA-FeIII and CAP@TF-PEG, these characteristic peaks showed noticeable changes in intensity: the C = O peak shifted to a lower wavenumber, while the N-H peak broadened, indicating the formation of hydrogen bonding or coordination interactions between the carbonyl and amino of CAP and the Fe³⁺ in the TA-FeIII or the polar groups in the TF-PEG [44]. Moreover, all major structural characteristic peaks of CAP were preserved in the complexes, confirming that the loading process did not disrupt its chemical integrity. These results directly demonstrated the successful loading of CAP onto the carrier TA-FeIII materials and revealed significant physicochemical interactions between them. In the XPS spectra (Figs. 1h, i), C, N, O and Fe were detected in CAP@TA-FeIII and CAP@TF-PEG. The characteristic peak at approximately 531.21 and 531.77 eV in the O1s spectrum was attributed to oxygen atoms coordinated to Fe³⁺ (M-O-C), indicating that the phenolic oxygen of TA or the carbonyl oxygen of CAP participates in the formation of coordination bonds [45]. The Fe 2p spectrum further revealed that, compared with CAP@TA-Fe, the binding energy of the Fe 2p 3/2 peak in CAP@TF-PEG showed a significant negative shift of approximately 1.7 eV (Figs. 1h’,i’), which proved that the addition of PEG significantly increased the electron cloud density of Fe center and modulates its coordination environment. The XRD patterns of CAP, CAP@TA-FeIII and CAP@TF-PEG were presented in Fig. 1j. XRD confirmed that CAP was crystalline, and the diffraction peaks of CAP@TA- FeIII (2θ = 8.66º, 11.41º, 20.75º, 22.49º, 25.05º, and 30.32º) match those from pristine CAP, indicating its intact crystal structures. Compared with CAP crystals, CAP@TA-PEG showed broad, featureless scattering profiles without any sharp crystalline diffraction peaks, indicating the predominantly amorphous nature of the complex, which was likely due to the incorporation of PEG [46, 47]. These results confirmed that CAP was molecularly dispersed within the amorphous carriers, with no crystalline phase detected, which was consistent with the strong intermolecular interactions (e.g., coordination or hydrogen bonding) revealed by FT-IR analysis. Such characteristics were expected to enhance the dissolution and release profile of the loaded pesticide CAP. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) revealed a single DTG peak at 294 °C for CAP@TA-FeIII (Fig. S7) and two peaks at 273.75 °C and 349.26 °C for CAP@TF-PEG (Fig. 1k), with a higher residual mass for CAP@TF-PEG (25.36% vs. 9.39%), consistent with the thermal behavior of PEG [48]. The transition from a crystalline state to a predominantly amorphous profile was a critical factor in enhancing the delivery performance of CAP. This amorphization not only increased the apparent solubility of the hydrophobic pesticide, but together with the hydrophilic PEG/BSA shell, also facilitated rapid dissolution upon triggering. Consequently, this structural modification translated into improved systemic mobility and sustained bioavailability within the rice plant.

Assessment of foliar behavior of CAP@TF-PEG

Effective targeted deposition of pesticides is a crucial step in ensuring disease and pest control efficacy, which not only significantly enhances pesticide bioavailability but also effectively reduces environmental pollution and decreases pesticide consumption [26, 49]. The critical factors affecting deposition, wettability and adhesion are usually evaluated by contact angle and surface tension measurements [50]. Reduced contact angle enhances leaf surface wettability, leading to greater droplet spreading, stronger pesticide adhesion, and a more uniform distribution [51]. Thus, the wettability of the CAP@TF-PEG formulation on rice leaf surfaces was evaluated systematically (Fig. 2a-c). Specifically, Fig. 2a displayed the contact angles of different droplets. The contact angle of ddH2O ranged from 136.39° to 136.98°. In contrast, the CAP SC formulation exhibited a contact angle of 127.48°-127.69°, representing only a modest reduction of approximately 9° relative to ddH2O, and corresponding to limited improvements in both spreading and wettability. A markedly greater reduction was observed for the CAP@TF-PEG formulation supplemented with the spray adjuvant alkyl ethyl sulfonate, with contact angles falling significantly to a range of 107.58°-124.92°. Notably, its minimum value of 107.58° approaches the critical threshold for hydrophilicity, highlighting a substantially enhanced wetting capability compared to both pure water and the CAP SC formulation (Fig. 2b). In addition, the surface tensions of CAP SC and CAP@TF-PEG in the concentration range of 15.625–1000 mg/L were 28.407–47.413 mN/m and 35.597–52.140 mN/m, respectively (Fig. 2c). Collectively, these results indicated that the CAP@TF-PEG effectively overcame the hydrophobic barrier of the rice leaves and significantly enhanced the wettability and spreading performance of the pesticide solution.

Stimuli-responsive release performance of CAP@TF-PEG

It is well established that ROS serve as early signaling molecules in plant defense, activating responses against diverse pathogens and herbivorous insects [52]. Based on this theory, a ROS-responsive nano-delivery system can be beneficial to control the release rate and location of pesticides according to the fluctuation of ROS levels in plants. As shown in Fig. 2d, in vitro release experiments were conducted, during which different doses of H2O2 were added to trigger the release of CAP in CAP@TF-PEG. In pure water, cumulative CAP release from CAP@TF-PEG was 6.04% ± 0.15% (n = 3, 48 h) and 9.52% ± 0.07% (n = 3, 168 h), indicating limited passive leakage. In contrast, the release fraction increased with the elevated H₂O₂ concentration, reaching 22.45% ± 0.15% (n = 3, 48 h) and 34.87% ± 1.35% (n = 3, 168 h) at 100 mM H₂O₂, significantly higher than those from 10 to 50 mM H₂O₂ stimulation. Additionally, CAP@TF-PEG was stable at pH 7.4 (168 h release 4.09% ± 0.10%, n = 3) and showed only moderate enhancement at pH 5.0 (11.30% ± 0.01%, n = 3), suggesting negligible pH-triggered release (Fig. 2e). Notably, at pH 5.0, the 168-h cumulative release induced by 10 mM H₂O₂ (34.87% ± 0.42%) was comparable to that achieved by 100 mM H₂O₂ under neutral conditions, implying that mildly acidic conditions loosened the MPN network structure and enhanced ROS sensitivity. However, at a high H₂O₂ concentration (100 mM), Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ cycling may induce Fenton-like reactions, generating highly oxidizing •OH radicals that promote TA crosslinking and formation of a denser shell [53], thereby partially restricting CAP core exposure and release (Fig. 2f). Previous studies have found that the concentration of H₂O₂ in healthy rice plants ranges from 8.39 to 12.58 ng/g, and under biotic stress, it reaches only up to 21.64 ng/g [54]. Additionally, the concentration of H₂O₂ in rice chloroplasts under salt stress was merely 15 µM [55]. which aligned with our research findings, demonstrating that a high concentration (e.g., 100 mM) of H₂O₂ is not required to induce CAP release.

Furthermore, the morphological changes of CAP@TF-PEG nanoparticles were visualized via SEM. Because the ROS-responsive shell is formed via TA-Fe³⁺ coordination, ROS oxidizes the phenolic hydroxyl groups of TA to quinone structures [35], thereby weakening Fe³⁺ chelation, disrupting the coordination network, and exposing the underlying CAP crystals. SEM images showed that CAP@TF-PEG formed spherical nanoparticles in water (Figs. 2g and S8a), but the spherical nanoparticles collapsed and exposed the underlying CAP crystals at H2O2 treatments (Figs. 2h, I and S8b, c), further indicating high stability in water and ROS-triggered disassembly of the shell. Overall, this multiple stimulus approach simulated the CAP release pattern in response to ROS changes in the plant, demonstrating the ROS responsive pesticide release performance of the nano-delivery system.

Optimized plant uptake, transportation and enrichment of CAP@TF-PEG

Plant uptake capacity and targeted delivery are critical for evaluating nano-pesticide performance [56]. To assess the ROS-targeting behavior of CAP@TF-PEG in rice, fluorescent-labeled CAP@TF-PEG was topically applied to leaves (including upper leaves) from two types of plants (injured and healthy), and tissue distribution was examined by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) at 24 h post-treatment. As shown in Fig. 3a, fluorescence could be detected in various tissues from two types of plants. Most CAP@TF-PEG material accumulated in leaves, and preferential association with mesophyll and perivascular cells was observed, which confirmed limited loading into the vascular system. Specifically, the stem accumulation of CAP@TF-PEG in injured plants was equivalent to that of leaves, indicating that the transportation and enrichment of CAP@TF-PEG could be triggered by injuries in plants (Fig. 3b). Additionally, the fluorescence intensity of injured plant stems was significantly higher than that of healthy plant stems (Fig. 3b), which was due to the fact that ROS burst in the injured plant stem induced the responsive release of CAP@TF-PEG, and the released CAP was absorbed by the cortical parenchyma surrounding the vascular bundle. Root application experiments further quantified the transport and enrichment of CAP@TF-PEG in plants. As shown in Fig. 3c, CAP treatment showed higher overall translocation and uptake in injured rice than CAP@TF-PEG treatment. Injury reduced transpiration, decreasing CAP levels below the injury site (e.g., 1.73 µg/g in injured vs. 7.13 µg/g in healthy rice). Notably, elevated ROS at the injury site triggered CAP@TF-PEG disassembly and CAP release, resulting in similar concentrations at the injury site (1.84 µg/g in injured vs. 1.97 µg/g in healthy rice). Interestingly, this ROS-responsive release enabled continued upward transport, leading to higher CAP levels above the injury site (0.646 µg/g vs. 0.17 µg/g) and increased delivery to leaves (0.33 µg/g) in injured rice, comparable to sole CAP treatment. These data indicated that CAP@TF-PEG underwent ROS-responsive release at wound sites, enhancing pesticide deposition and partially compensating for impaired long-distance transportation of CAP in injured plants.

To further verify its targeted delivery, molecular imaging technology was used to optically track the transport of CAP@TF-PEG in plants. Molecular imaging visualizes molecular and cellular interactions in vivo, providing real-time, non-invasive physiological information for the diagnosis of cancer, cardiovascular and neurological diseases [57–59]. Plant tissues contain abundant auto fluorescent components whose spectra overlap with commercially available fluorophores, limiting optical tracking of nanoparticle transport [60]. PET-CT, with higher sensitivity of a given molecular event (down to picomolar), therefore represents a promising strategy for precise in planta localization [61]. Zirconium-89, a positron-emitting isotope, was incorporated seamlessly into the structure of CAP@TF-PEG to provide precise detection of material concentration and distribution profiles. As shown in Figs. 3d, e, PET-CT scans at 8, 24 and 48 h revealed pronounced stem uptake in injured rice. At 8 h, the stem PET signal in injured rice (0.26 ± 0.06 kBq/mL, corresponding to 0.47 ± 0.09 µg/mL) was significantly higher than in healthy stems (0.02 ± 0.01 kBq/mL, corresponding to 0.04 ± 0.02 µg/mL), and the accumulation of CAP@TF/⁸⁹Zr-PEG in the stem of damaged plants was 8.63% ± 1.66% (applied dose/g), which was significantly higher compared to the accumulation in the stems of healthy plants, also at 0.77% ± 0.38% (applied dose/g) (Fig. S9), indicating that CAP@TF/⁸⁹Zr-PEG was transported via the vasculature to the lesion, where ROS bursts induced responsive shell disassembly and local retention of fragments. At 24 h, stem signals in injured and healthy plants (0.26 ± 0.05 vs. 0.15 ± 0.03 kBq/mL) were comparable, consistent with sustained accumulation in the wounded region and gradual uptake in healthy stems (Figs. 3e and S10b). At 8 and 24 h, PET activity in roots confirmed downward transport and root metabolism (Figs. 3f and S10a). At 48 h, no significant PET signal remained in either group, reflecting systemic clearance of CAP@TF/⁸⁹Zr-PEG. Overall, PET-CT imaging demonstrated that CAP@TF/⁸⁹Zr-PEG had ROS responsiveness in stems and achieved targeted accumulation at wounded rice tissues.

Bioactivity of CAP@TF-PEG against C. suppressalis in the Laboratory and field

The insecticidal efficacy of the CAP@TF-PEG complex was evaluated against the second-instar larvae of C. suppressalis using a rice seedling dipping bioassay [62]. As shown in Fig. 4a, all three CAP formulations had good insecticidal effects on larvae of C. suppressalis. With the increase of formulations’ concentration, the mortality rate of larvae increased gradually (Figs. 4d-f). The toxicity was quantified by determining the median lethal concentration (LC₅₀) and median lethal time (LT₅₀). As summarized in Table S2, the LC₅₀ values at 72 h post-treatment for CAP SC, CAP TC, and the CAP@TF-PEG complex were 117.28, 64.38, and 158.48 mg/L, respectively (Fig. 4c). These results indicated that CAP TC exhibited the highest acute toxicity, followed by CAP SC, whereas the CAP@TF-PEG complex showed the lowest immediate toxicity. Additionally, the LT₅₀ values at a concentration of 200 mg/L were 37.48 h for CAP SC, 41.06 h for CAP TC, and 54.79 h for CAP@TF-PEG (Table S3), which suggested that both CAP TC and CAP SC acted more rapidly. Notably, the toxicity of CAP TC and CAP SC plateaued after 72 h, while the CAP@TF-PEG complex maintained a sustained insecticidal effect, showing superior sustained pesticide release performance, which was similar with results obtained by Liu et al. [19]. Despite a relatively weaker initial toxicity, CAP@TF-PEG demonstrated superior practical potential owing to its sustained-release profile. This characteristic effectively extends the duration of bioactivity, reduces the need for frequent reapplications, and ensures long-term pest control efficacy, thereby enhancing pesticide utilization efficiency and minimizing the environmental footprint. Importantly, subsequent field trials demonstrated that the overall field efficacy of the CAP@TF-PEG complex was comparable to that of CAP TC and CAP SC (Fig. 5 and Table S4). After the application of formulations, the numbers of damaged whiteheads in rice were significantly less than those in the control group, and 60 g/ha CAP@TF-PEG had the best prevention effect (Figs. 5a, b). Moreover, the number of surviving pests in rice were also significantly less than those in the CAP SC group (Figs. 5c, d), which revealed that the nano-formulation by CAP@TF-PEG exhibited superior residual activity, aligning with its prolonged toxicity observed in the laboratory. The low mortality observed in the TF-PEG-only control group confirmed that the carrier itself has negligible insecticidal toxicity at the tested concentrations (Fig. S11). In addition, CAP@TF-PEG was also superior to CAP SC in protecting rice panicles (Figs. 5e, f). Similar to previous studies [19], CAP@TF-PEG showed better insecticidal efficacy in the field (Fig. 5g). In conclusion, the CAP@TF-PEG complex, while showing a lower acute toxicity in laboratory bioassays, provides a prolonged insecticidal effect and achieves comparable pest control in the field. These findings collectively underscore the promise of TF-PEG as an effective nanocarrier for pesticide delivery.

Synergistic mechanism of CAP@TF-PEG

CAP acts as an agonist modulator of the ryanodine receptor (RyR) in the musculature of the insect, leading to an uncontrolled release of intracellular calcium, which causes muscle contractions in the larva and eventually leads to death [11, 12]. To illustrate the synergistic mechanism of TF-PEG-loaded CAP, total RNA was extracted from the surviving nymphs at 72 h after the treatment for RNA-seq analysis. Sequencing quality and correlation among three biological replicates were summarized in Table S5 and Fig. S12. Compared to the CAP treatment, the larvae treated with CAP@TF-PEG exhibited 363 up-regulated and 169 down-regulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 6a). Notably, functional categorization of DEGs revealed significant enrichment in processes associated with pesticide resistance (Fig. 6b). As shown in Fig. 6c, the heatmap analysis demonstrated that CAP@TF-PEG treatment significantly down-regulated key genes related to transferase activity, oxidoreductase activity, signaling receptor binding, lipid metabolic process, ATP binding, transmembrane transport, structural constituent of cuticle and oxidoreductase activity. Additionally, twelve DEGs related with resistance were selected for qRT-PCR assay, and results were consistent to the transcriptome data (Fig. 6d) The CAP@TF-PEG treatment resulted in the down-regulation of UDP-glucuronosyltransferase 2, and Cytochrome P450 genes, which are key genes regulating drug metabolism in insects to resist pesticide stress [63, 64]. Furthermore, genes related with structural constituent of cuticle (Endocuticle structural glycoprotein, SgAbd-8; Ecdysone-dependent gene 78E, Edg-78E) were remarkably up-regulated with the help of the TF-PEG, which may be due to the fact that nanoparticles are more easily attached to the insect body surface, and the continuous physical contact signal will strongly induce the insect to reinforce the wall. Additionally, the significant reduction of lipid metabolic processes gene (acyl-CoA Delta-9 desaturase) might lead to abnormal energy storage and decreased water retention capacity of the epidermis, which further destroys the physiological homeostasis of pests from the metabolic level [65]. Overall, this coordinated gene activation in defense, detoxification, redox homeostasis and energy metabolism pathways suggested a synergistic mechanism to enhance pesticide activity.

Zebrafish toxicity, cytotoxicity and effects on soil microorganisms of CAP@TF-PEG

Although nanopesticides CAP@TF-PEG brought greater insecticidal efficacy, it can also possibly elevate the potential hazard to non-target species [66, 67]. Given that pesticides can enter aquatic environments via runoff, zebrafish were thus selected as the model organism to assess the acute toxicity of CAP@TF-PEG [68]. The LC50 values of CAP TC, CAP SC and CAP@TF-PEG were 149.211 mg/L, 75.993 mg/L and 693.179 mg/L toward zebrafish, respectively (Fig. 7a, b and Table S6). Specifically, the ratio of LC50 of CAP@TF-PEG to CAP TC and CAP@TF-PEG to CAP SC were equal to 4.65 and 9.12, respectively (Fig. 7c). These results demonstrated that TF-PEG had a substantial impact on enhancing the safety of zebrafish. This is attributed to the fact that CAP@TF-PEG has a core-shell structure, avoiding direct exposure to zebrafish and thus reducing the acute toxicity [69].

Pesticides have long-term negative effects on the environment and food safety, and pesticide exposure has been shown to damage human cells, raising public health concerns [70, 71]. Firstly, protoplast viability was assessed by fluorescein diacetate and propidium iodide (FDA/PI). As shown in Figs. 7d and S13, co-staining results revealed that TF-PEG alone had no inherent toxicity effect, with viability comparable to the H₂O control. While CAP treatment alone induced strong cytotoxicity, significantly reducing viability, this toxicity was markedly alleviated when CAP was encapsulated in the CAP@TF-PEG. The significantly higher viability in the CAP@TF-PEG group demonstrated that the TF-PEG formulation effectively mitigated the cytotoxicity of CAP. Then, murine fibroblasts L929 and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) (representing the impacts to mammalian cells) were employed to assess the cytotoxicity of CAP@TF-PEG. Cytotoxicity studies revealed that CAP@TF-PEG and CAP@TA-FeIII had no effect on L929 and HUVEC cells compared to the control (Figs. 7e, f and S14), which also demonstrated that the complexation of CAP with TF-PEG reduced its acute toxicity, making the nano-enabled pesticide formulation safer for non-target organisms.

As an important part in maintaining soil ecology, soil microorganisms are highly sensitive to environmental changes, making them reliable indicators for assessing soil quality and fertility [72]. However, studies have shown that various nanoparticles can adversely affect soil ecosystems by reducing bacterial diversity and altering community structure [73, 74]. To this end, this study systematically evaluated the soil ecological safety of CAP@TF-PEG by analyzing three dimensions of community structure, species composition and functional potential. Our results showed that the CAP@TF-PEG had no adverse impact on soil microbial ecosystem. A total of 6,928 OTUs were identified across all treatments, spanning 41 phyla and 997 genera. As shown in Figs. 7g, h, there was no significant difference in Chao1 index and Shannon index among TF-PEG, CAP and CAP@TF-PEG treatment. Similarly, beta diversity analysis also indicated highly similar community structures with no significant separation (Fig. 7i). Meanwhile, the soil microbial communities treated with TF-PEG, CAP and CAP@TF-PEG were highly consistent at the phylum level, with no significant changes in the relative abundance (Fig. 7j). Analysis of the relative abundance of the top 10 genera showed that most genera remained relatively stable, though with treatment-dependent fluctuations (Fig. 7k). Furthermore, PICRUSt2 functional prediction analysis revealed that the core metabolic pathway distribution remained highly consistent across all treatments (Fig. S15), demonstrating that the nano-formulation maintained the soil microbial community’s function. These findings highlighted the resilience of the soil microbial community at the phylum and genus levels, despite treatment-induced changes in specific taxa. While our results indicated high soil microbial stability at 20 days post-treatment, we acknowledged that the single-point sampling was a limitation of this study. Soil microbiomes often exhibit temporal dynamics, where initial inhibition may be followed by microbial recovery or succession. Our data reflected the ecological state during a key crop growth stage, yet future research incorporating multi-point longitudinal sampling is required to fully characterize the transient and long-term effects of MPN-based formulations on soil health.

CAP@TF-PEG promoted rice root growth

In order to evaluate the effect of CAP@TF-PEG on non-target plants, we studied rice as a model. Interesting, it was found that compared with CAP, CAP@TF-PEG treatment did not incur stress effects, but also significantly promoted the growth and development of rice roots (Figs. 8a, b). This phenomenon was closely related to the accumulation of endogenous auxin indole-3-acetic acid (IAA): mass spectrometry imaging showed that IAA was specifically enriched in the root tip region (Fig. 8c), and the IAA content in the seedlings continued to be higher than that in the CAP treatment group at the later stage of treatment (Fig. 8d), which provided direct physiological evidence for the growth-promoting effect observed by the phenotype. IAA is an important plant auxin, which mainly regulates the growth and development of plants [75]. To further elucidate whether this promotion is driven by nutrient supplementation from the TA-Fe3+ network or active physiological induction, we analyzed the nature of the plant response. While Fe3+ and tannic acid are essential micronutrients and antioxidants that can support plant health, transcriptome analysis suggested that the primary driver is the modulation of molecular signaling. Comparative transcriptome analysis between CAP@TF-PEG and CAP treatments identified 2,621 DEGs, with 1,443 upregulated and 1,178 downregulated (Fig. 8e). Analysis of the top 20 enriched KEGG pathways revealed that these DEGs were predominantly associated with metabolic pathways and plant hormone signal transduction, indicating that CAP@TF-PEG extensively regulated metabolic processes and hormonal signaling networks (Fig. 8f). Further analysis of the auxin signaling pathway identified 12 IAA-related genes that were significantly upregulated in the CAP@TF-PEG treatment group (Fig. 8g). These included four SAUR36 genes, two SAUR50 genes, three auxin-induced proteins (X15, 15 A, 12), an auxin-regulated gene involved in organ size (ARGOS), an auxin efflux transporter (PIN8), and an IAA2 gene, which were confirm by qRT-PCR (Fig. 8h). Previous studies have shown that SAUR36 mediates hypocotyl elongation in Arabidopsis [76], while ARGOS overexpression extends the cell division period, leading to enlarged leaves and floral organs [77, 78]. The concurrent induction of these genes in our study suggested that CAP@TF-PEG might similarly enhance root growth by prolonging cell division and promoting cell expansion in rice root tissues. Overall, this phenotypic improvement was associated with elevated auxin levels and coordinated upregulation of auxin-responsive genes, suggesting a mechanism whereby the nanocarrier-based delivery system modulates phytohormone signaling to promote plant development. This suggested a dual-action mechanism: the carrier provided a gradual release of beneficial elements while simultaneously reprogramming the plant’s hormonal network to enhance root expansion and cell division.

Conclusion

This study proposed a new nano-delivery platform for pest management based on ROS-responsive nanoscale metal-phenolic network, and its core innovation was to break through the technical bottleneck of traditional pesticide preparations, such as difficult deposition, short persistence and single function. An innovative nano-delivery system (CAP@TF-PEG) was developed, which encapsulated the chlorantraniliprole (CAP) and had ROS-cleavable sites. It was found that TF-PEG reduced the contact angle of CAP on ultra-hydrophobic rice leaves, thereby improving foliar spraying performance. Importantly, combined with the ROS-responsive release pattern in vitro, it was revealed that CAP@TF-PEG could not only respond rapidly to release the CAP during the ROS burst, but also regulate its fluctuating level. Additionally, this platform had unique damage targeting that TF-PEG could selectively accumulate drugs in damaged or pest-invaded tissues by phloem pathway, which greatly improves the utilization efficiency of pesticides, and combination of fluorescent tracing and quantitative assays was employed to demonstrate the capacity nanocarriers to redistribute CAP throughout the rice via the phloem pathway, which proved stronger plant uptake, transport and enrichment of CAP@TF-PEG. Bioactivity experiments showed that although CAP@TF-PEG had no difference in insecticidal effect with CAP SC in laboratory bioassay, it had excellent insecticidal persistence and achieved good pest control effect in the field. Crucially, this prolonged duration of activity offers significant economic value by potentially reducing the frequency of seasonal pesticide applications, thereby lowering labor costs and total chemical input. Transcriptome analysis further revealed that TF-PEG promoted the inhibitory effect of CAP on cuticle synthesis. In terms of safety, TF-PEG nanocarriers showed good biosafety to non-target organisms such as zebrafish, cells and soil microorganisms, and was unexpectedly found to significantly promote rice root development. This feature, combined with the synergistic enhancement of pesticide efficacy revealed by transcriptome analysis, marks the evolution of the platform from a single pest control tool to an intelligent delivery system that can simultaneously improve the health of the crop itself. Overall, this work not only presents a promising nano-formulation for pest control but also lays the groundwork for designing a new generation of plant health-oriented pesticide delivery systems.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Chlorantraniliprole (95%) was supplied by Meibang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (China). Ferric chloride (FeCl₃, Grade: CP) was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (China). Tannic acid (98%) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Polyethylene glycol 4000 (PEG-4000) was provided by Shantou Xilong Chemical Co., Ltd. (China). Bovine serum albumin (BSA, Grade: CP) was sourced from Shanghai Aladdin Industrial Corporation (China). Perylene-3,9-dicarboxylic acid was acquired from Bide Pharmatech Ltd. (China).

Synthesis of CAP@TF-PEG

A DMSO solution of CAP (5.7 mg/mL, 100 µL) was added to deionized water (20 mL) under ultrasonic conditions. Subsequently, TA (40 mg/mL, 100 µL) and FeCl₃ (6 mg/mL, 50 µL) were added, followed by sonication for 5 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was washed three times with deionized water to obtain the intermediate CAP@TA–FeIII. The CAP content was quantified by HPLC using a C18 reversed-phase column under isocratic elution, with acetonitrile/water (65:35, v/v) as the mobile phase and a detection wavelength of 245 nm. The obtained CAP@TA-FeIII was dissolved in deionized water (6 mL), after which PEG-4000 (900 µL, 20 mg/mL), BSA (600 µL, 10 mg/mL), TA (600 µL, 5 mg/mL), and FeCl₃ (96 µL, 3 mg/mL) were added. The mixture was stirred for 40 min, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and washed three times with deionized water to obtain CAP@TF-PEG.

Material characterization

The morphology of CAP@TF-PEG was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S-3400 N). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-F200) was used to further characterize the morphology of CAP@TF-PEG and to perform elemental mapping analysis. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was carried out using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha instrument, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on a Bruker D2 Phaser diffractometer with a Cu target at a scanning rate of 2°/min. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of CAP@TF-PEG were collected using a Nicolet iS20 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The interactions between CAP and the TA–Fe-based metal–phenolic network (MPN) were investigated by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC, Malvern MicroCal iTC200). Thermogravimetric (TG) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves of CAP@TF-PEG were obtained using a Rigaku thermogravimetric analyzer under a nitrogen atmosphere.

Surface tension and contact angle measurements

The surface tension and dynamic contact angles of chlorantraniliprole CAP@TF-PEG and CAP SC on fresh rice leaf surfaces were measured using a contact angle goniometer (POWEREACH, JC2000D1, China). A piece of double-sided adhesive tape (approximately 5 cm in length) was attached to a microscope slide. Fresh rice leaves were collected, and a segment of approximately 5–7 cm in length was cut along the midvein using scissors and then carefully positioned on the prepared slide. A 6 µL aliquot of each sample solution (200 mg/L) was applied to the leaf surface at room temperature. The dynamic contact angles of the droplets were recorded over a period of 160 s at 10-second intervals to evaluate the wetting behavior of the solutions on the leaf surfaces, using double-distilled water (ddH₂O) as a control. Surface tension was determined by the pendant drop method. Each treatment was replicated three times to ensure the reliability of the data.

ROS-responsive release study

CAP@TF-PEG (containing 7 mg of CAP) was dispersed in water containing Tween 80 (0.5%, w/v) and transferred into a dialysis bag (molecular weight cutoff: 3500 Da). The dialysis bag was immersed in release medium, and H₂O₂ solutions at different concentrations (10 mM, 50 mM, and 100 mM, all containing 0.5% Tween 80) were added to the external medium according to the experimental design. At predetermined time points (4, 18, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, and 168 h), 500 µL of the external release medium was withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh release medium. The concentration of released CAP was determined by HPLC using a C18 reversed-phase column under isocratic elution, with acetonitrile/water (65:35, v/v) as the mobile phase and a detection wavelength of 245 nm. PET-CT Imaging

Micro-PET-CT scans of rice plants were performed using a Super Nova® PET/CT scanner (Pingseng Scientific). Both injured and healthy rice plants were administered via leaf soaking. CAP@TA–Fe/⁸⁹Zr was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water with a total radioactivity dose of 350 µCi. PET-CT images were acquired at 8, 24, and 48 h post-administration. The images were reconstructed after completion of the scans.

Analysis of CAP in Plant Tissues

After drug treatment, 1 g of rice tissue was collected and homogenized with 10 mL of acetonitrile. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected and filtered three times through a 0.45 μm membrane. The filtrate was concentrated to 2 mL under a gentle nitrogen stream. An HLB solid-phase extraction (SPE) cartridge was sequentially conditioned with methanol and deionized water. The rice extract was then loaded onto the cartridge and retained, followed by washing with 5 mL of 50% methanol. Elution was performed with 10 mL of acetonitrile, added in two 5 mL portions, with the cartridge sealed and soaked for 10 min after each addition. The eluate was concentrated to 2 mL under nitrogen, and the CAP content was quantified by HPLC.

Synthesis of ⁸⁹Zr-Labeled CAP@TF/⁸⁹Zr-PEG

Briefly, an FeCl₃ solution (6 mg/mL, 100 µL) was mixed with ⁸⁹Zr (1 mCi) and used to replace the non-radioactive FeCl₃ solution during the synthesis of CAP@TA-Fe. The product was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm and washed three times with deionized water to obtain CAP@TA-Fe/⁸⁹Zr. The resulting CAP@TA-Fe/⁸⁹Zr was dispersed in deionized water (40 mL), followed by the addition of PEG-4000 (6 mL, 20 mg/mL), BSA (4 mL, 10 mg/mL), TA (4 mL, 5 mg/mL), and FeCl₃ (640 µL, 3 mg/mL). After stirring for 40 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and washed three times with deionized water. The radioactivity of both the starting materials and the final products was measured using a radioactivity counter (RM-905 A).

Toxicity assay

The stomach toxicity of chlorantraniliprole technical material, commercial formulation, and nano-formulation against Chilo suppressalis was evaluated using the leaf-dip method by Huang et al. [79]. Briefly, using chlorantraniliprole technical material as an example, a stock solution was serially diluted with two-fold steps using an aqueous solution of 0.1% Tween-80 to obtain a series of concentrations at 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 mg/L (active ingredient). For each concentration, 3 mL of the test solution was added to a dipping well. Second-instar larvae were immersed in the solution for 30 s. After immersion, the excess liquid was removed by placing the larvae on absorbent paper. The treated larvae were then transferred to rearing tubes containing fresh rice seedlings as a food source. An aqueous solution of 0.1% Tween-80 was used as a blank control. Each concentration treatment was applied to 24 larvae, with four replicates. LC50 was determined based on mortality recorded at 72 h after treatment, while LT50 was assessed using mortality data collected at 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h. Data from bioassays were considered valid only when the mortality rate of the control groups was less than 20%. Larvae were considered dead if they showed no coordinated movement when prodded gently with a soft brush. The probation analysis was performed to determine the lethal concentration regression equation and the median lethal concentration (LC₅₀). The same procedure was applied to evaluate the CAP SC (tested at concentrations ranging from 25 to 400 mg/L) and the CAP@TF-PEG (tested at concentrations ranging from 50 to 800 mg/L).

Field experiments

Two formulations (CAP SC and CAP@TF-PEG) were applied at three concentrations, with the effective doses of chlorantraniliprole being 30, 45, and 60 g/ha, respectively. Each treatment was replicated four times, and each plot had an area of 100 m². A blank control was also set. The rice variety planted in the field was Jinxiangnuo No.1, a long-grain glutinous rice, which was transplanted on May 15, 2025. The spray experiment was carried out at the tillering stage of rice on June 11. A knapsack electric sprayer was used, with a water application rate of 450 L per hectare, and the spraying was conducted uniformly. The investigation was performed at the panicle stage of rice on July 1, 2025. All white panicles in a 100 m² plot were collected with their stem bases retained, and then brought back to the laboratory for dissection to count the number of live insects and pupae.

Transcriptome analysis for the synergistic mechanism of CAP@TF-PEG

According to the bioassay results, the LC50 concentration of CAP@TF-PEG were chosen to test RNA-seq analysis for C. suppressalis. Total RNA was extracted after 3 d feeding using the AG RNAex Pro reagent (Accurate Biology, Hunan). Each treatment included three independent samples. The transcript libraries were constructed via Illumina sequencing platform. Clean reads were processed by trimming adaptor sequences and removal of low-quality reads (Q ≤ 5) and were assembled using Trinity software [80, 81]. TopHat2 was used to achieve the sequence alignment of clean reads with the reference genome, and obtain the localization information of reads on the reference genome [82]. The expression level of each transcript was presented by FPKM value. DESeq2 was applied for differential expression analysis between transcripts, and |log2(fold change)| > 1.0 and Padj < 0.05 were screen conditions [83]. BLASTX was used to annotate single genes in gene databases. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was carried out for gene enrichment and functional annotation. The expression levels of target genes were assessed using RT-qPCR. SYBR Green I (Vazyme, Nanjing) was used for RT-qPCR on a LightCycler®480 Real-Time PCR detection system. All primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Table S7.

Zebrafish acute toxicity and cytotoxicity

The zebrafish (Danio rerio) used in this experiment were purchased from a local aquarium market. A static toxicity test was conducted according to standard guidelines. Test solutions with a volume of 1 L were prepared in advance. For the compounds CAP SC and CAP TC, concentration gradients were set at 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, and 500 mg/L. For CAP@TF-PEG and TF-PEG, concentration gradients were set at 62.5, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 mg/L. A control group using test water only was also included. Each treatment group and the control group were set up with four replicates. Ten healthy zebrafish of uniform size were randomly allocated to each replicate. The entire fish transfer process was completed within 30 min. The test duration was 96 h. Mortality was observed and recorded at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after the onset of exposure. Dead fish were promptly removed upon observation. The median lethal concentration (LC₅₀) and its 95% confidence interval for each observation time point were calculated using Probit analysis.

Additionally, the activity of rice protoplasts was determined by FDA/PI double staining method. Rice protoplasts were isolated following a previously described protocol [84]. Briefly, protoplasts were obtained from 10-day-old yellow rice seedlings. The cell density of the isolated protoplasts was adjusted to 1 × 107 cells/mL using a hemocytometer. An equal volume of the treatment solution (final concentration: 200 ppm) was then mixed with the protoplast suspension. Fluorescein diacetate (FDA, final concentration: 10 µg/mL) and propidium iodide (PI, final concentration: 10 µg/mL) were added to the mixture as a vital stain to label live cells and was gently manually inverted 5–10 times. After a 5-minute incubation in darkness, green fluorescence and red fluorescence signals were visualized using an LSM750 confocal laser-scanning microscope (Zeiss, Germany). For each biological replicate, fluorescence images were captured from three randomly selected non-overlapping fields of view, and each treatment was performed in triplicate.

To investigate the cytotoxic effects of CAP@TA-Fe³⁺ and CAP@TA-PEG on L929 and HUVEC cells, multiple assays were employed, including the CCK-8 cell viability assay and live/dead cell staining observed by microscopy. Briefly, cells (1 × 10⁴ per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The cells were then treated with gradient concentrations of CAP@TA-Fe³⁺ or CAP@TF-PEG for 24 h. Cell viability in each well was quantitatively evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. In addition, cell apoptosis was assessed using a Calcein/PI staining kit. Cells were seeded into 12-well plates and incubated with gradient concentrations of CAP@TA-Fe³⁺ or CAP@TF-PEG for 24 h. After preparation of the Calcein/PI working solution, the staining solution was added to the cells, and the fluorescence signals were observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Soil microbial community analysis

Total microbial DNA was extracted from soil samples collected around the rice rhizosphere 20 days post-application (TF-PEG, CAP, CAP@TF-PEG) according to the instructions of the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.). To study bacterial community structure, PCR amplification was performed targeting the V4-V5 region of the 16S rRNA gene using the primers 515F (5’-barcode-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGG-3’) and 907R (5’-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3’). The detailed processes of PCR and subsequent library construction have been already reported [85]. The constructed sequencing libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with the PE250 strategy at BIOZERN Biotech. Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The optimized sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using UPARSE 7.1 with 97% sequence similarity level [86]. The most abundant sequence for each OTU was selected as a representative sequence. Taxonomic classification of representative sequences from each OTU was performed using the RDP classifier Bayesian algorithm (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/) against the Silva database, with a confidence threshold of 0.7.

Analysis of growth and development in rice seedlings treated with CAP@TF-PEG

Rice seeds (Oryza sativa L.) were surface-sterilized with 0.1% sodium hypochlorite for 15 min, followed by three rinses with sterile double-distilled water. The sterilized seeds were then immersed for 24 h in one of the following solutions: deionized water (H₂O), 5% acetone (ACE), CAP, TF-PEG, or CAP@TF-PEG. After treatment, the seeds were germinated and grown under controlled conditions. At 3 days after treatment (DAT), seedlings were photographed, and root lengths were measured.

Extraction and quantification of auxin, and MALDI-MSI analysis

Auxin were extracted from frozen seedling tissues as previously described [87]. Their concentrations were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS). Rice seeds were cryo-sectioned at 20 μm and thaw-mounted onto ITO slides. Tissue sections were spray-coated with DHNBA matrix using an automated HTX-TM Sprayer [88]. Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Mass Spectrometry Imaging (MALDI-MSI) was performed in positive ion mode at a 200 μm spatial resolution using a Bruker timsTOF fleX instrument. Raw data were processed using SCiLS™Lab 2024 software, including baseline correction, peak alignment, smoothing, and normalization, to generate ion images. The datasets were subsequently converted to imzML format, and putative annotation of IAA was performed by matching the accurate m/z values against mass spectrometry databases. In the resulting ion images, pixel intensity corresponds to the relative abundance of IAA, with a color gradient from purple (low intensity) to yellow (high intensity) representing areas of low to high IAA concentration, respectively.

Transcriptome analysis of growth and development in rice seedlings treated with CAP@TF-PEG

Total RNA was extracted from rice seedlings treated with CAP and CAP@TF-PEG (3 d) using three biological replicates per condition. RNA integrity was verified using agarose gel electrophoresis and a bioanalyzer. mRNA was enriched from total RNA and used to construct sequencing libraries, which were sequenced on an Illumina platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw reads were processed to remove adapters and low-quality sequences, and clean reads were aligned to the rice reference genome. Differential gene expression analysis between CAP and CAP@TF-PEG treatments was performed using the DESeq2 package, with significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with an adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05 and |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted on the identified DEGs using the clusterProfiler R package. Terms with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To validate RNA-seq results, cDNA was synthesized from the same RNA samples used for sequencing. qRT-PCR was performed using gene-specific primers (Table S7) designed with Primer Premier 5 (Premier Biosoft, Canada). The rice Actin gene (Os03g61970) served as an internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times, and all results were expressed as means ± standard errors. The ANOVA with Tukey HSD test was conducted using SPSS 20.0 (SPSS Inc., USA) at the P = 0.05 level of significance, and independent t-test was adopted to analyze the significant differences between two groups.
