Robot-Assisted Oral Examination: Challenges and Innovations
Authors: Yongcheng Ge, Wenfeng Wang, Ting Zhao, Dandan Liu, Sizheng Fan, Pengyuan Liu, Wei Li, Hongchen Sun, Xiaoqiu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo summarize and evaluate the core components, functional modules, and developmental challenges of intelligent oral examination robots, and to clarify their potential clinical relevance as well as the mechanisms by which robotics and artificial intelligence can enhance diagnostic efficiency and standardization in dentistry.Materials and MethodsThis study employed a systematic literature review methodology, retrieving relevant publications from the PubMed and ScienceDirect databases up to September 2025. The search strategy utilized Boolean operators to combine keywords such as 'robot,' 'AI,' 'oral diagnosis,' and 'intraoral scanner,' aiming to comprehensively cover the fields of robotic technology, oral diagnostics, artificial intelligence, and related sensing technologies. The initial search yielded approximately 1,027 articles, of which 134 were ultimately included after screening. Inclusion criteria comprised studies related to oral robotic technologies, applications of artificial intelligence in diagnosis, and robotic navigation, while exclusion criteria included non–peer-reviewed publications and studies with insufficient methodological descriptions. A combined approach of narrative review and critical analysis was adopted, with a focus on key technical domains, including hardware architecture, software integration, multimodal perception systems, and force feedback mechanisms.ResultsThe analysis identified several key technological components of intelligent oral examination robots, including high-resolution intraoral imaging systems, multimodal data fusion frameworks, force-sensing and adaptive control technologies, automated navigation systems, and AI-driven data-processing algorithms. These systems contribute to improved image acquisition accuracy, standardized diagnostic procedures, enhanced data management, and optimized clinical workflow. However, challenges remain in system integration, safety assurance, real-time responsiveness, and clinical validation.ConclusionsCurrent technological advances suggest that intelligent oral examination robots have significant potential to reduce clinician workload, improve diagnostic accuracy, and enhance consistency in oral examinations. Continued refinement in hardware–software integration, safety mechanisms, and clinical adaptability will be essential for broader implementation.Clinical RelevanceIntelligent oral examination robots may significantly improve diagnostic quality through automation, standardized data acquisition, and enhanced imaging capabilities. Their application could facilitate early disease detection, improve patient management, and streamline clinical workflows in modern dental practice.

Introduction

Oral examination is a clinical process in which dentists obtain an objective understanding of oral conditions and gather valuable diagnostic information through visual inspection, palpation, percussion, probing, auscultation, and clinician–patient communication.1 As the initial and foundational step of oral treatment, an accurate and thorough oral examination is essential for establishing an appropriate treatment plan.2 Although manual examination relies heavily on clinicians’ experience and judgment—thus retaining irreplaceable clinical value—the procedure itself is repetitive and highly standardized, and it inevitably carries limitations such as subjectivity and inter-examiner variability.

With the growing adoption of intelligent dental technologies, the need for automated and intelligent examination has become increasingly prominent. Intelligent oral examination not only represents a hallmark of modern dental care but also serves as an important pathway toward precision and personalized dentistry. By integrating robotics with cutting-edge artificial intelligence (AI), intelligent oral examination robots may acquire detailed clinical information and provide data-driven support for diagnosis and treatment, thereby representing an important future direction in smart dentistry. The widespread implementation of such technology may also improve the distribution of oral healthcare resources across regions by enabling deployment in primary care settings and underserved areas. This could help mitigate disparities in access to dental services and promote a more equitable, intelligent, and modernized oral healthcare system.

Medical robots are intelligent systems designed to assist with or autonomously perform medical tasks in clinical settings.3 Depending on the specific clinical scenario, these robots can be programmed—manually or automatically—to perform predefined operations, which are then translated into coordinated motions of various robotic subsystems to complete the intended medical task.4,5 In 1985, researchers used a PUMA 560 industrial robot to assist with stereotactic neurosurgical biopsy, marking the first documented use of robotics in surgical procedures and the beginning of medical robot development.6 Over the past three decades, medical robots have been successfully applied in surgical operations, endoscopic examinations, clinical rehabilitation and nursing care, and medical rescue and transport.7, 8, 9, 10 Currently, surgical robots represent the most widely adopted class of medical robots, with the Da Vinci Surgical System—approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2000—serving as the most prominent example and the world’s first laparoscopic robotic system.11 The Da Vinci system comprises a surgeon console, a patient-side manipulator system, and an imaging unit.12 During surgery, the primary surgeon operates remotely without direct patient contact; robotic arms and surgical instruments replicate the surgeon’s movements by relying on 3D visualization and motion-scaling technologies.13 The system offers various advantages, including enhanced visualization, tremor elimination, and superior precision, ultimately reducing intraoperative bleeding, postoperative pain, and tissue adhesions.14,15 Through continuous technological iteration, the Da Vinci system has achieved widespread application in cardiothoracic surgery, urology, gynaecology, and general surgery.16 The successful implementation of medical robots has also stimulated research and development in dental robotics, thereby enabling breakthroughs in traditional treatment modalities and workflows. Responding to clinical needs in dentistry, researchers have developed robots for various dental specialties. In 2000, Professor Lü Peijun at Peking University developed the first dental robotic system—a denture tooth-arrangement robot—marking the beginning of dental robotics.17 In 2013, Professor Zhao Yimin initiated the development of an autonomous dental implant robot, which completed the world’s first fully autonomous implant surgery in 2017.18 This achievement accelerated the development of dental robots, leading to systems designed for tooth preparation, denture tooth arrangement, root canal instrumentation, orthodontic archwire bending, tooth replantation, and microsurgical endodontic procedures.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 (Figure 1).Fig. 1Research status of dental robots. In recent years, artificial intelligence has made remarkable progress in the medical field. The integration of AI with robotics has further enhanced the intelligence, autonomy, and personalization of medical robots, marking a new developmental trajectory.28 AI serves as the decision-making engine, capable of learning and generating intelligent strategies, whereas the robot functions as the physical executor of those strategies.29 The primary goal of AI is to enable machines to perform complex tasks that typically require human intelligence. AI has permeated numerous medical domains, supporting multimodal data analysis—including clinical, genetic, and imaging data—assisting disease diagnosis, reducing human error by taking over repetitive tasks, predicting and managing diseases, and facilitating minimally invasive surgical procedures.30,31

AI-driven algorithms can improve disease detection accuracy, provide visual guidance based on anatomical references, support clinical decision-making, and enable predictive and simulation-based analyses.32,33 In 2015, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) were first applied to dental caries detection on radiographs, officially marking the beginning of the AI era in dentistry.34 AI can help establish powerful, standardized oral health databases capable of tracking complex oral diseases and supporting long-term management of chronic oral conditions.35,36

With the continual improvement of living standards and robotic technology, AI-enabled medical robots offer new possibilities for intelligent diagnosis and treatment in dentistry. Oral examination requires multiple small instruments to obtain clinically meaningful information. A robotics platform allows the integration of various instruments onto a single robotic arm equipped with high-precision, multi-degree-of-freedom joints capable of flexible and comprehensive manoeuvring within the confined oral space. Combined with high-speed cameras, real-time navigation, and advanced sensors, such robots can collect detailed information about dental hard tissues, periodontal tissues, mucosa, and other intraoral structures. Moreover, multimodal human–robot interaction enables automated history taking, communication of examination findings, and enhanced patient–clinician interaction.37, 38, 39

Based on these capabilities, robots can be used to perform oral examinations, assist clinicians in generating examination reports, and improve diagnostic efficiency. Furthermore, robotic examination systems can accumulate large-scale data on patients' oral health, contributing to epidemiological studies, evidence-based research, and the development of AI-driven diagnostic and therapeutic systems. As a result, the design and development of oral examination robots have emerged as a major research frontier in smart dentistry. Their potential scope of application includes routine oral check-ups, lesion screening, assessment of dental hard tissues and periodontal conditions, oral mucosal evaluation, tooth mobility assessment, and pulp vitality-related examinations. In recent years, several exploratory studies and prototype systems have demonstrated the feasibility of integrating intraoral imaging devices, robotic manipulators, and AI-based analysis for semi-automated oral data acquisition and screening. However, to date, no fully autonomous oral examination robot has been reported, and most existing work remains at a conceptual or early developmental stage. To promote broader applications of robotics in dentistry, this review envisions the potential system modules and workflow design for such robots. In this review, an intelligent oral examination robot is defined as an autonomous robotic system designed to independently perform core oral examination procedures, including intraoral data acquisition, multimodal sensing, preliminary analysis, and structured report generation under appropriate clinical supervision. Importantly, this concept is distinct from assistive robotic systems, in which the robot functions only as an auxiliary tool operated directly by a clinician. The system discussed in this manuscript is intended for autonomous examination rather than assisted operation, meaning that the robot is envisioned as the primary executor of the examination workflow, while the clinician remains responsible for supervision, interpretation, and final clinical decision-making.

Components of the oral examination robot

As a representative product of the deep integration between modern intelligent medical technology and clinical requirements in dentistry, an oral examination robot reflects the collaborative development of multiple disciplines, including advanced manufacturing, computer science, artificial intelligence, sensing, and control engineering.40,41 From a macroscopic perspective, its overall system structure can be divided into 2 core components: the hardware component system and the software system. The hardware component system, which consists of the robot body, mechanical arm, high-definition cameras, lighting devices, and saliva suction units, is mainly responsible for physical operations such as mechanical manipulation and data acquisition. The software system, deeply integrated with artificial intelligence, is responsible for processing multisource data and generating control, feedback, and operational commands.42, 43, 44 The two subsystems interact seamlessly through high-speed data transmission, jointly forming a highly precise, adaptable, and stable intelligent oral examination system and database. Hardware component system

The hardware part of the oral examination robot serves as the fundamental support for clinical operations. Its design must achieve high precision, flexibility, and stability, while also ensuring patient comfort and medical safety. Each hardware module must be optimized according to dental clinical needs and function cooperatively to complete complex examination tasks.

The mechanical arm, as the core actuator of the oral examination robot, serves as the main carrier for various information-acquisition tasks.45 The mechanical arm generally adopts a six-degree-of-freedom (6-DOF) design to mimic the movement characteristics of the human upper limb. It features multi-axis coordination, spatial positioning, and target navigation capabilities, enabling flexible micro-operations within the narrow oral cavity.40 'Six degrees of freedom' refers to translational movement along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes, and rotational movement around the X-, Y-, and Z-axes.46 A 6-DOF robotic arm refers to a manipulator that can move along three translational axes (X, Y, and Z) and rotate about three rotational axes (roll, pitch, and yaw). This configuration is commonly modelled in robotics literature. For example, Cheng47 established a dynamics model for a 6DOF robot using screw theory, which naturally encapsulates both translational and rotational degrees of freedom. Raghunathan’s48 kinematic study of a curved 6DOF arm further confirms the presence of these six independent motions. Zhang49 developed an inverse kinematics algorithm for a 6DOF arm in which the six joint angles map to the end-effector’s spatial pose.

End-effector of the mechanical arm

The end-effector is equipped with a lighting device, a wide-angle endoscope, and various oral examination instruments, allowing these components to be quickly replaced according to different examination tasks. The oral examination instruments integrated on the mechanical arm include tweezers, probes, percussion tools, etc. Tweezers are used to examine tooth mobility; combined with mechanical force-sensing technology and image recognition, they can precisely assess the degree of mobility. Probing uses an oral probe with built-in pressure sensors to accurately detect caries depth and periodontal pocket depth, providing reliable evidence for diagnosing periodontal disease and dental caries. Percussion, performed using percussion tools and force sensors, allows uniform evaluation of the periodontal and periapical tissue health. Pulp vitality testing is performed by equipping the robot with an electric pulp tester and current sensor to accurately assess pulp vitality and support diagnosis of pulpal diseases.

All examination instruments mounted on the end-effector are equipped with high-precision tactile sensors that can monitor, in real time, the contact force between the instruments and oral soft or hard tissues. Based on this feedback and image recognition, the control system automatically adjusts the amplitude and speed of mechanical arm movements, ensuring that operations are diagnostically effective without causing patient discomfort. High-speed imaging module

The imaging module is the core component of the machine vision system. Through image acquisition and visual analysis, it replaces human eyes and is essential to robotic and automated systems.55 The high-speed imaging module consists of a high-resolution microscopic camera and a wide-angle endoscope. The high-resolution microscopic camera extends from the main body of the mechanical arm and can move freely within ±30° in front of the patient’s face; the wide-angle endoscope is located at the distal end of the mechanical arm and enters the oral cavity together with other examination instruments.56

Both the microscopic camera and the wide-angle endoscope should support real-time image acquisition at ≥60 frames per second and 4K resolution, thereby enabling clear visualization of enamel cracks, early carious lesions, sinus tracts, and other subtle soft- and hard-tissue abnormalities.57 By combining near-infrared or fluorescence light sources, the wide-angle endoscope can significantly enhance contrast between diseased and healthy tissues, particularly in detecting plaque and caries boundaries.58, 59, 60 This module supports automatic focusing and optical image stabilization, ensuring sharp images even when the mechanical arm moves rapidly.61,62

In the system, the external high-speed camera captures patient micro-expressions, eye movements, and body gestures through multimodal sensor fusion and real-time computer vision algorithms. These data help assess pain or anxiety levels and are fed back to the robot control unit to enable adaptive behavioural adjustment.63,64 Based on optical flow (eg, Farneback) and 3D-CNNs (such as I3D), the system can quantify pain-related facial features (eg, AU4 brow lowering, AU6/7 periocular tightening) and classify them using PSPI scoring. Multi-view tracking (such as OpenPose skeletal estimation) can detect defensive movements, improving safety.65, 66, 67 Additionally, the external camera collects data on facial complexion, nutritional status, and mouth-opening pattern.

Irrigation and saliva suction module

Oral procedures require a relatively dry environment to ensure visibility and operational safety. This module uses a high-sensitivity water pump and controllable nozzle to precisely regulate water flow and direction for rinsing tooth surfaces or surgical fields. Meanwhile, the negative-pressure suction unit—powered by a miniature fan and flow-control valve—effectively removes saliva, blood, and irrigation fluid without interfering with examination. The system includes an automatic anti-blockage mechanism: micro-vibration self-cleaning within the suction channel activates after each task to prevent clogging, improving continuous-operation capacity and service life.

Lighting system

Because of the oral cavity’s depth, curved surfaces, and uneven light distribution, illumination plays a decisive role in examination quality. The system uses adjustable ring-shaped cold LED lighting, installed alongside the external microscopic camera and intraoral endoscope, providing bright, shadow-free illumination without generating heat. Brightness automatically adjusts based on camera feedback to match different scenes. Built-in polarizing filters reduce surface glare and improve image contrast and recognition accuracy.68 Near-infrared light assists in examining mucosa and gingival tissue, particularly in identifying vascular dilation and inflammatory edema.69

Software system components

The software system is the core manifestation of the robot’s intelligence, enabling automatic diagnosis, precise control, and personalized interaction.70 It covers the entire information-processing chain—from raw data acquisition to decision output—including image recognition, semantic analysis, feedback control, optical positioning, data management, and remote communication systems.71,72 These subsystems communicate through unified protocols and rely on high-performance processors and neural-network architectures to achieve real-time analysis and intelligent decision-making. The system must provide highly automated data-processing capabilities and improve stability, accuracy, and adaptability through machine learning.

Image recognition system

The image recognition system is essential for autonomous identification of oral lesions, determination of lesion types, and localization of target areas. Its workflow includes image classification, regional segmentation, feature extraction, and lesion determination.57,73 First, the system preprocesses raw images acquired by the imaging module through denoising, sharpening, and colour enhancement. Then, deep-learning models such as U-Net and Mask R-CNN segment structures including teeth, gingiva, and calculus, generating precise boundary maps.74, 75, 76 During feature extraction, the system analyses texture, edges, and morphological characteristics to detect subtle signs such as early caries, enamel defects, and gingival recession.77,78 Lesion classification employs multitask CNNs to classify and localize suspected lesions, referencing studies such as Li et al.79 on simultaneous detection of gingivitis, calculus, and plaque. The system compares detected features with a database of normal and abnormal tissue images and outputs diagnostic suggestions such as 'suspected caries' or 'supragingival calculus, grade II.' The entire process operates in a clinically feasible time frame and provides reliable auxiliary support for clinicians.80,81

Semantic interaction system

The semantic interaction system enables natural spoken communication between humans and the robot. Its main functions include speech recognition, semantic understanding, and conversational response generation.82 Using deep-learning speech models (such as Transformer-CTC or Conformer), the system can accurately transcribe patient speech and identify intent and context, enabling it to understand expressions such as 'It hurts a little' or 'I had a filling before'.83, 84, 85 The robot can then provide appropriate feedback such as 'Is the pain on the upper right side?' or 'The previous filling was on the upper left second molar. Please keep your mouth open.' The system is linked with the image-processing module, allowing automatic explanation of detected lesions. A directional microphone array combined with noise-suppression algorithms ensures speech recognition accuracy even in noisy environments.86,87 An emotional-modulation mechanism adjusts tone and rhythm to reduce patient anxiety and enhance human-robot rapport.88

Feedback control system

The control system serves as the robot’s central control unit, coordinating the movements of all actuators. It operates on a real-time operating system (RTOS) framework and uses inverse kinematics and multi-DOF optimization algorithms for dynamic trajectory planning, enabling precise motion control in complex spaces.89, 90, 91 The system adopts a hybrid architecture based on model predictive control (MPC) and reinforcement learning (RL). It considers oral spatial modelling and patient-behaviour prediction to adjust mechanical arm trajectory and posture at high frequency.92,93 A hybrid force–position feedback strategy integrates joint torque, end-effector tactile input, and image-based positional information to refine motion paths dynamically.94,95 During operation, the system generates initial trajectories via 3D reconstruction and uses historical examination data to establish risk maps, avoiding hypersensitive or diseased regions.96 Real-time image and force feedback ensure smooth and safe operation. For example, probing forces are controlled not to exceed 20–25 g, ensuring diagnostic accuracy without tissue damage.97 Robust fault-detection mechanisms monitor motor temperature, current, and position. If overload or abnormal movement occurs, the controller stops operation immediately and issues warnings.90,94,98 Lai’s research group introduced integrated tactile-feedback control to implant robots, enabling surgeons to perceive and adjust drilling forces in real time, significantly improving precision in delicate procedures and reducing risks such as drill deviation.99

Optical positioning and navigation system

To achieve high-precision navigation and targeting in the restricted oral cavity, the system incorporates multimodal positioning technologies. The optical positioning system uses infrared trackers and reflective markers: miniature reflective spheres adhered to the patient's maxillofacial region are tracked by external infrared cameras to determine their 3D spatial coordinates, achieving submillimetre positioning accuracy.100,101 The Polaris optical tracking system by NDI can eliminate environmental noise, detect markers attached to instruments, and compute spatial coordinates with submillimetre accuracy and >4 kHz sampling rates.102 The control system can define virtual working boundaries to avoid sensitive areas such as the soft palate or pharynx. Integration of IMU feedback with visual-inertial fusion algorithms corrects motion-induced image drift.103,104 The system provides key technical support for robot-assisted oral procedures through high-precision spatial positioning and real-time tracking. The robot compensates for patient micro-movements using preoperative planning data, visual feedback, and force control.105,106 For example, the Yakebot implant robot monitors head motion in real time, issuing control commands every 0.008 seconds, and the mechanical arm compensates within 0.2 seconds to ensure accuracy.40 (Figure 4)Fig. 4Research progress of positioning and navigation technologies. Three-dimensional imaging module

This module provides digital oral-model construction capability, forming the basis for preoperative planning, intraoperative navigation, and postoperative evaluation. Using structured-light projection technology, encoded patterns are projected onto tooth surfaces, and stereo cameras capture distortion data to compute 3D morphology. A single scan takes <2 seconds and is noninvasive.110,111 The system supports fusion with external CBCT data to help identify root lesions and root canal anatomy. Stereoscopic imaging is one of the most common 3D techniques.112,113 With extremely short capture times (1–2 ms), it minimizes motion artifacts and achieves highly accurate 3D reconstruction.114 In clinical applications, this technology has been used for quantitative evaluations of various soft-tissue morphological changes. For example, Alan et al.115 used 3dMD to measure facial swelling after third-molar extraction, and Barone et al.116 developed a standardized automatic workflow based on pre-/post-operative 3D facial scans that automatically performs surface registration as well as linear-distance and volumetric swelling quantification. Several studies have also used three-dimensional stereophotogrammetry or digital facial-scan data to assess the effects of medications and physical therapies on the resolution of postoperative facial swelling following third-molar surgery.117,118 Researchers can obtain facial 3D images rapidly and perform linear or angular measurements for diagnosis, surgical planning, and postoperative assessment. Personalized file and data management system

This system builds a complete, traceable electronic oral record for each patient. After each examination, the system automatically generates image reports, structural maps, and diagnostic suggestions, and compares them with previous data to extract trends such as '25% increase in calculus area' or 'caries progression into dentin.' All data are stored securely with encryption and linked to electronic medical records. Permission-based access ensures privacy. For special populations (children, elderly), customized examination frequencies and reminders can be set to support intelligent oral-health management.

Remote communication and diagnostic support system

To achieve remote medical collaboration and expert consultation, the system incorporates high-speed, low-latency communication technology.122 Images, 3D models, and robotic-operation data collected during examination can be transmitted in real time via 5G, enabling remote experts to annotate synchronously, discuss findings, and provide guidance. Using 5G and robotic technology, Niu’s group performed the world’s first ultra-remote laparoscopic robot surgery, demonstrating the feasibility of low-latency remote operation and breaking key technical barriers.123 The system also integrates with hospital information and image archiving systems, enabling interoperability and supporting regional medical collaboration.

Application of robots in oral examination

In clinical oral diagnosis and treatment, oral examination, as the most fundamental step, provides the basis for discovering and diagnosing diseases.124 Traditional oral examinations rely heavily on the clinician’s experience, technique, and subjective judgment, which inevitably introduces certain deviations and limitations. With the advancement of artificial intelligence, robotics, sensor technology, and human–machine interaction systems, intelligent oral examination robots have gradually demonstrated their unique advantages in clinical applications. By integrating high-definition imaging systems, tactile sensors, intelligent voice modules, and AI-based decision-making, such robots can efficiently, accurately, and systematically complete basic oral examinations, greatly improving the comprehensiveness and convenience of diagnosis and treatment and saving clinical resources. Oral examination robots can not only reduce the workload of dentists but also provide strong support in clinician–patient communication, standardized examination, pathological feature identification, data analysis, and clinical decision-making. The following sections describe in detail the application and potential of oral examination robots across various examination procedures. The following sections (Sections 3 and 4) outline a conceptual robotic system intended to illustrate potential applications and workflows. The described specifications are hypothetical and are not representative of an established or clinically validated platform.

Clinician–patient communication: AI-driven intelligent consultation and information collection

In oral diagnosis and treatment, effective clinician–patient communication is the premise for individualized and precise treatment.125 Using deep learning and natural language processing technologies, robots can simulate the linguistic patterns and interaction strategies of clinical dentists and engage in human-like dialogue.126 The consultation module, usually based on advanced conversational AI models, can guide patients through multi-round questioning to describe symptoms, medical history, and subjective feelings. The system analyses patients’ lexical choices, semantic structures, and emotional tone in real time and automatically labels key information, such as the nature of pain, duration, triggers, past medical history, and medication use.85,127,128

In addition, the robot can standardize the collected information and automatically enter it into the electronic medical record system, saving clinicians the time required for documentation and allowing them to devote more effort to professional judgment and patient care.

Inspection: high-precision imaging and micro-lesion recognition

In clinical work, inspection is the most intuitive initial examination method and relies on visual observation by the clinician. However, the resolution of the human eye is only about 0.2 mm, making it difficult to identify micro-lesions.129 To address this limitation, modern oral examination robots can integrate high-definition cameras, macro lenses, and near-infrared transillumination (NIRI) to obtain panoramic, high-definition images of facial features, mouth-opening pattern, oral mucosa, teeth, and gingiva.130,131 The AI-based image processing module performs edge detection, feature extraction, and deep-learning segmentation, using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and other algorithms to identify early caries, fissures, demineralization spots, pigmentations, and mucosal colour or shape abnormalities. Studies have shown that deep learning–based systems achieve higher accuracy than clinicians in detecting early caries and demonstrate high sensitivity and specificity in multi-lesion detection tasks.132 After imaging, the robot immediately labels the detected areas on a three-dimensional oral structure model, providing visual assistance for diagnosis.

Probing: flexible sensing technology and interactive feedback mechanism

Probing is a commonly used clinical method for evaluating carious cavities, periodontal pocket depth, and periapical pathology. Using flexible tactile sensors and probing tips, oral examination robots can perform continuous and standardized probing and provide real-time feedback on resistance changes and surface texture variations.133,134 In periodontal probing, the robot can automatically measure the depth of periodontal pockets at various sites around each tooth and compare the data with normal reference values to assist in diagnosis.

Percussion: force-feedback control and pain-level recognition

Percussion is an important diagnostic method for evaluating pulp and periodontal tissue conditions through tapping the tooth and assessing patient responses. The robot's mechanical arm integrates force-sensing modules, allowing precise control of percussion force and performing standardized light, moderate, and heavy percussion operations. The system compares percussion results with those from contralateral healthy teeth and adjacent teeth to ensure that the applied force remains within safe and clinically effective ranges. All percussion feedback is automatically recorded and added to the electronic medical record.

Palpation: flexible tactile perception and tissue condition assessment

Palpation involves detecting the characteristics of a lesion—such as hardness, boundary, and mobility—through touch. The oral examination robot, equipped with high-sensitivity tactile sensors and a multi-degree-of-freedom mechanical arm, can simulate the finger-touch motion of a clinician and perform systematic palpation of areas such as the temporomandibular joint and intraoral or extraoral masses. During palpation, the robot continuously records tissue elasticity and temperature changes. AI models analyse these characteristics to infer lesion properties. For example, inflammatory tissue typically feels soft and tender on palpation, while malignant tumours are often hard, have unclear boundaries, and show poor mobility. Because skin mechanics are complex, the development of tactile sensors remains highly challenging.135 Although current tactile sensors cannot fully replicate human skin mechanics, the combination of multi-channel sensing and large-scale data learning has already demonstrated strong potential for assisting in the diagnosis of common oral diseases.

Mobility examination: three-dimensional motion assessment and automated grading system

Tooth mobility is a key indicator of periodontal support. Traditionally, clinicians assess mobility by holding the tooth with tweezers and manually moving it in different directions. An intelligent oral examination robot can use its imaging system and mechanical arm to monitor tooth motion in buccolingual, mesiodistal, and vertical directions in real time. The image recognition system quantifies the range of motion, and the AI analysis center automatically classifies mobility based on established grading standards (eg, Miller classification),136 generating a diagnostic report.

Pulp vitality test: temperature-control system and synchronized feedback analysis

Pulp vitality is important for diagnosing pulpitis and apical periodontitis. Traditional thermal or electric tests have operational limitations. An intelligent robot can standardize and automate vitality testing. The mechanical arm, equipped with temperature-controlled (cold/hot) tips and micro-current stimulators, precisely controls stimulus intensity and duration. This is especially useful for large-scale pulp testing of multiple teeth, reducing repetitive workload for clinicians. During probing, percussion, palpation, and pulp vitality testing, the robot uses multimodal monitoring—speech recognition, facial expression analysis, and movement detection—to identify patient discomfort. If the system detects pain or discomfort, it immediately stops the ongoing operation and proceeds to the next examination. Furthermore, the robot analyses patient reactions using AI algorithms to quantify pain levels and enhance diagnostic consistency.

Workflow design and execution logic of the intelligent oral examination robot

To realize the standardization and efficiency of clinical oral examination procedures, the intelligent oral examination robot constructs a multi-module collaborative working system that integrates image acquisition and recognition, positioning, motion control, and data analysis and feedback. Its workflow covers the entire oral examination process, ensuring intelligent, automated, and safe operation. During oral examination, every command issued to the mechanical arm must be based on professional analysis of data obtained from the image system, speech recognition system, and tactile sensors, together with processing by the artificial intelligence module. Before each mechanical action, the system must notify the patient through voice prompts of the potential discomfort that may occur. During examination, the system activates multimodal pain-response monitoring—integrating facial expression recognition, audio analysis, and pupil-dilation monitoring—to track patient facial expressions and behaviours. These include pain-related facial expressions, sounds such as groaning or sharp inhalation, and immediate pupil dilation. When the robot detects signs of patient discomfort, the end-effector of the mechanical arm immediately withdraws from the examination area to ensure patient safety.

Preparation stage

When the system is in standby mode, the robot enters the initialization configuration stage: the external vision system is activated, and the high-definition camera is turned on to prepare for facial recognition and spatial positioning. Meanwhile, the mechanical arm performs joint homing, and the end-effector returns to its preset reset position to prevent accidental contact. The image-processing unit enters a standby mode, awaiting activation to load deep image-recognition algorithms. The purpose of this preparation stage is to ensure that the device enters a stable working state.

Establishment of facial and intraoral coordinate systems

When the patient sits in front of the device, the system automatically enters the patient identification and positioning stage. The external camera performs deep facial recognition to confirm identity and completes facial landmark–based coordinate calibration. Guided by voice prompts, the patient maintains a steady head position to establish a stable reference posture for forming the facial coordinate system for oral examination. Using facial landmark recognition and region segmentation algorithms, the system generates the facial coordinate system. With the high-definition camera capturing images, it first obtains extraoral facial data to complete inspection and palpation of the head and face. Then, following voice instructions for the patient to open their mouth, the visual recognition system identifies the lip boundaries. After the mechanical arm safely enters the oral cavity, the end-mounted high-definition camera obtains images of intraoral soft and hard tissues. After 3D modelling, the intraoral coordinate system is established, providing precise positional reference for subsequent mechanical arm path planning. Below, the intraoral examination logic is illustrated through examples of caries, tooth defects, pulpitis, and oral mucosal diseases.

Intraoral examination logic for dental caries

After safely establishing the intraoral 3D coordinate system, the high-definition camera and laser scanner on the mechanical arm scan the dentition comprehensively. Using the integrated Mask R-CNN model, the system performs real-time analysis of captured images, focusing on caries-prone areas such as pits and fissures, proximal surfaces, and cervical regions. It identifies visual features such as chalky-white enamel changes, brown spots, or visible cavities, and marks the 3D coordinates of all suspicious areas within the established intraoral coordinate system.137,138 For regions where inspection results are uncertain or early lesions are suspected, the robot activates optical-enhanced imaging. Using fluorescence or near-infrared optical modules at the end-effector, the system emits specific wavelengths of light and analyses returned fluorescence to quantify demineralization and visualize early invisible lesions using pseudocolour imaging, significantly improving diagnostic sensitivity. Next, the robot instructs the mechanical arm to perform intelligent probing and texture evaluation of visually or optically confirmed suspicious points. The mechanical arm extends a probing tip equipped with micro flexible pressure sensors to apply constant low force on the lesion surface. By analysing real-time force–displacement curves, the system detects 'falling-in' sensations and softened textures. When probing confirms the presence of caries and 3D positional analysis suggests involvement of deep dentin or proximity to the pulp, the system automatically activates the cold test. The temperature-controlled probe at the end-effector applies a standardized instantaneous 0°C cold stimulus to the affected tooth and to the contralateral healthy reference tooth. Facial micro-expression recognition and patient feedback allow the system to record the pulp response threshold and pain duration, providing an objective assessment of pulp vitality. Before examination, the voice interaction system informs the patient in detail that the robot will perform examination procedures and instructs them to keep their head stable and body relaxed. Finally, the system performs multimodal data fusion and intelligent grading. It integrates visual colour information, fluorescence intensity, tactile texture hardness, and patient expression and behavioural responses. Intraoral examination logic for periodontitis

To comprehensively evaluate overall periodontal health, the system first performs inspection, palpation, percussion, and mobility tests sequentially for each tooth. The high-definition camera analyses gingival colour and morphological changes in real time. Within the established 3D intraoral coordinate system, the periodontal probe equipped on the mechanical arm moves to the periodontal region. With a probing force of 20 g, the robot conducts periodontal probing. If multimodal pain detection captures a pain response, the examination stops immediately and the sensitive site is marked. Next, percussion is performed using the force-sensing percussion tip. Standard percussion forces are applied and compared with contralateral healthy teeth to objectively evaluate periodontal tissue inflammation.

After completing basic periodontal checks, the system automatically enters pulp vitality testing. The mechanical arm cleans and isolates the tooth surface, and the electric pulp test probe makes stable contact with the tooth. The system then outputs gradually increasing microcurrent. When a pain reaction is detected, the system records the current value and compares it with that of the healthy contralateral tooth to assess pulp nerve responsiveness. After all examinations are completed, the system integrates multimodal data and performs etiological-assisted analysis. Combining robot examination data with initial patient consultation information, the system constructs logical associations to infer major causative factors and provide key diagnostic support for designing targeted treatment plans.

Intraoral examination logic for pulpitis

Based on the initial information collected during patient consultation, the system preliminarily locates the primary pain region, and the mechanical arm first scans the corresponding teeth. Inspection is performed using the high-definition camera and visual recognition algorithms to detect morphological and colour changes such as caries or cracks that may involve the pulp. Next, standardized probing and quantitative percussion are performed sequentially. The force-controlled probe examines the tooth structure gradually from marginal regions to deeper areas, recording pain responses. Percussion evaluates apical tissue status. Then, the temperature vitality test is conducted. The temperature-controlled probe applies instantaneous cold or heat stimuli, and the system records the delay time between stimulus and response to distinguish reversible from irreversible pulpitis. Finally, electric pulp testing is performed as previously described. During all examinations, multimodal monitoring remains active. When signs of pain are detected, the mechanical arm immediately stops operation to ensure patient safety.

Intraoral examination logic for oral mucosal diseases

The examination of oral mucosal diseases follows a systematic progression. First, the high-definition camera on the mechanical arm captures panoramic images of the entire oral mucosa. Using the integrated DenseNet AI model, the system performs real-time preliminary screening to identify abnormal colour, morphology, and texture regions.139,140 If suspicious lesions are detected, narrow-band imaging (NBI) mode is activated to enhance visualization of mucosal vascular patterns. AI algorithms analyse NBI vascular morphology to assist in evaluating malignancy risk. For areas remaining uncertain or showing high-risk features, autofluorescence examination is performed. Blue light excites natural tissue fluorescence: normal mucosa appears bright green, while precancerous or cancerous tissues—with altered metabolism—show fluorescence loss.141,142 The system finally integrates all obtained data and automatically generates a structured report including lesion location, characteristics, and risk classification to support clinical decision-making.

Intraoral examination logic for oral and maxillofacial surgery

For oral and maxillofacial examination, the system first uses the high-definition imaging module to obtain 3D facial scans to detect facial symmetry, swelling, and discolouration. The mechanical arm performs standardized palpation using flexible pressure sensors to quantify tissue characteristics including hardness, elasticity modulus, and tenderness, identifying inflammation, fluid accumulation, fractures, or masses. After extraoral and intraoral evaluation, the system enters the functional movement analysis stage. Using the high-definition camera, the system records mandibular motion trajectories and measures mouth-opening degree and pattern. Flexible tactile sensors on the mechanical arm perform palpation of the temporomandibular joint to detect pain responses.

Challenges of oral examination robots

As described earlier, with the development of robotic and artificial intelligence technologies, medical robots are no longer uncommon, such as intelligent prosthetics and surgical robots. In the current context where surgical robotics has become a rapidly growing field, systems represented by the Da Vinci robot are mainly applied in clinical surgical procedures. Various types of dental robots are also being actively developed. As the first step in dental treatment, oral examination is both crucial and labour-intensive. Using robots to replace the standardized repetitive tasks aligns with the trend of high-technology development. The authors believe that the main challenges and innovation points for the development of oral examination robots are as follows:

Multimodal emotional human–robot interaction

During oral examination, robots must construct a human–robot interaction model that can understand communication across various interactive scenarios, thereby facilitating natural communication with patients.143,144 The inquiry service supported by such an interaction model should possess greater 'flexibility'.145 When patients cannot describe their symptoms with professional terminology, or when their responses do not follow the predesigned questioning path of the robotic system, the human–robot interaction model must have the capacity to respond more flexibly.146 This requires not only the integration of sufficient professional medical data during training but also the enhancement of the robot’s ability to reason across multimodal information. At present, robots that rely solely on visual signals (image data) to conduct independent and invisible reasoning and directly output results to clinicians often struggle to achieve adequate accuracy in medicine and are less likely to gain clinician acceptance. With advancements in statistical modelling, visual and speech recognition have become the most direct modes for interacting with personal and household robots. Significant progress has been made in speech recognition, enabling robots to interpret auditory input signals and execute necessary operations for natural language–based interaction.147 For medical robots, future multimodal data should incorporate information from tactile, temperature, and olfactory sensors to achieve deeper and more accurate understanding of patient physiological states and responses.148, 149, 150 It is important to note that existing clinical medical robots do not yet include human–robot interaction capabilities. Human–robot interaction models for medical applications require the deep involvement of medical experts. R&D teams with interdisciplinary backgrounds must iteratively refine the system to ensure that all outputs strictly follow medical logic. Meanwhile, during actual application, attention must be given to the frequency of newly emerging medical terminology, incorporating such new terms into the model’s 'knowledge base' to avoid delays in medical knowledge updates.

Innovation and development of flexible tactile sensors

Currently, sensory devices—represented by sensors—are becoming increasingly similar to human sensory organs (vision, touch, hearing). Sensor technology forms the basis for robotic control, interaction, and intelligent behaviour. Tactile perception contains a large amount of information and reflects various physical attributes of the contact target, such as position, shape, stiffness, softness, texture, and thermal conductivity.157 Robotic technology became a research hotspot in the 1970s, but tactile sensor technology was still in an early stage at that time. Early tactile sensors focused only on detecting contact pressure magnitude and identifying contact shapes. Although resistive, capacitive, and piezoelectric tactile sensors were already available in experimental form by 1990, their performance was limited by the material properties and data-processing capability of that era. They could only perform basic shape and temperature detection and lacked the ability to distinguish complex textures, material properties, or dynamic pressure variations, making it difficult to simulate the richness of human tactile perception. Nevertheless, these early explorations laid the groundwork for later advancements in flexible electronics and biomimetic tactile systems, pushing tactile perception from simple mechanical detection toward intelligent sensing.158 By the late 1990s, with the development of flexible sensor technologies, new materials, and new fabrication processes,159 flexible tactile sensors gradually entered the field of medical robotics. New flexible tactile sensing devices—represented by flexible, lightweight, scalable, and multifunctional electronic 'skins'—have become a research hotspot in current medical robotics.151,160 In 1998, D. J. Beebe and his team developed a flexible silicon-based tactile sensor using a Torlon dome structure. Human thumb experiments showed that the 1.5 mm dome height significantly improved sensitivity, allowing differentiation of loading directions and individual differences. In dynamic-force tracking, the sensor exhibited user-specific error characteristics, providing a practical solution for wearable tactile sensing devices.161 It should be noted that as the most newly emerging area of biomimetic sensing, tactile sensing faces three major bottlenecks: 1) The diffuse perception mechanism of touch fundamentally conflicts with the centralized sensor architecture of engineering systems: This requires large-area flexible sensing arrays and distributed data fusion, which are technically complex; 2) Tactile sensing involves multiple physical signals—shape, texture, friction, and deformation—coupled together during contact: The resulting nonlinear responses make it extremely difficult to separately extract each physical parameter from mixed signals; 3) Current flexible electronic technologies cannot replicate the mechanical properties and receptor distribution of human skin: Limitations in both hardware and software limit the ability to process tactile information efficiently, resulting in substantial performance gaps between current tactile sensors and biological skin in sensitivity, dynamic range, and power consumption. Sterilization and infection control challenges of intraoral end-effectors

In clinical applications, oral examination robots inevitably come into direct contact with patients’ oral mucosa, teeth, saliva, blood, and other biological media. Therefore, the sterilization and infection control of end-effectors and their associated components are critical prerequisites for clinical feasibility. Compared with conventional dental instruments, robotic end-effectors are structurally more complex, often integrating cameras, light sources, sensors, circuit interfaces, and micro-actuators, making it difficult to apply standard high-temperature and high-pressure sterilization procedures. Repeated disinfection may lead to material degradation, deformation, or reduced sensitivity of encapsulated sensors, which in turn can compromise image acquisition, force feedback, and operational stability. Future development should therefore go beyond functional design to establish reprocessing systems that meet clinical infection control standards in dentistry. These may include detachable disposable tips, modular sterile barrier designs, sterilization-resistant packaging materials, and standardized protocols for cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization.

Patient acceptance, anxiety response, and adaptation for special populations

The successful clinical adoption of oral examination robots depends not only on technological maturity but also on patient acceptance. During oral examinations, patients often experience anxiety, fear, and heightened gag reflexes. The introduction of a robotic arm into the oral cavity, along with contact-based operations and mechanical noise, may further exacerbate these responses. This issue is particularly pronounced in special populations such as children, older adults, and individuals with disabilities. Children may exhibit low compliance and fear toward unfamiliar devices; elderly patients may present with cognitive decline, sensory impairments, and limited neck mobility; and patients with disabilities may have specific needs related to communication, positioning, and emergency response. Accordingly, future designs should emphasize inclusivity and personalized adaptation, incorporating gentler voice guidance, smoother motion control, more flexible positioning support, and, when necessary, mechanisms for manual override. Balancing examination efficiency with patient comfort and trust remains a critical challenge for clinical implementation.

Cost-effectiveness and healthcare accessibility

To achieve capabilities such as imaging, sensing, navigation, interaction, and intelligent analysis, oral examination robots typically require integration of high-performance robotic arms, precision sensors, optical systems, and computing platforms, significantly increasing development, procurement, and maintenance costs. Beyond the initial investment, ongoing expenses include consumables, sterilization turnover, performance calibration, software updates, and data security maintenance. For primary healthcare institutions, small and medium-sized clinics, and resource-limited regions, the financial burden of such advanced systems remains substantial. Large-scale clinical deployment may therefore require governmental funding and policy support.

From a broader perspective, if oral examination robots can be progressively implemented in primary care settings, schools, eldercare institutions, and remote areas, their value could extend beyond auxiliary diagnostics to encompass oral health screening, disease risk prediction, long-term follow-up management, and health education. For populations such as children, the elderly, individuals with limited mobility, and those in underserved regions, such systems have the potential to enhance accessibility and coverage of oral healthcare services, facilitating early detection, early intervention, and timely treatment.

Technical limitations in moist, contaminated, and dynamic intraoral environments

Unlike controlled and relatively clean laboratory conditions, the real intraoral environment is characterized by confined space, high humidity, contamination, and continuous dynamic changes, posing significant challenges for robotic systems. Saliva, gingival crevicular fluid, and blood continuously cover tooth and mucosal surfaces, affecting visual imaging quality and potentially interfering with fluorescence, near-infrared imaging, and certain sensor modalities. Lens fogging, increased surface reflections, and liquid adhesion can introduce image artifacts, thereby reducing the accuracy of lesion detection.

Meanwhile, patients inevitably exhibit swallowing, tongue movement, jaw fatigue, and defensive micro-movements during examination. Within the confined oral cavity, these subtle movements may be amplified, leading to instrument displacement, abnormal contact forces, or positioning errors, thus affecting the consistency and safety of examinations. Future systems must therefore advance in anti-contamination design, rapid defogging, real-time motion compensation, compliant control, and robust perception algorithms to effectively adapt to the complexity of real clinical intraoral environments.

Insufficient evidence base and gaps in clinical validation

Although oral examination robots have the theoretical potential to improve standardization, repeatability, and precision, direct clinical evidence supporting their effectiveness remains limited. Most existing studies on robotic accuracy are still confined to laboratory settings, with a lack of large-scale, prospective clinical studies in real-world oral examination scenarios. In particular, for specific applications such as caries screening, periodontal probing, early detection of mucosal diseases, and pulp vitality testing, the extent to which robotic systems can outperform experienced dental practitioners in terms of accuracy, consistency, and efficiency has yet to be systematically validated.

Currently, available evidence is largely derived from research on dental implant robots and artificial intelligence-assisted caries detection. Studies on implant robots indicate that robotic assistance can provide high precision and repeatability in preoperative planning and trajectory execution. Similarly, AI-based caries detection has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in certain datasets. While these findings suggest the potential of intelligent systems to enhance diagnostic precision, they cannot be directly equated with the clinical maturity of oral examination robots. Future research should therefore establish standardized evaluation metrics and conduct multicentre, real-world studies to comprehensively assess their safety, accuracy, efficiency, and patient tolerance.

Outlook

Oral medicine is entering a new era characterized by robotic assistance and data-driven medical development. Robots can replace dentists in performing the complex yet essential procedures of oral examination and can generate individualized data. In the new era in which oral examination robots are integrated with artificial intelligence, intelligent diagnostic workflows and data management will make traditional oral diagnosis and treatment more intelligent and modernized, thereby improving the overall quality of oral care. The application and development of intelligent robots will, to a certain extent, guide oral medicine toward greater automation and intelligence. With ongoing research progress, oral examination robots will undoubtedly be developed and gradually applied clinically due to their repeatability, high efficiency, and automated characteristics. Future development of oral examination robots should focus on strengthening collaboration between medical and engineering disciplines. Dental experts should provide the core principles and procedures of oral examination for robot development, while engineering experts should design oral examination robots that meet clinical needs based on these principles and artificial intelligence technologies.

During the preparation of this review, several new achievements in robotic research were released by teams in China—for example, orthognathic surgery robots and the world’s first robot that integrates both bone grafting and implant placement. Dental robotic technology is undergoing rapid and 'explosive' development rather than gradual progression. Given the current accelerating pace of technological breakthroughs, the intelligent oral examination robot envisioned in this paper is expected to be realized and put into clinical application in the near future.
