Microfluidics-based engineered silver nanoparticles to control growth and biofilm formation in bacterial pathogens causing dental infection
Authors: M. Annish Shabiya, S. Ranjani, S. Hemalatha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dental infections caused by Streptococcus mutans and Enterococcus faecalis are major contributors to dental caries and root canal infections due to their strong biofilm forming ability across all age groups. This present study aimed to develop a microfluidics-assisted green synthesis approach for the fabrication of Clitoria ternatea-derived silver nanoparticles (CtAgNPs) and to evaluate their antibacterial and antibiofilm potential in dental pathogens. The bioactive compounds present in Clitoria ternatea floral extract act as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent within a microfluidics system. The synthesized CtAgNPs were characterized to confirm their physicochemical properties. UV-Visible spectroscopy reveals the surface plasmon resonance peak at 423 nm, FESEM imaging showed the spherical morphology with an average particle size of 151.6 nm, and a zeta potential of − 26.1 mV, indicating good colloidal stability. FT-IR analysis confirmed the presence of phytochemical-derived functional groups on the surface of CtAgNPs. Toxicity assessment using zebrafish (Danio rerio) embryos confirmed the biocompatibility and non-toxic nature of CtAgNPs. CtAgNPs demonstrated strong antibacterial activity with MIC values ranging from 3.125 to 6.25 µg/mL and exhibited more than 75% inhibition of biofilm formation in both S. mutans and E. faecalis. Mechanistic investigations revealed that the nanoparticles induced oxidative stress, characterized by increased lipid peroxidation (MDA), reduced antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT and SOD), and leakage of intracellular proteins and sugars, indicating membrane damage. Overall findings demonstrate that microfluidics-assisted green-synthesized CtAgNPs effectively inhibit the growth and biofilm formation of dental pathogens, highlighting their potential as an eco-friendly nanotherapeutic strategy for the prevention and management of dental biofilm-associated infections.

Introduction

Nanotechnology enables the manipulation of materials at the nanoscale (1–100 nm), where they exhibit unique physico-chemical and biological properties, including enhanced surface reactivity, mechanical strength, and optical behavior. These features have led to wide applications in medicine, environmental science, and biotechnology, particularly in antimicrobial strategies. In recent years, eco-friendly methods of nanoparticles synthesis have gained significant attention. Conventional chemical and physical synthesis methods are often expensive, require high energy input, and involve hazardous substances. To overcome these drawbacks, green synthesis approaches using biological sources including plant extracts, bacteria, fungi, and algae, are emerging as safe, sustainable, and cost-effective alternatives1.

Several plant-based AgNPs have demonstrated a wide range of biomedical applications, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anticancer, and anti-biofilm properties. Despite the abundance of biological sources for green synthesis of nanoparticles, namely leaves, roots, microbes, and algae, the floral extract of Clitoria ternatea was specifically selected in this study due to the high concentration of anthocyanins, flavonoids, and phenolic acid compounds, which are known to act as potent reducing and capping agents. These phytoconstituents not only facilitate efficient synthesis of nanoparticles, however it significantly enhances the antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory potential. Moreover, the floral matrix of C. ternatea provides a more uniform biochemical composition compared to other plant parts, leading to more consistent nanoparticles characteristics during microfluidics-assisted synthesis. Based on these characteristics, we hypothesize that silver nanoparticles synthesized by using Clitoria ternatea flower extract via a microfluidics-assisted approach will exhibit superior structural integrity, biocompatibility, and enhanced efficacy against dental pathogens, namely Streptococcus mutans and Enterococcus faecalis, particularly in terms of antibacterial and anti-biofilm activity2.

In comparison to the conventional batch methods, microfluidics reactors offer more advantages, including reduced reagent consumption, by-product formation, and facilitate rapid nucleation and growth. Microfluidics reactors with serpentine geometry provide precise control over fluid mixing, reaction kinetics, and thermal conditions at a nano level. This offers controlled and reproducible development of monodisperse nanoparticles3. The serpentine channel designs in microfluidics increase convection mixing by generating secondary Dean vortices, thereby leading to a narrower residence time distribution and tighter particle size control without the application of external energy. Furthermore, these reactors can be scaled up using parallelization and include integrated online monitoring for process optimization. Green synthesis of nanoparticles by using serpentine microfluidics integrates the bioactive compounds extracted from the floral extract with engineered precision in terms of uniformity, stability, and functional performance4,5.

Dental caries is more prevalent in lower-income populations with greater socioeconomic disparities. Dental caries start with the demineralization of enamel and dentin due to enhanced acid production from carbohydrates by Streptococcus mutans6. Enterococcus faecalis plays a key role in persistent endodontic infections and demonstrates high resistance to conventional antiseptics and antibiotics. These pathogens develop extracellular polymeric substances and form complex biofilms that prevent the penetration of therapeutic agents. In addition to oral diseases, S. mutans and E. faecalis are implicated in subacute bacterial endocarditis and systemic inflammatory conditions associated with cardiovascular and metabolic disorders7. The conventional approaches for the management of dental caries, including scaling, chlorhexidine rinses, and fluoride applications, have been successful in reducing surface plaque. However, they are not effective against deeper biofilms. Several new strategies, including photodynamic therapy, have been explored across the globe; however, dental infections continue to pose significant health problems across the demography8. In this context, silver nanoparticles are drawing growing interest because of their strong, multi-targeted antimicrobial properties and ability to disrupt resilient oral biofilms9. In response to these issues, the present study investigates the synthesis of silver nanoparticles by using the floral extract of Clitoria ternatea by employing a microfluidics-based approach and evaluating its antimicrobial and anti-biofilm efficacy in dental pathogens, namely Streptococcus mutans and Enterococcus faecalis.

Materials and methods

Extraction, phytochemical, and GC-MS analysis of Clitoria ternatea aqueous floral extract

Fresh Clitoria ternatea flowers were collected from Sri Chitra Nursery Garden, West Tambaram, Chennai (12.935461° N, 80.122322° E). Clitoria ternatea flowers were identified and authenticated by Dr. D. Narasimhan, Botanist, Chennai, and a voucher specimen was deposited at the School of Life Sciences, BSACIST, Chennai (Accession number: SLS-BSAU-25005). The flowers were shade-dried at room temperature for 5 days. 10 g of dried petals were boiled in 200 mL of autoclaved distilled water for 10 min, cooled, and filtered through Whatman No.1 filter paper. The filtrate was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min. Supernatant was again filtered three times by using Whatman no 1 filter paper to obtain a clear extract10. A set of qualitative phytochemical analyses was performed to identify the phytoconstituents present in Clitoria ternatea flower extract11.

GC-MS (Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry) analysis was carried out to identify the detailed qualitative and quantitative data of phytocompounds present in floral extract. The aqueous extract was concentrated and lyophilized into a fine powder. Lyophilized powder was diluted with methanol (1:1) and filtered (0.22 μm) by using a syringe filter. One microliter of the sample was injected into the GC–MS system (PerkinElmer Clarus 680/600 T GC/MS instrument, Standard capillary columns) and mass spectra were recorded in EI mode (70 eV), and compounds were identified by matching retention time and fragmentation patterns with the NIST library10.

Microfluidics system-mediated synthesis and characterization of Clitoria Ternatea-mediated silver nanoparticles (CtAgNPs)

Clitoria ternatea-mediated silver nanoparticles (CtAgNPs) were synthesized by using a microfluidics system embedded with serpentine micromixer with the dimensions (57 × 32 × 6 μm), and the total inlet-to-outlet residence time of the microfluidics serpentine mixer was 2.6 s at a flow rate of 10 mL h⁻¹. With eight serpentine loops, the residence time per loop was approximately 0.32 s. Using syringe pumps, 1:1 ratio of (1mM) silver nitrate solution and Clitoria ternatea aqueous flower extract are fed into a Polydimethylsiloxane microfluidics system. The synthesized CtAgNPs were characterized by using UV- Vis, FTIR, FESEM, EDAX, DLS, and Zeta potential10,12. Immediately after observing the prominent colour change, synthesized CtAgNPs were subjected to UV-Vis spectroscopic analysis to confirm the synthesis of nanoparticles. Spectral scanning was performed between the range of 200–800 nm by using a JASCO V-730 spectrophotometer with 1 cm quartz cuvettes, and baseline correction was done by using distilled water. Once the synthesis of CtAgNPs was confirmed by using UV visible spectroscopy analysis, the pure form of nanoparticles was obtained by subjecting the colloidal solution to repeated centrifugation 3 times at 6000 rpm for 10 min. The pellets were pooled together and washed with autoclaved distilled water to remove unreduced silver nitrate. The pellets were dried to fine powder and subjected to Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) analysis at a magnification of 66.1 kx using a TESCAN MIRA3 instrument. The working distance was set at 10.55 mm, and the SE (Secondary Electron) detector was used. Samples were imaged at an accelerating voltage of 10.00 kV to ensure optimal surface resolution. FESEM coupled with EDAX analyzer were used to study the elemental composition of synthesized CtAgNPs. Powder form of CtAgNPs were used for FTIR analysis by using a JASCO infrared spectrometer by scanning between the range from 399 to 4000 cm⁻¹ with a data resolution of 0.964 cm⁻¹. The fine powders of CtAgNPs (1 mg/ml) were dissolved in 10% DMSO and subjected to ultrasonication to obtain a colloidal solution with uniform dispersion of nanoparticles. Colloidal solution of CtAgNPs was subjected to Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) coupled with Zeta Potential analysis (Anton Paar Litesizer™) to analyze the particle size based on measuring hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index, and surface charge. Measurements were conducted at 25 °C in aqueous suspension, with all readings recorded in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Evaluating the toxicity of CtAgNPs in Danio rerio embryos

To evaluate biological compatibility of CtAgNPs, toxicity assessments were carried out in zebrafish (Danio rerio) embryos as an in vivo model. The toxicity study of CtAgNPs in the embryos of Danio rerio was reported following ARRIVE guidelines and was approved by institutional ethics committee of Sri Lakshmi Narayana Institute of Medical Sciences, Puducherry (NO.IEC/C-P/11/2023) in accordance with the institute guidelines and regulations. Different concentrations of CtAgNPs (1.5 µg/ml, 3.125 µg/ml, 6.2 µg/ml, 12 µg/ml, 25 µg/ml, 50 µg/ml, 75 µg/ml, 100 µg/ml) were selected to analyze dose-dependent effects. Wild-type zebrafish embryos were obtained from Tarun Fish Farm, Manimangalam, Chennai (601 301). Embryos were maintained in E3 embryo medium (5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl₂, 0.33 mM MgSO₄, pH 7.2) under standard laboratory conditions at 28 ± 1 °C with a 14:10-hour light-dark cycle. Danio rerio embryos were treated with different concentrations of CtAgNPs (1.5 µg/ml, 3.125 µg/ml, 6.2 µg/ml, 12 µg/ml, 25 µg/ml, 50 µg/ml, 75 µg/ml, 100 µg/ml) and control groups were maintained without treatment. The experiment was performed in triplicates by following OECD Guideline No.236 Fish Embryo Acute Toxicity (FET) Test. The LC50 of CtAgNPs was calculated using Probit Analysis (Finney, D. J. 1952)13. The embryos were observed up to 96 hpf by using a compound microscope. Parameters including hatching rate, heartbeat, morphological deformities, and survival rate were recorded to detect potential developmental or physiological abnormalities. The toxicity assessment using zebrafish embryos provided essential data on the biocompatibility and safety of CtAgNPs, supporting their use in eco-friendly applications11,14.

Investigating the antibacterial efficacy of Clitoria ternatea-derived silver nanoparticles (CtAgNPs)

Synthesized CtAgNPs were evaluated for antimicrobial activity using 96-well plates15. LB broth was filled in all wells, and CtAgNPs were serially diluted from the concentration of 0.39 µg/ml to 100 µg/ml. Each well was inoculated with 2 µl of bacterial culture, while controls included strains in LB broth. Ampicillin (25 µg/ml) served as a positive control. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and MIC was determined by visual turbidity assessment at 24 h. Growth kill curves were analyzed by measuring absorbance at 600 nm at 12 h and 24 h of incubation. MBC was determined using the drop plate method and identifying the concentration of CtAgNPs that completely kill the bacterial growth after 24 h of incubation period15.

Evaluation of antibiofilm property of CtAgNPs

Biofilm formation was evaluated using the Microtiter plate assay12,15, 100 µL of LB broth was added to each corresponding well, 100 µL of serially diluted CtAgNPs (1 mg/mL), and ampicillin were added. 2 µL of bacterial cultures are inoculated into each well. Plates were incubated in a shaker for 48 h at 37 degrees Celsius. The used medium was discarded, and plates were washed. After complete air drying, 0.1% crystal violet (100 µL) staining solution was added and kept for 15 min. Plates were washed with running water and dried. 100 µL of glacial acetic acid (30%) was added to destain the bound crystal violet. The absorbance was noted at 580 nm using a spectrophotometer. Absorbance value provided quantitative data on the degree of biofilm formation in both treated and untreated bacterial strains12,15.

Investigating the mechanistic action of CtAgNPs

To determine the impact of Clitoria ternatea-derived silver nanoparticles (CtAgNPs) on bacterial cell membrane integrity, Streptococcus mutans and Enterococcus faecalis were treated with CtAgNPs and ampicillin at their respective minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC). After incubation at 37 °C for 6 h, cells were centrifuged, and the collected supernatant was analyzed for leakage of proteins and sugars by using Bradford and Anthrone assays, respectively15. MDA and GSH are measured to observe oxidative damage caused by CtAgNPs. TBA and SDS were added to CtAgNPs and ampicillin-treated cells and incubated at 95 °C. The whole content was centrifuged. Supernatant was collected and the absorbance was measured at 530 nm for the measurement of MDA (lipid peroxidation biomarker) as described12,9. The bacterial lysates were treated with Ellman’s reagent to determine GSH levels by observing the absorbance at 412 nm.12,9, Catalase (CAT) activity was determined by tracking hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) breakdown at 240 nm. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured by recording the reduction of NBT at 560 nm, which corresponds to the ability of bacterial cells to neutralize superoxide radicals12,9.

Statistical analysis

All the experimental analyses were performed in triplicate, and statistical significance was determined by using the student t-test. Where * Denotes T-test (*P < 0.001).

The FTIR spectrum of Clitoria ternatea aqueous extract showed several characteristic absorption peaks indicating the presence of different functional groups. A broad peak at 3285.14 cm⁻¹ corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations, suggesting hydroxyl groups. Peaks observed at 2921.63 cm⁻¹ and 1627.63 cm⁻¹ represent C–H stretching of aliphatic compounds and C = O/amide I vibrations, respectively. Additional peaks at 1411.64 cm⁻¹, 1030.77 cm⁻¹, 630.609 cm⁻¹, and 510.08 cm⁻¹ indicate C–H bending, C–O stretching, and possible aromatic or halogen-associated vibrations (Fig. 2c). The FTIR spectrum of synthesized CtAgNPs revealed distinct functional groups which may be involved in synthesis and stabilization of nanoparticles. The peaks at 1643.35 cm⁻¹ and 1172.72 cm⁻¹ indicate C = O and C–O stretching, suggesting the presence of carbonyl and ester groups from Clitoria ternatea extract. Peaks around 671.23 cm⁻¹ to 439.77 cm⁻¹ confirm C–Br, Ag–O, and C–Cl vibrations, indicating successful synthesis of silver nanoparticles and metal-oxygen bonding. These observations support the effective capping and stabilization of CtAgNPs (Fig. 2d). The high-resolution micrographs of CtAgNPs showed a spherical morphology with particle size between 30.91 nm and 45.00 nm, with an average diameter of approximately 38 nm (Fig. 3a). Figure 3b shows the elemental composition of CtAgNPs with a strong peak at 3 keV, confirming the presence of silver. The EDAX spectrum revealed 63.41% of silver, followed by 16.75% of oxygen, 15.16% of carbon and 4.68% of chlorine (Cl) (Fig. 3c). The zeta potential of the CtAgNPs was observed as − 26.1 mV indicating good colloidal stability due to the electrostatic repulsion (Fig. 4a). The hydrodynamic diameter was measured as 157.1 nm with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 30.7% (Fig. 4b) reveals a moderate uniform size distribution. The diffusion coefficient was 0.6 μm²/s highlighting the mobility of CtAgNPs. The transmittance of 71.4% demonstrates the dispersion stability and optical characteristics of CtAgNPs.

Toxicity assessment of CtAgNPs in Danio rerio embryos

The survival of Danio rerio embryos exposed to CtAgNPs at concentrations ranging from 1 to 100 µg/mL was evaluated at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-fertilization (hpf) (Fig. 5a). The hatching rate of Danio rerio embryos at 48 hpf decreased with increasing concentrations of CtAgNPs, indicating a dose-dependent effect. The 3.125 µg/mL showed a (92%). The hatching rate gradually declined to 75% (6.25 µg/mL), 67% (12.5 µg/mL), and 60% (25 µg/mL). The reduction was observed at 50 µg/mL (42%) and 100 µg/mL (33%), suggesting that higher concentrations of CtAgNPs inhibit embryo hatching (Fig. 5b). The zebrafish embryo toxicity studies revealed a dose-dependent reduction in the survival rate with increasing CtAgNPs concentrations. At 24–72 hpf the survival rate was 91.67% (3.125 µg/mL), 83.33% (6.25 µg/mL), and 75% (12.5–100 µg/mL). At 96 hpf, the survival rate lowered to 83.33% (3.125 µg/mL), 58.33% (6.25 and 12.5 µg/mL), 50% (25 and 50 µg/mL), and 41.67% (100 µg/mL) (Fig. 5c). At lower concentrations (1–12.5 µg/mL), embryos exhibited high survival rates with minimal toxicity. Mild reductions in survival were observed at higher concentrations (50–100 µg/mL), with a gradual decline in survival over extended exposure times. The heartbeat of zebrafish embryos was observed at 48 h post-fertilization (hpf). Cardiac activity was visible under a compound microscope, indicating normal physiological development. No abnormalities in heart rate or rhythm were detected throughout the study. The (LC₅₀) of CtAgNPs was calculated as 35.637 µg/mL. Observable sublethal or teratogenic effects, including coagulated embryos, lack of somite formation, non-detachment of the tail, and lack of heartbeat was not observed throughout the exposure period.

Investigating the antibacterial and biofilm inhibition effect of CtAgNPs in dental pathogens

The antibacterial property of CtAgNPs was evaluated in two important dental pathogens namely S. mutans and E. faecalis. Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) of CtAgNPs was observed at 6.25 and 3.125 µg/ml, respectively in S. mutans and E. faecalis. Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) of CtAgNPs was observed at 12.5 and 6.25 µg/ml respectively in S. mutans and E. faecalis (Table 2). The tolerance level was recorded as 2 for both S. mutans and E. faecalis.

Exploring the mechanistic action of CtAgNPs in dental pathogens

The mode of antibacterial activity exhibited by CtAgNPs was evaluated by measuring the quantity of proteins and sugars leaked from E. faecalis and S. mutans after their exposure to CtAgNPs treatment. As shown in Fig. 7a, bacterial cells exposed to CtAgNPs showed more protein leakage compared to the control and the ampicillin treatment. In E. faecalis, protein leakage reached 45.4 µg/mg with CtAgNPs, compared to 21 µg/mg in the control and 37 µg/mg in ampicillin treated cells. Similar trends were also seen in S. mutans, where CtAgNPs induced protein leakage of 36 µg/mg, which is higher than the ampicillin treated cells (31.5 µg/mg) and control (8.5 µg/mg).

Similar to protein leakage, Fig. 7b shows that CtAgNPs increased the leakage of sugars in both the tested bacteria. In E. faecalis, sugar leakage was 21 µg/mg with CtAgNPs, compared to 14 µg/mg with ampicillin and 7.6 µg/mg in control. S. mutans showed a similar pattern, with 24 µg/mg of sugar leakage for CtAgNPs, 15.4 µg/mg for ampicillin and 7.7 µg/mg in the control cells.

To explore further, the oxidative stress response was assessed by measuring Malondialdehyde (MDA) and Glutathione (GSH) levels. As shown in Fig. 7c, cells with CtAgNPs treatment showed a greater increase in MDA levels. In E. faecalis, the MDA levels reached 5.9 µM/ml with CtAgNPs treatment, compared to 4.0 µM/ml with ampicillin treatment and only 1.5 µM/ml in the control cells. A similar pattern was seen in S. mutans, where CtAgNPs raised MDA levels to 5.3 µM/ml, which is much higher than the ampicillin treatment 3.0 µM/ml and 1.0 µM/ml in control. Further observations into the antioxidant defenses of the bacteria, GSH levels were measured. In E. faecalis, GSH dropped to around 44% after CtAgNPs treatment, compared to 62% in the ampicillin-treated cells, as represented in Fig. 7d. In S. mutans, GSH levels reduced to 48% with CtAgNPs treatment, while ampicillin-treated cells were observed as 78%. The activity of two important antioxidant enzymes, namely SOD and CAT were also recorded after CtAgNPs treatment. As shown in Fig. 7e, SOD activity dropped significantly in both E. faecalis and S. mutans when exposed to CtAgNPs. In E. faecalis, SOD activity lowered to 66% with ampicillin and dropped even further to 22% with CtAgNPs treatment. A similar trend was seen in S. mutans, where SOD activity decreased to 56.4% with ampicillin and 27% with CtAgNPs. Figure 7f shows that CAT activity followed the same pattern. In E. faecalis, the CAT activity dropped to 52.1% with ampicillin and 21% with CtAgNPs treatment, while in S. mutans, 42% and 20% with ampicillin and CtAgNPs treatment respectively.

Discussion

Phytochemical profile of aqueous floral extract of Clitoria ternatea reveals the presence of flavonoids, phenolic acids, steroids, triterpenoids, and saponins11. Similarly, GC–MS analysis of the aqueous extract confirmed the presence of multiple phytocompounds, indicating a complex profile with several volatile and semi-volatile constituents. These compounds play a crucial role in assisting the process of nucleation and stabilization of silver nanoparticles and enhance the biological activity of the CtAgNPs. The abundance and diversity of these phytochemicals make C. ternatea an excellent choice for green synthesis of nanoparticles5,10. The floral extract of Clitoria ternatea with rich phytocompounds can exhibit a synergistic antimicrobial effect on Streptococcus mutans and Enterococcus faecalis. These phytochemicals are reported to disrupt biofilm formation, bacterial adhesion, and metabolic pathways through multi-targeted mechanisms12.

The visible colour change indicates the effectiveness of Clitoria ternatea floral extract in the reduction of silver ions and stabilization. These results coincide with other studies, which demonstrated successful synthesis of CtAgNPs from Clitoria ternatea blue flowers10. It was reported that one of the features of metallic nanoparticles is SPR, an optical property in which conduction electrons vibrate collectively in response to specific light frequencies. The absorbance peak of CtAgNPs at 400 nm corresponds with the SPR peak of silver nanoparticles16. Aldehydes, terpenoids, flavonoids, amides, ketones, and carboxylic acids act synergistically as reducing and capping agents and promote the stability of nanoparticles. The phytocompounds of floral extract would have facilitated the conversion of Ag⁺ to Ag⁰ by preventing the aggregation during the synthesis of nanoparticles17.

FESEM was used to analyze the surface morphology, size distribution, and structural characteristics of the synthesized CtAgNPs. The relatively narrow range of size distribution and uniform spherical shapes can be attributed to the precise mixing of the solution facilitated by the serpentine micromixer. Similarly, Previously26 it was reported that synthesized CS-AgNPs were spherical in shape with a size ranging from 10 to 50 nm9. Previois study . used FESEM to characterize AgNPs synthesized using Azadirachta indica (neem) leaf extract, revealing well-dispersed nanoparticles with an average size of 20–40 nm18. It was reported that silver nanoparticles were synthesized in a continuous-flow microfluidics system by varying the flow of silver nitrate and reducing solution. This approach enabled precise control over reaction conditions, yielding well-crystallized metallic silver nanoparticles with crystallite sizes of 31–54 nm3.

The Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) analysis confirmed the predominant presence of silver (Ag) with a weight% of 63.41%, indicating successful CtAgNPs synthesis. The peaks corresponding to 15.16% carbon (C), 16.75% oxygen (O), and 4.68% chlorine (Cl) suggest the involvement of phytochemicals and possible residual salts in CtAgNPs stabilization. The strong Ag signal at ~ 3 keV further validates the metallic silver in CtAgNPs. These results coincide with the previous study which revealed that the CT-AgNPs have higher silver content, and it is noted in a peak at 3keV10. The presence of silver ions in CtAgNPs plays an important role in imparting its antimicrobial properties.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) is a useful analytical technique to identify the functional groups and biomolecules involved in the synthesis and stabilization of nanoparticles. FTIR results of Clitoria ternatea aqueous floral extract revealed their phytochemical-rich profile dominated by unsaturated and oxygen-containing functional groups, flavonoids, anthocyanins, and glycosides, which drive its bioactivity. The presence of O–H functional groups suggest phenolic and flavonoid compounds that contribute to antioxidant activity. The C–H stretching bands indicate aliphatic chains and lipid-related biomolecules present in the extract. The C = O/amide peak may be attributed to proteins or conjugated carbonyl compounds that can act as reducing or stabilizing agents. The C–O and lower wavenumber peaks confirm the presence of alcohols, polysaccharides, and aromatic phytochemicals, supporting the rich phytochemical composition of the aqueous floral extract.

The FTIR spectrum of CtAgNPs indicates the presence of several functional groups involved in nanoparticles synthesis and stabilization. The O–H stretching vibrations suggest the presence of phenols or alcohols that may act as reducing agents. The N–H stretching vibrations indicate amines or amides associated with proteins, which can function as natural capping agents to stabilize the nanoparticles and prevent agglomeration. The C–H stretching vibrations confirm the presence of aliphatic hydrocarbons that may contribute to stabilization. Peaks corresponding to C ≡ C and C = O stretching vibrations indicate the presence of alkynes, nitriles, and carbonyl-containing biomolecules that may act as reducing agent. The C = C, the C–N stretching suggests aromatic compounds. In addition, the C–O stretching vibrations indicate alcohols, esters, or carboxylic acids that contribute to the nanoparticle’s stabilization. Earlier studies also reported similar functional groups in the FTIR analysis of AgNPs synthesized using Azadirachta indica extract, highlighting the role of phenolic compounds and proteins in nanoparticles stabilization19.

In this present study the CtAgNPs has a hydrodynamic diameter of 157.1 nm, which highlights the uniform size distribution in colloidal solution. Repulsion between charged particles plays an important role in enhancing nanoparticles stability by minimizing agglomeration and maintaining dispersion in suspension, as reported by earlier studies. Dynamic Light Scattering confirms the nanoparticles size distribution based on the scattering of light and Brownian motion20. reported that Withania coagulans mediated silver nanoparticles showed a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 26–27 nm with a zeta potential of − 21.4 mV, indicating moderate colloidal stability21.

The zeta potential values ± 30 mV are considered as stable nanoparticles due to the electrostatic repulsion that prevents aggregation. In this present study, CtAgNPs has a zeta potential of − 26.1 mV which is similar to this range, suggesting good colloidal stability which is consistent with previous reports on plant-mediated AgNPs synthesis. Rhododendron arboreum extract-derived AgNPs showed negative electrophoretic mobility, highlighting the role of surface charge in nanoparticles stability22,23. The low conductivity observed in the present study supports stable nanoparticles dispersion, as high ionic strength is known to destabilize nanoparticles suspensions)24. The hydrodynamic size and moderate PDI observed in this study are comparable to AgNPs synthesized by using Moringa oleifera and Azadirachta indica, where similar size distribution and polydispersity are due to phytochemical-mediated synthesis18,23. Transmittance values are also influenced by nanoparticles size and concentration, affecting light scattering properties in colloidal systems25.

The toxicity of CtAgNPs was evaluated using zebrafish embryos following OECD Test Guideline No. 236 Fish Embryo Acute Toxicity (FET) test. The survival rate of Zebrafish embryos exposed to different concentrations ofCtAgNPs was monitored during the experimental period. In the present study, Danio rerio embryos treated with CtAgNPs have no development abnormalities, mortalities or delayed hatching throughout the period of exposure, indicating biocompatibility and non-toxic nature of CtAgNPs. Zebrafish embryos exposed to Citrus fusion–mediated AgNPs (50 µg/mL) showed normal hatching and no detectable developmental defects13. However, previous reports suggest that high concentrations of AgNPs may induce oxidative stress due to silver ion release, leading to reduced survival and developmental disturbances in zebrafish embryos27. The findings indicate that CtAgNPs are biocompatible at lower concentrations, with minimal toxicity compared to chemically synthesized silver nanoparticles. The consistent survival rates at 24 hpf suggest initial tolerance, while gradual reductions beyond 48 hpf reflect a time- and dose-dependent response. The delayed hatching at higher concentrations (25–100 µg/mL) further supports the need for cautious dosage consideration. Despite the delays the overall hatching rate remained relatively consistent, indicating that toxicity remains manageable. This reflects the importance of bioactive compounds in C. ternatea, which help in mitigating the cytotoxic effects associated with exposure to silver nanoparticles.

The CtAgNPs have strong antibacterial effects in S. mutans and E. faecalis compared with the antibiotics ampicillin. The effective anti-bacterial activity is possibly due to the slow release of silver ions and penetration of CtAgNPs into the bacterial cell. The MIC of CtAgNPs was observed at 6.25 and 3.125 µg/ml and MBC of CtAgNPs was observed at 12.5 and 6.25 µg/ml. The tolerance level of 2 for both S. mutans and E. faecalis which indicates that CtAgNPs has strong bactericidal potential. This low tolerance value highlights the potential effectiveness of the CtAgNPs in controlling dental pathogens. This strong antibacterial effect of CtAgNPs is probably because of several mechanisms, including oxidative stress, damaging bacterial membranes, and disturbing their metabolism. Their small size and large surface area help them adhere better to bacteria, leading to more damage and death. In addition, bioactive compounds from C. ternatea, namely polyphenols and flavonoids, contributed towards the antibacterial property by disrupting the cell membrane and inhibiting the functions of several enzymes involved in various important metabolic pathways. Similarly, earlier research reported that MIC and MBC values of 12.5 µg/mL and 25 µg/mL for plant-based AgNPs16. It was also reported MIC and MBC values as low as 6.25 µg/mL and 12.5 µg/mL in Aloe vera-derived nanoparticles24. The antimicrobial activity was evaluated in common oral pathogens, and the AgNPs showed significant inhibitory effects at concentrations as low as 25 µg/mL, with maximum inhibition observed at 100 µg/mL. The nanoparticles exhibited broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, particularly in Streptococcus mutans and Staphylococcus aureus28. Likewise, adding AgNPs to dental materials helps to prevent bacterial attachment and biofilm formation29.

The CtAgNPs exhibit strong antibiofilm activity, making them highly effective against persistent and biofilm-associated infections. Spherical shaped CtAgNPs with large surface area allows stronger interaction with EPS layers of the bacterial biofilms and makes it easier for membrane disruption and cell death21. In addition, the bioactive compounds from C. ternatea could improve their antibiofilm activity synergistically along with silver ions. Overall, these results support the idea that CtAgNPs could be a sustainable alternative to antibiotics for managing biofilms caused by dental pathogens. Their long-lasting eco-friendly synthesis and antibiofilm activity make CtAgNPs a very promising alternative for treating infections related to biofilms. Green nanoparticles exhibited better antibiofilm activity probably due to the damages induced in the bacterial membrane or by interfering the cell signaling pathways, which are necessary for biofilm formation12,15.

One of the mechanistic actions of CtAgNPs reveals that it disrupts the bacterial cell membrane, leading to the leakage of essential intracellular components such as proteins and sugars. The increased release of proteins highlights severe membrane damage and loss of cellular integrity, while the leakage of sugars reveals the breakdown of metabolic balance within the bacterial cells. The elevated levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) confirm that CtAgNPs induce oxidative stress, resulting in lipid peroxidation of the bacterial membrane. This oxidative damage further compromises membrane stability and enhances the leakage of cellular macromolecules. The major decrease in both SOD and CAT activity suggests that CtAgNPs significantly weakens the antioxidant system of bacteria and induce higher oxidative stress and more cellular damage. These results are consistent with the previous finding which investigated Solanum virginianum-mediated silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) in dental pathogens9,12,5. Green-synthesized graphene–silver nanocomposite (GO–Ag) using Trillium govanianum extract and tested in multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus. The nanocomposite displayed MICs of 31.25–125 µg/mL, with biofilm inhibition rates of 80–96%. TEM and live/dead assays showed severe bacterial membrane damage, corroborating membrane integrity disruption. ROS generation was confirmed, indicating oxidative stress as a key killing mechanism. They also observed a marked decrease in antioxidant enzyme activities, including catalase and SOD, indicating oxidative stress30,31. The findings from this study are particularly significant in the context of controlling dental pathogens, namely S. mutans and E. faecalis, which are key contributors to dental caries and endodontic infections. Traditional antibiotics often fail to eliminate these bacteria due to biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance. The potent antibacterial properties of CtAgNPs suggest their potential as alternative therapeutic agents in dental applications. Their ability to induce oxidative stress, disruption of bacterial membranes highlights their effectiveness in combating persistent dental infections.

Conclusion

This study underscores the immense potential of microfluidics-based silver nanoparticles (CtAgNPs), derived from Clitoria ternatea floral extract, as an effective and sustainable approach to combat dental infections. The fusion of green synthesis with microfluidics technology not only ensures precise control over nanoparticles characteristics, however, aligns with eco-friendly and biocompatible standards. Characterization studies confirmed that the CtAgNPs possess ideal size, shape, and stability, while in vivo testing using zebrafish embryos validated their safety at antibacterial concentrations. Importantly, CtAgNPs exhibit strong antibacterial effects in key oral pathogens namely Enterococcus faecalis and Streptococcus mutans, by disrupting their oxidative stress defenses and inhibiting biofilm formation. Overall, CtAgNPs offer a novel and sustainable solution for managing oral pathogens associated with tooth decay and root canal infections. Their potential application as dental care products, including toothpaste, mouthwash, and dental coating, could revolutionize oral hygiene by providing safer and more effective alternatives to antibacterial agents. Future research will be crucial in translating these findings for practical dental treatment.
