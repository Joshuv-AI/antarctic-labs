Uncertainty Quantification of Spline Predictors on Compact Riemannian Manifolds Journal: Nuclear Physics B
Charlie Sire, Mike Pereira

Abstract
To predict smooth physical phenomena from observations, spline interpolation provides an interpretable framework by minimizing an energy functional associated with the Laplacian operator. This work proposes a methodology to construct a spline predictor on a compact Riemannian manifold, while quantifying the uncertainty inherent in the classical deterministic solution. Our approach leverages the equivalence between spline interpolation and universal kriging with a specific covariance kernel. By adopting a Gaussian random field framework, we generate stochastic simulations that reflect prediction uncertainty. However, on compact manifolds, the covariance kernel depends on the generally unknown spectrum of the Laplace-Beltrami operator. To address this, we introduce a finite element approximation based on a triangulation of the manifold. This leads to the use of intrinsic Gaussian Markov Random Fields (GMRF) and allows for the incorporation of anisotropies through local modifications of the Riemannian metric. The method is validated using a temperature study on a sphere, where the operator’s spectrum is known, and is further extended to a test case on a cylindrical surface.



1
Introduction

Thin-plate spline interpolation
[
44
]
is one of the most widely used methods for predicting spatially distributed data. It consists in constructing an interpolating function that exactly matches the observed values at the measurement locations while being as smooth as possible according to a precise mathematical criterion. This notion of optimality, defined through the minimization of a roughness functional, is particularly well suited to settings with few observations in which the underlying phenomenon is expected to vary smoothly over space. However, thin-plate splines yield a single deterministic prediction over the domain and do not directly provide a measure of uncertainty. In many applications, uncertainty quantification is crucial, for instance to assess risk or compute predictive probabilities, which motivates the need for a full predictive distribution rather than a point estimate
[
37
]
. From this perspective, the spline predictor can be shown to be equivalent to the universal kriging predictor, as first established by Matheron
[
29
]
and later exploited for data defined on
\mathbb{R}

[
10
]
and on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
31
,
32
]
.

In this framework, the observations are viewed as a realization of a random field with an unknown mean function and a prescribed covariance structure, that are related to the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Laplacian operator
-\Delta
. Universal kriging then relies on a statistical model to provide not only a prediction but also an associated predictive variance at every point in the domain. To fully characterize this predictive uncertainty, a Gaussian assumption is typically adopted for the prior random field, leading to Gaussian process regression (GPR)
[
48
]
, which enables computation of the posterior distribution of the random field, that is, the conditional distribution given the observed values at the measurement locations. While the predictive mean and variance obtained from kriging and GPR are identical, the latter framework additionally allows for the simulation of posterior realizations of the random field
[
20
]
. These simulations provide a richer representation of uncertainty and facilitate the visualization and quantification of plausible scenarios under the assumed statistical model. Thus, exploiting Gaussian process regression with the appropriate covariance kernel makes it possible to incorporate uncertainty into the spline prediction problem.

However, when the domain of interest is a non-planar surface, several practical challenges arise. Studies on such non-Euclidean domains appear in a variety of contexts, for example in fluid mechanics when analyzing flows around cylinders
[
13
,
16
]
, or in neuroimaging when collecting data on the brain
[
26
]
. In theory, the equivalence between spline interpolation and universal kriging prediction still holds on compact Riemannian manifolds, as formally established in
[
24
]
using the framework of Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS), building on the pioneering work of Wahba on the sphere
[
45
]
. On manifolds, the mean function and covariance kernel underlying this equivalence are derived from the spectral decomposition of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, a natural generalization of the Euclidean Laplacian. In practice, however, the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator are generally unknown for surfaces that are neither planar nor spherical. This lack of spectral information prevents the exact construction of spline predictors and their associated covariance structures for data defined on such manifolds.

To overcome this limitation, and as proposed in
[
40
]
, one possible approach is to rely on a finite element approximation of random fields defined on compact manifolds, thereby working with a finite set of approximate eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. This type of approximation was first introduced by Lindgren
[
27
]
and later extended by Pereira
[
34
]
to incorporate local anisotropies into the covariance structure. This framework can then be adapted to construct spline predictors with preferred directions of dependence, which can significantly improve prediction accuracy when the underlying phenomenon is anisotropic.

However, a key difficulty arising in the finite-element approximation of spline predictors is that it requires working with a Gaussian Markov random field (GMRF, see
[
36
]
) whose covariance matrix is not invertible, referred to as an intrinsic GMRF. This type of model, investigated in particular in
[
2
]
, complicates both the formulation of the classical kriging equations and the generation of simulations. As shown in
[
39
]
and thoroughly studied in
[
3
]
, such simulations can be obtained through linear conditioning of non-intrinsic GMRFs. This strategy enables an extension of the work of
[
40
]
, which focused exclusively on computing an approximate solution to the spline interpolation problem, without addressing the associated uncertainty quantification of the resulting predictions.

Therefore, the contribution of this work is to propose a statistical model for the spline interpolation problem, with the aim of providing both spline-based predictions at every point of the investigated compact Riemannian manifold and a quantification of the associated uncertainty. A strategy for generating conditional simulations of the underlying random function is also proposed, enabling the visualization of predictive scenarios arising from spline interpolation problems.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the theoretical solution to the spline interpolation problem on compact Riemannian manifolds and establishes its equivalence with kriging. Section
3
presents the finite element approximation of this solution. Section
4
then details the strategy for generating conditional simulations of the target intrinsic GMRF, while Section
5
addresses the introduction of anisotropy and the estimation of the unknown parameters. Finally, the application test cases and corresponding results are presented in Section
6
, and Section
7
summarizes the contributions and outlines potential directions for future work.

Spline interpolation solution on compact manifolds

In this manuscript, the investigated domain is a smooth compact and connected manifold
\mathcal{M}

\mathbf{y}=(y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}

2.1
Formulation of the spline prediction problems

The spline interpolation problem on
\mathcal{M}
, denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, consists in finding the smoothest function, in a precise mathematical sense, that interpolates the observed data. More precisely, it consists in solving the following variational problem
[
22
,
11
]
.

Definition 1

(Spline interpolation problem on
\mathcal{M}
)
.

\mathcal{P}_{0}
the spline interpolation problem on
\mathcal{M}
consists in finding a function
u\in H^{2}(\mathcal{M})
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} E(u)\;&=\;\min_{v\in H^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\lvert\Delta v\rvert^{2}\,d\mu_{g}\\
u(\mathbf{s}_{i})\;&=\;y_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

When the observed data are corrupted by noise, seeking an exact interpolating solution is no longer appropriate. In this case, the smoothing spline problem, denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
, is more suitable, as it consists in minimizing the energy functional penalized by the prediction error at the observation points.

Definition 2

(Smoothing spline problem on
\mathcal{M}
)
.

\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
the smoothing spline problem on
\mathcal{M}
consists in finding, for
\tau>0
a function
u\in H^{2}(\mathcal{M})
minimizing

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-u(\mathbf{s}_{i})\right)^{2}+\tau^{2}\int_{\mathcal{M}}\lvert\Delta v\rvert^{2}\,d\mu_{g}

(2)

The analytical solutions of these problems are very similar, and are related to the spectrum of the operator
-\Delta.

2.2
Analytical solution to the spline prediction problems

To derive the solutions to the problems
\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}
, we rely on the Spectral Theorem
[
7
]
, recalled in
A

(\lambda_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}

(\phi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
the associated eigenfunctions, which form an orthonormal basis of

. The eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=0
corresponds to constant functions
[
42
]
, with
\phi_{0}=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\mu_{g}}.

As explained in
[
24
]
, the theory of RKHS is needed to obtain the solution of problems of the type
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
for
\tau\geq 0.
Some details were recalled in
[
40
]
, but are not evoked here. We simply introduce the kernel that will be central in the solution, which is defined as

K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{1})\phi_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{2}),\penalty\ \penalty\ (\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})\in\mathcal{M}^{2},

(3)

and the solution to the spline prediction problems is provided by Proposition
1
.

Proposition 1

(Spline prediction
[
46
]
)
.

The solution to the problem
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}
, which corresponds to the interpolating spline when
\tau=0
and to the smoothing spline when
\tau>0
, is given by

u^{\star}_{\tau}(\mathbf{s})=a_{\tau}\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s})+\mathbf{k}_{1}(\mathbf{s})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-a_{\tau}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}\right),

(4)

with

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}\ &=\left[K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j})\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\\
\mathbf{k}_{1}(\mathbf{s})&=\left(K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}),\dots,K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{n},\mathbf{s})\right)^{\top}\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{M}\\
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}&=\left(\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{1}),\dots,\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{n})\right)\\
a_{\tau}&=\left((\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}\right)^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}\mathbf{y}\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

The invertibility of the matrix
\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}
is discussed in
B
. For
\tau=0,

\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}
is positive definite if and only if any observation vector
\mathbf{y}
can be interpolated by a function in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
, while the inversibility is guaranteed when
\tau>0.

2.3
Equivalence with kriging

The solution
u_{\tau}
coincides with the universal kriging predictor
[
28
,
19
]
, with mean
a\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
, where
a\in\mathbb{R}
is an unknown parameter, covariance kernel
\sigma^{2}K_{1}
, and observation noise variance
\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}
when
\tau>0
. Here,
\sigma^{2}>0
is the unknown variance. Note that since
\phi_{0}
is constant in this setting, the model reduces to ordinary kriging
[
43
]
, which assumes a constant but unknown mean.

Under these assumptions, and with predictive mean equal to
u_{\tau}(\mathbf{s}_{0})
at a new point
\mathbf{s}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, the associated kriging variance is given by

V_{\text{UK}}(\mathbf{s}_{0})=\sigma^{2}\left[v(\mathbf{s}_{0})+K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{0},\mathbf{s}_{0})-k_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{0})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}k_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{0})\right]

(6)

with
v(\mathbf{s}_{0})=\left[\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{0})-\mathbf{k}_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{0})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}\right]^{\top}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}\right]^{-1}\left[\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{0})-\mathbf{k}_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{0})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}\right].

The term
\sigma^{2}v(\mathbf{s}_{0})
reflects the uncertainty associated with the unknown coefficient
a
appearing in the mean of the random field, while
\sigma^{2}\left[K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{0},\mathbf{s}_{0})-k_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{0})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}k_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{0})\right]
corresponds to the predictive variance of the simple kriging predictor, that is, when the mean is assumed to be known.

This analogy with universal kriging naturally paves the way for uncertainty quantification in spline prediction. However, to obtain the full predictive distribution rather than just the mean and variance, the idea is to introduce Gaussian assumptions and to work with Gaussian random fields (GRFs)
Z
and
E
. Since the parameter
a
governing the mean is unknown, the Bayesian kriging framework
[
9
]
is adopted by modeling it as a random variable
A
. The observations
\mathbf{y}
are then interpreted as noisy realizations of the GRF
Z
such that,

Y_{i}=Z(\mathbf{s}_{i})+\sigma\tau E_{i},\penalty\ \penalty\ 1\leq i\leq n

where

•

\left(Z\mid A=a\right)
is a GRF with mean
a\phi_{0}
and covariance kernel
\sigma^{2}K_{1}
, for all
a\in\mathbb{R}

•

A\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\alpha),
which is the
a priori
distribution of the unknown coefficient of the mean

•

\mathbf{E}=\left(E_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
is a centered Gaussian vector with
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{E})=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, independent of
A
and
Z.

This corresponds to the
a priori
information on the model, where
Z
again represents the underlying phenomenon of interest and
\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}
accounts for an additional measurement noise when
\tau>0
. Under the Gaussian assumptions, the posterior distribution of the random field
Z
can be computed explicitly, namely

\left(Z\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}=\mathbf{y}\right),

(7)

where
\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}
denotes the random vector of observations
\left[Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\right]^{\top}
. This conditional random field is itself a GRF, and its distribution is therefore entirely characterized by its mean and covariance kernel. As explained in
[
18
]
and recalled here in Proposition
2

\alpha\to\infty
is of particular interest.

Proposition 2

(Convergence of the conditional GRF.)
.

When
\alpha
, the variance of
A
, tends to infinity, corresponding to a non-informative prior, the conditional field
(Z\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}=\mathbf{y})
converges to a GRF whose mean and covariance coincide with the universal kriging predictor. They are given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} \underset{\alpha\to\infty}{\lim}\mathbb{E}\left(Z(\mathbf{s})\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}=\mathbf{y}\right)&=u^{\star}_{\tau}(\mathbf{s})\\
\underset{\alpha\to\infty}{\lim}\mathrm{Cov}\left(Z(\mathbf{s}),Z(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}=\mathbf{y}\right)&=\sigma^{2}\left[c(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})+K_{1}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})-k_{1}(\mathbf{s})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}k_{1}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})\right]\end{aligned}\right.

(8)

with
c(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime})=\left[\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s})-\mathbf{k}_{1}(\mathbf{s})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}\right]^{\top}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}\right]^{-1}\left[\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})-\mathbf{k}_{1}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1,\tau}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\text{obs}}\right].

The proof is provided in
C
. Note that the
a priori
mean
\mu
of the random variable
A
does not affect the results when
\alpha\to\infty
. Therefore, without loss of generality, we set
\mu=0
for the remainder of the analysis. The objective of the study is then to investigate this conditional GRF, with a particular focus on generating simulations that reflect both the spline predictor and its associated uncertainty. However, the covariance kernel
K_{1}
is linked to the spectrum of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
-\Delta
. For a general compact and connected manifold, these eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are not available in closed form, making standard Gaussian process regression computations intractable. To overcome this difficulty, and as already proposed in
[
40
]
, a finite-element approximation of the conditional GRF can be considered.

3
Finite element approximation of Gaussian Random Fields

Here, the objective is to construct a discretization of Gaussian random fields on the Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
by exploiting the finite element approximation framework, as introduced in
[
27
]
. We discretize the manifold
\mathcal{M}
by introducing a polyhedral approximation obtained by a triangulation
\mathrm{T}
with
m
nodes
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{m}\in\mathcal{M}
. In particular, we assume (as it is standard with surface finite element methods, cf.
[
12
]
) that the resulting triangulated surface is “close enough” to the manifold
\mathcal{M}
so that we can assume there is a one-to-one mapping between the points of
\mathcal{M}
, and those of its discretized counterpart. As a result, we use with an abuse of notation the same letter
\mathcal{M}
to denote the triangulated surface.

Associated with this triangulation, we define a set of compactly supported basis functions
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
on
\mathcal{M}
. Each basis function
\psi_{j}
is piecewise linear over
\mathrm{T}
, equals
1
at the node
\mathbf{c}_{j}
, and vanishes at all other nodes, corresponding to a first-order finite element discretization. This construction naturally yields the following matrices:

•

the mass matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, with entries
\left[\mathbf{M}\right]_{ij}=\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}
,

•

the stiffness matrix
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, with entries
\left[\mathbf{F}\right]_{ij}=\langle\nabla\psi_{i},\nabla\psi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}
,

•

the projection matrix
\mathbf{P}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
mapping the triangulation nodes to the observation points, with entries
\left[\mathbf{P}_{n}\right]_{ij}=\psi_{j}(\mathbf{s}_{i})
.

The mass matrix
\mathbf{M}
is symmetric positive definite, and the stiffness matrix
\mathbf{F}
is symmetric positive semi-definite
[
34
]
. Consequently, one can introduce a matrix square root
\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
satisfying
\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\,\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}=\mathbf{M}
, which may be obtained, for example, via a Cholesky factorization
[
1
]
. Owing to the local support of the basis functions
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, both
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{F}
are sparse matrices.

Let us propose a finite-element approximation of the statistical model introduced in Section

4
Predictive posterior simulations

4.1
Overview of the approach

In this section, we define
\mathbf{Z}_{\text{post}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}(\mathbf{y}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{post}})
, the limiting predictive distribution of Proposition
4
, i.e. we take

•

\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}(\mathbf{y})=\underset{\alpha\to\infty}{\lim}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}^{(m)}=\mathbf{y}\right)
as the posterior expectation of
\left(\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}^{(m)}=\mathbf{y}\right)
in the limit
\mathbb{V}(A)=\alpha\rightarrow\infty
.

•

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}=\underset{\alpha\to\infty}{\lim}\mathrm{Cov}\left(\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}^{(m)}=\mathbf{y}\right)
as the corresponding covariance kernel, which in particular does not depend on
\mathbf{y}
(see Proposition
4
).

Our objective is to generate simulations of this Gaussian vector. Two approaches may naturally be considered as possible starting points.

First, within this universal kriging framework, simulations of
\mathbf{Z}_{\text{post}}
can be derived from prior simulations of
\left(\mathbf{Z}\mid A=a\right)
, that has a covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
(as established in Proposition
3
). However, computing
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
requires the diagonalization of
\mathbf{S}
(defined in Equation
11
). Because
\mathbf{S}
is an
m\times m
matrix, where
m
denotes the number of triangulation nodes, this computation becomes computationally intractable for large
m
. To address this, the precision matrix, defined as the inverse of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, is exploited in practice. The idea is that Gaussian random vectors defined on a triangulation mesh can be viewed as GMRFs, which implies that their precision matrix is sparse. However, direct manipulation of the precision matrix is not feasible in our setting. Since
\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\operatorname{rank}\!\big(f(\mathbf{S})\big)=m-1
, with
f(\lambda_{0}^{(m)})=0
and
f(\lambda_{k}^{(m)})>0
for
1\leq k\leq m-1
, the random vector
\left(\mathbf{Z}\mid A=a\right)
is an intrinsic GMRF, rendering classical computational methods involving the precision matrix inapplicable.

Alternatively, one may approximate the
\alpha\to\infty
limit by simulating
\left(\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}^{(m)}=\mathbf{y}\right)
for a sufficiently large
\alpha
. These simulations leverage the invertibility of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\alpha}
, the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{Z},
defined as
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\alpha}=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}
. The computations leading to this covariance matrix are identical to those detailed in
C.1
. Its inverse, the precision matrix of
\mathbf{Z}
, is defined as:

\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\alpha}^{-1}=\sigma^{-2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}.

(13)

The structure of
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
is well-suited for sparse computations. Specifically, one can compute the Cholesky factorization of the sparse matrix
\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}
and subsequently obtain the Cholesky factorization of
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
via a rank-one update. However, this strategy leads to numerical difficulties that depend on the value of
\alpha
and relies on the approximation of a limit, which prevents exact computation.

The approach proposed here involves leveraging the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
for a fixed
\alpha
to derive exact simulations of
\mathbf{Z}_{\text{post}}
, thereby avoiding the need for a limiting approximation. This process is decomposed into the following steps:

1.

Generate simulations of the intrinsic GMRF
\left(\mathbf{Z}\mid A=a\right)
using
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
(Section
4.2
).

2.

Compute the posterior expectation
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}(\mathbf{y})
using
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
, without limit estimation (Section
4.3
)

3.

Construct exact simulations of
\mathbf{Z}_{\text{post}}
by combining the results from the previous two steps (Section
4.4
)

The overall procedure is summarized in Section
4.5
.

4.2
Simulating an intrinsic GMRF

Here, we detail the procedure to obtain simulations, for a given
a\in\mathbb{R},
of the intrinsic GMRF

\mathbf{Z}_{a}\vcentcolon=\left(\mathbf{Z}\mid A=a\right)\sim\mathcal{N}(a\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),

using the GMRF
\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{m},\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1})
. The work of

can be adapted here and leads to the following result, which is valid for all

Proposition 5

(Simulation of intrinsic GMRF)
.

Considering
\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}_{m},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\alpha})
, then

\left(a\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\mathbf{Z}\mid\left[\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{Z}=0\right]\right)=a\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\mathbf{Z}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(a\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}).

Proof.

The vector

\mathbf{Z}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{Z}=\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\right)\mathbf{Z}

is a centered Gaussian vector, since it is a linear transformation of the centered Gaussian vector
\mathbf{Z}
.
Its covariance is

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\right)\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{Z})\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\right)\left(\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\right).

As shown in
F
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{m}.
Then,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\right)\left(\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}-2\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}-2\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\text{ using }\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=1\text{ see\penalty\ \ref{formula_phibf}}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

∎

Since the simulation of

4.3
Computing the posterior expectation

The objective here is to compute
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}(\mathbf{y})
. The work of
[
40
]
establishes an analytical relationship between

\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{y})\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}\!\left(\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}^{(m)}=\mathbf{y}\right)=\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\left[\mathbf{P}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{y},

and
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}(\mathbf{y})=\underset{\alpha\to\infty}{\lim}\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{y}).

Proposition 6

(Computation of the posterior expectation.)
.

The posterior expectation
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}(\mathbf{y})
can be computed using
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{y})
and
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right),
using the relation

\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{y})+\Bigg(\frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}-\mathbf{h}\!\left[\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}\!\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\right]}{\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}\!\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\mathbf{h}\!\left[\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}\!\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\right]\Bigg)\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{y}),

where
\mathbf{h}\!\left[\mathbf{m}\right]=\frac{\mathbf{m}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{m}}{1-\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{m}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Using Proposition
6
which is valid for all
\alpha>0
, the posterior expectation
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}(\mathbf{y})
can be computed for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
, provided that
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{y})
is tractable for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
For the computation of
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{y})
, two scenario are distinguished.

1.

The first scenario corresponds to the study of interpolating splines, i.e., setting the noise
\tau=0
. To facilitate the computations in this situation, we require that the observation points
\mathcal{S}=(\mathbf{s}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are included among the triangulation nodes
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{m}
. More precisely, let
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\}
denote the set of indices such that
s_{k}=c_{i_{k}}
for
1\leq k\leq n
. This requirement is not restrictive in practice, as the triangulation can typically be designed to include the observation points, while simplifying the computations. Indeed, with this hypothesis, we have

(\mathbf{P}_{n})_{k,j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }j=i_{k},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

for
1\leq k\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq m,
leading to
\mathbf{P}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}=\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\right]_{I,I},
by denoting
\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{I_{1},I_{2}}
the submatrix of
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
consisting of the rows indexed by
I_{1}
and the columns indexed by
I_{2}
for any index sets
I_{1},I_{2}
. Then, we have the simplified results

\begin{cases}\big[\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{y})\big]_{\bar{I}}&=\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\big]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}^{-1}\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\big]_{\bar{I},I}\mathbf{y}\\
\big[\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{y})\big]_{I}&=\mathbf{y},\end{cases}

and the vector
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf{y})
can be computed with the following procedure.

The second scenario corresponds to the computation of smoothing splines, i.e., setting the noise

. In this case, the assumption that the observation points belong to the triangulation nodes is no longer required to obtain computational simplifications. Indeed, one has

\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\!\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{y}=\left(\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{n}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{y}

4.4
Generating the conditional simulations

Our approach exploits the prior simulations of
\left(\mathbf{Z}_{a}=\mathbf{Z}\mid A=a\right)
(Section

) to obtain simulations of
\mathbf{Z}_{\text{post}}
. To this end, we use the following result, which specifically addresses the simulation of residuals

.

Proposition 7

(Simulation of the residuals.)
.

For all
a\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathbf{Z}_{a}\sim\mathcal{N}(a\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),
and
\mathbf{E}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{n}).
Then, the vector of residuals

\mathbf{Z}_{\text{res}}=\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right)-\mathbf{Z}_{a}

is a centered Gaussian vector with covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{post}}.

Proof.

The objective is to compute

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{res}})&=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right)\right)-a\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\\
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{res}})&=\mathrm{Cov}\left[\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right)\right]-2\mathrm{Cov}\left[\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right),\mathbf{Z}_{a}\right]+\mathrm{Cov}\left[\mathbf{Z}_{a}\right]\end{aligned}\right.

(14)

using

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right)=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left[\mathbf{Z}_{a}\right]=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}.

Step 1:

Compute
\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right).
Using Proposition
4
, have

\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right)=\tilde{a}_{\tau}^{(m)}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}-\tilde{a}_{\tau}^{(m)}\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})

with
\tilde{a}_{\tau}^{(m)}=\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right).

Let us denote
\mathbf{b}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
and
\beta=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}\tilde{a}_{\tau}^{(m)}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\left[\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right]

(15)

\displaystyle=\beta^{-1}\mathbf{b}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\left[\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right]+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\left[\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\beta^{-1}\mathbf{b}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\right]\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\left[\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right].

Step 2:

Compute
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right)\right]
and
\mathrm{Cov}\left[\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right)\right]
. From step 1, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{m}_{\text{post}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{n}\mathbf{Z}_{a}+\sigma\tau\mathbf{E}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=a\left[\beta^{-1}\mathbf{b}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\right]\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}

\displaystyle=a\left(\mathbf{b}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{P}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=a\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\text{ from the definition of }\mathbf{b}.

5
Anisotropy and parameter estimation

5.1
Anisotropic splines

These spline-based simulations are particularly valuable as they quantify uncertainty alongside the predicted field. However, the covariance kernels introduced above,
K_{1}
and its finite-element approximation
K_{1}^{(m)}
, are isotropic, implying that the modeled phenomenon must exhibit identical behavior in all spatial directions. A natural way to incorporate directional anisotropy, while preserving the exact modeling framework presented here, is to modify the parametrization of the manifold
\left(\mathcal{M},g\right)
via its Riemannian metric
g
. This strategy is described in detail in
[
33
]
and is briefly summarized below.

Considering that
\mathcal{M}
is embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for any point
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{M}
we consider a coordinate chart
(U,x)
such that
\mathbf{s}\in U
, where
U
is an open subset of
\mathcal{M}
and
x:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a homeomorphism onto an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The Riemannian metric expressed in local coordinates is given by

g_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{s}})=\left(\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{s}}^{x}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\,\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{s}}^{x},

(17)

where
\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{s}}^{x}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{s}}^{x}
denote the coordinate representations of the tangent vectors
\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{s}}\in T_{\mathbf{s}}\mathcal{M}
in the chart
(U,x)
, and
\mathbf{G}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
is the matrix representation of the Riemannian metric at
\mathbf{s}
in these local coordinates. The matrix
\mathbf{G}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
is symmetric positive definite and admits the decomposition

\mathbf{G}^{x}(\mathbf{s})=\mathbf{R}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\,\mathbf{D}^{x}(\mathbf{s})^{2}\left(\mathbf{R}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\right)^{\top},

which can be interpreted as a local deformation operator. For
d\in\{2,3\}
, the matrix
\mathbf{R}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
corresponds to a rotation, while
\mathbf{D}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
is a diagonal matrix containing scaling factors. Under this interpretation, the Riemannian metric induces a local deformation of the manifold by first applying a rotation and then an anisotropic scaling that emphasizes preferred directions. These rotations and scalings are parametrized and can be specified locally on each element of the triangulation. The corresponding parameters are collected in a vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, which modifies the inner product on
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
and, consequently, the matrices
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{S}
.

5.2
Parameter estimation

We then have unknown parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
defining the anisotropies, and the variance
\sigma^{2}.
A standard approach is to rely on the universal kriging statistical model for the observations and to maximize the associated log-likelihood. It corresponds to the log-density of the random vector
\left(\mathbf{Y}_{\text{obs}}^{(m)}\mid A=a\right)
, which is maximized with respect to
\left(a,\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)

\mathcal{L}\left(a,\sigma,\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(n\log(2\pi)+\log\lvert\sigma^{2}\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\rvert+\sigma^{-2}\left(\mathbf{y}-a\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-a\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}
impacts both
\mathbf{K}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}.
The optimization leads to

\left\{\begin{aligned} a^{\star}&=a_{\tau}^{(m)}\\
\sigma^{\star}&=\left[\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbf{y}-a\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-a\mathbf{P}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\right]^{1/2}\end{aligned}\right.

(18)

and we end up maximizing in
\boldsymbol{\beta}
the concentrated log-likelihood
[
21
]

\mathcal{L}^{(C)}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=-n\log\left(\sigma^{\star}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log\lvert\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\rvert.

The computation of
\sigma^{\star}
and
\lvert\mathbf{K}^{(m)}_{\tau}\rvert
is detailed in
[
40
]
. Note that, in the smoothing spline setting, the noise variance
\tau>0
can be estimated jointly with
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

The first application concerns temperature prediction on the Earth, modeled as a spherical surface. The second considers an analytical function defined on a cylindrical surface. In both cases, anisotropies are estimated in local coordinate charts, namely spherical coordinates for the sphere and cylindrical coordinates for the cylinder. For both test cases, the locations of the observation points are fully controlled, so we consider the first scenario and focus on the interpolation problem. The results obtained in the second scenario are similar and are provided in the supplementary material

7
Summary and perspectives

This article extends the work of
[
40
]
by proposing a framework for spline interpolation on compact Riemannian manifolds, together with a quantification of the associated uncertainty. The approach relies on the equivalence between the classical solution to the interpolation problem and the universal kriging predictor. However, it is based on the spectrum of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
-\Delta
, which is generally unknown on compact Riemannian manifolds.

To overcome this difficulty, a finite-element approximation of the predictor is introduced, based on a triangulation of the manifold as proposed in
[
33
]
. This strategy avoids the need for the explicit spectrum of
-\Delta
and enables the incorporation of anisotropies in the covariance kernel through a parametrization of the Riemannian metric associated with the manifold. However, it leads to an intrinsic Gaussian Markov random field (GMRF) with a singular covariance matrix, which complicates both computations and simulations.

Adapting the methodology of
[
3
]
, we represent this intrinsic GMRF through linear conditioning of a non-intrinsic GMRF. The approach is illustrated on two test cases: the Earth, modeled as a spherical surface, and a cylinder. For both examples, the impact of anisotropy and the role of uncertainty quantification are emphasized. The predictive distribution is displayed over a selected subdomain to demonstrate that the predictive variance is consistent with the actual prediction error, even in situations where isotropic splines yield inaccurate point estimates. Finally, a scoring rule is evaluated to assess the overall predictive performance, taking into account both accuracy and uncertainty quantification.

Although this work offers a thorough investigation, several perspectives for future research remain. First, in this work we only considered constant anisotropy parameters. However, for certain phenomena, one may need to estimate a spatially varying anisotropy field. Such an extension would naturally lead to a very large number of parameters to infer through maximum log-likelihood estimation. Alternative strategies would therefore be worth exploring, for instance by modeling the anisotropy parameters themselves as a random field within a hierarchical framework. This would raise significant challenges, including the specification of a suitable prior statistical model for the anisotropy field and the implementation of a fully Bayesian approach.

Another promising direction is to investigate the link between spline prediction and the Matérn stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE), defined as follows
[
47
]
:

(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}Z=W,

(20)

where
(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
is a pseudo-differential operator and
W
denotes Gaussian white noise. As extensively studied in
[
2
]
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the solution to this SPDE is an intrinsic Gaussian field when
\kappa=0
. On a compact manifold
\mathcal{M}
, when
\alpha=2
, this solution is strongly related to our spline predictor as it admits a covariance kernel
K_{1}
, and a then detailed investigation of this result would be of particular interest. More generally, this perspective opens the way to extending to Riemannian manifolds the work of
[
2
]
on intrinsic Whittle–Matérn fields, and to comparing our approach with theirs, which involves the computation of the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. It may then be interesting to study the behavior of our spline predictor, and more generally of intrinsic fields, in extrapolation scenarios, i.e. when predictions are made far from the observation points, and to compare it with results obtained using classical Matérn solutions.

Finally, in our work we used the score defined in Equation
19
to assess the predictive performance at each point, while accounting for the associated uncertainty. This metric provides a convenient balance between accuracy and uncertainty quantification, and remains straightforward to compute when predictive simulations are available. However, it only relies on the marginal predictive variance at each location and does not incorporate the full covariance structure of the predictive distribution. More sophisticated scoring rules could therefore be considered, although their implementation becomes challenging when dealing with an intrinsic GMRF and a large number of prediction points (e.g., the entire triangulation mesh). In such situations, estimating the predictive covariance matrix empirically would require a very large number of independent simulations to obtain a sufficiently accurate estimator, while its analytical computation is delicate due to the manipulation of singular matrices.
