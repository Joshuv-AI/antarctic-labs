Pubertal timing predicts resting-state functional connectivity of cortical networks
Authors: Soudeh Ashrafipour, Nandita Vijayakumar, Niousha Dehestani, Samuel Berkins, Timothy J Silk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPubertal timing has profound implications for adolescent brain development and mental health outcomes; yet its influence on cortical resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) remains under explored. This study investigated whether individual differences in pubertal timing predict large-scale cortical rs-FC patterns during adolescence. Pubertal timing was quantified as puberty age gap (PAG); the difference between a model-predicted ‘puberty age’ and chronological age; where positive values reflect earlier timing and negative values indicate later timing.MethodsLeveraging data from the ABCD Study (N ≈ 3500), we examined the associations between PAG and resting-state functional connectivity across 12 large-scale brain networks defined by the Gordon parcellation. In addition to computing PAG using both physical and hormonal features, we also attempted to tease apart the contribution from adrenal and gonadal phases of maturation, and from physical (Pubertal Developmental Scale (PDS) items) vs hormonal features (testosterone, estradiaol, dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA)). Cross-sectional associations between PAG and rs-FC, as well as prospective associations between PAG and change in rs-FC, were assessed separately in males and females.ResultsCross-sectionally, earlier pubertal timing (defined as a greater PAG) was associated with altered resting-state functional connectivity patterns within and between key large-scale cortical networks. Most associations showed similar directional patterns to normative age-related rs-FC development, while a subset of associations involving higher-order control and executive systems appeared to be specific to pubertal timing. The most implicated connections involved primary sensorimotor, attention/salience, and control/executive networks, with patterns evident in both sexes but more wide spread in males. The longitudinal analyses yielded weaker associations relative to the cross-sectional findings, with no effects surviving FDR correction.ConclusionsThis study highlights the relevance of pubertal timing, beyond chronological age, in shaping cortical functional connectivity, particularly within sensorimotor, salience, and higher-order control networks. While many associations paralleled normative age-related rs-FC development, others appeared distinct from those observed for chronological age, suggesting that pubertal timing may explain additional variability in adolescent brain organization beyond age alone.Keywords: Neurodevelopment, Puberty, Pubertal timing, Neuroendocrine, Resting-state functional connectivity

Introduction

Adolescence, the transitional phase between childhood and adulthood, is a period of profound biological, cognitive, behavioral, and social transformations, marked by significant changes in brain structure and function (Crone and Dahl, 2012, Gracia-Tabuenca et al., 2021, Vijayakumar et al., 2018). While these neurodevelopmental shifts have traditionally been studied in relation to chronological age, emerging evidence suggests that many observed brain changes during this period may also be attributed to the concurrent developmental process of puberty (Giedd et al., 1999, Gottschewsky et al., 2024, Lenroot et al., 2007). Puberty, a critical developmental milestone, is marked by a series of biological transitions initiated by neuroendocrine processes that drive physical changes (Dorn and Biro, 2011, Vijayakumar et al., 2018). This process unfolds in two distinct yet overlapping phases: adrenarche and gonadarche, each contributing uniquely to the broader trajectory of pubertal development (Vijayakumar et al., 2018). Adrenarche typically begins around age 6–8 in girls and about 1 year later in boys (Saenger and Dimartino-Nardi, 2001). It is marked by the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, and a rise in adrenal androgens such as dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), which contribute to the emergence of secondary sex characteristics such as pubic hair, body odor, and acne (Vijayakumar et al., 2018). Adrenal androgens are also classified as neurosteroids (Maninger et al., 2009) and thus may influence behavior and psychological processes through their effects on the brain (Dorn and Biro, 2011). The second phase, gonadarche, which follows adrenarche by about 1–2 years, starting approximately one to two years later in boys than in girls, is driven by the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis and leads to the production of sex steroids, such as estrogen and testosterone, which guide reproductive maturation and secondary sexual characteristics (Mendle et al., 2019).

Pubertal maturation has been measured using both hormonal and physical indicators. Hormonal assays provide an objective measure of the neuroendocrine changes driving development and are often centered on DHEA, testosterone, and estradiol due to their accessibility and relevance to the key pubertal axes (Dorn and Biro, 2011, França and Cogo-Moreira, 2022, Vijayakumar et al., 2018). The secretion of various hormones leads to emergence of physical changes, including features that are shared across sexes (e.g., growth spurts and pubic hair), and features that are sex-differentiated such as breast development in girls, and testicular enlargement in boys (Dorn et al., 2006, Mendle et al., 2019). The physical changes, that occur across adrenal and gonadal phases, are used to define the staging of puberty, as described by Tanner (Marshall and Tanner, 1969).

Although adolescents progress through the same sequence of pubertal stages, inter-individual variabilities exist regarding the timing and tempo of these changes varies across individuals. Pubertal timing refers to the pubertal status of a child compared to their same-sex and -age peers (Vijayakumar et al., 2018), whereas pubertal tempo reflects the rate at which individuals progress through the stages of puberty (Cheng et al., 2020). In particular, prior research has found that pubertal timing has important implications for physical development, psychosocial adjustment, and mental health outcomes (Marshall and Tanner, 1969, Mendle et al., 2019, Platt et al., 2017). Adolescence is recognized as a high-risk period for the emergence of mental health problems and clinical disorders, and inter-individual variability in the timing of pubertal development have been found to amplify this risk (Ullsperger and Nikolas, 2017). Traditional approaches to studying pubertal timing focus on physical and hormonal indicators in isolation, which can miss the complementary strengths of each. To address this, recently, Dehestani et al. (2024) introduced Puberty Age Gap (PAG) to derive pubertal timing from physical indicators, hormone levels, or their combination. This is a normative modeling framework that builds on the established Brain Age Gap approach (e.g., Cole and Franke, 2017; Franke et al., 2012), which estimates biological age from neuroimaging features and quantifies individual deviations from normative developmental trajectories. Applied to pubertal development, PAG can integrate multimodal indicators while preserving item-level information (rather than averaging away variance), uses nonlinear, out-of-sample modeling to improve accuracy and generalizability, and yields a continuous timing index that avoids arbitrary categorical staging.

Pubertal processes are associated with both structural and functional brain development, with evidence suggesting sex-specific, and independent, contributions of physical and hormonal development beyond chronological age (Cheng et al., 2020, Dai and Scherf, 2019, Dehestani et al., 2023, Goddings et al., 2019, Gracia-Tabuenca et al., 2021). Despite the growing body of research on puberty and neurodevelopment, much of the work to date has focused on structural brain development, providing a limited account of how distributed brain regions interact to support complex cognitive and behavioral functions.

Resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC), captures the temporal coordination of neural activity between spatially distributed brain regions, and reflects the organization of large-scale brain networks (Edde et al., 2021). Across the lifespan, rs-FC undergoes substantial reorganization, with adolescence characterized by the refinement of previously established networks. Although there are inconsistencies in the literature regarding the developmental trajectories identified during adolescence (Sanders et al., 2023), a consistent pattern that emerges is a shift toward greater network segregation and integration, which reflects increased within-network connectivity, and reduced but more selective between-network connectivity. Collectively these findings suggest a transition from diffuse and widespread connections in childhood to more efficient and specialized network organization in adulthood (İçer, 2020, Sanders et al., 2023).

Importantly, puberty has been hypothesized to accelerate the maturation of functional neural circuits (Van Duijvenvoorde et al., 2019). However, these effects are not uniform across the brain, but instead vary across functional systems and connections. For example, Ernst et al. (2019) reported that pubertal development was associated with decreases in default mode network connectivity in girls but increases in boys, whereas Van Duijvenvoorde et al. (2019) observed predominantly negative associations between pubertal development and subcortical–cortical connectivity in both sexes, albeit involving different connections. Similarly, Fareri et al. (2015) demonstrated that increasing testosterone during puberty was negatively associated with connectivity between the ventral striatum and subgenual anterior cingulate cortex. Others have shown that certain fronto-striatal connections are associated with pubertal maturation beyond age effects (Ojha et al., 2022) and that different indices of pubertal development (including DHEA, testosterone, and perceived pubertal status) exhibit selective associations with functional connectivity within cognitive control networks (Pelletier-Baldelli et al., 2024), Together, these findings highlight that puberty-related changes in functional connectivity are connection-specific, measure-dependent, and directionally heterogeneous.

Comparatively, neuroimaging research on pubertal timing remains scarce. Emerging evidence suggests that earlier pubertal timing is associated with reduced corticolimbic connectivity (e.g., amygdala-hippocampus, Vijayakumar et al., 2023). In contrast, task-based findings indicate that earlier pubertal timing is associated with reduced activation in salience-related regions, amygdala, and hippocampus, while showing no association with amygdala–mPFC connectivity (Colich et al., 2023). At the same time, recent work failed to identify any associations with pubertal timing after accounting for confounds (Pelletier-Baldelli et al., 2024). A deeper understanding of how individual differences in pubertal timing relate to cortical network organization is essential for understanding adolescent brain development and its broader implications for cognitive and behavioral outcome.

The present study aimed to address this gap by examining how pubertal timing, operationalized as PAG, relates to resting-state functional connectivity of cortical networks in adolescence. Specifically, we investigated whether PAG was associated with rs-FC cross-sectionally, and whether PAG predicted longitudinal change in rs-FC over a two-year period. We ran all models separately for males and females to accommodate for sex specific maturation processes. We further examined whether adrenal or gonadal aspects of pubertal timing, or timing derived from physical or hormonal measures, differentially predicted patterns of rs-FC. Given that cortical network-level rs-FC effects of pubertal timing are under-explored, we did not specify directional hypotheses a priori.

Method

Participants

Data for this study were drawn from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study, the largest ongoing longitudinal project which follows a diverse cohort of nearly 12,000 children (47.84% females), aged 9–10 years at baseline (Casey et al., 2018; see Table 1 for detailed age distribution), recruited from 21 research sites across the United States. This study conducts annual assessments of mental and physical health, and collects biospecimen data, along with bi-annual MRI scans. Data from the baseline to the 4th year follow-up waves (Release 5.1) were used in current study. Participants were excluded based on the following criteria: biological sex recorded as neither male nor female; hormone and rs-fMRI data failing quality control; or missing data (Detailed data cleaning procedures are provided in the Supplementary Information (SI)). Following exclusions, the sample was selected based on data availability across baseline, 2-year follow-up, and 4-year follow-up scans. Participants with available data at both the 2-year and 4-year follow-ups were included in the later longitudinal interval (2-year to 4-year follow-up) and the corresponding 2-year cross-sectional analyses. The remaining participants with available baseline and 2-year follow-up data were included in the earlier longitudinal interval (baseline to 2-year follow-up) and the corresponding baseline cross-sectional analyses. This approach maximized sample retention across developmental intervals while ensuring that each participant contributed to only one longitudinal analysis.

Measures

Pubertal developmental scale

The Pubertal Developmental Scale (PDS; Petersen et al., 1988) was used to evaluate the physical changes associated with puberty. For both females and males, the scale measures changes in body hair, skin, and growth in height. Sex-specific items include breast development and menarche onset for females, and facial hair growth and voice deepening for males. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 indicating "no change has occurred" to 4 representing full pubertal maturity. Onset of menarche was the only binary variable with one indicating a “No” answer and four indicating “Yes”. This study utilized the parent-reported PDS, which is considered more reliable than self-report for the younger age range of our sample (Rasmussen et al., 2015), especially given the high rate of missing data and inconsistent reporting in self-reported measures.

Hormones

Saliva samples for pubertal hormones were collected in the ABCD Study using passive drool and processed by Salimetrics. For the present study, we focused on three key hormones: DHEA for both sexes as an adrenal androgen rising during adrenarche; testosterone for males as the primary gonadal horomone driving male genital development; and estradiol for females as the primary hormone stimulating secondary sexual characteristics such as breast development (Marceau et al., 2011). Hormone data were preprocessed following the protocol by Herting et al. (2020). This involved excluding participants with sex mismatches, defined as a discrepancy between the sex recorded at the hormone collection visit and the study’s primary demographic sex variable, or when sex was recorded as anything other than male or female. We also removed hormone values that exceeded assay detection limits or fell below thresholds for reliable measurement. To account for potential confounding factors, a linear regression model was used to adjust for covariates of caffeine intake, physical activity before saliva collection, time of collection, duration of collection, and time from collection to freezing. The regression residuals were computed and used as adjusted hormone values, separately for DHEA, estradiol, and testosterone; and separately for males and females. Details are provided in the SI.

Area deprivation index

Socioeconomic deprivation at the neighborhood level was indexed using the ADI (ABCD version derived from 2011 to 2015 ACS data). Following ABCD guidance, we used national percentile ranks at the census-tract level.

Race/ethnicity

Race and ethnicity, reported by participant’s parents, were categorized into five groups of White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, Other/Multi-race.

Mental health problems

The parent-reported Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL; Achenbach and Ruffle, 2000) was utilized to assess mental health problems for the purpose of training the PAG model. As this model uses a normative modeling framework, participants meeting criteria for clinical levels of symptoms were excluded from the training set. We selected a broad range of CBCL syndrome, DSM−5-oriented, and additional symptom scales, including internalizing and externalizing problems, depressive and anxiety symptoms, somatic complaints, rule-breaking behavior, aggression, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, sluggish cognitive tempo, and stress.

Head motion

To account for potential confounding effects of in-scanner movement on rs-FC, mean framewise displacement (FD) was included as a covariate in analytic models. Framewise displacement, a standard index of head motion, quantifies the average volume-to-volume change in head position across the rs-fMRI scan. The ABCD Study provides FD estimates derived from realignment parameters computed during preprocessing (Hagler et al., 2019). For each participant, mean FD across the resting-state run was extracted and used as a continuous variable to statistically control for head motion related artifacts.

Resting-state functional connectivity

Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) acquisition and preprocessing in the ABCD Study has been comprehensively detailed in prior publications (B. J. Casey et al., 2018; Hagler et al., 2019). In summary, participants underwent 20 min of rs-fMRI scanning while keeping their eyes open and passively fixating on a central crosshair. For the current analysis, we utilized pre-processed rs-fMRI connectivity data provided by the ABCD Data Repository. These metrics were derived using a seed-based correlation approach, in which regions of interest (ROIs), defined according to the Gordon cortical parcellation scheme, were organized into 12 predefined cortical networks: auditory (ad), cingulo-opercular (cgc), cingulo-parietal (ca), default mode (dt), dorsal attention (dla), fronto-parietal (fo), retrosplenial temporal (rspltp), salience (sa), sensorimotor-hand (smh), sensorimotor-mouth (smm), ventral attention (vta), and visual (vs). In addition, some ROIs were assigned to a "none" (n) category, which was excluded from the analyses. Pairwise Pearson’s correlations were computed between all ROI time series and subsequently transformed using Fisher’s z transformation. Network-level connectivity was quantified as the mean Fisher-transformed correlation values either within a single cortical network, between distinct cortical networks, or between cortical networks and subcortical regions. Analyses were restricted to the 12 Gordon cortical networks, yielding 78 features (12 within-network; 66 between-network). For quality control (QC), the “imgincl_rsfmri_include” variable was used to identify scans meeting minimum quality standards. Given the multi-site nature of the ABCD dataset, we applied harmonization to the rs-fMRI functional connectivity measures to reduce non-biological variability associated with site and scanner differences. Harmonization was performed using the neuroCombat method (Fortin et al., 2018), adjusting for both site and scanner effects while preserving biologically relevant variation. Additional details are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Statistical analysis

Pubertal timing

Pubertal timing was calculated using the ‘Puberty Age Gap’ method proposed by Dehestani et al. (2024), a novel out-of-sample approach that models pubertal timing by predicting chronological age from puberty-related features. This method uses Generalized Additive Model (GAM), a supervised machine learning technique, trained on a typically developing subsample to capture the normative relationship between these pubertal markers and chronological age. Models were stratified by sex and estimated using physical changes (each PDS item), hormone levels (Estradiol for females, Testosterone for males and DHEA for both sexes), and the combination of both measures. Puberty age was derived from the model’s predicted age (See Fig. 1), and the PAG – defined as the residual difference between predicted puberty age and actual chronological age – served as an indicator of pubertal timing, with positive values suggesting relatively earlier pubertal development compared to same-age, same-sex peers; and negative values reflecting later pubertal timing (Fig. 2). To reduce regression to the mean bias commonly observed in age-gap models, age related prediction bias was corrected prior to deriving PAG, following the procedure described by Dehestani et al. (2023). In order to tease apart physical and hormonal contributions, models were also run with physical and hormonal inputs separately. Additionally, to distinguish the key features of adrenarchal and gonadarchal processes, we assigned specific PDS items and hormone measures to each phase of puberty based on their established associations in the literature. For the adrenarchal phase in both sexes, we included DHEA levels and PDS items related to body hair growth and skin changes. For the gonadarchal phase, we selected estradiol levels and PDS items assessing breast development and menarche for females, and testosterone levels and PDS items evaluating voice deepening and facial hair growth for males (Fig. 1). It is important to acknowledge that assigning specific hormones or PDS items to a particular sex or pubertal phase does not indicate a strict biological separation. Instead, these assignments reflect the relative predominance of each measure as established in the literature. For instance, DHEA is present in both adrenarchal and gonadarchal phases but is primarily associated with the adrenal component, while estradiol and testosterone are present in both sexes, with estradiol levels generally higher in females and testosterone levels significantly higher in males. Furthermore, the distinction between physical and hormonal models should be interpreted cautiously, as physical pubertal measures were derived from self-reported PDS items whereas hormonal measures were based on biological assays. Accordingly, differences observed between these models may be artifacts of measurement modality (self-report versus biological markers), rather than entirely distinct biological components of pubertal development. Chronological age and resting-state functional connectivity

To establish normative developmental associations between chronological age and rs-FC, and provide a reference for interpreting PAG-related effects, complementary age-only analyses were conducted. In these models, chronological age was included as the sole predictor of rs-FC, without inclusion of PAG. Detailed results are provided in the SI.

Pubertal timing and resting-state functional connectivity

For all models described below, the primary model included PAG and chronological age as predictors. For sensitivity analyses, all models were repeated adjusting for ADI, site ID, race/ethnicity, and head motion, with further details provided in the SI. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across all rs-FC connections to address multiple comparisons.

I.Cross-sectional associationWe examined the association between pubertal timing and rs-FC at a single timepoint using univariate ordinary least squares (OLS) regression as implemented in the statsmodels package in Python. Separate models were run with each PAG measure as the predictor. The outcome variables were pairwise rs-FC values between and within twelve large-scale cortical brain networks, measured at the same timepoint as the PAG estimate (baseline or 2-year follow-up, depending on data availability – See SI for detailed assignment protocol). Each participant contributed data from only a single timepoint to the cross-sectional analyses, ensuring independence of observations and preventing individuals from being included multiple times across timepoints.

II.Longitudinal associationTo assess whether PAG predicted longitudinal changes in rs-FC over time, we repeated the analyses with the change in rs-FC (difference in Fisher z-transformed correlations between two timepoints) as the outcome. Again, separate models were run with each PAG measure as the predictor. To capture developmental variability across a wide age range, we used two observation windows, one covering baseline to 2nd year follow-up and the other covering 2nd year follow-up to 4th year follow-up. For individuals tracked in the first window, PAG was estimated at baseline and rs-FC change was defined as the difference between the second-year follow-up and baseline. While for participants included in the second time window, PAG was estimated at the two-year follow-up, and rs-FC change was defined as the difference between the fourth year and second year follow-ups. Participants could only contribute to one window, contingent on scan availability and maximizing the sample size (see SI for details on how each participant was assigned to a time window).

Results

All results reported in this section are based on the primary models, which included PAG and chronological age as predictors. Results from the sensitivity analyses, which additionally adjusted for ADI, race/ethnicity, site ID, and head motion, are provided in the SI.

Cross-sectional association between puberty age gap and resting-state functional connectivity

As shown in Fig. 3, both positive and negative associations were observed between PAG and rs_FC across sexes. Overall, positive associations were prominent within primary sensorimotor systems, whereas negative associations were more prominent across higher-order cognitive control and integrative networks.

Prior to FDR correction in females, there were several significant positive associations involving primary sensory networks, negative associations involving higher order control networks, and mixed associations involving attention and default mode/memory networks. Associations that survived FDR correction in females included a positive relationship between PAG and connectivity within sensorimotor systems (Sensorimotor-Hand and Sensorimotor mouth), alongside negative associations between PAG and connectivity within and between higher order control (within Cingulo-opercular) and attention (between Ventral Attention and Salience) networks.

While a broadly similar pattern of findings was identified in males prior to FDR correction, a broader set of associations survived FDR correction. Positive associations were again concentrated within sensorimotor systems, including increased connectivity between Sensorimotor-Hand and Sensorimotor-Mouth, within Sensorimotor-Hand, and between Sensorimotor-Hand and Auditory networks. In contrast, negative associations were primarily observed in higher-order networks, including reduced connectivity between Sensorimotor-Hand and Cingulo-Parietal network, as well as between Ventral Attention and both Cingulo-Operular and Salience networks.

Sensitivity analyses yielded a more conservative pattern of results (see SI).

Longitudinal association between puberty age gap and change of resting-state functional connectivity

In contrast to the cross-sectional findings, no associations survived false discovery rate (FDR) correction in the longitudinal models. Accordingly, the following results are reported at a trend level (uncorrected for multiple comparisons) and should be interpreted with caution.

At this exploratory level, no overlapping associations were observed between males and female (See Fig. 3), indicating divergent developmental trajectories in how pubertal timing influences changes in brain connectivity across sexes.

In females, PAG was associated with a broader set of longitudinal changes, predominantly involving executive control and primary sensorimotor networks. Higher PAG was associated with increased connectivity between Visual and Cingulo-Opercular, Sensorimotor-Hand and Salience, Cingulo-Parietal with both Auditory and Dorsal Attention, as well as decreased connectivity within Cingulo-Parietal, and between Frontoparietal and Ventral Attention, and Visual with both Cingulo-Parietal and Retrosplenial Temporal networks.

In contrast, in males, positive PAG was associated with more spatially restricted changes, primarily involving primary sensorimotor networks. Specifically, positive PAG was linked to decreased connectivity between Default Mode and Sensorimotor-Mouth, and increased in Sensorimotor-Hand and Visual, as well as Auditory and Frontoparietal networks.

Sensitivity analyses yielded a more conservative pattern of associations (See SI).

Differential effects of puberty age gap on functional connectivity across aspects of adrenal and gonadal phases of puberty

To determine whether the association between PAG and rs-FC differs based on aspects from different pubertal phases, we tested interaction effects of phase (adrenarche vs gonadarche) on PAG–rs-FC associations, in sex-stratified models both cross-sectionally and longitudinally. Across all models, no significant interaction effects were detected after FDR correction, indicating that the association between PAG and rs-FC did not differ meaningfully between the adrenal and gonadal aspects of pubertal measures.

Differential effects of puberty age gap on functional connectivity across pubertal measures (PDS vs. Hormone)

We also examined whether the effect of PAG on rs-FC differs depending on the operationalization of puberty (i.e., Physical (PDS) versus hormone-derived PAG). Only a small number of connections showed significant associations prior to FDR correction, and none remained significant after correction in either sex, suggesting a broadly consistent relationship between PAG and rs-FC across pubertal measurement modalities.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated whether pubertal timing predicts resting-state functional connectivity of cortical networks. Using a large longitudinal sample, we assessed this both cross-sectionally and over a subsequent two-year period. To account for the multifaceted nature of pubertal development, we computed PAG using various input features - aside from an overall PAG based on multiple pubertal features, we also calculated PAG specific to adrenal vs gonadal features, and specific to physical vs hormonal measurement modalities.

Findings indicated that, cross-sectionally, pubertal timing was associated with altered resting-state functional connectivity patterns within and between key large-scale cortical networks. The direction of associations was not uniform across cortical systems. Positive associations were more frequently observed among connections involving primary sensorimotor networks, whereas higher-order attention, salience, and executive systems more commonly showed negative associations. Primary sensorimotor networks mature relatively early and often show increasing functional specialization across development, whereas higher-order association networks undergo more prolonged refinement throughout adolescence (Casey et al., 2005; Zielinski et al., 2010). Such refinement may involve increasing network segregation and selective weakening of diffuse inter-network connectivity. Accordingly, some of the negative associations observed among higher-order systems may reflect accelerated functional reorganization rather than a simple reduction in connectivity. Nonetheless, there was notable heterogeneity in associations, including several negative associations between pubertal timing and several connections involving primary sensorimotor systems. Consequently, broad conclusions regarding whether pubertal timing is associated with increased or decreased connectivity within sensory versus association systems should be drawn cautiously.

Patterns discussed were evident in both sexes but more widespread in males. These findings align with prior evidence suggesting that pubertal development is associated with large-scale functional connectome reorganization, particularly involving attention and executive control systems (Gracia-Tabuenca et al., 2021) underscoring these systems as key loci of developmental changes during puberty. Most of these associations showed similar directional patterns to age-related rs-FC development, suggesting that early pubertal developers may experience an accelerated pattern of normative rs-FC development relative to their peers. In females, PAG-related associations involving Sensorimotor-Hand and Sensorimotor-Mouth connectivity, as well as connectivity between Ventral Attention and Salience networks, showed similar directional patterns to the complementary age-only models, suggesting that these associations may reflect accelerated normative rs-FC maturation in earlier pubertal developers. In contrast, the negative association observed within the Cingulo-Opercular network differed in direction from age-related effect, which was positive albeit non-significant, suggesting a potentially puberty-specific effect. Similarly, in males, PAG-related associations within primary sensorimotor and attention/salience systems demonstrated directional patterns consistent with age-related rs-FC development. Associations involving the Cingulo-Operular and Ventral Attention networks, as well as connectivity between Cingulo-Parietal and Sensorimotor-Hand systems, also showed negative directional trends in the age-only models despite not reaching significance, suggesting that these effects may likewise reflect accelerated maturation processes associated with earlier pubertal timing. Together, these findings suggest that pubertal timing may reflect both accelerated normative maturation and puberty-specific reorganization of higher-order functional systems.

The longitudinal findings failed to identify any significant associations between pubertal timing and change in rs_FC following FDR correction. However, at an uncorrected trend level, longitudinal PAG-related associations showed distinct patterns across sexes, with females showing greater involvement of higher-order control and executive networks, and males demonstrating more spatially restricted associations primarily involving primary sensorimotor systems. The direction of longitudinal PAG-related associations was heterogeneous across networks, suggesting that earlier pubertal timing was not uniformly associated with either increased or decreased rs-FC over time. Instead, the findings indicate network-specific developmental effects, with most PAG-related associations showing directional patterns consistent with age-related rs-FC change, even when the corresponding age-related associations did not reach significance. However, some associations differed in direction from the age-related effects, including connectivity between Sensorimotor-Hand and Visual networks in males, and between Visual and Cingulo-Parietal networks in females. Together, these findings suggest that earlier pubertal timing may, in most connections, reflect accelerated patterns of normative rs-FC developmental change, while in others may reflect distinct puberty-related developmental alterations. Given the uncorrected nature of these findings, they should be interpreted cautiously and require replication in future longitudinal studies.

Our findings also complement prior reports of heterogeneous and connection-specific puberty-related associations with functional connectivity. Similar to previous studies showing that pubertal effects vary across networks and sexes, we observed both shared and sex-specific patterns of association across cortical systems. However, whereas much of the existing literature has focused predominantly on cortico-subcortical or limbic connectivity, the present study demonstrates that pubertal timing is also associated with widespread alterations across cortical–cortical functional networks, particularly involving sensorimotor, salience, and higher-order control systems. Together, these findings suggest that puberty-related functional reorganization extends beyond subcortical circuitry and may involve broader reconfiguration of large-scale cortical network organization during adolescence. Moreover, by integrating both physical and hormonal measures of pubertal timing, we provide multimodal, more comprehensive characterization of puberty that enhances sensitivity to brain-puberty associations and captures complementary dimensions of maturation that may differentially relate to rs-FC. The predominantly overlapping cross-sectional patterns observed across sexes, alongside several sex-specific associations and more divergent trend-level longitudinal findings, suggest that pubertal timing may relate to both shared and sex-specific patterns of cortical functional organization during adolescence. These cortical changes likely reflect not only hormonal influences but also broader social and experiential transitions during puberty, such as shifts in social roles and self-perception, which contribute to experience-dependent cortical development. By combining cross-sectional and longitudinal approaches, the present study provides novel insights into how pubertal timing shapes large-scale cortical network reorganization, advancing understanding of puberty’s impact on brain functional connectivity.

The lack of significant phase-specific (adrenal vs. gonadal) or measurement-specific (physical vs. hormone-based) effects may stem from several factors. With respect to the measurement, physical changes occurring during puberty are driven by hormone levels, thus can not be considered completely independent from them; while hormonal meausres are a snapshot at a particular time, physical changes reflect the accumulation of hormonal development to date. Regarding phase-specific findings, while adrenal and gonadal aspects of puberty are distinct neuroendocrine processes, they also significantly overlap in timing and in the hormones involved. It is important to recognize the conceptual and biological difficulty in clearly distinguishing between adrenarche and gonadarche phases. While certain hormones are more prominently associated with one phase over the other, not all can be classified exclusively as adrenal or gonadal in origin. The hormonal systems involved in pubertal maturation are highly interdependent, and the temporal overlap between the adrenarche and gonadarche phases further complicates attempts to isolate their distinct contributions (Mendle et al., 2019, Vijayakumar et al., 2018). This overlapping nature makes it particularly challenging to detect statistically significant differences between these phases in brain connectivity analyses.

These findings have several implications for understanding adolescent brain development. First, they suggest that pubertal timing plays a meaningful role in shaping large-scale cortical connectivity, complementing the emphasis on subcortical–cortical pathways in prior work. By highlighting cortical–cortical networks, particularly sensorimotor, attention, and executive systems, our results point to mechanisms through which pubertal timing may influence higher-order cognitive and socio-emotional functioning. Second, the more widespread associations in males underscore the importance of considering sex-specific trajectories, which may contribute to differences in cognitive development and vulnerability to psychopathology across adolescence. Our findings suggests that both physical changes and hormone levels are broadly comparable indices of pubertal timing in relation to their associations with rs-FC. While neither emerged as uniquely predictive, considering both together offers a more comprehensive assessment of pubertal development. As mentioned earlier, hormone assays index immediate physiological activity, whereas physical indicators integrate cumulative hormonal exposure; and in combination, these provide a more sensitive and multidimensional representation of pubertal progression. In our analysis the combined approach did not necessarily yield a greater number of significant associations, but revealed distinct patterns compared with either modality alone, suggesting each captures partly non-overlapping aspect of puberty.

This study has several notable strengths, including the use of a large, well-powered longitudinal sample, the integration of both physical and hormonal indicators of puberty through the PAG approach, and a specific focus on cortical–cortical connectivity. Sex-stratified analyses also further strengthened the study. Nevertheless, despite these strengths, certain limitations should be acknowledged, and they point to promising directions for future research. First, the relatively narrow age range of the sample and the short interval between longitudinal assessments may have constrained our ability to capture the full trajectory of pubertal development and its neural consequences. Second, our analyses were restricted to 12 large-scale cortical networks defined by the Gordon atlas. While this parcellation facilitates interpretation at the network level, it may obscure more subtle or localized effects of pubertal maturation. Future work will benefit from applying more high-resolution or functionally derived parcellations (e.g., Glasser et al. 2016, Schaefer et al. 2018), which would enable detailed exploration of topological properties such as modularity, hubness, and segregation/integration using graph theoretical approaches. Such methods could reveal more subtle developmental patterns and effects of pubertal timing. Third, distinguishing adrenarchal and gonadarchal processes proved challenging due to their substantial temporal and hormonal overlap, complicating efforts to isolate their unique contributions. Fourth, although our multimodal operationalization of pubertal timing aims to address some limitations in prior work, each measurement type comes with limitations that will also influence our results. Only a single saliva sample was collected (at varied times across the day), and menstrual cycle phase was not captured in females. As hormones are subject to diurnal variation and short-term fluctuations, and estradiol also varies systematically across the menstrual cycle in females, measurement noise in hormone data may have attenuated observed associations. PDS rating, despite reflecting cumulative changes, is a subjective measure particularly when relying on parent-reports. Even though using parent-reports is in accordance with recommendations for this ABCD age range (Dehestani et al., 2024, Herting et al., 2020); parents may be less accurate for less visible physical changes, introducing additional error. Additionally, we excluded subcortical circuits, which are rich in sex steroid receptors and may be more sensitive to hormonal variations, potentially revealing differences between measurement modalities not observed in cortical networks. Future studies should additionally incorporate subcortical–cortical connectivity to better assess the neuroendocrine sensitivity of these systems. To advance mechanistic understanding, and clarify whether pubertal timing affect behavioral, cognitive and mental health outcomes through its impact on cortical rs-FC, future research should examine whether resting-state connectivity mediate the relationship between pubertal timing and these outcomes. Finally, while the present study focused on pubertal timing, future research should also investigate pubertal tempo as another important source of inter-individual variability in pubertal development. Given previous evidence linking pubertal tempo to adolescent behavioral and mental health outcomes, it will be important to determine how pubertal tempo relates to cortical rs-FC development, and whether alterations in functional connectivity mediate associations between pubertal tempo and cognitive, behavioral, or mental health outcomes. Examining pubertal timing and tempo together may ultimately provide a more comprehensive understanding of how pubertal maturation shapes neurodevelopmental trajectories across adolescence.

In conclusion, this study provides novel evidence that pubertal timing predicts large-scale cortical functional connectivity, with distinct cross-sectional and longitudinal patterns and sex-specific associations. By integrating both physical and hormonal measures in a large longitudinal cohort, our work extends prior research focused on pubertal stage and subcortical circuits. The findings underscore the importance of considering puberty as a dynamic, multifaceted process that integrates biological and psychosocial influences, shaping cortical development in ways that may have lasting implications for cognition, behavior, and mental health.
