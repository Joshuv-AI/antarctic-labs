Secure Two-Party Matrix Multiplication from Lattices and Its Application to Encrypted Control
Kaoru Teranishi

Abstract
In this study, we propose a two-party computation protocol for approximate matrix multiplication of fixed-point numbers. The proposed protocol is provably secure under standard lattice-based cryptographic assumptions and enables matrix multiplication at a desired approximation level within a single round of communication. We demonstrate the feasibility of the protocol by applying it to the secure implementation of a linear control law. Our evaluation reveals that the client achieves lower online computational complexity compared to the original controller computation, while ensuring the privacy of controller inputs, outputs, and parameters. Furthermore, a numerical example confirms that the proposed method maintains sufficient precision of control inputs even in the presence of approximation and quantization errors.



I
Introduction

Encrypted control is a paradigm for ensuring the security and privacy of cyber-physical systems by leveraging cryptographic primitives
[
13
,
7
]
.
In this framework, control algorithms are executed directly on encrypted data.
This enables a client owning a physical plant to outsource controller computation to untrusted computing resources without disclosing sensitive information, such as sensor measurements, control actions, and controller parameters.

A primary architecture for encrypted control is the single-server setting using homomorphic encryption
[
7
]
.
Despite significant theoretical advances, in such a setting, the computational burden on the client often exceeds that of the original unencrypted computation, even when implementing simple state-feedback controllers.
This issue stems from the intrinsic computational overhead of the encryption and decryption processes in homomorphic encryption.

To overcome the limitation of the single-server setting, several studies have explored encrypted control in multi-party architectures.
These frameworks usually employ secret sharing to protect sensitive information.
For example, linear control laws were initially implemented in a two-party setting using secret sharing
[
8
]
.
This approach was subsequently extended to polynomial control laws by integrating homomorphic encryption
[
9
]
.
The polynomial control was also realized using only secret sharing in settings with more than three parties
[
15
]
.
Recent advancements have also enabled the implementation of neural-network-based controllers
[
17
]
and dynamic controllers
[
16
]
in two-party settings.

A fundamental challenge in encrypted control using secret sharing is the secure evaluation of multiplications between two secret-shared values.
The initial implementation bypassed this issue by assuming that one of the values was public
[
9
]
.
Furthermore, a naive interactive protocol leveraging homomorphic encryption typically requires multiple rounds of communication between the computing parties
[
9
]
.
While replicated secret sharing
[
3
]
, as employed in
[
15
]
, offers an efficient alternative, it necessitates at least three non-colluding parties, thereby increasing the architectural complexity and deployment costs.
Another common strategy is the use of Beaver triples
[
4
]
, as seen in
[
17
,
16
]
.
However, their online generation again requires multi-round communication.
Even if these triples are generated and provided by the client to the computing parties
[
16
]
, this approach inherently increases the computational complexity and communication overhead of the client.
Consequently, simultaneously achieving low client-side complexity in a two-party setting with a single-round communication remains a significant challenge.

To address these challenges, this paper constructs a one-round two-party computation protocol for approximate matrix multiplication.
Our approach integrates additive secret sharing with lattice-based cryptographic primitives, specifically encryption and commitment schemes.
By exploiting the linearity of these lattice-based primitives, the proposed protocol enables the computing parties to securely evaluate approximate matrix multiplication within a single round of communication.
This approach eliminates the need for multiple interactions between the parties.
Moreover, the framework ensures that the online tasks of the client are restricted to the generation and reconstruction of shares, resulting in an online computational complexity lower than that of the original controller computation.

The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows.

•

We propose a one-round two-party computation protocol for the approximate matrix multiplication of fixed-point numbers.
The security of this protocol is guaranteed under the Learning With Errors and Short Integer Solution assumptions, which are standard assumptions in lattice-based cryptography.

•

We provide a theoretical analysis of the approximation errors inherent in the proposed protocol.
Sufficient conditions for the precision of fixed-point numbers are derived to guarantee that the approximation error remains below a specified level.

•

By applying the protocol to the implementation of linear control laws, we demonstrate that the proposed framework simultaneously achieves low online computational complexity for the client, one-round communication between the two parties, and the concealment of both signals and feedback gains.

Notations

Let
\mathbb{Z}

\mathbb{R}

For an integer
q\geq 2

\mathbb{Z}_{q}\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}\cap[-q/2,q/2)
denotes the set of integers within
[-q/2,q/2)
.
For any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, the reduction of
x

q
is given by
x\bmod q\coloneqq x-\lfloor\frac{x+q/2}{q}\rfloor q
, where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor

This modular reduction is applied for each element of vectors and matrices.
The maximum norm of a vector or matrix is denoted by
\norm{ \cdot}_{\max}
.
The notation
x\sample X

II-B

Secret sharing

A secret sharing scheme distributes a secret among multiple parties by splitting it into shares such that the original secret can be reconstructed from a subset of those shares.
In this study, we employ a standard secret sharing scheme defined over a matrix message space, configured for a two-party setting.

Definition 1
(Secret sharing)

A secret sharing scheme consists of polynomial-time algorithms
\mathsf{Share}
and
\mathsf{Reconst}
:

•

\llbracket X\rrbracket\leftarrow\mathsf{Share}(X)
:
The share generation algorithm takes a matrix
X
over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
as input and outputs shares
\llbracket X\rrbracket=(\llbracket X\rrbracket_{0},\llbracket X\rrbracket_{1})=(R,X-R\bmod q)
, where
R
is a random matrix over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
of appropriate dimensions.

•

X\leftarrow\mathsf{Reconst}(\llbracket X\rrbracket)
:
The reconstruction algorithm takes shares
\llbracket X\rrbracket=(\llbracket X\rrbracket_{0},\llbracket X\rrbracket_{1})
as input and outputs
X=\llbracket X\rrbracket_{0}+\llbracket X\rrbracket_{1}\bmod q
.

The secret sharing scheme satisfies
\mathsf{Reconst}(\mathsf{Share}(X))=X
for any matrix
X
over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.
Moreover, each share
\llbracket X\rrbracket_{i}
held by party
P_{i}
is uniformly random and thus leaks no information about
X
to that party.
We respectively define the addition/subtraction and constant multiplication of shares by
\llbracket X\rrbracket\pm\llbracket Y\rrbracket\coloneqq(\llbracket X\rrbracket_{0}\pm\llbracket Y\rrbracket_{0}\bmod q,\llbracket X\rrbracket_{1}\pm\llbracket Y\rrbracket_{1}\bmod q)
and
X\llbracket Y\rrbracket\coloneqq(X\llbracket Y\rrbracket_{0}\bmod q,X\llbracket Y\rrbracket_{1}\bmod q)
, where
X
and
Y
are matrices over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
of compatible dimensions.
It then holds that

\displaystyle\llbracket X\rrbracket\pm\llbracket Y\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\llbracket X\pm Y\bmod q\rrbracket,

(1)

\displaystyle X\llbracket Y\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\llbracket XY\bmod q\rrbracket.

(2)

We also define
\llbracket X\rrbracket Y
as similar to
X\llbracket Y\rrbracket
, and the transpose of shares as
\llbracket X\rrbracket^{\top}\coloneqq(\llbracket X\rrbracket_{0}^{\top},\llbracket X\rrbracket_{1}^{\top})
.
Since these operations can be performed locally by each party without any interaction, no information about the secrets
X
and
Y
is revealed throughout the computations.

III
Lattice Problems

Learning With Errors (LWE)
[
14
]
and Short Integer Solution (SIS)
[
2
]
are standard cryptographic problems defined over lattices.
These problems serve as the foundation for lattice-based cryptography and are applied to a wide range of applications, including encryption and commitment schemes.
This section provides a brief overview of the definitions and applications of the LWE and SIS problems.

III-A

Learning with errors

We begin by defining the LWE problem.
This problem requires distinguishing a structured distribution over a lattice from a uniform distribution.

Definition 2
(LWE)

Let
n
,
m
, and
q
be positive integers, and let
\chi^{d}
be a distribution defined over
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
The (decision) LWE problem is to distinguish between the two distributions
(A,A^{\top}s+e\bmod q)
and
(A,w)
, where
A\sample\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times m}
,
s\sample\chi^{n}
,
e\sample\chi^{m}
, and
w\sample\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
.
The LWE assumption states that no polynomial-time algorithm can distinguish these two distributions except with negligible probability.

In our framework, we employ discrete Gaussian distributions for the secret and error distributions.
A discrete Gaussian distribution
\chi_{\sigma}^{d}
over
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with a width parameter
\sigma>0
is defined by
\Pr[X=x]=\rho_{\sigma}(x)/\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma}(y)
for each
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
\rho_{\sigma}(x)={\exp}(-\pi\norm{x}^{2}/\sigma^{2})
.
In lattice-based cryptography, these distributions are typically treated as bounded distribution.
This property is formally captured by the following assumption.

Assumption 1

The discrete Gaussian distribution
\chi_{\sigma}^{d}
is
B
-bounded, i.e., for any sample
x=[x_{1}\ \dots\ x_{d}]^{\top}\sample\chi_{\sigma}^{d}
, the condition
\absolutevalue{x_i}<B
holds for some
B>0
except with negligible probability.

For the width
\sigma=3.2
commonly adopted in the literature
[
5
]
, the bound
B=10\sigma
is sufficient to satisfy the negligible probability requirement in practice.
Given
\chi^{n}=\chi_{\sigma}^{n}
and
\chi^{m}=\chi_{\sigma}^{m}
, the hardness of the LWE problem primarily depends on the dimension
n
, the modulus
q
, and the width parameter
\sigma
.
In general, the problem becomes more difficult as
n
or the ratio
\sigma/q
increases.
Because the number of samples
m
provided to a challenger does not affect the underlying hardness, we refer to
(n,q,\sigma)
as the LWE parameters.

The LWE problem is of significant interest because it provides a template for encryption schemes
[
14
]
.
For instance, a message
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
can be encrypted using a secret key
s
as

c=A^{\top}s+\mu+e\bmod q.

(3)

The LWE assumption implies that
A^{\top}s+e\bmod q
is computationally indistinguishable from a uniform distribution, meaning that a polynomial-time adversary learns nothing about
\mu
from
c
.
Furthermore, the encrypted message can be decrypted by

\begin{bmatrix}A^{\top}&c\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-s\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\mu+e\bmod q\approx\mu,

which allows for the approximate recovery of the original message.

III-B

Short integer solution

We next introduce the SIS problem.
This problem requires finding a short nonzero vector that satisfies a linear equation over a lattice, given a random coefficient matrix and a random bias.

Definition 3
(SIS)

Let
n
,
t
, and
q
be positive integers, and let
\beta>0
be a real number.
The (inhomogeneous) SIS problem is to find a nonzero vector
r\in\mathbb{Z}^{t}
such that
h=Ar\bmod q
with
\norm{r}\leq\beta
for some norm
\norm{\cdot}
, given
A\sample\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times t}
and
h\sample\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
.
The SIS assumption states that no polynomial-time algorithm can find such a short vector except with negligible probability.

The hardness of the SIS problem depends on the dimensions
n
and
t
, the modulus
q
, and the norm bound
\beta
.
For our purposes,
n
and
q
are chosen to be consistent with the LWE parameters, while
\beta
is determined by protocol construction.
Consequently, we refer to
t
as the SIS parameter.

The SIS problem can be employed for constructing commitment schemes.
A commitment scheme emulates the functionality of a locked box.
In the commitment phase, a sender places a message into a box, locks it, and sends the locked box to a receiver.
Later, in the opening phase, the sender provides the original message and the key, allowing the receiver to verify the content.
The receiver cannot read the message before the opening phase since the box is locked, and the sender cannot alter the message once the box has been sent.
In cryptography, these two properties are referred to as hiding and binding, respectively.

Following this intuition, we compute a commitment to a message
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
as

h=A\mu+Br\bmod q,

(4)

where
A\sample\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times m}
and
B\sample\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times t}
are random matrices, and
r\in\mathbb{Z}^{t}
is a short random vector
[
2
]
.
By the Leftover Hash Lemma
[
12
]
, the term
Br
is statistically close to a uniform random vector, thereby ensuring the hiding property.
Moreover, the SIS assumption implies that no polynomial-time adversary can find a distinct pair
(\mu^{\prime},r^{\prime})
such that

h=A\mu^{\prime}+Br^{\prime}\bmod q=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mu^{\prime}\\
r^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\bmod q

with
\mu^{\prime}\neq\mu
, thereby satisfying the binding property.

IV
Two-Party Matrix Multiplication

This section proposes a two-party matrix multiplication protocol.
The protocol is built based on an approximate inner-product computation from the LWE and SIS problems.
We first revisit this underlying computation and then present the detailed construction of the proposed protocol.
Furthermore, we derive the conditions for the bit lengths of input data to guarantee the desired precision of the results.
Finally, we provide a security analysis of the protocol.

IV-A

Approximate inner product from LWE and SIS

The approximate inner-product computation using LWE ciphertexts and SIS commitments is introduced in
[
1
,
6
]
.
Let
(n,q,\sigma)
and
t
be the LWE and SIS parameters, respectively.
Suppose we have LWE ciphertexts (
3
) of a vector
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
and the zero vector, and an SIS commitment (
4
) to a vector
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m}
, that is,

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=A^{\top}s+x+e\bmod q,

\displaystyle c^{\prime}

\displaystyle=B^{\top}s+e^{\prime}\bmod q,

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=Ay+Br\bmod q,

where
A\sample\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times m}
and
B\sample\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times t}
are public random matrices,
s\sample\chi_{\sigma}^{n}
is a secret key,
e\sample\chi_{\sigma}^{m}
and
e^{\prime}\sample\chi_{\sigma}^{t}
are error vectors, and
r\in\mathbb{Z}^{t}
is a short random vector.
We now consider the inner products
c^{\top}y=x^{\top}y+s^{\top}Ay+e^{\top}y
,
(c^{\prime})^{\top}r=s^{\top}Br+(e^{\prime})^{\top}r
, and
s^{\top}h=s^{\top}Ay+s^{\top}Br
.
By subtracting the third from the sum of first two, we can observe that the terms
s^{\top}Ay
and
s^{\top}Br
are eliminated.
This yields an approximate inner product of
x
and
y
as

\displaystyle c^{\top}y+(c^{\prime})^{\top}r-s^{\top}h\bmod q

\displaystyle=x^{\top}y+(\text{noise})\bmod q,

\displaystyle\approx x^{\top}y\bmod q,

(5)

where the term
e^{\top}y+(e^{\prime})^{\top}r
constitutes the noise.

The previous studies
[
1
,
6
]
have utilized the property (
5
) to compute shares of an inner product in the scenarios where the two parties respectively hold
x
and
y
.
By contrast, our focus is on computing the inner product while concealing
x
and
y
from the parties, ensuring that only the client knows these values.
The core observation to achieve this is that the left-hand side of (
5
) is linear in
y
,
r
, and
s
, which are the secret components of the LWE ciphertext and the SIS commitment.
Therefore, by leveraging the properties (
1
) and (
2
) of the secret sharing scheme, the approximate inner product can be computed over the shares of the secrets.
This approach suggests a method for outsourcing the inner-product computation to two untrusted parties.
Building on this idea, the next section constructs a multiplication protocol tailored for matrix messages of fixed-point numbers.

IV-B

Proposed protocol

The approximate inner product (
5
) can be extended to the multiplication of
d_{1}\times d_{2}
and
d_{2}\times d_{3}
matrices.
Let
x_{i},y_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d_{2}}
for
i=1,\dots,d_{1}
and
j=1,\dots,d_{3}
.
For each vector, we compute the LWE ciphertexts (
3
) and the SIS commitments (
4
) as
c_{i}=A^{\top}s_{i}+x_{i}+e_{i}\bmod q
,
c^{\prime}_{i}=B^{\top}s_{i}+e^{\prime}_{i}\bmod q
, and
h_{j}=Ay_{j}+Br_{j}\bmod q
, where
A\sample\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times d_{2}}
,
B\sample\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n\times t}
,
s_{i}\sample\chi_{\sigma}^{n}
,
e_{i}\sample\chi_{\sigma}^{d_{2}}
,
e^{\prime}_{i}\sample\chi_{\sigma}^{t}
, and
r_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{t}
.
By arranging these vectors as columns, we obtain the following matrix representations,

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&\cdots&c_{d_{1}}\end{bmatrix}=A^{\top}S+X^{\top}+E\bmod q,

\displaystyle C^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}c^{\prime}_{1}&\cdots&c^{\prime}_{d_{1}}\end{bmatrix}=B^{\top}S+E^{\prime}\bmod q,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}&\cdots&h_{d_{3}}\end{bmatrix}=AY+BR\bmod q,

where the uppercase letters denote the horizontal concatenation of their lowercase counterparts, e.g.,
S=[s_{1}\ \cdots\ s_{d_{1}}]
.
Note that
X^{\top}=[x_{1}\ \cdots\ x_{d_{1}}]\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d_{2}\times d_{1}}
appears in a transposed form within
C
to ensure dimensional compatibility.
As a result, these matrix forms satisfy the approximate relation

C^{\top}Y+(C^{\prime})^{\top}R-S^{\top}H\bmod q\approx XY\bmod q

(6)

analogous to the vector case in (
5
).

Although the approximate matrix multiplication (
6
) is defined over
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, practical applications, including control systems, require computations to be performed over real-valued matrices.
To bridge this gap, we introduce a fixed-point number representation.
Let
\mathbb{Q}_{\langle k,\ell\rangle}\coloneqq\{2^{-\ell}z\mid z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\langle k\rangle}\}
denote the set of
k
-bit fixed-point numbers with an
\ell
-bit fractional part, where
\mathbb{Z}_{\langle k\rangle}\coloneqq\{-2^{k-1},\dots,2^{k-1}-1\}
is the set of
k
-bit integers.
A matrix
X\in\mathbb{Q}_{\langle k,\ell\rangle}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
is encoded into an integer matrix
\bar{X}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\langle k\rangle}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
by scaling with
2^{\ell}
, i.e.,
\bar{X}=2^{\ell}X
.
By replacing
X
and
Y
in (
6
) with their encoded values
\bar{X}=2^{\ell}X
and
\bar{Y}=2^{\ell}Y
, we obtain an approximate matrix multiplication framework over fixed-point numbers.

Consequently, we propose a two-party matrix multiplication protocol in Fig.

.

\mathsf{Mult}

\mathsf{Setup}

\bar{X}=2^{\ell}X\in\mathbb{Z}_{\langle k\rangle}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}

.
Finally, it also generates the shares
\llbracket S\rrbracket

S

P_{i}

In the online phase, the client and the two parties invoke
\mathsf{Mult}.\mathsf{Online}

The client first encodes the input matrix
Y\in\mathbb{Q}_{\langle k,\ell\rangle}^{d_{2}\times d_{3}}

\bar{Y}=2^{\ell}Y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\langle k\rangle}^{d_{2}\times d_{3}}

It then generates the shares
\llbracket\bar{Y}\rrbracket

\llbracket\bar{Y}\rrbracket_{i}

P_{i}

) and (

).
Subsequently, they open the shares to reconstruct

We now establish the approximate correctness of the proposed protocol.
The correctness relies on the noise term inherent in the computation of (
6
).
To ensure that the protocol output
Z
accurately approximates the desired matrix product
XY
, we need to show that the reconstructed value
\bar{Z}
does not suffer from modular wrap-around (i.e., overflow) and that the magnitude of the noise term remains sufficiently small.
The following lemma guarantees that such overflow is avoided when the bit length of the inputs is bounded according to the cryptographic parameters and matrix dimensions.

Lemma 1

Consider the
\mathsf{Mult}
protocol in Fig.
1
.
Under Assumption
1
with
\sigma=3.2
and
B=10\sigma
, if

6\leq k<\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\frac{q-128t}{d_{2}},

(7)

then it holds that

\bar{Z}=\bar{X}\bar{Y}+E\bar{Y}+E^{\prime}R

for all
X\in\mathbb{Q}_{\langle k,\ell\rangle}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
and
Y\in\mathbb{Q}_{\langle k,\ell\rangle}^{d_{2}\times d_{3}}
, where
\bar{Z}=\mathsf{Reconst}(\llbracket\bar{Z}\rrbracket)
and
R=\mathsf{Reconst}(\llbracket R\rrbracket)
.

Proof:

The properties (
1
) and (
2
) of the secret sharing scheme imply that

\displaystyle\llbracket\bar{Z}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\llbracket C^{\top}\bar{Y}+(C^{\prime})^{\top}R-S^{\top}H\bmod q\rrbracket,

\displaystyle=\llbracket\bar{X}\bar{Y}+E^{\top}\bar{Y}+(E^{\prime})^{\top}R\bmod q\rrbracket.

Let
\hat{Z}=\bar{X}\bar{Y}+E^{\top}\bar{Y}+(E^{\prime})^{\top}R
.
If
\hat{Z}
belongs to
\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d_{1}\times d_{3}}
, its reduction modulo
q
satisfies
\hat{Z}\bmod q=\hat{Z}
.
In this case, the reconstruction implies
\bar{Z}=\mathsf{Reconst}(\llbracket\bar{Z}\rrbracket)=\mathsf{Reconst}(\llbracket\hat{Z}\rrbracket)=\hat{Z}
.

The maximum norm of
\hat{Z}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\norm\big{\hat{Z}}_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{ \bar{X} \bar{Y} }_{\max}+\norm{ E^\top\bar{Y} }_{\max}+\norm{ (E')^\top R }_{\max},

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2(k-1)}d_{2}+2^{k-1}Bd_{2}+2Bt.

For
k\geq 6
, we have

2^{2(k-1)}d_{2}+2^{k-1}Bd_{2}+2Bt\leq 2^{2k-1}d_{2}+2^{6}t,

where
2^{k-1}\geq B=2^{5}
.
Hence, the condition
\hat{Z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{d_{1}\times d_{3}}
holds if
2^{2k-1}d_{2}+2^{6}t<q/2
.
Multiplying by two and taking the logarithm, we obtain
2k+\log_{2}d_{2}<\log_{2}(q-128t)
, which implies (
7
).
∎

While Lemma
1
establishes an upper bound on the data size to prevent modular wrap-around, the following theorem provides a lower bound on the fractional bit length required to achieve a desired level of precision.

Theorem 1

Consider the
\mathsf{Mult}
protocol in Fig.
1
.
Suppose the condition (
7
) is satisfied.
Under Assumption
1
with
\sigma=3.2
and
B=10\sigma
, for every
\epsilon>0
, if

\ell>\frac{1}{2}\quantity[ k + 4 + \log_2 \frac{ d_2 + t }{ \epsilon} ],

(8)

then it holds that

\norm{ XY - Z }_{\max}<\epsilon

for all
X\in\mathbb{Q}_{\langle k,\ell\rangle}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
and
Y\in\mathbb{Q}_{\langle k,\ell\rangle}^{d_{2}\times d_{3}}
.

Proof:

Since the condition (
7
) is satisfied, Lemma
1
guarantees that
Z=XY+2^{-2\ell}\quantity( E^\top\bar{Y} + (E')^\top R )
.
Thus, the maximum norm of the difference is bounded by

\displaystyle\norm{ XY - Z }_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-2\ell}\norm{ E^\top\bar{Y} + (E')^\top R }_{\max},

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-2\ell}\quantity( 2^{k - 1} B d_2 + 2 B t ),

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k-2\ell+4}(d_{2}+t),

where
2^{k-1}\geq B
and
B=2^{5}
are applied.
This implies that
\norm{ XY - Z }_{\max}<\epsilon
holds if
2^{k-2\ell+4}(d_{2}+t)<\epsilon
.
Taking the logarithm of both sides, we obtain (
8
).
This completes the proof.
∎

Note that the fractional bit length
\ell
is implicitly constrained by (
7
), as the total bit length
k
must exceed
\ell
.
This inherent trade-off between
k
and
\ell
defines a feasible parameter window that simultaneously avoids overflow and attains the target precision.
We later demonstrate that such a parameter window indeed exists for standard LWE and SIS parameters.

IV-C

Security Analysis

We next proceed to analyze the security of the proposed protocol.
In this section, the computing parties are assumed to be semi-honest and non-colluding.
Under this model, the parties follow the protocol specifications but may individually attempt to infer private information from transcripts generated during the protocol execution.
Due to space limitations, we provide a high-level summary of the security analysis here.
The security of the protocol is formally established via the simulation paradigm
[
10
]
, demonstrating that no polynomial-time adversary can learn any information beyond its own inputs and outputs.

Theorem 2

Under the LWE and SIS assumptions, the computing parties
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
learn nothing about the inputs
X
and
Y
and the output
Z
throughout the execution of the
\mathsf{Mult}
protocol in Fig.
1
.

Sketch of proof:

The privacy of the input matrices
X
and
Y
is guaranteed by the LWE and SIS assumptions.
Similar to the vector case discussed in Section
III
, under these assumptions, the LWE ciphertexts
C
and
C^{\prime}
are computationally indistinguishable from uniform random matrices.
Furthermore, the SIS commitment
H
is not only unforgeable but also statistically close to a uniform random matrix due to the Leftover Hash Lemma.
Moreover, by the construction of the secret sharing scheme, the shares
\llbracket S\rrbracket_{i}
and
\llbracket\bar{Y}\rrbracket_{i}
are distributed uniformly at random for each party
P_{i}
.
Consequently, every message that
P_{i}
receives during the protocol execution appears uniformly random and independent of the input matrices, thereby ensuring that the inputs and the output remain concealed throughout the entire computation.

VI
Conclusion

We proposed a one-round two-party computation protocol for the approximate matrix multiplication of fixed-point numbers based on the LWE and SIS problems.
We also established sufficient conditions for fixed-point number precision to guarantee the desired approximation error bound.
When applied to linear control laws, the protocol achieved lower online computational complexity for the client than the original computation, thereby mitigating the client burden of encrypted controls.
Future work will extend this framework to dynamic controllers and optimize the computational costs for the computing parties.
