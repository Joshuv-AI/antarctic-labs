Flexible infrared camouflage eutectic gallium-indium for thermoelectric energy harvesting
Authors: Zechang Wei, Min Seok Kim, Zhen Yu, Chenyang Cai, Mengjuan Zhou, Jiang Wang, Hong Lei, Haojie Lu, Yaoxin Zhang, Yu Fu, Young Min Song, Swee Ching Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Integrated infrared camouflage materials capable of energy harvesting and sensing hold great potential for enhancing operational efficiency in military settings. A key challenge, however, lies in achieving such multifunctionality without complex fabrication processes. In this study, we address this issue by introducing eutectic gallium‑indium (EGaIn) as a versatile material that combines infrared camouflage with thermoelectric sensing. EGaIn exhibits remarkable infrared camouflage performance over a broad temperature range. It suppresses its own thermal radiation by reflecting ambient infrared radiation, concealing its infrared signature for infrared camouflage. In addition to its infrared camouflage function, one of the most notable features of EGaIn is its ability to generate voltage from small temperature gradients. This thermoelectric capability enables self‑powered, real‑time monitoring of physiological states like body temperature and breathing patterns. Our work highlights the pathways toward military technologies that combine infrared camouflage with signal transmission capabilities.

Introduction

In nature, camouflage has been widely adopted by various animal species as a survival strategy to evade predators and prevent extinction. In modern warfare, infrared camouflage technology has emerged as a critical approach to conceal infrared signatures emitted by military equipment and personnel1–3. Such materials are designed to suppress the infrared characteristics of objects, enabling them to exhibit a radiation temperature lower than their actual temperature4,5. While conventional infrared camouflage materials can effectively hide soldiers’ infrared radiation on the battlefield6, this sole function falls short of addressing the multifaceted demands of complex combat scenarios. Integrating sensing capabilities into infrared camouflage systems would allow soldiers to maintain infrared camouflage while transmitting vital signals, thereby creating tactical advantages. However, a major challenge remains: the fabrication of such multifunctional materials often involves complex processes. Thus, there is a pressing need to identify suitable materials and manufacturing approaches for developing infrared camouflage materials with integrated sensing functionalities.

Early approaches to infrared camouflage often focused on reducing the target’s temperature to minimize its thermal contrast with the environment, thereby suppressing detectable infrared emissions7. To this end, thermally insulating materials, such as aerogels8–10 and foams11–13, have been widely applied to reduce surface temperatures. When these insulators cover high-temperature objects, they restrict heat transfer, effectively diminishing the infrared radiation discrepancy between the target and its surroundings. Phase change materials (PCMs) represent another attractive option for thermal infrared management14–16. By cyclically absorbing and releasing latent heat during phase transitions, PCMs help maintain low radiative heat output from high-temperature objects. Despite these advantages, both thermal insulation materials and PCMs present practical limitations: insulators often require substantial thickness, while PCMs operate within narrow temperature ranges. Additionally, materials including plasmonic metasurface17 and photonic crystals18,19 have been employed in the fabrication of infrared camouflage materials. Moreover, such systems usually depend on conductive additives to achieve sensing functionality20,21, and their manufacturing processes remain comparatively complex.

Sensing materials with signal conversion capabilities are also crucial in military applications, as real-time environmental perception and communication are key to military operations. Hydrogels22–24, films25,26, and aerogels27,28 have been widely used to fabricate wearable sensors, which can convert mechanical deformation, strain, or pressure into resistance change signals. In terms of thermoelectric sensing, germanium telluride compounds29, bismuth telluride-based thermoelectric materials30, and organic-inorganic composite materials31 have also been developed to generate voltage by using temperature differences and achieving self-powered sensing. In addition to the materials mentioned above, triboelectric materials32, photoelectric materials33, moisture-induced electric generator34,35, and piezoelectric materials36 are also capable of generating electrical signals in response to external stimuli. Despite these advancements, most existing sensing materials mainly focus on signal conversion and lack integrated functions such as infrared camouflage, which limits their application in scenarios that require both infrared camouflage and communication capabilities.

Recent developments have introduced alternative infrared camouflage materials, including MXenes37, noble metals38,39, and metamaterials40,41. Among them, MXenes and noble metals offer intrinsic electrical conductivity, making them potentially suitable for integrated sensing. However, issues such as limited mechanical flexibility and poor processability impede their scalable production for soldier-worn systems. These challenges underscore the need for new material platforms that combine infrared concealment with multimodal functionality.

Eutectic gallium-indium alloy (EGaIn) is a metallic material that exists in a liquid state at room temperature. It possesses excellent electrical conductivity, good fluidity, and low toxicity42–44. These characteristics enable it to achieve fast fabrication without the need for high-temperature processing or complex equipment. As a result, it has broad application prospects in flexible electronics45, circuits46, reconfigurable devices47, and soft robots48. In stretchable electronic devices, EGaIn’s liquid nature allows it to withstand repeated stretching and bending without breaking, demonstrating superior mechanical durability compared to traditional solid metals and conductive composite materials. In addition to these characteristics, EGaIn resemble aluminum and copper with infrared camouflage performance49–52. Unlike rigid MXene films or noble metal layers, its fluid nature and intrinsic adhesion allow it to conform readily to flexible polymer substrates, adapting to diverse geometries and use cases. Compared with the previously reported infrared camouflage materials53–59, EGaIn-based infrared camouflage material has the significant feature of a simple preparation process. Furthermore, its high electrical conductivity and thermoelectric sensitivity enable the translation of external stimuli into quantifiable electrical responses, positioning EGaIn as a compelling candidate for sensing and signal transmission applications.

Herein, we present a groundbreaking study demonstrating the remarkable capability of EGaIn in reflecting ambient infrared thermal signals and thermoelectric performance, thereby achieving infrared camouflage and sensing properties. Notably, EGaIn achieved an apparent infrared radiation temperature suppression due to high reflectance. When applied to flexible polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrates (P-EGaIn) (Fig. 1a), P-EGaIn showed excellent stretchability and camouflage performance. EGaIn achieves its high-temperature concealment by reflecting surrounding ambient infrared radiation, effectively masking its real infrared radiation features (Fig. 1b). In Fig. 1c, P-EGaIn also presents thermoelectric feature, which can generate voltage signals by designing asymmetric temperature difference structure. This work not only highlights the superior infrared camouflage and thermoelectric performance of EGaIn but also offers important insights into thermal concealment mechanisms and extend the application to electrical signal transmission, paving the way for the development of innovative multifunctional flexible EGaIn-based infrared camouflage materials.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the fabrication process and dual functionalities of P-EGaIn.a Preparation of the P-EGaIn. b Infrared camouflage mechanism. c Thermoelectric performance.

Infrared camouflage performance of EGaIn

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, the EGaIn exhibited silver-colored and smooth liquid drop-like morphology. The reflectance of EGaIn in the range of 2.5–15.3 μm is shown in Fig. 3a. The reflectance of EGaIn in the range of 8–14 μm was high, representing that EGaIn can reflect most of the infrared radiation in 8–14 μm. The unique characteristic endows EGaIn with infrared camouflage performance, which can reflect the surrounding thermal infrared radiation to mask its own infrared radiation. Figure 3b summarizes the real temperature of EGaIn (collected by a thermoelectric couple), the radiation temperatures of EGaIn, and the hot plate below it. The EGaIn presented a similar infrared radiation temperature (23.6 °C) to its real temperature (23.1 °C), and close to the ambient temperature (about 24.0 °C). To evaluate the infrared camouflage performance of EGaIn in detail, the EGaIn with different thicknesses (0.1 cm, 0.25 cm, and 0.45 cm) (Supplementary Fig. 2) were attached to hot plate, and the temperature of the hot plate was set to five values (about 100.0 °C, 150.0 °C, 200.0 °C, 250.0 °C, and 300.0 °C). Figure 3c is the infrared radiation image of hot plate (about 103.6 °C). Figure 3d–f is the infrared thermal images of EGaIn with different thicknesses on a hot plate (about 100.0 °C). Supplementary Figs. 3–5 show the infrared radiation images of EGaIn on hot plates with different temperatures. According to infrared radiation images, the radiation temperatures of EGaIn with different thicknesses (0.1 cm, 0.25 cm, and 0.45 cm) on the hot plates were summarized in Fig. 3g. The radiation temperature of EGaIn increased as the hot plate’s temperature rose, but the radiation temperatures of EGaIn were much lower than that of the hot plates. When the temperature of the hot plate reached ~300.0 °C, the EGaIn with thicknesses of 0.1 cm, 0.25 cm, and 0.45 cm only showed radiation temperatures of 97.4 °C, 96.7 °C, and 97.9 °C, respectively, thus showing excellent infrared camouflage performance. We also recorded the evolution curves of the radiation temperatures of the hot plate and the EGaIn with different thicknesses (0.1 cm, 0.25 cm, and 0.45 cm) on the hot plates (Fig. 3h). In Fig. 3h, when the temperature of the object increased to ~330.0 °C, the EGaIn with different thicknesses (0.1 cm, 0.25 cm, and 0.45 cm) can reduce the radiation temperature by 231.2 °C, 231.3 °C and 229.7 °C, respectively, and exhibited superior infrared camouflage performance at the high temperature (Supplementary Fig. 9). The above results indicate that EGaIn at a thin thickness, even as low as 0.1 cm also exhibited extraordinary infrared camouflage performance over a wide temperature range. In Fig. 3i, the EGaIn was heated at about 200.0 °C for 6 h, it can maintain its low radiation temperature without significant fluctuations during the heating process. Figure 3j shows the radiation temperature evolution curves of EGaIn (thickness of 0.25 cm) on a hot plate, the hot plate, and the real temperature evolution curve of EGaIn during the heating process. The radiation temperature of EGaIn (38.3 °C) was much lower than the real temperature of EGaIn (108.3 °C) after heating for 120 s. And, the real temperature of EGaIn was consistent with that of the hot plate.

According to the curve in Fig. 3a, EGaIn can reflect most of the infrared radiation in 8–14 μm, which can reflect the surrounding thermal infrared radiation to mask its real infrared radiation. This observation led us to hypothesize that the high‑temperature infrared camouflage performance of EGaIn originates primarily from its strong infrared reflectivity, specifically by reflecting the infrared radiation of the ambient air above it. To test this hypothesis, we set up an experiment to monitor the ambient air temperature above the EGaIn on a hot plate during heating process. A thermocouple was positioned at vertical distances of 1.5 cm, 5 cm, and 10 cm from the EGaIn surface to record the ambient air temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 10). During the heating process, the ambient air temperature above the EGaIn can be increased, the EGaIn will reflect the infrared radiation of the heated ambient air. The ambient temperatures at the three distances are presented in Fig. 3k–o for five heating temperatures (~98.7 °C, 152.2 °C, 200.7 °C, 241.3 °C, and 295.4 °C). It should be noted that EGaIn does not reflect infrared radiation from a single and fixed distance, it should reflect the integrated radiation of the entire ambient air above it. In Fig. 3p, even heated EGaIn to about 152.2 °C, 200.7 °C, 241.3 °C, and 295.4 °C, the average ambient air temperatures above EGaIn were also close to the infrared radiation temperatures of EGaIn. These results support our hypothesis that the high‑temperature infrared camouflage capability of EGaIn is primarily attributable to its high infrared reflectivity, which allows it to reflect the surrounding ambient infrared radiation and effectively conceal its own thermal radiation.

The schematic diagram of the infrared camouflage mechanism is shown as follows (Fig. 3q, r): when a high-temperature hot plate is coated with EGaIn, which possesses high infrared reflectance, the EGaIn surface effectively reflects its surrounding ambient infrared radiation, including the thermal radiation originating from the air above the surface. Consequently, the area of the hot plate that is coated with EGaIn exhibits a lower apparent radiative temperature compared to the uncoated area when observed through an infrared camera. In all, the infrared camouflage performance of EGaIn is based on its high reflectance in 8–14 μm, it can reflect the surrounding ambient infrared radiation to mask its real infrared radiation signals. Our work not only extends the application of EGaIn to the high-temperature infrared camouflage field but also lays a theoretical foundation for other infrared camouflage materials.

Furthermore, the changes in the surface morphology, optical properties and infrared camouflage performance of EGaIn materials after high-temperature oxidation and ultrasonic treatment were also studied. The original EGaIn surface was smooth and highly reflective. However, heating it at 200 °C will cause thermal oxidation, forming oxides of gallium and indium, which caused the surface rough and significantly reduces the reflectivity (Supplementary Fig. 11). The reflection spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 12) further confirms that as the processing temperature increased, the reflectivity of the material in the 200–2500 nm range gradually decreased. Ultrasonic treatment converted EGaIn into micron-sized powder particles (Supplementary Fig. 13). Infrared thermal images (Supplementary Figs. 14, 15) show that at the same temperature of the hot plate, the EGaIn powder exhibited a much higher radiation temperature than the bulk EGaIn. This indicates that due to the loss of reflectivity, the infrared camouflage performance of the EGaIn powder has significantly degraded. Accordingly, bulk EGaIn and EGaIn powder were irradiated by a xenon lamp and evaluated their absorbance by measuring the increase in surface temperature60,61. Under simulated solar illumination (1 kW/m², Supplementary Fig. 16), the surface temperature of EGaIn powder increased rapidly from 19.4 °C to 59.2 °C within 25 min, while bulk EGaIn warms only moderately, directly confirming its higher absorptivity and compromised infrared camouflage performance. Thus, whether it is thermal oxidation or ultrasonic treatment, both will destroy the continuous metallic surface of EGaIn, resulting in a significant decline in its infrared reflection ability and a marked deterioration in its infrared camouflage performance.

Infrared reflection performance of P-EGaIn

Although the fluidity of EGaIn endows it with programmability, the random flow characteristic still limits its application in multi-scenario camouflage fields. As shown in Fig. 4a, P-EGaIn consisting of PDMS film and EGaIn was fabricated to expand its applications. EGaIn can be coated on a flexible polymer substrate for infrared camouflage, and maintain infrared camouflage performance after bending and stretching (Fig. 4b). To show the infrared reflection performance of P-EGaIn, P-EGaIn was heated at different temperatures. Then, a hot object (≈75.0 °C) and an ice cube (≈−11.0 °C) were positioned 25 cm above P-EGaIn, and we recorded the infrared radiation temperature changes of P-EGaIn. According to Supplementary Movie 1, 2, 3, the P-EGaIn and mirror presented different infrared reflection performances when the hot object and the ice cube were positioned 25 cm above them. In Supplementary Figs. 17, 18, the P-EGaIn showed an infrared radiation temperature of about 23.7 °C, and its infrared radiation temperatures changed to about 52.5 °C and −5.3 °C when the hot object and the ice cube were positioned 25 cm above it. Compared with the P-EGaIn, the mirror showed lower reflectance than EGaIn (Supplementary Fig. 19). In Supplementary Figs. 20, 21, the mirror showed an infrared radiation temperature of about 23.6 °C, and its infrared radiation temperatures changed to 28.7 °C and 18.7 °C when a hot object and an ice cube were positioned 25 cm above it. When the P-EGaIn and mirror were placed on a hot plate (~100.0 °C), P-EGaIn showed an infrared radiation temperature of about 36.7 °C, and the mirror showed an infrared radiation temperature of about 94.8 °C (Supplementary Movie 4). In Supplementary Movie 5, 6, compared with P-EGaIn, the mirror still showed insignificant infrared radiation temperature changes when the hot object and the ice cube were positioned 25 cm above them. In Supplementary Figs. 22, 23, the infrared radiation temperatures of P-EGaIn changed from 36.7 °C to 61.7 °C and 6.7 °C when the hot object and the ice cube were positioned 25 cm above it. In Supplementary Figs. 24, 25, the infrared radiation temperatures of the mirror changed from 94.8 °C to 97.9 °C and 91.5 °C when the hot object and the ice cube were positioned 25 cm above it. To further verify the infrared reflection capability of P-EGaIn, two experiments were conducted. First, a human finger making a V gesture was placed above P-EGaIn, and an infrared camera was used to detect the infrared signals (Supplementary Fig. 31a). Second, silicone sheets in the shapes of a pentagram, hexagram, and heart were placed above P-EGaIn under both hot and cold conditions (Supplementary Figs. 32–34). The results showed light red shapes under hot conditions and dark blue shapes under cold conditions. These findings confirm that EGaIn can effectively conceal its own infrared radiation by reflecting ambient infrared radiation, demonstrating its excellent infrared reflection capability.

Thermoelectric performance of P-EGaIn

Beyond its infrared camouflage performance, EGaIn also exhibits thermoelectric power generation functionality (Fig. 5a). Once a temperature difference is created across the two sides of thermoelectric material, the electricity can be generated62,63. As shown in Fig. 5b, the output voltage of P-EGaIn increased proportionally with rising temperature difference value, as measured between its two sides. Figure 5c shows the thermoelectric power generation stability of P-EGaIn. A temperature difference of about 15 °C was applied across the two sides of P-EGaIn, and the output voltage of P-EGaIn showed no obvious change even after 10 cycles. Figure 5d, e are the output voltage evolution curve and the temperature difference evolution curve of P-EGaIn, Fig. 5f, g are the current evolution curve and the temperature difference evolution curve of P-EGaIn. The output voltage evolution curve and current evolution curve present similar shape to the temperature difference evolution curve. Figure 5h, i is the output voltage values of P-EGaIn at different temperature differences, Fig. 5j, k is the current values. As shown in Fig. 5l, the material was bent for 3000 times, and its output voltage remained stable at approximately 0.05 mV, which is nearly identical to its initial output. In Fig. 5m, when a hot object (about 35.0 °C) and a cold object (about −15.0 °C) were applied to the hot side of P-EGaIn, the output voltage not only indicated the temperature difference but also reflected whether the temperature was high or low through the positive or negative sign of the voltage. As depicted in Fig. 5n, the output voltage of the thermoelectric sensing array increased proportionally with the number of sensors in contact with fingers, indicating that the output voltage rises as more thermoelectric sensing units become operational. Electronic signal transmission application of P-EGaIn

The results in Fig. 5 prove that the P-EGaIn has thermoelectric performance and satisfactory temperature sensitivity, and present the promising application in temperature sensors. As shown in Fig. 6a, P-EGaIn was used to monitor human’s respiratory heat. The cold side of P-EGaIn was exposed to the environment, and the hot side of P-EGaIn was closed to human mouth for monitoring respiratory heat. During the human breathing process, once the hot side detect the respiratory heat, the temperature difference of P-EGaIn changes, and the output voltage will also change accordingly. The breathing states of human can be recognized by the thermal output voltage peak values and signal shapes that generated from P-EGaIn. Figure 6b shows the output voltage evolution curve of P-EGaIn, which contains four breathing states of human (sighing, coughing, deep breathing and speaking). Among them, the deep breathing shows highest peak values, and the peak values of sighing follow closely behind. The main difference of the left two breathing states is the shapes of the signals are different, the peak width of speaking is wider than coughing. These significant differences in output voltage curves present an opportunity for P-EGaIn to be utilized in respiration monitoring. Beyond the respiration monitoring, our P-EGaIn also can be used in displaying Morse code through voltage signals. In Fig. 6c, each letter in Morse code is represented by a unique combination of dots and dashes64. For distinguishing the dot and dash, the low peak signal (short breath) represents dot (Fig. 6d) and the high peak signal (long breath) represents dash (Fig. 6e). By utilizing Morse code, the transmission and encryption/decryption of information are efficiently achieved. To verify the feasibility of P-EGaIn for information transmission, the voltage signals of English letters (e.g., A, B) are shown in Fig. 6f, g. Importantly, P-EGaIn can express not only English letters, but also some English words SOS and HELP (Fig. 6h, i). Printed circuits & infrared camouflage uniforms

In this section, the applications of EGaIn were broadened to infrared camouflage circuit and infrared camouflage uniform. Traditional circuit can generate heat during the power-on process, which can be detected by infrared camera. This presents a potential vulnerability, especially when used in military electronic equipment, where heat signatures could compromise the system’s stealth and security. The fluidity of EGaIn provides it with significant potential for applications in direct ink writing printing (Fig. 7a). Figure 7b shows the EGaIn-based circuit fabricated by flexible printer. When a lamp was embedded in this EGaIn circuit, the lamp can be lit up with an external power supply was connected. The EGaIn circuit also presented a very low resistance (2.48 Ω), which indicates its advantages in electronics (Fig. 7c). Carbon nanotube (CNT) and graphene oxide (GO) are the normal ink materials, which can be used to print electric circuit. When the CNT film, GO film and EGaIn was placed on a hot plate (Fig. 7d), the EGaIn presented the different colors with the CNT film and GO film. The radiation temperatures of CNT film, GO film and EGaIn were 153.0 °C, 130.0 °C and 81.2 °C (Fig. 7e), respectively. The low radiation temperature of EGaIn is mainly ascribed to its high reflectance, making it a promising candidate for the application in infrared camouflage circuits. Discussion

In this work, we have extended the utility of liquid metal, specifically EGaIn, to the realm of infrared camouflage and presented its high-temperature infrared camouflage mechanism. EGaIn achieved an apparent infrared radiation temperature suppression due to high reflectance. Diverging from conventional infrared camouflage mechanisms, by leveraging its high reflectance in 8–14 μm, the high-temperature infrared camouflage of EGaIn is realized by reflecting the surrounding ambient infrared radiation to mask its real infrared radiation. The fluidic attributes of EGaIn enabled uniform coating on PDMS film, facilitating the creation of P-EGaIn films with ease. Furthermore, the scalability of P-EGaIn production proved its adaptability for complex scenarios, such as equipping soldiers on the battlefield with infrared camouflage uniform. Remarkably, P-EGaIn was sensitive to temperature changes, due to its thermoelectric performance, making it applicable in fields such as respiratory monitoring and Morse code transmission. The discovery of EGaIn’s infrared camouflage and its thermoelectric performance underpin its substantial potential in modern military equipment.

Methods

Materials

Liquid metal (Eutectic gallium indium (EGaIn) was fabricated by mixing the gallium (75 wt%) and indium (25 wt%), which were purchased from Shenyang Jiabei Trading Co., Ltd. MAX (Ti3AlC2) (400 mesh) was supplied by 11 Technology Co., Ltd. Rubber was brought from TaoBao. Lithium fluoride (LiF, 99%) was bought from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Hydrochloric acid (HCl) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Group Co., Ltd. The polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) prepolymers were purchased from Dow Corning. Aqueous dispersion of multi-walled carbon nanotubes (CNT) (~2.8 wt%) was supplied by XFNANO Materials Tech Co., Ltd.

Preparation of P-EGaIn and flexible P-MXene

The polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) prepolymers were mixed in ethanol solution, and the round petri dish (diameter of about 9.5 cm) with 20 mL PDMS prepolymers ethanol solution was at 80 °C to get PDMS film65–67. The EGaIn was evenly coated on the PDMS film with a coating density of about 30.0 mg/cm2 to obtain the P-EGaIn film. The EGaIn was coated on the midpoint of the PDMS film (coating density of about 30.0 mg/cm2, the EGaIn thickness was ~44.9 μm) to fabricate flexible P-EGaIn film.

MXene (Ti3C2Tx) was prepared by the following procedures68,69: 1 g LiF was dissolved in 9 M HCl solution with a 50 mL poly(tetrafluoroethylene) flask as a container and stirred for 0.5 h at 35 °C. Then the 1 g MAX (Ti3AlC2) was slowly added into the solution and stirred at 35 °C for 24 h. The neutral pH of the solution was achieved by centrifugation for many times with deionized water. Finally, the supernatant (MXene dispersions) was obtained by repeat sonication and centrifugation of the solution several times. The MXene was evenly coated on the midpoint of the PDMS film (coating density of about 30.0 mg/cm2) to to obtain the flexible P-MXene film.

Infrared camouflage experiments

The EGaIn coating with different thicknesses (0.1 cm, 0.25 cm, and 0.45 cm) was attached on the hot plates. The temperature of the object was set to four ranges (about 100.0 °C, 150.0 °C, 200.0 °C, 250.0 °C, and 300.0 °C) to evaluate the infrared camouflage performance of EGaIn. An infrared camera was used to record the radiation temperature of EGaIn and hot plate.

Characterization

The infrared camera (FLIR ONE PRO (3rd generation)) was used to test the radiation temperature of the samples. The thermocouple (JK 808 multi-channel temperature detector) was used to record the real temperature of samples. The reflectance was measured by an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet 6700) with an IR integrating sphere. The mechanical stable tests were conducted on WDS-50KN mechanical testing machine. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Regulus 8100, Hitachi, Japan) and microscope (Keyence, VHX-1000) were used to observe the morphology of the sample. The authors affirm that human research participants provided informed consent for publication of the images in Fig. 7i, j, m.
