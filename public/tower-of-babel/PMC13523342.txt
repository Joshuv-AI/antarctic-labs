Interferon-induced interstitial lung disease: a rare adverse effect induced by pegylated interferon-alpha in a patient with chronic hepatitis B-case report
Authors: Haoyue Yu, Shuangshuang Zhang, Yu Zhou, Qingqing Wen, Yan Wang, Xuemei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterferon (IFN)-induced interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a rare but potentially fatal complication. While predominantly reported in patients with chronic hepatitis C, its occurrence during therapy for chronic hepatitis B is exceedingly rare. Although steroids are widely used empirically, the optimal therapeutic regimen remains to be elucidated.Case presentationWe reported a case of a middle-aged, non-smoking Asian female patient with chronic hepatitis B who received weekly subcutaneous injection of pegylated interferon-alpha (Peg-IFNα) (alternating 135 μg or 180 μg). One month after treatment initiation (following the fifth dose), she developed exertional dyspnea, dry cough, and a low-grade fever. High-resolution computed tomography revealed bilateral interlobular septal thickening with subpleural ground-glass opacities. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated a reduced diffusion capacity, however, uncommon in ILD, the patient also exhibited pulmonary hyperinflation. Serological tests did not reveal any abnormalities in white blood cell count, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, autoantibodies, or anti-respiratory virus antibodies. Echocardiography confirmed structurally normal cardiac chambers and preserved left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction of 71%). After excluding infectious, autoimmune, and cardiogenic causes, the IFN-induced ILD was diagnosed. After discontinuation of Peg-IFNα and initiation of methylprednisolone therapy, the patient’s respiratory symptoms improved markedly within 24 h and resolved completely within a week. During follow ups, no respiratory symptom reoccurred, and chest imaging and the pulmonary function resolved gradually.ConclusionIn this study, we reported a female case who developed an IFN-induced ILD while monotherapy with IFN for chronic hepatitis B, and who experienced rapid clinical improvement after initiation of steroid therapy. To our knowledge, IFN-induced ILD is exceedingly rare in the context of PEG-IFNα monotherapy for chronic hepatitis B, particularly in female patients. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for IFN-induced ILD when unexplained pulmonary symptoms arise during therapy. Early recognition and appropriate steroid administration appear to be effective strategies for managing disease progression.

Background

Interferon (IFN)-induced interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a rare but potentially fatal adverse event, with an estimated incidence ranging from 0.01% to 0.3% [1, 2]. Notably, major reported cases have been associated with IFN therapy for chronic hepatitis C, typically in combination with ribavirin [3–5]. In contrast, IFN-induced ILD arising during Peg-IFNα monotherapy for chronic hepatitis B is exceedingly rare. The few cases documented in this specific context have primarily originated from China, perhaps reflecting the substantial burden of chronic hepatitis B in this region [5, 6]. In this case, we reported a female patient who developed an IFN-induced ILD after receiving IFN monotherapy for chronic hepatitis B, and undertake a literature review of such an unusual adverse effect.

Case presentation

We reported the case of an Asian non-smoker female in her 50 s, who was admitted due to progressive exertional dyspnea and dry cough for three weeks. Further history revealed that she had a chronic hepatitis B for over 20 years. Repeated assessments consistently demonstrated undetectable hepatitis B virus replication, with hepatitis B virus-DNA levels remaining below the assay detection limit. On March 26, 2025, she underwent a serological test again and the results indicated hepatitis B virus-DNA was undetectable, hepatitis B virus surface antigen (HBsAg) of 1.470 IU/mL, and hepatitis B virus surface antibody (HBsAb) of 36.450 IU/L. After evaluating the patient’s condition, the liver disease expert concluded the patient met the criteria for an inactive HBsAg carrier with a very low baseline HBsAg titer (1.47 IU/mL, < 500 IU/mL), undetectable HBV DNA, and positive HBsAb, constituting a preferred candidate for functional cure. According to the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Chronic Hepatitis B (2022) and the Expert Consensus on Functional Cure of Chronic Hepatitis B, PEG-IFNα monotherapy is recommended in this patient to achieve HBsAg loss (functional cure). Following the advice of the liver disease expert, since March 27, 2025, she had been receiving weekly subcutaneous injection of Peg-IFNα (alternating 135 μg or 180 μg). On April 24, she received the fifth injection of Peg-IFNα (180 μg). Three days later, she developed exertional breathlessness, nocturnal dry cough, and a low-grade fever (38 °C). A chest high-resolution computed tomography (CT) at local hospital (on April 30) revealed bilateral interlobular septal thickening with subpleural ground-glass opacities. She was subsequently advised to discontinue the Peg-IFNα, however, on May 1 she still received her sixth injection (135 μg). Within 24 h, her symptoms worsened dramatically, including chest tightness, shortness of breath, dry cough, paroxysmal choking cough at night, and orthopnea, and was even unable to finish a complete sentence. Furosemide treatment (20 mg per day) for three consecutive days was ineffective. Compared with the chest CT on April 30, the follow-up CT on May 12 demonstrated interval worsening of opacities and progressive inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 1).

On admission, her vital signs were stable (temperature 36.3 °C, heart rate 74 bpm, respiratory rate 20 bpm, blood pressure 97/63 mmHg). Arterial blood gas analysis on room air revealed pH of 7.49, PaO2 of 87 mmHg, PaCO2 of 40 mmHg, and O2 saturation of 98.7%. Lung auscultation revealed coarse breath sounds without wheezes or crackles, cardiac and abdominal examinations were unremarkable, and no rash or lymphadenopathy was found. As shown in Table 1, serologic tests revealed hepatitis B virus-DNA was undetectable (< 20 IU/mL), as well as human immunodeficiency virus/hepatitis C virus serology, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide, antinuclear antibodies, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, and anti-respiratory virus antibodies were negative. Complete blood count revealed low level of lymphocyte, eosinophil, and red blood cell, with the white blood cell and the neutrophil were normal. Blood electrolyte tests revealed low level of Na+ (134.00 mmol/L), K+ (3.00 mmol/L), and Ca2+ (1.13 mmol/L). Echocardiography confirmed normal dimensions of all cardiac chambers and preserved left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction of 71%). Pulmonary function tests on May 20 indicated that the DLco decreased to 5.58 mmol/min/kPa (75% of the predicted value), and the RV/TLC significantly increased to 54.95% (149% of the predicted value). Given the severity of her respiratory symptoms precluding further cooperation, invasive procedures such as bronchoscopy or lung biopsy were omitted.​ Consequently, after systematically excluding infectious, autoimmune, and cardiogenic etiologies, a diagnosis of IFN-induced ILD was established, despite the atypical presence of hyperinflation.

After admission, Peg-IFNα was discontinued immediately. Intravenous methylprednisolone (40 mg daily) was administered for five consecutive days, with potassium supplementation and omeprazole for gastric protection. The patient’s dyspnea and speech interruption was markedly improved within 24 h of steroid initiation, and the dry cough resolved after 6 days, with exercise tolerance returned to near normal. On hospital day seven, she was discharged with normal arterial blood gas analysis on room air (pH of 7.39, PaO2 of 94 mmHg, PaCO2 of 38 mmHg, and O2 saturation of 100%). In regular follow ups, no respiratory symptom reoccurred, and inflammatory features in chest CT resolved gradually (Fig. 1). Pulmonary function tests repeated five days post-discharge confirmed significant physiological improvement: the DLco improved to 6.34 mmol/min/kPa (85% of the predicted value), and the RV/TLC decreased to 52.01% (141% of the predicted value). Discussion and conclusions

This female case exhibited typical features of IFN-induced ILD, including receiving monotherapy with IFN for chronic hepatitis B, respiratory symptoms temporally associated with IFN injections, chest imaging of progressive interstitial changes, impaired pulmonary diffusion capacity, and rapid response to steroid therapy. However, a distinctive and atypical feature of this case was the pulmonary hyperinflation, which is uncommon in ILD.

We reviewed previously published cases (ending on February 27, 2026), to identify cases of IFN-induced ILD occurring during monotherapy for chronic hepatitis B. We searched in PubMed using the term “(“interferons”[MeSH terms] OR “interferon*”) AND (“hepatitis B, chronic”[MeSH terms] OR “chronic hepatitis B”) AND (“lung diseases”[MeSH terms] OR “pulmonary complication*” OR “respiratory complication*” OR “lung injury” OR “interstitial lung disease” OR “pneumonitis”)”, nine results were obtained; and searched in EMBASE using the term “(‘interferon’/exp OR interferon*) AND (‘chronic hepatitis B’/exp OR ‘chronic hepatitis B’) AND (‘lung disease’/exp OR ‘pulmonary complication*’ OR ‘respiratory complication*’ OR ‘lung injury’ OR ‘interstitial lung disease’ OR ‘pneumonitis’)”, 139 results were obtained. After fully assessed all results’ titles, abstracts, and full texts, only two cases from China were included [5, 6].

A detailed comparison between these published cases and ours is presented in Table 2. While all three patients were around their 50 s to 60 s, our case is the first reported female​ with this specific adverse event. Most notably, the latency period from IFN initiation to symptom onset was markedly abbreviated in our patient (one month), representing the shortest interval reported to date compared to up to ten months in the previous cases. This remarkably short temporal association serves as a critical clinical alert, suggesting that IFN-induced pulmonary toxicity can manifest precipitously. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for respiratory complications even during the nascent stages of therapy. Radiographically, all three cases exhibited ground-glass opacities, while with varying accompanying features such as consolidation or interlobular septal thickening. Physiologically, a reduction in diffusion capacity was consistent across all patients, aligning with typical ILD features [7]. However, a distinctive finding in our case was the significant pulmonary hyperinflation.​ Given the patient’s non-smoking status and absence of prior lung disease, pre-existing obstruction was unlikely. While this hyperinflation may reflect transient drug-induced bronchospasm or decreased compliance, distinguishing functional changes from structural damage requires longitudinal pulmonary function assessment.​ Importantly, drug-induced bronchiolocentric interstitial pneumonia—which can present with ground-glass opacities and coexisting hyperinflation—remains a pertinent differential diagnosis that cannot be excluded as a contributing factor to the observed hyperinflation [8, 9]. As a subset of drug-induced lung disease, the timely recognition of IFN-induced ILD is important to ensuring a favorable prognosis [10]. However, establishing this diagnosis remains a clinical challenge, which hinges on the exclusion of alternative etiologies [5, 11]. The clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, and the most frequently described CT and histopathologic patterns are non-specific [5, 11]. This diagnostic dilemma was evident in our case.​ While the patient’s clinical presentation was initially ambiguous, the treating physician recognized the temporal association with Peg-IFNα and advised immediate discontinuation of the drug. This decision highlights the critical importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion​ among medical staff. Although rare, clinicians must be vigilant for IFN-induced ILD, particularly in patients who develop new-onset respiratory symptoms within the first few months of therapy. In such scenarios, prompt imaging and pulmonary function assessment are warranted [5]. Although bronchoscopy, bronchoalveolar lavage cytological examination, and lung biopsy could provide direct evidence for the diagnosis of IFN-induced ILD, these examinations are often precluded due to their invasiveness, particularly in patients suffering severe symptoms.

The treatment of IFN-induced ILD involves three important aspects: IFN discontinuation, steroid therapy, and supportive care [2, 12, 13]. The cornerstone of managing IFN-induced ILD is prompt discontinuation of the offending agent. Although the administration of steroid, dosage, and route remains controversial, substantial evidence supports their efficacy in inducing remission [14–18]. Our case exemplifies this therapeutic paradigm.​ Following the cessation of Peg-IFNα and the initiation of intravenous methylprednisolone (40 mg daily), the patient demonstrated a rapid clinical response, with complete resolution of symptoms within days. This rapid reversibility underscores the importance of early steroid administration. Although determining the precise dose based on disease severity is crucial to prevent relapse [5], our experience suggests that a moderate dose of methylprednisolone (40 mg) is both safe and effective for patients with chronic hepatitis B, provided that viral reactivation is closely monitored. It is imperative to acknowledge the theoretical risk of steroid-induced hepatitis B reactivation. Consequently, a careful assessment of viral load and liver function prior to and during therapy is warranted. Nevertheless, early initiation of pulse steroid therapy should be considered in patients with rapidly progressive disease, followed by a stepwise taper to a lower maintenance dose once clinical stability is achieved [5].

In conclusion, IFN-induced ILD in patients with chronic hepatitis B is extremely rare. For respiratory symptoms after IFN injections, the possibility of IFN-induced ILD should be considered. While chest CT is indispensable for characterizing interstitial changes, the diagnosis ultimately rests on a comprehensive clinical assessment and the exclusion of alternative etiologies. Given the rarity of IFN-induced ILD in patients with chronic hepatitis B, optimal therapeutic strategies remain to be fully elucidated. Future investigations are needed to clarify the role of steroid in the management of IFN-induced ILD, including optimal dosing, treatment duration, and timing of initiation. Furthermore, studies should examine how specific comorbid conditions influence disease course and response to therapy, as such factors may modify both short-term outcomes and long-term recovery.

There were several limitations in this case. First, invasive procedures such as bronchoscopy, lavage cytology, or lung biopsy were precluded by the severity of the patient’s condition; consequently, histopathologic confirmation could not be obtained. Furthermore, although serology for respiratory viruses was negative, the absence of direct detection methods (e.g., PCR assays of nasopharyngeal swabs, respiratory secretions, or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid) limits our ability to definitively exclude an active viral infection. These decisions were made to minimize patient discomfort and avoid unnecessary healthcare expenditures. Second, serum levels of KL-6 and heat shock protein 47 were not measured, which may be effective for IFN-induced ILD [19, 20]. Third, currently, long-term follow-up data is lacking, extended monitoring of symptoms, chest imaging, and pulmonary function would be valuable for evaluating treatment efficacy. Finally, because she had never experienced any respiratory system symptoms, the patient had not undergone a pulmonary function test before this course. Data of baseline pulmonary function would be helpful for assessing the severity of the patient’s current condition.
