Local Limit of Random Regular Bipartite Planar Maps
Nicolas Tokka

Abstract
We prove the existence of the local limit of uniform random d d -regular bipartite planar maps, for every d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 , as the number of vertices tends to infinity. The proof relies on a bijection between maps and so-called blossoming trees established in a previous work. After proving local convergence of the associated decorated trees, we extend the bijection to infinite trees and transfer the convergence to planar maps. The limiting object is almost surely one-ended and recurrent for the simple random walk.

Introduction

In the last two decades, the study of local limits of large random planar maps has attracted significant interest. The subject was initiated by the seminal work of Angel and Schramm
[
2
]
, who proved in 2003 that uniform planar triangulations with
n
faces converge in distribution, for the local topology, to a probability measure supported on infinite triangulations. The limiting object is now known as the Uniform Infinite Planar Triangulation (UIPT). This result laid the foundations for the investigation of local limits of large random maps.

Shortly thereafter, Krikun
[
14
]
established the analogous convergence of uniform quadrangulations toward the Uniform Infinite Planar Quadrangulation (UIPQ). Independently, Chassaing and Durhuus constructed another model of random infinite planar quadrangulation. Their approach relies on a powerful bijective perspective, using the Cori–Vauquelin–Schaeffer bijection
[
20
]
between quadrangulations and decorated plane trees. Afterwards, Ménard
[
16
]
proved that both these infinite random quadrangulations have the same law.

Chassaing and Durhuus’s method can be summarized in two steps. First, prove the local convergence of the associated random trees obtained by applying the Cori–Vauquelin–Schaeffer bijection. Second, extend this bijection to the case of infinite trees to define their infinite random quadrangulation.

This general strategy has proved robust and has since been implemented in a variety of settings. It has been notably implemented via the generalized bijection known as the
mobile bijection

[
6
]
. This framework has led to a large number of results on local limits of random maps, e.g.
[
10
,
15
,
22
,
23
]
.

Another powerful bijective scheme has been used similarly to define families of infinite random maps. There are called
mating-of-trees
bijections. These bijections generalize Mullin’s bijection
[
18
]
and encode some families of decorated maps by two-dimensional walks, see for example
[
9
,
13
,
21
]
.

Our work is based on a third bijective paradigm between maps and
blossoming trees
with charge constraints. While this paradigm was introduced twenty years ago
[
19
,
4
,
5
]
, it was not clear how to use it to derive probabilistic convergence results. The present work addresses precisely this gap using a recent version of the blossoming bijection due to Albenque, Ménard, and the author
[
1
]
.
Specifically, we study local limits of uniform
d
-regular bipartite planar maps. Precise definitions are given in Section
2
. Our main result establishes the local convergence of these maps, as their number of vertices tends to infinity, toward a limiting random infinite map that we call the Uniform Infinite
d
-Regular Bipartite Planar Map (
d
-UIRBPM), see Theorem
2
.

Previous works also studied local convergence of bipartite maps
[
3
,
7
,
22
]
, but we want to emphasize that, in our work, we study maps with vertex degree
d
, and which are furthermore required to be bipartite. In contrast, the models of maps studied in
[
3
,
22
]
consist of maps with only control on face degrees, and which are bipartite (i.e. all faces are required to have even degree). The only work with similar constraints is the convergence of Eulerian planar triangulations by Carrance
[
7
]
, which relies in particular on the so-called layer decomposition, which is both technical and specific to triangulations. The special case
d=3
of the present article recovers this result in the dual setting. Moreover, the fact that we can have a simpler proof stems from the fact that, as already mentioned, we use another type of bijections. In contrast to Carrance’s approach, our work is more robust and allows to fully control the vertex degree distribution as we plan to discuss in future work, see Section
5
. Moreover, to our knowledge, this is the first proof of a local limit theorem for planar maps that is based entirely on the blossoming-tree paradigm, rather than on the mobile bijection aforementioned.

We proceed by first studying the local convergence of uniform well-charged trees corresponding to
d
-regular bipartite maps. We then show that these random trees converge in distribution to an explicit infinite plane tree with a unique spine, that is, a single infinite branch starting from the root. Moreover, we identify the limiting distribution as that of a multitype Bienaymé–Galton–Watson tree conditioned to survive. This is achieved in Section
3
.
In a second step, we extend the blossoming bijection to infinite trees and prove its continuity on the support of the limiting tree law. This allows us to transfer the local convergence of trees to the local convergence of maps, and therefore establishing the existence of the
d
-UIRBPM. This is covered in Section
4
.

The nature of
d
-UIRBPM implies directly that it is almost surely recurrent for the simple random walk. This follows from a theorem of Gurel-Gurevich and Nachmias in
[
12
]
, which ensures recurrence for local limits of uniformly rooted finite maps whose root vertex degree has an exponential tail. This is automatically satisfied in our regular setting.

As mentioned above, beyond the regular case, the method developed here appears to be flexible and suggests several extensions, which are discussed in Section
5
. We strongly believe that uniform bipartite planar maps with bounded degrees and prescribed degree distributions also converge, for the local topology, to the distribution of an infinite bipartite planar map. In addition, this generalization for bipartite maps with vertex degree
2
and
d
could lead to the study of local limits of maps decorated by statistical physics models such as the Ising model or the hard particle model. We plan to tackle these questions in future work and give some indications of how to address them in Section
5
.

2
Definitions and main result

2.1
Maps

A
planar map
is a proper embedding of a connected planar graph on the 2-dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, considered up to orientation-preserving homeomorphisms.

The
edges
and
vertices
of a map are the natural counterparts of the edges and vertices of the underlying graph. The
faces
of a map
\mathrm{m}
are the connected components of the complement of its embedded graph. The sets of vertices, edges, and faces are denoted by
\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{m})
,
\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{m})
and
\mathrm{F}(\mathrm{m})
, respectively. Note that loops and multiple edges are allowed.

The
corners
of a map are ordered pairs of consecutive half-edges around a vertex, where a
half-edge
is one of the two components obtained by splitting an edge at its midpoint. The
degree
of a vertex or a face is defined as the number of its incident corners. A map is
d
-regular
if all its vertices have degree
d
.

Maps are assumed to be
rooted
, meaning that one of their corners is designated as the origin. This corner is called the root corner and is indicated by a double arrow in figures. The vertex and the face incident to this corner are called the
root vertex
and the
root face
, respectively.

A
plane map

(\mathrm{m},\mathrm{f})
is a pair consisting of a map
\mathrm{m}
together with a marked face
\mathrm{f}\in\mathrm{F}(\mathrm{m})
. A plane map has a canonical embedding in the plane via stereographic projection, by choosing the
outer infinite face
as the marked face. In particular, the outer face is not necessarily its root face. Finally, a
plane tree
is a plane map with a single face.

Bipartite maps

A
bipartite
map is a map that admits a proper
2
-coloring of its vertices. Note that a bipartite map has exactly two proper 2-colorings, which differ only by swapping the color of all vertices.

In this work,
each bipartite map will be endowed with one of these two colorings
, say in black and white. We denote the black and white vertices of a bipartite map
\mathrm{m}
by
\mathrm{V}_{\!\bullet}(\mathrm{m})
and
\mathrm{V}_{\!\circ}(\mathrm{m})
, respectively. Finally, a rooted bipartite map is said to be
black-rooted
or
white-rooted
depending on the color of its root vertex.

Regular bipartite maps

For any
d\geq 3
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}^{d}
the set of all finite, black-rooted,
d
-regular bipartite planar maps.

Remark 1
.

Note that every
d
-regular bipartite planar map has the same number of black and white vertices. Thus, the total number of vertices is even. Conversely, for any
n\geq 1
, there exists such a map with exactly
2n
vertices.

For any
n\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
the subset of
\mathcal{M}^{d}
consisting of maps with
n
black vertices. Similarly, the corresponding sets of such maps with an additional marked face are denoted by
\mathcal{M}^{d,\bm{\mathrm{f}}}
and
\mathcal{M}^{d,\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{n}
, respectively.

Denote by
M^{d}_{n}
the number of maps in
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
, for all
n\geq 1
, and let
M^{d}(z)
be its associated generating series, i.e.:

M^{d}(z)\coloneqq\sum\limits_{\mathrm{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{d}}z^{|\mathrm{V}_{\!\bullet}(\mathrm{m})|}=\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}M^{d}_{n}z^{n}.

Similarly, define
{M}^{d,\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{n}
and
{M}^{d,\bm{\mathrm{f}}}(z)
for maps in
\mathcal{M}^{d,\bm{\mathrm{f}}}
. A straightforward application of Euler’s formula shows that:

{M}^{d,\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{n}=\left(2+(d-2)n\right)M^{d}_{n}.

2.2
Local convergence of random maps

We first define a distance on
\mathcal{M}^{d}
. For
\mathrm{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{d}
and
R\geq 0
, let
B_{R}(\mathrm{m})
denote the submap of
\mathrm{m}
consisting of all vertices at graph distance at most
R
from the root vertex, together with all edges that have at least one extremity at distance less or equal to
R-1
from the root vertex. Moreover,
B_{R}(\mathrm{m})
inherits the root corner from
\mathrm{m}
. For
\mathrm{m},\mathrm{m}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}^{d}
, we set:

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{loc}}\left(\mathrm{m},\mathrm{m}^{\prime}\right)\coloneqq{\left(1+\mathrm{sup}\left\{R\geq 0:B_{R}(\mathrm{m})=B_{R}(\mathrm{m}^{\prime})\right\}\right)}^{-1}.

(2)

The topology on
\mathcal{M}^{d}
induced by
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is known as the
local topology
. The closure
(\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{d},\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{loc}})
of the metric space
(\mathcal{M}^{d},\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{loc}})
is a Polish space, and the elements of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{M}^{d}
are referred to as
infinite
d
-regular bipartite rooted planar maps
. Finally, such a map
\mathrm{m}
is said to be
one-ended
if, for every finite submap
\mathrm{m}^{\prime}\subset\mathrm{m}
, exactly one connected component of
\mathrm{m}\setminus\mathrm{m}^{\prime}
contains infinitely many faces.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 2
.

For any
n\geq 1
, denote by
\mathbb{P}^{d}_{n}
the uniform probability measure on
\mathcal{M}^{d}_{n}
.

Then, the sequence
\left(\mathbb{P}^{d}_{n}\right)_{n}
converges weakly, for the local topology, to a limiting probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{d}_{\infty}
supported on the set of one-ended infinite
d
-regular bipartite planar maps.

The proof relies on a bijection between plane bipartite maps and a certain class of decorated trees, which we define in the next section. We first prove local convergence of this class of trees. Next, we show that the bijection extends naturally to the infinite setting, which allows us to conclude the proof.

3
Local convergence of well-charged trees

In this section, we define a family of decorated plane trees, first introduced in
[
4
]
and called
well-charged trees
, which are in bijection with bipartite plane maps. We then analyze the local convergence of the uniform distribution on these trees as their size grows to infinity.

3.1
Bijection between bipartite plane maps and well-charged trees

Definition of well-charged trees

In this work, a
blossoming tree
is a
bipartite
plane tree in which each corner may carry some half-edges. Here, bipartite means that the tree is endowed with one of its two proper
2
-colorings, in accordance with the definitions given in Section
2
. A half-edge attached to a black vertex is oriented outward and is referred to as an
opening stem
, and a half-edge incident to a white vertex is oriented inward and is called a
closing stem
. A rooted blossoming tree
\mathrm{t}
is called
white
or
black
according to the color of its root vertex, denoted by
\varnothing_{\mathrm{t}}
.

The
offspring
of a vertex is the ordered collection of its incident half-edges, excluding the one that leads to its parent. The
height
of a vertex is its distance from the root vertex, and the
height
of a stem is the height of its unique incident vertex.

The
charge
of a blossoming tree
\mathrm{t}
is the difference between the number of its closing stems and opening stems, and is denoted by
\mathrm{c}(\mathrm{t})
. If
\mathrm{t}
is rooted, then the
charge
of a vertex
v\in\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{t})
is defined as the charge of the subtree of
\mathrm{t}
rooted at
v
and is denoted by
\mathrm{c}_{\mathrm{t}}(v)
, or simply
\mathrm{c}(v)
when the context is clear.

Finally, a rooted blossoming tree
\mathrm{t}
is
well-charged
if it meets the following charge conditions:

\left({C}\right):~\begin{cases}\mathrm{c}_{\mathrm{t}}(v)\leq 1,~\forall v\in\mathrm{V}_{\!\bullet}(\mathrm{t}),\\
\mathrm{c}_{\mathrm{t}}(v)\geq 0,~\forall v\in\mathrm{V}_{\!\circ}(\mathrm{t}).\end{cases}

(3)

For
n\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{T}^{d}_{n}
the set of black-rooted well-charged
d
-regular trees of charge
0
with
n
black vertices, and we write
\mathcal{T}^{d}\coloneqq\sqcup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{T}^{d}_{n}
.

Closure of well-charged trees

Following
[
1
,
4
]
, we now describe the
closure
of a blossoming tree of charge
0
. Let
\mathrm{t}
be a blossoming tree of charge
0
. Consider the ordered, cyclic sequence of its opening and closing stems obtained by performing a clockwise walk around
\mathrm{t}
.

Figure 1:
A tree
\mathrm{t}
in
\mathcal{T}^{4}_{3}
with its charge function (left), the same tree drawn differently with the matching of its stems (middle), and its closure (right).

The sequence induces a perfect matching between the opening and closing stems as in a parenthesis word. The closure is then obtained by merging each matched pair of stems into a single edge. Each edge is drawn in the plane so that, when oriented from the opening stem to the closing stem, the infinite face lies to its left.

We can prove that the resulting object is a plane map and that it does not depend of the order in which the edges are added.

Proposition 3

(Theorem 2.5,
[
1
]
, see also
[
4
]
)
.

For any
d\geq 3
and
n\geq 1
, the
closure
operation
\Phi
is a one-to-one correspondence between
\mathcal{T}^{d}_{n}
and
\mathcal{M}^{d,\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{n}
. In particular,

|\mathcal{T}^{d}_{n}|=M^{d,\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{n}.

Convergence of random well-charged trees

(\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{d},\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{loc}})

Proof of Theorem

Let

To this end, we first study in greater detail the structure of the finite well-charged trees.

Structure of well-charged trees and generating functions

We begin by investigating the possible charge values and offspring for trees in
\mathcal{T}^{d}
. A direct consequence of the degree constraints yields the following result.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathrm{t}\in\mathcal{T}^{d}
. Then, for any
v\in\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{t})
:

•

If
v\in\mathrm{V}_{\!\circ}(\mathrm{t})
, then
\mathrm{c}(v)=d-1
. Moreover, its ordered offspring consists of
d-1
elements, each of which is either a closing stem or a black vertex.

•

If
v\in\mathrm{V}_{\!\bullet}(\mathrm{t})
, then
\mathrm{c}(v)=0
if
v
is the root vertex and
\mathrm{c}(v)=1
otherwise. Moreover, its ordered offspring consists of a single white vertex together with opening stems, exactly
d-1
if
v
is the root vertex and
d-2
otherwise.

Moreover, all the possibilities can occur.

Next, for enumerative purposes, we study the
recursive decomposition
of well-charged trees. A
planted
blossoming tree is a non-rooted blossoming tree with an additional distinguished dangling half-edge, which is neither an opening nor a closing stem. The unique vertex incident to this distinguished half-edge is called the
root vertex
. Such a tree is called
black
or
white
, depending on the color of its root vertex. Finally, a planted blossoming tree is
well-charged
if it satisfies the same charge conditions
({C})
as before, see (
3

A tree in
\mathcal{B}

The
root-decomposition
of a rooted or planted well-charged tree
\mathrm{t}
consists of the finite sequence, ordered according to the planar embedding, of stems and planted well-charged trees incident to the root vertex.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of all finite planted well-charged
black

1

\mathcal{W}
be the set of all finite planted well-charged

{d-1}

Denote by

and

their associated generating series:

\mathrm{B}(z)=\sum\limits_{\mathrm{t}\in\mathcal{B}}z^{|\mathrm{V}_{\!\bullet}(\mathrm{t})|}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{W}(z)=\sum\limits_{\mathrm{t}\in\mathcal{W}}z^{|\mathrm{V}_{\!\bullet}(\mathrm{t})|}.

Then, we have the following result.

Lemma 7
.

The generating functions satisfy the following relations:

\mathrm{W}(z)=(1+\mathrm{B}(z))^{d-1}\text{,}\quad\mathrm{B}(z)=z(d-1)\mathrm{W}(z)\text{,}\quad{M}^{d,\bm{\mathrm{f}}}(z)=zd\,\mathrm{W}(z),

and
\mathrm{B}(z)
is characterized by the Lagrangian equation:

\mathrm{B}(z)=z(d-1)(1+\mathrm{B}(z))^{d-1}.

(5)

Moreover, these generating functions share the common radius of convergence

\rho_{d}=(d-2)^{(d-2)}/(d-1)^{d}

Proof.

The first two relations follow from Lemma
6
. The statement concerning the offspring of the root vertex of a tree
\mathrm{t}\in\mathcal{T}^{d}
of this lemma, combined with Proposition
3
yields the third relation.

The Lagrangian equation characterizing
\mathrm{B}(z)
follows by direct substitution, and allows one to determine its radius of convergence, see
[
11

. Finally, we can derive explicit expressions of
\mathrm{W}(z)
and

∎

Explicit computation of (
4
)

In order to prove the existence of (
4
), as
n
tends to infinity, and compute its value, we introduce the following notation. We denote by
m_{k}(\mathrm{t})
the number of vertices of
\mathrm{t}
at height
k
, and we set
n_{k}(\mathrm{t})=|\mathrm{V}_{\!\bullet}(B_{k}(\mathrm{t}))|
. To begin, assume that
k
is odd. Thus, the cut of
\mathrm{t}
at height

leads to

together with an ordered collection of stems and

trees in

\mathbb{P}\big(B_{k}(\bm{T}_{n})=B_{k}(\mathrm{t})\big)=\frac{1}{{M}^{d,\bm{\mathrm{f}}}_{n}}\cdot[z^{n-n_{k}(\mathrm{t})}]\mathrm{W}(z)^{m_{k}(\mathrm{t})}=\frac{[z^{n-n_{k}(\mathrm{t})}]\mathrm{W}(z)^{m_{k}(\mathrm{t})}}{d\,[z^{n-1}]\mathrm{W}(z)},

where

extracts the coefficient of

in a formal power series. Both numerator and denominator can be computed explicitly by applying the Lagrange inversion formula
[

on

\frac{(d-2)n+1}{n-n_{k}(\mathrm{t})}\,\frac{d\,m_{k}(\mathrm{t})\,(d-1)^{n-n_{k}(\mathrm{t})}}{d\,(d-1)^{n-1}}\,\binom{(d-1)(n+m_{k}(\mathrm{t})-n_{k}(\mathrm{t}))-1}{n-n_{k}(\mathrm{t})-1}\binom{(d-1)n}{n}^{-1}.

(6)

The case where

Closure operation

We extend the closure operation to trees in
\mathcal{S}^{d}
. Let
T\in\mathcal{S}^{d}
. We define its closure
\Phi(T)
as follows: starting from the root, we perform a walk along the tree to the right. Whenever an opening stem is encountered, say the stem with index
l
, we merge it with the stem with index
\sigma_{T}(l)
into a single edge. The edge is drawn so that it does not cross any edge, vertex, or stem, and such that if it were oriented from the opening stem to the closing stem, then the outer infinite face would lie on its left.

After this merging step, at most two faces have incident stems, one of which is the infinite face. On the finite face, the number of opening and closing stems coincide and is finite. We perform the same merging procedure there.

Once these mergings have been completed, we continue the walk around the tree until the next opening stem, and we repeat the procedure. Figure
3
illustrates the first steps of the closure procedure.

By construction, this leaves us with an infinite map
M=\Phi(T)
, meaning that it admits an embedding in the plane such that every bounded subset of the plane intersects only finitely many edges and vertices. This can be proved similarly to the argument described in
[
22
]
. In addition,
M
is one-ended since
T
has a single spine.

Moreover, we can identify the corners of
T
with those of
M
, and we canonically root
M
at the root corner of
T
. Note that, contrary to the finite case,
M
has no marked face.

Finally, note that no unmatched stems remain in
M
by Proposition
9
. Thus, the closure of
M
belongs to
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{d}
.

.

Let

and let

. Recall the definition of the local distance, see (

). We show that there exists

such that for all

, if
B_{R^{\prime}}(T)=B_{R^{\prime}}(T^{\prime})
, then

B_{R}(\Phi(T))=B_{R}(\Phi(T^{\prime})).

(8)

If
T
is finite, then this statement holds by taking any
R^{\prime}>0
such that
B_{R^{\prime}}(T)=T
. Thus, we may assume that
T
is infinite.

We adapt the approach of
[

]
. The subsequent constructions are illustrated in Figure

R_{1}>0
sufficiently large so that all vertices in

are contained in

, i.e.

\mathrm{V}\big(B_{R}(\Phi(T))\big)\subset\mathrm{V}\big(B_{R_{1}}(T)\big).

The existence of
R_{1}
follows from the fact that each vertex of
T
has degree
d
. Next, observe that there are only finitely many stems in
T
incident to the vertices in
B_{R_{1}}(T)
, so that we can denote by
k^{-}
the minimal index among these stems, and by
k^{+}\geq k^{-}
the maximal index. Set
x=\max_{k^{-}\leq k\leq k^{+}}{\mathcal{C}_{T}(k)}
and define

\displaystyle K^{-}

\displaystyle=\sup\{k<k^{-}:~\mathcal{C}_{T}(k)>x\},

\displaystyle K^{+}

\displaystyle=\inf\{k>k^{+}:~\mathcal{C}_{T}(k)\geq x\}.

Both
K^{-}
and
K^{+}
are finite by Proposition
10
. By construction,
K^{-}
is the index of a closing stem
s^{-}
, and
K^{+}
is the index of an opening stem
s^{+}
.

Now, for any
s\in\mathrm{S}(T)
with index

K^{-}\leq\sigma_{T}(k)\leq K^{+}
by construction. Hence, no stem with index in

is matched to a stem with index in
\mathbb{Z}\setminus[K^{-},K^{+}]
. Let

R_{2}=\max(h_{\mathrm{spine}}(s^{-}),h_{\mathrm{spine}}(s^{+})),

(9)

so that no stem with spine height greater than

is matched to a stem incident to a vertex in
B_{R_{1}}(T)
. Finally, take

R^{\prime}=\max\{h(s):s\in\mathrm{S}(T),h_{\mathrm{spine}}(s)\leq R_{2}\},

where
h(s)
stands for the height of stem

. This yields the following inclusion, which completes the proof:

B_{R}(\Phi(T))\subset\Phi(B_{R^{\prime}}(T)).\qed

5
Perspectives for bipartite maps with general degree distribution and the Ising model

In this section, we outline several ongoing research directions concerning the local limits of random bipartite maps with general degree distributions and random maps endowed with an Ising configuration.

Local limit of bipartite maps with general degree distribution

First, we expect that Theorem
2
to also hold for bipartite planar maps with bounded degrees and with a prescribed degree distribution. More precisely, let
\mathcal{M}_{n}
be the set of all bipartite planar maps with
n
vertices and let
q_{\bullet},q_{\circ}:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be two weight functions. Let
\bm{M}_{n}
be a random bipartite map sampled from
\mathcal{M}_{n}
with probability proportional to its weight, defined by

\prod_{v\in\mathrm{V}_{\!\bullet}(\mathrm{m})}q_{\bullet}({\deg(v)})\prod_{v\in\mathrm{V}_{\!\circ}(\mathrm{m})}q_{\circ}({\deg(v)}).

In the case of bounded degrees, that is, when
q_{\bullet}
and
q_{\circ}
are nonzero only for a finite set of indices, we expect
\mathcal{M}_{n}
to converge in distribution, for the local topology, to a random one-ended bipartite planar map whose distribution depends on
q_{\bullet}
and
q_{\circ}
.

The proof in this setting should be similar in spirit to the one provided here. Indeed, it would rely on the same blossoming bijection and the argument would proceed in the same steps. First, we establish the local convergence of the corresponding family of blossoming trees. Second, we extend the bijection to infinite trees, allowing the local convergence to be transferred to maps. However, the argument would be more elaborate than in the present work, since there are no explicit enumerative formulas in this broader setting. Instead, we could rely on the Drmota-Lalley-Wood Theorem to study the generating functions of blossoming trees analytically, together with the general results of Stephenson
[
22
]
regarding the local limit of multitype Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees.

A natural further step would be to extend this result to the case of unbounded degrees. However, this would require additional theoretical results, as the family of trees arising from the bijection is more complex. Nevertheless, we expect a similar local convergence and a corresponding limiting distribution.

Local limit of maps endowed with Ising configuration

As a continuation of the previous perspective, we also expect to derive the local convergence of planar maps decorated with an Ising configuration from the local convergence of bipartite plane maps with vertex degree
2
and
d
. Let us be more precise.
A
spin configuration
on a map
\mathrm{m}
is a mapping
\sigma:\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{m})\rightarrow\{-1,+1\}
. An edge
e\in\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{m})
is said to be
monochromatic
if its two endpoints are mapped to the same value. The Ising model on planar maps consists in studying random finite planar maps endowed with a spin configuration, where the probability of sampling a map
(\mathrm{m},\sigma)
is proportional to
\nu^{m(\mathrm{m},\sigma)}
, where
m(\mathrm{m},\sigma)
denotes the number of monochromatic edges of
\mathrm{m}
, and where
\nu
is a fixed non-negative real number (representing the inverse temperature of the model).

A well-known method for studying maps endowed with a spin configuration relies on a connection with bipartite maps. Consider a map endowed with a spin configuration
(\mathrm{m},\sigma)
, and for each vertex
v\in\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{m})
, color
v
in black if
\sigma(v)=-1
and in white if
\sigma(v)=+1
. Note that, at this stage,
\mathrm{m}
may not be bipartite in terms of this vertex-coloring. Then replace each edge of
\mathrm{m}
by a chain of arbitrary length consisting of black and white vertices of degree
2
, so that the resulting map becomes bipartite. Necessarily, a monochromatic edge is replaced by a chain of odd length, whereas a non-monochromatic is replaced by a chain of even length. This transformation allows to get a functional equation between the generating functions of both models, see
[
1
,
4
]
. Observe that if
\mathrm{m}
is
d
-regular, then the bipartite maps obtained in this manner have vertex degree exactly
2
and
d
.

We expect that this transformation could lead to a proof of local convergence of
d
-regular maps endowed with an Ising configuration, at least in the regime
\nu\leq 1
, which corresponds to the anti-ferromagnetic Ising model.

Appendix A
Multitype Bienaymé-Galton-Watson and proof of Proposition
8

The purpose of this appendix is to prove Proposition
8
, whose proof relies on the theory of multitype Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees.
We begin by recalling useful definitions concerning multitype trees and random multitype Bienaymé-Galton–Watson trees; see
[
22
, Section 2.1]
for definitions with the Ulam formalism.

A.1
Definitions and notation

Multitype trees

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a finite set. A
\mathcal{K}
-type tree
is a pair
(\mathrm{t},\kappa)
consisting of a plane tree
\mathrm{t}
(possibly infinite), either rooted or planted, together with a function
\kappa:\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{t})\rightarrow\mathcal{K}
. The value

is called the

. When the context is clear, we simply write

instead of
(\mathrm{t},\kappa)
. The root of such a tree

is denoted by
\varnothing_{\mathrm{t}}
. We denote by
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{K},(i)}
the set of all finite
\mathcal{K}
-type trees
\mathrm{t}
for which
\varnothing_{\mathrm{t}}
has type
i\in\mathcal{K}
.

For any
\mathcal{K}
-type tree
\mathrm{t}
and
v\in\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{t})
, the
list of types of the ordered offspring
of
v
in

is defined by

\bm{\mathsf{w}}_{\mathrm{t}}(v)\coloneqq\left(\kappa(u_{1}),\ldots,\kappa(u_{l})\right),

where
\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{l}\right)
denotes the ordered offspring of
v
.

Finally, we denote by
\mathsf{W}_{\mathcal{K}}\coloneqq\cup_{n\geq 0}{\mathcal{K}^{n}}
the set of all finite lists of types. For
\bm{\mathsf{w}}\in\mathsf{W}_{\mathcal{K}}
, denote its size by

and for any

|\bm{\mathsf{w}}|_{i}\coloneqq\big|\left\{1\leq j\leq|\bm{\mathsf{w}}|:\bm{\mathsf{w}}_{j}=i\right\}\big|.

Multitype Bienaymé-Galton–Watson distribution

First, an
ordered offspring distribution
is a sequence
\bm{\zeta}=\left(\zeta^{(i)}\right)_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
, where
{\zeta}^{(i)}
is a probability measure on
\mathsf{W}_{\mathcal{K}}
. We then define the distribution of a
\mathcal{K}
-type Galton–Watson tree
rooted at a vertex of type

and with ordered offspring distribution

as the following measure:

\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\zeta}}^{(i)}\!\left(\mathrm{t},\kappa\right)\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{\{\kappa(\varnothing_{\mathrm{t}})=i\}}\prod\limits_{v\in\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{t})}{\zeta^{(\kappa(v))}{\big(\bm{\mathsf{w}}_{\mathrm{t}}(v)\big)}},

(12)

for any finite tree

.
Note that
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\zeta}}^{(i)}
is, in general, only a sub-probability. It becomes a probability measure in the critical case, which is defined below. In that case, a tree sampled from this distribution is referred to as a
\bm{\zeta}
-Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree with root type
i
.

Criticality, Extinction, and Irreducibility

For ease of notation, we index
\mathcal{K}=\{\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{K}\}
. We define the

of
\bm{\zeta}
as the matrix
M=\left(m_{i,j}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq K}

is the expected number of type

m_{i,j}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\!\left(|\bm{\mathsf{w}}^{(i)}|_{j}\right)=\sum\limits_{\bm{\mathsf{w}}\in\mathsf{W}_{\mathcal{K}}}{|\bm{\mathsf{w}}|_{\kappa_{j}}\,\zeta^{(\kappa_{i})}(\bm{\mathsf{w}})},

where
\bm{\mathsf{w}}^{(i)}
is a random variable on
\mathsf{W}_{\mathcal{K}}
with distribution
\zeta^{(\kappa_{i})}
.

We say that
\bm{\zeta}
, or equivalently

.

We turn to describe a well-known sufficient condition on

that guarantees that a

-Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree is almost surely finite. Specifically, we say that
\bm{\zeta}

such that
\zeta^{(i)}(\{\bm{\mathsf{w}}:|\bm{\mathsf{w}}|>1\})>0
.

Proposition 12

(
[
17
]
)
.

If
\bm{\zeta}
is non-degenerate and critical, then a
\bm{\zeta}
-Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree, regardless of the root type, is almost surely finite.

Finally, the offspring distribution
\bm{\zeta}
, or equivalently

when for all

, there exists

Remark 13
.

Usually,

is required to be irreducible and critical. In that case, the Perron-Frobenius theorem applies and ensures that:

•

The integer

•

The unique, up to multiplicity, left and right eigenvectors of

have non-negative coefficients. More precisely, we denote them by

and

\sum_{i}{a_{i}}=1

\sum_{i}{a_{i}b_{i}}=1

•

Moreover, all the coefficients of

and
\bm{b}
are positive.

In next section,

will be critical but not irreducible. Still, it will satisfy the first two consequences of the Perron-Frobenius theorem. In this case, we then say that

For simplicity, we now write

and

and

holds.

Multitype Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree conditioned to survive

Under certain hypotheses (including irreducibility and criticality),
the distribution of a
\bm{\zeta}
-Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree converges weakly, for the local topology, to the distribution of a multitype Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree (with more types), called

-Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree conditioned to survive

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\bm{\zeta}}^{(i)}

When

are grafted. On the spine, the vertices follow a

\bm{\zeta}

{\widehat{\zeta}}^{(i)}(\bm{\mathsf{w}})\coloneqq\Big(\frac{1}{b_{i}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{|\bm{\mathsf{w}}|}b_{\bm{\mathsf{w}}_{j}}\Big)\,\zeta^{(i)}(\bm{\mathsf{w}}).

Then, for each vertex on the spine with offspring

A.2
Proof of Proposition

We proceed as follows. First, we describe an explicit offspring distribution

Definition of the offspring distribution

First, we define the set of types: let
\mathcal{K}\coloneqq\{\varnothing,\bullet,\circ,\mathsf{s}\}

•

\varnothing

•

\bullet

•

\mathsf{s}
represents the opening and closing stems.

Remark 14
.

To simplify further explanation, we identify the types and their representations. Conversely, we identify any tree in
\overline{\mathcal{T}}^{d}
with its natural representation as a
\mathcal{K}
-type tree.

Next, we define the offspring distributions. Roughly speaking,

•

stems, i.e., vertices with type
\mathsf{s}
, are infertile,

•

the offspring of a vertex with type
\bullet

is sampled uniformly from the set of possible offspring described in Lemma

•

the offspring of a vertex with type

\mathrm{B}(z)^{k}

This is written formally as follows, where for any an ordered sequence of types

its associated vector that counts the occurrence of each type:

\displaystyle\zeta^{(\varnothing)}(\bm{\mathsf{w}})\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{\{L(\bm{\mathsf{w}})=(0,0,1,d-1)\}}\frac{1}{d},

\displaystyle\zeta^{(\bullet)}(\bm{\mathsf{w}})\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{\{L(\bm{\mathsf{w}})=(0,0,1,d-2)\}}\frac{1}{d-1},

\displaystyle\zeta^{(\circ)}(\bm{\mathsf{w}})\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\mathds{1}_{\{L(\bm{\mathsf{w}})=(0,k,0,d-1-k)\}}\frac{(d-2)^{d-1-k}}{(d-1)^{d-1}},

\displaystyle\zeta^{(\mathsf{s})}(\bm{\mathsf{w}})\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{\{\bm{\mathsf{w}}=\varnothing\}}.

The formula obtained for vertices with types

Remark 15

As
\mathsf{s}
is an infertile type, it does not affect criticality but prevents irreducibility of
\bm{\zeta}
.

Since black vertices produce only white children and white vertices produce only black children,
\bm{\zeta}
-multitype Galton-Watson tree is bipartite. Moreover, by construction, it is almost surely well-charged, in the sense defined in (

Properties of the offspring distribution

We prove that
\bm{\zeta}
is non-degenerate and almost-irreducible-critical, so that

exists. First, non-degeneracy is immediate by definition. Then, by direct computations, the mean matrix of

Remark 16
.

Note that if
\bm{\zeta}
was irreducible and critical, we could have provided a more direct proof of Theorem
5
and Proposition
8
. Indeed, it is easily seen that a
\bm{\zeta}
-Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree conditioned to have
n
black vertices is uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, up to the identification introduced above. Then the general result of
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
would imply their convergence in distribution, for the local topology, to the law of the
\bm{\zeta}
-Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree conditioned to survive.

However,
\bm{\zeta}
is not irreducible, and our proof therefore relies on explicit enumerative formulas for maps and trees in the case of
d
-regular maps.

Alternatively we could have reduced the model to an irreducible one, by removing the stems, then apply the Stephenson theorem and finally glue back the stems. We expect this strategy to work in the context of arbitrary vertex degrees.
