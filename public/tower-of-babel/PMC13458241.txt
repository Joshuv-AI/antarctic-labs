Clinicopathological findings, correlations and outcomes in patients with renal disease and living with antiretroviral‐treated human immunodeficiency virus infection
Authors: Jacqueline A. Evans, Christopher S. B. Sia, Gopal Basu, Orla O’Brien, Alan H. Pham, Rowan G. Walker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsAntiretroviral therapy (ART) has modified the incidence of renal complications and the patterns of renal disease in people living with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (PLWH). We reviewed (2011–2021) the progress and outcomes (follow‐up to mid‐2025) of 158 such individuals.MethodsRenal biopsies (59 subjects (BxGp)) were appraised, categorised and semi‐quantitated, and correlated with demographic, clinical and laboratory findings, and the progression of renal parameters was compared with 99 non‐biopsied subjects (NBxGp). Longer‐term outcomes (combined death and/or progression to end stage kidney disease (ESKD)) for both groups were assessed by regression.ResultsProteinuria was the commonest presentation, and levels were higher (P < 0.001) in BxGp subjects, especially those with glomerulonephritis, but did not correlate with semi‐quantitated acute or chronic histology scores. Conversely, estimated glomerular filtration (eGFR) levels did correlate with both acute (r = −0.6397; P < 0.05) and chronic (r = −0.4451; P < 0.01) histology scores, but overall rates of eGFR deterioration were not different between BxGp and NBxGp.With cessation of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), proteinuria specifically improved (P < 0.01), and eGFR stabilised in BxGp subjects with acute tubular injury.Proteinuria (but not eGFR) consistently predicted adverse longer‐term outcomes in unadjusted and Lasso regression models.ConclusionsRenal biopsy is required for accurate diagnosis in ART‐treated PLWH developing renal disease. Proteinuria is a high‐priority prognostic marker in PLWH with renal disease but does not reliably predict underlying histopathology, and it was the only consistent risk factor predictor of longer‐term adverse outcomes of ESKD and/or death.

Background

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) agents employed to control Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection have led to impressive improvements in patient morbidity and mortality. However, some ART agents were found to exhibit nephrotoxicity, both acute and chronic. 1 Progressive kidney biopsies in people living with HIV (PLWH) performed for an increasing range of ART‐associated kidney disease syndromes, such as the electrolyte disorders associated with proximal tubular dysfunction, acute kidney tubular injury (ATI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD: impaired glomerular filtration rate (GFR) ± proteinuria), showed renal histological features to be quite diverse adding to the already complex renal pathology seen prior to ART treatment for HIV. 2 , 3

Specific (potentially reversible) renal histopathology findings have been observed in cases involving tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) use, where ATI and tubular dysfunction (Fanconi‐like syndrome) are combined with the presence of acute tubular injury and mitochondrial abnormalities and frequently tubular proteinuria. 4 , 5 Risk factors for these acute changes include hypertension, age, pre‐existing kidney disease, diabetes and concurrent nephrotoxic agent use, all of which may be associated with non‐specific chronic histological features. 6 Furthermore, severe acute proximal tubular injury with rapidly declining GFR may occur even after TDF cessation. 7

In the successful ART therapy era, both proteinuria and reduced GFR have been identified as key risk factors for mortality, morbidity and CKD progression. 8 , 9

From a dual cohort of 158 PLWH (59 with renal biopsies and 99 without) with renal disease and treated with ART, we reviewed and compared the renal parameters (function, protein excretion, urinary sediment) along with other potential risk factors (laboratory, clinical and demographic), and we evaluated the impact of these factors on final outcomes. In the biopsied subjects, we also categorised the primary diagnosis renal histological findings and summated background acute and chronic histology features and correlated these with renal parameter findings.

Methods

Study design, subjects and outcomes

The study cohort consisted of all subjects who had been referred to (128 patients) or were already attending (30 patients) the Renal Service between 2011 and 2021. Subjects were followed until mid‐2025. Fifty‐nine patients (37%) had renal biopsies: 10 (36%) prior to 2011 and 49 (38%) between 2011 and 2021. Two patients were biopsied prior to the HIV diagnosis.

Subjects undergoing a renal biopsy (Biopsy Group (BxGp)) and those not biopsied (No Biopsy Group (NBxGp)) had data collected for relevant demographic variables (age, gender, weight), clinical cardiovascular risk features (smoking, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, hyperuricaemia (also combined as a Cardiovascular Risk Score)), coinfection (Hep B/HepC) and cancer history. Laboratory measures (at first referral to the renal service (baseline) and at latest review (follow‐up)) were collected from patient records and included proteinuria (UPCR (urinary protein/creatinine ratios), albuminuria (UACR (urinary albumin/creatinine ratios)) and estimated GFR (eGFR) using the CKD‐EPI estimating equation derived from serum creatinine, 10 urinary sediment (urinary erythrocytes (URBCs)/white cells (UWCCs) counts), blood urea and serum phosphate.

For BxGp subjects, where a baseline urinary albumin ratio was available, a urinary albumin/protein ratio (UAPR) was retrospectively calculated to ascertain tubular proteinuria. Further demographic information pertaining to HIV infection (duration of HIV, ART therapy that included TDF prior to or at renal biopsy and HIV viral load) was also obtained. TDF was usually discontinued in subjects from either group if it was thought to be clinically linked to their renal presentation.

The inclusion of the NBxGp as a comparator was considered important to provide insight into any selection bias for biopsy, to help avoid over‐generalising biopsy findings to all PLWH with renal disease and to provide a comparator for the trajectory of HIV‐related renal disease when a biopsy has not been done.

Ethics approval was provided by the Alfred Health Ethics & Research Governance Committee (Project 705/19).

Renal histology

The original 59 renal biopsy reports (of which 53 (90%) had been reported by one of three renal pathologists) were systematically reviewed by RW and JE to classify the primary histological diagnosis and background secondary acute and chronic tubulo‐interstitial histological features, as applicable, and were assigned to one of three primary diagnostic pathology categories (Glomerulonephritidies (GN), Acute Tubular Injury (ATI), Chronic Nephropathies (CN)).

The secondary acute and chronic tubulo‐interstitial histological features were semi‐quantitated. An acute semi‐quantitative histopathology severity score (acute score) was determined (scale: 0–5) from the reported presence and severity (absent/mild/mild–moderate/moderate/moderate–severe/severe respectively) of recognised pathological features of ATI and/or acute tubular necrosis and taking into account the presence/absence of tubulitis, tubular ectasia, tubular casts, tubular hyaline droplets, tubular microcysts, cytoplasmic vacuolisation, interstitial oedema and/or acute interstitial inflammation. 11 The presence of specific TDF lesions was also recorded. 5

Similarly, a chronic histopathology score (chronic score) was estimated from biopsy reports on a summated scale of 0–10 (scale: 0–3 (mild, moderate, severe)) for three features (global sclerosis, interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy) and arterial sclerosis (scale: 0–1 (0 = none/mild, 1 = moderate/severe). 12

Renal parameters (eGFR, proteinuria and URBCs) were compared between diagnostic categories, and acute and chronic histological scores across all biopsies were correlated with relevant renal parameters.

Statistics

Statistical considerations were performed with STATA 19 (Statistics/Data Analysis), Statacorp, Texas, USA. Categorical variables were expressed as number and percentage and continuous variables as either mean (±SD) or median (interquartile range (IQR)) as appropriate. Intergroup and intragroup comparisons for continuous variables were analysed with t‐tests and chi‐squared (or Fisher's exact test) was used for categorical variable comparisons. Spearman's rank correlation was computed to assess the relationship between acute/chronic scores and renal parameters. Univariate logistic regression was used to relate the outcome of death and/or dialysis (end stage kidney disease (ESKD)) using plausible causative baseline risk variables. Additionally, two conservative extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) LASSO‐logistics regression models were employed to select the most useful baseline prognostic risk factors for the above outcomes.

Results

Baseline characteristics

At baseline, BxGp subjects were younger, had lower body weight and were less frequently hypertensive but had significantly higher levels of proteinuria and haematuria compared to NBxGp subjects (Table 1). The use of renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone inhibitors (for either hypertension or proteinuria modification) was higher in BxGp. However, no significant differences were observed across any renal function measures (eGFR, serum creatinine, blood urea) or tubular function (serum phosphate) between the groups.

Biopsy group characteristics

The mean (± SD) age BxGp at biopsy was 50.9 (± 11.9) years. The median (IQR) duration of HIV to biopsy was 14.1 (8.2–22.2) years.

Proteinuria (43 subjects) and renal impairment (38 subjects) were the commonest recorded biopsy indications (Table S1), including 23 subjects who had both. The mean eGFR was 56.2 (± 27.4) mL/min/1.73m2 and median UPCR (IQR) was 102 (54–250) mg/mmol. Haematuria was a biopsy indication (or part indication) in 18 subjects.

A high proportion of BxGp (66%) had been exposed to TDF at the time of and/or just prior to biopsy, but only five subjects had hypophosphataemia (serum phosphate <0.73 mmoL/L) to indicate proximal tubular dysfunction. However, 23 of 47 subjects (49%) who had a urinary albumin estimation (close to biopsy) had a UAPCR <0.4 (Table S1).

A low HIV viral load, indicating control of HIV, was documented in all but two subjects (Table S1).

Renal histopathology

Because individual renal biopsy reports recorded diverse and widespread overlapping pathological features, differentiating between primary diagnosis and secondary features was on occasion debatable. The individual histological summary details are found in Table S1.

Nonetheless, for pathology as originally reported, the most common primary diagnostic category was glomerulonephritis (GN) (25 cases), of which the podocytopathy, focal and segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), was the most prevalent (nine cases). 3 , 13 All cases were FSGS (NOS (Not Otherwise Specified)), and in no case was the ‘collapsing’ variant indicating the likely presence of HIV‐associated nephropathy (HIVAN) seen. 3 Of ‘immune complex kidney diseases in the setting of HIV’, membranous nephropathy (eight cases) and IgA nephropathy (five cases) were the most prevalent. Twelve GN cases had a range of one or more mild to severe chronic secondary histological features (Table S1), and four subjects had acute secondary features.

Acute Tubular Injury (ATI) (ranging in severity from mild tubular injury to acute tubular necrosis (reflected in the acute scores)) was the primary diagnostic category in 22 subjects (Table S1). All had been exposed to TDF and 17 (77%) had histological features consistent with TDF‐specific toxicity with varying severities of proximal tubular eosinophilic inclusions representing giant mitochondria. 4 Notably, 18 of these subjects (82%) also exhibited mild to moderate chronic interstitial or chronic vascular histological features. Only two subjects had florid acute interstitial nephritis (AIN).

Ten subjects constituted a broad primary diagnostic category of Chronic Nephropathies (Chronic Tubulo‐Interstitial Disease/Diabetes/Hypertensive Nephropathy/Hypertensive Vasculopathy) (CN) (Table S1). All had additional mild or moderate secondary chronic interstitial features, and two of these cases were also reported with secondary FSGS.

There were no cases of thrombotic microangiopathy, and two subjects had normal renal histology.

Biopsy group: renal parameter comparisons and clinicopathological correlations

For BxGp subjects, the median (range) duration from baseline to time of biopsy was 14.0 (6.5–21.5) months. For all renal parameters, there were no differences between baseline measures and the measures at the time of biopsy.

At biopsy, renal function (eGFR) estimates and urinary protein measures were higher for the GN primary diagnostic category compared to either ATI or CN categories (Fig. 1). However, even allowing for missing UACR values, the proportion of subjects with UAPR measures of <0.4 (Table S1) was higher in the ATI category compared to the others (ATI: 77%; GN: 30%; CN: 63%; chi2 P = 0.015). URBCs, although numerically elevated in the GN group category, did not reach statistical significance compared to ATI and CN categories (data not shown).

Outcomes: ESKD and death/ESKD

Twenty‐nine subjects had an outcome of ESKD or combined death/ESKD at latest follow‐up, 16 in the NBxGp (11 deaths, five ESKD (three of whom subsequently died)), 13 in the BxGp (three deaths, 10 ESKD (five of whom subsequently died)).

Predicting outcomes from a modified list of potential and/or plausible baseline risk variables (derived from Table 1) was problematic given small event numbers and population size. However, unadjusted odds ratios (95% confidence interval) (OR (95% CI)) for predicting either of the two outcomes for relevant variables (Table 2) showed proteinuria (measured continuously or categorically) having the highest ORs.

Furthermore, as shown in Table 2, using EBIC‐penalised LASSO logistic regression models, only proteinuria measures (either as a continuous (Model A) or categorical variable (Model B)) were consistently selected as the strongest predictors of both outcomes. Discussion

The current study is the largest Australasian and South‐East Asian PLWH renal biopsy series thus far reported. We have confirmed the evolving renal pathology landscape of disappearance of HIVAN but also illustrated the increasing predominance of TDF related ATI.

Of various histopathology features previously described in PLWH, classic HIV‐associated nephropathy (HIVAN)‐associated FSGS, in the past quite common in patients with HIV, was not identified in our cohort. 3 , 4 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Associated with successful ART therapy, HIVAN has been declining in prevalence even in populations with large numbers of susceptible African American patients, especially those with APOL1 risk alleles. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Australia has an essentially Caucasian population, 15 and APOL1 risk alleles have not been reported in the Australian indigenous population. 22 Long‐term successful viral suppression with ART probably explains the absence of HIVAN in our biopsy cohort.

The distinction between HIVAN FSGS and FSGS associated with chronic ART nephrotoxicity (FSGS(NOS)) can be problematic, as is distinguishing between FSGS (NOS) and secondary causes of FSGS such as ageing, obesity, diabetes and hypertension, all prevalent in PLWH. 3 , 15 , 18 , 23 , 24 , 25 The increasing incidence of FSGS (NOS) supports a view of it being a ‘partially treated or an attenuated form’ of HIVAN or possibly secondary FSGS from multiple pathophysiological processes associated with ageing and the improved life expectancy of PLWH. 3 , 15 , 19 , 20 , 23 , 24 Another possible reason for the absence of HIVAN in the cohort is that pathologists do not consistently use a standard FSGS classification, such as the Columbia Classification, and in any event, the prognostic value of the variants of FSGS remains debated. 14 , 26

In our study, the prevalence of ‘immune complex kidney diseases in the setting of HIV’ (specifically membranous, IgA disease and membranoproliferative glomerulonephritidies) seems consistent with most other reports from the current era where these are considered most likely a concurrent disease. 14 , 15 , 19 , 23

Acute kidney (tubular) injury (ATI) is an important predictor of heart failure, cardiovascular events, ESKD and mortality in PLWH. 27 Of the high proportion of our subjects with ATI tubulopathy, although mostly mild or moderate severity, nearly all had exposure to TDF. 4 TDF‐induced renal tubular toxicity is more likely with pre‐existing kidney disease or poorly controlled HIV disease, with longer overall antiviral treatment duration, older age and elevated baseline creatinine concentrations. 28 , 29 In the ATI histological category in our study, nearly all subjects had well‐controlled HIV yet showed a range of non‐specific chronic vascular and interstitial changes, indicating significant co‐existing CKD. ATI represented nearly 40% of primary diagnoses in our study; although the presence of acute inflammatory infiltrate was less than reported by others, numerous biopsies actually had chronic interstitial infiltrate. 4 , 15 This suggests considerable overlap between HIV‐associated ATI tubulopathy and the previously described HIV‐associated tubulointerstitial‐dominant disease. 3 , 4 , 15 , 23 As a primary diagnosis, tubulointerstitial‐dominant disease (3%–33% of biopsies) has not only increased in prevalence in the era of ART therapy but is strongly associated with ART therapy that includes TDF. 20 , 23 Importantly, cessation of TDF was associated with comparative stabilisation of renal function and reduction in proteinuria in most of our ATI category subjects.

Proteinuria was the most frequent indication for biopsy and not surprisingly levels of proteinuria were greater in biopsied subjects compared to non‐biopsied subjects at baseline. Higher proteinuria levels in subjects with GN likely reflect the high proportion of patients with FSGS (often associated with nephrotic range proteinuria), but proteinuria is also prominent in PLWH with ATI and AIN. 4 The UAPR values (where measured) as an indication of tubular proteinuria did retrospectively correlate with the underlying pathology (ATI), but in our study the UAPR was not systematically collected or calculated before biopsy. 30 UAPR measures should perhaps become a routine part of the clinical evaluation in any PLWH considered for biopsy.

The clinicopathological correlation findings in our study are novel. Previously, correlations typically focused on HIV‐associated glomerular disease patterns and clinical features. 15 , 19 , 31 Our study clearly confirms correlations between the severity of the non‐specific chronic and acute tubulointerstitial features and eGFR, findings which may have prognostic implications. 19 It further emphasises the importance of kidney biopsy in accurately diagnosing and managing HIV‐associated kidney disease, given that histological findings cannot be reliably inferred from either clinical presentations or renal parameter measures. 3 , 5 , 14 , 19 Furthermore, the numerous biopsy subjects exhibiting a range of secondary or background tubulointerstitial disease underscores the diversity and complexity of HIV‐associated kidney disease and the difficulty in differentiating between primary and secondary histology features. The mean age at biopsy of 50.9 years (greater than most other studies) coupled with a high prevalence of comorbidities across the whole cohort (Table 1) may explain some of the non‐specific chronic histological features in BxGp. Despite these histological findings, our study found comparable and largely well‐maintained eGFR levels, like those reported in another Australasian study. 15

Previously we found that age and lower baseline eGFR predicted CKD (eGFR <60 mL/min). 32 However, our current study highlights proteinuria (but not eGFR) as a much stronger predictor of more severe adverse outcomes such as progression to ESKD or death in PLWH, like observations in non‐HIV CKD populations. 33

Therapeutic options for renal disease in PLWH are limited outside cessation of potential nephrotoxins (e.g. tenofovir), use of renin‐angiotensin blockade regimens and control of comorbid conditions. 3 , 5 , 15 , 23 , 34 However, a renal biopsy may provide at least some guidance to therapy. 4 , 23 Reducing glomerular hyperfiltration with sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2I) is likely to be beneficial in PLWH with FSGS. 35 The adverse effect profile of SGLT2I is generally acceptable, but further randomised controlled trial studies are required.

This study has significant limitations. The whole cohort was selected by virtue of being referred to a renal service and as such most likely represents a population of PLWH with more severe renal disease. Furthermore, within the cohort, biopsied subjects were predictably selected based on clinical concern, such as heavier proteinuria, sudden deterioration in kidney function or more unusual presentations. As such they do not necessarily represent the full spectrum of renal pathology in PLWH as the selection process may over‐estimate the apparent prevalence of certain more severe pathologies (e.g. FSGS and immune‐complex disease) and under‐represent more indolent pathologies. Biopsy thresholds may also vary substantially between centres, influenced by factors such as the availability of resources, clinician and pathologist experience and expertise. The indications for biopsy in our setting may therefore differ from those used elsewhere, which limits the generalisability of our findings. In fact, lower indication thresholds for biopsy may have existed in our study, as evidenced by a relatively larger number of cases of ATI as a primary histological diagnosis compared to other studies.

Furthermore, outcome comparisons between biopsied and non‐biopsied subjects require careful interpretation. As the decision to biopsy is inherently influenced by clinical severity and physician approach, the characteristics of the two groups will predictably differ at baseline. Such differences potentially confound associations between histological diagnoses/renal parameters and outcomes, even after adjustment, making direct comparisons limited.

Finally, our study is essentially observational and largely retrospective, and the study size makes interpretation of potential causal links to outcomes uncertain. It is, however, an important contribution to the knowledge in this area and underscores the disconnect between clinical and pathology features and supports the need to consider biopsy given the high incidence of acute features that are potentially manageable/treatable particularly by withdrawal of relevant toxic agents. 4 , 18 , 23

In conclusion, in this real‐world dual cohort of 158 PLWH with renal disease (59 with a renal biopsy), followed over a considerable period (median 7.5 years), renal function was relatively stable across most subjects, but 12 subjects (eight in the BxGp) did progress to ESKD. Proteinuria was the commonest feature across the whole cohort, and the highest levels were observed in GN subjects. However, proteinuria neither correlated with prevalent acute or non‐specific chronic histological features nor reliably predicted the diagnosis in biopsied subjects, but it did predict (unlike eGFR) long‐term outcomes of death and/or ESKD across the whole cohort. ATI with and without inflammation was a very frequent histological finding and TDF was implicated in nearly all instances, prompting withdrawal of this agent and improvement in proteinuria and relative stabilisation of eGFR.
