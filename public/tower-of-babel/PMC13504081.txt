An exploratory study of the pharmacokinetics, safety, and erythropoietic effects of roxadustat in healthy cats
Authors: Fumitaka SHITAMORI, Reo NISHI, Nanami YASUDA, Hakchung KIM, Junko SORA, Eri OCHIAI, Makoto HASEGAWA, Shingo MAEDA, Yasuyuki MOMOI, Tomohiro YONEZAWA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypoxia-inducible factor–prolyl hydroxylase (HIF-PH) inhibitors stimulate endogenous erythropoietin (EPO) production and are used for the treatment of anemia of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in humans. In veterinary medicine, molidustat (MOL) has demonstrated acceptable safety and efficacy with anemia of CKD in cats. Roxadustat (ROX) is another HIF-PH inhibitor that has a longer half-life than MOL, allowing for less frequent dosing. Therefore, ROX may offer an advantage in improving medication adherence. However, the safe dose range and erythropoietic effects of ROX in cats remain unclear. In the present study, an exploratory pharmacokinetic analysis of single-dose ROX administration was conducted in healthy cats to assess its safety and erythropoietic effects. In addition, a repeated-dose study was performed to evaluate safety and tolerability during continued administration. In the single-dose study, no apparent adverse events were observed at doses ≤20 mg/kg; however, severe adverse events including vomiting and systemic clinical signs leading to death were observed at 80 mg/kg, indicating that high-dose administration should be avoided. Following ROX administration, increases in reticulocyte counts and serum EPO concentrations were observed, suggesting erythropoietic activity in cats. In the repeated-dose study, ROX administered at 5–8 mg/kg for six weeks was not associated with apparent adverse events based on clinical condition and complete blood count. These results suggest that this dose range may serve as a candidate for future clinical evaluation in CKD cats.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common disorder in cats, and it is estimated that 30–65% of CKD cats develop anemia as the disease progresses [3]. With CKD and renal fibrosis, peritubular fibroblasts transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts which produce little erythropoietin (EPO). This decrease in EPO production is considered a major cause of anemia of CKD [14]. In addition, anemia itself can cause weakness, lethargy and inappetence, thereby reducing overall quality of life (QOL). Furthermore, as a compensatory response to anemia, the risk of cardiovascular complications such as hypertension and heart failure is increased [14]. Moreover, anemia exacerbates the progression of CKD through tissue hypoxia [14]; therefore, anemia of CKD represents a condition that requires appropriate treatment.

Administering erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) is one of the effective treatments for correcting anemia of CKD [14]. However, there is a risk of inducing neutralizing anti-EPO antibodies because ESAs involve the administration of exogenous EPO. The presence of these antibodies not only leads to treatment resistance but may also cross-react with endogenous EPO produced by the animal, potentially resulting in severe adverse effects such as pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) [14].

In 2023, molidustat (MOL) was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of anemia of CKD in cats [17]. MOL is a hypoxia-inducible factor–prolyl hydroxylase (HIF-PH) inhibitor. HIF, particularly HIF-2, is a transcription factor that regulates EPO synthesis in peritubular fibroblasts of the kidney, and expression of HIF-2 is required for EPO production [9, 14]. PH promotes the degradation of HIF; however, HIF-PH inhibitors suppress PH activity, thereby stabilizing HIF and enhancing EPO production [9]. HIF-PH inhibitors stimulate endogenous EPO production and are not associated with the formation of anti-EPO antibodies, which is a major concern with ESAs therapy. In addition, because they are administered orally, HIF-PH inhibitors are expected to reduce patient stress associated with frequent clinic visits compared with ESAs that require repeated injections.

In addition to MOL, other HIF-PH inhibitors include roxadustat, daprodustat, and vadadustat [7]. Although these agents are considered to exert similar pharmacological effects as HIF-PH inhibitors, differences in their half-life (t1/2) and plasma concentration profiles have been reported in mice and humans [2, 7, 11]. Furthermore, HIF-PH inhibitors differ among agents in terms of dosing frequency and considerations when co-administered with phosphate binders [5, 7, 12]. Therefore, appropriate agent selection according to the patient’s clinical condition is recommended. Accordingly, considering HIF-PH inhibitors other than MOL as therapeutic options in cats may be important for expanding the treatment strategies for anemia of CKD.

Roxadustat (ROX) was the first HIF-PH inhibitor to be clinically applied in humans. ROX has a longer t1/2 than other HIF-PH inhibitors and is therefore administered three times weekly, whereas MOL is given once daily [2, 5]. As a result, ROX has the advantage of imposing a relatively lower burden on medication adherence in patients with CKD who require multiple concomitant medications. However, to date, there have been no reports of ROX administration in cats, and its efficacy and the dosage range associated with adverse events in this species remain unclear. In this study, with the aim of expanding the therapeutic application of HIF-PH inhibitors for anemia of CKD in cats, we conducted an exploratory pharmacokinetic analysis of ROX following single-dose administration in healthy cats and assessed its efficacy and safety. In addition, the safety and tolerability of repeated dosing was also evaluated.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental animals

The experiment regarding the pharmacokinetics, efficacy and safety of ROX was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Graduate School of Agricultural and Life Sciences, The University of Tokyo (P19-118). Four mixed-breed, castrated male cats maintained in a conventional animal facility were used. They were six to seven years old, with a mean body weight of 3.3 kg (range, 2.5–5.0 kg). The cats underwent veterinary medical check-ups every other month and were evaluated as clinically healthy before the experiments began.

The study was conducted in accordance with predefined humane endpoints. Administration of ROX was immediately discontinued, and appropriate symptomatic or supportive treatment was initiated if severe deterioration in general condition or blood test results met any of the following criteria: decreased activity (e.g., obtundation or reduced level of consciousness), severe vomiting (Grade ≥3, VCOG-CTCAE v2 [8]), severe diarrhea (Bristol Stool Chart score of 7 [1]), fever (≥40°C), or significant weight loss (>10% of body weight).

Furthermore, the study was terminated and appropriate treatment was provided if neurological abnormalities, gastrointestinal perforation, or severe infection (e.g., peritonitis) were observed. In cases where severe sequelae were considered unavoidable or the prognosis remained poor despite intensive care, euthanasia was performed by overdose administration of pentobarbital and potassium chloride in accordance with institutional animal ethics guidelines and authorized regulations governing controlled substances.

Oral administration of ROX

A single-dose study of orally administered ROX in healthy cats was performed to determine the pharmacokinetic profile, efficacy and safety. This study consisted of a screening visit, a single administration, and follow-up evaluations for a minimum of 12 weeks after administration. In the single-dose study, the initial ROX dose was 20 mg per cat (equivalent to 4.0–8.0 mg/kg) administered orally, which corresponded to the lowest available tablet dose (defined as the 4 mg/kg group). Treatment potency was evaluated at four dose ranges: 4.0–8.0 mg/kg, 10.0–11.1 mg/kg, 20.7–22.4 mg/kg, and 80.0–88.9 mg/kg in healthy cats. These dose ranges were determined to allow exploratory characterization of the pharmacokinetic profile across a wide dose spectrum. A washout period of seven days was scheduled between each dose. Appetite, activity, urination, and defecation were monitored at different time points depending on the dosage group. In the 4 mg/kg group, these parameters were assessed at 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 hr post-administration, whereas in the 10, 20, and 80 mg/kg groups, assessments were conducted at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hr post-administration. Blood samples were collected at the same time points to evaluate plasma ROX concentration, plasma EPO concentration (FUJIFILM, Tokyo, Japan), and reticulocyte count (RETIC) using ProCyte Dx (IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, MA, USA).

A repeated-dose study was performed based on the findings of the single-dose study, with the aim of assessing safety and tolerability under continued administration. The same cats used in the single-dose study were enrolled. One cat was excluded due to an adverse event observed during the single-dose study, resulting in a total of three cats being evaluated. A washout period of three weeks was allowed between the single-dose and repeated-dose studies. ROX was administered to three cats at 5.2–8.1 mg/kg orally three times per week for six weeks. They were monitored daily for general physical condition. Blood samples were collected from each cat prior to dosing and at two, four, and six weeks after the start of treatment and complete blood counts (CBC) were analyzed using ProCyte Dx.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Pharmacokinetic analysis of ROX and its predominant metabolites were conducted using standard methods with an LC–MS system (Prominence nano + LCMS-IT-TOF, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The peak plasma concentration (Cmax) and time to peak concentration (Tmax) were obtained directly from the observed data, and Tmax was determined based on the median value. The area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) was calculated using the Area Under the Curve function in Prism version 10.5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The terminal rate constant (ke) was obtained by regression analysis of the terminal log-linear portion of the concentration–time curve. The elimination t1/2 was calculated using the formula 0.693/ke.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism version 10.5.0. Repeated analysis in the single-dose study was conducted using the Friedman test, followed by Dunn’s test, with the significance level set at P<0.05. In cases with missing data, a mixed-effects model with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons versus the baseline (0 hr) value was applied.

However, the clinical condition progressively deteriorated and no improvement was observed in clinicopathological findings. Based on these findings, the prognosis was considered poor. Euthanasia was therefore performed three days after the initiation of treatment in accordance with predefined humane endpoints, using pentobarbital and potassium chloride.

DISCUSSION

HIF-PH inhibitors are used as therapeutic agents for anemia associated with non-dialysis-dependent CKD in humans. In veterinary medicine, MOL is currently the only HIF-PH inhibitor approved by the FDA for the treatment of anemia of CKD in cats. The availability of multiple HIF-PH inhibitors is important to address inter-individual differences in efficacy and adverse effects, as well as potential supply instability. In this study, we focused on ROX and evaluated its pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety following single and repeated administration in healthy cats.

Because no previous reports have described the administration of ROX in cats and the number of animals available for this study was limited, doses of 4, 10, 20, and 80 mg/kg were selected, and an exploratory pharmacokinetic evaluation was conducted in healthy cats. As a result, ROX exposure increased in a dose-dependent manner; however, dose proportionality was not observed across the entire dose range. Between 10 and 20 mg/kg, a 2-fold increase in dose resulted in an approximately 2.5-fold increase in AUC, suggesting an overall dose-dependent increase in exposure within this range. In contrast, a marked deviation from dose proportionality was observed at 4 mg/kg, while at 80 mg/kg, a plateau in Cmax and prolongation of the t1/2 were noted. These findings suggest nonlinear pharmacokinetics at both the lower and higher dose ranges.

At 4 mg/kg, Tmax was delayed compared with the other dose levels, suggesting slower absorption at the lower dose range. In addition, plasma concentrations at this dose were relatively low and close to the lower limit of quantification, making them more susceptible to measurement error and the influence of sampling timing. Consequently, Cmax and AUC may have been underestimated at this dose level. Therefore, the deviation from dose proportionality observed at 4 mg/kg may reflect delayed absorption and uncertainty in exposure estimation at the lower dose range, rather than true nonlinear pharmacokinetics. No adverse events were observed at doses of 4–10 mg/kg, and a discernible elimination phase was identified, suggesting that these dose levels were well tolerated. Therefore, to improve the accuracy of exposure estimation at lower doses, optimization of sampling time points and a limited follow-up study including an intermediate dose (e.g., 8 mg/kg) would be desirable.

At 80 mg/kg, a plateau in Cmax and non-dose-proportional increases in AUC were observed, suggesting that absorption may have been limited at higher dose levels. At 80 mg/kg, prolongation of the t1/2 (9.5 hr) was observed, which may reflect saturation of metabolic and/or elimination pathways or an apparent prolongation due to delayed absorption (flip-flop kinetics). Therefore, this dose should be regarded as an upper exploratory level, and exposure data at doses ≤20 mg/kg would be more appropriate for informing clinical dose selection.

In humans, a single oral dose of 100 mg of ROX represents a commonly used clinical dose, and the reported Cmax in healthy adults at this dose ranges from 6.3 to 10.6 μg/mL [5]. Assuming a reference value of 10 μg/mL, the Cmax values observed in healthy cats in the present study corresponded to approximately 0.5-, 4.2-, 8.1-, and 8.2-fold higher exposures at doses of 4, 10, 20, and 80 mg/kg, respectively. From the perspective of Cmax, these findings suggest that systemic exposure in cats may have already exceeded the human therapeutic range at a dose of 10 mg/kg. Notably, no adverse events were observed at doses of 4 or 10 mg/kg in the present study, suggesting that a dose range of approximately 4–10 mg/kg may represent a reasonable starting point for future clinical evaluation in cats. Future studies employing exposure-guided and stepwise dose-escalation strategies in a limited number of animals will be necessary to further characterize the pharmacokinetics and safety profile within the 4–10 mg/kg dose range. Based on the present findings, however, repeated administration at doses of≥20 mg/kg should be approached with caution.

The t1/2 ranged from 4.1 to 5.0 hr at doses of 4–20 mg/kg, demonstrating relatively consistent values across this range. In healthy humans, the reported t1/2 of ROX is 8.3–16.0 hr [5], suggesting that the t1/2 may be shorter in cats. In humans, ROX is metabolized primarily in the liver; hydroxylation is mediated by CYP2C8, and glucuronidation is mediated by UGT1A9 [5]. Cats are known to have limited glucuronidation capacity, and no enzyme directly corresponding to human CYP2C8 has been identified in this species; therefore, the principal metabolic enzymes responsible for ROX in cats remain unclear. Accordingly, the shorter t1/2 observed in cats compared with humans may suggest the involvement of alternative metabolic pathways in the elimination of ROX. In addition, smaller animals generally have higher hepatic blood flow per unit body weight, which may result in greater clearance on a per-kilogram basis and could partly explain the findings observed in this study. In fact, the t1/2 of ROX in mice was 3.5 hr [11]. In humans, ROX is administered three times weekly; however, considering the shorter t1/2 observed in cats, adjustment of the dosing frequency may be considered if the therapeutic response is insufficient. Nevertheless, careful safety evaluation would be required in such cases.

ROX administration at 10–80 mg/kg increased RETIC between 24–72 hr and elevated plasma EPO concentrations, peaking at 12 hr. These findings suggest that ROX is likely to have erythropoietic activity in cats. However, high plasma EPO is not necessarily beneficial; in humans, excessive ESAs administration is strongly linked to cardiovascular risk [10]. Although no feline EPO reference range exists, a prior study in 40 healthy cats reported a median of 9 IU/L (range, 1.0–26.7 IU/L) [13]. In the present study, levels reached approximately 1,000 and 3,000 IU/L in the 20 and 80 mg/kg groups, respectively. Even if endogenous, such marked elevations may increase cardiovascular risk. Therefore, doses below 10–20 mg/kg are preferable to avoid excessive EPO elevation.

In humans, gastrointestinal adverse events such as vomiting and diarrhea have been reported with ROX, and thromboembolism is recognized as a serious adverse event [5, 6]. In the present study, vomiting was observed in two of four cats and severe systemic adverse events leading to death were observed in one cat following administration of 80 mg/kg ROX. Although it remains unclear whether these adverse events were related to peak exposure (Cmax) or cumulative exposure (AUC), the Cmax observed at 80 mg/kg was comparable to that at 20 mg/kg. This finding may suggest that toxicity at the highest dose was not solely driven by peak concentration. Instead, cumulative exposure or other dose-related pharmacodynamic effects may have contributed. Further investigation is required to clarify the exposure–toxicity relationship.

Although reports on the use of HIF-PH inhibitors in cats are limited, a previous study administering MOL to cats with CKD identified vomiting as the most common adverse event, occurring in six of 15 cases [4]. Activation of HIF-2 has been shown to promote mucosal inflammation and fibrosis and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in humans [15], which may potentially lead to gastrointestinal signs such as vomiting and diarrhea.

Furthermore, one cat developed regenerative anemia, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, and subsequently died after exhibiting severe adverse events. Although the exact cause of death could not be determined, the presence of severe thrombocytopenia in addition to regenerative anemia and AKI suggests a severe systemic disorder accompanied by hemorrhage or hemolysis. In particular, multiorgan dysfunction secondary to a consumptive coagulopathy, such as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) or thromboembolism, may have been involved. Thromboembolism has also been reported as a serious adverse event in humans, and although its association with HIF-PH inhibitors, including ROX, has not been fully elucidated, the involvement of transferrin has been proposed in human medicine. HIF regulates transferrin expression and has been shown to increase its production [16]. In addition, transferrin can interact with coagulation factors, potentially promoting a hypercoagulable state [16]. Accordingly, elevated transferrin levels accompanying the rapid erythropoiesis induced by HIF-PH inhibitors may be involved in the pathogenesis of thromboembolism. In a human randomized phase three trial in non–dialysis-dependent CKD patients with anemia, AKI was reported in 2.0% more patients in the ROX group compared with the placebo group [6]. Whether this AKI occurred secondary to thromboembolic events or represented an independent adverse event remains unclear; however, it should be recognized as an adverse event requiring careful attention. Taken together, HIF activation associated with high-dose ROX administration in cats may be related to adverse events such as vomiting and thromboembolism, and high-dose administration should therefore be avoided.

The repeated-dose study in healthy cats was conducted to evaluate safety and tolerability during continued administration, with ROX administered at 5.2–8.1 mg/kg for six weeks. During this period, no apparent adverse events were observed on physical examination or CBC analysis, suggesting that doses up to 8.1 mg/kg were generally well tolerated in healthy cats. Notably, in the repeated-dose study, one cat had a baseline HCT of 29.7%, which was slightly below the reference range, and its HCT increased to 46.0% after six weeks of ROX administration. However, the repeated-dose study was primarily designed to evaluate the safety and tolerability of ROX rather than its erythropoietic effects. Therefore, this increase in HCT should be interpreted with caution and it cannot be conclusively attributed to the erythropoietic effect of ROX. Further studies including long-term administration at this dosage are warranted. Based on the present findings, these dose levels may serve as candidate doses for future clinical evaluation in cats with CKD, and exploratory clinical trials initiating at 5–8 mg/kg may be considered. However, cats with CKD often present with comorbidities, and the effects of ROX under such conditions remain unclear. The risks of vomiting and thromboembolism observed in the single-dose study may be further exacerbated in CKD cases. Accordingly, careful monitoring in clinical CKD patients is recommended, including physical and hematologic examinations, in addition to blood pressure assessment and coagulation profile evaluation.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size in both the single-dose and repeated-dose studies was small. In particular, the standard deviation of Cmax was large in the single-dose study, indicating substantial inter-individual variability. In particular, in the lower dose range of 4–10 mg/kg, where deviation from dose proportionality was suggested, additional limited confirmatory studies are warranted, especially at 5–8 mg/kg which has been proposed as a candidate dose range for CKD cats. Another limitation of this study is the absence of blood pressure measurements. In a 2023 study evaluating MOL in CKD cats [4], systemic blood pressure was assessed longitudinally; similar evaluation is warranted for ROX. Finally, iron status was not evaluated in this study, and the relationship between iron status and the erythropoietic effects of ROX was not assessed. Future clinical studies in CKD cats should include evaluation of iron parameters, particularly serum iron and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC), as well as consideration of iron supplementation when appropriate.

In the present study, ROX increased RETIC and serum EPO concentrations in healthy cats, indicating erythropoietic activity. In the single-dose study, no adverse events were observed at doses ≤20 mg/kg; however, from the perspective of avoiding excessive deviation from physiological endogenous EPO levels, doses ≤10 mg/kg may represent a safer range. In contrast, severe adverse events were observed at 80 mg/kg, and such high doses should be avoided. In the repeated-dose study, doses of 5–8 mg/kg were generally well tolerated during continued administration. Future clinical trials in CKD cats are warranted based on these dose ranges.
