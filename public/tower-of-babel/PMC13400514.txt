Natural fish gelatin-ajwain edible coatings improve shelf life and quality of chicken nuggets
Authors: Mujahid Siddiq, Muhammad Bilal, Ghulam Husnain, Aisha Shabbir, Muhammad Zeeshan, Muhammad Asif Bajwa, Mohamed Fawzy Ramadan, Muhammad Sajid Arshad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the impact of edible coatings made from fish gelatin (FG) and ajwain extract (AE) on the quality, antioxidant activity, microbial safety, and shelf life of chicken nuggets. Antioxidant properties were assessed via DPPH· radical scavenging and total phenolic content (TPC), while lipid oxidation was monitored through TBARS and peroxide value (POV). Microbial quality was evaluated using total aerobic and coliform counts. Nuggets treated with AE2% + FG3% showed the highest antioxidant activity and lowest oxidation and microbial levels. These coated samples also exhibited superior color stability (L*, a*, b*) and received higher sensory scores for appearance, texture, taste, and overall acceptability throughout 14 days of refrigeration. The coating effectively enhanced the physicochemical and microbiological stability of chicken nuggets, indicating the potential of FG and AE as natural, halal, and sustainable alternatives to synthetic preservatives for improving meat product quality.Graphical abstract

Introduction

An estimated 3.75–4 million metric tons of gelatin are manufactured annually worldwide. Nevertheless, non-mammalian sources account for only 2% of total production. Cut sections and bones are thought to make up around 7–8% of the live weight of broilers, producing about 9.5 million tons of poultry by-products that might be used as a substitute non-mammalian source for gelatin extraction (Mokrejš et al., 2019; Abedinia et al., 2020). Gelatin is a protein obtained through the partial hydrolysis of collagen and can be extracted from various animal sources. The primary commercial sources include cattle bones, hides, pig skins, and fish. Gelatin can be derived from both agricultural and non-agricultural sources; however, no plant-based source of true gelatin exists (Ahmed et al., 2020; Julie Chandra et al., 2023). Interest in halal gelatin production has increased, and edible halal gelatins have been successfully produced and characterized from non-mammalian sources, including insects such as melon and sorghum bugs (Mariod and Fadul 2013; Yuan et al., 2024).

Pakistan is rich in freshwater fish resources, with several economically important species present in its rivers. Among these, Labeo rohita (Rohu) is one of the most important freshwater fish species and is widely consumed due to its affordability, high nutritional value, and suitability for aquaculture. Fish is an excellent source of high-quality protein, polyunsaturated fatty acids (particularly omega-3 fatty acids), calcium, zinc, and iron. Due to its nutritional composition and relatively low cost, fish plays a vital role in human nutrition and food security. Moreover, fish by-products such as scales represent a valuable and underutilized resource for the extraction of functional biomaterials, including gelatin. The term halal refers to any product or activity that is permissible according to Islamic law. Halal certification is increasingly important for food products, meat, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, food ingredients, and food-contact materials in many Muslim countries. In the food industry, gelatin is widely used for its excellent functional properties, including gelling, emulsifying, stabilizing, water-binding, and texturizing. It is commonly used in dairy foods, confectionery, baked goods, and meat products to improve texture, stability, and moisture retention (Huang et al., 2020).

Ajwain (Trachyspermum ammi L.) is a medicinally important seed spice belonging to the Apiaceae family. Ajwain seeds contain approximately 2–4% essential oil, with thymol as the major active component. Traditionally, ajwain has been used for the treatment of gastrointestinal disorders such as dysentery, diarrhea, indigestion, and bronchial complications. It is also known for its antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antihypertensive properties. In traditional medicine, ajwain seeds are used as a liver tonic and to relieve respiratory congestion and cough-related disorders (Kumar & Singh, 2021; Hanif et al., 2021). Medicinal plants are vital sources of bioactive compounds and have long been used in the prevention and treatment of various diseases. It is estimated that approximately 25% of modern medicines contain plant-derived compounds. Ajwain includes a wide range of bioactive constituents, including carbohydrates, proteins, fibers, alkaloids, tannins, saponins, flavonoids, sterols, and volatile oils such as thymol, p-cymene, γ-terpinene, and α- and β-pinene. Additionally, ajwain seeds are rich in essential minerals, including calcium, iron, phosphorus, and nicotinic acid (Asif & Hashmi, 2020).

Ajwain has broad applications in food, medicine, cosmetics, and flavoring industries. Its seeds contain significant amounts of carbohydrates (24.6%), proteins (17.1%), crude fat (21.1%), crude fiber (11.9%), and various phytochemicals, making it a potential natural antioxidant and antimicrobial agent (Singh and Meghwal 2019). The incorporation of plant-based bioactive compounds into edible coatings has gained considerable attention due to their ability to deliver functional compounds directly to food surfaces, thereby enhancing shelf life and quality (Singh et al., 2022). Considering the growing demand for halal, safe, and functional meat products, the use of fish-scale-derived gelatin combined with natural plant extracts, such as ajwain, may offer an effective strategy to improve the quality, antioxidant potential, and shelf stability of chicken nuggets.

Against this background, the present study was designed to evaluate the effectiveness of halal fish gelatin extracted from fish scales and ajwain extract as bioactive edible coating materials for chicken nuggets. Specifically, the study aimed to investigate the impact of individual and combined applications of fish gelatin and ajwain extract on the antioxidant activity, lipid oxidation stability, microbial quality, color attributes, sensory characteristics, and overall shelf life of chicken nuggets during refrigerated storage. This research sought to assess the potential of these natural, halal-compatible bioactive coatings as sustainable alternatives to synthetic preservatives to improve the quality and safety of meat products.

Materials and methods

Procurement of raw materials and treatment plan

Raw materials were procured from the local fish market in Faisalabad, Pakistan. Rohu (Labeo rohita) fish scales were carefully collected from local fish shops and transported to the laboratories of Government College University, Faisalabad, for further experimentation. Upon collection, the scales were thoroughly washed with tap water to remove adhering impurities and undesirable materials before further processing. Chicken nuggets were prepared under different treatment plans, including an uncoated control and coated treatments comprising combinations of ajwain extract (AE) and fish scale gelatin (FSG). The treatment formulations were as follows: control (uncoated), AE 1% + FSG 2%, AE 1% + FSG 3%, AE 1.5% + FSG 2%, AE 1.5% + FSG 3%, AE 2% + FSG 2%, and AE 2% + FSG 3%.

Extraction of ajwain extract

Ajwain seeds were cleaned to remove foreign materials and impurities. The cleaned seeds were soaked overnight in a glass container filled with distilled water. After soaking, the seeds were ground in a grinder, and the resulting slurry was filtered through muslin cloth to obtain the aqueous ajwain extract, which was used to prepare edible coatings.

Preparation of chicken nuggets

Fresh chicken meat was minced and marinated with standardized spices and seasonings, then refrigerated for 4 h to allow uniform absorption of the marinade. Edible coating solutions were prepared using AE and FSG according to the designated treatment formulations. The marinated meat was shaped into uniform nuggets, coated as per treatment design, and subsequently deep-fried in cooking oil until adequately cooked.

Extraction of gelatin from Rohu fish scales

Gelatin extraction from Rohu fish scales was carried out following a previously reported method with minor modifications (Rafael et al., 2021). Fish scales were washed three times with tap water and treated with 1% (w/v) food-grade acetic acid solution for 40 min to facilitate collagen swelling. The samples were then washed three times with distilled water to remove residual acid. Subsequently, gelatin extraction was performed using distilled water at a sample-to-water ratio of 1:7 (w/v) in a water bath at 60 °C for 2 h without stirring. The extract was filtered through filter cloth, then through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtrate was concentrated by heating in a water bath at 60 °C for 8 h until its volume was reduced to one-third of its original volume. The clear extract was strained through a sieve, oven-dried, and ground into a powder using a mortar and pestle. The powdered gelatin was stored in airtight containers for further use. Each extraction was performed in triplicate. In a complementary step, the filtered gelatin solution was dried at 40 °C until brittle gelatin sheets were formed. These sheets were diluted with 2 mL of distilled water and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis (Kuwahara 2021).

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC)

The total phenolic content of the coated chicken nuggets was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method. A 0.5 mL sample extract was mixed with 1 mL of 10% Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and incubated for 6 min. Subsequently, 2 mL of 20% sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) solution was added, and the mixture was incubated for 60 min at 30 °C. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Model: U-2900). Total phenolic content was expressed as grams of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of sample based on a standard calibration curve.

DPPH· radical scavenging activity

The antioxidant activity of chicken nugget samples was evaluated using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH·) radical scavenging assay (Baliyan et al., 2022). A stock solution of DPPH· was prepared by dissolving 24 mg of DPPH· in 100 mL of methanol. The working solution was adjusted to an absorbance of approximately 0.973 at 517 nm. A volume of 3 mL of DPPH· solution was mixed with 100 µL of the ground chicken nugget sample. A control was prepared by adding 3 mL of DPPH· solution to 100 µL of methanol. The mixtures were incubated in the dark for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 517 nm. Peroxide value (POV)

The peroxide value of coated chicken nuggets was determined by titrimetric analysis as described by Sallam et al. (2004). A 3 g sample was dissolved in hot water at 60 °C for 3 min, followed by agitation with 30 mL of an acetic acid-chloroform mixture (3:2, v/v) for fat extraction. The mixture was filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and 0.5 mL of potassium iodide was added to the filtrate. The liberated iodine was titrated with 0.01 N sodium thiosulfate solution, using starch as an indicator. where S is the volume of titrant (mL), N is the normality of sodium thiosulfate, and W is the weight of the sample (g).

Microbial analysis

Microbial analysis was performed following standard guidelines. A 10 g sample was aseptically transferred into 90 mL of Maximum Recovery Diluent (MRD; composition: peptone 1.0 g/L, sodium chloride 8.5 g/L, pH 7.0 ± 0.2; Oxoid, UK) to obtain a 1:10 dilution. This 10:90 ratio is a practical alternative to the 25:225 ratio, as both achieve the same 1:10 dilution required for enumeration, and MRD is specifically formulated in accordance with ISO standards to maximize bacterial recovery and maintain cell viability during analysis (Linton et al., 2003). Serial decimal dilutions were prepared from this initial suspension. For total aerobic count, 0.1 mL aliquots from appropriate dilutions were spread onto Plate Count Agar (PCA; Oxoid, CM0325, UK) and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. For coliform count, 0.1 mL aliquots were spread onto Violet Red Bile Agar (VRBA; Oxoid, CM0107, UK) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, plates containing 30–300 colonies were counted, and the results were expressed as logarithmic colony-forming units per gram of sample (log CFU/g).

Color measurement

Color attributes of chicken nuggets were measured using a Hunter Lab colorimeter (Chromameter CR-400, pulsed xenon lamp, 400–700 nm; Tokyo, Japan). Color parameters L* (lightness), a* (redness), and b* (yellowness) were recorded as the mean of eight random readings from different locations on the sample surface (Krzywicki, 1979).

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was conducted by a trained panel of 15 panelists (8 female, 7 males; ages 22–35 years) from the Department of Food Science, GCUF. Panelists were recruited based on their interest, availability, and lack of known aversions to chicken meat. Before the formal evaluation, two training sessions (each 1 h) were conducted over two days. During training, panelists were familiarized with the 9-point hedonic scale (1 = dislike extremely; 9 = like extremely) and the specific attributes to be evaluated: appearance, texture, odor, taste, and overall acceptability. Different commercial chicken nugget samples were used as reference points on the scale. The training also included discussions on cleansing the palate with water and unsalted crackers between samples. For the formal test, nugget samples (coded with random three-digit numbers) were presented to panelists individually in a randomized order under white light in a standardized sensory booth at room temperature (Tobin et al., 2013).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate (three independent batches of nuggets), and each batch was analyzed in duplicate for each parameter. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The data were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with treatment (7 levels) and storage day (4 levels: 0, 3, 7, 14) as fixed factors, using SPSS Statistics software (version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Following a significant ANOVA, post-hoc mean comparisons were performed using Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) test. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Antioxidant activity (DPPH· radical scavenging activity)

The DPPH· radical scavenging activity of chicken nuggets varied significantly (p < 0.05) among treatments and across storage periods. At day 0, the highest DPPH· value was observed in nuggets coated with AE2% + FSG3%, whereas the lowest value was recorded in the uncoated control samples (Table 1). This clearly indicates that incorporating ajwain extract and fish scale gelatin enhanced the product’s antioxidant capacity. A gradual decline in DPPH· activity was observed across all treatments as storage time progressed up to 14 days. This reduction may be attributed to the depletion of antioxidant compounds due to oxidative reactions during refrigerated storage.

However, during storage, coated samples consistently maintained higher DPPH· values than uncoated samples, indicating the protective effect of AE and FSG against oxidative degradation. The presence of phenolic compounds in the ajwain extract and the fish scale gelatin’s capacity to form protective films are responsible for the much higher antioxidant activity of the coated chicken nuggets compared to the control. These results are consistent with those of Bashir et al. (2022), who found that meat products treated with natural antioxidants have higher DPPH· activity. In a similar vein, Shahvandari et al. Total phenolic content (TPC)

The total phenolic content of chicken nuggets was significantly affected by coating formulation and storage time (p < 0.05). At day 0, the maximum TPC was recorded in nuggets coated with AE2% + FSG3%, while the lowest TPC was observed in uncoated control samples (Table 1). An increasing trend in TPC was evident with higher concentrations of ajwain extract and fish scales gelatin. During refrigerated storage, TPC values gradually decreased in both coated and uncoated samples. This decline may be attributed to the oxidation and degradation of phenolic compounds over time. However, coated samples showed a slower reduction in TPC than the control, indicating that the coating matrix effectively preserved phenolic compounds during storage. The significantly higher TPC in coated nuggets confirms the contribution of ajwain extract as a rich source of phenolic compounds, while fish scales gelatin likely acted as a carrier and protective barrier. These results are consistent with Banerjee et al. (2020), who reported increased TPC in meat nuggets fortified with enoki mushroom stem waste. Similar trends were observed by Sutha and Chandirasekaran (2021) in lemongrass oil-treated chicken nuggets and by Madane et al. (2019) in Moringa oleifera-enriched chicken nuggets. Lishianawati and Yusiati (2021) also reported a decline in TPC during storage, with treated samples retaining higher phenolic content than control samples.

Lipid oxidation (TBARS values)

TBARS values, which indicate lipid oxidation, differed significantly (p < 0.05) among treatments and storage periods. At day 0, the lowest TBARS value was observed in nuggets coated with AE2% + FSG3%, whereas the highest value was recorded in uncoated control samples (Table 2). This demonstrates the effectiveness of ajwain extract and fish scales gelatin in suppressing lipid oxidation. An increasing trend in TBARS values was observed in all samples during refrigerated storage, likely due to progressive lipid oxidation over time. However, coated samples showed significantly lower TBARS values compared to uncoated samples throughout storage. The reduced lipid oxidation in coated nuggets may be due to the antioxidant compounds in the ajwain extract and to the oxygen-barrier properties of the gelatin coating. These findings are consistent with those of Rahman et al. (2021), who reported reduced TBARS values in antioxidant-treated beef patties during refrigerated storage. Similar observations were made by Bashir et al. (2022) in pomegranate peel-coated chicken nuggets and by El-Sohaimy et al. (2022) in quinoa-coated chicken nuggets. Biswas et al. (2022) also reported lower TBARS values in fish nuggets treated with dragon fruit peel powder.

Peroxide value (POV)

Peroxide value is an important indicator of primary lipid oxidation in meat products. The POV of chicken nuggets was significantly influenced by coating treatments and storage duration (p < 0.05). The highest POV was observed in uncoated samples at day 0, whereas coated samples, particularly AE2% + FSG3%, showed the lowest POV (Table 2). A gradual increase in POV was observed in all treatments during storage, reflecting the formation of lipid hydroperoxides due to auto-oxidation. However, coated samples exhibited significantly lower POVs compared to uncoated samples at all storage intervals. This suggests that the antioxidant constituents of ajwain extract effectively inhibited peroxide formation, while fish scales gelatin provided a protective coating matrix. These results are consistent with Noor et al. (2022), who reported reduced POV in shrimp patties treated with Asparagus racemosus powder. Similar findings were reported by Khatun et al. (2022) in carrot- and ginger-enriched chicken nuggets and by Lishianawati et al. (2021) in black garlic-treated duck nuggets.

Microbial quality

The total aerobic count of chicken nuggets increased significantly (p < 0.05) with storage time in both coated and uncoated samples (Table 3). Uncoated control samples exhibited the highest microbial load throughout storage, whereas coated samples, particularly AE2% + FSG3%, showed the lowest total aerobic counts at all sampling intervals. The reduction in microbial growth in coated samples may be attributed to the antimicrobial properties of ajwain extract and the barrier effect of fish scale gelatin, which limits oxygen and moisture transfer. These findings are comparable to those reported by Arshad et al. (2022), who observed reduced microbial growth in beef nuggets treated with bioactive peptides. Similar antimicrobial effects were reported by Bhat et al. (2015) using aloe vera coatings and by Shahzad et al. (2022) using citrus honey in chicken nuggets. Coliform bacteria were detected at higher levels in uncoated samples compared to coated samples during storage (Table 3). In coated nuggets, particularly AE2% + FSG3%, coliforms were either undetected or present at very low levels during the early storage period, indicating the coating system’s antimicrobial efficacy. With increasing storage time, coliform counts increased gradually; however, levels remained significantly lower in coated samples than in controls. These results agree with those of Shahzad et al. (2022), who reported reduced coliform counts in chicken nuggets treated with citrus honey. Arshad et al. (2022) and Khatun et al. (2022) also reported similar reductions in coliform growth in treated meat products. Azizkhani et al. (2023) evaluated composite edible coatings made from chitosan, gelatin, and starch, combined with bitter orange peel extract, to improve the quality and shelf life of refrigerated chicken fillets. Chicken fillets coated with the bioactive formulations showed lower microbial counts compared to uncoated controls, reduced lipid oxidation, improved physicochemical stability, and enhanced sensory properties.

Color characteristics (Hunter L*, a*, and b* values)

The color attributes of chicken nuggets were significantly influenced by coating formulation and storage time. The L* (lightness) values of coated nuggets were higher than those of uncoated samples and increased slightly with higher concentrations of ajwain extract and fish scales gelatin (Table 4). This increase in lightness may be associated with the surface characteristics and reflective properties of the coating materials. The a* (redness) values were significantly higher (p < 0.05) in coated samples compared to uncoated ones, indicating better color stability during storage. Similarly, b* (yellowness) values were higher in coated nuggets, particularly at higher coating concentrations, which may be attributed to the natural pigments in the ajwain extract and to oxidative changes during storage (Table 5). These findings are consistent with previous studies by Waskar et al. (2009), Jang et al. (2008), and Naveena et al. (2008), who reported improved color stability in meat products treated with herbal and plant-based coatings. Yaqoob et al. (2020) also observed increased L*, a*, and b* values in meat products enriched with plant extracts.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory attributes of chicken nuggets, including appearance, texture, odor, taste, and overall acceptability, were significantly affected (p < 0.05) by coating treatments and storage duration (Fig. 1). In general, sensory scores declined with increasing storage time in all samples. However, coated samples consistently received higher scores than uncoated controls throughout the storage period. Coated nuggets exhibited improved texture and odor stability, likely due to reduced lipid oxidation and microbial growth. Taste and overall acceptability scores were also higher in coated samples, particularly those treated with AE2% + FSG3%, indicating better consumer acceptability. These findings are in agreement with Kumar et al. (2011), Shoaib et al. (2018), and Reddy et al. (2022), who reported enhanced sensory qualities in coated and plant extract-treated chicken nuggets. Overall, the results demonstrate that coatings based on ajwain extract and fish scales gelatin effectively improved antioxidant capacity, oxidative stability, microbial safety, color attributes, and sensory quality of chicken nuggets during refrigerated storage.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that chicken nuggets could be effectively preserved for up to 14 days under refrigerated storage when coated with edible coatings formulated with ajwain extract (AE) and fish scale gelatin (FSG). Based on comprehensive evaluations of sensory, physicochemical, biochemical, and microbial attributes, the incorporation of fish scale gelatin, combined with 2% ajwain extract, ensured acceptable quality and enhanced shelf stability of chicken nuggets. The combined application of ajwain extract and Rohu fish scales gelatin significantly improved the overall quality attributes of chicken nuggets, including consistency, oxidative stability, and microbial safety. Physicochemical, microbial, and physical analyses confirmed that both AE and FSG effectively preserved and protected the nuggets during storage. Even after extended storage, samples containing ajwain extract and fish-scales gelatin maintained satisfactory quality characteristics compared with uncoated controls. Furthermore, coatings enriched with AE and FSG exhibited higher antioxidant potential and better protection against lipid oxidation, as evidenced by reduced TBARS and peroxide values. Among the tested formulations, the combination of AE2%+FSG3% proved to be the most effective in maintaining quality, sensory acceptability, and storage stability. Overall, the phytochemical and physicochemical profiles of chicken nuggets were significantly enhanced by the incorporation of ajwain extract and fish scales gelatin, indicating their potential as natural, halal, and functional coating materials for extending the shelf life of meat products.
