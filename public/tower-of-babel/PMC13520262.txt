An integrated analysis of MEN1 reveals its associations with clinical outcomes, cellular pathways, and the immune microenvironment
Authors: Hao Li, Xinyu Gong, Yanwei Yao, Ping Bi, Qiang Li, Xiaoru Wang, Jian Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The menin protein, encoded by the Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 1 (MEN1) gene, serves as a critical regulator of cellular processes in human cancers. While menin-targeted therapies are undergoing clinical investigation, a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of MEN1's diagnostic, prognostic, and immunomodulatory roles remain lacking. Here, we systematically evaluated MEN1 expression patterns and clinical relevance using multi-omics databases and immunohistochemical profiles from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA). MEN1 exhibited ubiquitous expression in normal tissues but was frequently upregulated across multiple malignancies, correlating with adverse survival outcomes. Elevated MEN1 expression demonstrated strong diagnostic utility and positive associations with tumor mutation burden (TMB), suggesting complementary value in cancer detection. Immune landscape analysis revealed significant correlations between MEN1 expression and immune checkpoint genes, as well as immune cell infiltration patterns. Functional characterization implicated MEN1 in activating cell cycle progression, DNA damage response, and oncogenic signaling pathways, as validated through Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA). These findings position MEN1 as a promising prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target with implications for immuno-oncology and precision medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04460-x.

Introduction

Cancer, characterized by uncontrolled cellular proliferation and evasion of regulatory mechanisms, remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Recent advances in oncology have introduced diverse therapeutic modalities, including surgical resection, cytotoxic chemotherapy, ionizing radiation, molecularly targeted agents, and immunotherapeutic strategies. Despite these innovations, the prognosis for patients with advanced malignancies remains suboptimal. High recurrence rates and limited survival benefits persist across many tumor types [2, 3]. This clinical impasse underscores the urgent need for identifying novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve patient outcomes.

Immunotherapy has emerged as a transformative paradigm in oncology, demonstrating unprecedented efficacy across diverse malignancies by reactivating host antitumor immune responses [4, 5]. Central to this therapeutic revolution is the modulation of immune checkpoint pathways, a complex network of ligand-receptor interactions that either suppress or activate immune effector functions. Dysregulation of these pathways, particularly through inhibitory molecules such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, plays a pivotal role in tumor immune evasion [6, 7]. Clinical interventions targeting these checkpoints have yielded durable responses in multiple cancers, though predictive biomarkers for patient stratification remain an unmet need [8, 9]. Consequently, comprehensive analysis of tumor-immune microenvironment interactions and checkpoint expression profiles holds significant clinical promise.

MEN1is an autosomal dominant cancer syndrome caused by heterozygous germline mutations in the tumor suppressor gene MEN1, characterized by predisposition to neuroendocrine tumors in the parathyroid, pituitary, and pancreatoduodenal regions [10]. The MEN1 gene, located on chromosome 11q13, spans 7 kb and contains 10 exons that encode a 610-amino acid scaffold protein, menin, which mediates epigenetic regulation through histone modification complexes [11–13]. Over 500 distinct pathogenic MEN1 variants have been cataloged, predominantly resulting in loss-of-function alterations [14–16]. Somatic MEN1 inactivation is also observed in sporadic endocrine tumors, underscoring its broader relevance beyond hereditary syndromes [17].

Recent mechanistic studies have expanded the functional repertoire of MEN1 beyond traditional tumor suppression. Jin et al. demonstrated that menin regulates RNA splicing fidelity by modulating RNA polymerase II processivity, thereby preventing R-loop-induced genomic instability, a pathway implicated in cancer cell plasticity [18]. Complementary work by Ye et al. revealed that MEN1 promotes ferroptotic cell death through inhibition of the mTOR-SCD1 axis in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors [19]. Furthermore, MEN1 deficiency was shown to accelerate pancreatic tumorigenesis via dysregulation of thymidylate synthase (hTS), establishing a direct link between MEN1 status and tumor aggressiveness [20]. These findings collectively suggest that MEN1 exerts multifaceted tumor-suppressive functions, though its role remains underexplored across cancer types.

Leveraging the expanding repertoire of multi-omics databases, we conducted a systematic investigation of MEN1's prognostic and immunological significance using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [21] and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) [22] datasets. Our integrated analysis revealed robust associations between MEN1 expression, tumor-infiltrating immune cell dynamics, and immune checkpoint expression profiles. These findings position MEN1 as a potential dual-purpose biomarker for predicting cancer prognosis and guiding immunotherapeutic decision-making. Collectively, this work provides a comprehensive framework for understanding MEN1's contextual roles in oncogenesis and immunoregulation, with implications for precision oncology.

Materials and methods

Comprehensive gene expression profiling

The expression levels of MEN1 in normal tissues were analyzed using data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project (https://commonfund.nih.gov/GTEx), a public resource that facilitates investigation of the relationship between genetic variation and gene expression across human tissues [23]. Boxplots of expression levels were then generated using the ggplot2 package within the R/Bioconductor framework. We also performed a multi-tiered analysis of MEN1 mRNA expression patterns in cancer cell lines using the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database [24]. To establish robust differential expression profiles, we conducted paired comparisons of MEN1 transcript levels between primary tumors and adjacent normal tissues from TCGA cohort. Complementary validation was performed using 10 independent GEO datasets (GSE42568, GSE29570, GSE26566, GSE41657, GSE6344, GSE30219, GSE26712, GSE28735, GSE66229, GSE65144). The tissue origin and transcriptomics platform for each dataset are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. To address batch effects between TCGA and GTEx datasets, we applied a standardized normalization pipeline: expression values (TPM) from both databases were log2‑transformed, and quantile normalization was performed after combining the datasets. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to verify the removal of batch effects prior to differential expression analysis. Differential expression analysis was carried out using the tidyverse, limma, and ggplot2 R packages, comparing MEN1 expression between tumor and adjacent normal tissues with a significance threshold of adjusted p ≤ 0.05. All expression data were normalized using standardized protocols and visualized using ggplot2.

Proteomic validation via immunohistochemistry

Protein-level expression of menin was analyzed using immunohistochemistry (IHC) data from the HPA [25]. Tissue-specific expression patterns were systematically evaluated across normal tissues and eight cancer types (colorectal, breast, cervical, glioma, gastric, testicular, thyroid, urothelial), with comparative analysis of staining intensity and localization between normal and malignant tissues.

Multidimensional survival analysis

The prognostic impact of MEN1 expression was assessed using the Kaplan–Meier plotter platform (http://kmplot.com/analysis/)) [26] Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to evaluate associations between MEN1 expression and four clinical endpoints: overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free interval (DFI), and progression-free interval (PFI). Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and log-rank p-values were calculated to quantify prognostic significance.

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed using the R package survival to assess the independent prognostic significance of MEN1 expression. Patients with missing survival data (time or event) or non-positive survival time were excluded. Pan-cancer analyses were conducted stratified by cancer type, with automatic removal of non-variable categorical covariates to ensure model stability. The Cox model was adjusted for age, gender, and tumor stage, with overall survival as the primary endpoint. HRs, 95% CI, and p-values were estimated for each variable. Forest plots visualizing adjusted HRs and 95% CIs were generated using the forestplot package.

Diagnostic performance evaluation

Diagnostic utility of MEN1 was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis across tumor types [27]. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to investigate the relationship between MEN1 expression and tumor mutational burden (TMB), with statistical significance assessed via two-tailed t-tests (p < 0.05).

Immune microenvironment characterization

Tumor-infiltrating immune cell (TIIC) composition was quantified using the cell-type identification by estimating relative subsets of RNA transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm (v1.03) applied to TCGA gene expression data [28]. The CIBERSORT algorithm with the LM22 gene signature matrix (Supplementary Tables S2) based on TPM-normalized gene expression data. Analyses were performed for each TCGA cancer type with 1000 permutations and quantile normalization. Only tumor specimens were included after excluding normal tissue samples. Spearman’s correlations between MEN1 expression and immune checkpoint molecules (including PD-L1/CD274) were further evaluated using ssGSEA [29] and validated by Pearson correlation analysis (R software v4.2.2). The ssGSEA algorithm implemented in the R package GSVA was applied to calculate the normalized enrichment score (NES) of each immune cell type for individual tumor samples. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was then performed to explore the correlations between MEN1 expression levels and ssGSEA-based immune cell enrichment scores across diverse cancer types. Correlation coefficients and corresponding p-values were calculated, and the overall pan-cancer correlation landscape was visualized via annotated heatmaps with significance markers (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

To evaluate the association between MEN1 expression and tumor microenvironment characteristics—including stromal content, immune infiltration, and tumor purity—the ESTIMATE algorithm was applied to pan-cancer TCGA cohorts, using log₂-transformed counts per million (log₂CPM) from primary tumor samples to calculate StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore. Tumor purity was further derived from the ESTIMATEScore using the formula TumorPurity = cos (0.6049872018 + 0.0001467884 × ESTIMATEScore). Spearman’s rank correlation was then performed to assess relationships between log₂-transformed MEN1 expression and each score per cancer type, with correlation coefficients and p-values calculated (significance denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001), and the resulting pan-cancer correlation patterns were visualized using polar plots to illustrate the strength and direction of associations across diverse malignancies.

Functional annotation and pathway analysis

To screen for MEN1‑related differentially expressed genes (DEGs), TCGA pan‑cancer RNA‑seq count data were downloaded from the GDC hub using the UCSCXenaTools R package [30]. Only standard TCGA htseq‑count datasets were retained to ensure uniform sample grouping and data reliability. Raw expression matrices were preprocessed by truncating Ensemble gene IDs to 15 characters to remove version suffixes, followed by between-array quantile normalization to reduce cross-sample bias. Ensemble IDs were annotated to official gene symbols via the tinyarray package. For MEN1-related differential expression analysis, tumor samples from each cancer type were divided into MEN1-high and MEN1-low subgroups based on median MEN1 expression. Differential expression analysis was performed using limma and edgeR: normalized count matrices were constructed as a DGEList object, followed by factor normalization, voom transformation, linear model fitting, and empirical Bayesian correction. Genes with |log₂ (fold change, FC) |≥ 1.5 and p < 0.05 were considered MEN1-associated DEGs, categorized into upregulated, downregulated, and stable groups. Full DEG lists for each cancer type were retained for subsequent enrichment analyses. Volcano plots generated by the EnhancedVolcano package visualized DEG distributions. GSEA was performed to characterize the biological functions and signaling pathways associated with aberrant MEN1 expression, and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was further applied to validate the enrichment results [31].

For GSEA, tumor samples from each cancer type were stratified into MEN1-high and MEN1-low subgroups according to the median MEN1 expression level. Consistent with standard GSEA guidelines, all detected genes rather than pre-filtered DEGs were included to construct a ranked gene list based on log2FC values between the two subgroups, while genes with zero log2FC values were excluded. The hallmark gene set collection (h.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) was used as the reference pathway annotation dataset. All GSEA analyses were conducted in R using the clusterProfiler and fgsea packages with a fixed random seed (set.seed = 1234) to ensure reproducibility. The analytical parameters were set as follows: minimum gene set size = 10, maximum gene set size = 10,000, and the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method was employed for multiple-testing correction to control the false discovery rate (FDR). A nominal p-value cutoff of 1.0 was adopted to capture all enrichment results for subsequent screening and visualization. Enrichment score (ES), normalized enrichment score (NES), nominal p-value, and FDR-adjusted q-value were calculated for each annotated pathway. All enrichment results were sorted in descending order by NES, and pathways with FDR < 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. GSEA enrichment curves and gene rank plots were visualized using the enrichplot and cowplot packages.

For GSVA validation, the Hallmark gene set collection (h.all.v7.5.1) was utilized as the reference database. Based on log2-transformed TPM expression profiles of TCGA tumor samples, the enrichment scores of 50 hallmark biological pathways were quantified across all cancer types using the GSVA package. Spearman’s correlation analysis was then performed to evaluate the correlations between MEN1 expression levels and pathway enrichment scores. Standardized and optimized heatmaps generated via ggplot2 were used to visualize the pan-cancer correlation patterns, systematically revealing the functional pathways associated with MEN1 dysregulation. In addition, volcano plots were constructed to intuitively exhibit the distribution of MEN1-related DEGs across different cancer types.

Statistical framework

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism (v9.4.0). Comparative analyses employed unpaired t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for two-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA for multi-group comparisons, and two-way ANOVA for interaction effects. Correlation significance was assessed via Pearson's coefficient (r). Kaplan–Meier survival differences were evaluated using log-rank tests. All p-values were two-tailed with significance thresholds: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Given this background, profiling tumor-infiltrating immune cells is essential for oncology research. Herein, we applied ssGSEA to comprehensively analyze the correlations between MEN1 expression and diverse tumor-infiltrating immune cells across cancers. Our results indicated apparent associations between MEN1 and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in most cancer types (Fig. 6D). Specifically, in uveal melanoma (UVM), MEN1 expression showed strong positive correlations with the majority of immune cell populations, including central memory CD4⁺ T cells, central memory CD8⁺ T cells, CD56bright natural killer (NK) cells, gamma delta T cells, NKT cells, NK cells, activated CD8⁺ T cells, Th1 cells and activated CD4⁺ T cells as well as Th2 cells (p < 0.001). In contrast, in glioblastoma (GBM), MEN1 was strongly negatively correlated with most immune cells such as activated B cells, immature B cells, activated CD8⁺ T cells, mast cells and Th1 cells (p < 0.001). Collectively, MEN1 was positively correlated with most tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in UVM, while negative correlations predominated in other cancer types (Fig. 6D).

A previous study by Su et al. demonstrated that MEN1 knockout did not affect in vitro cell proliferation in multiple solid tumors, but exerted divergent effects on tumor growth in immunodeficient and immunocompetent mice. Mechanistically, loss of MEN1 reshapes MLL1 chromatin occupancy, elevates H3K4me3 levels at repetitive genomic regions, induces double-stranded RNA production, and increases infiltration of neutrophils and CD8⁺ T cells. In addition, pharmacological disruption of the menin-MLL interaction suppresses tumor growth in a CD8⁺ T cell-dependent manner. Those findings have revealed multifaceted roles of MEN1 in regulating the TME, supporting the functional relevance of MEN1 in tumor immunity [39].

Immune checkpoint blockade has become a promising immunotherapeutic strategy, which reinvigorates anti-tumor immunity by blocking the interaction between immune receptors and their ligands [40]. To further characterize the potential immunological links of MEN1, we performed Pearson correlation analysis to evaluate the associations between MEN1 and canonical immune checkpoint genes across cancers. The results revealed distinct correlation patterns in different tumor types. In cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), MEN1 expression was strongly negatively correlated with CD274, CTLA4, HAVCR2, LAG3, PDCD1, PDCD1LG2 and TIGIT (p < 0.001), while positively correlated with SIGLEC15 (p < 0.05). Similar correlative relationships between MEN1 and immune checkpoint genes were also observed in other cancer types (Fig. 6E).

PD-L1 (encoded by CD274) binds to PD-1 on effector T cells to impair anti-tumor immunity, and PD-L1-targeted immunotherapy has yielded encouraging clinical outcomes [41]. Figure S6 summarizes the correlations between MEN1 and PD-L1 across pan-cancer. For example, a strong positive correlation between MEN1 and PD-L1 was observed in cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL) (r = 0.62, p = 6.2 × 10⁻⁶).

Importantly, all findings presented above are derived solely from correlation analyses. Although our results suggest potential connections between MEN1, immune infiltration and immune checkpoint molecules, these correlative observations are hypothesis-generating and cannot establish direct regulatory relationships. Further validations using independent cohorts, protein-level detection and functional experimental models are required to clarify the causal interplay between MEN1 and tumor immunity.

Discussion

In recent years, the role of MEN1 in tumorigenesis has increasingly attracted attention. Studies have revealed that MEN1 is associated with metastasis and poor prognosis in cancers [42–44]. Functionally, menin represses tumorigenesis by inhibiting cell proliferation through interactions with histone-modifying enzymes (MLL, EZH2, HDACs), transcription factors (JunD, NF-κB, PPARγ), and signaling pathways (TGF-β, Wnt/β-catenin) [45]. Notably, menin exhibits context-dependent duality: it acts as a tumor suppressor in neuroendocrine tumors, cholangiocarcinoma, and melanoma, but as a tumor promoter in leukemia, colorectal cancer, and gliomas [46–49]. Expression of menin is significantly altered across malignancies and associates with prognosis in a cancer-type-dependent manner. For example, in bladder cancer Furthermore, in bladder cancer, menin is significantly upregulated and correlates with poor prognosis, promoting cell proliferation via the TFAP2C/β-catenin axis [50]. From a diagnostic perspective, circulating miR-1301-3p and miR-24-3p serve as non-invasive biomarkers for MEN1 syndrome, and NET mRNA or methylome signatures help distinguish localized from metastatic disease in MEN1-associated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors [51]. However, most diagnostic studies focus on MEN1 syndrome rather than sporadic cancers, and the utility of menin itself as a pan-cancer diagnostic marker remains underexplored. These findings provide a strong rationale for our pan-cancer analysis addressing this gap. Given the limited exploration of the MEN1 gene in the context of cancer, we conducted a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis to elucidate its biological functions and potential regulatory pathways in the development of various tumors.

In this study, we estimated the expression pattern of the MEN1 gene across various cancers and its prognostic value. We found that MEN1 is upregulated in most cancer types, and high MEN1 expression is associated with poor OS, DSS, DFI, and PFI. However, we also acknowledge that MEN1 expression exhibits cancer‑type‑dependent heterogeneity; for instance, GEO validation data revealed downregulation in hepatocellular carcinoma and no significant changes in cervical, renal, pancreatic, and thyroid cancers. Therefore, while MEN1 is significantly upregulated in several cancer types, it is not universally overexpressed across all malignancies. Nevertheless, MEN1 holds certain diagnostic and prognostic value in pan‑cancer analysis. Collectively, these findings strongly suggest that MEN1 can serve as a potential biomarker for tumor diagnosis and prognosis.

The occurrence of cancer is influenced by both the surrounding environment and the tumor microenvironment (TME) [52]. The TME, comprising immune cells, the extracellular matrix, mesenchymal cells, and inflammatory mediators, impacts tumor growth, metastasis, and clinical survival outcomes [53]. While effective immune responses may have antitumor effects, cancer cells have evolved multiple mechanisms, including antigen presentation dysfunction and the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, to facilitate tumor escape from immune cell attack. This study explored the correlation between MEN1 expression and the level of immune infiltration in pan-cancer. Overall, MEN1 was negatively correlated with the immune cells studied in most tumor tissues. Previous studies have reported that immune infiltration indeed affects patient prognosis [54, 55]. However, in UVM, MEN1 was positively correlated with most immune cells.

Various types of cancers express high levels of PD-L1 and utilize the PD-L1/PD-1 signal to evade T-cell immunity. Blocking the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway has consistently demonstrated significant antitumor effects in advanced cancer patients and is considered the gold standard for developing new immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) and combination therapies [56]. Therefore, further understanding the regulatory mechanisms of PD-L1 can bring substantial benefits to cancer patients by improving the efficacy of current PD-L1/PD-1 blockade or other ICBs. Recently, Zhang et al. found that MEN1 deficiency stabilizes PD-L1 and promotes tumor immune escape in lung cancer [57]. The possible mechanism is that PD-L1 is inhibited by menin overexpression, and MEN1 inactivation promotes the deubiquitinating activity of COP9 signalosome subunit 5 (CSN5), subsequently increasing PD-L1 levels [57]. This study explored the correlation between MEN1 and PD-L1 in various cancers. A certain linear relationship existed between MEN1 and PD-L1 in a considerable number of cancers. Manipulating the PD-1/PD-L1 axis can not only improve the efficacy of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) but also eliminate adverse events in immunotherapy due to T-cells being affected in non-tumor sites [58]. Abnormal expression of MEN1 may also mediate the release of certain small molecules by tumor cells, regulating the tumor immune escape process, which will be a molecular mechanism to be explored in our subsequent studies. These results all indicate that MEN1 may play an important role in regulating tumor immunity.

Cancer is a disease characterized by uncontrolled cell growth and proliferation. Tumor cells differ from normal cells in proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [59]. Among these cells, cell cycle dysregulation is one of the most important features [60]. In this study, to explain the potential molecular mechanisms by which MEN1 affects cancer prognosis, we conducted ssGSEA and found that the high MEN1 expression group was strongly enriched in the G2M CHECKPOINT, E2F TARGETS, DNA REPAIR, MYC TARGETS V2, and MYC TARGETS V1 groups. The high enrichment intervals of MEN1 all pointed to the regulation of the cell cycle, which helped us understand the key role of MEN1 in tumor development. Meanwhile, GSVA also validated this conclusion.

The results of all the above analyses indicate that MEN1 is a key diagnostic and therapeutic target for multiple cancers. We believe that the development of specific inhibitors or activators targeting MEN1 can effectively improve disease progression and prognosis in cancer patients. However, the research on targeted drugs is not limited to biomarkers that regulate tumor cell function. Additionally, tumor immunotherapy is also a promising method for treating tumors [61]. We have been searching for biomarkers that activate tumor immune responses and induce immune escape. To our delight, the results of pan-cancer analysis showed that MEN1 may play a key role in the immune response of multiple cancers.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the important role of MEN1 in multiple cancers. These findings will provide new ideas and support for the regulatory role of MEN1 in tumor treatment and have important clinical significance.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our pan‑cancer analyses suggest context‑dependent MEN1 duality—tumor‑suppressive in endocrine tumors via loss versus pro‑tumorigenic in non‑endocrine cancers with wild‑type upregulation—but these findings are from retrospective public data and bioinformatics, lacking prospective validation and protein‑level evidence. Elevated MEN1 expression correlates with poor prognosis and oncogenic pathways, yet causality and direct oncogenic activity are unproven; upregulation may be a passive adaptive response. Second, the divergent roles based on tissue origin, genetic background, and dysregulation mode (loss vs. overexpression) lack functional validation in vitro/in vivo, and the mechanism of how MEN1 overexpression drives malignancy without loss remains unclear. Third, bulk RNA‑seq limits resolution of cell‑type‑specific interactions in the tumor microenvironment, necessitating single‑cell or spatial transcriptomics. Finally, all conclusions are correlative (e.g., MEN1 with TMB or PD‑L1), precluding causal inference. To address these, we will perform gain‑/loss‑of‑function assays, ChIP‑seq, and animal models to clarify whether MEN1 upregulation causally promotes aggressiveness or is a bystander biomarker.
