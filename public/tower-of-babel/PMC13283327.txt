Integrating community Live Reviews into academic publishing: an exploratory study
Authors: Christopher I. Petkov, Vanessa Fairhurst, Monica Granados, Yogita Chudasama, Gail Rodney, Syeda A. Jabeen, Stephen David, Anna S. Mitchell, P. Christiaan Klink, Bahar Mehmani, Daniela Saderi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcademic peer review is fundamental for scientific knowledge dissemination, and various initiatives are exploring how the peer review process could be more open, efficient and rewarding. We report an exploratory study where a live community-based review approach was integrated into the editorial workflow of an academic journal (Current Research in Neurobiology; CRNEUR).MethodsThis study was conducted with five manuscripts (‘cases’) submitted as preprints, which underwent Live Review—a structured collaborative review session led by PREreview, an organization with the mission to advance equity and openness in scholarly evaluation. With each case, PREreview team members facilitated a 90-min online discussion where registered participants provided real-time discussion and worked together on the online structured peer review document. Authors could join as observers or to answer questions. Participants then volunteered to write up the session notes into a final review and summary statement. Review participants had the option to sign the review. The finalized review was then published on PREreview’s open preprint review platform approximately two weeks after the Live Review session, and it was assigned a CC BY 4.0 license and Digital Object Identifier (DOI) linked to the DOI of the reviewed preprint allowing reviewers to be recognized for their contribution. The published review was then incorporated into CRNEUR’s editorial workflow to inform editorial decisions and manuscript outcomes.ResultsWe quantified the speed to first and final editorial decision of the community review (n = 5) in comparison to a larger sample (n = 27) of articles that went through a standard review process at CRNEUR during the same timeframe. First decision times in days after manuscript submission of the Live Reviews were within the Inter Quartile Range (IQR) of the standard review process (community review: median = 75, IQR: 41.3; standard review: median 92, IQR: 41.5), as were final decision times (community review: median 138, IQR: 22.5; standard review: median 211, IQR: 166.0). A survey of the Live Review attendees (n = 13; 30% response rate) on a scale of 1 ‘Highly Disagree’ to 5 ‘Highly Agree’ showed median ‘Agree’ to ‘Highly Agree’ scores on several questions including the review being respectful, time efficient and scientifically rigorous (median scores: 5, 4, 4, respectively).ConclusionsThe innovative Live Review approach was as efficient as the standard review process in the journal and was rated positively by those surveyed. The small sample size inherent to exploratory studies limits conclusions generalizing to larger sample sizes. We discuss how live, community-based review approaches could be further developed, scaled and sustained.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41073-026-00210-5.

Background

Peer review is essential for maintaining integrity in the dissemination of scientific knowledge, and since its inception over 300 years ago has been implemented to assess scientific content prior to article publication [1]. However, the traditional peer review process faces significant challenges such as the process being slow, lacking transparency, training for early career researchers and offering few incentives for reviewers to complete timely high-quality reviews [2, 3]. Several developments are underway to improve the peer review process, requiring empirical evaluation and comparison to the standard review process.

The peer review process remains the slowest component in many journal evaluation frameworks [4], unable to keep pace with the rapid growth of scientific content [5]. Editors in many journals are struggling to recruit reviewers who face time constraints and receive limited incentives for reviewing manuscripts [2, 6]. Journals such as eLife, have reversed the traditional process to increase the speed of publication by immediately publishing preprints after an initial editorial evaluation, after which the manuscripts can undergo peer review [7]. Authors can also engage with journal-independent peer review providers (such as Review Commons or PREreview) to have their preprints peer reviewed, after which the authors may choose to submit the revised manuscript along with the reviewer comments to a journal willing to expedite its publication [3, 8, 9]. Whether these modifications to the peer review process are more efficient requires empirical comparison to the more traditional review process adopted by many journals.

In the life sciences, peer review has traditionally been conducted anonymously, with reviewers’ identities concealed. While anonymity can protect reviewers and encourage candid feedback, it also renders the process opaque, making it difficult to identify bias, assess conflicts of interest or ensure accountability. To address these concerns, various peer review models that fall under the label of ‘open peer review’ have emerged [10], aiming to introduce greater transparency by making review reports publicly available [11–15]. Revealing reviewer identities can be associated with more thoughtful and constructive feedback [16, 17]. However, open peer review can also discourage participation from early-career researchers, who may be concerned about potential retribution by critiquing the work of other scholars [16, 18–20]. Hybrid models—where reviewers collaborate, discuss their assessments and then report them to the journal editors—have shown promise in maintaining review quality while reducing turnaround times [21]. Also, some journals have experimented with directly involving authors in the manuscript acceptance decision-making process after an initial editorial selection process [22].

Community review sessions can now be efficiently conducted online via journal messaging boards or online videoconferencing meetings that the authors can attend, the latter of which we refer to as Live Review. Moreover, integrated digital library systems are available to assist authors, editors, and reviewers [23]. Many scientists are familiar with ‘journal clubs’ where a special interest academic group reviews a recently published manuscript or preprint in real time. Journal clubs could be considered a form of community Live Review, although the objectives of an academic journal club differ from those of the peer review process and there is substantial variability in how journal clubs are structured. Organizations like PREreview that incorporate structured peer review documents are experimenting with how community Live Review ‘journal clubs’ can harness the rigor of an academic community’s journal club with the final objective of completing a publicly available peer review of an unpublished manuscript. The aim is that both reviewers and authors could benefit from such a process, for instance by including training opportunities in peer review for early-career researchers or structured revision guidance for the authors of the manuscript. Although open peer review developments such as Live Reviews hold promise for improving the peer review process, empirical evaluation of the process and comparison with the more traditional process is needed, including how efficiently it can integrate into academic journal workflows.

In this exploratory study, we developed and evaluated a structured Live Review process that was integrated within an academic journal’s editorial workflow. We first overview the collaborating parties that developed this exploratory study, PREreview and Current Research in Neurobiology (CRNEUR). Then we describe the Live Review process, how the five manuscripts, as cases, that participated in this study were conducted and their outcomes. We obtained several measures, including participation rates, editorial decision times and (reject, revise, accept) outcomes. These measures were used to compare the five cases with a larger sample of manuscripts submitted to the journal during the same year. We also report survey information on participants’ perceptions of the efficiency, rigor and balance of the Live Review process. We acknowledge the inherent limitation of the small sample size in this exploratory study. Accordingly, the data, code and materials from this study are shared in the Supplementary Materials and on the Open Science Framework (see Data and Code Sharing Statement) to assist efforts to further develop and evaluate community Live Reviews. Our goal is to support continuing innovation and empirical evaluation of the peer review process, and we offer suggestions about how Live Review could be further evaluated as an option for authors when considering a journal for peer review or to implement independently.

Methods

Study aim and rationale

In this exploratory study our aims were to, 1) develop a Live Review process guided by the PREreview team; 2) conduct five planned Live Review sessions, at a pace of approximately 1–2 per month in 2023, with different journal handling editors; and, 3) report outcome data comparing these case with standard review manuscripts submitted to the journal in 2023 during the timeframe of the 5 cases that were not desk rejected from all journal submissions. We obtained data on first and final editorial decision times directly from the Elsevier Editorial Manager system. We also obtained participation and completion rates. Finally, for the 5 cases, we report survey results of Live Review participant demographics and participation experience and impressions. Our decision on selecting five cases was based on the notion that case studies of 1–2 are not as useful as larger samples, five was feasible to take through the PREreview process (approx. 1–2 per month in 2023), and the question on scaling up to more than five would benefit from seeing the results from the first five compared to standard review data.

Participating organizations

Overview of PREreview

PREreview is an independent organization fiscally sponsored by the US 501(c) [3] Code for Science and Society. Its mission is to bring greater balance and transparency to the scholarly peer review process. To fulfill that mission, PREreview takes an integrative approach that combines people-centered technological solutions, training and mentorship and community and capacity building. Through the PREreview preprint review platform—independent of journals and preprint servers—anyone with an ORCID account can contribute constructive feedback to preprints posted across a growing number of preprint servers. The reviews can be signed using the real identity of the reviewer—which is tied to their ORCID record—or a unique pseudonym, thereby providing an opportunity for those who are concerned about their identity being known to participate while maintaining accountability for their participation through the platform. Reviewers can automatically add their preprint review contributions to their ORCID records, which gives them the option to add visibility to their contributions and to be more easily credited. In addition to maintaining a preprint review platform, PREreview organizes peer review workshops, cohort-based training programs and collaborative review sessions known as Live Reviews (Fig. 1). Overview of Current Research in Neurobiology

Current Research in Neurobiology (CRNEUR) is an Elsevier journal for primary research in neuroscience. It was founded in 2019 with the objective of being an experimental platform in scientific publishing. The journal founding editors, in their editorial introduction, defined a goal to actively work to balance editor, author and reviewer interactions and to experiment with innovations in peer review and publishing [24]. The journal is open access and provides authors with several options in how their manuscripts can be reviewed, including authors selecting either a single (reviewer) or double (reviewer and author) anonymous review process. The journal also publishes reviewer, author and editor interactions during the review process with all accepted manuscripts. The journal guidance provided to authors, reviewers and handling editors encourages cordial and well-reasoned disagreement between authors and reviewers, with authors having a role in the outcome of their manuscripts. The journal guides its editors to strike a balance between reviewer, author and editor interests and interactions. The editorial workflow for the journal is illustrated in Fig. 1A. The editorial workflow that interfaced with and integrated the outcome of the PREreview process is illustrated in Fig. 1B.

Study design and setting of the Live Review process for five cases

During 2023, PREreview and CRNEUR partnered to host five Live Review sessions. These sessions generated five open published review reports, which were either used to complement or serve as the sole method of peer review to inform the decision-making of the journal handling editors. In the context of this collaboration, we adopted the PREreview Live Review approach, inviting a diverse community of researchers with relevant expertise to collaboratively discuss and review a preprint that had also been submitted to CRNEUR, with the authors’ consent and participation in the exploratory study (see Ethics in Declarations section; Fig. 1B). Live Review sessions were typically scheduled approximately one month after author agreement, allowing time for participant recruitment and advance reading of the preprint. Participants were recruited through advertisements shared with PREreview online communities and CRNEUR reviewers via the journal’s standard reviewer recruitment workflow in Editorial Manager. These announcements included a registration link that provided registered participants with access to the online videoconferencing meeting for the live session. Below, the Live Review manuscripts are presented as case reports 1 through 5 (Tables 1, 2). During the call, participants were invited to review the preprint in a single 90-min online interactive videoconferencing session. The session was facilitated by the PREreview team using an openly shared notes document that contained specific participation guidelines and a series of structured questions that guided the discussion. The structured peer review documents are available with this manuscript (Supplementary Materials). The structured peer review document we used is distinct from others available [25]. Preprint authors and CRNEUR editors involved in handling the manuscript were invited to attend the session. Authors were encouraged to participate but were guided to limit their contributions to brief introductions and clarifications in response to direct questions. Editors participated as observers only. Reviewers were not required to speak or appear on camera and could contribute exclusively through the shared online document. The structured review document included a roll call section for participants to identify their role and affiliation, although reviewers could choose to remain anonymous. Throughout the session, facilitators incorporated working periods for silent, asynchronous contributions to the review document, enabling participants to engage without pressure to speak.

After the review session, one or more reviewers volunteered to take the lead in collating reviewer notes and integrating them into a complete final review. This final review document was published on PREreview.org with a citable DOI and a CC BY Attribution 4.0 International License. The authors of the review could choose to name themselves to obtain credit for the review or to remain anonymous. Those choosing to remain anonymous could choose a pseudonym, e.g., Red Fox randomly allocated to users of the PREreview platform upon account creation. The published, publicly available review was then integrated into the handling editor’s workflow by the CRNEUR journal team. The handling editor then relied exclusively on the reviews to make an initial decision (Cases 2–4) or considered them alongside standard peer reviews obtained separately by the handling editor (hybrid Cases 1 and 5).

Preprint selection and editorial handling process

PREreview and CRNEUR conducted these 5 cases at a rate of approximately one a month from 04/2023 to 10/2023 (Table 2). During this period, the authors of five randomly selected manuscripts that were not desk rejected by the handling editor were invited (i.e., that fitted the journal scope and were deemed by the editor to be strong enough to merit sending out for review). All of the authors whose manuscripts were invited to take part in the Live Review process agreed and provided consent to take part in the process (see Declarations section). Then their preprint was evaluated during the PREreview Live Review session as described above. Then the final published preprint review was integrated into the journal workflow for the handling editor to make their decision. Three different CRNEUR editors served as handling editors for the five submitted manuscripts that made up the PREreview cases. Because we aimed to conduct a case every one to two months within the editorial handling year 2023, manuscripts from those submitted in the months surrounding the planned Live Review session were selected, which depended on the submissions available at that time. Of the five manuscripts taking part in this exploratory study, all received a handling editor first decision of ‘revise’. The authors of all five manuscripts chose to revise and resubmit their manuscripts, and all five were ultimately given a final decision of ‘accept’ as a peer-reviewed publication in CRNEUR (see Tables 1, 2 and 3 for overview on the cases, timelines and links to the published manuscripts and their reviews). Data collection

We obtained data from several measures throughout the Live Review manuscript evaluation process and from the Editorial Manager workflow, including participation rates, editorial decision times and (reject, revise, accept) outcomes. We also encouraged LiveReview attendees to complete a survey after taking part in the sessions. Our data collection materials are provided in the Supplementary Materials section.

Control group data

Our comparative control group dataset was subject to a more traditional ‘standard’ review process. This comprised all the manuscripts that were submitted to CRNEUR in 2023 that were not desk rejected (e.g., were sent out for review) encompassing the timeframe of the five cases. As with the five Live Review cases, we obtained first and final editorial decision times directly from the Elsevier Editorial Manager workflow system. We also report participation and completion rates, including editorial outcomes. We were unable to obtain survey data from the standard submissions to CRNEUR during the same timeframe to compare to the Live Review survey results.

Statistical testing and quantitative comparisons

Given the small Live Review sample size (n = 5) and the unknown shape of the underlying distribution from which they are sampled, statistical testing was deemed inappropriate. Therefore, we report central tendency medians and variability using quartiles and Inter Quartile Range (IQR). Comparisons between the Live Review sample data and those for the standard review manuscripts (n = 27) are based on overlap of variability quantified by the IQR.

Results

General approach for the five cases

We conducted 5 Live Review sessions during 2023, approximately one per 1–2 months. During the piloting period, journal editors were asked to nominate manuscripts for Live Review which they had not desk rejected and could be sent out for regular peer review. Subsequently, authors were contacted to obtain consent that their manuscript could take part in the Live Review process and this exploratory study. All invited authors agreed to take part, and for Cases 2–5 some of the authors joined the session. In the 2–3 weeks before the scheduled Live Review a recruitment campaign was started, with reviewer invitations to participate. This involved contacting potential reviewers using the standard CRNEUR reviewer recruitment process in the Elsevier Editorial Manager portal, posting the upcoming Live Review session on the PREreview communications channels (social media and newsletters) and advertising the session to relevant scientific communities of experts (e.g., to the community on Advances and Perspectives in Auditory Neuroscience, APAN). Those that registered for participation were directed to the online preprint of the manuscript, asked to read it carefully and to join the Live Review session at the scheduled time.

The scheduled Live Review sessions occurred online via videoconferencing, facilitated by two PREreview team members. The facilitators welcomed the attendees and explained that they were attending a Live Review session as part of an exploratory study being conducted with CRNEUR. They also informed the attendees that they could leave the session at any time and could unmute their microphone and/or turn on their camera to join the discussion or decide to anonymously work on the online structured peer review document. If the authors were in attendance, the PREreview facilitators asked them to avoid joining the discussion unless a question was posed to them directly that they were comfortable answering. The PREreview facilitators then introduced the online structured peer review document and asked all the attendees taking part in reviewing the manuscript to sign a roll call – anonymously if that was their preference. Then the chairs led the review session, allowing the reviewers a few minutes at a time to work to populate the main sections of the online structured review document. The session attendees discussed the manuscript amongst themselves during the 90-min session, sometimes asking the authors a question. After the Live Review session completed, one to three of the attendees took part in finalizing the review notes. The review was published on the PREreview.org website, with individuals having the option to sign the review with their real name or a unique pseudonym. Summary data is shown in Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4. Case 1

The first manuscript for the exploratory study was an original research report contributed by Bianco and colleagues [26], entitled “Implicit auditory memory in older listeners: From encoding to 6-month retention.” (Table 1; Case 1). The process was as described above, with the following additional notes or differences. This was one of our two Hybrid Cases whereby the handling editor also invited and assigned two reviewers via the standard review process, which were conducted in parallel to the Live Review. The authors were interested in but were unable to attend the Live Review session. An editor observing the session anecdotally noted that the reviewers for this session appeared comfortable unmuting their microphones and some also chose to stay visible by leaving their camera turned on during the entire discussion session. The handling editor for this case noted that there was good correspondence with the Live Review and the standard review comments, which informed their first ‘major revision’ decision (summarized in Table 4). The authors relied on both sets of comments to revise and resubmit their manuscript, which after a secondary minor revision was accepted.

Case 2

The second manuscript for the exploratory study was an original research report contributed by Mueller and colleagues [27] entitled "DREADD-mediated amygdala activation is sufficient to induce anxiety-like responses in young nonhuman primates." (Table 1; Case 2). The process was as described above, with the following additional notes or differences. The authors attended the Live Review session as a single group joining from a conference room at their institution. One observing editor anecdotally noted that with the authors present, there seemed to be more reluctance by the attending reviewers to unmute their microphone or show themselves on camera. Instead, for this session, the reviewers opted to mostly work quietly to populate the online document. There was some verbal discussion with the authors, led by the PREreview team asking the authors if they wanted to respond to some of the questions being raised on the document. After the review session, two of the attendees took part in finalizing the review, which was published on PREreview.org with one reviewer naming themselves and the other opting for a pseudonym (Green Eagle; Table 1).

Case 3

The third manuscript for the exploratory study was an original research report contributed by Espejo and David [28] entitled “A sparse code for natural sound context in auditory cortex.” (Table 1; Case 3). The lead author attended the Live Review session and some of the discussion attendees took part by showing themselves on camera or discussing the manuscript with the camera turned off. Five session attendees worked on the online working document during the 90-min session. After the review session, one of the attendees led in finalizing the review.

Discussion

Structured Live Reviews, as an open and collaborative review process, hold promise for addressing the speed, rigor, transparency and reviewer incentivization and training concerns raised with the more traditional peer review process. This Live Review exploratory study was conducted in collaboration between PREreview and the journal CRNEUR. A community-based Live Review approach was implemented with five manuscripts and integrated into the editorial workflow of the journal CRNEUR. In evaluating the process, we obtained comparison data from articles submitted to the journal during the same year that followed the journal’s standard review process. This allowed us to evaluate participation rates and editorial outcomes such as first and final editorial decision times. Although interpretation is limited by the small sample size, we found that reviewer completion rates and editorial decision times were similar between the Live Review and the standard review process. Participants who completed the survey generally considered the Live Review to hold merit on several questions including the process being rigorous, respectful and well guided. Next, we discuss the key observations and suggest how the Live Review process could be further developed, evaluated and sustained.

Participation and review completion rates

We found that approximately half of the participants that registered for the Live Review sessions attended, with one third of those taking an active role in completing the online published review (Fig. 2). Our median Live Review attendance was similar to the median number of reviewers that CRNEUR editors needed to invite for review for traditionally reviewed manuscripts (7 versus 6, respectively). The Live Review and standard review processes showed similar review completion rates from those, respectively, attending or invited (33%, Table 2). Given that the review session needed to take place live and online at a fixed time, the scheduled times may have been inconvenient for some individuals. Most of our surveyed participants were from the USA (Fig. 3). To achieve the optimal number of reviewers for a given Live Review session, organizers will need to consider how many reviewers they would like to have complete the review process (e.g., 2–3) and account for participant attendance attrition. We recommend doubling that number to approximate the number of reviewers attending and then possibly doubling it again during the recruitment campaign to obtain sufficient registrations to ensure a 2–3 reviewer completion rate, as we were able to achieve (Table 2). To obtain 2–3 completed reviews, it might thus be necessary to attain 8–12 registrants for the Live Review. The optimal recruitment numbers may change in the future if this type of review becomes more common.

Editorial first and final decision times

We compared the time (in days) from article submission to the initial and final editorial decision for the Live Review cases to editorial decision times for the standard review manuscripts in CRNEUR during the same year. We observed that the Inter-Quartile Ranges (IQR) overlapped in decision times, suggesting that the Live Review can be as expedient as the standard review process in terms of editorial decision times, neither faster nor slower. First decision times are affected by the speed of the journal’s initial triage process, the editor’s decision-making time and the time to obtain a sufficient number of completed reviews for the editor to make a first decision. Final decision times will include the time the authors took to revise their manuscript (if a revision decision was made) and to resubmit it. The Live Review session appeared to have similar editorial decision-making and author revision turnaround times as the standard review process. Interestingly, the comparisons differed substantially for other factors. For instance, we typically set the recruitment campaign time to 1 month to allow sufficient time for advertising the sessions and for individuals to register and read the paper prior to the Live Review session. This can be longer than the editorial recruitment time needed to invite reviewers (which in our experience is typically 1–2 weeks). Presuming reviewers take a similar amount of time to read the paper under both processes, the speed advantage of the Live Review session is in having the review conducted and completed during the 90-min Live Review session. After the Live Review session, the review could be completed and published online within 2 weeks. Integrating that review within the journal workflow only needs a few days to make it available to the handling editor. Therefore, some facets of the Live Review process can be slower or quicker than the standard review, and, as our comparison data suggest, the process can be similar in terms of decision times to the standard review process. It is also possible that our piloting process could be made more efficient. The longest delay we faced was with Case 5 session needing to be re-advertised and rescheduled because too few registered participants attended the initial session (Table 2). We recommend that during registration the organizers query which of those participating plan to review and the confidence with which they are likely to attend.

Live Review participant impressions

Several components of the Live Review approach were scored on the high end of our scale by the attendees taking the survey, although we recognize the low sample size and the possibility that the low survey completion rate (30%) may have introduced a self-selection bias (Fig. 4). We were unable to obtain survey data from those involved in the standard review process as a point of reference. Nonetheless, the Live Review participant survey results were interesting, showing high median marks of 4 or 5 on a scale of 1 (‘Strongly Disagree’) to 5 (‘Strongly Agree’) in response to the Survey questions available in the Supplementary Materials. These included questions on the utility of the experience, the training component, reviewers being constructive, the review being well guided and respectful, time efficient and having scientific rigor. As such, these survey results are a snapshot of the impressions from some of the Live Review session participants provided and cannot be directly compared to other peer review experiences. We recommend that further surveys are obtained from those involved in open review and standard review scenarios. Therefore, we provide our study materials as Supplementary Materials for further empirical testing.

Structured peer review implementation

Despite its importance, peer review often lacks formal training and structured mentorship. Researchers may also be unaware of the growing set of peer review tools that are available to help reviewers to showcase and develop their academic critical-thinking skills [3, 9, 23]. Scholars are often expected to learn to review by reviewing. Without guidance, this can lead to inconsistent review quality and organization of the review report, lengthening the review process for everyone involved. Reviewers may also waste time carefully proofreading manuscripts for typographical errors, if, for example, they are not provided guidance on what to focus on in their review. While some journals and organizations are beginning to provide reviewer training, or to apply a formal structure to their peer review process (often in the form of guiding questions, documents or tools), structured peer review approaches are not yet standardized or widespread across disciplines or journal workflows [3, 9, 13, 25].

Each of the five cases implemented a structured peer review approach developed by the PREreview team. This focused on a few key questions that the reviewers could work on during the 90-min review session (Supplementary Materials). Our approach is not dissimilar to other structured peer review approaches [25], although we opted to streamline the sets of questions asked in the review to focus on: 1) summary overview of the manuscript; 2) manuscript strengths; 3) major recommendations; and 4) minor recommendations. The structured review document offered a live platform for reviewer comments and notes to be placed in the appropriate sections of the online working document. The document also assisted the session organizers in guiding the discussion. The organizers typically allocated 5–10 min per key focus area for reviewers to discuss, ask authors questions if they chose and to add their notes to the online document. Streamlining the structured peer review document was an active decision on our part to ensure that the participants did not enter the session feeling overwhelmed by the large number of questions, some of which were optional. Although our initial structured peer review document focused on original manuscripts, the final Case 5 was a review manuscript, for which the structured peer review document worked as intended with only minor modifications relevant for review articles.

Internet access problems

A potential issue we were concerned about with the online sessions was if attendees’ internet connection was unstable or they were unable to work on the online document, e.g., if the online document was blocked by the researcher’s institution. These concerns did not present as issues for any of the five cases but should be considered when implementing online Live Reviews. We recommend sharing the structured peer review document with the key questions in advance to those participating in the discussion, with the review being submitted to the editors as a standard review if the participant cannot take part in all or some of the Live Review working session.

Recruitment campaign strategy

Journal editors tend to invite reviewers with expertise in the topic of the manuscript. However, in designing this study, we made a conscious decision not to target the recruitment campaign solely towards more experienced reviewers. Instead, we focused on involving a wider range of attendees with differing experience. We advertised broadly via the PREreview social media channels and supplemented this with editors recruiting reviewers via the database resources in the Elsevier Editorial Manager system. During each Live Review session, attendees were asked to add their name to a ‘roll-call’ section of the collaborative notes document online indicating their name, affiliation, location and preferred pronouns. This information was not distributed beyond the authors involved in this exploratory study. It was only used to contact individuals that expressed interest in completing the final review document or for contacting previous reviewers to evaluate a resubmitted revised manuscript. The survey information indicated there was a good mix of both more and less experienced reviewers taking part in the Live Review sessions, with all attendees in each session indicating they had some experience of completing a review for a journal with a more traditional peer-review process. Notably, 92% of the attendees reported not having previously reviewed a preprint. Therefore, for those involved in this study, the Live Review process was new to the vast majority. Moreover, the PREreview discussion leads (in our case PREreview staff) were able to identify reviewers who could act in the role of ‘chairs’ of the discussion session. These ‘chairs’ led the completion of the online review document within the two-week completion timeframe with other reviewers interesting in completing the review.

Incentivizing reviewers

For incentivizing reviewers [2, 6, 15], platforms such as ORCID allow review reports to be publicly shared under open licenses and assigned persistent identifiers (e.g., DOIs), enabling reviewers to claim credit. Public attribution is not universally desirable, because concerns about safety, power dynamics and perceptions of rigor remain. Accordingly, some open peer review infrastructures, including PREreview, prioritize reviewer agency by offering flexible options for signed, ORCID-linked or pseudonymous contributions, while maintaining the benefits of openly available peer review. In our study, the act of taking part in the review being published online was intended to encourage the reviewers to work towards a high-quality review output, much like a published academic manuscript. Each review was published on PREreview.org with a DOI and CC BY 4.0 License for Live Review participants to be recognized for their contributions.

Author and editor presence during the sessions

For all cases, we offered the authors the opportunity to join the Live Review session to observe and to answer any questions they were comfortable addressing that were posed to them by the reviewers. Possibly because of this guidance and the facilitation of the session surrounding the structured peer review documents, we did not have any issues with authors trying to bias the session discussion or feeling compelled to rebut every raised issue. The observing editors noted that the interactions remained professional, and the authors only answered questions that were posed to them. The editors also noted that there were some sessions (such as Case 2) where the presence of the authors may have influenced the reviewers to spend more time silently and with their cameras turned off working on the online document. Nonetheless, that was a well-attended session and two reviewers from it worked to complete the review (Case 2; Table 2). One of the manuscript authors in the survey noted: “I greatly appreciated the uniqueness of this process in allowing an interactive discussion between authors, reviewers, and editors, as well as inviting researchers of all backgrounds to participate. Although I attended this as an author of the manuscript, I would be keen on taking the role of a reviewer in the future.”. Journal workflow integration

After the online review was published, CRNEUR journal staff were able to quickly add the review to the Elsevier Editorial Manager workflow for the handling editor. The main issue reported by the editors was the difficulty in re-contacting reviewers who took part in the review process, because, if they had not been recruited through the journal’s system, they were not formally registered as reviewers in the journal editorial manager system. To resolve this issue, either the Live Review reviewers could be recruited exclusively through the journal’s reviewer recruitment system, or reviewers recruited through a public campaign during the Live Review session could be asked if they are willing to provide contact information for the handling editor to follow up, if necessary, on a manuscript resubmission. At the beginning of each Live Review session, we conducted a voluntary ‘roll call’ where individuals could note their name, institution and contact details to be contacted by editors to follow up on their review, if necessary. This information was only available to the research team.

Next generation journal clubs

While traditional academic journal clubs are not designed to produce formal peer review, initiatives such as the Preprint Club, PREreview.org and ASAPbioCrowd Preprint Review are experimenting with ways to translate journal club discussions into timely, constructive and openly available feedback for preprint authors. PREreview-organized Live Review events extend the journal club model beyond institutional boundaries by convening researchers from around the world in facilitated online meetings. These community Live Review “journal clubs” harness the rigor and collaborative discussion typical of academic journal clubs while explicitly focusing on the goal of completing a structured preprint review that is shared publicly and available to authors.

Scaling up Live Reviews

A central challenge in adopting and evaluating Live Reviews at scale is moving from a small number of facilitated pilots to routine use, while reducing reliance on intensive mediation by PREreview staff. This exploratory study demonstrated that identifying experienced reviewers to guide discussions was feasible, suggesting that facilitation capacity itself is not a primary barrier. One promising pathway to scale LiveReviews is engaging existing communities—such as research groups and journal clubs—that already meet to discuss preprints. To support sustainable scaling, PREreview has invested in capacity-building approaches, including training community members to lead Live Reviews through programs such as PREreview Champions. Since this exploratory study inception, PREreview has organized an average of one Live Review per month, primarily in partnership with JMIR Publications (JMIRxMed, JMIRxBio and JMIRxPsy). Moreover, to date the PREreview platform hosts more than 1,600 published preprint reviews across 31 preprint servers, contributed by over 3,900 reviewers and 45 review clubs. Over the past two years, these events have had a median attendance of 13 participants and have resulted in more than 20 review reports being incorporated into journal editorial workflows. This bodes well for efforts to scale Live Reviews and integrate them into journal workflows, demonstrating substantial community engagement in collaborative preprint review beyond PREreview-facilitated events.

Study limitations

The main limitation of this exploratory study is the small sample size of the five manuscripts (cases) that underwent the Live Review process. Another limitation is the potential self-selection of survey responders, given that we only had a 30% survey response from our attendees. We declared limitations above that the Live Review scheduling can be biased towards western hemisphere time zones and have noted the participant dropout rates in participation.

Conclusions

This exploratory study reports on a community Live Review approach that was piloted with five manuscripts as cases, evaluated along several dimensions to standard review papers within the same journal. The exploratory study data are limited by the small sample size but appear to compare favorably to data from standard review papers in the journal. Additional development, implementation and evaluation with larger sample sizes is required, and we discussed how approaches such as these could be improved, scaled and sustained to address issues raised with the traditional peer review process.
