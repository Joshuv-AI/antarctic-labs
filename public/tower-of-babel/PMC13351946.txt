The impact of COVID-19 on spine surgery: temporal trends in complications and patient-reported outcomes across a statewide cohort
Authors: Ali Mehaidli, Jianhui Hu, Richard Easton, Ahmad Almaat, Gary George, Alqasim Elnaggar, Victor Chang, Doris Tong, Lonni Schultz, David Nerenz, Kari Jarabek, Jamie Myers, Nicole Schaub, Bashar Jawich, Jad Khalil, Kevin Taliaferro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic substantially disrupted spine surgery worldwide, with widespread deferral of elective procedures, shifts in patient selection, and reallocation of perioperative resources. Existing studies have produced conflicting findings on whether complication rates and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) worsened during the pandemic, and most are limited to single centers or short timeframes. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on patient characteristics, adverse events (AEs), and PROs following cervical and lumbar spine surgery across four prospectively defined pandemic phases in a statewide multicenter cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the Michigan Spine Surgery Improvement Collaborative (MSSIC) to analyze patients undergoing lumbar or cervical spine surgery during pre-COVID (January 2019–February 2020), onset-COVID (March 2020–September 2020), active-COVID (October 2020–February 2022), and post-COVID (March 2022–December 2022) phases. Patient demographics, surgical characteristics, 30- and 90-day AEs, and PROs were compared across cohorts using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables.ResultsAmong 47,831 patients (33,532 lumbar; 14,299 cervical), demographics remained stable across phases. Lumbar onset-COVID patients had higher durotomy, urinary retention, and return-to-operating room (OR) rates, while cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak and 30-day readmissions declined during active-COVID. Cervical surgery showed increased dysphagia during active-COVID and higher return-to-OR and readmissions during onset and post-COVID, with cerebrovascular accident (CVA) and urinary retention rates decreasing over time. PROs were consistent, though lumbar patients more often achieved back pain minimal clinically important difference (MCID) during onset-COVID. Cervical patients reported higher 1-year satisfaction during onset, active, and post-COVID compared with pre-COVID. Functional recovery diverged: lumbar return-to-work fell at onset but stabilized, while cervical return-to-work stayed steady and improved post-COVID. Readmission causes shifted, with pulmonary complications rising initially then declining, while electrolyte, pharmacologic, and stroke-related etiologies decreased progressively.ConclusionsSpine surgery outcomes remained largely stable through the COVID-19 pandemic. Although certain complications temporarily increased during onset-COVID, outcomes normalized as systems adapted.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among 47,831 patients undergoing cervical or lumbar spine surgery across a statewide multicenter registry, the onset-coronavirus disease (COVID) period was associated with transient increases in select perioperative complications, including durotomy, urinary retention, 90-day return to OR, and 90-day readmission, particularly in lumbar patients.

• These complication trends largely stabilized to or below pre-pandemic levels during the active and post-COVID phases, while patient-reported outcomes (PROs) were preserved or modestly improved across all pandemic phases.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior single-center and national-database studies have produced conflicting findings on whether spine surgery outcomes worsened during the COVID-19 pandemic, and most are limited to short pre/post comparisons that do not account for the evolving phases of the pandemic.

• This statewide, multicenter analysis evaluates outcomes across four prospectively defined pandemic phases (pre-, onset, active, and post-COVID) anchored to both biological pandemic milestones and Michigan-specific policy events, demonstrating that early disruptions in surgical complications were transient and that PROs remained resilient throughout the pandemic.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Spine surgical care can be sustained at high quality during major public health disruptions when systems implement rigorous infection control, careful patient selection, and integrated telemedicine follow-up.

• These findings support continued investment in adaptive perioperative protocols and registry-based quality monitoring to prepare for future pandemics and other prolonged disruptions to elective surgical care.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic significantly disrupted health care services globally, with recommendations for and subsequent postponement and cancellation of elective surgical procedures (1,2). Early in 2020, healthcare systems worldwide were forced to restructure surgical schedules, reallocating critical resources and limiting patient exposure to the virus, which resulted in notable delays and cancellations of non-urgent surgeries (1,3-5). Estimates predicted approximately 28 million elective operations being deferred or canceled globally during the initial 12-week peak of pandemic-related shutdowns, creating a considerable backlog of surgical cases and raising concerns regarding the potential adverse impact of delayed care on patient outcomes (3).

During the peak of the initial COVID-19 surge, most institutions suspended or limited elective spine procedures, prioritizing only urgent and emergent surgeries involving severe neurologic deficits, spinal cord compression, acute trauma, tumors, or cauda equina syndrome (5,6). As a result, spine surgeons faced unprecedented challenges balancing the need for timely surgical interventions with the heightened risk of viral transmission and limited healthcare resources. At the onset of the pandemic, there were marked reductions in spine surgery case volumes, particularly from March through May 2020, with procedure rates closely correlating with local COVID-19 case trajectories (2,6-10). By late 2020 and into 2021, spine centers worldwide gradually began to restore elective services and address the accumulated backlog, implementing new safety protocols to safely resume routine operations (4). Evidence from multicenter studies demonstrated that elective spine surgery could safely recommence without triggering significant COVID-19 outbreaks when strict infection prevention measures were observed (4).

Despite these adaptations, considerable uncertainty persisted regarding the net impact of COVID-19 disruptions on surgical outcomes in spine patients. Early studies yielded varied findings. A study by Louie et al. during the initial COVID-19 surge reported no significant difference in postoperative complications for urgent spine surgeries compared to pre-pandemic historical controls (11). Likewise, a study by Shao et al. using data from a tertiary medical center indicated that postoperative outcomes remained largely unchanged despite significant changes in surgical volume and patient selection during the pandemic restructuring (12).

In contrast, other analyses raised concerns about potentially worsened outcomes during the pandemic. A comprehensive study from Song et al. using American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS-NSQIP) data involving over 27,000 lumbar fusion cases found significantly increased rates of perioperative morbidity, including pneumonia, deep vein thrombosis, and sepsis, in patients operated on during 2020 compared with 2019 (13). Another study by Deng et al. echoed increased rates of perioperative pneumonia, respiratory failure, pulmonary embolism, and sepsis (14). Beyond complication rates, larger-scale national data have also pointed to a meaningful shift in the underlying patient population. In a nationwide German cohort of more than 200,000 patients hospitalized for spine pathologies, Dengler et al. demonstrated that the proportion of high-frailty patients increased during the pandemic, with high-frailty patients accumulating disproportionately large increases in comorbidity burden compared with low-frailty patients (15). This shift toward a more frail and comorbid surgical population provides important context for interpreting any observed change in perioperative complication rates during the pandemic. Additional studies also explored the impact of the pandemic on patient-reported outcomes (PROs). For example, a study by Lin et al. noted less postoperative PRO improvement in patients undergoing spine surgery during the pandemic, possibly reflecting decreased availability of postoperative rehabilitation and increased psychosocial stressors during the pandemic (16).

These mixed results highlight the need for further investigation into how pandemic-related changes influenced spine surgery outcomes across different time periods and geographic locations. Against this backdrop, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of a statewide spine surgery registry, assessing variations in patient demographics, surgical complications, readmission rates, reoperation rates, and PROs across four distinct temporal phases of the COVID-19 pandemic: pre-COVID (prior to March 2020), onset COVID (initial lockdown and disruption phase), active COVID (subsequent waves with adaptation through early 2022), and post-COVID (stabilization period in late 2022). We hypothesized that the early disruptions associated with COVID-19 might temporarily increase complication rates and negatively impact PROs, but that subsequent institutional protocol adaptations would mitigate these effects over time. By leveraging multicenter, longitudinal registry data, this study aims to provide a robust assessment of how the COVID-19 pandemic influenced spine surgery outcomes and to contextualize these findings within the growing international literature on surgical care during major public health crises. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-1-0049/rc).

Methods

Study design and data source

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Ethical approval and informed consent were waived because the study used only de-identified data from the Michigan Spine Surgery Improvement Collaborative (MSSIC) registry and was therefore exempt from Institutional Review Board review. This was a retrospective cohort study utilizing data from the MSSIC (https://www.mssic.org) (17), a statewide, multi-center quality improvement initiative funded by Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan and Blue Care Network that prospectively collects detailed clinical and patient-reported data on spine surgeries performed at participating hospitals across Michigan. Over the course of our study period (January 2019–December 2022), MSSIC encompassed approximately 29 participating hospitals and 2 ambulatory surgery centers spanning academic, community, and rural settings throughout the state, capturing both neurosurgical and orthopedic spine practices and representing the majority of high-volume spine surgery centers in Michigan (18). Participation is voluntary, and the registry is therefore not an exhaustive census of every spine procedure performed in the state, but it provides what we believe to be the most comprehensive multi-institutional, prospectively collected dataset of degenerative cervical and lumbar spine surgeries available in Michigan. The MSSIC registry includes deidentified information on demographics, baseline health status, surgical characteristics, postoperative complications, and longitudinal outcomes. For this study, we analyzed lumbar and cervical spine surgery cases conducted between January 2019 and December 2022. In addition to MSSIC data, we incorporated publicly available statewide data on COVID-19 hospitalizations in Michigan to inform the temporal framework of the pandemic (Figures 1,2) (19-22).

Patient selection

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they underwent lumbar or cervical spine surgery at a participating MSSIC institution between January 2019 and December 2022. All patients had to have complete information on surgery type, date of surgery, and relevant postoperative outcome measures. Patients were excluded if they lacked key demographic data or underwent procedures outside of the defined lumbar or cervical categories. Based on surgery date, each case was assigned to one of four COVID-19 timeframes: (I) pre-COVID (January 2019–February 2020), representing baseline operations; (II) onset-COVID (March 2020–September 2020), encompassing the initial pandemic wave and associated lockdowns; (III) active-COVID (October 2020–February 2022), corresponding to peak surges, variants, and vaccination efforts; and (IV) post-COVID (March 2022–December 2022), a period of relative stabilization in hospitalizations and surgical volumes (Figure 1). The final analytic sample included 33,532 lumbar cases and 14,299 cervical cases.

Exposure and outcome measures

The primary exposure variable was the COVID-19 timeframe during which the surgery was performed, categorized into four distinct phases as defined above. Primary outcomes included both clinical adverse events (AEs) and PROs. AEs tracked within 90 days of surgery included urinary retention, surgical site infection (SSI), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak, durotomy, myocardial infarction (MI), cerebrovascular accident (CVA), weakness, dysphagia, 90-day return to operating room (OR), and readmissions at 30 and 90 days. PROs included Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System-Physical Function (PROMIS-PF), EuroQol 5 Dimensions (EQ-5D), 0–10 leg and back pain numeric rating scores, and return to work status, assessed at baseline, 90 days, and 1 year postoperatively. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds were used to distinguish PRO improvements where applicable.

Covariates

Covariates included a broad range of patient-level factors grouped into several domains: sociodemographic variables (e.g., age, sex, race, education), baseline pain and functional status scores, comorbidities [e.g., diabetes, smoking status, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class], and surgery-related factors (e.g., clinical presentation, surgery duration, length of stay). The MSSIC registry’s standardized data collection and definitions ensured consistency of covariate records across time periods and institutions.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a univariate analysis to compare patient characteristics and surgical outcomes across four COVID-19 timeframes, stratified by surgery type (lumbar vs. cervical). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all covariates and outcomes, which were grouped into six domains: sociodemographics, baseline scores, other baseline factors and comorbidities, admission/surgery-related factors, AEs, and PROs. Readmission reasons were also explored. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Continuous variables were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed data or Kruskal-Wallis rank tests for non-parametric distributions. Statistical significance was defined as a P value <0.05.

Results

A total of 47,831 patients were included, with 33,532 undergoing lumbar spine surgery and 14,299 undergoing cervical spine surgery. Demographics were stable across cohorts, with no clinically meaningful differences in mean age or sex distribution. Baseline comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, smoking, and ASA classification were similar across time periods, though lumbar patients had slightly higher rates of ASA > II during the onset-COVID phase (56.1%, P=0.04) (Table 1).

Lumbar procedures

For lumbar cases, perioperative complication patterns shifted subtly across the pandemic. Urinary retention peaked during onset-COVID (4.57%) compared with pre-COVID (4.10%), active-COVID (3.04%), and post-COVID (3.34%) (P<0.001). SSI rates declined modestly during active COVID (1.87% vs. 2.18% pre-COVID, P=0.31), while CSF leaks decreased in both onset (0.8%) and active phases (0.6%) compared with pre-COVID (1.09%, P=0.001). Durotomy was most common during onset-COVID (5.84%) before gradually declining in active (5.03%) and post-COVID (4.77%) phases (P=0.03). Similarly, 90-day return to OR was highest during onset (5.3%) relative to pre-COVID (4.8%), active (4.0%), and post-COVID (3.4%) (P<0.001). Readmission patterns were mixed: 30-day readmissions fell to their lowest level during active COVID (3.72% vs. 4.40% pre-COVID, P=0.047), while 90-day readmissions briefly spiked at onset (7.84%) before dropping below pre-COVID rates in the active (6.42%) and post-COVID (6.83%) phases (P=0.02) (Table 1).

Cervical procedures

In cervical surgery, dysphagia up-trended in the onset COVID phase (3.30%) and peaked during the active-COVID period (4.18%) compared with pre-COVID (2.87%). It was then noted to decrease in the post-COVID phase (3.47%) (P=0.007). CVAs were more common pre- and post-COVID (4.7% and 5.25%) than during the active phase (3.89%, P=0.03). Urinary retention rates declined steadily over the pandemic, reaching a low of 1.97% during active COVID (P<0.001). Ninety-day return to OR rose during onset (4.9%) but fell again in post-COVID patients (2.5%, P<0.001). Compared with the pre-COVID phase, durotomy peaked in onset-COVID (1.68%) before falling in subsequent active-COVID and post-COVID phases (0.98% and 0.89%) (P=0.048). Readmission trends paralleled lumbar cases: 90-day readmission was highest at onset (7.12%) and remained elevated post-COVID (6.61%) compared with pre-COVID (5.26%, P=0.009), while 30-day readmissions did not differ significantly across timeframes (Table 1).

PROs

PROs remained relatively stable. In lumbar patients, more individuals reached the MCID for back pain at 1 year during onset-COVID (73.5%), active-COVID (72.5%), and post-COVID (71.8%) compared with pre-COVID (69%, P=0.007). Among cervical patients, satisfaction rates at 1 year were higher during all COVID phases (83.8%, 82.8%, and 81.8% for onset, active, and post, respectively) relative to pre-COVID (79.2%, P=0.01). Functional recovery showed some disruption: lumbar patients were less likely to return to work within 90 days during onset-COVID (56.4% vs. ~65% in other phases, P=0.01), whereas cervical patients’ 1-year return-to-work rates improved in the post-COVID phase (86.2%, P=0.02) (Table 1).

Readmission etiologies

Analysis of readmission causes revealed several shifts. Among lumbar patients, pulmonary complications rose significantly during onset-COVID compared with pre-COVID (11.9% vs. 7.6%, P=0.044). Pharmacologic causes dropped sharply at onset-COVID (0.3% vs. 3.7% pre-COVID, P=0.005), and electrolyte abnormalities also declined over time (14.5% pre-, 14.2% onset- vs. 12.0% active- and 8.0% post-COVID, P=0.005). For cervical patients, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-confirmed new stroke decreased markedly from 2.3% pre-COVID to 0.3% during active COVID (P=0.04). Readmissions due to urinary retention and electrolyte disturbances also fell across the pandemic (P=0.004, P=0.006). Renal complications dipped during the onset, spiked back to pre-COVID levels in the active phase, then declined again post-COVID (9.9% pre vs. 5.5% post, P=0.03). CSF leak-related readmissions increased from 1.8% pre-COVID to 4.1% at onset, then decreased post-COVID (2.5%, P=0.003). Finally, cardiac causes trended downward across phases, except for a brief peak during active COVID (8.5%, P=0.03).

Discussion

Our findings highlight the significant but transient impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on spine surgery outcomes, particularly during the onset phase. The disruption caused by the initial surge of COVID-19 was associated with higher rates of perioperative complications, such as urinary retention, durotomy, and 90-day return to OR rates among lumbar patients, as well as increased 90-day return to OR rates for cervical procedures. These elevated complication rates may be explained by multiple factors, including deferred elective procedures, increased surgical complexity due to the prioritization of urgent cases, and limitations in perioperative resources during the initial COVID-19 surge (4,5,11,12). A complementary mechanism is the documented shift in the underlying spine surgery population during the pandemic toward higher frailty and greater comorbidity burden, as shown in the nationwide German cohort by Dengler et al. (15). Even when standard demographics appear stable, as in our cohort, an increase in frailty and comorbidity load could plausibly contribute to the early rise in durotomy, urinary retention, return to OR, and 90-day readmission that we observed during the onset-COVID phase, particularly in lumbar patients. The observed peak in return to OR rates during the onset-COVID phase similarly reflects this shift in patient selection and case prioritization. Subsequently, the stabilization of complication rates during active and post-COVID phases demonstrates the efficacy of adaptive practices in maintaining surgical quality and patient safety during this major public health disruption. Finally, reduced readmission rates attributed to pharmacological, cardiac, and electrolyte causes across COVID phases may suggest heightened patient reluctance to return to overwhelmed hospital systems and potentially contract the virus in conjunction with potential improvements in perioperative medical management and stricter patient monitoring.

The maintenance of PROs and satisfaction scores across both lumbar and cervical cohorts further reinforce the effectiveness of these adaptive healthcare measures. Patient satisfaction and functional recovery, indicated by higher rates of MCID achievement in lumbar back pain and improved return-to-work rates for cervical patients, were preserved or enhanced during COVID-19 periods compared to pre-pandemic levels. Despite the initial increases in complications, these findings suggest that patient-centered outcomes remained resilient to pandemic-related disruptions, potentially due to heightened surgical scrutiny, optimized patient selection criteria, and rigorous perioperative management practices enacted during the COVID-19 phases. Additionally, telemedicine emerged as a powerful tool during the pandemic, providing enhanced opportunities for consistent postoperative follow-up, which may have contributed positively to long-term patient satisfaction and recovery (2,3,11,23,24). These results differ from those of Lin et al., who observed reduced postoperative improvements in EQ-5D during the pandemic (16). In our cohort, EQ-5D outcomes for lumbar and cervical patients did not differ significantly across COVID phases at 90 days or 1 year.

More importantly, patient selection likely played a critical role in maintaining outcome stability. With elective procedures postponed, patients who proceeded to surgery early in the pandemic were predominantly those with urgent or emergent conditions requiring timely intervention (3-6,25). As a result, the pandemic cohorts likely included patients with less deferrable conditions with more defined surgical indications.

Limitations

While this study provides important insights, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational retrospective design does not allow for interpretation of direct causal inference between the pandemic and observed outcomes. Unmeasured variables, including changes in surgical and anesthetic practices or variations in patient adherence to postoperative protocols, could confound our findings. Second, our post-COVID timeframe reflects a stabilization phase rather than a definitive end to the pandemic; COVID-19 continues to evolve and influence healthcare practices even beyond our study period. Third, as our data derive from a single-state collaborative registry, the generalizability of our findings is limited. The pandemic response varied substantially across U.S. states and internationally, with meaningful differences in surgical volume restrictions, triage protocols, vaccine rollout timing, and resource allocation. Our results therefore reflect the Michigan experience and may not extend to regions that experienced more severe or prolonged disruption, fewer resources, or different policy responses. To partially address this, we deliberately defined our four COVID-19 phases using a combination of national pandemic milestones (initial surge, dominant variants, vaccine availability) and Michigan-specific events (state of emergency declaration, executive order suspending non-essential activities, statewide hospitalization curves), so that the temporal framework reflects both biological pandemic stages and the local policy environment in which these surgeries actually took place (19-22). Even so, formal multi-region or multi-registry analyses will be needed before the trends observed here can be assumed to apply elsewhere. Fourth, this analysis was limited to univariate comparisons, which do not account for potential confounding interactions between variables; a multivariate approach would be necessary to isolate independent predictors of outcomes but was not feasible due to the low incidence of certain AEs and the large number of covariates relative to events, which limited statistical power and model stability. Finally, low incidence rates of certain complications may limit the statistical power to detect subtle differences between cohorts.

Despite these limitations, our study provides robust, multicenter, longitudinal data evaluating the pandemic’s impact on spine surgery outcomes, complementing existing single-center and national database analyses. The preservation and even improvement of outcomes observed in our analysis support the idea that surgical systems can rapidly adapt to maintain high standards of care during widespread disruptions, provided that appropriate measures such as rigorous infection control, careful patient selection, and telemedicine integration are implemented and sustained.

Conclusions

Overall, the onset-COVID period was associated with the greatest deviation in clinical outcomes. However, these trends largely stabilized during the active and post-COVID periods. Importantly, PROs did not deteriorate during the pandemic and in some cases improved, suggesting effective adaptation of surgical and perioperative care practices. Despite the challenges imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic, spine surgery outcomes across Michigan remained stable, with only modest variations in complications and consistently favorable patient-reported recovery.
