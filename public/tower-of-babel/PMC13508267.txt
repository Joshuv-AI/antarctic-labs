Background

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have been successfully isolated from diverse tissues including endometrial polyps, umbilical cord, menstrual blood, and bone marrow. These cells uniformly express characteristic surface markers such as CD90, CD73, and CD105, while showing low or no expression of CD45, CD34, CD14/CD11b, CD79α/CD19, and HLA-DR [1]. UC-MSCs, owing to their abundant sources, high accessibility, and low immunogenicity, serve as ideal seed cells for cell therapy products, providing novel strategies for medicine. Their multifunctional capacities in immunomodulation, multipotent differentiation, hematopoietic support, and tissue repair demonstrate significant therapeutic potential in regenerative medicine and autoimmune diseases [2].

However, the clinical application of UC-MSCs confronts heterogeneity challenges. The absence of standardized manufacturing protocols results in batch-to-batch variations in cell yield, viability, and differentiation potency, thereby compromising therapeutic reliability and consistency [3]. This functional heterogeneity is influenced by multiple factors including donor variability, isolation methods, and passage number: prolonged passaging can lead to phenotypic changes such as accelerated senescence, diminished differentiation capacity, and dysregulated immunomodulation, resulting in unstable therapeutic in vivo efficacy. Notably, different MSCs subpopulations defined by multiple markers (e.g., CD106, CD271, CD146, CD140a, SSEA-4, STRO-1) exhibit significantly divergent functional properties in immunomodulation, tissue repair, differentiation potential, and angiogenesis [4–8]. Our prior work identified a PDGFBhigh subpopulation with superior capacity for enhancing human hematopoietic stem cell engraftment, highlighting the clinical value of functionally specialized subsets [9].

The NOTCH signaling pathway is an evolutionarily conserved intercellular communication mechanism. Ligand-receptor binding triggers receptor cleavage, releasing the intracellular domain (NICD), which translocates into the nucleus to regulate the expression of target genes, thereby participating in cell fate determination and tissue homeostasis maintenance [10, 11]. This pathway is essential for hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), regulating their self-renewal, stemness maintenance, lineage commitment and differentiation (e.g., T cell development), regeneration under stress conditions, and the quiescence-activation switch [12–15]. In mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), NOTCH signaling promotes osteogenic differentiation (e.g., upregulating MSX2 through Jagged-1 activation) [16] and is involved in microenvironment construction. As key components of the HSC niche, MSCs indirectly affects hematopoietic support through NOTCH-mediated functional regulation [17, 18].

Although single-cell sequencing has been employed to dissect MSC heterogeneity, current studies suffer from three critical limitations: (1) restricted passage observation windows failing to capture dynamic heterogeneity; (2) unclear mechanisms linking functional subsets to specific culture passages; (3) a lack of cross-passage functional potential tracing. Consequently, there is an urgent need for a longitudinal single-cell resolution assessment of UC-MSCs during in vitro expansion. To address this, we implement cross-passage single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to systematically characterize heterogeneity dynamics in expanded UC-MSCs during clinical-grade cell culture. This study aims to elucidate the differences in cellular functions associated with the cultivation stage and to identify molecular signatures defining key functional subsets. Our findings will establish a theoretical framework for standardization of UC-MSC production and advance personalized treatment paradigms. Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The clinical application-grade umbilical cord-derived MSCs isolated from two umbilical cords were provided by Sinogene C&G Stem Cells Co., Ltd. UC-MSCs were cultured using an MSC serum-free medium kit (primary) (NC0103 + NC0103.S, Yocon, China). The cells were separately digested and passaged using a mild digestive enzyme free of xenogeneic proteins (NC1004.1, Yocon, China) when they reached 80–90% confluence. Once the UC-MSCs had been passaged to the desired passages: P2, P4, P6, P9, P10, P14, and P15, the cells at those passages were collected for single-cell RNA sequencing or other experiments.

10 × Single-cell transcriptome sequencing

Following 10 × Genomics protocol, scRNA-seq was done with Chromium 3′ V3 Chemistry. A single-cell suspension was mixed with barcoded gel beads in a microfluidic device. Cells were encapsulated in droplets for reverse-transcription. cDNA was processed to form a library, sequenced, and data were analyzed using barcodes. After sequencing, the raw data were processed. The barcodes were used to assign each transcript to its originating cell. An Illumina Novaseq6000 sequencer was used to sequence the single‐cell RNA‐seq libraries (performed by Novogene Technology, Beijing). Raw sequencing files were processed through the Cell Ranger pipeline (Cell Ranger v6.0.2, 10 × Genomics) aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome (GRCh38 v3.0.0), generating feature-barcode matrices for downstream single-cell analyses. We loaded the Seurat v4 package and performed two rounds of screening to eliminate low-frequency genes and cells with insufficient gene content [19]. Data were normalized using “NormalizeData”, and the top 2000 variable genes were identified via “FindVariableFeatures”. To mitigate cell-cycle interference in cell heterogeneity analysis, “CellCycleScoring” was applied for cell-cycle scoring. Given the strong cell-cycle influence, we removed the G2/M and S-phase score difference to eliminate the effect while retaining cell type distinctions. Subsequently, we normalized and dimension-reduced the data, setting 30 PCA “dims” and a 0.5 “resolution” in “FindClusters”. Cells were then anchored, clustered, and differential genes between sub-clusters were identified using “FindAllMarkers”. The R package ggplot2 [20] (version 3.5.1) was used for data visualisation.

Multi-sample integration and clustering

Apply the “IntegrateData” function in the Seurat package to remove batch effects, setting dims to 30. Normalize and reduce data dimensions. Anchor cells with the top 20 PCA results and cluster at a 0.5 “resolution”. Visualize via UMAP.

Pseudotemporal sequence analysis

Using the Monocle2 package and “DDRTree” algorithm, set the top 500 highly variable genes as ranking genes [21–23]. Group these genes by k-means based on lineage expression patterns and visualize the lineage structure.

Cell stemness inference

CytoTRACE package [24] was used to infer cell differentiation states from detectable gene expression numbers. Cellular stemness potential was quantified using CytoTRACE-generated Stemness Scores, with subsequent visualization and comparative ranking across identified subclusters.

Functional enrichment analysis

Score cells for different gene sets with the GSVA package. Combine study—specific gene sets using the Seurat's “AddModuleScore” for functional scoring. Following identification of cluster-specific DEGs using the “FindAllMarkers” function, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed through the clusterProfiler R package [25]. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for inter-group comparisons, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Significantly differentially expressed genes were identified using a mild log2 fold change threshold (log2FC > 0.25) and adjusted P-value (adj.p) < 0.05. The enriched terms were subsequently ranked by -log10(P value) to prioritize biological processes with highest statistical significance. Terms with adj.p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Transcription factor analysis

Employ the SCENIC package for enrichment analysis of transcription factor differential activation among stages [26]. Calculate average enrichment scores, identify significant ones, and visualize the results.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Intercellular communication patterns across subpopulations were predicted using the CellChat package [27]. The “selectK” algorithm was employed to infer the optimal number of signaling patterns. Subsequent application of the “identify Communication Patterns” function, coupled with “netAnalysis_river” visualization, generated heatmaps and river plots to delineate dominant communication networks.The “netVisual_circle” function was specifically utilized to visualize signaling crosstalk between defined signaling pathways or subpopulations, providing a circular layout representation of ligand-receptor interaction networks.

Flow cytometry sorting

UC-MSCs passaged to P6 were labeled with MLPH (bs-18784R-FITC, Bioss Antibodies, USA) and LPXN (bs-13653R-APC, Bioss Antibodies, USA) antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min. Then, 2 ml of PBE was added to wash the antibodies, followed by centrifugation at 1,200 rpm, 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended. MLPHhighLPXNhigh and MLPHlowLPXNlow UC-MSCs were sorted using the BD FACSAria II.

UC-MSCs support CB CD34+ cell hematopoietic capacity

Each well of a 24-well plate was seeded with 3 × 104 MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs or MLPHlowLPXNlow UC-MSCs, with a control group. The plate was incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The next day, 1 × 104 CB Lin− (CD3, CD14, CD16, CD19, CD20, CD56) CD34+ cells were added to each well, supplemented with 100 ng/ml SCF (300-07-100ug, PeproTech, USA), 10 ng/ml TPO (300–18-100ug, PeproTech, USA), 10 ng/ml Flt3-L (300–19-50ug, PeproTech, USA), 1% L-Glu (25,030,081, Gbico, USA), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (10,000 U/ml, 15,140,122, Gbico, USA). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. On day 7, cells were stained with antibodies against CD34 (343,504, Biolegend, USA), CD133 (566,596, BD Biosciences, USA) and analyzed using FACS Canto II.

T cell proliferation inhibition assay

Each well of a 48-well plate was seeded with 1 × 105 MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs or MLPHlowLPXNlow UC-MSCs, with a control group. The plate was incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The next day, 1 × 105 CFSE (C34570, ThermoFisher, USA)-labeled T cells were added to each well, followed by the addition of ImmunoCult™ Human CD3/CD28 T Cell Activator (10,971, STEMCELL Technologies, Canada) at a final concentration of 25 μl/ml. After 5 days of culture, T cell proliferation was assessed using FACS Canto II.

UC-MSC co-transplantation assay​​

Healthy female NOG mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Vital River Laboratories. Female NOG mice received 2 Gy irradiation. Within 6–8 h post-irradiation, mice were co-transplanted with either 2 × 105 MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs or MLPHlowLPXNlow UC-MSCs, along with 5 × 104 CB CD34+ cells. Control group mice received only 5 × 104 CB CD34+ cells. 12 weeks post-transplantation, Mice were euthanized by gradual exposure to carbon dioxide and human CD45+ (hCD45+) cell engraftment in peripheral blood and bone marrow was analyzed by flow cytometry. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Blood Diseases Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. The procedures were conducted in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility of the institute, with mice housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle at controlled temperature and humidity. All cell transplantations were performed via tail vein injection without the use of anesthesia. The work in this study has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Statistical methods

GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) were used for the analysis and visualization of statistical data. Unpaired Student’s t tests were applied for statistical comparisons between two groups and differences with a P value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For scRNA-seq data processing and analysis, the Seurat package (v4.4.0) in R software (v4.2.0) was utilized, with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test used for differential expression analysis. All experiments and key analyses were repeated at least three times.

Result

Single-cell sequencing reveals heterogeneity among different passages of UC-MSCs during in vitro culture

To explore the functional subsets and cellular heterogeneity of expanded UC-MSCs, we isolated UC-MSCs from two human fetal umbilical cords for in vitro passage and culture. We classified the passage 2 (P2) UC-MSCs cultured in vitro as early stage, the passage 4 (P4) and passage 6 (P6) UC-MSCs as middle stage, and the passage 9 (P9), passage 10 (P10), passage 14 (P14), and passage 15 (P15) UC-MSCs as late stage. These cells from different passages were collected for 10 × genomics single-cell sequencing and analysis (Fig. 2A).

After data cleaning and filtering, a total of 78,178 high-quality cells were obtained. Then, we performed dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis on these cells and examined their characteristics using standard MSC identification markers. Among them, the markers CD105 (ENG), CD73 (NT5E), and CD90 (THY1) were highly expressed in the sample cells, while CD45 (PTPRC), CD34, and CD14 showed negligible or low expression in the sample cells (Fig. 2B). Finally, we obtained a UMAP atlas of UC-MSCs comprising 14 cell subpopulations and the passage stage composition of the corresponding each subpopulation cells (Fig. 2C, D; Supplementary Fig. S1A-C). Meanwhile, given that UC-MSCs are stem cells with proliferative potential, the ratio of different cell cycle is also worthy of attention. Thus, we further scored cell cycle phases using cell cycle-related genes to analyze cell cycle distribution across different culture stages and subpopulations. The heterogeneity in cell cycle composition was higher among subpopulations than between consecutive culture stages (Fig. 2E, F; Supplementary Fig. S1D, E).

To further characterize the functional properties of UC-MSCs subpopulations, we identified the top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each subpopulation. Meanwhile, the subpopulations-specific marker genes and functionally relevant DEGs were visualized using a heatmap (Fig. 2G). The subpopulations C0 and C5 highly expressed genes associated with the G1-phase cell cycle genes, such as JUNB, POLD4, MXD4, and FJX1; subpopulations C1 and C4 highly expressed the G2/M phase genes, including CCNB2, FAM83D, CENPA, and CDCA2; while subpopulations C2 and C6 were enriched for S-phase genes like FAM111B, DTL, MCM10, and FBXO5. These findings collectively indicate that these specific subpopulations may reside in an actively self-renewing phase. Functional profiling of the remaining clusters revealed distinct specialization tendencies: C8 exhibited elevated FOXA2 regulating differentiation [28], MLPH mediating intracellular protein transport [29], and LPXN participating in cell adhesion and TGF-β signaling [30]. C12 showed enriched NEAT1 positively modulating inflammatory responses and COL6A1 driving endodermal differentiation and osteogenesis [31, 32]. C13 uniquely expressed COL3A1 for immune suppression and chondrocyte/smooth muscle differentiation [33], CXCL6 for neutrophil chemotaxis [34], and LRRC75A enhancing ossification via Wnt signaling [35]. Significantly, C3 co-expressed chemotactic CXCL6, osteogenic LRRC75A, and SEPT7 modulating MAPK pathways [36]; C7 specifically upregulated LRRC75A and SEPT7; C9 enriched COX7A1 involved in oxidative phosphorylation electron transfer [37], DRAP1 mediating TGF-β signaling [38], and IFITM3 regulating type I interferon immunity [39]. C10 highly expressed CYBA in IL-6/VEGF inflammatory signaling and LOX driving osteogenic mineralization [40, 41], while C11 upregulated TGF-β-associated DRAP1 and DNA repair gene PCLAF [42]. These markers facilitate our future selection of subpopulations and are expected to serve as quality control biomarkers for hUC-MSCs.

Transcriptome data reveals metabolic changes and functional characteristics of mid-passage UC-MSCs

Among all passage stages, mid-passage UC-MSCs demonstrated superior functional properties. These cells represent the primary population used in clinical infusion. The GSEA enrichment analysis revealed that multiple pathways were enriched in middle passage stage UC-MSCs compared to early passage stage cells. These pathways can be summarized into the following aspects: cell stemness, cell fate, cellular respiration, damage repair capacity, immune regulation, cell adhesion, and cell cytoskeletal function. Specifically, the stem cell maintenance pathway was markedly enhanced in mid-passage UC-MSCs (Fig. 3A). This alteration suggests that stemness-enriched cellular subpopulations may progressively dominate during in vitro culture or that UC-MSCs counteract cellular aging through a negative feedback mechanism. Correspondingly, in terms of cell fate, the intrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway was negatively regulated while the cell cycle was positively regulated in the middle stage of UC-MSCs (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Fig. S2A).

The pathway enrichment threshold was set at P value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25. Moreover, mid-passage UC-MSCs exhibited upregulation in multiple pathways related to respiration and energy metabolism compared to the early-passage. Cellular respiration in mid-passage UC-MSCs showed a general upregulation, which at the subcellular level manifested as enrichment in mitochondrial respirasome (Fig. 3C). This change suggests a shift in the respiratory pattern of mid-passage UC-MSCs, characterized by the upregulation of aerobic respiration-related pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation and ATP biosynthetic processes (Fig. 3D). This suggests that in vitro culture conditions have a broad impact on the metabolic patterns of UC-MSCs. Generally, the differences between the in vitro culture environment and the umbilical cord are multifaceted, including not only alterations in environmental oxygen levels but also changes in the physical support structure of the cells. For example, various types of mesenchymal cells constitute the three-dimensional microenvironment structure for cell growth in the umbilical cord. Whereas, a single cell type of MSCs is spread on the surface of plastic culture dishes for cultivation in vitro. Therefore, it is possible that changes in the culture environment led to the upregulation of pathways related to cell adhesion molecule binding and cytoskeletal structural components in mid-passage UC-MSCs compared to early-passage cells (Fig. 3E). In terms of cellular function, as anticipated, mid-passage UC-MSCs exhibit greater maturity and more comprehensive functionality compared to naive early-passage UC-MSCs. The entries related to immunomodulation and injury repair in mid-passage UC-MSCs showed a certain degree of upregulation (Fig. 3F, G).

Next, we attempted to analyze the changes occurring in mid-passage UC-MSCs during passage culture from multiple dimensions. DEGs analysis revealed that compared to the early culture stage, some immune regulation-related genes (PTGER4, HGF, TGFB1, and SEMA3A) in mid-passage UC-MSCs showed upregulation, along with a series of upregulated tissue repair and vascular endothelial regeneration-related genes (PTGES2, VEGFA, ADM, and ANGPTL4). Conversely, the gene mediating inflammatory senescence (THBS1) displayed downregulation (Fig. 3H; Supplementary Fig. S2C, D). In addition to functionally related DEGs, mid-passage UC-MSCs exhibited higher expression of a series of transcription factors compared to early-passage UC-MSCs. These included transcription factors associated with MSCs stemness or fate determination such as TEAD2, PBX1, GATA2, SOX11, KLF4, and FOXP1, showed varying degrees of upregulation compared to early-passage UC-MSCs (Fig. 3I). Collectively, these results revealed that appropriately passaged mid-passage UC-MSCs acquire enhanced functional superiority, providing insights for therapeutic optimization.

Comprehensive evaluation of stemness potential and cellular functions in UC-MSCs subpopulations

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of each UC-MSCs subpopulation, we performed functional scoring on the UC-MSCs subpopulations using gene sets related to MSCs immunotherapy and hematopoietic support, and ranked them according to the median scores of the subpopulations (Fig. 4A). Moreover, the R package CytoTRACE was used to score and rank the stemness potential of each subpopulation (Fig. 4B). Given the high stemness potential score of the C4 subpopulation and the comprehensive advantages of the C8 subpopulation, both C4 and C8 subpopulations emerged as ideal candidate target subpopulations.

To optimize our selection, we further employed pseudotime analysis to predict the developmental trajectory of UC-MSCs, with results suggesting that the C8 subpopulation occupied an upstream position in the cellular hierarchy (Fig. 4C, D; Supplementary Fig. S3A). Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis of upregulated genes in the C8 subpopulation revealed enrichment for platelet alpha granules and MSCs proliferation pathways, highlighting the combination characteristics of stemness potential and proliferative capacity in the C8 subpopulation (Fig. 4E). Additionally, the C8 subpopulation also showed elevated expression of genes associated with injury repair, immunomodulation, and vascular endothelial regeneration, such as PTGES2, HGF, VEGFA, and TGFB1 (Supplementary Fig. S3B, C). Based on comprehensive evaluation through cellular functional scoring, we identified the C8 subpopulation as the optimal candidate (Fig. 4F; Supplementary Fig. S3D).

Cell–cell communication analysis revealed the stemness maintenance mechanism of C8 subpopulation

To further analyze the molecular mechanism of the C8 subpopulation’s superior stemness and proliferation potential, we examined intercellular communication among UC-MSCs subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B). We hypothesized that the cellular characteristics of the C8 subpopulation may originate from the transduction of specific cell signaling pathway. Therefore, we employed the R package CellChat to infer the outgoing signaling patterns of UC-MSCs, with the output patterns of each subpopulation categorized into four distinct patterns (Fig. 5A).

A, B CellChat analysis predicts unique outgoing signal patterns for each subgroup and the signaling pathways associated with each outgoing pattern. C The NOTCH signaling pathway network among various subpopulations within UC-MSCs. D Heatmap of the roles of various subpopulations in the NOTCH signaling pathway transduction. E Violin plot of NOTCH signaling pathway receptors expression level in UC-MSCs. F The portion of upregulated and downregulated DEGs in the C8 subpopulation that are associated with the NOTCH signaling pathway

Our analysis revealed that the C8 subpopulation was independently classified into a specific signal output pattern (Fig. 5B), which is associated with the NOTCH signaling pathway. By illustrating the signal network of the NOTCH signaling pathway among UC-MSCs subpopulations, we observed that the signal network originating from C8 was particularly prominent (Fig. 5C). In the transduction of the NOTCH signaling pathway, the C8 subpopulation held greater importance compared to other subpopulations and played a more substantial role than other subpopulations (Fig. 5D). However, the C8 subpopulation showed limited involvement in canonical WNT signaling (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Further investigation revealed NOTCH2 as the dominant NOTCH receptor in UC-MSCs, with expression levels significantly exceeding other NOTCH receptors (Fig. 5E). Notably, NOTCH2 expression patterns across subpopulations aligned with NOTCH signaling pathway network from cell–cell communication analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4D).

Among the DEGs upregulated in the C8 subpopulation, we identified key factors of the NOTCH signaling pathway. These factors included the NOTCH signaling ligand JAG1, the target gene HES1, and other NOTCH-related genes (Fig. 5F). This may suggest that the C8 subpopulation maintains the stemness potential of cells by highly expressing key factors of the NOTCH signaling pathway. In addition, we also found that the expression levels of JAG1 and HES1 were relatively high during the mid-passage (Supplementary Fig. S4E). These findings provide further evidence for understanding the functional mechanisms of the C8 subpopulation and for future artificial regulation of key factors in the signaling pathway to enhance the stemness capacity of UC-MSCs.

The MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs from C8 subpopulation exhibit hematopoietic support and immunosuppressive capabilities

To validate our bioinformatics predictions, we sought to identify surface markers and verify the cellular functions of the target subpopulation. DEGs analysis showed high MLPH and LPXN expression in the C8 subpopulation. Both markers were also enriched in mid-passage UC-MSCs (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. S5A). Therefore, we compared hematopoietic support, immunosuppression, and NOTCH signaling pathway scores between MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs and MLPHlowLPXNlow UC-MSCs (Fig. 6B). The MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs demonstrated advantages in both functions. Moreover, MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs exhibited significantly higher NOTCH2 expression levels and NOTCH2 postive percentages compared to MLPHlowLPXNlow UC-MSCs (Fig. 6C). We then isolated MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs by flow cytometry. To validate the hematopoietic support capacity of MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs, we performed functional co-culture assays with hematopoietic cells. Co-culture with MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs significantly promoted the expansion of cord blood (CB) CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells and CD34+CD133+ hematopoietic stem cells compared with MLPHlowLPXNlow UC-MSCs (Fig. 6D). However, for CD34+CD90+ cells and CD34+CD201+ cells populations, the supportive effect of MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs showed no significant advantage over MLPHlowLPXNlow UC-MSCs, though it still markedly enhanced their proliferation compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Nevertheless, the existing results consistently indicated that MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs possess superior hematopoietic support capacity.

To verify whether MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs exhibited immunosuppressive effects, we co-cultured MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs or MLPHlowLPXNlow UC-MSCs with T cells in a medium containing T cell activators. The results demonstrated that MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs significantly suppressed the proliferation of CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells compared to MLPHlowLPXNlow UC-MSCs (Fig. 6E; Supplementary Fig. S5C).Furthermore, co-culture with MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs resulted in a significantly lower proportion of activated CD4+CD25+ T cells compared to the MLPHlowLPXNlow group. Although no similar advantage was observed for CD8+ T cells with MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs, they still significantly reduced CD8+ T cell activation relative to the control group (Supplementary Fig. S5D). These results demonstrated that MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs not only exhibited significant hematopoietic support advantages but also displayed enhanced immunosuppressive capabilities.

To further validate the hematopoietic support capacity of MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs in vivo, we co-transplanted CB CD34+ cells and UC-MSCs into irradiated NOG mice. After 12 weeks, we assessed the proportion of hCD45+ cells in the peripheral blood and bone marrow of the co-transplanted mice. Although the MLPHhighLPXNhigh group showed only a modest advantage over the MLPHlowLPXNlow group in the bone marrow, its pronounced superiority in peripheral blood relative to both the MLPHlowLPXNlow and control groups indicates a stronger hematopoietic support potential. (Supplementary Fig. S5E).

Discussion

Despite significant advances in the clinical application of UC-MSCs, their functional heterogeneity and long-term culture stability remain critical issues limiting therapeutic efficacy. In vitro passaging and expansion is precisely the key step to obtain sufficient quantities of UC-MSCs for clinical treatment. Previous studies have employed single-cell sequencing to characterize UC-MSC subpopulations [43]. However, their analyses typically covered limited passage ranges. This leaves room for further exploration of functional mechanisms and long-term culture dynamics. Moreover, current UC-MSCs sourcing and processing methods differ notably among research groups. Passage classification systems also show inconsistencies. For instance, Cheng et al. classified UC-MSCs at passages P2–P10 as early-passage, P11–P20 as mid-passage, and > P20 as late-passage, with senescence detected via SA-β-gal staining in P11/P12 UC-MSCs [44]. Whereas, Shi et al. defined UC-MSCs below P4 as early-passage and those beyond P16 as late-passage, noting that late-passage UC-MSCs retained osteogenic potential comparable to early-passage cells [45]. These variations highlight opportunities for protocol harmonization. The functional evolution of UC-MSCs during in vitro passaging and expansion represents an important direction urgently requiring in-depth research.

Here, we utilized a clinical-grade cell culture system to obtain UC-MSCs meeting safety requirements. Using single-cell transcriptomics across passages, we constructed a dynamic cellular atlas. This approach revealed previously unrecognized functional transitions. Our findings demonstrate that mid-passage UC-MSCs exhibited superior functionality compared to early-passage cells. For example, mid-passage UC-MSCs highly expressed a series of functional genes compared to early-passage cells, covering immune regulation, tissue repair, vascular endothelial regeneration (PTGER4 [46–48], TGFB1 [49], SEMA3A [50], HGF [51], VEGFA, PTGES2 [52], ANGPTL4, ADM [53]); while lowly expressing functional genes related to inflammatory aging (THBS1 [54]). In terms of transcription factors, the expression of TEAD2 [55], PBX1 [56, 57], GATA2 [58], SOX11 [59], KLF4 [60], and FOXP1 [61] in mid-passage UC-MSCs showed upregulation compared to the early-passage. Previous studies have demonstrated that these transcription factors are closely associated with MSC proliferation, stemness regulation, and cell fate determination. We speculate that this may be due to a certain degree of functional differentiation occurring in UC-MSCs during passaging, making them more mature compared to the progenitor cells. Appropriately passaged UC-MSCs may be more suitable for in vitro therapeutic applications.

Although late-passage UC-MSCs demonstrated certain functional advantages similar to mid-passage, their increased aging potential raises concerns. Therefore, mid-passage cells remain preferable for therapeutic use. Notably, our findings indicate that mid-passage cells exhibit comprehensive yet moderate differences in functional pathways, genes, and transcription factor expression compared to early-passage cells. For instance, functional enrichment analysis revealed that mid-passage UC-MSCs are enriched in multiple functional pathways, but the NES values of these enrichments are generally not highly significant (NES range 1–2). This phenomenon may arise from multiple factors. We hypothesize that although the in vitro culture environment differs substantially from the native umbilical cord milieu of UC-MSCs, cells of the same type predominantly exhibit adaptive metabolic regulation in response to environmental changes. The metabolic and functional disparities caused by environmental shifts and natural cellular development accumulate gradually across passages, representing a continuous process. Consequently, these differences may not be as pronounced as those induced by tumors, inflammation, or artificial interventions. Nevertheless, the existing results still suggest functional advantages of mid-passage UC-MSCs over early-passage UC-MSCs, providing a rationale for optimizing UC-MSC culture protocols in clinical applications and identifying the preferred culture stage for therapeutic use.

At the subpopulation level, we employed multiple approaches to evaluate UC-MSC subpopulations regarding their stemness and proliferation potential, immunomodulatory effects, and multilineage differentiation capabilities. As a result, we obtained an ideal subpopulation (C8), which exhibits both stemness and proliferation capacity alongside well-rounded cellular functionalities. Further investigation into the mechanisms underlying C8 subpopulation stemness maintenance revealed a close association with the NOTCH signaling pathway. While NOTCH signaling pathway's role in hematopoietic stem cells is well-documented, its MSC-specific functions remain understudied. Notably, both JAG1 and HES1 highly expressed in the C8 subpopulation were previously reported to maintain undifferentiated states via NOTCH signaling [15]. Furthermore, parent stem cells can activate NOTCH2 as niches in their daughter cells to block differentiation [62]. These mechanisms may collectively explain the dual advantages of C8 subpopulation in stemness maintenance and hematopoietic support. Concurrently, the pivotal role of the C4 subpopulation in WNT signal transduction elucidated its intrinsic properties as another subpopulation with high stemness potential (Supplementary Fig. S4C).

Based on these analytical findings, we proceeded to screen specific cell subpopulations and validated the conclusions through in vitro experiments. MLPH and LPXN are highly expressed functional markers in the C8 subpopulation. Their protein products localize to the cell membrane, enabling flow cytometry detection. Based on these properties, we selected MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs as representative of our target subpopulation. In vitro experiments demonstrated that MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs possess hematopoietic support and immunosuppressive capabilities, corroborating our transcriptomic findings. Furthermore, CD133+ hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) exhibited a megakaryocytic differentiation bias [63], potentially linked to the platelet α-granule process as identified in the GO terms of C8 subpopulation. This provides a cellular-level explanation for the mechanistic basis of UC-MSCs in immunotherapy and hematopoietic support applications.

Collectively, our study identifies MLPH and LPXN as a signature marker combination for the C8 subpopulation of UC-MSCs. This enables the direct isolation of MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs via flow cytometry, which can then be expanded at scale in vitro. Such a strategy holds promise for generating off-the-shelf cell therapy products, addressing the clinical demand for readily available, ready-to-use stem cell therapeutics. However, this translational approach also presents notable challenges. Consistent with our passage-dependent analyses, MLPH and LPXN are predominantly upregulated in mid-to-late passage UC-MSCs. As a result, the proportion of MLPHhighLPXNhigh cells is too low in early passages to support efficient and sufficient isolation. Therefore, further investigations, including single-cell sequencing of UC-MSCs across a broader range of passages, are warranted to systematically profile the expression dynamics of MLPH and LPXN. Defining the optimal time window for collecting these high-expression cells will be critical to overcoming current limitations and advancing the clinical utility of this functionally distinct UC-MSC subpopulation.

Based on the current results, further exploratory studies can be designed to deepen our understanding of the C8 subpopulation and its clinical prospects. In basic research, in vivo transplantation assessment may be conducted in subsequent work. The engraftment discrepancy between NOTCH2-knockdown MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs and control cells could be compared in mouse co-transplantation models, which may indirectly indicate the contribution of NOTCH signaling to the in vivo colonization and functional maintenance of UC-MSCs. For clinical application, the C8 subpopulation with dual high expression of MLPH and LPXN can be considered in future transplantation attempts. Post-transplant dynamic detection of peripheral blood markers such as JAG1 and HES1 would help evaluate the in vivo homing properties of C8 cells and the activity of the NOTCH pathway. In addition, comparative analysis with routine MSC treatment may better highlight the unique advantages of this subpopulation and offer a theoretical reference for the optimization of clinical cell therapy strategies.

This study also has limitations, such as insufficient coverage of donor heterogeneity. Future research should expand sample sizes and incorporate epigenetic analyses to further distinguish technical noise from true biological variation. However, overall, our study focuses on aspects overlooked in many previous studies. We explored the mechanisms behind functional differences in UC-MSCs across passages. We also examined the necessity of proper culture for UC-MSC functionality. Additionally, we characterized functional subpopulations and their associated signaling mechanisms. Ultimately, these findings will advance UC-MSCs from empirical application to precision medicine, providing theoretical support for the standardization of cell therapy products.

Conclusions

In this study of serially passaged UC-MSCs, mid-passage cells demonstrated superior functional properties relevant to clinical applications compared to early-passage cells. Through bioinformatics analysis, we identified a functionally distinct subpopulation characterized by elevated NOTCH signaling activity, which correlates with enhanced hematopoietic support and immunosuppressive potential. We further established MLPH and LPXN as practical markers for isolating this functional subpopulation in vitro, with experimentally validated MLPHhighLPXNhigh UC-MSCs consistently displaying these key functional properties. Collectively, these findings advance our understanding of UC-MSC heterogeneity and provide a translational framework to select and enrich high-potential cells, offering new opportunities to optimize MSC-based therapies for clinical use and guiding future investigations into NOTCH signaling in MSC function.
