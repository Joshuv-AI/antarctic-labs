Interior singularity and branching of geodesics in real-analytic sub-Riemannian manifolds
T. Rossi, A. J. A. Schiavoni Piazza, A. Socionovo

Abstract
. We study the regularity and branching of strictly abnormal minimizing geodesics in sub-Riemannian geometry. We construct examples of real-analytic sub-Riemannian manifolds admitting minimizing geodesics that lose regularity at an interior point of their domain and exhibit branching, thereby resolving longstanding open questions.



1.
Introduction

A sub-Riemannian manifold is a triplet
(M,\Delta,g)
, where
M
is a smooth and connected manifold,
\Delta\subset TM
is a smooth bracket-generating distribution (i.e., a distribution satisfying the Hörmander condition), and
g
is a smooth Riemannian metric on
\Delta
. A
horizontal
(or,
admissible
) curve is an absolutely continuous trajectory in
M
which is tangent to
\Delta
almost everywhere. When
\Delta=TM
, the pair
(M,g)
is a Riemannian manifold and every absolutely continuous curve is admissible. The length of a horizontal curve
\eta:[0,T]\to M
is defined as

(1.1)

L_{\rm SR}(\eta)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{g(\dot{\eta}(t),\dot{\eta}(t))}\,dt.

The Chow-Rashewskii Theorem ensures that
M
is horizontally path-connected, and thus the sub-Riemannian distance between two points
p,q\in M
, given by

(1.2)

d_{\rm SR}(p,q)\coloneqq\inf\{L_{SR}(\eta)\mid\eta:[0,T]\to M\text{ horizontal, }\eta(0)=p,\ \eta(T)=q\},

is well-defined, continuous, and compatible with the manifold topology. Given
p,q\in M
, a horizontal curve
\eta:[0,T]\to M
joining them is a
length-minimizer
if

(1.3)

d_{\rm SR}(p,q)=d_{\rm SR}(\eta(0),\eta(T))=L_{\rm SR}(\eta).

If, in addition,
\eta
is parametrized by arc-length, it is referred to as a
minimizing geodesic
.
The study of length-minimizing curves is one of the most important and difficult topics in sub-Riemannian geometry. The Pontryagin Maximum Principle (cf.
[
3
, Chapter 4]
) determines first-order necessary conditions for a horizontal curve to be a minimizing geodesic. Every horizontal curve satisfying those conditions is called an
extremal curve
, and thus, every minimizing geodesic is an extremal curve. Extremal curves are divided into two non-disjoint classes, called
normal
and
abnormal
(or
singular
) curves. Normal curves are solutions of a geodesic equation, and thus they are smooth and locally minimizing. These are the only types of extremal curves appearing in Riemannian geometry. Instead, abnormal curves are a priori no more regular than absolutely continuous, and their length-minimality properties are unknown. Thus, the difficulty in studying of minimizing geodesics lies in the possible presence of
strictly abnormal
extremals, i.e., those curves that are abnormal but not normal. We refer the reader to
[
3
,
15
,
18
,
24
,
27
]
for an exhaustive introduction to sub-Riemannian geometry and to the problems related to minimizing geodesics.

In this paper, we deal with two central problems concerning strictly abnormal minimizing geodes-ics: regularity and branching.

1.1.
The regularity problem

The problem of the regularity of sub-Riemannian geodesics originated in the 1980s with the works of Strichartz in
[
29
,
30
]
, where he incorrectly claimed that every sub-Riemannian minimizing geodesic was smooth. Later, in 1994, the first example of a strictly abnormal curve that is length-minimizing was discovered by Montgomery
[
23
]
. After that, many other examples have been found, see
[
1
,
20
]
, but all these curves are
C^{\infty}
-smooth, and they remained the only known instances of strictly abnormal minimizing geodesics for almost 30 years. Beyond these examples, a few general results on strictly abnormal geodesics have been obtained in recent years. On the one hand, building upon
[
19
]
, it was proved in
[
13
]
that abnormal minimizing geodesics cannot have corners, and later in
[
14
,
25
]
, that they must have a tangent line. In some special cases, including real-analytic three-dimensional or metabelian (cf.
[
17
, Def. 3.2]
) sub-Riemannian manifolds, minimizing geodesics are of class
C^{1}
, see
[
4
,
10
,
17
]
, but in general, the
C^{1}
regularity remains an open question. On the other hand, necessary conditions for the minimality of abnormal curves can be derived by the differential analysis of the end-point map (see
[
3
, Chapter 8]
for a detailed introduction to the topic). This theory is well understood up to the second order, see
[
2
,
12
]
, and it has been partially extended to the
n
-th order in
[
6
]
. A recent and significant advance in the theory has been made in
[
9
]
, where the authors find sub-Riemannian structures exhibiting minimizing geodesics which are not
C^{\infty}
at a boundary point. In particular, the lowest regularity achieved with these examples is that of a minimizing geodesic of class
C^{2}\setminus C^{3}
. Nevertheless, a main question is still open: can a sub-Riemannian minimizing geodesic lose regularity at an interior point of its domain?

1.2.
The branching problem

In the study of metric spaces, detecting whether minimizing geodesics can branch has become increasingly relevant, particularly in connection with the Monge problem of optimal transport (see e.g.
[
8
,
11
,
5
]
). A minimizing geodesic
\eta:[0,T]\to M
in a metric space
(M,d)
is
branching
at time
t\in(0,T)
if there exists a minimizing geodesic
\eta^{\prime}:[0,T]\to M
such that

(1.4)

\eta|_{[0,t]}\equiv\eta^{\prime}|_{[0,t]}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\eta|_{[0,\tau]}\neq\eta^{\prime}|_{[0,\tau]}\quad\text{for all }\tau\in(t,T].

Typical examples of branching metric spaces (i.e., metric spaces admitting branching geodesics) are graphs, locally finite CW-complexes, or (sub-)Finsler manifolds equipped with non-strictly convex norms (see
[
21
,
7
,
16
]
for examples of branching in sub-Finsler geometry). On the contrary, non-branching spaces include Riemannian manifolds, uniformly convex Banach spaces and Alexandrov spaces with curvature bounded from below. Only in recent years have the first examples of branching sub-Riemannian manifolds been discovered in
[
22
]
. In the aforementioned contribution, the authors study branching of normal geodesics, showing that this is equivalent to a jump in the rank of the differential of the end-point map along the curve. Exploiting this characterization, they are able to produce examples of families of strictly normal minimizing geodesics by gluing together the Heisenberg and the Martinet structure. Note that this gluing is not real-analytic; indeed, normal geodesics cannot branch in a real-analytic sub-Riemannian manifold. The work
[
22
]
left a few questions open: firstly, does an example of a strictly abnormal branching minimizing geodesic exist? Secondly, can minimizing geodesics branch in a real-analytic sub-Riemannian manifold?

1.3.
The main result

In this paper, we give an affirmative answer to the regularity and branching questions raised above. On
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with coordinates
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
, let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be the vector fields defined by

(1.5)

X_{1}(x)\coloneqq\partial_{x_{1}},\qquad X_{2}(x)\coloneqq\partial_{x_{2}}+P(x)^{2}\partial_{x_{3}},

where, for
b\in\mathbb{N}
,
b\geq 5
and odd,
P
is the polynomial defined by

(1.6)

P(x)\coloneqq x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{b},\qquad\forall\,x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}.

We denote by
\mathscr{M}\coloneqq(\mathbb{R}^{3},\Delta,g)
the sub-Riemannian manifold where
\Delta=\mathrm{span}\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
and
g
is the metric obtained by declaring
\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
to be an orthonormal basis. Let us also consider the curves
\gamma,\bar{\gamma}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined by

(1.7)

\gamma(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\Big(0,t,\frac{t^{2b+1}}{2b+1}\Big),\quad&t<0,\\
(t^{q},t,0),\quad&t\geq 0,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\gamma}(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\Big(0,t,\frac{t^{2b+1}}{2b+1}\Big),\quad&t<0,\\
(-t^{q},t,0),\quad&t\geq 0,\end{cases}\qquad\text{where }q\coloneqq\frac{b}{2}.

For
s,\varepsilon>0
, we denote by
\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}
and
\bar{\gamma}_{s,\varepsilon}
the restrictions of
\gamma
and
\bar{\gamma}
to the interval
[-s,\varepsilon]
, respectively. When
s=0
, we omit the subscript
s=0
and write
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
and
\bar{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
. In
[
9
]
, it is proved that
\gamma_{\varepsilon},\bar{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
are length-minimizing for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. Here, we significantly improve that result.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist
\varepsilon,s>0
such that the curves
\gamma_{s,\varepsilon},\bar{\gamma}_{s,\varepsilon}
are length-minimizing in
\mathscr{M}
.

Both curves
{\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}}
and
\bar{\gamma}_{s,\varepsilon}
are of class
C^{\lfloor q\rfloor}\setminus C^{\lfloor q+1\rfloor}
at 0, which is an interior point of their domain. The examples with the lowest regularity are obtained for
b=5
, for which
{\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}},\bar{\gamma}_{s,\varepsilon}
are length-minimizers of class
C^{2}\setminus C^{3}
. This regularity is the lowest obtainable through this construction, as shown in
[
28
]
. Since
\bar{\gamma}(t)=\gamma(t)
for all
t\leq 0
, the curves
{\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}}
and
\bar{\gamma}_{s,\varepsilon}
provide the first example of strictly abnormal branching length-minimizers in a real-analytic (even polynomial) sub-Riemannian manifold. This constitutes our second main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist real-analytic sub-Riemannian manifolds admitting branching strictly abnormal minimizing geodesics.

We observe that our examples are distributions of Martinet-type in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, with the Martinet region that itself branches. In this framework, it is easy to see that branching strictly abnormal curves do exist, while it is far more demanding to prove that they are length-minimizing. Furthermore, our example of branching length-minimizers is “discrete”, as opposed to
[
22
]
where the authors find a one-parameter family of strictly normal branching geodesics.

1.4.
Further directions

The sub-Riemannian manifold
\mathscr{M}
is real-analytic, of dimension three and metabelian, cf.
[
17
, Def. 3.2]
. As previously mentioned, minimizing geodesics in three-dimensional or metabelian real-analytic sub-Riemannian manifolds must be of class
C^{1}
, cf.
[
10
,
17
]
. Thus, it remains an open question whether this result is sharp. More precisely, we have the following.

Open problem
.

Find a minimizing geodesic in a three-dimensional or metabelian real-analytic sub-Riemannian manifold of class
C^{1}
but not
C^{2}
, with a singularity at an interior point of its domain.

1.5.
Structure of the paper

The paper is fully devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Note that, in light of Proposition
2.1
below, it is enough to show that
{\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}}
is a length-minimizer, for small
s,\varepsilon>0
. The proof of this fact is by contradiction, assuming that there exists a shorter (normal) curve joining the endpoints of
{\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}}
, and it is divided into several steps, identified by the sections of the paper.

In Section
2
, we reduce the geodesic problem in
\mathscr{M}
to the problem of minimizing the Euclidean length
L(\cdot)
among plane curves
\omega\in{\rm AC}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{2})
, parametrized by arc-length and subject to the constraints
\omega(0)=(0,-s)
,
\omega(T)=(\varepsilon^{q},\varepsilon)
, for some
s,\varepsilon>0
, and

(1.8)

\int_{0}^{T}\dot{\omega}_{2}(t)P(\omega(t))^{2}dt=-\frac{s^{2b+1}}{2b+1}.

By the Pontryagin maximum principle, the set of (plane) optimal competitors, denoted by
\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, contains only (projections of) normal curves and, thus, every
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
is real-analytic. Moreover, since
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
is parametrized by arc-length, there is a real-analytic function
\theta:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\dot{\omega}_{1}(t)=\cos\theta(t)
and
\dot{\omega}_{2}(t)=\sin\theta(t)
, and that satisfies

(1.9)

\dot{\theta}(t)=\lambda_{\omega}Q(\omega(t)),

where
\lambda_{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}
is a multiplier, and
Q(x):=\partial_{x_{1}}(P(x)^{2})=4x_{1}P(x)
. Note that the Martinet region
\mathscr{S}
is the zero-locus of
Q
, i.e.,

(1.10)

\mathscr{S}=\{Q=0\}=\{P=0\}\cup\{x_{1}=0\}.

Next, in Section
3
, we crucially improve the Liu–Sussmann-type estimate of
[
9
, Prop. 2.2(ii)]
. This result finds its origin in
[
20
]
and provides an estimate on the distance of an optimal competitor from
\mathscr{S}
in terms of
\varepsilon>0
. More precisely, define

(1.11)

\widetilde{\beta}_{\omega}:=\max_{[0,L(\omega)]}|\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))|,

where
\widetilde{P}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the function

(1.12)

\widetilde{P}(x_{1},x_{2}):=\begin{cases}P(x),\quad&x_{2}\geq 0,\\
x_{1}^{2},\quad&x_{2}<0.\end{cases}

Then,
\widetilde{\beta}_{\omega}=0
if and only if
\omega
is the projection of a curve in
\mathscr{S}
and, in Corollary
3.3
, we prove that
\widetilde{\beta}_{\omega}=o(\varepsilon^{3q-1})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Compared to
[
20
,
9
]
, where the latter estimate is obtained using that
P=0
along the
whole
abnormal curve, our strategy relies instead on the minimality of
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
.

Using the improved Liu–Sussmann-type estimate, we provide a precise description of optimal competitors, with the final aim of comparing their length with the length of
{\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}}
. Even though this study is similar to the one in
[
9
]
, there are some non-trivial difficulties to be accounted for. Indeed, the ODE (
1.9
) for an optimal competitor naturally involves
P
. Instead, since
{\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}}
, with
s>0
, is not supported in
\{P=0\}
, the right quantity to consider for length estimates is
\widetilde{\beta}_{\omega}
, which is defined using
\widetilde{P}
. This requires an ad hoc adaptation of most of the arguments in
[
9
]
, together with new crucial ideas to complete the qualitative analysis of
\omega
. The goal of this analysis, carried out in Sections
4
to
8
, is to show that:

(1)

the multiplier
\lambda_{\omega}
in (
1.9
) is negative;

(2)

the competitor is supported in the positive region of
\widetilde{P}
, i.e.,
\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))>0
for all
t\in(0,L(\omega))
;

(3)

the competitor has one single loop, which is quantitatively far away from the starting point;

(4)

the function
t\mapsto\omega_{1}(t)
is strictly increasing up to the
x_{1}
-axis;

(5)

the maximum of the function
t\mapsto\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))
is achieved inside the loop.

More in detail, in Section
4
, we deduce length estimates of the level sets of
\widetilde{P}
. Here, a new argument tailored to
\widetilde{P}
is needed. In Section
5
, we report some geometric obstructions to optimality taken from
[
9
]
. These obstructions are local in nature and do not depend on the starting point. In Sections
6
and
7
, we study loops of optimal competitors. The main challenge here is to localize the loops to the region
\{x_{1}\geq C\varepsilon^{q},x_{2}\geq C\varepsilon\}\cap\{P>0\}
, cf. Proposition
7.1
and Lemma
7.7
. This localization procedure is crucial to translate estimates for
P
into estimates for
\widetilde{P}
, and to show that there is a unique loop. Additionally, in Corollary
7.12
, we show that the first component of any optimal competitor is strictly increasing up to the intersection with the positive
x_{1}
-axis, which is unique and transversal. In Section
8
, we exploit Corollary
7.12
to relate the maxima of
P
and of
\widetilde{P}
along a competitor, see Lemma
8.2
. This allows us to prove that
\widetilde{P}\circ\omega>0
and that the maximum of
\widetilde{P}\circ\omega
is achieved inside the loop.

Finally, we conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
in Section
9
. The final contradiction argument is based on length estimates for the unique loop of an optimal competitor, which can be obtained similarly to those in
[
9
]
, once all the intermediate ingredients have been established for
\widetilde{\beta}_{\omega}
and
\widetilde{P}
.

2.
Preliminaries and reduction to
\mathbb{R}^{2}

We reduce the study of horizontal curves in
\mathscr{M}
and their length to the study of curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. An absolutely continuous curve
\eta:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is horizontal in
\mathscr{M}
if and only if

(2.1)

\dot{\eta}(t)=\dot{\eta}_{1}X_{1}(\eta(t))+\dot{\eta}_{2}X_{2}(\eta(t)),\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

Consider the projection onto the first two coordinates
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e.,
\pi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\coloneqq(x_{1},x_{2})
. For any
T>0
, define a projection map
\mathrm{pr}:{\rm AC}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{3})\to{\rm AC}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{2})
, as

(2.2)

\mathrm{pr}(\eta)\coloneqq\pi\circ\eta,\qquad\forall\eta\in{\rm AC}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{3}).

Denote by
L(\cdot)
the Euclidean length of a plane curve. Since
\{X_{1},X_{2}\}
is an orthonormal frame for
\Delta
, with respect to
g
, from (
1.1
) and (
2.1
) it follows that

(2.3)

L_{\rm SR}(\eta)=\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\dot{\eta}_{1}(t)^{2}+\dot{\eta}_{2}(t)^{2}}dt=L(\mathrm{pr}(\eta)),

for every horizontal path
\eta
. In particular, for all
s>0
and as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the length of the curve
{\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}}=\gamma|_{[-s,\varepsilon]}
, see (
1.7
), is

(2.4)

\displaystyle L_{\rm SR}(\gamma_{s,\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=s+\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\sqrt{1+q^{2}t^{2q-2}}dt=s+\varepsilon+\frac{q^{2}}{2(2q-1)}\varepsilon^{2q-1}+o(\varepsilon^{2q-1}).

If we restrict to horizontal curves, the projection map admits a right inverse.
Indeed, given a plane curve
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in{\rm AC}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{2})
, set

(2.5)

\omega_{3}(\omega,t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\omega}_{2}(\tau)P(\omega(\tau))^{2}d\tau,\quad t\in[0,T].

Then, the function
\mathrm{lift}:{\rm AC}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{2})\to{\rm AC}([0,T],M)
, defined as

\omega\mapsto\mathrm{lift}(\omega)\coloneqq(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3}(\omega,\cdot)),

takes values in the set of horizontal curves and trivially satisfies
\mathrm{pr}\circ\mathrm{lift}=\rm{Id}
. Note that curves lifted in this way will always satisfy
\omega_{3}(0)=0
. Adding a constant to the third component gives a different right inverse for the projection function.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by
\phi(x)=(-x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
. A horizontal curve
\eta:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is length-minimizing in
\mathscr{M}
if and only if the curve
\bar{\eta}\coloneqq\phi\circ\eta_{3}
is.

Proof.

We first prove that
\phi
preserves horizontal curves in
\mathscr{M}
, as well as the variation on the third coordinate. Indeed, from (
2.1
), we have

(2.6)

\dot{\eta}_{3}=\dot{\eta}_{2}P(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})^{2}=\dot{\eta}_{2}P(-\eta_{1},\eta_{2})^{2}=\dot{\bar{\eta}}_{2}P(\bar{\eta}_{1},\bar{\eta}_{2})^{2}=\dot{\bar{\eta}}_{3}.

Then, the fact that
\phi
is an isometry of
\mathscr{M}
follows from (
2.3
).
∎

As a consequence, we may focus on proving Theorem
1.1
for
{\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}}
. Moreover, according to the discussion above, the problem of finding length-minimizing curves between two points in
\mathscr{M}
is equivalent to the problem of minimizing the Euclidean length of curves between their projections in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, under the additional constraint given by the variation of the third coordinate.

Definition 2.2
.

Given
s,\varepsilon>0
, we say that
\omega\in{\rm AC}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a
competitor
for
\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}
, and we write
\omega\in{\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}}
, if the following conditions hold:

(1)

\omega(0)=\pi(\gamma(-s))
,
\omega(T)=\pi(\gamma(\varepsilon))
;

(2)

\omega_{3}(\omega,0)=-\frac{s^{2b+1}}{2b+1}
;

(3)

T=L(\omega)
and
\omega
is parametrized by arc length;

(4)

\omega
is different from the arc-length reparameterization of
\mathrm{pr}({\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}})
.

If, additionally,
L(\omega)=d_{\rm SR}(\gamma(-s),\gamma(\varepsilon))
, we say that
\omega
is an
optimal competitor
for
\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}
and we write
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
.

We recall that the Pontryagin Maximum Principle (cf.
[
3
, Chapter 4]
) implies that the lift of any
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
must be a normal or an abnormal curve, joining the points
\gamma(-s)
and
\gamma(\varepsilon)
.

Remark 2.3
.

Denote by
\mathscr{N}_{s,\varepsilon}
the set of normal curves, parametrized by arc-length, and joining
\gamma(-s)
and
\gamma(\varepsilon)
. Denote by
\mathscr{N}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
the set of curves in
\mathscr{N}_{s,\varepsilon}
which are length-minimizing. Since
\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}
is the only abnormal trajectory joining its endpoints (up to reparameterization), condition (iv) above implies that
\mathrm{pr}(\mathscr{N}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\rm{opt}})=\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\rm{opt}}
.

For any competitor
\omega\in{\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}}
, we denote
I_{\omega}\coloneqq[0,L(\omega)]
. For any normal competitor
\omega\in\mathrm{pr}(\mathscr{N}_{s,\varepsilon})
, we define the
angle map

\theta=\theta_{\omega}:I_{\omega}\to\mathbb{R}
as the unique
C^{\infty}
function such that
\theta(0)\in[-\pi,\pi)
and

(2.7)

\dot{\omega}_{1}(t)=\cos(\theta(t)),\quad\dot{\omega}_{2}(t)=\sin(\theta(t)),\quad\text{for all }t\in I_{\omega}.

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix
\varepsilon,s>0
. For all
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
there exists
\lambda=\lambda_{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(2.8)

\dot{\theta}(t)=\lambda Q(\omega(t)),\quad t\in I_{\omega}.

The quantity
\lambda Q(\omega(t))
is the curvature of
\omega
at the point
\omega(t)
.

Proof.

On the one hand, by (
2.7
),
\dot{\theta}
is the signed curvature of
\omega
. On the other hand, since
\mathrm{pr}(\mathscr{N}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\rm{opt}})=\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\rm{opt}}
, see Remark
2.3
, we can apply the Pontryagin Maximum Principle to any lift of
\omega
. Hence, recalling that the sub-Riemannian Hamiltonian is

(2.9)

H(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}(h_{1}^{2}(\lambda)+h_{2}^{2}(\lambda))=\frac{1}{2}\left(p_{1}^{2}+\left(p_{2}+P(x_{1},x_{2})^{2}p_{3}\right)^{2}\right),\qquad\forall\,\lambda=(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})\in T^{*}M,

where
h_{i}(\lambda)=\langle\lambda,X_{i}\rangle
, we have that any lift
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})
of
\omega
must satisfy

(2.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlcrl}\dot{p}_{1}&\!\!=-\partial_{1}(P^{2})(\omega(t))h_{2}(t)p_{3}(t),&&\dot{\omega}_{1}&\!\!=h_{1}(t),\\
\dot{p}_{2}&\!\!=-\partial_{2}(P^{2})(\omega(t))h_{2}(t)p_{3}(t),&&\dot{\omega}_{2}&\!\!=h_{2}(t),\\
\dot{p}_{3}&\!\!=0,&&\dot{\omega}_{3}&\!\!=P(\omega(t))^{2}\dot{\omega}_{2}(t).\end{array}\right.

Therefore, we easily see that, for every
t\in I_{\omega}
,

(2.11)

\begin{split}\left(\ddot{\omega}_{1}(t),\ddot{\omega}_{2}(t)\right)=\left(-Q(\omega(t))h_{2}(t)p_{3}(t),Q(\omega(t))h_{1}(t)p_{3}(t)\right)=p_{3}(t)Q(\omega(t))\left(-\dot{\omega}_{2}(t),\dot{\omega}_{1}(t)\right).\end{split}

As
\omega
is parametrized by arc-length, this means that its signed curvature at time
t
is given by
Q(\omega(t))p_{3}(t)
. Since
p_{3}(t)\equiv p_{3}(0)
, we conclude the proof.
∎

For the next lemma, recall that normal curves in
\mathscr{M}
are real-analytic, see for instance

3.
A new Liu–Sussmann Lemma

A key ingredient in the proof of optimality of the abnormal curve in
[
9
,
20
]
is an estimate on the maximum value of

along an optimal competitor. We study the analogous quantities in our case.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\varepsilon,s>0
and let
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
. We define the two quantities

(3.1)

\begin{split}&\beta=\beta_{\omega}\coloneqq\max_{t\in I_{\omega}}|P(\omega(t))|,\\
&\widetilde{\beta}=\widetilde{\beta}_{\omega}\coloneqq\max_{t\in I_{\omega}}|\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))|,\end{split}

where
P
and
\widetilde{P}
are defined in (
1.6
) and (
1.12
), respectively.

We recover the asymptotic estimate on
\beta
and
\widetilde{\beta}
, which generalizes
[
9
, Lem. 3.5(v)]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\varepsilon_{\star}>0
be the parameter identified by
[
9
, Thm. 1.1]
. For every
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{\star}
and
\delta>0
there exists
s(\delta,\varepsilon)>0
such that, for every
0<s<s(\delta,\varepsilon)
and every
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, it holds

(3.2)

{\rm dist}_{\rm Haus}\left({\rm supp}(\omega),{\rm supp}(\mathrm{pr}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}))\right)\leq\delta,

where
{\rm dist}_{\rm Haus}(A,B)
is the Hausdorff distance between
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

The argument is an application of Ascoli-Arzelà theorem.
Given two non-empty compact sets
K_{0},K_{1}\subset M
, define the set
\Gamma_{K_{0}\to K_{1}}\subset{\rm AC}([0,1],M)
of minimizing geodesics, parametrized by constant speed, starting from
K_{0}
and ending in
K_{1}
. Note that a curve
\eta\in\Gamma_{K_{0}\to K_{1}}
is parametrized by speed
d(\eta(0),\eta(1))
, therefore the family
\Gamma_{K_{0}\to K_{1}}
is equi-Lipschitz and

(3.3)

L\coloneqq\max_{p\in K_{0},\,q\in K_{1}}d(p,q)<+\infty

is a bound on the equi-Lipschitz constant. Moreover, by the triangle inequality, any curve of the family is supported in the bounded set
\{p\in M\,|\,d(p,K_{0})\leq L\}
. By
Ascoli-Arzelà,
\Gamma_{K_{0}\to K_{1}}
is compact in the topology of uniform convergence (note that
\Gamma_{K_{0}\to K_{1}}
is closed by lower semi-continuity of the distance).

Now, consider the compact sets
K\coloneqq\{\gamma(\varepsilon)\}
and, for
s\geq 0
,

(3.4)

K_{s}\coloneqq\left\{\gamma(\tau)\,|\,\tau\in[-s,0]\right\}.

Thus, the set
\Gamma_{K_{s}\to K}
is compact and, if
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
is the reparametrization of
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
with constant speed equal to
L(\gamma_{\varepsilon})
, then
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}\in\Gamma_{K_{s}\to K}
, for every
s\geq 0
. By compactness, for every
s\geq 0
, there exists a curve
\eta_{s}\in\Gamma_{K_{s}\to K}
such that

\|\eta_{s}-\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}=\max_{\eta\in\Gamma_{K_{s}\to K}}\|\eta-\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}
. We claim that

(3.5)

\|\eta_{s}-\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\to 0,\quad s\to 0.

By contradiction, assume that there exists
\delta_{0}>0
and a sequence
s_{n}\to 0
, such that

(3.6)

\|\eta_{s_{n}}-\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\geq\delta_{0}.

Reasoning similarly as before, the sequence
\{\eta_{s_{n}}\}
is uniformly equibounded and equi-Lipschitz. By Ascoli-Arzelà Theorem, there is a converging sub-sequence. By lower semi-continuity of the distance and since
K_{s}\to\{\gamma(0)\}
, as
s\to 0
, the limit is a minimizing geodesic parametrized by constant speed and joining
\gamma(0)
and
\gamma(\varepsilon)
. Thanks to
[
9
, Thm. 1.1]
, the limit is
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
, contradicting (
3.6
).

In addition, recall that the set of optimal competitors
\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\rm opt}
contains curves parametrized by arc-length, which are projections onto
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of minimizing geodesics joining
\pi(\gamma(-s))
and
\pi(\gamma(\varepsilon))
. The projection
\mathrm{pr}
, see (
2.2
), is continuous, hence uniformly continuous on compact sets. As a consequence, it preserves uniform convergence of bounded family of curves, thus from (
3.5
), it follows that

(3.7)

\max_{\eta\in\Gamma_{K_{s}\to K}}\|\mathrm{pr}(\eta)-\mathrm{pr}(\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon})\|_{\infty}\to 0,\quad\text{as }s\to 0.

Since convergence in the uniform topology implies Hausdorff convergence of supports, we get

(3.8)

\max_{\eta\in\Gamma_{K_{s}\to K}}\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{Haus}}({\mathrm{spt}}(\mathrm{pr}(\eta)),{\mathrm{spt}}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}))\to 0,\quad\text{as }s\to 0,

where we used that
{\mathrm{spt}}(\mathrm{pr}(\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}))={\mathrm{spt}}(\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon})={\mathrm{spt}}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})
. We conclude the proof as (
3.8
) is equivalent to the thesis.
∎

. Plugging in (
4.17
) in the expression (
4.16
) and recalling that
\xi=o(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, by Lemma
4.2
2
, it follows that

L_{3}=\varepsilon\xi\chi(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\xi\frac{m_{1}^{2}-q^{2}\varepsilon^{b-2}}{\sqrt{1+m_{1}^{2}}+\chi(\varepsilon)}=\varepsilon\xi\chi(\varepsilon)-q^{2}\varepsilon^{b-1}\xi^{2}\frac{2(q-1)+o(1)}{\sqrt{1+m_{1}^{2}}+\chi(\varepsilon)}\geq\varepsilon\xi\chi(\varepsilon)-C_{1}\varepsilon^{b-1}\xi^{2},

for some constant
C_{1}>0
, provided that
\varepsilon_{0}
is sufficiently small. This proves (
4.15
).

All in all, putting together the inequalities
(
4.11
), (
4.14
), and (
4.15
), we obtain

(4.18)

\begin{split}L(\nu_{s,\varepsilon}(\cdot,{\varrho}))&\geq s+\hat{y}_{0}+L(\gamma|_{[\hat{y}_{0},\varepsilon]})-C_{1}\varepsilon^{b-1}\xi^{2}=L({\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}})+\hat{y}_{0}-L(\gamma|_{[0,\hat{y}_{0}]})-C_{1}\varepsilon^{b-1}\xi^{2}.\end{split}

By (
2.4
),
L(\gamma|_{[0,\varepsilon]})=\varepsilon+\frac{q^{2}}{2(b-1)}\varepsilon^{b-1}+o(\varepsilon^{b-1})
, hence, by Lemma
4.2
1
, up to shrinking
\varepsilon_{0}
, we find a constant
C_{2}>0
such that
\hat{y}_{0}-L(\gamma|_{[0,\hat{y}_{0}]})\geq-C_{2}\hat{y}_{0}^{b-1}
. Thus, we have

(4.19)

L(\nu_{s,\varepsilon}(\cdot,{\varrho}))\geq L({\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}})-C_{2}\hat{y}_{0}^{b-1}-C_{1}\varepsilon^{b-1}\xi^{2}=L({\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}})-C_{2}C_{3}{\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}-C_{1}\varepsilon^{b-1}\xi^{2},

where, in the last equality, we used that
\hat{y}_{0}^{b-1}=C_{3}{\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}
for some
C_{3}>0
, by (
4.5
). We claim that

(4.20)

\varepsilon^{b-1}\xi^{2}\leq{\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}.

This concludes the proof as inequality (
4.19
) implies (
4.8
), with
K=C_{2}C_{3}+C_{1}
. To prove (
4.20
), observe that, by Lemma
4.2
3
,
\varepsilon^{b-1}\xi^{2}\leq C_{4}\varepsilon^{b-1}\alpha=C_{4}{\varrho}\varepsilon^{-1}
for some constant
C_{4}>0
. Using the assumption on
\varrho
, we see that
\varrho\leq C\varepsilon^{3q-1}\leq\varepsilon^{b}
since
b>2
, or equivalently,
{\varrho}\varepsilon^{-1}\leq{\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}
. Therefore,
\varepsilon^{b-1}\xi^{2}\leq C_{4}{\varrho}\varepsilon^{-1}\leq{\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}
, proving (
4.20
) and concluding the proof.
∎

A direct consequence of Proposition
4.1
is a rough upper bound on the length of loops of optimal competitors.

Corollary 4.3
.

There exist
K,\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for all
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
, all
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, and every loop
\ell
of

, it holds

(4.21)

Proof.

Let

be given by Proposition
4.1
with

. Up to shrinking

if needed, we may assume
\varepsilon_{0}<1
. Fix
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
,
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, and assume that
\omega
has a loop

be loop interval of

as the constant speed reparametrization of

(4.22)

\nu(0)=\pi(\gamma(-s)),\quad\nu(1)=\pi(\gamma(\varepsilon))\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathrm{spt}}(\nu)\subset D_{\widetilde{\beta}}

5.
Geometric obstructions to optimality

In this section, for the reader’s convenience, we report some results on the geometry of plane curves and some obstructions to optimality.

5.1.
Winding number of plane curves and weighted area

Let
\eta:[0,\tau]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a closed continuous curve. For any
y\not\in\mathrm{spt}(\eta)
, the winding number of
\eta
around
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is defined as

\mathrm{ind}(\eta,y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\eta}\frac{d\zeta}{\zeta-y},

under the usual identification
\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{C}
. The function
y\mapsto\mathrm{ind}(\eta,y)
is holomorphic outside
{\mathrm{spt}}(\eta)=\eta([0,\tau])
, and integer-valued. For every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define

\mathscr{E}_{k}(\eta)\coloneqq\{y\not\in\mathrm{spt}(\eta)\,|\,\mathrm{ind}(\eta,y)=k\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathscr{E}(\eta)\coloneqq\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\mathscr{E}_{k}(\eta),

so that the sets
\mathscr{E}_{k}(\eta),\,\mathscr{E}(\eta)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
are open and
\mathscr{E}(\eta)
is bounded. In addition, if
\eta
is simple, by the Jordan Curve Theorem, it divides the plane into two disjoint open connected components. Between those two components, we define
\mathscr{D}(\eta)
to be the bounded one. In this case, we have

(5.1)

\mathscr{D}(\eta)=\mathscr{E}_{\sigma}(\eta)=\mathscr{E}(\eta),

where
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}
. We say that the curve
\eta
is positively (resp. negatively) oriented if
\sigma=1
(resp.
\sigma=-1
) and
\sigma
is called the orientation of the curve.

We define the weighted area of a closed continuous curve
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

A(\eta)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k\,\mathscr{L}_{Q}\left(\mathscr{E}_{k}(\eta)\right),

where

\mathscr{L}_{Q}\left(\mathscr{E}_{k}(\eta)\right)\coloneqq\int_{\mathscr{E}_{k}(\eta)}Q(x)\,dx\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}.

A straightforward application of Stokes’ Theorem shows that, for a closed curve
\eta
,

(5.2)

A(\eta)=\int_{\eta}P(x)^{2}dx_{2}=\int_{I_{\eta}}\dot{\eta}_{2}(t)P(\eta(t))^{2}dt.

Remark 5.1
.

For every
s,\varepsilon>0
and
\omega\in{\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}}
, we may consider the closed curve
\eta\coloneqq\omega\ast\mathrm{pr}(\check{\gamma}_{s,\varepsilon})
, where
\check{\gamma}_{s,\varepsilon}
is the inverse reparametrization of
{\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}}
. Then, by Definition
2.2
2
and the Stokes’ theorem, we see that
A(\eta)=0
.

Finally, we recall a useful inequality on the weighted area of a closed continuous curve
\eta
, which is a consequence of Radó’s isoperimetric inequality, cf.
[
26
]
, namely

(5.3)

|A(\eta)|\leq\frac{L(\eta)^{2}}{4\pi}\sup_{x\in\mathscr{E}(\eta)}|Q(x)|.

5.2.
Geometry of plane curves

For any pair of linearly independent vectors
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, denote by
\angle(v,w)\in(-\pi,\pi)
the oriented angle between them. If
v=\mu w
, for some
\mu>0
, we set
\angle(v,w)\coloneqq 0
. Note that, with this definition,
\angle(v,w)=-\angle(w,v)
. Moreover, we set

(5.4)

\begin{split}\mbox{Tan}^{1}(v,w)&\coloneqq\big\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid\angle(v,u)\in\big(\angle(v,w),\pi\big)\big\},\\
\mbox{Tan}^{-1}(v,w)&\coloneqq\big\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid\angle(v,u)\in\big(-\pi,\angle(v,w)\big)\big\}.\end{split}

Let
\eta\in{\rm AC}([0,\tau],\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a closed, simple curve, parametrized by arc-length. We say that
\eta
is piecewise smooth if there are
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=\tau
, such that
\eta|_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}
is smooth for all
i=0,\ldots,N-1
. For every
i
, we denote by
\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{-})
(resp.
\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{+})
) the left (resp. right) derivative of
\eta
at
t_{i}
, with the convention that
\dot{\eta}(t_{0}^{-})\coloneqq\dot{\eta}(t_{N}^{-})
and
\dot{\eta}(t_{N}^{+})\coloneqq\dot{\eta}(t_{0}^{+})
. Moreover, we set
\delta_{i}\coloneqq\angle(\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{-}),\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{+}))
.

Remark 5.2
.

We adopt the following conventions for angles and tangents when the curve
\eta
, with orientation
\sigma
, has a cusp at
t_{i}
, i.e., when
\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{-})=-\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{+})
:

(1)

if the cusp points outside
D(\eta)
, i.e., if
r\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{-})\notin\mathscr{D}(\eta)
for all
r>0
, then we set

(5.5)

\delta_{i}\coloneqq\sigma\pi,\quad\mathrm{Tan}^{\sigma}(\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{-}),\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{+}))\coloneqq\emptyset,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Tan}^{-\sigma}(\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{-}),\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{+}))\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{r\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{+})\mid r>0\}.

(2)

if the cusp points inside
D(\eta)
, i.e., if
r\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{-})\in\mathscr{D}(\eta)
for small
r>0
, then we set

(5.6)

\delta_{i}\coloneqq-\sigma\pi,\quad\mathrm{Tan}^{\sigma}(\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{-}),\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{+}))\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{r\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{+})\mid r>0\},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Tan}^{-\sigma}(\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{-}),\dot{\eta}(t_{i}^{+}))\coloneqq\emptyset.

Note that, in case (i) we have
\sigma\delta_{i}>0
, while in case (ii) we have
\sigma\delta_{i}<0
, independently of
\sigma
.

Finally, since
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is parametrized by arc-length, there exists a piecewise smooth function
\alpha:[0,\tau]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\dot{\eta}(t)=(\cos\alpha(t),\sin\alpha(t))
for all
t\in[0,\tau]\setminus\{t_{0},\ldots,t_{N}\}
. We can define the signed curvature of
\eta
as

(5.7)

\kappa(t)=\dot{\alpha}(t)\qquad\forall t\in[0,\tau]\setminus\{t_{0},\ldots,t_{N}\}.

Remark 5.3

(Gauss-Bonnet for plane curves)
.

If
\eta
has orientation
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}
, then, the Gauss-Bonnet Theorem implies that

(5.8)

\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\sigma\kappa(t)dt+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\sigma\delta_{i}=2\pi.

Viceversa, if Gauss-Bonnet Theorem (
5.8
) holds with
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}
, then
\sigma
is the orientation of
\eta
.

We collect now some geometric lemmas on plane curves. We refer to
[
9
]
for the proof of Lemmas
5.4
and
5.5
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\eta:[0,\tau]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a piecewise smooth, closed, simple, and arc-length parametrized curve, and let
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}
be its orientation, cf. (
5.1
). Assume that
\angle(\dot{\eta}(t^{-}),\dot{\eta}(t^{+}))\neq\sigma\pi
, cf. Remark
5.2
.
Then, for any
t\in[0,\tau)
, there exists
u\in\mbox{\rm Tan}^{\sigma}(\dot{\eta}(t^{-}),\dot{\eta}(t^{+}))
such that

\eta(t)+ru\in\mathscr{E}(\eta),\quad\forall r\in(0,1).

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\eta:[0,\tau]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
as above and fix
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}
. Then, it holds:

(1)

if
\sigma\kappa|_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}\geq 0
, and
\sigma\delta_{i}\in[0,\pi]
, for every
i=0,\ldots,N-1
, then
\mathscr{D}(\eta)
is convex,
\sigma
is the orientation of
\eta
, and
\sigma\delta_{i}\in[0,\pi)
, for every
i=0,\ldots,N-1
;

(2)

if there are
i_{1},i_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
, with
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
, such that

\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\sigma\kappa(t)\,dt\geq 0\quad\mbox{and}\qquad\sigma\delta_{i}\in[0,\pi],\quad\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\setminus\{i_{1},i_{2}\},

then
\sigma
is the orientation of
\eta
and, in particular,
\mathscr{D}(\eta)=\mathscr{E}_{\sigma}(\eta)
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\eta:[0,\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a smooth, simple and closed curve, parametrized by arc-length. Assume that its curvature
\kappa(t)
has constant sign
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}
and that the angle
\delta\coloneqq\angle(\dot{\eta}(\tau^{-}),\dot{\eta}(0^{+}))
satisfies
\sigma\delta\in[0,\pi)
. Then, there exists
t_{*}\in(0,\tau)
such that
|\kappa(t_{*})|\geq\pi\,\tau^{-1}=\pi L(\eta)^{-1}
.

Proof.

By the Gauss-Bonnet Theorem (
5.8
) applied to the smooth curve
\eta
, we have

(5.9)

\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma\kappa(t)dt=2\pi-\sigma\delta>\pi.

Hence, by the mean value theorem, there exists
t^{*}\in[0,\tau]
such that

(5.10)

|\kappa(t^{*})|=\sigma\kappa(t^{*})=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\sigma\kappa(t)dt>\frac{\pi}{\tau}.\qed

6.
Loops of optimal competitors

This section is devoted to an in-depth study of loops of optimal competitors. In particular, we show that any optimal competitor must have self-intersections, and thus, loops, cf. Definition
3.5
.

Given
s,\varepsilon>0
, let
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\rm{opt}}

\omega
is real-analytic, the set
\mathrm{spt}(\omega)\cap\mathrm{spt}(\gamma)\subset\{\widetilde{P}=0\}
is finite. We index the points of intersection as follows: we set
N=N(\omega)\coloneqq\#\big(\mathrm{spt}(\omega)\cap\mathrm{spt}(\gamma)\big)-1
and we define the unique times
\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}(\omega)
,

, such that

(6.1)

0=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\dots<\tau_{N}=L(\omega)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\widetilde{P}(\tau_{i})=0,\text{ for all }i=0,\dots,N.

Remark 6.1
.

By construction, the intervals
(\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1})\subset[0,L(\omega)]
are the maximal intervals where the functions

and
t\mapsto Q(\omega(t))
have constant sign. Moreover, note that by Lemma
2.5
, for every
1\leq i\leq N
, we have
\omega(\tau_{i})\in\{\widetilde{P}=0,x_{1}>0\}
and thus,
\omega_{2}(\tau_{i})>0>\omega_{2}(\tau_{0})=-s
.

Definition 6.2
.

Given
s,\varepsilon>0
and
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, we set
\mathscr{I}=\mathscr{I}(\omega)\coloneqq\{0,\dots,N(\omega)-1\}
and

, for

. We say that:

(1)

is

, and

is referred to as a

, if
\widetilde{P}\circ\omega|_{I_{i}}\geq 0
;

(2)

I_{i}
is
negative
, and

is referred to as a

, if
\widetilde{P}\circ\omega|_{I_{i}}\leq 0
.

Finally, we write
\mathscr{I}_{+}
(resp.
\mathscr{I}_{-}
) for the set of positive (resp. negative) indices.

Observe that the maps
I_{i}\ni t\mapsto\widetilde{P}(\omega(t)),Q(\omega(t))
are positive if

and negative if

.

Definition 6.3
.

Given
s,\varepsilon>0
and
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, let

be a loop of
\omega
, with associated interval

is the

of

is

(resp.,

{J_{\ell}}\subset I_{i}

(resp.,

).

.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for every

,

(2)

(4)

\omega_{1}(t)<\big(1+\frac{b}{\sqrt{b-1}}\big)\varepsilon^{q}

Proof.

Proof of

0\in\mathscr{I}_{+}

denotes the inverse reparametrization of

and, recall that, by Remark

. The open set

Hence, by Stokes’ Theorem, it holds

A(\eta)=\int_{I_{\eta}}\dot{\eta}_{2}(t)P^{2}(\eta(t))dt=\sigma\int_{\mathscr{D}(\eta)}Q(x,y)dxdy\neq 0,

where

, giving a contradiction.

Proof of
2
. Observe that, if

,

is a straight line by Lemma

.

Proof of
3

(0,-s)
and

. We argue by contradiction, comparing

with the length of the concatenation of two line segments
[(0,-s),\omega(t)]\ast[\omega(t),(\varepsilon^{q},\varepsilon)]

such that
\omega_{2}(t)=2\varepsilon
. Then, it follows

Lemma 7.4

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0

Proof.

Consider the square

, for some

, as

. Then, the weighted area enclosed by

\begin{split}|A(\partial R^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})|&=\int_{\varepsilon-\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\int_{\varepsilon^{q}}^{\varepsilon^{q}+\alpha}4x_{1}(x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{b})dx_{1}dx_{2}\\

1

∎

Lemma 7.6

Lemma 7.7

\varepsilon_{0}>0

and

\ell

negative

\ell

positive

Lemma 7.8
.

There exist
C,\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for all
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, the first simple loop
\ell
of
\omega
satisfies

(7.28)

|\lambda|\beta_{\ell}^{2}\leq C.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be given by Lemma
7.6
. Take
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
with first simple loop
\ell
. Set

. We claim that

(7.29)

\frac{N}{4}\leq x_{\ell}\leq 4N.

To prove (
7.29
), we distinguish two cases, according to the sign of

.

Case 1:
P(x_{\ell},y_{\ell})>0
.
We have
0<x_{\ell}^{2}=y_{\ell}^{b}+\beta_{\ell}
. If
N=|y_{\ell}|^{q}
, then
|y_{\ell}|^{b}\geq\beta_{\ell}
and, in particular,
y_{\ell}^{b}>0
. Hence, we deduce that

N^{2}=y_{\ell}^{b}\leq y_{\ell}^{b}+\beta_{\ell}=x_{\ell}^{2}\leq 2y_{\ell}^{b}=2N^{2},

which implies
N\leq x_{\ell}\leq\sqrt{2}N
.
If instead
N=\sqrt{\beta_{\ell}}
, then, by Lemma
7.7
, we have
x_{\ell}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\beta_{\ell}}=\frac{1}{2}N
. While the upper bound is given by

. Thus,
2^{-1}N\leq x_{\ell}\leq\sqrt{2}N
.

Case 2:

We have

, which implies that
0\leq\beta_{\ell}<y_{\ell}^{b}
and thus

. Therefore, we easily see that

. For the lower bound, we distinguish two cases: either

or

x_{\ell}^{2}=y_{\ell}^{b}-\beta_{\ell}\geq\frac{1}{2}y_{\ell}^{b}=\frac{1}{2}N^{2}

. In the other case, we use Lemma
7.7
to obtain

Claim (
7.29

(7.30)

\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{4N}\leq\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{x_{\ell}}\leq 4N.

Consider the interval
\displaystyle I_{c}\coloneqq\Big[t_{\ell}-c\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{x_{\ell}},t_{\ell}\Big]
, where

has to be chosen later. Observe that, by Lemma
7.7
, and since
\omega
is parametrized by arc-length, we have

(7.31)

t_{\ell}\geq x_{\ell}\geq\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{4x_{\ell}}\geq c\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{x_{\ell}},

and hence
I_{c}\subset[0,L(\omega)]
. By
|\dot{\omega}_{1}|\leq 1
, (
7.29
) and (
7.30
), for

we have

(7.32)

\omega_{1}(t)\leq x_{\ell}+|\omega_{1}(t)-x_{\ell}|\leq x_{\ell}+c\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{x_{\ell}}\leq 4(1+c)N,

and, similarly,

(7.33)

\omega_{1}(t)\geq x_{\ell}-|\omega_{1}(t)-x_{\ell}|\geq x_{\ell}-c\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{x_{\ell}}\geq\left(\frac{1}{4}-4c\right)N,

From

) and (

,

(7.36)

In particular, the sign of

does not change for every

, and thus we can apply Lemma

to bound the total curvature of

such that, for all

of

Proof.

\omega|_{I_{0}}
is injective and the open set

enclosed by

and the arc of

from

to

in

\max_{(x,y)\in\overline{\Omega}}\widetilde{P}(x,y)=\max_{\tau\in I_{0}}\widetilde{P}(\omega(\tau))\leq\widetilde{\beta}\leq\varepsilon^{M},

where, in the last inequality, we used Corollary

. On the other hand, by convexity of

. Since

(7.43)

\varepsilon^{M}\geq\max_{(x,y)\in\overline{\Omega}}\widetilde{P}(x,y)\geq P\left(\frac{1}{2}\omega(\tau_{1})\right)=\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{b}}\right)\omega_{2}(\tau_{1})^{b}.

By Lemma
6.7
2
, we have
\omega_{2}(\tau_{2})\leq\omega_{2}(\tau_{1})
. Moreover, as

is injective, the first simple loop
\ell
of

.
∎

Corollary 7.11

(Uniqueness of the loop)
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and all
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}

\omega

. Moreover, only one of the following situations may occur:

(3)

and

, i.e.,

, with

and

.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be as in Proposition
7.1
, and fix an optimal competitor
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, with
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
. Then, the first simple loop
\ell
of
\omega
is contained in the region
\{x_{2}\geq C\varepsilon\}
, for some constant

. We distinguish two cases.

Case 1:

.
We claim that, in this case,

is the unique loop of
\omega
and

, so that we are in situation

. First, by Lemma
6.6
2
, we have that

contains only the loop
\ell
. If by contradiction

, then

. By Lemma
6.6
3

admits a loop

\omega_{2}(s_{\bar{\ell}}^{-})\geq\frac{C}{2}\varepsilon
. Thus, we obtain a contradiction by Lemma

2

Case 2:

\omega|_{I_{0}}
is injective. By Lemma
6.8
,

. Since
\ell
is contained in the region

,

is the unique loop of

by Lemma
6.6
3
. Moreover,

must be injective for every

by Lemma

2

. This implies that

is the unique loop. Finally, Lemmas
6.6
3
and

imply
\mathscr{I}\subset\{0,1,2\}

or

.
∎

For the next result, we introduce the following notation: for
\varepsilon,s>0
and
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, define

(7.44)

T_{0}=T_{0}(\omega)\coloneqq\min\{t\in I_{0}\mid\theta(t)\leq 0\}

Note that the definition is well-posed since

.

Corollary 7.12
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and for all
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
,
T_{0}(\omega)>0
and there is a unique

such that
\omega_{2}(t_{\omega})=0
. Moreover,
\theta(t_{\omega})\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be such that Proposition
7.1
and Corollary
7.11
holds, and fix an optimal competitor
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, with
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}
. Let
\ell
be the unique loop of
\omega
, with loop interval
J_{\ell}
. We start by proving the following claim:

(7.45)

Assume that

and assume by contradiction that

. By the definition of
T_{0}
,
\theta(t)>0
for all

. Since

. Therefore,
\omega_{1}
is strictly monotone in

and thus,
\omega|_{I_{0}}
cannot have self-intersections. This is in contradiction with

. Conversely, assume that
T_{0}<\tau_{1}
. Note that either
T_{0}=0
or
\theta(T_{0})=0
. Consider the half-line
r(t)\coloneqq\omega(T_{0})+t\dot{\omega}(T_{0})
,

and the curve
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by

(7.46)

\Gamma(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}(0,t-s)\quad&t\in(-\infty,0);\\
\omega(t)\quad&t\in[0,T_{0}];\\
r(t-T_{0})\quad&t\in[T_{0},+\infty).\end{cases}

By construction,
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{\mathrm{spt}}(\Gamma)
has two unbounded connected components and we let
\Omega
be the one not containing
(\varepsilon^{q},\varepsilon)
. Observe that, since
\omega_{1}
is strictly increasing on
[0,T_{0}]
and
[0,T_{0}]\subsetneq I_{0}
,
{\mathrm{spt}}\left(\mathrm{pr}({\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}})\right)\cap{\mathrm{spt}}(\Gamma)=\{(0,-s)\}
and
\widetilde{P}|_{\Omega}>0
. Since
\theta
is monotone decreasing in
I_{0}
, it follows that
\omega(T_{0}+\delta)\in\Omega
, for small

. Consequently, there is

, such that
\omega(t)\in\partial\Omega
, and we let

. By Lemma
2.5
, either
\omega(t_{*})\in{\mathrm{spt}}(\omega|_{[0,T_{0}]})
or
\omega(t_{*})\in{\mathrm{spt}}(r)
. If
\omega(t_{*})\in{\mathrm{spt}}(\omega|_{[0,T_{0}]})
,
\omega|_{I_{0}}
has a loop
\ell
, with

. If, instead
\omega(t_{*})\in{\mathrm{spt}}(r)
, by
[
9
, Lem. 3.4]

has a loop
\ell

) is proved. We now conclude the proof: we distinguish the two cases, where either

or

.

Case 1:

. From the proof of the claim (
7.45
) it follows that
{\mathrm{spt}}(\ell)\subset\overline{\Omega}\subset\{x_{2}\leq\omega_{2}(T_{0})\}
and, in particular,
\omega_{2}(T_{0})\geq\omega_{2}(t)\geq C\varepsilon
, for every

. Since
\omega_{2}(T_{0})\geq C\varepsilon
, then

(indeed, if

, then

) and, by Lemma

,

intersects exactly once the positive

-axis at a time

.

Case 2:

. By (
7.45
),

and, since

has exactly one intersection with the

cannot intersect the positive

. Finally, as

, combining Proposition

-axis.
∎

8.
Estimates of
\widetilde{P}
along optimal competitors

This section is devoted to collect some estimates involving the functions
P
and
\widetilde{P}
when they are evaluated on optimal competitors. These estimates play a double role: on the one hand, they lead us to prove that
\widetilde{P}
is positive on optimal competitors; on the other hand, they will be used in the next section to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 8.1
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for every
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and every
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, the first simple loop
\ell
of
\omega
satisfies

(8.1)

\displaystyle|\lambda_{\omega}|\beta_{\ell}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be as in Corollary
7.11
, and fix an optimal competitor
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, with
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
. Let
\ell
be the first loop of
\omega
, with loop interval
J_{\ell}
. For every
t\in I_{\omega}
, define
F(t)\coloneqq\lambda P(\omega(t))^{2}-\sin(\theta(t))
, where
\theta
is the angle map of
\omega
. Using (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we compute

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=2\lambda P(\omega(t))\left(2\omega_{1}(t)\cos(\theta(t))-b\omega_{2}(t)^{b-1}\sin(\theta(t))\right)-\dot{\theta}(t)\cos(\theta(t))

\displaystyle=-\dot{\theta}(t)\frac{b}{2}\frac{\omega_{2}(t)^{b-1}}{\omega_{1}(t)}\sin(\theta(t)).

Hence, for every time interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]\subset J_{\ell}
, with
t_{1}<t_{2}
, we deduce that

(8.2)

\left|F(t_{2})-F(t_{1})\right|\leq\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left|\dot{\theta}(t)\frac{b}{2}\frac{\omega_{2}(t)^{b-1}}{\omega_{1}(t)}\sin(\theta(t))\right|\leq\pi b\frac{(2\varepsilon)^{b-1}}{C\varepsilon^{q}},

where, in the last inequality, we used Proposition
7.1
and Lemma
6.5
2
to estimate
\omega_{1}\geq C\varepsilon^{q}
, for a suitable
C>0
, and we also used Lemma
6.4
3
to estimate
\omega_{2}\leq 2\varepsilon
and Lemma
6.6
1
to estimate
\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|\dot{\theta}|dt\leq 2\pi
. Therefore, for every time interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]\subset J_{\ell}
, we have

(8.3)

\left|F(t_{2})-F(t_{1})\right|\leq C_{0}\varepsilon^{q-1},

where
C_{0}=\pi b2^{b-1}C^{-1}
. By definition of
F
, the latter inequality implies that

(8.4)

\left|\lambda\left(P^{2}(\omega(t_{2}))-P^{2}(\omega(t_{1}))\right)\right|\geq|\sin(\theta(t_{2}))-\sin(\theta(t_{1}))|-C_{0}\varepsilon^{q-1}.

By (
5.8
), the times
t_{1},t_{2}\in J_{\ell}
can be chosen so that
|\sin(\theta(t_{2}))-\sin(\theta(t_{1}))|\geq 1
. Thus, up to shrinking
\varepsilon_{0}
, we get

\Big|\lambda\Big(P^{2}(\omega(t_{2}))-P^{2}(\omega(t_{1}))\Big)\Big|\geq 1/2.

The conclusion now follows by observing that
|P^{2}(\omega(t_{2}))-P^{2}(\omega(t_{1}))|\leq 2\beta_{\ell}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 8.2
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for all
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and all
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
, with loop
\ell
, the following holds. Let
t_{*}\in I_{\omega}\setminus J_{\ell}
is a local maximum for
t\mapsto\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))
satisfying
\widetilde{P}(\omega(t_{*}))>0
. Assume that either
t_{*}\in I_{0}
, or
\omega_{2}(\tau_{i})\leq\varepsilon
, for all
i\in\mathscr{I}
. Then, we have

(8.5)

|\lambda|\widetilde{P}(\omega(t_{*}))^{\frac{q+1}{q}}\leq\frac{b(b-1)}{8}.

Proof.

By Corollary
7.12
, there is a unique
\bar{t}\in I_{0}
such that
\omega_{2}(\bar{t})=0
and
\omega_{1}|_{(0,\bar{t}]}
is strictly increasing and positive, and thus the same holds for

. Therefore, it must be
t_{*}\geq\bar{t}
, which implies
\widetilde{P}(\omega(t_{*}))=P(\omega(t_{*}))
, and
t_{*}
is a local maximum for
P\circ\omega
. If we show that, under our assumptions,

(8.6)

\sin(\theta(t_{*}))>0,

then, the conclusion follows by
[
9
, Lem. 3.14]
. We now prove (
8.6
). Assume by contradiction that
\sin(\theta(t_{*}))\leq 0
. Since
\omega

to the graph of

\Gamma_{\varrho}
, with
{\varrho}\coloneqq P(\omega(t))
, cf. Section
4
, then
\sin(\theta(t_{*}))<0
and
\cos(\theta(t_{*}))<0
as well. Let
i\in\mathscr{I}_{+}
be such that
t_{*}\in I_{i}
and define the curve

\eta:(-\infty,t_{*}]\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad\eta(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathrm{pr}(\gamma)(t),\quad&t\in(-\infty,\tau_{i}],\\
\omega(t),\quad&t\in(\tau_{i},t_{*}],\end{cases}

and the half-line
r(t)\coloneqq\omega(t_{*})+t\dot{\omega}(t_{*})
, for

. Since

{\mathrm{spt}}(\eta)\cap{\mathrm{spt}}(r)\neq\emptyset
. Then, the minimum

\bar{\eta}:[t_{0},t_{*}+t_{1}]\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad\bar{\eta}(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\eta(t),\quad&t\in[t_{0},t_{*}],\\
r(t-t_{*}),\quad&t\in(t_{*},t_{*}+t_{1}],\end{cases}

5.7
3

We are now in position to conclude the proof of (

). Since

, by Corollary
7.11

or

The next proposition shows that
\widetilde{P}
is positive along an optimal competitor. This implies that the only case of Corollary

that can happen is item

, i.e., the one where

.

Proposition 8.3
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for all
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and for all
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
,
\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))>0
, for every

.

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity, in this proof,
C>0
denotes a generic constant depending only on

, whose value may change from line to line. Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be small enough such that all the previous statements hold. Take
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
and
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
. From Lemma
6.8
and Corollary
7.11
,
\omega
admits a unique loop
\ell
, and, either
J_{\ell}\subset I_{0}
(and then
I_{0}=I_{\omega}
) or
J_{\ell}\subset I_{1}
. In particular,
\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))>0
for all

if and only if
J_{\ell}\subset I_{0}
. Suppose, by contradiction,
J_{\ell}\subset I_{1}
and we distinguish the two cases where
\omega_{2}(\tau_{2})\leq\varepsilon
or
\omega_{2}(\tau_{2})>\varepsilon
.

Case 1:
\omega_{2}(\tau_{2})\leq\varepsilon
.
Set
\widetilde{\beta}_{+}\coloneqq\max_{t\in I_{\omega}}\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))
and let

. Since
t_{*}
is a local maximum of
\widetilde{P}\circ\omega
with
\widetilde{P}(\omega(t_{*}))>0
and

, Lemma
8.2
implies that

(8.8)

|\lambda|\widetilde{\beta}_{+}^{\frac{q+1}{q}}\leq C.

Combining the latter inequality with Lemma

, we conclude that

(8.9)

\widetilde{\beta}_{+}\leq C\beta_{\ell}^{\frac{b}{q+1}}.

We apply Proposition
4.1
to the curve
\eta\coloneqq\omega|_{[0,s_{\ell}^{-}]}\ast\omega|_{[s_{\ell}^{+},L(\omega)]}
, with
\varrho\coloneqq\widetilde{\beta}_{+}
, to deduce that

(8.10)

L(\omega)=L(\eta)+L(\ell)\geq L(\gamma_{s,\varepsilon})-C\widetilde{\beta}_{+}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}+L(\ell)\geq L(\omega)-C\beta_{\ell}^{\frac{b-1}{q+1}}+L(\ell),

where, in the last inequality, we used (

(8.11)

L(\ell)\leq C\beta_{\ell}^{\frac{b-1}{q+1}}\leq C\beta_{\ell},

where in the last inequality we used that

. Conversely, we can find a lower bound on

, combining Proposition
7.9
, together with Lemma
6.4
3
-
4
, obtaining

(8.12)

L(\ell)\geq C\beta_{\ell}\varepsilon^{-q}.

The last two inequalities lead to the contradiction

.

Case 2:
\omega_{2}(\tau_{2})>\varepsilon
.
In this case, we set
\widetilde{\beta}_{+}\coloneqq\max_{t\in[0,\tau_{2}]}\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))
. Let
t_{*}\in[0,\tau_{2}]\setminus I_{1}=I_{0}
be such that
\widetilde{P}(\omega(t_{*}))=\widetilde{\beta}_{+}
. As before,
t_{*}\in I_{0}\setminus J_{\ell}
is a local maximum of
\widetilde{P}\circ\omega
with
\widetilde{P}\circ\omega(t_{*})>0
. Then, reasoning as in the previous case, Lemmas
8.1
and

imply

(8.13)

\widetilde{\beta}_{+}\leq C\beta_{\ell}^{\frac{b}{q+1}}.

We apply Proposition
4.1
to the curve
\eta\coloneqq\omega|_{[0,s_{\ell}^{-}]}\ast\omega|_{[s_{\ell}^{+},\tau_{2}]}
, to deduce that

(8.14)

L(\eta)\geq L(\gamma_{s,\omega_{2}(\tau_{2})})-C\widetilde{\beta}_{+}^{\prime 1-\frac{1}{b}}\geq L(\omega)-C\beta_{\ell}^{\frac{b-1}{q+1}},

where, in the last inequality, we used (
8.13
) and the estimate
L(\gamma_{s,\omega_{2}(\tau_{2})})\geq L({\gamma_{s,\varepsilon}})\geq L(\omega)
which holds since
\omega(\tau_{2})>\varepsilon
and
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
. Therefore, we get

(8.15)

L(\omega)=L(\eta)+L(\ell)+L(\omega|_{I_{2}})\geq L(\eta)+L(\ell)\geq L(\omega)-C\beta_{\ell}^{\frac{b-1}{q+1}}+L(\ell).

We reach a contradiction reasoning as in the previous case.
∎

9.
Proof of the main theorem

Along this section
C>0
denotes a
generic constant depending only on
b
, and its value may change from line to line. Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be such that all the statements in the previous sections hold, and fix
(\varepsilon,s)\in\mathscr{I}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
, and
\omega\in\mathscr{C}_{s,\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{opt}}
. Recall that
\omega
has a unique loop
\ell
and, by Proposition
8.3
,
J_{\ell}\subset I_{0}=[0,L(\omega)]
, and thus,
\widetilde{P}\circ\omega\geq 0
and
\widetilde{\beta}=\max_{t\in I_{\omega}}\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))
. Define the two quantities

(9.1)

\zeta\coloneqq\sqrt{\widetilde{\beta}\varepsilon^{-b}}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad{\varrho}\coloneqq\left(\widetilde{\beta}\varepsilon^{-1}\right)^{\frac{b}{b-1}}.

Note that, since
\widetilde{\beta}\varepsilon^{-b}<\varepsilon^{M-b}<1
by Corollary
3.3
(recall that
M>b
fixed),
\varrho<\widetilde{\beta}
. In addition, if
t_{*}\in I_{0}\setminus J_{\ell}
is a local maximum for
P\circ\omega
, then combining Lemmas
8.1
and
8.2
, we obtain that

(9.2)

\widetilde{P}(\omega(t_{*}))\leq C\widetilde{\beta}^{\frac{b}{q+1}}\leq{\varrho}<\widetilde{\beta}.

This fact implies, in particular,

(9.3)

\widetilde{\beta}=\beta_{\ell}.

At this stage, let us define the curve

(9.4)

\nu\coloneqq{\mathrm{spt}}(\omega|_{[0,s_{\ell}^{-}]})\cup{\mathrm{spt}}(\omega|_{[s_{\ell}^{+},L(\omega)]}),

whose length, by optimality of
\omega
, satisfies

(9.5)

L(\gamma_{s,\varepsilon})\geq L(\omega)=L(\ell)+L(\nu).

We divide the proof into the two cases where
\widetilde{P}(\omega(s_{\ell}^{\pm}))\leq{\varrho}
and
\widetilde{P}(\omega(s_{\ell}^{\pm}))>{\varrho}
.

Case 1:
\widetilde{P}(\omega(s_{\ell}^{-}))\leq{\varrho}
.
In this case, since
\varrho<\widetilde{\beta}
, we may apply Proposition
4.1
to
\nu
and estimate
L(\nu)\geq L(\gamma_{s,\varepsilon})-C{\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}
. Therefore, by (
9.5
) we get

(9.6)

L(\ell)\leq C{\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}=C\widetilde{\beta}\varepsilon^{-1}.

However, by Propositions
7.9
and
7.1
,
L(\ell)\geq C\widetilde{\beta}\varepsilon^{-q}
, which is a contradiction for small
\varepsilon>0
.

Case 2:
\widetilde{P}(\omega(s_{\ell}^{-}))>{\varrho}
.
Define
t^{-},t^{+}\in I_{\omega}
by

(9.7)

t^{-}\coloneqq\max\Bigl\{t\in[0,s_{\ell}^{-}]\,|\,\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))={\varrho}\Bigr\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad t^{+}\coloneqq\min\Bigl\{t\in[s_{\ell}^{+},L(\omega)]\,|\,\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))={\varrho}\Bigr\}.

Then, by construction,
0<t^{-}<s_{\ell}^{-}<t_{\ell}<s_{\ell}^{+}<t^{+}<L(\omega)
. Moreover, by (
9.2
), we deduce that

(9.8)

\widetilde{P}(\omega(t))<{\varrho}\qquad\mathrm{for\;all\;}t\in I_{0}\setminus(t^{-},t^{+}).

Lemma 9.1
.

Under the assumptions and notations above, we have
\omega_{2}(t^{-})<\omega_{2}(t^{+})
and

(9.9)

t_{\ell}\left(1-\zeta\right)<t^{-}\qquad\text{and}\qquad t^{+}<t_{\ell}\left(1+\zeta\right).

Proof.

Combining Corollary
4.3
with (
9.3
) and the estimate
t_{\ell}\geq y_{\ell}
, we get

(9.10)

\frac{t_{\ell}-s_{\ell}^{-}}{t_{\ell}\zeta}\leq\frac{L(\ell)}{t_{\ell}\zeta}\leq C\widetilde{\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{b}}y_{\ell}^{\frac{b}{2}-1}<\frac{1}{2},

because
\widetilde{\beta},y_{\ell}\to 0
as

\varepsilon\to 0
. This shows that
s_{\ell}^{-}>t_{\ell}\left(1-\frac{\zeta}{2}\right)
. Assume now by contradiction that
t^{-}<t_{\ell}(1-\zeta)
and set
I\coloneqq[t_{\ell}(1-\zeta),s_{\ell}^{-}]
. Then, by (
9.7
) and (
9.8
), it holds

(9.11)

P(\omega(t))>{\varrho},\quad\text{for all }t\in I.

The contradiction is reached by estimating from above and from below the total curvature of
\omega
on
I
. By the Gauss-Bonnet Theorem (
5.8
), we can estimate from above

(9.12)

\int_{I}|\dot{\theta}(t)|dt\leq C.

On the other hand, exploiting (
9.11
) and (
9.10
), we can estimate from below

(9.13)

\int_{I}|\dot{\theta}(t)|dt=4|\lambda|\int_{I}P(\omega(t))\omega_{1}(t)dt\geq 4|\lambda||I|{\varrho}\min_{t\in I}\omega_{1}(t)\geq 2|\lambda|t_{\ell}\,\zeta\,{\varrho}\min_{t\in I}\omega_{1}(t).

We claim that

(9.14)

\min_{t\in I}\omega_{1}(t)\geq C\varepsilon^{q}.

Indeed, by Proposition
7.1
, we have
\omega_{2}(s_{\ell}^{-})\geq C\varepsilon
, and, recalling that
s_{\ell}^{-}<t_{\ell}<\varepsilon
, it holds
|I|\leq t_{\ell}\zeta\leq\varepsilon^{1+\frac{M-b}{2}}
, having used Corollary
3.3
and (
9.1
). Since
M>b
and
|\dot{\omega}_{2}|\leq 1
, we estimate

(9.15)

\omega_{2}\big(t_{\ell}(1-\zeta)\big)=\omega_{2}(s_{\ell}^{-})-\int^{s_{\ell}^{-}}_{t_{\ell}(1-\zeta)}\dot{\omega}_{2}(\tau)d\tau\geq C\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{1+\frac{M-b}{2}}\geq C\varepsilon,

provided that
\varepsilon>0
is small. The latter inequality, together with Lemma
6.5
(ii), implies (
9.14
). By (
9.14
) and Lemma
8.1
, and recalling (
9.1
) and (
9.3
), we can continue from (
9.13
) to deduce that

(9.16)

\int_{I}|\dot{\theta}(t)|dt\geq C|\lambda|t_{\ell}\,\zeta\,{\varrho}\,\varepsilon^{q}\geq C\widetilde{\beta}^{-2}t_{\ell}\,\zeta\,{\varrho}\,\varepsilon^{q}=Ct_{\ell}\widetilde{\beta}^{\frac{b}{b-1}-\frac{3}{2}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{b}{b-1}}\geq C\widetilde{\beta}^{\frac{b}{b-1}-\frac{3}{2}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{b-1}},

where, in the last inequality, we used that
t_{\ell}\geq y_{\ell}\geq C\varepsilon
, by Proposition
7.1
. Finally, combining the estimates (
9.12
) and (
9.16
), we reach a contradiction for small
\varepsilon>0
, since, for all
b\geq 5
,

(9.17)

\frac{b}{b-1}-\frac{3}{2}<0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\beta}^{\frac{b}{b-1}-\frac{3}{2}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{b-1}}\leq C.

We finally prove the inequality
\omega_{2}(t^{-})<\omega_{2}(t^{+})
. Assume by contradiction that
\omega_{2}(t^{-})\geq\omega_{2}(t^{+})
.
If
\omega_{2}(t^{-})=\omega_{2}(t^{+})
then it holds that
\omega(t^{-})=\omega(t^{+})
, contradicting the uniqueness of the loop. If instead
\omega_{2}(t^{-})>\omega_{2}(t^{+})
, let
U
be the open and bounded region with boundary

\Gamma\coloneqq{\mathrm{spt}}(\omega|_{[0,t^{-}]})\cup\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid\widetilde{P}(x)={\varrho},x_{1}>0,-s\leq x_{2}\leq\omega_{2}(t^{-})\}\cup\{(x_{1},-s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid 0\leq x_{1}\leq\sqrt{\varrho}\}.

Then, by (
9.7
) and (
9.8
),
\omega(t^{+}+\delta)\in U
for
\delta>0
sufficiently small. Since
\omega(L(\omega))=\pi(\gamma(\varepsilon))\notin U
, there exists
\bar{t}\in(t^{+},L(\omega))
such that
\omega(\bar{t})\in\partial U
.
If
\omega(\bar{t})\in{\mathrm{spt}}(\omega|_{[0,t^{-}]})
, we contradict the uniqueness of the loop; if
\omega(\bar{t})\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid\widetilde{P}(x)={\varrho},\,x_{1}>0,\,-s\leq x_{2}\leq\omega_{2}(t^{-})\}
, we contradict (
9.8
); and if
\omega(\bar{t})\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid x_{2}=-s,\,0\leq x_{1}\leq{\varrho}^{\frac{1}{a}}\}
, then
\omega_{2}(\bar{t})=-s
and we reach a contradiction combining Proposition
7.1
and Lemma
6.5
1
.
∎

Define
s^{\pm}\coloneqq\omega_{2}(t^{\pm})
, and let
\bar{\nu}:[0,L(\bar{\nu})]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the curve given by the concatenation

(9.18)

\bar{\nu}=\omega|_{[0,t^{-}]}*\Gamma_{\varrho}|_{[s^{-},s^{+}]}*\omega|_{[t^{+},L(\nu)]},

where
\Gamma_{\varrho}(t)=(f_{\varrho}(t),t)=\big(\sqrt{t^{b}+{\varrho}},t\big)
, cf. Section
4
.
By (
9.8
), it holds
{\mathrm{spt}}(\bar{\nu})\subset E_{\varrho}
and thus, applying Proposition
4.1
, we estimate

(9.19)

L(\bar{\nu})\geq L(\gamma_{s,\varepsilon})-C{\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}\geq L(\ell)+L(\nu)-C{\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}},

where we used (
9.5
) in the second inequality. Hence, we obtain the following lower bound on
\varrho
:

(9.20)

C{\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}\geq L(\ell)+L(\nu)-L(\bar{\nu})\geq L(\ell)+L([\Gamma_{\varrho}(s^{-}),\Gamma_{\varrho}(s^{+})])-L(\Gamma_{\varrho}|_{[s^{-},s^{+}]}),

where
[\Gamma_{\varrho}(s^{-}),\Gamma_{\varrho}(s^{+})]
is the segment joining
\Gamma_{\varrho}(s^{-})
to
\Gamma_{\varrho}(s^{+})
. To estimate from below the latter quantity, we use
[
9
, Prop. 2.1(ii)]
, whose proof is unchanged in our setting: for all
0<\bar{t}<t
, and
{\varrho}>0
, we have

(9.21)

L\left(\Gamma_{\varrho}|_{[\bar{t},t]}\right)-L([\Gamma_{\varrho}(\bar{t}),\Gamma_{\varrho}(t)])\leq\frac{1}{2}q^{2}(q-1)t^{b-1}\left(1-\frac{\bar{t}}{t}\right)^{2}.

In particular, we apply (
9.21
) with
t=s^{+}
and
\bar{t}=s^{-}
, obtaining

(9.22)

L([\Gamma_{\varrho}(s^{-}),\Gamma_{\varrho}(s^{+})])-L(\Gamma_{\varrho}|_{[s^{-},s^{+}]})\geq-C(s^{+})^{b-1}\Big(1-\frac{s^{-}}{s^{+}}\Big)^{2}.

As
t_{+}>t_{\ell}
, by Lemma
6.5
1
and Proposition
7.1
, it holds
s^{+}\geq C\varepsilon
. Moreover, as a consequence of Lemma
9.1
, we deduce that
0\leq s^{+}-s^{-}=\omega_{2}(t^{+})-\omega_{2}(t^{-})\leq t^{+}-t^{-}\leq 2t_{\ell}\zeta\leq 4\varepsilon\zeta
. Therefore, we obtain

(9.23)

0\leq 1-\frac{s^{-}}{s^{+}}\leq C\zeta.

From (
9.22
) and (
9.23
), and from the definition of
\zeta
in (
9.1
), we finally deduce

(9.24)

L([\Gamma_{\varrho}(s^{-}),\Gamma_{\varrho}(s^{+})])-L(\Gamma_{\varrho}|_{[s^{-},s^{+}]})\geq-C\varepsilon^{b-1}\zeta^{2}=-C\varepsilon^{-1}\widetilde{\beta}.

We are in position complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
. From (
9.19
) and (
9.24
), we get

(9.25)

L(\ell)\leq C\left({\varrho}^{1-\frac{1}{b}}+\varepsilon^{-1}\widetilde{\beta}\right)=2C\varepsilon^{-1}\widetilde{\beta}.

From the latter estimate and Proposition
7.9
, we derive the inequality

(9.26)

\varepsilon^{-q}\widetilde{\beta}\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}\widetilde{\beta},

which is a contradiction for small
\varepsilon>0
.
