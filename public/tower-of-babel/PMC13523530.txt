Profiling the disease burden in Teso subregion during community-based medical education and research services at Soroti University: a prospective study protocol
Authors: Ronald Opito, Amos Odiit, Hellen Akurut, Emmanuel Ayikobua Tiyo, Joash Okoboi, Letizia Maria Atim, Clement Munguiko, Lawrence Obado, Bonniface Oryokot, Fred Kirya, Ruth Muhindo, David Aderu, Patrick Lubogo, Stephen Econyu, Simon Icumar Omeke, Samuel Kabwigu, Amos Deogratius Mwaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunity-based medical education and research services (COBMERS) place medical students in primary health care facilities to strengthen their skills in community health service delivery, teamwork, leadership and professional ethics within limited-resource settings. Beyond training, COBMERS enables universities to generate evidence on local disease epidemiology, transmission dynamics, and control practices, resulting in a tangible, contextualized impact on the communities. There is however limited data on disease profile in Teso subregion, with limited epidemiological research in the area. This prospective study aims to profile the disease burden in the Teso subregion through COBMERS, thereby informing community-level interventions and health policy.MethodsThe study will employ convergent parallel mixed-methods design, integrating quantitative and qualitative approaches. Quantitative components will include cross-sectional surveys to determine prevalence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), and infectious diseases, complemented by prospective cohort studies to assess temporal trends. Qualitative data will be gathered through focus group discussions and key informant interviews to explore community perceptions, health system capacity, and effectiveness of preventive measures. A community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach will guide all phases, ensuring that research is co-designed with community members, health practitioners, and local authorities. Medical students, under the supervision of faculty and site mentors, will serve as primary data collectors, integrating research with their COBMERS training.DiscussionThe study is expected to provide evidence on the prevalence, trends, and risk factors of major diseases in the Teso subregion, alongside community perspectives on health priorities and barriers to care. Findings will stir up debates to inform tailored interventions, strengthen primary health care, and guide policy formulation. Additionally, embedding research within COBMERS is expected to build local research capacity among site mentors and students, fostering sustainability and continued community-university collaboration. This protocol therefore provides an integrated framework that combines epidemiological surveillance, community engagement, and capacity building. By leveraging COBMERS and CBPR, the study seeks to provide actionable evidence to reduce the burden of NCDs, infectious diseases, and NTDs while promoting health system resilience in Eastern Uganda.Keywords: Disease burden, COBMERS, Community, Non-communicable diseases, Epidemiological surveillance, Medical students.

Background

Uganda, like any other low- and middle-income countries continues to experience a high and rapidly changing burden of both communicable and non-communicable diseases. Evidence from Eastern Uganda show that communicable diseases, including malaria, lower respiratory infections, gastro-intestinal tract conditions and urinary tract infections account for more than 80% of diagnosed illnesses, with significant disparity in gender and age [1]. Nationally, malaria accounted for more than 12 million cases and close to 3000 deaths in 2022 alone, with children under 5 most affected [2]. Other communicable diseases such as TB are observed to be on the increase, with a case notification increasing sharply from 29,640 cases in 2020 to 72,768 in 2024 with significant clustering observed in Northeastern Uganda [3].

On the other hand, non-communicable disease (NCD) burden has been rapidly increasing in Uganda and other sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries in the last decades, with significant variation in prevalence by sex and socioeconomic statuses, yet little progress is being made in implementation of prevention strategies [4, 5]. Several individuals remain undiagnosed with these NCDs [6] and approximately 53% of all-cause mortality have been attributed to NCDs among adults in Eastern Uganda [7].

Despite the changing disease burden in Uganda, epidemiological disease profiling in Teso remains limited, and fragmented with the few studies focusing on infectious diseases [8], and national aggregates masking critical district-level variations that drive disparity in health outcomes.

Soroti University, established in 2015 as Uganda’s 9th Public University operates a community-based medical education and research services (COBMERS) program that places medical students in rural health facilities as part of their service learning. This model creates a unique opportunity for systematic, community level disease surveillance in the region.

COBMERS aims to develop a complete medical worker who understands primary healthcare and teamwork for improved health of the population [9]. Research has been incorporated into medical education in many medical schools worldwide, with a focus of teaching research skills and the subsequent applications of those skills after graduation [10, 11].

However, the potential of COBMERS program to generate sub-regional epidemiological disease profile has not been explored. Profiling the disease burden in Teso sub-region will leverage Soroti University’s COBMERS program to generate the first comprehensive, district level disease profile to directly inform resource allocation, enable targeted interventions and contribute to Uganda’s national health sector strategic plan.

Problem statement

There is no consolidated and current profile of disease burden across Teso sub-region. The limited attempts to understand the disease and health problems of the region by Soroti University students and staff [12–14], are fragmented with no clear impact observed. Existing regional and national reports often mask local variations, and the lack of granular up-to-date local data affects health planning where local health officials lack evidence to allocate limited resources to high burdened diseases and makes prevention efforts reactive rather than targeted.

Therefore, this research project will leverage Soroti University’s COBMERS program to document disease prevalence, trends, distribution and their determinants. The resulting evidence base will inform district health planning and guide targeted community interventions to reduce preventable morbidity and mortality in Teso sub-region.

Research project objectives

The overall objective of this prospective study is to profile the burden of diseases in Teso subregion through community based medical education and research services.

The specific objectives are outlined below.

Assess the prevalence of common non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in Teso subregion and the capacity of the health workers to diagnose and manage them

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are chronic diseases that are non-transmissible and usually result from an interplay of genetics, environmental, behavioral, and physiological factors [15, 16]. Such diseases include mental health conditions, cardiovascular disease, cancers, diabetes and, chronic lung disease among others [16, 17]. Risk factors for non-communicable diseases are diverse, and more lifestyle related as compared to infectious diseases. Some of the noticeable risk factors include physical inactivity, poor diet, excessive alcohol consumption and tobacco use. Others include elevated blood pressure, sugar and lipids in blood [18]. Most risk factors for non-communicable diseases are thus modified.

There has been an exponential increase in the burden of NCD in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) in the past two decades, driven by risk factors such as physical inactivity, poor diet, air pollution, high blood pressure and diabetes among others [19]. Because of the harm they do to the nation’s socioeconomic development and the morbidity and mortality associated with them, NCDs are currently a significant public health problem and therefore, it is important to design interventions to curb increasing mortality and morbidity due to them.

Although there has been an increasing burden of NCDs, SSA healthcare systems remain inadequately prepared to face this challenge [20]. A survey among 47 African countries done in 2020 revealed that no country was fully ready to integrate NCD services into primary health care [21]. Notwithstanding the fact that the Uganda Ministry of Health has prioritized NCD prevention, early diagnosis, and management, research on the capacity of public sector health facilities to address NCDs is limited [22]. We therefore aim at unearthing the barriers to detection of NCDs in the community, therefore improving the capacity of community health centers to detect and manage non-communicable diseases.

The study will determine the prevalence of non-communicable diseases and act as a baseline for targeted intervention. The study will help identify barriers to optimum service delivery such as health worker knowledge gaps, logistical barriers and personnel gaps among others. This information will be the basis for targeted intervention and improvement of service delivery for NCD prevention and management. In this study, community perceptions on management of NCDs shall be obtained and used in the design and implementation of programs for improvement of diagnosis and management of NCDs that are appropriate to the setting. Data from the study will help us design an intervention to improve detection and management of NCDs at community health centers. Early detection and proper management of non-communicable diseases will reduce morbidity and mortality, hence improving community productivity. This may lead to overall economic growth of the community. The intervention can also be scaled up to other areas of the country hence promoting the country’s economic growth through reduction of morbidity and mortality.

To determine the trends of common infectious diseases over a period of 10 years. (TB, Malaria, UTIs, hepatitis B, STIs, RTIs etc.) within the Teso sub-region and assess effectiveness in the preventive measures over the same period

Infectious diseases pose a significant public health challenge globally and particularly in low-income countries such as Uganda. These diseases contribute substantially to morbidity, mortality, and economic burden in communities, necessitating a comprehensive understanding of their prevalence, distribution, and impact on healthcare systems [23]. In Uganda, infectious diseases such as malaria, HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, diarrheal diseases, and respiratory infections are major contributors to the disease burden [24–27].

Localized studies specifically focusing on the burden of infectious diseases among patients attending community health facilities in the Teso Sub-Region are limited. Understanding the epidemiological landscape and trends in transmission of infectious diseases in this context is crucial for informing evidence-based healthcare strategies and improving health outcomes. Targeted and community tailored interventions aimed at reducing the morbidity and mortality associated with infectious diseases are needed to enhance current efforts. By gathering robust data on disease prevalence, demographic characteristics, clinical presentations, and healthcare utilization patterns, this study seeks to inform targeted interventions, optimize resource allocation, and ultimately reduce the burden of infectious diseases in the Teso Sub-Region.

To establish the prevalence of common NTDs (Schistosomiasis, Onchocerciasis, trypanosomiasis etc.) around wetland communities in eastern Uganda and the effectiveness of control measures

Neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) continue to afflict billions of individuals in silence, particularly in SSA, with profound effects, accounting for 14.5 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [28]. Schistosomiasis is observed as the most common NTD, leading to 11,792 deaths globally per annum and reducing the average lifespan of an infected individual by over 20% [29].

The financial impact of schistosomiasis coupled with decreased animal yields and increased animal mortality also presents a significant burden to the communities affected by it.

Current control strategies predominantly rely on preventive chemotherapy, mainly using praziquantel. However, there are reports of emerging drug resistance and the high cost of chemotherapy, especially in resource-limited settings. Additionally, the continuous interactions between livestock, humans, and contaminated environments perpetuate reinfections, hindering disease elimination efforts. Recent cuts in funding for schistosomiasis control further exacerbate these challenges [30–33].

Therefore, this study aims to establish the prevalence of schistosomiasis around water bodies in eastern Uganda, determine community and health worker perspectives and treatment approaches and document its findings to inform policy and interventions.

Methodology

Study design

This study will employ convergent parallel mixed-methods design, where quantitative and qualitative data will be collected simultaneously and then merged to provide a comprehensive understanding of the research questions. Data integration and triangulation will be done at the point of interpretation of results from the different sources to complement the findings. The quantitative study will employ cross-sectional surveys for prevalences while prospective cohort studies will be conducted to analyze the trends of community disease prevalences and incidences (NCDs, NTDs and infectious diseases).

We shall use a community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach to conduct qualitative studies to examine the local understanding of disease burden and co-design acceptable preventive measures. The community will participate in the problem analysis to give priority areas for the research and in data collection process to provide oversight and monitoring of the research process, in addition to deciding how the findings should be locally disseminated and utilized. The use of CBPR is justified because it addresses power imbalance that can distort participant responses, enhances validity by grounding data collection and interpretation in lived community experiences, and increases the relevance and uptake of findings by ensuring research questions and solutions reflect community priorities [34–37]. Study area (Soroti University and Teso region)

The study will be conducted by a team of researchers and students from Soroti university. Soroti university is one of the public universities in Uganda established by an Act of Parliament in 2015 to provide science based high education services to the country (https://sun.ac.ug/). The university is in Arapai, Soroti City East, Soroti City, 7 km along Soroti-Moroto road. The university is rapidly growing with a students’ population of about 500 and this is projected to increase exponentially in the next five years (https://sun.ac.ug/). The university offers training in health education to students of medicine, nursing, laboratory science and physiotherapy, and is in the process of expanding to provide more undergraduate medical education in the areas of midwifery among others. As part of their training, the pre-service health workers are exposed early and routinely to the community through community based medical education and research services (COBMERS). This process involves placing students to the primary health care facility, not routinely used as a teaching hospital, for a specified period, usually 4-6weeks to enable them to get acquainted with provision of community health services, teamwork and develop positive attitudes towards the community.

Soroti University has identified ten (10) sites in Teso subregion to act as COBMERS sites. These sites include Katakwi Hospital, Amuria Hospital, Kaberamaido Hospital, Atutur Hospital, Kalaki HCIV, Bukedea HCIV, Kumi HCIV, Serere HCIV, Ngora HCIV and Kapelebyong HCIV. These sites can be expanded, and some replaced based on the need and the situational assessment of the suitability (in terms of students’ accommodation and availability of site tutors). Additionally, there are eight (8) lower health facilities within Soroti City identified to introduce students to COBMERS in their earlier years of study. These facilities include, Eastern Division HCIII, Soroti HCIII, Princess Diana HCIV, TASO Soroti Center of Excellence, Kichinjaji HCIII, Western Division HCIII, Marie stopes Uganda Soroti Center, and Murapesuru HCII. Besides these lower health facilities, the students are placed for their routine clinical exposure in Soroti Regional referral hospital which is the main referral hospital for Teso sub-region. In addition to the 10 sites in Teso, there are some sites in Lango, Northern Uganda, that is Alebtong HCIV, Dokolo HCIV and Apac District Hospital which are being assessed for accreditation. These sites will provide comparisons to the Teso sub-region.

The Teso sub-region is located in Northeastern Uganda and comprises of 10 districts of Serere, Soroti, Soroti City, Ngora, Amuria, Kalaki, Kapelebyong, Kaberamaido, Katakwi, Kumi and Bukedea, with a total population of approximately 2,500,000 [38].

Study population

To assess the capacity of health workers in Teso subregion to diagnose and manage NCDs, the study population will be the health workers from Teso subregion working in HCIIIs, HCIVs and district hospitals. For the prevalence of different NCDs in the region, the study population will be children (Pediatric NCDs like Acute kidney injury, early onset diabetes and congenital heart diseases) and adults (Hypertension, Diabetes, Cancers, etc.) both coming to the facilities-COBMERS sites and in the community who will be accessed by the students while conducting their home visits.

To determine the trends of infectious diseases and the prevalence of common neglected tropical diseases, all the community members susceptible to the infectious and neglected tropical diseases will be the study population. The trends will be determined through monthly and annual review of hospital-based records on the infection rates.

The sample size per site for quantitative study will be estimated using the Krejcie and Morgan Table (1970). For all the sites, the population served is more than 100,000 people, so the sample size per site will be 384 participants for the quantitative study.

Data collection procedures

Quantitative data

Data will be collected with investigator administered questionnaire and data abstraction tools, installed into android phones of students loaded with the online Kobo toolbox software (https://www.kobotoolbox.org/).

As part of their COBMERS program, students who will work as research assistants (RA) to collect data in the community will be trained in the use of online data collection tools and basic analysis techniques and they will be required to conduct site specific analysis and present reports to the department of Public Health. The research assistants (RA)/students will be trained on the data collection method to use, consenting process, interview technique and use of the kobo toolbox for online data collection. This training will be conducted on a yearly basis prior to sending students to the field for COBMERS, since there will be different cohorts of students per year. They will also be trained on the disease burden in the region and the priority diseases for the study. Additionally, the research assistants will train on how to handle special populations like the children, people living with HIV, pregnant and breastfeeding mothers.

The RA will approach the clients during the day when they have come to receive medical services at the study sites, explain the purpose of the study and take them through the consenting processes for those who will accept to participate in the study. For community-based surveys, the students (RAs) will work with the site supervisors, the village health teams and local council one chairpersons to map the community and identify households or community points for the interviews to be conducted.

The prospective participants will be allowed to ask questions regarding the study. After their concerns are addressed, the study participants will be offered informed consent forms. The target participants for the community and facility-based surveys will be adults of consenting age, 18 years and above and the interview will be conducted in local languages, Ateso and Kumam, with few interviews conducted in English. The RAs/Students who know the local language will be the ones to ask the questions, while the other students will moderate the interview process.

Qualitative data collection approach

Since we shall use community based participatory research approach, the community will play a pivotal role in the study. The Focused Group Discussions (FGDs) will be conducted with the community members to identify the disease burden which they think are priorities for the university and the health sector.

Four FGDs will be conducted per COBMERS site, each comprising of 8–10 participants aged 18 years and above and purposively selected based on their relevance on the research topics. The FGDs will be conducted at community levels, mobilization done by the village health teams (VHTs) and the interviews will be conducted by the students, where they will assign within their groups roles for interviewers, observers and moderators. The students will be joined by the researchers as observers. The FGDs will be conducted in local languages (Ateso and Kumam) and will take about 45–60 min each addressing community concerns of common disease burden and priority for research and will be audio recorded for transcription.

KIs will be used to identify the capacity of the health facilities to diagnose and manage NCDs, the control measures for NCDs and infectious diseases, whether they are working or not, and their opinion on why the interventions are not controlling the diseases. The KIs will also be used to identify the priority research areas in the region and interventions that can be done to curb the disease burden. The participants focused for KIs are the district health teams, including the DHO, assistant DHOs, facility in charges, VHTs, political and administrative leaders of the districts and religious leaders and interviews will be conducted in their respective offices. 10–12 KIs will be conducted per COBMER site to include a mix of the study participants and enable reaching saturation on the area of the research. The KIs will be conducted in English, by the students while for their COBEMRS placement and they will be joined by the researchers who will be observers of the interview processes. Each interview will take about 45 min and will be audio recorded for transcription later.

Data management and analysis

Data from this study will be managed centrally at the Department of Public Health, School of Health Sciences of Soroti University. The quantitative data collected online will be saved into the central database at the department and managed by an expert data manager/biostatistician who will be specifically responsible for data management and analysis.

Quantitative data analysis

Data from the Kobo toolbox software will be downloaded onto an excel sheet in a password protected laptop at the end of every day of data collection. The COBMERS site mentors will review the data at the end of every data collection period for consistency and then upload it into the database. Data cleaning will be done at each COBMERS site by the team and inconsistent and inaccurate information will be removed, or verification made, more so for the already existing records. The cleaned data will be downloaded from the database, then exported from excel to STATA version 16.0 statistical software for further management and analysis.

Data will be analyzed at three different levels. At univariate level, the socio-demographic and medical characteristics such as age, marital status, and community disease profile for both NCDs and Infectious diseases will be summarized and presented in form of frequencies, percentages, median, mean, and standard deviation.

Geospatial maps will be generated to show the distribution of both NCDs and infectious diseases while trends analysis for the infectious and neglected tropical diseases will be generated over time to show the changes in the disease profile.

Generalized linear models such as modified Poisson regression models will be used to conduct multivariate analysis. First, we will perform a bivariate analysis by regressing each independent variable to the outcome and later subject all variables with biological relevance or confounders to a multiple regression, adjusting each variable with the sociodemographic characteristics. Results from the final models will be presented as adjusted prevalence ratio. From bivariable and multivariate analyses, predictors whose 95% confidence interval (CI) do not include the null value (1.0) will be considered statistically significant.

Qualitative data analysis

The purposively selected key informants and focus group discussions will be interviewed. The qualitative interviews of key informants will help to identify the capacity level for the diagnosis of NCDs in the health facilities, while focused group discussions will be used to identify community perceptions of common health problems and why they are persisting besides the interventions in place.

Qualitative data will be analyzed using thematic approaches. The audio recorded data will be transcribed verbatim and entered in Nvivo software for processing and further analysis. Data will be read and re-read to identify emergent and recurrent themes and concepts to develop a coding tree. Codes will be applied to label the text, and once all the text has been coded, memos, and display matrices will be developed to summarize and compare each code in detail for sub-themes, nuances, and patterns.

Qualitative data will be analyzed at group level (providers and service recipients). Using content and thematic analysis to categorize verbal or behavioral data for purposes of classification, summarization, and tabulation. Key themes and trends emerging from the data will be used in relation to the objectives of the study. These will then be used to triangulate quantitative data. Direct quotations from the interviews and FGDs will also be used to support the interpretations. Typical quotes will also be selected and included in reports to emphasize the reasons for the reported quantitative performance.

Quality assurance and control

Since the data will be collected by students during their community placement for COBMERS, they will first undergo didactic training on research methodologies, focusing on quantitative and qualitative data collection methodologies, ethical processes in data collection, consenting processes and quality assurance approaches. In addition, the data collection will be supervised by the site mentors and the researchers during their field support supervision. Specifically, the research investigators will join the students during qualitative interviews.

The data collection tools will be pretested in Princess Diana HCIV for its consistency and accuracy in collecting the required information. The survey questionnaires and data abstraction tools will then be uploaded into Kobo toolbox, which has additional quality control measures to ensure accurate data is collected.

Quality assurance and control for the qualitative study will be maintained by ensuring the trustworthiness of the study through following the Lincon and Guba model (1985), That is, Credibility will be enhanced through rigorous data collection and analysis which will involve member checking and triangulation. Dependability will be achieved by ensuring transparent and well-documented research processes, detailed descriptions of data collection, transcription and analysis procedures, with a clear audit trail. Confirmability will be ensured by keeping a reflexive journal and an audit trail of the analysis process. Transferability will be ensured by providing detailed description of the research context, user personas, user journey maps and any specific conditions that might affect user behavior and connecting specific user insights to theories or conceptual frameworks to ensure the work has wider applicability.

Dissemination plan

This study will adopt CBPR approach and as such the first plan for dissemination is to the community where data is collected from. This will be done on an annual basis as students are posted to the community for their COBMERS practice.

Additionally, annual dissemination seminars will be organized at the university to disseminate the research findings to key stakeholders, including the university staff, leaders of community-based organizations, local political leadership, district health officers and in charge of the COBMERS sites. The officials from the Ministry of Health will also be invited to join the dissemination meeting to enable the results to feed into policy and inform programming.

The results will be presented during national and international scientific conferences for greater impact. In addition, the findings will be published in peer-reviewed journals to allow sharing with a wider audience.

Discussion

This protocol describes a comprehensive, mixed-methods approach to profiling the burden of disease in the Teso subregion, integrating epidemiological surveillance with community-based participatory research (CBPR) through the COBMERS platform. By combining quantitative prevalence and trend analyses with qualitative exploration of community perceptions, the study is designed to generate both robust epidemiological data and contextually grounded insights that can inform policy and practice.

The convergent parallel design strengthens the study’s capacity to address complex, interlinked health challenges. Quantitative analyses will provide objective measures of the prevalence and trends of NCDs, infectious diseases, and NTDs, while qualitative engagement will ensure that local perspectives, priorities, and lived experiences shape the interpretation of findings and the design of interventions. The CBPR approach—anchored in established partnerships with health practitioners, community members, and non-governmental organizations—ensures that the research process is collaborative, culturally safe, and oriented toward sustainability [41].

A key innovation of this study is its integration with COBMERS, which not only facilitates data collection across diverse settings but also embeds capacity building for site mentors and health worker trainees. This dual focus on evidence generation and workforce development is expected to strengthen local health systems and foster a culture of continuous learning and improvement.

The findings are anticipated to have direct policy relevance, offering evidence-based recommendations for disease prevention, control, and health system strengthening in the Teso subregion. Best practices identified through the study can be adapted for use at national and potentially global levels, while alternative approaches to disease control can be piloted and refined locally.

Potential limitations include reliance on routine health facility data, which may vary in completeness and quality, and the possibility of wide confidence intervals for some estimates due to small sample sizes in subgroups. These will be mitigated through rigorous data cleaning, triangulation with qualitative findings, and cautious interpretation of results.

In summary, this protocol outlines a strategically designed study that bridges epidemiological surveillance, community engagement, and capacity building. By leveraging the COBMERS platform and CBPR principles, it aims to produce actionable evidence that advances both academic knowledge and public health impact in Eastern Uganda.
