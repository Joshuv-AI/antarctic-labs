Dual functions of thermospermine in plant growth and stress responses
Authors: Valerio Foschi, Riccardo D’Incà, Mitsuru Saraumi, Paraskevi Tavladoraki, Taku Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In seed plants, putrescine, spermidine, and spermine are ubiquitously present, whereas a structural isomer of spermine, thermospermine (TSpm), is synthesized mainly in the vascular tissue. Initially identified in the bacterium Thermus thermophilus, TSpm was later shown to be synthesized in Arabidopsis thaliana by ACAULIS5 (ACL5). ACL5 gene homologs may have been acquired early in plant evolution via endosymbiotic gene transfer from a cyanobacterial ancestor. Loss-of-function acl5 mutants exhibit a dwarf phenotype and excessive vascular xylem formation. Subsequent studies, including analysis of suppressor-of-acl5 (sac) mutants, revealed that TSpm exerts a critical role in the repression of vascular xylem proliferation by acting in upstream open-reading-frame (uORF)-dependent translational regulation of specific mRNAs. A recent study revealed functional TSpm binding to the peptidyl transferase center of 25 S rRNA promoted by methylation of residue U2952. Like other polyamines, TSpm has also been shown to participate in stress responses, enhancing tolerance to salt, drought, heat, and pathogen challenges in multiple species. Collectively, TSpm represents a unique polyamine with dual roles in xylem development and stress adaptation, whose evolutionary origin and molecular mechanisms provide insights into the specialization of polyamine biology. Further studies in nonvascular plants and algae are needed to elucidate the ancestral functions of TSpm.

Introduction

Numerous studies to date have shown that polyamines in seed plants play diverse roles ranging from growth-related processes, such as seed germination and senescence, to stress responses (Cona et al. 2006; Kusano et al. 2008; Takahashi and Kakehi 2010; Moschou et al. 2012; Tiburcio et al. 2014; Sobieszczuk-Nowicka 2017; Blázquez 2024). As in animals and fungi, plants possess a biosynthetic pathway of putrescine (Put), spermidine (Spd), and spermine (Spm) (Fig. 1). A distinctive feature of plants is their ability to synthesize a structural isomer of Spm, thermospermine (TSpm). TSpm was first discovered in the hyperthermophilic bacterium Thermus thermophilus and named after the generic name (Oshima 1979, 2007). More recently, in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, a loss-of-function mutant of the ACAULIS5 (ACL5) gene exhibiting a dwarf phenotype with excessive xylem formation (Fig. 2a, b; Hanzawa et al. 1997) allowed to discover TSpm presence also in plants. ACL5 was initially reported as a gene encoding a putative Spm synthase (SPMS) because the structural difference between TSpm and Spm was overlooked in the study, which used HPLC with commonly applied dansyl chloride derivatization of polyamines for detection (Hanzawa et al. 2000). Instead, to discriminate between these structural isomers, GC-MS or HPLC with benzoyl chloride derivatization or N-ethoxycarbonyl-N-pentafluoropropionyl is required (Ahou et al. 2014; Takahashi 2024). Unfortunately, in polyamine research, it is still often the case that studies do not distinguish between Spm and TSpm in their analyses creating difficulties in data interpretation. On the basis of new analyses, ACL5 was later shown to encode a TSpm synthase (TSPMS) (Knott et al. 2007). Since then, a rapidly growing body of evidence has accumulated demonstrating the importance of the unique functions of TSpm, which were completely unknown in the previous century. The progress is based primarily on the isolation and analysis of a series of suppressor mutants of the acl5 dwarf phenotype, named suppressor of acl5 (sac; Imai et al. 2006), as well as on studies of other plant species (Takano et al. 2012). In this review, we summarize recent advances in our understanding of metabolism, physiological functions, and molecular mechanisms of the action of TSpm in land plants, focusing on its dual roles in vascular development and stress responses, and discussing whether these functions reflect distinct or interconnected mechanisms.

Origin of TSPMS

Although Put and Spd are widely distributed in the plant kingdom, Spm is not detected in mosses but is present in the lycophyte Selaginella moelendorffii (Takahashi 2024), as well as in seed plants. Based on the gene structure of SPMS, it has been assumed that the gene for SPMS evolved from that for Spd synthase (SPDS) in the ancestor of seed plants through gene duplication and change of function, independently of animals and fungi (Minguet et al. 2008). Furthermore, a recent study suggests that the SPDS gene duplication that generated SPMS occurred at least 440 million years ago in the plant lineage and was coincident with the emergence of a vascular system (Li et al. 2026).

In contrast to Spm, TSpm is widely detected in the plant kingdom (Takano et al. 2012). Indeed, functional homologs of the Arabidopsis ACL5 gene are found throughout land plants and in algae (Solé-Gil et al. 2019). The TSPMS enzyme activity was first demonstrated for the ACL5 homolog in the diatom, Thalassiosira pseudonana (Knott et al. 2007). TSPMS was likely acquired by endosymbiotic gene transfer from the cyanobacterial ancestor of the chloroplast, although the closest ACL5 homolog is from the Chloroflexota, a phylum of bacteria including aerobic thermophiles (Li et al. 2026). Chloroflexota-type TSPMS are found in many protist phyla, often correlated with secondary endosymbiosis of red or green algae, but were acquired by horizontal gene transfer also in phyla that have not possessed algal plastids. Interestingly, several data suggest that the ACL5 gene product has changed substrate specificity during plant evolution and that it acquired strict TSpm synthesis activity during emergence of vascular plants (Takahashi 2024). Substantially, ACL5 homologs in early-diverging vascular plants, such as lycophytes, and in nonvascular plants, such as mosses and liverwort, may be mainly involved in the synthesis of Norspermine (NSpm) from Norspermidine (NSpd) (Fig. 1). The domain-swapping analysis of two ACL5 homologs in Selaginella moellendorffii with significant differences in substrate specificity revealed that the β-hairpin structural region consisting of β-strands 1 and 2 at the N-terminus is involved in substrate specificity and has contributed to ACL5 evolution and diversification (Takahashi 2024).

In animals or fungi, no TSPMS gene has been found. However, in stony corals (such as Orbicella faveolata and Pocillopora damicornis), homologs of the human SPMS were shown to have predominantly TSPMS activity (Li et al. 2026). Furthermore, a TSpm moiety is contained in a component of the venom of the digger wasp Philanthus triangulum, Philanthotoxin 433 (Eldefwari et al. 1988), but its biosynthetic origin is unknown.

Biosynthesis of TSpm in vascular plants

TSPMS is localized in the cytoplasm and has been shown to act as a homotetramer by a study on its crystal structure in Medicago truncatula (Sekula and Dauter 2018). In Arabidopsis, Spm is ubiquitously present in all tissues, whereas the ACL5 gene is expressed exclusively in the xylem (tracheary element) precursor cells (Clay and Nelson 2005; Muñiz et al. 2008). Furthermore, TSpm total content at the whole plant level is approximately one-tenth that of Spm (Naka et al. 2010). ACL5 expression is upregulated by auxin (Hanzawa et al. 2000) and its transcription involves the homeodomain-leucine zipper protein ATHB8 (Baima et al. 2014). ACL5 transcription further involves heterodimeric basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) complexes of LONESOME HIGHWAY (LHW) with the auxin-inducible TARGET OF MONOPTEROS5 (TMO5) or TMO5-LIKE1 (T5L1) (Katayama et al. 2015; Vera-Sirera et al. 2015) which promote cytokinin biosynthesis required for xylem cell proliferation (Ohashi-Ito et al. 2014; Smet et al. 2019). In acl5 mutants carrying a point mutation in the coding region, the accumulation level of the mutant acl5 mRNA increases to several times that of the wild-type plants. When seedlings are grown in liquid culture on medium supplemented with TSpm, ACL5 mRNA level is drastically reduced in both wild-type plants and acl5 mutants (Kakehi et al. 2008). This negative feedback regulation of ACL5 expression by TSpm occurs because TSpm enhances the synthesis of bHLH proteins of SAC51 family, which competitively inhibit the formation of LHW-TMO5 and LHW-T5L1 dimers (Fig. 2c; Katayama et al. 2015; Vera-Sirera et al. 2015).

The substrates for TSpm synthesis by TSPMS are Spd and an aminopropyl group supplied by decarboxylated S-adenosylmethionine (AdoMet; Fig. 1). In Arabidopsis, four genes encode AdoMet decarboxylase (AdoMetDC/SAMDC), which catalyzes the decarboxylation of AdoMet. Among them, SAMDC4 is expressed specifically in vascular tissues and is negatively regulated by TSpm, suggesting that it is specifically involved in TSpm biosynthesis. Indeed, a loss-of-function mutant of SAMDC4 was identified as a bushy and dwarf mutant, bud2 (Ge et al. 2006). The SAMDC4/BUD2 gene expression is also induced by auxin in an ATHB8-dependent way (Baima et al. 2014) and is down-regulated by TSpm (Kakehi et al. 2010; Alabdallah et al. 2017). Furthermore, the bud2 mutation results in hyposensitivity to auxin and hypersensitivity to cytokinin (Cui et al. 2010). Unlike the other three AdoMetDC/SAMDC genes whose mRNA 5′ leaders contain two highly conserved overlapping upstream open reading frames (uORFs) involved in Spd/Spm-regulated translation (Hanfrey et al. 2005), SAMDC4/BUD2 contains no uORF. Altogether, these data isuggest that SAMDC4/BUD2 gene may be specifically involved in TSpm biosynthesis. However, the contribution of LHW/TMO5 and LHW/T5L1 complexes on the regulation of SAMDC4 expression remains unknown.

Recently, N-propylputrescine, a Spd analog lacking the terminal amino group, was chemically synthesized and shown to act as a competitive inhibitor of TSpm biosynthesis and to induce excessive xylem differentiation. Application of this compound to plants together with auxin has been shown to have a synergistic effect on ectopic xylem differentiation, and it was therefore named xylemin after its xylem-inducing activity (Yoshimoto et al. 2016). Xylemin has been proposed as a useful chemical tool for both the study of TSpm function in various plant species and the control of xylem induction and woody biomass production.

Catabolism of TSpm

In plants, polyamine catabolism is mediated by copper amine oxidases (CuAOs) and FAD-dependent polyamine oxidases (PAOs; Tavladoraki et al. 2016). CuAOs are mainly involved in the oxidation of diamines such as Put and cadaverine at the primary amino groups, whereas PAOs oxidize triamines and tetraamines at the secondary amino groups. Plant PAOs are characterized by a wide variability in gene structure, subcellular localization, catalytic properties, and physiological roles, and are distributed to three different phylogenetic clades (Bordenave et al. 2019; Salvi and Tavladoraki 2020). Arabidopsis has five PAO genes, AtPAO1 to AtPAO5, among which the cytosolic AtPAO1 and AtPAO5 exhibiting substrate specificity for Spm, TSpm, and NSpm (Tavladoraki et al. 2006; Fincato et al. 2011; Ahou et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2014). Other PAOs oxidize Spd or Spm in peroxisomes. In accordance with ACL5 expression, AtPAO5 is highly expressed in vascular tissues, differently from AtPAO1 which is highly expressed in the root transition region between the meristematic and the elongation zone mainly involving cortical tissues (Fincato et al. 2012). AtPAO5 oxidizes the carbon on the exo-side of the N4-nitrogen of TSpm and back-converts it to Spd. Because this reaction does not involve O2 as an electron acceptor, AtPAO5 is classified as a dehydrogenase rather than as an oxidase (Ahou et al. 2014). This suggests that AtPAO5 has a role in polyamine homeostasis but not in H2O2 production, in contrast to other PAOs so far characterized (Cona et al. 2006; Tisi et al. 2011; Tavladoraki et al. 2016). The contribution of AtPAO5 to TSpm homeostasis has been confirmed by characterization of atpao5 mutants and AtPAO5 over-expressing Arabidopsis plants (Alabdallah et al. 2017).

AtPAO5 homologs are present in all plant species analyzed so far, most of them having a simple, intron-less, gene structure (Salvi and Tavladoraki 2020). Furthermore, similarly to AtPAO5, a tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) AtPAO5 homolog, SlPAO3, has been shown to have a role in TSpm homeostasis, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated slpao3 mutants presenting increased TSpm levels (D’Incà et al. 2024). However, further studies are necessary to verify whether tomato and other plant AtPAO5 homologs have catalytic properties similar to those of AtPAO5. Of note, AtPAO5 homologs from the glycophytes Selaginella lepidophylla and S. moellendorffii (SelPAO5 and SmPAO5, respectively) have been shown to differ from AtPAO5 in mode of substrate oxidation producing NSpd from TSpm (Sagor et al. 2015; Takahashi 2024). It has been hypothesized that in S. moellendorffii, NSpd produced by SmPAO5 is further converted to NSpm by the action of the NSpd-specific SmACL5-1 (Fig. 1; Takahashi 2024).

TSpm suppresses vascular xylem proliferation

The Arabidopsis acl5 mutant shows excessive differentiation of xylem tracheary elements in vascular tissues (Fig. 2b). Because tracheary elements are dead cells, excessive xylem differentiation may reduce the relative number of living parenchyma cells required for stem elongation and result in dwarfism (Hanzawa et al. 1997). Plant height can be partially restored by applying TSpm to the shoot apex. This effect cannot be substituted by Spm, which is also endogenously present, but can be mimicked by NSpm. This suggests that the tandem aminopropyl structure (N-C3-N-C3-N) common to TSpm and NSpm is required for physiological activity. However, NSpd itself (N-C3-N-C3-N), which lacks one amino group (Fig. 1), does not show a recovery effect, indicating that a tetraamine structure is also necessary (Kakehi et al. 2010). When wild-type Arabidopsis plants are grown on medium containing high concentrations of TSpm, xylem differentiation in roots is suppressed, resulting in markedly reduced uptake of water and inorganic ions and consequent growth inhibition (Tong et al. 2014). The inhibitory effect of TSpm on xylem differentiation has also been confirmed in the atpao5 mutant (Alabdallah et al. 2017), populus plants overexpressing PttACL5 (Milhinhos et al. 2013), cotton plants overexpressing GhACL5 (Mo et al. 2015), tomato mutants of SlPAO3 (D’Incà et al. 2024), and even in the obligate parasitic plant Cuscuta campestris (Nagao et al. 2025). Taken together, these findings indicate that TSpm plays a crucial role in moderately suppressing the differentiation of xylem tracheary elements at least in dicotyledonous plants.

In monocots, rice ACL5 overexpression lines have been generated and shown to have highly increased TSpm levels and exhibit reduced area of metaxylem vessels in roots and leaves, together with reduced grain size and yield, as well as leaf rolling in response to high temperature and humidity (Mai et al. 2024). Similarly, exogenous TSpm in rice seedlings has been shown to suppress xylem vessel expansion in roots though not in leaves (Miyamoto et al. 2019). On the other hand, a study revealed the absence of TSPMS in the genome of the important crop plant barley, Hordeum vulgare, suggesting that barley TSPMS was lost before domestication (Tanwar et al. 2022). The dwarfing of crops in the so-called Green Revolution is associated with defects in a gibberellin biosynthetic gene required for stem elongation. It remains to be addressed whether the deficiency in TSpm synthesis is also related to dwarfing or other traits during the domestication of barley.

TSpm may also be involved in vascular development in gymnosperms. In the Scots pine, an ACL5 homolog is highly expressed in the procambial cells of the embryo and in the megagametophyte cells destined to die via morphologically necrotic cell death (Vuosku et al. 2019). Functions of TSpm in ferns remain to be investigated. In the non-vascular bryophyte Marchantia polymorpha, knockout mutants of MpACL5 exhibit severe growth retardation in the vegetative organ, thallus, abnormal dichotomous branching at meristems, and morphological defects such as short stalks and fasciation in the sexual reproductive organ, gametangiophore (Furumoto et al. 2024; Solé-Gil et al. 2025). However, given the strong NSpm synthesis activity of ACL5 homologs in bryophytes (Takahashi 2024), the Mpacl5 mutant phenotype might be attributable to the defect in NSpm synthesis.

TSpm acts in the uORF-mediated mRNA translation

The molecular mechanisms by which TSpm suppresses xylem differentiation and negatively regulates ACL5 gene expression have been initially elucidated through genetic analyses of suppressor mutants of the acl5 phenotype, such as sac51-d, in which the dwarf phenotype of acl5 is completely rescued (Imai et al. 2006). The mRNA of SAC51, whose main ORF encodes a bHLH protein, contains a long 5’ leader sequence upstream of the authentic AUG start codon, within which multiple uORFs encoding short peptides are present. In sac51-d, one of these uORFs that is conserved across plant species is mutated. The uORFs are well known to often reduce the translation efficiency of the main ORF. In sac51-d, a premature stop codon is generated within the uORF, resulting in increased translational efficiency of the main ORF and consequently, suppression of xylem differentiation and recovery of stem elongation appear as dominant traits. Reporter gene analyses further demonstrated that TSpm cancels the inhibitory effect of the SAC51 uORF on translation of the main ORF (Fig. 2d; Imai et al. 2006). In Arabidopsis, SAC51 forms a gene family with three highly homologous genes, SACL1, SACL2, and SACL3, all of which contain the conserved uORF. Multiple mutant alleles of the conserved uORF of these genes have been identified as suppressor mutants of acl5 (Vera-Sirera et al. 2015; Cai et al. 2016; Nishii et al. 2023; Mutsuda et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2025). In addition to the SAC51 family, the causal genes of other dominant suppressor mutants that rescue the acl5 phenotype have been identified. While sac52-d, sac53-d, and sac56-d represent dominant or semi-dominant alleles of the genes encoding ribosomal proteins RPL10, RACK1, and RPL4, respectively (Imai et al. 2008; Kakehi et al. 2015), sac59 was found to be a recessive mutant of JMJ22 encoding a D6-class Jumonji C protein (JMJD6) involved in RNA processing (Matsuo et al. 2022). These mutations have collectively been shown to promote translation of the SAC51 main ORF without TSpm.

Plants carrying double knockout mutations in the main ORF of SAC51 and SACL3 exhibit no suppression of xylem differentiation even in the presence of high concentrations of TSpm and are therefore insensitive to TSpm, suggesting that these two genes constitute the principal factors mediating the TSpm response. Based on this finding, a genetic screen for TSpm-insensitive mutants in Arabidopsis was conducted, resulting in the isolation of four mutants, insensitive to TSpm1-4 (its1-its4; Saraumi et al. 2025). The causal genes were found to encode, respectively, an rRNA methyltransferase with the SPOUT domain, an rRNA pseudouridine synthase (CBF5/NAP57/dyskerin), an RNA processing-related protein (STIPL1), and a plant-specific RNA-binding protein (PHIP1). In these mutants, reduced translation or no TSpm-responsive translation of SAC51 was observed, suggesting that interactions between TSpm and RNA play a crucial role in uORF-mediated translational regulation. More recently, the causal gene of a xylem-overproliferation mutant overachiever (ovac) was shown to encode a rRNA methyltransferase (Ko et al. 2026) identical to ITS1. Detailed analyses revealed that OVAC is responsible for the 3’-methyl modification of residue U2952 of 25 S rRNA located near the peptidyl transferase center. Notably, this modification promotes TSpm interaction with the ribosomes bridging the P-site tRNA with m3U2952 residue to enhance translation of the SAC51 gene family while simultaneously repressing translation of LHW (Fig. 2d). LHW also constitutes a gene family some members of which present conserved uORFs which however are distinct from those of the SAC51 family. Because LHW-TMO5 and LHW-T5L1 heterodimers act as key factors that induce xylem cell proliferation through cytokinin biosynthesis (Fig. 2c; Ohashi-Ito et al. 2014; Smet et al. 2019), these findings indicate that TSpm exerts opposite regulatory effects on uORF-mediated translation in the two gene families involved in the promotion and repression of xylem formation (Fig. 2d), allowing a fine-tuned regulation of cell differentiation. The possibility that TSpm controls different physiological, metabolic and stress-related pathways through a similar mechanism remains an open question.

Roles of TSpm in stress defense responses

Plant polyamines, particularly Spm and Put, have been extensively shown to be implicated in stress responses (Fig. 3; Alcázar et al. 2020; Hasan et al. 2021; Blázquez 2024). Considering their common characteristic of containing multiple amino groups, it is reasonable to think that TSpm shares stress-related functions with Spm, although TSpm specific functions under stress conditions have been also suggested. Numerous studies across different plant species report the regulation of TSpm biosynthetic and catabolic genes by stress conditions (Liu et al. 2018; Takahashi et al. 2018; Li et al. 2020; Ebeed 2022). Furthermore, both in Arabidopsis and Marchantia, acl5 mutants exhibit increased sensitivity to salt stress compared to the wild-type plants (Alet et al. 2012; Shinohara et al. 2019; Furumoto et al. 2024). In addition, exogenous application of TSpm enhanced tolerance to salt stress in wild-type Arabidopsis plants, and restored stress tolerance in acl5 to levels comparable to wild-type plants (Shinohara et al. 2019). The increased sensitivity to salt stress of the Arabidopsis acl5 mutants was attributed to the excessive xylem differentiation which causes excessive salt uptake, rather than a direct effect of reduced TSpm levels. Indeed, multiple knockout mutants of SAC51 family members, which deregulate both ACL5 expression and xylem differentiation, exhibit high salt sensitivity in parallel to excessive xylem differentiation despite the increased TSpm levels (Shinohara et al. 2019). Coherently, atpao5 mutants with increased TSpm levels are less susceptible to salt stress and drought stress than the wild-type plants (Sagor et al. 2016; Zarza et al. 2017). However, this stress-tolerance phenotype was associated with modulation of gene expression, hormonal and metabolic pathways, as well as reactive oxygen species (ROS) production (Sagor et al. 2016; Zarza et al. 2017). In tomato plants, the improved tolerance to drought stress exhibited by slpao3 mutants was correlated with improved water resource management (D’Incà et al. 2024). The reduced xylem differentiation of slpao3 compared to the wild-type plants was also shown to lead to diminished xylem hydraulic conductivity that limits water loss, and to reduced vulnerability to embolism (D’Incà et al. 2024).

TSpm also contributes to defence responses to temperature stresses. Transformation of Arabidopsis plants with a construct bearing the ACL5 gene under the control of a heat-shock gene promoter, which can restore the acl5 dwarf phenotype (Hanzawa et al. 2000), confers a degree of tolerance to heat stress (Shinohara et al. 2019). Furthermore, in Marchantia, knockout mutants of MpACL5 display altered responses to heat stress compared to the wild- type plants (Furumoto et al. 2024), while in wucai, Brassica campestris L., foliar application of TSpm alleviates cold stress symptoms (Wu et al. 2025). The underlying mechanisms to the plant defence responses to temperature stresses associated with TSpm are so far unclear.

There are further data supporting a role of TSpm in plant biotic stress responses. Exogenous TSpm in Arabidopsis promoted the expression of pathogen-related genes and restricted the proliferation of cucumber mosaic virus (Sagor et al. 2012). Exogenous supply of TSpm and overexpression of ACL5 enhanced resistance to Pseudomonas viridiflava bacteria, while acl5 mutants were more susceptible to the bacterial pathogen than the wild-type plants (Marina et al. 2013). It was suggested that PAO-mediated oxidation of TSpm and CuAO-mediated oxidation of Put derived from TSpm oxidation contribute to the increased resistance to P. viridiflava exhibited in the ACL5 overexpression lines (Marina et al. 2013). Indeed, inhibitors of PAOs and CuAOs prevented the protective effect of TSpm against bacterial infection. A similar mechanism was suggested in cucumber, Cucumis sativus L., in which colonization by beneficial Pseudomonas fluorescens rhizobacteria resulted in increased resistance to Botrytis cinerea infection (Zhu et al. 2021). The increased resistance was associated with rhizobacteria-mediated induction of CsACL5 expression leading to increased amounts of TSpm. Accordingly, transgenic cucumber plants ectopically expressing the CsACL5 gene exhibited reduced infection symptoms by B. cinerea, while CsACL5 gene silencing weakened the rhizobacteria-induced cucumber resistance to B. cinerea. The rhizobacteria-mediated improved resistance against B. cinerea was further linked to pathogen-induced upregulation of PAO and CuAO activities leading to TSpm catabolism (Zhu et al. 2021), as supported by inhibitor studies. In cotton, Gossypium spp., exogenous supply of TSpm was shown to inhibit growth of the fungus Verticillium dahlia and virus-induced gene silencing of GhACL5, the expression of which is induced upon infection, led to enhanced susceptibility to the fungal pathogen (Mo et al. 2015). In responses to plant pathogens, polyamine catabolism is tightly involved as a source of hydrogen peroxide that functions in defense (Yoda et al. 2009). However, given that TSpm oxidation is not directly coupled to hydrogen peroxide production (Ahou et al. 2014), other metabolic products and alternative mechanisms might be involved in TSpm defense functions, such as transcriptional and translational regulation of stress-related genes.

As descrbed above, Spm is strongly implicated in plant stress responses. Indeed, Arabidopsis loss-of-function mutants of the SPMS gene, which do not present morphological abnormalities, exhibit increased sensitivity to salt stress (Yamaguchi et al. 2006; Alet et al. 2012) and heat shock (Sagor et al. 2013), while transgenic Arabidopsis plants overexpressing SPMS show higher level of thermotolerance compared to wild-type plants (Sagor et al. 2013). Furthermore, exogenously applied Spm protects plants from a variety of stress conditions (Xu et al. 2020; Hasan et al. 2021). Although the mechanism of Spm-mediated stress tolerance has not been fully elucidated, several studies evidenced that Spm regulates ion channels (Pottosin and Shabala 2014) and has a specific role in guard cells (Liu et al. 2000; Shi et al. 2010). In mammals, intracellular Spm is well known to block inward-rectifying potassium channels (Ficker et al. 1994; Lopatin et al. 1994). It is possible that TSpm can substitute for Spm in the stress defence mechanisms although these two polyamines show different tissue distribution in the vascular plants.

Taken together, the mechanisms of action of TSpm in plant stress responses may be multifaceted. Furthermore, direct protective functions of TSpm under harsh environmental conditions, such as those proposed for thermophilic bacteria containing TSpm together with various uncommon polyamines (Sakamoto et al. 2022; Fujiwara and Fukuda 2026) cannot be excluded in plants.

Concluding remarks and perspectives

In summary, TSpm possesses highly specialized functions distinct from those of other polyamines. It should be noted, however, that other polyamines, such as Put, Spd and Spm, also fulfill unique and important roles, for example in the hypusination of eukaryotic translation factor 5 A and as precursors for alkaloid and uncommon polyamine biosynthesis in certain plant species (Fig. 3). While retaining the properties of a tetravalent cation shared with Spm, TSpm regulates the translation of mRNAs containing specific uORFs, thereby suppressing xylem differentiation (Fig. 2). Stress-related functions shared by TSpm and Spm may have been inherited by SPMS, which was acquired later during plant evolution. Alternatively, translational regulation may represent an ancestral function of TSpm, with its physiological roles becoming progressively restricted and specialized over evolutionary time to regulate xylem tracheary element differentiation and possibly other processes.

Future studies should address the physiological roles of TSpm in bryophytes and unicellular algae, which lack developed vascular tissues, under both normal and stress conditions, to better understand the ancestral and conserved functions of TSpm. Further work is also necessary to uncover the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying TSpm-mediated translational regulation and to identify additional targets. Given that TSpm interacts with specific methylated RNA bases and that pseudouridylated RNA is also implicated in its function, it remains unclear to what extent TSpm interacts with diverse RNA modifications and structures. Regarding polyamine-RNA interactions, elucidation of the precise mechanisms by which Spd and Spm regulate translation is anticipated through analyses of their target sites in uORF-mediated translational regulation of AdoMetDC/SAMDC, particularly in light of their structural differences from Tspm. A recent study further demonstrated that Put binds to actively translating ribosome complexes while Put, Spm, and their combinations with flg22, a fragment of bacterial flagellin, increase the abundance of actively translating polysomes in Arabidopsis (Murillo et al. 2025).

Several studies have reported polyamine transporters in plants (Mulangi et al. 2012; Fujita and Shinozaki 2014; Martinis et al. 2016). However, the cell-to-cell movement of TSpm, which is produced in a highly restricted manner in vascular tissues, remains largely unexplored. Thus, the significance of both local and long-distance transport of TSpm in the execution of its function also remains an important subject for future investigation. Addressing these questions will be essential for a comprehensive understanding of the biological significance of TSpm in plant development and stress adaptation.
