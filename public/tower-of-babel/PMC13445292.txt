Total Hip Arthroplasty for Giant Hip Synovial Chondromatosis With Acetabular Involvement Complicated by Osteoarthritis: A 12-Year Follow-Up of a Case and Literature Review
Authors: Yu-zhou Shi, Ming-rui Xie, Bo Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synovial chondromatosis (SC) is a rare benign synovial disorder. Giant hip SC with acetabular involvement in the setting of end-stage osteoarthritis remains exceptionally uncommon, with limited long-term evidence available. We report a 50-year-old woman with giant SC of the left hip and acetabular invasion (Kellgren–Lawrence grade IV osteoarthritis), treated with cementless total hip arthroplasty via the Moore posterior approach combined with lesion resection and synovectomy. At 12-year follow-up, the patient achieved a Harris Hip Score of 98, with complete pain relief, stable prosthesis fixation, and no recurrence or malignant transformation. In addition, a literature review of 203 reported hip SC cases (2018-2024) demonstrated recurrence and revision rates of 10.34% and 22.58%, respectively, following total hip arthroplasty. No previously reported cases described giant SC with acetabular involvement.

Introduction

Synovial chondromatosis (SC) is a rare benign synovial proliferative disorder, characterized by cartilaginous or osteochondral nodule formation within the synovium of joints, bursae, or tendon sheaths [1]. It predominantly affects large joints, most commonly the knee; hip involvement is rare [1,2]. SC is more prevalent in adults, with a male predominance [1,2]. Clinically, patients typically present with progressive joint pain, swelling, and limited range of motion (ROM), occasionally with palpable masses [1,2].

Treatment for hip SC is determined by lesion size, degree of joint destruction, and presence of OA. For patients with small-to-medium lesions and no severe joint damage, arthroscopic synovectomy and loose body removal are first-line options [3,4]. Patients with giant nodules or extensive synovial involvement require open debridement. However, in cases complicated by end-stage osteoarthritis (OA) (Kellgren–Lawrence grade IV [K-L IV]) and bone involvement (eg, acetabular lesions), total hip arthroplasty (THA) is necessary to restore joint function [5].

In 1977, Milgram [6] proposed a classic staging system for SC. In 1994, Edeiken [7] supplemented this classification with a “giant type” (defined as a mass > 1 cm, formed by aggregation of multiple small cartilaginous nodules or growth of a single nodule), emphasizing its benign synovial origin (presenting as a giant mass) to distinguish it from malignant lesions.

SC is generally differentiated from malignant transformation (most commonly chondrosarcoma arising from SC) by imaging and histopathological features. SC typically demonstrates multiple well-defined intra-articular calcified nodules with relatively preserved cortical bone, whereas malignant transformation may present with more aggressive features such as ill-defined margins, cortical erosion, and cellular atypia, including nuclear pleomorphism [7,8]. In 2006, Subasi et al. [9] reported a typical case, discussed its imaging differentiation from parosteal osteosarcoma, and further established the clinicoradiopathological features of “giant SC.” Owing to its large size and potential bone involvement, surgical resection is challenging; additionally, literature on the long-term efficacy of THA for such cases remains limited [5].

This report describes a case of giant SC involving the left hip and acetabulum, accompanied by K-L Ⅳ OA. The patient was treated with cementless THA, and the long-term efficacy and safety of this intervention were evaluated through a 12-year follow-up. We also discuss the surgical technique, functional outcomes, and key considerations during treatment.

Case history

In July 2013, a 50-year-old female was admitted to our hospital, with 20 years of left hip pain and swelling; over the past 6 years, her symptoms had gradually worsened, accompanied by claudication. She had no history of hip trauma, systemic disease, or prior hip surgery. Her body mass index was 19.5 kg/m2, which falls within the normal range.

Physical examination revealed obvious claudication, left hip swelling and stiffness with restricted ROM, and a hard, fixed, ill-defined mass palpable in Scarpa’s triangle. The right hip was normal with full ROM. Laboratory tests (complete blood count, inflammatory markers [C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate], and tumor markers) were all within normal limits. The preoperative Harris Hip Score (HHS) was 32, indicating severe hip dysfunction.

A pelvic anteroposterior radiograph (Fig. 1) revealed pelvic tilt, a large diffuse calcified mass in and around the left hip joint (involving the obturator region, acetabulum, and medial pelvic acetabular wall), symmetric narrowing of the femoral head joint space, and subchondral sclerosis. The right hip joint appeared normal. Based on the clinical manifestations, imaging, and laboratory findings, the diagnoses were (1) giant SC of the left hip with acetabular involvement and (2) left hip OA (K-L Ⅳ). Preoperatively, the size, location of the lesion, and acetabular bone mass were thoroughly evaluated using radiographs, CT scans, and 3D reconstruction. The surgical goals were as follows: (1) complete removal of visible large loose bodies; (2) subtotal resection of chondromatous lesions invading the acetabulum; (3) total synovectomy to reduce the risk of recurrence; and (4) performance of cementless THA to restore hip joint function, with appropriate acetabular reaming to address bone invasion and ensure prosthesis stability.

The patient was in lateral decubitus under general anesthesia. One g tranexamic acid wasadministered intravenously before skin incision; controlled hypotension (mean arterial pressure: 60-70 mmHg) reduced intraoperative blood loss. The hip joint was accessed via the Moore posterior approach. Following incision of the posterior joint capsule, large loose bodies in the posterior joint space were removed first. The femoral neck was exposed, and an osteotomy was performed at its base. After femoral head extraction, extensive destruction of the femoral head and acetabular cartilage, prominent depressions and protrusions on the joint surface, and acetabular lesion invasion were noted. A total synovectomy was carried out to resect all visible synovial tissue with metaplastic changes. Subtotal resection of the chondromatous lesions was then performed under C-arm fluoroscopy guidance. Given the extensive acetabular invasion, complete resection of all lesions was not achievable—this was to prevent excessive bone loss and subsequent acetabular instability.

A total of at least 11 large nodules (each >2 × 2 cm in diameter) were resected during the procedure (Fig. 4a). The lesional synovium exhibited diffuse or focal hyperplasia and thickening, with a villous or nodular surface. It presented as grayish-white or pale yellow and had a relatively tough texture, analogous to cartilaginous tissue. Cartilaginous nodules of varying sizes were noted on the surface of some synovial segments; these nodules were either attached to the synovium via thin stalks or had detached to form loose bodies (Fig. 4b). These findings confirm that SC arises from synovial metaplastic lesions.Figure 4(a) Resected chondromatous lesions, including at least 11 large nodules (each with a diameter >2 × 2 cm). The acetabulum was appropriately reamed to remove residual chondromatous tissue and prepare a stable bed for the acetabular component. A cementless acetabular cup (United Orthopedics, Taiwan, China) was press-fit implanted, with the subsequent insertion of a polyethylene liner. After femoral medullary cavity reaming, a cementless femoral stem (United Orthopedics, Taiwan, China) was implanted. The hip joint was reduced, and joint ROM was tested to confirm stability and no impingement. The posterior joint capsule and soft tissues were repaired in layers.

The operation lasted 2.5 hours, with intraoperative blood loss of 550 mL, and no blood transfusion needed. Resected synovial tissue and loose bodies were sent for pathological examination. Weight-bearing walking with axillary crutches and physical therapy were initiated on postoperative day 2.

Gross examination of resected specimens showed multiple cartilaginous nodules (2 × 2 to 3 × 4 cm) with smooth surfaces and hard texture. Microscopic examination (Fig. 5) revealed lobulated hyaline cartilage nodules surrounded by synovial tissue fragments, with hyaline chondrocyte proliferation, abundant bone trabeculae (indicating calcification), and articular cartilage degeneration consistent with secondary OA. No granulomas or malignant cells were identified in the resected femoral head marrow. These pathological findings confirmed the diagnosis of SC without malignant transformation.Figure 5Microscopic exam: lobulated hyaline cartilage nodules (synovial-surrounded), chondrocyte proliferation, abundant bone trabeculae (calcification), and articular cartilage degeneration (secondary OA-consistent).

At 1 year postoperatively, the HHS improved to 95 (excellent function); the latest follow-up HHS was 98. Left hip ROM was as follows: flexion, 120°; extension, 10°; abduction, 40°; adduction, 25°; internal rotation, 40°; and external rotation, 35°. Postoperative radiographs (Fig. 7a-e) at 1, 2, 3, 5, and 12 years showed residual loose bodies absorbing from year 2; no chondromatous recurrence at final follow-up. The patient and her family expressed high satisfaction with the surgical outcome, rating it as “excellent.” The patient reported, “Hip pain has completely resolved, and I have fully resumed normal work and daily activities. I am highly appreciative of the surgical intervention.”

Literature review

A literature review summarized the current management of hip SC. Literature (2018–2024) was retrieved from PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science using keywords: “hip synovial chondromatosis,” “surgical treatment,” “total hip arthroplasty,” “THA,” and “long-term follow-up.” Inclusion criteria included English-language clinical studies (case reports, case series, and retrospective cohort studies) reporting treatment and follow-up outcomes of hip SC. Exclusion criteria comprised non-hip SC studies, basic science research, review articles, conference abstracts, duplicate publications, and studies without extractable outcome data.

Two independent reviewers screened studies in 22 stages: initial title and abstract screening, followed by full-text assessment. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Data extraction was performed independently using a standardized form and cross-checked for accuracy. Extracted variables included patient demographics, lesion characteristics, treatment modality, recurrence, revision, and follow-up duration.

Treatments included arthroscopic surgery (164 cases, 80.79%), THA (31 cases, 15.27%), and open debridement (8 cases, 3.94%), with a follow-up duration of 0.5-35 years.

Recurrence occurred in 21 patients (10.34%): 17 cases (10.37%) post-arthroscopic surgery, 3 cases (9.68%) post-joint replacement, and 1 case (12.5%) post-open debridement.

Revision surgery was performed in 11 patients (12.5%, 11/88), including 7 cases (22.58%) post-hip replacement and 4 cases (2.44%) post-arthroscopic surgery and none post-open debridement.

Malignant transformation occurred in 1 patient (0.49%), who developed synovial chondrosarcoma secondary to SC. No studies reported giant SC cases with acetabular involvement.

Notably, these studies should be interpreted with caution due to substantial heterogeneity among the included studies. In addition, the inclusion of single-case reports may have introduced publication bias and potentially overestimated the pooled recurrence and revision rates.

Discussion

Preoperative CT and 3D reconstruction detail chondromatous nodule size, location, and bone involvement, facilitating surgical planning [[10], [11], [12]]. MRI and CT complement each other in evaluating calcifications and bone erosion [11,12]. In this case, CT/3D reconstruction clearly visualized the lesion and acetabular involvement, enabling subtotal resection while preserving bone for acetabular component implantation.

Hip SC treatment depends on lesion size, extent, joint destruction, and concurrent OA. Arthroscopic synovectomy is first-line for small-to-moderate lesions [11,13,14], while open debridement suits large nodules or extensive synovial involvement [11,15,16]. This patient had end-stage OA (K-L Ⅳ) and acetabular lesions, so THA was performed [5,10,11].

Small residual loose bodies are common postoperatively, especially in extensive lesions [14,16]. In this case, 2-year postoperative radiographs showed spontaneous absorption of residual loose bodies, suggesting that small residual loose bodies may gradually be absorbed over time, consistent with previous reports [11,14]. This observation may support a more conservative surgical approach aimed at avoiding excessive resection and potential bone loss.

For a giant hip SC with acetabular involvement, the Moore posterior approach provides excellent exposure for loose body removal and acetabular lesion management [10]. Subtotal resection combined with total synovectomy may help reduce recurrence risk [5]. In the present case, 11 large nodules were resected, and total synovectomy was performed, which may plausibly have contributed to the favorable long-term outcome observed in this case.

The prosthesis type for SC-related THA depends on age, bone mass, and life expectancy [5,10]. The 50-year-old patient received a press-fit acetabular cup and cementless femoral stem for long-term osseointegration, with stable fixation at 12-year follow-up.

Acetabular reaming is critical for removing residual tissue and establishing a stable implant bed but must avoid over-reaming to prevent fractures [5,10]. Appropriate reaming was performed for this patient’s acetabular bone erosion.

Notably, hip SC is associated with a high THA revision rate [5]. Our literature review found a 22.58% (7/31) THA revision rate, a 2.44% (4/164) arthroscopic revision rate, and no open debridement revisions—with THA rates exceeding other hip conditions.

SC recurrence risk factors include unresected synovium, high Milgram stage, and numerous loose bodies [1,[16], [17], [18]]. van der Valk et al. [1] reported 19% overall recurrence (23% post-arthroscopy), while our review showed 10.34% overall recurrence (10.37% arthroscopic, 9.68% THA, 12.5% open debridement).

SC malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma is rare (1%-5%) [8], typically in long-course, recurrent cases [8,17]. This patient had a 20-year preoperative history and 12-year follow-up without transformation, but long-term surveillance remains necessary [8,17,19].

Current controversies and future considerations

Several controversies remain unresolved in the management of SC and its treatment outcomes. One key issue concerns the management of residual loose bodies. To date, no consensus has been established regarding the “safe size threshold” for retained small residual fragments. While some studies suggest that even minimal residuals may increase the risk of recurrence [11,16], others emphasize the importance of preserving bone stock and minimizing surgical invasiveness [10,14].

Another debated topic involves prosthesis selection for SC patients undergoing THA. The existing literature lacks unified criteria for implant choice and provides limited evidence on differences in long-term implant survival. Cemented prostheses offer the advantage of immediate stability; however, cemented all-polyethylene acetabular components have been associated with an increased risk of revision [5]. Conversely, cementless designs may reduce this risk but lack sufficient data supporting their long-term osseointegration and durability [5,10].

A third area of controversy relates to the appropriate surveillance intensity for detecting malignant transformation. Although SC carries an estimated 1%–5% risk of malignant change [8], there is no established consensus regarding the optimal frequency or duration of postoperative monitoring [17,19]. Current recommendations emphasize dynamic observation, individualized risk stratification, multi-dimensional imaging assessment, and long-term follow-up. Nevertheless, the challenge remains in striking a balance between early detection of malignancy and the efficient use of healthcare resources.

In view of this, regular postoperative follow-up may be beneficial for SC patients after THA. Annual clinical evaluation combined with long-term pelvic radiographic surveillance should be considered to assess prosthetic stability, detect lesion recurrence, and monitor potential malignant transformation. For patients with a prolonged disease course or prior recurrence, supplementary CT or MRI examinations may assist early clinical assessment [5,17].

Future research directions should focus on refining diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Advances in imaging technology—particularly the development of high-resolution MRI—may enhance the detection of non-calcified loose bodies and early bone marrow involvement, thereby improving surgical planning. Long-term, multicenter cohort studies are warranted to directly compare the outcomes of cemented vs cementless prostheses and to validate the safety thresholds for residual loose bodies. However, given the rarity of hip SC, conducting large multicenter studies may be challenging. Additionally, exploration of serum or synovial biomarkers may offer valuable tools for predicting recurrence or malignant transformation, ultimately paving the way toward more personalized and risk-adapted follow-up strategies.

Although the present study provides valuable clinical insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-case report, the findings are inherently limited in statistical power and generalizability and therefore cannot establish definitive causal relationships between the surgical interventions and the favorable long-term outcome observed. Second, the literature review was restricted to 3 databases (PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science), which may have introduced selection bias. In addition, the included studies differed in design, sample size, treatment strategy, and follow-up duration, resulting in substantial heterogeneity that may have influenced pooled outcome estimates. Publication bias associated with case reports and small case series should also be considered. Third, the absence of a control group limited comparisons between the present surgical strategy and alternative approaches. Finally, although the 12-year follow-up represents a major strength of this study, even longer-term surveillance remains necessary to further evaluate prosthetic durability, recurrence risk, and the potential for malignant transformation.

Summary

This study presents a 50-year-old female with a rare left hip giant SC with acetabular involvement and K-L Ⅳ OA, together with a literature review of 203 cases. The patient underwent cementless THA via the Moore posterior approach with subtotal resection and synovectomy for bone preservation, achieving stable prosthetic fixation and a 12-year recurrence-free follow-up. These findings may support the potential long-term effectiveness of cementless THA for extensive bone-invading SC with end-stage OA, although conclusions remain limited by the single-case design.
