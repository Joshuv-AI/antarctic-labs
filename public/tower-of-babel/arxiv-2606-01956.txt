Accurate inner stellar density slopes from projected surface densities in galaxies SciPy ( Virtanen et al. 2020 )
Jorge Sánchez Almeida

Abstract
The inner slope of the three-dimensional stellar density in dwarf galaxies ( ρ ′ ​ [ 0 ] \rho^{\prime}[0] ) is a sensitive probe of possible departures from the collisionless cold dark matter (CDM) paradigm, since cored stellar distributions ( ρ ′ ​ [ 0 ] = 0 \rho^{\prime}[0]=0 ) cannot easily reside within the cuspy potentials CDM predicts for low-mass systems. Photometry alone offers an observationally inexpensive way to constrain ρ ′ ​ ( 0 ) \rho^{\prime}(0) , making this approach particularly attractive for the faint galaxies most relevant to dark matter (DM) studies. Inferring volume densities, however, requires deprojecting the observed stellar surface density, Σ ⁡ ( R ) \Sigma(R) , a procedure that is notoriously ambiguous in the presence of noise. To avoid explicit deprojection, we derive an expression (Eq. [ 9 ]) to obtain ρ ′ ​ ( 0 ) \rho^{\prime}(0) directly from the radial derivatives of Σ ⁡ ( R ) \Sigma(R) , assuming spherical symmetry and smooth finite density profiles. All projected profiles are shown to have the same central functional form, independent of the underlying volume density (Eq. [ 20 ]). As a result, the derivatives of Σ ⁡ ( R ) \Sigma(R) can be extrapolated to the center using constraints from larger radii, which in turn yields ρ ′ ​ ( 0 ) \rho^{\prime}(0) . As an illustration, we apply the method to six ultra-faint dwarf (UFD) galaxies, finding that all of them have a surface density with the same shape, from which the presence of stellar cores is inferred ( ρ ′ ​ [ 0 ] ≃ 0 \rho^{\prime}[0]\simeq 0 ). The technique also has the ability to diagnose ρ ′ ​ [ 0 ] > 0 \rho^{\prime}[0]>0 , corresponding to galaxies with a central stellar mass deficit potentially linked to black-hole scouring, MONDian dynamics, or deviations from CDM.



I
Introduction

The inner slope of the three-dimensional stellar distribution in dwarf galaxies is emerging as a fundamental quantity for assessing deviations of the dark matter (DM) from the collisionless cold dark matter (CDM) paradigm. It plays a key role on the latest reincarnation of the classical core–cusp problem
(
Bullock & Boylan-Kolchin 2017
;
Del Popolo & Le Delliou 2021
, e.g.,)
. If the stellar volume density
\rho
of a galaxy has a core, namely, if

\rho^{\prime}(r)=\frac{d\rho(r)}{dr}\simeq 0~{\rm when}~r\to 0,

(1)

then the DM potential hosting the stars can hardly has the inner cusp predicted by CDM
(
Navarro et al. 1997
, a NFW potential, as described by)
.
The inconsistency arises because this combination of stars and DM requires an unphysical distribution function that becomes negative somewhere in the phase space. The non-negativity of the distribution function is a long-standing consistency requirement in models of gravitating stellar systems
(
Lynden-Bell 1962
;
Hernquist 1990
;
Ciotti & Pellegrini 1992
;
An & Evans 2006
, e.g.,)
. From these early works, it is known that two-component models combining quasi-isothermal density profiles (cores) and
de Vaucouleurs 1948
profiles (more cuspy) face consistency problems, as they produce negative distribution functions near the center
(
Ciotti & Pellegrini 1992
;
Ciotti 1999
)
.

An & Evans 2006
established a theorem, later generalized by
Ciotti & Morganti 2010
, relating the central logarithmic slope of the density profile to the anisotropy of the stellar velocity distribution. In particular, it rules out core density profiles embedded in NFW potentials for isotropic stellar orbits.

Sánchez Almeida et al. 2023
proposed exploiting this tension in the context of DM nature studies, as it allows the presence of NFW halos to be discarded from photometry alone, which is particularly suitable for objects too faint to be accessible by standard techniques.
Strictly speaking, this mathematical incompatibility discussed by
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2023

holds for spherically symmetric systems with stars in isotropic orbits, but these assumptions can be relaxed to axi-symmetric systems
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2024a
)
, radially biased orbits
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2023
)
, and even potentials with small cores
(
Ciotti & Pellegrini 1992
;
Sánchez Almeida 2024
)
. This incompatibility is important because the CDM cosmology predicts the DM halos of small galaxies (
M_{\star}<10^{6}M_{\odot}
) to be cuspy independently of the stellar feedback
(
Peñarrubia et al. 2012
;
Tollet et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
so, in principle, finding stellar cores in such galaxies would imply the need to go beyond CDM, as it has been suggested already
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2024b
)
. The strength of this approach for diagnosing whether the DM is CDM or not lies in its reliance on photometry alone, which is particularly valuable for faint galaxies below the critical mass threshold, where traditional spectroscopic techniques for recovering the shape of dark matter halos are challenging. Therefore, this method has the potential to increase the available statistics with data from ongoing deep imaging surveys like those provided by the Euclid satellite
(
Laureijs et al. 2011
)
or the Rubin Observatory
(
Ivezić et al. 2019
)
.

In essence, the technique needs to recover the central density gradient (Eq. [
1
]) from the measured projected stellar surface density
\Sigma(R)
. Assuming spherically symmetry,
\Sigma(R)
is just the Abel transform of
\rho(r)
,

\Sigma(R)=2\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{\rho(r)\,r}{\sqrt{r^{2}-R^{2}}}dr,

(2)

with
R
the projected distance from the center. Thus, to recover
\rho^{\prime}(0)
from the observed
\Sigma(R)
one has to apply the inverse Abel transform,

\rho(r)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma^{\prime}(R)}{\sqrt{R^{2}-r^{2}}}dR.

(3)

The direct numerical usage of Eq. (
3
) to recover
\rho^{\prime}(0)
is prone to systematic errors for a number of reasons, among others:
(a) catastrophic loss of precision when evaluating the integrand close to the lower limit, which contains a singularity,
(b) numerical noise dominates derivatives of
\Sigma
at small radii,
(c) differentiation amplifies the errors in
\Sigma
,
(d) since the transform is defined until
\infty
, extrapolation at large radii affects the result, and truncation of the integral introduces systematic bias,
(e)
\Sigma
is known only at discrete points so that interpolation is required which introduces kinks and oscillations in the result,
and, thus, (f) error estimates are hard to quantify rigorously.
The present work shows a procedure to overcome these difficulties using an approximate analytic solution to the Abel inversion problem, that holds where it is needed at
r\to 0
. We also show how it can be applied to observed surface densities to infer the inner slope of three-dimensional distributions of stars.
In addition to spherical symmetry, this procedure assumes the volume density
\rho(r)
to be finite, of finite derivative, and of finite extent. While these assumptions are restrictive, they are expected to represent real stellar systems, where infinites are physically unlikely and spherical symmetry is often a reasonable approximation.

II
Derivative of the central volume density from surface densities

Changing variables to
u^{2}=R^{2}-r^{2}
, the inverse Abel transform in Eq. (
3
) becomes

\rho(r)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma^{\prime}(\sqrt{u^{2}+r^{2}})}{\sqrt{u^{2}+r^{2}}}du,

(4)

where the integration limits are constant and derivatives can be taken directly. Using this expression,

\rho^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{r}{\pi}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(\sqrt{u^{2}+r^{2}})}{u^{2}+r^{2}}du-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma^{\prime}(\sqrt{u^{2}+r^{2}})}{(u^{2}+r^{2})^{3/2}}du\Bigg].

(5)

Assuming
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(r)\not=0
when
r\to 0
, then

r\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(\sqrt{u^{2}+r^{2}})}{u^{2}+r^{2}}du\simeq r\,\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(r)\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\frac{1}{u^{2}+r^{2}}du+r\,\int_{r_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(u)}{u^{2}}du\simeq\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(r)\arctan(r_0/r),

(6)

where
r_{0}
has been chosen small enough for
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(\sqrt{u^{2}+r^{2}})
to be
\sim\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(r)
in the first integral whereas the dependence on
r
of the second integral is neglected since the integrand never diverges and
r\to 0
. Similarly,

r\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma^{\prime}(\sqrt{u^{2}+r^{2}})}{(u^{2}+r^{2})^{3/2}}du\simeq\Sigma^{\prime}(r)\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\frac{1}{u^{2}+r^{2}}du+r\,\int_{r_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma^{\prime}(u)}{u^{3}}du\simeq\frac{\Sigma^{\prime}(r)}{r}\arctan(r_0/r),

(7)

where we have assumed that
\Sigma(\sqrt{u^{2}+r^{2}})/\sqrt{u^{2}+r^{2}}\simeq\Sigma^{\prime}(r)/r\not=0
when
r\to 0
in the first integrand. Note that
\Sigma^{\prime}(r)
could not be pullet out of the first integral in Eq. (
7
) since it is not constant when
r\to 0
, whereas
\Sigma^{\prime}(r)/r
is (to be shown in Sect.
III
). Using the approximations in Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
), Eq. (
5
) reduces to

\rho^{\prime}(r)\simeq\frac{\arctan(r_0/r)}{\pi}\left[\Sigma^{\prime}(r)/r-\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(r)\right],

(8)

and noting that
\arctan(r_0/r)\to\pi/2
as
r\to 0
, it becomes

III
General form of the surface density profiles at small radii

In order to derive the shape of the surface density profile in the innermost regions, we repeat the analysis carried out in the previous section but this time to infer
\Sigma(R)
by integration of
\Sigma^{\prime}(R)
. In order to use the Abel transform to compute
\Sigma^{\prime}(R)
, it is convenient to transform Eq. (
2
) into an integral with constant limits. The change of variables
l^{2}=r^{2}-R^{2}
renders

\Sigma(R)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}})\,dl,

(14)

so that

\Sigma^{\prime}(R)=2R\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{\prime}(\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}})}{\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}}}\,dl.

(15)

Following the thread of arguments in Sect.
II
, the integral in the right-hand-side of Eq. (
15
) can be approximated for
R\to 0
, provided
\rho
is a well behaved function with finite
\rho^{\prime}(0)
. When
R\to 0

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{\prime}(\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}})}{\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}}}\,dl\simeq\rho^{\prime}(R)\int_{0}^{R_{0}}\frac{dl}{\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}}}+\int_{R_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{\prime}(\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}})}{\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}}}\,dl,

(16)

with
R_{0}
an arbitrary distance as small as needed for
\rho^{\prime}(l)
to be replaced with
\rho^{\prime}(R)
in the first integral. The first integral can be integrated analytically to yield,

\int_{0}^{R_{0}}\frac{dl}{\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}}}=\ln(R_0+\sqrt{R_0^2+R^2})-\ln R\simeq\ln(2R_0/R).

(17)

The second integral in Eq. (
16
) is finite and independent of
R
when
R\to 0
,

\int_{R_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{\prime}(\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}})}{\sqrt{l^{2}+R^{2}}}\,dl\simeq\int_{R_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{\prime}(l)}{l}\,dl\equiv I^{\prime}(R_{0}).

(18)

Using Eqs. (
15
), (
17
), and (
18
), and maintaining the leading terms in
R\ln R
and
R
, one finds

\Sigma^{\prime}(R)\simeq 2\rho^{\prime}(0)\,R\ln(2R_0/R)+2RI^{\prime}(R_{0}),

(19)

which can be integrated analytically
to yield

\Sigma(R)=\Sigma(0)+\Sigma_{1}\,R^{2}\ln R+\Sigma_{2}\,R^{2}+\dots.

(20)

The dots at the end of the previous expression represent terms that go to zero faster than
R^{2}
when
R\to 0
, and

\Sigma_{1}=-\rho^{\prime}(0),

(21)

with

\Sigma_{2}=\rho^{\prime}(0)\,\left[\ln(2R_0)+\frac{1}{2}\right]+I^{\prime}(R_{0}).

(22)

Note that the expression for
\Sigma(R)
in Eq. (
20
) satisfies

\frac{1}{2}\left[\Sigma^{\prime}(R)/R-\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(R)\right]=-\Sigma_{1}+\dots=\rho^{\prime}(0)+\dots,

(23)

which corresponds to the first order term when expanding
\rho^{\prime}(R)
in
Eq. (
9
). As shown in Appendix
B
, including the
R^{3}
term in Eq. (
20
) introduces a linear contribution,
\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)\,R
, on the right-hand side of Eq. (
23
).

There are several conclusions to be drawn from Eq. (
19
):
(a) Independently of the exact value of
\rho^{\prime}(0)
,
\Sigma^{\prime}(R)\to 0
when
R\to 0
. Thus all well behaved volume density profiles produce surface densities with cores (i.e., with
\Sigma^{\prime}[0]\to 0
), as shown in fairly general terms by
Valenciano et al. 2025
. The difference between surface densities with and without three-dimensional cores lies in the relative weight of the two last terms in Eq. (
20
). In the limit where the volume density has a core (i.e.,
\rho^{\prime}[0]=0
), the dependence of
\Sigma
on
R
is purely quadratic.
(b) Equation (
19
) implies that all surface density profiles from spherically symmetric finite volume densities have the same shape at the center (Eq. [
20
]). This suggests that
\rho^{\prime}(0)
can be inferred using Eq. (
8
) by extrapolating to the center the function
\Sigma(R)
observed off the center. This procedure is illustrated in Sect.

with observations of UFD galaxies.
(c) In addition to
\rho^{\prime}(0)
, the surface density depends on
I^{\prime}(R_{0})
, which integrates the whole density profile to infinity (Eq. [
18
]). Thus, even when
R
is small, the coefficients defining
\Sigma(R)
depend on
\rho(r)
at all radii.
(d) Equations (
19
) and (
20
) hold even when
\rho^{\prime}(0)>0
, which is a possibility discussed in some detail in Sect.
V
.

IV

Sánchez Almeida et al. 2024b

Moreover, this shape is inconsistent with the stars living in a NFW potential, likely because the corresponding three-dimensional stellar density profile has a central plateau or core (see Sect.
I

V
Discussion and conclusions

The inner slope of the three-dimensional stellar density distribution in dwarf galaxies (
\rho^{\prime}[0]
) emerges as a key parameter for characterizing possible deviations of the true DM from the otherwise highly successful CDM paradigm. The reason can be traced to the incompatibility between a cored stellar distribution (Eq. [
1
]) and the gravitational potential produced by CDM model galaxies when stellar feedback is negligible (see Sect.
I
). Since the stellar distribution can be inferred from photometry alone, it is observationally inexpensive compared to the spectroscopic techniques commonly used to determine the structure of dark matter halos
(
Cappellari 2008
;
Bonnivard et al. 2016
;
Vasiliev 2019
, e.g.,)
. While the standard spectroscopic approach is clearly preferred for bright galaxies when sufficient data can be obtained, it becomes a significant challenge for low-mass galaxies, which are precisely the systems needed to constrain the nature of DM. Thus, the alternative approach, based on the presence or absence of photometric cores, becomes particularly interesting, as it may allow the sample size to be increased enough to assess, with confidence, some of the claimed deviations from CDM (see Sect.
I
).

The determination of whether the three-dimensional stellar distribution contains a core must rely on the observed, plane-of-the-sky projected surface density. Deprojecting real data, which are inevitably affected by noise, is highly ambiguous. Here, we work out a practical procedure to estimate the inner slope directly, thereby bypassing the need for an explicit deprojection. Under a set of reasonable assumptions (namely, spherical symmetry and a smooth finite stellar density profile), we find that the inner slope can be directly obtained from the radial derivatives of the observed surface density (Eq. [
8
]). This formal expression is always valid, but numerically computing derivatives amplifies the noise in the data, calling its practical use into question. To address this issue, we have developed a workaround. We demonstrate that the radial dependence of the stellar surface density corresponding to any volume density is always the same; see Eqs. (
20
) – (
22
). By fitting the observed surface density to functions that include this functional form,
\Sigma(R)
and its derivatives can be extrapolated to
R=0
, allowing
\rho^{\prime}(0)
to be estimated from Eq. (
8
) while keeping noise effects minimal.

The technique has been tested on real data from UFD galaxies. These objects are particularly relevant in the present context, as they have been claimed to exhibit potential deviations from CDM
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2024b
)
. In Sect.
IV
, we simultaneously fitted a set of six UFDs, each with two renderings, using a combination of a projected exponential and a projected Schuster–Plummer profile that satisfies Eq. (
20
), thereby retaining the flexibility to recover
\rho^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
(Eq. [
24
]). All galaxies are assumed to have a density profile with the same shape but with a different scaling in central density and radius. The resulting fit is shown in Fig.
3
, which is very good since
\chi^{2}/\nu\sim 1.08
. Importantly, the fit works equally well for each individual UFD, confirming that all UFDs share the same shape (see Fig.
4
in Appendix
C
). The extrapolation to the center of the fitted profile yields
\rho^{\prime}(0)\simeq 0
, as shown by the red line and region in Fig.
1
. The existence of such stellar core explains the incompatibility of these profiles with cuspy NFW potentials pointed out by
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2024b
.

Some of the individual UFD surface densities may hint at the presence of a central dip (see, e.g., Horologium I and II in Fig.
4
, Appendix
C
). If dips like these were confirmed to be real rather than a statistical fluctuation, they may be extremely telling from a physical stand point. In our parlance, the dip implies
d\Sigma(R)/dR>0
at some small but non-zero
R
. Because of Eq. (
15
), the dip requires
\rho^{\prime}(r)
to be positive over a range of radii. Using Eq. (
19
), and keeping in mind that
I^{\prime}(R_{0})
is negative (see Eq. [
18
]),
d\Sigma(R)/dR>0
implies
\rho^{\prime}(r)>0
at the center. Thus, for the dip to exist, the volume density of stars must have a central depression, dubbed here as
central hole
. This central hole could be caused by a number of very interesting physical mechanisms, including core scouring by several massive black holes
(
Hopkins & Hernquist 2010
;
Nasim et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
, the expansion of the dark matter halo driven by self-interactions or other physics beyond the standard CDM framework
(
Sánchez Almeida et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, or it may indicate that gravity is governed by MOND (MOdified Newtonian Dynamics;
Milgrom 1983
) rather than by Newtonian gravity
(
Sánchez Almeida 2022a
)
. We bring this up here because the above formalism allows the retrieval of a volume density gradient of any sign. In particular, when equipped with the appropriate approximations (Eq. [
20
]), it enables the measurement of
\rho^{\prime}(r)>0
. Thus, the tools developed here may offer a way to explore the exotic physical mechanisms mentioned above.

Two further results and conclusions deserve explicit mention:
(a) The scaling used to display the estimate of
\rho^{\prime}(0)
in Fig.
1
is based entirely on observational data and is independent of any shifts in the amplitude or radial extent of the observed surface density.
(b) When
R\to 0
, the surface density
\Sigma(R)
scales as
R^{2}\ln R
if
\rho^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, or as
R^{2}
if
\rho^{\prime}(0)=0
(see Eqs. [
20
]–[
22
]). This implies that
d\Sigma/dR\to 0
as
R\to 0
, so that
\Sigma(R)
always exhibits a central plateau for any finite
\rho(r)
. Consequently, information about the value of
\rho^{\prime}(0)
is encoded in the way
\Sigma(R)
declines from this plateau. The mentioned approximation is accurate to within one percent for
R\lesssim R_{12}
(Fig.
2
).

Appendix A
Surface density and its derivatives for two analytical volume densities

For an exponential volume density profile (Eq. [
11
] with
\rho_{s}=r_{s}=1
), the corresponding surface density is

\Sigma(R)=2\,R\,K_{1}(R),

(A1)

with
K_{1}
the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order one
(
Gradshteyn & Ryzhik 1965
)
. The first derivative of
K_{1}
is

K^{\prime}_{1}(R)=-\frac{1}{2}\left[K_{0}(R)+K_{2}(R)\right],

(A2)

since there are general recurrent equations for the derivatives of these functions
(
Abramowitz & Stegun 1972
)
. Thus, in this case,

\Sigma^{\prime}(R)=-RK_{0}(R)+2K_{1}(R)-RK_{2}(R).

(A3)

and

\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(R)=\frac{R}{2}K_{-1}(R)-2K_{0}(R)+RK_{1}(R)-2K_{2}(R)+\frac{R}{2}K_{3}(R).

(A4)

For a Schuster-Plummer profile (Eq. [
10
] with
\rho_{s}=r_{s}=1
), the corresponding surface density is
(
Sánchez Almeida 2022b
, e.g.,)

\Sigma(R)=\frac{4/3}{(1+R^{2})^{2}},

(A5)

with

\Sigma^{\prime}(R)=-\frac{16\,R}{3\,(1+R^{2})^{3}},

(A6)

and

\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(R)=\frac{32\,R^{2}}{(1+R^{2})^{4}}-\frac{16}{3\,(1+R^{2})^{3}}.

(A7)

Appendix B
Alternative derivation of Eq. (
20
)

Equation (
9
) takes also the form of

\frac{d}{dR}\left[\frac{\Sigma^{\prime}(R)}{R}\right]\simeq-2\frac{\rho^{\prime}(R)}{R},

(B1)

which can be integrated expanding de volume density around zero,
\rho^{\prime}(R)\simeq\rho^{\prime}(0)+\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)R+\dots
, so that

\frac{\Sigma^{\prime}(R)}{R}\simeq P_{1}-2\rho^{\prime}(0)\ln R-2\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)R+\dots,

(B2)

with
P_{1}
a constant of integration. A second integration yields,

\Sigma(R)\simeq\Sigma(0)-2\rho^{\prime}(0)\frac{R^{2}(2\ln R-1)}{4}+\frac{P_{1}}{2}R^{2}-\frac{2}{3}\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)R^{3}+\dots,

(B3)

or, equivalently,

\Sigma(R)\simeq\Sigma(0)-\rho^{\prime}(0)\,R^{2}\,\ln R+\frac{1}{2}\left[P_{1}+\rho^{\prime}(0)\right]\,R^{2}-\frac{2}{3}\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)R^{3}+\dots,

(B4)

which is Eq. (
20
) showing an additional
R^{3}
term in the expansion. A term-by-term comparison between Eqs. (
22
) and (
B4
) gives the equivalence

\frac{P_{1}}{2}=\rho^{\prime}(0)\,\ln(2R_0)+I^{\prime}(R_{0}),

(B5)

showing that the integration constant
P_{1}
encodes the information associated with the splitting radius
R_{0}
and the integral
I^{\prime}(R_{0})
used in the main text. Because of Eq. (
B1
),

\frac{1}{2}\left[\Sigma^{\prime}(R)/R-\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(R)\right]\simeq\rho^{\prime}(0)+\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)\,R+\dots.

(B6)
