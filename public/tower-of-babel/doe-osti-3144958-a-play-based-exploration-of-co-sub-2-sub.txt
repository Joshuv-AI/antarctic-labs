EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report documents the results of "A Play-Based Exploration of CO 2 Storage in the Illinois
Basin" (DE-FE0032366), a project funded by the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Fossil
Energy and Carbon Management and conducted by the Illinois State Geological Survey (ISGS)
at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign in partnership with Visage Energy. The project
period was January 1, 2024, through December 31, 2025, and all milestones and tasks were
completed on schedule. The principal deliverable of the project is a public website, https://ccs-
play.web.illinois.edu/, that hosts the project's integrated geospatial database, individual play
element suitability maps, composite play maps, and three interactive StoryMaps that together
provide a basin-scale, play-based screening resource for commercial geologic carbon storage in
Illinois.
The primary objective of the project was to build an integrated geospatial database, drawing on
existing subsurface, surface, and societal data, to support entities screening areas of Illinois for
commercial geologic carbon storage. Secondary objectives were to test the database by applying
play-based exploration and analysis methods (PBE) to generate composite maps that delineate
areas of highest suitability for storage site development, to share the database with DOE and
regional stakeholders, and to provide the public with access to the database and resulting maps
through a purpose-built website.
PBE is a systematic approach to basin-scale evaluation long employed by the petroleum industry.
This project adapted the methodology for carbon capture and storage (CCS) by expanding the
traditional play concept to include three play element groups: subsurface geologic factors
(storage formation properties, containment, and groundwater protection), surface features (land
use and transportation infrastructure), and societal factors (community acceptance). Twenty-four
play elements were identified with data of sufficient quality and coverage to support defensible
suitability criteria and were carried forward to final mapping.
The project assembled a comprehensive geospatial database integrating subsurface data from
ISGS, the Midwest Geological Sequestration Consortium (MGSC), the Midwest Regional
Carbon Initiative (MRCI), and NATCARB repositories; surface infrastructure data from public
sources; and societal data drawn primarily from U.S. Census sources. Significant data-
improvement work supported the database build, including correcting hundreds of legacy well
locations, digitizing more than 1,300 well construction records through collaboration with the
DOE Consortium Advancing Technology for Assessment of Lost Oil & Gas Wells (CATALOG)
and its Oil and Gas Regulatory Record Digitizer (OGRRE) tool, compiling a statewide 2D
seismic database, and producing a refined statewide fault and fold geodatabase.
Suitability criteria for each play element were developed based on best practices from literature
and expert judgment, applying a five-tier classification scheme: most suitable, more suitable,
suitable, less suitable, and a separate "More Information Needed" category for areas with
insufficient data. A blue-to-yellow color gradient was adopted in place of the traditional red-to-
green scale to promote clarity, neutrality, and accessibility, and to avoid the "stoplight" framing
inappropriate to a relative suitability assessment. The criteria were applied to each play element
to generate Common Risk Segment (CRS) maps, which were then combined into composite play
maps for the subsurface, surface, and societal play element groups. Subsurface composites were

generated separately for the Mt. Simon Sandstone and the St. Peter Sandstone. The composite
maps delineate a broad suitable to most suitable fairway across the southern two-thirds of Illinois
for the Mt. Simon, and a narrower fairway across the southern half of the state for the St. Peter.
The project website serves multiple audiences, including the general public, researchers, CCS
project developers, local governments, and community stakeholders. Its design was guided by
the POUR principles of accessibility and the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG),
and it includes 24 individual play element pages with a common structure, interactive web maps
hosted in ArcGIS Online, and three StoryMaps that introduce CCS in the Illinois Basin, walk
through the suitability workflow, and present the PBE results.
In addition to the project website, the project improved the public accessibility of DOE-funded
data through existing ISGS web assets. Digitized well construction records were contributed to
the Illinois Oil and Gas Resources map (ILOIL), and 2D seismic profiles were made available
through the ISGS Geophysics Data Viewer. The project team also organized the Illinois Basin
Collaboration and Insights workshop, held October 1–3, 2025, which brought together more than
80 attendees from research institutions, service companies, CCS project developers, and
regulators for panel discussions, technical presentations, and structured feedback on the project's
play maps and StoryMaps.
Community benefits activities conducted during the first year of the project produced two
substantive deliverables and supported student workforce development. A literature review of
social characterization assessments established the theoretical and methodological foundations
for evaluating community-level impacts of CCS projects, and a Stakeholder Mapping Analysis
applied that framework to the Illinois Basin to identify stakeholders, characterize community
vulnerability profiles, and inform engagement planning. The Subsurface Energy Resources
Summer Experience Program (SERSEP) provided three student participants with skill-
development workshops and hands-on CCS research experience, with two participants
continuing on the project after the program concluded. Activities under the Community Benefits
Plan were ceased on January 28, 2025, in accordance with DOE direction.
The PBE framework, geospatial database, CRS and composite play maps, and project website
developed through this project provide a versatile foundation for future work. Five priority
directions for expansion are identified in this report: incorporating additional play elements that
were scoped but not implemented (e.g., private water wells, gas storage wells, existing rights-of-
way); developing derivative play elements that combine multiple raw data layers (e.g., net
thickness, porosity-thickness products, volumetric storage potential, dollar value of storage per
area); implementing more sophisticated and auditable methods for combining CRS maps into
composites; evolving the website from a curated presentation toward an interactive interface that
supports user-driven exploration; and generalizing the suitability framework so that the same
database can be repurposed for subsurface uses other than CO2 storage, including natural gas
storage, hydrogen storage, compressed air energy storage, and geothermal applications. Each
direction builds directly on the products of this project and addresses needs that emerged clearly
from project work and from stakeholder feedback received at the Illinois Basin Collaboration
and Insights workshop.

INTRODUCTION
Objectives
The objective of this project was to build a database using existing subsurface, surface, and
societal data for entities screening areas of Illinois for commercial geologic carbon storage
(GCS). Sub objectives were to 1) Test the database using play-based exploration and analyses
methods (hereafter PBE) to create composite maps that clearly delineate areas in the state with
the highest suitability for storage site development, 2) Share the database with the US
Department of Energy (DOE), the four existing DOE regional initiative (RI) projects, and the
Area of Interest 1 Recipients under Regional Initiative to Accelerate Carbon Capture, Utilization,
and Storage (CCUS) Deployment: Technical Assistance for Large-Scale Storage Facilities and
Regional Carbon Management Hubs funding opportunity (DE-FOA-0002799), and 3) Provide
the public with access to the database and resulting composite maps, specifically those screening
Illinois for commercial storage sites or those potentially impacted by the development of such
sites.

Motivation
This project was designed to build an integrated geospatial database and test that database using
PBE to map areas with high potential for GCS site development in Cambro-Ordovician aged
strata in the Illinois portion of the Illinois Basin.
In exploration geology, a play is a set of prospects in the same region that are controlled by the
same set of geological circumstances. PBE is a systematic approach to basin-scale evaluation
long employed by the petroleum industry to identify prospective areas for oil and gas
development based on the play concept (White, 1988). PBE produces maps that depict regional
trends in geology relevant to exploring for a particular play as polygons. These play maps show
the main fairway(s) or areas where the specific play is likely to be successful and additional site-
specific characterization work is warranted. As important, the maps also show off-fairway areas
that have a low likelihood of success due to one or more defined risk factors. In addition to
hydrocarbon applications, PBE has been used in geothermal exploration and, more recently, has
been suggested as a useful method in exploration for GCS sites (Bump et al., 2021).
The Illinois Basin has among the highest technically accessible GCS resources in the United
States (U.S. Geological Survey Geologic Carbon Dioxide Storage Resources Assessment Team,
2013). The Basin hosts one of the nation’s first commercial-scale Carbon Capture and Storage
(CCS) projects (Illinois Industrial Carbon Capture and Storage; IL-ICCS) in Decatur, Illinois,
and in the past few years has seen publicly and privately funded projects conduct site-specific
characterization for both storage facilities associated with stand-alone point-source carbon
emitters and potential carbon management (CM) hubs.
Many of the earliest studies focused on the Cambrian Mt. Simon Sandstone, a proven storage
reservoir at Decatur. As a result, the Mt. Simon Sandstone is generally top of mind among CCS
project developers, but studies have shown that other Cambro-Ordovician formations may be
equally suitable for GCS (e.g., Greb et al., 2012; Leetaru et al., 2014) if not preferred in certain
areas of the Basin due to regional variations in Mt. Simon reservoir quality.
Given the wide distribution of point-source carbon emitters in Illinois (NATCARB lists 188
 8

unique sources) and the consequent need to have varied options in terms of storage reservoirs
and potential GCS sites, both the Mt. Simon Sandstone and the St. Peter Sandstone were
included in the analysis of potential storage reservoirs in the PBE to help ensure statewide
coverage. The reservoirs occur within the Cambro-Ordovician stratigraphy (Figure 1) and
include porous sandstones and discrete porous intervals within otherwise low-porosity
carbonates. The challenge is to high-grade the most promising areas of the state irrespective of
the target reservoir. A PBE that integrates data from stacked reservoirs reduces exploration risk
and expense, creates investment assurance, and allows future studies to focus on the most
promising sites.
The PBE described herein used, and added value to, existing subsurface datasets (e.g., wells,
seismic surveys, maps, and models) developed through numerous site-specific to regional-scale
studies already managed and controlled by the Illinois State Geological Survey (ISGS). Land
surface data (e.g., political boundaries, public infrastructure, locations of point-source carbon
emitters, etc.) and societal data (e.g., population density, sole source aquifer areas, community
water supply wells, etc.) were also gathered from public sources. These disparate datasets were
combined into an integrated geospatial database that was the basis of the PBE.
In adapting the PBE to a GCS context, subsurface geologic formations, surface features, and
societal factors were all mapped as distinct play elements. Subsurface reservoir and confining
units remain important play elements, but additional geologic formations were also included as
potential brine injection intervals for disposal of brine extracted to accommodate CO 2 storage in
the designated reservoir. Also, the hydrocarbon source and charge are replaced by anthropogenic
land surface and societal play elements. The project team developed the criteria (e.g., minimum
porosity and thickness for a storage reservoir, minimum thickness for confining unit(s), social
acceptability of industrial development) necessary for classifying each play element in terms of
risk to CO2 storage site development.
The PBE produced maps that identify prospective areas (i.e., potential sub-regions, sensu NETL,
2017) within Illinois that have the strongest indications of successful GCS sites by applying the
defined criteria to the play element maps to construct Common Risk Segment (CRS) maps that
are consistent with the data density and the geological conceptual model of each unit. CRS maps
are generally simple three-color maps that show regional variations in suitability for GCS site
development. Finally, the CRS maps were convolved into final play maps that identify prospects
(areas of high volumetric potential, low composite risk), areas of conditional potential (areas of
high volumetric potential with specific, limiting high risks), and critical risks (areas of high risk
with non-zero volumetric potential of any size) across the state of Illinois.
The final composite play maps serve as vital communication tools to provide decision support to
resource managers who manage large-scale storage facilities or CM hubs where multiple projects
and hubs are being planned or where there is competition for storage resources within the State.
As such, the database and the resulting play maps have been shared via a custom website with
embedded story maps supported by narrative text and links to download data and map layer files.
The maps and the website have been formatted to be easily digestible by the public to facilitate
communication of site selection rationale to a wide audience of stakeholders, without the need
for a keen understanding of CCS.
Whether maturing a CCS project following best practices for site screening, selection, and

 9

characterization (e.g., NETL, 2017), or within a resource management system (e.g., SPE, 2017),
the PBE developed herein is ultimately designed to enhance geological data gathering, analysis,
and sharing to create the knowledge base required to inform the development of large-scale
storage facilities or CM hubs in Illinois. By integrating disparate datasets together in a
comprehensive geospatial database, new site-specific or regional reservoir models can quickly be
developed that incorporate existing well penetrations, pressure distribution, and salinity
gradients. Furthermore, the versatility of the PBE is such that, once developed, the risk criteria
used to develop the CRS maps can be redefined based on the preferred injectant (e.g., natural
gas, hydrogen) and the play maps can be regenerated to show prospects for a range of energy
storage scenarios that may compete for available pore space. PBE and the resulting play maps
are an excellent way to combine and translate complex geological and social analysis into
business-input and community engagement tools that are easy to understand.

Background
The Illinois Basin is increasingly recognized as a nationally significant subsurface system for
multi-resource and multi-use energy applications, including GCS, natural gas storage, industrial
wastewater disposal, and pressure management through brine injection. These subsurface uses
are not independent: they often draw on the same regional stratigraphy, the same legacy drilling
footprint, and the same basin-scale constraints related to pressure, salinity, seal integrity, and
subsurface infrastructure. As Illinois moves toward expanded deployment of carbon management
technologies, driven by industrial decarbonization needs and the emergence of new Class VI
permitting activity, there is a growing need to evaluate storage resources in a manner that
accounts for coexisting and competing subsurface demands rather than treating each application
as a standalone project.
A key enabling feature of the Illinois Basin is the presence of a thick, laterally extensive
Cambrian–Ordovician storage complex, comprised of multiple porous and permeable reservoir
intervals interbedded with regionally persistent confining units. This deep stratigraphic
framework supports both storage capacity and operational flexibility, because it provides (1)
multiple reservoir targets with variable depth, thickness, and petrophysical character; (2) stacked
sealing intervals that can impede vertical migration; and (3) opportunities to separate injection
objectives across formations (e.g., CO2 storage in one interval and brine disposal in another)
when permitted and technically appropriate. Together, these attributes position the Illinois Basin
as a leading example of a “subsurface portfolio” in which long-term carbon storage can be
developed alongside other subsurface services, provided that basin-scale stratigraphic variability
and subsurface risk drivers are explicitly incorporated into characterization and modeling
workflows.
Illinois Basin Storage Reservoirs
The Illinois Basin hosts multiple Cambro-Ordovician reservoir intervals with potential for
geologic carbon storage. Five of these reservoirs were considered as play elements for this
analysis: the Mt. Simon Sandstone, the Ironton-Galesville Sandstone, the Knox Group
Dolomites, the Knox Group Sandstones, and the St. Peter Sandstone. Key petrophysical
properties of these reservoirs, including lithology, porosity type, depth range, maximum
pressure, maximum temperature, and salinity, are summarized in Table 1. Regional variations in
the presence and thickness of each reservoir across the state of Illinois are illustrated in Figure 2.
Together, Table 1 and Figure 2 capture both the vertical stratigraphic diversity and the lateral
heterogeneity of the storage complex, underscoring the importance of a multi-reservoir, play-
based approach to evaluating carbon storage potential across the Basin. Each reservoir is
described in further detail in the subsections below.

Mt. Simon Sandstone
Regional mapping of the Mt. Simon Sandstone indicates the formation is present across much of
Illinois and is thickest in its depocenter in the northeastern quarter of the stste (Figure 2). It is
below the critical depth required for carbon storage throughout the state. The Mt. Simon has
been subdivided into Lower, Middle, and Upper units (Freiburg et al., 2014). Regionally, the
Lower and Upper units can have good to excellent reservoir characteristics, whereas the Middle
does not. The Upper Mt. Simon is used for natural gas storage and waste disposal (i.e., UIC
Class I) in the Illinois Basin (Morse and Leetaru, 2005) and the Lower Mt. Simon is used for
commercial-scale CO2 injection at the Archer Daniels Midland (ADM) ethanol facility in
Decatur, IL (Greenberg, 2021).
Within the Lower Mt. Simon, high porosity (~20%) and permeability (ca 100’s of mD) is
associated with arkosic zones that occur along the flanks of Precambrian hills in which there was

 13

ample feldspathic source material. Diagenetic (post-depositional) alteration of the rocks through
dissolution of feldspar grains originally deposited as part of the sediment material enhance the
reservoir properties of these zones. The best carbon storage potential is thus believed to coincide
with arkosic zones which are not universally encountered in Lower Mt. Simon strata, although
they are correlative across much of central Illinois.
The Mt. Simon Sandstone is generally understood to unconformably overlie Precambrian
crystalline basement rocks of the Eastern Granite Rhyolite Province. However, in some areas the
Mt. Simon has been observed to overlie the Argenta Formation (e.g., Freiburg et al., 2015).
Where the Argenta Formation was cored and characterized at the Decatur site, it was found to be
well cemented and impermeable, thus not a good candidate for carbon storage.
Galesville and Ironton Sandstones
The Cambrian Galesville Sandstone and overlying Ironton Sandstone are part of the Knox Group
in the Illinois Basin. Both intervals are a mixed siliciclastic, dolomitic, and shale-rich sequence
immediately overlying the Eau Claire Formation. Typically, the formations are referred to as the
Ironton/Galesville sequence and range in total thickness from over 200 ft in northern Illinois to
between 100-200 ft in central Illinois (Figure 2). The unit pinches out (likely non-deposition) to
the south and grades into a dolomitic interval to the southeast. Portions of both the Ironton and
Galesville contain highly permeable and porous sandstone with average porosities ranging
between 10 to 20%, and average permeabilities ranging between 70 to 500md. These porous
units are low salinity aquifers in northern Illinois and used as active natural gas storage fields
where the salinity increases.
There is a high degree of lateral and vertical facies and stratigraphic variability within the
Ironton/Galesville both in the northern and central portions of the Illinois Basin. A detailed
sequence stratigraphic and facies evaluation of this unit across the Basin has not been completed
using modern techniques. There is confusion in terminology whether the Ironton/Galesville is
overlain by the Davis Shale or Franconia Formation. In portions of central Illinois, it appears the
unit is overlain by shale. However, in other locations, the shaly unit is more dolomitic and
argillaceous and has been ascribed to the Franconia. As these units provide the confining zone
for storage in the Ironton/Galesville, additional research is required on this issue.

Knox-St. Peter Storage Complex
Potential CO2 storage reservoirs occur in discrete porous intervals within the Potosi, Oneota, and
Shakopee Dolomites, and the Gunter, New Richmond, and St. Peter Sandstones. Shales of the
Maquoketa Group create the primary seal for the storage complex, but the dense dolomite that
comprises the bulk of the Knox Group likely serves as an effective confining unit. The Knox-St.
Peter Storage complex is particularly important as a storage target in areas of the Illinois Basin
where the Mt. Simon Sandstone lacks suitable properties for carbon storage.

Knox Group Dolomites
Within the Knox Group, the Potosi, Oneota, and Shakopee Dolomites are relatively pure, fine- to
medium-grained dolomites that have a cumulative thickness of a few hundred ft in northern
Illinois to over 2,000 ft in southern Illinois. The dolomites are generally dense and non-porous,
but discrete, isolated porosity intervals occur in association with either fractured and brecciated
zones, or intervals of vuggy porosity that exhibit cavities lined with drusy quartz or dolomite
crystals that have been interpreted to represent paleokarst and hydrothermal alteration,
respectively (Pittenger et al., 2012). Such zones have extremely high permeability and are
notorious for lost circulation while drilling. As a result, the zones are commonly used for
wastewater disposal in oilfields and in industrial applications (Greb et al., 2012). Where multiple
discrete porosity zones occur in conjunction with lateral continuity and high permeability, they
can be considered stacked reservoirs.
Knox Group Sandstones
Within the Knox Group dolomites are intervals of interbedded dolomite and sandstone that,
where not carbonate cemented, can be porous and permeable and can reach several hundred feet
in thickness. The Gunter and New Richmond Sandstones occur below Oneota Dolomite and
gradationally below the Shakopee Dolomite, respectively. The Gunter Sandstone has a
widespread but patchy distribution in the subsurface of central and southern Illinois and is up to
20 ft thick. The New Richmond is present in the western/northwestern part of Illinois and ranges
in thickness from approximately 20 ft to 145 ft (Figure 2).
St. Peter Sandstone
The St. Peter Sandstone overlies the Knox Group dolomite succession along a significant
unconformity and is developed as a thick, clean sandstone in some parts of the Basin. In central
and southern Illinois, the St. Peter Sandstone ranges in thickness from 50 to 250 ft. The St. Peter
Sandstone is used for natural gas storage in Illinois at depths shallower than approx. 3,250 ft but
below the occurrence of ground water of less than 10,000 ppm Total Dissolved Solids (TDS).
The St. Peter in these fields has an average porosity of 14 to 16% and permeability of 150 to
400md (Barnes and Ellett, 2014).
Illinois Basin Confining Units
Effective long-term containment of injected CO2 depends not only on the storage reservoir but
on the integrity and lateral continuity of the overlying confining units. In the Illinois Basin, two
regionally extensive low-permeability formations serve as the primary seals for the Cambro-
Ordovician storage complex: the Eau Claire Formation, which directly overlies the Mt. Simon
Sandstone, and the Maquoketa Group, which caps the Knox–St. Peter storage complex. Key
properties of these confining units, including lithology, thickness range, and depth, are
summarized in Table 1. Regional variations in the thickness of the Eau Claire Formation and the
Maquoketa Group, along with the locations of mapped faults across Illinois, are illustrated in
Figure 3. The spatial distribution of confining unit thickness is a critical input to the play
analysis, as areas where these units are thin or structurally disrupted represent elevated risk to
CO2 containment. Each confining unit is described in further detail in the subsections below.
Maquoketa Group
The Ordovician Maquoketa Group is composed of a heterogeneous sequence of carbonates, silts,
 16

and clay-rich lithologies and functions as a seal to the Cambro-Ordovician storage complex in
the Illinois Basin. In a regional-scale lithofacies model developed by Medina et al. (2020), the
Maquoketa Group was separated into five lithofacies: (1) limestone; (2) muddy limestone; (3)
calcitic/dolomitic shale; (4) silty shale; and (5) shale. These five lithofacies can be grouped into
three major units consisting of an upper clastic, middle carbonate, and lower clastic, which is
consistent with previously proposed stratigraphic subdivisions of the Brainard Shale, Fort
Atkinson Limestone, and Scales Shale, respectively.
Eau Claire Formation
The Eau Claire Formation consists of laminated, fissile shale to silty shale with abundant
siltstone beds in the lower half, and limestone to argillaceous limestone in the upper half with
regional variations in dominant lithology across the Basin. In east-central Illinois and west-
central Indiana, the lowermost Eau Claire facies, directly overlying the Mt. Simon, is dominated
by shale and tight siltstone. The shaley lower half directly overlies the Mt. Simon Sandstone
resulting in an impermeable barrier to vertical fluid migration, as modeling has indicated both
advective flow and ionic diffusion into the Eau Claire are insignificant (Roy et al., 2014).

Faults
Faults and fault zones are generally well constrained in Illinois and are concentrated in the
southern part of the state, although the Plum River and Sandwich Fault Zones of northern Illinois
(Figure 3) are among the longest faults in the state (Nelson, 1995). In central and southern
Illinois, there are several large structural folds (with associated oil field development), that are
likely associated with blind faults in Precambrian crystalline basement (Nelson, 1995).

Social Characterization Assessment
As part of the community and labor engagement effort, the project team produced a
comprehensive literature review titled “Integrating Justice in Energy Development: The Role of
Social Characterization Assessments in Sustainable Infrastructure Projects.” This review
examined the role of Social Characterization Assessments (SCAs) in ensuring that large-scale
 18

energy projects are aligned with the principles of environmental justice. A SCA is a
comprehensive analytical process that gathers and analyzes data on demographic composition,
social practices, cultures, norms, economic activities, political structures, and known harms faced
by a community in the context of a proposed project. The primary aim is to identify the social
assets, vulnerabilities, needs, and expectations of different groups, particularly focusing on
communities that have been marginalized or underserved and that might be disproportionately
affected by environmental changes or development projects.
The review examined the theoretical foundations underpinning SCAs, drawing on the work of
Rawls (1971) on justice as fairness and Bookchin on social ecology, and integrated insights from
stakeholder theory, participatory approaches, systems thinking, and social capital frameworks.
These theoretical foundations establish that large-scale energy infrastructure projects must
respect and incorporate environmental justice principles from the outset, engaging with affected
communities as equal partners, transparently addressing potential social and economic impacts,
and working collaboratively to find solutions that respect both the environment and the rights of
all community members.
The review evaluated both quantitative and qualitative research methodologies for SCAs,
including systematic mapping using federally administered tools such as the EPA’s EJScreen and
the Climate and Economic Justice Screening Tool (CEJST), both of which were operating as
federally administered tools at the time of the SCA literature review but are no longer hosted by
the federal government as of this writing, as well as surveys, interviews, focus groups, case
studies, and observational research. Sampling methods reviewed included simple random
sampling, stratified random sampling, cluster sampling, convenience sampling, and snowball
sampling. The review emphasized that a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative data
(e.g., census data, demographic indicators) with qualitative insights (e.g., stakeholder interviews,
community narratives), produces the most comprehensive understanding of the social landscape.
Case studies from diverse geographical and sociopolitical contexts were examined to illustrate
the practical consequences of conducting or omitting SCAs. The review contrasted the failed
CCS projects in Barendrecht, Netherlands, where inadequate social characterization and late-
stage community engagement led to project cancellation (Akerboom et al., 2021), with the
successful Huamantanga Water Funds project in Lima, Peru, where proactive social impact
assessment from the outset fostered significant trust between the project team and community
and led to precisely aligned interventions (Bremer et al., 2016).
The review also addressed the specific cultural, socioeconomic, and political context of Illinois,
noting the significant urban-rural divide that shapes perspectives on mining, energy transition,
and environmental justice. Urban areas, particularly in and around Chicago, tend to emphasize
environmental justice and sustainability, while rural parts of Illinois, particularly in the southern
and central regions, have cultural traditions that honor mining and agriculture. This diversity
underscores the necessity of conducting location-specific SCAs rather than applying uniform
assumptions about community sentiment. The review concluded with strategic recommendations
for practitioners, including the importance of proactive and early engagement, the use of
systematic mapping as a foundational step, the integration of cultural sensitivity into all
communications, and the need for continuous feedback collection and response throughout the
project lifecycle.

Stakeholder Mapping Analysis
Building on the SCA literature review, a Stakeholder Mapping Analysis was conducted to
systematically identify, categorize, and profile the stakeholders capable of influencing or being
influenced by CCS project implementation in the Illinois Basin (Johnson et al., 2026). The
analysis delineated affected parties, assessed relative influence and benefit pathways, and
documented interests, concerns, and prospective roles. Stakeholder classes identified included
governmental and regulatory bodies, local authorities, community organizations, environmental
advocates, industry partners, academic institutions, landowners, and the general public.
Categorization by interest and influence enabled the design of targeted communication and
engagement strategies characterized by proactivity, transparency, and role clarity.
Preliminary profiles of demographic, economic, and health characteristics within the Illinois
Basin were developed to inform engagement planning by integrating multiple data sources,
including socioeconomic indicators from EJScreen and CEJST, infrastructure proximity data,
and historical industrial development patterns. The report examined existing health impacts and
disparities across the region, including the geographic distribution of functional limitations and
disabilities, life expectancy variations, cardiovascular disease prevalence, respiratory health
impacts (particularly asthma), and cancer prevalence patterns. Environmental exposure
challenges were also characterized, including wastewater discharge risk, toxic air releases, and
drinking water non-compliance patterns across the Basin.
Socioeconomic factors analyzed included educational attainment profiles, regional economic
conditions (with particular attention to low-income communities), and unemployment patterns.
The analysis highlighted that communities in the Illinois Basin exhibit significant variation in
vulnerability profiles across census tracts, with overlapping burdens most pronounced in areas
such as East St. Louis, Hopkins Park, and selected tracts in the greater Chicagoland area. A heat-
map comparison of community and risk indicators across Illinois census tracts was developed,
integrating measures of income, disability prevalence, climate hazard exposure, air-toxics cancer
risk, water discharge exposure, educational attainment, job access, and household energy burden
to identify areas where cumulative burdens are most concentrated.
The report concluded that insights from the stakeholder mapping analysis should guide targeted
environmental monitoring, regulatory compliance, and infrastructure planning for future CCS
projects, recommending the establishment of baseline measurements prior to field activities,
integration of health-protective design criteria into siting and construction plans, coordination
with utilities and local agencies, and publication of accessible metrics to document progress.

Subsurface Play Elements
Subsurface play elements were derived from datasets and are generally first order attributes of
geologic units. Subsurface storage formations and confining units are the fundamental technical
elements required for a viable carbon storage project.
Subsurface play elements were organized into three subgroups:
  Storage – Play elements related to storativity and volumetrics
  Containment – Play elements related to secure long-term storage
  Water – Play elements related to groundwater quality and protection
Storage Play Elements
Injection Zone Depth
Injection zone depth is defined as the vertical distance from land surface to the top of the
designated CO2 injection interval within a geologic storage formation. For this element, injection
zone depth is derived from subsurface stratigraphic interpretations that identify the depth to the
top of the target injection formation using well data and regional geologic models. Only
formations identified as potential CO2 storage reservoirs are included. For example, the top of
the Mt. Simon Sandstone is used for the Mt. Simon Injection Zone Depth, and the top of the
Galena Dolomite is used as a conservative proxy for the St. Peter Injection Zone Depth.
Storage Formation Gross Thickness
Storage formation thickness is defined as the vertical thickness of a geologic formation identified
as a potential CO2 storage reservoir. For this element, storage formation thickness includes the
interpreted thickness of regionally mapped storage formations based on stratigraphic correlations
derived from well data and geologic models. Only formations designated as potential CO 2
storage reservoirs are included.
Containment Play Elements
Confining Unit Thickness
Confining unit thickness is defined as the vertical thickness of the primary geologic confining
unit that overlies a designated CO2 storage reservoir. For this element, confining unit thickness
includes the interpreted thickness of regionally continuous low-permeability formations
identified as confining units based on stratigraphic correlations and subsurface data. Only
confining units associated with formations identified as potential CO 2 storage reservoirs are
included.
Saline Springs
Saline springs are defined as natural discharge points where groundwater with elevated salinity
emerges at the land surface. For this element, saline springs include only locations identified and
documented as saline springs in authoritative hydrogeologic datasets compiled by state or federal
agencies. Springs not classified as saline or occurrences inferred without documented
observation are not included.

 28

Legacy Wells
Legacy wells are defined as wells that were previously drilled for groundwater, oil and gas
exploration or production, natural gas storage, waste disposal, or other subsurface purposes and
that penetrate one or more geologic units relevant to CO 2 storage or containment. For this
element, legacy wells include wells documented in state well databases that intersect identified
storage formations, confining units, or both. Currently active or properly plugged wells are
included if they meet these criteria.
Faults
Faults are defined as geologic structures characterized by displacement of rock units and
identified as faults in authoritative geologic mapping and subsurface interpretation datasets. For
this element, faults include only structures explicitly mapped and classified as faults by state or
federal geological surveys based on surface mapping, subsurface data, geophysical
interpretation, or integrated analyses. Unmapped or inferred faults not represented in the source
datasets are not included.
Seismic Hazard
Seismic hazard is defined as the estimated probability of earthquake ground motion occurring at
a location over a specified time period, as quantified by probabilistic seismic hazard models. For
this element, seismic hazard includes ground-motion estimates derived from regional or national
seismic hazard assessments produced by authoritative scientific agencies. The element represents
modeled hazard, not individual earthquake events or mapped faults.
Water Play Elements
Injection Zone Formation Water Salinity
Injection zone formation water salinity is defined as the concentration of total dissolved solids
(TDS) in formation water within the designated CO 2 injection interval of a geologic storage
formation. For this element, formation water salinity includes measured or interpreted TDS
values associated with the injection zone, derived from formation water samples, geophysical
logs, or regional hydrogeologic models. Only data attributed to formations identified as potential
CO2 storage reservoirs are included.

Surface Play Elements
Surface play elements relate to features at the land surface that could impact the cost, logistics, or
feasibility of CO2 storage site development. Surface play elements were organized into two
subgroups:
  Land use – Play elements that involve the classification of land usage that could impact
 the cost of development of CO2 storage or infrastructure.
  Transport – Play elements that involve existing or proposed surface infrastructure that
 could be used to transport CO2.

 29

Land Use Play Elements
Coal and Non-fuel Mines (Underground/Surface)
Active coal mines are defined as surface or underground coal mining operations that are
permitted and actively producing coal at the time of data compilation. For this element, active
coal mines include only mines identified as active in authoritative state or federal mining
datasets. Inactive, abandoned, reclaimed, or permitted-but-not-operating mines are not included.
Non-fuel mines are defined as surface or subsurface mining operations that extract non-energy
mineral resources, including but not limited to limestone, dolomite, sand, gravel, clay, and
industrial minerals. For this element, non-fuel mines include only mines identified in
authoritative state or federal mining datasets as permitted, active, or historically recorded non-
fuel mining operations. Coal mines and other fuel-producing mines are not included.
Wetlands
Wetlands are defined as areas identified as wetlands in authoritative federal or state wetland
inventories based on hydrology, vegetation, and soil characteristics. For this element, wetlands
include only features classified as wetlands in the source datasets at the time of data compilation.
Areas not mapped or classified as wetlands in these inventories are not included.
Karst Terrain
Karst terrain is defined as areas underlain by soluble carbonate bedrock (such as limestone or
dolomite) where dissolution processes have produced characteristic landforms. For this element,
karst terrain includes only areas identified and delineated as karst or karst-prone in authoritative
geologic and hydrogeologic datasets compiled by state or federal agencies. Areas without
mapped karst features or karst-prone geology are not included.
Transport Play Elements
CO2 Point Source Location
CO2 point source locations are defined as stationary facilities that report carbon dioxide
emissions to a regulatory or inventory reporting program. For this element, CO 2 point sources
include only facilities with geographically referenced emission records in authoritative federal or
state emissions datasets at the time of data compilation. Mobile sources and facilities without
reported location data are not included.
Existing Transportation Infrastructure (Roads, Rivers, and Rails)
Transportation infrastructure (roads) is defined as publicly and privately maintained roadway
corridors used for vehicular travel. For this element, roads include only those roadway segments
identified in authoritative transportation or mapping datasets as existing roads at the time of data
compilation. Planned, proposed, or decommissioned roads are not included.
Transportation infrastructure (rivers) is defined as navigable rivers and waterways used for
commercial or industrial transportation. For this element, rivers include only waterway segments
identified in authoritative federal or state datasets as navigable or commercially utilized
waterways at the time of data compilation. Non-navigable streams, drainage ditches, and
intermittent waterways not designated for navigation are not included.

 30

Transportation infrastructure (rails) is defined as railroad corridors used for freight or passenger
rail transport. For this element, rail infrastructure includes only rail lines identified in
authoritative transportation or mapping datasets as existing railroads at the time of data
compilation. Abandoned, removed, or proposed rail lines are not included.

Societal Play Elements
For CCS projects to be viable, the impacted community must find the proposed project to be
acceptable. All societal play elements fall into the same category, which is acceptance.
The Societal CRS Map indicates a suitable fairway across the entire state. Areas shown as white
space generally correspond to city limits, where additional information is needed to assess
suitability from a public-acceptance perspective. Areas classified as less suitable correspond to
the Mahomet Aquifer Sole Source Aquifer, which has specific restrictions on the siting of carbon
capture and storage projects under Illinois Public Act 104-0119.
City Limits
City limits are defined as the legally established boundaries of incorporated municipalities,
including cities, villages, and towns. For this element, city limits include only incorporated
municipal boundaries recognized in authoritative governmental boundary datasets.
Unincorporated areas and census-designated places without legal incorporation are not included.
Parks
Parks are defined as land areas that are formally designated for public use and managed by a
federal, state, or local government entity. For this element, parks include only lands identified in
authoritative governmental geospatial datasets as public parks, recreation areas, or conservation
areas. Privately owned recreational lands, conservation easements without public designation,
and informal open spaces are not included.
Historic Places
Historic places are defined as properties that are formally listed on the National Register of
Historic Places (NRHP). For this element, historic places include only properties with an active
listing status in the National Park Service National Register of Historic Places database at the
time of data compilation. Properties that are locally designated but not listed on the NRHP are
not included.
Federally Owned Land
Federally owned land is defined as land parcels for which the United States federal government
holds ownership or administrative responsibility. For this element, federally owned land includes
parcels identified in authoritative federal land-ownership or land-management datasets as being
managed by federal agencies. Lands under private ownership, state ownership, or local
ownership are not included.
Tribal Lands
Tribal lands are defined as lands held in trust by the United States for federally recognized tribal
nations or lands over which a federally recognized tribe exercises governmental authority. For
this element, tribal lands include only lands identified in authoritative federal datasets as trust

 31

lands, reservation lands, or other legally recognized tribal land holdings. Lands without formal
federal recognition or cultural significance without a legal land designation are not included.
Population Density
Population density is defined as the number of people per unit area, calculated from population
counts divided by land area. For this element, population density is derived from the most recent
decennial census population data and is expressed as persons per unit area using standardized
census geography. The element represents population distribution at the resolution of the source
census units and does not represent individual residences or structures.
Community Water Supply Wells
Community water supply wells are defined as groundwater wells that serve a public water
system providing water for human consumption to at least 15 service connections or regularly
serving at least 25 individuals for at least 60 days per year. Setbacks are required from
community water supply wells. Suitability was assessed based on distance, incorporating the
200-foot and 400-foot minimum setback requirements set by IEPA: areas within 400 feet (0.08
miles) were classified as less suitable, areas from 0.08 to 0.5 miles as less suitable (suitable),
areas from 0.5 to 1 mile as more suitable, and areas beyond 1 mile as most suitable.
Sole-Source Aquifer Area
Sole source aquifer areas are defined as geographic areas designated by the U.S. Environmental
Protection Agency (EPA) under Section 1424(e) of the Safe Drinking Water Act as relying on a
single aquifer system for at least 50 percent of their drinking water supply and for which no
reasonably available alternative drinking water source exists. For this element, sole source
aquifer areas include only areas with an official EPA designation in effect at the time of data
compilation.

Play Element Suitability Criteria
The purpose of defining suitability criteria is to provide a basis for developing suitability maps.
The suitability criteria defined herein are provided as an example and are based on best practices
as defined in literature, where available (Table 3). Criteria defined here and maps derived
therefrom represent a snapshot in time and can be refined or revised in the future based on new
data or improved practices in the CCS industry.
Suitability classifications are divided into four relative categories: less suitable, suitable, more
suitable, and most suitable. A fifth category, “More Information Needed,” was applied to areas
where data were insufficient to make a suitability determination.
Some societal play elements do not lend themselves to establishing objective, quantitative
suitability criteria. Rather, these play elements are included and mapped to create awareness. For
areas that are otherwise suitable based on surface, subsurface, and other social play elements,
local communities can better understand the nature of their resource and make informed
decisions regarding its value, and developers can review social play element data to make
informed decisions regarding the approach to community engagement. Failure to adequately
consider these factors could lead to project delays, increased costs, and damage to the reputation
of CCS as a viable climate change mitigation technology.

ASSEMBLING THE GEOSPATIAL DATABASE
An integrated geospatial database, built and populated with all available data and shared among
relevant stakeholders, is the foundational knowledge base required to inform the development of
large-scale storage facilities or CM hubs in Illinois. To better understand the spatial distribution
of CCS potential and suitability in Illinois, the project compiled datasets relevant to subsurface
geology and hydrology, surface infrastructure, and societal considerations.
Subsurface data from ISGS, MGSC, MRCI, and NATCARB repositories were gathered to
develop a subsurface dataset of geological and hydrological attributes relevant to CO 2 storage for
Cambro-Ordovician formations in Illinois. Subsurface data types included seismic surveys, well
data (geophysical logs, core descriptions, etc.), and other relevant data. Maps and models
developed as part of previous studies were reviewed and incorporated into the dataset.
Publicly available and previously compiled data were used to develop a dataset of surface
infrastructure relevant to CO2 storage site development in Illinois. Publicly available societal
data from the US Census Bureau and other relevant sources were leveraged to develop a social
dataset. All relevant datasets were combined into a common geospatial database.

Data Cleaning and Improvement
Significant effort was devoted to cleaning, improving, and standardizing existing datasets to
ensure compatibility and cohesion in the final geospatial database. Some existing data from
projects conducted over the last few decades required cleanup, digitization, and conversion to
ensure consistency. Key data improvement activities included:
  Correcting hundreds of legacy well locations using georeferenced historical maps. The
 project team worked with ISGS geoscientists to combine stratigraphic formation tops
 from wells in Illinois that penetrate the Cambro-Ordovician Storage complex to build a
 database of control wells. This effort involved evaluating and reconciling disagreement in
 interpretation among datasets and resolving redundancy caused by non-uniform naming
 conventions.
  Integrating and digitizing well construction history data, expanding the ISGS wells
 database with information about well construction (e.g., casing records) that is critical for
 assessing the risk of legacy wellbores to CO2 containment.
  Compiling a statewide 2D seismic database and merging recent STATEMAP updates.
 Physical (paper) copies of vintage 2D seismic survey data in ISGS possession were
 reviewed and compared to the existing digital database of scanned vintage 2D seismic
 data. Location data for vintage 2D surveys were compiled for database construction, and
 modern 2D seismic data for which ISGS has digital “.segy” format files were cataloged
 with associated metadata.
  Building on previous USGS-funded work to create a refined, statewide fault and fold
 geodatabase.
  Collecting, reprojecting, and organizing surface infrastructure data, including railways
 and roads. Collecting, transforming, and organizing EPA FLIGHT tabular data on point

 36

 sources of CO2. Collecting, reprojecting, and organizing social data, including U.S.
 Census tabular data for multiple societal play elements.

Digitization
Well Construction Records – OGRRE Tool
The project team collaborated with the DOE Consortium Advancing Technology for Assessment
of Lost Oil & Gas Wells (CATALOG) team on the development and implementation of their Oil
and Gas Regulatory Record Digitizer (OGRRE) tool to digitize well construction data from
existing regulatory well records in Illinois. The CATALOG team was provided with examples of
different formats of well construction reports for the purpose of training the OGRRE tool. Over
1,300 well records representing the deepest wells in Illinois most relevant to future CCS projects
were provided, including wells that penetrate the Precambrian basement, Mt. Simon Sandstone,
and Eau Claire Formation. These well records were digitized during the project, and the
CATALOG team expanded the functionality of the OGRRE tool from well construction records
to also include well drilling reports, well integrity reports, and well plugging affidavits based on
files shared by ISGS. Digitized records were algorithmically and manually QA/QC’d and
cleaned OGRRE output data were added to the ISGS institutional database.
Seismic Survey Data
Physical (paper) copies of vintage 2D seismic survey data in ISGS possession were reviewed and
compared to the existing digital database of scanned vintage 2D seismic data (pdf or tiff format).
Location data for vintage 2D surveys were compiled for database construction. Modern 2D
seismic data for which ISGS has the digital “.segy” format were cataloged and metadata were
created. Physical 2D seismic data that were not part of the existing digital scanned database and
that had location data were digitized. Location data for modern digital seismic data were
gathered with all required geographic coordinate conversions.

Compilation of the Geospatial Database
The geospatial database was compiled by integrating all subsurface, surface, and societal datasets
into a common spatial framework. For the play elements that were defined as part of Task 4, data
were gathered or digitized and organized on an internal ISGS file server. Milestone D (Complete
Subsurface, Surface, and Social Datasets) was completed on September 30, 2024.
The project team investigated the application of ArcSDE as an internal platform for hosting and
keeping the geospatial database up to date and discussed developing static file geodatabases to
meet the deliverable requirements. An automated process was developed within GIS to import,
clip, and assign the correct spatial reference to play element datasets, streamlining the addition of
play element datasets to the combined geodatabase. All relevant metadata for the subsurface,
surface, and societal datasets were documented to accompany the final project geodatabase.
The geodatabase was structured as a file geodatabase with feature datasets organized around the
three primary categories of play elements: subsurface, surface, and societal. All datasets were
imported using standardized naming conventions and grouped according to their play element
categories. Each dataset was stored as a feature class or raster layer with standardized attributes
and coordinate systems. All layers were reprojected to NAD 1983 UTM Zone 16N for consistent

 37

statewide alignment. Data sources included ISGS institutional datasets (subsurface), the Illinois
Department of Transportation, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, US EPA National Emissions
Inventory, National Wetlands Inventory, ILMINES database, and National Land Cover Database
(surface), and the U.S. Census Bureau, and ESRI (societal).
Before use in the suitability model, each dataset within the geodatabase was reviewed and
processed to: clip to the Illinois area boundary; remove null or erroneous values; normalize
attributes for comparison across layers by converting raw values into consistent units (for
distance-based datasets, all distances were converted to miles before applying suitability
criteria); and reclassify values based on suitability criteria. Datasets that extended beyond Illinois
were clipped to the Illinois state boundary. For datasets that did not fully cover Illinois but
contained sufficient surrounding data, kriging was used to interpolate continuous rasters for
analysis. For geologic rasters such as seal geometry, thickness, and depth where no additional
data existed outside the mapped area, NODATA was assigned to areas beyond the available
coverage. For point-based datasets that did not cover the entire state, such as legacy well
penetrations or saline springs, the Distance Accumulation tool was used to generate continuous
rasters statewide.
Ongoing efforts to standardize quality and presentation of each play element continued
throughout the project, ensuring compatibility and cohesion as a final product. 24 play elements,
as described in the previous section, were carried forward to final suitability mapping and
display on the project website. The geodatabase and associated metadata were prepared for
delivery to DOE and sharing with stakeholders.
The geospatial database was compiled as an ESRI file geodatabase. Associated metadata for the
geodatabase layers was compiled as a standalone Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. A README file
was created to document the purpose and contents of the geospatial database. These three files
were combined in a .ZIP file and added to DOE’s EDX repository, where it is available for
download.

PERFORMING PLAY-BASED EXPLORATION AND MAPPING
Development and Application of the Play-Based Exploration Workflow
The PBE methodology, long employed by the petroleum industry for basin-scale evaluation, was
adapted for application to CCS in the Illinois Basin. In the traditional petroleum context, a play is
a set of prospects controlled by the same set of geological circumstances, and PBE
systematically identifies prospective areas for development. The adaptation to CCS expanded the
play concept to include three play element groups (subsurface geologic factors, surface features,
and societal factors) replacing the hydrocarbon source and charge with anthropogenic land
surface and societal play elements.
The workflow followed a stepwise process: (1) define play elements based on available data; (2)
assemble the geospatial database with all relevant subsurface, surface, and societal datasets; (3)
define suitability criteria for each play element based on best practices from the literature; (4)
generate play element maps showing the spatial distribution of raw data; (5) apply suitability
criteria to create Common Risk Segment (CRS) maps for each play element; and (6) combine
CRS maps into composite play maps that depict relative suitability for CCS development across
the state. This workflow is documented in the Suitability Workflow StoryMap on the project
website (https://ccs-play.web.illinois.edu/).

Play Element Maps
Play element maps were generated for each of the 24 play elements carried forward from the
definitions phase using the compiled geospatial database. These maps display the spatial
distribution of each play element’s raw data across the state of Illinois. Play element datasets
were added to ArcGIS web maps and clear, consistent, and accessible symbology was developed
for each element. Each of the 24 play elements received a dedicated page on the project website
(https://ccs-play.web.illinois.edu/play-elements/), with a consistent structure that includes the
play element definition, description, suitability classification, and an interactive web map.

Common Risk Segment Maps
Suitability criteria were applied to the play element maps to generate Common Risk Segment
(CRS) maps using the suitability modeling capabilities in ArcGIS (Figure 4). The project team
met to review CRS maps and gathered additional feedback on suitability criteria, refining the
maps accordingly. CRS maps were completed for all play elements.
Each play element dataset was reclassified using the Reclassify tool in ArcGIS Pro based on
criteria defined by subject matter experts and reviewed literature. Continuous datasets such as
injection zone depth, salinity, and distance to infrastructure were reclassified using numeric
breakpoints. Categorical datasets such as land cover, protected areas, or population density were
reclassified by assigning suitability values to each category. Subsurface datasets including
thickness, depth, salinity, seismic hazard, and well penetrations were reclassified into a
standardized 0–3 suitability scale. The 0–3 numerical values correspond to the four relative
suitability tiers, less suitable (0), suitable (1), more suitable (2), and most suitable (3), with cells
assigned NoData where insufficient data were available to make a determination, corresponding
to the "More Information Needed" tier introduced below. Surface datasets including land cover,

 39

wetlands, mines, aquifers, and existing or proposed infrastructure were reclassified through
expert-led discussions. Societal datasets including population density, land ownership
constraints, community water wells, and cultural resources were similarly reclassified. All rasters
were clipped to the Illinois boundary and resampled to a uniform cell size, projection, and extent
using ArcGIS Pro environment settings to ensure alignment during composite map generation.
A Python script was developed to automate the process of creating composite suitability maps by
combining multiple reclassified raster layers. The script merges standardized rasters using
priority logic so that pixels with a value of 0 (less suitable) always override other suitability
classes in any given cell. This priority system ensures that areas marked unsuitable in any input
layer remain unsuitable in the final composite. The script reads directly from the project
geodatabase, accepts multi-value inputs so analysts can select all reclassified rasters associated
with a play element group at once, and is wrapped in a custom ArcGIS toolbox for use through a
simple interface without editing the underlying code. CRS maps were produced for each play
element group (subsurface, surface, and societal) that reflect the combined influence of multiple
related play elements.
The detailed methodology for the suitability mapping workflow, including the generalized
workflow steps, the Python scripting approach, and the specific Illinois application, is
documented in full in the companion report: “Development and Application of the Play-Based
Exploration Workflow for CCS in Illinois” (Situ et al., 2026).
A five-tier suitability classification system was developed to effectively communicate to non-
specialists the meaning of the color scales on common risk segment and composite play maps.
The five tiers are defined as follows: “Most suitable” describes areas where current information
strongly supports that conditions are highly favorable for carbon storage projects, representing
the best candidates for further study and potential development. “More suitable” describes areas
that appear favorable based on available information, though not as ideal as most suitable areas,
still presenting good opportunities worth further evaluation. “Suitable” describes areas that meet
the basic requirements but may have some limitations, requiring additional studies to confirm
potential. “Less suitable” describes areas where conditions could make carbon storage projects
more difficult to develop or less likely to succeed, potentially facing greater technical,
environmental, or social challenges. “More Information Needed” describes areas where not
enough data are currently available to make a judgment, which may become more or less suitable
as new information is collected.
A blue-to-yellow color gradient was applied for the play suitability maps in place of a traditional
red-to-green scale to promote clarity, neutrality, and accessibility in data interpretation. While a
red-to-green scheme is often seen as intuitive, evoking a “stoplight” metaphor of poor-to-good
conditions, it can unintentionally introduce cognitive bias, with red perceived as “bad” or
“dangerous.” This framing is inappropriate for suitability assessments, where lower suitability
does not imply risk or negativity, but simply lower favorability under the applied criteria.

Composite Play Maps
Composite play maps were developed by combining CRS maps for multiple play elements
within each play element group: subsurface, surface, and societal. The subsurface composite for
the Mt. Simon Sandstone was generated using CRS maps for storage formation thickness,
formation water salinity, caprock (Eau Claire) thickness, legacy well density, mapped faults,
seismic hazard, and proximity to saline springs. A separate subsurface composite was generated
for the Knox-St. Peter storage complex using CRS maps for St. Peter salinity, Maquoketa
thickness, St. Peter thickness, Galena depth, faults, seismic hazard, saline springs, and St. Peter
legacy wells density.
The surface composite was generated from CRS maps for rivers, roads, rails, wetlands, karst,
coal mines, and non-fuel mines. An alternative surface composite was also produced using only
wetlands, karst, coal mines, and non-fuel mines, with rivers, rails, and CO₂ point source locations
overlaid as reference features rather than contributing to the suitability raster. Natural Gas
Storage Field and IBDP/ICCS polygons were symbolized and overlaid on composite maps to
indicate suitable areas of known subsurface storage activity.
The composite maps applied a five-tier classification scheme with a null value (no data)
category. Corrections and refinements to individual play element CRS maps were made
iteratively based on team review, with adjustments including addition of the fifth null/no data
classification, revision of color ramps, and recategorization of certain features (e.g., reverting
federally owned land and parks to binary classifications, moving the sole source aquifer element
to the societal category).

Challenges and Limitations
Several challenges and limitations were encountered during the PBE and should be considered
when interpreting the results. First, data gaps exist in the geologic coverage: some geologic
datasets do not cover the entire state, requiring interpolation in some cases. For point-based
datasets with limited geographic distribution, the Distance Accumulation tool was used to
generate continuous statewide rasters, but this introduces uncertainty in areas far from the
original data points. For geologic rasters where no additional data existed outside the mapped
area (e.g., seal geometry, thickness, and depth), NODATA was assigned rather than extrapolated,
meaning those areas are classified as “More Information Needed” rather than assessed.
Second, uncertainty exists in the suitability criteria thresholds. These thresholds are based on
literature review and expert-led discussions and deliberations, but ultimately involve professional
judgment and may not apply equally at every location. Actual site performance can vary
depending on local conditions that are not captured at the screening scale of this analysis.
Third, the composite maps treat all CRS layers within a group equally, but in practice not all
factors carry the same importance for every potential project. No weighting scheme was applied
in this analysis, representing a conservative and transparent approach but one that may not reflect
the relative significance of individual play elements for specific use cases.
Fourth, the analysis represents a snapshot in time. New data points may become available that
would alter the suitability maps. However, the geodatabase and workflow were designed so that
updated layers can be refreshed without rebuilding the entire workflow from scratch, provided
that updated layers follow the same schema, naming conventions, and spatial reference as the
original play element datasets.

SHARING THE GEOSPATIAL DATABASE, PLAY MAPS, AND WEBSITE

Goals and Audience
The project website, A Play-Based Exploration of CCS in the Illinois Basin, was created to

stakeholders to make informed decisions to achieve their goals.
The website hosts many forms of communication tools such as StoryMaps, technical reports,

may be more interested in the composite maps to inform their potential CCS site location.

OPPORTUNITIES FOR EXPANSION
The PBE framework, geospatial database CRS maps, composite play maps, and project website
developed through this project provide a versatile foundation that can be expanded, refined, and
adapted to support a wide range of future applications. The work completed here represents a
snapshot in time: it integrates the best subsurface, surface, and societal data currently available to
the ISGS, applies suitability criteria informed by a substantial body of CCS research and
stakeholder input, and delivers the results through a public website with interactive web maps.
Each of these components, however, was designed to be extended. This section outlines five
priority directions for future work that build directly on the products of this project:
incorporating additional play elements, developing derivative play elements that combine
multiple raw data layers, implementing more sophisticated and auditable methods for combining
layers into composite play maps, evolving the web interface to support user-driven exploration,
and generalizing the suitability framework so that the same database can be repurposed for
subsurface uses other than carbon storage.

Additional Play Elements
The 24 play elements implemented as CRS maps in this project were selected because they are
well-defined, supported by publicly available or readily digitized data, and broadly applicable
across the Cambro-Ordovician storage complex in Illinois. During the project, a number of
additional play elements were considered, scoped, or partially developed but were ultimately not
included in the final composite analysis. These represent the most immediate opportunities for
incremental improvement of the geospatial database and CRS map suite. Future iterations of the
analysis should consider adding the following play elements:
  Private water wells. Private water wells may be more susceptible to contamination than
 community water supply wells because they are typically not subject to the same
 monitoring requirements. A private water well play element could be represented as a
 density map with contour lines used to separate suitability classes, providing a useful
 complement to the existing community water supply well play element.
  Distinct regulatory boundary play elements. The current regulatory boundaries play
 element treats state and county lines as a single binary layer. Separating state and county
 boundaries into two distinct play elements would better capture the differing regulatory
 regimes a project must navigate when crossing each type of boundary, and would allow
 users to apply different weights to each.
  Gas storage wells. Existing subsurface natural gas storage operations represent a
 consensus-important consideration for CCS siting, both because they compete for pore
 space and because they constrain the local pressure regime. Gas storage wells could be
 added as a standalone play element or merged with the existing UIC wells play element,
 with polygon outlines for natural gas storage below the Maquoketa Group included as a
 distinct overlay.
  Existing rights-of-way. Oil and gas pipeline rights-of-way and other existing utility
 corridors may provide a lower-cost path to building CO2 transportation infrastructure
 than developing new greenfield rights-of-way. A right-of-way play element would be

 53

 particularly valuable when the framework is used to evaluate transportation network
 design alongside storage siting.
  Additional geologic structures. Faults are currently the only structural feature included as
 a play element. Folds, flexures, and other structural features that may impact storage
 favorably (e.g., as structural traps for buoyant CO2) or unfavorably (e.g., as conduits for
 fluid migration) could be added as additional structural play elements as characterization
 data become available. Similarly, distance from basement is captured today only at a
 coarse resolution and would benefit from refined mapping in areas of active CCS
 development.
Beyond these specific additions, the suitability criteria applied to every play element in this
project represent best current judgment and should be revisited periodically as new data become
available, as the CCS industry matures, and as stakeholder feedback accumulates. Project
assumptions such as the 2.5-mile CO2 plume radius and the 10-mile Area of Review (AoR)
radius are reasonable starting points but may need to be adjusted for specific project scenarios,
reservoir types, or regulatory contexts.

Derivative Play Elements
The play elements implemented in this project are, with few exceptions, single-attribute layers
derived directly from a single underlying dataset (e.g., gross thickness, depth, salinity, or
distance to a feature). Many of the most informative quantities for CCS site evaluation, however,
are not single attributes but derived products that combine two or more raw layers. These
derivative play elements are not yet implemented in the project geodatabase but can be generated
from the data already assembled, and they represent a high-value direction for future work.
Several categories of derivative play elements are particularly promising. Net thickness maps,
generated by applying a porosity cutoff to gross thickness, would refine the storage reservoir
play elements by representing only the rock volume that contributes meaningfully to storage
capacity. Porosity-thickness product (φh) maps, the standard reservoir-engineering metric for
storage and flow capacity, integrate two of the most important reservoir attributes into a single
layer that better predicts injectivity than either attribute alone. Clay-rich thickness within
confining units, derived by combining gross seal thickness with lithologic data, would more
accurately capture actual sealing capacity than gross thickness alone.
A second category of derivatives moves from rock properties toward storage potential.
Volumetric storage potential maps, constructed by combining net thickness, porosity, depth-
dependent CO2 density, and an efficiency factor, translate the reservoir quality information into
per-area estimates of storage capacity expressed in tonnes of CO2 per unit area. Maps that
incorporate pressure and salinity gradients from existing regional reservoir models would further
refine these estimates and support evaluation of pressure management requirements. Building on
these, dollar value of storage per area could be estimated by combining storage capacity with
current and projected market values for storage (e.g., 45Q tax credits, voluntary market prices) to
produce maps that are directly meaningful to project developers, investors, and private
landowners.
A third category captures interactions among planned and existing projects. Maps showing
overlapping AoRs for multiple planned Class VI projects, or for combinations of CCS, natural
 54

gas storage, and other Class I and II UIC operations, would help regulators and developers
identify areas where careful coordination among operators is required. As more Class VI
applications are submitted in Illinois, this kind of derivative product is likely to become
increasingly important.
These derivative maps directly address one of the key gaps that motivated this project: the lack
of integration among the components of a CCS project in basin-scale screening tools. The
geodatabase developed here contains the underlying layers necessary to construct each of the
derivative products described above. The next phase of work should prioritize moving these
derivative play elements from concept to implementation, beginning with porosity-thickness and
net thickness maps for the principal storage reservoirs.

More Sophisticated and Auditable Composite Play Maps
The composite play maps produced in this project combine CRS maps using a straightforward
minimum-suitability approach: at each location, the composite suitability class is set by the least-
suitable contributing CRS map. This conservative approach has clear advantages. It is simple to
implement, easy to communicate to non-technical audiences, and reflects the geological reality
that any single critical risk factor (e.g., a thin reservoir, a nearby legacy well, or proximity to a
fault) can disqualify a site regardless of how favorable the other factors are. It is also the
approach most consistent with traditional PBE practice in the petroleum industry.
The minimum approach, however, has limitations. Composite suitability is controlled by
whichever single layer happens to be least suitable at a given location, which means the relative
importance of the other contributing factors is invisible to the user. Layers of varying data
quality, spatial resolution, and conceptual importance are treated as if they were interchangeable.
And the same minimum value can be reached by very different combinations of underlying
conditions, reducing the diagnostic value of the composite map for users who want to understand
why a particular location received its rating.
Future iterations of the composite play maps should explore more sophisticated and auditable
approaches to combining CRS layers. Several methods are well established in suitability
modeling and could be implemented within the existing ArcGIS Pro workflow. Weighted linear
combination assigns numerical weights to each contributing layer, with weights set on the basis
of expert judgment, data quality, or stakeholder priorities, and produces a continuous-valued
composite that reflects the relative importance of each input. Weight sensitivity analysis, in
which weights are varied systematically and the resulting composite maps compared, exposes
which areas are robust to weighting choices and which are sensitive to them. Layer
normalization rescales each CRS map to a common range so that no single layer dominates the
composite simply because of differences in its native value distribution. Multi-criteria decision
analysis (MCDA) methods such as the analytic hierarchy process can be used to derive
defensible weights from pairwise comparisons among layers.
Equally important is that any future composite-mapping approach should be auditable. The
minimum approach used in this project is auditable in the sense that the user can identify the
single controlling layer at any location, but it does not capture the influence of subordinate
layers. A future workflow should make it possible for a user (or a reviewer) to query any pixel of
a composite map and see the contribution of every input layer to the final score, the weight

 55

applied to each layer, and the suitability class of each underlying CRS map. This would
substantially strengthen the defensibility of the composite maps in regulatory, planning, and
stakeholder-engagement contexts.

An Improved Web Interface for User-Driven Exploration
The project website (https://ccs-play.web.illinois.edu/) presents the play elements, CRS maps,
and composite play maps as a curated set of pre-computed products. Users can browse individual
play element maps, read the rationale for each suitability classification, and view a small number
of composite maps generated by the project team using fixed combinations of contributing
layers. This design effectively communicates the project results to a broad audience and meets
the project's primary outreach objectives.
The natural next step is to move from a curated, read-only presentation toward an interactive web
interface that allows users to generate their own composite play maps. Such an interface would
let a user select which play elements to include in a composite map; assign weights to each
selected play element; choose a combination method (minimum, weighted average, weighted
minimum, or other); and view the resulting custom composite map alongside summary statistics
describing how much of the state falls in each suitability class. The interface should also expose
the auditability features described in the previous section, so that clicking on any location returns
a breakdown of how each contributing layer contributed to the final score.
This kind of interactive interface would substantially expand the utility of the project products. A
project developer could, for example, generate a composite map that emphasizes the play
elements most relevant to the developer's specific reservoir target and emissions source. A
regulator could examine how a proposed weighting scheme affects the spatial distribution of
high-suitability areas. A community organization could generate composites that emphasize
societal play elements relevant to their local context. The same underlying database and CRS
maps would support each of these use cases without requiring the project team to anticipate
every possible combination in advance.
Implementation of this functionality will require additional development effort beyond the
current website. The CRS maps would need to be served from a GIS server capable of
performing on-the-fly raster algebra (e.g., ArcGIS Image Server, GeoServer, or an equivalent),
the user interface would need to be designed with attention to accessibility and ease of use for
non-specialist audiences, and clear documentation would be needed to ensure that user-generated
composites are interpreted appropriately. The existing website architecture and the consistent
structure of the 24 play element pages provide a strong starting point for this expansion.

Adapting the Framework for Other Subsurface Uses
Subsurface pore space is increasingly in demand for a range of uses beyond CO₂ storage,
including cyclical or seasonal storage of natural gas, hydrogen, and compressed air; brine
disposal and pressure management; and fluid cycling for geothermal applications. In Illinois and
across the Illinois Basin, these uses often draw on the same regional stratigraphy, the same
legacy drilling footprint, and the same basin-scale constraints related to pressure, salinity, seal
integrity, and subsurface infrastructure. The Cambrian-Ordovician storage complex that is the
focus of this project hosts multiple stacked reservoir intervals interbedded with regionally

 56

persistent confining units, making it a leading example of a subsurface portfolio that could
support several of these uses simultaneously, given appropriate planning and coordination.
The play elements assembled in this project are largely use-agnostic: gross thickness, formation
water salinity, depth, distance to faults, distance to legacy wells, and distance to community
water supply wells are all relevant to any deep injection or storage application, whether the
injectant is CO2, natural gas, hydrogen, or wastewater. What differs between use cases is not the
underlying data but the suitability criteria applied to that data. A formation depth that is ideal for
CO2 storage may be too deep for compressed air energy storage; a salinity range that is
acceptable for CO2 injection may be unacceptable for hydrogen storage because of biological or
geochemical considerations; a distance from a legacy well that is acceptable for brine disposal
may be too small for CO 2 injection because of differences in plume size and risk profile.
This decoupling of data layers from suitability criteria is a powerful feature of the PBE
framework as implemented here. The geodatabase, once assembled, can be reused indefinitely;
what needs to change to support a new subsurface use is the set of suitability thresholds applied
to each layer. A future direction for this work is therefore to develop a flexible mechanism for
swapping suitability criteria, in which the user (or the project team) defines an alternative criteria
set for a given subsurface use and the entire suite of CRS maps and composite play maps is
regenerated automatically. Combined with the user-driven web interface described in the
previous section, this would make it possible to generate prospect maps for hydrogen storage,
compressed air energy storage, geothermal, brine disposal, and other applications using the same
database that supports the CCS analysis.
Such a capability is particularly relevant in the current regulatory and commercial environment.
The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency is beginning to face the prospect of reviewing
separate UIC Class VI applications with overlapping AoRs, and demand for pore space to
accommodate hydrogen and natural gas storage is expected to grow alongside continued growth
in CCS. Regulators, project developers, and impacted communities all benefit from a common
framework that can be used to evaluate the availability of subsurface resources, identify potential
conflicts among planned projects, and communicate the trade-offs associated with different uses
of the same subsurface volume. The PBE workflow developed here is well positioned to support
that role with relatively modest additional investment.

Summary
Taken together, the five directions described above describe a coherent path from the products
delivered by this project toward a substantially more capable basin-scale decision-support
resource for subsurface energy applications in Illinois. The first two directions, additional play
elements and derivative play elements, expand the inputs to the analysis. The third direction,
more sophisticated and auditable composite mapping, improves the analytical engine that
combines those inputs. The fourth direction, an improved web interface, places that engine in the
hands of end users. The fifth direction, flexible suitability criteria, generalizes the entire system
to support subsurface uses beyond CCS. Each direction builds on the geodatabase, workflow,
website, and stakeholder relationships developed during this project, and each addresses a need
that emerged from the project work and feedback from the Illinois Basin Collaboration &
Insights workshop. Pursuing these directions will require continued investment, but the
foundation built by this project makes that investment efficient and the expected return high.
