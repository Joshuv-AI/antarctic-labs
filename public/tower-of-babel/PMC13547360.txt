Protective effects of propofol, ketamine, and their combination against H2O2-induced oxidative stress in A549 cells: an evaluation of metabolic activity and antioxidant defense
Authors: Zeyno Nuhoğlu, Abdurrahman Aksoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the loss of cell viability and antioxidant effects of ketamine, propofol, and their combination, against hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-induced oxidative stress in A549 cells. IC50 values were determined by applying different concentrations of H2O2 (100–2000 µM), propofol, ketamine, and a propofol-ketamine combination (10–2000 µg/mL). Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay after 24 h. Based on the observed reduction in metabolic activity profiles, three specific concentrations (75, 125, 250 µg/mL) were selected to evaluate the antioxidant responses. The protein levels of lysed cells were determined, and malondialdehyde (MDA), total antioxidant status (TAS), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and total glutathione (tGSH) were measured. Treatment with propofol, ketamine, and their combination was associated with lower levels of the lipid peroxidation marker MDA and with higher levels of antioxidant parameters (TAS, SOD, tGSH) under H2O2-induced oxidative stress, as compared with H2O2-only controls. The propofol-ketamine combination showed enhanced effects compared to individual treatments by further modulating antioxidant properties in A549 cells, warranting further investigation into their potential supportive roles in lung cancer models.

Introduction

Oxidative stress results from an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the body’s antioxidant defenses, leading to cellular damage through structural impairment or altered signaling pathways. It plays a crucial role in various diseases, including cancer, aging, and neurodegeneration, with evidence linking it to the initiation and progression of carcinogenesis1,2.

Cancer is a significant cause of mortality globally3, with lung cancer remaining the leading cause of cancer-related deaths and the most commonly diagnosed malignancy as of 20224. Oxidative stress is key driver of lung inflammation and carcinogenesis2. While surgery is the primary treatment for lung cancer, perioperative factors such as anesthetic agents may influence prognosis, prompting increased research into their effects on treatment outcomes5.

Propofol and ketamine are commonly used intravenous anesthetics; with distinct pharmacological properties, propofol is primarily employed for anesthesia induction, maintenance, and sedation, while ketamine exhibits sedative, analgesic, bronchodilatory, and sympathomimetic effects6–8. Combining these agents provides clinical benefits, including hemodynamic stability, reduced respiratory depression, and improved postoperative analgesia9. Propofol exhibits vigorous antioxidant activity, particularly in reducing lipid peroxidation, as demonstrated by in vivo and in vitro studies10,11. In contrast, the effects of ketamine on oxidative stress remain unclear, with some studies suggesting it reduces ROS production in neutrophils12, while others indicate it increases ROS levels in the brain. Additionally, propofol and ketamine have been shown to inhibit tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and migration while promoting apoptosis. Although the individual effects of these anesthetics have been studied, the potential of their combination to modulate oxidative stress in lung cancer models remains largely unexplored. Recent evidence suggests that various bioactive compounds can protect against oxidative stress-induced cellular damage. For instance, tannic acid has been shown to protect neuroblastoma cell against H2O2-induced oxidative stress by suppressing oxidative damage and apoptosis13. Furthermore, combination therapies targeting multiple cellular pathways have demonstrated enhanced antitumor efficacy, as reported in gastric adenocarcinoma models14. These findings highlight the importance of investigating combined treatment strategies to achieve superior antioxidant and cytotoxic outcomes. In this context, the combined efficacy of propofol and ketamine under H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress in A549 cells represents a novel area of investigation. To our knowledge, no prior study has evaluated the protective potential of this anesthetic combination against H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage in a lung adenocarcinoma cell model. The present study assesses the dose-dependent effects of propofol, ketamine, and their combination on metabolic activity (MTT assay) and on H₂O₂-induced changes in oxidative stress biomarkers (MDA, SOD, tGSH, TAS) in A549 cells. We hypothesize that the combination of propofol and ketamine will exert a stronger protective effect against H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage in A549 cells than either agent administered alone, as evidenced by greater attenuation of lipid peroxidation (MDA), enhanced antioxidant defense capacity (SOD, tGSH, TAS), and improved preservation of metabolic activity (MTT), potentially through complementary modulatory pathways acting on overlapping redox-related targets.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Propofol (97%) and H₂O₂ (35%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Sa. Louis, MO, USA). Ketamine (99%) was purchased from Arion (Istanbul, Türkiye). Master stock solutions were prepared in DMSO with mixing at 1500 rpm to enhance solubility and stability for both drugs, which were stored at -20 °C before use. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO with purity higher than 99%) was procured from Sigma-Aldrich. Working solutions were freshly diluted using a culture medium without serum. Acetonitrile, 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane (TEP), 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH), sodium hydroxide, perchloric acid, and sulphuric acid were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. These reagents were of analytical grade unless stated otherwise.

Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ) was obtained from a MilliporeSigma™ Milli-di Simplicity 185 Ultrapure Water System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Triton X-100, NP-40, MTT, and PMSF were procured from Bio Basic (Bio Basic Inc., Canada). Trypan blue (0.5%), Trypsin-EDTA, and MEM-Eagle with Non-Essential Amino Acids were purchased from Biological Industries (Beit Haemek, Israel). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4; Sigma-Aldrich) was prepared and sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min using a Nüve OT-40 L autoclave (Ankara, Türkiye).

Cell culture

The A549 (CCL-185) cell line used in the experiments was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, lot # 70018877, Manassas, VA, USA). Human A549 cells were grown in Ham’s F-12 Nutrient Mixture (Ham’s F-12) medium containing 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 1% (v/v) L-glutamine (200 mM), 1% (v/v) sodium pyruvate, 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin A and 100 U/mL streptomycin), and 1% (v/v) non-essential amino acids. The media were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Sa Louis, MO, USA). All supplements were sourced from Biowest (Bradenton, FL, USA).

Cells were seeded in 75 cm² flasks (with standard screw caps and filters) and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The medium was changed every other day, passaged according to the manufacturer’s protocol when the cells reached confluence. All the procedures were carried out in a laminar flow hood (MN-120, Nüve, Türkiye) under sterile conditions. Under these conditions, cells grew well and were consistent with the morphological characteristics of well-differentiated A549 cells.

Measurement of cell viability- MTT assay and treatment

The MTT assay was performed following some modifications to the protocol of Siddiqui et al. (2008)15. Cells (1 × 104 per well) were seeded in transparent 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with the serum-free medium containing different concentrations (10–2000 µg/mL) of propofol, ketamine, their combination, and H₂O₂ (100–2000 µM). The H₂O₂ concentration range was kept wide based on similar oxidative stress models in the literature, and to observe the loss of cell viability in the cells. As a result of preliminary screening tests, a concentration of 500 µM, which reduces cell viability by approximately 50% (IC50), was selected as the final study concentration to observe the protective effects clearly16. )The cells were exposed to these anesthetics and H₂O₂ for 24 h at 37 °C. Following this, tetrazolium bromide salt (5 mg/mL of stock in PBS) was added to the plates (10 µL/well in 100 µL cell suspension), and the plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The reaction mixture was carefully taken out. Then, 100 µL/well of DMSO was added to each well and pipetted to homogenize the content. The plates were kept on an orbital shaker (WiseShake, SHO-2D Digital Orbital Shaker, Türkiye) for 15 min at room temperature and then read at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite F50, Mannedorf, Switzerland). All tests were prepared three times. In each test, 0.2% DMSO was used as the negative control. Untreated cells were used to obtain the absorbance of 100% viability. The results of different absorbances were presented as a percentage of the control (Fig. 1). Observation of cellular morphology

To determine the effects of propofol, ketamine, a combination of propofol and ketamine, and H2O2 on A549 cells, the differentiated A549 cells were plated at a density of 0.1 × 106 cells/well in a 6-well dish. When cell density reached approximately 70 to 80%, the cells were exposed to the indicated drugs for 24 h. Then, the cell morphologic changes were examined in detail under an inverted phase contrast microscope at magnification x 10 (Zeiss Vert. A1, Jena, Germany) (Fig. 2).

Biochemical analysis

Cell lysis

Cells reaching 80–90% confluency were counted using trypan blue, and seeded into T25 flasks at a density of 4 × 10⁶ cells in 5 mL of medium, and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After incubation, cells were harvested, washed, and centrifuged under cold conditions. A lysis buffer containing 150 mM (0.438 g) NaCl, 50 mM (0.302 g) Tris-HCl, 1% (v/v) NP-40 (500 µL), and 1 mM PMSF was prepared and brought up to a final volume of 50 mL with autoclaved ultrapure water. The buffer adjusted to pH 8.5 was added, followed by centrifugation and was sonicated (Sonics VCX 130 PB, 130 W, 20 kHz, Sonics and Materials Inc., Newton, USA) in an ice bath to complete cell lysis. Lysates were stored at − 80 °C until further biochemical analysis.

2.5.2. Measurement of MDA

The method of Mateos et al. (2004) was applied with some modifications to determine the MDA level in cell lysates18. MDA levels in the cell supernatant were quantified using the DNPH derivatization method and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). External calibration standards were prepared within the 0.1–10 nmol/mL concentration range using TEP in PBS.

For sample preparation, 125 µL of the cell supernatant was treated with 25 µL of 6 M aqueous sodium hydroxide, followed by incubation at 60 °C for 30 min. Protein precipitation was achieved by adding 35% perchloric acid, and the mixture was centrifuged at 2800 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was transferred to an Eppendorf tube and mixed with DNPH prepared in hydrochloric acid. This reaction mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, a 50 µL aliquot was injected into an HPLC system (Shimadzu LC-20AT Prominence, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a photodiode array detector (SPD-M20A, 310 nm) (Fig. 3).

Chromatographic separation was performed using an ACE 5 C18 column (4.0 × 125 mm, 5 μm particle size, ACT, Scotland). The mobile phase was prepared by mixing ultrapure water (0.2% acetic acid) with acetonitrile (62:38, v/v), and then filtered and degassed. The mobile phase was applied isocratically at a 6 mL/min flow rate. The column oven was set at 45 °C. The method exhibited satisfactory linearity (R² = 0.999), with a limit of detection (LOD) and a limit of quantification (LOQ) of 0.003 nmol/mL and 0.01 nmol/mL, respectively. Protein content was determined using the Bradford assay (Bradford, 1976) with its reagent19. The MDA levels were normalized to protein content and expressed per milligram.

Measurement of SOD activity and contents of tGSH and TAS

The SOD activity (OD 440 nm) and contents of tGSH (OD 405 nm) and TAS (OD 570 nm) were measured in cell lysates using the Superoxide Dismutase Activity Kit (ab65354, Abcam), Glutathione Assay Kit (ab239709, Abcam), and Total Antioxidant Status Kit (ab65329, Abcam) respectively. All colorimetric analyses (SOD, tGSH, TAS) were validated by strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s protocols. The sensitivity and reproducibility of the methods were confirmed by the inclusion of standard curves and low coefficients of variation (CV < 10%) in each experimental setup. All obtained biochemical data were normalized to the total protein amount determined by the Bradford method.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times. Differences were analyzed with Prism software (version 10.3.1; GraphPad, San Diego, USA). This program calculated IC50 values from dose-absorbance viability data by nonlinear regression analysis. Before variance analysis, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to examine whether the data were normally distributed. The homogeneity of variances between groups was evaluated with the Brown-Forsythe test. Values were expressed as mean ± SEM. Multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a Tukey post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as a P-value of less than 0.05.

Results and discussion

Findings on the determination of cell viability

The underlying mechanisms by which propofol, ketamine, and their combination exert loss of cell viability or antioxidant effects on A549 lung epithelial cells have not yet been fully elucidated. Numerous studies have revealed that H₂O₂ initiates loss of cell viability and inhibits proliferation through various mechanisms in A549 lung epithelial cells. The observed decrease in cell viability following 24-h exposure to 500 µM H₂O₂ supports the hypothesis that oxidative stress plays a key role in compromising cellular integrity in this model20. This reduction is presumed to result from apoptosis caused by mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative damage induced by H₂O₂ in A549 cells. The findings of the present study confirm that H₂O₂ exposure induces significant loss of cell viability in A549 lung epithelial cells, which is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that H₂O₂ triggers oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptotic loss of cell viability21,22.

In this study, A549 cells were exposed to 500 µM H₂O₂ for 24 h, and changes in cell viability were assessed. A significant decrease in cell viability was observed only in the H₂O₂ group compared to the control group. However, it was found that propofol significantly prevented H₂O₂-induced loss of cell viability in the P2 + H₂O₂ and P3 + H₂O₂ groups (p < 0.001). Notably, propofol exhibited strong cytoprotective effects, significantly improving cell viability in H₂O₂-treated groups23. This aligns with literature that suggests propofol is a potent antioxidant by scavenging reactive oxygen species24. Similarly, in the K3 + H₂O₂ group, high-dose ketamine significantly reduced loss of cell viability induced by H₂O₂ (p < 0.001). Ketamine showed a protective effect at higher concentrations, which may be attributed to its NMDA receptor antagonism and potential antioxidant activity25. Furthermore, the propofol-ketamine combination significantly prevented H₂O₂-induced cell loss in the com2 + H₂O₂ and com3 + H₂O₂ groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4).

The obtained data indicate that propofol and ketamine, whether administered individually or in combination, possess the potential to preserve metabolic activity under oxidative stress conditions. This effect of propofol may be related to its free radical scavenging properties reported in the literature, while the effect of ketamine may be associated with potential antioxidant activities described in existing studies. However, since specific death pathways (e.g., apoptosis) were not directly investigated in this study, further analyses are required to determine the exact nature of the observed effects on loss of cell viability.

Findings on the biochemical analysis

The effects of propofol on MDA, SOD, TAS, and tGSH levels

Propofol has been shown to significantly affect oxidative stress, particularly by modulating malondialdehyde (MDA), a key marker of lipid peroxidation. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that propofol reduces MDA accumulation and mitigates H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage. For example, studies in PC12 cells and cardiomyocytes have revealed that propofol reduces MDA levels and protects against oxidative injury in a dose-dependent manner. These effects are attributed to the compound’s ability to modulate antioxidant signaling pathways such as NADPH oxidase inhibition and GSK3β/Nrf2 activation26. In the present study, increasing concentrations of propofol significantly reduced MDA levels compared to the control group (p < 0.01) and attenuated the H₂O₂-induced rise in MDA (p < 0.05), consistent with previous findings. Furthermore, while only the highest propofol dose (P3 + H₂O₂) showed a significant increase in superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity compared to the control (p < 0.05), all propofol-treated H₂O₂ groups (P1–P3 + H₂O₂) demonstrated a significant reversal of H₂O₂-induced SOD reduction (p < 0.01).

These results support earlier research showing that propofol can enhance antioxidant defenses in a dose-dependent manner, particularly by upregulating SOD activity through mechanisms involving Nrf2 and NLRP3 signaling. In addition, propofol was shown to reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and improve cell viability under oxidative stress conditions27,28. Total glutathione (tGSH) levels were also significantly elevated with increasing propofol concentrations (p < 0.01), aligning with reports of propofol enhancing glutathione-related enzymatic activity and protecting against ischemic cellular damage. Similarly, total antioxidant status (TAS) levels significantly increased in the P1, P2, and P3 + H₂O₂ groups compared to the control (p < 0.05). They were significantly higher in the P2 + H₂O₂ and P3 + H₂O₂ groups compared to the H₂O₂ group alone (p < 0.05), indicating a dose-dependent protective effect of propofol (Table 1).

In conclusion, propofol was found to decrease MDA levels while enhancing SOD, tGSH, and TAS levels in A549 cells under oxidative stress, indicating its potential role as an effective antioxidant. These findings suggest that propofol may help protect lung cancer cells from ROS-induced oxidative damage and may be a candidate for therapeutic interventions targeting oxidative stress (Fig. 5).

The effects of ketamine on MDA, SOD, TAS, and tGSH levels

H₂O₂ significantly increased MDA levels, a marker of lipid peroxidation, indicating oxidative damage to cell membranes, consistent with previous findings29. However, treatment with the K3 + H₂O₂ compound markedly reduced MDA levels, even below control levels (p < 0.001), suggesting strong antioxidant and lipid peroxidation-inhibiting effects. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) is a key antioxidant enzyme responsible for the dismutation of superoxide radicals. SOD activity decreased significantly in cells treated with H₂O₂, reflecting weakened antioxidant defenses, which is consistent with Xu et al. (2016)30. K3 + H₂O₂ treatment strongly boosted SOD activity, indicating its potential to enhance cellular antioxidant capacity, whereas K2 + H₂O₂ had a moderate effect, and K1 + H₂O₂ showed minimal impact. Glutathione (tGSH) levels are a key indicator of intracellular antioxidant capacity. A decrease in tGSH is typically associated with increased oxidative stress. H₂O₂ treatment led to a significant reduction in tGSH levels (p < 0.01); however, administration of K3 + H₂O₂ resulted in tGSH levels surpassing the control group’s levels. This finding suggests that K3 may exert a regulatory effect on glutathione metabolism (p < 0.05). Similarly, previous studies have reported that propofol and other phenolic compounds can elevate tGSH levels and activate glutathione reductase enzymes31. H₂O₂ reduced TAS levels, indicating weakened antioxidant defenses. K3 + H₂O₂ treatment significantly increased TAS, surpassing both the H₂O₂ (p < 0.05) and control groups (p < 0.01), suggesting a broad activation of the antioxidant system. This aligns with Crispo et al. (2010), who found that polyphenolic compounds can enhance TAS under oxidative stress32 (Table 2).

The study shows that K3 + H₂O₂ effectively counters H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage by enhancing both enzymatic (SOD) and non-enzymatic (tGSH, TAS) antioxidant defenses. K2 + H₂O₂ offered moderate protection, while K1 + H₂O₂ was less effective. K3 + H₂O₂ appears to be a promising candidate for treating oxidative stress-related conditions, though further in vivo studies are needed to confirm its potential (Fig. 6).

The effects of propofol and ketamine combination on MDA, SOD, TAS, and tGSH levels

The combination of propofol and ketamine, commonly called “ketofol,” demonstrated strong antioxidant effects, reducing MDA levels and enhancing SOD, tGSH, and TAS activities in H₂O₂-exposed A549 cells (Fig. 7). In all three combination groups (com1- to com3 + H₂O₂), MDA levels were significantly reduced, and antioxidant markers were markedly elevated, particularly in com3 + H₂O₂ (Table 3). These findings agree with previous in vivo studies showing ketofol’s anti-inflammatory and antioxidative potential in lung tissue33,34.These data suggest that the agents may have an additive interaction in mitigating oxidative damage.

Conclusion

Research on cancer treatments is evolving rapidly; however, chemotherapeutic drugs frequently have adverse effects on unintended tissues because they produce free radicals that damage cells. Therefore, incorporating drugs with anticancer and antioxidant properties is essential to improve treatment efficacy and reduce toxicity. This study demonstrates that propofol, ketamine, and their combination exhibit dose-dependent preservation of metabolic activity and enhance antioxidant defenses in A549 human lung cancer cells under H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress. These agents significantly decreased lipid peroxidation (MDA levels) and increased antioxidant defense markers (SOD, tGSH, TAS), suggesting their potential to reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced oxidative injury in this in vitro model. Notably, the propofol-ketamine combination exerted an enhanced antioxidant effect by further modulating cellular properties in A549 cells compared to individual treatments. Given the role of oxidative stress in ccellular health, anesthetic agents with antioxidant properties may offer supportive benefits during the perioperative period. These findings provide a preliminary foundation for further research into the potential utility of propofol and ketamine in managing oxidative stress during anesthesia, though further in vivo and mechanistic studies are required to establish clinical relevance.

Limitations

Although the A549 cell line is a valuable in vitro model for lung adenocarcinoma, it does not replicate the complexity of in vivo systems, such as interactions with immune cells, the tumor microenvironment, or systemic metabolism. This study also did not include normal (non-cancerous) lung epithelial cells, limiting the assessment of drug selectivity and potential toxicity in healthy tissues. Moreover, the study evaluated only four oxidative stress related markers (MDA, SOD, tGSH, TAS), which may not comprehensively reflect redox dynamics. Although this study has characterized the effects of propofol and ketamine on antioxidant capacity, it did not directly investigate specific molecular pathways such as apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, or the Nrf2/NLRP3 axes. Future research incorporating protein and gene expression analyses is required to elucidate the definitive roles of these mechanisms. Additionally, a key limitations of the study is that a formal synergy analysis (e.g., combination index or isobologram) was not performed for the propofol and ketamine combination. In addition, the 24-hour exposure period may not adequately represent long-term cellular responses. Future studies should explore longer time points, repeated dosing, and in vivo validation to better establish clinical relevance.
