Kinetically templated fibrillar self-assembly via two-dimensional nanocrystals enables 21% efficient organic solar cells
Authors: Zhenye Li, Rujin Zhou, Jingchuan Chen, Xuanang Luo, Xinrui Liu, Yingjie He, Feng Peng, Wenkai Zhong, Lei Ying
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe performance of organic solar cells (OSCs) is critically governed by the fibrillar organization of bulk-heterojunction active layers, yet controlling long-range, directional fibrillar morphology under nonequilibrium processing remains challenging. Here we report that two-dimensional cobalt phosphosulfide (CoPS3) nanocrystals act as kinetic templates that reprogram molecular self-assembly during film formation. Through collective electronic interactions with conjugated polymer donors and small-molecule acceptors, CoPS3 modulates molecular densification and phase separation pathways, enabling refined, long-range fibrillar networks without increasing crystallinity. The resulting morphology enhances exciton dissociation and charge transport, and inhibits recombination, delivering a power conversion efficiency of 21.0%. This templating strategy is effective across multiple OSC systems, simultaneously improving efficiency and storage stability, offering a promising route for kinetic morphology control in organic optoelectronics.Subject terms: Solar cells, Solar cells

Introduction

Organic solar cells (OSCs) have witnessed remarkable progress over the past decade1,2 with power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) now exceeding 20%3,4. These advances have been primarily enabled by the development of non-fullerene acceptors and refined donor/acceptor (D/A) combinations, which together optimize light absorption, energy-level alignment, and charge separation5,6. As molecular design gradually matures, further improvements in device performance increasingly hinge on the ability to construct active-layer morphologies that support efficient exciton diffusion, interfacial charge transfer, and long-range charge transport7,8.

Among the various morphological motifs explored in OSCs, fibrillar networks are widely regarded as key structural features underlying high-performance devices9,10 Such networks can provide domain sizes compatible with exciton diffusion lengths while offering percolated pathways for charge transport11. Despite their frequent observation, fibrillar morphologies in solution-processed films often exhibit limited continuity, poor directional correlation, and coexistence with amorphous mixed regions. These structural imperfections introduce energetic disorder and transport bottlenecks, ultimately constraining performance12,13.

Current morphology-control strategies in OSCs predominantly focus on enhancing crystallinity through molecular design, solvent additives, or post-treatment processes14,15. However, improved crystalline order does not necessarily translate into well-organized fibrillar networks under nonequilibrium film-formation conditions16. During solution deposition, organic semiconductors must satisfy multiple, often competing system-level requirements, including phase-separated shape, length scale, purity, and interfacial packing. This is a rapid kinetic arrest process, where controlling crystallinity alone is insufficient17,18. Effective morphology control requires regulating the self-assembly pathways, particularly the temporal sequence of molecular densification and phase separation that determines how fibrillar structures emerge and become kinetically locked.

Here, we introduce two-dimensional (2D) cobalt phosphosulfide (CoPS3) nanocrystals19,20 as a kinetic modulator for regulating the fibrillar self-assembly mechanism in organic photovoltaic films. We show that the anisotropic and electronically interactive surfaces of CoPS3 influence the molecular densification and phase-separation pathways during film drying, thereby guiding donor and acceptor molecules toward more extended and directionally correlated fibrillar networks without substantially increasing crystallinity. This kinetically regulated morphology contributes to improved exciton dissociation, charge transport, and recombination suppression, enabling OSCs with PCEs of up to 21.0% and broad applicability across multiple D/A systems.

Results

Kinetically templated self-assembly via 2D nanocrystals

The structures of D18, L8-BO and CoPS3 nanocrystals are displayed in Fig. 1a. As illustrated in Fig. 1b, conventional morphology control in polymer-based organic semiconductors typically relies on polymer fibrillation, which creates confined nanoscale regions that subsequently direct molecular assembly and phase separation21,22 In contrast, we propose a kinetically templated self-assembly mechanism in which 2D CoPS3 nanocrystals modulate the assembly pathway during non-equilibrium film formation (Fig. 1c). 2D CoPS3 nanocrystals provide anisotropic and electronically interactive interfaces that are expected to influence the adsorption, alignment, and densification of conjugated materials, particularly the non-fullerene acceptors, during rapid solvent evaporation. Rather than directly enhancing lattice-level crystallinity, such kinetic modulation may alter the temporal sequence and cooperativity of molecular aggregation and phase separation, thereby favoring the development of more extended and directionally correlated fibrillar networks. This process enables the formation of long-range, high-purity fibrils within a weakly ordered matrix, which become the dominant structural factor governing exciton diffusion, interfacial charge separation, and charge-transport percolation in the active layer.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) topographic analysis reveals that the exfoliated CoPS3 nanocrystals exhibit an ultrasmooth morphology with an average size of ~18 nm (Fig. 2a, b). Considering the typical C-C bond length in conjugated backbones, this dimension corresponds to the length scale of over one hundred C-C bonds, suggesting that the CoPS3 nanocrystals can interact with multiple conjugated molecular segments rather than isolated molecular units. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) further confirms the local crystalline integrity of the exfoliated CoPS3 nanocrystals, displaying well-resolved lattice fringes with interplanar spacings of 0.43 nm and 0.22 nm, which are assigned to the (020) and (131) planes, respectively (Fig. 2c). These results demonstrate that CoPS3 retains its characteristic crystalline lattice after exfoliation while maintaining a thin two-dimensional geometry that is fundamentally distinct from conventional molecular additives23,24. Crucially, effective kinetic templating requires that the nanocrystals can be well distributed throughout the thin film rather than forming isolated aggregates. Scanning electron microscopy combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) elemental mapping of D18:L8-BO:CoPS3 blend films shows a spatially dispersed phosphorus signal over the probed region (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating that the distribution of CoPS3 within the bulk heterojunction and ensuring that CoPS3 interfaces are readily accessible to both donor and acceptor molecules during film formation.

To probe the interfacial affinity of CoPS3 nanocrystals toward the active-layer components, full-atomic molecular dynamics simulations were performed (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 2). The calculated binding energies for CoPS3/D18 (–25.10 kcal mol−1) and CoPS3/L8-BO (–30.83 kcal mol−1) interfaces both exceed that of the D18/L8-BO pair (–22.44 kcal mol−1), while remaining weaker than the D18:D18 and L8-BO:L8-BO interactions. This binding-energy hierarchy supports the role of CoPS3 as a kinetic regulator rather than a dominant binding partner, enabling it to modulate local donor-acceptor organization during film solidification without disrupting the intrinsic self-aggregation of D18 and L8-BO.

Electron density difference analyses reveal a highly selective and complementary interaction landscape (Supplementary Fig. 3). At the CoPS3/D18 interface, charge redistribution is primarily localized on the BDT-based donor segments, whereas at the CoPS3/L8-BO interface, electronic modulation is concentrated on the thiadiazole core and cyano-indanone end groups. Such site-specific interactions indicate that CoPS3 engages distinct molecular motifs of the donor and acceptor, enabling simultaneous but differentiated interfacial electronic interactions with both components. To further clarify the nature of these interactions, interaction region indicator (IRI) analysis was performed (Supplementary Fig. 4). The interfacial IRI isosurfaces are dominated by green regions without pronounced blue features, indicating that the CoPS3/organic interactions arise mainly from weak van der Waals-type noncovalent attractions rather than covalent or ionic bonding. The CoPS3/L8-BO pair exhibits more spatially extended weak-attraction regions than the CoPS3/D18 pair, suggesting a stronger interfacial affinity toward L8-BO. These weak, site-specific, electron-density-mediated interfacial interactions can modulate the local adsorption and organization of donor and acceptor molecules during film formation. Hole-electron population analyses show delocalized transition orbitals extending across CoPS3 ﻿and adjacent organic molecules (Supplementary Fig. 5), supporting the presence of intermolecular electronic coupling beyond a purely geometrical templating effect. UV-vis-NIR absorption shows that the L8-BO-dominated absorption band exhibits an additional ~10 nm red shift around 800 nm after CoPS3 incorporation, while CoPS3 itself contributes negligible absorption in this region (Supplementary Fig. 6). This result suggests that CoPS3/L8-BO interfacial interactions promote local electronic coupling and organization of L8-BO during film formation.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) measurements were further used to experimentally probe the interfacial interactions between CoPS3 and the organic components (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). For D18, the N 1 s and F 1 s core-level peaks show negligible binding-energy shifts after CoPS3 incorporation, suggesting the absence of strong localized charge transfer at these heteroatom sites, while the attenuation of the C=O stretch related FTIR band around 1770 cm−1 indicates perturbation of the D18 conjugated backbone25. In contrast, L8-BO exhibits more pronounced XPS changes, with the N 1s and F 1s signals shifting toward higher binding energy, together with broadened C≡N stretching at ~2210 cm−1 and attenuated C-O/C-N vibrations at 1136 cm−1 in the FTIR spectra. These spectroscopic changes provide experimental support for differentiated, noncovalent interfacial electronic coupling between CoPS3 and the donor/acceptor components, consistent with the electronic-structure calculations.

To further connect these interfacial electronic interactions with the resulting molecular organization, we analyzed the intermolecular radial distribution function g(r) between D18 and L8-BO in the blend with and without CoPS3 (Fig. 2e, f). The incorporation of CoPS3 leads to an increased D18/L8-BO g(r) over the characteristic intermolecular distance range, indicating an enhanced probability of regulated donor-acceptor spatial association relative to the pristine blend. This result suggests that the CoPS3-mediated interfacial interaction not only perturbs the electronic environment of adjacent molecules, but also modifies their local packing correlations during assembly.

The structural, spatial, electronic, and molecular organization results establish the 2D CoPS3 nanocrystals as anisotropic, electronically interactive platforms that are well dispersed within the bulk heterojunction. Such attributes allow CoPS3 to modulate molecular adsorption, alignment, and densification during rapid solvent evaporation, thereby setting the physical and electronic foundation for kinetically templated self-assembly.

We next monitored the film-formation process using in situ UV-vis-NIR absorption spectroscopy to resolve the molecular densification kinetics during spin coating (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 9). For both pristine and CoPS3-containing blend films, the spectral evolution can be divided into three distinct stages: an initial solvent-dominated regime with decreasing absorption intensity, a rapid densification regime characterized by pronounced absorption growth and red shifts, and a final kinetically frozen solid-state regime26. Notably, the incorporation of CoPS3 substantially alters the temporal characteristics of the densification stage. Time-resolved peak tracking reveals that the onset of molecular densification is delayed and the corresponding densification window is significantly extended in the presence of CoPS3, particularly for the acceptor component. Meanwhile, the acceptor absorption peak exhibits an additional red shift of ~10 nm, suggesting an increased local electronic coupling during film formation. This spectral shift is unlikely to arise from enhanced long-range crystalline ordering, since the GIWAXS-resolved crystalline characteristics of the blend films remain nearly unchanged after CoPS3 incorporation, as discussed below. Instead, it suggests that CoPS3 promotes the formation of locally associated acceptor species in non-crystalline or weakly ordered regions, as further supported by TD-DFT calculations showing red-shifted absorption for locally stacked acceptor configurations relative to isolated molecules (Supplementary Fig. 10)27. This prolonged assembly window indicates that CoPS3 delays molecular densification and suppresses premature kinetic arrest, enabling a more cooperative, time-extended molecular rearrangement, rather than a rapid, uncorrelated collapse28. Such kinetic reprogramming provides the necessary temporal freedom and favorable local intermolecular association for directional self-assembly to propagate across larger length scales.

To determine whether the modified assembly behavior is accompanied by enhanced crystalline ordering, we performed grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) on blend films. As shown in Fig. 3b, the π-π stacking distances, lamellar spacings, and coherence lengths remain nearly identical upon CoPS3 incorporation (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 1). These results indicate that CoPS3 preserves the intrinsic crystalline packing in D18:L8-BO blend29.

The most striking consequence of the kinetically reprogrammed assembly process emerges at the mesoscale, as revealed by atomic force microscopy-infrared (AFM-IR) mapping (Fig. 3c). By selectively probing the characteristic vibrational mode of the acceptor (1072 cm−1), AFM-IR directly visualizes the spatial distribution and continuity of the phase-separated fibrillar network (Supplementary Fig. 12). The CoPS3 blend film displays a fundamentally different morphology characterized by markedly narrower, longer, and more directionally aligned fibrils across the imaging area. Orientational analysis yields a high Hermans orientation factor (S2D) of 0.81, compared to 0.41 for the pristine blend (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 13). The average fibril length increases from 121 nm to 143 nm, while the fibril width is reduced from ~17 nm to ~12 nm (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Consistent with these real-space observations, N K-edge resonant soft X-ray scattering (RSoXS)30, whose contrast at 400.2 eV is predominantly weighted by L8-BO-rich domains, as supported by the energy-dependent integrated scattering intensity that follows the near-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (NEXAFS) feature of L8-BO rather than that of D18 (Supplementary Fig. 15), reveals a scattering feature at 0.017 Å−1 in the CoPS3 blend film, corresponding to a fibril-to-fibril distance of 37 nm. In contrast, the pristine blend shows only weak intensity variations in this q-region (Fig. 3e, f). This scattering signature supports the formation of acceptor-rich fibrillar domains with higher purity in the CoPS3 blend. This assignment is further supported by C K-edge RSoXS at 284.4 eV, where the CoPS3 blend shows a shoulder at 0.0182 Å−1, corresponding to a characteristic spacing of ~35 nm (Supplementary Fig. 16), in good agreement with the fibril-to-fibril distance obtained from the N K-edge analysis. Complementary C K-edge RSoXS at 285.2 eV, which probes the overall compositional fluctuations of the blend, reveals a shift of the characteristic scattering feature from 0.0080 Å−1 to 0.0066 Å−1 upon CoPS3 incorporation, corresponding to a change in the larger-scale characteristic spacing from ~78 nm to ~95 nm (Supplementary Figs. 17–18).

Therefore, CoPS3 acts as an electronically interactive 2D kinetic template that modulates the temporal sequence of molecular densification and multi-scale phase separation during film formation. This electronically interactive nature originates from the site-specific interfacial electronic coupling between CoPS3 and the conjugated donor/acceptor motifs, as reflected by the interfacial electron-density redistribution and spectroscopic variations. Such electronic coupling may provide transient interfacial interaction sites for adjacent organic molecules and help reshape the local interfacial energy landscape during solvent evaporation, thereby linking the distinctive electronic characteristics of CoPS3 to its morphology-regulating capability. Unlike conventional molecular solid additives that typically regulate morphology via solubility, miscibility, drying kinetics, nucleation, or crystallization modulation, CoPS3 guides fibrillar assembly without substantially enhancing the intrinsic crystalline ordering. This kinetically templated pathway enables the emergence of high-purity, narrow, long-range, and highly aligned fibrils, providing superior percolation pathways for exciton dissociation and charge transport, setting the structural foundation for the enhanced device performance.

Photovoltaic performance

Photovoltaic performance was evaluated using device structure of ITO/2PACz/active layer/PNDIT-F3N/Ag (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 19). CoPS3 nanocrystals were incorporated into the active-layer precursor through a direct solution-blending strategy, in which a chloroform dispersion of CoPS3 was added to the D:A solution at defined loadings relative to the total mass of donor and acceptor, while all other processing conditions were kept identical to those of the control devices. The loading-dependent device optimization from 0 to 3 wt% is summarized in Supplementary Table 2, identifying 0.5 wt% CoPS3 as the optimal concentration. Current density versus voltage (J–V) curves and device parameters are summarized in Fig. 4b, c and Table 1. Compared with the pristine devices, the devices incorporating 0.5 wt% CoPS3 exhibit a simultaneous enhancement in short-circuit current density (JSC) and fill factor (FF), while maintaining a comparable open-circuit voltage (VOC), resulting in a high PCE of 21.0% (VOC = 0.935 V, JSC = 27.8 mA cm−2, FF = 80.7 %). Notably, CoPS3 nanocrystals were incorporated by simple low-loading solution blending, without introducing additional device-fabrication steps. The performance enhancement was reproducible across different device batches (Supplementary Fig. 20), supporting the practical reliability of this additive strategy and its potential compatibility with scalable OSC processing. The device achieved a certified PCE of 20.53% (Supplementary Fig. 21), placing it among the highest reported for single-junction OSCs (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 3). External quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra confirm improved photocurrent generation across the 500–830 nm range (Fig. 4e). In addition, dark J–V curves show nearly identical reverse-bias current densities for the control and CoPS3-containing devices, indicating that CoPS3 incorporation does not induce additional leakage current or shunt pathways (Supplementary Fig. 22). Storage stability testing shows that CoPS3-based device exhibits slower degradation under N2 storage conditions, retaining 94.7% of its initial PCE after 1000 h, outperforming the control device (87.5%, Fig. 4f). This balanced improvement across device metrics highlights the kinetically templated fibrillar morphology, which enables concurrent optimization of charge generation and transport without relying on increased crystallinity.

Device physics and photophysical processes

Photocurrent density versus effective voltage (Jph–Veff) analysis reveals slightly enhanced exciton dissociation and charge collection in CoPS3-based devices (Fig. 5a)31,32. The calculated maximum exciton generation rate (Gmax) increases from 1.62 × 1028 to 1.69 × 1028 m−3 s−1, while the exciton dissociation probability (Pdiss) improves from 97.5% to 98.4% upon CoPS3 incorporation (Supplementary Table 4). These enhancements are consistent with the refined, long-range fibrillar morphology, which provides more accessible and continuous D/A interfaces for exciton harvesting33.

Charge transport properties were subsequently examined using space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 23)34. Upon incorporation of 0.5 wt% CoPS3, both hole (μh) and electron (μe) mobilities are substantially enhanced. Relative to the control blend, μh increases from 0.58 × 10−3 to 0.94 × 10−3 cm2 V−1 s−1, while μe exhibits a more pronounced increase from 0.43 × 10−3 to 0.90 × 10−3 cm2 V−1 s−1 (Supplementary Table 5), resulting in a more balanced μh/μe ratio of 1.04. Such balanced transport is consistent with the refined and continuous fibrillar morphology, which facilitates carrier percolation and contributes to the enhanced JSC and FF. By contrast, excessive CoPS3 loading of ≥1 wt% gradually reduces both mobilities and weakens the transport balance (Supplementary Fig. 24), suggesting disruption of the optimized fibrillar transport pathways. Photo-CELIV measurements independently confirm this mobility enhancement (Supplementary Fig. 25), indicating accelerated and more balanced charge transport enabled by the CoPS3-templated fibrillar network. In parallel, capacitance–voltage (C−2–V) analysis reveals an elevated built-in potential (Supplementary Fig. 26), suggesting a strengthened internal electric field that facilitates charge extraction and suppresses space-charge accumulation under operating conditions35. Frequency-dependent capacitance measurements further show that the CoPS3-containing device exhibits a slightly increased dielectric constant compared with the control device, increasing from 2.98 to 3.24 in the representative frequency region (Supplementary Fig. 27). This increase suggests an enhanced effective dielectric response of the blend film, likely associated with the intrinsic dielectric response of CoPS3 and interfacial polarization. Consistently, DFT/TD-DFT calculations indicate that adjacent CoPS3 lowers the exciton binding energy of nearby L8-BO from 0.74 to 0.25 eV and that of D18 from 0.66 to 0.35 eV (Supplementary Fig. 28). These results suggest that local screening/polarization effects associated with CoPS3 weaken electron-hole Coulomb attraction, thereby facilitating exciton dissociation and contributing to reduced recombination losses.

Recombination processes were further analyzed through light-intensity (Plight)–dependent J–V measurements. The ideality factor extracted from the VOC–Plight relationship decreases from 1.22 in the control device to 1.07 in the CoPS3-based device (Fig. 5c), indicating suppressed trap-assisted recombination36,37. Meanwhile, the Plight dependence of JSC yields an exponential factor approaching unity for the CoPS3 device (Supplementary Fig. 29), consistent with reduced bimolecular recombination38. Transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements reveal prolonged carrier lifetimes and a slightly reduced nongeminate recombination rate coefficient k(n) upon CoPS3 incorporation (Supplementary Fig. 30)39,40. Correspondingly, the recombination order decreases from 2.85 to 2.60, moving closer to the ideal bimolecular recombination regime. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the CoPS3-induced fibrillar network not only enhances carrier generation and transport but also effectively suppresses both trap-assisted and bimolecular recombination, thereby enabling the substantial improvements in device performance41.

To directly probe exciton transport and dissociation dynamics, femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy was employed to quantify the exciton diffusion length (LD), defined as LD = (Dτ)1/2, where D is the diffusion coefficient, and τ is the exciton lifetime42. Fluence-dependent decay analyses of neat D18, D18:CoPS3, L8-BO, and L8-BO:CoPS3 thin films reveal pronounced excitation-intensity dependence of exciton dynamics (Supplementary Figs. 31–32). The extracted LD values increase significantly upon CoPS3 incorporation, from 22 nm to 34 nm in D18 and from 16 nm to 30 nm in L8-BO (Fig. 5d, e), indicating markedly enhanced exciton diffusion capability that well matches with the fibrillar width seen in AFM-IR.

Consistent with accelerated exciton transport, triplet exciton dynamics probed at 1450 nm exhibit faster triplet formation in D18:L8-BO:CoPS3 films (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Figs. 33 and 34), suggesting more rapid exciton arrival and quenching at D/A interfaces43,44. To further resolve the interfacial hole-transfer process, selective 800 nm excitation of L8-BO was performed. Two-dimensional fs-TA maps and corresponding spectral slices reveal strongly correlated ground-state bleaching (GSB) dynamics between the two materials: the decay of the L8-BO GSB at 820 nm coincides with the rise of the D18 GSB at 591 nm, directly evidencing hole transfer from L8-BO to D18 (Fig. 5g, h and Supplementary Fig. 35). Biexponential fitting of the GSB kinetics separates ultrafast interfacial exciton dissociation (τ1) from the slower exciton diffusion to donor–acceptor interfaces (τ2)45 Upon CoPS3 incorporation, both processes are accelerated, with τ1 decreasing from 0.44 ps to 0.32 ps and τ2 from 3.80 ps to 2.31 ps at 591 nm (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Table 6). These results indicate that the CoPS3-induced refined fibrillar morphology is associated with faster exciton diffusion and dissociation, which in turn improves device JSC and FF.

Generality of kinetically templated fibrillar assembly in OSCs

To evaluate the generality of 2D nanocrystals as kinetic morphology regulators, CoPS3 was further incorporated into two additional D/A combinations: PTzBI-dF:L8-BO and PM6:Y6 (Fig. 6a). Consistent with the D18:L8-BO blend, in situ UV-vis-NIR absorption measurements reveal that CoPS3 delays the onset of molecular densification and extends the assembly window in both PTzBI-dF:L8-BO and PM6:Y6 systems (Supplementary Fig. 36), indicating a delayed drying-induced molecular densification and suppressed premature kinetic arrest. The accompanying acceptor absorption red shift suggests more developed local acceptor association during the prolonged assembly process. This shared kinetic modulation is directly reflected in the final blend morphology: despite the disparate chemical compositions of these D/A pairs, AFM-IR mapping reveals the emergence of similarly refined, long-range, and compositionally pure fibrillar networks upon CoPS3 incorporation in both systems (Fig. 6b–e). These results indicate that CoPS3-assisted kinetic templating is not limited to a specific D/A combination, but can regulate morphology across the multiple blend systems.

Consistent with the morphological evolution, devices based on both systems exhibit reproducible and concurrent improvements in photovoltaic performance (Fig. 6f, g and Supplementary Table 7). In PTzBI-dF:L8-BO and PM6:Y6 devices, incorporation of CoPS3 increases the PCE from 17.3% and 17.2% to 19.0% and 19.4%, respectively, driven by simultaneous enhancements in JSC and FF without compromising VOC. The corresponding EQE spectra confirm enhanced photoresponse across the characteristic absorption bands of both PTzBI-dF:L8-BO and PM6:Y6 systems (Supplementary Fig. 37). To further evaluate the generality of this strategy, two additional blend systems, PM6:L8-BO and D18:eC9, were examined. Upon CoPS3 incorporation, the average PCEs of PM6:L8-BO and D18:eC9 devices increase from 18.6% and 17.9% to 19.8% and 19.3%, respectively, with corresponding maximum PCEs of 20.2% and 19.6% (Supplementary Fig. 38 and Supplementary Table 8). In both cases, the performance enhancement mainly arises from concurrent increases in JSC and FF, while VOC remains nearly unchanged, further supporting morphology-mediated improvements in charge generation and transport. These results demonstrate the broad applicability of CoPS3 as a kinetic morphology regulator across different D:A combinations, although its effectiveness is expected to depend on the specific intermolecular interactions, miscibility, and film-formation kinetics of a given blend system. Beyond efficiency enhancement, CoPS3 incorporation also leads to markedly improved storage stability (Supplementary Fig. 39). Under accelerated aging conditions, both PTzBI-dF:L8-BO:CoPS3 and PM6:Y6:CoPS3 devices retain substantially higher fractions of their initial PCEs compared with the corresponding control devices, highlighting the robustness of the kinetically templated fibrillar morphology.

To further assess whether the observed performance improvements share a common transport origin, charge carrier mobilities were evaluated using SCLC measurements (Supplementary Figs. 40–41 and Supplementary Tables 9–10). Across all tested systems, including neat donors, neat acceptors, and their blends, CoPS3 consistently enhances both hole and electron mobilities. In blend films, this mobility enhancement manifests as more balanced and accelerated charge transport, consistent with the formation of continuous, long-range fibrillar pathways.

Discussion

We demonstrate that 2D CoPS3 nanocrystals function as electronically interactive kinetic templates that modulate molecular densification during film formation, thereby reprogramming bulk-heterojunction self-assembly into refined, directional, and long-range fibrillar networks. This kinetically optimized mesoscale morphology is associated with enhanced exciton diffusion and dissociation, faster and more balanced charge transport, and suppressed recombination, enabling a PCE of 21.0% in D18:L8-BO devices. This morphology-directing mechanism is not limited to a specific material system but extends robustly to additional OSC platforms, delivering consistent improvements in efficiency and storage stability. Beyond establishing CoPS3 nanocrystals as a versatile additive for high-performance OSCs, this work highlights a broader strategy for controlling organic semiconductor morphology through kinetic pathway engineering, pointing to new opportunities for leveraging 2D materials as multifunctional regulators in organic and hybrid optoelectronic technologies.

Methods

General materials

D18, L8-BO, and PNDIT-F3N were purchased from Dongguan Volt-Amp Optoelectronics Tech. Co., Ltd. Chloroform and 2PACz were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received.

Synthesis and liquid-phase exfoliation of CoPS3 nanocrystal

High-quality CoPS3 nanocrystals were synthesized from bulk single crystals prepared via an optimized chemical vapor transport (CVT) method. Stoichiometric amounts of high-purity cobalt (99.99%), red phosphorus (99.999%), and sulfur (99.998%) were weighed according to a molar ratio of Co:P:S=1:1:3, and the mixture was sealed in a double-walled quartz ampoule under high vacuum (10−5 Torr). The sealed ampoule was placed in a three-zone tube furnace and subjected to a controlled thermal program, in which the source zone was ramped to 750 °C at 2 °C min−1 while the growth zone was maintained at 700 °C, establishing a 50 °C temperature gradient to facilitate crystal growth. The reaction was sustained for 144 h to ensure complete conversion and well-developed crystallization, followed by cooling to room temperature at 0.5 °C min−1 to minimize thermal stress and preserve crystal quality.

For liquid-phase exfoliation, the bulk CoPS3 crystals were first mechanically ground into a fine powder using an agate mortar to increase the effective surface area for solvent interaction. A total of 50 mg of powder was dispersed in 50 mL of anhydrous isopropyl alcohol (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), which had been degassed with argon for 30 min. The suspension was then subjected to probe sonication (Q700, Qsonica) using a 3 mm titanium probe at 60% amplitude (420 W), employing a pulse mode of 5 s on and 10 s off to suppress overheating. Sonication was performed for 8 h under continuous argon protection, with the temperature maintained at ~15 °C using an external cooling bath. The resulting dark green dispersion was sequentially centrifuged at 1006.2 × g for 10 min to remove unexfoliated bulk residues, followed by 2683.2 × g for 30 min to isolate monolayer and few-layer CoPS3 nanosheets.

General characterizations

UV-vis-NIR absorption and PL spectra were recorded using an Absorption-Photoluminescence Spectral Analysis System (APLS-AS II). Molecular simulations were carried out using Gaussian 16. Molecular geometries were optimized at the B3LYP-D3/6-31G** and PM6-D3 levels, followed by binding energy (Ea) calculations at the B3LYP-D3/TZVP level. Wavefunction analysis and visualization were performed using Multiwfn46 and VMD47. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out using Forcite with optimized structures of D18 tetramer, L8-BO molecule, and CoPS3 nanocrystal. The simulation procedure comprised the following steps. First, an amorphous cell containing D18 tetramers and L8-BO molecules at a weight ratio of approximately 1:1.2, with or without CoPS3 nanocrystals, was constructed with an initial density of ~0.1 g cm−3. Second, a thermal annealing protocol was performed under the NPT ensemble, in which the temperature was gradually increased from 300 K to 1000 K in seven steps, with each step lasting 140 ps, and then symmetrically cooled back to 300 K. This annealing cycle was repeated five times to sufficiently randomize the chain conformations. Finally, equilibration was carried out under the NVT ensemble at 300 K for 1000 ps to obtain a stabilized structural snapshot for subsequent analysis.

In situ absorption during film drying was monitored using a Maya2000 Pro spectrometer (Ocean Insight) under xenon lamp illumination during spin coating. fs-TA measurements were performed using a Yb:KGW laser with a fundamental wavelength of ~1030 nm. Pump pulses at 800 nm were generated by a second-harmonic noncollinear optical parametric amplifier. The probe beam was a supercontinuum produced by focusing a small portion of the 1030 nm beam onto a sapphire plate for visible detection or a YAG plate for NIR detection. AFM images were obtained by a Bruker Multimode 8 microscope in tapping mode. HR-TEM was performed on a JEOL JEM-F200 microscope. GIWAXS was performed on a Xenocs Xeuss 2.0 system equipped with an Excillum MetalJet-D2 X-ray source (70.0 kV, 2.8570 mA; λ = 1.341 Å). The incident angle was fixed at 0.20°, with a sample-to-detector distance of ~210 mm. Scattering patterns were collected using a DECTRIS PILATUS3 R 1 M detector. RSoXS was conducted at beamline 11.0.1.2 of the Advanced Light Source (ALS). Thin films were spin-coated onto PEDOT:PSS-coated silicon substrates, floated off by dissolving the PEDOT:PSS layer in deionized water, and transferred onto PDMS membranes with a 2.5 mm aperture for transmission-mode measurements. Scattering images were acquired using a Princeton Instruments PI-MTE CCD detector (pixel size: 0.027 × 0.027 mm). Infrared spectroscopy and AFM-IR imaging were performed using an Anasys nanoIR3 system (Bruker). AFM-IR images were analyzed using GTFiber48, with orientation correlation functions extracted via custom MATLAB routines.

Fabrication and characterization of OSCs

Pre-patterned indium tin oxide (ITO) substrates were sequentially cleaned by detergent washing, followed by ultrasonication in deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol for 15 min each, and then dried overnight in an oven. The substrates were treated with oxygen plasma for 4 min prior to spin-coating a (2-(9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl)phosphonic acid (2PACz) ethanol solution at 3000 rpm for 20 s, followed by thermal annealing at 100 °C for 10 min.

The active layers were deposited from chloroform solutions of D18:L8-BO, PTzBI-dF:L8-BO, and PM6:Y6. For D18:L8-BO, the mass ratio was 1:1.3 with a total concentration of 12 mg mL⁻¹, and the solution was spin-coated at 6500 rpm for 30 s. For PTzBI-dF:L8-BO, the mass ratio was 1:1.3 with a total concentration of 13.5 mg mL⁻¹, and the solution was spin-coated at 6700 rpm for 30 s. For PM6:Y6, the mass ratio was 1:1.5 with a total concentration of 16 mg mL⁻¹, and the solution was spin-coated at 3500 rpm for 30 s.

For CoPS3-containing blends, the exfoliated CoPS3 nanocrystals were transferred in chloroform to prepare a stock dispersion with a concentration of 1.0 mg mL−1. Before use, the CoPS3 dispersion was sonicated for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion. A calculated volume of the CoPS3 dispersion was then added to the corresponding D:A chloroform solution to obtain the desired CoPS3 loading, which is defined as the weight percentage relative to the total mass of donor and acceptor. Unless otherwise specified, the optimized CoPS3 loading was 0.5 wt%. The mixed precursor solution was stirred at 80 °C for 2 h before spin coating. All other fabrication parameters, including D/A ratio, total concentration, spin-coating speed, and annealing temperature, were kept identical to those of the corresponding control devices.

After spin coating, the active layers were thermal annealed at 100 °C for 5 min. A PNDIT-F3N methanol solution (1 mg mL−1) was subsequently spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 20 s to form the cathode interlayer. Finally, a 100 nm Ag electrode was thermally evaporated under high vacuum (<1 × 10−4 Pa).

J–V characteristics were measured under simulated AM 1.5 G illumination using a calibrated solar simulator (Sirius-SS150A, 150 W, AAA grade, Zolix Instruments) connected to a Keithley 2450 SourceMeter. EQE spectra were acquired using a customized EQE system (Zolix Instruments) calibrated with a certified silicon reference cell. Photo-CELIV measurements were performed using a PAIOS system (FLUXiM, Switzerland). The relationship between VOC and Plight follows VOC∝nkT/q in Plight. The slope under open-circuit conditions reflects the dominant recombination mechanism: a slope near kT/q indicates bimolecular recombination, while a slope near 2 kT/q indicates trap-assisted recombination. The relationship between JSC and Plight follows JSC ∝ (Plight)S, where S values closer to 1 indicate less bimolecular recombination.

Fabrication and characterization of charge-only devices

Hole and electron mobilities were determined using hole-only (ITO/2PACz/active layer/Ag) and electron-only (ITO/ZnO/active layer/PNDIT-F3N/Ag) device configurations, respectively. J–V characteristics were measured with a Keithley 2450 source measurement unit. The carrier mobilities were extracted by fitting the high-field regime of the J–V curves to the trap-free SCLC model described by the Mott-Gurney law: J=98ε0εrμV2d3, where J is the current density, μ is the zero-field mobility, ε0 is the vacuum permittivity, εr is the relative dielectric constant of the active layer, and d is the film thickness. The voltage V is defined as the applied voltage minus the built-in potential (V = Vappl−Vbi).
