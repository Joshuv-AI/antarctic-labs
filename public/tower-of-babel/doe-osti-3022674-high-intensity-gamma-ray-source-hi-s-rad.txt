1 Introduction
High Intensity Gamma-ray Source (HIGS) is a Free-Electron Laser (FEL) based Compton back-
scattering γ-ray source. The HIGS facility is located on the campus of Duke University and operated
by Triangle Universities Nuclear Laboratory (TUNL). Currently, HIGS can produce mono-energetic
γ-rays between 2 and 100 MeV with linear and circular polarizations [4]. Experiments were per-
formed using HIGS by Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL). The purpose of our experiment
was to characterize the response of Carestream General Purpose Image Plates as a function of γ-ray
energy. The experiments were done for an energy range of 3 to 13 MeV. The image plates were
configured with and without a tantalum screen. The relative gain of the image plates with the
tantalum screen as a function of energy is determined. Analysis of the image plate data with and
without the screen showed a difference in the penumbral image generated by the collimation of the
γ-ray source. This data is used to determine the size of the tantalum cascade on the film as a
function of γ-ray energy. The result is compared with GEANT4 Monte Carlo code using the Tool
for Particle Simulations (TOPAS).

2 Experimental Arrangement and Parameters
The experimental arrangement of the radiographic experiment at HIGS was very simple. A 19.05
mm-diameter lead collimator upstream of the image plate holder defined the gamma flux (Figure
2-1). The image plate vinyl holder was located 4690 mm from the output of the collimator and
mounted in a fixed frame. The vinyl holder contained a single 5" x 7" Carestream General Purpose
(GP) image plate or and 0.492mm-thick tantalum sheet with an image plate in contact with the
tantalum.
 HIGS measures the relative beam flux using a scintillator detector which views the FEL mirror.
This detector is calibrated in situ relative to a 100% efficiency NaI detector placed into an attenuated
beam. The attenuation coefficients are also measured in situ in order to extrapolate the unattenuated
flux and calibrate the scintillator detector to measure absolute flux. For spectral measurements of
the beam, HIGS employs a High-Purity Germanium (HPGe) detector located downstream of the
experimental set-up to measure the beam energy profile. Exposures to the gamma flux were timed
by a clock to obtain the total flux. Together these techniques allow for flux measurements on a given
run to be accurate to 5%. Typical exposure times were 10 to 15 minutes. A plot of the observed
gamma flux and flux rate as a function of energy is shown in Figure 2-2. The change in the curve
at 6 MeV corresponds to a change in laser mirror at HIGS.
 A Radcal high dose ion chamber was mounted on the back of the image plate holder. The active
area of the ion chamber was specified to be 9 mm x 20 mm. Unfortunately, the vertical dimension of
the gamma flux was almost exactly the same size as the active area of the ion chamber. Alignment
was critical. Figure 2-3 is a plot of the ratio of Radcal to gamma flux as a function of gamma
energy. As shown by the data there was possibly a misalignment at around 5 MeV. There is a slight
difference in Radcal/Gamma Flux with and without the Ta screen. One interesting feature is that
response of Radcal is relatively flat from 3 to 13 MeV at approximately 2.1x108 gammas/R.

3 Experimental Data
A typical x-ray image without the tantalum screen is shown in Figure 3-1. The image plate shown
was scanned by a Carestream HPX-Pro CR image plate scanner. The scan aperture was 25 µm
and the gain was 10. The distribution of photo-stimulated luminescence (psl) for each image at
each energy was analyzed. The radius was defined by the derivative of the circular distribution. A
rectangular image was selected that was twice the circular radius. The marginal-x distribution was
obtained and then a linear fit to the background of the marginal distribution was subtracted. It
was less that 1%. The background subtracted marginal distribution was integrated to produce the
total number of counts (psl) on a single image.

 Figure 3-1: Typical image plate result at 8 MeV

error was the result of using two different image plate vinyl assemblies. Neither assembly had the
tantalum screen pressed firmly against the film as is common practice for screened radiographic

4 Penumbral Analysis
The penumbral edge of the image distribution (3-1) contains information on the interaction of
the radiation with the image plate detector. Figure 4-1 and 4-2 show the normalized radially
averaged distributions and derivatives of both without and with the tantalum screen. All energies
are overlayed. The average peak derivative for all energies is 10.44±0.01 mm and 10.43±0.01 mm
for without and with Ta screen, respectively. Given the geometry of the experiment, the average
divergence of the gamma beam calculated from the collimator is 194±20µm. Simply put, the HIGS
source shape distribution is extremely reproducible for a large range of energies and has an extremely
small divergence.

5 Summary and Conclusion
The HIGS facility produces a high quality single energy gamma-ray beam suitable for calibration
of radiographic systems and individual detectors. The flux rate (5x107 γ/s) was sufficient to collect
data sets in 10 to 15 minutes.
 The image plate signal gain was enhanced by the addition of a tantalum screen. The gain is a
strong function of energy. From 3 to 13 MeV, it increases a factor of 4. The gain, when combined
with a bremsstrahlung spectra, tends to flatten the system response at high energy. When combined
with a filtered spectra, it tends to shift the peak sensitivity of the system to about 4 MeV.
 The quality of the source allowed the extraction of meaningful penumbral data related to the
size of image plate tantalum cascade distributions for various configurations and energies. Since the
data is taken at single energy, code calculations of the experiment were much easier to set-up and
run. The simulation fit to the data required restricting the energy loss in the film to electrons and
positrons only.

Appendix A: TOPAS Simulations and FWHM Calculations

A.1 TOPAS Geometry
The Tool for Particle Simulations (TOPAS) is primarily utilized in the field of medical physics for
Monte Carlo simulations of particles through certain materials and geometries [2]. For this TOPAS
simulation a simple geometry was implemented. The environment where the simulation took place
was a 1m3 cube with a centered cylinder representing the Ta foil with a radius of 0.25 cm and
thickness of 0.0492 cm. The face of the foil is normal to the z-axis of the cube and is at z=0, see
Figure A-2. Figure A-1 shows the environment as a purple cube with the white disc in the center
as the Ta foil, for visual purposes the image of the environment has dimensions of 5 cm3 .

A.2 TOPAS Source
The photon source has a 10 micron diameter beam and simulations were run with energies ranging
from 1 to 20 MeV. The photon beam created contained 106 photons. The source beam in the
simulation was emitted from the +z-axis of the environment cube and is sent to the −z-axis direction
through the Ta foil.

A.3 TOPAS Scoring
To find the FWHM at each energy, the TOPAS phase space score was implemented on the -z face of
the foil. This score calculates and records the type of particle, the x,y coordinates, and the energy of
the particles. To use the Phase Space score, TOPAS requires the surface of interest to be specified.
The score includes the minimum and maximum energies of photons, positrons, and electrons, and
many additional parameters. The Phase Space scorer produces two data files, one containing all
the data from the run and another containing headers for each column in the file. By using Python
coding methods, these data files are able to be unpacked and analyzed further [3].

A.4 FWHM Calculations
With the x,y coordinates from the particles at the -z face of the foil, calculating the FWHM becomes
possible. Using python’s jupyter notebook, a plot was created to display the position of each particle
with a colored gradient to indicate the energy, seen in Figure A-5. From here, the normal distribution
of the particle distance from the center was p  calculated, see Fig A-6. This resulting plot was then
used to find the FWHM with the formula 2σ 2 ∗ ln(2) where σ represents the standard deviation.
