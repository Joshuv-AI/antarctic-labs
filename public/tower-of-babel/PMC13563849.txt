MRSA transmission in hospitals across Alberta, Canada: a comparative study combining unidentified colonized cases upon admission
Authors: Ruoyu Li, Rob Deardon, Na Li, John Conly, Jenine Leal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a prevalent bacterium in medical settings. It can result in bloodstream infections and other severe illnesses, and patients may have a high risk of dying from these conditions. This study aimed to examine MRSA transmission in hospitals across Alberta and how undetected colonized patients on admission affect MRSA spread.MethodsWe used a Bayesian compartmental model to simulate MRSA spread across 106 acute care hospitals in Alberta. The model assumes that MRSA spreads through interactions between susceptible individuals and MRSA patients, including both hospital-acquired (HA) and community-acquired (CA) MRSA cases. Data from Edmonton, Calgary, rural Alberta and all regions in Alberta were used to fit the model. We also assumed that a small percentage of patients were colonized upon admission but were not promptly identified in hospitals, and we tested the model assumptions. Transmission rates were estimated using a Bayesian framework with Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms. We compared transmission rates across several regions in Alberta and assessed the effect of MRSA prevalence at hospital admission on MRSA transmission.ResultsWhen the model fit the data in hospitals in all areas of Alberta, the average number of susceptible people colonized due to patients with HA-MRSA colonization, patients with HA-MRSA infections, patients with CA-MRSA colonization, and patients with CA-MRSA infections per contact per month are around 0.03 (95% CI: (0.001, 0.101)), 0.08 (95% CI: (0.002, 0.278)), 0.01 (95% CI: (0.000, 0.033)) and 0.05 (95% CI: (0.001, 0.182)), respectively. Assuming 0.8 colonized cases per 1000 admissions, the average number of susceptible people who become colonized due to patients with HA-MRSA infections per contact per month in Edmonton, Calgary and rural hospitals are 0.05 (95% CI: (0.001, 0.174)), 0.43 (95% CI: (0.018, 1.152)) and 0.22 (95% CI: (0.007, 0.722)), respectively. Assuming more unidentified colonized cases in hospitals, transmission rates between susceptible people and infected patients increase marginally.ConclusionMRSA transmission rates appear to be marginally higher in Calgary hospitals compared to Edmonton, and rural hospitals seem to have lower transmission rates than in Calgary but higher than in Edmonton, with 95% credible intervals (CIs) overlapping. Our model also suggests that infected patients have a higher probability of transmitting MRSA to susceptible people in most Alberta hospitals.Keywords: MRSA transmission, Hospital admission, Colonization and infections, Compartmental models, Bayesian framework

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a widely distributed Gram-positive bacterium in human populations. Roughly 30% of individuals carry it constantly [1], while 60% of individuals carry it sporadically [2]. Many infections, including those in the bloodstream and lungs, are secondary to S. aureus. However, the overuse of methicillin-like beta-lactam antimicrobials has led to the emergence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). MRSA has caused significant morbidity and mortality in recent years. For example, MRSA was responsible for around 130,000 deaths worldwide in 2021 [3]. In 2022, MRSA accounted for around 21% of antimicrobial-resistant bloodstream infections in Canada [4]. Between 2018 and 2022, the death rate in Canada for MRSA bloodstream infections ranged from 16.3% to 19.8% [5].

People in acute care facilities are susceptible to MRSA colonization through direct skin contact with colonized or infected patients, particularly those with open wounds or compromised immune systems. Colonization may also occur via contact with contaminated healthcare workers (HCWs) or medical equipment [6]. Furthermore, a small proportion of people are already colonized or infected at the time of admission, with reported prevalence ranging from 0.25 to 2 cases per 1000 admissions [7]. Meanwhile, colonized patients in acute care hospitals are susceptible to infection [8]. Infections are more common in patients who have comorbidities and chronic disorders. Invasive treatments like indwelling urinary catheters can also be associated with infections since MRSA will quickly colonize and adhere to a catheter surface once it is implanted. Therefore, MRSA is considered a serious nosocomial bacterium, and extended hospitalization increases the risk of exposure to MRSA [9]. Hospital-acquired MRSA (HA-MRSA) cases refer to patients who become colonized or infected with MRSA while in the hospital.

MRSA has also grown increasingly prevalent in people who are not hospitalized. These individuals typically have not been admitted to any hospitals in the last year, so they are characterized as cases of community-acquired MRSA (CA-MRSA) colonization or infections. Hospitalization may be necessary for patients with CA-MRSA found in the community, resulting in potential transmission to more hospitalized patients [10].

Deterministic or stochastic compartmental models have often been used to examine the dynamics of MRSA spread in hospitals. Both kinds of mathematical models divide populations into distinct compartments, and each compartment symbolizes a particular group of people. They could be colonized or infected patients or others, such as healthcare professionals, and people in each compartment are usually assumed to be homogeneous [11]. The order of compartments demonstrates how MRSA transmits among distinct groups of people [12]. The deterministic approach typically uses ordinary differential equations to simulate an outbreak. In contrast, stochastic models take into account the uncertainties in an epidemic by using probability distributions. The uncertainties could be attributed to environmental or hereditary factors. MRSA latent durations or infectious periods are also frequently defined using stochastic models [11].

We can employ compartmental models to study the spread of MRSA, based on patient data, to better understand and predict transmission dynamics. The data typically record the number of positive cases in a certain time period in hospitals, but information regarding some characteristics that could affect transmission may not be recorded completely. Such latent information could include colonized patients upon admission, the rate of contaminated healthcare professionals among all hospital staff, and those who become colonized due to MRSA in the environment such as contaminated medical equipment [13]. When the data are linked to additional sources to increase their information, it can make models more adaptable [14]. For instance, Wang and Ruan [13] utilized hospital data to study the rate of environmental contamination caused by contaminated HCWs or MRSA patients. Their study assumed that MRSA spreads through contact from colonized patients to susceptible people. HCWs may contaminate MRSA and then contribute to the transmission. MRSA in healthcare settings may also affect its spread. In their model, certain parameters were approximated using both a deterministic and a stochastic formulation, whereas the values of other factors, such as the colonization rate upon admission, were derived from earlier studies [15, 16]. Similarly, Lee et al. [12] predicted the MRSA dissemination rate using stochastic models based on healthcare surveillance data. The model included susceptible individuals, as well as identified undiagnosed MRSA patients. When the model was fitted to the data, additional information such as the rate of colonization upon admission and discharges were based on previous research [17, 18].

Here, we employ a stochastic model to illustrate how MRSA disseminates in Alberta hospitals. Data used in the study come from hospitals across all areas of Alberta, and then are stratified into three additional groups: hospitals in Edmonton, Calgary, and rural areas including central, south and north areas of Alberta. To compare MRSA transmission across regions, we analyze each dataset separately and fit the model to each in a Bayesian framework. In some months, the number of MRSA colonizations in Calgary and rural Alberta hospitals is lower than the number of new infected individuals. The reason could be that certain individuals admitted to hospitals have been colonized [15], but these colonized patients are not included in our model or represented in the data. Therefore, our analyses assume a proportion of unidentified (or hidden) colonized patients upon admission to the model. We also consider a sensitivity analysis in which this proportion of undiagnosed colonizations is varied. By employing a model that attempts to capture the principles of transmission dynamics and making assumptions about unidentified colonized patients upon admission, we can compare MRSA transmission in different regions in Alberta.

Section 2 introduces the data used on MRSA in Alberta hospitals. The four datasets include data from hospitals in all areas in Alberta combined, then Edmonton, Calgary, and rural locations separately. Section 3 explains the peculiarities of MRSA transmission. We present the parameter set and likelihood function of the model. We also describe the Bayesian MCMC framework and demonstrate the assumptions of unidentified colonized patients upon admission. The results of the analysis in different regions of Alberta are shown in Sect. 4, along with the shift in transmission rates as the prevalence rate of colonized cases upon admission rises. Section 5 is the discussion of the study.

Data

Data consisting of the number of monthly MRSA colonized and infected cases in Alberta acute care hospitals are utilized in this study. The dataset originates from a provincial surveillance data management system used by the Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) program of Alberta Health Services (AHS) and Covenant Health [19]. At each hospital under the management of AHS and Covenant Health, the IPC surveillance program conducts surveillance for antimicrobial-resistant organisms. More than 4.5 million individuals in Alberta get acute care services from 106 acute care hospitals [20].

We concentrate on patients admitted to acute care facilities in Alberta from September 2018 to September 2023. Incident cases of MRSA, those identified with MRSA for the first time in hospitals, are included and classified as either colonization or infection. Cases of MRSA are divided into three classes based on their previous hospitalization and the time of identification during the hospitalization. The three classes are hospital-acquired (HA), healthcare-associated (HCA), and community-acquired (CA). Patients with HA-MRSA have MRSA identified at least three days after being admitted. Patients are considered to have HCA-MRSA or CA-MRSA if they were diagnosed with MRSA within two days of their admission. CA-MRSA patients are those who did not have any hospitalizations in the previous year, while HCA-MRSA patients did [19].

Bayesian inference and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods

To estimate model parameters in the study, we use a Bayesian framework and Metropolis-Hastings Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods [26]. The prior density P(Θ) represents our beliefs regarding the parameters before seeing the data D. After taking into account the observed data, our updated belief regarding the parameters is represented by the posterior density P(Θ|D). The posterior is proportional to the likelihood function multiplied by the prior [27], i.e., . Here, we consider weakly informative priors, which increase the dependence of the posterior on the observed data. To determine how sensitive the posterior mean estimate is to prior assumptions, we consider three independent gamma priors (, , and ) for each of our rate parameters (Table 2).

A package nimble [28–30] in R [31], v4.4.1] is used to draw samples from the posterior using MCMC. The chains are run for 60,000 iterations with an initial burn-in of 10,000 iterations removed. Convergence is assessed visually using trace plots.

Additional MRSA-colonized cases upon admission

Initially, the model is fitted to data from hospitals in all areas of Alberta, and the posterior mean of transmission parameters is estimated. The model is then fitted with data from hospitals in different areas of Alberta. These include hospitals in Edmonton, Calgary, and rural Alberta.

In the model framework, new cases of HA-MRSA infections are assumed to arise from HA-MRSA-colonized patients because some HA-MRSA-colonized patients may develop infections in hospitals, but data from Calgary and rural Alberta hospitals show that the amount of HA-MRSA-colonized patients is slightly lower than the number of new HA-MRSA-infected patients over a few months (1–4 cases lower). This could be because some patients admitted to hospitals may have been colonized with MRSA prior to admission [15], which are omitted from our model and data. We then hypothesize that a certain proportion of people admitted to hospitals are colonized with MRSA [12, 13], but these patients are not recognized on the first or second day of admission due to a suboptimal screening rate (66%-86%) [32], so these colonized patients are assumed to be new cases of HA-MRSA colonization as they are identified at least three days after admission [19].

Let λ be the prevalence of individuals on admission who are colonized with MRSA, and this prevalence is assumed to range from 0.0008 to 0.0012, i.e., 0.8 to 1.2 MRSA-colonized cases per 1000 admissions to hospitals [7]. Introducing unidentified colonized cases upon admission may marginally lower the number of admitted susceptible persons while slightly increasing the number of HA-MRSA-colonized patients in hospitals:

To assess the impact of assumed additional MRSA-colonized cases on the transmission rate in hospitals, we consider several values of λ with a 0.0001 increase in model fitting to data from hospitals in Calgary and rural Alberta, i.e., , 0.0009, 0.0010, 0.0011, 0.0012. When fitting our model to datasets from hospitals in all areas of Alberta, we use λ = 0, 0.0008, 0.0009, 0.0010, 0.0011, 0.0012, because the model can be fitted without making assumptions about hidden colonizations. When fitting the model to data from Edmonton hospitals, we employ λ = 0, 0.0008, 0.0009, 0.0010, 0.0011, because the assumption λ = 0.0012 is not appropriate for data from Edmonton hospitals and the number of new HA-MRSA colonizations will exceed the number of susceptible individuals near the end of time. This also demonstrates that the percentage of people who are colonized upon hospital admission in Alberta may be very low. Therefore, maintaining λ at a low value can not only assess how unidentified colonized patients affect MRSA transmission in hospitals, but it also has little effect on the actual transmission rate. Furthermore, the introduction of unidentified colonized patients (λ) is expected to marginally raise the transmission rate between susceptible people and unidentified cases (σ).

Discussion

MRSA is a bacterium that can quickly spread among patients in acute care hospitals. Significant mortality rates in Canadian hospitals are caused by MRSA infections [7]. In this study, we focused on a stochastic compartmental model that can be used to simulate MRSA transmission in hospitals in multiple areas of Alberta. These areas include Calgary, Edmonton and rural Alberta, and they cover the entire province of Alberta.

When the model was fitted to data from all areas of Alberta, it predicted that for each contact with HA-MRSA-infected and CA-MRSA-infected patients, an average of 0.08 and 0.05 susceptible people would become new colonized patients each month. Each contact with HA-MRSA-colonized and CA-MRSA-colonized patients results in an average of 0.03 and 0.01 susceptible individuals becoming colonized per month, respectively. This indicates that infected patients spread the bacteria to more susceptible individuals than colonized patients. Patients with HA-MRSA colonization have an infection rate of 0.42. These results are similar to those from the analysis of the Edmonton hospital data.

Then, we assumed that certain individuals were colonized with MRSA upon admission [15] but were not promptly identified by medical staff. After a few days in the hospital, they were diagnosed with new HA-MRSA colonization. In Calgary hospitals, under the posterior mean estimates, HA-MRSA-colonized patients, HA-MRSA-infected patients, CA-MRSA-colonized patients, and CA-MRSA-infected patients colonize about 0.04, 0.5, 0.04, and 0.2 susceptible persons per contact per month, respectively, assuming 0.8 colonized patients per thousand admissions. The transmission rates appear to be marginally higher in Calgary than in Edmonton, but the credible intervals are relatively wide and overlapping which creates uncertainty in the interpretation. The marginal difference may be related to variations in infrastructure, such as the availability of multi-bed rooms. Patients in single-bed rooms may have easy access to hand rub dispensers, which are often located near the patient’s bed [33], and hand hygiene is the most effective strategy for controlling MRSA transmission in hospitals [34]. Transmission rates in rural areas seem to be lower than in Calgary but higher than in Edmonton, with their credible intervals overlapping. This uncertainty could be attributed to a variety of factors, such as the rural population’s smaller and more dispersed size, the hospitals’ patient mix, divergent practices, or a failure to detect colonized patients upon admission. We also performed an exploratory analysis combining data from Calgary hospitals and rural areas, and the results are consistent with using each dataset separately. Our findings also show that as the prevalence of unidentified colonized individuals rises in hospitals across Alberta, more susceptible individuals are colonized by infected patients than by colonized patients. As might be expected, more bacteria are shed by infected patients, or infected patients stay longer in hospitals and interact with others more frequently [35].

Our study does have limitations. In the model, there is some oversimplification of the MRSA transmission process. When healthcare personnel come into contact with MRSA patients, they may become contaminated and then colonize others who are susceptible. Bacteria in the environment may potentially affect transmission [13]. Because the prevalence rate of colonized cases upon admission is unknown, the inclusion of additional unobserved colonized patients upon admission may cause the model to exaggerate or underestimate real transmission rates in hospitals. Since we only have data from Alberta, data from other Canadian provinces are not included. We examine hospital data in each region of Alberta separately. The reason for this is that patient data is consolidated in multiple regions. As a result, the analysis excludes hospital transfers across regions, such as patients being transported between cities or rural areas, or being shifted from one city to another [36]. Furthermore, more people may be hospitalized during the respiratory season. Congested corridors in hospitals can lead to rapid MRSA transmission [37], but we did not find seasonal changes in the data.

Looking ahead, we could explore MRSA transmission patterns across different provinces employing the same analytical framework as our study. Notably, Ontario and several other regions report significant hospital-acquired MRSA infection rates [7]. Such a comparative analysis would illuminate transmission trends nationwide. At the same time, we ought to take into account additional elements that might influence the spread of MRSA within hospital settings, including examining the role of healthcare personnel who may carry MRSA or investigating how the presence of environmental bacteria affects transmission rates [13]. The model framework might be expanded by including compartments representing contaminated or uncontaminated HCWs and MRSA in the environment, such as medical equipment. MRSA in the environment has the potential to colonize patients and contaminate HCWs [13]. HCWs may also act as a vector for MRSA spread from colonized to uncolonized patients, especially through their contaminated hands [38]. Moreover, MRSA can spread rapidly among children and older adults, as these populations are particularly susceptible to infection [9]. Consequently, our model can be employed to analyze data that categorizes patients into various cohorts based on differing age demographics or gender proportions, enabling researchers to compare transmission rates across these distinct patient populations. Finally, the model could evaluate the efficacy of MRSA interventions across Alberta’s regions.

Conclusion

This study employed a Bayesian compartmental model to compare MRSA spread in Alberta hospitals and evaluate the impact of undetected colonized cases upon admission. The primary findings include that transmission rates appear to be marginally higher in Calgary than in Edmonton, which could be attributed to infrastructure variations such as multi-bed rooms. Rural locations seem to have lower transmission rates than Calgary but higher than Edmonton, which could be due to many factors such as population size and patient mix. Our modelling was consistent with infected patients having a higher propensity to transmit MRSA to susceptible individuals, possibly because they excrete more bacteria. These findings could help infection control specialists implement targeted interventions for MRSA patients in various hospitals to reduce MRSA transmission.
