Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

The National Renewable Energy Lab (NREL), Frontier Energy, and the industry partners worked
together to help SAE J2601-5 develop an H35 high-flow (HF) medium-duty (MD) and heavy-
duty (HD) fueling protocol. The team upgraded NREL’s hydrogen filling simulations (H2FillS)
model to accommodate an MC Formula fueling (t-final) table generation capability by leveraging
NREL’s high-performance computing system. Based on protocol boundary conditions (e.g.,
allowable maximum flow rate, range of storage system size) set by SAE J2601-5, the team
generated the fueling tables and then validated the reliability of those tables by installing them on
NREL’s HD dispenser and ZBT’s H35HF dispenser and then performing H35HF fueling
experiments. Through the validation process, this team certified that the fueling tables generated
were reliable to install in commercial H35HF dispensers and then performed H35HF fueling of
commercial MD/HD vehicles.

Summary of Research Results:

Task 1: Define boundary conditions for H35HF fueling protocol

This task entailed closely coordinating with industry stakeholders to define all relevant boundary
conditions and assumptions for the protocol. Frontier Energy coordinated and disseminated a
survey of all relevant industry stakeholders to determine the appropriate boundary conditions and
assumptions to be utilized in the fueling protocol. On the vehicle side, this included vehicle and
compressed hydrogen storage system (CHSS) manufacturers and vehicle fleet operators. On the
station side, this included hydrogen station, dispenser, and component manufacturers. After the
survey, the results were discussed in meetings with industry stakeholders to ensure alignment
and consensus on all items.

Outcome – Successful

Table 1 shows select survey results affecting station providers. Through the survey, the team
found that station providers aimed to fill H35 MD/HD vehicles within 15 minutes, even if the
ambient temperature was high, and to set the peak mass flow rate of gaseous hydrogen at 120
g/s. In terms of the ambient temperature range where the fueling process can be performed,
station providers preferred the same range used in the SAE J2601 light-duty (LD) fueling
protocol. In terms of the precooling temperature range, the LD fueling protocol allows station
providers to set their station precooling temperatures between -20°C and -40°C; however, station
providers sought to accommodate a warmer precooling temperature of up to 20°C.

Table 2 shows select survey results affecting vehicle OEMs. Vehicle OEMs also seemed to be
hoping their vehicles could be filled in 15 minutes at least, even when the ambient temperature is
high. The answered ambient temperature range, acceptable initial hydrogen pressure in vehicle
storage tanks, and type of vehicle storage tanks are consistent with those of the SAE J2601 LD
fueling protocol. In terms of the onboard storage system size range, vehicle OEMs seem to hope
to include a size of up to 70 kg.

planning to collect additional information from station providers and vehicle OEMs, so the team
combined the information collected by SAE J2601-5 with the survey results obtained under the

Task 2: Upgrade H2FillS to generate fueling (t-final) tables

In this task, NREL enhanced the H2FillS model so that it was capable of simulating defueling
and subsequent refueling of a CHSS. In addition to integrating the defueling and subsequent
refueling feature, NREL enhanced the H2FillS model so that it could perform the fueling
simulations required to calculate the MC Formula t-final equation coefficients in a “batch
process” mode. NREL enabled this “batch process” mode for the defueling / refueling
simulations.

Outcome – Successful

 NREL developed a defueling algorithm where the decrease in the hydrogen temperature and
pressure in a CHSS could be simulated while the CHSS is discharging hydrogen (this emulates
the hydrogen consumption process of an MD/HD truck when hydrogen is supplied to its fuel cell
for a continuous long-distance drive). After developing the defueling algorithm, NREL collected

 3

a broad range of defueling experimental data and verified its reliability through comparison with
collected experimental data. Figure 1 shows an example validation result. As Fig. 1 shows, the
simulated temperature and pressure drop in the CHSS matched the corresponding data. In the
other comparisons, similar expected relationships were observed.

 NREL programmed H2FillS to perform a subsequent fueling simulation after a defueling
simulation is finished, which allowed the project team to derive the target pressure, an important
capability for the fueling protocol development as described in the SAE J2601 standard [1]. So,
in this task, NREL developed the process to derive the target pressure by combining the
defueling and subsequent fueling capabilities. Fig. 2 shows the process that NREL developed for
the target pressure derivation.

Task 3: Generate fueling (t-final) tables

NREL conducted a set of constant pressure ramp rate fueling simulations under hot-case
conditions according to Appendix H, Section H.2.1 of SAE J2601. The assumptions and
boundary conditions differed based on the deliverables from Task 1. The t-final equation
coefficients were derived in accordance with Section H.2.2 of SAE J2601.

Outcome – Successful

This task required many constant ramp rate simulations to derive the t-final values under wide
ranges of ambient temperatures, precooling temperatures, initial CHSS pressures, and CHSS
sizes. For the simulations required for this task, NREL integrated the upgraded MD/HD version
of the H2FillS model with NREL’s high-performance computing (HPC) system, composed of
the Kestrel and Eagle supercomputers, where almost an infinite number of fueling simulations
can be run in parallel [2]. NREL set the boundary conditions received through the surveys in
Task 1 and the survey results SAE J2601-5 collected to the H2FillS model and then ran it on the
HPC (Fig. 3 depicts the process to derive the t-final values on the HPC and shows parts of the
actual t-final tables). Through the simulations, NREL successfully obtained all the t-final values
with SAE J2601-5. All the fueling tables generated in this task were reflected in the SAE J2601-
5 MD/HD HF fueling protocol TIR that was published in February 2024 [3].

Task 4: Generate target pressure tables

NREL conducted a set of defueling and subsequent fueling simulations at the slowest constant
pressure ramp rate for each fuel delivery temperature category. These cold case fueling
simulations were conducted in accordance with Section H.3.2.

Outcome – Successful

 In Task 2, NREL successfully upgraded the H2FillS model to accommodate a defueling and
subsequent fueling simulation capability. In this task, NREL integrated this capability with the
HPC system and ran the combined simulations of the defueling and subsequent fueling
processes. After successfully running simulations on the HPC system, as shown in Fig. 3, NREL,
in consultation with SAE J2601-5, generated all the target pressure tables according to the
guidance in Section H.3.2 of SAE J2601 (see Figure 4).

Task 5: Implement MD/HD version of MC Formula fueling method in H2FillS

NREL programmed the H35HF version of the MC Formula fueling method into the HD-H2FillS
model so that users could understand the effectiveness and efficiency of the MC Formula-based
fueling method. After the MC Formula was implemented into the HD-H2FillS model, NREL
conducted fueling simulations to confirm validity.

Outcome – Successful

In this task, NREL successfully programmed the H2FillS model to perform the H35HF version
of the MC Formula fueling method described in SAE J2601-5. Along with the MC Formula
implementation, the project team also incorporated the t-final min coefficient developed by SAE
J2601-5 to adjust the gaseous hydrogen peak mass flow rate to the protocol’s upper limit of 120
g/s. After the implementation was complete, NREL ran the model with the NREL HD vehicle
simulator configuration of a 2,161-L storage system under the conditions shown in Table 3.
Figure 5 depicts the temperature, pressure, and mass flow profiles of hydrogen dispensed based
on using the MC Formula control logic. Figure 5(a) shows the dispenser output conditions at an
ambient temperature of 20°C and a precooling temperature of -40°C, representing fast fueling.
Figure 5(b) shows the dispenser output conditions at an ambient temperature of 20°C and a
precooling temperature of 20°C, representing slow (ambient temperature) fueling. As Fig. 5
shows, the mass flow rates always stayed below the protocol upper limit of 120 g/s, which was
also observed in the other simulations, as shown in Table 3. The simulation results prove that the
t-final min coefficient functions sufficiently regulate the peak flow rate below its upper limit.

As Fig. 6 shows, for the 2,161-L CHSS, temperatures increased but only up to 70°C, even in the
ambient temperature fueling simulation. As Table 3 shows, even at an ambient temperature of
50°C, the maximum storage system temperature did not reach 75°C; therefore, the simulation
results prove that the H35HF version of the MC Formula fueling method is sufficiently safe.

In mid-2024, NREL was implementing the H70HF version of the MC Formula fueling method in
the H2FillS model under a different CRADA project. Once the implementation is complete,
NREL will release the upgraded H2FillS model to the public (expected by Dec. 31, 2024). The
model should help users simulate the MD/HD version of MC Formula, SAE J2601-5, fueling
without conducting experiments with the exact H35HF dispensing hardware and a large onboard
storage system. This means the H2FillS model can provide modeling-based insights into how
effective and efficient the MD/HD version of the MC Formula fueling method is and how the
fueling method works for vehicles with large onboard storage systems.

Task 6: Develop protocol validation calculator

To facilitate the correct programming and validation of stations using the H35HF MC Formula
protocol, NREL developed a “Validation Calculator.” The Validation Calculator was
programmed in C++ and made available as a Windows executable file. The Validation
Calculator utilizes station input data (e.g., station pressure, mass flow, fuel delivery temperature,
etc.) and then executes the MC Formula protocol second by second. The Validation Calculator
automatically compares the station control parameters (pressure corridor, pressure targets) to the
Validation Calculator’s calculated parameters to ensure that they agree within an acceptable
margin. NREL ran the validation calculator using a strategic set of fueling data from the H2FillS
model from Task 5 to demonstrate and document that the Validation Calculator is functioning
correctly.

 12

Outcome – Successful

Now that the SAE J2601-5 H35HF fueling protocol is available to the public, H35HF fueling
stations can be built based on the protocol guidance. However, there is no tool to confirm the
protocol is accurately implemented in commercial dispensers. Therefore, NREL, in consultation
with SAE J2601-5, developed a protocol validation calculator. As Fig. 7 shows, in the calculator,
station providers need to set protocol-related parameters (e.g., Option A or B table,
communications or non-communications fill). Station providers also need to set data that were
collected during a fueling event (e.g., hose temperature and pressure and amount of hydrogen
dispensed). With the two types of user-defined input, the calculator evaluates if the hose pressure
is accurately controlled based on the protocol, and the fueling finishes at the correct fueling
termination timing. With the calculator, station providers can verify that the fueling protocol is
accurately implemented in their dispensers to safely fill commercial vehicles at their stations.

The NREL team distributed the first beta version of the Validation Calculator in July 2024 and
plans to release the final version publicly in December 2024.

Task 7: Implement H35HF fueling protocol in NREL’s HD dispenser

NREL programmed its high-flow heavy-duty dispenser with the MC Formula fueling protocol.
After programming, a shakedown experiment was conducted utilizing the Validation Calculator
from Task 6 to confirm that the programming was correct.

 13

Outcome – Successful

NREL successfully implemented the fueling protocol in the HD dispenser and can now perform
protocol fueling. The reliability of the protocol implementation was also confirmed with the
protocol Validation Calculator developed in Task 6. Fig. 8(a) shows the actual and target
dispenser profiles: one is the target dispenser pressure signal sent from the PLC program where
the protocol was implemented, and the other is the measured actual dispenser pressure. As Fig.
8(a) shows, there were disparities between the actual and target dispenser pressures. These
disparities resulted from a flow control valve that did not actuate quickly enough to support
H35HF fueling. Therefore, even if the target dispenser pressure is perfectly controlled based on
the protocol, the actual dispenser pressure could not keep up with the target. However, as Fig.
8(b) shows, NREL was able to confirm that the target dispenser pressure profile was sufficiently
accurate by analyzing the target dispenser pressure profile with the Validation Calculator,
meaning that NREL correctly implemented the fueling protocol in the HD dispenser.

Task 8: Protocol validation at NREL HD station

NREL conducted fueling experiments to determine fueling performance over a range of
conditions (e.g., ambient temperature, fuel delivery temperature, initial pressure, etc.). The
fueling performance and experimental results were documented. NREL’s HD vehicle dispenser
and HD vehicle simulator will be used for this experiment. Even though the tanks in the HD
vehicle simulator are H70, this experiment is primarily to validate the fueling protocol function
and performance. The type IV tanks in the NREL HD vehicle simulator will possibly be
constructed outside the assumptions and boundary conditions of the MC Formula fueling
protocol. But if the experiment shows that temperatures are still within bounds on these tanks,
this should demonstrate that the protocol has sufficient embedded margin, which is expected.

Outcome – Successful

Figure 9(a) shows the NREL HD station where the protocol validation experiments were
conducted, and Fig. 9(b) shows the NREL HD vehicle simulator whose storage size corresponds
to a Class 8 truck. At this HD station, NREL conducted two fueling experiments with a 1429-L
onboard storage system consisting of four 244-L type IV tanks, a 197-L type III tank, and a 256-
L type III tank. The used tanks are circled in red in Fig. 8(b). Other experimental conditions are
found in Table 3.1.

Figure 10 shows the temperature, pressure, and mass flow profiles of the dispensed hydrogen. In
both experiments, the dispenser temperatures were sufficiently cooled close to -40°C, which was
the target value. However, in the first experiment shown in Fig. 10(a), the actual dispenser
pressure (black line) was not accurately controlled to follow the ideal profile (gray line). This is
because the actuation speed of the HD station’s flow control valve is very slow to precisely
support SAE J2601-5 fueling [4]. To compensate, the project team made changes to the HD
dispenser and observed that the dispenser pressure was almost accurately controlled to ramp up
at the same rate as the target pressure profile in the second test shown in Fig. 10(b). In both
experiments, the HD vehicle simulator was filled in about 200 seconds or less. The fueling
protocol is designed to ensure that fueling time never falls below 200 seconds to prevent severe
hot spots from being generated. Accordingly, the project team conducted the protocol validation
experiments at fueling speeds corresponding to the top speed that the protocol tolerates. Also, the
fueling protocol sets 120 g/s as the upper limit of the mass flow; however, the mass flow rates in
the experiments significantly exceeded the protocol upper limit. In other words, the experiments
at NREL were conducted under conditions beyond the protocol boundaries to evaluate the
protocol’s reliability on the safe side. As mentioned above, the fueling protocol is designed not
to fall below a fueling time of 200 seconds or exceed the mass flow rate above 120 g/s with the t-
final min equation that is a function of the CHSS volume. However, in these experiments, NREL
did not implement the t-final min equation because the equation could potentially fall below the
pressure ramp rate of 7.6 MPa/min. When pressure ramp rates are low, severe thermal
stratification is sometimes observed in vehicle tanks due to incomplete mixing. To prevent
thermal stratification from occurring in the HDVS tanks, 7.6 MPa/min was set as the lower limit
to ensure the safety of the HDVS tanks.

 15

Figure 11 depicts the HD vehicle simulator hydrogen temperatures when hydrogen was
dispensed based on the fueling protocol as shown in Fig. 10. The temperatures in the type IV
tanks were measured with on-tank valve sensors, and the temperatures in the type III tanks were
measured with sensors that were inserted from the back tank walls. The individual HD vehicle
simulator tanks are not insulated, and some of them are almost exposed to sunlight. Accordingly,
there was variation in the initial hydrogen temperatures. As aforementioned, in both experiments,
the HD vehicle simulator tanks were filled at speeds beyond the fueling protocol boundary;
however, the hydrogen temperatures in the type IV tanks did not even reach 60°C, which was
more than 25°C below the protocol upper limit temperature (85°C). The project team believed
the reason for such large temperature deviations is that the ambient temperatures were low when
the experiments were carried out. Also, the project team observed significant differences
between the type IV and III tank temperatures. One of the reasons would be the difference in the
thermophysical properties of the tank materials. Another possible reason would be the
temperature sensor mounting positions: the temperatures in the type IV tanks were measured
with on-tank valve sensors that were positioned within the front (fill end) of the tanks, whereas
the temperatures in the type III tanks were measured with sensors positioned within the aft (non-
fill) end of the tanks. Future testing could be considered to determine if this difference in
measurement location is significant.

Task 9: Field experiments for H35HF fueling protocol validation

The project team conducted field validation experiments of the H35HF MC Formula protocol. In
lieu of testing at an existing commercial H35HF hydrogen station, the project team used a well-
instrumented test apparatus by ZBT GmbH.

Outcome – Successful

NREL subcontracted Hydrogen and Fuel Cell Center ZBT GmbH to perform the H35HF fueling
protocol validation experiment. Figure 11 shows the ZBT H35HF dispenser and an onboard
storage system. ZBT’s H35HF dispenser is equipped with commercially available H35HF

 18

components, akin to commercial stations. ZBT has three types of onboard storage systems
consisting of 244-L type IV, 350-L type IV, and 322-L type III tanks (ZBT does not have a large
onboard storage system). Figure 12 shows schematic diagrams of how the ZBT onboard storage
systems are connected to the H35HF dispenser. As Fig. 12 shows, when the fueling experiment
was performed, the hydrogen temperature in each tank was monitored at multiple positions to
capture the peak hydrogen temperature caused by hot spots or thermal stratification. Table 4
shows the experimental matrices for a total of 11 fueling experiments that were conducted under
the project (details of the ZBT facility can be found in the PRHYDE deliverable [5]).

Figure 13 shows the dispenser temperature, pressure, and mass flow rate profiles collected
during Tests #9 and #11, which are fast and slow fueling experiments, respectively, conducted
with the 350-L type IV tank. The slow fueling experiment corresponds to ambient temperature
fueling. Figure 14 shows the hydrogen temperatures and pressure measured at four locations in
the tank when hydrogen was dispensed under the fast and slow fueling conditions shown in Fig.
13. In the fast-fueling experiment, the tank was filled at a speed close to the protocol upper limit
(200 seconds), but the peak tank temperature increased only up to 68°C, and severe hot spots
were not observed. Conversely, in the slow (ambient temperature) fueling experiment, the
project team observed slight thermal stratification generated in the tank; however, the difference
in all the temperature data is 10°C at most, and the peak temperature was only around 80°C.
Although it is noted that there was an approximately 10°C drop in the hydrogen temperature
within the tank at about 550 seconds and then a subsequent increase in the temperature of about
the same magnitude, which might be explained by the leveling off tank pressure occurring in this
time span. Similarly, ZBT conducted the fueling experiments under various conditions and with
different tanks, and then the project team confirmed that the peak temperatures in the tanks never
exceeded the 85°C upper limit (as Table 4 shows). The experiments with the dispenser with the
appropriate H35HF hardware proved that the SAE J2601-5 H35HF fueling protocol is
sufficiently conservative.

Finally, the project team mapped the experimental conditions covered under the project, as
shown in Fig. 15. The areas highlighted in blue were covered under the project, and the areas
highlighted in red are the areas identified for future validation. For this project, relatively small
onboard storage systems were used, and protocol validation experiments were performed mainly
under middle to fast fueling conditions. In the ambient temperature fueling experiment at ZBT
(Fig. 14(b)), a degree of thermal stratification was observed under ambient temperature
conditions; however, the experiment was conducted only with a single tank storage system, and
the fueling speed was not especially slow due to the low ambient temperature. Therefore, the
project team concluded that a future project should evaluate the extent of thermal stratification in
large onboard storage systems under much slower fueling conditions.
