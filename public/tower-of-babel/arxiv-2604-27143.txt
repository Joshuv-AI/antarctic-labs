Enhancing Linux Privilege Escalation Attack Capabilities of Local LLM Agents
Benjamin Probst, Andreas Happe, Jürgen Cito

Abstract
Recent research has demonstrated the potential of Large Language Models (LLMs) for autonomous penetration testing, particularly when using cloud-based restricted-weight models. However, reliance on such models introduces security, privacy, and sovereignty concerns, motivating the use of locally hosted open-weight alternatives. Prior work shows that small open-weight models perform poorly on automated Linux privilege escalation, limiting their practical applicability. In this paper, we present a systematic empirical study of whether targeted system-level and prompting interventions can bridge this performance gap. We analyze failure modes of open-weight models in autonomous privilege escalation, map them to established enhancement techniques, and evaluate five concrete interventions (chain-of-thought prompting, retrieval-augmented generation, structured prompts, history compression, and reflective analysis) implemented as extensions to hackingBuddyGPT . Our results show that open-weight models can match or outperform cloud-based baselines such as GPT-4o. With our treatments enabled, Llama3.1 70B exploits 83% of tested vulnerabilities, while smaller models including Llama3.1 8B and Qwen2.5 7B achieve 67% when using guidance. A full-factorial ablation study over all treatment combinations reveals that reflection-based treatments contribute most, while also identifying vulnerability discovery as a remaining bottleneck for local models.



1
Introduction

Penetration testing simulates cyberattacks to identify vulnerabilities in computer systems. A common sub-task is
privilege escalation
, where an attacker manipulates a system to execute actions with higher privileges not intentionally granted to them. We focus on automated Linux privilege escalation, where a low-privilege user attempts to obtain
root
access.

Recent work on
hackingBuddyGPT
shows that cloud-based Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 can achieve success rates of around 80% on standardized privilege-escalation benchmarks
[
10
]
. However, cloud-based LLMs introduce substantial security, privacy, and sovereignty concerns: all interaction data (including command histories, system output, and sensitive configuration details) must be transmitted to an external providers. This is particularly problematic in the penetration-testing context, where often sensitive infrastructure details are processed. Cloud-based models also introduce supply-chain dependencies and availability risks, motivating the use of locally hosted open-weight alternatives.

Small Language Models (SLMs), i.e., open-weight LLMs small enough to run on local hardware, are a natural candidate. Unfortunately, prior work
[
10
]
reports success rates of only 8–16% for SLMs such as Llama3 8B and Llama3 70B, compared to roughly 80% for cloud-based models, severely limiting their practical applicability.

In this work, we present a systematic empirical study whether this gap can be reduced through targeted system-level and prompting interventions. We analyze execution traces from autonomous privilege-escalation runs and identify six recurring failure modes of SLMs: unstructured exploration, command repetition, hallucinated capabilities, ignored outputs, excessive command complexity, and missing exploitation knowledge. We map these to established enhancement techniques which are individually well-known, but whose effectiveness and interactions in the privilege-escalation domain have not been studied. We select five concrete treatments for implementation and evaluation.

We implement these treatments as extensions to
hackingBuddyGPT
and evaluate their impact across multiple SLMs. With the full treatment stack, Llama3.1 70B achieves a success rate of 83%, while Llama3.1 8B and Qwen2.5 7B reach 67%, effectively matching or surpassing GPT-4o. A full-factorial ablation study (
2^{5}
combinations) quantifies individual and combined contributions, revealing that reflection-oriented components (Analyze, Chain-of-Thought, Retrieval Augmented Generation) are the primary drivers, enabling success rates of up to 67% when combined.

Research Questions

Motivated by these observations, we investigate the following research questions:

•

RQ1
To what extent can established system-level and prompting treatments close the performance gap between small open-weight LLMs and cloud-based models for automated Linux privilege escalation?

•

RQ2
Which interventions, and which combinations, contribute most to performance gains, and what failure modes remain?

Contributions

This paper makes the following contributions:

•

A systematic failure-mode analysis of SLMs in autonomous Linux privilege escalation, identifying six domain-specific deficiencies and mapping them to candidate interventions (Section
4
).

•

An open-source prototype extending
hackingBuddyGPT

[
10
]
with five interventions, enabling reproducible experimentation (Section
6
).

•

Empirical evaluation across multiple models showing significant performance gains, including an ablation study quantifying individual component contributions (Sections
7
and
8
).

Background & Related Work

2.1
Small Language Models (SLMs)

Open-weight LLMs provide public access to their pre-trained parameters, enabling deployment on user-controlled hardware.
Small Language Model (SLM)
are open-weight LLMS with fewer than approximately 32B parameters that run on local hardware. As SLMs share the same transformer-based architectures as larger LLMs, most optimization and enhancement techniques apply to both classes.

Local deployment of SLMs offers advantages in security, privacy, and operational independence. In penetration-testing, cloud-based LLMs require transmitting sensitive data (configuration files, credentials, command outputs) to external providers. Locally hosted models avoid this issue entirely while also eliminating supply-chain dependencies such as service outages, pricing changes, or model deprecations.

The primary limitation of local model deployment are hardware requirements: very large models require hundreds of gigabytes of GPU memory, but SLMs can run on contemporary consumer GPUs
[
27
]
. While SLMs generally under-perform larger LLMs, prior work shows that they can be competitive when appropriately adapted
[
2
,
36
,
20
]
.

2.2
LLM Enhancing Techniques

Despite their general capabilities, LLMs often exhibit deficiencies in structured reasoning, long-horizon planning, and domain-specific knowledge. A variety of enhancement techniques have been proposed to mitigate these limitations.

Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
prompting encourages models to generate intermediate reasoning steps before producing an answer, improving performance in multi-step reasoning tasks
[
25
,
30
,
25
,
17
,
3
]
. CoT can be elicited via few-shot exemplar traces
[
30
]
, zero-shot cues such as
"Let’s think step by step"

[
13
]
, or Plan-and-Solve prompting
[
28
]
which separates planning from execution.

Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)
augments LLMs retrieving relevant documents from external knowledge sources and incorporating them into the prompt
[
5
,
14
]
, extending the model’s knowledge without modifying its parameters. RAG has been applied in domains requiring specialized or rapidly evolving knowledge, including medicine
[
31
]
, finance
[
35
]
, legal reasoning
[
34
]
, and penetration testing
[
20
,
12
,
32
]
.

Fine-tuning
adapts an LLM to a specific domain by further training it on curated data. Parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA
[
11
]
or QLoRA
[
4
]
reduce cost by introducing few trainable parameters and operating on quantized models. Fine-tuning has shown effectiveness across diverse tasks including penetration-testing
[
36
,
21
,
2
,
20
]
).

Privilege escalation depends on reconnaissance within a vast search space where exhaustive exploration is infeasible. Prior work shows that
High-Level Guidance
can substantially improve LLM performance by directing attention toward promising attack paths
[
10
]
. Guidance refers to task-specific hints that indicate plausible exploration directions without revealing concrete exploitation steps. In the employed benchmark, each task is associated with a single hint, e.g.,
“there might be some bad sudo binaries on the system”
or
“it looks like there is a periodic backup script”
, pointing to relevant subsystems or vulnerability classes. Importantly, these hints do not constitute solution sketches or executable commands. They model the kind of contextual information that is routinely available in realistic penetration-testing workflows: output from automated enumeration tools (e.g.,
LinPEAS
,
linux-exploit-suggester
), organizational threat intelligence, or preliminary findings from earlier assessment phases. In this sense, guidance represents a lightweight form of human-in-the-loop supervision rather than oracle knowledge.

2.3
Related Work

LLMs have demonstrated strong performance across a range of cybersecurity tasks, including source code vulnerability detection
[
24
]
, security patch generation
[
1
]
, and automated penetration testing
[
32
,

.

Linux Privilege Escalation.
Prior work shows that cloud-based LLMs such as GPT-4-turbo can successfully perform automated Linux privilege escalation
[
10
]
. However, the same evaluations demonstrate that small open-weight models, including Llama3 8B and Llama3 70B, perform poorly even when provided with human guidance, indicating fundamental limitations in reasoning, exploration, and exploitation.

Automated LLM-based Penetration Testing Frameworks.
Several frameworks have been proposed for LLM-driven penetration testing.
PentestGPT

[
3
]
uses a modular parsing/reasoning/generation design but requires human intervention.
AUTOATTACKER

[
32
]
is fully automated with a
summarizer
,
planner
,
navigator
, and
experience manager
, but none of its evaluated open-weight models successfully solve any task.
PenHeal

[
12
]
adds remediation advice but does not evaluate any open-weight models. To the best of our knowledge,
hackingBuddyGPT

[
10
,
8
]
is the only framework explicitly designed for automated Linux privilege escalation. It employs a single-loop architecture in which the LLM iteratively generates commands based on the current system state. Its results highlight the effectiveness of cloud-based models but expose severe limitations of open-weight models. Overall, existing work provides limited evidence that open-weight models can be effective for autonomous privilege escalation without substantial architectural support.

Using SLMs for Privilege Escalation.

Peres

[
29
]
follows the planner/commander/summarizer pattern
[
32
,
12
]
to delegate priv-escalation tasks to multiple specialized LLMs (three 7B models). While
Peres
achieves promising results, it similarly reports that guidance is required to reach acceptable success rates with open-weight models. A direct quantitative comparison is not feasible:
Peres
evaluates on a different benchmark with different vulnerability types, iteration limits, and success criteria. Conceptually, the two approaches are complementary:
Peres
distributes reasoning across multiple specialized models, while we enhance a single model with system-level interventions. Both approaches converge on the finding that open-weight models require substantial scaffolding to be competitive, and that guidance remains essential for discovery.

Hackphyr 7B
[
22
]
fine-tunes Zephyr-7B
[
26
]
with penetration-testing data. Rather than focusing on Linux privilege escalation, it targets network security and evaluates performance using NetSecGame
[
6
,
23
]
, a simulated environment with a simplified action space. Hackphyr outperforms baseline models and approaches the performance of significantly larger models such as GPT-4.

Methodology

3.1
Treatment Selection Process

To identify why SLMs underperform cloud-based models
[
10
,
32
]
, we qualitatively analyzed execution traces from GPT-4-turbo and two SLMs during autonomous privilege escalation, identifying key behavioral differences and failure modes. Based on these findings and established enhancement techniques (Section
2.2
), we selected treatments (Section
4
) for investigation. The preliminary analysis is detailed in the appendix (Section
0.A
).

3.2
Experiment Design

We integrate the treatments into an LLM-driven prototype (Section
3.3
) connected to vulnerable test VMs (Section
3.5
). Each combination of model and treatment configuration is run three times with a maximum of 40 rounds per run. We additionally conduct an ablation study using Llama3.1 8B.

3.3
Prototype

We implement our treatment ideas as extensions to the existing
hackingBuddyGPT
framework
[
10
]
. Its core architecture (Figure
1
) consists of a single control loop: in each iteration, a
query_next_command
prompt, containing the goal, scenario information, and action history, is used to generate a command which is executed on the target over SSH. The loop repeats until root access is achieved or a maximum iteration count is reached.

3.4
LLM Selection and Configuration

For our evaluation we chose a selection of common cloud-based models as baseline to compare our selected open-weight models against.

Cloud-based Models

For the cloud-based models, we use OpenAI GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-11-20) and OpenAI GPT-4o mini (gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18). They were released around the same time as the Meta Llama3.1 series, and are accessible and cost-effective to run.

Open-Weight Models

We select Llama3.1 8B, Llama3.1 70B, Qwen2.5 7B, and WhiteRabbitNeo 7B. Llama3.1 8B and Qwen2.5 7B are SLMs runnable on a single contemporary GPU. Llama3.1 70B can still run on consumer-grade GPUs. WhiteRabbitNeo
1
1

1

https://www.whiterabbitneo.com/
is fine-tuned on cybersecurity topics (misconfigurations, credentials, vulnerabilities) with Qwen2.5-Coder 7B as base model.

The 7B/8B models are hosted locally via
llama-cpp-python
2
2

2

https://github.com/abetlen/llama-cpp-python
; Llama3.1 70B uses the Microsoft Azure AI API; OpenAI models use the OpenAI API. Temperature is set to
0.8
for all models, following the configuration used in prior work
[
10
]
, which balances diversity in command generation with output coherence. Platform defaults are used for all other parameters.

Model Refusals

The only LLM exhibiting model refusal was Llama3.1 8B, which refused to generate exploitation commands in a substantial fraction of iterations. While various jailbreaking methods have been researched
[
33
]
, we use
Llama-3.1-8B-Lexi-Uncensored-V2
3
3

3

https://huggingface.co/Orenguteng/Llama-3.1-8B-Lexi-Uncensored-V2
, accessed 8.3.2025
, a fine-tuned variant of Llama3.1 8B with safety filters removed. This is representative of real-world offensive use, where practitioners routinely deploy uncensored variants. We note that this fine-tune preserves the base model’s architecture and general capabilities; the removal of safety training does not introduce new failure modes (hallucinations, repetition) beyond those already present in the base model.

Context size

The size of a model context indicates how much information can be processed by the model at once. Current models from OpenAI, Anthropic or Meta can have a context size of up to 128k
4
4

4

https://ai.meta.com/blog/meta-llama-3-1/
, accessed 7.2.2025
, or even 200k
5
5

5

https://platform.openai.com/docs/models
,
https://docs.anthropic.com/en/docs/about-claude/models
, accessed 7.2.2025
. Although such a large context size may be easily accessible when using an API, it also increases memory requirements, which is often the limiting factor when running a model locally
[
15
,
7
]
. We limit the context size for all tested models to roughly 8k, with the goal of striking a balance between memory consumption and necessary space.

3.5
Testbed and Baselines

We use the
benchmark-privesc-linux
testbed
[
9
]
, consisting of 12 test cases spanning five vulnerability classes: SUID/sudo misconfigurations (3 tests), password hygiene issues such as reuse or weak passwords (3 tests), information disclosure through config files or shell history (3 tests), Docker group membership (1 test), and cron-based escalation (2 tests). Table
5
in the Appendix lists all scenarios. Each test case runs in a dedicated Debian-based VM with a single intentionally planted vulnerability, a low-privilege user account, and SSH access. This isolation ensures that destructive commands do not affect other tests and that each run starts from a clean state. A run succeeds if the agent obtains a root shell or correctly identifies the root password within the iteration limit.

Metrics and Analysis

For each test, we collect timestamps, end state (root achieved or not), and per-iteration data including all queries, prompts, answers, token counts, and timing. Following
[
32
,

For qualitative analysis, we process the generated output and examine the quality of the generated content, the behavior of the model (e.g. repetition, missing low hanging fruits, running in circles, etc.) and the influence of the different components. For quantitative analysis, we focus on the amount of tokens generated by each component, the number of solved test-cases and the necessary amount of iterations for each specific test-case.

Chain of Thought (CoT)

CoT improves the reasoning capabilities of an LLM and could help with multiple problems. For example, during the reasoning step of CoT, the LLM may realize that it has already executed a specific command and should therefore suggest an alternative approach. Similarly, additional reasoning capabilities may reduce hallucinations as the model has to explain its steps.

Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) for Exploitation Knowledge

RAG aims to improve the quality of the generated answers by augmenting the prompt with additional knowledge. This feature can enrich the model with domain specific knowledge such as specific commands, possible vulnerabilities, or general exploits. This could solve one the problem of knowledge gaps.

Structure via Prompt (SvP)

An essential part of automated penetration testing is structured exploration. By default, we use the same approach as
[
10
]
and
[
32
]
, where the LLM is prompted with the current worldview and asked to generate the next action. We augment this by including a curated checklist of common privilege-escalation attack vectors and their enumeration commands directly in the prompt. This checklist was generated using GPT-4o and manually validated against established references (Section
6
). The checklist provides structure and discovery coverage without revealing exploitation steps, so the LLM still must reason about how to exploit any findings.

Context Management And History Compression

Another crucial step for automated penetration testing is keeping track of the attack history and storing important extracted information and potential vulnerabilities. To minimize storage while keeping relevant information, compressing the action history is of special interest. Current LLM-based automated penetration testing systems
[
32
,
12
,
10
]
use an additional LLM prompt to summarize the most recent output and update its current worldview.
[
10
]
compare this method to a baseline, where, instead of summarizing the output, all previous commands and the respective outputs are included in the prompt. Their evaluation shows that depending on the model, summarizing can lead to a substantial boost or decrease in success rate. We propose a modified approach that keeps all previously executed commands but only the command output of the most recent executed one. This reduces time and overhead, as
[
10
]
have shown that the additional LLM-callout can significantly increase the runtime. The major downside of this approach is that potential useful information is lost after the command output has been pruned from history. To reduce information loss, we propose to include multiple past outputs instead of just the last one.

No Duplicates

An intuitive solution to reduce repetition is to not allow repeated commands. Each time an already executed command is generated, the LLM is prompted again until a new command is returned. This is synergistic to using SLMs as there are no direct costs associated with the LLM invocation. To avoid infinite loops, after a configurable number of LLM-generations has been reached, invocation of a previously used command is allowed.

Reflective State Analysis

One important abilities for penetration-testing is analyzing the last command output, and basing the next executed action on this information. As shown in Chapter
4
, Llama3.1’s capabilities for integrating information are lacking as it issues random commands instead of grounding its selection on the experienced command outputs. To combat this, we force the LLM to perform an analysis by using the context compression method
[
32
,
10
]
, in which the LLM updates its worldview after each iteration with the most recent output. To further improve the quality of the analysis, we explicitly prompt the LLM for an analysis instead of prompting it to update its current worldview.

State

The last method we propose is called State. Its structure is identical to the mentioned history compression method
[
10
,
32
]
, but instead of replacing the action history the compressed history is included in addition to the history, potentially combined with the
History Compression
method. Updating the state after each iteration triggers an implicit analysis, which should help with the problem of the LLM ignoring the outputs, reduce repetition, and provide missing structure.

5.1
Treatment Selection

We conduct a preliminary analysis (Section
0.A
) to identify promising treatments. Based on the results, we made two key adaptations: (1) RAG-derived information is included in the
analyze_cmd
prompt rather than the
query_next_command
prompt, reducing clutter and allowing pre-processing with the full command output; (2) guidance information is included in both prompts rather than only
query_next_command
.

No Duplicates
and
State
were excluded as they did not improve results during the preliminary analysis.
State
increased prompt clutter, consistent with prior observations that state mechanisms can either improve or decrease efficacy
[
10
]
, and the problems it targets were already addressed by other interventions.
No Duplicates
was excluded because command repetition was already substantially reduced by the Analyze intervention: by forcing the LLM to reflect on its previous output, Analyze implicitly discourages repeating commands that yielded no useful information. Our preliminary analysis (Section
0.A
) confirmed that repetition was no longer a dominant failure mode once Analyze was enabled, making the explicit deduplication mechanism redundant.

6
Prototype

Figure 2:
Architecture of the prototype.

We extend
hackingBuddyGPT
’s architecture (Figure
2
) with new components. The core
query_next_command
prompt (Figure
7
in the Appendix) includes scenario information, instructions, and the analysis, generating chain-of-thought reasoning from which commands are extracted and executed. After execution, the output is forwarded to
rag_prompt
(Figure
9
, generating a vector store search query) and
analyze_cmd
(Figure
8
, producing an analysis incorporating the command output, retrieved RAG documents, and optional guidance). The analysis feeds back into the next
query_next_command
iteration.

CoT

Standard zero-shot CoT
[
13
]
proved ineffective for our models. We instead combine Plan-and-Solve
[
28
]
with zero-shot prompting into
Extract-and-Think
:
"Let’s first understand the problem and extract the most important facts from the information above. Then, let’s think step by step and figure out the next command we should try."
Commands are extracted via
<command></command>
tags.

RAG

We use
langchain
with two data sources:
HackTricks

[
19
]
(43 pages on Linux privilege-escalation attacks, split into chunks of 1000 tokens) and
GTFOBins

[
18
]
(390 UNIX binaries exploitable in misconfigured systems). We use
text-embedding-3-small
from OpenAI as embedding model. In each iteration, the LLM generates a search query based on the last command output, relevant documents are retrieved and trimmed to 1200 tokens, and the result is included in the analysis prompt.

Analyze

After each iteration, the LLM is provided with the executed command, its output, and then tasked to analyze it. The analysis is included in the next
query_next_command
prompt.

History Compression

Instead of including all the commands and their respective output in each
query_next_command
prompt, we remove all outputs except the most recent one.

SvP

The SvP document provides the LLM with a structured checklist of common Linux privilege-escalation attack vectors and their associated enumeration commands. We generated it by prompting
gpt-4o-2024-05-13
for a comprehensive, unbiased enumeration of Linux privilege-escalation techniques, organized by vulnerability class (SUID/sudo misconfigurations, cron jobs, writable files, kernel exploits, etc.), with each entry listing the relevant reconnaissance commands. We manually reviewed the output to remove duplicates and verify command correctness against standard references (GTFOBins
[
18
]
, HackTricks
[
19
]
). The resulting document contains 47 commands across 12 vulnerability classes and is included verbatim in the
query_next_command
prompt. Since SvP contains only enumeration commands—not exploitation payloads—the risk of introducing hallucinated or harmful content is limited; the LLM uses these commands for discovery and must still independently reason about exploitation. The full SvP content and generation history are available in our artifact repository.

Figure 3:
Ablation Study using Llama3, including guidance and both baselines and all our suggested treatment ideas.

Without guidance or treatments enabled, none of the open-weight models can match the performance of GPT-4o. Qwen2.5 7B, LLama3.1 8B, GPT-4o mini and WhiteRabbitNeo have success rates of
8\%
, while Llama3.1 70B reaches
17\%
. GPT-4o, on the other hand, achieves
42\%
.

Enabling guidance improved results for all models but Qwen3 whose score remains constant. Llama3.1 8B only solves a single additional test case, while the other models significantly improve performance, with GPT-4o reaching
67\%
. Guidance improved the performance, but the gap between GPT-4o and the other models remains.

The treatments increases the success rate of all tested models. The gain for the open-weight models is insignificant, as it only allowed the SLMs to solve a single additional test case. GPT-4o-mini showed substantial improvement in it’s performance, matching the baseline performance of GPT-4o with
42\%
.

Enabling treatments and guidance leads to significant improvements for all tested models. WhiteRabbitNeo reaches 42%, while both Llama3.1 8B and Qwen2.5 7B are able to match baseline guidance GPT-4o with 67%. GPT-4o mini and Llama3.1 70B outperform GPT-4o and have a success rate of
75\%
and
83\%
respectively.

Impact of Guidance.

Guidance consistently improved the success rate of the tested models, with the exception being baseline Qwen2.5 7B, where the success rate remains
8\%
. Although guidance increases the performance for the baseline runs, the change was only significant for GPT-4o. We discuss
baseline+guidance
in Section
8.3
.

Guidance combined with the treatments leads to drastic increases for almost all models. It allows Llama3.1 8B and Qwen2.5 7B to match GPT-4o, and GPT-4o mini and Llama3.1 70B to outperform GPT-4o. Llama3.1 70B almost reaches human-level success rate
[
10
]
.

Without narrowing down the search space, LLMs can indefinitely explore different attack vectors without ever coming close to the vulnerability. For example, not a single test case of the vulnerability class
information disclosure
is solved without guidance.

7.2
Token And Context Usage

We analyze token usage across models using the
cl100k_base
encoder from
tiktoken
to normalize across different tokenizers. SLMs consume substantially more tokens than larger models: Llama3.1 8B uses 281% more output and 163% more input tokens compared to GPT-4o mini, while Qwen2.5 7B uses 236% and 143% respectively. Llama3.1 70B is closer to GPT-4o mini at 125% output and 108% input.

Qwen2.5 7B has the longest average length for the analysis with 563 tokens. Llama3.1 8B’s average is slightly shorter with 510. The mean of the bigger models is less than halve that of the smaller models, with 226 tokens for GPT-4o mini and 193 for Llama3.1 70B. Llama3.1 8B has the longest average CoT length, with 328 tokens, followed by Llama3.1 70B with 214 tokens, Qwen2.5 7B with 178 tokens and GPT-4o mini with 76 tokens. GPT-4o mini has the longest average search query with 102 tokens, followed by Llama3.1 70B with 95 tokens. Llama3.1 8B has 20% less than GPT-4o mini with 81 tokens and Qwen2.5 7B has almost 50% less, with 52 tokens.

To investigate the impact of the different treatments, we conduct an ablation study using Llama3.1 8B with enabled guidance mechanism. With five different treatments, we have
2^{5}=32
possible combinations. For our purposes, only 24 are relevant: eight combinations are redundant due to the dependency between RAG and Analyze. Since the information provided by RAG is only included through the
Analyze
prompt, combinations where RAG is enabled without Analyze are functionally equivalent to variations were both are disabled. The results are shown in Figure
3
and Table

8
Discussion

We organize this discussion around our two research questions, then examine emergent failure modes, implications, threats to validity, and limitations.

8.1
RQ1: Closing the Performance Gap

Without any treatments, SLMs achieve 8–17% success rates compared to GPT-4o’s 42%. With the full treatment stack and guidance, Llama3.1 70B reaches 83% and both Llama3.1 8B and Qwen2.5 7B reach 67%, matching or surpassing GPT-4o’s guided baseline of 67%. Even GPT-4o mini improves from 8% to 75%. These results confirm that the poor baseline performance of SLMs reflects inadequate scaffolding rather than an inherent capacity limitation.

However, the gap closes fully only when guidance is provided. Without guidance, treatments alone yield modest SLM improvements (8% to 17%), while GPT-4o mini benefits substantially (8% to 42%). This asymmetry points to a deeper issue explored in Section
8.3
: vulnerability
discovery
remains the primary bottleneck for open-weight models.

8.2
RQ2: The Dominance of Reflection-Based Treatments

The ablation study reveals a clear hierarchy: reflection-oriented interventions (Analyze, CoT) are the primary drivers of performance gains, while structural interventions (SvP, History Compression) play supporting roles.

Analyze appears in all top-ten configurations and achieves 50% in isolation, making it the single most impactful treatment. Its effectiveness stems from addressing SLMs’ core deficiency: the failure to
integrate command outputs
into subsequent decisions. Analyze forces the model to explicitly process each output before selecting the next action, transforming the baseline’s “act-then-act” pattern into a “reflect-then-act” loop. This addresses the root cause of multiple failure modes (repetition, ignoring outputs, unstructured exploration) rather than treating symptoms individually.

CoT and RAG amplify this through complementary mechanisms. CoT structures reasoning within the command-generation step, reducing hallucinated capabilities and complex invalid commands. RAG fills domain-specific knowledge gaps surfaced during analysis, e.g., providing exploitation techniques for specific SUID binaries that SLMs lack in their parametric knowledge. The best-performing ablation configuration (
Analyze+CoT+RAG
, 67%) demonstrates that these three components are largely additive, each addressing a distinct bottleneck in the observe-reason-act cycle. Notably, even the 8B-parameter Llama3.1 reaches 67% with the full stack, suggesting that the treatments externalize reasoning steps that larger models perform implicitly, compensating for capacity limitations rather than merely amplifying existing capabilities
[
2
]
.

8.3
The Discovery Bottleneck

The most significant remaining limitation is vulnerability discovery. Without guidance, no model (including GPT-4o) searched for passwords in files (test-7/8/10), despite this being a standard manual check. This explains the impact of guidance: it primes the discovery phase, allowing models to focus on exploitation.

Combining guidance with treatments substantially increased success rates across all models. Without treatments, the improvements from guidance alone were significantly smaller, with Qwen2.5 7B showing no improvement at all. Log analysis showed that both Llama3.1 8B and Qwen2.5 7B located the respective vulnerability in 7 out of 12 test-cases but either ignored the information or failed to exploit it, highlighting that the intervention stack is what enables models to
act
on guidance.

Discovery involves two distinct sub-challenges. The first is
finding
potentially vulnerable configurations: SvP addresses this by providing enumeration directions, and models consistently locate relevant binaries when SvP covers the vulnerability class. However, SvP’s coverage is limited by its curated content; for example, the checklist omitted credential searches in user files, leaving information-disclosure vulnerabilities unaddressed. The second is
recognizing
findings as exploitable: in test-1, all models found a
python
binary with a set SUID bit but focused on common SUID binaries instead. Analyze improves recognition but remains bounded by the model’s parametric knowledge.

These findings converge with those of
Peres

[
29
]
, which similarly reports that guidance remains essential for open-weight models. Both approaches—multi-model delegation and single-model scaffolding—arrive at the same conclusion: current open-weight models lack the broad reconnaissance intuition needed for autonomous discovery.

Realism of Guidance.

While our benchmark uses manually authored hints, comparable information is routinely produced by automated enumeration tools such as
LinPEAS
or
linux-exploit-suggester
, which highlight misconfigured SUID binaries, writable cron jobs, or credential files at a similar abstraction level. Integrating such tools as a pre-processing step is a natural extension of our pipeline. Crucially, guidance alone is insufficient (baseline+guidance reaches only 8–33% for SLMs), confirming that the interventions are the enabling factor.

8.4
Emergent Failure Modes

While the treatments resolve the six original failure modes (Section
4
), they introduce new ones characteristic of scaffolding-dependent systems.

Guidance-Induced Errors.
Guidance can both mislead and be misinterpreted. In test-11, the hint
“there is a periodic backup script”
caused all models to search for a file literally named
backup
. More subtly, in test-3, Llama3.1 8B correctly identifies an exploitable
sudo
/
tar
misconfiguration but refuses to execute the exploit: it interprets
“there might be some bad sudo binaries”
as a warning not to trust
sudo
, preventing it from running the correct exploitation command.

RAG-Induced Hallucinations.
RAG can overwhelm models with information about tools absent from the target system, leading to hallucinated commands referencing
searchsploit
,
exploit.sh
, or
backdoor.sh
. This suggests that RAG retrieval should be filtered against the target’s actual tool inventory.

Multi-Command Dilution.
SLMs frequently return multiple commands per iteration (see Figure
5
in the Appendix), unlike GPT-4o mini which consistently returns one. This dilutes the subsequent RAG search query, which is generated from the combined output of all commands rather than a single focused result.

8.5
Implications

Our findings carry implications for both sides of the security equation. From a
defensive
perspective, discovery, not exploitation, is the primary bottleneck for automated attackers. Minimizing attack and thus reconnaissance surfaces directly limits automated exploitation, even against capable scaffolding. From an
offensive
perspective, locally hosted models are now viable for autonomous privilege escalation, eliminating the need to transmit sensitive data to external providers. The token overhead of SLMs (up to 281% more output tokens) represents a compute cost borne entirely locally, a favorable trade-off when data sovereignty is a concern.

8.6
Threats to Validity

Selection bias.
Both the model selection and the benchmark could be subject to bias. An exhaustive privilege-escalation benchmark is infeasible, and frequent SOTA releases preclude evaluating all models. To mitigate this, we selected three well-known LLM families (Llama3.1, Qwen2.5, GPT) covering both open-weight and closed-source models.

Randomness.
We use a non-zero temperature (0.8) to reflect real-world conditions and run each configuration three times. More repetitions would improve statistical confidence but were infeasible due to resource constraints.

Data contamination.
The benchmark testbed
[
9
]
was released in 2023, before the training cutoff of all evaluated models, so benchmark details may appear in training data. This applies equally to all models, preserving relative comparisons. The low SLM baselines (0–17%) further suggest that exposure alone does not confer exploitation capability.

8.7
Ethical Considerations

All experiments were conducted on isolated virtual machines under the full control of the research team; no real-world systems were targeted or affected. The benchmark vulnerabilities are synthetic and intentionally introduced, and no unauthorized access to third-party infrastructure occurred at any point. We acknowledge the dual-use nature of this research: improving the autonomous attack capabilities of locally hosted models could lower the barrier for malicious use. However, we believe that understanding these capabilities is essential for the defensive community, as it enables the development of more effective detection and hardening strategies against automated privilege-escalation attacks. To support reproducibility and responsible use, all artifacts will be made publicly available upon acceptance.

9
Conclusion

We presented a systematic empirical study of whether established system-level and prompting interventions can close the performance gap between small open-weight models and cloud-based LLMs for autonomous Linux privilege escalation. Our results demonstrate that a combination of five interventions (chain-of-thought prompting, retrieval-augmented generation, structured prompts, history compression, and reflective analysis) enables models with fewer than 10B parameters to match or surpass cloud-based models like GPT-4o, while reducing context usage. A full-factorial ablation study identifies reflection-based components as the primary drivers of these gains.

However, vulnerability discovery remains a bottleneck for open-weight models without guidance, suggesting that defenders should prioritize minimizing information leakage and reconnaissance surfaces as the most effective countermeasure against automated local attackers.

0.A.3
Treatment Analysis.

Our selected treatments are successful in dealing with the problems discussed in Chapter
4
. SvP gives the LLM more structure in its approach, allowing both, GPT-4o mini and LLama3.1 8B to find the vulnerabilities in all runs in all test cases, for which SvP contains a relevant command.

RAG is effective at filling knowledge gaps. In most runs, when the vector store contains relevant information, RAG provides the LLM with that knowledge at least once, provided the vulnerability is found.

The Analyze component resolves the problem of the LLM ignoring the outputs of executed commands. This allows LLama3.1 8B, for example, to solve test-2 and the GPT-4o mini, thereby increasing its success rate on that test.

The last problem that we had was severe repetition. This issue is also no longer present. Commands are sometimes still repeated, but this is not necessarily a downside and can even be advantageous. For example, the LLM can execute
sudo -l
multiple times, if it does not identify the vulnerability after the first iteration.

CoT works as intended and achieves its goal, of enhancing the LLMs reasoning capabilities. In most iterations, both LLama3.1 8B and ChatGPT-4o mini show a clear thought process. While Llama3.1 8B also extracts facts, ChatGPT-4o mini tends to ignore this part of the CoT template and instead states its reasoning and suggested command.

For history compression, we also found, that it fulfills its goal of removing noise. The major disadvantage of History Compression is that if the LLM identifies essential information but does not use it immediately in the next iteration, that information is lost because the output is purged and the next one is included. The test runs for our initial prototype show that this problem is not severe. In most cases, the vulnerability is found multiple times. Furthermore, if all the outputs are included in the
query_next_command
prompt, it is unlikely that the LLM would successfully extract the correct output, among all the irrelevant outputs and the analyze component, and then correctly exploit it.

New Problems

Although the treatments are successful in resolving identified problems and substantially increasing the performance of the tested models, it also introduces new issues. The inclusion of RAG and Analyze led to a significant increase in the information in the
query_next_command
prompt, which overloads it. This results in a variety of problems. For example, models are far more likely to extract useless facts and miss important information during the CoT process. This happens frequently to LLama3.1 8B, where it ignores the guidance or essential information from the analysis. An additional consequence of an overflowing prompt is the "Lost in the Middle" Problem
[
16
]
, which states, that performance can significantly decrease, depending on the position of relevant information in the prompt.

Another issue, that we identified is, that the analysis is often irrelevant and acts as noise in the
query_next_command
prompt, possibly misleading and confusing the LLM. This can occur, if the LLM is missing knowledge to make a proper analysis or if the most recent output does not contain any relevant information and the LLM overanalyzes the situation.

Finally, we also found, that in some scenarios RAG provides the correct command/way to
exploit a vulnerability, but the LLM changes it to an invalid/useless one and
executes this new version.
