On the Argument of the Lerch, Chowla–Selberg Formula and CM Values of η ⁡ ( τ ) \eta(\tau)
Henri Cohen

Abstract
We give a completely explicit Lerch, Chowla–Selberg formula without absolute values of the Dedekind eta function, and give some applications, in particular to the argument of individual CM values of the eta function. We also give precise conjectures on these CM values and a large table supporting them.

Introduction

Definition 1.1

Let
D<0
be congruent to
0
or
1
modulo
4
.

(1)

We denote by
Q(D)
the set of quadratic numbers

\tau=(-b+\sqrt{D})/(2a)
(or, equivalently, of binary quadratic forms

(a,b,c):=ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2}
) such that
b^{2}-4ac=D
,
a>0
, and
\gcd(a,b,c)=1

modulo the natural action of
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. A representative of an element
of
Q(D)
will be called a
CM point
of discriminant
D
.

(2)

We let as usual
h(D)=|Q(D)|
,
w(D)
the cardinality of the
number of roots of unity in the quadratic order of discriminant
D

(so
w(-3)=6
,
w(-4)=4
, and
w(D)=2
for
D<-4
), and

h^{\prime}(D)=h(D)/(w(D)/2)
, so that
h^{\prime}(D)=h(D)
for
D<-4
.

(3)

We denote by
F(D)
the standard set of representatives of
Q(D)
,
in other words satisfying the additional conditions
|b|\leq a\leq c
and

b\geq 0
if either
|b|=a
or
a=c
.

Definition 1.2

For
D
a negative fundamental discriminant, we define the
Chowla–Selberg gamma quotient by

\CS(D)=\left(\prod_{1\leq j\leq|D|}\Gamma(j/|D|)^{\mbox{$\left(\frac{D}{j}\right)$}}\right)^{1/h^{\prime}(D)}\;.

Thus,
\CS(-3)=(\Gamma(1/3)/\Gamma(2/3))^{3}
,
\CS(-4)=(\Gamma(1/4)/\Gamma(3/4))^{2}
, and
otherwise the exponent is
1/h(D)
.

The Lerch, Chowla–Selberg formula, as generalized in
[
3
]

is as follows:

Theorem 1.3

Let
D<0
be congruent to
0
or
1
modulo
4
,
and write
D=D_{0}f^{2}
with
D_{0}
a fundamental discriminant. Then

\left(\prod_{\tau\in Q(D)}\Im(\tau)|\eta(\tau)|^{4}\right)^{1/h(D)}=c(D_{0},f)\dfrac{\CS(D_{0})}{4\pi|D|^{1/2}}\;,

with

c(D_{0},f)=\prod_{p\mid f}p^{e(p)},\text{\hskip 10.22217ptwith\hskip 10.22217pt}e(p)=\dfrac{(1-p^{-v_{p}(f)})\left(1-\mbox{$\left(\frac{D_{0}}{p}\right)$}\right)}{(1-1/p)\left(p-\mbox{$\left(\frac{D_{0}}{p}\right)$}\right)}\;.

The original formula was for
D=D_{0}
fundamental, hence
c(D_{0},f)=1
. Note
a misprint in Section 10.5 of
[
2
]
, where the factor
p-\mbox{$\left(\frac{D_{0}}{p}\right)$}

is incorrectly written as
p-\mbox{$\left(\frac{D_{0}}{p}\right)$}/p
.

The first goal of this paper is to give a formula for the same product, but
without the modulus in front of
\eta(\tau)
. We will then give results and
precise conjectures on
individual
values of
\eta(\tau)
.

Note first that by modularity,
the expression
\Im(\tau)|\eta(\tau)|^{4}
is
invariant
under the action
of
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
, so taking the product over
Q(D)
makes sense. On the
contrary,
\Im(\tau)\eta(\tau)^{4}
is
not
invariant, so we have to
specify which set of representatives we choose. Thus, we define the
function that we will study in the first part of this paper as follows:

Definition 1.4

For
D<0
congruent to
0
or
1
modulo
4
, we define

P(D)=\prod_{\tau\in F(D)}\dfrac{\eta(\tau)^{4}}{|\eta(\tau)|^{4}}=\dfrac{\prod_{\tau\in F(D)}\Im(\tau)\eta(\tau)^{4}}{(c(D_{0},f)\CS(D_{0})/(4\pi|D|^{1/2}))^{h(D)}}\;,

where
D=D_{0}f^{2}
with
D_{0}
fundamental and
F(D)
is the standard
set of representatives of
Q(D)
as defined above.

Theorem
1.3
tells us that the two definitions of
P(D)
are
equivalent, and evidently its modulus is equal to

1
, so our goal is to understand its argument.

2
The Main Theorem

Theorem 2.1

Let
D<0
be congruent to
0
or
1
modulo
4
.
Define
U(D)=\emptyset
if
v_{2}(D)=3
or
v_{2}(D)=4
or
D\equiv 4\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,16)
,
and otherwise
U(D)
to be the set of positive divisors
d
of
|D|
such
that
d\leq\sqrt{|D|}
and
\gcd(d,|D|/d)=2^{v}
with
v=0
if
D\equiv 1\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
,

v=1
if
D\equiv 12\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,16)
, and
v=2
if
D\equiv 0\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,32)
.

Let
U^{-}(D)
(resp
U^{+}(D)
) be the set of elements
d
of
U(D)
such that

d\leq\sqrt{|D|/3}
(resp
d>\sqrt{|D|/3}
). We have

P(D)=e^{-(i\pi/6)(|U^{-}(D)|-3|U^{+}(D)|)}\prod_{d\in U^{+}(D)}\dfrac{d-\sqrt{D}}{d+\sqrt{D}}\;.

Warning: the stated congruences are for
D
itself,
not
for
|D|=-D
.

Proof.
It is immediate to check that
\eta(-\overline{\tau})=\overline{\eta(\tau)}
.
If follows that if both
(-b+\sqrt{D})/(2a)
and
(b+\sqrt{D})/(2a)

belong to
F(D)
, the product of their eta values is a positive real number,
and
\eta(\tau)
is also positive real if
b=0
. Since we are only concerned
with the argument of
P(D)
, it follows that the only
(a,b,c)
which can
contribute to the argument are the boundary cases for which
a=c
or
a=b
.

The case
a=c
occurs for
D=b^{2}-4a^{2}=-(2a-b)(2a+b)
and
0\leq b\leq a
.
Set
d=2a-b
and
d^{\prime}=2a+b=|D|/d
. The condition
b\geq 0
means that

d\leq\sqrt{|D|}
. The condition
b\leq a
is easily seen to be equivalent to

d\geq\sqrt{|D|/3}
. We have
a=(d^{\prime}+d)/4
,
b=(d^{\prime}-d)/2
, and the last condition
is that we need
a
and
b
to be integral with
\gcd(a,b,c)=\gcd(a,b)=1
,
in other words
\gcd(d^{\prime}+d,2(d^{\prime}-d))=4
.

If
D\equiv 1\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
, we have
d\mid D
odd, and since
dd^{\prime}=-D\equiv 3\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
,
we have
d\equiv 1\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
and
d^{\prime}\equiv 3\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
or the reverse, so

4\mid(d^{\prime}+d)
and
2(d^{\prime}-d)\equiv 4\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,8)
, hence the
2
-part of the gcd
is indeed equal to
4
, so the condition is
\gcd(d,d^{\prime})=1
.

If
D\equiv 0\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
, the integrality of
b
implies that
d
and
d^{\prime}
must
both be even. Thus, if
D\equiv 4\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,16)
, i.e,
|D|/4\equiv 3\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
,
we have
d/2\equiv 1\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
and
d^{\prime}/2\equiv 3\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
or the reverse,
so
8\mid d^{\prime}+d
and
4\mid d^{\prime}-d
, hence
8\mid\gcd(d^{\prime}+d,2(d^{\prime}-d))
, so the
gcd condition cannot be satisfied, in other words
U(D)=\emptyset
.

If
D\equiv 12\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,16)
, we now have
d/2\equiv d^{\prime}/2\equiv\pm 1\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
,
so
d^{\prime}+d\equiv\pm 4\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,8)
and
2(d^{\prime}-d)\equiv 0\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,16)
, so the
2
-part
of the gcd is equal to
4
, so the condition is
\gcd(d/2,d^{\prime}/2)=1
, i.e.,

\gcd(d,d^{\prime})=2
.

If
v_{2}(D)=v\geq 3
, write
d=2^{w}m
with
m
odd, so
d^{\prime}=2^{v-w}m^{\prime}
with

m^{\prime}
odd. Exchanging
d
and
d^{\prime}
if necessary we may assume
w\leq v/2
.
If
w<v/2
, we have
v_{2}(d^{\prime}+d)=w
and
v_{2}(2(d^{\prime}-d))=w+1
, so the gcd condition
implies that
w=2
, so
v\geq 5
. If
w=v/2
, we have
v_{2}(d^{\prime}+d)\geq w+1
and

v_{2}(2(d^{\prime}-d))\geq w+2
, so the gcd condition implies
w=1
hence
v=2
,
which is excluded. Summarizing, this shows that if
v=3
or
v=4
the
gcd condition cannot be satisfied, in other words
U(D)=\emptyset
.

Finally, if
v_{2}(D)\geq 5
, i.e.,
D\equiv 0\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,32)
, the above argument
shows that we must choose
d\equiv 4\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,8)
hence
d^{\prime}\equiv 0\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,8)
, and thus
the gcd condition is equivalent to
\gcd(d,d^{\prime})=4
.

We have thus shown that there is a bijection between the cases
a=c

and the elements
d\in U^{+}(D)
, and we have

\tau=(-b+\sqrt{D})/(2a)=(-(|D|/d-d)+2\sqrt{D})/(|D|/d+d)
.
Now we check that
\tau_{1}=-1/(\tau+1)=-1/2+\sqrt{D}/(2d)
.
Thus
q_{1}=e^{2\pi i\tau_{1}}
is a negative real, so from the product expansion
we deduce that
\eta^{4}(\tau_{1})=re^{-2\pi i/12}

for some positive real
r
. Thus by modularity

\eta^{4}(-1/\tau_{1})=\eta^{4}(\tau+1)=-\tau_{1}^{2}re^{-2\pi i/12}
, so

\eta^{4}(\tau)=e^{-2\pi i/6}\eta^{4}(\tau+1)=e^{3i\pi/6}\tau_{1}^{2}r
.
Setting
r_{1}=r|\tau_{1}^{2}|
, we deduce that

\eta^{4}(\tau)=e^{3i\pi/6}r_{1}\dfrac{\tau_{1}}{\overline{\tau_{1}}}=e^{3i\pi/6}r_{1}\dfrac{d-\sqrt{D}}{d+\sqrt{D}}\;,

for some other positive real number
r_{1}
.

The other case
a=b
occurs for
D=a^{2}-4ac=-a(4c-a)
. Set
d=a
and

d^{\prime}=4c-a=|D|/d
. The condition
a\leq c
is equivalent to
d\leq\sqrt{|D|/3}
.
Since
c=(d+d^{\prime})/4
, the integrality of
c
together with

\gcd(a,b,c)=\gcd(a,c)=1
is equivalent to
\gcd(d+d^{\prime},4d)=4
, and
since
4d-2(d+d^{\prime})=2(d-d^{\prime})
this is equivalent to
\gcd(d+d^{\prime},2(d-d^{\prime}))=4

hence to the same arithmetic conditions, so
d\in U^{-}(D)
. Here we have

\tau=-1/2+\sqrt{D}/(2d)
, and as above, we deduce
from the product expansion that
\eta^{4}(\tau)=re^{-i\pi/6}
for some positive
real
r
, proving the theorem since the product of the positive reals involved
is equal to
|P(D)|=1
by the Chowla–Selberg formula.

Note that both cases can occur simultaneously only for
D=-3
, and one
checks that the result is still valid in that case.

\sqcup

Recall that the choice of the standard set of representatives
F(D)
is not
completely canonical: it corresponds to choosing the interior of the
standard fundamental domain of
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
together with the boundaries
the half-line
\Re(\tau)=-1/2
,
\Im(\tau)\geq\sqrt{3}/2
, and the small arc

|\tau|=1
,
-1/2\leq\Re(\tau)\leq 0
. But, if desired, we can modify
F(D)
by
choosing as second boundary, instead of a small arc, the segment

\Re(\tau)=1/2
and
1/2\leq\Im(\tau)\leq\sqrt{3}/2
. This corresponds to
changing all occurrences of
a=c
,
i.e., the quadratic forms
(a,b,a)
, into
(2a-b,2a-b,a)
. If we call

F^{\prime}(D)
this new set of representatives, and
P^{\prime}(D)
the corresponding
Chowla–Selberg quotient, by construction the set
U^{+}(D)
disappears, and we
obtain the simpler formula

P^{\prime}(D)=e^{-(i\pi/6)|U(D)|}\;.

Corollary 2.2

(1)

If
D\equiv 0\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,8)
we have
P(D)=1
.

(2)

Let
p
be prime. If
D=-p
with
p\equiv 3\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
or
D=-4p
with

p\equiv 1\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
we have
P(D)=e^{-2i\pi/12}
.

Proof.
(1) is clear since
U(D)=\emptyset
. For (2), if
D=-p
with

p\equiv 3\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
we have
U(D)=\{1\}
so
U^{+}(D)=\emptyset
and
U^{-}(D)=\{1\}

so the result follows. Similarly, if
D=-4p
with
p\equiv 1\allowbreak\ ({\rm{mod}}\,\,4)
we have

U(D)=\{2\}
and the result is also clear.

Application to Individual Values of
\eta(\tau)

Individual values of the modulus
|\eta(\tau)|
have been given explicitly
starting from the pioneering work in
[
4
]
, see also
[
1
]
.
Here we simply give some immediate consequences of the above results for

\eta(\tau)
itself.

Lemma 3.1

If
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
are two CM points in the
same quadratic field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{D_{0}})
then
\eta(\tau_{1})/\eta(\tau_{2})
is
an algebraic number.

Proof.
Since they are both in the same quadratic field, there exist
rational numbers
a\neq 0
and
b
such that
\tau_{2}=a\tau_{1}+b
, so clearing
denominators, integers
A\neq 0
,
B
, and
C\neq 0
such that

C\tau_{2}=A\tau_{1}+B
. Now by a basic theorem of CM theory
\eta(C\tau_{2})=\alpha\eta(\tau_{2})
for some algebraic number
\alpha
, and we also have

\eta(C\tau_{2})=\eta(A\tau_{1}+B)=e^{2\pi iB/24}\eta(A\tau_{1})=\beta\eta(\tau_{1})

for some algebraic number
\beta
, so

\eta(\tau_{2})=(\beta/\alpha)\eta(\tau_{1})
.

Theorem 3.2

Let
\tau
be a CM point of discriminant
D
, and
as above write
D=D_{0}f^{2}
with
D_{0}
fundamental. There exists an algebraic
number
\alpha(\tau)
such that

\Im(\tau)\eta(\tau)^{4}=\alpha(\tau)\dfrac{c(D_{0},f)\CS(D_{0})}{4\pi|D|^{1/2}}\;,

or, equivalently, if
\tau
corresponds to
(a,b,c)
:

\eta(\tau)^{4}=a\alpha(\tau)\dfrac{c(D_{0},f)\CS(D_{0})}{2\pi|D|}\;.

Proof.
By Theorems
1.3
and
2.1
, we have

\prod_{\tau\in Q(D)}\eta(\tau)^{4}=\alpha_{1}(\CS(D_{0})/\pi)^{h(D)}

for some algebraic number
\alpha_{1}
. By the above lemma, all the factors
on the left-hand side are proportional up to an algebraic number, and since

|Q(D)|=h(D)
by definition, the result follows.

\sqcap
\sqcup

The factors
\Im(\tau)
,
|D|^{1/2}

c(D_{0},f)

removed without changing the validity of the theorem since they are
all algebraic, but in view of the Chowla–Selberg formula, the above

1

From this, we can trivially deduce the following general result which is
certainly classical:

Proposition 3.3

Let
f

integral or half integral weight

modular group, and assume that the coefficients of the Fourier expansion

For any CM point
\tau

(hence its square

Proof.
Indeed, by CM theory we know that

is
an algebraic number, and by Theorem
3.2

\eta(\tau)/|\eta(\tau)|

is algebraic, so the result follows.

Some Conjectures

I have largely extended the above table (see below), and interestingly enough,
some precise conjectures have emerged, backed by a reasonable amount of data.

Conjecture 4.1

Let
\tau=(-b+\sqrt{D})/(2a)
with
D=b^{2}-4ac<0
, where
as usual
a>0
and
\gcd(a,b,c)=1
, and write
D=D_{0}f^{2}
with
D_{0}

fundamental. As above, set

\alpha(\tau)=\dfrac{\eta(\tau)^{4}}{a\cdot c(D_{0},f)\CS(D_{0})/(2\pi D)}

and let
\beta(\tau)
as in the above definition.

(1)

The numbers
a\cdot\alpha(\tau)
and
a/\alpha(\tau)
are algebraic

integers
.

(2)

The norm of
\alpha(\tau)
as an algebraic number (i.e., the product of
its algebraic conjugates) is equal to
\pm 1
(since it is usually not an
algebraic integer, this of course does not mean that it is a unit),
and in fact perhaps always equal to
1
.

(3)

Write
h(D)=2^{v}m
with
m
odd. The minimal polynomial of

a\cdot\beta(\tau)
is of the form
B(x^{jm})
with
j=1
if
v=0
,
and
j\mid 3\cdot 2^{v-1}
if
v\geq 1
, and where the degree of
B
is equal
to
h(D)
if
a=1
and to
h(D)
or
2h(D)
if
a>1
.

(4)

The number
\alpha(\tau)^{\lcm(6,h(D))}
belongs to the ring class field
of the quadratic order of discriminant
D
, of degree
2h(D)
, and
when
b=0
or
a=b
it even belongs to its subfield of degree
h(D)

fixed by the automorphism
\sqrt{D}\mapsto-\sqrt{D}
.

These conjectures are perhaps not difficult to prove using known results
on CM theory.

Remarks.

(1)

(1) and (2) mean that if
\alpha(\tau)
is of degree
2d
,
up to a multiplicative constant its characteristic polynomial with integer
coefficients is of the form

a^{d}x^{2d}+c_{2d-1}a^{d-1}x^{2d-1}+\cdots+c_{d}x^{d}+c_{d-1}ax^{d-1}+\cdots+c_{1}a^{d-1}x+a^{d}\;.

If it has odd degree
2d-1
, it has a similar shape, but in all the
examples I have tested the polynomial is monic with constant term
-1

(this may be true in general, but I do not have enough evidence to conjecture
it).

(2)

The exponent
\lcm(6,h(D))
in (4) can be explained for at least
two reasons. First, it may be reasonable to study

\alpha(\tau)^{6}
instead of
\alpha(\tau)
, since this would correspond to

\Delta(\tau)
instead of
\eta^{4}(\tau)
. Second, one could hope to
get rid of this
6
by considering
\beta(\tau)
instead of
\alpha(\tau)
,
but for instance for
D=-15
, we really need an exponent divisible by

3
in
\beta(\tau)
or
\alpha(\tau)
to land in the required ring class field.

(3)

These conjectures imply that the degree of
\alpha(\tau)
or of
\beta(\tau)

is roughly proportional to
h(D)^{2}
, and in particular grows quite
fast as
|D|\to\infty
. This is in contrast with the CM values of modular

functions
such as
j(\tau)
, whose degrees are proportional to

h(D)
, not to its square.

These conjectures suggest the following algorithm to compute explicitly

\alpha(\tau)
, hence to deduce an explicit expression for CM values of

\eta(\tau)
:

(1)

Using the standard
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
transformations
\tau\mapsto\tau+1

and
\tau\mapsto-1/\tau
, we may assume that
\tau
