Introduction and main results

We consider homogenization of
boundary value problems for
semilinear second-order elliptic systems of the type

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{x_{i}}\Big(a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(\varepsilon,x)\partial_{x_{j}}u^{\beta}(x)+f_{i}^{\alpha}(x,u(x))\Big)=0\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u^{\alpha}(x)=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right\}\alpha=1,\ldots,n.

(1.1)

Here
\varepsilon>0
is the small homogenization parameter, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the system dimension.
In (
1.1
) and in what follows repeated indices are to be summed over

\alpha,\beta,\ldots=1,\ldots,n
and

i,j,\ldots=1,\ldots,N
, where
N\in\mathbb{N}
is the space dimension.
We suppose that

\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

(1.2)

\displaystyle a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon,\cdot)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),\;\operatorname*{ess\phantom{|}\!sup}\left\{\left|a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon,x)\right|:\,\varepsilon>0,\,x\in\Omega\right\}<\infty,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{ess\phantom{|}\!inf}\left\{a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(\varepsilon,x)v^{\alpha}_{i}v^{\beta}_{j}:\;\varepsilon>0,\,x\in\Omega,\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{nN},\;v^{\alpha}_{i}v^{\alpha}_{i}=1\right\}>0,

(1.4)

\displaystyle u\in C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})\mapsto f_{i}^{\alpha}(\cdot,u)\in L^{p_{0}}(\Omega)\mbox{ are $C^{1}$-smooth with certain }p_{0}>N.

(1.5)

More in detail formulated, assumption (
1.5
) means that there exists
p_{0}>N
such that for all
u\in C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
the functions

f_{i}^{\alpha}(\cdot,u(\cdot))
belong to
L^{p_{0}}(\Omega)
, and that the maps

u\mapsto f_{i}^{\alpha}(\cdot,u(\cdot))

are
C^{1}
-smooth from the function space
C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
into the function space
L^{p_{0}}(\Omega)
.
This is satisfied if, for example,
the functions
f_{i}^{\alpha}
are products of the type

f_{i}^{\alpha}(x,u)=g_{i}^{\alpha}(x)h_{i}^{\alpha}(u)

(no summation over
\alpha
or
i
)
with
g_{i}^{\alpha}\in L^{p_{0}}(\Omega)
and

h_{i}^{\alpha}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

or if
f_{i}^{\alpha}
are finite sums of such products. More general sufficient conditions for (
1.5
) are stated below in Appendix 2 of the present paper.

Further, we suppose that the family of diffusion tensor functions
[a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(\varepsilon,\cdot)]
H-converges for
\varepsilon\to 0
to a tensor function
[\hat{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}]

with
\hat{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, which means the following:

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{For any $\phi\in W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})$
and for the corresponding $u_{\varepsilon},\hat{u}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})$,}\\
\mbox{defined by }\partial_{x_{i}}(a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(\varepsilon,\cdot)\partial_{x_{j}}u_{\varepsilon}^{\beta})=\partial_{x_{i}}(\hat{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}\partial_{x_{j}}\hat{u}^{\beta})=\phi^{\alpha},\mbox{ one has for $\varepsilon\to 0$ that}\\
\mbox{$u^{\alpha}_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\hat{u}^{\alpha}$
weakly in $W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)$
and $a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(\varepsilon,\cdot)\partial_{x_{j}}u_{\varepsilon}^{\beta}\rightharpoonup\hat{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}\partial_{x_{j}}\hat{u}^{\beta}$
weakly in $L^{2}(\Omega)$.}\end{array}\right\}

(1.6)

Finally, we suppose that

N=2

(1.7)

or

a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon,x)=0\mbox{ for all }\alpha>\beta\mbox{ and }\varepsilon>0\mbox{ and for almost all }x\in\Omega.

(1.8)

A vector function
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is called weak solution to the boundary value problem (
1.1
) if it satisfies the variational equation

\int_{\Omega}\Big(a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(\varepsilon,x)\partial_{x_{j}}u^{\beta}(x)+f_{i}^{\alpha}(x,u(x))\Big)\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi^{\alpha}(x)dx=0\mbox{ for all }\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}),

and similarly for the homogenized boundary value problem

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{x_{i}}\Big(\hat{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(x)\partial_{x_{j}}u^{\beta}(x)+f_{i}^{\alpha}(x,u(x))\Big)=0\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u^{\alpha}(x)=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right\}\alpha=1,\ldots,n

(1.9)

and for its linearization (
1.10
).
Also, we denote by

\|u\|_{\infty}:=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\operatorname*{ess\phantom{|}\!sup}_{x\in\Omega}|u^{\alpha}(x)|

the norm in the Lebesgue space
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Our main result is the following

Theorem 1.1

Suppose (
1.2
)-(
1.6
).
Further, suppose that (
1.7
) or that (
1.8
) is satisfied.
Finally, suppose that there is given a weak solution

u=u_{0}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})

to (
1.9
) such that
the linearized homogenized boundary value problem

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{x_{i}}\Big(\hat{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(x)\partial_{x_{j}}u^{\beta}(x)+\partial_{u^{\beta}}f_{i}^{\alpha}(x,u_{0}(x))u^{\beta}(x)\Big)=0\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u^{\alpha}(x)=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right\}\alpha=1,\ldots,n

(1.10)

does not have nontrivial weak solutions
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Then there exist
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
\delta>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
there exists exactly one weak solution

u=u_{\varepsilon}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
to (
1.1
) with
\|u-u_{0}\|_{\infty}<\delta
.
Moreover,

\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\|_{\infty}\to 0
for
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The idea of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is rather simple: We use, similarly to the proof of the classical implicit function theorem,
the sequence
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which
is defined by

\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{x_{i}}\Big(a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(\varepsilon,x)\partial_{x_{j}}u_{l+1}^{\beta}(x)+\partial_{u^{\beta}}f_{i}^{\alpha}(x,u_{0}(x))\left(u_{l+1}^{\beta}(x)-u_{l}^{\beta}(x)\right)+f_{i}^{\alpha}(x,u_{l}(x))\Big)=0\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u^{\alpha}_{l+1}(x)=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}

for
\alpha=1,\ldots,n
.
But it is not simple to answer the question in which function space (with respect to which norm) this sequence converges.
We take Sobolev spaces
W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
p>2
, but
p\approx 2
, in the case (
1.7
) and Sobolev-Morrey spaces
W^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\lambda>N-2
, but
\lambda\approx N-2
, in the case (
1.8
).
Also, it is not simple to answer the question what should be an appropriate starting element
u_{1}
of the sequence. It turns out that
u_{0}
is not an appropriate
starting element.
We take an
\varepsilon
-depending
u_{1}
defined by

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{x_{i}}\Big(a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(\varepsilon,x)\partial_{x_{j}}u_{1}^{\beta}(x)+f_{i}^{\alpha}(x,u_{0}(x))\Big)=0\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u^{\alpha}(x)=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right\}\alpha=1,\ldots,n.

(1.11)

Remark that neither the Sobolev spaces nor the Sobolev-Morrey spaces nor this starting element appear in the formulation of Theorem
1.1
, they are hidden only.

This approach is well-known for singularly perturbed nonlinear ODEs and elliptic and parabolic PDEs, see
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
34
,
35
,
38
,
42
,
47
,
50
]
. It is based on the generalized implicit function theorem
of R.J. Magnus
[
34
, Theorem 1.2]
and on several of its modifications. Hence, these
generalized implicit function theorems permit a common approach to nonlinear singular perturbation and homogenization.

Roughly speaking, the reason why the classical implicit function theorem cannot be applied
to (
1.1
) is the following:
The linearized operators converge for
\varepsilon\to 0

in a very weak sense only
(in the sense of H-convergence),
not with respect to a uniform operator norm.
Remark that in the classical implicit function theorem one cannot omit, in general, the assumption, that the linearized operators converge for
\varepsilon\to 0
with respect to a uniform operator norm (cf.
[
29
, Section 3.6]
).

Remark 1.2

The concept of H-convergence was introduced by F. Murat and L. Tartar (see, e.g.
[
54
]
or
[
17
, Section 13]
).
Assumption (
1.6
) is satisfied, for example, for periodic homogenization, i.e.

a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(\varepsilon,x)=\tilde{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(x/\varepsilon)\mbox{ with $\mathbb{Z}^{N}$-periodic $\tilde{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$}

or for periodic homogenization with localized defects, i.e.

a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(\varepsilon,x)=\tilde{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(x/\varepsilon)+b^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(x/\varepsilon)

with

\mathbb{Z}^{N}
-periodic
\tilde{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and

b^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{r^{N}}\int_{\|y-x\|<r}|b^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(y)|dy=0\mbox{ for almost all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

see
[
4
,
5
]
. In these cases the homogenized diffusion tensor is
x
-independent and can be represented
rather explicit as

\hat{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}=\int_{(0,1)^{N}}\left(\tilde{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(y)+\tilde{a}^{\alpha\gamma}_{ik}(y)\partial_{y_{k}}v^{\gamma\beta}_{j}(y)\right)dy,

where the
nN
correctors
v^{\beta}_{j}=(v^{1\beta}_{j},\ldots,v^{n\beta}_{j})\in W^{1,2}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\beta=1,\ldots,n
,
j=1,\ldots,N
,
are are the weak solutions to
the
nN
cell problems

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{y_{i}}\left(\tilde{a}^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(y)+\tilde{a}^{\alpha\gamma}_{ik}(y)\partial_{y_{k}}v^{\gamma\beta}_{j}(y)\right)=0\mbox{ for }y\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\\
v^{\alpha\beta}_{j}(\cdot+z)=v^{\alpha\beta}_{j}\mbox{ for }z\in\mathbb{Z}^{N},\;\displaystyle\int_{(0,1)^{N}}v^{\alpha\beta}_{j}(y)dy=0\end{array}\right\}\alpha=1,\ldots,n.

In the case of periodic homogenization with localized defects and with one space dimension, i.e.
N=1
, correctors are not needed, in this case the representation is

[\hat{a}^{\alpha\beta}]=\left(\int_{0}^{1}[\tilde{a}^{\alpha\beta}(y)]^{-1}dy\right)^{-1}.

For this particular case Theorem
1.1
has been proved in
[
49
]
.

For H-convergence of families of diffusion tensor functions describing laminated or perforated materials see, e.g.
[
54
, Chapters 12, 15 and 16]
.

Remark 1.10

There exists an astonishing analogy between approaches to singularly perturbed problems and to homogenization problems (cf.
[
39
,
40
]
). The analogy consists in
the use of so-called approximate solutions, i.e. of families (with family parameter
\varepsilon>0
)
of functions which satisfy the problem (i.e. differential equations, boundary conditions etc.) approximately for small singular perturbation parameter
\varepsilon
or for small homogenization parameter
\varepsilon
, respectively.
Usually those families of approximate solutions are ”constructed” by means of ansatzes, streched variables, correctors, formal asymptotic expansions, smoothing operators, cut-off functions etc., and they are choosen according of the requirements of the problem.
Often it is not clear at the very beginning, if for
\varepsilon\to 0
those approximate solutions approximate (and in which sense) exact solutions of the given problem or not.

In the present paper we use the family of approximate solutions
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
, which is defined in (
3.13
) or, what is the same, by
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}:=u_{1}
with
u_{1}
from
(
1.11
).
The advantage of this choice is that
no additional regularity assumptions for
u_{0}
and, hence, no smoothing operators are needed. This is new and allows to work in Morrey spaces (where smooth functions are not dense)
and, this way, to prove Theorem
1.1
in the case (
1.8
).

The disadvantage of these approximate solutions
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
is that they
are defined as the solutions of the linear boundary value problems (
1.11
)
and, hence, to determine
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
numerically is not much simpler than to determine the exact solutions
u_{\varepsilon}
to (
1.1
) numerically. Another disadvantage of
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
is that they do not help to estimate the rate of convergence of
\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\|_{\infty}\to 0
for
\varepsilon\to 0
, because Lemma
3.2
below is proved by assuming the contrary.

Other families of approximate solutions
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
, which are constructed by means of two-scale asymptotic expansions, the function
u_{0}
, correctors, cut-off functions and smoothing operators, are used, e.g. in
[
53
, Chapters 3.1 and 3.2]
or
[
40
,
48
]
, and under additional smoothness assumptions they allow to estimate the rate of convergence
\|u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\to 0
for
\varepsilon\to 0
.

For another analogy of techniques used in singular perturbation theory and in homogenization theory see Lemma
3.1
below.

Remark 1.11

What concerns existence and local uniqueness
for periodic homogenization
of semilinear elliptic PDEs
(without assumption of global uniqueness), besides
[

,

]

we know only the result

[

]
for scalar semilinear elliptic PDEs of the type

2
Abstract setting of the boundary value problems (

)
and (

As usual, the norm in the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

is denoted by

\|u\|_{1,2}:=\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\left(|u^{\alpha}(x)|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\partial_{x_{i}}u^{\alpha}(x)|^{2}\right)dx\right)^{1/2}.

The subspace
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

of
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

is the closure with respect to this norm
of the set
of all
C^{\infty}
-maps
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with compact support,
and
W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):=W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
is the dual space to

W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with dual space norm

\|\phi\|_{-1,2}:=\sup\left\{\langle\phi,\varphi\rangle_{1,2}:\;\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}),\|\varphi\|_{1,2}\leq 1\right\},

where

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1,2}:W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}

is the dual pairing.

Let us introduce linear bounded operators
\hat{A},A_{\varepsilon}:W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

(for
\varepsilon>0
)
and
D:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{nN})\to W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

by

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\hat{A}u,\varphi\rangle_{1,2}&:=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\hat{a}_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}(x)\partial_{x_{j}}u^{\beta}(x)\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi^{\alpha}(x)dx,\\
\left\langle A_{\varepsilon}u,\varphi\right\rangle_{1,2}&:=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon,x)\partial_{x_{j}}u^{\beta}(x)\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi^{\alpha}(x)dx,\\
\left\langle Dg,\varphi\right\rangle_{1,2}&:=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}g_{i}^{\alpha}(x)\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi^{\alpha}(x)dx,\end{array}\right\}\mbox{ for all }\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(2.1)

Because of (
1.4
) and (
1.12
) and the Lax-Milgram lemma the operators
\hat{A}
and
A_{\varepsilon}
are bijective from
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto

W^{-1,2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\|A_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\|_{{\cal L}(W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n});W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}))}<\infty.

(2.2)

Further, we consider the superposition (or Nemycki) operator

F:C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{p_{0}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{nN})
which is defined by

\big(F(u)\big)(x):=[f_{i}^{\alpha}(x,u(x))]\mbox{ for almost all }x\in\Omega.

(2.3)

It is
C^{1}
-smooth because of assumption (
1.5
).

By definition, a function
u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a weak solution to the boundary value problem (
1.1
) if and only if

A_{\varepsilon}u+DF(u)=0,

(2.4)

and

is a weak solution to the homogenized boundary value problem (

) if and only if
\hat{A}u+DF(u)=0
, in particular

\hat{A}u_{0}+DF(u_{0})=0.

(2.5)

Moreover, by assumption of Theorem
1.1
we have that

\left\{u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n}):\;(\hat{A}+DF^{\prime}(u_{0}))u=0\right\}=\{0\}.

(2.6)

The following theorem, which
follows from the H-convergence assumption (

),
is well-known in homogenization theory for linear elliptic equations and systems
(see, e.g.

[

Theorem 2.1

Let be given
u\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\phi\in W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and sequences
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\ldots>0
and

such that

\varepsilon_{l}\to 0
for

and

\displaystyle u_{l}\rightharpoonup u\mbox{ for }l\to\infty\mbox{ weakly in }W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(2.7)

\displaystyle\|A_{\varepsilon_{l}}u_{l}-\phi\|_{-1,2}\to 0\mbox{ for }l\to\infty.

(2.8)

Then
\hat{A}u=\phi
.

Proof
Define
\bar{u}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and, for
l=1,2,\ldots
,
\bar{u}_{l}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
by

\hat{A}\bar{u}=A_{\varepsilon_{l}}\bar{u}_{l}=\phi.

Because of assumption (
1.6
) it follows that

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{\alpha}_{l}\rightharpoonup\bar{u}^{\alpha}\mbox{ for }l\to\infty\mbox{ weakly in }W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega),

(2.9)

\displaystyle a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon_{l},\cdot)\partial_{x_{j}}\bar{u}^{\beta}_{l}\rightharpoonup\hat{a}_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{x_{j}}\bar{u}^{\beta}\mbox{ for }l\to\infty\mbox{ weakly in }L^{2}(\Omega).

(2.10)

Moreover, because of (
2.2
) and (
2.8
) we have that
\|u_{l}-\bar{u}_{l}\|_{1,2}=\|u_{l}-A_{\varepsilon_{l}}^{-1}\phi\|_{1,2}\to 0
.
Hence, (

) yields that

for

weakly in

. Therefore (

, and from (

for

In order to introduce Morrey spaces and Sobolev-Morey spaces (cf., e.g.
[

,

,

,

) we denote for

and

\Omega_{x,r}:=\{\xi\in\Omega:\;\|\xi-x\|<r\},\mbox{ where $\|\cdot\|$ is the Euclidean norm in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Take
\lambda\in[0,N)
.
The Morrey space
L^{2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the subspace of the Lebesgue
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with norm

\|u\|_{2,\lambda}:=\sup_{x\in\Omega,r\in(0,1]}r^{-\lambda/2}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega_{x,r}}|u^{\alpha}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\right)^{1/2}

which is defined by

L^{2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):\|u\|_{2,\lambda}<\infty\}
.
The Morrey space
L^{2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

is continuously embedded into the Lebesgue space

L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and

L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\mbox{ is continuously embedded into }L^{2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\mbox{ for }p\geq 2\mbox{ and }\lambda=N(1-2/p).

(5.1)

The Sobolev-Morrey space
W^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the subspace of
the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})

with norm

\|u\|_{1,2,\lambda}:=\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\left(\int_{\Omega}|u^{\alpha}(x)|^{2}dx+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{x_{i}}u^{\alpha}\|^{2}_{2,\lambda}\right)\right)^{1/2},

which is defined by

W^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{u\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):\;\|u\|_{1,2,\lambda}<\infty\}
.
It is well-known that

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}W^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\mbox{ is compactly embedded into }C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})\\
\displaystyle\mbox{for }N-2<\lambda<N\mbox{ and }0\leq\alpha<1-\frac{N-\lambda}{2}.\end{array}\right\}

(5.2)

Further, we denote
W_{0}^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):=W^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

And finally, the Sobolev-Morrey space
W^{-1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
(sometimes called

is the subspace of
W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})=W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}

with norm

\|\phi\|_{-1,2,\lambda}:=\sup\left\{r^{-\lambda/2}\langle\phi,\varphi\rangle_{1,2}:\;x\in\Omega,r\in(0,1],\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}),\|\varphi\|_{1,2}\leq 1,\mbox{supp}\,\varphi\subset\Omega_{x,r}\right\},

which is defined by

W^{-1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{\phi\in W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):\;\|\phi\|_{-1,2,\lambda}<\infty\}
, and it is continuously embedded into
W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
It is well-known (cf. e.g.
[

)
that

D\mbox{ is bounded from $L^{2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{nN})$ into $W^{-1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})$ for all $\lambda\in[0,N)$.}

(5.3)

6
Appendix 1: About Meyers and Morrey estimates for solutions to linear elliptic systems

Let
\Omega
be
a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
For
r\in(0,1)
we denote by
{\cal M}_{r}
the set of all diffusion tensor functions

[a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}]\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}N^{2}})
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname*{ess\phantom{|}\!inf}\left\{a^{\alpha\beta}_{ij}(x)v^{\alpha}_{i}v^{\beta}_{j}:\;x\in\Omega,\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{nN},\;v^{\alpha}_{i}v^{\alpha}_{i}=1\right\}>r,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{ess\phantom{|}\!sup}\left\{\left|a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}(\varepsilon,x)\right|:\,x\in\Omega,\,\alpha,\beta=1,\ldots,n,\,i,j=1,\ldots,N\right\}<1/r,

and for
a=[a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}]\in{\cal M}_{r}
we denote
A_{a}\in{\cal L}(W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n});W^{-1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}))
by

\langle A_{a}u,\varphi\rangle_{1,2}:=\int_{\Omega}a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}(x)\partial_{x_{j}}u^{\beta}(x)\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi^{\alpha}(x)dx\mbox{ for all }u,\varphi\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

The following result is about maximal Sobolev regularity for linear elliptic systems with non-smooth data in the version of
K. Gröger, see
[
25
, Theorems 1 and 2 and Remark 14]
:

Theorem 6.1

For all
r\in(0,1)
there exists
p_{r}>2
such that for all
a\in{\cal M}_{r}
and all
p\in[2,p_{r}]
the linear operator
A_{a}

is an isomorphism from

W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto
W^{-1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover,

\sup_{a\in{\cal M}_{r}}\|A_{a}^{-1}\|_{{\cal L}(W^{-1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n});W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}))}<\infty\mbox{ for all }p\in[2,p_{r}].

Remark 6.2

W^{1,p}
-estimates of solutions to elliptic boundary value problems
often are called Meyers type estimates
because of the initiating paper
[
36
]

of N.G. Meyers, see also
[
20
,
37
]
. For the case of smooth boundaries
\partial\Omega
see

[
3
, Theorem 4.1]
. See also
[
12
]
for
\Omega
being a cube and for continuous diffusion coefficients as well as for certain transmission problems and applications to linear elliptic periodic homogenization.
Meyers type estimates are applied to homogenization problems for elliptic boundary value problems with localized defects also in
[
5
]
.

For
r\in(0,1)
denote by

{\cal M}^{t}_{r}(\Omega)
the set of all triangular elements of

{\cal M}_{r}(\Omega)
, i.e.

{\cal M}^{t}_{r}(\Omega):=\left\{[a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}]\in{\cal M}_{r}(\Omega):\;a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}=0\mbox{ for }\alpha>\beta\right\}.

In
[
23
, Theorem 4.1]
and

[
24
, Lemma 6.2 and Theorem 6.3]

there is proved the following
maximal Sobolev-Morrey regularity result for almost triangular elliptic systems with non-smooth data,
which is similar to Theorem
6.1
:

Theorem 6.3

For all
r\in(0,1)
there exists
\lambda_{r}>N-2
such that for all
a=[a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}]\in{\cal M}^{t}_{r}

and for all
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{r}]
the linear operator
A_{a}

is an isomorphism from

W_{0}^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto
W^{-1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Moreover,

\sup_{a\in{\cal M}^{t}_{r}(\Omega)}\|A_{a}^{-1}\|_{{\cal L}(W^{-1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n});W_{0}^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}))}<\infty\mbox{ for all }\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{r}].

Remark 6.4

If
a=\left[a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\right]\in{\cal M}^{t}_{r}
, then

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{for all $\alpha=1,\ldots,n$ the $N\times N$ matrices $\left[a_{ij}^{\alpha\alpha}(x)\right]$ (no summation over $\alpha$)}\\
\mbox{are positive definite uniformly with respect to $x\in\Omega$,}\end{array}\right\}

(6.1)

i.e. condition (
6.1
) is weaker than condition
a\in{\cal M}^{t}_{r}
(with certain

).
But even this weaker condition implies that

A_{a}
is an isomorphisms from

onto
W^{-1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with appropriate
\lambda>N-2
.
More exactly, for all
a\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{n^{2}N^{2}}
with
a_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}=0
for

and with (

are isomorphisms from

W_{0}^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto
W^{-1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with appropriate

.

Remark 6.5

The set of isomorphisms between two Banach spaces is open with respect to the uniform operator norm.
Therefore for all
a_{0}\in{\cal M}^{t}_{r}

and for all
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{r}]
there exists
\varepsilon_{a_{0},\lambda}>0
such that for all

with

the linear operator

is an isomorphism from

W_{0}^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto
W^{-1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
In this sense one can say that the linear operator
A_{a}
is
an isomorphism from

W_{0}^{1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto
W^{-1,2,\lambda}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
if the diffusion tensor function

is close to be triangular.
It seems to be an open question if

depends on

via

only or not.

Consider the boundary value problem (

) with

)-(

), (

7
Appendix 2: About
C^{1}
-smooth superposition operators

C(\overline{\Omega})\to L^{p}(\Omega)

Let
\Omega
be
a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
and let be given a function
f:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

such that

f(x,\cdot)
is differentiable for almost all
x\in\Omega
, and

f(\cdot,u)
is measurable for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
.

(7.1)

Further, suppose that there exists
p\geq 2
such that for all compact

K\subset\mathbb{R}
there exists
g_{K}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
such that

|f(x,u)|+|\partial_{u}f(x,u)|\leq g_{K}(x)\mbox{ for almost all $x\in\Omega$
and all $u\in K$},

(7.2)

and

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\mbox{for all $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $\delta_{\varepsilon,K}>0$ such that}\\
\mbox{$|f(x,u)-f(x,v)|+|\partial_{u}f(x,u)-\partial_{u}f(x,u)|\leq\varepsilon g_{K}(x)$}\\
\mbox{for almost all $x\in\Omega$
and all $u,v\in K$
with $|u-v|\leq\delta_{\varepsilon,K}$.}\end{array}\right\}

(7.3)

Lemma 7.1

Suppose (
7.1
)–(
7.3
). Then
there exists
F\in C^{1}(C(\overline{\Omega});L^{p}(\Omega))
such that

(F(u))(x)=f(x,u(x))\mbox{ for almost all }x\in\Omega\mbox{ and all }u\in C(\overline{\Omega}).

(7.4)

Proof
Assumption (
7.1
)
yields that
f
is a Caratheodory function, i.e.
f(x,\cdot)
is continuous for almost all
x\in\Omega
, and
f(\cdot,x)
is measurable for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore
f(\cdot,u(\cdot))
is measurable for all measurable
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

(cf., e.g.
[
1
, Section 1.2]
).

Take
u\in C(\overline{\Omega})
. Then there exists a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
u(x)\in K
for almost all
x\in\Omega
. Therefore assumption (
7.2
) yields that

\int_{\Omega}|f(x,u(x))|^{p}dx\leq\int_{\Omega}|g_{K}(x)|^{p}dx<\infty.

Hence, by (
7.4
) is defined a map

F:C(\overline{\Omega})\to L^{p}(\Omega)
.

Similarly one shows that

\partial_{u}f(\cdot,u(\cdot))v(\cdot)\in L^{p}(\Omega)

for all
u,v\in C(\overline{\Omega})
. In other words: For any
u\in C(\overline{\Omega}
there is defined a linear operator
L(u):C(\overline{\Omega})\to L^{p}(\Omega)
by

(L(u)v)(x):=\partial_{u}f(x,u(x))v(x)\mbox{ for almost all }x\in\Omega\mbox{ and all }v\in C(\overline{\Omega}).

This linear operator is bounded because assumption (
7.2
) yields that

\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{u}f(x,u(x))v(x)|^{p}dx\leq\|v\|_{\infty}^{p}\int_{\Omega}|g_{K}(x)|^{p}dx

if the compakt
K\subset\mathbb{R}
is taken such that
u(x)\in K
for almost all
x\in\Omega
.

Now, let us show that the map
u\in C(\overline{\Omega})\mapsto L(u)\in{\cal L}(C(\overline{\Omega});L^{p}(\Omega))
is continuous. Take
u\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and a compakt
K\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
u(x)+v(x)\in K
for almost all
x\in\Omega
and all
v\in C(\overline{\Omega})
with
\|v\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.
Then assumption (
7.3
) imlies that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta_{\varepsilon,K}>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|[(L(u+v)-L(u))w](x)\right|^{p}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|\Big(\partial_{u}f(x,u(x)+v(x))-\partial_{u}f(x,u(x))\Big)w(x)\right|^{p}dx\leq\varepsilon^{p}\|w\|_{\infty}^{p}

for all
u,v,w\in C(\overline{\Omega})
with
\|v\|\leq\delta_{\varepsilon,K}
.

And finally, let us show that
F
is differentiable from
C(\overline{\Omega})
into
L^{p}(\Omega)
and that

F^{\prime}(u)=L(u)
for all

u\in C(\overline{\Omega})
.
Again, take
u\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and a compakt
K\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
u(x)+v(x)\in K
for almost all
x\in\Omega
and all
v\in C(\overline{\Omega})
with
\|v\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.
Then assumption (
7.3
) imlies that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta_{\varepsilon,K}>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|[F(u+v)-F(u)-L(u)v](x)\right|^{p}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|\Big(f(x,u(x)+v(x))-f(x,u(x))-\partial_{u}f(x,u(x))\Big)v(x)\right|^{p}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\partial_{u}f(x,u(x)+sv(x))-\partial_{u}f(x,u(x))\Big)v(x)ds\right|^{p}dx

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{p}\int_{\Omega}|g_{K}(x)|^{p}dx\,\|v\|_{\infty}^{p},

if
\|v\|_{\infty}\leq\delta_{\varepsilon,K}
.
