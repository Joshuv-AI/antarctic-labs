Controlled assembly of two-dimensional porphyrin heterostructures toward directed energy transfer and charge separation
Authors: Yifei Lei, Jiangshan Zhang, Yifei Han, Shixing Lei, Song-Hai Xie, Shu Wang, Zhiyuan Huang, Dan-Wei Zhang, Zhan-Ting Li, Li-Zhu Wu, Chen-Ho Tung, Jia Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe exceptional efficiency of natural light-harvesting systems arises from their precisely organized supramolecular architectures. Reproducing such structural control in synthetic aqueous assemblies, particularly over size and dimensionality, remains a formidable challenge. Here, we report a general seeded-growth strategy that enables precise, hierarchical assembly of two-dimensional (2D) porphyrin heterostructures in water. Integrating π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions, the porphyrin amphiphiles follow a metastable assembly pathway that yields kinetically controlled nanosheets or heterostructures. This approach provides unprecedented control over the nanostructure area across two orders of magnitude, establishing a versatile platform for complex functional architectures. By integrating a cobalt–porphyrin acceptor via block co-assembly, we construct 2D donor–acceptor heterostructures that achieve a directed energy funneling. Ultrafast spectroscopic analysis combined with global fitting reveals the mechanism: the controlled 2D heterostructures promote exciton migration at rates 2.5-fold greater than in homostructures and drive the formation of a fully charge-separated state on a sub-nanosecond timescale, with dynamics that scale with platelet dimensions. This work establishes a synthetic route to biomimetic 2D heterostructures and elucidates the structural determinants of directed exciton and charge flow, offering key design principles for advanced biomimetic systems.Subject terms: Self-assembly, Light harvesting, Self-assembly

Introduction

Precisely organized supramolecular architectures serve as fundamental modules for natural light-harvesting systems. Pigments, proteins, and lipids form extraordinary structures that provide a specialized microenvironment for energy transfer and charge separation1–3. In green sulfur bacteria, energy transfer occurs at a nearly 100% efficiency through the primary antenna, known as the chlorosome, owing to the formation of pigment arrays between thousands of self-assembled bacteriochlorophylls (BChl) without the involvement of scaffold proteins4–6. These BChl aggregates inherently adopt a two-dimensional (2D) structural motif, which presents a directed exciton migration within the chlorosome (Fig. 1a)7. Hierarchical antennas serve as heterostructures to further stimulate the charge separation, ultimately driving photosynthesis8–10.

Inspired by biological systems, constructing supramolecular systems via functional heterostructures has emerged as a promising strategy to achieve directed energy flow and enhanced photochemical reactivity11–13. Pioneering efforts to mimic the chlorosome have included the chemical modification of natural chlorophylls14,15, co-assembly of chromophores with biomolecules16–18, and the aggregation of synthetic amphiphiles19–22, yielding nanostructures with tunable size and dimensionality. Nevertheless, integrating precisely controlled long-range order, directed energy transfer, and functional charge separation within a single aqueous assembly system remains a formidable challenge12,23–25. The size‑controlled assembly of small molecules into 2D nanostructures remains rare in aqueous media, largely owing to the complexity of the assembly pathways25. These pathways are governed by a delicate balance of competing noncovalent interactions, which can be tuned by the molecular design of conjugated amphiphiles26. A fundamental obstacle is that small amphiphiles with ill‑matched interactions in water tend to form disordered, short‑range aggregates, resulting in significant energy dissipation27,28. Seeded growth, which leverages cooperative non-covalent interactions to guide hierarchical assembly, offers a promising pathway to overcome this limitation and achieve definitive structural control29–31.

Herein, we report a chlorosome-inspired, 2D controlled supramolecular heterostructure system via a general seeded-growth strategy. We designed a family of cationic amphiphilic zinc porphyrins, integrating π–π stacking motifs, hydrogen-bonding units, and variable numbers of hydrophobic alkyl chains (Fig. 1b), which are commonly employed in other chromophore assembly systems32–34. We establish that three alkyl chains are optimal for driving the formation of extended 2D platelets, as they provide a multivalent interaction network that shapes a favorable free-energy landscape for assembly. The resulting porphyrin arrays were structurally resolved by cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) and solution-phase X-ray scattering. Critically, by implementing a seeded-growth approach, we achieved precise and tunable control over the size and dispersity of these 2D platelets (Fig. 1c). Exploiting the complementary photophysics of zinc and cobalt porphyrins, we constructed 2D block co-assembly heterostructures that exhibit directed energy funneling, as evidenced by pronounced fluorescence quenching. The excited-state dynamics of these architectures were quantitatively unraveled through global fitting analysis of transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS), revealing accelerated kinetics and charge separation. This work thereby establishes a versatile biomimetic platform for systematically investigating and engineering directed energy transfer and charge separation in artificial light-harvesting systems (Supplementary Table 1).

Results

Preparation of the 2D zinc-porphyrin platelets

We designed a series of porphyrin-based amphiphiles incorporating three functional motifs: a hydrophilic zinc-porphyrin headgroup bearing methylpyridine cations, an amide connector, and hydrophobic alkyl tails (Fig. 1b). Zinc porphyrins were selected as the photosensitizing core due to their excellent photostability and cost-effectiveness11,15. To direct the assembly toward 2D structures, we tuned the number and positions of hydrophobic chains to target a packing parameter (P value, defined as the hydrophobic-to-hydrophilic volume ratio) close to one35–37. Following the route outlined in Supplementary Fig. 1, the porphyrin-based amphiphile of TPPZn, and control molecules of o-DPPZn, m-DPPZn, and SPPZn bearing one to three alkyl chains were successfully synthesized. Their structures were confirmed by NMR spectroscopy, ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption, and fluorescence emission spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 2). The assemblies were prepared in a methanol–water mixture and characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The morphology of TPPZn assemblies was found to depend critically on the volume ratio of MeOH to H2O. At MeOH contents >70%, no well-defined nanostructures were observed. Reducing the MeOH fraction to 33–66% led to the formation of 2D nanosheets, while at <30%, 1D nanowires became dominant (Supplementary Fig. 3). Using the optimized solvent (MeOH/H2O, v/v = 1:1), TPPZn assemblies adopt a characteristic 2D ribbon-like morphology (Supplementary Fig. 3d). The 2D nanostructures showed strong light scattering, which implied the large nanostructures as observed (Supplementary Fig. 5a). In contrast, m-DPPZn and o-DPPZn showed discrete nanoaggregates with dimensions less than 10 nm as observed by scattering and TEM experiments (Supplementary Figs. 4a, b and 5b). SPPZn formed spherical micelles, a morphology in line with our earlier findings (Supplementary Fig. 4c,d)22.

The self-nucleated 2D assembly of TPPZn was first characterized. To complete the self-nucleation process, a 0.1 mM solution was annealed by heating to 60 °C for 30 min, followed by slow cooling and equilibration at ambient temperature for over 24 h. The resulting large, self-nucleated nanostructures (denoted SN) adopted a parallelogram shape with an average apex angle of 67° (Fig. 2b). The 2D assemblies showed an average thickness of 6.8 nm under atomic force microscopy (AFM) images (Fig. 2c). In cryo-TEM images, curled and folded nanosheets can be sharply found in solution (Supplementary Fig. 6)38. A group of stripes parallel to the long side of 2D platelets was observed. The stripe spacing was measured at 2.4 nm using an inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) on a portion of the assembly (Fig. 2d,e). These stripes are on account of an alignment of porphyrin arrays driven by hydrophobic interaction in SN. Additionally, the synchrotron small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) showed a sharp peak at q = 2.6 nm−1, which matched the 2.4 nm stripes (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) of SN showed a strong peak at q = 15.7 nm−1, suggesting a 0.40 nm π-π stacking distance (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 7b).

Integrating these findings, we propose a molecular stacking model for the 2D platelets39, featuring a tail-to-tail misaligned bilayer, as illustrated in Fig. 2g. Along the vertical axis (z-axis), the full length of TPPZn molecule measures ~4.5 nm (Supplementary Fig. 8), with the hydrophobic alkyl chains buried internally and the cationic porphyrin headgroups exposed to the solvent (Supplementary Fig. 9). Along the long axis (x-axis) of the platelet, zinc porphyrins form a parallel array, with amide groups adopting a cis orientation to optimize both π-π interactions and hydrogen bonding (Supplementary Fig. 9c). We hypothesize that the angle between the porphyrin plane and the x-axis is ~67°, matching the observed apex angle of the parallelogram of SN (Fig. 2b). Along the short axis (y-axis), the TPPZn arrays exhibit a face-to-face distance of 2.4 nm, with solvent molecules likely occupying the interstitial spaces between the hydrophilic regions (Supplementary Fig. 9d).

Comparison of the absorption spectra of TPPZn in MeOH and SN in MeOH/H2O (v/v = 1:1) revealed changes that correlate with the proposed stacking mode (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). In MeOH, TPPZn showed no light scattering, indicating that it existed as isolated monomers. In contrast, ground-state coupling within the 2D porphyrin arrays of SN broadens the molecular energy levels relative to those of the monomers, resulting in a distinct broadening of the Soret band (Supplementary Fig. 10)40. Concurrently, the slight red shift of both the Soret and Q bands is characteristic of J-type aggregation, in which TPPZn molecules within each array adopt a slightly offset stacking geometry (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10)41. The tri-pyridinium cations, surrounded by solvent molecules and Cl− counterions, experience reduced intermolecular electrostatic repulsion, which accounts for the observed orientation of the porphyrins42,43. Accordingly, the self-assembly is driven mainly by hydrogen‑bonding and hydrophobic collapse of the alkyl chains, with additional stabilization from chromophore stacking (Fig. 2g). The thermal stability of the resulting 2D porphyrin lattice was directly corroborated by in situ SAXS. The characteristic scattering peak at q = 2.6 nm−1, corresponding to the 2.4 nm stacking periodicity, remained stable below 50 °C, attenuated at 50 °C, and disappeared completely upon heating to 60 °C (Supplementary Fig. 11). This confirms the structural integrity of the 2D nanoplatelets under 50 °C and defines the thermal window for subsequent seeded-growth experiments.

Homoepitaxial seeded growth of the 2D platelets

Next, controlled self-assembly experiments were performed using a seeded-growth method (Fig. 3)29,44. The SN displays a 2D parallelogram shape with an average area (An) of 2.7 × 105 nm2 and a polymer dispersity index (PDI) of 1.44 (Fig. 2a). Then the SN was sonicated in an ice-water bath to get seeds (An = 0.90 × 104 nm2, PDI = 2.03, Fig. 3b). By adding different molar ratios of monomer to seeds at 40 °C and then annealing, 2D platelets called GZn with controlled size formed (Fig. 3c–f). The average area of GZn followed a linear relationship to the molar ratio of monomer to seed (nm/ns, Fig. 3g) within a certain range and a controlled dispersity (PDI < 1.7). GZn became fractured and have a higher dispersity when nm/ns was above 8, due to the stronger stress in fragile large assemblies and the larger nucleation tendency at a high monomer concentration30. Additionally, the aspect ratio of the resulting GZn had a slight increase along with nm/ns and was larger than that of seeds (Supplementary Table 2), indicating that the growth on the long-axis is much faster than that on the short-axis at the beginning of the seeded-growth process. The method above demonstrated good reproducibility in a parallel-seeded-growth experiment (see Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Table 3).

Taken together, these results support a metastable assembly pathway for the 2D zinc‑porphyrin nanostructures. To quantitatively resolve the self‑nucleation dynamics, we performed temperature‑dependent absorption studies (Supplementary Fig. 13). The cooling curve of SN monitored at 440 nm displayed a characteristic non‑sigmoidal profile, which was well described by the Meijer–Schenning–van der Schoot nucleation-elongation model45–47. This analysis yielded a critical elongation temperature (Tₑ) of 48 °C and a substantial enthalpic driving force (ΔH = –37.4 kJ mol−1), indicating a cooperative assembly mechanism. Upon rapid cooling to 15 °C, the solution initially contained small aggregates with no noticeable broadening in the absorption spectra; these aggregates slowly transformed into large nanosheets, with fluorescence quenched over time (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). This transformation was further monitored by time‑dependent UV–vis absorption. For SN, the absorbance of TPPZn decreased exponentially after a lag time, accompanied by a red shift of the Q‑band within 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 16a). Lower concentrations of TPPZn resulted in longer lag times, indicating that the small aggregates serve as an on‑pathway, metastable intermediate in route to SN (Supplementary Fig. 16b,e)48. For the seeded‑growth kinetics, the monomer–seed mixture (nm/ns = 6) transformed into GZn6 with a shorter lag time and faster initiation compared to the self‑nucleation process (Supplementary Fig. 16c–e). Based on these observations, we propose the energy landscape depicted in Fig. 3h: metastable aggregates form through cooperative assembly of monomers and then convert into nanosheets by overcoming an average energetic barrier, which is lowered by the introduction of 2D seeds after nucleation48–50.

Heteroepitaxial growth of the 2D platelets

Building on the controlled homoepitaxial growth, we extended the seeded-growth strategy to construct 2D heterostructures by integrating a second metal center, enabling the spatial organization of energy donors and acceptors on a single nanosheet51–53. We first characterized the cobalt–porphyrin amphiphile (TPPCo) as the energy acceptor. Under identical conditions (MeOH/H2O, v/v = 1:1), TEM revealed that TPPCo predominantly assembles into one-dimensional (1D) nanoribbons accompanied by small spherical clusters (Supplementary Fig. 17a). Time-dependent UV–vis absorption spectra recorded over 24 h showed the emergence of a peak at 400 nm, suggesting a distinct stacking motif for the cobalt porphyrins compared to their zinc analogues (Supplementary Fig. 17b). This difference is attributed to the influence of the metal center on the spatial arrangement of weakly coordinating axial ligands, which directs cobalt porphyrins toward an alternative assembly pathway28,54. We then employed the size-controlled zinc-porphyrin platelet GZn6 (Fig. 3e) as a template for heteroepitaxial growth due to its optimal size and low dispersity (Fig. 4a). A solution of TPPCo in MeOH was added to GZn6 at varying molar ratios (expressed as Co/Zn). TEM analysis revealed that at Co/Zn ratios below 1, the overall platelet morphology remains unchanged. However, protrusions appeared at the edges, and the platelet area did not increase linearly with the amount of TPPCo added (Supplementary Fig. 18b–f and Supplementary Table 4). This suggests a surface‑limited epitaxial process rather than solution‑phase co‑assembly. At Co/Zn ratios above 1, competitive self‑nucleation of TPPCo became evident, leading to the formation of 1D nanostructures alongside the 2D platelets (Supplementary Fig. 18g). Even with stepwise addition of TPPCo monomers, the same morphological features were observed, indicating a distinct growth mechanism that persists even at low monomer fractions (Supplementary Fig. 19). We therefore focused on the well-defined heterostructures formed at low Co/Zn ratios (0.1 and 0.2, Supplementary Table 4). This controlled epitaxial growth is envisioned to create a donor–acceptor interface on the 2D surface, mimicking the hierarchical energy-transfer architecture found in natural light-harvesting systems55.

We then verified that the heteroepitaxial growth preserved the fundamental architecture of the 2D heterostructures. AFM measurements confirmed that both GZn6 and the resulting heterostructure GZn6-Co (Co/Zn = 0.2, An = 8.9 × 105 nm2, PDI = 1.48) maintained a consistent thickness of ~6.8 nm (Supplementary Fig. 20). Similarly, SAXS profiles showed the persistence of the characteristic scattering peak at 2.4 nm in GZn6, GZn6-Co, and the initial SN (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 21), confirming the structural integrity of the porphyrin arrays throughout the process. We then probed the photophysical consequences of incorporating the cobalt–porphyrin acceptor. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) under 405 nm excitation revealed that while the zinc-porphyrin platelets were highly fluorescent, the growth of TPPCo caused pronounced fluorescence quenching, particularly localized at the platelet edges (Supplementary Fig. 22). To move beyond intensity-based mapping and gain deeper insight into the energy-transfer dynamics, we employed fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM), which provides spatially resolved excited-state decay kinetics12,56. FLIM analysis of individual GZn6-Co (Co/Zn = 0.2) platelets clearly differentiated the donor and acceptor regions (Fig. 4b–d). By fitting the decays with a tri-exponential model, we obtained distinct lifetimes for the inner donor region (τD = 1.4 ns), the outer acceptor region (τA = 2.1 ns), and an ensemble average (τAve = 2.0 ns) (Fig. 4e–g). A consistent trend was observed for GZn6-Co with a lower acceptor ratio (Co/Zn = 0.1), where τD, τA, and τAve were 1.8 ns, 4.2 ns, and 3.0 ns, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 24). The τD of GZn6, GZn6-Co (Co/Zn = 0.1), and GZn6-Co (Co/Zn = 0.2) were determined to be 2.2 ns, 1.8 ns, and 1.4 ns, respectively (Supplementary Table 5). Notably, the donor lifetime exhibited a progressive shortening, while the acceptor lifetime was concurrently prolonged. This spatial gradient in lifetime—shorter in the interior and longer at the edges—provides direct evidence for directed energy funneling from the zinc-porphyrin donor core to the cobalt–porphyrin acceptor periphery within a single block co-assembled platelet (Supplementary Figs. 23–26)12. The block co‑assembly pathway was further investigated by time‑dependent UV–vis absorption spectroscopy. For epitaxial growth at a Co/Zn ratio of 1, a minor decrease and broadening of both the Soret and Q bands were observed without any spectral shift, whereas the characteristic shoulder peak at 400 nm typically associated with self‑assembled TPPCo did not appear (Supplementary Fig. 27a). These observations suggest that the conversion of TPPCo metastable aggregates into 2D heterostructures is limited, compromising the living nature of the growth at the GZn edges. Nevertheless, the process exhibited faster dynamics than the self‑assembly of TPPCo alone (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 27b)51.

To directly compare epitaxial growth with random co‑assembly, we prepared co‑assembled heterostructures (denoted Zn&Co) by mixing TPPZn and TPPCo in MeOH at a 1:1 molar ratio, then diluting the mixture to a final concentration of 0.1 mM for each component in MeOH/H2O (v/v = 1:1). The solution was annealed at 60 °C for 30 min and subsequently stored at 15 °C for 14 days. Under these conditions, the self‑nucleation of Zn&Co required a longer time than that of TPPZn alone, and the resulting nanostructures exhibited heterogeneous sizes and shapes (Supplementary Fig. 28). Fluorescence quenching was more pronounced in Zn&Co than in the epitaxially grown GZn6‑Co heterostructures (Supplementary Fig. 29), consistent with the known differences between block‑type nanostructures and random co‑assemblies57.

Energy transfer and excited-state dynamics of 2D assemblies

To quantify the energy-transfer capability indicated by FLIM, we performed Stern–Volmer analysis on the heterostructures. Fluorescence quenching was measured upon the incremental addition of TPPCo (from 0.1 to 2 equiv.) to various 2D zinc-porphyrin assemblies (SN, seeds, and GZn platelets) under 430-nm excitation (Supplementary Fig. 30). The Soret-band absorption has no change in position and less than 25% increase at a Co/Zn ratio below 1, indicating little effects related to inner filters (Supplementary Fig. 31). In the representative case of GZn6, quenching yielded an energy transfer efficiency (ΦET) of 0.50 at Co/Zn = 1 (Supplementary Fig. 30e). The Stern–Volmer plot fitted by the emission at 650 nm afforded a quenching constant (KSV) of 2.1 × 105 M−1 (Supplementary Fig. 30 f). This value is comparable with those of typical donor–acceptor quenching systems governed primarily by energy migration, particularly in confined supramolecular assembly (Supplementary Table 6)22,28,58. Insufficient donor–acceptor contact in the porphyrin arrays, combined with the acceptor-growth-dependent decrease in τD, rules out conventional dynamic or static quenching as the primary pathway59. Instead, we propose a directed photophysical sequence: initial homogeneous exciton migration among the densely packed zinc-porphyrin donors, which funnels excitation to interfacial sites, followed by heterogeneous energy transfer to the adjacent cobalt–porphyrin acceptors60,61. Notably, GZn-Co heterostructures of different sizes exhibited comparable ΦET and KSV values at identical Co/Zn ratios (Supplementary Table 6). This size independence within the controlled regime indicates that the radiative energy-transfer characteristics are not governed by the overall platelet dimensions, but are rather dictated by the local molecular packing and donor–acceptor interface established during the block co-assembly57.

For comparison, we performed control experiments using assembled or free porphyrins (Supplementary Fig. 32). A self‑sorted mixture containing GZn and pre‑assembled TPPCo nanostructures exhibited a lower ΦET (0.35) and a smaller KSV (1.0 × 105 M−1) than the epitaxially grown heterostructures. These values are comparable to those of the cationic free‑molecule system (ZnTMPP and CoTMPP, ΦET = 0.37 at Co/Zn = 1 and KSV = 1.2 × 105 M−1, Supplementary Fig. 32c–e). FLIM images of the self‑sorted system at Co/Zn = 0.2 showed a lifetime (τ = 2.2 ns) across both the inner and outer regions of individual nanosheets, identical to that of the donor alone (Supplementary Fig. 33). These findings underscore the fundamental difference between self‑sorting and block co‑assembly architectures57. For GZn and CoTMPP system, the fluorescence intensity and lifetime reflect that there is no significant energy transfer (Supplementary Fig. 34). Furthermore, while SN and seeds could form heterostructures, their broad size distribution and associated strong self-quenching led to significantly diminished ΦET (Supplementary Table 6). Collectively, these comparisons highlight that the controlled, long-range order assembly within the block co-assembly architecture, which pre-organizes donors and acceptors in close proximity, is crucial for promoting efficient exciton migration and direct energy transfer55,56.

To gain a deeper understanding of the relationship between the size of 2D porphyrin assemblies and excitation energy transfer, we conducted femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS) experiments using a 400 nm laser pulse. 2DZn platelets, including SN, seeds, and GZn with various sizes and PDI, were used to prepare 2D heterostructures by the heteroepitaxial growth at Co/Zn = 0.2 (Supplementary Fig. 35). All samples exhibited the photo-bleaching peak of TPPZn at 440 nm and singlet excited-state absorption bands above 470 nm within 1 ps, which gradually decayed within 5 ns (Supplementary Figs. 36–41). The two dips centered at 570 nm and 630 nm corresponded to the Q-band bleaching of porphyrins, and the board peak at 700 nm and 650 nm may be attributed to the zinc-porphyrin radical cations and cobalt–porphyrin radical anions, respectively (Fig. 5a, d)22,62,63. To differentiate these excited-state species, global fitting of the fs-TAS data revealed distinct kinetic models: the donor-only 2DZn platelets were best described by a double-stage decay, whereas the 2D heterostructures required a triple-stage model, indicating the emergence of an additional kinetic process upon acceptor integration22,64.

Specifically, GZn6 (An = 7.6 × 105 nm2, PDI = 1.33) and GZn6-Co (Co/Zn = 0.2, An = 8.9 × 105 nm2, PDI = 1.48) were selected (Fig. 5a–f). In the triple-stage model, the localized porphyrin singlet excited state (state 1, 4.0 ps, k1 = 2.5 × 1011 s−1) decays to an intermediate exciton-migration state (state 2, 28 ps, k2 = 3.5 × 1010 s−1) that ultimately affords the fully charge-separated state (state 3, 376 ps, k3 = 2.7 × 109 s−1, Fig. 5f). The 28 ps lifetime of State 2 of GZn6-Co is attributed to exciton-migration preceding a rapid electron injection to the acceptor, culminating in interfacial charge separation65. The photogenerated electron-hole pairs at state 3 of GZn6-Co quickly recombined through fluorescence emission and non-radiative annihilation55. In contrast, the donor-only GZn6 platelet was best described by a double-stage model: exciton delocalization (state 1, 10.2 ps, k1’ = 9.8 × 1010 s−1) followed by exciton recombination (state 2, 254 ps, k2’ = 3.9 × 109 s−1) without proceeding to an efficient charge-separated state (Fig. 5c)65.

Collectively, these analyses yield three key findings. First, exciton migration is significantly accelerated in heterostructures through the effective coupling between the acceptor and the donor, with k1 exceeding k1’ by a factor of 1.6 to 2.5 (Table 1)66. Second, the dynamics exhibit a positive size correlation: heterostructures with larger sizes and aspect ratios exhibit faster decay kinetics, particularly for the initial exciton migration (k1 and k1’) (Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43). This trend parallels the directed energy funneling in enlarged natural antenna systems, where longer porphyrin arrays enhance exciton coupling between chromophores67. Third, well-defined heterostructures like GZn6-Co enable a fast electron injection (<0.38 ns) within the charge-separated state lifetime (τ3, Table 1), as compared to a series of benchmark supramolecular systems (Supplementary Table 7). In contrast, seed-Co with an irregular surface exhibits a reduced lifetime of the charge-separated state (Table 1)65. We therefore conclude that long-range order and precise dimensional control in these 2D porphyrin heterostructures are critical for facilitating directed excited-state energy transfer, manifested as enhanced exciton migration and efficient charge separation.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a general seeded-growth strategy for the controlled assembly of 2D metalloporphyrin heterostructures in water, mimicking the chlorosome. The metastable assembly pathway of these amphiphiles reveals the kinetic control governing the seeded‑growth and epitaxial‑growth processes. This approach enabled the fabrication of size-tunable 2D platelets and, through block co-assembly, zinc–cobalt heterostructures with spatially defined donor–acceptor domains. The spatial gradient fluorescence lifetime presented a statistically averaged result as the core evidence for the block co-assembly. Attributed to donor–acceptor coupling, exciton migration in the 2D heterostructures is accelerated by up to 2.5-fold and drives the formation of a charge-separated state. Notably, the excited-state kinetics scaled with the platelet area, mirroring natural antenna systems. This work establishes a pathway-regulation strategy for controlled 2D porphyrin-assembly platform, paving the way for property tuning of advanced artificial photosynthetic systems.

Methods

Synthesis of target molecules

The synthetic procedure and characterization are described in general remarks in the Supplementary Information. For nuclear magnetic resonance spectra of the compounds in this Article, see Supplementary Figs. 44–77.

Controlled self-assembly

Self-assembly was conducted in 4 mL or 20 mL vials in heating modules. TPPZn solid was first dissolved at 1 mM in MeOH, part of which was diluted by MeOH and H2O, resulting in a 0.1 mM MeOH/H2O (v/v = 1:1) solution. The solution was heated in a 60 °C heating module for 30 min, slowly cooled to 15 °C, and placed for more than 24 h (annealing). The freshly prepared 2DZn platelets (SN) were then sonicated with a sonication rod (120 W) in an ice-water bath at 0 °C for 15 min, followed by reannealing at 40 °C for 10 min to obtain seeds. The 1 mM TPPZn MeOH solution was then used to prepare a monomer solution containing 0.5 mM TPPZn and 2% (v/v) 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (TFE) in MeOH. Seeds were quartered, diluted to 10 μM in MeOH/H2O (v/v = 1:1), and placed in a 40 °C heating module together with the monomer solution. 10 min later, a varying volume of monomer solution was added to the seeds, vortexed for 4 s, and slowly cooled to 15 °C. For heteroepitaxial growth, monomer solution with [TPPCo] = 0.5 mM in MeOH was added to GZn with [TPPZn] = 30 μM in MeOH/H2O (v/v = 1:1) at molar ratios of monomer to seed (Co/Zn) at 40 °C and cooled periodically to 15 °C.

Electron microscopy

TEM images were obtained on a Tecnai G2 F20 S-Twin or a JEOL JEM-2100, with acceleration voltages of 80 kV or 200 kV. A sample solution (5 μL) was dropped onto a copper grid with a 6-nm-thick carbon layer, wicked with filter paper, and dried in a desiccator overnight. Cryogenic TEM was performed using a JEOL CryoARM at 300 kV with a Gatan K3 CCD camera.

Atomic force microscopy

AFM images were captured on a Bruker JPK NanoWizard Sense AFM. The microscope was equipped with the DirectOverlay 2 software module and a Tap150AI-G probe (tip radius of 10 nm). A sample solution (20 μL) was deposited on a clean, flat silicon surface for 5 min and removed by a dry N2 flow.

Fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy

FLIM was performed on a PicoQuant MicroTime 200 using a 100× oil-immersion objective. The detector was a Single-Photon Avalanche Diode (SPAD). The dwell time was 5 μs. SymPhoTime 64 was used to fit the lifetime using a 3rd-order decay curve. A sample solution (4 μL) was sealed between two cover slides. Samples are imaged using the 405 nm excitation laser and 561-690 nm dark field detector. Triethylenediamine (DABCO) was added to the sample solution (2% w/v) when Co/Zn was 0.2 or 0.5 to suppress fluorescence quenching.

Femtosecond pump-probe transient absorption

The fs-TAS measurements were performed using the HELIOS FIRE instrument (Ultrafast Systems). The pump pulses at 400–750 nm were generated with an optical parametric amplifier (Apollo-T, Ultrafast Systems). The femtosecond white light probe pulses were generated by focusing the 1 kHz Ti: Sapphire amplifier (SPFIRE ACE, 800 nm fundamental, 100 fs pulse width) output onto sapphire (fs-TA, VIS: 420–800 nm). The samples were placed in 2 mm quartz cuvettes, nitrogen was bubbled through the solutions, and the solutions were vigorously stirred during all measurements.
