Fabrication of a quaternary ammonium chitosan/pomelo peel extract nanocarrier system for tea polyphenol delivery: characterization, stability, digestibility, and release properties
Authors: Chao Ai, Bo He, Xuanlin Luo, Ruobing Wang, Jingling Kang, Xinyi Li, Wenli Xiao, Zhaohong Zhong, Lei Chen, Xi Xie, Huifan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe application of tea polyphenols (TP) is constrained by their inherent chemical instability. In this study, a nanocarrier delivery system for TP was fabricated via electrostatic self-assembly between a natural pomelo peel extract (PP) and quaternary ammonium chitosan (QAC). PP was characterized as a protein-pectin complex, containing 60.1% protein and 23.2% polysaccharides, of which 75.6% was galacturonic acid. PP formed stable complexes with QAC at pH ∼4, achieving a TP encapsulation efficiency of 39.6%. The QAC-PP nanocarrier significantly improved TP stability under simulated gastrointestinal conditions, retaining 96.3% of TP after 1 h of gastric digestion and 59.5% after a subsequent 2 h of intestinal digestion. Furthermore, after 12 h of UV irradiation, 58.9% of encapsulated TP remained, substantially higher than the 43.0% retention observed for free TP. This work successfully valorizes pomelo peel waste into an effective nanocarrier, presenting a viable strategy to enhance the stability of TP.Keywords: Pomelo peel, Tea polyphenols, Nanocarrier, Stability, Digestion

Introduction

Tea polyphenols, the primary bioactive constituents in tea, have garnered significant interest for their diverse health benefits, including anti-inflammatory, lipid- and glucose-metabolism regulating, neuroprotective, and gut microbiota-modulating effects. However, the practical application of TPs remains constrained by two major limitations: high susceptibility to environmental degradation (e.g., light, heat, oxygen) leading to loss of bioactivity (Tang et al., 2023), and poor stability in the gastrointestinal tract, particularly in the acidic gastric environment (Chen & Yang, 2020). It has been reported that less than 5% of orally administered TPs are effectively absorbed in the intestine, with the majority being rapidly metabolized or excreted (Yin et al., 2022). Importantly, TP absorption is highly pH-dependent, favoring the weakly alkaline intestinal milieu over the acidic stomach (Gui et al., 2023). This physiological insight highlights the need for intestine-targeted delivery strategies to enhance TP bioavailability.

Recent advances in pharmaceutical nanoparticles have led to the development of food-grade targeted delivery systems, which offer a promising approach to enhancing the stability and absorption of bioactive compounds. Among these systems, nano- or micro-capsules formed through electrostatic interactions between polysaccharides and proteins have demonstrated significant potential for nutrient protection and delivery (Wang et al., 2024). These systems effectively protect sensitive ingredients from environmental stressors and promote their controlled release in the intestine by facilitating their passage through the stomach (Gao et al., 2021). Plant-derived polymers are particularly attractive as wall materials due to their biocompatibility, safety, and modifiable functional groups (Ai et al., 2025; Wen, Lin, et al., 2025).

Pomelo, a major fruit crop in Southeast Asia, generates substantial biological waste—peel and pulp accounting for up to 30% of its total weight—posing both environmental and economic challenges (Wandee et al., 2019). Pomelo peel is rich in biopolymers such as pectin, proteins, and cellulose, and one important application of these biopolymers is their use as encapsulation wall materials (Cao et al., 2024). Therefore, valorizing pomelo peel waste into functional materials may represent a promising strategy for encapsulation applications. Electrostatic self-assembly between anionic plant polymers and cationic macromolecules offers a simple, safe, and scalable route to construct encapsulation systems. For instance, olive pectin–chitosan nanocomplexes achieved high anthocyanin encapsulation efficiency (EE) (91.97 ± 0.33%) and exhibited controlled release under in vitro simulated digestion, with minimal gastric release (34.95%) and nearly complete intestinal release (99.10 ± 0.14%) (Xie et al., 2023). In another study, Wen, Tang, et al. (2025) developed quercetin-loaded whey protein–pectin nanoparticles that exhibited high gastrointestinal retention (40.39 ± 0.01%) and significantly enhanced apoptotic activity, inducing HepG-2 cell apoptosis at a rate of 83.6%. These studies demonstrate the feasibility of using plant-derived biopolymers for encapsulation, yet the potential of pomelo peel extract as a direct wall material has not been explored. Exploiting its ionization characteristics via electrostatic self-assembly could simultaneously enable bioactive encapsulation and pomelo by-product valorization.

Quaternary ammonium chitosan (QAC), a water-soluble chitosan derivative with a permanent positive charge, presents a promising cationic partner for electrostatic complexation. Compared with chitosan, QAC exhibits superior solubility, antibacterial activity, film-forming ability, and biodegradability (Huang et al., 2023), making it an ideal material for constructing nanocarriers that protect bioactive compounds during storage and digestion (Liu et al., 2024). Given the positive charge of QAC and the negative charge that plant polymers can exhibit under appropriate pH conditions, we hypothesized that electrostatic complexation between QAC and the pomelo peel extract could produce a pH-responsive nanocarrier suitable for stabilizing and delivering TP. This strategy not only is important for the stabilization of TP but also provides a new approach for the valorization of pomelo peel waste.

In this study, we extracted a protein-rich fraction from pomelo peel via salt-assisted hot water extraction and fabricated TPs-loaded nanocarrier through electrostatic assembly with QAC. The structural and physicochemical properties of the nanocarrier were systematically characterized using dynamic light scattering, fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscope (SEM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), transmission electron microscope (TEM), and circular dichroism. We further evaluated their ultraviolet (UV) light stability, in vitro digestion stability and release behaviors. This work elucidates the assembly mechanism and functional performance of pomelo peel-derived nanocarrier, providing a sustainable strategy for the high-value utilization of pomelo by-products and the development of efficient oral delivery systems for TP.

Materials and methods

Materials

The pomelo peel used in this study was provided by Meizhou Zhenbao Jinyou Industrial Co., Ltd. (Meizhou, China). QAC (degree of substitution 92%) was supplied by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), while TP (purity ≥98%) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other reagents used for chemical analyses were of analytical grade, unless otherwise specified. Reagents for chromatographic analyses were of chromatographic grade.

Preparation of the protein-rich extract from pomelo peel

Fresh pomelo peel was thoroughly rinsed three times with distilled water to remove surface impurities and then dried in an oven at 50 °C for 48 h until a constant weight was achieved. The dried material was ground into a fine powder and passed through a 20-mesh sieve. The extraction process of PP is shown in Fig. 1. Briefly, approximately 50.0 g of the resulting powder was mixed with deionized water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:15 (w/v). The pH of the suspension was adjusted to 11.0 using 1 M NaOH, and the mixture was subjected to water bath extraction at 50 °C for 1.5 h with continuous stirring. The slurry was subsequently centrifuged at 4500 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The pH of the supernatant was carefully brought to 7.0 using 0.1 M HCl. The neutralized solution was incubated in a water bath at 50 °C for 30 min, after which a second centrifugation step was performed at 4500 ×g for 10 min. To precipitate the protein-rich fraction, the pH of the supernatant was lowered to ∼4.0 using 0.1 M HCl, and the solution was centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 20 min. The resulting pellet was redissolved in deionized water and dialyzed (MWCO 1000 Da) against distilled water for 24 h with frequent water changes. The retentate was finally freeze-dried to obtain the protein-rich extract of pomelo peel, which was named as PP and stored at −20 °C until further use.

The scheme of sequential extraction of PP from pomelo peel.

Chemical features of PP

The Chemical features of PP were measured following a previously described method (Ai et al., 2019). The composition of PP was characterized as follows. The protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl method. The total sugar content was quantified by the phenol‑sulfuric acid method, following a 12-h trypsin digestion pretreatment. The galacturonic acid (GalA) content was measured via the quantitative carbazole‑sulfuric acid colorimetric method. The content of neutral sugars was analyzed by high-performance anion-exchange chromatography with pulsed amperometric detection (HPAEC-PAD) after acid hydrolysis. The molecular weight distribution was determined by high-performance size-exclusion chromatography (HPSEC) coupled with a multi-angle light scattering detector.

Nanocarrier preparation

TP@QAC-PP nanocarrier was fabricated via an electrostatic interaction-driven self-assembly method, adapted from (Chen, Ai, et al., 2024; Chen, Lv, & Zhong, 2024) with minor modifications. Specifically, protein-rich extract powder was dissolved in distilled water under continuous stirring to obtain a 1% (w/v) aqueous solution. The solution was subsequently centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 20 min to remove insoluble impurities and stored at 4 °C prior to use. Separately, QAC was dispersed in distilled water and incubated in a water bath at 50 °C for 2 h under stirring to ensure complete dissolution. TP were then added and thoroughly mixed to form a homogeneous solution containing 1% (w/v) QAC and 0.5% (w/v) TP, which was also stored at 4 °C. For nanoscale complex assembly, the protein-rich extract solution and the QAC–TP mixture were combined at a 1:1 volume ratio under mechanical stirring. The pH of the resulting mixture was adjusted to a range of 2 to 6 using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH, as required for subsequent experiments. The resulting mixture was subjected to ultrasonication for 5 min using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (SCIENTZ-IID, Xinzhi, China) operating at a frequency of 20 kHz and a power of 260 W with a pulsed working mode (5 s on / 5 s off). Finally, the resulting nanoscale complex suspension was centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected and freeze-dried to obtain TP@QAC-PP nanocarrier powder.

EE of nanocarrier

To determine the EE of the nanocarrier for TP, the freeze-dried nanocarrier powder was reconstituted in deionized water to obtain a nanocarrier solution at a concentration of 10 mg/mL. This solution was then dialyzed against deionized water using a dialysis bag (MWCO 1000 Da) for 12 h. Subsequently, 1 mL of this solution was mixed with 5 mL of ferrous tartrate solution and thoroughly shaken. The mixture was then diluted to 25 mL with a pH 7.5 phosphate buffer solution. The absorbance was measured at 765 nm a Cary 60 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies Inc., China). Zeta potential and particle size

The zeta potential and particle size of the protein-rich fraction, QAC, and the resulting nanocarrier formulations were determined utilizing a Zetasizer Nano ZSE (Malvern Instruments, UK) in accordance with the procedure outlined in (Ai et al., 2023) at 25 °C. TP, PP, QAC, and nanocarrier powders were dissolved in distilled water. The pH of the solutions was adjusted using 100 mmol/L H2SO4 or 100 mmol/L NaOH. For zeta potential measurements, the sample concentration was kept identical to that used in the preparation process, while for particle size measurements, the 1 mg/mL sample was employed.

FTIR

Each freeze-dried PP, TP, QAC, and TP@QAC-PP was thoroughly mixed with KBr at a mass ratio of 1:150 (4 mg sample to 600 mg KBr). The mixture was carefully ground in an agate mortar to ensure uniform particle size and pressed into a transparent pellet under hydraulic pressure. FTIR spectra were collected using a Bruker VERTEX70 spectrometer (Germany) following a modified procedure based on Wen, Zhou, et al. (2025). Measurements were performed in transmission mode over the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1 with a spectral resolution of 0.5 cm−1. Each spectrum was acquired as an average of 32 consecutive scans to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio, and background correction was performed using a pure KBr pellet as the reference.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy

The dialyzed protein-rich extract was diluted with deionized water to a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. Separately, the freeze-dried nanocarrier powder was reconstituted in deionized water to form a 0.3 mg/mL solution, which corresponded to a protein-rich extract equivalent concentration of approximately 0.1 mg/mL. The pH of both solutions was adjusted to 6.0 using dilute HCl and/or NaOH. Far-ultraviolet circular dichroism (CD) spectra were acquired at room temperature using a Chirascan V100 spectrometer (Applied Photophysics Ltd., UK), following a modified procedure adapted from Zhang et al. (2022). Measurements were performed over a scanning wavelength range of 190–400 nm with a response time of 2 s.

SEM, TEM, and AFM

A small quantity of TP@QAC-PP powder was affixed to conductive adhesive and sputter-coated with a thin gold layer using a Quorum Q150R ES sputter coater operated at 10 mA for 180 s. The morphological features of the samples were then examined using a TESCAN MIRA-3 scanning electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Secondary electron images were acquired with an SE detector to visualize the surface morphology of the nanocarrier.

The molecular morphology of the nanocarrier was examined by AFM on a Multimode 8 instrument (Bruker). Briefly, the nanocarrier sample was dissolved in ultrapure water and diluted to a final concentration of 3 μg/mL. A 2 μL aliquot of the solution was uniformly deposited onto a freshly cleaved mica substrate and air-dried at room temperature for 30 min. AFM imaging was conducted in Tapping Mode™ using a nitride silicon probe (P-S10). Topographical data were acquired and processed using Nanoscope Analysis software to generate 2D and 3D images.

To monitor morphological changes of the nanocarrier during digestion, samples collected at different stages of the in vitro digestion process were freeze-dried and reconstituted in deionized water at a concentration of 15 mg/mL. The specimens were then examined using a HITACHI H-7650 transmission electron microscope (Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV (Sun et al., 2025).

Thermogravimetric analysis

The thermal stability of the samples was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) on a Mettler Toledo TGA/DSC 3+ HT/1600 instrument. Approximately 8 mg of sample was subjected to TGA under a nitrogen atmosphere (20 mL/min) with a heating program from 30 to 500 °C at 10 °C/min.

UV light stability

The stability of free and encapsulated TP under UV light was measured according to a previously reported method (Ai et al., 2023) with appropriate modifications. Aqueous solutions (1.5% w/v) of TP@QAC-PP and pure TP were prepared. Prior to irradiation, 5 mL of each solution was sealed in clear serum bottles. The samples were subjected to UV irradiation inside a light-proof chamber (50 × 50 × 50 cm) equipped with a 20 W UV lamp (Jinweishi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), maintaining a fixed distance of 20 cm between the lamp and samples. Irradiation was conducted for 12 h at ambient temperature (25 ± 1 °C). During the exposure period, the TP content was quantified at 2 h intervals using a validated UV–Vis spectrophotometric method. The photostability was expressed as the retention rate (%) of TP, calculated relative to the initial concentration prior to irradiation.

Simulated dynamic digestion in vitro

The in vitro digestion behavior was investigated using a bionic dynamic gastrointestinal system (Xiaodong Yijian Instrument & Equipment Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) following previously established methodologies (Fu et al., 2025) with appropriate modifications. Simulated gastric fluid (SGF) was prepared by dissolving the following analytical-grade reagents in distilled water: KCl (0.5 g/L), KH2PO4 (0.1 g/L), NaHCO3 (2.0 g/L), NaCl (2.7 g/L), (NH4)2CO3 (0.05 g/L), MgCl2·6H2O (0.02 g/L), and CaCl2·2H2O (0.2 g/L). A total volume of 250 mL was prepared and adjusted to pH 1.58 using 6 mol/L HCl to mimic fasting gastric conditions. Pepsin (1.8 g) was then incorporated and dissolved under gentle stirring. The resulting SGF was filter-sterilized through a 0.22 μm membrane before use. Similarly, the simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) was formulated with KCl (0.5 g/L), KH2PO4 (0.1 g/L), NaHCO3 (7.0 g/L), NaCl (2.0 g/L), MgCl2·6H2O (0.06 g/L), and CaCl2·2H2O (0.08 g/L) in distilled water. The solution (500 mL) was adjusted to pH 6.8 using 6 mol/L HCl, after which trypsin (1.8 g) and bile salts (4.8 g) were added and dissolved under continuous stirring. Sterile SIF was obtained by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane. Following the introduction of a 200 mL liquid sample into the system within 1 min via the inlet funnel, 5 mL aliquots were collected every 30 min to monitor the temporal evolution of the digest composition. Samples were taken from the gastric sampling port during the first h (at 30 and 60 min), and subsequently from the duodenal sampling port for the remaining 2 h (at 90, 120, 150, and 180 min).

Simulated release in vitro

The in vitro release profile of the TP@QAC-PP nanocarrier was determined using a dialysis method, following a modified protocol adapted from the literature (Ai et al., 2023). Two release media were prepared: an acidic buffer solution (pH 2.0, composed of citric acid and HCl) and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0). Both media contained 0.1 wt% Tween-80. A 5 mL aliquot of the 1 wt% nanocarrier solution was loaded into a pre-treated dialysis bag (MWCO 8000 Da). The bag was then immersed in a beaker containing 95 mL of the corresponding release medium. The release study was conducted for 6 h in a thermostatic water bath maintained at 37 °C under continuous stirring. At predetermined time intervals, samples were withdrawn from the external release medium. The concentration of TP released from the dialysis bag was quantified using a Cary 60 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, China) by measuring the absorbance at a wavelength of 765 nm. Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, all experiments in this study were conducted in triplicate. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's test were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0), with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussions

Yield and chemical composition of PP

As shown in Table 1, the yield of PP was 10.8%, with a molecular weight of 111 kDa. Further compositional analysis revealed that its protein content was 60.1%, and total sugar was 23.2%. Previous studies have demonstrated that neutral sugar chains in pectin are covalently linked to proteins (Alba & Kontogiorgos, 2017). To further characterize other components in PP, the material was treated with proteases, and the remaining fractions were analyzed. The results showed that the residual portion primarily consisted of pomelo peel pectin, with the following monosaccharide composition: galacturonic acid (75.6%), rhamnose (1.7%), arabinose (6.3%), galactose (8.9%), glucose (1.3%), xylose (0.8%), and ash content (1.2%). This composition exhibits high similarity to the pomelo peel pectin structure extracted by (Wandee et al., 2019), confirming that PP is a composite of both protein and pectin.

Appearance, particle size, and zeta potential

The visual appearance of QAC, TP, PP, and the TP@QAC-PP nanocarrier under varying pH conditions is depicted in Fig. S1. While QAC and PP solutions showed negligible color changes across pH 2–6, both TP and TP@QAC-PP developed a reddish-brown tint at pH 6, indicative of structural transformations in TP at elevated pH. This color shift likely arises from pH-sensitive molecular rearrangements within the TP components. As shown in Fig. 2A, QAC maintained a positive zeta potential across the pH range of 2 to 6, with the value rising from +42.4 mV to +52.5 mV. For TP, the zeta potential decreased from −5.7 mV to −29.1 mV as pH increased from 2 to 6. By comparison,PP carried a weak positive charge of +2.3 mV at pH 2, which was mainly derived from its protein components. As the pH continued to rise, PP became strongly electronegative, with its zeta potential dropping to −34.3 mV. These charge characteristics confirm that TP, PP and QAC can assemble together via electrostatic attraction in aqueous solution. Notably, although both TP and PP are negatively charged substances within the tested pH range, the resultant TP@QAC-PP nanocarrier still exhibited an overall positive surface charge, ranging from +29.6 mV to +41.1 mV. This observation suggests incomplete electrostatic neutralization, consistent with earlier reports of polyelectrolyte complexes where conformational constraints limit the ionization or accessibility of functional groups (Zhang et al., 2020; Fan, Lu, Ouyang, & Ling, 2021). Such behavior is advantageous for colloidal stability and may prevent macroscopic precipitation.

Zeta Potential of TP, QAC, PP, and TP@QAC-PP (A) and average particle size of PP, QAC, and TP@QAC-PP (B) at Different pH.

Particle size distributions (Fig. 2B) further elucidated the pH-dependent interactions. QAC particles remained stable in size across the tested pH range. In the case of PP, the particle size decreased from 691.5 nm to 395.5 nm as the pH increased from 2 to 6. Notably, relatively larger particle sizes were observed for PP between pH 2 and 3, which can be attributed to its proximity to the isoelectric point under these conditions, a finding consistent with the zeta potential measurements. For the TP@QAC-PP nanocarrier, the particle size increased from 868.9 nm at pH 2 to 1383.0 nm at pH 4. The increase in particle size with the rising pH value indicates an enhanced interchain complexation and aggregation, driven by charge balancing. However, as the pH increased from 4.5 to 6, the particle size gradually decreased to 688.4 nm. Within this pH range, the electrostatic interactions continued to increase, making the dissociation of the complex unlikely. Therefore, the observed decrease in particle size can be attributed to the removal of larger aggregates during the centrifugation step in the TP@QAC-PP collection process.

Collectively, these findings highlight the critical role of electrostatic and structural rearrangements in formating the ternary nanocarrier with pH changes. The retention of a positive net charge and the reversible aggregation behavior underscore the potential of TP@QAC-PP as a pH-responsive delivery system.

Turbidity and EE

The turbidity profiles of QAC, PP, and the TP@QAC-PP nanocarrier across a pH gradient are presented in Fig. 3A. QAC exhibited minimal turbidity fluctuation, maintaining a consistently low value near 0.05, which underscores its colloidal stability under acidic conditions. In contrast, PP exhibited a distinct turbidity maximum (0.189) at pH 2.5, which then decreased and stabilized around 0.11 at higher pH. This trend agrees with the particle size data (Section 3.2) and indicates aggregation near pH 2.5. As a protein-pectin composite, PP reaches a pseudo-isoelectric state at this pH, where reduced electrostatic repulsion promotes interparticle association (Archut et al., 2022). The subsequent turbidity decrease suggests aggregate restructuring as ionizable groups regain charge at higher pH. For the TP@QAC-PP nanocarrier, turbidity increased progressively from 0.03 to 0.69 as pH rose from 2 to 6, reflecting continuous formation of insoluble aggregates driven by electrostatic complexation (Alavi & Chen, 2022). The TP retention rate in the supernatant decreased from 93.3% at pH 2 to 42.3% at pH 6 (Fig. 3B), indicating that an increasing proportion of TP was incorporated into coacervates or precipitates and subsequently removed during the centrifugation step of nanocarrier preparation. In case of the EE, it was highly pH-dependent: it increased from 7.3% at pH 2 to a maximum of 39.6% at pH 4, and then decreased to 14.8% at pH 6. The initial increase in EE aligns with the enhanced electrostatic attraction between the anionic groups on PP and the cationic QAC, which promotes the formation of structured microcapsules. Although PP carries a weak positive charge at pH 2, making major electrostatic interaction with QAC unlikely, a residual encapsulation of 7.3% was observed, likely due to hydrogen bonding between TP and the biopolymer matrix (Dong et al., 2023). The decline in EE beyond pH 4 coincides with extensive aggregation and precipitation of QAC-PP complexes, which entrapped TP molecules and were subsequently removed during sample preparation. However, the substantial discrepancy between TP retention rate and EE suggests that a considerable amount of free TP remained unencapsulated during the electrostatic complexation process between PP and QAC. In summary, the EE of TP in TP@QAC-PP is governed by a delicate balance of electrostatic driving forces and aggregation extent. Moderate pH (around 4) optimizes encapsulation via electrostatically structured complexation, while lower pH relies more on non-electrostatic interactions and higher pH leads to excessive precipitation that removes encapsulated TP from the supernatant phase.

Further evidence of conformational rearrangement comes from the circular dichroism CD spectra of PP before and after complexation (Fig. S2). Secondary structure analysis (Fig. 4B) reveals a decrease in random coil content from 39.3% to 23.2%, accompanied by increases in β-turn (15.0% to 23.0%), β-sheet (24.9% to 30.1%), and α-helix (20.9% to 23.6%). This shift toward more ordered secondary structures indicates that the incorporation of PP into the ternary complex promotes structural regularization, likely through intermolecular interactions that stabilize defined conformational states (Liu et al., 2023).

Micromorphology

SEM micrographs of TP@QAC-PP are presented in Fig. 5A. TP@QAC-PP is observed to form large, continuous aggregates with irregular contours, and no well-defined, discrete particles with uniform size or shape can be clearly distinguished across the field of view. To further elucidate the structural details, AFM was employed (Fig. 5B). The nanocarrier exhibits irregular bead-like units, suggesting the formation of cavity-like structures after macromolecular complexation. Given that the height of these units considerably exceeds the ∼1 nm typical of single polymer molecules (Wan et al., 2019), the observed morphology suggests a composite architecture. Linear connections between beads are also visible, which may result from intra- or intermolecular hydrogen bonding leading to aggregation, a phenomenon consistent with observations in other protein-based complexes (Ma et al., 2025).

Thermogravimetric analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was employed to evaluate the thermal decomposition behavior and stability of the samples by monitoring mass changes as a function of temperature. As shown in Fig. 6A, four distinct stages of mass loss were observed. The first stage (30–110 °C) corresponds primarily to the evaporation of free water within the nanocarrier. The measured mass loss in this stage (ranging from 88.4% to 94.6%) aligns well with the moisture content data presented in Table 1. The second stage (110–205 °C) exhibits moderate mass loss, attributable to the release of structurally bound water through further dehydration processes. A significant mass loss occurs in the third stage (205–350 °C), mainly due to the cleavage of covalent bonds in the polymer backbones. The onset of thermal degradation for QAC was observed near 250 °C, consistent with reported data on chitosan-based materials (Martínez-Camacho et al., 2010). This characteristic contributed to a distinctly different thermal profile for the TP@QAC-PP nanocomposite compared to free TP. Specifically, the maximum degradation rate of TP@QAC-PP occurred at approximately 250 °C, notably later than that of free TP (∼225 °C). This shift suggests that the QAC-PP matrix enhances thermal stability by shielding TP from premature degradation, likely through intermolecular interactions and structural encapsulation. In the fourth stage (>350 °C), the mass loss rate gradually decreased, as most readily decomposable organic components had already degraded. The remaining residue may consist of carbonized polysaccharide and protein fractions stabilized by strong hydrogen bonding and other non-covalent interactions (Ai et al., 2024). The relatively high residual mass in this stage may also be correlated with the inherent ash content of the samples. When the temperature reached 500 °C, TP exhibited the highest carbonization rate, approximately 49.5%, while TP@QAC-PP showed a lower rate of 37.1%. This difference can be attributed not only to the low carbonization rate of QAC itself (15.4%), but also to the encapsulation structure, which may impede the formation of a carbonized layer on the surface of the TP powder.

UV light stability and free radical scavenging ability

Due to the UV sensitivity of TP, the UV light stability of free TP and TP@QAC-PP was evaluated, as shown in Fig. 6B. The retention of free TP decreased drastically to 43.0% after 12 h of UV irradiation. In contrast, the TP content in the TP@QAC-PP nanocarrier remained at 58.9% under the same conditions. This enhanced photostability can be attributed to a dual protective mechanism provided by the nanocomposite shell. First, the macromolecular structures of QAC and PP serve as a physical barrier, effectively scattering and reflecting UV radiation (Guo et al., 2023). Second, photon absorption by unsaturated bonds within the shell matrix reduces the effective UV exposure to TP, thereby mitigating photodegradation. This mechanism is consistent with previous studies on polysaccharide-protein nanocomplexes (Guo et al., 2021).

The antioxidant activities of TP@QAC-PP and free TP are presented in Fig. 6C and D. Initially, the TP@QAC-PP composite demonstrated significantly higher (p < 0.05) free radical scavenging activity, with rates of 58.3 ± 0.9% for DPPH and 80.4 ± 0.7% for ABTS+∙, compared to free TP (43.5 ± 1.1% and 60.3 ± 1.0%, respectively). This difference can be attributed to the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of QAC-PP, which neutralize free radicals via hydrogen atom transfer (Xiang et al., 2025). As the duration of UV irradiation increases, the gap in free radical scavenging activity between free TP and TP@QAC-PP widens progressively, indicating that TP@QAC-PP possesses enhanced stability under UV exposure. Upon 12 h of UV exposure, TP@QAC-PP maintained superior stability, retaining DPPH and ABTS+∙ scavenging rates of 46.4 ± 1.1% and 59.4 ± 0.7%, respectively. These values were significantly higher (p < 0.05) than those of the non-encapsulated TP, which decreased to 20.3 ± 0.8% and 24.6 ± 1.3%. The sustained antioxidant activity of TP@QAC-PP under UV stress aligns with the higher TP retention profile documented in Fig. 6B. These results collectively confirm that the QAC-PP encapsulation effectively shields TP from UV degradation, thereby preserving its bioactivity.

Simulated digestion in vitro

The protective effect of QAC-PP nanocarrier on encapsulated TP was evaluated using an in vitro simulated digestion system. As shown in Fig. 7A, the particle size of TP@QAC-PP decreased from 688.3 nm to 376.6 nm during the gastric phase. This reduction can be attributed to two main factors: (a) partial enzymatic degradation of the protein component in PP, consistent with reported proteolysis-induced size reduction in protein-based nanoparticles (Chen, Lv, & Zhong, 2024; Lin et al., 2024), and (b) the low gastric pH (< 2), which suppresses the ionization of PP and weakens electrostatic interaction with QAC, leading to partial dissociation of the nanocarrier. Despite this structural change, TP@QAC-PP maintained significant protective capacity, with a TP retention rate of 96.3%—over 30% higher than that of free TP (61.0%) after gastric digestion (Fig. 7B).

Particle size changes of TP@QAC-PP during in vitro simulated digestion (A), retention rate of free TP and TP@QAC-PP during digestion (B), TEM images of TP@QAC-PP before digestion (C), after gastric digestion (D), and after intestinal digestion (E).

During the intestinal digestion phase, the particle size of TP@QAC-PP initially increased to 421.3 nm. This change was likely due to the elevated pH, which enhanced the ionization of QAC and PP, as well as nanocarrier aggregation, possibly induced by bile salt cations (Ai et al., 2025; Kihara et al., 2025). As digestion progressed, particle size decreased to 241.9 nm, attributable to further enzymatic hydrolysis of PP. Although the final TP retention decreased to 59.5%, it remained substantially higher than that of free TP (12.4%), demonstrating the system's sustained protective function.

TEM imaging further confirmed the structural evolution of TP@QAC-PP during digestion. Undigested samples exhibited an irregular agglomerated morphology with discernible particles (Fig. 7C). After gastric digestion, the particulate structure was no longer observed (Fig. 7D). By the end of the intestinal phase, the nanocarrier had decomposed into smaller assemblies (Fig. 7E), which supports the progressive disassembly indicated by the size and retention data.

Simulated release in vitro

Beyond stabilizing bioactive compounds through encapsulation, achieving controlled release is crucial for developing functional nanocarrier systems. The visual changes of the simulated release process of TP@QAC-PP shown in Fig. S3. TP@QAC-PP exhibited an initial burst release, with 21.0% and 19.1% of TP released within the first 30 min under acidic and neutral conditions, respectively (Fig. 8). This rapid release phase can be attributed to the immediate dissolution of surface-associated TP, consistent with the osmotic release mechanism described by Boostani and Jafari (2021). Subsequently, the release rate gradually decreased and reached a plateau after 2 h. In terms of cumulative release, TP@QAC-PP released 49.8% of its payload at pH 7.0, significantly higher than the 45.7% released at pH 2.0. This pH-dependent behavior indicates enhanced release performance in neutral environments, similar to intestinal conditions, which may be advantageous for intestinal-targeted delivery applications. Despite this difference in extent, the overall release profiles followed similar patterns across pH conditions, demonstrating that TP@QAC-PP maintains consistent release kinetics while responding to environmental pH variation.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the structural characteristics, pH-responsive behavior, and functional performance of TP@QAC-PP nanocarrier through comprehensive characterization. Structural analysis revealed significant molecular interactions and conformational changes with increasing pH, evidenced by FTIR blue shifts and transitions to more ordered secondary structures. AFM imaging identified an irregular bead-like morphology with cavity structures, while dialysis experiments confirmed successful encapsulation of TP within the microsphere core. Functional assessment demonstrated notable protective effects during in vitro digestion. The nanocarrier maintained 96.3% TP retention in the gastric phase despite enzymatic degradation and pH-dependent attenuation of electrostatic interactions. Throughout intestinal digestion, TP retention remained substantially higher than that of free TP, confirming the sustained protective capacity of the delivery system. Release studies revealed that TP@QAC-PP displayed burst release followed by sustained kinetics, with significantly higher cumulative release in neutral media compared to acidic conditions. This pH-dependent release profile, combined with the demonstrated structural stability and digestive protection, establishes TP@QAC-PP as a promising delivery system for bioactive compounds in pharmaceutical and food applications, particularly for intestinal-targeted delivery. While the in vitro dynamic digestion model used in this study provides useful insights, further in vivo studies in animal models are necessary to confirm the bioavailability and efficacy of TP@QAC-PP.
