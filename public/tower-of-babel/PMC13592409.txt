Nitrogen‐Doped GeTeS Ovonic Threshold Switching Selector With Optimized Performance Trade‐Off for 1S–1R Phase‐Change Memory
Authors: Min Su Kang, Jong Min Joo, Jun Young Choi, Ji Eun Park, Dong Hyun Kim, Jin Suk Oh, Tae Geun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOvonic threshold switching (OTS) selector devices are critical for suppressing sneak‐path leakage current and ensuring cell selectivity in high‐density crosspoint memory arrays, while enabling nanosecond‐scale switching and low‐power operation. However, amorphous chalcogenide‐based OTS selectors face challenges including limited durability, poor thermal stability, and an inherent trade‐off between switching speed and threshold voltage (V th). To address these issues, this study proposes a Ge–Te–S (GeTeS) mixed composition fabricated by co‐sputtering Te and S, combining the fast switching and low V th of GeTe with the high durability and thermal stability of GeS. Nitrogen‐doped GeTeS (N‐GeTeS) was then developed by incorporating nitrogen into the GeTeS matrix. Nitrogen doping suppresses unstable bonds via strong S─N bond formation, reduces trap density, improves V th uniformity, and widens the band gap to reduce off‐current. The resulting N‐GeTeS OTS device demonstrates excellent durability (≈1010 cycles), high crystallization temperature (≈470°C), fast switching speed (≈18 ns), low V th (≈1.5 V), low leakage current (≈2.8 × 10−9 A), and a high on/off ratio (≈3×105). In a one‐selector‐ one‐resistor configuration integrated with phase‐change memory, the N‐GeTeS selector enables stable memory cell operation through threshold voltage‐controlled current blocking. Overall, N‐GeTeS shows strong potential for scalable, high‐density memory array applications.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), big data, and cloud services has led to an explosive increase in data throughput, further exposing the limitations of the traditional von Neumann architecture, in which computation and memory are physically separated, and data movement itself becomes a performance bottleneck [1, 2]. To alleviate these limitations, a memory expansion and sharing architecture based on compute express link (CXL) has emerged [3]. This architecture enables flexible utilization of memory resources through high‐speed, low‐latency interconnects between processors and memory devices [4]. These trends necessitate next‐generation memory technologies capable of simultaneously overcoming the performance, integration, and non‐volatility limitations of DRAM and NAND Flash. In this context, storage‐class memory (SCM), which aims to combine DRAM‐level speed and endurance with NAND‐level non‐volatility and high density, has been actively investigated as a promising alternative [5, 6, 7]. Representative SCM candidates, including phase‐change RAM (PRAM), resistive RAM (RRAM), magnetoresistive RAM (MRAM), and ferroelectric RAM (FRAM), operate based on distinct physical mechanisms such as phase transition, filament formation and rupture, spin‐dependent tunnel magnetoresistance, and ferroelectric polarization reversal, respectively [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. However, when these memories are scaled into crosspoint array architectures, they commonly suffer from unwanted leakage current flowing through non‐selected cells that share identical word and bit lines [13]. This sneak‐path current induces read disturbances, increases power consumption, and accelerates device degradation, thereby deteriorating overall array performance [14]. To address these challenges, an ovonic threshold switching (OTS) selector device was proposed, as shown in Figure 1a [15].

Electrical conduction in amorphous OTS materials is governed by trap‐mediated transport. Under low electric fields, injected electrons are captured by traps, effectively suppressing current flow. As the electric field increases, the Poole–Frenkel (PF) effect lowers trap barriers, while thermally assisted hopping (TAH) between traps gradually enhances conductivity, as illustrated in Figure 1b [16, 17, 18]. When the applied voltage approaches the threshold voltage (V th), trap emission and hopping processes accelerate abruptly, forming transient conductive paths that lead to a sudden increase in current, referred to as threshold switching (TS), as shown in Figure 1c. This behavior enables the OTS selector to effectively block current through non‐selected cells while delivering sufficient drive current to the selected cell [19].

Based on this conduction mechanism, OTS selectors must simultaneously satisfy multiple electrical performance requirements for stable operation in high‐density 1S–1R memory arrays. In particular, an extremely low off‐state leakage current (I off) is required to effectively suppress sneak currents in unselected cells, and high selectivity is essential to prevent data interference in large‐scale crossbar architectures. Furthermore, high endurance and excellent thermal stability are required to ensure reliability under repetitive switching operations, and maintaining a low threshold voltage is also crucial to enable low‐power operation within the system's voltage range. Because these requirements are intrinsically determined by charge transport characteristics and the electronic structure of materials, they often exhibit inherent trade‐offs. Therefore, the development of high‐performance OTS selectors requires not only the improvement of individual performance metrics but also a comprehensive material‐ and device‐level optimization strategy that considers the interrelationships among charge transport properties, defect states, thermal stability, and operating voltage [20].

Among the reported OTS materials, GeTe‐ and GeS‐based devices exhibit trade‐offs among the key performance parameters required for OTS selectors, including endurance, thermal stability, leakage current, switching speed, and threshold voltage [21, 22]. These competing characteristics fundamentally limit the realization of high‐performance OTS devices. GeTe2 exhibits fast switching speed and a low V th owing to its relatively narrow bandgap (≈0.67 eV) and low activation energy (≈0.21 eV). However, its weak atomic bonding makes it vulnerable to poor thermal stability and limited endurance. Repeated electrical pulsing can induce atomic rearrangement and localized crystallization through Joule heating, leading to performance degradation [23]. In contrast, GeS2 demonstrates excellent thermal and structural stability, as well as high endurance, owing to its strong bond energy (≈31.3 eV) and low trap density (≈7.27 × 1017 cm−3). Nevertheless, its wide bandgap and limited charge transport pathways result in a relatively high threshold voltage and slow switching speed [24].

To overcome these intrinsic material trade‐offs, this study proposes a novel OTS material based on a Ge–Te–S mixed composition (GeTeS), synthesized through the co‐sputtering of Te and S. The proposed GeTeS material integrates the fast switching characteristics of GeTe2 with the structural stability of GeS2. As a result, GeTeS achieves a lower V th and faster switching speed than GeS2 while providing improved endurance, thermal stability, and reduced leakage current compared with GeTe2. Furthermore, nitrogen (N) doping was introduced to address the remaining challenges associated with thermal instability, critical voltage fluctuation, device uniformity, and off‐state leakage current in GeTeS. Nitrogen acts as a bond‐stabilizing dopant that promotes bond rearrangement within the amorphous network, suppressing structurally unstable bonds such as S─S and S─Te while facilitating the formation of a more stable bonding configuration [25]. This modification reduces defect‐related trap states, thereby improving switching‐path reproducibility and enhancing device uniformity by narrowing the critical voltage distribution. From an electronic structure perspective, nitrogen incorporation increases the effective energy barrier, suppressing thermally and electrically induced charge injection below the critical voltage and consequently reducing the off‐state leakage current [26].

In addition, an Al2O3‐based buffer layer was introduced to further enhance operating characteristics by reducing leakage current and improving interface stability [27, 28]. As illustrated in Figure 1d, an N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS device incorporating this buffer layer was fabricated on a Si/SiO2 substrate with a device critical dimension (CD) of 5 µm. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the formation of an amorphous N‐GeTeS film, while energy‐dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping verified the presence of well‐defined and unmixed layers, as shown in Figure S1. To elucidate the conduction mechanism, trap‐related parameters were extracted using the TAH and PF models, providing insight into charge transport behavior. Furthermore, the proposed OTS selector was integrated with Sb2Te3‐based phase‐change memory (PCM) to demonstrate stable operation in one selector‐one resistor (1S–1R) configurations. In summary, the N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS device effectively mitigates the performance trade‐offs observed in previously reported OTS materials. Owing to its enhanced electrical performance and reliability, it exhibits strong potential for scalability in next‐generation high‐density crosspoint memory array applications, and establishes a viable material and device strategy for reliable OTS selector integration in advanced SCM architectures [29].

Discussion

Exploration of GeTe, GeS, and GeTeS Chalcogenide Materials

This study systematically compared the material properties of representative GeTe (Te‐based) and GeS (S‐based) OTS materials, along with the mixed‐composition GeTeS proposed in this research. These three materials influence the core performance metrics required for OTS selectors, i.e., durability, thermal stability, threshold voltage, and switching speed, in different ways, arising from fundamental differences in bonding strength, crystallization temperature, trap characteristics, and electronic band structure [30]. XPS analysis confirmed that the deposited Ge–Te and Ge–S films exhibited Ge:Te and Ge:S atomic ratios of approximately 1:2, respectively, indicating Te‐rich and S‐rich compositions (Figure S2a,b) [31]. Furthermore, bonding‐state analysis revealed that the binding energies of the characteristic peaks were located at relatively higher values in GeS2 (≈31.3 eV) than in GeTe2 (≈30.4 eV). GeTeS, which exhibited intermediate binding energies between those of GeS2 and GeTe2, demonstrated a more stabilized bonding environment (Figure 2a–c). This shift in binding energy suggests that the S‐based network possesses stronger bonding interactions than the Te‐based network [32]. Consequently, GeTeS partially reflects the bonding‐strengthening effect of S, enhancing resistance to bond fracture and atomic rearrangement under repeated pulse application [33]. In other words, the increased bonding stability observed via XPS provides physical evidence that GeTeS maintains a more stable amorphous network under repeated switching stress compared to GeTe2. Thermal stability was evaluated through the crystallization behavior of the thin films. Resistance–temperature (R–T) analysis identified a transition region in which resistance changes sharply with increasing temperature, enabling quantitative comparison of the crystallization temperature (Tc) for each material (Figure 2d–f). Generally, a higher Tc indicates greater thermal stability of the amorphous phase, thereby reducing structural degradation and crystallization induced by Joule heating during device operation [34]. In this comparison, GeTe2 exhibits a relatively low Tc (≈200°C), suggesting a greater susceptibility to thermally induced structural transformation. In contrast, GeS2 demonstrates excellent thermal stability owing to its high Tc (>500°C). To further verify the crystallization behavior of the GeS2 film, additional XRD measurements were performed after annealing at various temperatures (Figure S3). No distinct crystalline diffraction peaks were observed up to 600°C, indicating that the crystallization temperature of the GeS2 film exceeds 500°C and that the amorphous structure is largely preserved throughout the investigated temperature range [24]. Notably, GeTeS exhibits an increased Tc (≈380°C) compared with GeTe2, demonstrating that the mixed‐composition design effectively mitigates the limitations associated with the thermal instability of GeTe2. This improvement provides a physical basis that is consistent with the enhanced durability observed in the device characteristics.

Finally, to elucidate differences in electronic transport affecting switching speed and threshold voltage, TAH conduction model‐based fitting (Figure S4) and UV–vis‐NIR analysis (Figure S5) were performed (Figure 2g–i) [35]. The activation energy (E a), average inter‐trap distance (Δz), and trap density (N t) extracted from TAH fitting quantitatively reflect the trap distribution and extent of conduction path formation in each material. E a represents the average energy required for an electron trapped in a state to hop to an adjacent trap or a conduction‐contributing state and can be extracted from temperature‐dependent I–V measurements. This is typically done using the Arrhenius equation: (1) lnR=lnR0+Ea1kT where R0 is the pre‐exponential factor, k is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the absolute temperature.

Δz represents the average distance an electron must travel between adjacent trap sites during thermal activation hopping. It can be determined from the subthreshold slope (STS) of the I–V curve under low electric fields: (2) STS=∂logI∂V=qkTΔz2d where d is the thin film thickness, set to 40 nm for these calculations.

N t represents the number of traps per unit volume. When Δz and Ea are known, N t can be estimated using: (3) I=2qANtΔzτ0exp−EC−EFkTsinhqVakTΔz2d where q is the elementary charge, A is the device area, τ0 is the escape attempt time for trapped carriers, (Ec–EF) is the energy difference between the conduction band and the Fermi level, k is the Boltzmann constant, T is the absolute temperature, and Va is the applied voltage.

Generally, shallow traps (low E a), small Δz, and high N t facilitate the formation of trap‐mediated conduction paths even under low electric fields, thereby reducing V th and accelerating the switching process. In contrast, deep traps (high E a), large Δz, and low N t require a stronger electric field to establish conductive pathways, resulting in an increased V th and slower switching dynamics [36]. In this comparison, GeTe2 exhibits trap characteristics favorable for charge transport (E a ≈ 0.21 eV, Δz ≈ 4.59 nm, N t ≈ 2.12 × 1018 cm−3) along with a narrow bandgap (E g ≈ 0.67 eV), which promotes rapid switching and low‐voltage operation. Conversely, GeS2 exhibits a more restrictive trap distribution (E a ≈ 0.53 eV, Δz ≈ 6.54 nm, N t ≈ 7.27 × 1017 cm−3) and a wide band gap (E g ≈ 1.57 eV), resulting in reduced leakage current and enhanced structural stability but limiting charge transport efficiency, thereby leading to a higher V th and slower switching behavior. GeTeS demonstrates intermediate trap characteristics (E a ≈ 0.32 eV, Δz ≈ 5.43 nm, N t ≈ 1.42 × 1018 cm−3) and an optimized bandgap (E g ≈ 1.13 eV), effectively balancing the advantages of both parent materials (Figure 2g–i). Consequently, GeTeS establishes a performance balance that enables a lower V th and faster switching speed than GeS2 while simultaneously providing improved leakage characteristics and enhanced stability compared with GeTe2. This analysis of the correlation between physical properties and electrical performance confirms that the GeTeS mixed‐composition design can effectively mitigate trade‐offs between key OTS selector performance indicators. The most favorable characteristics were observed at a Ge:Te:S = 4:3:2 composition ratio (Figure S6), consistent with the device characteristics, including current–voltage behavior, switching speed, and durability, presented in subsequent sections.

Electrical Performance of GeTe‐, GeS‐, and GeTeS‐Based OTS Devices

To compare device performance, GeTe‐, GeS‐, and GeTeS‐based devices were fabricated under identical conditions. Each device was formed on a Si/SiO2 substrate with a uniform 5 nm Al2O3‐based buffer layer and a 40 nm OTS material layer, and a CD of 5 µm, varying only the type of OTS material (Figure S7). I–V measurements (Figure 3a–c) show that the GeTe2 device exhibits a relatively low V th (≈ 0.8 V), favoring low‐voltage operation. However, it also displays a relatively high leakage current (≈ 1.6 × 10−6 A) in the subthreshold region, limiting the selectivity ratio (≈ 6 × 102). In contrast, the GeS2 device has a higher V th (≈ 4.0 V) but maintains an extremely low leakage current (≈ 1.5 × 10−9 A) below the threshold, resulting in excellent nonlinearity and a high selectivity ratio (≈ 6 × 105). The GeTeS device achieves a balance between these extremes, exhibiting improved leakage and selectivity while retaining low‐voltage characteristics. It achieves a lower V th (≈ 1.3 V) compared to GeS2, while suppressing leakage current (≈ 1.2 × 10−8 A) and enhancing selectivity (≈ 8 × 104) relative to GeTe2. These differences in I–V behavior correspond to variations in trap distribution and band structure described earlier. GeTe2 exhibits low V th due to its relatively high trap density and shallow traps, facilitating charge transport at low electric fields [37]. GeS2, with its low trap density, deep traps, and wide band gap, requires a higher electric field to form conduction paths, resulting in a higher V th while effectively suppressing subthreshold leakage [38]. GeTeS, with its mixed Te–S composition, simultaneously adjusts trap‐mediated conduction pathways and barrier characteristics, optimizing both low‐voltage operation and low leakage. Switching speed measurements (Figure 3d–f) further highlight material differences. GeTe2 devices achieve relatively fast switching (≈ 10 ns) due to rapid conduction path formation, while GeS2 devices switch more slowly (≈ 25 ns) because of larger trap spacing and higher activation barriers. GeTeS presents an intermediate performance, achieving faster switching (≈ 14 ns) compared to GeS2. Durability under repeated write pulses (Figure 3g–i) also differs among materials. GeTe2 devices exhibit critical voltage fluctuations and current degradation at relatively early cycles (≈ 7 × 107), whereas GeS2 devices maintain stable switching over a longer cycle range (≈ 2 × 1010). This is attributed to the strongly bonded network of GeS2, which suppresses atomic diffusion and structural rearrangement even under line heating during current drive [39]. Notably, the GeTeS‐based device shows significantly improved durability (≈ 3 × 109) compared to GeTe2, resulting from enhanced bonding stability in the mixed composition, which mitigates structural degradation of the amorphous network under repeated drive stress. In summary, GeTe2 is advantageous for low‐voltage, high‐speed operation but suffers from high leakage and limited reliability. GeS2 provides low leakage and high stability but is constrained by high V th and slower switching. GeTeS offers a promising platform that mitigates trade‐offs among key OTS selector performance metrics, simultaneously achieving low V th, low I off, high selectivity, fast switching, and enhanced durability. Despite these improvements, GeTeS‐based OTS selectors still face challenges for array applications, including critical voltage spread, uniformity, and off‐state leakage. To address these limitations, this study proposes a doping‐based strategy to further enhance device performance [40].

Exploration of GeTeS and N‐doped GeTeS Chalcogenide Materials

To address the remaining challenges of GeTeS‐based OTS materials, i.e., thermal stability, critical voltage dispersion, uniformity, and off‐state leakage, N doping was introduced. The bonding state and charge transport characteristics of GeTeS and N‐GeTeS were compared and analyzed.

XPS analysis (Figure 4a,b) reveals an additional bonding peak in the S 2p region after N doping, which is distinct from the existing bonding components, indicating the formation of new S─N bonds within the amorphous network. The S─N bond exhibits a relatively high binding energy (≈166.2 eV), suggesting strong covalent bonding interactions. The introduction of these stable bonds enhances the structural integrity of the amorphous network by suppressing bond breaking and atomic rearrangement under electrical stress. Meanwhile, the proportion of unstable bonds (S─S and S─Te) decreases, whereas the fraction of stable S─N bonds increases, indicating a transition toward a more robust bonding configuration that is favorable for improved durability during repeated switching cycles. This structural stabilization is further supported by quantitative analysis of the trap characteristics [41]. Comparison of N t extracted through fitting based on the TAH conduction model (Figure S8) shows that N‐GeTeS exhibits a lower trap density than GeTeS (≈ 1.4 × 1018 cm−3 → ≈ 6.2 × 1017 cm−3), indicating a reduction in defect‐related states within the amorphous network (Figure 4c). The reduced trap density enhances the reproducibility of critical conduction pathways, thereby suppressing inter‐cell and inter‐cycle variations in V th and improving operational stability. Therefore, N doping not only improves the average electrical performance of the device but also enhances device‐to‐device uniformity and reliability, which are critical requirements for large‐scale array applications. Finally, UV–vis‐NIR analysis (Figure 4d) shows that the E g increases after N doping (≈ 1.13 eV → ≈ 1.25 eV). This expansion raises the energy barrier, suppressing thermal and electric field‐induced charge injection in the subthreshold region. The larger band gap reduces off‐state leakage by weakening conduction and trap‐assisted leakage pathways, improving blocking characteristics and selectivity under low‐voltage operation. The optimal device performance was observed at a Ge:Te:S:N = 4.5:3:2:0.5 composition ratio (Figure S9). In summary, N doping simultaneously provides structural stabilization through S─N bond formation, reduces V th dispersion via lower trap density, and suppresses leakage current through band gap expansion. The following section details how these material improvements translate into enhanced electrical performance at the device level.

Result

Electrical Performance of GeTeS‐ and N‐doped GeTeS‐Based OTS Device

This study systematically evaluated the impact of N doping on the electrical performance and reliability of GeTeS‐based OTS selectors. Figure 5a compares the current–voltage (I–V) characteristics of GeTeS and N‐doped GeTeS devices. The GeTeS device exhibits abrupt switching at a low V th (≈ 1.3 V) but shows a limited leakage current (≈ 1.2 × 10−8 A) in the subthreshold region. In contrast, the N‐doped GeTeS device maintains low‐voltage switching (≈ 1.5 V) while significantly suppressing subthreshold leakage (≈ 2.8 × 10−9 A), resulting in an improved selectivity ratio (≈ 3 × 105). The enhanced blocking characteristics in N‐GeTeS directly contribute to reducing sneak path currents and expanding read margins in crosspoint array operation [42]. Durability under repeated switching cycles is shown in Figure 5b. The GeTeS device exhibits gradual shifts in critical voltage and degradation in switching current during repeated cycling, whereas the N‐doped GeTeS device maintains stable switching characteristics over an extended cycling range (≈ 3 × 109 → ≈ 1 × 1010 cycles). To evaluate the compositional stability and reliability of the N‐GeTeS film during repeated switching operation, cross‐sectional TEM‐EDS line profile analysis was performed for both the initial state and the state after 1010 switching cycles (Figure S10). No significant changes in the elemental distributions of Ge, Te, S, and N were observed after cycling. These results demonstrate that the N‐GeTeS film maintains excellent compositional stability during repeated switching operation, with no observable evidence of significant Te migration even after 1010 switching cycles. This enhanced stability is attributed to the formation of strong S─N bonds induced by N doping, which reinforces the amorphous network and suppresses structural degradation during electrical cycling. The strengthened network effectively resists bond deformation and atomic migration under repeated pulse stress, minimizing structural distortion. N doping thus alleviates durability limitations of GeTeS, supporting the long‐term reliability required for array operation. Additional stabilization effects from N doping are evident in R–T measurements (Figure 5c). N‐GeTeS exhibits smaller resistance changes than GeTeS at elevated temperatures, indicating enhanced thermal and structural stability due to an increased Tc (≈ 380°C → ≈ 470°C). Cumulative distribution function (CDF) analysis further shows that N‐GeTeS has a narrower V th distribution (≈ 8.13% → ≈ 5.67%), confirming improved cell‐to‐cell uniformity and operational reproducibility (Figure 5d). This effect is attributed to N doping homogenizing defect (trap) distributions, leading to more consistent critical conduction path formation and reduced V th deviation. Switching speed measurements (Figure 5e) show that N‐GeTeS maintains nanosecond‐level operation (≈ 18 ns) but is slightly slower than GeTeS (≈ 14 ns). This is attributed to delayed conduction path formation resulting from increased band gap and reduced trap density. Finally, Figure 5f quantifies the variation in critical voltage as a function of N doping concentration. Increasing N content enhances voltage uniformity while gradually increasing V th, indicating improved reliability through the formation of stabilized bonding configurations, reduced trap density, and an increased energy barrier associated with the widened bandgap. To provide a comprehensive comparison of the device performance, a radar chart was constructed by normalizing the key OTS performance metrics (Figure S11). The radar chart demonstrates that N‐GeTeS achieves the most balanced performance among the evaluated parameters, including leakage current, threshold voltage, thermal stability, endurance, switching speed, and selectivity. These results confirm that optimized N doping simultaneously improves durability, operational reliability, and leakage suppression, highlighting the potential of N‐GeTeS as a selector device for 1S–1R cross‐point array applications.

Application of 1S‐1R Device Using OTS Selector and PCM

The previous section demonstrated that the N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS selector achieves superior performance metrics at the single‐device level compared to previously reported devices (Table 1). This section evaluates the operating characteristics of a 1S–1R device, in which an N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS selector is integrated in series with an Sb2Te3‐based PCM, to verify whether these material and electrical advantages are preserved in an actual 1S–1R structure. Figure 6a shows the structural schematic along with cross‐sectional TEM and EDS analysis of the 1S–1R device. The stacked structure, comprising an Sb2Te3‐based PCM, intermediate electrode, buffer layer, N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS layer, and top electrode on a W heater substrate, is clearly observed. EDS mapping confirms that each layer maintains the desired thickness and composition without interfacial diffusion (Figure S12). Figure 6b compares the I–V characteristics of a single Sb2Te3‐based PCM device and a single N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS device. The PCM device exhibits a sharp current increase at a specific voltage corresponding to the amorphous‐to‐crystalline phase transition, along with relatively high leakage current at low voltages. In contrast, the OTS device suppresses current to the nA level until reaching V th, followed by a sharp current surge upon threshold attainment. This nonlinear behavior satisfies the critical requirement for suppressing sneak currents in non‐selected cells within a 1S–1R configuration [43, 44].

Figure 6c demonstrates that the N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS selector effectively controls the programming of the PCM in the 1S–1R structure. Analysis of the I–V curve shows that in the low‐voltage region (< 0.5 V), the current is blocked below 10 nA while the OTS remains in the OFF state. Upon reaching the OTS threshold voltage, the selector turns on, supplying sufficient current to the PCM. The phase change depends on the write pulse conditions: low voltage with a long pulse induces a SET (crystalline) transition by reaching the crystallization temperature, whereas high voltage with a short pulse induces a RESET (amorphous) transition by locally reaching the melting temperature [45]. Thus, in the 1S–1R structure, SET and RESET operations are clearly distinguished. By utilizing the memory window (≈ 0.8 V) between VSET (≈ 2.1 V) and VRESET (≈ 2.9 V) as the read voltage, the memory state can be reliably read. These results confirm that the N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS enables stable PCM write and read operations in the 1S–1R structure, benefiting from its excellent leakage suppression. To verify current blocking and PCM programming behavior in the 1S–1R structure, the current flow before and after the OTS V th (OFF/ON states) and the Joule heating distribution during RESET operation were analyzed using COMSOL Multiphysics thermal‐electrical simulations. As shown in Figure 6d, when the OTS selector is below the critical voltage (OFF state), current is effectively blocked, resulting in minimal Joule heating. The maximum temperature at the center of the Sb2Te3 core is approximately 420 K, significantly below the melting temperature required for RESET, confirming that leakage current to non‐selected cells is suppressed and unintended programming is prevented. Conversely, when the OTS selector exceeds V th (ON state), current increases sharply and concentrates in the narrow W heater region. The resulting current density generates strong Joule heating, raising the Sb2Te3 core temperature to approximately 850 K, sufficient for RESET operation. These simulations quantitatively demonstrate that the N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS effectively blocks current in the OFF state and generates sufficient Joule heating only in the ON state. In a crosspoint array, this ensures precise programming of selected cells while suppressing sneak path currents in non‐selected cells, resolving crosstalk issues. Figure 6e compares the endurance of 1S–1R devices based on different OTS materials. The 1S–1R device using a GeTe2‐OTS exhibits characteristic degradation at an early cycle (≈ 3 × 103 cycles) during repeated operation, attributed to cumulative thermal stress from Joule heating in the Sb2Te3 PCM. This stress induces structural changes in the OTS layer (amorphous → crystalline), degrading device performance. In contrast, the 1S–1R device using an N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS maintains stable operation up to ≈ 106 cycles, comparable to conventional Sb2Te3‐based PCMs (Figure S13). This confirms that the N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS provides thermal and structural stability to withstand PCM operating conditions, making it a suitable selector for thermal‐driven PCM environments.

Figure 6f compares the required SET voltage as a function of PCM SET speed for 1S–1R structures employing different OTS selectors with the same Sb2Te3‐based PCM (Figure S14). Across all devices, SET pulse width decreased as the applied voltage increased. However, the required voltage for the same SET speed varied with the OTS material. The GeS2‐OTS required 4.6 and 5.8 V for 300 and 50 ns pulse widths, respectively, whereas the GeTe2‐OTS required 1.7 and 2.5 V. Notably, the N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS enabled SET at 2.1 and 3.2 V for the same pulse widths, achieving the same SET speed at approximately 2.5 V lower than the GeS2‐based 1S–1R structure. This demonstrates faster SET operation with reduced voltage stress compared to GeS2. Figure 6g compares RESET energy for different OTS selectors in the same 1S–1R structure. The RESET pulse width was fixed at t = 300 ns (Figure S15), with RESET induced by Joule heating in the Sb2Te3 PCM. All 1S–1R devices exhibit similar RESET current (IRESET ≈ 0.6 mA) due to the PCM's intrinsic properties. The calculated RESET energies are: GeTe2 = 0.44 nJ, GeS2 = 1.10 nJ, GeTeS = 0.50 nJ, and N‐GeTeS = 0.53 nJ. Conclusions

This study presents a material design strategy to address the performance trade‐offs in OTS selector devices, a critical challenge for next‐generation crosspoint memory arrays. Conventional GeTe and GeS materials exhibit conflicting characteristics in endurance, switching speed, and threshold voltage, limiting their practical application. The proposed GeTeS mixed composition combines the advantages of both materials, achieving a performance balance that simultaneously enhances durability and thermal stability while maintaining high‐speed, low‐voltage operation. Although the GeTeS composition partially mitigates these trade‐offs, N doping was introduced to further improve thermal stability, reduce critical voltage variation, and suppress leakage current. N doping strengthens the amorphous network through the formation of robust S─N bonds and improves operational reproducibility and uniformity by reducing trap density. As a result, N‐GeTeS exhibits excellent durability, high thermal stability, fast switching, low threshold voltage, high selectivity, and extremely low leakage current as a single OTS device. To demonstrate practical applicability, a 1S–1R device integrating N‐GeTeS with an Sb2Te3‐based PCM was fabricated and evaluated. Analysis confirmed that the N‐doped selector effectively blocks leakage current in the off state while enabling stable PCM programming in the on state through precise Joule heating control. Notably, the device maintained excellent durability under thermal stress and achieved a fast SET speed with approximately 49% lower RESET energy compared to GeS2‐devices, demonstrating low‐power operation. In conclusion, the N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS selector effectively mitigates trade‐offs among key performance metrics while satisfying the low‐leakage, high‐reliability requirements of 1S–1R integrated structures. This platform meets essential selector performance criteria for large‐area crosspoint arrays and provides a practical solution for advancing the commercialization of high‐density memory technologies.

Experimental Details

OTS Selector and 1S–1R Device Fabrication Process

The OTS selector device was fabricated on an Si/SiO2 substrate using an RF magnetron sputtering process. First, a 50 nm thick TiN layer was deposited as the bottom electrode. After alignment, a 5 nm Al2O3‐based buffer layer was formed. Subsequently, a 40 nm N‐doped GeTeS‐based OTS layer was deposited by co‐sputtering. Finally, a 50 nm thick TiN top electrode was deposited to complete device fabrication. No post‐deposition annealing was performed to preserve the amorphous state of the film. The detailed fabrication process is shown in Figure S16. For fabrication of the 1S–1R device, substrate preparation based on a heater structure was first required. Tungsten (W) was deposited onto a SiO2/Si wafer using chemical vapor deposition (CVD). A SiO2 insulating layer was formed through a shallow trench isolation (STI) process employing high‐density plasma (HDP) technology. Nanoscale vias were then patterned in the SiO2 layer using electron beam lithography and etching and subsequently filled with W to form the heater structure. The substrate was planarized by chemical mechanical planarization (CMP), yielding W heaters with a diameter of approximately 100 nm, as shown in Figure S17. Following heater formation, the device was fabricated on the heater substrate using RF magnetron sputtering. A 70 nm Sb2Te3‐based PCM layer was first deposited, followed by a 50 nm thick TiN intermediate electrode. A 5 nm thick Al2O3‐based buffer layer was then formed, followed by deposition of a 40 nm N‐GeTeS‐based OTS layer via co‐sputtering. Finally, 50 nm thick TiN was deposited as the bottom and top electrodes, completing the 1S–1R device structure. No post‐deposition annealing was performed to maintain the amorphous state of the films. The detailed fabrication process is shown in Figure S18.

Electrical Measurements

The electrical characteristics of the OTS selector device, PCM, and 1S–1R device were measured using a Keithley 4200A‐SCS parameter analyzer equipped with a 4225‐PMU ultra‐fast I–V module and a 4225‐RPM remote amplifier and switch module. This system enabled nanosecond‐scale pulse generation with a minimum pulse width of approximately 20 ns and supported DC measurements, AC voltage sweeps, pulse detection, and waveform capture. In this study, various voltage pulses were applied for SET and RESET operations, and a read voltage was used to evaluate memory states. Under these conditions, I–V, R–V, R–T, and R–I characteristics were measured with high precision.

Characterization

Cross‐sectional specimens for high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) were prepared using a focused ion beam (FIB) system (Hitachi‐NX5000) equipped with a liquid gallium liquid metal ion source operated at an acceleration voltage of 30 kV. HRTEM imaging was performed using a field‐emission transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai F20 G2, 200 kV) for nanoscale structural analysis. Elemental mapping was conducted using energy‐dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDS). X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using a Nexsa G2 system equipped with a monochromatized Al Kα x‐ray source operated at 1486.6 eV, 24.5 W, and 15 kV. The system provided a spatial resolution ranging from 10 to 400 µm. Depth profiling was carried out using a MAGCIS dual‐mode ion source with a pass energy of 58.70 eV. The Ge 3d spectrum was measured over a binding energy range of 25–35 eV with a step size of 0.1 eV, while the S 2p spectrum was measured over the range of 158–170 eV. UV–vis‐NIR spectroscopy was employed to determine the optical energy band gap (E g) of the OTS material. Transmission spectra were collected using a UV–vis‐NIR spectrometer over the wavelength range of 300–2000 nm. The absorption coefficient was calculated from the measured transmittance at each wavelength, and the optical E g was extracted using the Tauc method.

Thermal Simulation

A coupled thermal‐electrical simulation was performed using COMSOL Multiphysics to investigate line heating effects in the PCM device within the 1S–1R configuration. The thermal response was analyzed using the heat transfer equation shown in Equation (5): (5) ρcp∂T∂t−∇·κ∇T=Q where ρ denotes mass density, cp is specific heat capacity, κ represents thermal conductivity, T is temperature, and Q corresponds to the applied heat energy due to Joule heating. The material properties of the Sb2Te3‐based PCM device were as follows: density of 6500 kg/m3, specific heat capacity of 209 J/kg·K, and thermal conductivity of 3.3 W/m·K. The simulated device structure consisted of 50 nm thick bottom, middle, and top electrode layers, a 70 nm thick PCM layer, a 5 nm thick buffer layer, and a 40 nm thick OTS selector layer. This simulation enabled analysis of thermal response and heat distribution induced by line heating in the 1S–1R device, ensuring realistic and reliable simulation results.
