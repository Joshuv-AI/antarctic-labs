Double mini-locking plates for unstable Danis-Weber B distal fibular fractures: superior functional outcomes and lower implant removal rates
Authors: Xiao Wang, Fei Xiao, Zhongsheng Wei, Yongchang Chen, Haibo Xu, Lin Wei, Guoqing Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnstable Danis–Weber B distal fibular fractures require anatomical reduction. However, conventional lateral locking plates (LLP) are frequently associated with soft-tissue irritation, high implant removal rates, and limitations in rotational stability. This study aimed to evaluate whether a low-profile double mini-locking plates (DMLP) provides superior clinical outcomes compared to LLP.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study of 120 patients with unstable Danis–Weber B fractures treated between 2016 and 2020. Patients were divided into DMLP and LLP groups according to the fixation method employed. Associated posterior and medial malleolar fractures were treated as needed. Outcome measures included time to radiographic union, AOFAS ankle–hindfoot scores, complications, and implant removal rates over 36 months.ResultsThe DMLP group achieved significantly faster radiographic union than the LLP group (12.35 vs. 13.48 weeks, p = 0.032). Functionally, patients treated with DMLP demonstrated significantly higher AOFAS ankle–hindfoot scores from 3 months post-operatively through the final follow-up (p < 0.05). Although the DMLP technique required a longer operative time (p < 0.001), the overall complication rate was significantly lower (5.0% vs. 15.0%, p = 0.049). Notably, implant removal was performed significantly less frequently in the DMLP group (41.7%) than in the LLP group (65.0%) (p = 0.009).ConclusionsDMLP showed accelerated union and superior functional recovery compared to LLP. Although the operative time was longer, the technique appeared to be associated with fewer soft-tissue complications and a lower rate of secondary implant removal. DMLP may serve as a viable alternative for unstable fractures, though prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Open reduction and internal fixation remain the standard treatments for unstable Danis-Weber B fractures [3, 7]. Conventional lateral locking plates (LLP) are widely used, yet limitations persist regarding rotational control and implant-related morbidity [5, 8]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in biomechanically demanding scenarios, such as fractures with a low fracture line, severe comminution, or poor bone quality, where limited distal bone stock is available for screw fixation and adequate stability can be difficult to achieve [5]. Hardware prominence, soft-tissue irritation, wound complications, and the need for secondary implant removal remain common concerns in clinical practice [8, 9].

Double mini-locking plates (DMLP) have been proposed to improve multiplanar stability while minimizing implant prominence [10]. However, whether these potential mechanical advantages translate into superior clinical outcomes and fewer implant-related complications compared with LLP remains insufficiently established. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the clinical and implant-related outcomes of DMLP for Danis-Weber B distal fibular fractures and to compare these results with LLP.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This study was a retrospective cohort study. Consecutive patients with distal fibular fractures who underwent surgical fixation between June 2016 and March 2020 at a single center were screened for eligibility. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (Approval No. 2025-QX-001), and all procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion criteria were as follows: distal fibular fractures classified as Danis–Weber B and Lauge–Hansen supination-external rotation (SER) stage II, III, or IV on preoperative radiographs and computed tomography (CT); age 18 years or older; intact preinjury ankle function; surgical treatment within 14 days of injury; and complete clinical and radiographic follow-up data.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: open or pathological fracture; concomitant neurovascular injury requiring repair; severe medical comorbidity precluding surgery; preexisting ankle arthritis, hindfoot deformity, or functional impairment of the affected extremity; and concomitant injuries affecting the same extremity.

Surgical procedures and postoperative management

Patients were categorized into one of two groups based on the fixation strategy employed: the DMLP group using combined posterior and lateral plates (Fig. 1), or the LLP group alone. The choice of fixation method was not randomized, but rather determined by surgeon preference and implant availability at the time of surgery. In both groups, associated posterior malleolar and medial malleolar fractures, when present, were addressed according to fracture morphology using screws, plates, or suture anchor repair as appropriate, and syndesmotic stabilization was performed when indicated.

All operations were carried out by the same team of experienced orthopaedic surgeons under spinal or general anesthesia. Patients were positioned supine or in lateral decubitus depending on fracture morphology and surgical approach. A lateral or posterolateral approach was used to expose the distal fibula, followed by careful soft-tissue handling, anatomical fracture reduction, and temporary stabilization. Definitive fixation was then applied according to the assigned strategy. No postoperative cast immobilization was used in either group.

Early functional rehabilitation was initiated in all patients. Active ankle range-of-motion exercises commenced on postoperative day 3. After wound healing, partial weight-bearing with crutches was permitted based on the treating surgeon’s clinical assessment. Following consistent principles across both groups, the timing and extent of weight-bearing were individualized according to fracture type, radiographic assessment, fixation stability, and patient tolerance, with gradual progression to full weight-bearing during follow-up.

Outcome assessment and follow-up

Clinical and radiographic follow-up evaluations were performed at 1, 3, and 12 months post-operatively in all patients. Subsequent follow-up was stratified according to implant status. For patients in whom the implant was retained, follow-up continued until the final visit at 36 months. For patients who underwent implant removal, functional assessment was performed immediately before removal and at 3 months post-removal.

Ankle function was evaluated at each follow-up visit using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle–hindfoot score. Fracture union was assessed by serial plain radiographs, with CT performed when radiographic findings were equivocal. Radiographic criteria for fracture healing included the formation of continuous trabeculae.

Implant removal was not performed routinely. The decision to remove the implant was based on patient preference, implant-related discomfort, or implant-related complications, as determined through shared decision-making between the patient and the treating orthopaedic surgeon following confirmation of fracture healing. Patients were closely monitored for postoperative complications throughout the follow-up period, including but not limited to wound complications, infection, implant-related skin irritation, hardware loosening, delayed union, and nonunion.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared between groups using the independent-samples t-test, except for days from injury to surgery, which was analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and proportions and were compared using the chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies were small. Subgroup analyses for time to union and complication rates were stratified by fracture classification. A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and perioperative data

A total of 120 patients were included in the final analysis, with 60 in the DMLP group and 60 in the LLP group. Baseline demographic and injury-related characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, fracture classification, and injury mechanism, were comparable between the two groups (all p > 0.05; Table 1).

Regarding perioperative outcomes, the DMLP group had a significantly longer operative time and greater intraoperative blood loss than the LLP group (both p < 0.05), whereas the interval from injury to surgery did not differ significantly between groups (p > 0.05; Table 1).

Fracture union was achieved in all 120 patients. The mean time to radiographic union was significantly shorter in the DMLP group than in the LLP group (p = 0.032; Table 2), with the difference most pronounced in SER IV fractures (p < 0.001).

Ankle function improved progressively over time in both groups. At 1 month post-operatively, there was no significant difference in AOFAS ankle–hindfoot scores between the DMLP and LLP groups (p > 0.05). However, significant between-group differences emerged at 3 months and persisted through the final follow-up (all p < 0.05; Table 2).

In the subgroup of patients who underwent implant removal, AOFAS ankle–hindfoot scores at the time of removal and at 3 months post-removal were significantly higher in the DMLP group than in the LLP group (both p < 0.05). In patients who did not undergo implant removal, the functional advantage of the DMLP group persisted through the final follow-up at 36 months (p < 0.05; Table 2).

Implant removal was performed significantly less frequently in the DMLP group than in the LLP group (41.7% vs. 65.0%; p = 0.009; Table 3). In both groups, patient preference was the most common indication. Removals due to implant-related discomfort or complications were more frequently reported in the conventional plate group. No major adverse events were associated with implant removal in either group.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we aimed to evaluate the clinical and implant-related outcomes of DMLP compared with LLP for unstable Danis-Weber B fractures. The principal findings demonstrate that DMLP provides superior functional recovery, faster radiographic union, and significantly fewer implant complications. Specifically, the DMLP group achieved consistently higher AOFAS ankle–hindfoot scores from 3 months post-operatively through the 36-month follow-up, independent of subsequent hardware removal. Furthermore, the overall complication rate and the need for secondary implant removal were substantially lower in the DMLP cohort. These findings suggest that the low-profile, multiplanar design of DMLP may help address rotational instability and reduce hardware-related morbidity traditionally associated with LLP.

The superior early functional recovery observed in the DMLP group may be primarily attributable to a potentially optimized construct stability against rotational forces. This theoretical mechanical advantage might be particularly relevant given the limited bone stock available in the distal fibular fragment of Danis-Weber B fractures. Conventional LLPs are inherently constrained by their uniplanar screw trajectory, limiting the screw–bone interface area within the small distal fragment and potentially compromising fixation against the external rotational torque characteristic of this injury mechanism, particularly in osteoporotic bone [5, 11]. In contrast, the DMLP construct employs an orthogonal fixation strategy that likely increases the effective contact area between the implants and the bone. As validated in prior biomechanical investigations, this multiplanar configuration increases construct stiffness and load-to-failure by engaging the distal fragment from two distinct directions [10]. Clinically, we hypothesize that this enhanced purchase may minimize interfragmentary micromotion and promote a more stable environment for early functional recovery, facilitating earlier mobilization [12]. This mechanical advantage might partly account for the significantly higher AOFAS ankle–hindfoot scores observed at 3 months. Notably, the DMLP group contained a markedly higher proportion of SER IV fractures (70.0% vs. 48.3%). Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, such a substantial disparity in complex injury patterns remains clinically relevant. Because more severe fractures typically entail a more challenging recovery trajectory, this baseline imbalance introduces a potential confounding factor that complicates direct outcome comparisons. While the robust orthogonal fixation likely facilitated the favorable results observed in these demanding cases, it is difficult to distinctly isolate the therapeutic effect of the implant from this underlying bias. Consequently, the apparent functional superiority of the DMLP group must be interpreted with caution.

Beyond mechanical stability, the reduction of soft-tissue morbidity remains a critical consideration in implant selection. The subcutaneous nature of the lateral malleolus makes it susceptible to hardware prominence, a known limitation of the LLP profile (Fig. 3G, H) [13]. In contrast, the DMLP system addresses this concern through a low-profile, mini-fragment design that adheres closely to the bone surface. Furthermore, the posterior plate is positioned to avoid the peroneal groove (Fig. 1), which may help mitigate the risk of tendon irritation [11, 12, 14]. A notable finding in our study involves the rate of implant removal. While international literature often reports removal rates below 20%, the elevated rates observed in both our study cohorts reflect a specific clinical setting with a lower threshold for elective removal driven by patient preference [15–17]. However, within this context of high removal willingness, the DMLP group demonstrated a significantly lower rate (41.7%) than the LLP group (65.0%). While this difference may suggest that DMLP provides improved soft-tissue compatibility and fewer symptoms, these findings must be interpreted cautiously. Because implant removal lacked standardized clinical criteria, cultural factors and subjective patient preferences acted as a significant confounding factor. This makes it difficult to definitively isolate the impact of implant design from these culturally driven decisions. Therefore, although our data point toward potentially better soft-tissue tolerance with DMLP, further studies utilizing strict removal indications are necessary to validate this conclusion.

The pursuit of anatomical restoration and rigid stability involves a calculated surgical trade-off. We observed that the DMLP technique required a significantly longer operative time and resulted in higher intraoperative blood loss than the LLP technique. This reflects the technical complexity of contouring two separate plates and placing screws in orthogonal planes. Although prolonged operative time is generally considered a risk factor for perioperative complications [18, 19], the absolute increase of approximately 18 min in our study was not associated with adverse perioperative events. In contrary, the overall complication rate was lower in the DMLP group. This modest increase in operative time may be balanced by potential longer-term benefits, particularly a lower incidence of implant-related soft-tissue irritation and a reduced need for secondary implant removal.

Despite favorable clinical outcomes, the biomechanical rigidity of the dual-plate system merits consideration in specific demographics. As indicated by prior biomechanical analysis, the enhanced stiffness of the DMLP construct could theoretically induce stress concentration at the proximal end of the implants [10]. Although we did not observe periprosthetic fractures in this series, the potential for a “stress riser” effect remains relevant, particularly for elderly patients with osteoporotic bone [20, 21]. This introduces a complex decision-making process regarding long-term implant retention. Retaining a rigid construct in osteoporotic bone may exacerbate local stress shielding [21, 22], whereas removal involves additional surgical exposure and anesthesia risks [15–17]. Consequently, future studies with stratified analyses based on bone mineral density are necessary to determine whether the benefits of DMLP consistently outweigh these theoretical risks in the geriatric population.

This study is subject to several limitations inherent to its design. First, the retrospective, non-randomized design introduces an inherent risk of selection bias, as the choice of fixation was influenced by surgeon preference and implant availability. Although baseline characteristics were comparable, this lack of randomization necessitates cautious interpretation of our conclusions. Second, while the sample size of 120 patients was sufficient to detect differences in primary functional outcomes, it may lack the statistical power to identify rare complications, such as proximal peri-implant fractures theoretically associated with rigid dual plating. Third, the decision for implant removal was driven by a combination of clinical symptoms and patient request. This subjective component, coupled with regional cultural factors, limits the direct generalizability of our removal rate data to healthcare systems with stricter indications for hardware retention. Fourth, the 1-week difference in union time has limited clinical relevance due to interval bias from scheduled radiographic follow-ups. Finally, our mean follow-up period of 36 months, while adequate for assessing union and medium-term function, may not fully capture late-onset complications such as post-traumatic arthritis.

Conclusion

This retrospective analysis indicates that the DMLP system provides effective fixation for unstable Danis–Weber B distal fibular fractures. Compared with the conventional LLP group, patients treated with DMLP showed earlier radiographic union and superior functional outcomes. Although the dual-plating technique required longer operative times, it appeared to be associated with a lower incidence of soft-tissue complications and secondary implant removal within our cohort. These findings suggest that the DMLP construct may serve as a viable alternative to standard lateral plating. However, prospective, randomized trials are required to definitively confirm these clinical advantages.
