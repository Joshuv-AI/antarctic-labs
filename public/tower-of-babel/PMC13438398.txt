Sediment accumulation rate predicts the temporal resolution of marine fossil assemblages
Authors: Michał Kowalewski, Adam Tomašových, Rafał Nawrot, Daniele Scarponi, Martin Zuschin, Paolo G. Albano, Quan Hua, Darrell S. Kaufman, Susan M. Kidwell, Matias Ritter, Marcello G. Simões, Luis Torres, Bettina Bachmann, Michaela Berensmeier, Troy A. Dexter, Alexandra Haselmair, Ivo Gallmetzer, Claudio Pellegrini, Lukas Schweigl, Jan Steger, Matthew A. Kosnik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEven within a single sediment layer, fossil assemblages contain the remains of organisms that lived at different times, like cemeteries where humans who lived centuries apart are buried together. This temporal mixing, or time averaging (TA), determines which biological and geological processes are meaningfully archived in the fossil record. To understand the processes that control TA, we analyzed 384 samples from marine sediments deposited since the last ice age. TA ranged from decades to millennia and scaled predictably with the sediment accumulation time, inversely related to sediment accumulation rate (SAR). Despite many processes that could drive temporal mixing, our results demonstrate a powerful paleontological principle: SARs predict the temporal resolution of fossil assemblages.

In Earth sciences, temporal resolution—the amount of time encompassed by single rock layers or core intervals—determines which processes can be meaningfully reconstructed from the geological record (1–4). Accounting for temporal resolution is acutely relevant to fossil assemblages because they are affected by time averaging (TA), the temporal mixing of noncontemporaneous skeletal remains. Even at the finest sampling scale, fossil assemblages often aggregate remains of organisms that lived at different times (1, 2, 4–8). Here, we quantify the scale and variability of TA for late Quaternary marine assemblages and assess the predictive power of the primary drivers governing TA to derive a generalizable quantitative model for estimating the temporal resolution of paleontological data.

TA is ubiquitous in the marine fossil record because the generation times of organisms are short compared to the timescales over which sediments accumulate, and because biomineralized skeletal remains are sufficiently durable to persist in surface sediments through multiple burial-exhumation cycles driven by biological and physical mixing (6, 9–12). TA generally blurs the fine-scale spatiotemporal variability of climatic and ecoenvironmental processes (6, 9, 13, 14), exerts scaling effects on key parameters such as faunal turnover rates and diversity (4, 15–17), and coarsens geochronological age models based on individually dated fossils (13, 18–20). Based on stratigraphic and sedimentological contexts, it has long been recognized qualitatively that fossil assemblages undergo temporal mixing across a spectrum of ecological and evolutionary time scales, with direct consequences for research design, analysis, and interpretation (1, 2, 6, 21, 22).

TA can be directly quantified in late Quaternary assemblages by numerical dating of individual skeletal remains. Previous case studies, focused on surface sediments and cores from coastal-to-shelf settings, have yielded TA estimates that ranged from decades to millennia (9, 13, 19, 23–34). These studies were limited in spatiotemporal and sampling scope and did not comprehensively quantify the relative importance of potential drivers of temporal resolution, thus limiting our ability to derive generalizable predictive principles that govern TA. While some case studies (12, 26) indicated that TA is primarily controlled by sediment accumulation rate (SAR), other studies highlighted the importance of temporal variability in skeletal production (25, 35), vertical and lateral mixing (23, 27, 36), and the intrinsic skeletal durability of organisms (24, 29).

To quantify the scale and variability of TA and evaluate the effects of its potential drivers in a formalized multifactorial framework, we integrated data for 7,593 individually dated specimens of marine invertebrates collected by us from modern seafloors and late Quaternary successions (SI Appendix). This dataset includes the great majority of the existing numerical estimates of TA in marine assemblages. The data encompass “nearshore” (bays, lagoons, delta fronts, and the open-coast shoreface above fair-weather wave base), “shallow-subtidal” (between fair-weather and storm wave base), and “deep subtidal” (below storm wave base down to the shelf break) habitats (SI Appendix). The dataset consists of 384 samples, defined as monospecific sets of individually dated specimens (n ≥ 10), collected from 294 sampling units (surface grabs or sediment core increments), representing 118 sites (unique localities) from seven low-to-mid latitude regions of the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian oceans and the Mediterranean Sea (SI Appendix). The data are dominated by bivalve mollusks (319 samples), and the remaining 65 samples represent gastropods, scaphopods, brachiopods, echinoids, and foraminifera. In addition to TA estimates, based on newly recalibrated radiocarbon and amino acid racemization ages, our dataset includes multiple internally standardized variables (SI Appendix, Table S1) that represent, or correlate with, putative drivers of TA, allowing for a formal evaluation of their contribution to the temporal resolution of these samples (SI Appendix).

To evaluate the primary drivers of TA, we used two distinct approaches: 1) direct assessment of empirical results and 2) the congruence between the observed TA estimates and theoretical modeling predictions. The comparison between model predictions and empirical results allows us to evaluate the role of SAR in relation to other potential TA drivers—skeletal disintegration and mixing—for which we lack direct quantitative estimates.

Because SAR is scale dependent (37) and TA is dependent on both SAR and the vertical (stratigraphic) span of sampling units, SAR cannot be used directly to test the relationship with TA. Therefore, we estimated the sediment accumulation time (SAT): the time over which the sediment containing a given fossil assemblage was deposited. SAT depends on SAR but also accounts for the variation in vertical span of sampling units. SAR is the inverse equivalent of SAT only when all compared sampling units have the same stratigraphic thickness; for any given SAR, SAT will increase proportionally with an increase in the vertical span of sampling. We note that SAT is the amount of time represented by a given sediment thickness that is expected under a specific SAR without accounting for the effects of mixing. When the thickness of the surficial mixed layer exceeds the vertical span of sampling, as is the case of our sampling units, the time to final burial of skeletal remains below the sediment mixed layer (and thus TA) is expected to be longer than SAT. Therefore, SAT allows us to separate the effects of SAR from the effects of other factors such as mixing or disintegration. SAT (time over which a sedimentary unit accumulated) and TA (time represented by fossils contained in that unit) should be equivalent if SAR is the only significant driver of TA. Deviations from this relationship indicate influence of mixing (increase in TA relative to SAT), skeletal disintegration (decrease in TA relative to SAT), or other putative drivers.

We calculated SAT as the vertical span (cm) of a given sampling unit divided by the local, geochronologically informed SAR (cm/y) derived independently (SI Appendix, Table S2). This conversion not only accounts for the varied thicknesses of sampling units but also allows for expressing both TA and SAT in years, thus directly relating the time captured by the skeletal remains to the timescale of deposition of a sedimentary unit containing them. We derived numerical SAT estimates for 264 samples. In addition, order-of-magnitude classes of SATORD were estimated for all 384 samples (SI Appendix).

Results

To quantify TA, we applied six measures of statistical dispersion (SI Appendix, Table S3) to the age-frequency distributions of 384 monospecific samples. All measures indicated that estimated time averaging (ETA) spanned three orders of magnitude, from decades to millennia (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Tables S4 and S5). We focus here on ETA2SD (34, 36), which estimates dispersion as two SD of specimen ages after correcting for the geochronological imprecision associated with numerical dating (SI Appendix). The median ETA2SD was 1,990 y (interquartile range: 520 to 3,780 y). Millennial TA estimates (103–<104 y) were dominant (66% of the samples), but 20% of samples yielded centennial (102–<103 y) and 14% decadal (<102 y) estimates (SI Appendix, Table S4).

Empirical Assessment of Drivers.

Partitioning data into four ordinal categories of sediment accumulation time (SATORD) indicated that log10-transformed ETA2SD increased monotonically with an increase in SATORD (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Most samples (83%) with ETA2SD < 100 y were from sites with moderate-to-short SATORD, whereas 87% of samples with ETA2SD > 2,000 y came from sites with long-to-very-long SATORD. The strong relationship between SATORD and log10-transformed ETA2SD was observed for all data subsets, with the exception of nearshore environments (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). In contrast, partitioning data into the ordinal classes of sediment type, depositional system, or water depth did not demonstrate predictable trends in TA (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). For these data subsets, the median ETA2SD ranged from 880 y to 3,100 y and seven out of 10 median estimates were statistically indistinguishable from a randomized null model (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

Consistent with SATORD results, analyses based on quantitative SAT estimates indicated that log10-transformed ETA2SD strongly correlated with log10-transformed SAT (n = 264, r = 0.79, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Because our sampling units were only moderately variable in their vertical span, SAR closely approximated the inverse of SAT, and thus, resulted in a comparably high correlation with ETA2SD (r = -0.78, Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). When samples were aggregated within sampling units, resulting in a mixture of monospecific and multispecies TA estimates, a strong (r2 = 0.74; n = 183 sampling units; SI Appendix, Fig. S5), but significantly nonlinear (P < 0.001; 10,000-iteration bootstrap; Durbin–Watson test), TA–SAT relationship was observed. To reduce the effects of autocorrelation and pseudoreplication, we aggregated the sampling units across the sites (multispecies estimates) for which numerical SAT estimates were available (29 out of 118 sites). The nonlinearity was no longer statistically detectable (P = 0.44), and the resulting site-level regression model indicated that TA increased proportionally with SAT following a power law (r2 = 0.77; n = 29; ETA2SD = 101.77492 ∙ SAT0.64834; SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Multiple linear mixed-effects models, which treated sites as random effects and separated within-site and between-site comparisons, corroborated that both SATORD and numerical SAT estimates were the primary explanatory variables, with largest effect sizes, for all dispersion measures (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Tables S6–S8).

Model-Based Assessment.

The relationship between SAT and TA can be modeled using stochastic transition-rate matrices with three independent parameters: SAT, sediment mixing time (SMT), and skeletal disintegration time (SDT). This modeling approach supplements the empirical analysis, which evaluated the effects of mixing and destructive processes via indirect proxies only, by providing quantitative theoretical predictions that can be directly related to our empirical results. Simulated TA estimates were calculated for the uppermost core increment as two SD of modeled specimen ages, which allows for direct comparisons to our empirical TA estimates (ETA2SD). The four end-member simulations varied in their predictions (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). In the SAT-only model (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A), simulated TA estimates scaled with SAT at a two-to-one ratio for TA measured as two SD. When vertical mixing was included (SAT–SMT model), a strong dependency of simulated TA on SAT persisted but modeled TA estimates exceeded SAT by one order of magnitude, and the offset increased with SAT (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). In this model, simulated TA reflected the transit time needed to bury skeletal remains below the highly mixed surficial layer. The transit time, estimated as the thickness of the mixed layer divided by SAR, is expected to exceed SAT. This is because the thickness of the mixed layer in coastal-to-shelf settings typically spans tens of centimeters (12), whereas 94% of our sampling units are ≤ 10 cm thick. When both skeletal disintegration and SAT were included but mixing was absent (SAT–SDT model), disintegration suppressed simulated TA relative to SAT (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C). Finally, with all three drivers included (SAT–SMT–SDT model) the dependency of simulated TA on SAT weakened further (SI Appendix, Fig. S8D). These models, which assume that stratigraphic mixing is symmetrical and ignore the potential effects of lateral mixing and temporal variation in skeletal input, provide simple null expectations for predicting the importance of these key drivers of the temporal resolution of fossil assemblages.

When compared to the model predictions, the observed ETA2SD can be best explained as a function of SAT modulated by both SMT and SDT. Of the four models (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S8), the SAT–SMT model best predicted the observed upward shift in ETA2SD relative to SAT (Fig. 4B), but overestimated the observed ETA2SD for long SAT, suggesting stronger effects of vertical mixing than observed in the data. Conversely, the SAT–SMT–SDT model predicted the observed decline in ETA2SD as SAT increased (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9) but also produced low estimates of TA even when SAT was very long, which was incongruent with the observed ETA2SD (Fig. 4D). These low TA estimates were produced in simulations with fast disintegration (red symbols in Fig. 4 C–F). When SAT–SMT–SDT estimates were limited to simulations with slow disintegration (SDT > 100 y), the model matched the observed ETA2SD remarkably well (Fig. 4F), while still predicting the amplifying effect of mixing (Fig. 4I), suggesting that some skeletal remains must survive long enough in surface sediments to allow for extensive TA. In the real world, the survival time is not a simple function of disintegration rates but also reflects the sequestration and/or early diagenetic strengthening of skeletal remains caused by their intermittent or permanent burial into deeper sediment layers where disintegration processes are negligible (11, 36, 39–42). This congruence of the SAT–SMT–SDT (SDT > 100 y) model with our empirical TA estimates suggests that disintegration rates in the sampled settings are either truly slow or mitigated by burial and early diagenesis, as has long been suspected (11, 36, 39–42).

Given the confounding effects inherent to the dataset assembled from idiosyncratic case studies that varied in approach and goals, the strong dependence of temporal resolution of samples on SAT is remarkable. This dependence was not produced by a fortuitous clustering of regional estimates (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A), taxa (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B), water depth (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C), or burial status (surface versus subsurface samples; SI Appendix, Fig. S10D). The relationship was observed both within the most comprehensively sampled Adriatic Sea (SI Appendix, Fig. S10E) and when the Adriatic data were excluded (SI Appendix, Fig. S10F). The TA–SAT dependence was also not driven by variation in the vertical span of samples: it remained comparably strong when data were restricted to 5-cm-thick sampling units (SI Appendix, Fig. S10G). The correlation strengthened for samples from the shallow subtidal (r = 0.90, SI Appendix, Fig. S10H) but considerably weakened for nearshore settings (r = 0.18, SI Appendix, Fig. S10I).

Discussion

Our assessment of the temporal resolution of Quaternary marine assemblages represents the first quantitative synthesis based on a statistically robust number of multiregional TA estimates. We find that TA ranges across three orders of magnitude and that fossil assemblages should have sufficient temporal resolution for studying a broad spectrum of processes, including a substantial subset of samples (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S4) with sufficient acuity to evaluate centennial and even decadal processes; these samples were collected from settings with high SARs driven, for example, by river discharge (43) and anthropogenic effluents (12).

Our results support a straightforward paleontological rule that links together (A) the Earth system processes operating over relevant timescales, (B) the temporal resolution of fossil assemblages estimated by ETA2SD, and (C) SAR expressed as SAT. SAT (C) controls the resulting TA (B), which, in turn, determines the processes (A) that can be meaningfully interrogated using fossil assemblages. The dominance of samples with extensive (multimillennial) temporal mixing is representative for modern coastal-to-shelf environments because fast SARs (>1 cm/y) in such settings are geographically limited on the global scale (48).

The estimates of TA reported here are not artificially suppressed by limits of the geochronological methods because all dated specimens were within the range of radiocarbon or 14C-calibrated AAR dating (99% of specimens are from the most recent 16 kyr). Similarly, our focus on dating individual species rather than multispecies assemblages is unlikely to have substantially affected our results, given that multiple species from the same sampling units generally yielded ETA2SD estimates within the same order of magnitude (SI Appendix, Fig. S11; see also refs. 30 and 32). A strong TA–SAT relationship also persisted for multispecies estimates (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Between-species differences in the rate and timing of skeletal production, driven by their differential responses to environmental changes (36, 49–51), account for some outlier samples in the TA–SAT relationship (Fig. 2A) and for discrepancies in ETA2SD estimates among sympatric species (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). For example, a chemosymbiotic bivalve that exploded in abundance in response to 20th-century wastewater contamination was time-averaged over decadal-to-centennial time scales, even though multimillennial TA affected other bivalve species that inhabited the same habitat continuously (51). However, such outliers are too infrequent to suppress the strong TA–SAT relationship.

Drivers of TA.

The importance of SAR in controlling TA has long been anticipated from the association of taphonomically complex skeletal concentrations with discontinuity surfaces (6, 52–54) and quantitatively supported in local-scale dating studies (12, 26, 36, 55). Our results provide robust statistical support (SI Appendix, Figs. S5 and S6) for the overriding role and high predictive power of independent SAT estimates and are consistent with taphonomic models suggesting that differences in skeletal durability can be mitigated by bioturbation, which can minimize the dependence of TA on durability (36, 42).

The overwhelming dependency of TA on SAT demonstrates the fundamental role of SAR in controlling the temporal resolution of marine fossil assemblages irrespective of many interacting drivers known to influence TA, including lateral transport (23, 27), variable skeletal production (35, 50), and intrinsic differences in skeletal durability (24, 29). The TA–SAT offset that was observed here across the entire range of SAR estimates (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S4) suggests that the dated skeletal remains underwent vertical mixing along a sedimentary column about ten times thicker than the sediment increments from which they were collected. For our typical sampling unit with a vertical span of 5 cm, the observed TA–SAT offset suggests vertical mixing on a 50-cm scale, which is common in coastal-to-shelf habitats (12). That is, an assemblage collected from a 5 cm sampling unit is expected, typically, to aggregate skeletal remains sourced from a stratigraphic interval up to 50 cm thick.

The absence of a predictable relationship between TA and SAT within nearshore habitats (Figs. 1–3 and SI Appendix, Figs. S2, S4, and S10I) may reflect episodic exhumation and lateral transport of allochthonous skeletal remains that often occur in coastal settings (23, 27, 28, 56). The high frequency of burial-exhumation events and allogenic and autogenic environmental shifts can amplify TA even when SAT is short. Conversely, when older marine strata are absent from newly submerged nearshore areas, TA can be suppressed even when SAT is very long due to lack of relictual specimens from older transgressive phases. The combination of these processes can inflate TA when SAT is short and suppress TA when SAT is long (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S10I), producing nearshore age distributions that are broader and noisier than would be predicted from SAR alone.

With the notable exception of nearshore settings, our results support the key predictions of the SAT–SMT–SDT (SDT > 100 y) model (Fig. 4 F and I): TA primarily depends on SAT, vertical mixing increasingly inflates TA as SAT decreases, and disintegration increasingly suppresses TA as SAT increases. Thus, disintegration rates, which are either too slow or their effect on TA is nullified by other processes, become important only when SAT is long.

The high predictive power of the models, which ignore variation in productivity and intrinsic skeletal durability, is contrary to case studies that recognized these two factors as major drivers of TA (24, 29, 35, 51). Specifically, the model-data congruence points to the prolonged survival of variably durable skeletal remains in surface sediments due to sheltering effects of intermittent burial and early diagenesis (11, 32, 36, 39–42) and suggests that variation in productivity is too infrequent or too subdued to nullify the strong TA–SAT relationship.

Extrapolating TA Estimates across Time, Space, and Higher Taxa.

Although our TA estimates covered a broad spectrum of common depositional settings, they are unlikely to capture the total range of variation in TA that occurs in the fossil record.

First, highly resolved (subdecadal) marine archives have been well documented in the geological record, including varved sediments (57), exceptional instances of soft-tissue fossilization (58), and some oyster reefs (31). Conversely, TA can reach 105–106 y in settings with extremely low sedimentation rates and along regional discontinuity surfaces, as documented using 87Sr/86Sr dating or inferred from the regional stratigraphic context (52, 55, 59, 60). However, our TA estimates focus on the assemblages of common marine calcifying organisms from coastal and open-shelf environments that dominated in the Phanerozoic (61, 62) and are thus directly relevant for the majority of data derived from the marine fossil record.

Second, our dataset covers warm-temperate to tropical latitudes and thus does not include estimates from cold-water settings. The only TA estimates from high latitudes (Bering Sea), recently obtained for mollusk assemblages from open-shelf settings characterized by low SAR, yielded decadal-to-centennial estimates of TA (63). These remarkably low TA estimates, an order-of-magnitude lower than our estimates from comparable warm-water settings, were attributed to accelerated destruction of skeletal remains induced by higher carbonate dissolution rates (63). The SAT-SMT-SDT model (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S8D) supports this interpretation by indicating that destruction occurring over decadal or shorter timescales would suppress TA relative to SAT by at least one order of magnitude. The disparity in TA between cold-water and warmer water settings indicated by the Bering Sea study suggests that comparisons of samples collected across a wide range of latitudes would be prone to distortion owing to differing scales of TA (63). For example, because TA tends to inflate sample-level diversity (16), a latitudinal decrease in TA could potentially overprint the fossil record of latitudinal diversity gradients.

Third, our estimates likely overestimate TA in early Phanerozoic assemblages, which were subject to shallow and less intense bioturbation (64–66). The less intense bioturbation should not nullify the strong TA–SAT relationship (Figs. 1–4) but would suppress the inflating effects of sediment mixing (65) and thus reduce the offset between SAT and TA. Furthermore, the dominant early Phanerozoic taxa (e.g., trilobites, brachiopods, stalked echinoderms) differed in skeletal architecture from the mollusks that form the bulk of our data. However, our TA estimates for nonmollusk taxa (brachiopods, echinoids, foraminifera) are generally consistent with those observed for bivalve mollusks (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S11; see also refs. 25, 30, and 32), despite notable differences in skeletal characteristics.

Finally, the late Quaternary was characterized by high-amplitude sea-level oscillations often exceeding 100 m. These fluctuations occurred with a quasi-periodic ~100 ky pacing and exposed the shelf almost entirely during sea-level lowstands, likely impacting our observed TA estimates. During a large-scale sea-level rise, benthic shelf communities would have often colonized newly submerged nonmarine deposits, precluding the incorporation of relictual specimens from older marine strata and thus suppressing TA (29, 34). Conversely, in more offshore settings, characterized by sediment starvation or shelf-edge winnowing, TA can be amplified by the ecological condensation of species that lived at different times at the same location and experienced successively deeper-water conditions (26, 34, 67). Our postglacial study systems primarily represent transgressive and highstand phases of the sea-level cycle, thus encompassing times when sediment starvation is particularly common. However, our data included samples (17, 26) from lowstand and late highstand systems tracts that represented depositional settings with relatively high SAR. Consequently, moderate-to-high SARs (>0.1 cm/y) were well represented (38%) in our data.

In summary, our quantitative estimates of temporal resolution are directly applicable to warm-water post-Paleozoic assemblages subject to bioturbation and dominated by mollusks and other taphonomically comparable taxa. However, the strong TA–SAT relationship is likely to be pervasive in the marine Phanerozoic fossil record. First, the TA–SAT dependency was observed here within and across a wide range of environmental, taphonomic, and regional settings for both mollusk and nonmollusk samples (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Second, multiple recent studies point to a strong relationship between SAR and TA across a wide spectrum of oceanographic settings and higher taxa, from inner-shelf fish otoliths (30) to deep-sea foraminifera (68) and whale bone assemblages (60).

Conclusions

Despite many processes that influence TA, SAR emerged as the primary driver controlling the temporal resolution of late Quaternary marine benthic assemblages, with TA increasing proportionally to SAT. Because SAR, from which SAT is estimated, can be reasonably inferred from environmental and stratigraphic context, the power-law TA–SAT relationship is a powerful discovery. Using SAR, expressed as SAT (Fig. 5C), we can quantitatively predict TA and thus categorize marine fossil assemblages, except those from nearshore settings, into order-of-magnitude classes of TA (Fig. 5B). In this way, we can assess the temporal adequacy of fossil assemblages for studying Earth system processes (Fig. 5A).

Estimates of SAR can guide the quantitatively informed acquisition and interpretation of paleontological data. We can assemble a time series of samples with comparable temporal resolution, thereby minimizing the effects of TA on scale-sensitive parameters such as diversity measures (15–17). Similarly, we can target depositional settings with a specific range of SAR, to derive data with temporal resolution adequate for studying a process of interest (Fig. 5). Finally, our TA estimates indicate that the fossil assemblages dominant in the marine fossil record are likely to undergo temporal mixing on scales of centuries to millennia but mostly less than 10 kyr. Consequently, with the obvious exception of samples from stratigraphically condensed deposits, paleontological data should generally provide sufficient temporal resolution to characterize processes operating over Milankovitch and longer timescales. The finer resolution, needed to discriminate processes operating at subcentennial scales (e.g., population dynamics), can be achieved by targeting settings with high SARs and/or highly limited bioturbation (69).

The timescale of observation has long been known to exert strong scaling effects on the measured rates of Earth system processes, including evolution (70), climate (71), sedimentation (37), geomorphology (72), tectonics (72, 73), and rock cycling (73). For paleontological data, TA ultimately controls the observational timescale of assemblages and can modulate the record of ecoenvironmental trends that we strive to reconstruct. Here, we demonstrated that SAR predicts, to a first order, the scale of temporal mixing in marine fossil assemblages, allowing us to identify the temporal scales of Earth system processes that can be meaningfully analyzed.

Materials and Methods

Data.

The data were assembled by integrating internally standardized variables from multiple projects conducted by the authors (SI Appendix), which focused on dating skeletal remains of marine invertebrates from coastal to continental slope settings (SI Appendix, Figs. S12–S14). Abbreviations and variables are described in SI Appendix, Table S1.

The dataset includes 7,593 individually dated specimens representing 384 samples, collected from 294 sampling units sourced from 118 sites. Sites denote unique locations from which sediment cores or seafloor samples were collected. Sampling units represent single core intervals or bulk sediments collected from modern seafloor (SI Appendix). Because some sites (especially cores) included multiple sampling units, the total number of sampling units exceeds the total number of sites. The stratigraphic (vertical) thickness of sampling units varied from 1 to 20 cm, but 85% of sampling units ranged between 4 and 10 cm. Samples denote monospecific sets of individually dated specimens from a given sampling unit. Each sample produced a single postmortem age distribution from which TA was estimated. Only samples with ≥ 10 specimens were included (n = 10 to 91; median n = 17). For 62 out of 294 sampling units (31%), multiple co-occurring species were dated.

Dating Methodology.

All specimens were individually dated using 14C-calibrated amino acid racemization (AAR; 72% of the specimens) or radiocarbon (14C; 28% of the specimens) methods. In the case of specimens that were dated using multiple methods (for calibration or comparative assessment of those methods), a single age estimate was used based on the more precise method: 14C age for AAR-14C replicates, and graphite-target 14C age for replicates representing different 14C methods (74). To maximize internal consistency of the data, all 14C ages were recalibrated using OxCal v.4.4 (75), and Marine20 calibration curve (76) with regional prebomb marine reservoir correction (∆R) and postbomb marine calibration curves (SI Appendix).

The ratio of D- to L-isomers of amino acids is correlated with the time elapsed since the death of the organism (77, 78) and when paired with 14C dating can be used to estimate numerical age. Until recently, AAR was a preferred dating method for TA studies due to its low cost (11, 13, 19, 24–26). All AAR data were reevaluated using a consistent quality assurance process and recalibrated using a uniform methodology (79). Using a Bayesian inference framework (80), we recalibrated 14C ages to build updated AAR age models.

All specimen ages were reported as years prior to the year of specimen collection. Age estimates for individually dated specimens represent Bayesian posterior age-probability density distributions derived in radiocarbon (75) or AAR calibrations (80). Our analysis explicitly incorporated posterior probability distributions (SI Appendix) to account for nonnormally distributed and substantially variable dating uncertainties (20, 34, 36). For computational and analytical convenience, we used a Monte Carlo approximation in which 10,000 ages were sampled from each posterior probability distribution from which mean age, median age, age variance, and TA estimates were directly computed (SI Appendix). The Monte Carlo approximations produced robust estimates of central tendency and dispersion statistics (r > 0.99 in both cases).

Estimates of TA.

The magnitude of temporal mixing within a fossil assemblage can be quantified as the dispersion of an age distribution based on a random sample of specimens. In this study, we calculated six different dispersion measures (all expressed in years): IQR—interquartile range; ETA50—parametric estimate of IQR corrected for dating uncertainty (36); 2L2—2 times L2, a dispersion measure based on moments of ordered statistics (81); 2SD—2 SD, ETA2SD—2SD corrected for dating uncertainty (34); and RNG—age range (SI Appendix, Table S3). Most of these measures have been used in the literature, but the choice of the “correct” dispersion measure is largely philosophical and dependent on the question being investigated. In our data, all dispersion statistics were strongly correlated (SI Appendix, Fig. S15) and the choice of the dispersion measure does not affect the results (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C and Tables S5–S8). Here, we primarily focus on ETA2SD and ETA50 because these estimates account for dating uncertainty (34, 36).

Explanatory Variables.

Data were compiled for the following eight factors: 1) numerical SAT based on numerical SAR estimates; 2) SATORD based on ordinal SAR; 3) burial status (BUR); 4) sediment type (STY); 5) depositional system (DPS); 6) ordinal water depth (WD) categorized into depositional zones; 7) taxon (TAX); and 8) skeletal biomineralogy (MNR). We provide here a summary of these explanatory factors (for details see SI Appendix).

SAT—The SAR, which results from the balance between depositional and erosional forces, plays a crucial role in preservation of skeletal remains and in determining the age-frequency distribution of fossil assemblages (12, 26, 52–54, 82–84). SARs are measured here on the basis of the thickness of an increment that accumulated over a given time interval. Quantitative SAR (cm/y) for 264 samples, derived from sediment cores and suction sampling, were based on explicit age models informed by 14C or AAR ages of skeletal remains, by 14C-dated peat horizons, or by ages of key sequence-stratigraphic surfaces (SI Appendix, Table S2 and Fig. S16). These estimates were calculated on the basis of either a simple age-depth model (i.e., dividing the thickness of sampling units by the time interval over which they accumulated) or Bayesian age-depth models (17), with SAR (cm/y) estimated using the Bacon function [“rbacon” package; (85, 86); SI Appendix, Table S2]. SAT for each sampling unit was estimated as the total accumulation time, calculated by dividing the vertical span of a given sampling unit (cm) by the SAR (cm/y), resulting in numerical SAT estimates expressed in years.

SATORD—SATORD was estimated for all samples. Ordinal (order-of magnitude) estimates of SAR were based on geochronological age models, sequence-stratigraphic framework, and high-resolution seismic profiles from the areas adjacent to our sampling sites. All 384 samples were thus categorized into one of four ordinal categories of SAR: very low (<0.01 cm/y), low (0.01 to 0.1 cm/y), moderate (0.1 to 1 cm/y), and high (>1 cm/y). The reversed-rank ordinal scale representing estimates of SATORD (S—short, M—moderate, L—long, VL—very long), was used in the empirical assessments (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2) and the multiple linear mixed models (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). SATORD should be adjusted for the vertical span of sampling units. However, because of the limited variation in the vertical span of our sampling units, the adjustment does not change SATORD ranks for our samples. This equivalency of SATORD and the inverse of SAR is specific to our study system (see SI Appendix).

Burial (BUR)—Sampling units were categorized as 1) “surface”—from the fully mixed layer in active depositional systems; or 2) “subsurface”—buried below the active mixed layer (24, 36, 87). The surface mixed layer depth was estimated using geochemical, ichnological, and sedimentological criteria.

Sediment Type (STY)—Based on visual inspection or previously published data, the samples were classified as “sand” when dominated by grains between 2 mm and 0.063 mm or as “mud” when dominated by particles < 0.063 mm (88).

Depositional System (DPS)—Sites were categorized into three types: “carbonate” dominated by carbonate sediments; “mixed” a mixture of clastic and carbonate sediments; and “clastic” sediments dominated by terrigenous material, which may include coarse biogenic remains.

Water Depth (WD)—Factors affecting TA covary with water depth and include bioturbation (12, 89), taphonomic (90, 91), and sedimentary (59) processes. Due to paucity of quantitative paleo-water depth estimates (92–95), sampling units were assigned to depositional zones along the generalized onshore-offshore gradient (SI Appendix).

Taxon (TAX)—All dated species belong to six higher taxa: Bivalvia, Brachiopoda, Echinoidea, Foraminifera, Gastropoda, and Scaphopoda. Because samples are dominated by Bivalvia (83%, SI Appendix, Fig. S14), this variable was only used in some analyses (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S10B).

Skeletal Mineralogy (MNR)—Mineralogy is an ordinal taphonomic variable affecting shell chemical stability and diagenetic durability: 1) aragonite—most prone to alteration (90% of the samples); 2) high-Mg calcite—intermediate; and 3) low-Mg calcite—most durable [(96–98), but see ref. 99]. Except for 13 echinoid samples, the dataset lacks taxa prone to rapid disarticulation.

Theoretical Simulations.

We modeled four end-member scenarios (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S8) that predict the relation between TA in the uppermost core increment and SAT as a function of SAT, sediment mixing time (SMT), and skeletal destruction time (SDT). These scenarios include: 1) a SAT-only model with mixing and destruction absent (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A); 2) a SAT-SMT model with destruction absent (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B); 3) a SAT-SDT model with mixing absent (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C); and 4) a SAT-SMT-SDT model (SI Appendix, Fig. S8D). We used the stochastic transition-rate matrices that compute age-frequency distributions (sampled from probability density functions) in a sediment core under a specified burial rate (individuals move to an underlying increment), mixing rate (individuals move to an overlying or an underlying increment), and disintegration rate (individuals disintegrate to a point when they cannot be taxonomically identified) (36, SI Appendix). These models estimate TA for samples of 10 individually dated specimens located in the uppermost 1 cm increment and assume that 1) the rate of input to the surface layer is constant; 2) the surface mixed layer is subdivided into 1 cm increments; 3) the maximum mixing depth is 50 cm; and 4) the taphonomically-active zone where SDT > 0 is limited to the upper 10 cm. We simulated TA for 25,000 hypothetical sites, assuming random covariation among SAT, SMT, and SDT. SAT is randomly selected from waiting times between 1 and 10,000 y, SMT values range from 0.1 to 100 y, and SDT values range from 1 to 1,000 y (SI Appendix).

Analytical Methods.

Because key numerical variables (SAT, ETA2SD, ETA50) span four orders of magnitude and are nonnormally distributed, they were log10-transformed prior to all analyses. Plots visualizing the relationships between TA and explanatory variables were supplemented by Kruskal–Wallis test and Pearson’s correlation coefficient. To evaluate the relationship between log10-transformed SAT and log10-transformed ETA2SD, we used simple linear ordinary least square regression (SI Appendix, Fig. S17) and multiple linear mixed-effects models that explicitly account for within-site pseudoreplication by introducing random effects, using random intercepts for individual cores and random slopes for the effect of SAT on TA (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Tables S6–S8). To separately estimate the effects of explanatory variables on TA within and between sites, we also employed the within-between random effects models (100–103), using the “nlme” package (38). To reduce pseudoreplications and mitigate autocorrelation, ETA2SD estimates were further averaged across samples from the same sampling units or from the same site (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Tables S6–S8).

To assess the agreement of the models with the observed ETA2SD, we used a simple descriptive measure of deviation (DEV). In this approach, data and simulation estimates were binned (SI Appendix). For each bin, the mean TA was computed for both the model estimates and the observed values and the absolute difference between the two means was calculated. The estimates were then summed up across all bins to compute the total deviation (DEV) between the model estimates and the observed data with the 95% CI estimated by bootstrapping (SI Appendix). The DEV value increases as the mismatch between model estimates and observed data increases.
