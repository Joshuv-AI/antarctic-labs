The XRISM measurements of the black-hole spin in Cyg X-1 are highly model-dependent
Andrzej A. Zdziarski, Swadesh Chand, Michał Szanecki, Gulab Dewangan, Barbara De Marco

Abstract
We study the persistent black hole X-ray binary Cyg X-1, recently observed by XRISM Resolve and simultaneously by NICER and NuSTAR in its hard spectral state. We confirm the result of Draghis et al. that fits of the Resolve data alone with the simplest available relativistic reflection model, relxill , yield a black hole spin parameter close to the maximum, a ∗ ≈ 0.99 a_{*}\approx 0.99 . However, fitting with an improved, Comptonization-based model, relxillCp , yields a low a ∗ = 0.0 + 0.17 a_{*}=0.0^{+0.17} . A similarly low range is obtained with another Comptonization-based model, reflkerrD . Then, fits to the combined data require two Comptonization models but are consistent with any spin value. We conclude that the spin value of Cyg X-1 is strongly model-dependent. However, low spin values are consistent with the constraints from gravitational waves. All of the models constrain the inner disk radius to be ≲ \lesssim 10 gravitational radii, which is consistent with a recent finding of the weakness of thermal reverberation in Cyg X-1. The implied source geometry is that of an outflowing disk corona, which was also proposed to explain the X-ray polarization observed from this source.

I
Introduction

The archetypal black hole (BH) X-ray binary (XRB), Cyg X-1, was discovered as an X-ray source in 1964
(
Bowyer et al. 1965
)
, i.e., more than 60 years ago, and is the best-studied BH XRB to date. Nevertheless, substantial uncertainties in its parameters persist. In particular, according to
Miller-Jones et al. 2021
, the binary inclination of Cyg X-1 is
i=27\fdg 5_{-0.6}^{+0.8}
, the BH mass is
M=21.2\pm 2.2{M}_{\sun}
, and the distance to the source is
D=2.22^{+0.18}_{-0.17}
kpc (which we give here as the median values with 68% uncertainties). By contrast, a recent investigation by
Ramachandran et al. 2025
using detailed atmospheric non-LTE models of the donor found a significantly lower BH mass,
M\approx(12.7
–
17.8){M}_{\sun}
. When they used the rotational broadening measurement of
Simón-Díaz et al. 2017
, they obtained
i\approx 34\arcdeg
and
M\approx 14{M}_{\sun}
(see also figure 7 in
Ramachandran et al. 2025
).

Another uncertainty concerns the value of the BH spin parameter,
a_{*}
. Cyg X-1 has been measured to have very high spin in its soft spectral state (see
Done et al. 2007
for a spectral state definition) using the X-ray continuum method
(
McClintock et al. 2014
)
, e.g.,
a_{*}>0.9985

(
Zhao et al. 2021
;
Miller-Jones et al. 2021
)
,
0.99964^{+0.00003}_{-0.00007}

(
Steiner et al. 2024
)
. Recently,
Draghis et al. 2025b
, hereafter
23
, used the X-ray reflection method (
Bambi et al. 2021
and references therein) to analyze a XRISM
(
Tashiro et al. 2021
)
observation of Cyg X-1 in the hard spectral state, accompanied by a NuSTAR observation
(
Harrison et al. 2013
)
. They also obtained a high spin,
a_{*}\approx 0.98
.

However, Cyg X-1 is a high-mass X-ray binary (HMXB) with an OB supergiant donor
(
Walborn 1973
)
. As such, it belongs to the class of potential progenitors of BH mergers
(
Ramachandran et al. 2025
)
. The spins of merging BHs measured by gravitational waves are generally low; the modeled distribution of individual spins peaks at
a_{*}\approx 0.01
–0.23, and
\sim
90% have
a_{*}\lesssim 0.57

(
The LIGO Scientific Collaboration et al. 2025
)
. Furthermore, when comparing merger results with XRBs, only the spins of the first-born BHs are of interest. They are plausibly identified with the slower-spinning premerger BHs, while the faster-spinning ones undergo tidal spin-up
(
Qin et al. 2018
;
Mandel & Fragos 2020
;
Ma & Fuller 2023
;
Olejak & Belczyński 2021
)
. Some higher spins measured in mergers appear to originate from dynamical interactions
(
Tong et al. 2025
;
Antonini et al. 2025
)
, with accretion appearing ineffective at spinning up BHs
(
van Son et al. 2020
)
. The consensus from those studies is that natal BH spins are low. This finding aligns with stellar models that predict the core remains coupled to the outer envelope during expansion (upon leaving the main sequence), i.e., angular momentum transport is efficient
(
Spruit 2002
;
Fuller & Ma 2019
;
Belczyński et al. 2020
)
. Those models yield natal spins of
a_{*}\sim 0.01
–0.1.

For Cyg X-1, evolutionary calculations indicate that only hyper-Eddington conservative accretion, at
\dot{M}\sim 10^{-2}{M}_{\sun}
/yr, could spin up its BH to
a_{*}>0.98

(
Qin et al. 2022
)
. Those authors invoked the production of neutrino pairs in the accretion flow to remove excess angular momentum, thereby enabling the required mass accretion rate onto the BH. However, the density of such a flow remains orders of magnitude below that required for neutrino pair production, as shown by
Zdziarski et al. 2024
;
Zdziarski et al. 2026
. On the other hand, accretion rates onto the BH appear to be approximately limited to the Eddington rate, with most of the mass transferred from the donor forming strong winds, e.g.,
Fragile et al. 2025
.

The same tension applies to two other BH HMXBs with measured spins, LMC X-1 and M33 X-7, whose spins were also found to be high using the continuum method:
a_{*}=0.92^{+0.05}_{-0.07}

(
Gou et al. 2009
)
and
a_{*}=0.84\pm 0.05

(
Liu et al. 2008
)
, respectively. It remains unclear how to reconcile the gravitational wave results with those from BH HMXBs, a problem reviewed by
Zdziarski et al. 2026
.

An important caveat for the continuum method is that the theoretical models of accretion disks used in it
(
Novikov & Thorne 1973
;
Abramowicz et al. 1988
;
Sądowski 2009
)
predict that disks are unstable in the radiation-pressure-dominated regime
(
Lightman & Eardley 1974
;
Shakura & Sunyaev 1976
)
. This approximately corresponds to the range of Eddington ratios observed in the soft states of BH XRBs. However, observations show that the soft state is stable in X-rays
(
Gierliński & Done 2004
)
. Disks can be stabilized by large-scale magnetic fields, but this substantially alters the structure of the accretion flows, particularly by increasing dissipation in the upper layers of the disk (see
Zdziarski et al. 2026
and references therein). This effect has been phenomenologically modeled by adding a warm corona on top of the disk
(
Belczyński et al. 2024
;
Zdziarski et al. 2024
)
. This modeling has resulted in fits to the Cyg X-1 spectra with very low spin values,
a_{*}\approx 0
. Additionally, the disk’s inner edge can move downstream of the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), which would also reduce the fitted
a_{*}
.

There are also caveats regarding the other main method of spin measurement, which is based on X-ray reflection. For this method to be applicable, the disk must extend down to the ISCO, or very close to it. The space-time metric becomes weakly dependent on spin at
\gtrsim 10R_{\rm g}
, so if the disk is substantially truncated, spin cannot be measured. The method has been applied mostly in the hard spectral state, for which there are strong arguments for truncation (see, e.g.,
Done et al. 2007
). While studies by
Duro et al. 2011
,
Fabian et al. 2012
, and
23
show disks extending close to the ISCO of a maximally rotating BH, those by
Makishima et al. 2008
,
Yamada et al. 2013
, and
Basak et al. 2017
show substantial disk truncation in Cyg X-1. A related important issue concerns the shape of the incident spectrum, which likely consists of multiple spectral components and thus strongly affects the measurements (see, e.g.,
Zdziarski et al. 2021
).

Here, we revisit the Cyg X-1 data set associated with the XRISM observation. We spectrally fit the XRISM spectrum, as well as the NICER
(
Gendreau et al. 2016
)
and NuSTAR
(
Harrison et al. 2013
)
spectra from observations conducted during the XRISM observation.

II
The data, lightcurves and variability

We use the same XRISM observation as in
23
. Cyg X-1 was observed over half of its orbital period, at the phases
(
Gies et al. 2008
)
of 0.67–1.15. Absorption by the stellar wind increased strongly at orbital phases
\gtrsim
0.95.
23
split the observation into halves and analyzed them separately. In the present work, we aim to avoid complications associated with strong absorption while maximizing exposure. We thus choose, for our analysis, an initial part of the observation lasting about 42 h, excluding the strongly absorbed portion.

In addition, we analyze the NuSTAR observation, which was conducted entirely during the portion of the XRISM observation studied here. We also analyze two NICER observations taken during the XRISM observation. The second was partially taken during the absorbed phase, and we studied only the portion before the end of the interval we chose. The log of the observations is given in Table
1
, and the light curves and hardness ratios from NICER, NuSTAR, and XRISM Resolve are shown in Figure
1
. The observations were taken during a moderately hard spectral state of Cyg X-1, as shown in Figure
2
. This figure shows the count rates and hardness ratios from the MAXI X-ray monitor
(
Matsuoka et al. 2009
)
, with those corresponding to our chosen epoch marked in red.

We processed the XRISM Resolve data using HeaSoft V6.36 and CALDB version 20250915, following the procedures described in
Yamada et al. 2025
and
23
. For spectral extraction with
xselect
, we considered only high-resolution primary (Hp) events and excluded pixels 12 and 27. The small (S) and extra-large (XL) response matrix files (RMFs) were generated with the
rslmkrmf
task after excluding low-resolution secondary (Ls) events. The ancillary response file (ARF) was then derived using the
xaarfgen
task, with the exposure map produced by
xaexpmap
as input. The light curves were obtained in the 2–5 keV, 5–10 keV, and 2–10 keV bands, considering only Hp events.

Following
Yamada et al. 2025
, the Xtend data were processed over the same time interval as the Resolve. A circular region with a radius of 60 pixels was used to extract the source spectrum. The RMF was generated with the
xtdrmf
task, and the ARF was generated with
xaarfgen
after deriving the exposure map with
xaexpmap
.

The NuSTAR data were processed with the standard
nupipeline
task in NuSTARDAS, using the bright-source filtering option

task, which also produced the corresponding RMF and ARF. The same task was used to generate light curves in the energy bands 3–5 keV, 5–10 keV, and 3–78 keV.

nicerl2
pipeline and with the

nicerl3-spect

nicerl3-lc

(

, with the additional requirement that each bin contain at least 25 counts.

We have calculated the fractional variability, rms(
E
), as a function of photon energy over the frequency range 0.01–1 Hz using the
Stingray
package
(
Huppenkothen et al. 2019a
;
Huppenkothen et al. 2019b
)
. For the NuSTAR data, we have used the co-spectrum computed with the
HENDRICS
package
(
Bachetti et al. 2015
)
. However, we have found that the NuSTAR data show significantly lower variability than NICER and Resolve in the overlapping 3–10 keV energy range. This appears to be due to dead time in the NuSTAR data, which artificially lowers the rms (even when the co-spectrum is used;
Bachetti & Huppenkothen 2018
; M. Bachetti, private communication). On the other hand, the NICER and Resolve rms values show only minor differences in the Fe K line energy band, likely arising from their different instrumental responses
4
4

4

Due to NICER’s silicon drift detectors’ relatively broad energy response, variable high-energy continuum photons can be redistributed into the Fe K energy range, thereby enhancing the apparent variability in this band. In contrast, XRISM’s Resolve microcalorimeter, with its substantially higher spectral resolution, minimizes spectral mixing between the continuum and the narrow Fe K. As a result, the Fe K band in XRISM would show a lower fractional rms amplitude because it is less contaminated by variable continuum photons.
. Alternatively, the discrepancy may reflect intrinsic source non-stationarity combined with the XRISM observation’s longer temporal coverage.

The results, excluding NuSTAR, are shown in Figure
3
. We see the rms increase up to about 1.2 keV, then decrease. Similar results for another NICER observation are shown by
Basak et al. 2025
. They calculate the rms separately for four frequency bands and show that the hump around 1.2 keV and the decline of the rms above it are most pronounced at frequencies
<
0.5 Hz. This behavior is consistent with the findings of
Wilkinson & Uttley 2009
for two other BH XRBs, which they interpreted as long-term variability of the coronal emission driven by disk variability (see also
Uttley et al. 2011
). In that process, variable disk seed photons enter the corona, modulating its cooling and thus the spectral slope (see, e.g., figure 14 in
Zdziarski et al. 2002
). However, the decline of the rms at energies below
\sim
1 keV indicates the presence of a more stable disk component, which is difficult to explain in this scenario. For comparison, we show the rms spectrum of Cyg X-1 in the soft spectral state
(
Zdziarski et al. 2024
)
. Curiously, there is a similar hump at
\approx
1 keV. Those authors proposed that it may be caused by photon transmission through the stellar wind.

III
Spectral fitting

Spectral fitting is performed using
xspec

(
Arnaud 1996
)
with
\chi^{2}
statistics. Uncertainties are calculated at the 90% confidence level (
\Delta\chi^{2}\approx 2.71
;
Lampton et al. 1976
). We account for the absorption using the
tbfeo
model in
xspec
and assume the elemental abundances of the absorber from
Wilms et al. 2000
. In prior work, the column density toward Cyg X-1 was found to be
N_{\rm H}\approx(6\pm 2)\times 10^{21}
cm
-2
based on reddening toward the donor
(
Balucinska-Church et al. 1995
)
, and
\approx
4.65–
4.85\times 10^{21}
cm
-2
based on modeling of dust scattering toward Cyg X-1
(
Xiang et al. 2011
)
.
Tomsick et al. 2014
fitted the soft state of Cyg X-1, including absorption by the stellar wind. That state has strong soft emission, allowing a relatively precise determination of the column. They found
N_{\rm H}\approx 6.6\pm 0.2
and
6.5\pm 0.1\times 10^{21}
cm
-2
for their two models with a relativistic disk. Then,
HI4PI Collaboration et al. 2016
obtained
N_{\rm H}\approx 7\times 10^{21}
. Based on those works, we decided to constrain
N_{\rm H}\geq 5\times 10^{21}
cm
-2
.

When fitting multiple data sets, we account for residual differences in spectral calibration using the
plabs
model in
xspec
. It multiplies the model spectra by
KE^{-\Delta\Gamma}
, with
K=1
and
\Delta\Gamma=0
for the NuSTAR A and B units, and we let
K
and
\Delta\Gamma
vary freely for the NICER and XRISM Resolve data sets. We note that
plabs
is equivalent to the
CRABCORR
model, introduced by
Steiner et al. 2010
for the same purpose.

III.1
XRISM Resolve data

We have found that our Resolve spectrum lies significantly above the NICER data below 2.6 keV (see Section
III.3
). We therefore chose the energy range 2.6–12 keV for spectral fitting. By contrast,
23
used the 2–12 keV range because their data showed no systematic residuals below 2.6 keV. This minor difference may be due to the choice of the observation interval or details of the data extraction procedure. Still, we have found that including the 2.0–2.6 keV interval in our analysis has no significant effect on the results. Given the fitted energy range, we can only weakly constrain the X-ray absorption, so we use the
tbfeo
absorber model with the O and Fe relative abundances fixed at unity.

Since our aim is to constrain the continuum and the broad Fe K spectral components, we used the small (S) spectral response files. We used the results of
23
to model narrow emission lines present in the spectrum (see their figure 3). The figure shows two slightly broadened Fe K lines at 6.39 and 6.40 keV. We fitted them with Gaussians and, in subsequent analyses, kept their energy and width fixed at the best-fit values. We verified that their parameters have virtually no effect on the continuum-fitting results. In our initial analyses, we assumed the disk extends down to the ISCO and fit the spin parameter.

relxill
model
(
García & Kallman 2010
;
Dauser et al. 2010
;
García et al. 2013
)
, which calculates the reflection of an e-folded power-law spectrum from a disk with a density of
n=10^{15}
cm
-3
(typical for disks in active galactic nuclei) and a broken-power-law dependence of the irradiating flux,
\propto R^{-q_{1,2}}
below and above a break radius,
R_{\rm br}
. While
23
used
relxill
v.2.3, we use the current version, v. 2.8. We obtained results very similar to those of
23
(see Table
2
). In particular, we obtain the spin very close to the maximum,
a_{*}=0.988^{+0.003}_{-0.008}
, confirming the finding of
23
. We also obtain a similarly high disk inclination,
i=70^{+3}_{-5}\arcdeg
(while
23
obtained
i=62^{+1}_{-1}\arcdeg
). Such high values were rarely obtained earlier; e.g., the spectral fitting of
Tomsick et al. 2018
gave
i\approx 35
–
38\arcdeg
, while
Zdziarski et al. 2024
found

. Also, the current values of the orbital inclination are in the 27–
34\arcdeg
range (Section
I
). This model also yields a rather strong reflection. We constrained the relative reflection strength to
{\cal R}\leq 2
and obtained
{\cal R}=2.0_{-0.4}
.
23
obtained a similarly high value of

.

We then use the
relxillCp
model
(
García et al. 2018
)
, which uses a thermal Comptonization spectrum from
Zdziarski et al. 1996
as the incident spectrum and allows variable disk density. This is a more advanced and physically motivated model than
relxill
. It is better suited to Cyg X-1 because its hard-state spectra at
\lesssim
100 keV are better modeled by thermal Comptonization (e.g.,
Gierliński et al. 1997
;
McConnell et al. 2002
) than by the e-folded power law assumed in
relxill
. The fit results are shown in Table
2
and Figure
4
. We find results that differ significantly from those of
relxill
. The spin is now
a_{*}=0.00^{+0.17}
, compatible with gravitational-wave constraints. The inclination is
i=33^{+1}_{-2}\arcdeg
, compatible with the measured orbital inclination. The reflection is weak,
{\cal R}=0.4\pm 0.1
, consistent with an outflowing corona
(
Poutanen et al. 2023
)
that can explain the X-ray polarization measured in this source
(
Krawczynski et al. 2022
)
. The Fe abundance is now
>1
, consistent with an advanced evolutionary stage of the donor of Cyg X-1. The disk density is high,
\geq 10^{20}
cm
-3
, which agrees with the result for Cyg X-1 from
Tomsick et al. 2018
. The
\chi^{2}_{\nu}
is only slightly larger, by +2, than that of the previous model. We have also checked the effect of including energies down to 2 keV in the fit. This resulted in only systematic, mostly positive residuals below 2.6 keV, with virtually no effect on the results. In particular,
a_{*}=0.00^{+0.18}
was found.

We also consider another advanced reflection model,
reflkerrD

(
Niedźwiecki et al. 2019
)
. It uses the Comptonization model of
Poutanen & Svensson 1996
for the incident spectra, which is significantly more accurate than the model of
Zdziarski et al. 1996
used by
relxillCp
. We obtain spin and inclination values virtually identical to those from
relxillCp
(see Table
2
) and a
\chi^{2}_{\nu}
significantly lower than that of the previous models. However, the Fe abundance is relatively high,
\sim
3. This appears to be related to an older version of the reflection tables used by
reflkerrD
compared to those used by the current version of
relxillCp
.

We then searched for alternative solutions using
relxill
at low spin and
relxillCp
at high spin. For the former, there is a solution with
a_{*}=0
(and at
i\approx 35\arcdeg
) but with a significantly higher
\chi^{2}=3497/3365
. For the latter, there is a solution with
a_{*}\approx 0.9
and
\chi^{2}=3489/3364
. Thus, these solutions provide significantly worse fits than the main, global solutions.

We then tested disk truncation. For
relxill
, allowing the inner radius to vary does not improve the solution;
a_{*}
remains nearly unchanged, and the disk inner radius is constrained to 1.0–1.3
R_{\rm ISCO}
. Equivalently,
R_{\rm in}\lesssim 1.6R_{\rm g}
.

Although allowing the inner radius to be free does not improve the fit with
relxillCp
, the resulting constraints on
a_{*}
and
R_{\rm in}
differ. We find that the spin parameter is then completely unconstrained, i.e, can range from
a_{*}=0
to 1, whereas the allowed range of the truncation radius is limited to a narrow interval in
R_{\rm g}
. For a fixed
a_{*}=0

We now perform spectral fitting of the joint spectrum, including NICER, NuSTAR, and XRISM Resolve, over the 0.5–79 keV range. In our approach, we treat blackbody photons emitted by the accretion disk as seeds for Comptonization in the corona. This limits our choice of models. Although
relxillCp
includes Compton upscattering of blackbody photons, its temperature is fixed at 50 eV
(
García et al. 2018
)
, which is not applicable to our spectra. We therefore use only the
reflkerrD
model, which allows the seed blackbody temperature to vary freely. We also use its option in which the seed photons have a disk blackbody spectrum (with a maximum temperature of
T_{\rm bb}
) from the
diskbb
model
(
Mitsuda et al. 1984
)
.

The soft X-rays emitted by Cyg X-1 are absorbed by both the ISM and the donor’s stellar wind. The latter was taken into account by
Tomsick et al. 2014
using a table model based on the
xstar
ionization model
Kallman & Bautista 2001
. We tested that model. However, we found that including it has virtually no effect on the derived parameters, and the fit converges at the lowest column density allowed by this model. Thus, we decided to model absorption only with
tbfeo
. However, we allow the O and Fe abundances in that model to differ from unity.

We have found that the Resolve spectrum contains a significantly stronger Fe K complex than either the NuSTAR A or B spectra, while the NICER spectrum in that range agrees with the Resolve spectrum within the uncertainties (see Section
III.3
). Therefore, we used only the NuSTAR spectra in the 9–79 keV range (similar to
23
). We also found that allowing
q_{2}
and
R_{\rm br}
to be free has little effect on the final fits, so we kept them at the default values,
q_{2}=3
and
R_{\rm br}=15R_{\rm g}
.

We first consider a model with a single Comptonization component, as in the fits to the Resolve data alone. We obtained an unconstrained spin of
a_{*}=0.10^{+0.90}_{-0.10}
and an inclination of
i=42^{+1}_{-1}\arcdeg
at
\chi^{2}_{\nu}=4247/3975
(see Table
3
). However, fitting the broadband spectrum prevents this model from properly accounting for the Resolve spectrum. The
\chi^{2}
contribution to the broadband spectrum is 3581, which is by
>
100 higher than the
\chi^{2}
of the models used for the Resolve spectrum alone (see Table
2
). The fit also shows substantial wavy residuals (especially below 3 keV) and fails to reproduce the Fe K complex. We have also added a separate
diskbb
component (with the inner temperature equal to that used for Comptonization) to represent the unscattered disk emission. However, it did not improve the fit, and the best-fit
diskbb
normalization was null.

We have therefore added a second Comptonization component, first to the model without
diskbb
. We assumed that the electron and disk temperatures, the density, and the Fe abundance were the same as in the first component. This adds four free parameters. The fit shows a highly significant improvement, with
\chi^{2}_{\nu}=4153/3971
. Furthermore, we added a separate
diskbb
component in the same way as for the model with single Comptonization. In contrast to that model, this further improved the fit by
\Delta\chi^{2}=-47
(with the addition of one free parameter). The final
\chi^{2}_{\nu}=4106/3970
; see Table
3
. The F-test probability that the improvement with respect to the single-Compton model is by chance is
\approx 1\times 10^{-27}
. The
\chi^{2}
contribution to the Resolve data is 3495, with a moderate increase relative to the models for those data alone. The resulting parameters are now similar to those obtained for the Resolve data alone using either
relxillCp
or
reflkerrD
, except that the spin parameter is now unconstrained,
a_{*}=0.19^{+0.81}_{-0.19}
. Figure
5
shows the unfolded spectrum, the residuals, and the fit components. We find that the Fe K complex is dominated by reflection from the softer Comptonization component. The ionization parameter of the reflecting medium for the harder Comptonization is very high, and its reflection spectrum is virtually featureless. We note that the model’s maximum disk temperature,
0.15_{-0.01}^{+0.11}
keV, fully agrees with that obtained by
Basak et al. 2017
.

Then, we test for disk truncation. We have found results similar to those obtained with
relxillCp
for the Resolve data alone. Allowing truncation reduces the
\chi^{2}
only slightly and is not statistically required. Since the spin is unconstrained in this model, we consider two values:
a_{*}=0.1
and
0.998
. We then find that the disk inner radius must be
R_{\rm in}\lesssim 10R_{\rm g}
for the former and
\lesssim 8R_{\rm g}
for the latter. We also find that the disk inner radius is limited to
\lesssim 10R_{\rm g}
at any spin.

However, we find that the irradiation index for this model is very low,
q_{1}\approx 0.0^{+0.8}
. This indicates that the contribution to the observed emission from the innermost regions around the BH is very small. We have therefore tested a modified model with a constant
q_{1}=3
. In that case, the best fit also shows truncation, with
R_{\rm in}\approx 11R_{\rm g}
,
a_{*}\approx 0.76
, and
\Delta\chi^{2}\approx+2
relative to the model with a free
q_{1}
. Thus, a moderate disk truncation remains a viable possibility.

III.3
Calibration issues

Figure 6:
The NICER (green), Resolve (black), and NuSTAR (blue and red) data-to-model ratios for the fit with the two-

model, covering the full NuSTAR spectral range, 3–79 keV. We observe a substantial excess of Resolve counts in the Fe K range compared with those from NuSTAR and NICER. We also show the Xtend 0.5–10 keV spectrum unfolded with that model (not fitted).

In our fits so far, we have not included NuSTAR data below 9 keV, and the Fe K region has been fitted primarily with Resolve data. We now fit the data using the full NuSTAR spectral range, 3–79 keV. In this case, statistics in the Fe K region are dominated by NuSTAR. As before, we include the NICER data. We have obtained
\chi^{2}=4371/4046
with parameters mostly similar to those obtained when excluding the 3–9 keV NuSTAR data. In particular, the disk can be moderately truncated (
R_{\rm in}\lesssim 10R_{\rm g}
), but this only slightly improves the fit. The main differences are that the spin parameter is now constrained to be low,
a_{*}=0.10_{-0.10}^{+0.19}
, and the irradiation index is compatible with 3,
q_{1}=3.6_{-0.6}^{+0.5}
. However, the shape of the Fe K complex differs between NuSTAR/NICER and Resolve, with the Resolve residuals exhibiting a distinct hump, as shown in Figure
6
. In principle, this could be due to the shorter NuSTAR and NICER exposures and source variability. However, the hardness ratios of both NuSTAR and Resolve remained approximately constant throughout the observation, as shown in Figure
1
. Thus, this appears to be due to a significant difference in spectral calibration between the instruments, which should be resolved in the future. We also find that the Resolve spectrum increases steeply below the fitted 2.6–12 keV range, whereas the instrument team recommends that data above 1.7 keV be usable.

Figure
6
also shows the unfolded spectrum from the XRISM Xtend instrument in the 0.5–10 keV range. We added that spectrum to the previous fit and found that its shape is significantly different from those of the other detectors. We attempted to fit it, but the resulting fit was very poor, with
\chi^{2}=5956/4200
and strong wavy residuals. This indicates an issue with the current Xtend continuum calibration. A discrepancy between the Xtend and Resolve spectra has also been reported for the BH XRB GS 1354–64
(
Liu et al. 2026
)
.

IV
Disc-corona Energetics

The unabsorbed bolometric flux of our two-Compton model for the broadband spectrum is
\approx 5.2\times 10^{-8}
erg cm
-2
s
-1
. This is typical of the hard state (see Table 1 in
Zdziarski et al. 2002
). Assuming isotropy and a distance of
D=2.2
kpc, this corresponds to
\approx 3.0\times 10^{37}
erg s
-1
. The Eddington luminosity for an H abundance of 0.5 (characteristic of its donor star) is (2.3–
3.5)\times 10^{39}
erg s
-1
for a BH mass in the range of 14–
21{M}_{\sun}
(see Section
I
). Thus, the Eddington ratio is
\sim
1%.

The normalization of the
diskbb
component for this model is given in Table
3
. However, this represents only the unscattered disk flux. We have calculated the normalization of the
diskbb
emission that is scattered by our two Compton clouds by fitting the incident model spectra with
compps
, the Comptonization model assumed in
reflkerrD
. The total normalization is then
N_{\rm t}\approx 1.2\times 10^{6}
. Using the definition in
diskbb
, this can be converted to the disk inner radius by

R_{\rm in}=10^{5}f_{\rm in}f_{\rm col}^{2}\frac{D}{10\,{\rm kpc}}\left(\frac{N_{\rm t}}{\cos i}\right)^{1/2}\,{\rm cm},

(1)

where
f_{\rm col}\approx 1.3
–1.9 is the color correction
(
Davis & El-Abd 2019
)
, and
f_{\rm in}<1
is a correction factor that accounts for
diskbb
omitting the zero-stress term at the ISCO (for cases where the disk extends to the ISCO). For our values of
N_{\rm t}
,
D
and
i
, this corresponds to

\frac{R_{\rm in}}{R_{\rm g}}\approx 6\left(\frac{M}{21{M}_{\sun}}\right)^{-1}\frac{f_{\rm in}}{0.41}\left(\frac{f_{\rm col}}{1.3}\right)^{2},

(2)

where we scaled it to the value of
f_{\rm col}
for the fitted
kT_{\rm bb}
and an Eddington ratio of 0.01 (see figure 5 of
Davis & El-Abd 2019
), and to
f_{\rm in}=0.41
obtained by
Kubota et al. 1998
. For
M=14{M}_{\sun}
, the coefficient above is 9. Equation (
2
) indicates either the inner radius at the ISCO of a slowly rotating BH or a slightly truncated disk. Importantly, it is inconsistent with a disk extending to the ISCO of a maximally rotating BH. A caveat is that
diskbb
assumes a constant irradiation index of 3, whereas we allowed it to be free in an inner region. Also, the value of
f_{\rm in}=0.41
used above was calculated only for
a_{*}=0

(
Kubota et al. 1998
)
, and
f_{\rm in}\sim 1
if the disk is truncated above the ISCO. Also,
f_{\rm col}
can be
>1.3
. Taking that into account, we have a constraint of
R_{\rm in}>(6
–
9)R_{\rm g}
for the considered range of
M
.

The disk flux is

F_{\rm disk}=2\left(10^{4}\,{\rm cm}\over 1\,{\rm kpc}\right)^{2}N_{\rm t}\sigma T_{\rm bb}^{4}\approx 1.3\times 10^{-8}\,{\rm erg}\,{\rm cm}^{-2}\,{\rm s}^{-1},

(3)

where
\sigma
is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant. This can be compared with the observed flux in the coronal Comptonization spectra,
F_{\rm cor}\approx 2.5\times 10^{-8}
erg cm
-2
s
-1
. Taking 0.4 as the average observed reflection fraction and assuming an average albedo of 0.5 (given that the reflection of the dominant hard component arises from a strongly ionized medium), the irradiation-deposited flux is
0.4(1-0.5)2.5\times 10^{-8}
erg cm
-2
s
{}^{-1}\approx 5\times 10^{-9}
erg cm
-2
s
-1
. Thus, the disk emission has comparable contributions from both intrinsic dissipation and irradiation. On the other hand, the coronal flux,
F_{\rm cor}
, is about twice that of the disk,
F_{\rm disk}
. Also, as shown by comparing the total
diskbb
normalization,
N_{\rm t}
, to that of the unscattered disk emission,
N
, the corona covers a relatively small fraction of the disk,
(N_{\rm t}-N)/N_{\rm t}\approx 0.3
.

We can also check the self-consistency among the flux impinging on the disk, the disk density, and the ionization parameter, following
Zdziarski & De Marco 2020
. We consider only the reflected flux of the softer Comptonization component in the fit to the broad-band data, which equals
F_{\rm refl,2}\approx 6\times 10^{-9}
erg cm
-2
s
-1
, and the corresponding isotropic luminosity is
4\pi D^{2}F_{\rm refl,2}
. This flux is responsible for most of the observed Fe K component and thus must originate from the vicinity of the BH. Assuming it irradiates an area of
\sim 4\pi 10R_{\rm g}^{2}
(on both sides of the disk), we obtain the flux on the disk surface as
F_{\rm irr}=F_{\rm refl}D^{2}/(10R_{\rm g})^{2}
. The ionization parameter is defined as

\xi\equiv\frac{4\pi F_{\rm irr}}{n}.

(4)

This yields
\log_{10}\xi\approx 3.4
–3.7 for
M=21{M}_{\sun}
to
14{M}_{\sun}
. Given the simplified and approximate nature of this estimate, it is in good agreement with the value of
3.3\pm 0.1
in Table
3
.

V
Discussion and Summary

Our analysis has significantly extended the important work of
23
. While their study covered many details of the Fe K emission from Cyg X-1, particularly the narrow Fe K
\alpha
features measured for the first time by XRISM Resolve, it used only a single model for the relativistically broadened X-ray reflection spectrum,
relxill

(
Dauser et al. 2010
;
García et al. 2013
)
. This deviated from the previous practice of those authors
(
Draghis et al. 2023
;
Draghis et al. 2024
;
Draghis et al. 2025a
)
, who considered and tested a range of available relativistic reflection models, particularly the improved, physically motivated model
relxillCp

(
García et al. 2018
)
.

In our study, we have found a strong dependence of the fitted spin parameter on the model. While the
relxill
model
(
García et al. 2013
)
fitted to the Resolve data alone yields a spin parameter close to the maximal value,
relxillCp
yields
a_{*}=0^{+0.17}
. Similarly, fitting with another physically motivated reflection model,
reflkerrD

(
Niedźwiecki et al. 2019
)
, yields
a_{*}=0.18_{-0.18}^{+0.10}
. An argument for the low spin is that the high disk inclination obtained with the
relxill
,
\gtrsim\!60\arcdeg
, is inconsistent with both the binary inclination and most previous spectral-fitting results for Cyg X-1. By contrast, fitting with
relxillCp
and
reflkerrD
yields
i=33_{-2}^{+1}\arcdeg
and
i=36_{-4}^{+1}\arcdeg
, respectively, i.e., inclination ranges compatible with the binary inclination of
Ramachandran et al. 2025
. As a possible explanation of the high disk inclination, a precession of the BH spin axis was invoked by
23
. Such precession would then govern the disk inclination and, in principle, could alter it relative to that reported in previous studies. However, the actual period of that de Sitter, spin-orbit, precession is several thousand years, see equation (14) of
Zdziarski et al. 2023
.

We then studied the broadband spectra from NICER (0.5–10 keV), XRISM Resolve (2.6–12 keV), and NuSTAR (9–79 keV), with the Resolve spectrum chosen to dominate the Fe K range. We found that models consisting of a single plasma cloud that Compton-upscatters photons from the disk and reflection cannot properly account for the observed spectrum. Instead, models including two Comptonizing regions, their reflections, and an accretion disk provide a relatively good description of the broadband data. The geometry can then be similar to that found to describe the hard state of MAXI J1820+070 (see figure 4 of
Zdziarski et al. 2021
). The harder component is closer to the BH, as evidenced by hard X-ray lags
(
Kotov et al. 2001
;
Basak et al. 2025
)
, but it can also illuminate outer disk regions.

However, the spin parameter in this model is unconstrained,
a_{*}=0.18_{-0.18}^{+0.82}
. By contrast, including the full energy range of the NuSTAR detector (3–79 keV) in the broadband fit yields a low spin,
a_{*}=0.10_{-0.10}^{+0.19}
. Thus, the fitted spin depends on both the model and the data set. Nevertheless, our results are consistent with low spin and, therefore, with the spin measurements from gravitational waves (see Section
I
).

A notable result from our reflection spectroscopy is that the disk in Cyg X-1 appears at most weakly truncated, with the inner disk radius
R_{\rm in}\lesssim 10R_{\rm g}
. Thus, the disk either extends to the ISCO of a slowly rotating BH or is weakly truncated. By contrast, the normalization of the disk component in our broadband modeling implies that its inner radius is several
R_{\rm g}

or more
. Taken together, these results indicate that the disk extends down to
\sim 10R_{\rm g}
, consistent with the ISCO of a slowly rotating BH but not with that of a fast-rotating one. Thus, if
a_{*}\sim 1
in Cyg X-1, its disk has to be truncated. The disk’s proximity to the BH is also consistent with the weak soft X-ray lags in Cyg X-1, as reported by
Basak et al. 2025
. The amplitude of the lag is
\approx
1 ms (see figure 10 in
Basak et al. 2025
). If due to reverberation, this corresponds, at face value, to the light travel time over
\approx
(10–
15)R_{\rm g}
, depending on the BH mass.

At the same time, the corona in the hard state of Cyg X-1 extends horizontally, as indicated by the X-ray polarization angle aligned with the jet of this source
(
Krawczynski et al. 2022
)
. Thus, the source geometry is that of a slab corona. However, static coronae exhibit soft,
\Gamma>2
, X-ray spectra due to feedback between the disk and corona
(
Haardt & Maraschi 1991
;
Poutanen et al. 2018
)
, whereas we observe a hard spectrum with
\Gamma\sim 1.7
(see Table
2
). This discrepancy can be resolved if the corona is outflowing at a mildly relativistic velocity
(
Beloborodov 1999
)
. Our results are consistent with the outflowing corona model of
Poutanen et al. 2023
in the variant where the underlying disk extends near the ISCO. The reflection fractions in our models are significantly less than unity due to relativistic beaming away from the disk. Additionally, our finding that the inner parts of the disk are only weakly irradiated,
\propto R^{-q_{1}}
with
q_{1}\approx 0^{+0.8}
(see Table
3
), is consistent with a mildly relativistic outflow of the corona, which then reduces the irradiation of regions below the corona’s edges. In addition, the model of
Poutanen et al. 2023
explains the high degree of linear polarization in X-rays,
4.0\%\!\pm\!0.2\%
, measured in the hard state of Cyg X-1
(
Krawczynski et al. 2022
)
.

On the other hand, Cyg X-1 is the only source found so far in which the soft X-ray (thermal) reverberation is very weak
(
Basak et al. 2025
)
. Those authors contrast this source with the transient XRB MAXI J1820+070, which exhibits pronounced soft X-ray lags and has a geometry consistent with disk truncation
(
De Marco et al. 2021
;
Dziełak et al. 2021
;
Zdziarski et al. 2021
)
. Soft X-ray lags were also observed in GX 339–4
(
De Marco et al. 2015
;
De Marco et al. 2017
)
and H1743–322
(
De Marco & Ponti 2016
)
.

To explain this difference,
Basak et al. 2025
noted that the relatively low luminosity of Cyg X-1 places it on the lower branch of the hardness-luminosity diagram (soft-to-hard transition for transients), whereas most evidence for disk truncation lies on the upper branch. However, reverberation lags on the lower branch of GX 339–4 were, on average, longer than those on the upper (hard-to-soft) branch
(
De Marco et al. 2017
)
. We propose that the specific geometry of Cyg X-1 may be related to its accretion mode from the stellar wind. This may continuously replenish the inner parts of the accretion flow, thereby preventing truncation.

We also note that the low disk temperature we obtained,
0.15\pm 0.01
keV, implies that disk emission in the 0.5–1 keV range (used to measure the soft lags by
Basak et al. 2025
) accounts for only
\approx
1/3 of the total absorbed flux in that range (using the model with a disk and two
reflkerrD
components, which yields nearly the same fraction whether in photons or energy). Therefore, the intrinsic lag is significantly diluted; see figure 20 in
Uttley et al. 2014
.
