 Executive Summary
 This describes the results of the project funded by the U.S. Department of Energy’s Wind Energy
 Technologies Office that investigated the concept of wind as a virtual synchronous generator
 (WindVSG), implemented it in hardware, and validated it by combining the advantages of
 modern dynamic inverter technologies with the static, dynamic, and transient electromechanical
 properties of synchronous machines. During this project, we demonstrated how to control the
 inverters of wind turbine generators (either alone or in parallel with other grid-forming sources,
 such as battery energy storage systems) so that wind power behaves like a synchronous machine-
 based power plant with a conventional prime mover. For this purpose, testing was conducted at
 the National Laboratory of the Rockies’ Advanced Research on Integrated Energy Systems
 facility with a real 2.5-MW wind turbine generator operating in grid-forming mode under
 dynamic and transient conditions. The team also developed models and conducted simulations
 for grid-forming wind power to evaluate the stability impacts of grid-forming operation on the
 power grid. This report describes efforts by the National Laboratory of the Rockies team in
 collaboration with an industry partner during the 3-year project.

 1 Introduction
 The successful integration of wind power into power systems worldwide has largely relied on
 conventional grid-following (GFL) power converters, which are capable of providing many
 essential reliability services. Combining conventional GFL wind generation with energy storage
 can further increase system flexibility and enhance the ability of wind power to deliver such
 services. However, a fundamental limitation of GFL inverter-based resources (IBRs) is their
 inability to ensure stable grid operation when the level of conventional voltage-source generation
 is significantly reduced.

 As a result, grid stability has become a major concern in the renewable energy integration
 community. An adequate capacity of grid-forming (GFM) sources is essential for stable grid
 operation, even when substantial inertia-like responses are available from GFL converters. A
 power system cannot operate stably without a sufficient level of voltage source generation,
 regardless of how much inertia is present. Conversely, systems with little or no inertia and high
 rates of change of frequency can still operate stably if they have sufficient GFM capacity and
 appropriately designed controls. This suggests that, in future power systems, GFM capability
 may be more critical than inertia itself.

 In addition, GFM inverters can provide all existing and future advanced reliability services at a
 level comparable to or better than conventional technologies. They also enhance grid resilience
 and security by enabling capabilities such as islanded operation and black-start functionality. In
 this regard, important questions that need to be addressed are:

 • What are the impacts of degrading grid strength and short-circuit current levels on grid
 stability, transient performance (fault ride-through), and the adequacy of protection?
 • How can we alleviate the degrading system inertia impacts on power system frequency
 stability?
 • How can we address the increasing numbers of stability issues caused by control
 interactions, oscillations, and resonances in IBR-dominated grids?
 • Who has the responsibility of forming the grid (or grid formation) in the absence of
 synchronous generators?
 • How can the grid be black-started after blackouts, and how can it operate when it is
 divided into many smaller islands?
 This project investigated the concept of wind as a virtual synchronous generator (WindVSG),
 implemented it in hardware, and validated it by combining the advantages of modern dynamic
 inverter technologies with the static, dynamic, and transient electromechanical properties of
 synchronous machines. During this project, we demonstrated how to control the inverters of
 wind turbine generators (WTGs) (either alone or in parallel with other GFM sources, such as
 battery energy storage systems [BESS]) so that wind power behaves like a grid forming
 generation asset. For this purpose, testing was conducted at the National Laboratory of the
 Rockies’ (NLR’s) Advanced Research on Integrated Energy Systems (ARIES) facility with a
 real 2.5-MW Type WTG operating in GFM mode under dynamic and transient conditions. The
 team also developed models and conducted simulations for GFM wind power to evaluate the
 stability impacts of GFM operation on the power grid. This report describes efforts by the NLR
 team in collaboration with an industry partner during the 3-year project.

 2 Differences Between GFL and GFM Operation
 Modern IBRs use voltage source converters (VSCs) as their power electronic interface with the
 grid. Conventionally, VSCs have been operated to control the injection of active and reactive
 power into the grid, so their output frequency and voltage do not directly control the grid
 frequency and voltage. This is called GFL mode of operation. In GFL mode, the resource totally
 depends on the grid voltage and frequency established by other generation assets operating on
 the grid. More precisely, GFL converters can operate in voltage control mode by rapidly
 adjusting their reactive power levels to follow the voltage set points on their terminals or at the
 points of interconnection (POIs). They can also influence the grid frequency stability by
 adjusting their active power outputs to contribute to the load-generation balance, frequency
 response, or regulation; however, even in this mode, GFL devices cannot operate without the
 established power grid. GFL converters are often referred to as “current source” converters.
 Although this definition is generally correct, GFL converters are still VSCs that operate in P and
 Q control mode.

 A basic equivalent diagram of a voltage source converter (VSC) operating in GFL mode with P
 and Q control is shown in Figure 1. It requires a phase-locked loop (PLL) to extract the grid
 voltage phase angle from the instantaneous voltage waveform measurements. The PLL-measured
 phase angle is essential for keeping the converter synchronized with the grid by controlling the
 phase angle rotation in the converter. The active and reactive power are regulated by the inner
 current-control loop. If the grid voltage is lost, the converter cannot operate in this mode, and it
 must be de-energized. The GFL converter cannot restart until the grid voltage is restored;
 however, GFL converters can ride through various types of short-term grid voltage and
 frequency faults. GFL converter controls are used in most grid-connected IBRs, including wind
 and solar photovoltaic (PV) generation. GFL converters can provide many grid-supporting
 functions at different timescales. Such functions are achieved by using different forms of active
 and reactive power control that can provide services to the grid at different timescales.

 Figure 1. General diagram of GFL operation
 Many studies and field experiences from around the world demonstrated that GFL controls can
 become unstable under low-grid-strength conditions. This fact presents an increasing stability
 risk for power systems with very high shares of GFL IBRs because the grid strength will be

 2

 significantly lower as the numbers of dispatched synchronous machines decreases. To address
 this issue, the robustness and stability of GFL IBR converter controls has been a subject of
 intensive research by original equipment manufacturers, utilities, reliability organizations, and
 academia. These issues include tuning the IBR controls and their PLL schemes to improve the
 stability of GFL wind and solar power plants exposed to low-system-strength conditions. But
 even with some improvements, as shares of GFL IBRs keep increasing, it will become more
 challenging to maintain system stability without using additional stability-enhancing equipment,
 such as synchronous condensers.

 GFM converters are considered a resource that can expand the regions of stable operation under
 wider ranges of grid strength conditions. GFM converters directly control their output voltage
 and frequency by acting as a voltage source. In areas of decreasing short-circuit strength, GFM
 IBRs might be able to expand the region of stability or help support the grid [1]. A general
 diagram of a GFM converter is shown in Figure 2. Unlike GFL control, GFM control uses the
 measured instantaneous voltage signal instead of the calculated phase angle signal produced by
 the PLL; therefore, the GFM response and support to the grid is much faster in transient
 timescales. This is achieved by maintaining a constant internal voltage phaser at the transient and
 sub-transient timescales; therefore, GFM IBRs can provide automatic load tracking (or response
 to load/generation steps) and reactive power/voltage regulation support. In addition, GFM IBRs
 can operate as an open-circuit voltage circuit, serving as a black-start resource and operating
 isolated loads.

 Figure 2. General diagram of a GFM converter
 GFM battery converters are becoming a mainstream technology in the industry, with many
 vendors providing such technology at different power ratings. GFM operation by wind and PV
 systems are still in the demonstration stages. GFM controls for inverters are not standardized and
 can be implemented in several ways. Some main methods used in the industry include droop-
 based GFM control, virtual synchronous machine, and virtual oscillator control. In this project,
 we adopted the droop-based GFM control for its simplicity and robustness. We implemented it in
 simulation models and demonstrated it by testing it on a real utility-scale WTG.

 4 Development of Model WTGs Operating in GFM
 Mode
 Types 3 and 4 WTGs are the two most used turbine topologies today, with many manufacturers
 in different countries mass-producing these multimegawatt wind turbines for grid-scale
 operation. The grid impacts and the performance of such machines in GFL mode are well
 understood, and in many cases, the performance is standardized through national and
 international grid codes and standards. In contrast, for GFM operation for types 3 and 4, there is
 a knowledge gap on how to control and operate GFM wind turbines, how to account for this new
 mode of operation in the turbine design stage, and in understanding the stability, reliability, and
 resilience benefits.

 What does it take for a wind turbine to become GFM? The general answer is not much, at least
 from an electrical design viewpoint. The conversion to GFM operation is essentially a control
 software upgrade with no need for new electrical hardware components of the drivetrain and the
 power converter in Type 3 and Type 4 wind turbines. In some cases, however, certain
 modifications are needed depending on the design characteristics of a given turbine. NLR began
 testing a multimegawatt Type 3 WTG in GFM mode during 2021. This turbine uses the same
 components as those for GFL operation with the controls upgraded to operate on programmable
 f-P and V-Q droops. Since 2019, Siemens-Gamesa has been successfully demonstrating GFM
 operation of the 69-MW Type 4 Dersalloch wind power plant (WPP) in Scotland. This power
 plant demonstrated stable performance in GFM mode during weeks of operation with controls to
 emulate various levels of inertia constant, H.

 The general diagram of the Type 3 topology is shown in Figure 5. This type of wind turbine uses
 a wound-rotor induction generator directly coupled with the grid and a partially scaled power
 converter connected to the rotor circuit. The power rating of the converter is typically near 30%
 of the overall generator capacity and is defined by limits of the variable-speed operation.

 Figure 5. Type 3 wind turbine topology
 Type 4 wind turbines use a full-scale power converter, which acts as an interface between the
 generator stator windings and the grid. The Type 4 turbine can be geared or gearless, as shown in

 6

 Figure 6, where a permanent-magnet, low-speed, synchronous generator is coupled directly with
 the wind rotor without a gearbox.

 Figure 6. Type 4 wind turbine topology
 In GFL operation, the wind turbine converter controls the level of the injected current, depending
 on the active and reactive power set points, with a specific phase angle difference from the
 voltage at the point of common coupling; therefore, to inject the desired levels of power, the
 turbine controller needs to calculate the reference current, which, in turn, requires knowledge of
 the grid voltage fundamental phasor. For this purpose, a PLL is used to measure the phase angle
 of the grid voltage at the POI. Using additional outer control loops, it is possible to control the
 active and reactive power injections to provide additional frequency- and voltage-responsive
 services.

 The general control diagram of a GFM Type 4 WTG developed by NLR is shown in Figure 7,
 with specific control functions for both the grid-side converter and the machine-side converter.
 In GFM operation, the wind turbine converter is controlling the point-of-common-coupling
 voltage magnitude and phase; therefore, there is no need for the PLL to measure the voltage
 phasor (unless it is needed for the provision of certain frequency response services or grid
 resynchronization). In this case too, however, like GFL operation, it is possible to use outer loops
 to control the levels of the injected active and reactive power when operating in grid-connected
 mode. In island mode, the GFM wind turbine will operate as a “swing bus,” adjusting its active
 and reactive power to follow the load. The benefit of PLL-free operation is better stability and
 avoidance of various interactions with the power controller. A PLL-free GFM controller offers a
 relatively simpler method that allows the converter to synchronize with the grid and operate on
 active power-frequency droop and reactive power-voltage droop.

 5 Experimental Platform
 Under this project, NLR industry partner developed and deployed GFM controls on their
 commercial 2.5-MW Type 3 wind turbine drivetrain installed at NLR’s 5-MW dynamometer
 facility. The turbine DFIG generator and power converter are connected to the NLR grid
 simulator through the turbine transformer (Figure 13). The NLR grid simulator, or the
 controllable grid interface (CGI), is rated at 7 MW and operates at 13.2 kV. It allows emulating
 any desired grid condition on the terminals of the test article, including voltage, phase angle, and
 frequency deviations in an open-loop manner. The CGI is interfaced with a real-time digital
 simulator (RTDS), so closed-loop emulation of grid conditions at POI is possible as well by
 using different real-time models of power systems under normal or faults conditions. Emulations
 of strong or weak gird interconnections are done by using real-time network impedance models.
 The CGI is also used to conduct frequency scans and to measure the impedance characteristics of
 any test article. The 5-MW dynamometer operates at variable speed and can emulate the
 dynamics of a wind rotor operating under variable-wind-speed conditions.

 6.2 Phase Jump Tests
 In power systems, events such as voltage phase jumps occur because of line or capacitor
 switching, load steps, and reclosers operation can cause large sub-cycle transients in IBRs.
 Depending on the magnitude of the phase jump, the transients can be more or less severe. Large
 phase jumps can cause transformer saturation that, in turn, will result in large transients of active
 power. In the case of wind power generation, such transients in active power can result in
 generator torque transients as well. GFL IBRs rely on PLL frequency for various types of
 ancillary services, and phase jumps will appear as sudden jumps in PLL errors. After the phase
 jump is over, the IBR should adapt its own angle reference to that of the grid. GFM IBRs do not
 require PLLs to operate, and they should automatically, almost instantaneously respond by
 injecting or absorbing power to oppose the change in voltage phase angle.

 Figure 18 and Figure 19 show the measured response of GFM 2.5-MW Type 3 WTGs to
 negative and positive phase jumps, respectively. (The WTG is connected to the grid, with the
 POI SCR = 3 and X/R = 10). In both cases, the GFM turbine shows an almost instantaneous
 response to oppose the phase jump. In both cases, the phase jump took place at t = 2 s when the
 turbine power production was at the 2-MW level. After the first jump was created by the CGI,
 the turbine demonstrated the initial active power response. Although the turbine’s prefault power
 level was dispatched at its maximum continuous capacity (no active power headroom left), the
 Type 3 machine is still capable of providing a short-term increase of active power because of the
 specifics of the Type 3 electric topology. With greater headroom available, the GFM Type 3
 machine can provide a larger active power response.

 16

 Figure 18 and Figure 19 show that active and reactive power oscillations took place for
 approximately 200 ms immediately after the phase jump event. These oscillations are caused by
 an inrush current in the turbine transformer. These inrush currents are caused by a sudden shift in
 the voltage phase angles, pushing the transformer magnetic flux above its saturation point.

 17

 Figure 18. Phase jump test for a 2.5-MW GFM Type 3 WTG (-30o)

 Figure 19. Phase jump test for a 2.5-MW GFM Type 3 WTG (+30o)
 The saturation impact on the test waveforms shown in Figure 18 and Figure 19 accentuate the
 importance of considering the transformer saturation when modeling IBRs (both GFM and
 GFL). Any transformer will saturate when the transformer core is fully magnetized and operating
 at its maximum magnetic flux. Typical causes for transformer saturation include, but are not
 limited to, phase jumps, high-voltage conditions, underfrequency, DC offsets induced by an

 18

 additional magnetic flux in the core, phase mismatch, and residual flux. IBR plants are exposed
 to all these conditions under field operation and need to provide robust and reliable performance
 during such transient events; therefore, it is important to validate full IBR systems, including not
 only inverters but also full models of their transformers, including saturation and hysteresis [26].

 The voltage recovery after transient events such as voltage sags or phase jumps can also saturate
 transformers, which, in turn, can cause inrush currents similar to those that are produced during
 transformer energization. Some residual flux always remains in the transformer core during
 voltage sags and phase jumps. When the voltage is restored, the level of the transformer
 magnetizing current depends on a level of residual flux, as explained in Figure 20 (a single-phase
 transformer example is used for simplicity). After the voltage recovery, a transient flux is
 produced and added to the residual flux, reaching high flux magnitudes. At that point, the core is
 fully saturated. As a result, the air-core inductance of the winding will significantly decrease,
 causing a high-amplitude inrush current. The worst condition to result in the deepest saturation
 and the highest level of inrush current is when the fault inception and the fault clearing happen
 when the voltage is crossing zero in the same. This is because the flux waveform lags the voltage
 by 90°. Such conditions have a low probability of occurring in real systems in the field, but they
 can be emulated by the grid simulators during the IBR testing.

 Figure 20. Explanation of transformer inrush current during energization after voltage faults. A
 similar phenomenon can occur after voltage phase jumps
 A transformer magnetizing current contains odd-order harmonics. Such odd harmonics can
 interact with the system impedance on both sides of the transformer, including the inverter or the
 rest of the grid. Such interactions, in turn, can result in harmonic overvoltages. If the inverter or
 the system impedances have resonance frequencies near the harmonics of the magnetizing
 current, the voltage harmonics could be amplified and further increase the flux, causing an even
 higher level of saturation in the transformer. This will further increase the magnetizing current,
 causing an additional increase in the voltage harmonics. This amplification phenomenon is called

 19

 ferroresonance. It is a resonance between the system impedance and the transformer core, and it
 can have a destructive impact on the equipment.

 These considerations make it clear that transformer saturation is an important factor impacting
 the transient performance of IBR plants, and it cannot be ignored. Failure to include transformer
 saturation in electromagnetic transient (EMT) models of IBR plants will result in incomplete
 models and could produce incorrect results under several types of transient conditions.

 The simulated response to a phase jump of a Type 4 GFM wind turbine operating at P=0.5 p.u. is
 shown in Figure 21 for negative and positive phase jumps, respectively. The figure includes a
 case with and without transformer saturation enabled in the model. The impact of the transformer
 saturation is clearly shown in the active/reactive power time series immediately after the phase
 jump event, which was introduced at t = 0.1 s and t = 0.6 s. These simulations show the
 importance of considering transformer saturation when modeling transient events caused by
 phase jumps and voltage sags to ensure the correct evaluation of transient current levels. Such
 high inrush currents can produce voltage harmonics, which, under certain grid characteristics,
 can lead to parallel resonances in the system.

 7.1 Measured Impedance Characteristic of a Type 3 GFM WTG
 Impedance-based analysis has become the mainstream approach for the stability analysis of IBR-
 based systems. Many utilities now require original equipment manufacturers to provide the
 impedance characteristics of WTGs and other IBRs to support system stability studies.
 Impedance characteristics can be used to validate the dynamic performance of EMT simulation
 models over a broad frequency range. These developments have made the impedance
 measurement of utility-scale wind turbines and inverters an important grid integration test. Under
 U.S. Department of Energy Wind Energy Technologies Office funding, NLR developed the Grid
 Impedance Scan Tool (GIST). GIST is based on software to scan the impedance of any IBR,
 including WTGs using EMT models. The same GIST software was implemented in NLR’s CGI-
 based test platform for the frequency scanning of real megawatt-scale IBRs. The system is being
 used by several U.S. Department of Energy- and industry-sponsored projects to evaluate the
 stability impacts of wind turbine and inverter systems [27].

 sequence response at frequency fp. A similar frequency-coupled response is present in currents
 for the injection of a negative-sequence voltage perturbation. The sequence admittance with

 7.2 Evaluation of SSR Risk in Type 3 GFM WPP
 WPPs with DFIG-based Type 3 wind turbines are known to be prone to subsynchronous
 resonance (SSR) problems when they operate within the proximity of series-compensated
 transmission lines [3]. The root cause of SSR in standard Type 3 wind turbines is well
 researched. Existing commissioned Type 3 wind turbines operate in GFL mode, in which they
 are controlled as current sources feeding the power grid. The impedance modeling of standard
 Type 3 GFL wind turbines has demonstrated that they exhibit a negative resistance and hence
 negative damping at subsynchronous frequencies. Moreover, it has also been shown that the
 negative resistance is due to the proportional gain of the current controller in the turbine’s rotor-
 side converter getting divided by the frequency-dependent slip; the latter is negative at
 subsynchronous frequencies and consequently creates a negative resistance in the wind turbines’
 impedance [3], [9]. The negative resistance can reduce the damping of the potential resonance
 between the WTGs, which presents inductive characteristics, and the series compensation
 capacitors inserted in transmission lines; hence, manufacturers are required to reduce the risk of
 SSR events in Type 3 wind turbines through appropriate design, such as adding additional
 damping controllers and modifying the filter design [11], [12].

 To measure the small-signal impedance responses of the device at a specific operation point, the
 dynamometer operates the turbine drivetrain at a constant speed, and the turbine control system
 sets the active and reactive power references. Figure 30. shows the impedance measurements of
 the wind turbine under test operating at two different power levels (0.9 MW [red] and 2 MW
 [green]). The impedance measurements experimentally confirmed the positive resistance
 behavior of the Type 3 GFM wind turbine, where the impedance phase response is confined
 within −90◦ to +90◦ at subsynchronous frequencies (highlighted in the red box in Figure 30.).
 Further, such positive damping behavior at subsynchronous frequencies from the GFM turbine is
 not dependent on a specific operating condition. To understand the fundamental mechanism that
 improves the damping characteristics in Type 3 wind turbines when they are operated with GFM
 control, sequence impedance models were developed using the GFM control design for Type 3
 wind turbines.

 Type 3 GFM wind turbines naturally exhibit positive resistance or damping in the
 subsynchronous frequency range. This behavior is independent of the operation condition. A
 detailed analysis of the damping characteristics of the Type 3 topology was published by the
 project team in [18]. The main conclusion from this paper was that it identified that the Type 3

 orientation, which naturally compensates for the classic negative resistance behavior in GFL
 Type 3 wind turbines regardless of the turbine operating condition. This property of Type 3 GFM
 wind turbines provides a potential mitigation for SSR damping without the need for additional
 damping controllers. The demonstrated ability of Type 3 GFM wind power to provide (1) grid-
 stabilizing GFM capability, (2) higher levels of fault currents, and (3) potential mitigation of the
 SSR problem makes it an attractive option for further large-scale land-based wind power
 deployment.

 8.2 Wind-Only Microgrid Test
 An interesting test was conducted to demonstrate the ability of GFM Type 3 WTGs to operate an
 islanded system without energy storage. Instead, load shedding was used to balance the system
 when the 1.5-MW GFL and 2.5-MW GFM Type 3 wind turbines were operating in island mode.
 The test setup is shown in Figure 37. First, both the 1.5-MW Type 3 GFL WTG at the NLR test
 site and the 2.5-MW GFM Type 3 wind turbine on NLR’s 5-MW dynamometer are operated in
 grid-connected mode (the CGI breaker B-main is closed). At some point, B-main opens, so the
 island is formed consisting of both WTGs and load. For stable operation, the GFL 1.5-MW

 9 Testing Parallel Operation of GFM WTG and GFM
 BESS
 The parallel operation of GFM IBRs can be achieved by using V/Q and f/P voltage and
 frequency droops for load sharing and stable operation without having any communication
 between individual IBRs. Any change in the power system (such as load or voltage steps) will
 cause a response in either the active or reactive power of an IBR depending on the type of
 disturbance. The performance of the droop controls will be largely impacted by the actual droop
 settings. Suboptimal droop settings (such as too small or too aggressive) can have a significant
 impact on the stability of parallel operation. In addition, for Q/V droop, the transformer
 parameters for each IBR need to be accounted for the correct droop performance because of the
 voltage drop across the IBR transformers (caused by their reactive consumption).

 In this project, we tested the parallel operation of a 2.5-MW Type 3 GFM wind generator and a
 1-MW GFM BESS using the setup shown in Figure 41. The grid is represented by NLR’s CGI,
 which can emulate various dynamic and transient conditions on its 13.2-kV bus. Both the GFM
 wind generator and the GFM BESS have their own step-up transformers, bringing the inverter
 voltages up to the 13.2-kV level.

 Figure 41. Configuration for testing the parallel operation of a GFM WTG and a GFM BESS
 A number of tests were conducted using the setup shown in Figure 41 and commanding the CGI
 to introduce voltage magnitude, frequency, and phase angle steps at different levels of grid
 strength. Any instabilities in either the GFM BESS or the GFM wind generator, as well as the
 impacts they have on each other caused by such steps, can be observed this way. During these
 tests, we varied the POI SCR levels using the CGI controls and the f/P and V/Q droop settings of
 the GFM BESS. The lowest SCR level used in these tests was SCR = 3. This is because the CGI
 control itself was becoming unstable at lower SCR levels; it has nothing to do with the devices
 under test. The results of voltage step tests are shown in Figure 42 and Figure 43 for the GFM
 wind generator and the GFM BESS, respectively. Combined, these two systems were able to
 maintain stable operation in all cases except when the BESS f/P droop setting was less
 “aggressive.” For the cases when the f/P droop was within the range from 1–3 Hz/per unit (p.u.)
 of power, the system remains stable after the voltage steps at higher emulated SCR value;
 however, when SCR = 3 and the GFM BESS droop setting was 5 Hz/p.u., the voltage step
 caused the system go into the undamped oscillation mode, which eventually tripped both
 systems. These results demonstrate the importance of adequate droop settings for the stable
 parallel operation of GFM IBRs.

 10 Simulations of GFM WPPs
 A model of a large WPP connected to a transmission substation was used to simulate the
 operation of a large GFM WPP. The same model can be used for simulations for both land-based
 and offshore WPPs. The cable parameters can be easily switched between overhead and subsea
 options, and shunt compensation can be turned on if necessary. Substation 1 is offshore, with a
 1,230-kV export cable modeled as a buried subsea cable, and Substation 2 is land based. The
 model also includes a synchronous condenser and a GFM BESS that can be activated for some
 simulation scenarios. In addition, by opening the circuit breaker between the plant POI and the
 grid, the island operation of the GFM WPP can be simulated as well. The model, shown in
 Figure 46, represents the 100-MW project connected to a 500-kV transmission system
 (represented by a voltage source and impedance) through a 230-kV underground export line. The
 SCR at the POI can be set to any level to represent a strong or weak grid interconnection. A 66-
 kV collector system is used to connect individual wind turbines to the plant substation. In this
 system, we used the NLR-developed models of Type 4 GFM wind turbines.

 Figure 46. One-line diagram of a high-voltage AC-interconnected WPP
 A simulation case with a 150-ms, three-phase, zero-voltage fault applied at the plant POI is
 shown in Figure 47. This simulation was conducted with the POI SCR = 5; however, the actual
 SCRs at individual GFM WTG terminals were significantly lower because of the impedances of
 the transformers as well as the export and collector cables. Saturation models were enabled in all
 transformers (substations and individual WTGs). A 100-MW GFM WPP can successfully ride
 through such faults and restore the prefault production. The time series in Figure 47 represents
 the total exports to the 500-kV grid. Some oscillations in the total active and reactive power are
 observed during recovery, but they are quickly damped. Figure 47 shows the voltage and current
 waveforms, the active and reactive power, and the PLL-calculated frequency at the plant POI. A
 high short-circuit current contribution at the plant 230 kV bus can be observed during the

 these Type 4 WTGs were modeled with current-limiting controls. Overcurrents are caused by
 long export lines with significant capacitance of underground cables that use shielding and have
 their conductors close together. The capacitive current, combined with normal fault current,
 creates large electromagnetic transients and high-frequency overcurrents.

 11 Stability Services by GFM Wind Power
 Historically, oscillations in power systems have always been an area of concern. Low-frequency
 inter-area modes (0.1 to 1 Hz) are present in power systems of different types and topologies and
 caused by an exchange of energy among groups of generators that are separated by impedances
 of transmission lines [19]. Inter-area oscillation modes are characterized by their frequencies,
 damping ratios, and shapes. If they are not properly damped, such oscillations can pose serious
 reliability risks to power systems [20]. Because of their system-level nature, inter-area
 oscillations can impact large geographic areas of the grid. Many historic events have been
 recorded in the U.S. Western and Eastern interconnections, in the Texas grid, and in Europe [21],
 [22], [23]. Local-mode or regional inter-plant oscillations are smaller in geographic footprint and
 faster in frequency than inter-area oscillations, but they can still cause harmful voltage and
 power oscillations, leading to stress on equipment and possible power disruptions. Some
 examples of local oscillations manifesting in measured grid frequency are shown in Figure 51.

 Figure 51. Examples of local oscillation events recorded by NLR in Colorado
 Traditionally, synchronous generators of conventional power plants are equipped with power
 system stabilizers. IBRs—such as wind, solar PV generation, and battery systems—can modulate
 their outputs to provide oscillation damping services to the grid. For this purpose, depending on
 the nature of the oscillations, the active and reactive power of IBR plants can be modulated with
 specific amplitudes, frequencies, and phase angles to effectively damp the oscillations that might
 be present in the system. To effectively damp the oscillations, the IBR plant needs to be able to
 control its power both above and below the operation point, similar to what is shown in Figure
 52(1a) for an example case when the IBR plant provides a 0.7-Hz oscillation damping response.

 46

 Figure 52. IBR active power strategies for damping services
 Such modulations will require headroom or the curtailed operation of IBR plants. If the plants
 happened to be curtailed for economic reasons or for the provision of other reliability services,
 then such short-term modulations can be provided essentially “for free.” Always keeping the
 plant curtailed for a damping response that lasts only approximately 30–40 s may be
 economically feasible since it is not going to cause a loss of large amount of energy. Another
 option can be a strategy shown in Figure 52(2a). This strategy does not require plant curtailment.
 In this modulation, only half of the sinusoidal oscillation damping power waveforms are
 produced by the IBRs. Care must be taken to ensure that the first harmonic of such a “half-
 waveform” modulation has the necessary amplitude and phase angle to ensure successful
 oscillation damping. This method can be an effective way to provide an oscillation damping
 response without precurtailment; however, it is important to consider the possible impact of
 higher harmonics in power that will be inevitably present (see Figure 52(2b) and (2c)) and ensure
 that such harmonics do not create a risk of undesired resonance in the power system. The third
 method is depicted in Figure 52(3a). The IBR plants do not require precurtailment but can be
 curtailed immediately after oscillations are detected to provide a full damping response. After the
 oscillations are successfully damped, the plants can return to the prefault power level.

 The ability of a real 1.5-MW wind turbine to modulate its active power under variable wind was
 tested by NLR. The turbine operates with a real commercial WPP controller. The modulation set
 point was provided to the power plant controller and then communicated to the wind turbine
 controller. The example test results of the turbine response to a 0.2-Hz active power modulation
 signal are shown in Figure 53. In this test, the turbine was precurtailed to provide headroom for
 the full sinewave modulation (like the case described in Figure 52(2a)). The measured “half-
 sine” modulation without precurtailment is shown in Figure 54 (the same as the method shown in
 Figure 52(3a)). In both cases, the turbine demonstrates the ability to follow the modulation

 47

 signals with an “end-to-end” time delay of approximately 300–400 ms. This time delay needs to
 be considered when designing the delay compensation to ensure the proper phase angle of the
 response in the systems with communication latencies.

 Multiple approaches are possible for damping inter-area oscillations in power systems with high
 levels of IBRs. A conventional approach using power system stabilizer schemes such as the one
 shown in Figure 56 can be implemented. This is a control based on taking the local
 measurements of grid frequency at the plant POI and using them to provide a damping response
 by the plant if oscillations occur. Unlike conventional synchronous plants, where power system
 stabilizer schemes are implemented in excitation systems, the IBRs have independent control of
 the active and reactive power; therefore, it is possible to control the active power of an IBR plant
 using a tuned scheme similar to the one shown in Figure 56. In conventional synchronous plants,
 the power system stabilizer provides a modulation of the field voltage that damps out power or
 generator speed oscillations though automatic voltage regulator control.

 approach was first demonstrated in [24]. Constant improvements in the speed and the

 determinism of wide-area monitoring systems (WAMS) make this approach technically feasible

 12 Conclusions
 GFM technology for IBRs is gaining traction in the energy industry as the power grid continues
 to evolve with increasing shares of IBRs and retiring conventional generators. GFM control by
 IBRs can replace some services that synchronous generators have been providing. Mainstream
 wind power based on Type 3 and Type 4 electric topologies, as an IBR technology, is fully
 capable of providing GFM services. This project performed testing for Type 3 WTGs and
 simulations for both Type 3 and Type 4 WTGs. Although GFM wind is not yet commercially
 available (like GFM battery storage), it can make a quick market entry when it is needed because
 it does not require hardware or electrical design changes in WTGs. There are still several aspects
 related to the controls and mechanical impacts of GFM by wind power that the industry can
 address when there is a market in place to incentivize the provision of such services. This project
 demonstrated how utility-scale wind power generation can operate as a GFM resource and
 provide a wide range of stability and resilience services through the grid. Testing and simulation
 efforts demonstrated that variable wind power, like conventional synchronous generation, can
 operate as a voltage source and contribute to grid strength and stability. Specific
 accomplishments of this project include:

 • The first demonstration of GFM operation by a utility-scale 2.5-MW Type 3 wind turbine
 generator was performed. NLR worked with the vendor of the generator to test its GFM
 controls in NLR’s 5-MW dynamometer facility using the 7-MVA grid simulator (CGI).
 Different modifications of GFM controls for this generator were tested under various
 dynamic and transient grid conditions emulated by the CGI, including fault ride-through,
 step changes in turbine power set points, and varying grid strength.
 • The stable operation of GFM wind with other GFL and GFM technologies in grid-
 connected and island modes was tested and demonstrated. The ability of GFM wind to
 participate in the black-start process in coordination with the GFM BESS was
 demonstrated as well.
 • EMT models of Type 3 and Type 4 WTGs in GFM mode were developed in this project.
 The EMT model of GFM Type 3 wind turbine was validated using real test data.
 • An impedance characterization test method was developed and used in this project to
 evaluate the impedance characteristics of a real GFM wind turbine under tests. The
 impedance characterization methods were also used with EMT models of GFM wind
 generators to identify their stability characteristics using the NLR-developed GIST.
 • Through simulations and testing, the project demonstrated how GFM wind power can
 provide wide-area stability services in the form of inter-area oscillation damping.
 The team continues research to provide answers to some remaining questions related to the role
 of GFM wind power in the power grid:

 • Under a different U.S. Department of Energy Wind Energy Technologies Office-funded
 project, some industry partners are working with NLR to conduct a field demonstration
 for a large commercial WPP operating in GFM mode. This project will help address
 questions related to the control and GFM operation of large WPPs consisting of dozens of
 multimegawatt WTGs. The field demonstration will also reveal any possible impacts that
 GFM operation might have on the turbine mechanical loading and component life, and it
 will help develop mitigation strategies if such impacts prove to be substantial.

 • Methods for determining the optimal placement of GFM wind in the grid and
 determining the optimal ratios between GFM and GFL sources under different grid
 strength and contingency conditions are also ongoing NLR research efforts funded by the
 U.S. Department of Energy Wind Energy Technologies Office.
 A final concluding thought from the WindVSG project: The wind energy industry has a unique
 but narrow window of opportunity to test, validate, procure, and gain experience with GFM
 technology now, before the need to provide such services becomes critical, forcing less optimal
 and more expensive ad hoc solutions. The wind energy industry must take advantage of the
 opportunity to maintain grid reliability through the transition to GFM operation at low (or near
 zero) additional cost. System operators and reliability entities must incentivize requirements for
 GFM controls. Otherwise, projects that are currently in interconnection queues will be built with
 conventional GFL controls, creating needs for additional grid-stabilizing equipment in the near
 future.
