Middle Meningeal Artery Arising From the Basilar Artery: A Case Report and Review of a Rare Anatomic Variant
Authors: Jordan M. Rasmussen, Luke J. Weisbrod, Mithun G. Sattur, Nicholas Borg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND IMPORTANCE:The middle meningeal artery (MMA) typically arises from the internal maxillary artery and enters the cranium through the foramen spinosum. Rare embryological deviations may result in anomalous origins, including from the basilar artery, a variant with only a handful of angiographically confirmed cases reported. The middle meningeal artery (MMA) typically arises from the internal maxillary artery (IMAX), a terminal branch of the external carotid artery, and enters the cranium through the foramen spinosum to supply the dura mater. Owing to its consistent anatomy, the MMA is central to neurosurgical and endovascular procedures. Embryologically, it derives from the stapedial arterial system, which regresses and is incorporated into the external carotid circulation. Rare deviations in this process may result in anomalous origins, including from the ophthalmic, internal carotid, or basilar artery.1-5

An MMA arising from the basilar artery is exceedingly uncommon, with only a small number of angiographically confirmed cases reported.3,6 Most reported cases have been incidental angiographic findings without surgical pathology.4-12 Shared features include origin from a lateral pontine branch of the basilar artery, absence or hypoplasia of the ipsilateral foramen spinosum, and lack of the usual external carotid contribution.4,5,9 Only one prior case was associated with a meningioma, and none involved both a meningioma and an arteriovenous malformation (AVM).9

This report describes the first case of a basilar MMA supplying both a World Health Organization (WHO) grade 2 falcine meningioma and a contralateral parasagittal AVM. Beyond its clinical relevance, it further characterizes the anatomic course and embryological basis of the basilar MMA variant, emphasizing its developmental plausibility and consistency with prior reports.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

A 56-year-old man presented with progressive right leg weakness and recurrent falls. Neuroimaging demonstrated a left falcine meningioma, a right frontal meningioma, and a right parasagittal AVM (Figure 1).

Axial T2-weighted fat-suppressed MRI of the brain without contrast. A hypointense left parafalcine dural-based mass is visualized, consistent with a meningioma. A smaller right parasagittal flow void is also noted, corresponding to the known arteriovenous malformation.

Cerebral angiography demonstrated an anomalous MMA arising from the midbasilar trunk, approximately 1 cm proximal to the superior cerebellar artery (SCA). On anteroposterior and lateral projections (Figure 2A and 2B), the vessel coursed laterally with an initial posterior deflection, enlarged near the trigeminal ganglion, and then assumed a standard intracranial MMA distribution. Based on angiographic features and embryological considerations, the vessel appeared to course past, rather than through, the foramen spinosum.

Digital subtraction angiogram through left vertebral artery injection demonstrating the anomalous right middle meningeal artery arising from the basilar artery in anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) projections.

A noncontrast axial computed tomography confirmed an intact foramen (Figure 3). Three-dimensional angiographic reconstruction further delineated the anomalous artery, highlighting the posterior loop before its anterolateral ascent to the dura (Figure 4).

Axial noncontrast head computed tomography with bone reconstruction demonstrating an intact right foramen spinosum.

Three-dimensional angiographic reconstruction, anteroposterior view, demonstrating the anomalous middle meningeal artery arising from the midbasilar trunk.

The patient underwent staged surgical management with resection of the AVM, followed by resection of the left falcine meningioma. Pathology demonstrated a WHO grade 2 meningioma in the falcine location and a WHO grade 1 meningioma in the right frontal region. Intraoperatively, the left canonical MMA was surgically ligated during dural opening, achieving adequate tumor supply control without the need for embolization of the variant vessel. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. Written informed consent for publication of this case was obtained from the patient. This study was conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines, and institutional review board approval was not required for a single-patient case report.

DISCUSSION

Literature Review and Anatomic Features

Across the published basilar-origin MMA cases, a recurring pattern emerges: origin 5 to 10 mm proximal to the SCA from a lateral pontine branch or directly from the basilar trunk, often with a lateral or inferolateral course and caliber increase near the trigeminal ganglion before assuming a typical MMA trajectory. The relationship to the foramen spinosum is variable: present in some cases,6 absent in others,5,11 and not specified or not discussed in several reports.4,7-10,12

In our patient, the foramen spinosum is intact, yet the angiographic course suggests the basilar-origin MMA likely runs past the spinosum and augments its caliber through subtle collateral inflow in the trigeminal/foramen spinosum region before assuming a standard intracranial MMA distribution, an observation that aligns with prior case descriptions of caliber increase near the trigeminal ganglion and the concept of convergent dural supply intracranially.3,6,7

In Waga's angiographic case,7 the anomalous branch arose 9 mm proximal to the SCA, coursed laterally and inferiorly, enlarged near the trigeminal ganglion, and then “assume[d] the normal course of the anterior branch of the MMA.” In the case described by Shah et al,4 the MMA originated from an enlarged lateral pontine branch; the distal segment supplied meatal territory and the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) distribution in the setting of diminutive anterior inferior cerebellar artery and absent PICA, yet intracranially followed a standard dural path. In Katz et al,8 the anomaly was described as a basilar–MMA anastomosis associated with a cerebral aneurysm, although detailed course and foramen spinosum status were not specified. Embryological Interpretation

The MMA derives from the stapedial system: at ∼4 to 5 weeks, the stapedial artery (from the hyoid/second arch) divides into maxillo-mandibular and supraorbital divisions. Annexation and regression events then link the supraorbital division to the intracranial MMA tree, while the maxillomandibular division is assimilated by the external carotid system to form the IMAX. The foramen spinosum forms around the IMAX–MMA connection when this channel becomes established. By ∼10 weeks, the stapedial stem regresses and the MMA is typically an IMAX branch traversing the foramen spinosum.6,13

Concurrently, a perineural vascular plexus develops around the trigeminal ganglion. Primitive carotid–basilar connections (e.g., the trigeminal artery and lateral pontine branches) interface with this plexus, providing a potential basilar-to-trigeminal conduit retained when annexation/regression favors posterior inflow.5,7

This framework explains the observed variability. When the foramen spinosum is present but the basilar artery provides the dominant inflow, partial IMAX-MMA formation permits osseous development of the foramen spinosum, but this channel may regress or become angiographically occult, leaving the basilar-trigeminal connection as the principal supply.4,7,9 Conversely, the absence of foramen spinosum reflects failure of the IMAX annexation, with persistence of the basilar-trigeminal connection as the exclusive route into the middle cranial fossa.5,6,8 Persistent lateral pontine branches can also maintain contact with the trigeminal plexus, forming a conduit from the basilar trunk to the MMA tree and explaining the recurrent caliber increase near the trigeminal ganglion.1,2,4-10

In this context, the intact foramen spinosum in our patient suggests partial IMAX-MMA development, while dominant basilar inflow supports persistence of a basilar-trigeminal connection.

CONCLUSION

Basilar-origin MMA variants, though rare, follow reproducible anatomic and embryological patterns with direct implications for neurosurgical and endovascular practice. This case represents the first reported example supplying both a meningioma and an AVM and underscores the importance of recognizing posteriorly derived dural inflow when planning operative or endovascular interventions.
