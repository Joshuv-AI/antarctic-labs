Disseminated BCGosis in immunocompromised infants: a five-case series in the context of a national BCG vaccination policy change
Authors: Wejdan Alhusaini, Ghazal Sanyour, Rahaf Kutbi, Yousef Alhusaini, Renad Alomari, Renad Algarni, Walaa Mansouri, Alaa Al-Juaid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisseminated Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) infection (BCGosis) is a rare but serious complication that primarily affects infants with underlying immunodeficiency disorders. In Saudi Arabia, where consanguinity rates are high and primary immunodeficiency disorders are more prevalent, concerns have been raised regarding the safety of routine neonatal BCG vaccination. In 2019, the national immunization schedule was revised to delay BCG vaccination from birth to six months of age.MethodsThis retrospective case series was conducted at a tertiary referral center in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. Pediatric patients diagnosed with disseminated BCGosis between 2016 and 2023 were identified through electronic medical records. Disseminated BCGosis was defined as systemic infection with involvement of two or more noncontiguous organ systems with microbiological or molecular confirmation of Mycobacterium bovis. Clinical, microbiological, and immunological data were collected and analyzed descriptively.ResultsFive cases of disseminated BCGosis were identified during the study period, all occurring in immunocompromised infants who had received BCG vaccination at birth. Underlying primary immunodeficiency disorders included defects in the IL-12/interferon-gamma pathway and severe combined immunodeficiency. The median age at presentation was approximately 7 months. Clinical manifestations included persistent BCG-site inflammation, lymphadenopathy, and systemic symptoms. The interval from symptom onset to diagnosis ranged from 4 weeks to 7 months, with longer delays observed in cases presenting initially with localized disease. All cases were confirmed microbiologically. One patient died despite treatment, while the remaining patients responded to prolonged anti-tuberculous therapy and adjunctive immunotherapy. No cases of disseminated BCGosis were identified at our institution in the four years following the implementation of delayed BCG vaccination.ConclusionsThis case series describes disseminated BCGosis occurring exclusively in immunocompromised infants vaccinated at birth and not being observed after the implementation of delayed BCG vaccination. Given the observational design and small sample size, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Delaying BCG vaccination may reduce exposure to live vaccination in vulnerable infants without apparent compromise of tuberculosis control. Multicenter studies are needed to validate these observations and inform immunization strategies in high-risk populations.

Introduction

Global BCG

Vaccination with Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine is widely implemented in several regions as part of national immunization programs in tuberculosis (TB)-endemic regions. BCG vaccination remains a key strategy for preventing severe forms of TB in infants, particularly tuberculous meningitis and miliary TB. The vaccine is derived from a live attenuated strain of Mycobacterium bovis and has been included in the World Health Organization’s Expanded Program on Immunization since 1974 [1, 2]. Although generally considered safe, adverse effects may occur, most commonly regional lymphadenitis [3].

BCG vaccine strains in Saudi Arabia

In Saudi Arabia, BCG vaccination has been routinely administered since 1970 to newborns as part of the National Immunization Program [4]. Since 2005, the Danish strain (SSI 1331) has been used, replacing the Pasteur 1173-P2 and Tokyo 172-1 strains [3–7]. Following this change, an increase in adverse effects, particularly BCG-related lymphadenitis, was reported, with incidence rising to 10.4 per 1000 vaccinations [3, 5].

While localized lymphadenitis is the most frequently reported complication, disseminated BCG infection (BCGosis) is a rare but serious adverse outcome, with reported rates in Saudi Arabia ranging from 0.1 to 4.3 cases per million vaccinated children [8–10]. Disseminated BCGosis is typically characterized by systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or irritability, along with involvement of two or more organs beyond the injection site, including lymph nodes, skin, bone, or liver, with microbiological or histological confirmation of Mycobacterium bovis.

Immunodeficiency and consanguinity

Severe BCG-related complications are strongly associated with underlying primary immunodeficiency disorders. Defects in the IL-12/interferon-gamma pathway, including severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), chronic granulomatous disease (CGD), and Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD), are well-established risk factors for disseminated BCGosis [10–12].

In Saudi Arabia, high rates of consanguinity contribute to an increased prevalence of autosomal recessive primary immunodeficiency disorders. Reported consanguinity rates range between 50% and 60% [13], which is associated with a higher burden of inherited immunodeficiency conditions. As a result, a substantial proportion of infants may have undiagnosed immunodeficiencies at birth, placing them at increased risk of severe and disseminated BCG infection following routine neonatal vaccination [8–10].

In addition, improvements in early detection of immunodeficiency disorders, including newborn screening programs for conditions such as SCID, may facilitate earlier identification of high-risk infants prior to vaccination [11, 12]. Therefore, administration of the live-attenuated BCG vaccine at birth may expose vulnerable infants to an increased risk of disseminated infection before underlying immunodeficiency is recognized.

Rationale

In response to these concerns, the Saudi National Immunization Program revised its policy in 2019, delaying BCG vaccination from birth to six months of age to allow time for the diagnosis of underlying immunodeficiency disorders [14, 15]. However, local data evaluating the clinical impact of this policy change on disseminated BCG disease remain limited.

Therefore, this study describes institutional experience with disseminated BCGosis before and after the national BCG vaccination policy change.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a retrospective case series conducted at King Abdulaziz Medical City in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, a tertiary care center and a major referral center for infectious diseases and primary immunodeficiency disorders, serving a large and diverse catchment population.

Case identification

Cases were identified through a comprehensive review of electronic medical records between 2016 and 2023. Case identification was initially performed using diagnostic coding within the BestCare electronic medical record system by searching for cases labeled as disseminated BCG infection. Identified cases were then confirmed through detailed chart review, including clinical diagnosis supported by microbiological and molecular testing, such as culture and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for Mycobacterium bovis. To ensure complete case capture, electronic medical record review was supplemented by microbiology laboratory records and relevant diagnostic reports.

Case definition

Disseminated BCG infection (BCGosis) was defined as a systemic infection caused by Mycobacterium bovis following BCG vaccination, characterized by persistent systemic symptoms (e.g., fever, irritability, weight loss), involvement of two or more noncontiguous organ systems (e.g., lymph nodes, skin, bone, liver, lungs), and microbiological or molecular confirmation of Mycobacterium bovis [8–10].

Inclusion criteria

All pediatric patients diagnosed with disseminated BCG infection at King Abdulaziz Medical City in Jeddah during the study period were included. No sampling or case selection was applied, and all eligible cases identified during the study period were analyzed and included.

Consideration of potential confounders

Several factors may have influenced the identification and referral of cases during the study period. The COVID-19 pandemic likely affected healthcare utilization, potentially delaying presentation or altering access to tertiary care services. In addition, referral patterns to specialized centers can vary over time, particularly for patients with suspected immunodeficiency or complex infections, which may have influenced the number and profile of cases seen at our institution. Furthermore, screening practices for primary immunodeficiency disorders have evolved in recent years. The gradual introduction of newborn screening programs for severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) may have facilitated earlier recognition and referral of affected infants. These considerations are important when interpreting the findings of this case series.

Case descriptions

Case 1

A 9-month-old male presented to King Abdulaziz Medical City, a tertiary referral center in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, with recurrent upper respiratory tract infections and progressive left axillary swelling. He developed fever, erythema, and tenderness, followed by spontaneous rupture with purulent discharge. The patient had a persistent left deltoid swelling since 2 months of age following BCG vaccination, which fluctuated in size and intermittently discharged minimal bloody fluid.

Three weeks prior to admission, a rapidly enlarging painful axillary swelling developed, associated with fever (39–40 °C) and irritability. There was no history of weight loss, night sweats, or TB exposure.

Past medical history: Multiple ER visits for respiratory infections and one episode of pneumonia. Development was appropriate. Family history was significant for consanguinity, two unexplained infant deaths, and confirmed familial IL-12 deficiency.

Examination: Febrile but hemodynamically stable. Left deltoid swelling (3 × 4 cm) with erythema and discharge. Left axillary mass (4 × 5 × 6 cm), tense and tender. Preauricular lymphadenopathy present.

Investigations: Mild lymphocytic leukocytosis. AFB culture from axillary lymph node confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, with PCR confirming M. bovis. Imaging showed mediastinal and abdominal lymphadenopathy with hepatosplenomegaly.

Immunology: Homozygous IL-12B mutation.

Management and outcome: Treated with anti-TB therapy and interferon-gamma for 22 months with good response. Later developed Mycobacterium avium lymphadenitis, treated successfully.

Case 2

A 9-month-old male presented with recurrent swelling at the BCG site since 2 months of age, associated with intermittent fever.

History: Two previous severe pneumonia episodes requiring intubation.

Examination: Febrile, stable. BCG site swelling (2 × 4 cm) with sinus discharge. Left axillary lymphadenopathy.

Investigations: Gastric lavage cultures positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, confirmed as M. bovis by PCR.

Immunology: Not completed due to clinical deterioration.

Outcome: Despite anti-TB therapy, the patient developed septic shock and died in PICU.

Case 3

A 5-month-old female presented with fever and right thigh swelling. Unlike typical administration in the deltoid region, BCG vaccination had been administered in the thigh, as the infant was delivered at home and received delayed immunization.

Examination: Febrile, stable. Right thigh abscess (3 × 4 cm) with erythema and tenderness. Mild axillary lymphadenopathy.

Investigations: Culture from thigh aspirate positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. Ultrasound confirmed abscess.

Immunology: Mutations in IL21R, IL3RA, and TLR2.

Outcome: Successfully treated with anti-TB therapy and interferon-gamma for 18 months.

Case 4

An 8-month-old male presented with progressively enlarging bilateral axillary masses and chronic eczematous rash.

History: Received BCG at birth in our institution.

Examination: Afebrile, tachycardic. Bilateral axillary lymphadenopathy (2–3 cm).

Investigations: Culture and PCR confirmed M. bovis. Chest X-ray showed diffuse infiltrates.

Immunology: RAG-1/RAG-2 mutation.

Outcome: Treated successfully with anti-TB therapy and interferon-gamma over 24 months.

Case 5

A 2-month-old female presented with axillary lymphadenopathy and swelling at the BCG site, associated with lethargy and poor feeding.

History: Positive consanguinity and sibling death due to septic shock.

Examination: Lethargic but afebrile. Axillary lymph node enlargement.

Investigations: PCR positive for M. bovis. CT showed widespread lymphadenopathy.

Immunology: RAG-1/RAG-2 mutation.

Outcome: Successfully treated with prolonged anti-TB therapy and interferon-gamma.

Discussion

Interpretation of findings

This case series describes disseminated BCGosis occurring exclusively in immunocompromised infants who received the BCG vaccine at birth, with no cases observed following the national policy change delaying vaccination to six months of age. All affected infants had confirmed or strongly suspected primary immunodeficiency disorders, particularly those involving T-cell dysfunction or defects in the IL-12/IFN-γ pathway, consistent with previously described risk profiles [8–10].

The World Health Organization recommends BCG vaccination at birth in countries with a tuberculosis incidence exceeding 40 cases per 100,000 population. However, in Saudi Arabia, TB incidence is below 10 cases per 100,000 population, allowing flexibility in vaccine timing without compromising population-level protection [4]. In response to epidemiological surveillance data demonstrating declining pediatric TB rates, the Saudi Ministry of Health revised the national immunization schedule in 2019 to delay BCG vaccination until six months of age [14, 15].

Notably, no cases of disseminated BCGosis have been identified at our center in the four years following this policy change. Given the observational nature of the study, this temporal association should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in settings where immunodeficiency disorders are prevalent due to high consanguinity rates [13]. Since many primary immunodeficiencies are clinically diagnosed within the first six months of life, postponing administration of a live-attenuated vaccine provides a critical window for detection of vulnerable infants [11, 12, 14].

Comparison with previous studies

Globally, BCG vaccination has demonstrated protective effects not only against tuberculosis but also against other infections through trained innate immunity and Th1-polarized adaptive responses [2, 4]. Immunological studies have shown that BCG induces macrophage and dendritic cell activation, cytokine production (including IL-6 and IL-10), and downstream signaling pathways such as STAT3 and PD-L1, contributing to its broad immunomodulatory effects [16]. However, strain-specific differences in immunogenicity and adverse event profiles have been well documented. The Danish (SSI 1331) strain, used in Saudi Arabia since 2005, has been associated with stronger immune activation but higher rates of lymphadenitis and systemic complications compared with other strains [3–5]. Anderson et al. reported increased mortality associated with BCG-Denmark in Ugandan infants, highlighting a possible trade-off between immunogenicity and safety [6].

Within Saudi Arabia, multiple studies have reported disseminated BCGosis predominantly among infants with SCID and other inborn errors of immunity, emphasizing the heightened vulnerability of this population [8, 9]. Our findings are consistent with these reports but extend the literature by providing post-policy data demonstrating an absence of disseminated BCGosis following delayed vaccination. Given the lack of a formal comparative methodology, these findings are presented descriptively rather than as evidence of causation. These findings complement emerging national evidence supporting delayed BCG administration in SCID patients and strengthen the argument for broader policy adaptation in similar high-risk settings [14].

Strengths

While disseminated BCGosis in immunodeficient infants has been previously reported, data examining institutional experience before and after a national vaccination policy change remain limited. This study’s primary strength lies in its evaluation of disseminated BCGosis before and after a national vaccination policy change, offering real-world observational data. Detailed clinical, microbiological, radiological, and genetic characterization of cases enhances diagnostic accuracy. Additionally, focusing on a population with high consanguinity provides valuable insights applicable to other regions with a high burden of primary immunodeficiency disorders [4, 10, 13].

Limitations

The study is limited by its single-center design and small sample size, which restrict generalizability and preclude definitive causal inference. Potential confounders include improvements in genetic testing, increased awareness of immunodeficiency disorders, and expanded newborn screening for SCID, which may have contributed to earlier identification of high-risk infants [11, 12]. Changes in referral or reporting practices over time may also have influenced case detection. Additional potential confounders include the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on healthcare utilization and vaccination practices.

Additionally, the total number of BCG-vaccinated infants during the study period was not available, and therefore incidence rates could not be calculated.

The absence of disseminated BCGosis after implementation of the delayed vaccination policy should therefore be interpreted cautiously given the observational nature of the study.

Implications for practice

These findings have important implications for immunization strategies in regions with low TB burden and high prevalence of primary immunodeficiency. Delaying BCG vaccination may improve the safety profile of immunization programs by reducing exposure to live vaccination in vulnerable infants, without apparent compromise of population-level tuberculosis control [4, 14]. Tailoring vaccine schedules based on population-specific genetic and epidemiological risks represents a pragmatic approach to optimizing public health outcomes. Consideration of BCG strain selection may further mitigate adverse events in vulnerable pediatric populations [5, 16, 17, 18].

Future research

Multicenter studies across Saudi Arabia and other high-consanguinity regions are needed to validate these findings and assess their generalizability. Prospective cohort studies and national surveillance data would strengthen causal inference regarding vaccination timing and adverse outcomes. Integration of broader neonatal genetic screening for primary immunodeficiency could further refine vaccine decision-making [8, 9]. Additionally, future research should continue to explore strain-specific safety profiles and the non-specific protective effects of BCG vaccination [14, 17].

Conclusion

This case series describes disseminated BCGosis occurring only in immunocompromised infants vaccinated at birth, with no cases observed following delayed BCG vaccination. The findings highlight the policy relevance of adjusting vaccine timing in populations with high rates of primary immunodeficiency. Delayed administration may reduce exposure to live vaccination in vulnerable infants without apparent compromise of tuberculosis control. Multicenter studies are needed to validate these observations and guide evidence-based immunization strategies.
