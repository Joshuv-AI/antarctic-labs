An intelligent MRI-based all-in-one diagnostic strategy for axillary lymph node status in breast cancer
Authors: Xiang Zhang, Ya Qiu, Yun Su, Qinyue Yao, Haojiang Li, Shaoyan Zheng, Xinwei Tang, Weifeng Su, Rouzi Yusufu, Yingying Huang, Xiaohong Chen, Chuang Gao, Miaomiao Ding, Shuyi Yang, Yanqin Zeng, Yudong Li, Jinghua Zhao, Yuqin Peng, Zhiyao Liu, Rui Chen, Zehong Yang, Lang Xiong, Ning Mao, Zhuangsheng Liu, Jun Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An integrated diagnostic strategy of preoperative identification of sentinel lymph node (SLN) metastasis, SLN metastatic burden, and non-SLN (NSLN) metastasis in breast cancer remains to be developed to guide axillary surgery de-escalation. Here we develop a magnetic resonance imaging-based hierarchical multitask deep learning model, breast cancer axillary lymph node network (BCALN-Net) to predict SLN metastasis, SLN metastatic burden, and NSLN metastasis in 6,271 breast cancer patients. BCALN-Net achieves high performance in predicting SLN metastasis, SLN metastatic burden, and NSLN metastasis and exhibits robust performance across molecular subtypes, clinical tumor stages, clinical node stages, estrogen receptor statuses, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 statuses, menopausal statuses, and SLN metastatic burdens. In pooled analysis of 4,081 patients, BCALN-Net also shows superior performance in predicting the omission of axillary invasive procedures and added value over clinical criteria. BCALN-Net holds the potential to provide an integrated diagnostic strategy of ALN status to help axillary surgery de-escalation in breast cancer patients.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer deaths in women worldwide1. Surgery is the primary treatment choice for early breast cancer2. Identification of axillary lymph node (ALN) status, including the absence or presence of sentinel lymph node (SLN) metastasis, SLN metastatic burden, and the absence or presence of non-SLN (NSLN) metastasis (i.e., metastases in other ALNs that are not SLNs) is a pivotal part of surgical management, which will assist in guiding adjuvant therapy (e.g., systemic therapy, radiation therapy, or both) and local control of axillary disease3. ALN status is also a crucial predictor of locoregional recurrence and overall survival in patients with breast cancer4,5.

Clinically, SLN biopsy (SLNB) and ALN dissection (ALND) are the standard of care for assessing the ALN status of breast cancer6. Traditionally, if SLNs were determined to be metastasis by SLNB, ALND would be sequentially performed; if SLNs were pathologically negative, further ALND could be omitted5,6. However, ALND and SLNB are both invasive procedures that may lead to morbidities, such as lymphedema, pain, reduced strength and range of motion of the upper extremity, with prevalence from 16.5 to 40% for ALND and from 3.7 to 21.7% for SLNB, which have an adverse impact on activities of daily living, health-related quality of life, and the ability to return to work7. Despite the relatively minimal invasive nature7, SLNB carries nonnegligible risks, such as increased anesthesia time and expense due to intraoperative pathologic examination of SLNB, even a second trip to the operating room8. More importantly, recent randomized clinical trials demonstrated that ALND9–11, even SLNB12,13, could be avoided in selected patients9–13. For instance, SLNB would be omitted for patients with small breast cancer up to 2 cm and negative ALNs determined by ultrasonography (the SOUND trial)12, or patients with clinically node-negative staged as T1 or T2 invasive breast cancer (the INSEMA trial)13. Only SLNB is needed, and ALND can be omitted for patients with SLN low-burden (i.e., 1–2 metastatic SLNs) and no NSLN metastasis, as shown in the SENOMAC9, ACOSOG Z001110, and AMAROS trials11. For patients who have NSLN metastasis9 or SLN heavy-burden (i.e., ≥3 metastatic SLNs)10, the omission of ALND is not safe9,10. Thus, there is an explicit requirement for an integrated strategy that can reliably identify the status of SLN and NSLN preoperatively, including the presence of SLN metastasis, SLN metastatic burden, and the presence of NSLN metastasis, which enables a new management paradigm of the axilla. This integrated strategy would be highly helpful for optimizing the de-escalation of axillary surgical interventions in patients with breast cancer.

Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) is a noninvasive means with the capacity to comprehensively assess the breast primary tumor and ALN statuses14–17. Clinically, mpMRI includes T1-weighted imaging, T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) imaging, which can provide multidimensional diagnostic information of lesions, such as morphology, molecular diffusion function, as well as tissue microcirculation perfusion and vascularity18,19. Previously, mpMRI showed poor to moderate discriminatory power in distinguishing the metastatic from non-metastatic ALN in breast cancer with a reported area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) ranging from 0.54 to 0.8820,21. When integrated with deep learning, mpMRI showed an improved performance with a reported AUC ranging from 0.71 to 0.92 and a weighted AUC of 0.7922. The performance of deep learning-enabled mpMRI remains suboptimal, which may be associated with limited input information (e.g., primary breast tumor23,24). More importantly, these deep learning models were developed to accomplish a single task of predicting the overall status of ALNs23–25, or two individual tasks of predicting the statuses of SLNs and NSLNs26. A deep learning model, which can be used as an artificial intelligence (AI) tool to provide an integrated diagnosis strategy of the status of ALNs, including the presence of SLN metastasis, SLN metastatic burden, and the presence of NSLN metastasis, remains to be developed.

In this work, we develop a hierarchical multitask deep learning framework, breast cancer axillary lymph node network (BCALN-Net, Fig. 1), for comprehensively predicting the ALN statuses, including the presence or absence of SLN metastasis, SLN metastatic low-burden or heavy-burden, as well as the presence or absence of NSLN metastasis by using multiregional information of primary breast tumor and ipsilateral axillary region from mpMRI. A 3D-nnUNet-based multiregional automated segmentation model (BCALNSeg-Net) is developed to automatically delineate the mask of the primary breast tumor and the ipsilateral axillary region. To the best of our knowledge, BCALN-Net is trained and validated on the largest real-world mpMRI dataset (acquired from 39 MRI scanners and four vendors) to date with a total of 6271 breast cancer patients across ten tertiary hospitals, including a pooled training dataset (n = 2190), a pooled internal test dataset (n = 531), and three pooled external test datasets (n = 1418, n = 1282, and n = 850, respectively). BCALN-Net yields robust performance for each predictive task in all the test datasets as well as subgroups across molecular subtype, clinical tumor stage, clinical node stage, estrogen receptor (ER) status, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, menopausal status, and SLN metastatic burden. Whether BCALN-Net can assist in the decision-making of axillary surgery for patients with invasive breast cancer is determined in real-world datasets with the actual pathological results as the reference standard under two important clinical scenarios, including the omission of SLNB and the omission of ALND. In addition, BCALN-Net can provide added value in identifying patients who would benefit from omitting SLNB or ALND, when compared with the SOUND trial12 and SENOMAC trial9 criteria. Results

Dataset and study design

A nationwide, large breast mpMRI dataset of 7556 females was collected from ten medical centers across China. The mpMRI was performed using 39 MRI scanners from four vendors. Details about the MR scanner in each center are presented in Supplementary Data 1. The baseline (pretreatment) breast mpMRI of each patient, with transverse T2WI, transverse DWI with a high b value of 600/800/1000, and transverse DCE with peak-enhanced phase, was collected to develop and test the multitask predictive AI models. The details of acquisition parameters of T2WI, DWI, and DCE in each center are shown in Supplementary Data 2.

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Supplementary Fig. 1), 6271 eligible patients were included, and 1285 patients were excluded (Supplementary Table 1). We used the breast mpMRI data from Center 1 and Center 2 as the primary dataset for predictive model development, and data pooled from all the other medical centers as external test sets. Specifically, mpMRI data of 2721 patients from Center 1 and Center 2 were used for the development of the mpMRI-based deep learning predictive model and divided into a pooled training cohort (n = 2190) and a pooled internal test cohort (n = 531) at a ratio of 8:2. A 10-fold cross-validation was performed within the pooled training dataset to further validate performance. Patients from Center 3 to Center 10 were divided into three pooled external test cohorts: pooled external test cohort 1 (n = 1418, patients from Center 3 and Center 4), pooled external test cohort 2 (n = 1282, patients from Center 5 and Center 6), and pooled external test cohort 3 (n = 850, patients from Center 7, Center 8, Center 9, and Center 10).

Clinicopathologic characteristics of patients

The clinicopathologic characteristics of the 6271 eligible patients are presented in Supplementary Data 3. The median age of all included patients is 51 years (interquartile ranges [IQR], 44–59 years). More patients had clinical T1 and T2 stages (5838 of 6271 [93.1%]) and clinical N0 stages (4632 of 6271 [73.9%]) breast cancers. The histological type in most patients is invasive ductal carcinoma (4774 of 6271 [76.1%]). The histological grade in most patients is II (intermediate) and III (high) grades (5114 of 6271 [81.6%]). The number of patients who had the molecular type of luminal A, luminal B, HER2-positive, and triple-negative breast cancer was 1104 of 6271 (17.6%), 4090 of 6271 (65.2%), 605 of 6271 (9.7%), and 472 of 6271 (7.5%), respectively. In total, 4916 of 6271 (78.4%) patients did not have SLN macrometastases, 1355 of 6271 (21.6%) patients had SLN macrometastases, including 1138 of 1355 (84.0%) patients with SLN low-burden and 217 of 1355 (16.0%) patients with SLN heavy-burden. Four hundred twenty-six of 6271 (6.9%) patients had NSLN metastasis.

Performance of the multiregional automated segmentation model

To save the workload of segmentation and facilitate the application of the AI model to the clinic, a multiregional automated segmentation model, namely BCALNSeg-Net, based on the nnU-Net framework27 was developed to automatically delineate the masks of both the primary tumor and the axillary region. The volumes of interest (VOIs) of the breast tumor and axillary region, segmented manually by the radiologists in a total of 1200 patients randomly selected from the training cohort, were used for the training of BCALNSeg-Net. Dice similarity coefficients (DSCs) in segmentation between the radiologists and BCALNSeg-Net ranged from 0.747 ± 0.105 to 0.759 ± 0.133 for breast tumor and from 0.718 ± 0.146 to 0.764 ± 0.085 for axillary region, and average symmetric surface distance (ASSD) ranged from 10.602 ± 8.704 to 12.836 ± 4.514 for breast tumor and from 11.316 ± 4.612 to 13.574 ± 2.897 for axillary region. This indicates the reproducibility of segmentation between the radiologists and BCALNSeg-Net, and the stable performance of the automated segmentation model for the annotation of breast tumors and the axillary region. The VOIs of the breast tumor and axillary region annotated by BCALNSeg-Net in the training, internal test, and external test cohorts were used for the development and test of the hierarchical multitask deep learning framework, namely BCALN-Net, to integrally identify ALN statuses of breast cancer.

Performance of multitask predictive models

To determine the optimal predictive model, three individual predictive models based on the BCALN-Net framework were established for each predictive task with different inputs. Model 1 took VOIs of both the breast tumor and the axillary region as input. Model 2 took the VOI of the primary breast tumor alone as input, and model 3 took the VOI of the axillary region alone as input. The outputs of predictive models were the probability of metastatic ALN statuses, including the presence or absence of SLN metastasis (task 1), SLN low-burden or heavy-burden (task 2), as well as the presence or absence of NSLN metastasis (task 3). In addition, a predictive model (model 4) based on clinicopathologic features, including clinical tumor stage, clinical node stage, histological type, ER status, and progesterone receptor (PR) status for task 1, patient age for task 2, and MRI-reported tumor size for task 3, was also developed to predict these three tasks using univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses.

Predictors as selected by univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses in model 4 for three predictive tasks (task 1, task 2, and task 3) are shown in Supplementary Tables 2, 3, and 4, respectively. The performances of model 1, model 2, model 3, and model 4 for these three tasks in each cohort are listed in Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Data 4 and 5. In all the cohorts, model 1 outperformed the other three models in most of the performance metrics, especially for the comparisons of AUCs (Supplementary Table 6). Model 1 achieved higher AUC for task 1 (AUC range, 0.908–0.975), task 2 (AUC range, 0.858–0.993), and task 3 (AUC range, 0.875–0.927) compared with model 2 for task 1 (AUC range, 0.826–0.884), task 2 (AUC range, 0.752–0.960), and task 3 (AUC range, 0.717–0.950), model 3 for task 1 (AUC range, 0.779–0.794), task 2 (AUC range, 0.661–0.964), and task 3 (AUC range, 0.712–0.840), and model 4 for task 1 (AUC range, 0.586–0.754), task 2 (AUC range, 0.502–0.608), and task 3 (AUC range, 0.527–0.648), respectively, in each cohort. Thus, model 1 is the top-performance model in all three prediction tasks.

Confusion matrices and ROC curves of model 1 in three tasks are shown in Fig. 2b, c, respectively. Specifically, the model 1 yielded AUCs of 0.975, 0.993, and 0.927 for three tasks, respectively in the training cohort. The performance of model 1 was tested on a pooled internal and three pooled external test cohorts collected from ten medical centers (n = 4081; 501 patients from the pooled internal test cohort, 1418 patients from pooled external test cohort 1, 1282 patients from pooled external test cohort, 2850 patients from pooled external test cohort 3). In the internal test, external test 1, external test 2, and external test 3 cohorts, the model 1 yielded a high and stable AUCs of 0.939, 0.908, 0.922, and 0.927 for task 1, 0.878, 0.880, 0.858, and 0.870 for task 2, as well as 0.891, 0.875, 0.911, and 0.895 for task 3, respectively.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated an mpMRI-based AI model for the integrated assessment of ALN status in patients with breast cancer using a large multicenter dataset. The proposed hierarchical multitask deep learning framework, namely BCALN-Net, yielded an AUC ranging from 0.908 to 0.939 for the prediction of presence of SLN metastasis (task 1), from 0.858 to 0.880 for prediction of SLN metastatic burden (task 2), and from 0.875 to 0.911 for the prediction of presence of NSLN metastasis (task 3) in a pooled internal and three pooled external test cohorts. BCALN-Net outperformed the clinicopathological model based on clinical tumor stage, clinical node stage, histological type, ER status, and PR status for task 1, patient age for task 2, and MRI-reported tumor size for task 3. In addition, BCALN-Net is better than the MG model and the US model. Although BCALN-Net is based on mpMRI, which consists of three sequences, i.e., T2WI, DWI, and DCE imaging, rather than a single sequence, this AI model can address several clinically important tasks of assessing ALN statuses using an all-in-one strategy. More importantly, the established BCALN-Net also exhibited strong generalization capability across stratified subgroups, such as molecular subtypes, clinical tumor stages, clinical node stages, ER statuses, HER2 statuses, menopausal statuses, and SLN metastatic burdens for each task. In two clinical scenarios, BCALN-Net showed high performance in predicting the omission of SLNB or ALND. More patients could be identified by BCALN-Net as candidates for the omission of SLNB or ALND compared with the criteria of the SOUND trial12 and SENOMAC trial9, respectively. BCALN-Net has the added value compared with the standard-of-care in current clinical practice, which is crucial for optimizing the de-escalation of axillary surgical interventions in patients with breast cancer.

In recent years, an increasing number of randomized controlled trials have been reported on how to select an axillary management strategy to reduce the use of ALND for positive SLNs9–11 and how to provide an option to avoid SLNB for clinical ALN-negative breast cancer12,13 to achieve individualized and precise minimally invasive treatment in breast cancer. Previously, noninvasive medical imaging approaches, including MRI, mammography, and ultrasound, have been used for preoperatively assessing ALN status to reduce the complications of invasive SLNB or ALND15 and serve as important tools to guide de-escalation decision-making for axillary procedures17. Among various imaging methods, the potential advantage of MRI over mammography and ultrasound is that it gives a more global view of the axilla with a satisfactory soft tissue resolution8, which is favorable for the detection of potentially abnormal ALNs.

Notably, radiomics and deep learning approaches have been used to develop AI-enabled predictive biomarkers in radiology28. Radiomics can extract high-throughput quantitative imaging hand-crafted features from medical images via machine learning methods, following a series of standard processes to aid various processes in clinical practice29. Indeed, several investigations have explored the value of MRI-based radiomics method to evaluate the ALN status, including the presence of overall ALN metastasis (AUCs ranged from 0.805 to 0.88)30–32, presence of SLN metastasis (AUCs ranged from 0.74 to 0.863)33–35, SLN metastatic burden (AUCs ranged from 0.75 to 0.77)36, or presence of NSLN metastasis (AUCs ranged from 0.770 to 0.847)37,38. However, all these radiomics models were developed to evaluate a single task of ALN status. As such, these single-task radiomics models cannot meet the requirement for predicting the presence of SLN metastasis, the SLN metastatic burden, and the presence of NSLN metastasis in an all-in-one approach, which is important for decision-making in current clinical practice in breast cancer. In addition, whether a single task radiomics model or their combined can be used to integrally predict the multitasks of ALNs remains to be determined.

In contrast, deep learning involves learning novel feature representations through trainable convolutional operations based on discriminating patterns in training data, and the predictive model can be trained coinstantaneous with learning feature representations28. In our study, a multitask deep learning framework was adopted. This framework is a joint learning paradigm, which is capable of simultaneously predicting several clinical-related outcomes and leveraging the inherent correlations among related tasks to achieve synergistic improvements in performance, generalization, and computational efficiency39,40. Previously, several deep learning-based models using the VOI of the breast primary tumor from DCE images have been developed to predict a single task of the overall status of ALN23,25. Only one convolutional neural network based on the VOI of breast primary tumor from DCE images has been developed to achieve the prediction of two tasks, which yielded an AUC of 0.768–0.889 for the prediction of SLN metastasis and 0.728–0.880 for the prediction of NSLN metastasis26. However, these deep learning models only use features from the breast tumor without considering the differences in the tumor microenvironment between the ALN and primary tumor site. In addition, using a single MRI sequence, such as DCE imaging, would neglect the complementary information from other different MRI sequences41. For example, a deep learning model based on the breast tumor and the most suspicious metastasis ALN of the ipsilateral axilla from DCE images could provide an auxiliary tool to identify ALN metastasis in breast cancer, with an AUC of 0.907 and 0.852 in the internal and external test datasets, respectively41. This study also showed that the deep learning model built up by the VOI of breast primary tumor and ALN had a higher AUC than the model constructed either based on the VOI of breast primary tumor (AUC, 0.907 vs. 0.786) or the VOI of ALN (AUC, 0.907 vs. 0.848) in the internal test cohort41. Similarly, our study results showed that BCALN-Net, established based on the VOIs of the breast primary tumor and axillary region, is superior to either the model developed using the VOI of the breast primary tumor alone or the axillary region alone for each task. These results further confirm that the VOI of breast tumor combined with the VOI of ALN may provide complementary information and therefore more comprehensively reflect the status of ALN than the single VOI from the breast primary tumor or from ALN. Thus, in our study, a multitask deep learning model integrating features from mpMRI (i.e., T2WI, DWI, and DCE) and multi-regions (i.e., primary breast tumor and ipsilateral axillary region) was developed to predict three tasks involved in the assessment of ALN status, including the presence of SLN metastasis, SLN metastatic burden, and the presence of NSLN metastasis. To facilitate the clinical deployment of the proposed multitask deep learning model, a user-friendly, fully automated multiregional segmentation system, namely BCALNSeg-Net, was dedicatedly established to automatically delineate the masks of primary breast tumors and axillary regions to overcome the drawbacks of time-consuming and labor-intensive manual annotation workload.

By using the output masks from BCALNSeg-Net, we trained BCALN-Net to end-to-end predict the three tasks as needed in clinical practice. We also validated BCALN-Net in different clinical scenarios using a real-world dataset, including patients who would omit unnecessary SLNB if they were predicted as SLN negative by BCALN-Net, and patients who would undergo SLNB alone and omit ALND if they were predicted as SLN low-burden and NSLN negative by BCALN-Net. The results of our study demonstrated that BCALN-Net had a high NPV of 94.0 and 94.9% and an accuracy of 88.6 and 98.2% in predicting the omission of SLNB or the omission of ALND in patients from the combined internal and external test cohorts. Notably, our results showed that with the assistance of BCALN-Net, more patients would be identified to spare SLNB (according to the SOUND trial criteria12) or ALND (according to the SENOMAC trial criteria9). These results demonstrate that BCALN-Net has an incremental value in assisting the clinical decision of the de-escalation treatment plan for axillary surgery of breast cancer.

A common goal in the development of medical imaging AI tools is to make available tools that help train robust, generalizable, and reproducible deep learning models to guide patient-tailored management in real-world applications42. In our study, a large-scale multicenter dataset was used to validate the generalizability of the proposed BCALN-Net, which could provide essential evidence that boosts trust and confidence in AI-driven diagnostic support systems for clinical use43. To avoid overly optimistic results commonly encountered in retrospective studies, we also developed BCALN-Net on a mixture of data from ten different institutions with large sample sizes. Specifically, BCALN-Net in our study were constructed in the pooled training dataset (2190 patients from Center 1 and Center 2) and tested in the pooled internal test dataset (531 patients from Center 1 and Center 2), pooled external test cohort 1 (1418 patients from Center 3 and Center 4), pooled external test cohort 2 (1282 patients from Center 5 and Center 6), and pooled external test cohort 3 (850 patients from Center 7 to Center 10), respectively. In these 10 centers, mpMRI data were acquired from 39 MRI scanners and four vendors with different scanning protocols, which is helpful for reducing the vendor dependency of the established BCALN-Net. In addition, stratification analysis was performed in subgroups of different molecular subtypes, clinical tumor stages, clinical node stages, ER statuses, HER2 statuses, menopausal statuses, and SLN metastatic burdens on BCALN-Net to further validate the feasibility of the model in various clinical settings. Molecular subtypes, including luminal A, luminal B, HER2-positive, and triple-negative breast cancer, are mainstream standards for treatment selection and prognosis evaluation44. In our study, subgroup analysis based on the molecular subtypes showed that BCALN-Net had a stable predictive capability with AUCs ranging from 0.895 to 0.928 for task 1, from 0.856 to 0.896 for task 2, and from 0.858 to 0.903 for task 3. Furthermore, several randomized clinical trials demonstrated that breast cancer patients with different clinical tumor stages were able to benefit from the de-escalation strategy for axillary invasive procedures. For example, the SOUND trial showed that breast cancer patients with T1 stage lesions can avoid the unnecessary SLNB12. The ACOSOG Z0011 trial10, and AMAROS trial11 showed that breast cancer patients with clinically node-negative and cT1 and cT2 stage lesions could omit the routine use of ALND. The SENOMAC trial showed that breast cancer patients with clinically node-negative and T1 to T3 stage lesions could omit unnecessary ALND9. Thus, stratified analysis was performed based on the clinical tumor stage (i.e., T1 stage, T2 stage, and T3/T4 stages) and clinical node stage (i.e., N0 stage and N1/N2/N3 stages) in our study. The result showed a stable predictive performance of BCALN-Net for task 1 with an AUC ranging from 0.887 to 0.937, for task 2 with an AUC ranging from 0.853 to 0.931, and for task 3 with an AUC ranging from 0.843 to 0.916. Besides, as developing a noninvasive and effective prediction model suitable for patients with 1–2 positive SLNs can avoid ineffective ALND and achieve personalized cancer management9,10, stratified analysis based on the SLN metastatic burden was also conducted in our study for task 3. The result showed that BCALN-Net achieved an AUC of 0.885 for the SLN low-burden subgroup and 0.909 for the SLN heavy-burden subgroup. Taken together, BCALN-Net maintained stable predictive performance in each stratified subgroup in the pooled test cohort, which suggests the robustness and generalized capability of this deep learning model. In addition, the heatmaps showed that BCALN-Net could focus on the breast primary tumor and ALN with their surrounding regions through an attention mechanism, highlighting the interpretability of BCALN-Net in clinical applications.

Our study had some limitations. First, this is a retrospective study, which might be subject to inherent selection bias. Further prospective and randomized studies are warranted to validate the predictive performance and the clinical benefit of BCALN-Net in axillary de-escalating treatment in breast cancer patients before the actual use of this deep learning model in a real-world clinical setting. Second, the clinical relevance of the proposed BCALN-Net was developed based on the Chinese population. It might be necessary to test the generalizability of this model in patients across ethnic groups and geographical locations, and in countries with active population-based screening programs. Third, the SLN macrometastatic burden, but not the ALN total macrometastatic burden or ALN micrometastases, was predicted by BCALN-Net in our study. ALN total heavy burden may have a correlation with the survival outcome for breast cancer patients45, which is beyond the scope of this study. In addition, the results of several international clinical trials9–11 demonstrated that the discrimination between SLN macrometastatic low-burden and SLN macrometastatic heavy-burden is a key factor for the axillary de-escalation strategy. Fourth, we focused on evaluating the performance of BCALN-Net without comparing it directly to the radiologic assessment of nodal metastasis by radiologists. However, it is known that the presence of SLN metastasis, SLN metastatic burden, and the presence of NSLN metastasis can be hardly assessed by morphological features on mpMRI due to the challenge of identifying and distinguishing the SLN and NSLN node-by-node on MRI images26,36.

In conclusion, the proposed BCALN-Net established from the masks of both breast tumor and the axillary region on mpMRI provides a noninvasive and all-in-one approach for predicting the presence of SLN metastasis, SLN metastatic burden, and presence of NSLN metastasis preoperatively. This hierarchical multitask deep learning framework holds great potential in assisting decision-making of appropriate axillary de-escalating treatment in patients with breast cancer.

Methods

Ethics approval

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations and was approved by the institutional ethics committees at each participating institution, including Sun Yat-Sen Memorial Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University, Guangzhou, China (Center 1, approval number SYSKY-2024-896-01), Sun Yat-Sen University Cancer Center, Guangzhou, China (Center 2, approval number B2020-333-Y02), Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital, Yantai, China (Center 3, approval number 2022-247), the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of South China University of Technology, Foshan, China (Center 4, approval number NYKY-2024-189-01), the First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University, Ganzhou, China (Center 5, approval number 22SC-2025-101), Jiangmen Central Hospital, Jiangmen, China (Center 6, approval number 2025-284A), Shantou Central Hospital, Shantou, China (Center 7, approval number 2025-036), the Affiliated Kashi Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University, Kashi, China (Center 8, approval number 2025-39), People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, Urumchi, China (Center 9, approval number KY2025080501), and the First People’s Hospital of Qinzhou, Qinzhou, China (Center 10, approval number A-20250601). The requirement for patient-informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The study was carried out in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM)46. Before model training and testing, all data underwent de-identification processes.

Dataset

We curated a nationwide breast mpMRI dataset of 7556 females from ten medical centers across China (Center 1, n = 2000; Center 2, n = 934; Center 3, n = 1359; Center 4, n = 359; Center 5, n = 738; Center 6, n = 1034; Center 7, n = 699; Center 8, n = 189; Center 9, n = 159; and Center 10, n = 85). The mpMRI was performed using 39 MRI scanners from four vendors with the following distribution: Siemens (n = 2618 patients), GE Healthcare (n = 2024 patients), Philips (n = 839 patients), and United Imaging Healthcare (n = 790 patients).

Patients

A total of 7556 consecutive female patients with breast cancer were identified from the electronic medical record system from ten centers (Center 1, January 6, 2016 to July 31, 2024; Center 2, February 16, 2011 to September 28, 2022; Center 3, August 4, 2017 to October 21, 2021; Center 4, January 8, 2018 to December 14, 2023; Center 5, May 3, 2018 to December 11, 2023; Center 6, July 24, 2019 to December 27, 2023; Center 7, September 8, 2016 to August 31, 2023; Center 8, July 25, 2018 to March 29, 2024; Center 9, March 30, 2021 to April 18, 2023; and Center 10, March 14, 2020 to October 13, 2022). Sex was determined based on the official IDs. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) patients who were diagnosed with invasive breast cancer by postoperative histology; (b) patients who had pathologically confirmed ALN status by axillary procedures, including either SLNB or sequential SLNB and ALND; (c) patients who underwent mpMRI within 3 weeks before breast and axillary surgery; (d) no neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy was performed before mpMRI examination or breast and axillary surgery. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) history of breast cancer or axillary surgery before mpMRI; (b) poor mpMRI image quality, such as artifacts, which was assessed according to the five-point Likert-type scale (5 = excellent, 4 = good, 3 = acceptable, 2 = poor, and 1 = unacceptable)47 by two radiologists (radiologist 1: Q.Y., with 10 years’ experience in breast cancer diagnosis; radiologist 2: Z.H.Y., with 20 years’ experience in breast cancer diagnosis) in consensus; (c) incomplete clinicopathologic data.

Clinicopathologic data

Clinicopathologic data of patients, including the patient age, menopausal status, clinical tumor stage, clinical node stage determined by manual palpation, histological type, and grade of breast tumour, status of ER, PR, HER2 and Ki-67, molecular subtype, MRI-reported breast imaging reporting and data system category, breast background parenchymal enhancement on MRI, MRI-reported tumor type, MRI-reported tumor number, MRI-reported tumor size, and MRI-reported ALN status, were collected from the electronic medical record system of each center.

The postoperative pathological results of SLNB and ALND were collected as the reference standards for the determination of SLN metastasis, SLN metastatic burden, and NSLN metastasis. The status of SLN metastasis determined by SLNB includes the presence (SLN positive) and absence of SLN metastasis (SLN negative), and SLN metastatic burden includes SLN low-burden (1–2 metastatic SLNs) and SLN heavy-burden (≥3 metastatic SLNs)36. The NSLN status was determined by ALND in patients who had positive SLN, which included the presence (NSLN positive) or absence of NSLN metastasis (NSLN negative).

Breast MRI

Breast MRI was performed for each patient using a 1.5 T or 3.0 T MR scanner from different vendors with dedicated phased-array breast coils (Supplementary Data 1). Breast MRI performed in all participating centers routinely included T2WI, DWI, and DCE imaging, which are predefined to cover both the breast and the axillary region. The acquisition parameters of these sequences differed because they were optimized by each scanner’s vendor. During scanning, the initial field of view was individually adjusted based on the body size of each patient to ensure full coverage of the entire axillary region (Supplementary Data 2). DCE acquisition was performed after the intravenous injection of gadolinium contrast agent at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg, followed by saline flush with a power injector. Transverse T2WI, transverse DWI with a high b value of 600/800/1000, and transverse DCE with peak-enhanced phase were collected to develop BCALN-Net. MRI data of 2721 patients from Center 1 and Center 2 were used for the development of BCALN-Net and divided into a pooled training cohort (n = 2190, including 1579 patients from Center 1 and 611 patients from Center 2) and a pooled internal test cohort (n = 531, including 380 patients from Center 1 and 151 patients from Center 2) at a ratio of 8:2. Patients from Center 3 to Center 10 were divided into three pooled external test cohorts: pooled external test cohort 1 (n = 1418, including 1120 patients from Center 3 and 298 patients from Center 4), pooled external test cohort 2 (n = 1282, including 597 patients from Center 5 and 685 patients from Center 6), and pooled external test cohort 3 (n = 850, including 546 patients from Center 7, 105 patients from Center 8, 127 patients from Center 9, and 72 patients from Center 10).

Development of a multiregional automated segmentation model

The VOIs of breast tumors and axillary regions were manually delineated by radiologists in a total of 1200 patients randomly selected from the training cohort for the development of the multiregional automated segmentation model, BCALNSeg-Net. The manual segmentation was performed by four radiologists (radiologist 3: S.Z., with 13 years’ experience in breast cancer diagnosis and 4 years’ experience of MR images segmentation; radiologist 4: Z.L., with 2 years’ experience of breast cancer diagnosis and 2 years’ experience of MR images segmentation; radiologist 5: X.T., with 4 years’ experience of breast cancer diagnosis and 2 years’ experience of MR images segmentation; radiologist 6: Y.P., with 3 years’ experience of breast cancer diagnosis and 2 years’ experience of MR images segmentation) on the ITK-SNAP platform (version 3.6.0, http://www.itksnap.org) with 300 patients for each radiologist. The VOI of the breast tumor was manually placed along the contour of the primary breast tumor, and the VOI of the axillary region containing ALNs and adjacent fat tissue at the axillary level I8. For each patient, the VOI of the breast tumor and the VOI of the ipsilateral axillary region were delineated on T2WI, DWI, and DCE, respectively. The manually segmented VOIs were set as the reference standard to develop BCALNSeg-Net to automatically output the masks of both primary breast tumors and axillary regions on mpMRI. BCALNSeg-Net was constructed based on the nnU-Net framework with the preprocessed 3-channel mpMRI volume as the input. The nnU-Net framework took the 3D U-Net architecture as backbone, enhanced by a residual encoder to improve feature propagation and convergence, which eliminates the need for manual architecture tuning by dynamically adapting the network structure, patch size, and training pipeline to the underlying dataset27. The detailed processes for BCALNSeg-Net development are shown in Supplementary Methods.

The performance of BCALNSeg-Net was assessed by the DSC and ASSD50 between the VOIs delineated by the radiologists and the VOIs segmented by BCALNSeg-Net. The VOIs of the breast primary tumor and the ipsilateral axillary region in the remaining 5071 patients were segmented by BCALNSeg-Net.

Construction of multitask predictive models

The VOIs of the breast tumor and axillary region annotated by BCALNSeg-Net in the training, internal test, and external test cohorts were used for the development of BCALN-Net, an mpMRI-based, hierarchical multitask deep learning framework constructed to integrally identify ALN statuses of breast cancer, including discriminating between presence and absence of SLN metastasis (task 1), SLN low-burden and SLN heavy-burden (task 2), as well as presence and absence of NSLN metastasis (task 3). The automated segmentation outputs were supervised and adjusted (if any) using the ITK-SNAP platform (version 3.6.0, http://www.itksnap.org) by another two radiologists (radiologist 7: H.L., with 10 years’ experience in breast cancer diagnosis and 4 years’ experience of MR images segmentation; radiologist 8: X.Z., with 12 years’ experience of breast cancer diagnosis and 7 years’ experience of MR images segmentation).

Model architecture

BCALN-Net consisted of two sets of 3D densely connected convolutional networks (3D-DenseNet 121), which were used as the backbone of the proposed model. Each set of 3D-DenseNet 121 had two encoders. Each individual encoder processed the VOI of the breast primary tumor or the VOI of the axillary region to capture region-specific features separately in a specified task. Unlike conventional deep learning models constructed for a single specific task, BCALN-Net leveraged shared and task-specific feature representations between the two 3D-DenseNets to accomplish the hierarchical multitask for predicting ALN statuses. Harnessing the inherent correlation of these multiple related tasks, BCALN-Net applied a joint learning paradigm to achieve reciprocal benefits in improving performance, enhancing generalizability, and reducing overall computational cost in a realistic clinical practice setting. The architecture of BCALN-Net was designed to extract rich, hierarchical feature representations from mpMRI to accomplish three interconnected predictive tasks, as the DenseNet architecture can exploit dense connectivity to alleviate the vanishing-gradient problem, to strengthen feature propagation, facilitate feature reuse, and substantially reduce the number of parameters51. A stochastic feature dropout mechanism was introduced during training to randomly zero out subsets of feature maps with a predefined dropout rate of 0.2, encouraging the model to learn robust and diverse representations. The encoded feature vectors from the primary tumor and axillary encoders were aggregated using an attention-based multiple instance learning module, and then concatenated into a joint feature representation, which was passed through a fully connected multilayer perceptron with nonlinear activations to output the classified results for each predictive task. Details of the model architecture of BCALN-Net are presented in Supplementary Methods.

Predictive models

Three individual predictive models established for each task based on the BCALN-Net framework took VOIs of both the breast tumor and axillary region (predictive model 1), the VOI of the primary breast tumor alone (predictive model 2), and the VOI of the axillary region alone (predictive model 3) as input, respectively. The outputs of predictive models were the probability of metastatic ALN statuses, including the presence or absence of SLN metastasis (task 1), SLN low-burden or heavy-burden (task 2), as well as the presence or absence of NSLN metastasis (task 3). In addition, a predictive model (predictive model 4) based on clinicopathologic features was also developed to predict these three tasks using univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses, because several pretreatment clinical and pathologic characteristics have been reported to be associated with SLN metastasis34,52, SLN metastatic burden36,53, and NSLN metastasis38,54 in breast cancer.

Data augmentation

To improve the generalization ability and robustness of BCALN-Net across institutions, patients, MRI scanners, and MRI scanning protocols, a comprehensive data augmentation pipeline was employed during model training. This pipeline operated on the multiregional masks, with the three channels corresponding to T2WI, DWI, and DCE sequences. The augmentations were applied online in a stochastic manner and can be grouped into three categories: (1) spatial transformations, (2) intensity-based augmentations, and (3) domain-specific MRI augmentations. Details of the data augmentation are presented in Supplementary Methods. Each augmentation method was applied independently with probability 0.5, and parameters were randomly sampled within biologically plausible and modality-specific ranges.

Performance evaluation of multitask predictive models

The performance of all predictive modes was evaluated in the pooled training cohort and then validated in the pooled internal test cohort and three pooled external test cohorts, respectively. To further verify the generalized capability of the models in different subgroups, stratified analyses were conducted according to molecular subtypes (i.e., luminal A, luminal B, HER2 positive, and triple-negative breast cancer), clinical tumor stages (i.e., T1, T2, and T3/T4 stages), clinical node stages (i.e., cN0 and cN1/cN2/cN3), ER statuses (i.e., ER positive and ER negative), HER2 statuses (i.e., HER2 positive and HER2 negative), menopausal statuses (i.e., premenopause and postmenopause) for all three predictive tasks, and according to SLN metastatic burdens (i.e., SLN low-burden and SLN heavy-burden) in task 3. The clinicopathologic characteristics of the patients in the subgroup according to molecular subtype are shown in Supplementary Data 7.

Clinical impact assessment of multitask predictive models

Decision curve analysis was used to evaluate the net benefits of multitask predictive models for each task under different threshold probabilities. To evaluate the clinical impact of BCALN-Net in assisting de-escalation decision-making and management of axillary surgery, the numbers and proportion of patients who may spare unnecessary surgical procedures were calculated in different clinical scenarios in the combined pooled internal and pooled external test cohorts. The clinical scenario involved: (1) patients who would spare SLNB if predicted by BCALN-Net as SLN negative, (2) patients who would only undergo SLNB but omit ALND if predicted by BCALN-Net as SLN low-burden and NSLN negative. The actual pathological results were used as the reference standard to analyze the performance of BCALN-Net in these two clinical scenarios.

In addition, the number and percentage of patients who would be omitted from SLNB according to the SOUND trial criteria (i.e., breast cancer up to 2 cm and a negative preoperative axillary ultrasonography result)12, and the number and percentage of patients identified by BCALN-Net in simulated clinical scenario 1 who met the SOUND trial criteria were calculated in the combined internal and external test cohorts. Similarly, the number and percentage of patients who would be omitted from ALND according to the SENOMAC trial criteria (i.e., clinically node-negative, primary T1 to T3 breast cancer, and one or two sentinel-node macrometastases)9, and the number and percentage of patients identified by BCALN-Net in simulated clinical scenario 2 who met the SENOMAC trial criteria were also calculated in the combined internal and external test cohorts. Decision curve analysis was used to evaluate the net benefits of BCALN-Net compared with the SOUND trial criteria in clinical scenario 1 and the SENOMAC trial criteria in clinical scenario 2.

Benchmarking against mammography- and ultrasonography-based models

To perform a benchmarking against other imaging modalities, including mammography and ultrasonography, an additional cohort (n = 256) was collected from Center 1 between August 2024 and October 2025. These patients had pretreatment mammography, breast and axillary ultrasonography, and breast MRI. MG and US models were constructed respectively by adapting the BCALN-Net architecture, replacing its 3D components (3D-DenseNet) with 2D-DenseNet. The MG and US models were trained using the additionally collected cohort, and this data was not used for training the original BCALN-Net. The predictive performances of the MG model, US model, and BCALN-Net were evaluated using this same cohort from Center 1, enabling a direct comparison of their predictive efficacy. For mammography and ultrasonography, the rectangular regions of interest (ROIs) of the breast tumor and ipsilateral axillary region were annotated separately by a radiologist (radiologist 1: Y.Q.) on the ITK-SNAP platform (version 3.6.0, http://www.itksnap.org). For MRI, the VOIs of the breast tumor and the ipsilateral axillary region of patients were segmented by BCALNSeg-Net. Using the ROIs of the breast tumor and axillary region, the MG model and US model were constructed by employing a 2D-DenseNet instead of a 3D-DenseNet. A 3-fold cross-validation was performed within the cohort to validate the predictive performance of models. The process of model development and predictive performance of BCALN-Net, MG model, and US model are shown in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 6. The benchmarking was performed among BCALN-Net, MG model, and US model for the three tasks.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.4.1, https://www.r-project.org). P values less than 0.05 were defined as statistically significant in two-tailed analyses. Continuous variables were reported as medians and IQRs, or as means ± standard deviations when appropriate, and categorical variables were recorded as numbers and percentages. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify potential clinicopathologic risk factors for each predictive task. For the three predictive tasks, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, and NPV were calculated according to the confusion matrix, and the 95% CI for each value was calculated. The performance of predictive models was compared using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The AUCs of these models were compared using the DeLong test. Comparisons of sensitivities, specificities, accuracies, PPVs, and NPVs between different models were performed using McNemar’s test. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample size was determined by data availability from the participating institutions during the study period. Data samples were excluded based on pre-defined exclusion criteria. The datasets from Center 1 and Center 2 were assigned to the development set and randomly divided into training and internal test sets. The datasets from Center 3 and Center 7 were assigned to three independent external test sets. During the data processing, all data were first de-identified to remove any patient-related information. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
