Low-hygroscopic solvents enable ambient blade coating of efficient perovskite solar cells
Authors: Zhijian Li, Wenjie Zhao, Junyao Gao, Chunmin Liu, Tao Chen, Jingchen Yang, Hao Sun, Zhiheng Liao, Zongming Huang, Guan-Yin Gao, Yuan Li, Junfa Zhu, Weiren Cheng, Shangfeng Yang, Yu Tong, Zhengguo Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Scalable manufacturing of high-efficiency perovskite solar cells (PSCs) requires blade coating not only of the perovskite layer, but also of other functional layers, including organoammonium halide surface passivation layers and self-assembled monolayers (SAMs), under ambient conditions. However, organoammonium iodides and SAMs are highly hygroscopic and prone to hydrolysis and desorption, respectively, and they are commonly processed from hygroscopic alcohol. As a result, despite its importance for scalable production, ambient blade coating of these moisture-sensitive interfacial layers has rarely been demonstrated. Here, we report a general low-hygroscopic solvent system for blade coating hygroscopic organoammonium halides and SAMs. We mix alcohol with low-polarity alkane, where the alcohol dissolves the functional materials and the alkane suppresses moisture uptake during the blade-coating process under ambient conditions. Comprehensive chemical and optoelectronic characterizations confirm that low-hygroscopic solvent system is a general approach to blade coat both passivation layers and SAMs under high humidity. The devices with air blade-coated SAMs, perovskite, and passivation layers achieve a certified efficiency of 26.1% with negligible decrease even fabricated at 80% relative humidity. This work solves one of the most challenging issues of scalable fabrication of perovskite photovoltaics in ambient air.

Introduction

In state-of-the-art perovskite solar cells (PSCs), the self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) layer, perovskite layer, and the surface passivation layer are typically fabricated using solution process1–9. Ambient processing of those layers is therefore highly needed for low-cost commercial manufacturing10–13. Numerous efforts have been spent on improving the blade coating of perovskite films in ambient air, such as solvent engineering14, additive engineering15, and interfacial modification16. Surface passivation layers and SAMs are also essential for achieving efficient blade or slot-die coated PSCs and modules17–21. But those materials are generally more sensitive to humidity than perovskites22,23. Therefore, those layers were generally fabricated in N2 glove box1,3,5,24, and ambient fabrication has not yet been reported. This issue limits the efficiency of blade or slot-die PSCs and modules fabricated in ambient air.

A major challenge is the strong humidity sensitivity of surface passivation materials and SAMs during the blade-coating process16,25. Organoammonium halides, such as phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI)26–29, ethylenediamine diiodide (EDAI)18,30–32, 3-(methylthio)propylamine hydroiodide (3MTPAI)5,33, are the most popular passivation materials. These materials are very prone to hydrolysis due to the ammonium ligands34. As for the SAMs, carbazole-phosphonic acid-based derivatives are the most widely used in PSCs7,24,35,36. It is reported that water molecules can coordinate with the phosphonic acid anchoring groups, weakening their bonding to the fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) surface and ultimately causing desorption of the SAMs37. Nevertheless, highly hygroscopic alcohol is typically used to dissolve organoammonium halides materials and SAMs26,28,38. The moisture it absorbs can readily induce hydrolysis of ammonium passivation layers and desorption of SAMs under ambient conditions25,37. Developing humidity-tolerant blade-coating strategies in ambient conditions is therefore crucial for the commercialization of PSCs.

In this work, we report a low-hygroscopic solvent system for blade-coating both passivation layers and SAMs in ambient air and even under high humidity up to 80%. We mix alcohol with a low-polarity alkane to suppress moisture absorption during blade coating in ambient air, thereby effectively preventing hydrolysis of ammonium passivation layers and desorption of SAMs. The devices with blade-coated SAMs, perovskite layer, and surface passivation layer achieve a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 26.1% with a minor decrease even at 80% relative humidity (RH), while the efficiency decreased to 20.7% when using hygroscopic alcohol as processing solvent. These results demonstrate the significant advantages of a low-hygroscopic solvent system in the scalable fabrication of PSCs in ambient air.

Results

Low hygroscopic solvent systems for passivation layer

We first investigate the blade-coating of the passivation layer, which plays a critical role in improving the performances of blade-coated devices. We used popular PEAI as passivation material in this work because it effectively reduces surface defect states including undercoordinated Pb2+ and halide vacancies (Supplementary Fig. 1). Figure 1a schematically illustrates the blade-coating process for fabricating perovskite solar cells, including sequential blade coating of the SAM layer, perovskite layer, and passivation layer. The quality of the blade-coated passivation layer is affected by the blade-coating speed, blade gap, and passivation solution concentration (Supplementary Fig. 2). The device performance decreased sharply with increasing humidity when the PEAI was blade-coated in ambient air using isopropanol (IPA) as the processing solvent, with the PCE dropping dramatically from 26.1% at 0% RH to 20.7% at 80% RH (Fig. 1b, d, Table S1), and J-V hysteresis becoming more pronounced (Supplementary Fig. 3). We therefore mix low-polarity n-octane with IPA to suppress moisture absorption. Increasing the n-octane proportion results in larger contact angles and slower evaporation of the mixed solvent (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5). Because of the lower polarity nature of the alkane solvent, the mixed solvent won’t damage the perovskite surface. It should be noted that a minimum IPA content of 15 vol% was required for complete dissolution of PEAI (Supplementary Fig. 6). We therefore fixed the solvent ratio at 15 vol% IPA and 85 vol% n-octane in subsequent experiments (Supplementary Fig. 7). The blade-coating parameters for the passivation layer were also optimized for this mixed solvent system (Supplementary Fig. 8). Under optimized conditions in a nitrogen glovebox (0% RH), blade-coated perovskite solar cells achieved a champion PCE of 26.3% (certified PCE of 26.1%) with negligible hysteresis, comparable to devices with spin-coated PEAI as the surface passivation layer (Supplementary Figs. 9, 10, 11). The target devices show minor degradation and negligible hysteresis across the humidity from 0% to 80% (Fig. 1c, e, Supplementary Figs. 12, 13, Table S1). The integrated current densities calculated from the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra are in agreement with the JSC values from the J-V curves (Supplementary Fig. 14, Table S2).

It is worth to note that the low-hygroscopic solvent system is a general approach which also works for other organoammonium iodide passivation materials (Fig. 1f–h). It also extends to alkanes with different chain lengths, enabling solvent-property tuning, such as boiling point, viscosity, and vapor pressure, to match specific processing requirements (Supplementary Fig. 15). Therefore, the low-hygroscopic solvent system can be tuned to fit for different deposition methods with different requirements on solvent properties. Among these solvents, n-hexane or n-nonane result in slightly lower performance, which should be due to their excessively fast or slow evaporation during blade-coating (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17). ‌‌

Mechanism of different solvent system in processing passivation layer

Figure 2a schematically illustrates the process of moisture-induced degradation during perovskite passivation with different solvent systems. Compared with hygroscopic IPA, hydrophobic n-octane effectively suppresses moisture absorption and reduces the formation of surface defects. Experimental characterizations further demonstrate the above mechanism. X-ray diffraction (XRD) peak intensity of PEAI powders decrease significantly after only 2 min exposure to 80% RH, indicating rapid hydrolysis of the crystalline structure and confirming the instability of the passivation layer to moisture (Fig. 2c). Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (FTIR) spectra of IPA solutions exposed to varying humidity for 2 mins show a pronounced H-O-H bending vibration at 1650 cm−1, with its intensity increasing significantly as humidity increases, indicating substantial moisture absorption (Fig. 2d). In contrast, the IPA/n-octane mixed solvent shows no detectable H-O-H bending, indicating the negligible moisture absorption of the mixed solvent in ambient air (Fig. 2e). FTIR spectra confirmed that all the alkane solvents do not absorb moisture compared with IPA (Supplementary Fig. 18). It should be noted that the weak O-H stretching feature at 3330 cm−1, attributed to IPA, is diminished due to the rapid evaporation of IPA in the mixed solvent during ambient exposure and measurement. These results demonstrate that n-octane effectively suppresses the absorption of moisture from humid air.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show no obvious morphological differences between the control and target films because the passivation layer is very thin (Supplementary Figs. 19, 20). However, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) shows that PEAI-perovskite interactions are nearly disappear in the control films but still preserved in the target films under high humidity (Supplementary Fig. 21). Grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) further reveals that the corresponding PEAI-induced 2D diffraction features are significantly weakened in the control films but remain clear in the target films (Supplementary Fig. 22), indicating that the introduction of n-octane has little effect on the crystallinity of the passivation layer while improving its humidity tolerance. Consistently, both the photoluminescence (PL) intensity and carrier lifetime decreases significantly as the relative humidity increases due to moisture-induced defects (Fig. 3a, b). In contrast, target films maintain much stronger PL emission and show only a minor reduction in lifetime. The result was also confirmed by the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements (Supplementary Figs. 23, 24). We also employed Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) to examine the surface potential of perovskite films with different post-treatments under various humidity conditions (Fig. 3d, e). For control films, the potential shifts from −194 mV at 0% RH to −331 mV at 80% RH, accompanied by a broadened distribution. This gradual shift toward a more negative contact potential difference (CPD) indicates an increase in the surface work function of the control films, in agreement with the humidity-induced surface energy level shifts observed by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) (Supplementary Fig. 25), which impedes surface electron transport and charge extraction41,42. In contrast, target films exhibit only a minor shift while maintaining a narrow distribution, demonstrating suppressed moisture-perovskite interactions and uniform surface passivation, as also supported by UPS results (Supplementary Fig. 26).

Device characterizations

We carried out a series of transient and steady-state measurements to examine the influence of different solvent systems on photovoltaic performance. Transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements show that the carrier lifetimes of the control devices decrease significantly with increasing humidity (Fig. 4a). Meanwhile, transient photocurrent (TPC) response time become slower under high humidity, indicating hindered carrier extraction (Fig. 4c). In contrast, target devices show only minor variations in both TPV and TPC with increasing humidity, demonstrating their robustness against moisture (Fig. 4b, d). The recombination resistance (Rrec) also decreases significantly under high humidity, evidenced from the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results (Fig. 4e, f) and reverse leakage current density (Supplementary Fig. 27). By comparison, target devices retain substantially higher Rrec with only minor variations, demonstrating their stabilized interfacial behavior. The beneficial effect of the solvent strategy is also evident in light-emitting diode (LED) mode (Fig. 4g, h). The EQE of the control devices decreases significantly from 11.5% at 0% RH to 5.7% at 80% RH, whereas target devices retain robust electroluminescence. These measurements provide consistent evidence that perovskite films processed with the low-hygroscopic solvent exhibit more stable charge transport, suppressed non-radiative recombination, and superior optoelectronic performance.

In addition to improved photovoltaic performance, our devices processed with the low-hygroscopic solvent system also exhibited substantially enhanced operational stability. When tracked at the maximum power point (MPP) under continuous illumination at 25 °C, devices fabricated with both solvent systems in the glovebox (0% RH) retained about 95% of their initial efficiency after 1000 hours (Supplementary Fig. 29). In contrast, devices passivated under 80% relative humidity showed pronounced differences: the control device retained only 55% of its initial efficiency after 1000 hours, whereas the target device retained 89%. The severe PCE degradation in the control device should be mainly attributed to FF decay caused by ion migration, which is accelerated by its higher defect density43,44. We further conducted stricter stability measurements under continuous illumination at 65 °C, where target devices still showed markedly improved stability compared with the control device (Supplementary Fig. 30). These results further demonstrate that the optimized low-hygroscopic solvent systems improve the humidity tolerance of fabricating PSCs in ambient air.

Low hygroscopic solvent systems for SAMs

Our low-hygroscopic solvent system is also applicable to the deposition of hygroscopic SAMs. As mentioned above, water molecules can coordinate with the phosphonic acid anchoring groups, causing desorption of the SAMs37. We tested four popular SAMs (Fig. 5b), and a minimum ethanol content of 10 vol% was required for complete dissolution of Me-4PACz (Supplementary Fig. 31). The most commonly used Me-4PACz was blade-coated across 0-80% RH using either ethanol or the low-hygroscopic solvent, and the results show that the low-hygroscopic solvent system can enhance the humidity tolerance of the SAM layers and significantly improve device performance under 80% RH (Fig. 5c, d, Supplementary Figs. 32, 33). Both PL and TRPL results proved that the target devices have less traps processed under different relative humidity (Supplementary Fig. 34). Notably, the low-hygroscopic solvent system is also applicable to other SAMs (Fig. 5e, f).

To further demonstrate the scalability of our low-hygroscopic solvent system, a blade-coated perovskite minimodule with an active area of 12 cm2 was fabricated under ambient air (Supplementary Fig. 35). The SAM layer, perovskite layer, and passivation layer were all deposited by blade coating. The device achieved an aperture-area efficiency of 21.4%, with a VOC of 7.04 V (six subcells connected in series), a JSC of 3.92 mA cm−2, and a fill factor of 0.776. The minimodule achieved a certified PCE of 21.1% at the Chinese National PV Industry Measurement and Testing Center (Supplementary Fig. 36).

Discussion

In this work, we report a low-hygroscopic solvent system to deposit both passivation layers and SAMs in ambient air. The low-hygroscopic solvent system is composed of an alkane and an alcohol, where the alcohol dissolves the moisture-sensitive materials while the alkane suppresses moisture absorption during blade-coating. We demonstrated that using low-hygroscopic solvents is a general approach for ambient fabrication of PSCs. First, our low-hygroscopic solvent can be adopted to deposit a broad range of organoammonium iodide passivation materials in ambient air. Second, it is applicable to a wide range of SAM layers, enabling these SAMs to form compact monolayers that are strongly anchored to the FTO substrate under an ambient-air process. Third, the low-hygroscopic solvent system comprises various combinations of alcohols and hydrophobic alkanes with different chain lengths, providing tunable boiling points and viscosities to control film formation across diverse solution-based deposition techniques, including spin coating, blade coating, and inkjet printing.

Methods

Materials

Lead iodide (PbI2, 99.99%), lead bromide (PbBr2, 99%), [2-(3,6-Dimethoxy-9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl] phosphonic acid (MeO-2PACz), [2-(3,6-Diphenyl-9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl] phosphonic acid (Ph-2PACz), and [4-(3,6-Dimethyl-9H-carbazol-9-yl)butyl] phosphonic acid (Me-4PACz, 99%) were purchased from TCI Chemicals. Formamidinium iodide (FAI, 99.99%), methylammonium bromide (MABr, 99.99%), and fullerene (C60, 99.0%) were purchased from Liaoning Youxuan New Energy Technology Co., Ltd. Methylammonium chloride (MACl, 99.9%), phenethylammonium iodide (PEAI, 99.5%), 3-(methylthio)propylamine hydroiodide (3MTPAI, 98%), 1,3-Diammoniumpropane iodide (PDAI2, 99.5%), 3-Fluoro-phenethylammonium iodide (m-F-PEAI, 99%), 4-Trifluoromethyl-benzylammonium iodide (CF3PMAI, 99%), [4-(3,6-Diphenyl-9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl]phosphonic acid (Ph-4PACz), and bathocuproine (BCP, 99%) were purchased from Xi’an Yuri Solar Co., Ltd. Cesium iodide (CsI, 99.999%), rubidium iodide (RbI, 99.9%), n-hexane (99%, ultra-dry), n-heptane (99%, ultra-dry), n-octane (99%, ultra-dry) and n-nonane (99%, ultra-dry) were purchased from J&K Scientific. Lithium fluoride (LiF, 98.5%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%, anhydrous), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.9%, anhydrous), isopropyl alcohol (IPA, 99.5%, anhydrous), and ethanol (99.5%, anhydrous) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Perovskite precursor solution

The perovskite composition is FA0.925MA0.005Cs0.05Rb0.02Pb(I0.995Br0.005)3. The perovskite precursor was prepared in mixed solvents of DMF:DMSO (volume ratio 4:1). The chemical molar ratio of CsI, RbI, FAI, MABr, PbI2 and PbBr2 is 0.05, 0.02, 0.925, 0.005, 0.995 and 0.005, respectively, and the concentration is 1.5 M. Additional 5 mol% PbI2 and 20 mol% MACl were added to the precursor. The precursor solution was stirred at room temperature for 12 h and then filtered through 0.22 μm polytetrafluoroethylene filters before use.

Device fabrication

Pre-patterned FTO substrates were sequentially sonicated with deionized water, acetone, and ethanol for 20 min. NiOx layers were deposited by magnetron sputtering, followed by annealing at 400 °C for 2 h and storage in a 70 °C drying oven. The substrates were treated with ultraviolet-ozone for 15 min before use. The hole transport layer was prepared by blade-coating a Me-4PACz (0.4 mg ml−1 in ethanol) on the NiOₓ substrates, and then annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. The speed of the blade was set as 50 mm s−1. The distance between the blade and substrate is approximately 70 μm. The perovskite layer was blade-coated with a gap of 100 μm and a speed of 3 mm s−1. Immediately after coating, the wet film was transferred into a vacuum chamber connected to a pump. Upon opening the valve to the pump system, the chamber pressure dropped below 10 Pa within 10 s and was maintained under low pressure for 1 min. The chamber was then vented to atmospheric pressure. The film was subsequently annealed at 100 °C for 30 mins. For the passivation layer, PEAI was dissolved at 1 mg mL−1 in IPA or an IPA/n-octane mixed solvent (15:85 v/v). The solutions were blade-coated onto the perovskite films in ambient air with controlled humidity, using a gap of 60 μm and a speed of 20 mm s−1, and then annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. Finally, 25 nm C60, 6 nm BCP and 80 nm Cu were thermally evaporated on the films under a high vacuum of 6 × 10−4 Pa, respectively.

Perovskite minimodule (12 cm2) fabrication

Perovskite minimodules were fabricated on 5 × 5 cm2 FTO substrates just like small-sized devices, except for increasing the volume of solutions used for blade-coating. In the minimodule fabrication, a three-step laser scribing process (P1, P2, and P3) was used to realize the series connection of subcells. P1 was first defined on the FTO substrate, followed by P2 adjacent to the P1 lines before Cu electrode evaporation, and P3 after deposition of the Cu top electrode.

DFT calculations

DFT calculations for the PEAI-perovskite surface interaction were performed using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) with the projector augmented-wave method and a plane-wave basis set. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional with DFT-D3 dispersion correction was used. The FAPbI3 (001) slab used lattice parameters of a = b = 19.08 Å and c = 32.00 Å. A 1 × 1 × 1 Gamma k-point mesh and a plane-wave cutoff energy of 450 eV were used. Structures were relaxed until the maximum force was below 0.02 eV Å−1 with an energy convergence criterion of 1 × 10−5 eV.

Binding energies between solvent molecules were calculated using the Gaussian 16 software package. The B3LYP functional combined with the D3BJ dispersion correction was employed for all calculations. Geometry optimizations and vibrational frequency analyses were carried out using the 6-311 G(d,p) basis set for all atoms. Single-point energy calculations were subsequently conducted on the optimized geometries at the M062x-D3/6-311 + G(d,p) level of theory to obtain more accurate electronic energies.

Characterizations of the solvents

FTIR measurements were conducted using a Nicolet 8700 spectrometer with a spectral range of 4000–600 cm−1. Before the measurements, solvent solutions were exposed to air with different relative humidity levels for 2 min. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a TG 209F1 (NETZSCH) at 50 °C under nitrogen to compare the evaporation rates of different solvents. Contact-angle measurements were carried out using a UNI-CAM (GITSOFTTECH) system with SmartDrop software.

Device characterizations

J-V characteristics of the solar cells were measured in an N2-filled glovebox using an AM 1.5 G solar simulator (SS-X50, Enlitech), and the data were recorded with a Keithley 2601B Source Meter. Spectra intensity was calibrated utilizing a certified WPVS standard solar reference cell (SRC-2020, Enlitech) at an irradiance of 100 mW cm−2. The J-V curves were measured under a forward scan from -0.1 to 1.3 V and a reverse scan from 1.3 to −0.1 V, using a scan rate of 20 mV s−1 in a nitrogen-filled glovebox. The active device area was 0.0676 cm2, which was defined by a metal mask. The IPCE was measured in a.c. mode on the xenon lamp-based system (Newport TLS260-300X). The scan range was from 300 to 900 nm. The TPV and TPC measurements under 520 nm excitation were performed using a waveform generator (Rigol DG822), a pre-amplifier (Stanford Research Systems SR570), and a digital oscilloscope (PicoScope 4262). EIS measurements were performed using an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E) under a bias potential of 1 V. The solar cells were measured in LED mode in a N2-filled glovebox using a home-made motorized goniometer setup consisting of a Keithley 2400 sourcemeter unit, a calibrated Si photodiode (FDS-100-CAL, Thorlabs), a picoammeter (4140B, Agilent), and a calibrated fiber-optic spectrophotometer (UVN-SR, StellarNet Inc.).

Stability characterization

The operational stability of unencapsulated devices was tested at the MPP in a nitrogen-filled glovebox at room temperature. The light source was a 1 sun equivalent white LED. The devices tested in the MPP measurement had an architecture of FTO/NiOx/Me-4PACz/Perovskite/C60/BCP/Cu.
