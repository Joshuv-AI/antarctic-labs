A Spectral Framework for Multi-Scale Nonlinear Dimensionality Reduction
\authororcid Zeyang Huang0000-0003-3945-1274, \authororcid Angelos Chatzimparmpas0000-0002-9079-2376, \authororcid Thomas Höllt0000-0001-8125-1650, and \authororcid Takanori Fujiwara0000-0002-6382-2752

Abstract
Dimensionality reduction (DR) is characterized by two longstanding trade-offs. First, there is a global–local preservation tension: methods such as t-SNE and UMAP prioritize local neighborhood preservation, yet may distort global manifold structure, while methods such as Laplacian Eigenmaps preserve global geometry but often yield limited local separation. Second, there is a gap between expressiveness and analytical transparency: many nonlinear DR methods produce embeddings without an explicit connection to the underlying high-dimensional structure, limiting insight into the embedding process. In this paper, we introduce a spectral framework for nonlinear DR that addresses these challenges. Our approach embeds high-dimensional data using a spectral basis combined with cross-entropy optimization, enabling multi-scale representations that bridge global and local structure. Leveraging linear spectral decomposition, the framework further supports analysis of embeddings through a graph-frequency perspective, enabling examination of how spectral modes influence the resulting embedding. We complement this analysis with glyph-based scatterplot augmentations for visual exploration. Quantitative evaluations and case studies demonstrate that our framework improves manifold continuity while enabling deeper analysis of embedding structure through spectral mode contributions.

Introduction

keywords
Visualization, dimensionality reduction, manifold learning, embedding algorithms, spectral graph, UMAP

Dimensionality reduction (DR) methods are widely used to visualize high-dimensional data, such as gene expressions
[
2
]
, images
[
8
]
, or feature descriptors
[
14
]
.
Among existing approaches, neighborhood embeddings such as t-SNE
[
68
]
and UMAP
[
47
]
are particularly popular.
These methods construct a weighted neighborhood graph in the high-dimensional space and optimize a low-dimensional embedding to preserve local similarities.
These approaches contrast with classical spectral methods such as Laplacian Eigenmaps
[
3
]
, reflecting a global–local trade-off: methods like UMAP prioritize local separation, whereas Laplacian Eigenmaps better preserves global structure.
Recent work has sought to understand and mitigate this trade-off by analyzing the embedding process
[
12
,
6
,
7
,
14
,
15
]
.
In particular, Böhm et al.
[
7
]
characterize neighbor embedding methods along an attraction–repulsion continuum.
Stronger attraction during optimization leads to more continuous manifold structure, while stronger repulsion produces sharper cluster separation.

Böhm et al. characterize the global–local trade-off at the level of the optimization mechanism
[
7
,
14
]
, rather than establishing an explicit connection between the graph representation of data—which encodes both local and global structure—and its low-dimensional embedding.
When an embedding shows separated clusters, it remains unclear whether this separation reflects true data structure or arises from the optimizer distorting the underlying manifold to favor local compactness.
The key unresolved challenge is therefore not only the existence of a global–local trade-off in commonly used DR methods
[
37
,
1
,
32
]
, but also how to make this trade-off explicit and controllable.

Furthermore, most nonlinear DR methods, including parametric variants
[
54
,
26
]
, ultimately produce only a fixed set of point coordinates without an explicit account of how it is constructed.
Many visual analytics systems attempt to provide explanations by post-hoc parameter tuning or by visualizing certain data properties to compare embeddings
[
35
]
.
These analyses can show that the resulting embedding changes under parameter variations, however, they do not explain which structures in the graph representation of the data drive these changes or at what scale they influence the embedding.
In contrast, Laplacian Eigenmaps
[
22
,
3
]
provide a direct connection between graph structure and embeddings, but are often too rigid to be expressive enough to depict real-world high-dimensional data.

In this paper, we investigate the global–local trade-off from a different perspective by focusing on the graph representation underlying the embedding rather than on the optimization process alone.
Inspired by graph signal processing methods
[
57
,
17
]
, we introduce a spectral framework that constrains the cross-entropy optimization used in UMAP
[
47
]
by using spectral modes (graph frequencies) induced by the graph Laplacian
[
57
]
(see
Section

1
).
Specifically, the embedding is expressed as a linear combination of spectral modes, and the optimization operates on the coefficients of these modes rather than directly on embedding coordinates.
As a result, the objective remains neighborhood-preserving, while the degrees of freedom are explicitly controlled through a selected spectral subspace.

This spectral formulation addresses the challenge of controlling the global–local trade-off by replacing a single embedding produced by an implicit force balance with an explicit multi-scale optimization process.
We optimize the embedding within progressively expanding spectral subspaces, producing a sequence of coarse-to-fine intermediate embeddings.
From a spectral perspective, low-frequency modes capture coarse global organization and manifold continuity, while higher-frequency modes introduce increasingly fine local variations and detail.
With a few low-frequency modes, the embedding resembles Laplacian Eigenmaps, whereas when all spectral modes are used, it gains greater optimization flexibility and approaches a UMAP-like solution.

The lack of analytical transparency can be addressed by explicitly expressing the embedding as a linear combination of spectral modes with learned coefficients.
This formulation enables analysis of which modes dominate the embedding, how efficiently the data can be approximated within a reduced spectral subspace, and how individual data points participate in these modes.
This explicit mapping spectral modes and the embedding further enables new forms of visual analysis.

In summary, our spectral formulation enables the following analytical capabilities:

•

combining neighborhood-preserving cross-entropy optimization with spectral modes, making the optimization space explicit;

•

transforming the global–local trade-off into a progressive multi-scale embedding sequence;

•

enabling direct global and local analysis of the embedding through spectral decompositions; and

•

improving global structure preservation while quantifying how compactly the embedding can be represented within a reduced spectral subspace.

1
Background and Related Work

Our framework is inspired by the complementary strengths used for neighbor embeddings: spectral methods such as Laplacian Eigenmaps and force-based approaches such as UMAP.
In this section, we position our work in relation to these paradigms, as well as prior research on the analysis of DR results and spectral graph methods.

1.1
Neighbor Embeddings

DR aims to extract low-dimensional embeddings (also referred to as projections or representations) of high-dimensional data while preserving meaningful structure
[
69
,
13
]
.
Among various methods, neighbor embedding methods such as UMAP
[
47
]
, t-SNE
[
68
]
, and Laplacian Eigenmaps
[
3
]
focus on preserving relationships between nearby data points.
These methods typically construct a
k
-nearest neighbor (
k
NN) graph and define similarities between instances, followed by an embedding process that balances attractive forces between neighbors and repulsive forces among all points.
In recent work, Böhm et al.
[
7
]
analyze these methods within an attraction–repulsion framework, showing that commonly used neighbor embedding methods such as LE
[
3
]
and UMAP
[
47
]
can be placed along a continuum of force balance.
They further highlight how different optimization strategies implicitly prioritize global versus local structure.
While this unified view explains how different embeddings arise from varying force balances, control over these trade-offs remains implicit in the optimization process.
In contrast, our framework introduces a spectral basis that enables explicit control over the contribution of global and local structures prior to optimization.
This formulation not only supports flexible embedding construction but also enables the analysis of the resulting embeddings through a spectral-mode (graph-frequency) perspective, providing insight into how different structural components influence the embedding.

Building on these connections, next, we describe Laplacian Eigenmaps and UMAP as representative spectral and force-based approaches, respectively.

1.2
Laplacian Eigenmaps

Let
\mathbf{X}=[{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\cdots{\mathbf{x}}_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
denote the input data, where
N
and
M
are the numbers of data points and dimensions, respectively.
Laplacian Eigenmaps
[
3
]
approximate the manifold structure of
\mathbf{X}
by constructing a neighbor graph.
We focus on a
k
NN graph, which is commonly used in modern neighbor embedding methods, although the original formulation also considers an
\epsilon
-ball graph.
The
k
NN graph is then converted into a weighted adjacency matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, for example, using a heat kernel:
\smash{w_{{i},{j}}=\exp(-\left\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\right\rVert^{2}/t)}
with parameter
t
.

A symmetric normalized Laplacian matrix is then computed as

\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}

(1)

where
\mathbf{D}
is the diagonal degree matrix with entries

d_{i}=\sum_{j}w_{{i},{j}}
and
\mathbf{I}
is an identity matrix of size
N\times N
.

Finally, the low-dimensional embedding is obtained by eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{L}
by

\mathbf{L}\mathbf{u}_{{n}}=\mathbf{\lambda}_{{n}}\mathbf{u}_{{n}},

(2)

where eigenpairs
\{\mathbf{\lambda}_{{n}},\mathbf{u}_{{n}}\}
are ordered such that

0=\mathbf{\lambda}_{0}\leq\mathbf{\lambda}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mathbf{\lambda}_{N-1}
, and eigenvectors are orthonormal.
The first eigenvector
\mathbf{u}_{0}
is constant, and often referred as a trivial eigenvector.
The other non-trivial eigenvectors
\{\mathbf{u}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{u}_{N-1}\}
are called
spectral modes
.
These spectral modes can form a graph Fourier basis over the data points
[
57
]
(refer to
Section

1.5
for details).
Spectral modes associated with small eigenvalues correspond to low-frequency components, while those with larger eigenvalues correspond to high-frequency components.
Low-frequency components vary smoothly over strongly connected nodes and capture coarse global structure, whereas high-frequency components encode finer local variation or noise
[
57
]
.
A low-dimensional embedding is formed by selecting the spectral modes corresponding to the
S
smallest nonzero eigenvalues.
For visualization, typically
S=2
is chosen to match 2D visualization spaces.
Following the ordering of spectral modes, such an embedding typically captures mainly the low-frequency properties of the graph and as such global structure.

Our framework leverages the spectral properties to relate embedding structure to graph frequencies, enabling analysis of how different components of the data influence the resulting embedding.

1.3
UMAP

Similar to Laplacian Eigenmaps, UMAP constructs a weighted
k
NN graph
\mathbf{W}
to model local relationships between data points.
In UMAP, the edge weights are defined as:

w_{{i},{j}}=\exp(\penalty\ -\max(0,\penalty\ \mathrm{dist}({\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{j})-\rho_{i})\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ /\penalty\ \penalty\ \sigma_{i}\penalty\ )

(3)

where
\rho_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
are local connectivity parameters chosen to distribute data points uniformly over the manifold and
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot)
is a distance function (the Euclidean distance by default).

UMAP then computes an embedding by minimizing a cross-entropy objective.
Let
\smash{\mathbf{Y}=[{\mathbf{y}}_{1}\cdots{\mathbf{y}}_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M^{\prime}}}
denote the embedding coordinates, where typically
M^{\prime}=2
.
The embedding is initialized using a heuristic strategy, commonly based on Laplacian Eigenmaps.

In the embedding space, pairwise similarities are modeled as

q_{i,j}=(1+a\left\lVert{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-{\mathbf{y}}_{j}\right\rVert^{2b})^{-1}

(4)

where
a
and
b
control the shape of the similarity function and are typically derived from the
min_dist
hyperparameter.
The embedding is then optimized to minimize the cross-entropy between the high-dimensional similarities
w_{{i},{j}}
and the low-dimensional similarities
q_{i,j}
.
In practice, UMAP symmetrizes the fuzzy graph and applies stochastic optimization with negative sampling to reduce calculation time.

Cross-entropy optimization is also used in other DR methods such as LargeVis
[
61
]
, due to its flexibility and computational efficiency.
Our framework adopts this optimization strategy but applies it to a spectral subspace.
Specifically, we optimize an
M^{\prime}
-dimensional embedding of a higher-dimensional spectral subspace (e.g.,
S=20
), enabling a balance between expressiveness and explainability of the resulting embedding.

1.4
Analysis and Understanding of DR Results

Low-dimensional embeddings serve not only as visual summaries, but also as tools for reasoning about clusters, trajectories, and relationships among groups.
This broader role makes their interpretation a central challenge in DR
[
16
]
.
Prior work has addressed this challenge from three complementary perspectives: evaluation of embedding quality, post hoc interpretation with visual analytics, and theoretical or methodological analysis of nonlinear DR mechanisms.

Embedding evaluation commonly relies on measures of neighborhood preservation
[
31
]
, distortion
[
53
]
, stability
[
36
]
, class separation
[
34
]
, and related quality criteria
[
44
]
.
These measures assess how well an embedding preserves structural properties of the original data and whether it is suitable for downstream analytical tasks.
A recent survey further situates these measures within the broader challenge of reliable visual analytics with DR
[
35
]
.

Visual analytics approaches support interactive inspection of uncertainty
[
2
]
, exploration of cluster relationships
[
76
]
, and analysis of embedding artifacts
[
29
]
.
Other methods interpret regions and patterns in the embedding in terms of original attributes and local feature contributions
[
21
,
11
,
64
]
, and characterize visual structures through logical predicates or sparse explanatory partitions
[
48
]
.
Selected clusters can be analyzed through contrastive feature analysis
[
23
]
or supervised models such as decision trees
[
5
]
and explainable boosting machines
[
55
]
.
Interactive systems further support steering, comparison, and task-driven analysis, enabling analysts to refine embeddings, compare cluster structure, and relate patterns to downstream analytical goals
[
24
,
43
,
46
,
50
]
.
These approaches support assessment and interpretation of embeddings, but remain largely post hoc because they analyze results after construction rather than the underlying embedding process.

Theoretical and methodological work increasingly examines the internal mechanisms of nonlinear DR methods
[
6
,
16
]
.
For example, Cai et al.
[
9
]
show that the early stage of t-SNE is equivalent to power iterations on the graph Laplacian, implicitly performing spectral clustering, while later stages exhibit amplification and stabilization dynamics.
Couplet et al.
[
12
]
propose a natively interpretable variant of t-SNE in which each embedding coordinate is expressed as a linear combination of input features, with regularization enforcing local coherence.
More broadly, recent work shows that representation learning objectives can often be interpreted through spectral embedding formulations
[
1
]
.

In contrast to these approaches, our work integrates analysis directly into the embedding process by parameterizing embeddings in a spectral subspace.
This formulation enables explicit control over the global–local trade-off and supports intrinsic, multi-scale analysis of how spectral modes contribute to the embedding, rather than relying on post hoc interpretation.

1.5
Spectral Graph Methods

Laplacian eigenvectors serve as embedding coordinates in Laplacian Eigenmaps
[
3
,
28
]
.
More generally, spectral graph theory
[
60
]
interprets these eigenvectors as an ordered basis for graph signals.
It has been widely studied in areas such as spectral graph neural networks
[
20
,
73
,
17
]
, spectral diffusion metrics
[
42
]
, graph diagnostics
[
67
,
63
]
, and visualization methods
[
52
,
4
]
.
The ordering of eigenvalues provides a multiscale view of graph structure.
This also induces a natural approximation hierarchy, making spectral representations useful for DR
[
57
]

and in turn provides an explicit multiscale description of the structural components present in the graph prior to embedding.

Spectral graph methods have long been used for DR, either explicitly through Laplacian Eigenmaps
[
3
]
or through diffusion-based methods that exploit the spectrum of graph operators to capture manifold structure and trajectories
[
10
,
49
,
32
]
.
Recent work further shows that spectral initialization can improve optimization stability and global organization in methods such as UMAP and t-SNE
[
37
]
.
Beyond embedding methods, spectral graph representations have also been used in interactive and analytical systems.
SpectralNET
[
22
]
extends Laplacian Eigenmaps into an interactive system that visualizes spectral quantities such as eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and densities.
SpectralMAP
[
75
]
uses spectral decomposition of the data to construct a global filter that guides manifold approximation.
While these approaches use spectral representations for embedding or analysis, they typically produce a single embedding without exposing how individual spectral modes influence the result.
In contrast, our approach treats the spectral basis as a controllable subspace for optimization, enabling explicit multi-scale analysis of how spectral modes shape the embedding.

2
Demonstrating Examples

Our work approaches neighbor embeddings through the lens of spectral graph analysis.
As discussed, neighbor embeddings consist of two key components: (1) constructing a graph representation of high-dimensional data and (2) optimizing an embedding to preserve this graph structure.
We observe that these two components serve distinct roles in data analysis.
Spectral analysis of the graph reveals intrinsic structures across multiple scales, ranging from coarse global organization to fine local variations.
In contrast, cross-entropy optimization arranges these structures in a low-dimensional space while preserving neighborhood relationships.
By combining these roles, our framework enables explicit control over the balance between global and local structure in the embedding process, while supporting analysis of how different structural components influence the resulting embedding.

The combination of spectral decomposition and cross-entropy optimization enables control over coarse-to-fine structure preservation, allowing layouts to maintain global manifold geometry while progressively incorporating finer-scale patterns.

3
Spectral Cross-Entropy Embedding as a Multi-scale View of High-dimensional Data

We introduce a spectral cross-entropy embedding framework that provides a multi-scale view of high-dimensional data.
Our approach separates the roles of structure representation and embedding optimization by combining a spectral basis derived from the graph representation with cross-entropy-based embedding.
Our embedding is operated within a spectral subspace.
This design enables explicit control over the balance between global and local structure and supports analysis of how different spectral modes influence the resulting embedding.

We first present an overview of the framework, followed by the detailed procedure for each component of the framework.

3.1
Overview

Our framework generates embeddings from high-dimensional data by combining spectral decomposition with cross-entropy-based optimization.
The embedding process involves four main steps: (1) weighted
k
NN graph construction, (2) spectral modes extraction, (3) selection of a spectral subspace, and (4) cross-entropy-based optimization of a low-dimensional embedding.
Beyond embedding, the framework also supports analysis through (5) progressive subspace construction and embedding as well as (6) visualization of spectral modes’ influence.

The first two steps follow existing neighbor embedding approaches.
We construct a weighted
k
NN graph
\mathbf{W}
as in UMAP
[
47
]
(i.e., a fuzzy graph), as described in
Section

1.3
.
We then compute spectral modes over
\mathbf{W}
as described in
Section

1.2
.

Our approach mainly differs in the subsequent steps.
Rather than directly optimizing the positions of data points, we operate in an
S
-dimensional spectral subspace and learn a projection mapping onto an
M^{\prime}
-dimensional embedding space (typically
M^{\prime}=2
for visualization).
In this formulation, cross-entropy optimization is used to identify an informative embedding subspace from the spectral subspace, rather than to directly adjust pairwise relationships between data points.
This separation enables structured control over the embedding process and facilitates analysis of how spectral modes influence the embedding.

As demonstrated in
Section

2
, progressive subspace construction and embedding allows spectral modes to be added incrementally, enabling coarse-to-fine refinement of the embedding.
This process reveals how different spectral modes contribute to the resulting visual patterns.
We further provide visualizations to understand the embedding in terms of spectral modes, where low-frequency modes capture global organization and high-frequency modes encode finer local variation.

3.2
Spectral Subspace

The Laplacian spectrum obtained from
Eq.

2
provides an ordering of graph signals from coarse to fine scales
[
57
]
.
Spectral modes associated with small eigenvalues capture smooth variations over the neighborhood graph, while those with larger eigenvalues encode increasingly localized structure.
This property enables a multiscale representation of the data.
By selecting spectral modes for embedding optimization, we explicitly control the spectral bandwidth of the representation, thereby making the global–local structure trade-off explicit and adjustable.

Let
\mathbf{U}{}_{S}=[\mathbf{u}_{1}\,\cdots\,\mathbf{u}_{S}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times S}
denote the subspace spanned by the
S
spectral modes corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues.
When
S
is small, the subspace is restricted to low-frequency modes, and the resulting embedding captures only coarse global structure, such as connected components or manifold-level organization.
As
S
increases, higher-frequency modes are incorporated, allowing finer local variation to appear in the embedding.
In the limit where
S=N-1
, the subspace spans the full spectrum, and the resulting embedding becomes close to those produced by conventional neighbor embedding such as UMAP.

Thus,
S
serves as a direct control for balancing global and local structure, while enabling a structured and analyzable multiscale embedding.

3.3
Embedding Space Optimization

Given a selected spectral subspace
\mathbf{U}{}_{S}
, we learn an embedding by optimizing a projection of this subspace.
Rather than directly optimizing the coordinates of individual data points, our method parameterizes the embedding as:

\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{U}{}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{P}{}_{S},

(5)

where
\mathbf{P}{}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times M^{\prime}}
is a projection matrix mapping the spectral subspace to an
M^{\prime}
-dimensional embedding space.

We adopt the same low-dimensional similarity definition
q_{i,j}
as UMAP (see
Eq.

4
) and optimize the projection matrix by minimizing the cross-entropy objective:

\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathbf{P}{}_{S})=-\sum_{i,j}w_{{i},{j}}\log q_{i,j}-\gamma\sum_{i,j}\log(1-q_{i,j}),

(6)

where
w_{{i},{j}}
denotes the edge weight in the weighted
k
NN graph and
\gamma
controls the strength of repulsion, taken from the UMAP implementation (set to 5 by default).
This formulation is fully differentiable once
\mathbf{U}{}_{S}
is computed
[
15
]
, and we follow UMAP’s negative sampling strategy for efficient optimization.

This reparameterization shifts the optimization from point coordinates to a low-dimensional subspace, enabling the embedding to remain expressive while providing analytical transparency.
In particular, the learned projection matrix
\mathbf{P}{}_{S}
encodes how spectral modes contribute to the embedding.
This property enables a new form of analysis for nonlinear DR, with further support from visualizations (
Section

3.5
).

In summary, our approach reformulates embedding optimization as learning a projection from a spectral subspace, combining the flexibility of optimization-based methods with the structured and analyzable representation provided by spectral analysis.

3.4
Progressive Embedding Strategy

The spectral reparameterization of the cross-entropy objective enables optimization within a progressively expanding spectral subspace:

\mathbf{U}{}_{s_{1}}\subset\mathbf{U}{}_{s_{2}}\subset\cdots\subset\mathbf{U}{}_{s_{T}}=\mathbf{U}{}_{S},\quad s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdots<s_{T}

(7)

where the schedule
\{s_{r}\}_{r=1}^{T}
defines how the dimensionality of the spectral subspace is increased.

At the initial stage (
s_{1}
), we optimize the embedding using
\mathbf{U}{}_{s_{1}}
and obtain a projection matrix
\mathbf{P}{}_{s_{1}}
.
At each subsequent stage (
s_{r}
), we expand the subspace to
\mathbf{U}{}_{s_{r}}
and initialize the projection matrix by augmenting the previous solution:

\mathbf{P}{}_{s_{r}}=[\,\mathbf{P}{}_{s_{r-1}}\,;\,\delta_{s_{r}}\,]

where
\delta_{s_{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(s_{r}-s_{r-1})\times M^{\prime}}
contains small random values.
Optimization then proceeds within the expanded subspace.
This process is repeated until the full subspace
\mathbf{U}{}_{S}
is reached.
While more sophisticated stage-allocation strategies could be incorporated, in practice, given a total number of optimization epochs
n_epochs
, we allocate
\lfloor\texttt{n\_epochs}/T\rfloor
epochs to each stage.

This strategy incorporates spectral modes from coarse to fine scales during optimization.
Early stages, constrained to low-frequency modes, capture global organization, while later stages progressively introduce higher-frequency modes that refine local structure.

As a result, the optimization produces not only a final embedding
\mathbf{Y}_{s_{T}}
but also a sequence of intermediate embeddings
[\mathbf{Y}_{s_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{Y}_{s_{T}}]
.
This progression provides an interpretable view of the embedding process: by observing how the embedding evolves as additional spectral modes are incorporated, we can identify which structures are supported by low-frequency graph geometry and which emerge only with higher-frequency modes.

3.5
Visual Explanations of the Layout

As shown in
Eq.

5
, the embedding
\mathbf{Y}
is expressed as a projection of spectral modes rather than an opaque set of coordinates.
This formulation allows each embedding to be decomposed into a weighted combination of spectral modes, facilitating structured understanding of the embedding.

This factorization provides two complementary perspectives:

Global mode-wise explanation.

Each column of
\mathbf{U}{}_{S}
corresponds to a spectral mode, ordered from low to high frequency.
The corresponding rows of
\mathbf{P}{}_{S}
determine how strongly each mode contributes to the embedding axes.
The magnitude of each row of
\mathbf{P}{}_{S}
reflects the global influence of that spectral mode on the embedding.

Local point-wise explanation.

Each row of
\mathbf{U}{}_{S}
corresponds to a data point and encodes its participation in each spectral mode.
By referring to each row of
\mathbf{P}{}_{S}
together, we can see how spectral modes combine to determine the position of each point in the embedding.

Based on this formulation, we design two complementary visualizations: a global view that summarizes how spectral modes are utilized across the dataset, and a local view that explains how these modes combine to produce individual data point positions.

The global view
consists of one line-chart visualization: the spectral response plot.
The spectral response plot (e.g.,
Fig.

6
) visualizes how each spectral mode contributes to the embedding.
It plots eigenvalue indices on the
x
-axis and the magnitudes of the corresponding rows of
\mathbf{P}{}
on the
y
-axis.
In this way, large responses concentrated at small eigenvalues indicate that coarse, global structure dominates the embedding, whereas stronger responses at larger eigenvalues indicate increased fine-scale or oscillatory detail.

Complementarily, we can also visualize the distribution of eigenvalues from first to last by using
y
- and
x
-coordinates to eigenvalues and their indices, respectively.
This reveals the ordering from smooth low-frequency modes to oscillatory high-frequency modes and highlights eigengaps that suggest structural scale transitions.

The local view
explains how spectral modes combine to determine the position of each data point.
For each data point, we visualize its participation in spectral modes and how these modes contribute to its final embedding.
As shown in
Fig.

2
, we use a petal glyph
[
56
]
to encode the first several spectral modes (by default, 10 modes).

For data point
{\mathbf{x}}_{{n}}
, its participation to the
{s}
-th spectral mode is given by the corresponding entry of the spectral space matrix,
u_{{n},{s}}
.
We use its magnitude
|u_{{n},{s}}|
to quantify the strength of participation.
Let
{\mathbf{p}{}}_{{s}}
denote the
{s}
-th row of the projection matrix
\mathbf{P}{}
.
The contribution of the
{s}
-th spectral mode to the embedding of
{\mathbf{x}}_{{n}}
is then computed as
\left\lVert u_{{n},{s}}\cdot{\mathbf{p}{}}_{{s}}\right\rVert
.

Figure 2
:
Petal glyph.

The outline length of each petal represents
|u_{{n},{s}}|
.
The filled length represents
\left\lVert u_{{n},{s}}\cdot{\mathbf{p}{}}_{{s}}\right\rVert
.
Comparing these two lengths reveals how cross-entropy optimization amplifies or suppresses individual spectral modes.
We further encode this change using color: red indicates that a mode is emphasized, while blue indicates that it is suppressed.
Color intensity reflects the magnitude of this change.

Petals are arranged clockwise in order of increasing frequency, making the spectral order directly visible.
This design allows users to identify which modes dominate the position of each point and how local structure emerges from spectral modes.
Alternatively, petal orientation can encode directional influence in the embedding.
Specifically, each petal can be aligned with the direction of the corresponding row of the projection matrix
{\mathbf{p}{}_{s}}
.This encoding indicates how the
{s}
-th spectral mode contributes to the position of a data point in the embedding, making dominant directional effects directly observable.

Although the discrete petal glyph can display only a limited number of spectral modes due to screen space constraints, it provides an effective explanation of local layout formation.
When data points within the same visual area exhibit similar dominant low-frequency modes, the cluster is supported at a coarse spectral scale.
This is particularly informative for multi-class data, where clusters with strong low-frequency contributions indicate coarse separability, while clusters lacking such patterns require higher-frequency modes for separation.
We demonstrate this capability in
Section

5.2
.

Together, the global and local views establish a mapping between spectral modes and embeddings.
The global view explains
which
spectral modes dominate the embedding, while the local view explains
how
these modes combine to determine the positions of data points.

4.1
Reconstruction Error

Our spectral framework constructs an embedding progressively in spectral subspaces of increasing size
S
.
A natural practical question is therefore how large must
S
be before the resulting embedding is effectively indistinguishable from the full non-trivial spectrum solution, i.e.,
S_{\text{full}}=N-1
for a dataset of size
N
, since we exclude the trivial constant eigenvector associated with eigenvalue zero.
To quantitatively measure whether a low-dimensional spectral subspace already captures most of the meaningful structure of the final embedding, or whether higher-frequency modes are essential, and guide the users to choose
S
, we introduce the reconstruction error
\mathcal{E}_{S}
.

Formally, given the final embedding
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{full}}
calculated by optimization on the entire spectrum
S_{\text{full}}
with the corresponding subspace
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{full}}
and projection matrix
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{full}}
,
we define the
S
-mode error measure:

\mathcal{E}_{S}:=\|\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{full}}-\mathbf{Y}\|=\|\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{full}}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{full}}-\mathbf{U}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{S}\|

(8)

Note that
\mathcal{E}_{S}
is defined with respect to the final embedding, not the original high-dimensional data.
It therefore measures the spectral expressiveness of the current embedding rather than absolute embedding quality.
Nonetheless, it is analytically useful in two ways.
First, it provides dataset-level insights into the spectral complexity of the embedding.
Embeddings dominated by coarse graph structure, i.e., based on graphs with a low-frequency organization in broad large-scale neighborhoods
[
57
,
19
]
, exhibit rapid error decay, whereas embeddings that rely on fine local detail decay more slowly.

Second, this helps us choose a value of
S
required to create a visualization within a reasonable tolerance, that is, the smallest
S
that is almost as good as the full embedding.
In this sense, the first
R
modes act as a spectral sketch of the embedding, and
\mathcal{E}_{R}
quantifies the information lost by that snapshot.

The curves show how rapidly the spectral subspace approaches the final embedding, and reveal clear dataset-dependent differences in spectral complexity.
Our spectral formulation produces a clear two-regime behavior.
First, the error drops rapidly within the first
S\leq 30
spectral modes to
\mathcal{E}_{S}<0.4
.
This indicates that these first few modes capture more than
60\%
of coarse, large-scale embedding structure, corresponding to the low-rank nature of complex systems
[
63
]
.

On the other hand, following the initial steep drop, the error decreases much more slowly, and reaching the full embedding requires substantially more spectral modes.
This suggests that low-frequency modes recover the dominant global structure, while finer dataset-specific details are distributed across higher-frequency modes.

),

This suggests that high-frequency modes remain important for capturing single-cell expression structure, aligning with recent domain findings
[

.

In summary, our spectral formulation provides a linear decomposition that supports direct dataset-level comparisons, with reconstruction error presented here as one illustrative example.
The formulation can potentially support a broader range of analyses.
As a practical guideline, the required subspace size
S
is strongly data dependent.
In general, smooth manifolds are well captured by compact low-frequency modes, whereas datasets with richer fine-scale structure require a substantially larger number of modes.

4.2
Time and Memory Complexity Analysis

Here, we show that restricting optimization to a subspace also gains a computational advantage.
After constructing the graph, we form the normalized Laplacian
\mathbf{L}
and compute a spectral basis once.
Computing or storing the full spectrum is not required for the core proposed framework.
A naive full eigendecomposition of the
S\times S
Laplacian requires
O(S^{3})
time and
O(S^{2})
memory.
For handling large-scale datasets, we can avoid computing the full eigenbasis by only computing a truncated set using sparse iterative eigensolvers
[
40
]
.
Thus, preprocessing depends on graph sparsity, the number of requested modes, and eigensolver convergence, and is typically far cheaper than a dense
O(S^{3})
full eigendecomposition.
We note that such spectral preprocessing is common in DR pipelines, as methods such as UMAP already employ spectral initialization
[
47
,
37
]
.

For the optimization process, let
|E|
be the number of edges and
T
the number of epochs.
Each update for a pair of points now works by combining
S
modes to reconstruct their positions, and then updating the corresponding
S
coefficients.
Therefore, the cost of each update scales linearly with
S
, rather than
N
.
Given that each epoch processes
O(|E|)
pairwise interactions, the total optimization cost is
O(S\cdot T\cdot|E|)
.

Compared to UMAP
[
47
]
for 2D embeddings, our approach introduces an additional eigendecomposition cost for
S>2
, but reduces the effective optimization dimension from
N
to
S
.
When
S\ll N
, the optimization is substantially cheaper.
Compared to Laplacian Eigenmaps
[
3
]
, the spectral basis computation cost is of the same order, but our approach additionally performs optimization in the spectral subspace.

4.3
Progressive Embedding Quality

We analyze the stages of our progressive embedding strategy across subspaces of increasing size and compare it to Laplacian Eigenmaps, UMAP, and PHATE quantitatively and qualitatively.
In the experiments reported in
Table

2
and
Fig.

4
, we use 10 progressive stages that equally divide the spectrum, denoted by
S_{1}
–
S_{10}
, partitioning the non-trivial spectrum into 10 equally sized segments, to capture coarse-to-fine refinement.
The total number of epochs is evenly divided across stages, with 50 epochs per stage.
While
S_{10}
denotes the final stage of the progressive run, for comparison, we also include a non-progressive full-spectrum optimization, denoted by
S_{full}
, which uses the full non-trivial spectrum from the beginning and is trained for the full 500 epochs.

Quantitative evaluation of nonlinear embeddings is inherently challenging because there is no single notion of ground-truth structure preservation in high-dimensional data, and different measures emphasize different quality aspects
[
33
,
36
,
44
,
35
,
74
]
.
For that reason, it is worth noting here that our aim is not to outperform the baselines on all quality measures.
Rather we focus on how our method recovers graph-frequency structure through progressively adding frequency bands
and use quantitative evaluation primarily as confirmatory evidence.

To cover different aspects of our method, including preservation of manifold topology, neighborhood relations, and large-scale geometry, we used a compact set of label-free measures.
We intentionally avoid label-based or cluster-separation measures
[
34
]
, as such measures reward whether classes become visually compact in the embedding and can score high while substantially distorting manifold geometry.

In particular, we use
DEMaP

[
49
]
originally designed to measure manifold preservation of developmental trajectories in single-cell datasets.
DEMaP computes the Spearman rank correlation between graph-geodesic distances in the original space and Euclidean distances in the embedding and as such rewards manifold preservation.
Thus, higher values indicate that pairs of points that are close or far apart along the data manifold remain correspondingly ordered in the embedding.

In addition, we select multiple measures from the ZADU library
[
33
]
.
We use
Spearman’s
\rho

[
58
]
and
non-metric stress
and
scale-normalized stress

[
38
,
33
]
to capture global geometry preservation. Spearman’s
\rho
evaluates whether the global ordering of pairwise distances is preserved, without assuming a linear relation between original-space and embedding-space distances.
Non-metric stress and scale-normalized stress assess global geometric distortion while discounting monotone mismatch or trivial rescaling effects, making them more appropriate than raw stress for nonlinear DR embeddings

To measure neighborhood preservation we use
continuity

[
71
]
and
mean relative rank error
(MRRE) for missing neighbors
[
39
]
. Both quantify whether true neighbors in the original space remain nearby after projection, with MRRE additionally penalizing the loss of highly ranked neighbors more strongly than the loss of marginal ones.

Observations.

We show the full results of the quantitative and qualitative analysis in
Tables

3
and
4
, respectively, for the Brownian Tree,
C. elegans
, and Fashion-MNIST datasets (see
Table

2
).

As shown in
Table

3
, neighborhood preservation measures improve substantially with progressing embeddings from early to late stages for all three datasets.
For the synthetic Brownian tree dataset, the highest continuity (0.950) and MRRE (0.964) for all methods are achieved at the final progressive stage (
S_{10}
), slightly outperforming UMAP (0.944 and 0.961, respectively).
For the preprocessed
C. elegans
single-cell dataset, the improvement is more pronounced.
The highest Continuity (0.970) and MRRE (0.974) are achieved at
S_{10}
, exceeding PHATE (0.965 and 0.970) as well as UMAP and Laplacian Eigenmaps.
For Fashion-MNIST, the results are more diverse.
The progressive stages remain competitive and improve monotonically across stages, with final results very close to the highest scores in PHATE.

Regarding the DEMaP measure, PHATE achieves the highest score for all three datasets, which is consistent with its diffusion-based objective and confirms that it is a strong topology baseline under a geodesic-faithfulness criterion
Notably, DEMaP is proposed by the authors of PHATE to specifically value these properties.
Nevertheless, our proposed method achieves competitive DEMaP scores and clearly improves over Laplacian Eigenmaps and UMAP.

For global geometry preservation measures, the strongest gains appear on the
C. elegans
dataset.
Our approach achieves the best Spearman’s
\rho
(0.366), non-metric stress (0.396, lower is better), and scale-normalized stress (0.418, lower is better) at
S_{10}
, outperforming all baselines.
This indicates that the final embedding preserves the global rank ordering of pairwise distances more faithfully while introducing less overall distortion.
For the Brownian Tree, the best (lowest) stress values occur slightly before the final stage, at
S_{8}
(0.383 non-metric stress and 0.400 scale-normalized stress), whereas
S_{10}
and
S_{full}
obtain the best neighborhood-preservation scores.
This behavior is consistent with the intended coarse-to-fine stages of the progressive construction.
Intermediate stages with low- to mid-frequency modes can lead to smoother global geometry, while later stages recover finer local detail.

Effect of progressive spectral expansion.

Qualitatively,
Fig.

4
shows for all three datasets that the global shape of the embeddings at late stages closely resembles UMAP, however, without the seemingly arbitrary breaks in topology.
The topology is closer to the more connected PHATE, where all three datasets seem to be densely connected.
Interestingly, the progressive version at the final
S_{10}
creates more continuous and traceable results compared to
S_{\mathrm{full}}
, although both operate on the full spectrum.
Especially, for the
C.elegans
dataset, the result at
S_{10}
shows the branching structure more clearly than with
S_{\mathrm{full}}
.
Overall, these qualitative observations are also reflected in the quantitative results, where 12 of the 18 top scores within our method are achieved with one of the progressive stages (8 of them at
S_{10}
), with only Fashion-MNIST showing equal or marginally better results with
S_{\mathrm{full}}
.

By dividing the non-trivial full spectrum into discrete stages, early updates are forced to organize the embedding using only smooth low-frequency directions, and higher-frequency modes are only introduced when the coarse geometry is established.
While the spectrum is equally partitioned in (
Table

3
and
Fig.

4
), a natural alternative is a logarithmic schedule that allocates more stages and epochs to low frequencies and fewer, broader stages to high frequencies (e.g.,
Section

5.1
).
Conceptually, dividing into log scale would make early optimization even more biased toward coarse global organization, in a way conceptually resembling t-SNE early exaggeration
[
9
]
, but with an explicit tie to the graph spectrum.

Overall, these results support the main claim of our spectral framework that the progressive spectral construction provides interpretable intermediate embeddings and coarse-to-fine decomposition without substantially compromising, and in some cases slightly improving, the quality of the final embedding.

6
Discussion and Future Work

The goal of this paper is not to propose yet another DR algorithm that simply produces “better” final embeddings.
Rather, we present a different formulation of graph-based dimensionality reduction that is inspired by graph signal processing
[
57
,
17
,
1
]
and run optimization
[
47
,
15
]
under a linear-combined spectral coordinate system, keeping the graph frequency structure explicit throughout the embedding process.

By optimizing the embedding in a spectral subspace instead of directly over point coordinates,
updating spectral mode coefficients rather than moving points directly, the embedding becomes a structured object that supports controlled subspace optimization, multi-scale construction, and direct interpretation of how the final embedding is formed.

Several directions could further extend this formulation.
First,
rather than selecting a fixed truncation of the spectrum, one could parameterize the spectral response by a learnable filter
[
17
,
73
]
applied to
\mathbf{P}
.
This would provide a more expressive alternative to hard subspace selection while preserving the interpretability of the spectral parameterization.

Another direction is related to the known issue of the reliability of kNN graphs in neighbor embeddings
[
31
,
53
]
and the corresponding graph augmentation techniques.
Our proposed framework may offer a useful perspective for revealing and explaining the effect of graph augmentation in graph-based DR
[
49
,
32
]
.
Under the proposed framework, different objectives and graph modifications may amplify or suppress different parts of the spectrum, thereby favoring different balances between coarse global organization and local separation.
Studying these methods in a common spectral language may therefore provide a principled way to better understand graph-based DR methods.
Overall, we see this work as a step toward a more structured view of nonlinear dimensionality reduction, where the embedding is not only a final scatterplot but also a decomposable and analyzable representation of graph structure.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a spectral framework that combines neighborhood-preserving cross-entropy optimization with Laplacian graph frequencies, thereby optimizing embeddings in controlled spectral subspaces rather than directly in unconstrained point coordinates.
By progressively expanding these spectral subspaces, the method produces coarse-to-fine snapshots that make the global–local trade-off explicit and inspectable.
Across quantitative evaluations and case studies on synthetic, image, and single-cell data, this formulation preserves manifold continuity and provides interpretable global and node-level explanations through spectral responses and glyph-based encodings.
More broadly, this work reframes DR layouts as multiscale, decomposable graph-signal objects.
