TTN mutation-positive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries: A rare coexistence
Authors: Sangram Chakraborty, Bhargavi Dhulipudi, Shweta Bhakru, Nageswara Rao Koneti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (CCTGA) is a rare congenital cardiac malformation, in which the morphologic right ventricle (RV) supports the systemic circulation. Systemic RV hypertrophy is usually secondary to pressure overload, while intrinsic myopathic hypertrophy due to sarcomeric gene mutation is exceedingly uncommon. We report a 37-year-old male who presented with exertional dyspnea and chest discomfort. Echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging revealed atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial discordance consistent with CCTGA, accompanied by asymmetric hypertrophy of the systemic RV and interventricular septum without significant outflow obstruction. Late gadolinium enhancement demonstrated patchy myocardial fibrosis. Genetic testing identified a heterozygous truncating variant in the TTN gene (exon 46, c. 14414del, p.Thr4805 LysfsTer24), confirming familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). The present case highlights an extremely rare coexistence of CCTGA and TTN-related HCM of the systemic RV. Recognition of this overlap requires high clinical suspicion, multimodal imaging, and molecular testing, which together enable accurate diagnosis, family counseling, and long-term management of this unique phenotype.

INTRODUCTION

Congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (CCTGA) is an uncommon congenital cardiac anomaly characterized by atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial discordance. It accounts for approximately 0.5%–1.4% of all congenital heart defects and may remain asymptomatic for decades when unaccompanied by additional lesions.[12] The systemic right ventricle (RV) in CCTGA has a tendency to develop atrioventricular (tricuspid) valve regurgitation, conduction abnormalities, and eventual systemic ventricular failure.[3]

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common inherited cardiomyopathy, with a prevalence of 0.16%–0.29% in the general population, and is classically defined by unexplained left ventricular (LV) wall thickening exceeding 15 mm.[4] RV involvement in HCM is less common.

The coexistence of genetically confirmed systemic RV HCM in CCTGA is exceptionally rare, posing diagnostic and management challenges.[567] The TTN gene encodes titin, a sarcomeric protein that maintains myocardial elasticity and mechanical stability. Truncating variants in TTN (TTNtv) are best known for dilated cardiomyopathy but occasionally produce hypertrophic phenotypes through impaired sarcomere compliance.[8]

CASE REPORT

A 37-year-old male presented with New York Heart Association Class II exertional dyspnea and intermittent chest pain for 6 months. There was no history of palpitations or syncope.

On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Cardiac auscultation revealed normal S1 and a single S2 without murmurs. A 12-lead electrocardiogram [Figure 1a] showed sinus rhythm, a PR interval of 160 ms, normal QRS axis (+45°), biatrial enlargement, and deep T-wave inversions over precordial leads, consistent with systemic RV hypertrophy (RVH). Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography [Figure 1b] demonstrated situs solitus, levocardia, and atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial discordance consistent with CCTGA. The systemic RV and interventricular septum were markedly hypertrophied, without significant systemic outflow obstruction (gradient: 20 mmHg). Grade II diastolic dysfunction was present, whereas systemic RV systolic function remained preserved.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging [Figure 1c and d] confirmed asymmetric septal hypertrophy (maximum diastolic septal thickness: 22.5 mm) and RV free-wall hypertrophy (16 mm) and no evidence of RV outflow tract obstruction. Late gadolinium enhancement revealed patchy midmyocardial fibrosis involving approximately 20% of the RV myocardium.

Twenty-four-hour Holter monitoring showed sinus rhythm without AV block or tachyarrhythmia. Clinical exome sequencing identified a heterozygous pathogenic TTNtv gene (exon 46, c. 14414del, p.Thr4805 LysfsTer24), confirming HCM with autosomal dominant inheritance.

His father had died suddenly at 60 years of age, presumed secondary to HCM in view of the genotype positive in the indexed case. On screening the siblings, the elder had HCM without any outflow obstruction by transthoracic echocardiography in the presence of normal AV-VA concordance. He refused to undergo any further genetic assessment.

HCM risk of Sudden cardiac death (SCD) at 5 years was 5.15% (American heart association (AHA) Calculator). According to the 2022 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for the management of ventricular arrhythmia to prevent SCD along with sarcomere mutation, there was a Class II A indication for an implantable cardioverter defibrillator.[9]

DISCUSSION

CCTGA is a rare congenital cardiac malformation, in which atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial discordance results in physiologic correction of blood flow. The morphologic RV functions as the systemic ventricle and is exposed to systemic afterload throughout life. Over time, this unique hemodynamic burden may lead to hypertrophy, progressive dilation, tricuspid (systemic AV valve) regurgitation, conduction abnormalities, and systemic ventricular failure.[3]

The diagnostic criteria for RVH are not well standardized. McKenna et al. classified RVH as mild (6–8 mm), moderate (9–12 mm), and severe (>12 mm), whereas Foale R et al. defined RVH as RV wall thickness >5 mm and severe RVH as >10 mm.[1011]

Systemic RVH in CCTGA is most often adaptive, secondary to pressure overload or outflow tract obstruction. However, distinguishing true HCM from adaptive hypertrophy is essential because the underlying mechanisms, clinical course, and familial implications differ markedly. True HCM represents a primary sarcomeric disorder, whereas adaptive hypertrophy is a physiological response to increased afterload.[5] The distinction may be challenging, particularly when the systemic ventricle is morphologically right, as imaging thresholds for hypertrophy were historically defined for the LV.

Our case represents an extremely rare coexistence of genetically confirmed HCM affecting the systemic RV in a patient with CCTGA. The hypertrophy pattern was asymmetric and accompanied by patchy late gadolinium enhancement on CMR, suggesting a myopathic process rather than a pressure-overload response. Molecular analysis confirmed a truncating TTN variant.

Previous reports have described “pseudo-HCM” in CCTGA resulting from subaortic stenosis or fibromuscular obstruction without intrinsic sarcomere disease.[67] In contrast, our patient’s presentation demonstrated both structural (CCTGA) and genetic (sarcomeric) contributors to RVH, highlighting the importance of advanced imaging and molecular studies in adults with congenital heart disease and unexplained or disproportionate hypertrophy.

The TTN gene encodes titin, a large sarcomeric protein that provides elasticity and regulates force transmission. TTNtv are most often associated with dilated cardiomyopathy, but can occasionally manifest as hypertrophic phenotypes. The variant detected in our patient (exon 46, p.Thr4805 LysfsTer24) lies within the Novex 2 isoform encoded in the I-band region [Figure 2], which modulates sarcomere compliance. Disruption of this region may decrease elasticity and promote maladaptive hypertrophy.[8]

This case exemplifies how congenital and genetic mechanisms can interact: systemic pressure loading of the morphologic RV in CCTGA, combined with a sarcomeric TTN mutation, produced a phenotypic expression of HCM. Recognition of such overlap is clinically important, as genetic predisposition may accelerate systemic RV dysfunction, alter arrhythmic risk, and influence family screening strategies.

Clinically, this report reinforces three principles: (i) multimodal imaging (echocardiography and CMR) is essential to differentiate pressure-induced from myopathic hypertrophy, (ii) genetic testing should be considered in adults with CCTGA and disproportionate hypertrophy or family history of cardiomyopathy, (iii) long-term follow-up is critical, as both adaptive and genetic systemic RVH can progress to dysfunction, arrhythmia, or conduction block requiring pacing.

CONCLUSION

This case highlights how congenital anaotomy of RV facing systemic pressures along with genetic predisposition can interact to shape unique cardiomyopathic phenotypes.
