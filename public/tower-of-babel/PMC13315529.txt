Multifocal autoimmune pancreatitis complicated by pancreatic insufficiency and segmental portal hypertension: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Nahla Ennejjari, Salma Abouchiba, Younes Abdourabbih, Ismail Chaouche, Hajar O. Chahdi, Amal Akammar, Nizar El Bouardi, Badreeddine Alami, Meryem Boubbou, Mustapha Maaroufi, Moulay Y.A. Lamrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autoimmune pancreatitis is an increasingly recognized variant of chronic pancreatitis, classified into 2 subtypes by the international consensus diagnostic criteria: type 1, associated with IgG4 (Immunoglobulin G4)-related disease, and type 2, a pancreas-specific disorder often linked to inflammatory bowel disease. The diagnosis is based on histology, imaging of pancreatic parenchyma/ducts, serology (serum IgG4 levels), other organ involvement or extrapancreatic manifestations, and response to the steroid treatment. Cross-sectional imaging, including computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), is crucial for diagnosing both autoimmune pancreatitis subtypes, which exhibit a wide range of imaging patterns, the most commonly described being diffuse pancreatic enlargement with a fibrous pericapsular rim. Multifocal presentations of autoimmune pancreatitis are less common and often more challenging to diagnose because of an important overlap with pancreatic malignancies. Autoimmune pancreatitis is commonly described as a cause of exocrine pancreatic insufficiency; it can also be associated with severe compressive complications, such as biliary dilation and portal hypertension. We report a rare case of a multifocal pattern of type 2 autoimmune pancreatitis, revealed by pancreatic insufficiency, imaged with both CT and MRI, which initially raised suspicion of malignant pancreatic masses, and which was complicated by segmental portal hypertension.

Introduction

Autoimmune pancreatitis is an increasingly recognized variant of chronic pancreatitis characterized by inflammatory changes and fibrosis within the pancreatic tissue [1,2]. The International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria guidelines recently defined 2 distinct types of autoimmune pancreatitis [3]. Type 1 is an autoimmune disease mediated by immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) involving all organs. In contrast, type 2 is a distinct pancreatic disease that may be occasionally accompanied by inflammatory bowel disease [4].

Cross-sectional imaging, including computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, is crucial for diagnosing both AIP subtypes. Several imaging patterns have been described [5], the most common feature being diffuse pancreatic enlargement. The multifocal pattern of autoimmune pancreatitis is a rare presentation that has been infrequently reported in the literature, with most cases associated with elevated serum IgG4 levels, suggesting a type 1 phenotype. This pattern has been reported less frequently in the setting of type 2 autoimmune pancreatitis and is challenging to diagnose because it shows overlapping imaging features that may mimic malignant pancreatic masses [6].

We report a case of a 62-year-old woman presenting with bifocal pancreatic masses, ultimately diagnosed with autoimmune pancreatitis, complicated by pancreatic insufficiency and segmental portal hypertension secondary to splenic vein encasement. We aim to highlight imaging features that should raise suspicion of autoimmune pancreatitis and to emphasize the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to establish a diagnosis and avoid unnecessary interventions.

Case report

We report the case of a 63-year-old woman who has been followed in the gastroenterology department of our hospital since 2010 for an exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, presenting with chronic diarrhea, steatorrhea, and a weight loss of approximately 10 kg. Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy was instituted, with marked improvement in clinical manifestations.

The patient presented to the emergency department of our hospital in October 2022 with febrile jaundice, abdominal pain, nausea, and elevated levels of cholestatic biological markers. She underwent an abdominal ultrasound, which demonstrated a 15 mm common bile duct dilation featuring an abrupt tapering with no visible cause of obstruction. An abdominal CT was then performed (not available in our data) and showed a poorly defined, hypodense mass centered on the pancreatic head with slight progressive enhancement. The mass appeared to encase the distal part of the common bile duct, causing extra- and intra-hepatic biliary ducts dilation, raising concern for malignancy. Despite 3 subsequent endoscopic biopsies returning negative, suspicion of a pancreatic malignancy remained high due to the persistent mass effect and progressive biliary obstruction. Consequently, she underwent 3 endoscopic drainage procedures between November 2022 and December 2023, with placement of 2 plastic stents and ultimately a metallic stent during her last ERCP in December 2023.

Three days after this last ERCP (December 2023), the patient consulted again at the emergency department for the acute setting of abdominal pain, high fever, and marked asthenia. Physical examination demonstrated cutaneous and mucosal jaundice and a temperature of 39 degrees. The abdominal examination revealed right upper quadrant abdominal tenderness with radiation to the back, without any palpable mass or organomegaly. It also revealed generalized scratch marks. The rest of the systemic examination was unremarkable. Initial serum and biochemical evaluations revealed an elevated total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase, and aspartate aminotransferase, as well as elevated CRP and leukocytosis. The remainder of the laboratory data, including serum lipase, was otherwise within normal limits.

The patient underwent a triphasic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen (Fig. 1), which revealed the presence of 2 focal pancreatic tissular masses, respectively centered on the cephalic and the caudal pancreatic regions, showing the same imaging characteristics, ie, lobulated margins, hypoattenuation, and hypoenhancement after IV contrast. The first mass measured around 6 cm in diameter and encased the biliary confluence, with upstream extra- and intrahepatic biliary duct dilation. It also exhibited intimate contact with the portal vein over a circumference of less than 180 degrees, with the hepatic artery over a circumference of approximately 270 degrees, and with the retrohepatic inferior vena cava, with loss of the separating fat plane and visualization of a small marginal thrombus within the latter. No thrombosis of the portal vein was seen. The biliary stent seemed to be correctly positioned within the main bile duct lumen, ensuring selective drainage of the left biliary ducts, which showed evidence of aerobilia, and persistent dilatation of the right intrahepatic biliary ducts. Diffuse thickening and enhancement of the biliary duct walls were also evident, consistent with cholangitis on imaging. The second mass was centered on the pancreatic tail, encasing the splenic pedicle, with preserved patency of the splenic artery and an occlusion of the splenic vein, complicated by splenic infarcts and imaging features of segmental portal hypertension, including moderate splenomegaly (estimated craniocaudal diameter of 16 cm), multiple perisplenic, perigastric, and intramural gastric collateral veins. The rest of the pancreatic parenchyma showed homogeneous enhancement, with a slight diffuse enlargement of the gland and mild peripancreatic stranding. CT scan also demonstrated enlarged centimetric lymph nodes in the hilar region, as well as intrahepatic fluid collections within the segments VII and VIII, with a peripheral enhancing wall, consistent with hepatic abscesses. Concurrently, the patient experienced an acute episode of hematemesis. An urgent esophagogastroduodenoscopy was performed, revealing grade II gastric varices with red color signs in the gastric fundus. To manage the acute bleeding and reduce the risk of immediate recurrence, the patient was started on beta-blocker therapy.

Given the highly suspicious radiographic features raising concern for a bifocal pancreatic neoplastic process on the index CT, an abdominal MRI was performed during the same hospitalization for further characterization (Fig. 2). It confirmed the presence of 2 ill-defined masses in the head and tail regions of the pancreas, which appeared hypointense on T1- and T2-weighted images, without restricted diffusion. Both showed slight progressive contrast uptake, more pronounced on delayed phases, suggesting a fibrous enhancement pattern. The remainder of the pancreas was atrophic, with mild dilation of the pancreatic duct. Histopathologic evaluation of the biopsy samples revealed features consistent with type 2 autoimmune pancreatitis, including large areas of fibrosis, periductal lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, and neutrophils in close approximation to the ductal epithelium, which explicitly confirmed the presence of granulocytic epithelial lesions (GELs). Immunohistochemical staining was performed, including IgG4 markers (which were negative, < 10 plasma cells per high-power field) and pan-cytokeratin to assess ductal architecture. Core biopsies ruled out alternative diagnoses, showing no atypical epithelial proliferation indicative of pancreatic adenocarcinoma, no monoclonal lymphoid populations suggestive of lymphoma, and no features of a nonspecific inflammatory mass. The serum IgG4 assay was within the reference range.

Following this comprehensive diagnostic workup, a targeted management strategy was instituted. The patient was started on beta-blocker therapy to manage the acute variceal bleeding and reduce the risk of immediate recurrence. Simultaneously, she was treated with intravenous antibiotic therapy for the infectious complications, resulting in favorable clinical and radiologic outcomes; drainage of the hepatic abscesses was avoided due to the presence of ascites. Additionally, anticoagulation was initiated with low-molecular-weight heparin to treat the retrohepatic inferior vena cava thrombus and the associated extensive thrombotic phenomena. An abdominal CT performed after ten days of treatment (Fig. 3) demonstrated interval resolution of the intrahepatic collections, of the thrombus in the inferior vena cava, as well as of the splenic infarcts, showing a return to homogeneous splenic enhancement and confirming that the SV occlusion did not persist.Fig. 3Abdominal triphasic CT performed after ten days of intravenous antibiotherapy and anticoagulation. The patient was initiated on prednisone 40 mg daily, with a noticeable improvement of her clinical symptoms and liver function tests during the course of her hospital stay. She was then discharged on oral steroid therapy, which was tapered over 3 months according to standard protocols, and acenocoumarol for the marginal thrombosis of the IVC. At the 1-month and 6-month follow-up visits, the patient was completely asymptomatic with normal LFT trends and normal CA19-9 levels (<15 U/mL). Two follow-up abdominal MRIs were performed at 6 and twelve months, and showed stability of the lesions. This radiological stability, characterized by persistent volume reduction of the initial masses and a nonrestrictive, purely fibrous delayed enhancement pattern, was consistent with residual inactive fibrosis rather than persistent active inflammation or malignancy. Furthermore, follow-up magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) demonstrated partial regression of the main pancreatic duct narrowing without any new strictures. Following this initial stabilization, the patient had no recurrent episodes of gastrointestinal bleeding during the remainder of the follow-up period.

Discussion

AIP is an uncommon form of chronic pancreatitis characterized by inflammation and fibrosis. It is generally considered a benign yet heterogeneous condition. It was initially described as being the pancreatic manifestation of IgG4-related disease, and has only recently been divided into 2 distinct phenotypes by the International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria (ICDC) guidelines [7], based on histology, imaging of pancreatic parenchyma/ducts, serology (serum IgG4 levels), other organ involvement or extrapancreatic manifestations, and response to the steroid treatment. Type 1 AIP, also known as lymphoplasmacytic sclerosing pancreatitis (LPSP), seems to be the pancreatic manifestation of the recently proposed IgG4-related disease, characterized by swelling of the pancreas, elevated serum IgG4 levels, extrapancreatic lesions (eg, sclerosing cholangitis, sclerosing sialadenitis, and retroperitoneal fibrosis), and pancreatic infiltration with IgG4-positive plasma cells [[8], [9], [10]]. Type 2 AIP, also known as idiopathic duct-centric pancreatitis AIP or AIP with granulocytic epithelial lesions (GELs), also shows a massive inflammatory infiltrate of the pancreatic tissue, but is characterized by different cellular components and an inflammation distribution pattern [9]. In this subtype, inflammation is predominantly periductal, in contrast to type 1, which shows a lobule-centered pattern of injury. This difference in the infiltration distribution may be one of the reasons why type 2 AIP less commonly shows a capsule-like rim on imaging than type 1, because this radiological finding represents inflammatory extension from the lobules to pancreatic adipose tissue. In contrast to type 1, type 2 infiltration is neutrophil-rich, with none or very few IgG4-positive plasma cells [11].

Clinical manifestations of both phenotypes are similar and may overlap, commonly including obstructive jaundice, epigastric pain, and weight loss. However, type 2 AIP is characterized by an absence of serum IgG4 elevation and extrapancreatic manifestations, except for inflammatory bowel disease, which has been cited in up to 40% of cases (75%, ulcerative colitis; 15%, Crohn’s disease; 10%, unclassified) [12].

Imaging features of the 2 subtypes of autoimmune pancreatitis are similar and indistinguishable, both on CT and MRI. The most common imaging pattern of AIP consists of diffuse pancreatic enlargement, with loss of the normal fatty lobulations, resulting in a sausage-shaped pancreas [6], associated with an irregular narrowing of the main pancreatic duct. The affected pancreatic parenchyma typically shows hypoattenuation on CT, hypointensity on MRI T1-weighted images, and slightly hyperintensity on T2-weighted images, as well as homogeneous delayed enhancement in the portal venous phase, and contrast retention in the delayed phase [13], the latest showing high sensitivity and specificity (respectively 83% and 85% in a large recently published systematic review) [14]. A peripheral capsule-like rim is another common feature of AIP, present in approximately 50% of cases, demonstrating hypoattenuation on CT, intrinsic T1- and T2-hypointensity on MRI, and moderate postcontrast enhancement on both imaging modalities. Two other imaging patterns of autoimmune pancreatitis are well recognized: focal or segmental lesions and multifocal lesions. The focal or segmental form of AIP is the second-most common [5,8] and typically involves the pancreatic head. The multifocal form of AIP is less frequent, most often associated with type 1 AIP [15], with few cases reported in the literature in the setting of type 2 AIP. This form is the most challenging one to diagnose, possibly mimicking malignant etiologies such as pancreatic adenocarcinoma and lymphoma, which can lead to inappropriate treatment. Imaging plays an essential role in the diagnosis of segmental forms of autoimmune pancreatitis, in correlation with clinical, biological, and histopathological findings.

Both CT and MRI can show suggestive abnormalities, with the MRI being superior to CT when it comes to ruling out pancreatic malignancies. In addition to morphological characteristics of pancreatic masses, which include iso- to slightly hyperintensity on T2-weighted sequences and delayed enhancement on equilibrium phases, diffusion-weighted MRI (DWI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps are valuable for differential diagnosis. AIP typically exhibits more pronounced restricted water diffusion, characterized by increased signal intensity on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and reduced apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values, in contrast to pancreatic adenocarcinoma and normal pancreatic tissue [8]. MRCP is also very useful in the evaluation of the main pancreatic duct, which typically demonstrates segmental and multifocal irregular narrowing of the pancreatic duct.

Both CT and MRI can show suggestive abnormalities, with MRI being superior to CT when it comes to ruling out pancreatic malignancies due to its higher soft-tissue contrast and multiparametric capabilities. Beyond morphological characteristics, diffusion-weighted MRI (DWI) paired with apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps serves as a vital tool for differential diagnosis. Autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) typically presents with restricted water diffusion, but findings are highly variable depending on the histopathological phase, especially in type 2 or focal variants. While restricted diffusion often marks active cellular inflammation—characterized by increased signal intensity on DWI and reduced ADC values compared to pancreatic adenocarcinoma [8]—it may be absent in late-stage, chronic, or hypocellular fibrotic masses, making diffusion restriction a biomarker of disease activity rather than an absolute rule [16]. Recognizing this variability is crucial; a lack of diffusion restriction does not exclude AIP, provided that other suggestive features are present. These include morphological findings such as iso- to slight hyperintensity on T2-weighted sequences, delayed enhancement on equilibrium phases, and the key contributions of MRCP. MRCP remains exceptionally useful in evaluating the main pancreatic duct, characteristically demonstrating segmental, long, or multifocal irregular narrowing without the marked upstream dilation typical of pancreatic adenocarcinoma.

Although imaging studies are critical for identifying features suggestive of autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP), they typically fail to differentiate between its 2 subtypes. Furthermore, while the histological confirmation of type 2 AIP is straightforward in surgically resected specimens, achieving a definitive diagnosis from small tissue biopsies remains challenging. In these ambiguous cases, secondary diagnostic pillars–specifically normal serum IgG4 concentrations and the absence of systemic, extrapancreatic manifestations—become crucial to exclude type 1 disease. A short-term corticosteroid trial (0.6-1.0 mg/kg of prednisolone), followed by a reassessment of imaging findings and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) levels after 2 weeks, serves as a valuable diagnostic tool [7,12]. However, this therapeutic trial must strictly follow a pancreatic biopsy to prevent confounding the histological interpretation and delaying potential malignancy diagnoses.

Portal hypertension associated with AIP is rarely reported and has been exclusively linked to the diffuse form of the disease. The literature postulates several concurrent mechanisms, such as mechanical pressure from pancreatic swelling, regional tissue inflammation and fibrosis, and phlebitis obliterans [4]. A case of segmental portal hypertension caused by splenic vein obstruction was described by Chang et al. [17]. in the setting of a diffuse “sausage-like” form of AIP, which resulted in splenomegaly, collateral pathways, and gastric varices. Splenic vein obstruction is reported in approximately 7%-20% of AIP cases [18], with 50%-75% of these instances complicated by gastric varices [19], as it was the case in our patient, who furthermore experienced an acute episode of gastrointestinal bleeding due to endoscopically-proven ruptured gastric varices. While a few isolated reports have documented variceal bleeding in diffuse or Type 1 AIP, our case presents a unique clinical scenario as the first documented instance of this hemorrhagic event occurring secondary to SV obstruction in a patient with Type 2 AIP. Despite its rarity, clinicians must remain vigilant regarding portal hypertension as a potential complication of AIP, actively screening for suggestive imaging signs to prevent such severe hemorrhagic events.

Both type 1 and type 2 AIP respond to steroids, with significant symptom relief and resolution of imaging findings typically observed within 2-3 weeks of initiating treatment. The standard regimen involves prednisone at a dose of 40 mg daily for 4 weeks, followed by a weekly taper of 5 mg. Still, some patients may require prolonged treatment with low- dose steroids (2.5-5 mg oral prednisone) [20]. In contrast to type 1 AIP, in which relapse is relatively common (30%-50%), disease relapse is uncommon in type 2 AIP (10%); therefore, maintenance therapy is unnecessary in most patients [12]. In cases of steroid failure, intolerance, or recurrent relapse, immunomodulatory agents such as rituximab, as well as surgical treatment, may be considered. It usually consists of pancreaticoduodenectomy or distal pancreatectomy, depending on pancreatic involvement, with some reports of total pancreatectomy in refractory diffuse AIP [8,21].

Conclusion

In conclusion, multifocal autoimmune pancreatitis represents a diagnostic challenge due to its overlapping imaging features with pancreatic malignancies, particularly in type 2 AIP where serum IgG4 levels are normal and extrapancreatic manifestations are absent. This case underscores the value of advanced imaging modalities, such as CT and MRI, in identifying suggestive patterns like delayed enhancement and lack of restricted diffusion, alongside histopathological confirmation to differentiate AIP from cancer. The occurrence of segmental portal hypertension secondary to splenic vein encasement is a rare complication of both types of AIP and highlights the potential for vascular complications. A multidisciplinary approach integrating clinical, radiological, serological, and histological data is essential for accurate diagnosis and timely initiation of steroids, which offer excellent response rates and low relapse rates in type 2 AIP. Future research should focus on refining diagnostic criteria for atypical presentations to minimize invasive procedures and improve outcomes in this underrecognized entity.
