Environmental and genetic factors influencing the spatial differentiation of wild Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa quality in the eastern Foothills of the Taihang Mountains
Authors: Hancong Li, Minghui Fang, Chunhe You, Yunsheng Zhao, Changqi Ai, Yaning Biao, Yanmei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZiziphus jujuba var. spinosa (Z. jujuba), an important medicinal and edible plant, is well-known for its sedative properties. However, the factors influencing spatial variation in Z. jujuba quality, as well as the relative contributions of environmental and genetic factors, remain poorly understood. In this study, the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains were selected as the study area. By integrating phenotypic and chemical analyses, SCoT molecular markers, and mixed-effect modeling, we systematically investigated the spatial differentiation of Z. jujuba quality and its influencing factors. The results showed that: (1) Z. jujuba quality exhibits pronounced spatial heterogeneity. Phenotypic traits varied significantly among production regions, with coefficients of variation ranging from 2.52% to 21.46%, and generally displayed a spatial pattern of “high in the north and low in the south.” Among the chemical constituents, spinosin, 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin, and jujuboside A showed a distribution pattern characterized by “high in the central and southern regions and low in the northern region,” whereas jujuboside B decreased from south to north. (2) The percentage of polymorphic loci reached 79.43%, whereas overall genetic diversity remained at a moderate level. Genetic variation mainly originated from within populations (92.00%), indicating a genetic structure characterized by high gene flow and low differentiation. (3) The environmental factors explained a larger proportion of quality variation than genetic factors, with environmental and genetic factors accounting for 85.91% and 5.12%, respectively. Elevation and temperature were identified as the primary factors affecting leaf area, leaf nitrogen content, and jujuboside B, whereas kernel yield, single-seed weight, leaf water content, and 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin were jointly associated with elevation, temperature, precipitation, soil factors, and genetic background. We established an association framework integrating environment, gene, and quality based on these findings, revealing a macro-level pattern of quality formation characterized by a greater contribution of environmental gradients, responsiveness of genetic background, and their combined interaction. Therefore, this study provides a scientific basis for understanding the geo-authentic formation processes and for the conservation and sustainable utilization of Z. jujuba resources in the Taihang Mountains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09280-5.Keywords: Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa, Phenotypic traits, Chemical composition, Genetic diversity, Geographical environment

Introduction

Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa (hereafter referred to as Z. jujuba), a member of the Rhamnaceae family, is native to China [1]. It commonly grows in low mountainous and hilly regions and is well adapted to drought and cold conditions [2]. The seeds of Z. jujuba are widely used in traditional Chinese medicine because of their calming and sedative effects, particularly in the treatment of insomnia. In recent years, its marked demand and economic value have increased continuously [3]. The Taihang Mountains are recognized as one of the major production areas of authentic medicinal Z. jujuba. Owing to the region’s complex geographical environment and genetic background, substantial phenotypic and chemical variation has been observed among populations. However, it remains unclear whether the spatial differentiation of Z. jujuba quality is primarily influenced by genetic differentiation, environmental selection, or the combined effects of both factors. Moreover, the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors to quality differentiation have not yet been quantitatively evaluated. Clarifying the factors influencing the spatial differentiation of wild Z. jujuba kernel quality at the regional scale is therefore of great significance for germplasm resource conservation, geo-authenticity evaluation, and the development of traditional Chinese medicine resource science.

Z. jujuba possesses both medicinal and edible value [4]. Its quality evaluation mainly involves phenotypic traits and chemical constituents. In terms of phenotypic characteristics, traits such as single-fruit weight, single-stone weight, single-kernel weight, double-kernel rate, kernel yield rate, leaf length, leaf width, and fresh leaf weight exhibit substantial geographical variations at the local scale [5–8]. Regarding chemical constituents, spinosin, 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin, jujuboside A, and jujuboside B are recognized as the major quality-control compounds [9–12]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the contents of medicinal compounds in Z. jujuba seeds from China are generally higher than those from neighboring countries such as Laos and Myanmar [13]. Significant differences have also been reported among different production regions within China [14–17]. For example, Z. jujuba produced in Xingtai, Hebei Province, contains relatively high levels of the four key chemical constituents [18–20], whereas Z. jujuba from Shandong and Henan Provinces is particularly rich in saponin compounds [17, 21]. However, existing studies still present several limitations. First, phenotypic analyses have mainly focused on single sampling sites and lack systematic integration of multiple organs, including fruits, kernels, leaves, and related traits. Second, studies on chemical composition exhibit substantial scale inconsistency. Local-scale studies are often limited by narrow sampling ranges, whereas large-scale studies frequently rely on commercially purchased samples with unclear geographical origins, making it difficult to accurately characterize spatial differentiation patterns. Third, despite the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains being recognized as a core production area for Dao-di Z. jujuba medicinal materials, detailed investigations into the spatial differentiation of quality traits in this region remain scarce and require further study.

The respective contributions of genetic and environmental factors to the quality formation of Z. jujuba remain insufficiently understood. Existing studies have largely examined genetic diversity and environmental responses independently, with limited integrative analyses addressing their combined effects. At the genetic level, wild Z. jujuba populations generally exhibit relatively high genetic diversity, particularly in northern China [22, 23]. Previous studies have shown that genetic variation predominantly originates from within-population variation [24]. However, contrasting patterns of population genetic structure have been reported among different regions. For example, populations in Shanxi Province exhibit high genetic diversity but weak gene flow and strong differentiation [25]. Populations distributed along the Yellow River Basin display moderate genetic diversity and differentiation accompanied by relatively strong gene flow [26]. Meanwhile, populations from the shell ridge area of Huanghua, Cangzhou, show low genetic diversity and weak gene flow [27]. Nevertheless, these studies did not directly investigate the relationships between genetic diversity and quality-related traits. At the environmental level, the phenotype traits of Z. jujuba are influenced by longitude, elevation, altitude, precipitation, temperature, and soil properties, including moisture, pH, and sand content [28–31]. Its chemical composition has also been shown to be closely associated with soil texture, sand content, and soil organic carbon levels [14]. Although preliminary studies have explored the relationships between environmental factors and phenotypic or chemical traits, the interactions among quality characteristics and genetic background have not yet been systematically examined. Collectively, two major knowledge gaps remain in current research. First, there is a lack of systematic investigation into the genetic diversity of Z. jujuba populations across continuous geographic regions. Second, studies addressing the associations among genetics, environment, and quality traits are still lacking, thereby limiting understanding of the factors influencing quality formation and the identification of key determinants affecting Z. jujuba.

Therefore, this study focuses on wild Z. jujuba populations distributed in the authentic production area along the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains, with the following objectives: (1) to systematically integrate phenotypic and chemical data from multiple tissues, including fruits, stones, kernels, and leaves, in order to characterize spatial variation patterns and comprehensively evaluate Z. jujuba quality across different production regions; (2) to reveal the spatial distribution patterns of population genetic diversity and environmental factors based on samples with precise geographic coordinates and associated environmental information; and (3) to clarify the correlation among genetics, environment, and quality traits, elucidating the key factors influencing Z. jujuba quality, and explore the potential patterns of quality formation. This study aims to assess the relative contributions and combined effects of environmental and genetic factors in shaping Z. jujuba quality, providing a theoretical basis for germplasm resource conservation and geo-authenticity research, as well as a representative case for advancing studies in traditional Chinese medicinal resources and ecological adaptation.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area was located along the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains (34°35′–40°19′ N, 110°15′–116°27′ E), a region characterized by a temperate continental monsoon climate. The terrain is predominantly composed of mountains and hills, with elevations generally decreasing from north to south and from west to east. The mean annual temperature ranges from 8 to 16 °C, and the mean annual precipitation is approximately 600 mm. The dominant soil types are cinnamon soils and brown soils. Natural vegetation in the region is mainly composed of wild Z. jujuba shrublands, accompanied by species such as Vitex negundo var. heterophylla and Lespedeza bicolor Turcz.

Sampling design

Field sampling was conducted in late August 2023. Ten sampling sites were located along a north-south transect across the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains. All sampling sites were located in low mountain and hilly regions dominated by wild Z. jujuba shrub communities and were selected to ensure broad spatial coverage of the entire foothill area. At each site, ten healthy adult individuals without visible signs of pests or diseases were randomly selected as biological replicates. To ensure spatial independence, the distance between sampled individuals exceeded 100 m. All samples were collected from non-protected and non-restricted areas in accordance with relevant local and national regulations. Basic information for the sampling sites is presented in Table 1.

Sample collection and processing

From each sampled individual, ten mature fruits and ten mature leaves were collected from the east, south, west, and north orientations of the canopy. In addition, young leaves were collected for DNA extraction and genetic analyses. For each individual, ten fruits and ten mature leaves were randomly selected, sealed in sample bags, placed in portable refrigerated containers, and transported to the laboratory on the day of collection. At each sampling site, undisturbed soil cores (0–20 cm depth) and 500 g composite soil samples were collected using a ring knife for the determination of soil physicochemical properties. Young leaf samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen after collection and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. For all measured traits, individual-level values were calculated as the arithmetic mean of ten replicate measurements. The plant voucher specimens (numbered Z.jujuba-2023-001 to Z.jujuba-2023-100) have been stored in the laboratory of the School of Pharmacy, Hebei University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, and the soil samples (numbered Soil-2023-001 to Soil-2023-100) have been stored in the Soil Laboratory of the School of Geographic Sciences, Hebei Normal University for long-term reference.

Measurement of phenotypic traits

All plant materials were identified as wild Z. jujuba by Prof. Yunsheng Zhao. Fresh fruit weight (SFW) and fresh leaf weight (LFW) were measured using an electronic balance with a precision of 0.001 g, whereas fruit horizontal diameter (FHD) and fruit vertical diameter (FVD) were measured using a digital caliper with a precision of 0.01 mm. Samples were dried at 60 °C for 72 h to determine fruit dry weight (SFDW), from which fruit shape index (FSI) and fruit water content (FWC) were calculated. Fruit flesh and stones were manually separated. Single-stone weight (SSW), stone horizontal diameter (SHD), and stone vertical diameter (SVD) were subsequently measured, and stone shape index (SSI), edible rate (ER), and stone yield rate (SR) were calculated. The stones were then manually cracked, and the kernels were removed for the determination of kernel horizontal diameter (KHD), kernel vertical diameter (KVD), and single kernel weight (SKW), from which kernel shape index (KSI) and kernel yield rate (KR) were calculated.

Fresh leaves were weighed and subsequently dried at 60 °C for 72 h to determine leaf dry weight (LDW), from which leaf water content (LWC) was calculated. Leaves were scanned and analyzed using ImageJ software to measure single-leaf length (SLL), single-leaf width (SLW), and leaf area (LA). Leaf shape index (LSI) and specific leaf area (SLA) were subsequently calculated. After drying and grinding through a 0.15 mm sieve, leaf carbon (LCC) and leaf nitrogen contents (LNC) were measured using an elemental analyzer, and the leaf carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (LC/N) was calculated. Detailed calculation formulas are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Determination of chemical constituents

Quantitative analyses were conducted using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS) system. Reference standards of spinosin, 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin, jujuboside A, and jujuboside B were purchased from Chengdu Push Bio-technology Co., Ltd., all with purities ≥ 98.0%. Standard solutions were prepared by serial dilution in methanol to generate seven concentration gradients ranging from 25 to 5000 ng/mL.

Z. jujuba samples were dried at 60 °C, after which kernels were separated and ground through a 40-mesh sieve. Approximately 100 mg of kernel powder was accurately weighed and extracted with 70% ethanol using ultrasonic extraction for 90 min (600 W, 40 kHz). After cooling, the extract volume was restored to the original volume with 70% ethanol and subsequently filtered. A 500 µL aliquot of filtrate was mixed with 1.5 mL ultrapure water and vortexed thoroughly. The solution was then filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter. Subsequently, 200 µL of the filtrate was mixed with 800 µL of methanol to obtain the final sample solution for analysis. Detailed chromatographic conditions, together with method validation parameters including linearity, precision, repeatability, stability, and recovery rates, are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

DNA extraction and genetic diversity analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissues using a CTAB Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (DN–14, Aidlab, Beijing, China). DNA quality and concentration were evaluated by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop One spectrophotometry. DNA concentrations ranged from 64.10 to 694.43 ng/µL, with A260/A280 ratios between 1.80 and 1.95, indicating that all samples met the quality requirements for subsequent analyses.

A total of 100 previously published polymorphic SCoT primers for Z. jujuba were selected as candidate markers [32]. DNA samples from five representative populations (BJ, SH, LC, CX, and JY) were randomly selected for primer screening. Ultimately, ten primers exhibiting clear banding patterns, high polymorphism, stable amplification across three independent replicates, and no tailing effects were selected for subsequent analyses (Table S2). PCR amplification was performed in a 20 µL reaction system containing 1 µL DNA template, 8 µL ddH₂O, 10 µL 2×Taq Plus Mix, and 1 µL primer. The amplification program consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 4 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50–64 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 90 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplified products were subsequently stored at 4 °C. PCR products were separated by 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized using a gel imaging system. Bands were scored according to their presence or absence at identical migration positions, with presence recorded as “1” and absence as “0”.

Determination of soil physicochemical properties

Soil bulk density (BD), total porosity (TP), water content (WC), and saturated water capacity (SWC) were measured using the ring knife drying method [33]. Soil particle-size distribution was measured using a Malvern Mastersizer 3000 laser diffraction particle-size analyzer. Soil total nitrogen (STN) was determined using the Kjeldahl method, soil organic carbon (SOC) using the potassium dichromate external heating method, and soil total phosphorus (STP) using the sodium hydroxide fusion molybdenum-antimony colorimetric method. Soil pH was measured using the electrode potential method, whereas electrical conductivity (EC) was determined using a conductivity meter [34].

Data analysis method

Experimental data were organized and processed using Microsoft Excel 2019. Total phenotypic variation for each trait was partitioned into inter-regional and intra-regional components using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The coefficients of within-region and among-region variation were subsequently calculated. Effect size (η²) was used to quantify the proportion of total trait variation explained by production region. All statistical analyses were conducted using the aov function in R 4.5.2.

A binary 0/1 matrix was constructed based on SCoT molecular marker data. Genetic diversity indices, including the observed number of alleles (Na), effective number of alleles (Ne), Nei’s gene diversity index (H), Shannon’s information index (I), number of polymorphic loci (NPL), and percentage of polymorphic loci (PPL), were calculated using PopGene 32 software. Genetic identity and genetic distance among populations were estimated, and an unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) dendrogram was constructed based on Nei’s genetic distance.

The chemical compositions of Z. jujuba were comprehensively evaluated using grey relational analysis. Hierarchical cluster analysis was subsequently performed to classify quality-related indices of wild Z. jujuba and identify representative variables. The selected indicators were subjected to the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test (KMO > 0.6) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (P < 0.05). Principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted. A comprehensive quality evaluation model was then established using the ratio of the loading value of each indicator to the square root of the corresponding eigenvalue as the coefficient and the variance contribution rate as the weighting factor.

Spearman correlation analysis was performed using IBM SPSS v26.0. Linear mixed-effects models were applied to investigate the factors influencing quality variation, using the lme4 package in R 4.5.2. Fixed effects included environmental variables retained after multicollinearity screening based on the variance inflation factor (VIF < 5), together with the first principal component that originated from genetic diversity indices through principal component analysis. Production region was included as a random intercept effect. For each core quality indicator, three models were fitted separately: a full model, an environment-only model, and a genetics-only model. Model parameters were estimated using the maximum likelihood method. Marginal R² and conditional R² values were calculated using the MuMIn package. The marginal R² of the full model was further partitioned into unique environmental marginal R², unique genetic marginal R², and shared marginal R² components to quantitatively evaluate the independent and joint contributions of environmental and genetic factors. Figures were generated using the ChiPlot online platform (https://www.chiplot.online), iTOL, and Origin 2021.

Results

Spatial variation and comprehensive evaluation of Z. jujuba quality in the eastern Foothills of the Taihang Mountains

Spatial variation in phenotypic diversity of Z. jujuba

Statistical analysis of 25 phenotypic traits of Z. jujuba collected from the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains revealed coefficients of variation ranging from 5.96% to 34.35%, with KR exhibiting the highest variation and ER the lowest. Trait variability differed markedly among organs, following the order: kernel (19.27%) > leaf (16.52%) > stone (14.94%) > fruit (13.29%). These results indicate that kernel-related traits exhibited the highest degree of phenotypic divergence (Table S3).

Because the above estimates of variation were obtained from observations at the individual-plant level, total variation was further partitioned into inter-regional and intra-regional components. This clarifies the sources of phenotypic divergence. The coefficients of variation within and among production regions ranged from 4.96% to 31.64% and from 2.52% to 21.46%, respectively (Table 2). Variance decomposition further showed that traits including FHD, SFW, FWC, ER, SKW, and LWC had η² values > 0.29 (P < 0.001), indicating that their variation was predominantly attributable to differences among production regions. In contrast, FSI, SHD, SSW, KHD, LSI, and LCC showed no significant inter-regional differences (P > 0.05). Moreover, their intra-regional coefficients of variation exceeded the corresponding inter-regional values, suggesting that these traits were primarily influenced by variation among individuals within regions (Table 2).

Variations in the major chemical constituents of Z. jujuba kernels

The contents of spinosin, 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin, jujuboside A, and jujuboside B in kernels of Z. jujuba collected from different production regions in the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains were determined. The concentrations of these compounds ranged from 1.31 to 4.52 mg/g, 0.68 to 2.34 mg/g, 0.56 to 1.85 mg/g, and 0.32 to 1.01 mg/g, respectively. Among these four compounds, spinosin exhibited significantly higher concentrations than the other three constituents. (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2a). Spatially, spinosin, 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin, and Jujuboside A exhibited a distribution pattern characterized by higher concentrations in the central–southern regions and lower concentrations in the central–northern regions, whereas jujuboside B showed a decreasing trend from south to north. Specifically, the highest contents of spinosin, 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin, and jujuboside A were detected in Linzhou, located in the central-southern part of the study area, and were significantly higher than those in other production regions (P < 0.05). In contrast, the lowest levels of these three compounds were observed in Yuanshi, located in the central-northern region. Jiyuan, situated at the southernmost margin of the study area, exhibited the highest jujuboside B content, which was significantly higher than that of all other regions except Linzhou (P < 0.05).

To further validate these spatial patterns and quantitatively classify producing-region groups, hierarchical clustering analysis was performed based on the contents of the four chemical constituents. The ten production regions were grouped into two major clusters: one comprised Jiyuan, Jiaozuo, Yixian, Lingshou, Lincheng, and Yuanshi, whereas the other included Linzhou, Cixian, Shahe, and Beijing (Fig. 2b). To evaluate the stability of chemical composition within the study area, coefficients of variation were calculated for the four compounds. The coefficients of variation for spinosin, 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin, jujuboside A, and jujuboside B were 3.79%, 4.08%, 3.91%, and 3.05%, respectively, indicating relatively stable concentrations of these compounds in Z. jujuba kernels across the study region. Comprehensive evaluation based on grey relational analysis showed that Linzhou exhibited the highest overall quality, whereas Lingshou ranked the lowest (Table S4).

Comprehensive evaluation of Z. jujuba quality in the Taihang Mountains

In this study, a total of 29 phenotype and chemical indicators were included to comprehensively evaluate the quality of wild Z. jujuba in the Taihang Mountains. A “clustering-based dimensionality reduction combined with PCA weighting” strategy was adopted for the comprehensive quality assessment. In order to minimize bias caused by high intercorrelation among variables, hierarchical clustering analysis was first performed to classify the 29 indicators into ten groups (Fig. 2c). The most representative variables within each cluster were LC/N, LNC, LWC, SHD, SVD, jujuboside B, 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin, SR, SKW, and LA, respectively. Principal component analysis (PCA) was subsequently applied for objective weighting, and five principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted (Table S5). Principal component 1 (PC1), primarily represented by leaf carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, leaf nitrogen content, and leaf water content, explained 25.17% of the total variance. PC2 was mainly associated with stone horizontal diameter and stone vertical diameter, and explained 16.05% of the variance. PC3 was dominated by jujuboside B and 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin (15.12%), PC4 by kernel yield rate and single-kernel weight (12.01%), and PC5 by leaf area (10.60%). Principal component equations were constructed based on loading coefficients and the square roots of eigenvalues, and a comprehensive evaluation model was subsequently established using the entropy-weighting method. The resulting rankings indicated that Linzhou exhibited the highest overall quality, whereas Lincheng ranked the lowest (Table S4).

Genetic and geographical variation in Z. jujuba from the eastern Foothills of the Taihang Mountains

Genetic diversity variation

Genetic diversity analysis of Z. jujuba populations from the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains revealed that a total of 159 loci were amplified using 10 SCoT primers. The average number of polymorphic loci (NPL) was 126.30, with an average percentage of polymorphic loci (PPL) of 79.43%. This indicates substantial genetic polymorphism within the populations. Among all populations, the highest PPL value was observed in Lingshou (Table 3). Despite the relatively high level of polymorphism, the overall genetic diversity remained at a moderate-to-low level. The observed number of alleles (Na = 1.79), effective number of alleles (Ne = 1.37), Nei’s gene diversity index (H = 0.23), and Shannon’s information index (I = 0.36) were all comparatively low (Table 3).

Geographic and environmental heterogeneity

Factors influencing quality variation in Z. jujuba

In order to investigate the effects of geographical environmental and genetic factors on Z. jujuba quality, while minimizing analytical redundancy caused by strong correlations among traits, we selected 10 representative indicators from the previous comprehensive evaluation (Sect. Comprehensive evaluation of Z. jujuba quality in the Taihang Mountains) as response variables. Linear mixed-effects models were subsequently applied to partition variance, quantify the relative contributions of environmental and genetic factors, and identify the key determinants influencing quality variation.

The results demonstrated that, for all ten core quality indicators, the net environmental explanatory rate (R²_env) exceeded the net genetic explanatory rate (R²_gen). The relative environmental contribution (Env %) ranged from 54.96% to 98.59% (mean = 85.91%), whereas the relative genetic contribution (Gen %) ranged from 0.04% to 13.21% (mean = 5.12%). These findings indicate that environmental factors played a major role in quality variation in Z. jujuba, whereas the independent contribution of genetic diversity was comparatively limited (Table 5). Furthermore, traits including SKW, LA, LNC, LWC, 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin, and jujuboside B exhibited evident environment-genotype combined effects, with combined explanatory rates exceeding the corresponding net genetic contributions. These results suggest that variation in these traits depends not only on environmental conditions but also on associations between environmental and genetic factors (Table 5). Random effects explained only a small proportion of variation in SVD (R²_site = 0.0198), LWC (0.0551), 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin (0.0854), and jujuboside B (0.0255), whereas the regional variance components for the remaining traits were effectively zero. This indicates that, after accounting for fixed environmental and genetic effects, residual heterogeneity among production regions was negligible for most traits (Table 5).

Discussion

Spatial patterns of quality variation and environmental factors of Z. jujuba in the eastern Foothills of the Taihang Mountains

The coefficient of variation is an important indicator for evaluating plant responses to environmental heterogeneity [36, 37]. In the present study, phenotypic traits of Z. jujuba from the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains exhibited substantial overall variation, with kernel-related traits, particularly KR and SKW, showing the highest degree of divergence, consistent with previous reports [6, 30]. Because kernel traits are directly associated with reproductive success and offspring establishment, plants inhabiting environmentally heterogeneous regions may optimize local resource allocation by adjusting seed size and mass, consistent with the “risk diversification” strategy [38]. Traits such as single-fruit weight, single-kernel weight, and leaf water content displayed a clear decreasing trend from north to south, with the highest values concentrated in the northernmost regions. This pattern corresponded closely with the increasing drought-stress gradient from south to north across the study area and likely reflects a resource-allocation strategy jointly influenced by water availability and temperature. Lower temperatures and reduced precipitation in northern regions may decrease respiratory consumption and prolong the growing period, thereby favoring the accumulation of photosynthates in seeds. These findings are consistent with previously reported global patterns of seed-mass variation [39] and the leaf economics spectrum theory [40].

The spatial distribution of chemical constituents exhibited a pattern characterized by “high concentrations in the central-southern regions and low concentrations in the central-northern regions”, which appears to be associated with the combined effects of soil and climate. The concentrations of the four major chemical constituents in Z. jujuba kernels from the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains were significantly higher than those previously reported for populations in Shanxi Province [14], while simultaneously exhibiting extremely low variability (CV < 5%). This combination of “high concentration and high stability” is consistent with the findings of He et al. [41] and supports the traditional Dao-Di medicinal concept described in the Xin Xiu Ben Cao: “when removed from its native soil, the material may remain morphologically similar but differ in efficacy” [42]. From a spatial perspective, the central-southern regions are primarily characterized by parent materials that originated primarily from weathered gneiss and limestone [43]. The resulting sandy soils provide favorable hydrothermal and aeration conditions that are highly suitable for the growth of Z. jujuba [41]. In addition, these soils are relatively low in total phosphorus, and nutrient limitation may stimulate secondary metabolism [41], a mechanism previously demonstrated in medicinal plants such as Salvia miltiorrhiza [44] and Artemisia annua [45]. Climatically, the central-southern regions are characterized by moderate annual temperatures and relatively large diurnal temperature ranges. Such conditions may upregulate the expression of flavonoid biosynthesis-related genes, including PAL and CHS [46], promoting the accumulation of secondary metabolites [47]. The combined influence of soil and climate likely contributes to the formation of stable, high-concentration zones of key chemical constituents in the central-southern Taihang Mountains, which may represent one of the ecological foundations underlying the formation of geo-authentic medicinal production areas of Z. jujuba.

Genetic diversity characteristics and differentiation patterns of Z. jujuba in the eastern Foothills of the Taihang Mountains

The present study revealed that Z. jujuba populations in the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains exhibit a distinctive genetic pattern characterized by the coexistence of high genetic variation and relatively low genetic diversity. Specifically, the percentage of PPL was relatively high (79.43%), indicating substantial allelic variation and long-term historical accumulation of genetic variation. However, indices reflecting allelic richness and evenness, including Na (1.79), Ne (1.37), H (0.23), and I (0.36), were comparatively low, suggesting that the present populations possess a relatively single allelic composition. This pattern may reflect an evolutionary history involving post-bottleneck population expansion that was insufficient to fully restore rare alleles. The Taihang Mountains constitute an important hotspot for studies of temperate plant glacial refugia in northern China [48]. Long-term habitat stability within refugial areas may therefore have enabled ancestral populations of Z. jujuba to maintain relatively large effective population sizes, thereby facilitating the accumulation of abundant genetic variation [49, 50]. Nevertheless, climatic oscillations associated with glacial-interglacial cycles may have repeatedly driven population contractions and subsequent expansions. Recurrent bottleneck events could consequently have resulted in the loss of rare alleles [51]. In addition, localized selective pressures under environmentally heterogeneous habitats may have promoted the enrichment of specific alleles while eliminating others, further reducing overall genetic diversity [52]. The genetic diversity indices observed in this study were lower than those previously reported for closely related Ziziphus species distributed in Iran [53], potentially owing to the broader transcontinental distribution and larger population sizes of those taxa. Similarly, the genetic diversity detected here was lower than that reported for Z. jujuba populations from Inner Mongolia, Shandong, Shaanxi, and Aral [7], likely reflecting the combined influences of sampling scale, climatic habitat differences, and anthropogenic disturbance.

The studied populations also exhibited a genetic structure characterized by high gene flow, low differentiation, and predominance of within-population variation, resulting from the combined influences of biological traits and regional environmental conditions. Z. jujuba is highly tolerant of poor soils and drought stress, and its fruits readily root after dispersal, conferring strong dispersal capability. Moreover, the study region is geographically continuous and lacks strong physical barriers, conditions that facilitate extensive gene exchange among populations. Strong gene flow (Nm = 5.89) likely continuously homogenized genetic composition among populations, maintaining high genetic similarity (0.96–0.98) and low genetic differentiation (Gst = 0.08). In general, when Nm > 1, gene flow is considered sufficient to counteract the differentiating effects of genetic drift [54], which may explain why 92.00% of the total genetic variation was distributed among individuals within populations. These findings are consistent with previous studies on Z. jujuba populations in Shanxi Province [26] and central China [55]. The even higher levels of gene flow reported for populations distributed along the Yellow River Basin [23] may be attributable to long-distance dispersal facilitated by river systems [56]. Although UPGMA clustering divided the populations into two major branches, implying potential influences of environmental or historical factors, the overall genetic structure remained predominantly shaped by homogenization resulting from extensive gene flow. Collectively, the Z. jujuba populations distributed in the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains can therefore be regarded as a genetically cohesive population system.

Factors influencing quality variation in Z. jujuba from the Taihang Mountains

In the present study, variance partitioning based on linear mixed-effects models was applied to decompose total variation into environmental fixed effects, genetic fixed effects, environment-genotype combined effects, and random effects associated with production regions, thereby quantitatively evaluating the net explanatory power of each contributing factor. Under the current sampling scale and based on the selected SCoT molecular markers, the net environmental explanatory rate (54.96%-98.59%) was substantially greater than the net genetic explanatory rate (0.04%-13.21%), indicating that environmental conditions constitute the main determinants of quality variation in Z. jujuba. This finding is consistent with the traditional concept that the efficacy of geo-authentic medicinal materials is inseparable from the geographic environments of their production regions [57].

The present study further suggests that Z. jujuba quality is jointly associated with hydrothermal conditions and soil properties. Latitude, elevation, and lithology collectively shape hydrothermal regimes and soil environments, while variation in hydrothermal conditions influences phenotypic differentiation in plants [29, 58], and soil microenvironments influence the accumulation of chemical constituents in Z. jujuba [59–61]. Previous studies have shown that environmental factors can explain 56.70% of fruit-trait variation in Z. jujuba populations from the Taihang Mountains [30], with variance components among different provenances accounting for as much as 69.86%, and multiple traits exhibiting significant correlations with environmental variables [60]. He et al. [41] further demonstrated that hydrothermal conditions and soil properties contribute substantially to variation in the chemical constituents of Z. jujuba kernels, with moderate drought and low-nutrient conditions being particularly favorable for the accumulation of bioactive compounds. In addition, the “climate-dominant” theory of geo-authentic medicinal materials [62], together with studies demonstrating enhanced secondary metabolism in Ziziphus species under drought stress [63, 64], further supports the major role of environmental factors.

Although the independent contribution of genetic factors was comparatively limited, genetic effects nonetheless exerted non-negligible influences on traits such as SKW, LWC, and 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin content. Six traits, SKW, LA, LNC, LWC, 6’’’-feruloyl spinosin, and jujuboside B, exhibited significant environment-genotype combined effects, with the explanatory rates of these combined effects exceeding the corresponding net genetic explanatory rates in all cases. These findings suggest that the genetic influence on these traits is largely dependent on environmental context, reflecting substantial genotype-environment associations. Such associations are widely recognized as important factors influencing the formation of geo-authentic medicinal quality [62, 65] and the variation of secondary metabolism in medicinal plants [66, 67]. However, it should be noted that our interpretations are constrained by the current sampling scale and the use of SCoT markers, and further studies with broader sampling and higher-resolution markers are needed to validate these patterns.

Collectively, the quality variation of Z. jujuba in the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains can be characterized by a hierarchical pattern in which environmental gradients contribute more than genetic background, genetic background shows detectable responsiveness, and combined effects of the two are observed. The formation of Dao-Di quality, therefore, appears to result from the long-term association between specific hydrothermal-soil combinations and the underlying genetic background under persistent environmental selection pressure. These findings provide a theoretical basis for understanding the ecological and evolutionary processes of Dao-di formation in Z. jujuba from the Taihang Mountains.

Conclusion

This study revealed systematic spatial differentiation in the quality-related traits of Z. jujuba along environmental gradients in the eastern foothills of the Taihang Mountains. Phenotypic traits associated with growth and reproduction were more pronounced in northern populations, whereas key chemical constituents formed stable high-accumulation zones in the central and southern regions. These spatial patterns were largely explained by environmental gradients and accompanied by coordinated population responses under a genetic background characterized by high gene flow and low differentiation.

At a regional scale, this study is the first to establish a quantitative association framework for Z. jujuba combining environment, genotype, and quality. It enables the relative contributions and combined effects of environmental and genetic factors to be quantitatively evaluated. This framework provides a scientific basis for understanding the factors contributing to geo-authenticity in Z. jujuba. Nevertheless, several limitations remain. The sampling range was relatively concentrated, and only neutral molecular markers were employed. Therefore, the environment-genotype associations require further investigation through controlled experimental approaches. Future studies should integrate functional genomics with long-term in situ observations to further clarify the underlying patterns and their evolutionary origins.
