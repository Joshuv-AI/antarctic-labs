Clinical indications for dialysis: a study of patient demographics at Hargeisa Group Hospital, Somaliland
Authors: Naema Ibrahim, Abdiqani Abdikadir Mohamoud, Abdirahman Omer Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a significant global health concern, with kidney failure requiring dialysis or transplantation for survival. Data on clinical indications for dialysis initiation in resource-limited settings like Somaliland are scarce. This study aimed to identify the primary clinical indications for dialysis and their associated sociodemographic factors at Hargeisa Group Hospital. Unlike high-income settings where dialysis is often initiated based on laboratory markers found during routine monitoring, we hypothesized that indications in Somaliland would be primarily symptom-driven due to late-stage presentation and limited pre-dialysis care. Establishing this specific clinical profile is essential for tailoring local screening and intervention strategies.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study (adhering to STROBE guidelines) was conducted using medical records of 123 incident patients who initiated maintenance hemodialysis from May 2022 to August 2024. Descriptive statistics, along with Chi-square and logistic regression analyses, were used to assess the magnitude of indications and associated factors.ResultsUremic syndromes, particularly uremic gastritis (n = 54, 43.9%) and uremic encephalopathy (n = 36, 29.3%), were the most prevalent primary reasons for dialysis initiation. Maroodijeex accounted for the overwhelming majority of cases (92.7%). Significant demographic variations were observed; males showed a higher prevalence of uremic gastritis, while females predominantly presented with uremic encephalopathy (p < 0.05).ConclusionThis study highlights a high burden of late-stage CKD presentation (crash starters) in Somaliland. Addressing disparities in infrastructure and promoting equitable primary healthcare access to catch kidney impairment before uremic syndromes manifest is crucial for achieving better health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05077-8.

Introduction and background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a significant global health issue characterized by the progressive and irreversible loss of renal function. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines define CKD as an abnormality in kidney function or structure, typically assessed using the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and markers of kidney damage [1]. The final stage of CKD, known as kidney failure, involves a severe decline in function that necessitates permanent Kidney Replacement Therapy (KRT), such as maintenance dialysis or transplantation, for survival [2].

Epidemiological research on CKD in low-income regions faces primary challenges, particularly the scarcity of data on advanced CKD due to a reliance on registries that only track populations already undergoing KRT [3]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, CKD prevalence is estimated at approximately 13.9% [4]. In Somaliland, a region with a population of 3.5 to 4 million, healthcare facilities are nascent and highly centralized. This region faces a growing public health challenge as the prevalence of chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs) continues to rise amidst a health system still in recovery [5]. The infrastructure is currently limited by a severe shortage of specialized nephrologists and the absence of a national kidney disease registry [6]. Furthermore, hypertension—a primary driver of kidney failure—is highly prevalent in the Somali population, yet remains largely underdiagnosed and poorly managed due to limited primary care screening [7]. Without early detection and structured management, kidney function rapidly declines.

The threshold at which individuals experience symptomatic kidney dysfunction varies. Absolute indications for initiating dialysis encompass conditions such as uremic pericarditis, uremic encephalopathy, and bleeding diathesis, while common triggers include refractory metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, and fluid overload [8]. The rationale for focusing specifically on clinical indications in Somaliland is rooted in the region’s unique health system constraints and socioeconomic landscape. Unlike high-income settings where routine laboratory screenings allow for the “planned” initiation of dialysis, patients in Somaliland often navigate a fragmented healthcare system defined by significant out-of-pocket expenses and a lack of specialized services in rural areas [9]. Consequently, the clinical indication at the time of dialysis initiation serves as a critical proxy indicator of health system performance; it reveals the “diagnostic lag” between the onset of kidney failure and the first point of nephrological contact [10].

Furthermore, health-seeking behavior in the region is often reactive rather than preventive, influenced by the centralization of dialysis services in the capital, Hargeisa [11]. This fractured care cascade often leads to catastrophic outcomes, as seen in other medical disciplines in the region, where patients present to tertiary centers only when the disease has reached an advanced or life-threatening stage [12]. Understanding whether these patients present with manageable laboratory abnormalities or catastrophic systemic failures (such as uremic syndromes) is a public health necessity. Such data provide the empirical evidence required to justify the decentralization of nephrology services and the implementation of early screening programs within primary healthcare frameworks.

In resource-limited settings like Somaliland, particularly at Hargeisa Group Hospital (the primary public referral center in the capital), dialysis is crucial for managing kidney failure, as kidney transplantation is currently unavailable locally. Despite increasing demand for this life-saving intervention, data on the clinical conditions necessitating dialysis initiation in the region remain scarce. This study aims to identify the primary clinical indications for dialysis at Hargeisa Group Hospital, utilizing statistical modeling to uncover common demographic factors and highlight critical referral disparities.

Methods

This study utilized a retrospective cross-sectional design, adhering to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. The completed STROBE checklist is provided as a supplementary file. Data were collected from May 2022 to August 2024 at Hargeisa Group Hospital, Somaliland.

Study population and setting

Hargeisa Group Hospital is situated in the Maroodijeex region (the capital region of Somaliland) and serves as the highest-level tertiary public hospital providing hemodialysis. Because peripheral regions (such as Awdal, Saaxil, Sanaag, and Togdheer) have limited to no public dialysis infrastructure, patients are frequently referred to this central facility.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study evaluated a sample of 123 patients. Patients were included if they were: (1) Incident patients newly diagnosed with chronic kidney failure (ascertained clinically by an attending physician/nephrologist via eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m² alongside chronic sonographic kidney changes), (2) Initiated on regular maintenance hemodialysis (defined as surviving and receiving 2–3 sessions per week for at least 3 months), and (3) Over 18 years old. Patients were excluded if they had acute kidney injury (AKI), were initiated on dialysis in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) for acute physiological decompensation, had incomplete medical records, or had a failed kidney transplant. The focus on the adult population (≥ 18 years) was chosen because adult kidney failure at our center is predominantly driven by different etiologies (such as hypertension and diabetes) compared to pediatric cases, which often involve congenital anomalies. Furthermore, the adult hemodialysis unit at Hargeisa Group Hospital follows clinical protocols specifically calibrated for adult physiology, making them a distinct clinical cohort.

Data collection

Data were extracted retrospectively from physical and electronic medical records by the primary authors (NI and AA). A standardized form captured demographic details (age, sex, region) and the primary clinical indication for dialysis. During the data extraction process, 14 records were initially identified but excluded due to incompleteness. The most common missing variables were the specific clinical trigger for dialysis (the primary indication) or the exact date of maintenance initiation. We opted for a complete-case analysis approach rather than statistical imputation because the primary objective of this study was to report verified, physician-documented clinical triggers. Imputing a clinical indication (e.g., guessing a patient had uremic gastritis based on other labs) would compromise the descriptive integrity of the study and potentially misrepresent the physicians’ decision-making process. We acknowledge that this may introduce a slight selection bias toward patients whose records were more meticulously maintained by clinical staff.

To ensure diagnostic consistency, the primary indication was defined as the most acute, life-threatening clinical state that served as the immediate trigger for dialysis initiation, as documented by the attending nephrologist. In cases where multiple symptoms co-existed, the physician’s primary diagnosis in the admission note was used. For example, Uremic gastritis was defined clinically by severe, refractory nausea, vomiting, and dyspepsia directly attributed to severe uremia in the absence of other primary gastrointestinal pathologies.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted by the corresponding author (AOA) using R statistical software. Descriptive statistics summarize the magnitude of indications as absolute numbers (n) and percentages (%). Bivariate analysis utilizing Chi-square tests was performed to establish associations between the primary indications and sociodemographic factors (Sex and Age Group). Multinomial and binary logistic regression models were utilized to identify the predictive strength of age and sex on the likelihood of presenting with specific uremic syndromes.

Ethical considerations.

The study received ethical approval from the ethics committee at Hargeisa Group Hospital (HRH-160/2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles established in the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient confidentiality was maintained throughout the study, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Results

Demographic characteristics and geographic distribution

A total of 123 incident dialysis cases were analyzed, comprising 73 males (59.3%) and 50 females (40.7%). Dialysis initiation cases were distributed across a broad range of ages, heavily concentrated in the 45 to 74 age group. Regional distribution analysis highlighted stark geographic variations: the Maroodijeex region accounted for the vast majority of cases at 92.7% (n = 114). Conversely, referrals from Awdal, Saaxil, and Sanaag accounted for only 0.8% (n = 1) each, with Togdheer contributing 4.9% (n = 6) (Table 1).

Magnitude of clinical indications for dialysis

Advanced uremic syndromes were the dominant drivers for Kidney Replacement Therapy (KRT) initiation (Fig. 1). Uremic gastritis accounted for the largest proportion at 43.9% (n = 54) of all cases, followed by uremic encephalopathy at 29.3% (n = 36) and pulmonary edema at 18.7% (n = 23). Laboratory-driven indications such as refractory hyperkalemia (4.9%, n = 6) and metabolic acidosis (2.4%, n = 3) were rarely the primary documented triggers.

Factors associated with indications

Bivariate analysis revealed significant demographic variations in clinical presentation (Table 2). Chi-square tests indicated a statistically significant association between patient sex and the primary indication for dialysis (p < 0.05). Males exhibited a substantially higher prevalence of uremic gastritis (50.7% of all males vs. 34.0% of all females). Conversely, females predominantly presented with uremic encephalopathy (44.0% of all females vs. 19.2% of all males) (Fig. 2).

Regression analysis: predictors of uremic encephalopathy and other syndromes

To further evaluate risk factors for specific clinical presentations, regression modeling was performed. A binary logistic regression model assessing the likelihood of presenting with uremic encephalopathy (versus all other indications) confirmed that female sex was a significant independent predictor (Table 3). Females had more than twice the odds of presenting with uremic encephalopathy compared to males (OR: 2.34, 95% CI: 1.12–4.89, p = 0.02), controlling for age. Additionally, multinomial regression analyses using uremic gastritis as the baseline reference similarly demonstrated the predictive influence of gender and age on symptom manifestation at the time of dialysis initiation across various indications (Table 4).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive evidence detailing the clinical indications for initiating maintenance hemodialysis at Hargeisa Group Hospital in Somaliland. Assessing the magnitude of clinical presentations revealed an overwhelming burden of end-stage uremic syndromes, particularly uremic gastritis and uremic encephalopathy. The primary contribution of this research lies in empirically documenting how patients in this resource-limited setting almost exclusively present to the healthcare system at critical, life-threatening stages of kidney failure. The vast majority of these patients were crash starters who lacked prior nephrology follow-up or CKD awareness.

The findings of this study stand in stark contrast to clinical profiles documented in developed healthcare systems. Research from the United States and Europe indicates that metabolic acidosis, refractory hyperkalemia, and unmanageable fluid overload are the predominant, earlier-detected triggers for initiating Kidney Replacement Therapy (KRT) [13]. In contrast, our study recorded metabolic acidosis and hyperkalemia as the absolute lowest indications for dialysis. This discrepancy is likely not due to biological differences in disease progression, but rather the systemic lack of early CKD surveillance and the diagnostic lag inherent in the local health system. While patients in high-income countries are often transitioned to dialysis based on laboratory trends monitored over months, our cohort appears to bypass this window, presenting only when uremic toxins reach levels high enough to cause neurological or gastrointestinal failure [10, 13].

The dominance of patients from the Maroodijeex region (92.7%) strongly reflects geographic accessibility and referral disparities rather than a true regional prevalence of kidney disease. Because peripheral regions lack functional public dialysis infrastructure, patients living outside the capital face immense barriers to care, limiting the cohort primarily to those living in the immediate vicinity of the tertiary center. This concentration of care suggests that a significant portion of the rural and nomadic population may be dying from kidney failure without ever reaching specialized services—a phenomenon that mirrors the fractured care cascade documented in other medical emergencies within the region [12].

Furthermore, our analysis revealed pronounced gendered differences in symptom presentation: males primarily suffered from uremic gastritis, while females experienced higher rates of uremic encephalopathy. While the physiological basis for this remains unclear, it may suggest differences in health-seeking behavior or symptom reporting between sexes. In many low-resource contexts, systemic symptoms of uremia may be misinterpreted or ignored until they manifest as severe cognitive impairment or intractable gastrointestinal distress [14].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and reliance on medical record data may have introduced biases due to potential inaccuracies or missing historical information regarding prior primary care access. Second, the exclusion of 14 records with incomplete data points might introduce a selection bias, as cases with more detailed documentation may represent patients who had more consistent clinical oversight. Third, our findings are restricted to an adult population (≥ 18 years); therefore, the clinical indications for pediatric dialysis initiation in this region remain unexamined. Finally, because the study was conducted at a single urban tertiary referral center (Hargeisa Group Hospital), the results primarily reflect the urban population of Maroodijeex and may not be generalizable to the rural or nomadic populations of Somaliland, who face significantly higher barriers to accessing hospital-based care.

Conclusion

This study describes the high proportion of late-stage kidney failure presentation at a major tertiary hospital in Somaliland. The observed clinical profile—driven largely by advanced uremic syndromes rather than laboratory markers—suggests a need for improved early CKD detection. To mitigate this burden, a multifaceted approach is recommended: strengthening primary care services, decentralizing nephrology infrastructure beyond the capital, and fostering collaborative international partnerships.
