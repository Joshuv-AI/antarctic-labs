Healthcare utilization and costs in patients with rheumatoid arthritis using biologics or tofacitinib: a nationwide, population-based study
Authors: Yen-Ju Chen, Wei-Li Ho, Hsian-Min Chen, Yi-Ming Chen, Hsin-Hua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory systemic disease, and the use of biological agents in the treatment of RA in recent years has significantly improved RA disease activities and clinical outcomes. However, the greatly increased medical costs due to the high costs of biologics are a major concern. We aimed to investigate healthcare utilization and costs in patients with RA pre- to post-initiation of biologics or tofacitinib.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide, population-based study from 1996 to 2017 using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD). In total, 57,084 newly diagnosed RA patients aged ≥ 20 years were identified, of whom 10,566 patients using biologics or tofacitinib were selected and included in the final analysis. The dose adjustments of anti-rheumatic drugs and healthcare utilization and costs among RA patients 3 months before and 6 months after use of biologics were compared. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis evaluating healthcare utilization and costs over a 12-month period pre- to post-initiation of biologics or tofacitinib was conducted.ResultsRA patients had more frequent all-cause and RA-related outpatient department (OPD) visits after receiving biologics or tofacitinib, but fewer RA-related emergency room (ER) visits (0.00 ± 0.04 times/month, p = 0.005). There were fewer OPD visits and lower OPD healthcare costs in RA patients using tocilizumab (OPD visits: β − 0.20, p = 0.013; OPD costs: β − 16,366.92, p < 0.001) and abatacept (OPD visits: β − 0.41, p < 0.001; OPD costs: β − 4436.24, p < 0.001), compared with etanercept users. Moreover, significant dose reductions of concomitant anti-rheumatic drugs were observed in RA patients after biologics or tofacitinib, including corticosteroid, leflunomide, hydroxychloroquine, and cyclosporin. Between 10.8 and 47.0% of RA patients experienced a reduction in the dose of anti-rheumatic drugs.ConclusionsThis nationwide, population-based study revealed that the dose of concomitant anti-rheumatic drugs and RA-related ER visits significantly reduced after initiating biologics or tofacitinib. Compared with etanercept users, patients treated with tocilizumab or abatacept had significantly lower outpatient care-related visit numbers and costs. Key Points• This nationwide population-based study investigated healthcare utilization and costs in RA patients pre- to post-initiation of biologics or tofacitinib.• RA-related emergency room visits and doses of concomitant anti-rheumatic drugs significantly declined after starting biologic or tofacitinib therapy, suggesting improved disease control.• Tocilizumab and abatacept use were associated with fewer outpatient visits and lower costs than etanercept, offering more resource-efficient options for certain patients.• Older age, male sex, and higher comorbidity burden predicted more dose reduction of conventional anti-rheumatic medications, supporting individualized treatment planning and de escalation strategies in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10067-026-08185-w.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory systemic disease characterized by arthritis and extra-articular inflammation, causing joint damage, major disability, and increased mortality [1]. The prevalence of RA varies between 0.3 and 1% globally and is more prevalent in females [2]. The annual worldwide incidence was 14.9 per 100,000 in 2017, and was 15.8 per 100,000 in Taiwan between 2002 and 2007 [3]. Conventional synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) are the mainstay of treatment for RA, but biologic agents should be considered for difficult-to-treat or csDMARDs refractory RA patients [4]. Use of biological agents in the treatment of RA in recent years has significantly improved RA disease activities and clinical outcomes [1, 5].

Several biological agents have been approved to treat RA by the United States’ Food and Drug Administration (FDA), including tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNF inhibitors, such as etanercept, adalimumab, golimumab), anti-IL6 (such as tocilizumab), anti-CD80/CD86 (such as abatacept), and Janus kinase inhibitor (JAK inhibitor, such as tofacitinib). In Taiwan, RA patients are eligible to apply for biological agents without additional payment after treatment failure using at least two csDMARDs at the target dose for 2 months, modified from the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) recommendations and the 2021 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guideline for the management of rheumatoid arthritis [6, 7]. The healthcare network in Taiwan is a single-payer, universal system, which covers almost 99.9% of individuals in Taiwan. In Taiwan’s National Health Insurance program, patients who have a major illness, including RA, can be issued with a certificate of catastrophic illness, which entitles them to exemption from copayments.

Previous reports revealed that medical expenditures were higher in RA patients than in non-RA patients [8, 9], and importantly, the greatly increased medical costs are a major concern for policymakers and insurance companies due to the high costs of biologics and the rising number of patients receiving biologics. Birnbaum and colleagues demonstrated that the cost of medications for RA patients in the USA increased after the introduction of biologics, while the overall costs have decreased due to fewer emergency room (ER) visits and hospitalization days [10]. Chen KC and colleagues reported similar results for RA patients using TNF inhibitors in Taiwan, which was associated with a remarkable escalation of drugs costs as well as a reduction of total direct costs other than biologics [11]. However, these studies were based on relatively limited case numbers and, in Taiwan, have largely focused on TNF inhibitors; the utilization of healthcare resources after treatment with other biologic DMARDs and targeted synthetic DMARDs such as tofacitinib has not been comprehensively evaluated. Furthermore, although population-based analyses from single-payer or publicly funded healthcare systems have examined healthcare utilization and costs in patients with rheumatoid arthritis [12–14], most have addressed overall cost-of-illness or specific biologic classes rather than detailed patterns of biologic initiation, concomitant csDMARD dose tapering, and subsequent resource use within Taiwan’s National Health Insurance program. Moreover, the pattern of anti-rheumatic drug use after the introduction of biologics is also a component of healthcare utilization but this issue has seldom been highlighted in the literature. Thus, further study is warranted. In the present study, we aimed to investigate the healthcare utilization and costs in RA patients pre- to post-initiation of biologic agents in Taiwan using a population-based study design.

Methods

Data source and identification of participants

We conducted a nationwide, population-based study using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), which contains the medical data of approximately 23.69 million individuals (99.9% of Taiwan’s population). Participants with RA aged ≥ 20 years were enrolled, and the medical data were collected from 1 January 1996 to 31 December 2017. RA was diagnosed based on the 1987 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) revised classification criteria or the ACR/EULAR 2010 rheumatoid arthritis classification criteria [15, 16]. Newly diagnosed RA patients were identified using the International Codes of Diseases–Ninth Revision–Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) code 714.0 or 714.30–714.33 at least three times in outpatient visits or once in a hospitalization, and each diagnosis was further confirmed by the issuance of a certificate of catastrophic illness for RA. The catastrophic illness certification for RA was approved after a thorough review of medical records by two qualified rheumatologists in Taiwan. The date of the approval of catastrophic illness certification for RA was defined as the index date. Individuals were excluded if they did not receive biologics, if they received any biologic before the index date, or if biologics were administered within 6 months before the index date. Patients with fewer than three outpatient visits within 6 months before the index date or those who did not use ≥ 2 conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) concomitantly for more than 56 days within 3 months were also excluded. Moreover, participants who were treated with Rituximab were also excluded since Rituximab was reserved for refractory RA patients with secondary failure to first-line biological agent treatment according to the policy of Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Administration.

For the present analysis, we further restricted the cohort to patients initiating their first biologic or tofacitinib after the issuance of the catastrophic illness certificate for RA. The index biologic (or tofacitinib) was defined as the first reimbursed agent prescribed after the certification date. Patients who had received any biologic prior to this index biologic/tofacitinib were excluded, ensuring that each patient contributed only one treatment episode and was assigned to a single first-line biologic/tofacitinib group. Under this design, none of the included patients had prior exposure to other bDMARDs before their index agent.

Variable definition and outcome identification

Baseline characteristics included gender, age at RA diagnosis, age at bDMARD initiation, disease duration, and comorbidities. The comorbidities were assessed by Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [17], a weighted score evaluated by ICD codes of variable comorbidities documented within 1 year before the index date for three or more outpatient visits or a hospitalization.

Information and costs related to the use of anti-rheumatic medications were collected, including bDMARDs, tsDMARDs, csDMARDs, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and corticosteroids. The dose of NSAIDs was expressed as defined daily dose (DDD) per month, while others were shown as milligram (mg) per month. The World Health Organization (WHO) has defined DDD as the assumed average daily maintenance dose of a drug used for the main indication in adults. Drug exposure was defined as treatments within 6 months before the index date.

Healthcare resource utilization and costs of all-cause or RA-related outpatient department (OPD) visits, hospitalization, and ER visits 3 months before and 6 months after biologics use were recorded. The costs were calculated by the mean expenses per member per month (PMPM), and were shown as New Taiwan dollars (NT$). US$1 corresponded to approximately NT$30.19 in 2025. Differences in healthcare resource utilization and costs among RA patients 3 months before and 6 months after use of biological agents were compared, using these intervals to align with the mandatory monitoring schedules and reimbursement review cycles stipulated by Taiwan’s National Health Insurance program for biologic therapy. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis using an extended observation period of 12 months before and 12 months after the initiation of biologic agents or tofacitinib was conducted to confirm the robustness of our findings.

Statistical analysis

The demographic data of continuous variables are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as number (percent). To compare healthcare utilization and costs pre- to post-initiation of biologics, the paired t test and McNemar’s test were conducted for continuous parameters and categorical variables, respectively. The impact of biological agents use on dose adjustments for anti-rheumatic drugs and healthcare utilization and costs among RA patients were calculated by linear regression analysis and expressed as regression coefficient (β) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). All data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23.0. Statistical significance was set at p values less than 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics and comorbidities among RA patients using biologics

Between January 1, 1996 and December 31, 2017, there were 57,084 newly diagnosed RA patients aged ≧ 20 years, and 10,566 participants were included in the final analysis. After excluding those who did not use biological agents (n = 42,588), those who received biological agents before the index date or ≦ 6 months (n = 2016), those who visited the OPD fewer than three times within 6 months before the index date (n = 245), those who did not receive ≧ 2 csDMARDs concomitantly for over 56 days within 3 months before the index date (n = 1471), and those who were treated with rituximab (n = 198), 10,566 participants were included in the final analysis. Among RA patients treated with biological agents, 5094 patients used etanercept, 3002 patients used adalimumab, 926 used golimumab, 459 used tocilizumab, 639 used abatacept, and 446 used tofacitinib. The mean age of approval of catastrophic illness certification for RA and first biological agents use was 50.3 ± 12.4 and 55.9 ± 12.4 years, respectively (Table 1). The disease duration of RA was 5.6 ± 5.1 years. Regarding comorbidities status, the mean CCI was 1.5 ± 0.9, and 37.8% (n = 3989) of RA patients on biologics had a CCI score of more than two. Dose reduction of anti-rheumatic medications

Table 2 shows the differences in healthcare utilization and costs PMPM among RA patients pre- to post-initiation of biologic agents. After biological agents use, significant dose reductions of concomitant anti-rheumatic drugs were observed. Male patients with RA tended to taper the dose of corticosteroids, leflunomide, and cyclosporin to a greater extent compared with female RA patients. Patients with CCI ≧ 2 were more likely to have decreased their dose of NSAIDs, corticosteroids, methotrexate, or cyclosporin, compared with those with CCI < 2. In addition, the older patients were, the greater was their reduction in methotrexate dose, but there was less tapering of corticosteroids and cyclosporin. Furthermore, the longer disease duration was associated with the less dose reduction of NSAIDs, corticosteroids, methotrexate, and hydroxychloroquine.

Factors associated with healthcare utilization and costs in RA patients

Compared with RA patients on etanercept, RA patients on golimumab, tocilizumab, and abatacept had fewer all-cause and RA-related OPD visits, while RA patients on adalimumab had more all-cause and RA-related ER visits (Table 5). There were less frequent RA-related ER visits and RA-related hospitalization in male patients than in female patients, and less frequent OPD, ER, and hospitalization in RA patients with CCI ≧ 2 than in those with CCI < 2. Additionally, there were more all-cause and RA-related OPD visits in RA patients with longer disease duration. As for medical costs, patients using golimumab had more healthcare costs in all-cause and RA-related OPD visits, while those using tocilizumab and abatacept had less healthcare costs in OPD visits, compared with those using etanercept. Moreover, greater medical costs in all-cause ER visits were also observed in patients treated with adalimumab compared with those treated with etanercept. Sensitivity analysis using a 12-month observation window

To better capture delayed treatment effects and late-onset complications, we additionally performed a sensitivity analysis using an extended 12-month observation period pre- to post-initiation of treatments. After excluding patients who switched biologics within 1 year or had less than 1 year of follow-up, 6751 RA patients remained. The results of this 12-month analysis were broadly consistent with our main analyses and are detailed in the supplementary material (Supplementary Tables S1-S5). Initiation of biologic therapy was associated with a greater proportion of patients achieving marked tapering of several concomitant anti-rheumatic medications. Overall, 45.2% of RA patients reduced their NSAID dose, 62.7% reduced corticosteroids, 44.8% reduced methotrexate, 51.2% reduced sulfasalazine, 21.1% reduced leflunomide, 50.3% reduced hydroxychloroquine, and 15.9% reduced cyclosporine. With respect to healthcare utilization, patients had a modest but statistically significant increase in all-cause outpatient visits after starting biologics (mean change 0.1 ± 1.4 visits per month, p < 0.001), as well as more frequent RA-related outpatient visits (mean change 0.3 ± 0.8 visits per month, p < 0.001). When comparing individual biologics, RA patients treated with tocilizumab or abatacept consistently had fewer outpatient visits and lower outpatient visit-related costs than those treated with etanercept.

Discussion

This nationwide, population-based study demonstrated that although there were increased medical costs in the RA population after the introduction of biologics or tofacitinib, use of these agents was associated with lower healthcare utilization, in parallel with declines in the doses of concomitant anti-rheumatic drugs and fewer RA-related ER visits. Moreover, our sensitivity analysis utilizing a 12-month observation period pre- to post-initiation of treatments confirmed the robustness of these trends. There was less healthcare utilization and costs of OPD visits in RA patients using tocilizumab and abatacept, compared with those using etanercept. In addition, greater dose reduction of anti-rheumatic drugs after using biologics was seen in RA patients with male gender, old age, and CCI ≧ 2, while less dose reduction was found in those with longer disease duration.

We found more frequent OPD visits but fewer RA-related ER visits in RA patients after biologics use, which was also shown in previous studies in Western countries and in Taiwan [12, 18]. The possible reasons might be close monitoring of RA patients after initiation of a new treatment, the use of a case manager care system, and the implementation of Risk Management Plan (RMP) in Taiwan. RMP was introduced by the Food and Drug Administration in Taiwan (TFDA) in 2012 for patients scheduled to receive biological agents to evaluate the benefit-risk balance of drugs with the goal of minimizing drug risks. In the same year, the Taiwan Rheumatology Association (TRA) Biologics Safety Working Group reached a consensus on recommendations for screening and management of TB, HBV, and HCV infection before biologics [19]. The case manager in charge of each biologic would follow up on patients and remind them to come back to the OPD on time. Therefore, more frequent OPD visits in RA patients after biologics are more likely to reflect closer scheduled follow-up and monitoring, which may in turn be associated with more efficient healthcare utilization in the RA population, and should be interpreted as a hypothesis rather than a confirmed mechanism. Among users of each biological agent, there were fewer OPD visits with lower healthcare costs in OPD visits among RA patients receiving tocilizumab and abatacept when compared with those receiving etanercept. One possible hypothesis is that the recommended administration interval for etanercept (twice a week), which is more frequent than that for tocilizumab and abatacept, might contribute to more OPD visits if patients are not able to perform injections by themselves at home. From a healthcare planning perspective, these patterns suggest that tocilizumab or abatacept could be preferable options for patients who are unable to visit the OPD frequently, particularly in terms of resource use and potential cost savings, but these explanations remain speculative and require confirmation in future studies.

Our results revealed a marked dose reduction of concomitant corticosteroids and csDMARDs in RA patients using biologics. The result was consistent with previously reported data that showed dose reductions of corticosteroid and methotrexate occurred among etanercept, golimumab, and tocilizumab users [20–23], which may reflect improving clinical symptoms, amelioration of RA disease activities, and lower inflammation after initiation of biological agents, as suggested by prior clinical studies. Moreover, our results also revealed patients using tocilizumab experienced greater dose reduction of methotrexate and leflunomide when compared with patients using etanercept. Tocilizumab has been proved to be effective as a monotherapy in RA patients in randomized controlled trials [24]. Furthermore, real-world data reported by Pappas DA et al. suggested that 31.3% of RA patients initiating tocilizumab stopped methotrexate or reduced the dose, with improvements in Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) scores and patient-reported outcomes at 6 months and 12 months [25, 26]. Johansson FD et al. derived a remission prediction score for tocilizumab monotherapy using data from randomized controlled trials, followed by an external validation of this score from real-world data, and the discrimination was good with an area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.69 (95% CI 0.62–0.75) to 0.76 (95% CI 0.67–0.84) [27]. Our result might be the consequence of confounding by indication as physicians probably tended to taper csDMARDs after tocilizumab initiation in accordance with previous results showing that tocilizumab monotherapy is successful, but it might also reflect the pattern of real-world practice. Our findings implied that tocilizumab could be the first choice if patients are worried about the pill burden.

Greater dose reduction of anti-rheumatic drugs after using biologics was seen in RA patients who were male gender, older, and had a CCI score of ≧ 2, while less dose reduction was found in those with longer disease duration. While controversial, most studies have shown better efficacy of glucocorticoids, methotrexate, and other csDMARDs in male RA patients than in female RA patients, and the disease flare rate after tapering csDMARDs was lower in men than in women [28–31]. This might explain why male RA patients were more likely to reduce the dose of csDMARDs to a greater extent after introduction of biological agents compared with their female counterparts. In addition, considering the relatively poor health status of certain patients, especially the elderly and those with numerous comorbidities, physicians tend to reduce the csDMARD dose as much as possible after initiating biological agents. Nevertheless, for patients with longer disease duration, physicians were likely to taper csDMARDs slowly with close monitoring of disease activities. One post-marketing surveillance study in Japan revealed that the factors associated with methotrexate dose reduction among RA patients using golimumab included no biologics use previously and increased comorbidities such as chronic kidney diseases [32]. The above-mentioned factors all mirrored the treatment considerations by clinicians in order to improve clinical outcomes as well as to avoid adverse events.

The present study describes patterns of healthcare utilization associated with initiating biological agent therapy in a nationwide RA population, and provides useful information for policymakers and physicians. A previous study demonstrated a 3.5-fold increased risk of self-reported cost-related medication nonadherence in elderly RA patients, compared with a non-RA population with multiple comorbidities (odds ratio 3.52, 95% CI 2.63–4.71) [33]. Therefore, adequate allocation of healthcare resources is an urgent issue at present. In addition, it is worth noting that indirect costs were a major component of total costs in patients with RA (39–86%) [34]. Although indirect costs were not captured in our claims-based analysis, strategies that improve disease activity and prevent major disability could potentially reduce the overall economic burden at a societal level. From a policy perspective, it is conceivable that broader insurance coverage of effective therapies might help lower the socioeconomic burden of RA in part through reductions in indirect costs, but this should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating implication rather than a conclusion directly supported by our data.

However, this study has several limitations. First, RA patients were identified by ICD diagnostic codes and confirmed by catastrophic illness certificates. The number of RA patients might have been underestimated because some patients in Taiwan may not have applied for or been issued a catastrophic illness certificate for various reasons, including early RA without obvious bone erosion, having already obtained a certificate for another chronic disease, or finding the co-payments affordable. Second, important clinical information was not available in the NHIRD, including RA disease activity measures (e.g., DAS28), radiographic severity, and patient-reported outcomes such as health-related quality of life and functional status; consequently, residual confounding and potential indication bias related to treatment selection and dose tapering cannot be excluded. Third, we could not assess indirect social costs, such as productivity loss, using the claims database, and our cost estimates are therefore restricted to direct medical expenditures. Finally, our findings were generated within Taiwan’s single-payer, highly regulated healthcare system, in which access to biologics is tightly controlled; therefore, cost and utilization patterns should be interpreted in this specific policy context, and extrapolation to other healthcare systems should be made with caution. Further studies incorporating detailed clinical data, patient-reported outcomes, and indirect cost measures in different healthcare settings are needed to more comprehensively evaluate the impact of biologic therapy on healthcare utilization and costs in RA.

Conclusion

This nationwide, population-based study showed that, although medical costs increased after initiation of biologics or tofacitinib in patients with RA, use of these agents was associated with lower healthcare utilization, including reduced doses of concomitant anti-rheumatic drugs and fewer RA-related ER visits. Among individual biologic agents, treatment with tocilizumab or abatacept was associated with fewer outpatient visits and lower outpatient visit-related costs compared with etanercept. These findings provide useful reference information for policymakers and clinicians when considering the allocation of healthcare resources for RA management, although confirmation in other healthcare settings and with more detailed clinical and socioeconomic data is still needed.
