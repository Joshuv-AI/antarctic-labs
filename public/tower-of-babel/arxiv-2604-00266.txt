Abstract
. Let A ⋈ f , g ( J , J ′ ) A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime}) be the bi–amalgamation of a commutative ring A A with ( B , C ) (B,C) along the ideals ( J , J ′ ) (J,J^{\prime}) with respect to the ring homomorphisms ( f , g ) (f,g) . In this article, we study the basic homological properties of the bi–amalgamated algebra construction. We first calculate the dimension and depth of the bi–amalgamated algebra under fairly general circumstances and derive necessary and sufficient conditions for Cohen–Macaulayness in terms of maximal and big Cohen–Macaulay modules of A A . Furthermore, we characterize the Gorenstein property of the bi–amalgamated algebra through the canonical modules of f ⁡ ( A ) + J f(A)+J and g ⁡ ( A ) + J ′ g(A)+J^{\prime} . We apply our results to the theory of curve singularities by constructing Gorenstein algebroid curves through bi–amalgamated and amalgamated algebras. We also give a brief remark concerning the universally catenary property of A ⋈ f , g ( J , J ′ ) A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime}) .

Introduction

Let
A
and
B
be commutative rings, let
J
be an ideal of
B
, and let
f\colon A\to B
be a ring homomorphism. The set

A\bowtie^{f}J=\{(a,f(a)+j)\mid a\in A,\ j\in J\}

is called the
amalgamation
of
A
and
B
along
J
with respect to
f
, and it is a subring of
A\times B
. This construction extends the notion of amalgamated duplication introduced by D’Anna and Fontana in
[
10
]
. A more general framework for amalgamations was later developed in
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
, where amalgamated algebras were constructed via pull–back. This perspective allowed the authors to establish several transfer results for algebraic and homological properties from the rings
A
and
f(A)+J
to the amalgamated algebra
A\bowtie^{f}J
. Since then, amalgamations have attracted significant attention and have been successfully applied in various contexts; see, for instance,
[
15
]
and
[
24
]
.

Let
A
,
B
, and
C
be commutative rings, let
J
and
J^{\prime}
be ideals of
B
and
C
, respectively, and let
f\colon A\to B
and
g\colon A\to C
be ring homomorphisms satisfying

f^{-1}(J)=g^{-1}(J^{\prime}).

The set

A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})=\{(f(a)+j,g(a)+j^{\prime})\mid a\in A,\ (j,j^{\prime})\in J\times J^{\prime}\}

is called the
bi–amalgamation
of
A
with
(B,C)
along
(J,J^{\prime})
with respect to
(f,g)
. It is a subring of
B\times C
. Bi–amalgamations, which naturally generalize amalgamations and duplications, were introduced in
[
20
]
. On top of classical pull–back constructions, the rings
f(A)+J
,
A+J
, and furthermore the CPI–extension
C(A,I)
can be viewed as bi–amalgamations.

Subsequent works have focused on the transfer of specific algebraic properties to bi–amalgamations. The coherence property was studied in
[
26
]
. In
[
21
]
, necessary and sufficient conditions were established for a bi–amalgamation to be arithmetical, with applications to weak global dimension and the transfer of the semihereditary property. The transfer of Gaussian and Prüfer properties was investigated in
[
23
]
, where the authors recovered known results for amalgamations and constructed new examples of rings satisfying these properties. For further background on bi–amalgamated algebras, we refer to
[
26
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
, and
[
23
]
.

The study of Cohen–Macaulay and Gorenstein properties in this context originates from the amalgamated duplication
A\bowtie I
in the works of D’Anna
[
9
]
. It was shown that, under suitable annihilator conditions,
A\bowtie I
is Gorenstein if and only if
A
admits a canonical ideal isomorphic to
I
, and later generalizations characterized the quasi–Gorenstein and normal cases
[
3
,
28
]
. Furthermore, an application to singularity theory has been proposed, where amalgamated duplication was used as a tool to construct Gorenstein algebroid curves. Such algebroid curves are characterized through the symmetry properties of their value semigroups. The value semigroups of duplicated monomial algebroid branches are also constructed explicitly in
[
9
]
.

Investigations of Cohen–Macaulay and Gorenstein properties were generalized to the framework of amalgamations in
[
29
,
13
]
. Their main results include the calculation of the depth and dimension of amalgamation rings explicitly under suitable conditions, and the characterization of the Cohen–Macaulay and Gorenstein properties, along with a brief discussion of universally catenary rings. Our article will be primarily concerned with these results and their extensions to the bi–amalgamated algebra. They further studied embedding dimensions, generalized Cohen–Macaulay rings and modules, the Serre condition, and quasi– and generalized Gorenstein rings in relation to amalgamations. We have not deemed such investigations necessary for this paper and have kept them as open questions for further research. There have been no investigations regarding the construction of algebroid curves using amalgamated algebras in the literature to the best of our knowledge.

In Section 2, we calculate the dimension and depth of the bi–amalgamated algebra by using tools from local cohomology in Theorem
2.1
. In particular, we prove in Theorem
2.2
that under suitable assumptions,
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
A
is Cohen–Macaulay with maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules
J,J^{\prime}
.

In Section 3, we first prove a
\Hom
-lemma useful in our investigations in Lemma
3.1
. Then, we prove a necessary condition for
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
to be Gorenstein in Proposition
3.1
. Finally, we completely characterize the Gorenstein property of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
in Theorem
3.1
by using the pull–back description of bi–amalgamations under suitable assumptions.

In Section 4, we apply our results to the theory of curve singularities. We show that bi–amalgamation constructions applied to algebroid curves also yield algebroid curves in Theorem
4.1
. Furthermore, we determine when the bi–amalgamated algebroid curve is Gorenstein in Theorem
4.2
, utilizing our findings from the previous section. This gives us a way of constructing algebroid Gorenstein curves, which are of particular importance.

In Section 5, we give a brief remark on how the bi–amalgamated algebra is a homomorphic image of a polynomial ring over
A
in Proposition
5.1
. As a result, we characterize the universally catenary property of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
in Proposition
5.2
.

As previously mentioned, the bi–amalgamated algebra generalizes the classical pull–back constructions in the literature. We shall give a few examples of interest, which can be found in
[

]
. The amalgamated algebra can be obtained by setting

and

, yielding

Upon further specialization to

for an ideal

of

. In the case that

. The ring

with the projection

. Taking

This shows that the classical

2.
Cohen–Macaulay Property of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})

In this section, we characterize the Cohen–Macaulay property of the ring
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
. To do so, we first calculate the dimension and the depth of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
under fairly general circumstances. It was first shown in
[
9
]
that for a Cohen–Macaulay ring
A
, the ring
A\bowtie I
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
I
is a maximal Cohen–Macaulay
A
–module. This easily follows from the
A
–module isomorphism
A\bowtie I\cong A\oplus I
. When
J
is a finitely generated
A
–module, it is shown in
[
13
]
that under the condition
J\subseteq\jac B
, the ring
A\bowtie^{f}J
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
J
is a maximal Cohen–Macaulay
A
–module. This also follows from the
A
–module isomorphism
A\bowtie^{f}J\cong A\oplus J
. It is also remarked that it is considerably more difficult to extend these results to the case where
J
is not finitely generated. This case was thoroughly discussed in
[
29
]
. The main difficulty in establishing analogous results for the bi–amalgamated algebra is the fact that an analogous isomorphism of
A
–modules does not always hold. This problem first led us to investigate when such an isomorphism exists and adopt methods similar to that of
[
29
]
.

We first introduce some necessary definitions. The exposition here is largely based on
[
5
,
6
]
, where all the quoted facts can be found. For convenience, we suppress the underlying rings while discussing the dimensions and depths of modules. This is possible in all of our applications by
[
6
, Exercise 1.2.26]
. Let
A
be a Noetherian commutative ring with an ideal
\mathfrak{a}
and an
A
–module
M
. The
\mathfrak{a}
–torsion functor
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}(M)=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(0:_{M}\mathfrak{a}^{n}\right),

which is the set of all elements of
M
which are annihilated by some power of
\mathfrak{a}
. Clearly, the
\mathfrak{a}
-torsion functor is naturally equivalent to the functor
\displaystyle\varinjlim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Hom_{A}(A/\mathfrak{a}^{n},{}_{\bullet}).
The local cohomology functors
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{a}}
are the right derived functors of
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}
. The modules

\displaystyle H^{i}_{\mathfrak{a}}(M)\cong\varinjlim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\ext^{i}_{A}(A/\mathfrak{a}^{n},M)\cong\varinjlim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}H^{i}(\Hom_{A}(A/\mathfrak{a}^{n},I^{\bullet}))\cong H^{i}\left(\varinjlim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Hom_{A}(A/\mathfrak{a}^{n},I^{\bullet})\right)

are called the local cohomology modules of
M
with respect to
\mathfrak{a}
, where
I^{\bullet}
is an injective resolution of
M
. From these definitions it follows that for any two ideals
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
, we have
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}=\Gamma_{\mathfrak{b}}
precisely when
\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}}
. Therefore, if
\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}=\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}}
,
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{a}}(M)\cong H^{i}_{\mathfrak{b}}(M)
for all
A
–modules
M
. For a given ring homomorphism
f:A\to B
, let
|_{A}:B\text{-{Mod}}\to A\text{-{Mod}}
be the functor of restriction of scalars by
f
. Through the independence theorem, the functors
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{a}B}({}_{\bullet})|_{A}
and
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{a}}({}_{\bullet}|_{A})
are naturally equivalent, resulting in an
A
–module isomorphism
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{a}B}(M)\cong H^{i}_{\mathfrak{a}}(M)
for all
B
–modules
M
. Grothendieck’s vanishing theorem states that
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{a}}(M)=0
for all
i>\dim M
. From now on, let us assume that
(A,\mathfrak{m})
is a Noetherian local ring. Grothendieck’s non–vanishing theorem states that for a non–zero finitely generated
A
–module
M
, we have
H^{\dim M}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)\neq 0
. Depth of an
A
–module is intimately connected with the non–vanishing of the local cohomology modules and can be described as

\displaystyle\depth M=\inf\left(i:H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)\neq 0\right).

In the case where
M
is finitely generated, we obtain the classical depth given by the length of a maximal
M
–sequence in
\mathfrak{m}
. It follows from the above discussion that a finitely generated
A
–module
M
over a Noetherian local ring
(A,\mathfrak{m})
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
for all
i<\dim M
. Furthermore, it is a well-known fact that
\depth M\leq\dim A
whenever the left-hand side is finite, which is the case when
\mathfrak{m}M\neq M
. If
\depth M=\dim A
for a finitely generated
A
–module

, we say that
M
is a maximal Cohen–Macaulay
A
–module. In the case where

is not necessarily finitely generated, we say that

is a big Cohen–Macaulay

–module.

\displaystyle\partial^{\depth A/I_{0}}:H_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\depth A/I_{0}}(A/I_{0})\to H_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\depth A/I_{0}+1}(J\oplus J^{\prime})

2.1
) has a trivial kernel, in which case
\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})=\depth A/I_{0}+1
.

(2)

Let
I_{0}=\ker f\cap\ker g
. Assume that
f^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})\neq\mathfrak{m}
and
g^{-1}(\mathfrak{q}^{\prime})\neq\mathfrak{m}
for all
\mathfrak{q}\in\spec(B)\setminus V(J)
and
\mathfrak{q}^{\prime}\in\spec(C)\setminus V(J^{\prime})
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle\dim A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})=\dim A/(\ker f\cap\ker g)

and

\displaystyle\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})=\min(\depth A/(\ker f\cap\ker g),\depth(J\oplus J^{\prime})).

Proof.

(1) The map
\iota:A\to A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
with
\iota:a\mapsto(f(a),g(a))
gives us an embedding of
A/(\ker f\cap\ker g)
into
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
. Since
J
and
J^{\prime}
are finitely generated
A
–modules, they are also finitely generated
A/(\ker f\cap\ker g)
–modules and this makes
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
a finitely generated
A/(\ker f\cap\ker g)
–module as well. Thus
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is an integral extension of
A/(\ker f\cap\ker g)
and they have the same dimension. The depth inequality is a simple consequence of
[
6
, Exercise 1.2.26]
and
[
6
, Proposition 1.2.9]
applied to the exact sequence (
2.1
).

(2) Let us also note that
J
and
J^{\prime}
are naturally
A/(\ker f\cap\ker g)
–modules. Our assumptions with Proposition
1.1
(3) and Lemma
2.2
guarantee that
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is a local ring with the maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})=\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}
. Therefore, by the radical invariance and the independence theorem of local cohomology, we get

\displaystyle H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}\left(A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)\cong H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}\left(A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)\cong H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}\left(A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)

for all
i\geq 0
. By the preceding corollary, the exact sequence (
2.1
) splits. Thus, applying the local cohomology functor yields

(2.2)

\displaystyle H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}\left(A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)\cong H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(A/I_{0})\oplus H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(J\oplus J^{\prime}).

Let us take
i=\dim A/I_{0}
. By Grothendieck’s non–vanishing theorem,
H^{\dim A/I_{0}}_{\mathfrak{m}}(A/I_{0})\neq 0
and thus

\displaystyle H^{\dim A/I_{0}}_{\mathfrak{m}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}\left(A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)\neq 0.

Grothendieck’s vanishing theorem now gives us
\dim A/I_{0}\leq\dim A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
. Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\dim A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})>\dim A/I_{0}
. Taking
i=\dim A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
we have

\displaystyle H^{\dim A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}_{\mathfrak{m}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}\left(A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)\neq 0.

However this contradicts Grothendieck’s vanishing theorem as we have

\displaystyle H^{\dim A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}_{\mathfrak{m}}(A/I_{0})=H^{\dim A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}_{\mathfrak{m}}(J\oplus J^{\prime})=0.

Therefore we must have

\displaystyle\dim A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})=\dim A/I_{0}=\dim A/(\ker f\cap\ker g).

Now let us show the equality of depths. Taking
i<\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
, we obtain
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(A/I_{0})=H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(J\oplus J^{\prime})=0
and therefore

\displaystyle\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\leq\min(\depth A/I_{0},\depth(J\oplus J^{\prime})).

Taking
i=\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
, we obtain

\displaystyle H^{\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}_{\mathfrak{m}}(A/I_{0})\oplus H^{\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}_{\mathfrak{m}}(J\oplus J^{\prime})\cong H^{\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}_{\mathfrak{m}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}\left(A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)\neq 0.

Hence we have
H^{\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}_{\mathfrak{m}}(A/I_{0})\neq 0
or
H^{\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}_{\mathfrak{m}}(J\oplus J^{\prime})\neq 0
. In either case, we get

\displaystyle\min(\depth A/I_{0},\depth(J\oplus J^{\prime}))\leq\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})

which implies

\displaystyle\depth A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})=\min(\depth A/(\ker f\cap\ker g),\depth(J\oplus J^{\prime})).

This concludes the proof.
∎

3.
Gorenstein Property of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})

The aim of this section is to give some necessary and sufficient conditions for the Gorenstein property of the bi–amalgamated algebra
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
. The Gorenstein property for amalgamated duplication was characterized by D’Anna in
[
9
]
. Under the condition that
A
is a local Cohen–Macaulay ring with a proper ideal
I
,
A\bowtie I
is Gorenstein if and only if
A
has a canonical ideal which is isomorphic to
I
. Shapiro
[
30
]
pointed out and corrected an implicit assumption of
\Ann_{A}(I)=0
in the original proof, which, for Noetherian rings, is equivalent to
I
being a regular ideal. This investigation was further generalized to the quasi–Gorenstein property in
[
3
]
. The Gorenstein property for the amalgamated algebra was characterized using the pull–back construction in
[
13
]
. A study on quasi–Gorensteinness of amalgamated algebras can be found in
[
29
]
, where results of
[
13
]
were further improved. Here we use an approach similar to that of
[
13
]
and use the pull–back description of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
to characterize the Gorenstein property.

We now outline the definition of a canonical module and a Gorenstein ring, following the classical terminology in
[
18
,
6
]
. We call an
A
–module
\omega
a canonical module of the local ring
(A,\mathfrak{m})
if

\displaystyle\omega\otimes_{A}\widehat{A}\cong\Hom_{A}\left(H_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\dim A}(A),E_{A}(A/\mathfrak{m})\right),

where as usual
E_{A}
means the injective hull over
A
. If such a canonical module exists, then it is unique up to isomorphism and is finitely generated as an
A
–module with dimension
\dim A
.
A
is called quasi–Gorenstein if
H_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\dim A}(A)\cong E_{A}(A/\mathfrak{m})
. This is equivalent to the existence of a canonical module
\omega_{A}
of
A
that is a free
A
–module of rank one
[
1
]
. A quasi–Gorenstein and Cohen–Macaulay ring is called Gorenstein. A Noetherian local ring
A
admits a canonical module if and only if it is a homomorphic image of a Gorenstein ring. In fact, if
A
is a homomorphic image of a local Gorenstein ring
B
, then one may construct
\omega_{A}
as

\displaystyle\omega_{A}\cong\ext_{B}^{\dim B-\dim A}(A,B).

In order to start our investigation, we first need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\Ann_{g(A)+J^{\prime}}(J^{\prime})=0
, then

\displaystyle\Hom_{A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}\left(f(A)+J,A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)\cong J\times 0

holds as
f(A)+J
–modules.

Proof.

By Proposition
1.1

1
, we have the following

\displaystyle\Hom_{A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}\left(f(A)+J,A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\cong\Hom_{A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}\left(A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})/0\times J^{\prime},A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\cong\Ann_{A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})}(0\times J^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\{(f(a)+j,g(a)+j^{\prime}):g(a)+j^{\prime}\in\Ann_{g(A)+J^{\prime}}(J^{\prime})\}

\displaystyle=\{(f(a)+j,0):g(a)\in J^{\prime}\}.

Furthermore, since
g(a)\in J^{\prime}
, we get
a\in g^{-1}(J^{\prime})=I_{0}=f^{-1}(J)
. This implies that
f(a)\in J
, and consequently the first coordinate
f(a)+j
entirely belongs to
J
. Thus, the module is exactly
J\times 0
.
∎

Lemma
3.1
reduces to the classical constructions of
[
9
,
13
,
29
]
in special cases. To avoid confusion with the future injectivity assumption on
f
, we use the homomorphism
g
and the ideal
J^{\prime}
for the amalgamated algebra in this section.

Corollary 3.1
.

If
\Ann_{g(A)+J^{\prime}}(J^{\prime})=0
, then

\displaystyle\Hom_{A\bowtie^{g}J^{\prime}}\left(A,A\bowtie^{g}J^{\prime}\right)\cong g^{-1}(J^{\prime})\times 0

holds as
A
–modules.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
\Ann_{A}(M)=0
, then

\displaystyle\Hom_{A\ltimes M}(A,A\ltimes M)\cong M\times 0

holds as
A
–modules.

Corollary 3.3
.

If
\Ann_{A}(I)=0
, then

\displaystyle\Hom_{A\bowtie I}(A,A\bowtie I)\cong I\times 0

holds as
A
–modules.

We now establish a necessary condition for

to be Gorenstein, which is that
J
is a canonical module of
f(A)+J

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
(A,\mathfrak{m})
is a local Cohen–Macaulay ring,

is an injective ring homomorphism and

and
J^{\prime}
are finitely generated
A
–modules with

Proof.

The Gorensteinness of

implies the existence of a canonical

4.
Algebroid Curves Arising from Bi–amalgamations and Amalgamations

We say that a ring
A
is an algebroid curve over an algebraically closed field
k
if it is a one-dimensional, reduced, complete local
k
–algebra with the residue field
k
. Alternatively, by Cohen’s structure theorem, an algebroid curve is a one-dimensional, reduced, local ring of the form
A=k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket/\left(I_{1}\cap\cdots\cap I_{h}\right)
where
I_{1},\ldots,I_{h}
are prime ideals of
k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket
with height
n-1
. The ring
A_{i}=k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket/I_{i}
is called the
i
-th algebroid branch of
A
and the number
h
is called the number of branches. Algebroid curves arise naturally in the theory of curve singularities as completions of local rings of irreducible algebraic curves over
k
according to the topology induced by their maximal ideal
[
9
]
.

We will now define the valuation semigroup of the algebroid curve
A
following
[
14
]
. It can be shown that
Q(A)=k((t_{1}))\times\ldots\times k((t_{h}))
and the integral closure of
A
in
Q(A)
is given by
\bar{A}=k\llbracket t_{1}\rrbracket\times\ldots\times k\llbracket t_{h}\rrbracket
. Notice that the quotient field of
A_{i}
is
k((t_{i}))
and
\bar{A}_{i}=k\llbracket t_{i}\rrbracket
is a discrete valuation ring with the valuation
v_{i}
, given by the order of a power series. For an element
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{h})\in Q(A)
, we define
v(q)=(v_{1}(q_{1}),\ldots,v_{h}(q_{h}))
. Let
I
be any regular fractional ideal of
A
, then we set

\displaystyle v(I)=\{v(x):x\in I\ \text{is a regular element of }Q(A)\}.

The set
S=v(A)\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{h}
is a semigroup, called the valuation semigroup of
A
. The conductor
\zeta=\{q\in\bar{A}:q\bar{A}\subseteq A\}
of
\bar{A}
in
A
is then a regular ideal of
\bar{A}
. Thus it can be written as
\left(t_{1}^{\delta_{1}}\right)\times\ldots\times\left(t_{h}^{\delta_{h}}\right)
for a vector
\delta=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{h})
. The element
\delta
is called the conductor of
S
. It is characterized as the minimal element of
S
(with respect to the product order
\alpha\leq\beta
if
\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,h
) with the property that any vector strictly greater than
\delta
is in
S
, i.e.,
\delta+\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{h}\subseteq S
. To introduce the notion of symmetry, we need the following definition. Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{h}
be given. For each
i=1,\ldots,h
, we set

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}(\alpha)=\{\beta\in S:\beta_{i}=\alpha_{i}\ \text{and }\beta_{j}>\alpha_{j}\ \text{for all }j\neq i\}

with
\Delta(\alpha)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}\Delta_{i}(\alpha)
. Finally, we say that
S
is symmetric when
\alpha\in S
if and only if
\Delta(\gamma-\alpha)=\emptyset
, where
\gamma=\delta-(1,\ldots,1)
.

A beautiful theorem by Delgado
[
14
]
states that the semigroup
S
is symmetric if and only if the algebroid curve
A
is Gorenstein. In the case of algebroid branches with
h=1
, the semigroup
S
is numerical and the symmetry is the usual symmetry of semigroups in
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, which has been extensively studied
[
2
,
17
]
. The number
g=\max(\mathbb{N}\setminus S)
is called the Frobenius number and is crucial to the study of semigroups. Let us remark that
g=\gamma=\delta-1
and the symmetry condition is given by
\alpha\in S
if and only if
g-\alpha\notin S
. Delgado’s theorem was first proved for
h=1
by Kunz in
[
22
]
and the general case was elaborated further in
[
7
]
.

All algebroid curves are Cohen–Macaulay, thus
A
is Gorenstein if and only if it is quasi–Gorenstein. Therefore, it is natural to ask whether a canonical module always exists. A brief discussion (we refer the reader to
[
9
]
or
[
8
]
) shows that a canonical module, which can be identified as a fractional ideal, exists and can always be taken to satisfy
\omega_{A}\subseteq A
or
A\subseteq\omega_{A}\subseteq\bar{A}
for convenience. To determine when such a fractional ideal is a canonical module, we must introduce the canonical relative ideal
K
of
S
by

\displaystyle K=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{h}:\Delta(\gamma-\alpha)=\emptyset\},

which can be found in
[
8
]
. Obviously,
S
is symmetric if and only if
K=S
. In the case
h=1
, the canonical relative ideal is given by
K=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}:g-\alpha\notin S\}
. Jäger has shown in
[
19
]
that a fractional ideal
I
with
A\subseteq I\subseteq\bar{A}
is a canonical ideal if and only if
v(I)=K
. Under minimal assumptions, Jäger’s result also holds for
h\geq 2
(see
[
8
]
).

Amalgamated duplications of algebroid curves can be used as an efficient tool for constructing Gorenstein algebroid curves. Indeed, it is shown in
[
9
]
that the amalgamated duplication of an algebroid curve along a proper regular ideal is also an algebroid curve with twice as many branches. As a consequence of the characterization of the Gorenstein property for amalgamated duplication, the duplicated algebroid curve is Gorenstein if and only if it is obtained through duplication along a canonical ideal. To the best of our knowledge, amalgamated algebroid curves have never been investigated before in the literature. In this section, we prove analogous results in the context of bi–amalgamations. Our first result is that the bi–amalgamated algebra is also an algebroid curve.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
A
is an algebroid curve,
f:A\to B
and
g:A\to C
are two
k
–algebra homomorphisms into algebroid curves
f(A)+J
and
g(A)+J^{\prime}
where
J
and
J^{\prime}
are proper, regular ideals of
B
and
C
respectively, which are finitely generated
A
–modules with
f^{-1}(J)=g^{-1}(J^{\prime})
,
J\subseteq\jac(B)
and
J^{\prime}\subseteq\jac(C)
. Then
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is also an algebroid curve with
h_{f(A)+J}+h_{g(A)+J^{\prime}}
branches. Furthermore, there is a bijective correspondence between the branches of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
and those of
f(A)+J
and
g(A)+J^{\prime}
where corresponding branches are isomorphic.

Proof.

The condition
J\subseteq\jac(B)
and
J^{\prime}\subseteq\jac(C)
with Proposition
1.1
(3) implies that
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is local. It is furthermore reduced as it is a subring of
(f(A)+J)\times(g(A)+J^{\prime})
. Let us define a
k
–algebra homomorphism
(f,g):A\to A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
by
(f,g)(a)=(f(a),g(a))
. Under the action
a\cdot(b,c)=(f(a)b,g(a)c)
,
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is a submodule of
(f(A)+J)\times(g(A)+J^{\prime})
, which is a finite
A
–module. As
A
is Noetherian,
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is also a finite
A
–module and
(f,g)
is a finite morphism. By Theorem
2.1
,
\dim A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})=\dim A/(\ker f\cap\ker g)
. As
f(A)+J
and
g(A)+J^{\prime}
are one-dimensional, we get
\height(\ker f)=\height(\ker g)=\height(\ker f\cap\ker g)=0
, which implies
\dim A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})=\dim A=1
. The maximal ideal of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is
\mathfrak{m}_{A}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
by Proposition
1.1
(3). Therefore, using Proposition
1.1

1
, the residue field of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is
\left(A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)/\left(\mathfrak{m}_{A}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)=A/(\mathfrak{m}_{A}+I_{0})=k
as
I_{0}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_{A}
. This can alternatively be seen by
(f,g)^{-1}\left(\mathfrak{m}_{A}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)=\mathfrak{m}_{A}
or
\pi_{f(A)+J}^{-1}\left(\mathfrak{m}_{A}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)=\mathfrak{m}_{f(A)+J}
, where
\pi_{f(A)+J}:A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\to f(A)+J
is the natural projection to the first coordinate. The completeness is easily deduced from the finite morphism
(f,g)
with
\mathfrak{m}_{A}
and
\mathfrak{m}_{A}\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
inducing equivalent topologies. This concludes that
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is an algebroid curve.

We now investigate the branching behavior of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
. Let
L_{1},\ldots,L_{h_{f(A)+J}}
and
L_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,L_{h_{g(A)+J^{\prime}}}^{\prime}
be the minimal primes of
f(A)+J
and
g(A)+J^{\prime}
respectively. The fact
J\nsubseteq L_{i}
and
J^{\prime}\nsubseteq L_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}
for every
i
and
i^{\prime}
follows since
J
and
J^{\prime}
are regular,
f(A)+J
and
g(A)+J^{\prime}
are reduced, and in a reduced ring the set of zero divisors is the union of minimal primes. We claim that
\overline{L_{1}},\ldots,\overline{L_{h_{f(A)+J}}},\overline{L_{1}^{\prime}},\ldots,\overline{L_{h_{g(A)+J^{\prime}}}^{\prime}}
are the minimal primes of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
. It can be checked easily that
\overline{L_{i}}\neq\overline{L_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}}
from
J\subseteq\overline{L_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}}
. As
J\nsubseteq L_{i}
, the conditions of Proposition
1.1

2
apply and we obtain
\left(A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\right)_{\overline{L_{i}}}=(f(A)+J)_{L_{i}}
. Therefore, the minimality of
L_{i}
implies the minimality of
\overline{L_{i}}
. A similar argument shows that the ideals
\overline{L_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}}
are minimal. Now let
\mathfrak{P}
be a minimal prime ideal of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
. By Proposition
1.1

2
,
\mathfrak{P}
is of the form
\overline{L}
with
L\in\spec(f(A)+J)\cup\spec(g(A)+J^{\prime})
. It is obvious that non–minimality of
L
contradicts the minimality of
\mathfrak{P}
. This shows that
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
has
h_{f(A)+J}+h_{g(A)+J^{\prime}}
branches. It is easy to see that the projections
\overline{\pi}_{f(A)+J,i}:A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\to(f(A)+J)/L_{i},\overline{\pi}_{f(A)+J,i}(b,c)=b+L_{i}
and
\overline{\pi}_{g(A)+J^{\prime},i^{\prime}}:A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})\to(g(A)+J^{\prime})/L_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime},\overline{\pi}_{g(A)+J^{\prime},i^{\prime}}(b,c)=c+L_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}
have kernels
\overline{L_{i}}
and
\overline{L_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}}
. Thus they induce isomorphisms
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})/\overline{L_{i}}\cong(f(A)+J)/L_{i}
and
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})/\overline{L_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}}\cong(g(A)+J^{\prime})/L_{i^{\prime}}^{\prime}
. This completes the proof.
∎

As a corollary, we get the following novel result on amalgamated algebroid curves and the result of
[
9
]
on duplicated algebroid curves.

Corollary 4.1
.

Suppose
A
is an algebroid curve,
f:A\to B
is a
k
–algebra homomorphism into the algebroid curve
f(A)+J
where
J
is a proper, regular ideal of
B
, which is a finitely generated
A
–module with
J\subseteq\jac(B)
. Then
A\bowtie^{f}J
is also an algebroid curve with
h_{A}+h_{f(A)+J}
branches. Furthermore, there is a bijective correspondence between the branches of
A\bowtie^{f}J
and those of
A
and
f(A)+J
where corresponding branches are isomorphic.

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose
A
is an algebroid curve and
I
is a proper, regular ideal of
A
. Then
A\bowtie I
is also an algebroid curve with
2h_{A}
branches. Furthermore, there is a one-to-two correspondence between the branches of
A\bowtie I
and those of
A
where corresponding branches are isomorphic.

Instead of following the above algebraic approach, one can give an explicit representation of the algebroid curve
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
as a quotient of a power series ring. Indeed, let
A=k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket/I_{A}
be the quotient representation of
A
and let us define
\varphi_{B}:k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket\to\bar{B}
as the composition of the natural projection
\pi_{A}:k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket\to A
, the morphism
f:A\to B
, and the embedding
B\hookrightarrow\bar{B}
. Similarly, define
\varphi_{C}:k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket\to\bar{C}
as the composition of the natural projection
\pi_{A}:k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket\to A
, the morphism
g:A\to C
, and the embedding
C\hookrightarrow\bar{C}
. Clearly,
\ker\varphi_{B}=\pi_{A}^{-1}(\ker f)=\ker f^{L}+I_{A}k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket
and
\ker\varphi_{C}=\pi_{A}^{-1}(\ker g)=\ker g^{L}+I_{A}k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket
where
\ker f^{L}
and
\ker g^{L}
are ideals generated by some fixed liftings of minimal generating sets of
\ker f
and
\ker g
. Take liftings
i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\in k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrbracket
such that the projections
\overline{i_{1}},\ldots,\overline{i_{m}}\in A
form a minimal generating set of
I_{0}
. We shall identify
J
and
J^{\prime}
with their images in
\bar{B}
and
\bar{C}
.

Set
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}
to be new indeterminates. Define two functions

\displaystyle\psi_{B}:k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\to\bar{B};

\displaystyle\psi_{B}(x_{j})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{B}(x_{j}),

\displaystyle\psi_{B}(y_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\psi_{B}(z_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{B}(i_{j^{\prime}}),

and

\displaystyle\psi_{C}:k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\to\bar{C};

\displaystyle\psi_{C}(x_{j})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{C}(x_{j}),

\displaystyle\psi_{C}(y_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{C}(i_{j^{\prime}}),

\displaystyle\psi_{C}(z_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=0,

for all
j=1,\ldots,n
and
j^{\prime}=1,\ldots,m
, and then extend them to be ring homomorphisms. Furthermore, let us define the ring homomorphism

\displaystyle\Omega:k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\rrbracket\to\bar{B}\times\bar{C};\qquad\Omega(F)=(\psi_{B}(F),\psi_{C}(F)).

We claim that
\operatorname{im}\Omega=A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
. Given any
F\in k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\rrbracket
, we can decompose it uniquely as

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=F_{x}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+F_{x,y}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})+F_{x,z}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})

\displaystyle+F_{x,y,z}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}).

It follows from the definition that
\psi_{B}(F_{x,y})=\psi_{B}(F_{x,y,z})=\psi_{C}(F_{x,z})=\psi_{C}(F_{x,y,z})=0
and
\psi_{B}(F_{x})=\varphi_{B}(F_{x})=f(\overline{F_{x}})
,
\psi_{C}(F_{x})=\varphi_{C}(F_{x})=g(\overline{F_{x}})
. Therefore, we obtain
\Omega(F)=(f(\overline{F_{x}})+\psi_{B}(F_{x,z}),g(\overline{F_{x}})+\psi_{C}(F_{x,y}))
with
\psi_{B}(F_{x,z})\in(\varphi_{B}(i_{1}),\ldots,\varphi_{B}(i_{m}))=f(I_{0})=J
and
\psi_{C}(F_{x,y})\in(\varphi_{C}(i_{1}),\ldots,\varphi_{C}(i_{m}))=g(I_{0})=J^{\prime}
. This shows that
\operatorname{im}\Omega\subseteq A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
. Conversely, it is easy to see that any
(f(a)+j,g(a)+j^{\prime})\in A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is of the form
\Omega(F)
. This shows that
\operatorname{im}\Omega=A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
. Therefore, we have
\operatorname{im}\Omega=A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})=k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\rrbracket/\ker\Omega
. Furthermore,
\ker\Omega=\ker\psi_{B}\cap\ker\psi_{C}
and

\displaystyle\ker\psi_{B}

\displaystyle=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})+(z_{1}-i_{1},\ldots,z_{m}-i_{m})+\ker f^{L}+I_{A}k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\ker\psi_{C}

\displaystyle=(y_{1}-i_{1},\ldots,y_{m}-i_{m})+(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})+\ker g^{L}+I_{A}k\llbracket x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\rrbracket.

Now it can be checked that
\ker\psi_{B}
and
\ker\psi_{C}
are respectively intersections of
h_{B}
and
h_{C}
prime ideals with heights
n+2m-1
. This gives us the explicit representation of
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
as a quotient.

Now we can give the second main result of this section.

Theorem 4.2
.

The algebroid curve
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is Gorenstein if and only if
J
and
J^{\prime}
are isomorphic to canonical ideals of
f(A)+J
and
g(A)+J^{\prime}
respectively.

Proof.

The proof follows from the characterization Theorem
3.1
as all algebroid curves are Noetherian and Cohen–Macaulay, thus satisfy the Serre condition
(S_{1})
and are equidimensional.
∎

5.
A remark on the universally catenary property

Bi–amalgamations naturally generalize some classic pull–back constructions of polynomial and power series rings. In the previous section, we have also seen an application of the bi–amalgamated algebra construction to curve singularities. In this section, we describe the bi–amalgamated algebra as a homomorphic image of a polynomial ring. The results here generalize those of
[
29
]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Assume that
J
and
J^{\prime}
are generated by
\{j_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in M}
and
\{j^{\prime}_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in N}
respectively as
A
–modules. Then
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is a homomorphic image of
A[\{x_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in M},\{x^{\prime}_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in N}]
, where
x_{\mu}
and
x^{\prime}_{\nu}
are indeterminates.

Proof.

Define a homomorphism
\varphi:A[\{x_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in M},\{x^{\prime}_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in N}]\to A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
by
\varphi(a)=(f(a),g(a))
,
\varphi(x_{\mu})=(j_{\mu},0)
and
\varphi(x^{\prime}_{\nu})=(0,j^{\prime}_{\nu})
. It is obvious that
\varphi
is surjective and thus
A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is a homomorphic image of
A[\{x_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in M},\{x^{\prime}_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in N}]
.
∎

Corollary 5.1
.

Assume that
J
is generated by
\{j_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in M}
as an
A
–module. Then
A\bowtie^{f}J
is a homomorphic image of
A[\{x_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in M}]
, where
x_{\mu}
are indeterminates.

Corollary 5.2
.

Assume that
M
is generated by
\{m_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in M}
as an
A
–module. Then
A\ltimes M
is a homomorphic image of
A[\{x_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in M}]
, where
x_{\mu}
are indeterminates.

Corollary 5.3
.

Assume that
I
is generated by
\{i_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in M}
as an ideal of

. Then
A\bowtie I
is a homomorphic image of

, where

are indeterminates.

As a consequence of the above proposition, we reobtain a special case of the Noetherian characterization of

. Indeed, we get that whenever

and

are finitely generated

We say that a Noetherian ring

A

Proposition 5.2

Assume

A\bowtie^{f,g}(J,J^{\prime})
is universally catenary if and only if
A/I_{0}
