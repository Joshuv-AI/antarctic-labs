Nanomaterial-mediated functional remodeling of antigen-presenting cells: A novel strategy to break tumor immune escape
Authors: Hongzhi Dong, Xueyi Song, Yao Li, Qian Wei, Mingyi Ju, Yunong Li, Qi Cui, Jia Bi, Minjie Wei, Heran Li, Lin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the cross-integration of nanotechnology and immunology, nanomaterials have shown great potential in tumor treatment. This review begins with the nanomaterials have unique advantages in reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), including improving targeting, enhancing stability, increasing delivery efficiency, achieving controllable release, and endowing a multi-functional integrated platform. We then systematically reviews the recently research progress on the multi-mechanism regulation of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) by nanomaterials: (1) In the aspect of dendritic cell activation, we focus on the breakthrough achievements such as the activation of immune responses through the generation of immune signal complexes and the promotion of tumor neoantigen presentation; (2) In the field of macrophage reprogramming, we analyze the applications of nanomaterials in inducing macrophage polarization, improving the anti-tumor phagocytosis of macrophages, blocking macrophage autophagy, and enhancing the synergy of immune cells; (3) In the regulation of B cells, we explore the mechanism by which nanomaterials enhance the germinal center response and thereby exert anti-tumor immune effects. This article further discusses key challenges in clinical translation, including the dynamic fate of nanomaterials in complex TIME and the balance between immunogenicity and efficacy, and proposes future directions for accelerating clinical translation through computer-aided design (such as AI prediction of nanomaterial-immune cell interaction networks) and organ-on-a-chip evaluation systems. This review provides a theoretical framework and technical route for the development of next-generation tumor immunonanomedicines.Key words: Tumor immune microenvironment, Nanomaterials, Antigen-presenting cells, Immune regulation, Macrophage reprogramming, Dendritic cells, Tumor immunotherapy, Clinical translation

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a dynamic functional unit composed of tumor cells, stromal cells, immune cells, and extracellular matrix, which maintains the malignant progression of tumors through metabolic competition (such as glutamine deprivation), physical barriers (such as dense fibrosis), and immune suppressive signaling networks (such as the PD-L1/PD-1 axis)1. As a core functional subsystem of the TME, the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is characterized by pathological changes including antigen presentation defects: down-regulation of MHC-II molecules on dendritic cells (DCs); enrichment of immune checkpoints: overexpression of inhibitory receptors such as CTLA-4 and TIM-3; and cytokine imbalance: dominance of immunosuppressive factors such as TGF-β and IL-102. Antigen-presenting cells (APCs) play a central role in immune regulation in the TIME: (1) DCs: migrate to the draining lymph nodes through CCR7-mediated homing to activate tumor-specific CD8+ T cells, but their function is often inhibited by non-coding RNAs carried by tumor exosomes3,4; (2) tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs): M2 polarization (through the STAT3/PPARγ pathway) promotes vasculogenic mimicry, while CSF-1R inhibitors can induce their transformation to anti-tumor M1 type5, 6, 7; (3) B cells: mediate ADCC effects through IgG1 antibodies in tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS), but regulatory B cells (Bregs) can promote immune escape through IL-10 and IL-35 secretion8,9.

Although traditional cancer therapies such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy have successfully improved the survival conditions of many patients, these traditional therapies (surgery/chemoradiotherapy) have problems such as off-target toxicity (such as bone marrow suppression) and acquired drug resistance10,11. Nanomaterials have shown breakthrough advantages due to their EPR effect (enhanced permeability and retention effect) and active targeting modification (such as CD44 ligand coupling)12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20. However, recent studies have reported that although the EPR effect has long been regarded as a reasonable theory that drives the prosperity of cancer drug delivery, it has facilitated the development of various drug delivery systems, yet due to its failure to significantly improve treatment efficacy in clinical settings and its significant heterogeneity in the human body, it has been controversial in clinical-related treatments21, 22, 23. Therefore, the research suggests that the focus may need to shift to tumor extravasation strategies independent of EPR, such as enhancing extravasation through radiation or vascular disruption, or supplementing or replacing the EPR effect using methods like exosomes24. Current research focuses on intelligent responsive nanocarriers: pH/ROS dual-sensitive nanoparticles triggering drug release; multi-functional integrated platforms: nanoparticles with both PD-1 blockade and IDO inhibition25; and biomimetic delivery systems: nanovesicles based on tumor cell membrane camouflage for homotypic targeting.

The combination of artificial intelligence (AI) and nanomaterials demonstrates great potential in meeting clinical needs26. The integration of computer-aided design and organ-on-a-chip evaluation systems provides an innovative and accelerated path for the clinical translation of nano-immunotherapy. By analyzing the complex relationships between the physicochemical properties of nanomaterials and immune cell surface receptors and signaling pathways through AI technology, it is possible to predict their targeting efficiency, biocompatibility, and potential toxicity, enabling rational design of nanocarriers. For instance, machine learning models can optimize the surface modification strategies of nanoparticles based on a large amount of experimental data, enhancing their recognition accuracy for specific immune cells. Meanwhile, organ-on-a-chip technology, by simulating the microenvironment of human organs, can dynamically assess the targeting behavior, immune activation effects, and cross-organ toxicity of nanomaterials in vitro. This integrated strategy not only reduces the reliance on animal models but also reveals the real biological effects of nanomaterials in a simulated human system through multi-organ chip platforms, thereby shortening the research and development cycle and promoting the standardization and clinical translation of personalized nano-immunotherapy.

This review will systematically elaborate on the relationship between the physical and chemical properties of nanomaterials (such as surface charge and topological structure) and their impact on the functions of APCs; the application of targeted APC nanotechniques in overcoming T cell exhaustion; and the biological compatibility challenges and solutions faced in clinical translation. Through a multidisciplinary cross-perspective, it will provide a theoretical framework and technical roadmap for the development of next-generation immune nanomedicines.

Nanometer materials

Definition

Nanoscience is a discipline that studies the structure and molecules of substances. Based on the knowledge of nanoscience, the technology of applying it to practical applications such as devices is called nanotechnology. By observing and measuring substances at the nanoscale and being able to manufacture, assemble, and manipulate substances, nanotechnology can produce many materials, with nanomaterials being representative27,28. Nanomaterials refer to materials that have a structure of at least 1 to 100 nm in at least one dimension27,29,30.

Classification

Nanomaterials can be classified into four types based on their dimensions. Zero-dimensional nanomaterials (0-D) have all three dimensions within the nanometer range, i.e., less than 100 nm, such as nanoparticles and quantum dots. One-dimensional nanomaterials (1-D) refer to materials where one dimension is outside the nanometer range, while the other two dimensions are at the nanometer scale, such as nanotubes, nanorods, nanogrooves, etc. Two-dimensional nanomaterials (2-D) contain only one dimension within the nanoscale, while the other two dimensions are not included, such as nanosheets and nanolayers. Three-dimensional nanomaterials (3-D) or bulk materials are those where all dimensions are larger than 100 nm, such as bulk powders, nanowires, and nanotube arrays, etc.27,31,32 (Fig. 1).

Depending on the composition of the materials, nanomaterials can be broadly classified into three types: inorganic nanomaterials, carbon-based nanomaterials, and organic nanomaterials. Inorganic nanomaterials are composed of inorganic atoms, which are bonded together through covalent bonds or metal bonds33. They can be further divided into metallic nanomaterials, ceramic nanomaterials, and semiconductor nanomaterials. Among metallic nanomaterials, they can be further classified into magnetic nanomaterials (such as metals like gold, silver, copper, aluminum, iron, etc.), metal oxide nanomaterials (such as TiO2, Fe2O3, Al2O3, ZnO, etc.), and bimetallic nanomaterials (such as Al-TiO2-Ag, Ag-Cu, Au/Pd, Au-Pd@SiO2, Fe-Cu, and Ag-Au, etc.). Inorganic non-metallic nanomaterials, such as zeolites and silica-based nanomaterials, are widely used due to their excellent surface chemical properties and mesoporous structures27. Carbon-based nanomaterials are composed solely of carbon atoms31,32. Depending on their different forms of existence, they can be classified as graphene, fullerenes, carbon black, carbon quantum dots (CQDs), carbon nanofibers, or carbon nanotubes (CNTs). Organic nanomaterials are solid particles composed of natural or synthetic organic molecules (such as polymers, lipids, carbohydrates, and proteins, etc.). Polymer nanomaterials are divided into natural and synthetic types. Common natural polymers include chitosan and alginate, while common synthetic polymers include polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), and their copolymer, namely poly(lactic acid-co-hydroxyethyl acid) (PLGA). Lipid-based nanomaterials (LNP) include liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles, and nanostructured lipid carriers. Carbon-based nanomaterials or sugar-based nanomaterials are composed of complexes based on carbohydrates or carbohydrates themselves. Protein-based nanomaterials are composed of naturally occurring or artificially synthesized proteins, including collagen nanomaterials, gelatin nanomaterials, milk protein nanomaterials, and plant protein-based nanomaterials, etc.31,32,34.

Synthetic method

The synthesis of nanomaterials mainly adopts two methods: top-down and bottom-up approaches35, 36, 37 (Fig. 1). The top-down method refers to reducing the size of the material by mechanical force and modifying the material to the desired size through various means. The modification methods include mechanical grinding, high-energy ball milling, lithography, extrusion, chemical etching, sputtering, laser ablation, spark discharge, etc. Among them, lithography can be further divided into optical lithography, electron beam lithography, soft lithography, nano-imprint lithography, block copolymer lithography, and scanning probe lithography. This top-down method is suitable for large-scale production in industry, but it has the disadvantages of relying on expensive equipment and machines, requiring a large amount of energy, and the particle growth being uncontrollable27,29,38,39.

The bottom-up approach refers to the growth and self-assembly of atomic or molecular precursor nanomaterials. Nanoparticles are first obtained at the atomic level and then integrated into the desired materials through various methods such as co-precipitation, hydrothermal synthesis, inert gas condensation, microwave assistance, sol-gel nanomanufacturing, chemical synthesis methods, chemical vapor deposition, physical vapor deposition, template synthesis, biological synthesis, microemulsion method, ultrasonic method, polymerization method, self-assembly method, colloid method, plasma method, polycondensation method, high-temperature pyrolysis method, pyrolysis-polycondensation method, microfluidic method, etc. This bottom-up approach can produce amorphous particles with good solubility and, bioavailability and has the advantages of simplicity, rapidity, efficiency, and energy conservation, thus being suitable for laboratory production and synthesis of materials with smaller particle sizes. However, the bottom-up approach also has drawbacks, such as the amorphous particles often re-agglomerating, presenting stability issues27,29,38,39.

Physicochemical property

Unlike traditional macromolecular materials, nanomaterials have dimensions within the nanometer range, thus possessing many unique physical and chemical properties such as optical, magnetic, and electrical properties. Typical nanomaterials share the following common characteristics: high specific surface area, strong electrical conductivity, superparamagnetism, spectral shift of light absorption, and unique fluorescence properties. The optical properties of nanomaterials are usually related to the size, shape, and dielectric environment of the particles, especially scattering phenomena and reflectivity, which change with the size of the material40, 41, 42. Among them, metal and semiconductor nanomaterials, under the influence of quantum confinement and local surface plasmon resonance effects, exhibit linear absorption, photoluminescence emission, and nonlinear optical properties43. Plasmons originate from the collective excitation of conducting electrons in metals, and depending on the boundary conditions, they can be classified as bulk protons, surface propagating protons, and surface localized protons. Bulk protons cannot be excited by visible light under the action of longitudinal properties, while surface-propagating protons can propagate along the metal surface in a waveguide manner. When nanomaterials are exposed to visible light, the conducting electrons oscillate under the action of the oscillating electric field, the electron cloud shifts relative to the atomic nucleus, and the Coulomb force between the electron and the atomic nucleus generates a restoring force, causing the electron cloud to oscillate relative to the nucleus42. This process will generate uncompensated charges on the surface of the nanomaterials. Since the polarization of the nanomaterial surface is the main cause of the generation of the restoring force, these oscillations are called surface protons44.

Generally, compounds with magnetism contain “magnetic elements” in their molecular formula, namely iron (Fe), cobalt (Co), or nickel (Ni). However, in nanostructures, due to the uneven distribution of electrons, some nanomaterials also exhibit magnetism32,44,45, and the size, shape, and composition of the materials can affect magnetism27,46. Superparamagnetism is one of the interesting phenomena related to the size of nanomaterials. As the size of the nanomaterials decreases, the magnetic anisotropy of each nanoparticle also decreases46. When a magnetic field exists, superparamagnetic nanomaterials will show a high degree of magnetization. Once the magnetic field is removed, they will not retain any magnetization47. Compared with studies on size effects, there are far fewer studies on the influence of shape on the magnetic properties of the same volume of nanomaterials. One study found that there was a significant difference in magnetism between cube-shaped and spherical CoFe2O4 nanomaterials of the same volume48. Due to the different curvatures, the oxygen atoms in cube-shaped CoFe2O4 nanomaterials are less missing, and it is speculated that this leads to a smaller surface gravitational force of the cube structure, thus resulting in lower magnetism49 (Fig. 2).

Clinical translation challenges and next-generation nano platform

Because nanomaterials possess unique size and physical-chemical properties, they can be used for the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of some diseases. Although basic experiments have demonstrated the clinical potential of nanomaterials, there are still a series of challenges in the process of clinical transformation. Firstly, targeting failure and clearance mechanisms can lead to a significant reduction in the accumulation of nanomedicines at the lesion site. For example, the liver and spleen reticuloendothelial system (RES) can clear over 90% of intravenously injected nanomedicines, with only approximately 0.7% of the dose reaching the tumor site50; abnormal vascular structures in solid tumors (such as gaps >400 nm) may lead to insufficient extravasation of nanoparticles, and the actual contribution of the EPR effect is only 0.001%‒5% of the injected dose51; the acidic environment of lysosomes can destroy 60% of endocytic nanocarriers, and pH-sensitive lipids are required for endosomal escape52. Secondly, the limitation of tissue penetration causes a sharp decline in the delivery efficiency of nanomedicines. For example, the dense matrix of pancreatic cancer causes the penetration depth of nanomedicines to be less than 50 μm, only one-third of that in the liver cancer model51; tight junction proteins (claudin-5) in the blood‒brain barrier can lead to a delivery efficiency of less than 3% in the central nervous system53. At the same time, large-scale production and quality control can also affect the clinical transformation of nanomaterials. For example, after amplification by microfluidic technology, the particle size distribution of nanoparticles expands from 50 ± 5 to 80 ± 20 nm, resulting in a clinical batch drug efficacy deviation greater than 30%54; fluctuations of the encapsulation rate of lipid nanoparticles (LNP) ± 15% can cause a decrease in the consistency of drug efficacy54; if the purity of membrane protein extraction from biomimetic nanoparticles fluctuates by more than 20%, the targeting function of nanoparticles will be poor in repeatability, and nanoparticles still face batch-to-batch differences55. The safety issue of nanomaterials has also become one of the factors restricting their clinical transformation. Studies have shown that 40% of the nanomaterials entering the body are excreted through the liver and gallbladder, and long-term accumulation may induce liver fibrosis50; PEG-modified nanomedicines accelerate blood clearance (ABC phenomenon), and repeated administration can reduce their efficiency by 60%; carbon nanotubes can induce the release of complement C5a, leading to neutrophil infiltration; and silver nanoparticles, due to their broad-spectrum antibacterial properties, may disrupt the intestinal flora balance. In addition, there are other risks associated with the continuous interaction between nanomaterials and biological systems, such as their impact on long-term immune homeostasis, which is the precise balance maintained by the immune system between eliminating pathogens and maintaining self-tolerance56. Nanomaterials may disrupt this balance. They may provide prolonged, low-level co-stimulatory signals, causing unnecessary immune responses, inducing autoimmune diseases or allergies, or inducing antigen-specific immune tolerance, weakening the normal immune response of the body. The gut microbiota is also closely related to systemic immunity57,58. When nanomaterials are administered orally or through intravenous injection and then enter the gut through the hepatic-biliary circulation, certain nanomaterials directly come into contact with the gut microbiota, which may kill beneficial bacteria, leading to a decrease in microbiota diversity and ecological imbalance, and may also damage the bacterial cell membranes, affecting their metabolic activity, or develop drug resistance. On the other hand, nanomaterials may disrupt the intestinal epithelial barrier and increase intestinal permeability, making bacteria and other substances more likely to enter the bloodstream, thereby activating the systemic immune system and driving chronic inflammatory responses. Chronic inflammation is also associated with many major diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, metabolic syndrome, neurodegenerative diseases, cancer, etc.59. Future research can improve the delivery method of nanomaterials, enhance delivery efficiency, optimize production processes, improve safety, and accelerate the clinical transformation of nanomaterials.

To address the various issues encountered in the process of transforming nanomaterials from the laboratory to clinical application, the following tasks need to be carried out: Firstly, design target nanomaterials based on clinical requirements such as overcoming drug resistance, crossing barriers, reducing toxicity, targeting patient populations, and ideal administration routes; establish a comprehensive preclinical evaluation system; better predict the interaction between nanomaterials and the human immune system; and conduct systematic evaluations of the safety, biological distribution, metabolic pathways, etc. of the materials in vitro and in vivo. Then, establish a stable manufacturing and control system, design reproducible and scalable production processes, select raw materials with strict quality standards, test key indicators such as particle size distribution, potential, drug loading, release rate, etc., and develop specialized methods to characterize the physical and chemical properties, purity, stability, etc., of nanomaterials. Next, conduct phased clinical trials to assess the safety and tolerance of nanomaterials, study their pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, actively explore combined treatment regimens with existing standard therapies, and achieve synergistic effects. Finally, in each stage of preclinical research and clinical trials, formulate detailed risk management plans, monitor, prevent, and develop corresponding countermeasures for potential immune-related adverse reactions. Although there are still significant challenges in the process of transforming from the laboratory to clinical application, with the continuous development of nanomaterials, more and more nanomaterial immunotherapies are becoming a solid force in the field of tumor immunology, bringing new hope to patients.

In the field of medicine, the deep integration of AI and nanomaterial technology, leveraging the powerful high-dimensional data processing capabilities, efficient high-performance computing, and cutting-edge deep learning algorithm innovations of AI, is giving rise to a series of revolutionary breakthroughs, injecting strong impetus into key areas such as precision medicine, personalized treatment plan customization, and new drug research and development, and demonstrating unlimited application prospects and profound value. A class organ is a three-dimensional cell culture model that can reproduce the unique structure and important functions of certain organs. However, due to size limitations and metabolite accumulation, class organs are difficult to replicate the uniqueness of real organs. To overcome this limitation, Gong et al.60 designed a class organ chip including a micron-scale vascular network, using 3D printing technology. By simulating dynamic blood flow, it achieved nutrient supply and liquid infiltration, overcoming the size limitation caused by insufficient nutrition in class organs, providing new insights for designing and producing tumor and normal tissue models in vitro. Using AI algorithms, genomic screening and nanomedicine target selection can be carried out to design the best nanomedicines or multifunctional nanocarriers61. Some studies have used the random forest classifier algorithm in machine learning to test and analyze the specific omics characteristics of patients and identify cancer genes for classification62. Another study used machine learning to design and optimize spherical nucleic acids (SNA). SNA is a type of nanomaterial that can be used for immune regulation. Through AI algorithm screening and development of more efficient and effective SNA and evaluation of its various properties, it is of great significance for subsequent research63,64. Nowadays, the problem of antimicrobial drug resistance (AMR) is constantly emerging. AI intelligent nanoscale biological sensors can replace traditional AMR detection methods, achieving rapid, real-time, sensitive, and convenient solutions. Nanoscale biological sensors can conduct personalized monitoring and on-site detection, significantly shortening processing time, reducing labor costs, improving modern medical practice, and opening up pathways for alternative therapies and personalized medical methods65. The deep integration of AI and nanomaterials demonstrates unprecedented potential. AI not only accelerates the research and development process of nanomaterials through high-throughput screening and molecular design, but also optimizes nanomedicine delivery systems. At the same time, nanomaterials provide powerful tools for AI-driven personalized medicine. Looking to the future, with the continuous advancement of technology and strengthened interdisciplinary collaboration, the combination of AI and nanomaterials is expected to further revolutionize tumor treatment models, moving from traditional therapies to intelligent, personalized, and efficient directions, providing assistance for the cure and improvement of the quality of life of cancer patients worldwide.

The strategy targeting APCs

In the current immunotherapy process, there is often a problem of low antigen presentation efficiency, which makes it difficult to fully activate the immune response. To overcome this challenge, many studies have focused on the application of nanomaterials. Through ingenious design, nanomaterials have achieved targeted delivery to different types of APCs. The following will introduce several common strategies for targeting APCs with nanomaterials, including tumor microenvironment targeting, exogenous stimulation, and stimulus-responsive release (Fig. 3).

The strategies targeting APCs can be classified into three types: tumor microenvironment targeting, exogenous stimulation, and stimulus-responsive release. Tumor microenvironment targeting mainly includes passive targeting strategies such as the EPR effect and active targeting strategies. Exogenous stimulation strategies mainly include the magnetic heating effect and the photothermal effect. Stimulus-responsive release strategies include nanomaterials that are pH-sensitive, glutathione-sensitive, reactive oxygen species-sensitive, and enzyme-sensitive. This Figure was drawn by Figdraw.

The targeting of the tumor microenvironment can be classified into passive targeting and active targeting. In the passive targeting strategy, the EPR effect is one of the key mechanisms66,67. For instance, nanomaterials can take advantage of the EPR effect at the tumor site. By manipulating the physical and chemical properties such as size and charge of the nanocarriers, they can more easily accumulate in the tumor tissue and be subsequently taken up by the APCs in that area, thereby enhancing the immune response and improving the efficacy of anti-tumor immunotherapy68, 69, 70, 71. In the active targeting strategy, a common method is to attach specific ligands to the surface of the nanomaterials to bind with the receptors on the surface of APCs72. For example, nanoparticles modified with mannose ligands can specifically target the mannose receptors on the surface of macrophages73. When these modified nanoparticles enter the body, they can be recognized and taken up by macrophages, thereby achieving efficient delivery of the antigen within the macrophages, activating their antigen-presenting function and effectively triggering the immune response74,75. The folate-modified nanomaterials can target the TME expressing folate receptors. By binding to the folate receptors, they can promote the uptake of the nanomaterials and the antigens they carry by DCs, enhancing the antigen presentation efficiency and thereby activating the T cell immune response, exerting anti-tumor effects76, 77, 78, 79. The pairing of proteins and transferrin receptors is also a typical example of active targeting80. The transferrin receptors are abundantly expressed on highly invasive tumor cells. After attaching transferrin, a ligand of transferrin receptors, to the surface of nanomaterials, the nanomaterials can specifically bind to the transferrin receptors on tumor cells, thereby achieving precise delivery of antigens or drugs81, 82, 83. Similarly, by developing corresponding ligand-modified nanomaterials targeting specific receptors on the cell membrane, such as CD44, active targeting of APCs or tumor cells can be achieved, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy84, 85, 86, 87. There are also studies that use the cell membranes of APCs or tumor cells to coat nanospheres, and through the receptors expressed on the membranes, achieve active targeting of specific immune cells for the delivery of immunomodulators or antigens, etc.88, 89, 90.

In terms of targeting of exogenous stimuli to APCs, magnetic nanomaterials such as magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles can be guided to distribute and aggregate in the body by an external magnetic field. In immunotherapy, antigens can be combined with magnetic nanoparticles, and then the magnetic field can be used to localize them to specific lymph nodes or immune organs, allowing the antigens to accumulate around APCs in that area, improving antigen presentation efficiency, and thereby enhancing the immune response91, 92, 93. Moreover, magnetic nanomaterials can also be combined with other treatment methods such as magnetic hyperthermia therapy, further enhancing the therapeutic effect on tumors, and at the same time, through precise targeting of APCs, activating the immune system and exerting a synergistic therapeutic effect94. Photothermal nanomaterials are another type of exogenous stimulation targeting strategy. For example, some photothermal conversion materials86,95,96 can convert light energy into heat energy under near-infrared light irradiation, raising the temperature of tumor tissues locally. On the one hand, this directly kills tumor cells; on the other hand, it promotes the release of tumor-related antigens, while enhancing the activity and function of local APCs and activating the body's anti-tumor immune response, providing an efficient photothermal immunological synergistic treatment strategy for tumor treatment. It should be noted that in this section, the introduction of exogenous stimuli targeting APCs is not that the nanomaterials directly target APCs through magnetic, photothermal, etc. strategies. Instead, because the designed magnetic or photothermal nanomaterials change the TME (tumor microenvironment), such as by raising the temperature, they induce the death of tumor cells, promote the release of tumor-associated antigens, and indirectly activate APCs, thereby activating the body's anti-tumor immune response.

The strategies for triggering responsive release can be broadly classified into pH-sensitive nanomaterials, glutathione (GSH) sensitive nanomaterials, reactive oxygen species (ROS) sensitive nanomaterials, and enzyme-sensitive nanomaterials, etc.97,98 pH-sensitive nanomaterials can precisely release antigens based on the low pH characteristic of the tumor microenvironment75,79,83,99. For instance, they can release antigens by undergoing structural changes to enhance the local immune response. The intracellular GSH concentration in tumor cells and certain immune cells is relatively high, so GSH-sensitive nanomaterials can be designed. In a high GSH environment, the nanoparticles will disintegrate and release the antigens they are loaded with100,101. ROS-sensitive nanomaterials can also be used for targeting APCs. In inflammatory sites or during the activation of certain immune cells, the ROS level will increase. By encapsulating antigens in ROS-sensitive nanocarriers, when the nanoparticles reach regions with high ROS expression, they will trigger the degradation or structural changes of the nanocarriers, achieving controlled release of the antigens102, 103, 104. Enzyme-sensitive nanomaterials can release antigens in response to the presence of specific enzymes. For example, in the tumor microenvironment or certain immune cells, there are specific enzymes. By introducing their sensitive structures into the nanocarrier structure, when the nanoparticles enter regions with a higher level of enzymes, the sensitive structures will be cleaved by the enzymes, resulting in the destruction of the nanocarrier structure and the release of antigens105, 106, 107.

The three core strategies for nanomaterials to reshape the functions of APCs

Enhancing the efficiency and specificity of the immune response is the ultimate goal of the action of nanomaterials against antigen-presenting cells. Nanomaterials can act as carriers, protecting the antigens encapsulated within them from degradation108 and improving the uptake efficiency of APCs (antigen-presenting cells), thereby influencing the processing and presentation of antigens and activating T cells, triggering a strong immune response109. In addition, nanomaterials can also be functionalized to carry immune regulatory factors, such as cytokines or co-stimulatory molecules110, further regulating the intensity of the immune response by modulating the activation of APCs. Here, we summarize the regulatory effects of nanomaterials on APCs (dendritic cells, macrophages, and B lymphocytes) in the TIME, the immune effects produced by these influences, the corresponding mechanisms, and the potential value of nanomaterials in cancer immunotherapy.

Dendritic cells (DCs) activation: From antigen presentation to T cell priming

Dendritic cells (DCs) consist of a group of functionally specialized myeloid antigen-presenting cells. In the TIME, they can recognize and eliminate tumor cells and have the ability to restructure anti-tumor immunity and coordinate innate and adaptive immunity. In innate immunity, DCs produce protective cytokines (IL-6, IL-12) to regulate the sustained immune response. As antigen-presenting cells, DCs take up tumor antigens in the TIME and transfer them to immature T cells111,112, inducing T cell differentiation into effector T cells with various functions, generating immune-stimulating cytokines that can regulate the TIME, and ultimately causing the generation of tumor-specific cellular and humoral responses3,4,113. In addition, DCs also interact with other immune cells (such as NK cells, macrophages, and B cells), thereby generating a powerful and complete immune response112,114. To more systematically discuss the effects of nanomaterials on DCs in the TIME, we classified them into three categories based on the nature of the nanomaterials: inorganic nanomaterials, carbon-based nanomaterials, and organic nanomaterials. We will introduce the specific effects of each category of nanomaterials on DCs (Fig. 4).

The effects of inorganic nanomaterials, carbon-based nanomaterials and organic nanomaterials on dendritic cells. Inorganic nanoparticles mainly consist of metal nanoparticles and inorganic non-metallic nanoparticles such as MXP@OC, which can serve as carriers to load and deliver antigens, promoting the maturation of DCs. Carbon-based nanoparticles such as NY-ESO-1, CBP, PM, etc., enhance antigen presentation by regulating the expression of cell surface molecules, thereby promoting the progression of the immune response. Organic nanomaterials such as SA-Pchs-LNP, PD-K, etc., after modification, can be used as carriers to deliver antigens, prolonging the circulation time of the particles in the body, and effectively activating the immune response. The effects of inorganic nanomaterials on dendritic cells

Inorganic nanomaterials include metal nanoparticles and inorganic non-metal nanoparticles, which can affect the maturation, activation, and antigen presentation functions of DCs. For instance, Xu et al.115 synthesized chiral gold nanoparticles with a high optical anisotropy factor (g-factor) by irradiating with circularly polarized light (CPL). The results showed that when these nanoparticles combined with differentiation cluster 97 (CD97) and epidermal growth factor-like module receptor 1 (EMR1), they could open the mechanically sensitive potassium efflux channel and form an immune signaling complex known as the inflammasome, which could promote the maturation of mouse bone marrow-derived dendritic cells and trigger a strong immune response. To improve the weak immunogenicity of DC-mediated immunotherapy in tumors, Zhang et al.116 prepared a nanoplatform (MXP) based on Ti3C2 MXene, which has strong near-infrared photothermal conversion and antigen loading capacity. It can be used as an endogenous substance to induce tumor cell immunogenic cell death through photothermal effect and can also be used as an exogenous nanovaccine (MXP@OC) to deliver antigen ovalbumin (OVA) and CpG-modified oligodeoxynucleotide (CpG-ODN) as agonists to enhance the activation of DCs117. In summary, inorganic metal nanoparticles can stimulate the maturation of DCs, induce their activation, and present antigens to T cells to trigger a strong anti-tumor immune response. Inorganic non-metal nanoparticles, with their unique physical and chemical properties such as porous structure and chemical stability, can efficiently load and deliver antigens as carriers and play an important role in immunotherapy. The effects of carbon-based nanomaterials on dendritic cells

Carbon-based nanomaterials include carbon nanotubes, graphene, etc., which possess unique structures and mechanical and electrical properties. They can directly stimulate or act as antigen carriers to influence the functions of DCs. For instance, de Haar et al.127 used ultrafine carbon black particles (CBP) as an adjuvant to study its effects on DCs and co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86. The results showed that CBP combined with OVA induced the proliferation of CD4+ T cells in DO11.10 mice, led to the massive production of immunogenic sensitizing cytokines in the peribronchial lymph nodes (PBLN), increased the number of myeloid dendritic cells (mDC) in PBLN and the expression of co-stimulatory molecules CD80/CD86, and directly induced the maturation of DCs. Porter et al.128 studied the effects of two types of air particulate matter (PM) derived from automobiles, namely enriched particles from CEP (Car Enriched Particles) and DEP (Diesel Engine Particles), on the activation of DCs, and found that DEP increased the uptake of model antigens by DCs, exhibiting a non-classical activation pattern of DCs, enhanced the expression of CD40 and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) II molecules on the cell surface, and increased the production of cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-12, IFN-γ, and vascular endothelial growth factor. Some studies have also utilized multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) from carbon-based nanomaterials as antigen carriers, combined with TLR9 agonist CpG-ODN, to form an anti-tumor vaccine composed of the testicular cancer antigen NY-ESO-1129. The vaccine was rapidly taken up by DCs in vitro and in vivo, activated DCs, and released antigens, inducing antigen-specific immune responses mediated by CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells, significantly inhibiting the growth of B16F10 melanoma and prolonging the survival time of mice. However, not all carbon-based nanomaterials have the same effect on DCs. Tkach et al.130 compared three carbon-based nanomaterials with different shapes and properties, namely planar negatively charged graphene oxide (GO), spherical non-charged C60-fullerene, and spherical negatively charged C60-TRIS fullerene. Among them, C60-fullerene promoted the expression of MHC and co-stimulatory molecules on the surface of DCs, enhancing the ability of DCs to activate antigen-specific T cells, while GO downregulated the level of immunoproteasome LMP7, inhibiting the processing of protein antigens by the immunoproteasome in DCs, reducing antigen cross-presentation and T cell responses. The results suggested that when using carbon-based nanomaterials to enhance the immune activation ability or tolerance function of DCs in clinical applications, the influence of the diversity of their surface properties on the function of DCs needs to be considered. Furthermore, there are also relevant studies indicating that carbon-based nanomaterial derivatives of fullerenes, due to their strong antioxidant properties, can act as antioxidants for T cells, inhibiting the activation and proliferation of signals related to T cell receptors130,131. Overall, carbon nanotubes in carbon-based nanomaterials are often used as carriers to deliver antigens and regulate their functions after being taken up by DCs. Carbon black nanoparticles directly stimulate DCs, regulate the expression of surface molecules, enhance their antigen-presenting ability, and enhance immune responses. These research results make it possible to use carbon-based nanomaterials as immune adjuvants for cancer detection and treatment (Table 2).

The effect of organic nanomaterials on dendritic cells

Organic nanomaterials include peptide nanoparticles, lipid nanoparticles (LNP), polymer nanoparticles, etc. Some of them can effectively deliver antigens to DCs, thereby promoting the progression of the immune response. For example, Su et al.139 were inspired by the fact that bacteria containing d-amino acids are closely related to the activation of antibacterial immune responses and the protection of the host and developed chiral peptide nanoparticles PL-K and PD-K as nanoadjuvants for cancer nanovaccines. The results showed that compared with PL-K-OVA, PD-K-OVA could more efficiently activate DCs maturation, induce antigen cross-presentation, and thereby activate the specific T-cell-mediated immune response. Tang et al.140 developed mRNA vaccines (SA-Pchs-LNPs) that can target DCs and achieve effective escape from the endosome/lysosome, providing ideas for the development of DC-targeted vaccines. Researchers modified LNP with sialic acid (SA) and polyethylene glycol (PEG), and SA interacted with the sialic acid expressed by DCs and bound to immunoglobulin-like lectin-1 (Siglec-1), which was quickly captured by DCs and escaped from the early endosome. The lipid modification prevented the rapid clearance of the nanocarriers by the blood. The vaccine targeted DCs in the spleen and lymph nodes of B16 OVA melanoma-bearing mice, activated and produced a large number of antigen-specific T cells, initiated tumor immune responses, and effectively inhibited tumor growth. Chen et al.141 designed a hybrid membrane (HM) nanoparticle vaccine (HM-NPs) based on patient-removed tumor tissue to achieve the purpose of specifically triggering anti-tumor immune responses without obvious adverse reactions. The vaccine base was PLGA, using bacterial cytoplasmic membranes (EMs) and tumor cell membranes (TM) as antigens and adjuvants, promoting their maturation after entering DCs, stimulating cytokine secretion, and thereby activating T cells in the spleen, enhancing innate and adaptive immunity, and triggering a strong tumor-specific immune response in various mouse tumor models, inducing tumor regression. In summary, peptide nanoparticles and lipid nanoparticles have good biocompatibility, can avoid the degradation of therapeutic drugs before reaching the target site, and some can accumulate in the lymph nodes to enhance the antigen-presenting function of DCs and activate the immune system to inhibit tumor growth. Biodegradable polymer nanoparticles can efficiently load antigens, and after modification, they can load different adjuvants, integrate other treatment methods, and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy. Organic nanomaterials based on these characteristics have significant advantages in the development of cancer vaccines and cancer immunotherapy (Table 3).

Macrophage reprogramming: Breaking the constraint of M2 polarization

Macrophages are a diverse collection of cell types that have a wide range of functions in both homeostasis and pathological conditions6. In TIME, macrophages are generally referred to as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which include multiple subgroups from different sources. Under the stimulation of various cytokines in TIME, macrophages can polarize into two forms, exhibiting different functions: Type M1 macrophages prevent cancer progression by directly engulfing tumor cells or by generating immunostimulatory factors to activate anti-tumor immune responses5, 6, 7. Type M2 macrophages can exert anti-inflammatory and tumor-promoting effects by promoting angiogenesis, metastasis formation, therapeutic resistance, and the production of immunosuppressive factors152, 153, 154, 155, 156. Transforming M2-type macrophages into M1-type macrophages can enhance the secretion of cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, activate anti-tumor immune responses, and thereby restructure the TIME113,157,158. The regulatory effect of nanoparticles on macrophages mainly lies in regulating the polarization of macrophages, some nanomaterials promote the transformation of macrophages into the M1-type, thereby exerting anti-tumor immune effects. This can be specifically achieved through two ways of regulating macrophage polarization: through the classical pathway and the non-classical pathway (Fig. 5). Some studies have shown that by modifying nanomaterials or loading functional active components onto them, it is possible to regulate the polarization of macrophages to the M2 phenotype. This M2 polarization effect has been proven to support applications in treating inflammatory diseases157, inhibiting oxidative stress158, and promoting wound healing159.

Nanomaterials regulate macrophage polarization through two pathways: the classical pathway and the non-classical pathway. The classical pathway refers to the situation where nanoparticles are modified and then regulate macrophage polarization towards the M1 phenotype by influencing cellular pathways, delivering miRNA or siRNA, etc. The non-classical pathway includes generating reactive oxygen species, encapsulating nanoparticles in biological membranes or membrane-like structures, using natural substances, and other methods to regulate macrophage polarization. Regulation of macrophage polarization through classical pathways

Nanoparticles act as delivery carriers and regulate macrophage polarization through classic pathways such as NF-κB, STAT protein family, and Notch. Bacterial DNA containing CpG motifs and oligodeoxynucleotides (ODNs) has significant immunostimulatory effects on various subgroups of immune cells. CpG-ODNs transmit signals by interacting with TLR9, upregulate the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, induce the activity of CD4+ T cells, and trigger cytotoxic CD8+ T cell responses in the body. However, CpG-ODNs are degraded by nucleases in the body, and long-term application may lead to autoimmune diseases, liver, and kidney toxicity. To improve their stability, Cai et al.108 developed a nanomedicine virus-like particle (VLP) platform based on plant viruses, encapsulating CpG-ODNs within VLPs, targeting and delivering them to TAMs, inducing the phagocytic activity of macrophages, thereby exerting anti-tumor effects. These results have shown excellent therapeutic effects in mouse colon cancer and melanoma models, demonstrating great clinical prospects.

MicroRNA (miRNA) is a non-coding single-stranded RNA molecule widely present in eukaryotes that can be delivered into TIME to regulate TAM polarization and exert anti-tumor effects. Zang et al.160 designed calcium phosphate nanoparticles (CaP/miR@MNPs) for delivering immunomodulatory miRNA to macrophages. The pH-responsive material PEG2k-CDM-DOPE modified with nanoparticles enables PEG to shield the nanoparticles at physiological pH and separate them in the acidic tumor microenvironment. Subsequently, conjugated with mannan, miR155 is targeted and delivered to TAM, reactivating tumor-promoting TAM into anti-tumor macrophages, reversing the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and inhibiting the growth of S180 sarcoma. Lactic acid is the product of aerobic glycolysis in most solid tumors, which is the main reason for maintaining the acidic environment of the TME. Li et al.161 synthesized nanoparticles based on mesoporous organosilicon, loaded with hydroxyl camptothecin (HCPT) and monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT-4) inhibitory siRNA (siMCT-4). Lipid modification prolonged its circulation time in the blood, and the redox-responsive property enabled its release in tumor cells. The nanoparticles combined with siRNA silencing the expression of MCT-4 through siRNA therapy reduced lactic acid excretion, induced tumor cell apoptosis, and polarized TAM to the M1 phenotype, restoring the activity of CD8+ T cells, significantly removing the immunosuppressive TME. Moreover, the particles also enhanced the infiltration of immune-promoting cells in tumor tissues, inhibited tumor growth, induced the release of immune-promoting factors, and activated the immune response to inhibit lung metastasis of tumor cells. Some nanoparticles have multifaceted regulatory effects on macrophages, which can polarize macrophages to the M1 phenotype by influencing signaling pathways and transcription factors, and some nanoparticles can affect the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines by macrophages, thereby regulating the immune response. Nanomaterials regulate macrophage polarization through the classical pathway and reverse the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, providing a strong theoretical and experimental basis for the development of anti-cancer nanomedicines (Table 4).

Regulation of macrophage polarization through non-classical pathways

The non-classical pathways by which nanoparticles regulate macrophage polarization include generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), modifying biological membranes or membrane-like structures, using nanoparticles derived from natural substances, and other macrophage mechanisms. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are a class of highly reactive oxygen-containing compounds that can be generated through the Fenton reaction mediated by iron-based nanoparticles, including superoxide anion (O2‒), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl radical (·OH), etc. Zhang et al.169 reported a chiral nanozyme that was synthesized by modifying with chiral cysteine and composed of chiral mixed metal sulfides MoS2/CoS2 nanozymes with peroxidase (POD)-like and catalase (CAT)-like activities. The results showed that D-NPs exhibited more favorable pharmacokinetic properties and lower cellular uptake and, through POD-like activity, led to the production of extracellular OH, effectively transforming TAM from the M2 polarization to the anti-tumor M1 phenotype. At the same time, the nanozyme also exhibited similar CAT-like activity, decomposing H2O2 into oxygen to alleviate the hypoxic environment of the tumor, further promoting the M1 polarization of TAMs, and ultimately activating the T-cell-mediated anti-tumor immune response, significantly inhibiting tumor growth. Liu et al.170 developed a hybrid nanoparticle of iron-magnetic vortex domain iron oxide nanorings and graphene oxide (FVIOs-GO) to overcome the drawbacks of magnetic hyperthermia therapy. The FVIOs-GO-mediated magnetic hyperthermia therapy can trigger ICD, promote the polarization of TAMs from the M2 phenotype to the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, and simultaneously increase the infiltration of T lymphocytes in the TME. The efficient heat conversion of FVIOs-GO enables the enhancement of toxic ·OH and other ROS production by Fe3O4 nanoparticles, jointly inducing apoptosis of breast cancer cells. There are also relevant reports that provide a more detailed introduction to iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs)171. The reports indicate that IONPs can be involved in multimodal immunotherapy strategies. Specifically, they can polarize macrophages to the M1 phenotype172, activate the STING pathway and induce cell immunogenic ferroptosis, reprogram the immunosuppressive microenvironment173; target lymph nodes using the particles174, use cancer cell membranes for modification to achieve tumor homing and precise drug delivery175; monitor the migration of immune cells through magnetic particle imaging176, and promote the development of real-time diagnosis and treatment. Nanoparticles with membrane or membrane-like structures exhibit good biocompatibility, including natural cell membrane nanoparticles such as red blood cells and natural killer (NK) cell membranes; synthetic membrane-like nanoparticles such as liposomes and biomimetic nanoparticles; and membrane structure nanoparticles derived from extracellular vesicles, etc. Deng et al.177 reported a nanoparticle NK-NPs that used NK cell membranes to hide the loading of the photosensitizer TCPP. The NK-NPs targeted tumor cells with NK cell membranes, induced M1-type macrophage polarization, and produced anti-tumor immunity. The photosensitizer TCPP loaded on the nanoparticles DL through photodynamic therapy induced ICD, enhanced the tumor immune infiltration of effector T cells, improved anti-tumor immune efficiency, eliminated the primary tumor growth and inhibited the development of distant tumors. In the field of liver cell carcinoma (HCC) treatment, Han et al.152 conducted a study. They used HCC cell membranes and M2 macrophage-binding peptide (M2pep)-modified PLGA nanoparticles as carriers to load d-lactic acid (DL), successfully constructing the DL@NP-M2pep nanocapsule. This nanoparticle can precisely target and deliver DL to M2 TAM. DL can interact with TLR2 and/or TLR9 on the surface of macrophages, effectively promoting the polarization of macrophages from the M2 type to the M1 type, showing significant anti-tumor growth effects in xenograft models and in situ HCC mouse models induced by carcinogens, reshaping the immunosuppressive TME, and prolonging the survival time of mice. Furthermore, when this nanoparticle is combined with the anti-CD47 antibody (α-CD47, which can block the “don't eat me” signal), the experimental results show that compared to using either drug alone, the combined use of the two can significantly enhance the anti-HCC efficacy, providing a highly promising combined treatment strategy for HCC treatment. Some substances existing in nature have unique biological activities and have attracted attention in the field of regulating macrophages and tumor treatment. For example, Wan et al.178 developed a therapeutic hydrogel (UF@MRP) with PEG-crosslinked bee venom peptide scaffolds, which encapsulated the concentrated ultrafiltrate of tumor cell culture medium (RT-UF). UF@MRP effectively reshaped the TME by stimulating the maturation of DCs and reprogramming macrophages into the M1 phenotype. In various mouse tumor models such as B16F10 melanoma, Lewis lung cancer, and MC38 colon cancer, UF@MRP enhanced the efficacy of the anti-PD-1 antibody immune checkpoint blocker and prolonged the survival of mice. B-cell regulation: Balancing antibody production and immune tolerance

B cells, as key players in humoral immunity, can exert anti-tumor effects through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and complement activation. In various solid tumors, B cells migrate to the tumor-infiltrating microenvironment (TIME) and form tumor-infiltrating B cells (TIL-Bs)190,191. TIL-Bs exert anti-tumor immune effects by producing antibodies, presenting antigens, and generating factors that regulate other immune cells5,8,192,193. These functions play a crucial role in the complex process of tumor immune surveillance and immune escape, profoundly influencing tumor growth and metastasis190,194,195 (Fig. 6).

The regulatory effects of nanomaterials on B cells, including the use of lipid nanoparticles to deliver mRNA, or the modification of TLR4 and TLR7/8 agonists on nanoparticles to further trigger an immune response, enhance the B cells' ability to uptake antigens, activate the B cell function, and trigger a powerful Th1 cell immune response, or they can bind to the inhibitory co-receptor CD22 on B cells, inhibiting the function of B cells and inducing immune tolerance to delay the development of autoimmune diseases. Alameh et al.196 designed lipid nanoparticles (LNP) encapsulating nucleotide-modified mRNA vaccines, mRNA-LNP. The vaccine stimulates germinal center B cells, memory B cell responses, and long-lived plasma cells (LLPC), mediates the production of cytokine IL-6, efficiently induces Tfh cells, and triggers strong cellular and humoral immune responses. Modifying mRNA with nucleosides can prevent immune recognition and cause uncontrollable inflammation, but this modification also has limitations. To some extent, it eliminates the innate immune response that coordinates adaptive immunity. To address this issue, Han et al.197 developed a vaccine based on the LNP component of TLR7/8 agonists as a lipid adjuvant to enhance the adjuvant performance of mRNA-LNP vaccines. The results showed that the auxiliary lipid compounds enhanced mRNA delivery, could promote the activation of DCs in lymph nodes and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and significantly enhanced the innate immune ability of the vaccine by the TLR7/8 agonist activity. The lipid-aided vaccine also established a more efficient adaptive immune response against SARS-CoV-2, mainly including a strong Th1 cell immune response, antibody response, B cell response, and LLPC response. To predict the immunogenicity of candidate vaccines and strengthen the preclinical evaluation of vaccines, Jeger-Madiot et al.198 developed a lymphoid organ chip (LO chip) model to simulate the memory B cell response to evaluate mRNA vaccines. In the chip, the simulated antigen capture activates myeloid cells in the early stage, subsequently activates antigen-specific CD4+ T cells and B cells, forming a CD4+ T cell/B cell cluster, and the differentiation and migration of plasmablasts simulate the maturation of B cells in lymphoid tissues. Microfluidic perfusion promotes the massive expansion of antigen-specific memory B cells and PB, enabling the detection of secreted antibodies and their ability assessment. Silva et al.199 designed an immune-stimulating complex, ISCOMs, incorporating the TLR4 agonist monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA) and synthesized glycyrrhizin/MPLA nanoparticles, SMNP, to evaluate the synergistic effect of glycyrrhizin and TLR agonists. The results showed that the adjuvant containing glycyrrhizin exhibited the strongest humoral immune response, rapidly induced the production of a large number of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhanced the innate immune activation of the draining lymph nodes. Glycyrrhizin combined with a TLR4 agonist changed the lymphatic blood flow and lymph node permeability, enhanced the uptake of antigens by B cells in the draining lymph nodes, and enhanced the immune response. Nanoparticles show great potential in regulating B lymphocytes, through different modification methods, they can activate B cell functions and trigger strong innate and adaptive immune responses against viral invasion, providing new ideas for the development of cancer nanovaccines.

B cells play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases. Based on this discovery, some nanoparticles have been designed to inhibit the function of B cells for therapeutic purposes. For instance, researchers such as Brzezicka developed a hybrid nanoparticle that induces tolerance in T and B lymphocytes. The NP lipid bilayer encapsulated PLGA particles loaded with rapamycin, CD22L-NP(R)-OVA, with the lipid bilayer presenting the ligands of the protein antigen and the B cell inhibitory co-receptor CD22 (CD22L), working together to inhibit B cells from recognizing antigens. In mouse models, the nanoparticles could suppress rheumatoid arthritis. When combined with glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (GPI) and promoting the differentiation of Tregs, it inhibits antibody production and reduces the number of plasma cells, thereby inducing strong immune tolerance and delaying the progression of autoimmune diseases200. Álvarez et al.201 developed Nile red-labeled poly(lactic acid) nanoparticles (NR-PLA NPs), combined with the JAK protein inhibitor baricitinib (BARI), to inhibit specific proteins JAK1 and JAK2 in B cells. The results showed that BARI-NR-PLA NPs reduced the differentiation of B cells into plasma cells, the production of IgG/IgM and cytokines IL-10 and IL-6, significantly inhibited the JAK/STAT pathway, and provided potential therapeutic value for systemic lupus erythematosus treatment. Other studies have shown that fullerene derivatives, due to their strong antioxidant properties, have an inhibitory effect on B cell activation, inhibiting the production of reactive oxygen species and IgE in the B cell activation signaling pathway, and this anti-inflammatory mechanism can play a beneficial role in allergies and autoimmune diseases202.

Nanoparticles have shown great potential in regulating B lymphocytes. By modifying them through various means, their functions can be activated or inhibited. Some nanoparticles trigger strong innate and adaptive immune responses to combat viral invasion, while others induce immune tolerance to delay the development of autoimmune diseases. The ultimate goal is to enhance therapeutic effects and improve disease conditions, bringing hope for the development of more effective nanoparticles.

Clinical applications of nanomaterials

Anti-cancer nanomedicines refer to drugs that use nanotechnology to prepare anti-cancer drugs into particles with nanoscale dimensions to improve the pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, biodistribution, targeting ability, and other properties of the drugs, thereby enhancing the anti-cancer efficacy and reducing the toxicity to normal tissues. So far, some anti-cancer nanomedicines have been verified through clinical trials and have achieved satisfactory results. In the following, we will introduce the clinical applications of nanomaterials from three aspects: innate immune modulator (IIM) nanoparticles, paclitaxel nanoparticles made of biological materials, and nanoparticles loaded with new antigens (Fig. 7).

Clinical applications of innate immune modulators nanoparticles, biodegradable paclitaxel nanoparticles, and nanoparticles loaded with new antigens. Innate immune modulator nanoparticles target specific cells, promote the maturation of immune cells, and thereby exert immune effects. Paclitaxel nanoparticles can serve as immune modulators and influence the functions of immune cells to combat tumors. Nanoparticles loaded with new antigens precisely deliver the antigens, enabling patients to generate an immune response against the new antigens and facilitating the realization of personalized tumor immunotherapy. Clinical trials of IIM nanoparticles

IIM can effectively activate key immune cells and supplement the adaptive immune response, such as promoting the participation of natural killer cells in destroying tumor cells, activating macrophages to engulf tumor fragments, promoting the maturation and antigen presentation function of DCs, and activating T cells, etc. IIM functions by targeting specific cellular pathways, such as activating TLR, pattern recognition receptors RIG-1, MDA-5, etc. BO-112 is a double-stranded RNA with polyethyleneimine as the carrier, which can activate TLR3, RIG-1, and MDA-5, enhance the effect of immune checkpoint inhibitors. In a Phase I clinical trial (NCT02828098), BO-112 was used in combination with anti-PD-1 antibody to treat refractory tumors. Using BO-112 alone increased the expression of immune genes in patients by 46% and the circulating immune cells by 88%203. CMP-001 is a CPG oligonucleotide encapsulated in virus-like particles, which can activate TLR9 to induce type I interferons and increase the number of anti-tumor CD8+ T cells. In a Phase II clinical trial (NCT03618641) treating 30 patients with advanced melanoma, CMP-001 was combined with anti-PD-1 antibody nivolumab for treatment. The results showed that 70% of the patients had pathological responses, among which 15 (50%) achieved complete remission, and only 9 (30%) had no pathological response204. AST-008 is a spherical nucleic acid TLR9 agonist, and studies have shown that its combination with the PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab can increase the circulating levels of Th1-type cytokines and the number of T cells and NK cells. In a clinical trial on the treatment of advanced solid tumors with AST-008 combined with PD-1 blocker (NCT03684785), the results of the Phase Ib stage were that no dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) occurred at all dose levels, and 4 out of 19 patients showed a sustained response, and 2 of them had a response duration of more than 52 weeks205. Steinberg et al.206 reported a Phase I clinical trial, (NCT04752722) using EG-70 nanoparticles to deliver plasmids encoding IL-12 and RIG-1 agonists, triggering an anti-tumor immune response through intravesical administration to treat BCG-unresponsive non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). The result was that 67% of the 18 patients who completed one treatment cycle achieved complete remission, and a persistent effect was observed at all doses. All the above clinical trials using IIM nanoparticles were administered locally in the tumor or bladder, avoiding severe systemic cytokine elevation and systemic toxicity.

Clinical trials of paclitaxel nanoparticles

Abraxane is a nanoparticle formulation of paclitaxel, which is carried by albumin nanoparticles. Paclitaxel exerts its anti-tumor effect by binding to microtubules and stabilizing them, thereby preventing cell division. Studies have shown that paclitaxel can also act as an immunomodulator, activating DCs and NK cells, reducing the number of Tregs, and polarizing macrophages to the M1 phenotype. Most clinical trials using Abraxane are combined with PD-1/L1 inhibitors. For example, in a phase III clinical trial (NCT02425891) treating patients with metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, compared with the use of Abraxane alone and placebo, the combination of Abraxane with the anti-PD-L1 antibody atezolizumab increased the median progression-free survival (by 1.7 months) and median overall survival (by 3.7 months) of the patients. In PD-L1 positive patients, the combination of Abraxane and atezolizumab increased the median progression-free survival and median overall survival by 2.5 and 9.5 months, respectively, and no new adverse reactions were found207. In another phase III clinical trial (NCT02367781) treating non-squamous non-small cell lung cancer, patients were divided into the use of Abraxane, carboplatin chemotherapy, atezolizumab combination, or single use of carboplatin. Compared with the chemotherapy group, the combination treatment group increased the median overall survival by 4.7 months and the median progression-free survival by 1.5 months208.

Clinical trials of nanoparticles loaded with new antigens

Some researchers have conducted clinical trials using nanoparticles loaded with new antigens. For instance, Linda et al. reported a Phase III non-randomized clinical trial (NCT00045968), which used a DC vaccine (DCVax-L) loaded with tumor lysates in combination with standard treatment (SOC) to treat glioblastoma. The vaccine promotes the presentation of tumor antigens by DCs to T cells, triggering an anti-tumor response in the immune system. This trial employed the DCVax-L vaccine combined with SOC for treatment in patients with newly diagnosed glioblastoma (nGBM) and recurrent glioblastoma (rGBM), with patients receiving SOC alone serving as the control group. The results showed that compared to the control group, the median overall survival of 232 patients with nGBM increased by 2.8 months, and the 48- and 60-month survival rates increased by 5.8% and 7.3%, respectively. The median overall survival of 64 patients with rGBM increased by 5.4 months, and the 24- and 30-month survival rates increased by 11.1% and 6.0%, respectively209. The construction of personalized cancer vaccines (PCV) by loading new antigens has been proven to enable patients to generate immune responses against new antigens. For example, in a Phase I clinical trial (NCT02950766) involving 9 patients with high-risk, completely resected clear cell renal cell carcinoma (RCC), patients were divided into two groups: receiving PCV alone or PCV combined with subcutaneous ipilimumab injection. During the trial, no dose-limiting toxicity was observed, and in the 40.2-month follow-up period, none of the 9 patients had a recurrence of RCC, and all patients developed T-cell immune responses against new antigens210. Weber et al.211 conducted a Phase IIb clinical trial (NCT03897881) to evaluate an mRNA-based individualized antigen therapy (mRNA-4157). A total of 157 patients with completely resected high-risk melanoma were recruited. The patients were divided into two groups to receive mRNA-4157 combined with pembrolizumab or pembrolizumab alone. Compared to the single-agent treatment group, the combined treatment group reduced the recurrence-free survival period by 6% and 17% at 12 and 18 months, respectively. In a Phase I/II clinical trial (NCT04251117) treating patients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma who had received multiple tyrosine kinase inhibitors, a personalized therapeutic cancer vaccine (PTCV) was constructed using DNA plasmids encoding 40 new antigens and plasmids encoding IL-12, in combination with pembrolizumab. The results showed that the objective response rate was 30.6% in 36 patients, 3 patients achieved complete remission (8.3%), and 19 out of 22 patients (86.4%) with available peripheral blood mononuclear cell samples detected new antigen-specific T-cell responses212. These clinical trials demonstrate the significant potential of nanoparticles loaded with new antigens in activating antigen-presenting cells to stimulate immune responses. By precisely delivering new antigens, they can effectively activate T cells and trigger specific immune responses, providing new ideas and strategies for personalized immunotherapy, and are expected to bring new breakthroughs and application prospects in vaccine development and tumor immunotherapy.

Compared with traditional monoclonal antibodies, nanomaterials have a smaller molecular weight, which makes them more tissue-permeable and easier to target specific tissues, and enables flexible and multifunctional modification of their structure. They can exert their effects through various means, such as reshaping the tumor microenvironment. In general, current nanobiological immunotherapies have demonstrated controllable safety in clinical trials and exhibit unique therapeutic potential. In the future, the challenges that nanobiological immunotherapy may face include the selection of targets, overcoming drug resistance, continuously improving the safety of the materials, and developing cost-effective, efficient, and scalable production processes. Looking ahead, although there are many challenges, nanobiological immunotherapy undoubtedly represents an important direction for individualized and precise medicine. As a key driving force, it is continuously promoting precision medicine from theory to reality and expanding its clinical boundaries.

Prospect

The new paradigm of “spatiotemporal sequential regulation”: A nanomaterial combination approach to addressing the dynamic heterogeneity of APCs

With the rapid development of nanobiotechnology, the role of nanomaterials in regulating TIME is opening up new dimensions for cancer treatment. In the future, intelligent delivery systems designed based on nanomaterials are expected to achieve precise regulation of APCs in TIME: the shape, size, hydrophobicity, and surface functionalization of nanomaterials are crucial for their binding to APCs. Among them, composite nanomaterials have been developed as carriers for synergistic delivery of tumor antigens, serving as vaccines and immune-stimulating adjuvants for APCs213. This method can induce stronger antigen-specific antibody responses and CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocyte responses against cancer cells and effectively inhibit tumor growth. Based on the inherent physical properties of nanomaterials, targeted transportation of tumor antigens or adjuvants to DCs, macrophages, or B cells can be achieved, thereby further enhancing the immune stimulation effect. At the same time, the new generation of responsive nanodelivery systems will integrate TIME-specific activation (such as pH/ROS response), achieve targeted TIME delivery of loaded drugs and control their release, and further reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment. In addition, innovative strategies such as nanomaterial-mediated cell membrane mimetic technology, exosome engineering modification, and reshaping the antigen cross-presentation ability of APCs will promote the clinical translation of personalized tumor vaccines. It should also be noted that there are numerous challenges in the regulation and production of individualized nanovaccines. In terms of regulation, as individualized nanovaccines are an emerging type of personalized medical product, there is a lack of a clear regulatory framework. Secondly, the long-term safety and potential risks of individualized nanovaccines are unclear, making the safety assessment difficult. Moreover, the regulatory standards vary across different countries and regions, and the production process of vaccines is complex, making it challenging to establish quality control and standards based on individual patient needs. In terms of production, achieving large-scale production, improving efficiency, and reducing costs remain significant challenges. Additionally, the production process of individualized nanovaccines is complex, involving multiple steps, resulting in a long production cycle, difficult supply management, and high production costs due to the need for individualized customization. The challenges faced by individualized nanovaccines in regulation and production prompt us to consider how to balance technological innovation, policy optimization, and ethical norms to achieve harmony between technological progress and social equity, ensuring that the pursuit of scientific breakthroughs does not compromise the core goal of human health. Although these challenges are difficult, they are the necessary path towards a better future.

Call for the establishment of clinical transformation standards for nano-immune preparations (ISO/TC229 working group)

Compared with traditional chemical drugs, the nanomaterials applied in cancer treatment have many advantages, but there are still many challenges in the process of clinical translation. Firstly, the nanomaterials themselves have antigenicity. When they are recognized as foreign substances, the complement pathway will be activated, and they will be rapidly phagocytosed and cleared in the liver and spleen. The activation of the immune response of the nanomaterials may lead to serious complications, including allergic reactions, thrombosis, hemolysis, and even disseminated intravascular coagulation213. Secondly, if the nanomaterials are taken up by normal cells, they may cause severe toxicity through mechanisms such as redox cycling, free radical formation, and hydrophobic interactions, including oxidative stress, pulmonary inflammation, and organ and cell dysfunction214. Currently, even with the coating of targeting ligands, the nanomaterials can only deliver a small portion of the drugs to the predetermined location. Therefore, the targeting delivery ability of the nanomaterials still needs to be strengthened. Finally, the regulatory strategy of nanomaterials acting on APCs in the TIME is to reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment to an immunosupportive microenvironment, which poses a risk of tumor metastasis. Therefore, further research on the long-term effects of the microenvironment regulation strategy is necessary. Studies have shown that nanoparticles with a diameter of less than 100 nm have the most suitable surface chemical properties, can undergo long-distance transportation in the body, and enhance the internalization of immune cells, but properties such as surface charge, shape, and hydrophilicity still need to be further studied and determined to improve the pharmacokinetics, toxicity, biocompatibility, and biodistribution of the nanomaterials. In conclusion, the clinical application of nanomaterials requires the optimization of nanoscale structures to overcome the existing deficiencies, with the aim of providing a new method and means for cancer treatment. In summary, nanomaterials provide a transformative tool from “passive intervention” to “active programming” for the functional regulation of APCs. Through the deep integration of multimodal intelligent platforms, bionic engineering technologies, and personalized treatment systems, the immunotherapy of nanomaterials is expected to break through the immune tolerance barrier of solid tumors and achieve the transformation from “cold tumors” to “hot tumors” in the future. Although there are still many challenges at present, with the collaborative innovation of nanoscience, immunology, and clinical medicine, nanotechnology based on APC regulation will surely open a new paradigm for cancer treatment and ultimately lead to a precise, dynamic, and curative tumor immunotherapy era (Fig. 8).
