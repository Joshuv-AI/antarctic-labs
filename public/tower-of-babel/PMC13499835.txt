Peripheral and Autonomic Diabetic Neuropathy and Their Additive Risk of Major Adverse Liver Outcomes in Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Alex E Henney, Masoud Isanejad, Daniel J Cuthbertson, Uazman Alam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionType 2 diabetes (T2D) is a risk factor for the progression of liver disease, particularly relating to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and consequent major adverse liver outcomes (MALO). Given that diabetic neuropathy reflects advanced metabolic and microvascular injury, we investigated whether somatic and autonomic neuropathy in T2D is associated with MALO.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study using a large, federated health research network (TriNetX), adults with T2D were stratified into (i) diabetes alone, without coding of neuropathy, (ii) diabetes with peripheral neuropathy coding, (iii) diabetes with autonomic neuropathy coding, and (iv) diabetes with combined peripheral and autonomic neuropathy coding. Propensity score matching was performed to balance demographic, metabolic, and comorbidity profiles. The primary outcome was incident MALO, defined as hepatic decompensation, portal hypertension/stable varices, hepatocellular carcinoma, liver failure, or liver transplantation. Secondary outcomes included individual MALO endpoints, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and all-cause mortality. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs).ResultsAfter matching, a clear gradient was evident between the increased risk of MALO and the presence of neuropathy: peripheral neuropathy and risk of MALO (HR 1.95 [95% CI 1.85, 2.05]), autonomic neuropathy (HR 2.62 [2.52, 2.72]) and combined neuropathy (HR 3.21 [3.03, 3.41]). Associations were consistent across individual MALO endpoints, irrespective of obesity status, and remained significant following temporal washout analyses. Neuropathy also conferred greater risk for MALO compared to other microvascular complications (vs. retinopathy; 2.21 [2.05, 2.39]).ConclusionDiabetic neuropathy is associated with an elevated risk of MALO in T2D. Autonomic, and to a lesser extent peripheral, neuropathy identifies a particularly high-risk phenotype, supporting neuropathic burden as a surrogate marker, and potential driver, of advanced systemic metabolic injury and progressive liver risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03667-4.Keywords: Neuropathy, Diabetes, MASLD, Liver disease

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Type 2 diabetes is a major risk factor for progressive liver disease, yet there remains substantial heterogeneity in liver outcomes that is not fully explained by traditional metabolic risk factors, highlighting an unmet need for improved risk stratification.Diabetic neuropathy reflects advanced systemic metabolic injury, but its relationship with clinically meaningful liver outcomes has not been well defined.This study examined whether increasing neuropathic burden (peripheral, autonomic, and combined) is associated with a graded increase in risk of major adverse liver outcomes in individuals with type 2 diabetes. What was learned from the study? In a large propensity score-matched cohort, diabetic neuropathy was associated with significantly increased risk of major adverse liver outcomes, with a clear graded increase in risk from peripheral to autonomic to combined neuropathy, and the highest risk observed in individuals with both peripheral and autonomic involvement.These findings suggest that neuropathic burden identifies a high-risk hepatic phenotype within type 2 diabetes and may reflect both advanced systemic metabolic disease and potential neuro-metabolic contributions to liver disease progression. Associations were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses, supporting their consistency, although they should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) affects over 500 million individuals worldwide and represents a major driver of global morbidity and mortality [1], particularly in those with early-onset disease [2]. Beyond its well-established cardiovascular and renal complications [2], T2D is increasingly recognised as a significant risk factor for progressive liver disease [3], particularly as the degree of metabolic dysfunction escalates [4]. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is now the most common chronic liver disease globally, and at least 70–80% individuals with T2D are concomitantly affected [5]. Compared with individuals without diabetes, those with T2D exhibit accelerated fibrosis progression and substantially higher risks of liver-related morbidity (cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), liver transplantation) and liver-related mortality [3]. Importantly, however, the risk of major adverse liver outcomes (MALO) among individuals with T2D is heterogeneous, suggesting that markers of advanced systemic metabolic injury may help identify those at greatest hepatic risk.

Diabetic neuropathy is the commonest microvascular complications of T2D, affecting up to 50% of individuals with longer disease duration [6]. Peripheral and autonomic neuropathies represent biologically and clinically distinct entities, although they often occur in parallel. Peripheral neuropathy primarily involves somatic sensory fibres, primarily presenting with sensory loss and paraesthesia in distal limbs, strongly related to chronic hyperglycaemia, microvascular damage and disease duration [7]. In contrast, autonomic neuropathy affects small unmyelinated fibres responsible for cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary regulation, associated with substantially increased mortality in T2D [8]. The liver is innervated by both sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves. These nerves are derived from the splanchnic and vagal nerves that surround the portal vein, hepatic artery, and bile duct [9–11].

The degree of underlying hepatic steatosis, and associated metabolic dysfunction, increases the subsequent risk of developing both peripheral and autonomic neuropathy in patients with T2D [12–20]. However, emerging evidence suggests that a bidirectional relationship may exist, with autonomic dysfunction, specifically sympathetic overactivity, impaired parasympathetic tone, and systemic inflammatory dysregulation, directly influencing hepatic substrate metabolism and fibrogenesis [21]. Indeed, modulation of the autonomic nervous system favourably impacts liver and cardiometabolic outcomes: hepatic sympathetic denervation in rodents normalises steatosis [22], whilst renal denervation in humans improves insulin sensitivity and reduces risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [23–29]. Considering these data, one might speculate that an increasing neuropathic burden would confer a graded risk of advanced liver outcomes; however, this relationship is yet to be systematically examined.

We therefore investigated whether, among individuals with T2D, an increasing severity of diabetic neuropathy is associated with a stepwise elevation in risk of MALO. Specifically, we evaluated risk across those patients with T2D without any neuropathy, those with either of peripheral or autonomic neuropathy, and those with both peripheral and autonomic neuropathy.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a cohort study with anonymised data from TriNetX (TriNetX LLC, Cambridge, MA, USA), a global federated health research network with access to both inpatient and outpatient electronic health records (EHRs) from healthcare organisations internationally, primarily secondary and tertiary care providers in North America and Western Europe. This analysis was conducted on the Global Collaborative Network, containing data from approximately 170 million patients with access to diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values and genomic information worldwide. The data used in this study was collected on 28 February 2026. Further details on the network have been described elsewhere [30].

Definitions

Population

We identified all adults (> 18 years) in TriNetX. Three cohorts were created, each cohort with an exposure arm and reference arm. Within the exposure arm, patients had (i) cohort 1, T2D with peripheral neuropathy (DPN) coding; (ii) cohort 2, T2D with autonomic neuropathy coding; and (iii) cohort 3, T2D with peripheral and autonomic neuropathy coding. The reference arm comprised patients with T2D without peripheral or autonomic neuropathy. A cohort creation diagram is presented in Fig. 1.

Inclusion Criteria

Patients were identified as having T2D using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 code E11, or a HbA1c of ≥ 6.5%. Peripheral neuropathy was identified using ICD-10 codes E11.40–E11.42 for T2D with peripheral mono- or polyneuropathy, as well as G62–G63 for other unspecified peripheral neuropathies. Autonomic neuropathy was identified using the ICD-10 code E11.43 for T2D with autonomic neuropathy, as well as ICD-10 codes for other features of autonomic neuropathy spanning genitourinary (erectile dysfunction, bladder dysfunction), gastrointestinal (gastroparesis) and orthostatic hypotension. All coding of neuropathy must have occurred any time after the date of diagnosis of T2D to ensure that neuropathy was a result of T2D rather than other pathology.

Exclusion Criteria

For all cohorts/arms, patients were excluded if they had a history of type 1 diabetes, or if, prior to the index event, they had been diagnosed with the primary outcome of interest (see below), or were living with other causes chronic liver disease (including viral hepatitis, alcohol-related liver disease or alcohol-use disorder, autoimmune hepatobiliary disease, genetic error-of-metabolism liver disease). Importantly, patients with DPN should not have had a coded diagnosis of autonomic neuropathy (before or after the index event), and visa versa; except for cohort 3 where patients in the exposure arm both DPN and autonomic neuropathy were coded. Details for definitions of all diagnoses are presented in Supplementary Material Table 1.

Index Event

The index event for the exposure and reference arms followed either the date of diagnosis, via ICD-10 coding, of neuropathy for the exposure arms, or of T2D, for the reference arm. Patients were followed for up to 5 years until the first coding of the respective outcome of interest on their EHRs. Patients who did not develop incident MALO were censored at (i) end of the time window for analysis, or (ii) patient’s last known fact date; this included any patient who had died during the analysis window. Follow-up was defined for a maximum of 5 years from the index date. This duration was selected a priori to balance clinical relevance with data completeness, allowing sufficient time for development of meaningful liver-related outcomes while minimising loss to follow-up and variability in longitudinal data capture within the EHR system.

Propensity Score Matching (PSM)

Cohorts underwent PSM, in a 1:1 ratio, for age, sex, ethnicity, sociodemographic status, smoking, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, cardiovascular disease (CVD) (ischaemic heart disease (IHD), cerebrovascular accident (CVA) and peripheral vascular disease (PVD)), chronic kidney disease, liver transaminases, sodium, bilirubin, albumin, prothrombin time, HbA1c, body mass index (BMI), glucose-lowering therapy, antihypertensive and antiplatelet (given their chemoprotective benefits in MASLD [31]) medication. Definitions for all PSM covariates are presented in Supplementary Material Table 1.

Outcomes

Primary outcome was time-to-incident composite MALO.

Liver-Related Outcomes

Secondary outcomes of interest included time-to-incident individual MALO endpoints (including liver failure, decompensated liver disease (ascites, bleeding varices, hepatic encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome, hepatopulmonary syndrome), portal hypertension and stable varices, HCC and liver transplant).

MACE and all-cause mortality were also examined.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis for cohort data was performed in situ within the TriNetX platform. Normally distributed baseline characteristics are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD). PSM was performed using logistic regression. TriNetX uses ‘greedy nearest-neighbour matching’ with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations and difference between propensity scores < 0.1. We assessed covariate balance between groups using the standardised mean difference (SMD). SMD < 0.1 was considered well matched. The reference arm was considered the reference cohort (hazard ratio (HR) = 1) when compared against the exposure arm.

Survival Analysis

Survival analysis was performed to estimate the probability of an outcome, at daily time intervals, over 5 years from the index event. Time-to-event analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier survival estimates and Cox proportional hazards models applied to the PSM cohorts, with exposure status included as the sole predictor. No additional covariates are included in the post-matching Cox model. A HR, log rank test and Kaplan–Meier survival curve were generated. The proportional hazards assumption cannot be formally tested within the TriNetX platform (e.g. using Schoenfeld residuals); however, visual inspection of Kaplan–Meier curves can be undertaken. TriNetX uses the R Survival package v3.2–3.

Sensitivity Analyses

Additionally, for sensitivity analysis, we (i) stratified patients by BMI (≥ 30 or ≤ 27.5 (obesity vs. no obesity), kg/m2), (ii) performed a landmark analysis whereby we excluded incident cases of MALO within the first 12 months of the index event, (iii) directly compared the risk of outcomes in patients with DPN to autonomic neuropathy, (iv) changed the reference arm to patients with T2D and retinopathy to ensure results were driven by neuropathy rather than microvascular disease, more generally, and (v) accounted for diabetes duration by repeating analyses in patients who have lived with T2D for at least 10 years.

E Values

Additionally, we calculated E values, representing the minimum strength of association on the HR scale that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome, conditional on the measured confounders, to explain away the observed association; HR + √[HR × (HR − 1)] [32].

STROBE

The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed in the reporting of this cohort study [33].

Ethics Statement

This retrospective study is exempt from informed consent. The data reviewed is a secondary analysis of existing data, does not involve intervention or interaction with human subjects, and is de-identified per the de-identification standard defined in Section §164.514(a) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. The process by which the data is de-identified is attested to through a formal determination by a qualified expert as defined in Section §164.514(b)(1) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. This formal determination by a qualified expert refreshed on December 2020.

Cohort 1: Peripheral Neuropathy

Baseline Cohort Characteristics

There were 354,029 patients included in the DPN arm. Before matching, patients with DPN were more likely to be older (64 vs. 61 years), white (62% vs. 51%), smokers, living with features of the metabolic syndrome (hypertension and dyslipidaemia), as well as CKD and CVD. Their glycaemic control was worse (7.7% vs. 6.4%). After PSM, the cohort was deemed well matched, and each arm included 288,558 patients. The mean follow-up time was 1252 days in both arms.

Survival Analysis

DPN was associated with an increased risk of MALO (HR 1.95 [1.85, 2.05]), as well as all individual MALO endpoints: liver failure (2.34 [2.11, 2.60]), decompensated liver disease (1.99 [1.87, 2.13]), portal hypertension and stables varices (2.15 [1.91, 2.42]), HCC (1.65 [1.44, 1.89]), and liver transplant (1.65 [1.16, 2.37]). DPN was also associated with an increased risk of MACE (1.18 [1.17, 1.20]) and all-cause mortality (1.15 [1.13, 1.16]) (Table 2, Figs. 2, 3, and 4).

Stratified Analyses

DPN was associated with an increased risk of MALO regardless of degree of obesity (obesity (1.90 [1.74, 2.08]); no obesity (1.85 [1.67, 2.04])). MALO remained significantly associated with DPN following introduction of a time wash period after the index event (1.96 [1.84, 2.07]), and when compared to patients with diabetes and retinopathy (1.29 [1.19, 1.39]). Finally, DPN remained associated with MALO when patients were living with T2D for at least 10 years (1.49 [1.39, 1.60]) (Supplementary Material Table 2).

Cohort 2: Autonomic Neuropathy

Baseline Cohort Characteristics

There were 563,456 patients in the autonomic neuropathy arm. Before matching, patients with autonomic neuropathy were more likely to be older (66 vs. 61 years), white (57% vs. 51%), smokers, living with features of the metabolic syndrome (hypertension and dyslipidaemia), as well as CKD and CVD. Their glycaemic control was worse (7.1% vs. 6.4%). After PSM, the cohort was deemed well matched, and each arm included 443,412 patients. The mean follow-up time was 1250 days in the exposure arm, and 1245 in the reference arm.

Survival Analysis

Autonomic neuropathy was associated with an increased risk of MALO (HR 2.62 [2.52, 2.72]), as well as all individual MALO endpoints: liver failure (2.78 [2.57, 3.00]), decompensated liver disease (2.86 [2.73, 3.01]), portal hypertension and stables varices (2.46 [2.23, 2.70]), HCC (2.26 [2.05, 2.49]), and liver transplant (1.65 [1.26, 2.15]). Autonomic neuropathy was also associated with an increased risk of MACE (1.15 [1.13, 1.16]) and all-cause mortality (1.36 [1.34, 1.37]) (Table 2, Figs. 2, 3, and 4).

Stratified Analyses

Autonomic neuropathy was associated with an increased risk of MALO regardless of presence of obesity (obesity (2.69 [2.50, 2.91]); no obesity (2.41 [2.26, 2.56])). MALO remained significantly associated with autonomic neuropathy following introduction of a time wash period after the index event (2.41 [2.31, 2.52]). Autonomic neuropathy remained associated with MALO when compared to patients with diabetes and retinopathy (1.85 [1.72, 1.99]), and when compared head-to-head against DPN (1.40 [1.35, 1.46]). Finally, autonomic neuropathy remained associated with MALO when patients were living with T2D for at least 10 years (1.98 [1.89, 2.08]) (Supplementary Material Table 2).

Cohort 3: Combined Peripheral and Autonomic Neuropathy

Baseline Cohort Characteristics

There were 199,881 patients in the mixed peripheral and autonomic neuropathy arm. Before matching, patients with neuropathy were more likely to be older (66 vs. 61 years), white (65% vs. 51%), smokers, living with features of the metabolic syndrome (hypertension and dyslipidaemia), as well as CKD and CVD. Their glycaemic control was worse (7.6% vs. 6.4%). After PSM, the cohort was deemed well matched, and each arm included 145,049 patients. The mean follow-up time was 1437 days in the exposure arm, and 1263 in the reference arm.

Survival Analysis

Mixed peripheral and autonomic neuropathy was associated with an increased risk of MALO (HR 3.21 [3.03, 3.41]), as well as all individual MALO endpoints: liver failure (3.25 [2.88, 3.67]), decompensated liver disease (3.63 [3.36, 3.92]), portal hypertension and stables varices (3.02 [2.61, 3.49]), HCC (2.67 [2.25, 3.17]), and liver transplant (2.04 [1.40, 2.99]). Mixed neuropathy was also associated with an increased risk of MACE (1.72 [1.69, 1.75]) and all-cause mortality (1.19 [1.16, 1.21]) (Table 2, Figs. 2, 3, and 4).

Stratified Analyses

Mixed peripheral and autonomic neuropathy was associated with an increased risk of MALO regardless of degree of obesity (obesity (3.47 [3.10, 3.88]); no obesity (2.87 [2.60, 3.17])). MALO remained significantly associated with DPN following introduction of a time wash period after the index event (3.37 [3.14, 3.61]), and when compared to patients with diabetes and retinopathy (2.21 [2.05, 2.39]). Finally, mixed neuropathy remained associated with MALO when patients were living with T2D for at least 10 years (2.25 [2.09, 2.42]) (Supplementary Material Table 2).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate an association between diabetic neuropathy and progression to MALO. In this large propensity score-matched analysis of individuals with T2D, diabetic neuropathy was associated with a substantially increased risk of MALO, with a clear relationship according to neuropathy type and coexisting disease. After matching for demographic and cardiometabolic characteristics, DPN was associated with a near doubling in risk of composite MALO, while autonomic neuropathy conferred a greater than 2.5-fold increase. The highest risk was observed among individuals with a combined coding for peripheral and autonomic neuropathy. This graded pattern was consistent across all individual liver endpoints, including liver failure, decompensated liver disease, portal hypertension/varices, HCC, and liver transplantation. Associations remained robust across obesity strata, after temporal washout analyses, and when compared with individuals with diabetes and retinopathy, suggesting specificity beyond general microvascular disease. Autonomic neuropathy also conferred significantly greater risk than peripheral neuropathy alone. Neuropathy was additionally associated with higher risks of MACE and all-cause mortality, reinforcing its role as a marker of advanced systemic disease. Collectively, these findings demonstrate a stepwise relationship between neuropathic burden and progressive liver risk in T2D.

Comparison with Existing Literature

T2D is increasingly recognised as a major determinant of progressive liver disease, including advanced fibrosis, hepatic decompensation, HCC, and liver-related mortality; a meta-analysis of international cohorts demonstrated that T2D independently doubled the risk of hepatic decompensation after adjustment for demographic and metabolic factors [3]. However, heterogeneity in liver outcomes among individuals with T2D suggests that glycaemic control alone does not fully explain hepatic risk. In a Swedish national cohort of 230,992 individuals with T2D, the presence of additional metabolic syndrome traits conferred stepwise increases in MALO risk, with more than a twofold higher rate among those with multiple traits compared with diabetes alone [4]. Similar observations have been reported for cardiovascular, microvascular [20], and cancer outcomes in patients with MASLD [34].

Neuropathy represents one manifestation of this cumulative metabolic burden. DPN has consistently been associated with increased cardiovascular risk and mortality. Observational studies demonstrate independent associations between peripheral neuropathy and incident cardiovascular disease [35, 36], while autonomic neuropathy carries even stronger prognostic significance. A meta-analysis demonstrated more than a threefold increased risk of MACE and all-cause mortality among individuals with cardiac autonomic neuropathy [8]. Evidence also suggests links between autonomic dysfunction and liver disease. In two large cohorts of adults without baseline MASLD (confirmed by Fibroscan), reduced heart rate variability, reflective of autonomic imbalance, was associated with incident MASLD and correlated with non-invasive fibrosis indices [21, 37], whilst similarly, liver fibrosis indices are related to incident DPN in individuals with T2D [38]. However, prior studies have largely examined surrogate markers of fibrosis rather than hard clinical liver endpoints. To our knowledge, no large-scale longitudinal analysis has evaluated whether neuropathy severity confers graded risk of MALO.

Our findings therefore extend the literature by demonstrating a robust association between increasing neuropathic burden and clinically meaningful hepatic endpoints. The persistence of these associations when compared with another microvascular complication, retinopathy, strengthens the specificity of our findings and suggests that the relationship between neuropathy and liver disease may reflect a direct hepatic injury rather than simply an indirect marker of advanced systemic metabolic injury.

Biological Plausibility

The liver is densely innervated by sympathetic and parasympathetic fibres [9]. Sympathetic activation promotes hepatic gluconeogenesis and lipolysis, whereas parasympathetic activity modulates insulin sensitivity and anti-inflammatory pathways. Disruption of this neuro-metabolic axis may therefore contribute to hepatic steatosis and fibrogenesis [21]. Experimental studies support a role for autonomic signalling in hepatic lipid metabolism. Hepatic denervation in rodent models reduces steatosis and improves metabolic parameters [22], while interventions targeting sympathetic overactivity, including renal denervation in humans, have demonstrated improvements in insulin sensitivity and systemic metabolic regulation beyond blood pressure reduction alone [23–29]. These observations suggest that sustained autonomic imbalance may contribute to progression from steatosis to fibrosis.

Clinical Implications

These findings reinforce the interconnected nature of the cardio–kidney–metabolic axis and have implications for risk stratification in T2D. Identification of one microvascular complication should prompt vigilance for screening/surveillance of others. Although guidelines increasingly recognise T2D as a major risk factor for MASLD, implementation of screening strategies remains heterogeneous [39]. Autonomic neuropathy may identify individuals at heightened risk of progressive liver disease. Patients with cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction, gastroparesis, orthostatic hypotension, or other autonomic manifestations may benefit from earlier evaluation for advanced liver fibrosis using non-invasive strategies such as FIB-4 or enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) followed, where indicated, by transient elastography (Fibroscan). Incorporating neuropathy status into MASLD screening algorithms may therefore improve identification of individuals at highest risk of hepatic decompensation or HCC. These findings also raise potential therapeutic implications. Experimental models demonstrate that hepatic denervation can attenuate steatosis [22], while emerging clinical data suggest that autonomic-modulating interventions such as renal denervation and spinal cord stimulation may exert favourable metabolic effects beyond blood pressure reduction [23–29, 40]. Future studies should evaluate whether modulation of sympathetic or parasympathetic tone influences hepatic steatosis, fibrosis progression, or portal haemodynamics.

Limitations

First, analyses derived from EHRs are observational and susceptible to residual confounding. Data completeness may vary across healthcare organisations, and patient movement between systems may lead to incomplete longitudinal capture. Second, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Variables such as alcohol intake, diet quality, and physical activity were unavailable, although E values were calculated to estimate the potential impact of unmeasured confounding [32]. Although propensity score matching was performed across a broad range of demographic and clinical variables, several important factors could not be fully accounted for, including baseline liver disease severity or fibrosis stage, alcohol intake, lifestyle factors, and accurate socioeconomic status. These variables are strongly associated with both neuropathy and liver outcomes and may have influenced the observed associations. Detailed liver phenotyping was also unavailable. Histological data and advanced imaging measures are not routinely captured in EHRs, limiting the ability to stratify fibrosis stage. Although cohorts were well matched for factors associated with advanced fibrosis risk, including age, liver enzymes, platelet count, and cardiometabolic comorbidity, these remain indirect surrogates rather than definitive measures of disease severity. Consequently, differences in baseline liver health may have contributed to the observed associations. Similarly, neuropathy identification relied on ICD-10 coding rather than objective measures such as corneal confocal microscopy, intra-epidermal skin biopsy, or cardiac autonomic reflex testing. As neuropathy, particularly autonomic neuropathy, is frequently under-recognised in routine clinical practice [41], misclassification and under-ascertainment are possible. In routine care, screening for diabetic neuropathy primarily focuses on identifying patients at high risk of foot ulceration rather than detecting early disease [42, 43], in contrast to screening programmes for other microvascular complications that aim to identify pathology at earlier stages. Consequently, many individuals receive a formal neuropathy diagnosis only once more advanced disease is present, meaning milder or subclinical neuropathic phenotypes may not have been captured within the exposure groups. Specifically, peripheral and autonomic neuropathy frequently coexist, although one phenotype may predominate (DPN with relatively preserved autonomic features and vice versa) [44], and some individuals with subclinical neuropathy may therefore have been included within the reference group.

Further, the mean follow-up duration of approximately 3–4 years may be relatively short for the full natural history of progressive liver disease. As such, some liver-related events may reflect pre-existing or subclinical liver disease present at baseline rather than true incident disease progression, particularly among individuals at higher baseline risk. Although detailed liver phenotyping was not available, and baseline fibrosis stage could not be directly assessed, this raises the possibility of reverse causation contributing to the observed associations. To mitigate this, we performed sensitivity analyses incorporating a temporal washout period following the index date, which did not materially alter our findings. Nevertheless, residual risk of reverse causation cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting these results.

Finally, cumulative incidence was estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods, which may overestimate event rates in the presence of competing risks such as non-liver-related mortality. This is particularly relevant in a population with T2D, where cardiovascular and non-liver mortality are common and may preclude the occurrence of liver-related outcomes. As a result, absolute risk estimates for MALO may be inflated, especially among individuals with higher comorbidity burden. As analyses are performed in situ within the TriNetX environment, alternative competing-risk models could not be implemented, and this should be considered when interpreting absolute risk estimates. While competing risks may influence cumulative incidence estimates, their impact on relative effect measures is likely to be less pronounced; however, hazard ratios should still be interpreted cautiously within this context.

Taken together, these limitations mean that the observed associations should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate a clear graded relationship between neuropathic burden and progressive liver risk in T2D, with autonomic dysfunction emerging as a particularly strong predictor of adverse hepatic outcomes. While causality cannot be inferred, the consistency and gradient of associations across endpoints suggest that neuropathy may identify a high-risk hepatic phenotype within diabetes and reinforces the concept of an integrated neuro-metabolic axis linking systemic microvascular injury to hepatic disease progression. Future prospective and mechanistic studies should address whether modulation of autonomic function alters the trajectory of MASLD and its complications.
