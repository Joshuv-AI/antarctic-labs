Defining and managing high-risk acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in 2026
Authors: Mohamed Nazem Alibrahim, Mariem Ahmad Elsayed, Abdullah Nassir Alqahtani, Fahad Saeed Alshehri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) remains a highly heterogeneous malignancy in which outcomes are particularly poor for patients classified as having high-risk disease. Traditionally, high-risk AML has been defined by adverse baseline genetic features, including complex cytogenetics, TP53 alterations, and mutations associated with secondary or therapy-related disease. However, this static, genetics-centered definition is increasingly insufficient in the modern therapeutic era. Emerging evidence supports a more dynamic and context-dependent model in which risk is shaped not only by molecular architecture but also by treatment intensity, patient fitness, measurable residual disease (MRD), and evolving resistance mechanisms. Advances in genomic profiling have refined risk stratification frameworks, including ELN 2022 for intensively treated patients and the ELN 2024 classification for those receiving less-intensive therapies. In parallel, MRD has emerged as a powerful biomarker that reclassifies patients during treatment, identifying those with persistent, therapy-resistant disease despite morphologic remission. Biologically, high-risk AML is driven by the interplay of clonal evolution, epigenetic plasticity, leukemic stem cell persistence, and protective microenvironmental and immune interactions, all of which contribute to relapse. Therapeutically, the landscape has expanded to include targeted agents, venetoclax-based combinations, and transplantation strategies, yet outcomes remain limited in key high-risk subsets, particularly TP53-mutated disease and post-venetoclax relapse. Accordingly, current strategies emphasize rational combination therapies, MRD-guided treatment adaptation, and approaches targeting both leukemic cells and their supportive niches. In 2026, high-risk AML is best understood as a dynamic, treatment-context-dependent state. Improving outcomes will require integration of precision diagnostics, biologically informed therapy, and adaptive strategies designed to anticipate and overcome resistance.Keywords: Acute Myeloid Leukemia, High-Risk, Precision Diagnosis, Treatment

Introduction

Despite an unprecedented expansion of therapeutic options over the past decade, acute myeloid leukemia (AML) remains a highly lethal malignancy for a substantial proportion of patients. While survival has improved for selected molecular subsets, outcomes for patients commonly labeled as having “high-risk AML” continue to be poor, with high rates of primary refractoriness, early relapse, and treatment-related mortality. This persistent lethality reflects not only a lack of therapeutic innovation, but rather the biological complexity of AML, the context-dependence of treatment response, and the limitations of static risk frameworks in a disease that evolves under therapeutic pressure [1–3].

Historically, high-risk AML has been defined predominantly by adverse baseline genetic features identified at diagnosis, including complex cytogenetics, TP53 alterations, and mutations associated with secondary or therapy-related disease. These diagnostic frameworks, codified in successive World Health Organization (WHO) and European LeukemiaNet (ELN) classifications, have provided essential prognostic guidance, particularly for patients treated with intensive induction chemotherapy [4–6]. However, this genetics-centric and diagnosis-anchored approach increasingly fails to capture real-world clinical risk in the modern era, where therapeutic intensity, patient fitness, and molecularly targeted agents profoundly shape outcomes.

High-risk AML is increasingly understood as a dynamic state rather than a fixed diagnostic category. Measurable residual disease (MRD) assessment has emerged as a powerful predictor of relapse and survival across AML subtypes and treatment intensities. Persistent or recurrent MRD identifies patients with biologically aggressive, therapy-resistant disease, even when baseline genetics are not classified as adverse, and effectively defines a state of “functional high-risk AML” [7]. Recent international consensus efforts have further refined MRD methodologies, reporting standards, and clinical applications, positioning MRD not only as a prognostic marker but as an actionable tool guiding transplant decisions, maintenance strategies, and therapeutic escalation or de-escalation [8].

High-risk disease emerges from the interaction of intrinsic leukemia biology, treatment exposure, microenvironmental protection, and immune escape, rather than from genetics alone. Resistance mechanisms evolve over time, clonal architecture is reshaped by therapy, and vulnerabilities present at diagnosis may be lost or replaced at relapse. Consequently, effective management of high-risk AML requires an integrated approach that unifies molecular and cellular biology, context-appropriate risk stratification, precision diagnostics, rational therapeutic sequencing, and strategies to prevent or overcome resistance.

This review redefines high-risk AML as a dynamic, treatment-context-dependent state integrating molecular, MRD, and therapeutic dimensions.

Defining “high-risk AML” in 2026: one term, multiple contexts

The concept of AML with myelodysplasia-related changes (AML-MRC) from prior classifications has been replaced by more biologically precise categories: AML with myelodysplasia-related (MR) gene mutations and AML with MR cytogenetic abnormalities. These categories encompass genetic alterations that confer resistance to conventional intensive chemotherapy, including mutations in spliceosome genes (SRSF2, SF3B1, U2AF1, ZRSR2), chromatin modifiers (ASXL1, EZH2, BCOR), and transcription factors (RUNX1, STAG2). Critically, ICC 2022 elevates TP53-mutated AML to a distinct disease entity when the variant allele fraction exceeds 10%, superseding other classification schemes due to its association with genomic instability, complex karyotype, and profoundly adverse outcomes. WHO 2022 similarly recognizes MDS with biallelic TP53 inactivation (MDS-biTP53) as a high-risk entity, though the systems differ in their handling of cases with 10–19% blasts and TP53 mutations [9–11].

A practical definition of high-risk AML requires separating adverse disease biology from adverse treatment feasibility. A younger, fit patient with TP53-mutated AML has biologically high-risk disease but may still be a candidate for intensive, transplant-oriented therapy, whereas an older or frail patient with otherwise favorable genetics may face high clinical risk because comorbidities, organ function, frailty, and treatment tolerance constrain therapeutic options. In contemporary AML, risk therefore emerges from the interaction between leukemia genetics, patient fitness, and treatment context rather than from baseline molecular features alone [5].

For patients receiving intensive chemotherapy, the ELN 2022 classification remains the reference framework for genetic risk stratification. It incorporates major revisions, including reclassification of FLT3-ITD, recognition of myelodysplasia-related gene mutations as adverse risk, and adverse assignment for NPM1-mutated AML with adverse cytogenetics. However, ELN 2022 was derived largely from intensively treated cohorts and is less discriminatory in older or unfit patients treated with lower-intensity regimens, particularly hypomethylating agent-based therapy with or without venetoclax, where many patients cluster in the adverse-risk group [5].

This limitation led to the ELN 2024 less-intensive therapy framework, which was developed specifically for adults treated with HMA-based regimens, including azacitidine-venetoclax and azacitidine-ivosidenib in IDH1-mutated AML. In this setting, the prognostic weight of individual lesions differs from that seen with intensive therapy: TP53 alterations remain strongly adverse, and FLT3-ITD and RAS-pathway mutations are generally classified within intermediate-risk groups in less-intensive therapy settings, although their prognostic impact may worsen in specific biological or clinical contexts. As a result, a patient may be high risk in one therapeutic context and not in another [12]. Importantly, this framework was derived primarily from newly diagnosed patients receiving HMA-based regimens and may not fully apply to patients with prior HMA exposure or relapsed disease.

At the same time, measurable residual disease (MRD) has shifted risk assessment from a static baseline model to a dynamic, response-based one. The 2025 ELN-DAVID MRD update provides subtype-specific guidance for assay selection, interpretation, and clinical use, reinforcing MRD as both a prognostic biomarker and a treatment-guiding tool. MRD status now informs post-induction risk assessment, pre- and post-transplant planning, surveillance, and consideration of therapeutic escalation. Clinically, persistent MRD can redefine a patient with otherwise favorable baseline genetics as having “functional high-risk AML,” while deep molecular clearance may refine prognosis within some adverse-risk groups. In 2026, high-risk AML is therefore best understood as a dynamic state defined by the interaction of baseline biology, treatment intensity, and depth of response [8].

Importantly, these evolving classifications collectively support a broader conceptual shift in AML risk assessment. High-risk AML can no longer be viewed as a static category defined solely at diagnosis by adverse genetic features. Instead, risk evolves dynamically through the interaction of baseline molecular architecture, treatment intensity, depth of response, measurable residual disease persistence, and therapy-driven clonal adaptation. In this framework, biologic risk, treatment feasibility, and functional resistance states continuously interact over the disease course. Consequently, patients initially classified as intermediate risk may transition into “functional high-risk AML” through persistent MRD or early relapse, whereas some genetically adverse subsets may achieve durable disease control when deep molecular remission and effective transplant consolidation are achieved. This dynamic model increasingly underpins contemporary therapeutic decision-making in AML.

In the intensive-chemotherapy era, adverse risk was largely inferred from baseline genetics and cytogenetics, but in older or unfit patients treated with lower-intensity regimens, the prognostic behavior of several lesions is treatment-context dependent. VIALE-A established azacitidine/venetoclax as a new standard for patients ineligible for intensive chemotherapy, improving composite remission and overall survival compared with azacitidine alone; however, long-term follow-up confirms that this benefit does not translate into durable disease control for many biologically adverse subsets [13]. This is particularly evident in TP53-mutated AML, where response may occur but remissions are typically short, suggesting that venetoclax can deepen early cytoreduction without fully overcoming the evolutionary fitness of TP53-aberrant clones. The 2024 ELN less-intensive therapy recommendations therefore represent more than a technical modification of risk categories; they acknowledge that risk is not an intrinsic property of the leukemia alone, but a function of the therapeutic pressure applied to it [14]. In this framework, post-HMA/venetoclax relapse has emerged as a particularly adverse clinical state, with limited salvage feasibility and poor survival, effectively defining a new form of treatment-selected high-risk AML. Thus, the HMA/venetoclax era has exposed the limits of static genetic classification and supports a more functional definition of high-risk AML based on baseline biology, treatment context, depth of response, and resistance trajectory [15].

Molecular and genetic architecture of high-risk AML

Genomic heterogeneity and clonal evolution are central to treatment failure in AML. Deep sequencing studies have established that AML follows a branching evolutionary model in which founding mutations are present in the dominant clone at diagnosis, whereas subclonal lesions may persist at low frequency and expand under therapeutic pressure [16–18]. Relapses frequently arise from the survival and outgrowth of preexisting resistant clones rather than from acquisition of entirely new mutations [19]. Thus, resistance is often an evolutionary selection process rather than a de novo genetic event.

Certain genomic lesions consistently associate with chemoresistance and inferior outcomes. IDH1 and IDH2 mutations illustrate the complexity of interpreting molecular risk in AML. Although these alterations contribute to epigenetic dysregulation and leukemic stem-cell persistence, they do not uniformly define adverse-risk disease across treatment settings [20]. Rather than functioning primarily as universally high-risk lesions, IDH1/2 mutations are clinically important because they represent stable, targetable, and trackable molecular abnormalities that frequently persist throughout disease evolution [20]. Their persistence at relapse and utility in molecular monitoring therefore reflect clonal stability and therapeutic relevance rather than inherently adverse prognosis alone.

TP53 mutations, particularly in the context of complex karyotype, disrupt DNA damage response and apoptotic signaling, promote genomic instability, and facilitate rapid clonal diversification under cytotoxic stress [17, 21]. In contrast, the evolutionary behavior of FLT3-ITD and RAS-pathway mutations is often more dynamic and treatment-context dependent. Although FLT3-ITD may persist or expand at relapse in some patients, particularly after conventional chemotherapy, the widespread use of FLT3 inhibitors has demonstrated that FLT3-mutated clones may also contract or disappear entirely, allowing expansion of FLT3-wildtype resistant subclones. Similarly, NRAS and KRAS mutations frequently demonstrate instability between diagnosis and relapse, reflecting shifting clonal architecture under therapeutic pressure rather than linear evolutionary progression [22–26].

Mutations in DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 (“DTA” mutations) represent canonical clonal hematopoiesis-associated alterations that frequently precede leukemic transformation and may persist independently of active AML [27–30]. These mutations contribute to epigenetic dysregulation and altered hematopoietic differentiation but do not uniformly confer strong treatment resistance or independently define aggressive disease biology. Importantly, because DTA mutations often remain detectable in non-leukemic hematopoietic cells during remission, their persistence does not necessarily indicate residual AML and must be interpreted cautiously in molecular MRD assessment [30–32]. Nevertheless, these alterations may shape the evolutionary substrate from which resistant leukemic clones emerge under therapeutic pressure. Mutations in IDH1/2, early events in leukemogenesis, induce epigenetic remodeling through 2-hydroxyglutarate production and are typically retained at relapse, contributing to persistence of leukemic stem cell populations [33, 34].

Genetic lesions alone incompletely account for therapeutic resistance in high-risk AML. Relapse frequently reflects adaptive transcriptional reprogramming and epigenetic plasticity rather than acquisition of new dominant drivers. Dysregulated DNA methylation, histone modification, and enhancer circuitry collectively sustain leukemic stemness and buffer targeted inhibition [35, 36].

Relapse in AML may reflect transcriptional and epigenetic adaptation even in the absence of major new genetic drivers. Single-cell studies demonstrate that genetically distinct subclones can converge toward similar resistant transcriptional states under therapeutic pressure, highlighting epigenetic plasticity as an important mechanism of treatment failure [35, 37–40].

Microenvironment, immune biology, and leukemic stem cells

The bone marrow microenvironment plays a central role in the persistence and therapeutic resistance of high-risk AML by providing a protective niche that supports leukemic cell survival independent of genetic evolution [41]. Stromal cells, mesenchymal stem cells, endothelial cells, and extracellular matrix components collectively create a sanctuary that limits drug exposure and promotes resistance to cytotoxic therapy [42]. Among these mechanisms, the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis is critical for leukemic cell homing and retention within the marrow niche, thereby facilitating survival under treatment pressure [43, 44]. In parallel, adhesion-mediated interactions, particularly through VLA-4/VCAM-1 and CD44 pathways, activate anti-apoptotic signaling and further enhance resistance to chemotherapy [45]. Hypoxic conditions within the bone marrow niche additionally promote stemness and metabolic adaptation via HIF-1α signaling, reinforcing treatment resistance [46]. Together, these microenvironmental interactions contribute to the persistence of minimal residual disease and represent a key non-genetic driver of relapse.

Immune evasion further contributes to disease persistence in high-risk AML. Leukemic cells can downregulate antigen presentation through reduced expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, thereby impairing recognition by cytotoxic T cells [47, 48]. In addition, upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 promotes T-cell exhaustion and suppresses effective anti-leukemic immune responses [49]. The bone marrow microenvironment also fosters immunosuppressive conditions through expansion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and regulatory T cells, as well as secretion of inhibitory cytokines including IL-10 and TGF-β [50, 51]. These coordinated immune escape mechanisms allow residual leukemic cells to survive both chemotherapy and immunologic control, contributing to relapse.

Leukemic stem cells (LSCs) represent the central reservoir of therapy-resistant disease and the primary drivers of AML recurrence. These cells are typically quiescent, enabling them to evade cytotoxic agents that preferentially target proliferating cells [19, 52]. LSCs also exhibit increased expression of anti-apoptotic proteins such as BCL-2, conferring resistance to cell death under therapeutic stress [19, 53]. Importantly, genomic studies demonstrate that relapse frequently arises from pre-existing LSC-containing clones that survive initial therapy rather than from newly acquired mutations [47]. Interactions between LSCs and the bone marrow niche further reinforce stemness, survival signaling, and immune evasion, contributing to therapeutic resistance.

Precision diagnostics and monitoring

The biological complexity of high-risk AML necessitates diagnostic approaches capable of detecting disease beyond conventional morphologic thresholds. As therapeutic decisions increasingly depend on molecular vulnerabilities and early detection of relapse, precision diagnostics have become central to AML management. In this context, modern diagnostic strategies integrate comprehensive genomic profiling with longitudinal MRD monitoring to provide a dynamic assessment of treatment response and relapse risk [8].

Contemporary AML diagnostics combines morphologic evaluation with comprehensive cytogenetic and molecular profiling to establish disease classification, prognostic risk, and therapeutic eligibility. Current diagnostic workflows therefore include conventional cytogenetics, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels capable of detecting recurrent mutations and fusion transcripts relevant to AML biology and treatment selection [8]. Molecular profiling identifies clinically actionable genomic alterations in the majority of patients and forms the foundation of modern classification systems and risk-stratification frameworks [5, 54, 55].

in contemporary AML, MRD functions as a biologic stress test that reveals whether the dominant therapeutic strategy has successfully extinguished relapse-competent disease. This distinction is critical because baseline genetic risk and post-treatment MRD are not interchangeable forms of prognosis. Genetic classification estimates the probability of resistance before treatment begins, whereas MRD demonstrates resistance after therapy has already selected for surviving clones. The 2025 ELN-DAVID [56] recommendations therefore position MRD as a central clinical tool for harmonizing assay selection, interpretation, and treatment-guided application across AML subtypes. Clinically, persistent MRD can convert an otherwise favorable- or intermediate-risk case into “functional high-risk AML,” while MRD clearance may refine prognosis even among patients with adverse baseline features. However, MRD should not be interpreted in isolation. Its meaning depends on assay type, disease genotype, timing of assessment, treatment backbone, and whether the detected alteration represents residual leukemia or clonal hematopoiesis [57]. This is especially important for persistent DNMT3A, TET2, or ASXL1 mutations, which may reflect pre-leukemic clonal hematopoiesis rather than active AML [58, 59]. Therefore, the practical value of MRD lies not simply in labeling patients as positive or negative, but in integrating MRD kinetics with baseline genetics and treatment context to guide escalation, transplant timing, maintenance, or pre-emptive intervention.

Equally important, diagnostic samples establish molecular or immunophenotypic markers suitable for longitudinal MRD monitoring. Detectable lesions such as NPM1 mutations, core-binding factor fusions, FLT3-ITD, and other leukemia-specific alterations provide trackable targets for highly sensitive assays [60, 61]. Consequently, baseline diagnostic evaluation must be performed in a manner that allows subsequent MRD assay development and validation. When rare or patient-specific mutations are used as MRD targets, detectability should be confirmed at diagnosis using the intended assay platform to ensure reliable longitudinal monitoring [60].

MRD monitoring has emerged as the most powerful predictor of relapse and survival in AML and is increasingly integrated into therapeutic decision-making and clinical trial design [8]. Multiple complementary technologies are used for MRD detection, including multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC), quantitative PCR (qPCR), and next-generation sequencing (NGS). Because AML is biologically heterogeneous, no single approach is universally applicable; rather, optimal MRD assessment depends on the genetic subtype and the availability of trackable molecular markers [5, 54, 60].

Multiparameter flow cytometry represents the most broadly applicable MRD technique and can be used in the majority of AML cases [60]. MFC-MRD detects aberrant immunophenotypes using leukemia-associated immunophenotypes (LAIP) or “different-from-normal” approaches [62, 63]. Standardized antibody panels enable identification of abnormal blast populations, typically reported as the proportion of aberrant cells among CD45-expressing events, with a threshold of ≥ 0.1% commonly used to define MRD positivity [64–67]. While flow cytometry offers broad applicability and rapid turnaround, its analytical sensitivity is generally lower than molecular approaches and results require specialized expertise.

Molecular MRD detection provides higher analytical sensitivity and is preferred when leukemia-specific genetic markers are available. Validated qPCR assays exist for several recurrent alterations, including NPM1 mutations and fusion transcripts such as RUNX1::RUNX1T1, CBFB::MYH11, and PML::RARA these assays enable highly sensitive detection of residual leukemic cells, often reaching sensitivities of 10⁻⁴-10⁻⁵. Persistence or reappearance of NPM1-mutant transcripts during remission strongly predicts impending relapse, while transcript kinetics in core-binding factor AML provide quantitative measures of treatment response [68–73].

Emerging molecular MRD assays are also expanding applicability to additional AML subtypes, including KMT2A-rearranged disease, where sensitive fusion-based monitoring may identify molecular persistence before allogeneic transplantation and predict post-transplant relapse risk [74–76]. In these patients, serial molecular monitoring may create opportunities for pre-emptive therapeutic intervention, including early introduction of menin inhibitors before frank morphologic relapse [75, 77, 78]. This evolving strategy is currently being explored in studies such as the INTERCEPT trial [78] and further illustrates the growing role of MRD.

Advances in NGS technologies have further expanded the scope of MRD detection. Ultra-sensitive NGS assays allow monitoring of residual disease at variant allele frequencies approaching 10⁻⁴-10⁻⁵ and can track diagnostic driver mutations such as FLT3-ITD and other leukemia-specific variants [79–81]. NGS-based MRD detection has demonstrated prognostic significance both before and after allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation and may identify molecular relapse months before morphologic recurrence [59–84]. Interpretation of persistent mutations requires caution, however, particularly for genes associated with clonal hematopoiesis such as DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1, which may persist during remission without reflecting active leukemia [8].

The clinical interpretation of MRD requires integration with genetic risk, treatment context, and sampling time points. MRD provides prognostic information beyond baseline risk classification and refines relapse risk among patients who achieve morphologic remission [8]. Patients with persistent MRD after induction or consolidation therapy consistently demonstrate inferior relapse-free and overall survival compared with those who achieve MRD negativity [57].

The 2025 European LeukemiaNet (ELN-DAVID) MRD recommendations provide a standardized framework for MRD interpretation aligned with the ELN 2022 genetic risk classification [60]. MRD burden is stratified using assay-specific thresholds and interpreted within qualitative response categories to facilitate treatment-guided decision making [60]. This contextual approach recognizes that identical MRD levels may carry different prognostic implications depending on treatment timing and AML subtype.

MRD monitoring also enables early detection of molecular relapse prior to overt hematologic recurrence. Conversion from MRD negativity to detectable MRD exceeding assay-specific thresholds defines molecular relapse and may precede morphologic relapse by several months.5 Early detection allows implementation of pre-emptive strategies, including targeted therapy or early transplantation, aimed at preventing overt disease recurrence [58, 85].

MRD assessment further informs peri-transplant risk stratification. Although pre-transplant MRD positivity is associated with inferior outcomes following allogeneic transplantation, it is not considered a contraindication to transplantation [86]. Instead, MRD status may guide conditioning intensity, donor selection, and post-transplant maintenance strategies [87, 88]. In parallel, MRD negativity is increasingly used as a surrogate endpoint in clinical trials evaluating novel AML therapies, reflecting its strong correlation with long-term clinical outcomes [57, 60].

Treatment landscape

For younger and medically fit patients with high-risk AML, intensive chemotherapy remains a key component of treatment. The traditional backbone of therapy has been the combination of cytarabine and an anthracycline, commonly referred to as the “7 + 3” regimen, which consists of 7 days of cytarabine and 3 days of daunorubicin or idarubicin [5, 89, 90]. While this regimen can induce remission in a substantial proportion of patients, its long-term outcomes remain limited, particularly in patients with unfavorable cytogenetics or high-risk molecular features [89, 90].

To improve outcomes, alternative intensive regimens have been developed that incorporate additional nucleoside analogs or targeted agents. One example is the fludarabine, cytarabine, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, and idarubicin regimen (FLAG-IDA), which combines high-dose cytarabine with other cytotoxic agents to enhance antileukemic activity [91]. Similarly, the cladribine-based regimen consisting of cladribine, cytarabine, and idarubicin (CLIA) has demonstrated improved response rates in certain AML populations [92]. These intensified regimens aim to achieve deeper remission and increase the likelihood of successful consolidation therapy.

More recently, combinations of intensive chemotherapy with targeted agents have shown promising results. Venetoclax, a selective BCL-2 inhibitor, has demonstrated synergy with cytotoxic chemotherapy by promoting apoptosis in leukemic cells [93, 94]. When combined with intensive regimens such as FLAG-IDA or CLIA, venetoclax has produced high remission rates and deep molecular responses in newly diagnosed AML, including high-risk cases [92, 95]. For example, trials of CLIA-venetoclax reported composite remission rates exceeding 90% with high rates of MRD negativity [92].

An important therapeutic advance for secondary and myelodysplasia-related AML has been the development of CPX-351, a liposomal fixed-combination formulation of daunorubicin and cytarabine designed to optimize synergistic drug delivery [96]. In a pivotal phase III trial involving older adults with therapy-related AML or AML with myelodysplasia-related changes, CPX-351 demonstrated improved overall survival and higher rates of successful transplantation compared with conventional 7 + 3 chemotherapy [97, 98]. Importantly, the benefit of CPX-351 appeared particularly relevant in biologically adverse AML subsets enriched for complex cytogenetics and secondary-type molecular features. These findings established CPX-351 as a standard intensive induction option for selected patients with therapy-related or AML-MR disease and illustrated how biologically defined AML subsets may require therapeutically specialized induction strategies rather than uniform chemotherapy approaches.

Advances in genomic characterization have enabled the development of targeted therapies directed against specific molecular alterations that drive leukemogenesis. Several targeted agents have received regulatory approval in AML since 2017, including inhibitors of FLT3, IDH1/2, and BCL-2 [99].

FLT3 mutations, particularly FLT3-internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD), are among the most common adverse molecular abnormalities in AML and are associated with increased relapse risk and inferior survival when treated with conventional chemotherapy alone [100, 101]. The addition of FLT3 inhibitors to frontline therapy has significantly improved outcomes in this subgroup. In the phase III RATIFY trial, the FLT3 inhibitor midostaurin combined with standard induction and consolidation chemotherapy improved overall survival compared with chemotherapy alone [102]. Similarly, The QUANTUM-First trial further reinforced the principle that FLT3 inhibition must be integrated throughout the treatment continuum rather than restricted to induction alone. In newly diagnosed FLT3-ITD AML, the addition of quizartinib to intensive chemotherapy followed by maintenance therapy improved OS compared with chemotherapy alone, establishing prolonged FLT3 suppression as a clinically meaningful strategy. Importantly, however, relapse remained common despite improved survival, underscoring that FLT3 inhibition delays but does not fully eliminate resistant leukemic clones. The study also highlighted persistent challenges including emergence of secondary kinase-domain resistance mutations, uncertainty regarding optimal sequencing with transplantation, and the ongoing need for MRD-guided post-remission strategies. Thus, QUANTUM-First not only validated FLT3 inhibition in frontline AML, but also emphasized the increasingly dynamic nature of relapse risk in molecularly defined AML subsets [103].

IDH1 and IDH2 inhibitors have demonstrated clinical activity across multiple treatment settings, particularly in patients receiving lower-intensity therapy [104]. Combinations such as azacitidine plus ivosidenib or enasidenib represent important less-intensive therapeutic approaches for older or medically unfit patients with IDH-mutated AML, while combinations with intensive chemotherapy are being explored in selected fit populations [104, 105]. These approaches illustrate how molecularly targeted therapy increasingly allows treatment intensity to be adapted according to both disease biology and patient fitness [106]. Early-phase studies combining these inhibitors with standard induction therapy have shown encouraging response rates and survival outcomes [106].

Menin inhibitors represent one of the most promising emerging targeted approaches in AML, particularly in KMT2A-rearranged and NPM1-mutated disease. By disrupting HOXA/MEIS1-driven transcriptional programs, these agents have demonstrated encouraging activity in relapsed or refractory AML and may become important components of future combination strategies. Their biologic rationale and resistance mechanisms are discussed earlier in the manuscript [107].

Many patients with high-risk AML are older or have comorbidities that preclude intensive chemotherapy. Historically, outcomes in this population were extremely poor, with median survival of only a few months when treated with supportive care alone [108]. The development of hypomethylating agents (HMAs) such as azacitidine and decitabine provided a less intensive therapeutic option, although responses were modest [109, 110].

The introduction of venetoclax in combination with HMAs has significantly improved outcomes for patients who are not candidates for intensive chemotherapy. In the phase III VIALE-A trial, azacitidine combined with venetoclax significantly improved OS and remission rates compared with azacitidine alone in older or unfit patients with AML. However, longer follow-up and real-world experience have revealed important biological limitations of this approach [13]. While responses are often rapid and deep, durability remains poor in several adverse-risk subsets, particularly TP53-mutated AML and patients who relapse after HMA/venetoclax exposure [13, 111]. Consequently, VIALE-A simultaneously represented a major therapeutic advance and exposed the emergence of a new high-risk clinical state characterized by venetoclax-adapted resistance biology and limited salvage options. These observations have contributed directly to the development of treatment-context-specific risk models such as ELN 2024 [112]. Despite these improvements, long-term survival remains limited, highlighting the need for additional therapeutic strategies [113]. Despite initial responses, outcomes after relapse following HMA-venetoclax remain poor, with limited effective salvage options, highlighting a major unmet need in high-risk AML.

To further enhance efficacy, triplet regimens combining HMAs, venetoclax, and targeted agents have been explored. For example, combinations of azacitidine, venetoclax, and FLT3 inhibitors have demonstrated high response rates in FLT3-mutated AML [114]. Similarly, combinations including IDH inhibitors have shown encouraging clinical activity in IDH-mutated AML [115]. These triplet strategies aim to overcome resistance mechanisms and achieve deeper remissions in high-risk disease.

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains a critical component of curative therapy for many patients with high-risk AML. Historically, transplant decisions were driven mainly by diagnostic cytogenetic and molecular risk. However, contemporary evidence indicates that pre-transplant MRD captures a deeper layer of biologic resistance. MRD-positive remission before HSCT is not equivalent to MRD-negative remission. It identifies patients in whom chemotherapy or targeted therapy has reduced morphologic disease without eliminating relapse-competent clones [116–118]. Importantly, MRD positivity should not be viewed as a contraindication to transplantation, but rather as a signal that transplantation alone may be insufficient and should be integrated with optimized conditioning, post-transplant surveillance, and maintenance strategies [119–121]. Studies evaluating conditioning intensity suggest that residual molecular disease can modify the benefit-risk balance of transplant approaches, while post-transplant maintenance is becoming increasingly genotype- and MRD-informed [88]. In FLT3-ITD AML, for example, post-transplant FLT3 inhibition has provided proof of principle that maintenance therapy can reduce relapse risk, and the MORPHO [121] trial suggests that MRD may help identify patients most likely to benefit from gilteritinib maintenance. Thus, transplantation should be framed not as a single consolidation event, but as one component of an adaptive strategy in which MRD status, molecular genotype, conditioning intensity, and maintenance therapy are integrated to suppress post-remission clonal escape. Meta-analyses of clinical trials have demonstrated that transplantation in first complete remission improves survival compared with chemotherapy alone in patients with intermediate- or adverse-risk disease [122]. Consequently, HSCT is commonly recommended for eligible patients who achieve remission after induction therapy [5].

Advances in transplant techniques, including improved conditioning regimens, donor selection, and supportive care, have expanded the applicability of HSCT to older patients and those with comorbidities [123]. Reduced-intensity conditioning regimens have further increased the number of patients who can undergo transplantation safely.

Post-transplant maintenance therapy is an evolving strategy aimed at reducing relapse risk. Maintenance approaches using targeted agents such as FLT3 inhibitors have shown promising results in patients with FLT3-mutated AML [124]. Other strategies, including hypomethylating agents and venetoclax-based combinations, are currently under investigation to further improve long-term outcomes following transplantation [125].

Several novel therapeutic approaches are being investigated for patients with high-risk AML. Gemtuzumab ozogamicin, a CD33-targeted antibody-drug conjugate, is already incorporated into treatment for selected AML patients, while newer agents targeting CD123 and other antigens remain under investigation [126]. Other investigational strategies include bispecific antibodies, cellular therapies, and immune-based approaches designed to enhance antileukemic immune responses.

Additional strategies targeting signaling pathways such as RAS, MDM2, and other oncogenic drivers are also under development. As these therapies continue to evolve, combination regimens incorporating multiple targeted agents may further improve outcomes for patients with high-risk AML.

Resistance mechanisms: what’s actually driving treatment failure?

Many of the resistance mechanisms discussed throughout this review converge clinically during treatment exposure, where selective therapeutic pressure reshapes clonal architecture and promotes survival of biologically adaptable leukemic populations. Therapeutic resistance in AML emerges through dynamic interaction between clonal evolution, treatment-induced selective pressure, and adaptive survival programs. Rather than representing entirely new biology, relapse frequently reflects expansion of pre-existing resistant subclones that survive initial therapy and progressively acquire dominance under therapeutic pressure [5, 17, 19, 21, 127].

In addition to genetic drivers, LSCs represent a critical reservoir of therapy-resistant disease. LSCs remain central mediators of resistance because their quiescent state, metabolic adaptability, and apoptotic resistance allow persistence despite apparent morphologic remission [21, 128, 129].

Resistance is further reinforced by protective marrow microenvironmental interactions that sustain leukemic survival, immune escape, and therapy tolerance even in the setting of substantial cytoreduction [41, 42, 130].

Finally, resistance mechanisms also emerge during treatment with targeted therapies. For example, secondary kinase domain mutations or activation of alternative signaling pathways can confer resistance to FLT3 inhibitors, while metabolic adaptations and upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins such as MCL-1 can limit the efficacy of BCL-2 inhibition with venetoclax [131, 132].

Acquired resistance to menin inhibitors has also recently been described, particularly KMT2A-rearranged and NPM1-mutated disease, where the menin-KMT2A interaction sustains leukemogenic transcriptional programs through activation of canonical HOX/MEIS1 target genes [133, 134]. However, despite encouraging clinical activity, acquired resistance to menin inhibitors has increasingly been recognized as an emerging therapeutic challenge. Resistance may develop through secondary MEN1 mutations that impair inhibitor binding while preserving the menin-KMT2A interaction, as well as through adaptive epigenetic and transcriptional rewiring that restores leukemic self-renewal programs or suppresses alternative tumor suppressor pathways despite continued therapy [133, 134].

Strategies to overcome resistance

Recent translational research emphasizes proactive resistance prevention rather than reactive relapse management [135]. Three major strategies have emerged to overcome resistance: rational combinations, optimized sequencing approaches, and MRD-guided therapeutic adaptation.

Rational combination therapy seeks to suppress tumour adaptability by targeting complementary survival pathways simultaneously. Vertical inhibition involves blocking multiple nodes within the same oncogenic signalling cascade to prevent pathway reactivation. For example, dual inhibition of MEK and ERK has shown improved pathway suppression compared with single-agent targeting in MAPK-driven tumours. Similarly, combined PI3K and mTOR inhibition limits compensatory feedback activation frequently observed with monotherapy. By suppressing upstream and downstream components of the same pathway, vertical inhibition reduces adaptive escape through internal signalling redundancy [135, 136].

In contrast, orthogonal vulnerability strategies target biologically distinct dependencies that are not mechanistically overlapping. This approach often exploits synthetic lethality, such as combining DNA-damaging chemotherapy with targeted inhibition in specific molecular contexts. In AML, combining hypomethylating agents with BCL-2 inhibition enhances apoptotic priming and overcomes chemoresistance. Orthogonal combinations reduce cross-resistance because escape from one pathway does not confer survival advantage against the second agent. Moreover, combining cytotoxic therapy with immune modulation can enhance tumour antigen presentation and reshape the microenvironment. Successful implementation of rational combinations requires biomarker-guided selection to balance efficacy with toxicity [135, 136].

Residual disease enables detection of minimal tumour burden beyond morphologic remission. Increasing evidence supports the use of MRD as an adaptive therapeutic tool rather than solely a prognostic biomarker. Serial MRD monitoring may identify emerging resistant disease before overt hematologic relapse, thereby enabling pre-emptive intervention, therapeutic escalation, or early transplantation. In this framework, MRD becomes not only a marker of residual leukemia, but also a strategy for anticipating and potentially preventing clinically overt chemoresistance [135, 136].

Conclusions

High-risk AML remains a major clinical challenge despite substantial advances in molecular characterization and therapeutic development. Traditional definitions based solely on baseline genetics are increasingly insufficient, as risk is now understood to be dynamic and shaped by treatment context, MRD, and evolving resistance mechanisms. The integration of molecular profiling with MRD assessment has redefined high-risk AML as a functional and time-dependent state rather than a static diagnostic category.

Biologically, treatment failure in high-risk AML reflects the interplay of clonal evolution, epigenetic plasticity, leukemic stem cell persistence, and microenvironmental protection. These mechanisms collectively enable disease survival under therapeutic pressure and drive relapse, even in the absence of new dominant mutations. Consequently, effective management requires strategies that extend beyond targeting genetic lesions alone.

Therapeutically, the landscape is rapidly evolving with the incorporation of targeted agents, venetoclax-based combinations, and optimized transplantation approaches. However, durable remission remains limited for many patients, underscoring the need for rational combination strategies, MRD-guided treatment adaptation, and approaches that disrupt both leukemic cells and their supportive niches.

In 2026, improving outcomes in high-risk AML will depend on integrating precision diagnostics, dynamic risk assessment, and biologically informed therapeutic strategies. Future progress will likely emerge from personalized, adaptive treatment approaches designed to anticipate and overcome resistance, ultimately transforming high-risk AML into a more manageable disease.
