Comparison of canine adrenal gland size assessment using ultrasonography and computed tomography
Authors: Martina Krofič Žel, Ulla Urška Kočevar, Estera Pogorevc, Marko Jagodič, Darja Pavlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn humans, cats, and dogs, adrenal gland size appears slightly larger when measured by computed tomography (CT) compared to ultrasonography. However, CT measurement values have only been compared with ultrasound reference values retrospectively. This study aimed to directly compare CT and ultrasonographic measurement values of adrenal glands in dogs performed on the same day, under possible mimetic conditions. Thirty client-owned dogs without suspected adrenal disease scheduled for abdominal CT and ultrasound as part of diagnostic workup were enrolled. The maximum cranial and caudal pole diameter and length of both adrenal glands were measured using both modalities consecutively under the same anesthetic moment. Bland-Altman analysis of agreement between ultrasonographic and CT measurement values was performed. The results were also compared using the student’s t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney rank sum test for non-normal distribution.ResultsFor all investigated dimensions, the bias was positive, indicating that CT consistently produced larger measurement values than ultrasound.When stratification into different weight categories was applied, there was a significant difference in the measurement values of both poles of the left adrenal gland in all weight categories (P≤0.05). In contrast, for the cranial and caudal pole measurement values of the right adrenal gland, significant differences were found only in some weight categories. ConclusionsThe same imaging modality as originally used should be performed for patient follow-up. In larger dogs, greater discrepancies between ultrasonographic and CT acquired measurement values may be expected. When comparing measurement values acquired by CT and US, the information on the caudal pole size may be expected to be less variable than the cranial pole. Introduction

Information on the size of the adrenal glands in dogs is of great clinical value in veterinary endocrinology. The main clinical indications for adrenal size measurement are hyper- and hypoadrenocorticism, and the assessment of adrenal tumors [1].

Ultrasonography and measurement of the adrenal glands require an experienced operator and good equipment [2, 3]. Studies in dogs [1] and cats [4] report that ultrasonographic measurement of caudal pole height in the transverse plane has the least inter-operator variability.

On the other hand, Perfetti et al. [5] found the lowest intra-and interobserver variability in computed tomography (CT) measurements of adrenal length, with slightly higher variability in measurement values of caudal pole height on the transverse plane.

In humans, healthy cats [6, 7] and dogs [8], the size of the adrenal glands assessed by CT is reported to be slightly larger than that assessed by ultrasonography. This may be due to different sensitivities of the two methods [6], or to the fact that CT more easily allows to set the plane at which the maximum dimensions of the adrenal glands are determined [7].

A limitation of previous studies is that the values measured by CT have only been compared with reference values obtained with ultrasonography [7, 8], or have been performed retrospectively [6], where it is not known whether the ultrasonographic measurements were taken at the same time as the CT. In that study, ultrasonographic measurements of the adrenal glands were performed in non-sedated animals, whereas CT was performed under general anaesthesia.

To the authors’ knowledge, there is no published comparison of adrenal gland measurement values in dogs and cats performed on the same day, in the same animal, and under the same possible conditions (anaesthesia). Therefore, the aim of this prospective study was to compare two different imaging methods (CT and ultrasonography) for the assessment of adrenal gland size in dogs, with the emphasis on measurements being performed on the same day, in the same animal, and under the same possible conditions.

Results

Thirty dogs, 11 males and 19 females, were included in this study. Their average body weight was 16.58 ± 10.28 kg and the average age was 108.9 ± 37.7 months. In one of the dogs in the higher body weight category, the right adrenal gland could not be visualized due to a large amount of intraabdominal fat that prevented the use of the linear transducer.

According to the Bland-Altman analysis (Fig. 1, Supplement Table 1), measurement values on the right adrenal gland showed greater variability across all parameters (higher standard deviation and wider levels of agreement) compared with the left adrenal gland. For all investigated measurement values, the mean difference (bias) was positive, indicating that CT consistently produced larger measurement values than ultrasound.

A trend between the magnitude of the measurement values and the size of the difference between methods was observed for the cranial pole of the left adrenal gland and for the right adrenal gland length. For the remaining measurement values, this trend was absent or weak (Fig. 1, Supplement Table 1).

The best agreement between CT and ultrasound measurement values was observed for the caudal pole measurement values of both adrenal glands, which showed the smallest bias and the lowest variability. In these measurement values, the difference between methods did not increase meaningfully with larger gland sizes.

When comparing measurement values across different weight categories, we found a significant difference in the measurement values of both poles of the left adrenal gland in all weight categories. In contrast, for the cranial pole measurements of the right adrenal gland, significant differences were found in dogs in the middle and highest body categories. For the caudal pole measurement values of the right adrenal gland, significant differences were found in the lowest and highest body weight categories. Furthermore, right adrenal gland length was significantly different only in the highest weight category (Table 1; Figs. 2 and 3).

Discussion

Information about adrenal gland size is highly important to the clinician, particularly regarding the cranial and caudal diameters [9]. An increase in adrenal gland size is a common incidental finding on CT or ultrasonography. This may be observed in Cushing disease, chronic endogenous stress, or chronic disease. In contrast, adrenal glands that are initially within the lower normal size range but become progressively smaller over time in the same dog may have clinical significance when measured serially. It is common for clinical patients to undergo consecutive abdominal CT and ultrasonographic examinations, with measurement values compared as part of follow-up. Therefore, it is crucial to know what to expect when adrenal gland measurement values acquired by different imaging modalities are compared.

In all included dogs, compared with ultrasonography, CT consistently produced larger measurement values than ultrasound.

Furthermore, the cranial and caudal diameters of the left adrenal glands measured on CT were larger in all investigated body weight categories. In the highest body weight category, there was a significant difference in all measured values of the right adrenal gland and the largest difference in the measurement of the cranial diameter of the left adrenal gland. The clinician may expect that the larger the dog, the greater the possibility the adrenal gland measurement values will be larger on CT compared to ultrasonography. According to the results of our study, a suspected increase in adrenal gland size assessed on CT may not be confirmed on a subsequent ultrasonographic examination.

Furthermore, ultrasonographic reference values should not be used to assess adrenal gland size on CT. Both imaging modalities have different physical principles and should not be used interchangeably for the patient’s follow-up. In a study with human patients, morphometric measurements of several organs (liver, spleen, and kidneys) were conducted with CT and ultrasonography. It was concluded that follow-up examinations should be performed with the same imaging modality as used in the original examination [10]. According to the findings of the present study, the authors strongly agree with the statement. Bland-Altman analysis showed the greatest variability between the ultrasonographic and CT measurement value for all dimensions of the right adrenal gland compared to the left adrenal gland and the best agreement between CT and ultrasound for the caudal pole measurement values of both adrenal glands. However, this analysis does not provide any information on statistical significance nor information on measurements across different body weight categories [11], which showed slightly different results discussed later in the text.

A previous study [5] reported the highest intra- and inter-observer variability in cranial pole measurement values of adrenal glands on CT. It was proposed that this might be due to the close contact of the cranial pole of the adrenal gland with the kidneys, liver, epaxial muscles or abdominal masses and the lack of adipose tissue between the capsule of the adrenal gland and these organs [5]. In the present study, the authors shared a similar experience, especially in delineating the cranial pole of the right adrenal gland from surrounding structures on CT images. While a higher quantity of retroperitoneal fat aided in delineating the adrenal glands on CT images (a similar finding already described in cats [10]), image acquisition with ultrasonography was more difficult.

Furthermore, the measurement values of the length of the left adrenal gland did not significantly differ between the two imaging modalities. While adrenal gland length was reported to be consistent in CT and ultrasonography in cats [12, 13], it was reported that the craniocaudal dimension (length) of the adrenal glands showed the highest coefficient of variation on CT images in dogs [14]. In addition, adrenal gland length was expected to be underestimated on ultrasonography by a greater extent than on CT, where the plane which the maximum dimensions are determined is easier to be set [6]; however, in the present study, this was observed only in case of the right adrenal gland in the highest body weight category.

In the highest body weight category (dogs with body weight equal to or higher than 20 kg), the CT measurement values were significantly greater in five investigated parameters: in left cranial and caudal pole diameters, right cranial and caudal pole diameters, and length of the right adrenal gland. In contrast, in both lower weight categories, there was a significant difference in three of six investigated parameters: in the lowest body weight category these parameters were left cranial and caudal pole diameter and right caudal pole diameter; in dogs with body weight equal to 10 kg to less than 20 kg, the significantly different parameters were left cranial and caudal pole dimeters and right cranial pole diameter. The authors speculate that the difference in the largest weight category may have arisen due to greater X-ray scattering. The anatomic location of the adrenal glands is in the center of the body cavity. Furthermore, adrenal glands are relatively small compared to the size of the body. Currently available literature shows that adrenal gland size in dogs does not increase in a strictly linear manner with body weight. Only adrenal gland thickness has been shown to be significantly associated with body weight, age and sex [15, 16]. On the other hand, other adrenal dimensions, e.g. length and transverse width measured in different imaging planes, demonstrate variable or weaker correlations with body weight, exhibiting more heterogeneous relationships with body size rather than a uniform proportional increase [16–18]. Comparable trends have also been briefly described in cats, further supporting that adrenal gland growth does not follow a simple linear pattern across species [19, 20]. In addition, due to the small size of the adrenal glands, X-ray scattering may artificially elevate Hounsfield units in human adrenal glands as well, and potentially obscuring critical diagnostic thresholds [21]. On the other hand, ultrasonographic measurements were performed at an approximate transducer distance of 2.5 cm, where the measurements of the linear transducer used are most accurate [22]. Although ultrasonographic measurements were performed at an optimal transducer distance in all body weight groups, the observation that significant differences were predominantly detected in lower body weight categories may be explained by several factors. In smaller dogs, adrenal glands are absolutely smaller making the measurements more susceptible to partial volume effects and minor variations in probe positioning, which may have a proportionally greater impact on measured parameters. Furthermore, smaller adrenal glands may exhibit greater relative biological variability, and the higher spatial resolution of ultrasonography at short imaging depths may allow detection of subtle morphological or echogenic differences that are less apparent in higher body weight groups. In contrast, CT-based attenuation measurements in small adrenal glands are more prone to X-ray scattering and partial volume artifacts, potentially masking such differences.

In the present study, there was a significant difference between the two imaging modalities in the measurement values of the cranial and caudal poles of the left adrenal gland in all weight categories and a significant difference in the right caudal pole measurement values in the lowest weight category. Furthermore, there was a significant difference in the right cranial pole measurement values in both remaining weight categories. Therefore, given the greater variability in cranial pole measurement values between the CT and US methods, the authors suggest interpreting cranial pole adrenal gland enlargement measured on CT with care.

In previous studies, adrenal gland size measurement values were standardized to the L4 spinal cavity height on CT [23], while ultrasonographic measurements have been standardized to the aortic diameter [24]. Unfortunately, these measurement values cannot be used interchangeably.

To enable comparison of both modalities (ultrasonography and CT), reference values for the normal size of the adrenal glands in dogs of different body weights examined by CT still need to be established.

This study has some limitations. Although a priori power calculation was performed, dogs in different weight categories were not equally distributed. Furthermore, different anesthesia protocols were used in this study. While no effect of propofol on adrenal gland measurements in cats has been reported [25], to the authors’ knowledge, there are no data on the effect of other anesthetics.

No comparison with volumetric data was performed. Although dogs with suspected adrenal gland disease were not included, specific endocrine testing was not performed. However, the mean measurement values of CT and US were consistent with the upper limits previously reported for clinically healthy dogs of the same weight category for US [9].

Although the most reproducible results for adrenal gland measurement values acquired with CT are reported to be in the transverse plane [5, 26], we used the sagittal plane. The decision to use the sagittal rather than the transverse plane was made to achieve the most comparable results possible with the positioning and measurements of the adrenal glands on ultrasonography.

In addition, no data on the body condition score of the patients were available. Different body conformations and body condition scores could affect the amount of adjacent retroperitoneal fat, which enhances visibility and demarcates the adrenal gland borders on CT.

Conclusion

The same diagnostic imaging modality as originally used should be performed for patient follow-up. In larger dogs, greater discrepancies between ultrasonographic and CT acquired measurement values may be expected.

Materials and methods

Thirty client-owned dogs scheduled for abdominal CT and ultrasound as part of the diagnostic workup were enrolled. Dogs of various breeds were included. CT and abdominal ultrasound examinations were performed consecutively under the same general anesthesia. Different premedication protocols were used, according to the ASA (American Sociey of Anaesthesiologists) stage of the patients. Anaesthesia was induced with propofol (Propoven, Fresenius Kabi, IL, USA) titrated until muscle relaxation and absence of laryngeal reflex were achieved and endotracheal intubation was enabled. Anaesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (Sevorane, Abbvie Inc., IL, USA) in a mixture of oxygen (0.4 L/min) and air (0.8 L/min). Apnea was achieved by combining manual hyperventilation and propofol bolus. In all patients, CT was performed first, followed by US examination, both imaging procedures completed within 30 min after induction of anesthesia.

Dogs with suspected adrenal disease were excluded from the study. Adrenal disease was suspected based on clinical history, physical examination findings, abdominal ultrasonography, and routine laboratory results. Exclusion criteria included ultrasonographically detected nodular adrenal changes, a history or clinical signs consistent with adrenal disease (primarily polyuria/polydipsia and changes in body habitus indicating Cushing’s syndrome including endocrine alopecia and pendulous abdomen), and/or laboratory abnormalities suggestive of hyperadrenocorticism (including marked thrombocytosis, increased serum activity of alkaline phosphatase and low urine specific gravity).The number of dogs included was determined by a priori power calculation with a power of 80% and 5% significance level. According to their body weight, the dogs were divided into three categories: <10 kg, ≥ 10 to < 20 kg, and ≥ 20 kg.

All procedures complied with the relevant governmental regulations regarding animal welfare (reference number of the ethical review process 033 − 5/2025-16) and the owners signed an informed consent statement prior to inclusion. Ultrasonographic examination and measurements was performed always by the same researcher (MKŽ) using the abdominal ultrasound Mindray M9GI, (Mindray, China) with a linear array transducer (L12-4s) at a frequency of 8 MHz with axial resolution 0.19–0.30 mm.

The maximum cranial and caudal pole diameters and the length of both adrenal glands were measured in the longitudinal view (Figs. 4 and 5).

Ultrasonographic measurements were performed in the longitudinal view. Figures 4 and 5 show the position of the calipers to measure the maximum cranial and caudal pole diameter and length of the adrenal gland.

The abdominal CT was performed using a 16 slice Siemens Somatom 16 S (Siemens, Germany). The CT examination was performed in ventral recumbency. Acquisition parameters for the CT examination were helical scan mode, pitch 0.95, tube rotation time 1 s, tube voltage 110 kVp, tube current 110–260 mAs, slice thickness 1 mm and image matrix 512 × 512. Native and post-contrast images were acquired. Each animal received a bolus injection of 600 mg/kg body weight intravenous contrast medium (Omnipaque 350 mg/ml, GE Healthcare, Norway) into the left or right cephalic vein. The contrast medium was manually injected, and image acquisition was performed 30–60 s post-contrast injection.

The abdominal CT was performed either by UUK or EP, but all the measurements were performed by UUK.

A commercially available DICOM viewing software (Siemens Syngo VC40) was used to view the images and perform the measurements. Sagittal post-contrast images in the soft-tissue reconstruction window (window level 40, window width 350) were evaluated by UUK, who was not familiar with the results of previous ultrasonographic measurements. Measurements of maximal length and maximal cranial and caudal diameter were performed by placing the MPR axis parallel to the long axis of the evaluated adrenal gland as described elsewhere [5].

Computed tomographic measurements were performed by aligning the multiplanar reconstruction axis parallel to the long axis of the evaluated adrenal gland. Figures 6 and 7 show the position of the calipers to measure the maximum cranial and caudal pole diameter and the length of the adrenal gland.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using commercial software (Sigma plot 11.0). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine the distribution of the data. Based on its results, either parametric (student’s t-test) for normally distributed data or non-parametric (Mann-Whitney rank sum test for non-normally distributed data) statistical tests were applied.

Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). A value of P ≤ 0.05 was considered significant. Plots were prepared using OriginPro 2017.

Agreement between computed tomography (CT) and ultrasonography (US) in the measurement values of canine adrenal glands was evaluated using Bland-Altman analysis [11]. For each parameter, the mean difference between CT and US (bias) was calculated to determine whether one imaging modality consistently produced higher values. The variability of the differences was assessed by calculating the standard deviation and the 95% limits of agreement, defined as the bias ± 1.96 times the standard deviation. To assess proportional bias, the differences between methods were regressed against their mean values, and the coefficient of determination (R²) was used to describe the extent to which the difference depended on the magnitude of the measurement values. All analyses were performed using standard spreadsheet software following established Bland–Altman methodology.
