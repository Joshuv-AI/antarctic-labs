Direct resist-free patterning of an organic semiconductor via thermal scanning probe lithography
Authors: Kaimin Luo, Jun Li, Yanshu Gu, Tianlei Ma, Jingyuan Zhang, Liying Cao, Zhaohui Zhu, Heng Tang, Pengcheng Xu, Hao Jia, Min Tu, Xinxin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organic semiconductors are widely regarded as a key material platform for next generation flexible and wearable electronics. Realizing high-resolution, well-defined patterns is essential to scale organic field effect transistors for higher device density and greater electrical performance. Conventional photolithographic patterning technologies typically face the challenges of chemical contamination induced by photoresist, developers and chemical etchant. Here, we report a resist-free, maskless patterning approach for pentacene based on thermal scanning probe lithography (t-SPL), exploiting the localized thermochemical conversion of the soluble precursor 13,6-N-sulfinylacetamidopentacene into pentacene. By tuning key t-SPL parameters, including probe loading conditions and scanning velocity, well-defined pentacene nanostructures with a minimum full width at half maximum of 200 nm were achieved. Directly patterned pentacene channels were further integrated into field-effect transistors, demonstrating electrical functionality. This work establishes t-SPL as a viable route for direct, high-resolution patterning of organic semiconductors and provides a promising pathway toward further miniaturization and integration of organic electronic devices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04658-8.

Introduction

Organic electronics have advanced rapidly in fields such as displays, flexible electronics, and wearable devices in recent years [1,2]. Compared with conventional silicon-based semiconductors, the main advantage of organic semiconductors is that they can realize large-area manufacturing at low temperature and low cost, and are compatible with flexible substrates [3,4]. Pentacene is widely regarded as a promising organic semiconductor, owing to its high charge-carrier mobility [5], low-temperature processability [6], capability for large-area manufacturing [7,8], and broad substrate compatibility [9]. At the circuit-integration and system-application levels, pentacene has been reported to act as a p-type semiconductor for organic CMOS [10–12]. In the flexible sensor area, pentacene has also been employed to construct large-area flexible pressure-sensing arrays [13].

High-resolution patterning is critical for the integrated fabrication of organic semiconductor devices [1,14,15]. Precise definition of the semiconductor channel suppresses off-state current and parasitic leakage, mitigates electrical crosstalk between adjacent devices, and ultimately enables device miniaturization and higher integration density [14,16–20]. However, existing patterning strategies like photolithography suffer from two limitations. First, most approaches rely on auxiliary organic materials, such as photoresists, developers, and organic solvents, introduced through multistep processes involving exposure, development, etching, or lift-off [21,22]. These processes pose severe chemical compatibility and contamination risks for organic semiconductors. Because organic semiconductors are typically highly sensitive to solvents, plasma treatment, and residual contaminants, such exposure often leads to material degradation and performance deterioration [15,23–26]. Secondly, small feature size of conventional photolithography is intrinsically constrained by the optical diffraction limit. Although shortening the exposure wavelength toward the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) regime offers a potential route to higher resolution, it comes at the expense of substantially increased equipment complexity and cost [27,28].

For small-molecule organic devices such as pentacene, several patterning strategies have been developed. One common approach is shadow-mask-assisted deposition. For example, Vusser et al. patterned SU-8 photoresist and subsequently used it as a shadow mask to deposit pentacene under vacuum conditions [29]. While straightforward, conventional shadow-mask techniques typically yield device channel lengths on the order of tens of micrometers, with the achievable resolution limited by the mask geometry and the UV exposure tool . An alternative strategy employs a protective layer to enable patterning via conventional photolithography and etching. Zhou et al. first deposited pentacene layer and then coated it with a Parylene layer via chemical vapor deposition to fully encapsulate and protect the organic semiconductor. Then the standard photolithography followed by dry etching was used to define the pentacene pattern. This encapsulation-assisted process isolates the organic semiconductor from the chemicals used in lithography, thereby minimizing contamination of the pentacene [30].

Therefore, it is necessary to develop a patterning strategy that simultaneously enables low-contamination (or resist-free) and high-resolution linewidth. Scanning probe lithography (SPL) is a promising approach to pattern functional materials locally with high resolution. SPL is a maskless, direct patterning family in which a functionalized nanoscale probe tip in contact with the surface of the samples to induce the localize modification via tip-sample interaction (e.g. thermal, mechanical, electrical and diffuse) [31–34]. Among SPL techniques, thermal scanning probe lithography (t-SPL) utilizes a nanosized, heated tip to transfer heat directly to the sample surface [34]. The resulting tip-surface thermal interaction induces direct material modification for patterning [35,36]. Unlike electrical SPL , t-SPL uses heat to drive the reaction rather than electrical field, which represents a more general stimulus and is therefore compatible with a broad range of organic material systems. For instance, Fenwick et al. employed the t-SPL technique to pattern an organic semiconductor through the thermal conversion of poly(p-xylene tetrahydrothiophenium chloride) (PXT) to poly(p-phenylene vinylene) (PPV) [37].

In this work, we employed t-SPL to achieve direct resist-free patterning of pentacene through the thermal conversion of organic precursor 13,6-N-sulfinylacetamidopentacene. During the patterning process, a heated thermal probe was brought into contact with the sample surface to deliver heat and trigger the thermochemical reaction, achieving localized conversion and spatially selective patterning. We performed a set of characterizations to assess the properties of the spin-coated pentacene precursor films, gaining detailed insight into their quality. A systematic optimization of process parameters enabled the direct patterning of the pentacene precursor into lines with full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 200 nm. This t-SPL strategy was then used to write pentacene nanostructure as a channel for field effect transistor (FET), successfully demonstrating a viable, resist-free method for integrating organic semiconductors into electronic devices (Fig. 1).

Results and discussion

It has been reported that the precursor 13,6-N-sulfinylacetamidopentacene can be thermally converted to pentacene in the temperature range of 120–200 °C via a retro Diels-Alder reaction [38]. Hence, we employed this precursor for the direct patterning of pentacene by t-SPL technique [38,39].To obtain high-quality precursor films suitable for t-SPL processing, the effects of spin-coating speed on film thickness and morphology were investigated. The precursor was dissolved in chloroform with a concentration of 20 mg/mL and subsequently spin coated onto substrates. The spin-coated thin films exhibit uniform colors under visible light (Fig. 2a–c upper right), suggesting the formation of uniform films. The color difference is attributed to the optical interference associated with differences in film thickness. The film thicknesses at spin-coating speeds of 2000, 5000, and 8000 rpm, correspond to 142 nm, 109 nm, and 89 nm, respectively (Fig. 2a–c). The film thickness exhibits a monotonic decrease with increasing spin-coating speed (Fig. 2f), in agreement with the characteristic trend observed for spin-coated films.

The films prepared without hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS) treatment exhibited similar trend that higher spin-coating speed leads to thinner films. Notably, at the same spin speed, films formed on untreated substrates are consistently thinner than those on HMDS-treated substrates, which can be attributed to the enhanced surface adhesion provided by HMDS treatment (Fig. S2).

Considering that the subsequent t-SPL processing relies on localized heat transfer to trigger thermochemical reaction, thinner precursor films are more favorable for rapid heat propagation across the entire film thickness. Therefore, films prepared at a spin-coating speed of 8000 rpm were selected for subsequent experiments. The top-view SEM images show that the resulting film is uniform and smooth without visible defects and cracks (Fig. 2d). AFM analysis further reveals an average surface roughness (Ra) of only 0.5 nm (Fig. 2e), confirming the excellent surface smoothness and uniformity required for high-resolution t-SPL patterning.

After confirming the film morphology quality, the thermal response characteristics of the precursor were further evaluated. Thermogravimetric analysis of the precursor powder illustrated that the weight loss started at about 110 °C, reached a plateau at 200 °C. The total weight loss over this temperature range was about 27%, which is in accordance with the loss of N-sulfinylacetamide from the precursor. The corresponding differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curve also verified that an endothermic peak appeared within the same temperature range, indicating that a characteristic endothermic reaction occurs in this regime (Fig. 3a) [38].

Since the t-SPL processing area is spatially limited, it is difficult to carry out conventional spectroscopic and electrical characterization of the locally converted material. To validate the macroscopic-scale conversion, a control experiment was conducted in which a precursor film spin coated at 2000 rpm was thermally treated at 200 °C for 90 s under a nitrogen atmosphere with hot-plate. The precursor film is initially smooth and continuous, and the surface roughness (Ra) is only 0.2 nm (Fig. 3b). Whereas after the thermal treatment, the film is composed of nanosized crystallites from amorphous-like polymer (Fig. 3c). The UV-Vis spectrum (200–800 nm) of pentacene film obtained by thermal annealing shows two clearly separated spectral regions (Fig. 3d). As a large π-conjugated system, pentacene exhibits a characteristic absorption region between 500 and 700 nm. Moreover, a main absorption peak is observed at 664 nm, which is assigned to the S0-S1 transition of pentacene. The UV-Vis spectra of pentacene and precursor consistent with the absorption peaks reported in the literature [38, 40–42].

To further evaluate the properties of the treated film, a bottom-gate, top-contact thin-film transistor was fabricated. Figure 3e presents the transfer characterization which shows a threshold voltage of 38 V and an on/off ratio about 104. Figure 3f shows the output characteristic curve of the device under different gate voltages. The carrier mobility, µ, was calculated to be 0.0065 cm2 V− 1 s− 1. These results demonstrate that the pentacene films obtained via thermal induced conversion possess stable charge transport capabilities, suggesting the possibility of patterning pentacene by t-SPL processing.

It should be pointed out that hot-plate heating is a static and global thermal treatment. Its effect is mainly determined by the thermal properties of the material itself. In contrast, t-SPL is a highly localized thermal processing method. Its outcome depends not only on the material properties, but also on the writing parameters. In this work, direct nanoscale chemical characterization remains challenging because of the limited dimensions of the patterned regions. The hot-plate heating experiment can effectively verify that from the material perspective , t-SPL processing and patterning pentacene precursors is feasible.

(Fig. S6, S7) In the velocity range of 1-3 μm/s, the FWHM increase slightly and the height of patterned lines increase with increasing scanning velocity. (Fig. 5d, e) A lower scanning velocity increases the tip-sample interaction time and thus the thermal input . However, the final topography is not governed by heat input alone. In the present system, the thermal conversion of 13,6-N-sulfinylacetamidopentacene to pentacene is accompanied by volatile by-product desorption and molecular rearrangement or crystallization, as supported by the endothermic event and ~ 27% mass loss in the TGA/DSC data (Fig. 3a). Therefore, a lower scanning velocity increases the local thermal input, it also promotes more complete by-product removal and structural rearrangement, thereby reducing the feature height and limiting lateral broadening. It should be noted that the above discussion is regarded as a qualitative interpretation based on the observed topographic evolution, rather than a quantitative model of the local t-SPL conversion process.

After optimizing process parameters, t-SPL was used to fabricate the channel region of a pentacene FET (Fig. 6a, b). We used t-SPL to pattern FET with the active region W/L = 3μm/2.7μm. Highly organized pentacene crystalline domains were observed to form preferentially along the scanning direction (Fig. 6c). The width of each pentacene crystalline domains is approximately 100 nm. The result indicates that t-SPL can induce crystallization and pattern pentacene locally.

Figure 6d presents the transfer characteristics of the pentacene FET. The device exhibits an on/off current ratio of about 750, and the calculated carrier mobility is about 1.3 × 10− 6 cm2 V− 1 s− 1. The performance of the device is lower than that of the thin film transistor prepared by the hot plate thermal annealing. The reduced electrical performance of the t-SPL patterned devices may be attributed to several factors. These include non-uniformprecursor to pentacene conversion during the t-SPL process, poor semiconductor electrode interface in the bottom contact geometry. Future work will focus on two aspects. First, the patterning conditions, including probe loading, scanning velocity, and substrate assisted heating, will be optimized to improve channel morphology[44]. Second, the device interfaces and electrode contacts will be improved.

In conclusion, we developed a resist-free, direct t-SPL patterning strategy for pentacene based on the thermally induced conversion of 13,6-N-sulfinylacetamidopentacene to pentacene. The precursor-to-pentacene change was confirmed by thermal and spectroscopic analysis , as well as by the electrical performance of the fabricated thin-film FETs. The influence of process parameters (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{V}_{\mathrm{set}}$$\end{document}, scanning velocity) on the patterns has been investigated, resulting in the formation of a minimum line width of 200 nm. A FET has been successfully fabricated by directly defining the pentacene channel using t-SPL. Although t-SPL is currently limited in throughput due to its inherently serial single-probe operation, it remains highly valuable for high-resolution, resist-free, and localized patterning, especially in rapid prototyping and process exploration. In the future, its practical scalability could be further enhanced through multi-probe parallelization and integration with high-throughput techniques such as nanoimprint lithography or fast laser-writing.

Experimental section

Film fabrication: 13,6-N-sulfinylacetamidopentacene (95%) was obtained from Suzhou Junye. The solvent chloroform (99.0%) was purchased from Sinopharm. Hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS, 99.9%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals were used as received. The substrates were treated with HMDS vapor at 110 °C for 20 min. The precursor was dissolved in chloroform at a concentration of 20 mg/mL and spin-coated on the substrates consisted of silicon wafers covered with a 285 nm thick oxide layer. LeBo Science EZ6-S spin coater was used for spin-coating process. For UV-Vis characterization, the precursor films were prepared on quartz substrates. The substrates underwent the same treatment. The spin-coating speed was 2000 rpm.

Pattern fabrication: The sample films were placed on the sample holder of Bruker Icon Dimension AFM for t-SPL patterning in the ambient environment. The heating module of the probe was drove by Keysight E36312A DC power supply.

Materials characterization: The field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) measurements were performed on ZEISS Sigma 300 (the aperture: 30 μm and accelerating voltage: 5 kV). The thermal analyzer used was a NETZSCH STA449F5.The thermal analysis was performed at the heating rate of 10 °C/min in N2 environment. The UV–Vis spectroscopy measurements were performed on Shimadzu UV-3600i Plus with a step size of 1 nm.

Probe temperature calibration: The probe temperature was calibrated by Raman spectroscopy, since the Raman spectrum of silicon exhibits a pronounced temperature dependence. Specifically, the Raman shift is negatively correlated with temperature. As the temperature increases, the silicon Raman peak position redshifts, shifting toward lower wavenumbers. The Raman spectrum of the probe at an unknown temperature is acquired and the corresponding Raman redshift is extracted. The temperature is then estimated using a commonly adopted temperature coefficient of the peak position (− 0.022 cm−1/K) [45, 46].

Transistor fabrication and characterization: The gold electrodes(W/L = 8/2.7) were fabricated by wet chemical etching on heavily doped p-type silicon substrates capped with a 285 nm thermally grown oxide layer. First, a 5 nm Cr adhesion layer was first deposited, followed by the evaporation of a 40 nm Au film. Subsequently, a photolithography step was applied on top of the Au film using photoresist LC100A (DuPont Japan Ltd. film thickness: 1.4 μm). The electrodes were obtained after wet chemical etching (Cr etching solution: (NH4)2Ce(NO3)6, 8–18%, HNO3, 5–16%, etching time:5 s; Au etching solution: (NH4)2HPO4, 5–15%, KI, 15–20%, I2, 3–8%, etching time:10 s. JiangYin Jianghua Microelectronic materials Co.Ltd) and photoresist removal by acetone (99.5%, JiangYin Jianghua Microelectronic materials Co.Ltd). The transistor was characterized by Keithley 4200 A-SCS semiconductor parameter analyzer.
