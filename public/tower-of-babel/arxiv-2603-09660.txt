Proportionality Degree in Participatory Budgeting
Aris Filos-Ratsikas, Sreedurga Gogulapati, Georgios Kalantzis

Abstract
We initiate the study of the proportionality degree for participatory budgeting, with a particular focus on two popular methods: the Method of Equal Shares ( mes ) and Phragmén’s Sequential Rule. Among other results, we derive tight bounds (up to small constant factors) on the proportionality degree of these two rules, which showcase that, despite mes satisfying stronger axiomatic guarantees, the two rules have the same proportionality degree from a quantitative perspective. We complement our theoretical findings with an extensive experimental evaluation on real-world participatory budgeting datasets, the results of which closely mirror those of our developed theory. Our experiments also provide more insights into the comparisons between the rules.



1
Introduction

Participatory budgeting (PB) is a democratic process through which citizens directly influence the allocation of public funds. First implemented in Porto Alegre in 1989, PB has since been adopted globally at various scales, from neighborhoods and cities to schools and universities, as a mechanism to enhance civic engagement, improve transparency, and promote more equitable outcomes in public spending
[
15
;
45
]
. At its core, PB enables participants to propose, deliberate upon, and select projects proposals for funding within a fixed budget constraint, thereby embedding elements of direct democracy into budgetary decision-making.

The computational aspects of PB have received increasing attention in recent years, motivated by the need to design fair and efficient methods for aggregating citizen preferences into concrete budget allocations
[
8
]
. A significant line of work in this area adopts an axiomatic approach, where desirable properties of fairness and representation are formalized as axioms. In particular, axioms inspired by approval-based multi-winner voting such as Justified Representation (JR), Proportional Justified Representation (PJR), and Extended Justified Representation (EJR)
[
3
;
36
]
, have been adapted to the PB setting to capture the normative idea that cohesive groups of voters should be entitled to a share of the budget roughly proportional to their size
[
32
]
. These axioms offer qualitative guarantees and serve as important design goals for PB rules. However, axiomatic properties are inherently binary: a rule either satisfies a given axiom or it does not.

To complement the axiomatic approach, the notion of the
proportionality degree
has been introduced as a quantitative measure of how well a participatory budgeting rule achieves proportional representation
[
4
;
38
]
. The proportionality degree captures the extent to which a cohesive group of voters can secure a share of the budget in proportion to its size.
This notion has been extensively studied in the context of multi-winner voting, where the proportionality degree of prominent rules such as Phragmén’s rule
[
34
]
and the Proportional Approval Voting (PAV) rule
[
44
]
(see also
[
26
]
) is well understood. However, despite the growing body of work on PB, the proportionality degree of PB rules has remained unexplored.

This gap motivates our study: we aim to initiate a systematic examination of the proportionality degree of all the PB rules known to satisfy proportionality-related axioms. We particularly focus on the well known
Method of Equal Shares

[
32
]
and the
Phragmén
sequential
[
34
]
(see also
[
30
]
) rules. Both rules share a market-based intuition and have been recognized for their fairness-oriented properties in the literature.

1.1
Our Contribution

We derive tight lower and upper bounds (up to small constant factors) on the proportionality degree of these two rules.
mes
is known to satisfy strong axiomatic guarantees such as Extended Justified Representation (EJR), whereas Phragmén does not. However, somewhat surprisingly, both rules have the same proportionality degree guarantees from a quantitative perspective. This finding highlights a deeper structural similarity between these methods.

To complement our theoretical analysis, we also conduct an extensive experimental evaluation on real-world PB datasets covering 100 elections. The experiments include studying the proportionality degree of the
mes
and Phragmén rules, both with and without exhaustion. Additionally, we also compare their performance with a rule that greedily selects projects with respect to their approval score, which is widely used in practice. Our empirical results show that, with respect to proportionality degree, both the
mes
and Phragmén rules with exhaustion exhibit comparable performance in practice (with the latter performing slightly better than the former). Moreover, all the above rules clearly outperform the greedy rule.

Preliminaries

An approval-based
participatory budgeting (PB)
instance is a tuple
I=(N,\mathcal{T},B,C,\mathcal{A})
, where:

-

N
is a set of
n

voters
, and
\mathcal{T}
is a set of
m

projects
.

-

B
is the available

.

-

C
is the
cost profile
, namely
\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a function that associates a cost with each
t\in\mathcal{T}
. For each subset of projects
T\subseteq\mathcal{T}
, we define
\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(T\right)=\sum_{t\in T}\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(t\right)
for the total cost of
T
. We will use
\mathcal{C}
to refer to the collection of all possible cost profiles.

-

\mathcal{A}
is the
approval profile
, namely a collection of subsets of projects
A_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
which denote the projects that voter
i\in N
approves.

A subset of projects
W\in\mathcal{T}
is feasible if
\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(W\right)\leq B
. A participatory budgeting rule (PB rule)
f
takes as input an instance of the above form and returns a feasible subset of projects
W=f(I)
, which we refer to as the
proposal
of rule
f
.

To define the appropriate notions of proportionality, in multiwinner voting the literature has employed the concept of an
\ell
-large group. In the case of PB however, a notion that is based only on the number of projects is inadequate, since the “proportional rights” of each project’s group of supporters should also depend on the project’s cost. For this reason, the following notion of
T
-cohesiveness
has been defined for PB, see
[
32
;
30
]
.

Definition 1

(
T
-Cohesive Group
[
32
;
30
]
)
.

Given a PB instance
I
and a subset
T\subseteq\mathcal{T}
of projects, we say that a group of voters
V\subseteq N
is
T
-cohesive if and only if

T\subseteq\bigcap\limits_{i\in V}{A_{i}}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \frac{\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(T\right)}{B}\leq\frac{|V|}{n}.

We next define the notion of the
average satisfaction
of a set of voters
V
.

Definition 2

(Average Satisfaction)
.

Given a PB instance
I
, a subset of projects
W
and a subset of voters
V
, the average satisfaction of
V
is defined as

\emph{\text{avg}}_{W}(V)=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{i\in V}|W\cap A_{i}|.

We are now ready to define the proportionality degree.

Definition 3

(Proportionality Degree)
.

Given a PB instance
I
, the proportionality degree of a PB rule
f
for a subset
T\in\mathcal{T}
, denoted by
d_{f}(T)
, is defined as follows:

d_{f}(T)=\sup\left\{g(T):\min_{V\in\mathcal{V}(T)}\emph{\text{avg}}_{f(I)}(V)\geq\min(|T|,g(T))\right\},

where
\mathcal{V}(T)
denotes the set of all
T
-cohesive groups of voters.

The proportionality degree of a PB rule
f
for a given subset
T\in\mathcal{T}
of projects intuitively denotes the worst-case bound on the average satisfaction of
T
-cohesive groups of voters.
In particular, the proportionality degree of a rule for a set
T
represents the largest worst-case lower bound on the average satisfaction of any
T
-cohesive group of voters.

Finally, we refer to a well-known axiom of proportionality, namely the
Extended Justified Representation (EJR)
. EJR was first proposed for approval-based committee elections by
3
and extended to the participatory budgeting model by
32
.

Definition 4

(Extended Justified Representation (EJR) for (approval-based) PB
[
32
]
)
.

A rule
f
satisfies EJR if for each participatory budgeting instance
I
, each
T\subseteq\mathcal{T}
, and each
T
-cohesive group
V\subseteq N
of voters there exists a voter
i\in V
such that
|A_{i}\cap f(I)|\geq|T|
.

First, we provide the definition of the

Definition 5

(

[

)

Each voter is initially given an equal fraction of the budget, i.e. each voter is given

For each selected project

be the amount that voter

For

\sum_{i\in N_{t}}\min(b_{i},\rho)=\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(t\right)

where

. If no project is

In a nutshell, at each step, a project

Proportionality Degree of the Phragmén Sequential Rule

We now move on to studying the proportionality degree of another well-known rule for PB, namely the
Phragmén
Sequential rule
[
34
]
. This rule does not achieve the strong proportionality properties of EJR, but it satisfies the weaker axiom of Proportional Justified Representation (PJR), see
14
. One might feel inclined to believe that the stronger axiomatic properties of
mes
would result in a larger proportionality degree as well. However, as observed also by
38
, it is clearly conceivable for a rule to violate EJR while achieving a high proportionality degree. This is because while the satisfaction of
one
voter may be low, the satisfaction of the other voters in the same cohesive group may be fairly high.

We provide the definition of the
Phragmén
Sequential rule for PB below. Our definition follows the recent literature on computational social choice, e.g., see
[
30
;
32
]
.

Definition 6

(
Phragmén
rule
[
30
]
)
.

The rule starts with an empty proposal. Every voter gets credits continuously at the rate of one unit of credits per unit of time. At the first moment
t
when there is a group of voters
V
who all approve a not-yet-selected project
p
, and who together have
\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(p\right)
units of credits, the rule adds
p
to the proposal and asks the voters from
V
to pay the cost of
p
(i.e. the rule resets the balance of each voter from
V
), while the others keep their so far earned money. The rule stops when it would select a project which would overshoot the budget.

Next, we present the proofs for the proportionality degree of the
Phragmén
rule for the approval-based PB model. Our lower bound proof leverages a potential function argument, similarly as in
38
for the case of multiwinner elections. Namely we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Let
T\subseteq\mathcal{T}
, for each
T
-cohesive group of voters the proportionality degree of the
Phragmén
rule satisfies
d_{\textit{Phrag}}(T)\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(T\right)}{\max_{t\in T}\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(t\right)}-1\right)

I
, let
W
be the set of projects selected by the
Phragmén
rule. For the sake of contradiction, assume that there exists a
T
-cohesive group of voters

with

and average satisfaction
\text{avg}_{W}(V)<\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(T\right)}{\max_{t\in T}\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(t\right)}-1\right)

V

(i.e., the satisfaction of any unpicked voter in
A(T)
is at least as high as the satisfaction of a picked voter). Clearly, this has to be the
T
-cohesive group of voters with the smallest average satisfaction. Hence, the average satisfaction of this group is the proportionality degree of the sample
T
,
d_{f}(T)
.

5.

For each subset size
k
, we calculate the average of the proportionality degrees of all the sampled subsets of size
k
, which we denote by
d_{k}
.

6.

For each dataset, we iterate over all the possible subset sizes up to
\min(m,15)
and calculate the average proportionality degree of the dataset, i.e.,
\frac{\sum_{k}{d_{k}}}{\min(m,15)}
.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we studied the proportionality degree of fundamental rules for approval-based participatory budgeting, focusing on the Method of Equal Shares and Phragmén’s Sequential Rule, both from a theoretical and from an experimental perspective. The results in our work provide further evidence for the fairness guarantees of these rules, and additional support for their employment in practice over the greedy rule.

Interestingly, we managed to prove that both rules achieve the same proportionality degree theoretically, albeit with markedly different proof techniques. This leads us to conjecture that in fact both of these rules are optimal for the problem, in the sense that there is no other rule that achieves a better proportionality degree. To this end, below we state a theorem (with a proof in
Appendix

A
) which essentially upper bounds the proportionality degree of any rule by
\frac{\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(T\right)}{\max_{t\in T}\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(t\right)}-1
; proving an exactly tight bound (therefore establishing the optimality of the two rules) seems like an interesting technical challenge.

Theorem 6
.

There exists a PB instance
I
, a set of projects
T\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and a
T
-cohesive group
V\subseteq\mathcal{V}(T)
such that the average satisfaction of the voters in
T
from the outcome of any PB rule is at most
\frac{\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(T\right)}{\max_{t\in T}\operatorname{cost\hskip 1.42262pt\!}\left(t\right)}-1
.
