 SECTION 1.0
 INTRODUCTION

 Projections of the number of high level waste (HLW) canisters to be produced in the
Hanford Tank Waste Treatment and Immobilization Plant (WTP) (e.g., [1]) are based upon the
inventory of the tank wastes, the anticipated performance of the sludge treatment processes, and
current understanding of the capability of the borosilicate glass waste form. The WTP HLW
melter design, unlike earlier Department of Energy (DOE) melter designs, incorporates a glass
bubbler system. The bubblers create active glass pool mixing and thereby improve heat and mass
transfer and glass melting rate. The WTP HLW melters each have a glass surface area of 3.75 m2
and depth of ~1.1 m. The two melters in the HLW facility together are designed to produce up to
7.5 MT of glass per day at 100% availability. Further increases in HLW waste processing rates
can potentially be achieved by optimization of the feed and glass formulations, increasing the
melter operating temperature above 1150C, and by increasing the waste loading in the glass
product. Increasing the waste loading also has the added benefit of decreasing the number of
canisters for storage.

 The baseline WTP glass formulation efforts have been conservative in terms of
achievable waste loadings. These baseline formulations have been specified to ensure that the
glasses are homogenous, contain essentially no crystalline phases, are processable in joule­
heated, ceramic­lined melters, and meet WTP contract requirements. The overall WTP mission
will require the immobilization of tank waste compositions that are dominated by mixtures that
include aluminum, chromium, bismuth, iron, phosphorous, zirconium, and sulfur compounds as
waste loading limiting components. In order to improve waste loadings, DOE’s Office of River
Protection (ORP) initiated a testing program at the Vitreous State Laboratory (VSL) of the
Catholic University of America (CUA) to develop and characterize HLW glasses for wastes that
are limited by Al, Al plus Na, Bi, and Cr [1, 2]. Results of that work have demonstrated the
feasibility of increases in waste loading from about 25 wt% to 33­50 wt% (based on oxide
loading) in the glass, depending on the waste stream. It is expected that these higher waste
loading glasses will reduce the HLW canister production requirement by about 25% or more [3].
Furthermore, it has been shown that a key technological risk area relates to the strong
dependence of glass production rate on waste composition [3]. The extent of this variation across
the full spectrum of HLW waste types needs to be quantified in order to accurately project waste
treatment rates.

 Under a separate contract with Bechtel National Incorporated (BNI) to support the WTP,
VSL has developed and tested glass formulations for WTP HLW compositions to provide data to
meet the WTP contract requirements and to support system design activities [4­9]. That work
was based upon small­scale batch melts (“crucible melts”) using waste simulants. Selected
formulations were also tested in continuously fed, joule­heated melters (DM100 and DM1200)
[10­15]. That testing was directed towards waste streams from the then­planned early feed tanks
for the WTP (i.e., AZ­101, AZ­102, C­106/AY­102, and C­104/AY­101). These wastes are high
in iron (AZ­101, AZ­102 and C­106/AY­102) or thorium (C­104/AY­101) and are significantly

 10

different from those used in subsequent enhancement tests performed for ORP (i.e., wastes
limited by Al, Al/Na, Bi, and Cr). Baseline glass formulations to treat these high­Fe wastes were
developed under the BNI contract. During that time, the throughput requirement for the HLW
melter was initially 400 kg/(m2·day), which was subsequently increased to 800 kg/(m2·day). As a
result, the baseline high­Fe HLW glass formulations for WTP perform only slightly better than
the 800 kg/(m2·day) processing rate requirement. Furthermore, the baseline waste loadings for
the Fe­limited HLW compositions are only slightly higher than the BNI contract minimum. In
subsequent work performed for ORP on other HLW compositions, VSL has developed small­
scale test methods to assess processing rates of melter feeds and included them as an integral part
of glass formulation development. This methodology was used successfully to develop glass
formulations for high­Al Hanford HLW that showed processing rates in excess of
2000 kg/(m2·day) while achieving high waste loadings. The same methodology can be applied to
the development of improved glass formulations for other Hanford HLW streams in order to
provide ORP with a significantly more robust operating envelope with reduced risk of
throughput shortfalls. Consequently, a wide range of HLW Hanford waste streams rich in
constituents such as iron [16], bismuth [17], sulfur [18, 19], chromium [20], strontium [21],
zirconium [22], and manganese [23], which have the potential to limit waste loadings and
production rates, are being addressed in a series of glass formulation development and melter
testing tasks.

 This report describes results from work performed to develop and test new glass and feed
formulations in order to increase waste loadings for Hanford HLW containing aluminum, iron,
and sodium phosphates as well as to determine the effect of the form of phosphate in those HLW
streams on melter feed processing rates. A number of HLW batches to be processed at the WTP
have P2O5 concentrations that extend up to about thirteen weight percent on an oxide basis [24].
These high­phosphorus HLW streams frequently contain other components such as bismuth,
iron, calcium, sulfur, and various rare earth elements, which when combined with phosphorus,
can cause secondary phase formation or amorphous phase separation that can limit waste loading
or lead to poor cold cap behavior and slow processing rates. Previous studies for ORP have
explored some of the HLW streams that contain relatively high concentrations of bismuth [17] or
sulfur, both of which also contained significant amounts of phosphorus [18]; however, these
studies did not focus on HLW streams for which glass waste loading is limited primarily by
phosphorus. Consequently, tests were also conducted to develop high waste loading glass
formulations with good processing characteristics to treat a high phosphorus Hanford HLW
stream [25]. The present work addresses other high phosphorus HLW streams and particularly
the effects of various forms of phosphate (e.g., aluminum, iron, and sodium) on waste loading,
secondary phase formation, and processing characteristics. A high­phosphorus HLW
composition derived from the inventory data on insoluble components from the Best Basis
Inventory (BBI) Hanford Tank Waste Operations Simulator (HTWOS) model run (April 17,
2011) [26] was used as the basis for the present glass formulation development and melter test
work.

 Testing was conducted to identify glass and melter feed formulations that optimize waste
loadings and waste processing rates while meeting all processing and product quality
requirements for Hanford HLW containing aluminum, iron, and sodium phosphates. Phosphates
are present in Hanford tank waste in a variety of forms including iron, sodium, aluminum, and

 11

bismuth phosphates [27­29]. These differences in phosphate form have the potential to affect
waste and melter feed rheology, feed processing rates, and the extent of crystalline phases in the
glass product, as has been observed with different forms of aluminum and iron in HLW waste
simulants [30­32]. These issues were addressed by developing glass formulations for a Hanford
tank waste containing aluminum, iron, and sodium phosphates followed by melter testing with a
selected glass formulation using feed containing different phosphates likely to be present in
Hanford tank waste. A similar strategy was employed successfully for determining the
differences in the feed and glass properties and feed processing characteristics of HLW feeds
with different forms of aluminum, iron, and manganese [23, 30­32]. Testing was designed to
identify glass and melter feed formulations that optimize waste loadings and waste processing
rates while meeting all processing and product quality requirements. The work included
preparation and characterization of crucible melts to evaluate the effects of waste loading on
glass properties and the maximum waste loadings that can be achieved. The testing included
assessment of melt rate using a Vertical Gradient Furnace (VGF) system. Development of a
suitable glass formulation was informed by earlier work with high phosphate HLW compositions
[25]. The results from crucible­scale testing supported subsequent melter tests on a DM100
vitrification system designed to examine the effects of enhanced glass and feed formulations on
waste processing rate and product quality. DM100 melter tests were planned with feeds
containing three different phosphates under nominal conditions (1150°C glass temperature, 9 lpm
bubbling) so that the effect of the form of phosphate on feed processing rate and processing
characteristics could be determined. Samples of the glass product were collected and analyzed
for composition and crystalline phases. Data on processing rates and any processing difficulties,
off­gas composition, and system operating parameters were collected. This work builds on
previous work performed at the VSL for ORP to increase waste loadings and processing rates of
HLW glass formulations [3, 16, 20­23, 25, 30­38].

1.1 Test Objectives

 The primary objective of the work described herein was to develop and identify HLW
glass compositions and glass forming additive blends that achieve high waste loadings and
processing rates for high phosphorus HLW streams while maintaining acceptable glass
properties. Another objective was to determine the effect of phosphate form (aluminum, iron,
sodium) on feed processing properties, glass production rates, and product quality while
vitrifying a high phosphorus HLW stream. This was accomplished through a combination of
crucible­scale tests, vertical gradient furnace tests, and confirmation tests on a DM100 melter
system.

1.2 Quality Assurance

 This work was conducted under a quality assurance program that is based on Nuclear
Quality Assurance (NQA)­1 2008 that is in place at the VSL. The program is compliant with
applicable criteria of 10 CFR 830.120; Office of Civilian Waste Management DOE/RW­0333P,
Quality Assurance Requirements and Description (QARD) Revision 20; the American Society of
Mechanical Engineers (ASME) NQA­1, 2008 including NQA­1a­2009 addenda; and DOE Order

1.3 DM100 Melter System

        1.3.1 DM100 Feed System

 A schematic diagram of the DM100 vitrification system is shown in Figure 1.1. The
melter feed is introduced in batches into a feed container that is mounted on a load cell for
weight monitoring. The feed is stirred with a variable speed mixer and constantly recirculated
except for periodic, momentary interruptions during which the weight is recorded. A peristaltic
pump is used to provide a uniform delivery of feed to the melt surface. Feed is directed from the
recirculation loop that extends to the top of the melter and then diverted to the peristaltic pump,
which regulates the flow of feed through a Teflon­lined feed line and water­cooled feed tube into
the melter.

 1.3.2 Melter System

 Cross­sectional diagrams of the DM100­BL melter are shown in Figures 1.2.a­c. The
DM100­BL unit is a ceramic refractory­lined melter fitted with five electrodes: two pairs of
opposing Inconel 690 plate electrodes and a bottom electrode. Power can be supplied in either
three­phase or single­phase configurations. All of the tests in the present work were performed
with the upper and lower electrodes on each side connected together and powered by a
single­phase supply; the bottom electrode was not powered. Melt pool agitation is achieved by
either a removable lance entering from the top of the melter or a permanent bubbler installed
through the bottom electrode. In these tests, the lance bubbler was used. The glass product is
removed from the melter by means of an airlift discharge system. The melter has a melt surface
area of 0.108 m 2 and a variable glass inventory of between 110 kg, when only the bottom pair of
 P P

electrodes is used, and about 170 kg when both pairs of electrodes are used, which was the case
in the present tests.

 1.3.3 Off-Gas System

 For operational simplicity, the DM100­BL is equipped with a dry off­gas treatment
system involving gas filtration operations only. Exhaust gases leave the melter plenum through a
film cooler device that minimizes the formation of solid deposits. The film­cooler air has
constant flow rate and its temperature is thermostatically controlled. Consequently, under
steady­state operating conditions, the exhaust gases passing through the transition line (between
the melter and the first filtration device) can be sampled at constant temperature and airflow rate.
The geometry of the transition line conforms to the requirements of the 40­CFR­60 air sampling
techniques. Immediately downstream of the transition line are cyclonic filters followed by

 13

conventional pre­filters and high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters. The temperature of the
cyclonic filters is maintained above 150°C while the temperatures in the HEPAs are kept
sufficiently high to prevent moisture condensation. The entire train of gas filtration operations is
duplicated and each train is used alternately. An induced draft fan completes the system.

1.4 Experimental Methods

 1.4.1 Feed Conversion by Vertical Gradient Furnace (VGF) Testing

 Figure 1.3 shows a schematic diagram of the VGF setup. The temperature gradient inside
the VGF is maintained by two separate sets of heating elements, both of which are arranged in
cylindrical form and aligned along their axes. The inner heater is set at 1150oC, which is the
nominal temperature of the glass pool, and the ambient heater is set at 600oC, which is similar to
the melter plenum temperature. A ceramic crucible (4 inches tall) is used to contain the reacting
melter feed. The temperature gradient in the test crucible is shown in Figure 1.4. For a typical
feed conversion test, 10 grams of glass of identical chemical composition to the test feed
(expressed on an oxide basis) is preheated in the ceramic crucible positioned in the inner heater
before the dried melter feed (to yield 17 grams of glass) is introduced. Feed reactions under the
controlled temperature gradient are allowed to continue for the designated test duration
(typically, from 30 to 90 minutes) and then stopped by rapid cooling in room temperature air.
The top surface of the reacted feed material is then inspected and photographed. The crucibles
with their feed contents are then cross­sectioned to reveal the conversion progress of feed blends.
The saw cuts of the crucibles are performed dry (without lubricant) to avoid loss of any soluble
material.

 To characterize the reacted feed material, visual inspection and digital imaging of the top
(by photography) and cross section (using an optical scanner) of the reacted sample are
performed. The VGF results are used to evaluate the melt rate on a relative scale using the degree
of melting that has occurred, the structure of the feed materials that are undergoing reaction and
transformation, and the feed conversion progression with time. A VGF sample showing
relatively more feed conversion and melting is classified as fast whereas a sample showing
relatively less feed conversion and melting is classified as slow. In cases where multiple VGF
samples are tested, a numerical ranking of relative melt rate is assigned based on a correlation
using feeds that were tested on the VGF and whose melt rates had been determined previously in
DM100 melter tests [30]. The numerical rankings of feed samples based on the extent of feed
conversion from observation of the top and cross section of VGF samples is given in Table 1.1.
The rankings span the range of 1 to 6, with a feed sample ranked 1 expected to exhibit a fast
processing rate and a feed sample ranked 6 expected to show a slow processing rate.

 1.4.2 Feed Composition and General Properties

 Feed samples were taken directly from as­received drums and the melter feed
recirculation line during each test. Feed samples were poured into a platinum/gold crucible and
placed into a programmed furnace for drying and fusion to form a glass. The glass produced

 14

from this fusion was ground to less than 200 mesh and sealed in 20­ml vials for subsequent
analysis by X­ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF), or by acid digestion followed by
direct­current plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (DCP­AES) on the resulting solution. The
feed samples were also characterized for their density, pH, water content, and glass yield.

 1.4.3 Crucible and Melter Product Glasses

 The glass product is discharged from the melter into 5­gallon steel pails periodically
using an air­lift system. The discharged product glass is sampled at the end of each test by
removing sufficient glass from the top of the pails for compositional analysis and secondary
phase determination. In addition, sufficient glass from the end of tests with each form of
phosphate was collected to perform the Product Consistency Test (PCT, 7 days at 90oC) and
Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP). Prior to those tests, characterization with
respect to PCT, TCLP, melt electrical conductivity, melt viscosity, and secondary phases was
performed on the crucible melt formulations to identify the selection for melter testing and to
ensure compliance with the present WTP contract requirements. All of these procedures are
routinely conducted at VSL and, therefore, standard operating procedures are in place [40].
Sample preparation for chemical analysis begins with size reduction and sieving. All samples are
then subjected to XRF analysis to determine the concentrations of all elements except boron and
lithium. A series of National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) reference materials
are used for confirmation of the XRF data. Boron and lithium are determined by total acid
dissolution of ground glass samples in HF/HNO3 and subjecting the resulting solutions to
DCP­AES analysis. Glass samples from melter tests with different phosphate forms were also
analyzed for iron redox state by Mössbauer spectroscopy using glass prepared for XRF analysis.

                 1.4.3.1 Viscosity

 The melt viscosity, η, is measured using a Brookfield viscometer. Measurements are
performed in the temperature range of 950­1250ºC and the data are interpolated to standard
temperatures using the Vogel­Fulcher equation: ln η = [A/(T­To)]+B, where A, B, and To are
fitting parameters. The equipment is calibrated at room temperature using standard oils of known
viscosity and then checked at 950­1250ºC using a NIST standard reference glass (SRM 711).
Both precision and accuracy of the viscosity measurements are estimated to be within ±15
relative%.

 1.4.3.2 Electrical Conductivity

 The electrical conductivity, σ, of each glass is determined by measuring the resistance of
the glass melt as a function of frequency using a calibrated platinum/rhodium electrode probe
attached to a Agilent model 4194A impedance analyzer. Measurements are performed over
similar temperature ranges to those employed for the melt viscosity measurements. The results
are analyzed to obtain the DC electrical conductivity. The electrical conductivity data are then
interpolated to standard temperatures using the Vogel­Fulcher equation: ln  = [A/(TTo)] + B,

where A, B and To are fitting parameters. Estimated uncertainties in the electrical conductivity
measurements are  20 relative%.

 1.4.3.3 Product Consistency Test (PCT)

 The product consistency test (PCT; ASTM C 1285) [41] is used to evaluate the relative
chemical durability of glasses by measuring the concentrations of the chemical species released
from 100­200 mesh crushed glass (75­149 μm) to the test solution (de­ionized water in this case).
PCTs on the HLW glasses are performed at 90ºC, in accordance with the current WTP contract
requirement. The ratio of the glass surface area to the solution volume for this test is about 2000
m­1 (typically, 4 g of 100­200 mesh glass is immersed in 40 ml of deionized water). All tests are
conducted in triplicate, in 304L stainless steel vessels, and in parallel with a standard glass
included in each test set. The internal standard is the Argonne National Laboratory – Low
Activity Reference Material (ANL­LRM) reference glass [42] and/or the Defense Waste
Processing Facility – Environmental Assessment (DWPF­EA) glass [43], both of which have
undergone round­robin testing. The leachates are sampled at predetermined times, the first of
which is seven days. One milliliter of sampled leachate is mixed with 20 ml of 1M HNO3 and the
resulting solution is analyzed by DCP­AES; another 3 ml of sampled leachate is used for pH
measurement.

 1.4.3.4 Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP)

 The TCLP was performed at VSL using SW­846 Method 1311, which employs leaching
of crushed glass (< 3/8”) in a sodium acetate buffer solution for 18 hours at 22C with constant
end­over­end agitation. A mass of about 100 grams of glass is leached in 2 liters of TCLP
extract, according to the extraction method for non­volatiles. The surface area to volume ratio for
this test is about 20 m­1, which is about two orders of magnitude lower than that in the PCT. The
leachates are analyzed by DCP­AES according to VSL standard operating procedures.

 1.4.3.5 Secondary Phases

 Secondary phases in the glass samples were determined by optical microscopy and
scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive X­ray spectroscopy (SEM­EDS) in
combination with X­ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. Secondary phases due to crystallization and
phase separation can be identified using these methods. The samples for SEM analysis are
processed by grinding and sieving (­18 mesh, < 1 mm) in order to provide a representative
measure of the overall crystal fraction in the sample. Ground glass samples are mounted in
epoxy, polished to expose cross sections of the glass grains, coated with carbon, and examined
using SEM/EDS. The amount of crystals in glass samples was determined by SEM in
conjunction with image analysis.

 1.4.3.6 Crystalline Phases by X-Ray Diffraction

 Quantitative XRD was used to determine the crystal content in glasses and on solids from
the cyclone filter in the off­gas system. Powder diffraction data were gathered using a Thermo­

point. JADE9 Software was used to perform the search­match identification of the crystalline
phases as well as the whole pattern fitting to determine the weight percent of each crystalline
phase in the sample.

 1.4.3.7 Mössbauer Spectroscopy

 Mössbauer spectroscopy is an analytical technique that utilizes the recoil­free emission
and resonant absorption of gamma rays by nuclei bound in solids. The energies of the gamma ray
emitter (source) and absorber (sample) have to be closely matched and, therefore, the number of
elements that can be studied using this technique is limited. One of the elements that has been
studied extensively using Mössbauer spectroscopy is iron. In this work, Mössbauer spectroscopy
was used to measure the fraction of iron in the Fe2+ and Fe3+ states, which can be used as a
measure of the redox state of the glass sample. Mössbauer spectroscopy was also used to analyze
the various iron minerals in the feed materials and deposits in the off­gas system.

 Mössbauer spectra were collected using an American Magnetic/Ranger Scientific
MS­1200 system equipped with a 57Co source in a rhodium matrix with glass powder as the
sample. Both divalent and trivalent iron show doublet peaks in the Mössbauer spectrum and the
peak areas are proportional to the concentrations of the respective species in the glass. Even
though the peaks overlap partially, software can be used to deconvolute the peaks and calculate
the peak areas. The ratio of the areas of the Fe2+ and Fe3+ peaks is equal to the ratio of their
concentrations in the glass. The redox measurements are calibrated using a set of six standard
glass samples with Fe2+ contents ranging from 7 to 90% of the total iron (Fetotal). The standards
include a NIST traceable Obsidian Rock (SRM 278), five standard glasses analyzed by Corning,
Inc. for their redox state, and the DWPF­EA glass. The site populations provided by the fitting is
mol% Fe, assuming that the recoil­free fraction is the same for each site.

 The NIST standard iron foil (SRM 1541) is used to calibrate the instrument and
determine the zero velocity channel. Since Doppler shifts in energy are measured in Mössbauer
spectroscopy, the velocity is a measure of the shift in energy; knowing the zero velocity channel
therefore helps in identifying the Fe2+ and Fe3+ peaks.

                 1.4.3.8 K-3 Corrosion

 The Monofrax™ K­3 test coupons used in corrosion tests are cut from K­3 bricks. Since
the material that forms fused­cast K­3 varies from the surface of the brick to its interior (e.g., the

 17

interior material tends to contain larger and more numerous pores), the test coupons are cut from
material within one inch of the brick surface. All sides of the K­3 coupons are ground parallel
with a precision of better than 1 mil (0.001”). A typical K­3 coupon measures 0.395” × 0.595” in
cross­section and is long enough (≈ 3”) to be immersed in the molten glass to a depth of one
inch.

 The K­3 refractory corrosion tests are conducted using a modified ASTM refractory
corrosion procedure (ASTM C621) [44], building on experience gained from extensive
refractory corrosion tests at the VSL. For each test, the K­3 test coupon is first cemented to a
crucible cover made of Zirmul™ and baked. Before starting the corrosion test, the baked
coupons are preheated and then positioned in a 200 ml platinum crucible containing 170 grams
of pre­melted glass. A platinum sheet covers the glass to ensure that it cannot be contaminated
by Zirmul™. The platinum crucible containing the K­3 coupons and molten glass is set inside a
quartz crucible holder, which is then placed in a box furnace preheated to about 800°C. After the
furnace reaches the designated test temperature (nominally 1208°C), a platinum bubbling tube is
introduced into the molten glass from above through a slot in the Zirmul/platinum cover. Dry air
is bubbled through the molten glass at a constant rate of 8 cc/min controlled by a precision flow
meter. The bubbling rate, i.e., the number of gas bubbles generated inside the melt per minute, is
monitored using a pressure transducer interfaced to a computer via an analog to digital converter.
The temperature of the furnace is monitored using an S­type thermocouple positioned above the
crucible inside the furnace and checked before each test against a calibrated S­type
thermocouple. The standard glass­contact corrosion test is run for six days at 1208°C with
continuous air bubbling. All K­3 corrosion tests are performed at the same refractory surface
area (S) / melt volume (V) ratio of about 0.20 cm–1, which is 74% less than the S/V ratio
specified by the ASTM C­621 for static glass contact corrosion tests. Fresh K­3 test coupons are
used for each corrosion test. At the end of each test, the K­3 coupon is removed from the melt
and cooled to room temperature in a clean quartz crucible. The coupon is then sectioned
lengthwise to facilitate measurement of dimensional changes. Per ASTM C­621, the dimension
losses at the “neck” (the glass­air interface) and the “half­down” (half of the immersed length of
the coupon below the neck) locations are reported. The altered zone observed on the surface of
the test coupon is measured for its depth perpendicular to the surface based on visual differences
in texture and color under an optical microscope.

2.3 Development of Glass Formulations

 Earlier glass formulation testing for HLW waste Batch 4089, which has a P2O5 content of
13.70 wt% compared to the present value of 11.22 wt% in waste Batch 2636, resulted in the
selection of HLW­HP­16 as the target glass composition for melter testing [25]. This glass has a
waste loading of 51 wt% and a P2O5 content of 6.99 wt%, which is substantially higher than the
minimum component loading requirement of 3.0 wt%. The earlier testing results showed that
crystallization of secondary phases and sulfate solubility are the primary waste loading limiting
factors in developing HLW glasses for Batch 4089. By comparison, glass formulations for Batch
2636 were unlikely to be limited by sulfate solubility since the SO3 concentration in the waste is
only 0.75 wt%, much lower than that found in Batch 4089 (1.78 wt%), as discussed in Section
2.2. As is the case with most HLW glass formulations, secondary phase formation was expected
to be important in determining the achievable waste loading, with the major secondary phases
expected to be calcium/sodium phosphate and spinel. In addition, the high concentrations of
Na2O and Al2O3 in the present waste (at 37.36 wt% and 22.61 wt%, respectively) suggested that
nepheline formation and K­3 refractory corrosion would also play important roles in limiting the
waste loading.

2.4 Analysis of Feed Samples

 2.4.1 General Properties

 Sample names and measured properties of all melter feed samples are given in Table

chemical composition. Another feed sample was generated from as­received feed combined with
the chemicals necessary to produce the HLW­HPA­07 glass with Na3PO4 to determine the
amount of water to be added to achieve the target solids content of 500 g glass per liter feed. The
measured solids content of this sample was about twenty five percent above the target and
therefore corresponding amounts of water were added to each drum of feed used in the melter
tests. A melter feed sample with aluminum phosphate was made at the bench scale for VGF
testing even though no melter testing was conducted with that feed. Melter feed samples were
taken during each melter test from either an in­line sampling port or directly from the feed tube
at the end of a test. Density, pH, water content, glass conversion ratio, and oxide composition by
XRF and DCP­AES were measured on the samples. The measured glass conversion ratios for all
feed samples from the melter tests were within five and a half percent of the target on a weight­
per­weight basis, validating the use of the target conversion ratio for calculating glass production
rates. The water content, density, glass yield, and pH varied within very narrow ranges for the
melter feed test samples; no significant difference in these measured properties was discernable
for feeds with sodium and iron phosphates. The measured properties of feeds with aluminum
phosphate and sodium phosphates but without the added water trended as expected: higher
density (1.61 vs. 1.41 g/ml), higher glass yield (760 vs. 500 g/l), and lower water content (45 vs
58­60%). Feed without phosphate had a lower glass yield due to the absence of phosphorus and a
one unit higher pH compared to feeds with similar water content.

 2.4.2 Chemical Composition

 The methods used for analysis of the chemical compositions of feed sample are described
in Section 1.4.2. The boron and lithium oxide concentrations from the DCP­AES analysis were
used for normalizing the XRF data since their concentrations were not determined by XRF. The
analyzed compositions of the as­received feed are compared to the target composition in Table

the target compositions in Tables 2.17 – 2.20. The results for the as­received feed samples
without phosphate, calcium, iron, and a portion of the aluminum and sodium show good
agreement with the target composition for all the constituents; all oxides with concentrations
greater than one weight percent show deviations from target of less than eight percent, including
boron, which was only two and a quarter percent above target concentration. Also of note in the
analysis of the as­received feed is that no phosphorus was detected, as intended, and only trace
amounts of calcium and iron were detected in the as­received feed samples. The average analysis
of the feed from the various melter and VGF tests approximated the target composition with all
of the constituents, with target concentrations greater than one percent deviating by less than ten
percent except for iron surpluses of thirteen and fifteen percent in tests with aluminum phosphate
and without phosphate. Phosphorus deficits of between three and seven percent were measured

in feeds with sodium and aluminum phosphate and a surplus of near ten percent was measured

 2.4.3 Feed Rheology

 Samples of feeds from melter tests with different phosphates were subjected to
rheological characterization. The results from rheological characterization of a variety of other
melter feeds and waste simulants, as well as the effects of a range of test variables, are described
in detail in a separate report [49]. Melter feeds were characterized using a Haake RS75
rheometer equipped with either a Z40DIN or a FL22­SZ40 sensor to determine the yield stress
and the shear stress versus shear rate relationship. A typical set of measurements consists of
identifying the flow characteristics of the slurry by measuring the shear stress on the slurry at
controlled shear rates and temperatures. In these measurements, the shear rate values are preset
and are increased stepwise with a sufficient delay (typically 15 to 30 seconds) between steps to
ensure that the shear stress is allowed to fully relax and therefore is measured at equilibrium. The
apparent viscosity of the sample as a function of the shear rate can then be calculated as the ratio
of the shear stress to the shear rate. All of the measurements in this work were made at 25°C;
previous work [49], which examined a range of temperatures, showed a relatively weak effect of
temperature. Measured values for viscosity at selected shear rates and the yield stress are shown
in Table 2.22. All the feed samples from the present tests are considerably less viscous than
samples from previous tests with feeds with different sources of iron and aluminum at the same

 25

feed solids content [3, 16, 30, 31]. The very low measured viscosities of the feed samples from
the present tests obscure the comparison between feeds with different or no phosphates.

 2.4.4 Vertical Gradient Furnace

 The vertical gradient furnace has been used as a useful screening tool for ranking HLW
slurry feeds for their processing rate in waste glass melters [16, 21­23, 30­32]. Melter feed
samples with each of the three different phosphates, all producing the same HLW­­HPA­07 glass
composition, as well as the analogous feed without phosphate, were tested under the standard
VGF conditions in an attempt to evaluate the potential impact of the form of phosphate on melt
rate. Aliquots from one­liter sized slurry feed samples were dried and subjected to the test
protocol described in Section 1.4.1. The chemical compositions of the sampled feed are given in
Tables 2.17 ­ 2.20. In a standard VGF experiment, feed samples are examined for the extent of
feed reactions, melting, appearance of the feed sample such as the presence of foam, formation
of a dome­like structure, etc., that are indicative of the rate of feed conversion to glass. The
conversion of each type of feed material was evaluated primarily based on images of the tops and
cross sections of the reacted feed samples after 30, 60, and 90 minute VGF tests depending on
the observed extent of completion of the conversion reactions. The top and cross sectional
images of VGF samples are shown in Figures 2.2 and 2.3. A summary of the visual observations
and relative rankings of the feeds are compared to the previously tested HLW­NG­Fe2
composition with various forms of iron [16, 32], the HLW­HMn­20 composition with various
forms of manganese [23], and the HLW­E­Al­27 and HLW­Al­16 compositions with various
forms of aluminum [3, 30] in Table 2.23.

 The results of these VGF tests clearly indicate that the presence of phosphate and the type
of phosphate in the feed have a profound effect on the conversion of feed to glass reaction rate.
The feeds with either sodium or aluminum phosphates were slow to react, with a thick foamy
layer on the wall of the reaction vessel after 30­ and 60­minute VGF tests, resulting in a ranking
of 3. Typically, formation of such a thick layer full of gas vesicles indicates episodes of
degassing, foaming, and subsequent collapsing of an over­arching dome structure resulting in the
overall conversion rate from melter feed to glass being slower. Feed with iron phosphate was
much slower to react and exhibited a fully developed dome, resulting in a ranking of 6, which is
the slowest conversion rate given for the VGF testing procedure. The presence of calcium
phosphate in the form of apatite in the iron phosphate containing feed may also have exacerbated
the slow melting rate of that feed. Feed without phosphate was the most rapidly reacting feed,
suggesting that the phosphate itself reduces the conversion rate of feed to glass.

 As compared to other feeds previously subjected to VGF testing, the only feed that
performed as poorly as the iron phosphate containing feed was a feed containing high
concentrations of Al2O3, which processed on the DM100 at only 550 kg/m2/day, well below the
WTP baseline. Also of note is that, in general, the phosphate­containing feeds from the present
tests were converted to glass at much slower rates than the high iron, high manganese, and
reformulated high aluminum formulations. Conversion rates of feed to glass for the feeds from
the present tests were also much slower than that observed for the previous high phosphate glass
composition [25] indicating that slow melting is not exclusively attributable to phosphate but

 26

rather to a combination of other feed components with phosphate. The most apparent difference
between the two feeds is that the previously processed high phosphorus waste contains much less
sodium and therefore lithium carbonate was used as an additive.

 SECTION 3.0
 DM100 MELTER OPERATIONS

 Melter tests were conducted on the DM100­BL vitrification system between 10/19/16
 and 11/4/16 with the high phosphorus, HTWOS Model Run Waste Batch 2636 HLW simulant
 and the HLW­HPA­07 glass composition. Feed for the tests was obtained in a single batch
 without phosphorus, iron, and calcium as well as deficits in aluminum and sodium, enabling the
 addition of either Na3PO4, FePO4, Ca5(PO4)3(OH), or AlPO4 to evaluate the performance of
 feeds with different phosphates. After testing feeds containing Na3PO4, FePO4, and
 Ca5(PO4)3(OH), it was determined that the composition was highly corrosive to Inconel
 components including the bubbler, thermowell, and electrodes and therefore testing with the
 HLW­HPA­07 glass composition was discontinued. The bubbler and thermowell were replaced,
 the glass inventory in the melter was exchanged, and the melter testing program moved on to a
 different study with a high manganese HLW composition [23]. In parallel, bench scale testing
 was performed that confirmed the corrosive nature of the HLW­HPA­07 glass composition to
 Inconel and further, that the same formulation without phosphorus was not overly corrosive
 towards Inconel. After consultation with ORP it was agreed that the remaining melter test
 would be conducted with the HLW­HPA­07 glass composition without phosphorus in order to
 assess the effects of phosphate on the vitrification process; that test was conducted between
 1/24/17 and 1/26/17.

 In this series of melter tests, over one metric ton of glass was produced from tests
 processing over three metric tons of feed with and without phosphate. In all tests, the glass
 temperature was held constant at 1150°C, the feed solids content was constant at 500 g glass per
 liter of feed, and the waste oxide and glass compositions were identical for all tests with
 phosphate. Melt pool bubbling was held constant at 9 lpm for tests with each of the phosphates

 configuration and operating conditions used for previous tests with HLW simulants [3, 11­17,

 80­95% melt surface coverage for the DM100 since a 100% cold cap tends to lead to "bridging"
 in smaller melters. Summaries of the tests are provided in Table 3.1. The series of four
 nominally 50­hour tests were divided as follows:

  Na3PO4, nominal bubbling
  FePO4 and Ca5(PO4)3(OH), nominal bubbling

necessitating lowering the feed rate over much of the test. Feed with Na3PO4 required manual

during testing to transfer feed to the feed tank and adjust/replace tubing around the peristaltic

3.1 Corrosion of Inconel Components

 Following the completion of tests processing the HLW­HPA­07 formulation with FePO4,
and Ca5(PO4)3(OH), melter idling was targeted at 1100°C while waiting to receive the AlPO4 to
complete the testing. After about two days of idling, the glass temperature measured 5” from the
bottom of the thermowell increased about 25°C, as shown in Figure 3.7. After three days of
idling, glass temperatures spiked 25­50°C and became more variable. The Inconel 690 bubbler
was removed and examined and exhibited a limited amount of corrosion around the orifice, as

 31

shown in Figure 3.8. Thinning along the length of the bubbler ranged from negligible to seven
percent. After four days of idling, the thermocouple 10” from the bottom of the thermowell
failed completely. The Inconel 690 thermowell was removed after six days revealing substantial
damage, as shown in Figures 3.9 and 3.10. The bottom thirteen inches were missing from the
thermowell and the bottom ten inches remaining were severely corroded. Corrosion was
observed almost all the way up to the top of the glass pool with thermocouples exposed along
most of the length of the thermowell. Subsequently, the Inconel 601 riser in the airlift was
removed and inspected; it was found that 12” of material was missing from the bottom and much
of the remaining material was very brittle. There is no remotely collected data such as electrode
temperature, power supplied to the electrodes, and melt pool resistance, that would indicate
corrosion of the Inconel 690 electrodes; direct inspection of the electrodes is not possible since
the electrodes are permanently installed in the melter below the level of the molten glass.

 A cross­section from the remaining thermowell and scale from the exterior of the
corroded thermowell were examined by SEM to determine the mechanism of this corrosion, as
shown in Figures 3.11 and 3.12. The samples were initially inspected for any evidence of
corrosion by bismuth since such corrosion has previously been observed on bubblers from the
DM1200 [50]. No bismuth metal or bismuth nodules in contact with the corroded Inconel was
observed, ruling out bismuth as the agent of the corrosion. Conversely, phosphorus in the form of
spherical nickel phosphide is present in the glass near the metal interface. Some granulated and
angulated nodules were observed in the molten metal with significant amounts of phosphorus in
these particles. Sulfur in the form of chromium sulfide was observed at the metal boundaries
indicating that the sulfate in the feed also plays a role in the corrosion of the Inconel. The
remaining Inconel and molten metal droplets are variable in composition but are significantly
enriched in Ni­Fe and depleted in Cr, the former with adhering chromium oxide. The
involvement of phosphorus and sulfur in the corrosion of the Inconel is curious since the
previous high phosphorus formulation processed, which targeted higher amounts of phosphorus
and sulfur as well as the same amounts of organic carbon and nitrates, was not associated with
this level of corrosion [25]. Thus, further work is necessary to understand these differences and
the compositional ranges over which this type of corrosion is to be expected.

lithium as an additive and significantly more sodium (13 vs. 21 wt% Na2O). Changes in glass
pool composition are observed as increases in the concentrations of iron, bismuth, and sodium at
the expense of aluminum, boron, silicon, potassium, and sulfur during the transition to the
HLW­HPA­07 composition. The majority of these compositional changes occur during the initial
segment of the test. After the third test segment, a set of tests processing a HLW high manganese
composition [23] followed by a turnover with the HLW­HPA­07 composition without phosphate
was completed. Since these glasses are not included in Figures 4.1.a ­ 4.1.f, a discontinuity is
observed between the third and fourth test. Also, the target concentrations are different due to the
removal of phosphorus and renormalization of the target composition. The most apparent
compositional difference between the third and fourth tests is the near­seven weight percent P2O5
in the third test and the lack of phosphorus in the fourth. Minor changes in composition
occurring over the fourth test as the turnover is completed are increases in sodium at the expense
of silicon, manganese, potassium, zirconium, and lithium. Both chromium and nickel
concentrations in discharged glasses were above target values and the measured feed sample
concentrations due to corrosion of Inconel components and melter bricks. Nickel concentrations
decrease over the course of all tests except while processing iron phosphate, whereas chromium
concentrations more than double over the initial test then remain relatively constant. It appears
that fluorine and sulfur (excluding the test with iron phosphate) reach only about forty and eighty
percent of their respective target concentrations due to volatilization from the glass pool and cold
cap.

4.2 Chemical Durability of Discharge Glasses

 Samples of glass discharged from the end of the DM100 tests produced while processing
feeds with each of the phosphates were evaluated for chemical durability using the PCT and
TCLP methods. The PCT results are compared to those for the benchmark DWPF­EA and the
comparable crucible glasses in Table 4.7; the TCLP results are compared to the WTP delisting
limits [51, 52] and Universal Treatment Standard (UTS) limits in Table 4.8. Additionally,
concentrations of bismuth and manganese were measured in the TCLP leachates as a point of
comparison between the glasses since there are no regulatory limits for bismuth and manganese
by the TCLP procedure. The chemical durability determined for the melter and crucible
HLW­HPA­07 composition glasses by both of these methods is excellent. When phosphate is
removed from the formulation, the product is not as chemically durable. All measured
normalized PCT releases for the HLW­HPA­07 discharge glass samples with phosphorus are
greater than an order of magnitude lower than the corresponding values for the DWPF­EA glass.
The melter glass without phosphorus has PCT releases at least five times lower than the
corresponding values for the DWPF­EA glass. The lower PCT leachate concentrations for the

 34

glasses with phosphorus may be due to buffering of the leachate pH by phosphate, which would
reduce the extent of matrix dissolution; the leachate pH for the phosphorus­free glass is almost
one unit higher than those for the phosphorus­containing glasses (Table 4.7).

 All regulated TCLP leachate concentrations are well below the WTP delisting and UTS
limits for melter glasses targeting the HLW­HPA­07 composition. TCLP leachate concentrations
from the melter glass without phosphorus were all below the delisting limits; however, the lead
and chromium concentrations were above UTS limits. Measured sodium PCT releases for the
melter glasses were lower than those for the crucible glasses due perhaps to lower than target
concentrations of sodium in the melter glasses. No obvious trends in PCT release and TCLP
leachate concentrations are observed in the melter glasses generated from feeds containing the
sodium, iron, and calcium phosphates. The TCLP leachate concentrations are from four to more
than ten times higher for the melter glasses without phosphorus. Except for barium, the TCLP
leachate concentrations are about three times higher for HLW­HPA­07 melter glasses than the
corresponding crucible glass. The chemical durability of the HLW­HPA­07 glasses is generally
within the range measured on glasses produced from wastes limited by bismuth, chromium,
aluminum, and aluminum plus sodium [3], chromium and iron [20, 16], phosphorus [25],
strontium [21], manganese [23], and zirconium [22]. These results confirm that glasses can be
formulated with higher phosphate contents and meet glass product quality requirements. They
also demonstrate that phosphorus contributes to produce a more chemically durable glass as
measured by the PCT and TCLP methods.

4.3. SEM, XRD, and Mössbauer Analysis of Melter Glass Samples

 Discharge glass samples from the end of tests processing feeds with different phosphates
or no phosphates were subjected to XRD, SEM, and Mössbauer analysis to determine the
secondary mineral content and iron oxidation state. The results are summarized in Table 4.9 and
detailed descriptions of observed mineral phases are given in Table 4.10. SEM images and EDS
analysis of secondary phases in the discharge glasses are given in Figures 4.2 to 4.5. Similar
crystalline phases were detected in samples analyzed by both XRD and SEM although the SEM
analysis was complicated by the solubility of secondary phases that are lost during sample
preparation and the small crystal size of the secondary phases. In previous tests with HLW
streams rich in phosphates [25] detection of alkali phosphates in glasses by SEM was similarly
challenged when large quantities of phosphate minerals were detected by XRD. Glasses
produced from HLW feeds containing phosphates had five to eighteen percent of either sodium
or calcium phosphate while glasses produced from feed without phosphates produced very little
secondary phases. The combined phosphate mineral content measured by XRD accounts for the
majority of phosphorus present in the melter feed. The canister cooled crucible melt for the
HLW­HPA­07 glass was reported to contain nearly ten percent voids, which are assumed to have
contained sodium phosphate before sample preparation (see Table 2.7) resulting in similar
amounts of crystalline phases detected in the discharged glasses. This suggests that most of the
phosphate and possibly sulfate phases detected in discharge samples probably formed during
cooling in the discharge can. The phosphate crystals, if present as such, are not expected to affect
glass processing properties due to their small size. Feed containing sodium phosphate was more
likely to produce a glass containing apatite than feed containing that mineral. The sodium sulfate

 35

detected in the glass produced from the feed containing iron phosphate originated as a
contaminant in the iron phosphate reagent (see Table 2.21). No traces of iron phosphate were
detected in any of the glass samples. The amount of spinels detected in samples over the course
of the tests increased from 0.01 to 0.11 volume percent with the highest amount being associated
with a glass that did not contain any phosphorus. The amount of divalent iron ranged from none
detectable to 4.1% of total iron with no clear distinctions being made between the phosphates
used during testing.

 SECTION 5.0
 MONITORED OFF-GAS EMISSIONS

5.1 Particulate Sampling

 The melter exhaust was sampled for metals/particles according to 40­CFR­60 Methods 3,
5, and 29 at steady­state operating conditions during each test segment. The concentrations of
off­gas species present as particulates and gaseous species were derived from laboratory data on
solutions extracted from air samples (impinger solutions, filters, and rinse solutions) together
with measurements of the volume of air sampled. Particulate collection required isokinetic
sampling, which entails removing gas from the exhaust at the same velocity that the air is
flowing in the duct (40­CFR­60, Methods 1­5). Typically, a sample size of 30 dscf was taken at a
rate of between 0.5 and 0.75 dscfm. Total particulate loading was determined by combining
gravimetric analysis of the standard particle filter and chemical analysis of probe rinse solutions.
An additional impinger containing 2 N NaOH was added to the sampling train to ensure
complete scrubbing of acid gases. The collected materials were analyzed using DCP­AES for the
majority of the constituents and ion chromatography (IC) for anions. Melter emission fluxes are
compared to feed fluxes and emission samples taken while processing the same glass
compositions in Table 5.1. Notice the distinction that is made between constituents sampled as
particles and as "gas". The "gaseous" constituents are operationally defined as those species that
are scrubbed in the impinger solutions after the air stream has passed through a 0.3 µm heated
filter. All samples were well within the 90 ­ 110% limits for isokinetic sampling.

 Particulate emissions while processing the HLW­HPA­07 glass composition with
phosphates constituted from 0.13 to 0.17 percent of feed solids; particulate emissions while
processing comparable feed without phosphates constituted 0.92 percent of feed solids. The level
of carryover while processing the HLW­HPA­07 glass composition was considerably lower than
the range measured while processing various feeds containing high iron HLW simulants
processed on the same melter: AZ­102 (0.57 ­ 1.47 percent) [15]; C­106/AY­102 SIPP (0.61 to
0.81 percent) [12]; the former C­106/AY­102 baseline (0.3 ­ 0.74 percent) [45]; the
C­106/AY­102 high waste loading formulation (0.66 and 0.71 percent) [16]; high chromium
HLW­HCr­16 formulation (0.70 and 0.62 percent) [20]; high strontium HLW­HSr­7 formulation
(1.14 and 0.58 percent) [21]; high zirconium HLW­ZrA­07 formulation (1.57 and 2.51 percent)
[22]; high zirconium HLW­Zr­11 formulation (3.05 and 1.77 percent) [22]; high bismuth
HLW­Bi­F3 formulation (0.30 and 0.39 percent) [17]; high phosphate HLW­HP­16 formulation
(0.34 and 0.23) [25]; HLW­HMn­20 glass formulation (0.23 to 0.34 percent) [23]; and chromium
and bismuth limited HLW wastes (0.19 – 0.48) [3]. Conversely, carryover while processing feed
without phosphates was more than double those observed while processing most HLW waste
streams under the same conditions. Large effects on carryover of relatively small composition
changes have been observed in previous tests; for example, adding oxidants to feed in the form
of nitrates reduced bismuth emissions by a factor of more than fifty [35]. Carryover was also
higher for nonvolatile elements such as silicon while processing feed without phosphate,
indicating that conditions in the melter plenum and cold cap were more favorable for feed

 37

entrainment into the exhaust system. The relatively low particulate carryover in the present tests
was a result in part of the low volatile content of the feed. Much higher levels of carryover were
observed in tests with significant amounts of volatile constituents such as rhenium, cesium, and
chlorine that are emitted primarily as fine particulate [53]. Two of the volatile constituents in the
HLW­HPA­07 composition, fluorine and sulfur, are emitted in larger proportion as gaseous
species and therefore do not contribute as significantly to the calculated carryover of feed solids.
Modest increases in particulate carryover, 0.13 vs. 0.17 percent, were observed with increases in
bubbling rate; although smaller, this is in keeping with several previous tests processing HLW
compositions [3, 10, 11, 16, 20­23]. Carryover values for tests conducted with the nominal
bubbling rate with sodium, calcium, and iron phosphate vary from only 0.13 to 0.16 percent of
feed solids, indicating that the type of phosphate in the melter feed has a minimal effect on the
amount of particulate carryover.

 The composition of the particulate in the melter exhaust is reflective of the feed
composition, with some enrichments or depletions based on elemental volatility. Elements
present in the particulate at a proportion lower than in the feed are aluminum, silicon, nickel,
manganese, phosphorus, and zirconium, while chromium, halides, alkalis, lead, and sulfur were
emitted at rates considerably higher than that for the total particulate. Other elements were
emitted at similar rates to the overall solids carryover rate. The highest carryover was clearly
observed for the most volatile elements, sulfur and halides, as expected. Chlorine was detected in
the emissions particularly in tests with sodium phosphate due to its presence in chemicals as a
contaminant and its highly volatile nature. No difference in these trends was observed with the
various phosphates over the course of the tests although the absolute amount of all feed
constituents is five to ten times higher when processing feed without phosphates. Phosphorus is
one of the least volatile elements in the feed; the relative carryover of phosphorus is not affected
by the type of phosphate. Boron, chlorine, fluorine, and sulfur were the only elements detected in
the impinger solutions collected downstream of the heated particle filter in the sampling train,
which constitutes the “gas” fraction of the melter emissions.

5.2 Particulate Sampled from Off-Gas System

 The amount of material accumulated in the cyclones downstream of the film cooler is
listed in Table 5.2. The amount of material removed from the cyclones accounts for less than a
twentieth of a percent of the feed solids from the tests, which is lower than the particulate
sampled in the melter exhaust, suggesting that either the particulate is too fine to be removed by
the cyclone or solids are deposited in the line in between the exhaust sampling point and the
cyclone. The analyzed mineral content and chemical composition of material accumulated in the
cyclones is provided in Tables 5.2 and 5.3, respectively. Cyclone solids contain all of the feed
components in similar proportions to the feed for most elements with enrichments over feed for
the volatile elements chromium (2 times), lead (2 times), and sulfur (4­6 times), as well as an up
to twofold depletion of silicon. These proportions of volatile and non­volatile elements in the
cyclone particulate indicate bulk carryover of feed from the melter coupled with the loss of some
volatile components from the glass pool and cold cap. Chlorine, lanthanum, lithium, selenium,
titanium, thallium, and zinc were detected in the cyclone solids despite not being included in the
target feed composition due to contamination of present feed (chlorine for example) or carryover

 38

of residual material from preceding tests. The latter is best illustrated for the sample taken at the
end of testing: potassium, lithium, thallium, and zirconium were detected at relatively high
concentrations in the cyclone solids and were included in the HLW­HMn­20 composition [23],
which was processed before the present test without phosphate. Also of note is the low
phosphorus content of the sample taken after the test processing feed without phosphate as
compared to the other tests (0.4 vs. 8.2­9.6 wt% P2O5). The results from XRD analysis show that
the most abundant mineral phases detected in the cyclone deposits were present in the feed as
either the additive quartz or the waste components eskolaite and hematite, suggesting direct
entrainment of feed components into the melter exhaust stream. Small amounts of phosphate
mineral phases were detected in cyclone deposits taken after processing feed containing sodium
phosphates although no sodium phosphate crystals were detected in the deposits. Small amounts
of mineral phases containing chlorine were detected in the same deposits presumably originating
from the contamination of the sodium phosphate in the feed. Zircon and TlCl were present in
deposits from the end of testing as residuals from tests processing the HLW­HMn­20
composition [23].

5.3 Gases Monitored by FTIR

 Melter emissions were monitored in each test for a variety of gaseous components, most
notably CO and nitrogen species, by Fourier Transform Infra­Red Spectroscopy (FTIR). The
off­gas system temperature is maintained well above 100°C beyond the sampling port
downstream of the DM100 HEPA filter to prevent analyte loss due to condensation prior to
monitoring. The data, therefore, represent the relative concentrations of volatile gaseous species
in the melter exhaust. A summary of the range and average concentrations of gaseous species
monitored during the tests is provided in Table 5.4. The analytes listed in Table 5.4 are those that
were thought likely to be observed during the tests based on previous work; no other species
were detected in the off­gas stream by FTIR. The concentrations of the most abundant monitored
species, nitrogen monoxide, hydrogen fluoride, carbon dioxide, and water, are plotted in Figures
5.1.a ­ 5.2.d. Generally, emissions from the DM100 were relatively low as a result of the low
concentrations of nitrogen species and organic carbon in the feed. The most abundant nitrogen
species monitored was NO, which is in keeping with previous melter tests with both HLW and
LAW feeds. The concentrations of the most abundant measured species, water and carbon
dioxide increased with the feed processing rate since the feed water and carbon content were
nominally constant for all feeds. Consistent with the isokinetic sampling (see Section 5.1),
significant amounts of HF were emitted throughout each of the tests, with the highest
concentrations monitored while processing feed containing iron phosphate and the lowest test
average while processing feed without phosphate. The lowest HF concentrations were measured
at the onset of testing prior to achieving a fluorine concentration in the glass that was in steady
state with the process. Since the monitored HF concentrations show the same correlation with
processing rate as do water and carbon dioxide, the relative amounts of HF monitored in each
test is probably determined by the amount of fluorine present in each of the additives as an
impurity. Test average concentrations of carbon monoxide, ammonia, hydrogen cyanide, and
nitrous oxide were below detectable limits in all the tests. The scatter in the emissions data over
the course of the tests is due in part to changes in the cold cap.

 SECTION 6.0
 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

 This report details work performed to develop and test new glass and feed formulations
designed to increase waste loadings for Hanford HLW containing aluminum, iron, and sodium
phosphates as well as to determine the effects of the form of phosphate in Hanford HLW streams
on melter feed processing rates. A number of HLW batches to be processed at the WTP have
P2O5 concentrations that extend up to about thirteen weight percent on an oxide basis [24]. These
high­phosphorus HLW streams frequently contain other components such as bismuth, iron,
calcium, sulfur, and various rare earth elements, which when combined with phosphorus, can
cause secondary phase formation or amorphous phase separation that can limit waste loading or
lead to poor cold cap behavior and slow processing rates.

 The present work addresses high phosphorus HLW streams and the effects of various
forms of phosphate (aluminum, iron, and sodium) on waste loading, secondary phase formation,
and processing characteristics. The high­phosphorus HLW composition selected for testing is
batch Number 2636 from the HTWOS run given in the River Protection Project System Plan,
Revision 6 [24]. The work included preparation and characterization of crucible melts to evaluate
the effects of waste loading on glass properties and the maximum waste loadings that can be
achieved. The results from crucible­scale testing supported subsequent DM100 melter tests
designed to further assess the enhanced glass and feed formulations with respect to processing
rate and product quality. The most abundant components in HLW Batch 2636 are Na2O
(35.86 wt%) and Al2O3 (21.70 wt%), followed by P2O5 (10.77 wt%). The concentrations of
Bi2O3 and SO3 are moderately high at 5.25 wt% and 0.72 wt%, respectively.

 The initial set of glasses for this HLW stream were formulated and tested at waste
loadings of 42 to 55 wt%. The glasses showed less than 1 vol% crystals after heat treatment for
70 hours at 900ºC and no nepheline on CCC heat treatment. The crystalline phases were
phosphates and spinels, as expected. Characterization data showed that glasses that meet all of
the WTP processing and product quality requirements could be selected from this set. Therefore,
further glass formulation work explored waste loadings of more than 55 wt%. From the new set
of glasses that were tested at waste loadings of up to 64 wt%, HLW­HPA­07, at a waste loading
of 58 wt%, was selected as the candidate glass composition for melter testing. HLW­HPA­07
meets all of the WTP processing and product quality requirements and the CCC heat treated
glass sample shows PCT releases well below the WTP limits. SEM examination of the CCC heat
treated sample for crystal content showed about 1.75 vol% phosphate containing Na and Ca and
another 9.93 vol% voids, which are likely small water soluble alkali phosphate crystals that were
removed during samples preparation for SEM analysis.

 Although HLW­HPA­07 meets all of the WTP requirements for HLW glasses, during
 melter testing and idling of the melter in between tests it was discovered that this glass is highly
 corrosive to Inconel 690. Corrosion of Inconel components, including the bubbler, thermowell,
 and airlift lance, was discovered after testing feeds containing either Na3PO4 or FePO4 and

 40

 Ca5(PO4)3(OH). Therefore testing with the HLW­HPA­07 glass composition was discontinued.
 The bubbler and thermowell were replaced, the glass inventory in the melter was exchanged,
 and melter testing moved to a different study involving a series of tests with a high manganese
 waste [23]. In parallel, bench scale testing was performed, which confirmed the corrosive nature
 of the HLW­HPA­07 glass composition to Inconel and also showed that the same formulation
 without phosphorus was not overly corrosive towards Inconel. After consultation with ORP, the
 last melter test was conducted with the HLW­HPA­07 glass composition without phosphorus in
 order to assess the effects of phosphate on the vitrification process.

 Although there is no WTP specification for Inconel 690 corrosion by HLW glass, high
corrosion rates will deleteriously affect the lifetime of melter components such as bubblers,
thermowells, airlift lances, and electrodes. Therefore, additional glass formulation efforts are
needed to develop suitable glass formulations that address this issue. A further important
conclusion from this work is that Inconel corrosion testing should be integrated into the glass
formulation development work for these high phosphorus HLW streams.

 Tests on the DM100 were conducted at 1150ºC at the nominal bubbling rate of 9 lpm and
 also with optimized bubbling to achieve maximum production rates. The feeds with Na3PO4
 were processed at glass production rates of 970 and 1425 kg/m 2 /day with nominal and
 P P

 optimized bubbling, respectively. The rate obtained at nominal conditions was less than that
 achieved for most of the other high waste loading formulations and marginally above the WTP
 HLW baseline requirement; however, the rate increased by fifty percent with optimization of
 melt pool bubbling. Glass production rates under nominal conditions with different phosphate
 forms showed the following trend:

 No Phosphate > Na3PO4 > FePO4 + Ca5(PO4)3(OH),

where the production rate for slowest of these feeds was below the WTP HLW baseline
requirement of 800 kg/m2/day.

 Feed without phosphate was incorporated into the molten glass most rapidly and required
the least amount of manual intervention to remove deposits remaining on the melter walls after
discharging, whereas feed containing FePO4 and Ca5(PO4)3(OH) was not readily incorporated
into the glass pool and required frequent manual intervention to remove feed deposits. The same
melting trend was determined by VGF testing, which also evaluated feed containing AlPO4 to
melt at comparable rates to feed containing Na3PO4. VGF testing ranked feed containing FePO4
and Ca5(PO4)3(OH) as exceptionally slow melting.

 The effect of phosphate and phosphate type on production rate is striking: removal of
phosphate resulted in a 34 to 72% increase in rate, depending on the phosphate form. The
magnitude of the deleterious effect of phosphorus on rate is particularly impressive in view of
the relative small amount of P2O5 in the glass formulation. Thus, low melt rates that vary with
phosphate form represent a significant risk for processing high phosphorus HLW streams.

 Samples of the glass product were collected from each test and analyzed for composition,
crystalline phases, iron oxidation state, and product quality. Glasses produced from feed without

 41

phosphates produced very little secondary phases. Glasses produced from feeds containing
phosphates had five to eighteen percent of either sodium or calcium phosphate finely divided
crystalline phases, similar to that observed in a previous high phosphorus HLW composition
[25]; however, this crystallization is thought to occur during cooling and therefore should not
affect glass processing properties. Spinels were present at less than a tenth of a weight percent in
all of the samples. The iron oxidation state of melter glasses ranged from totally oxidized to
about four percent divalent iron. Glass from melter tests far exceeded chemical durability
requirements on the PCT and TCLP methods. Glass produced without phosphorus showed
higher leachate concentrations but still well below the WTP limits.

 Particulate emissions while processing the HLW­HPA­07 glass composition with
phosphates constituted from 0.13 to 0.17 percent of feed solids; particulate emissions while
processing comparable feed without phosphates constituted 0.92 percent of feed solids.
Carryover values for tests conducted with the nominal bubbling rate with sodium, calcium, and
iron phosphate vary from only 0.13 to 0.16 percent of feed solids, indicating that the type of
phosphate in the melter feed has a minimal effect on the amount of particulate carryover. The
composition of the particulate in the melter exhaust is reflective of the feed composition, with
some enrichments or depletions based on elemental volatility. The composition of the particulate
was confirmed by retrieval and analysis of solids from the cyclone filter in the off­gas system.
XRD analysis of the particulate indicates entrainment of feed minerals (quartz, eskolaite, and
hematite) and amorphous feed components in the exhaust as well as enrichments of the volatile
elements chromium, lead, and sulfur.

 In summary, these results further highlight the importance of evaluating the effects of all
probable forms of waste components on the vitrification process. While identifying the forms of
waste components is complicated by limited knowledge and sampling of tank wastes, as well as
chemical reactions occurring in tanks over time, during pretreatment, and in the melter feed, the
available information can be used to perform testing to assess the potential magnitude of these
effects and to develop useful bounds. The results from the present work indicate that the Batch
2636 high­phosphorus HLW simulant requires additional formulation development in order to
identify compositions that are acceptably corrosive to Inconel. However, this is an issue that
should also be addressed for the full range of high phosphate HLW streams. In addition, further
development is required to increase the processing rate for all types of phosphates likely to be
present in these high phosphate HLW streams. Finally, further work is required on alkali
phosphate phase separation in glasses developed for these high phosphorus HLW streams.
