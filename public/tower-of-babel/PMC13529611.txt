Cap-assisted endoscopic sclerotherapy for bleeding-predominant grade III hemorrhoids: efficacy and risk factor analysis focusing on two dimensions of bleeding score
Authors: Gao-jue Wu, Yating Xun, Siyan Yu, Xuejun Tang, Yan Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesCap-assisted endoscopic sclerotherapy (CAES) via a long needle optimizes the visualization of operation field and enables precise injection of sclerosant, yet evidence regarding its application in high-grade internal hemorrhoids (HDs) remains limited. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of CAES therapy for bleeding-predominant grade III symptomatic internal HDs, and explore potential risk factors associated with postoperative bleeding persistence and recurrence.MethodsThis single-arm retrospective observational study included 82 patients with bleeding-predominant grade III internal HDs who underwent a single CAES procedure by the injection of liquid polidocanol via a long needle. Treatment efficacy was assessed at 3 months post-CAES using the conventional Hemorrhoid Disease Severity Score (HDSS; based on the frequency of symptoms, including bleeding) and the score of the intensity dimension of the bleeding symptom. Bleeding recurrence was monitored during follow-ups. Logistic regression analysis was employed to explore potential risk factors associated with bleeding outcomes.Results (1) The overall efficacy rate was 85.4%. The effective rates for bleeding and prolapse symptoms were 96.3% and 58.2%, respectively. (2) During a follow-up of 24 months, the bleeding recurrence rate was 25.3% (20/79). (3) Exploratory logistic regression analysis suggested that a high pre-CAES bleeding intensity score (odds ratio [OR] = 2.887, p = 0.030) was significantly associated with non-excellent outcome (i.e., persistent bleeding); meanwhile, a high pre-CAES bleeding intensity score (OR = 3.981, p = 0.012) and a high post-CAES bleeding frequency score (OR = 3.803, p = 0.006) were significantly associated with post-CAES bleeding recurrence. (4) No severe complications were reported.ConclusionsCAES with long-needle injection of polidocanol may serve as an effective and safe therapeutic option for managing bleeding symptom in grade III internal HDs. The higher bleeding-associated scores, which encompass both bleeding frequency and intensity, may represent potential risk factors for persistent or recurrent bleeding after the CAES procedure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains Supplementary Material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10151-026-03371-z.Keywords: Hemorrhoids, Cap-assisted endoscopic sclerotherapy, Efficacy, Bleeding recurrence, Risk factor

Introduction

Hemorrhoids (HDs) represent the most prevalent anorectal disorder, affecting over 50% of the population in the USA and 51.56% in China, with associated annual healthcare costs ranging from 350 million USD to 2.5 billion USD in the USA [1, 2]. Symptomatic HDs are characterized by the symptom of bleeding, prolapse, pain, itching, and soiling [3], with bleeding and prolapse as the main symptoms, seriously impairing quality of life. The Hemorrhoid Disease Symptom Score (HDSS) is a widely adopted questionnaire that assesses hemorrhoid‑related symptoms, including bleeding, primarily on the basis of frequency of each symptom. While most studies have focused solely on bleeding frequency, a limited number of previous studies [4, 5] have assessed bleeding severity from a distinct perspective—namely, bleeding intensity, which characterizes the activity and magnitude of individual bleeding episodes. Accordingly, the present study tried to provide a comprehensive evaluation of bleeding symptoms by integrating frequency and intensity as two distinct yet complementary dimensions of bleeding assessment.

Sclerotherapy, a mini-invasive therapy, emerges as the preferred treatment option for patients with symptomatic internal HDs following the failure of conservative treatment, including diet and lifestyle adjustment, as well as pharmacological agents, such as low-dose anesthetics, astringents, and protectants, etc. Sclerotherapy fixes anal cushion and reduces bleeding through immediate vascular occlusion and subsequent local fibrosis [6]. Endoscopic injection sclerotherapy (EIS), a subtype of sclerotherapy that enables direct visualization for diagnosis and injection under endoscopy, has been recognized as a prominent and cost-effective therapy for internal HDs, particularly bleeding HDs [7]. Cap-assisted endoscopic sclerotherapy (CAES), a modified EIS technique involving the installment of a transparent cap in front of the colonoscope, offers enhanced exposure of the surgical field and facilitates precise injection of the sclerosants, especially when using long injection needles. Owing to these advantages, CAES is increasingly utilized as an alternative to or a “bridge treatment” before surgery [6].

The treatment options of internal HDs are primarily guided by the grades and patient-centered requirements, with a focus on symptom relief and improvement in quality of life [8]. For low-grade (grade I and II) symptomatic internal HDs, sclerotherapy is widely recommended as a first-line minimally invasive intervention following the failure of conservative treatments. However, the optimal management strategy for grade III symptomatic HDs remains controversial. While some guidelines classify grade III internal HDs as the most severe category suitable for sclerotherapy [9, 10], others recommend surgery instead [11]. This retrospective study aims to evaluate the symptom response, recurrence patterns, and feasibility of CAES treatment on bleeding-predominant grade III symptomatic internal HDs, with a specific focus on treatment outcomes for bleeding and prolapse; meanwhile, the potential risk factors for persistent and recurrent bleeding after CAES therapy were also explored.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-arm retrospective observational study focused on treatment outcomes of CAES therapy on bleeding-predominant grade III symptomatic internal HDs. The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi No.2 People’s Hospital, which also waived the requirement for written informed consent in virtue of the design for the retrospective study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Supplementary Material 1).

A total of 92 consecutive patients with grade III symptomatic internal HDs who met the inclusion criteria between January 2021 and March 2024 at the Department of Gastroenterology, Wuxi No.2 People’s Hospital, were recruited. However, ten patients were excluded from the final analysis, one of whom had chronic anal fissure and nine of whom were lost to follow-up within 3 months after CAES. Thus, a total of 82 patients were included in the final analysis of treatment efficacy, safety, and risk factors for bleeding persistence. Among them, a total of 79 patients who responded to CAES for bleeding were included in the analysis of bleeding recurrence and its potential risk factors (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart

Inclusion criteria were the following: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosed with symptomatic grade III internal HDs; (3) experienced failure of conservative treatments, including modifications of lifestyle and diet and medications; (4) received a single CAES procedure using a long injection needle; (5) and had a follow-up of no less than 24 months post-CAES.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) history or complication of anal fissures or fistulas, external HDs, thrombosed internal HDs, or inflammation bowel disease; (2) history of anorectal surgery, sclerotherapy, or rubber band ligation (RBL); (3) severe cardiac disease, hepatic disease, renal disease, or coagulopathy; (4) receiving anticoagulant therapies, pelvic radiotherapy, or chemotherapy; (5) being pregnant; and (6) incomplete clinical data or no follow-up recordings.

The grades of internal HDs were classified according to the Goligher system as follows [12]: grade I: no prolapse outside the anal canal; grade II: prolapse only with straining but could retract spontaneously; grade III: prolapse when straining or exertion requiring manual reduction; grade IV: a persistent and nonreducible prolapse.

The effectiveness and safety of CAES therapy were assessed at 3 months after CAES. Subsequently, patients underwent follow-up assessments at 6 months, 12 months, and 24 months after CAES procedure. During these follow-ups, recurrence of bleeding was recorded, and potential risk factors were analyzed.

CAES procedure

After standard bowel preparation, patients were placed in the left lateral position for the CAES procedure, which was performed by the same endoscopist with extensive operation experience. A short and transparent cap was installed at the top of the colonoscope (CF-H290I, Olympus, Japan). A long injection needle (FMT-DT-D, 10 mm in length, FMT Medical, Nanjing, China) was used for the injection of the polidocanol (10 ml:100 mg, 1%, lauromacrogol, Shanxi Tianyu Pharmaceutical, China) [13, 14] at three to seven sites above the dentate line with a total dosage of no more than 10 ml. For each injection site, 1–2 ml of liquid polidocanol was slowly injected to the submucosal layer followed by a slow withdrawn to form a “sclerosis pile” (Fig. 2). Patients were required to stay in bed for 2 h after the CAES procedure.

Assessment of treatment outcomes and follow-ups

Symptom severity was assessed by the HDSS. HDSS consists of five domains, each of which evaluates one HD-related symptom, including pain, itching, soiling, bleeding, and prolapse (requiring manual reduction). Each domain was scored on a 4-point scale on the basis of symptom frequency (0 = never, 1 = less than once per month, 2 = less than once per week, 3 = 1–6 days per week, 4 = daily or always), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 20 [15–17].

Short-term efficacy and safety evaluation was performed 3 months after CAES treatment [14, 18]. The primary outcomes included the short-term overall symptom resolution rate (assessed via the total HDSS score) and therapeutic efficacy of bleeding (evaluated by the bleeding frequency score), while the secondary outcomes comprised the recurrence of bleeding during the 24-month follow-up period and the short-term improvement of prolapse. The treatment outcome of CAES on overall symptoms of HDs was classified into three grades on the basis of the difference in HDSS before and after CAES treatment: (1) excellent (reduction ≥ 70%), (2) good (reduction of 30–69%), and (3) poor (reduction < 30% or even increased HDSS). The overall effective rate = the number of patients with excellent or good effect/total number of patients × 100%.

The efficacy of CAES on symptoms of bleeding and prolapse was classified into three grades according to the HDSS bleeding frequency score (score of bleeding domain in HDSS) and HDSS prolapse score (score of prolapse domain in HDSS), respectively, as follows: (1) excellent, score = 0; (2) good, score improvement ≥ 1, and score ≤ 2; and (3) poor, failing to achieve excellent or good grade. Adverse events and complications were also recorded. In addition, the internal HD grades (according to the Goligher system) at 3 months after CAES procedure were also recorded.

For assessing the severity of bleeding symptom, both of the dimensions, including bleeding frequency and intensity, were evaluated. In addition to the conventional HDSS bleeding frequency score, this study employed the bleeding intensity score, which was rated on a 3-point scale: 0 (no bleeding), 1 (blood on the toilet paper), 2 (blood on stool), and 3 (blood dripping into the toilet) [4, 5].

The patients were followed up at the 6, 12, and 24 months after CAES procedure, and the recurrence of bleeding was recorded. Bleeding recurrence was defined as the re-emergence of bleeding symptoms occurring at least once weekly during follow-up in patients who had initially achieved an excellent or good response at 3 months post-CAES [19]. Consequently, the analysis of recurrence was restricted to treatment responders (n = 79). Patients classified as nonresponders (n = 3), i.e., those with poor bleeding outcomes at the 3-month evaluation, were excluded from the recurrence analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). There were no missing data for any enrolled patient. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Continuous variables with normal distribution and non-normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and median [25th and 75th percentile], respectively. The categorical variables were recorded as number (percentage). For the assessment of treatment outcomes, the generalized estimating equation was used to compare the differences in the scores before and after CAES among the different outcome grades (excellent, good, and poor outcome). Additionally, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to compare the scores between the two different time points (pre-CAES versus post-CAES), with Bonferroni correction used to adjust for the multiple comparisons. Spearman’s correlation analysis was applied for correlation analysis between the two different bleeding scores. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to evaluate bleeding-free survival during follow-ups, which represented cumulative treatment success.

To identify the risk factors for persistence or recurrence of bleeding, univariate and multivariable logistic regression was performed. However, owing to the limited number of events—only 16 patients experienced persistent bleeding and 20 patients experienced recurrent bleeding—the final multivariate analysis was restricted to a small set of variables that demonstrated the strongest and most consistent clinical and statistical associations. Consequently, in the risk factor analyses for bleeding persistence and recurrence, the events per predictor variable (EPV) were 16 and 10, respectively. These EPV values generally align with the “10 EPV”rule (EPV ≥ 10) aimed at reducing overfitting [20]. Nonetheless, the explored risk factors should be interpreted as exploratory signals rather than definitive estimates.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of these, 44 were male and 38 were female individuals, with a mean age of 51.9 ± 14.9 years (ranged from 22 to 85 years). The baseline characteristics of patients are detailed in Table 1.

Primary outcome: overall treatment outcome assessed by HDSS

As one of the primary outcomes, the overall treatment outcome assessed by HDSS indicated a 77.8% median decrease in HDSS total score (pre- versus post-CAES: 9.0 [6.0, 10.3] versus 2.0 [0.0, 5.0], p < 0.05; Table 2) in all of the 82 patients. Among them, 85.4% (70/82) of patients reported satisfactory improvement; in addition, 53.7% (44/82) and 31.7% (26/82) of them achieved excellent and good treatment outcomes, respectively, while 14.6% (12/82) reported poor outcomes. The patients across all three outcome grades exhibited significant reductions in HDSS scores (all p < 0.05; Table 3). The median HDSS score decreased by 100% and 60% in patients with excellent and good outcomes, respectively. Even in patients with poor responses, a significant reduction of 16.7% was observed (9.0 [8.0, 11.0] pre-CAES versus 7.5 [7.0, 9.8] post-CAES, p < 0.05; Table 3).

Primary outcome: treatment outcome on bleeding

CAES demonstrated an efficacy rate of 96.3% (79/82) in managing bleeding symptoms. Among patients with grade III HDs, 80.5% (66/82) achieved excellent outcomes, defined as the complete cessation of bleeding at 3 months after CAES procedure; meanwhile, 15.8% (13/82) exhibited good outcomes, characterized by a 50.0% reduction of median HDSS bleeding frequency score (post- versus pre-CAES: 2.0 [1.0, 2.0] versus 4.0 [3.0, 4.0], p < 0.05; Table 3). However, 3/82 (3.7%) reported poor outcomes with no significant improvement in bleeding frequency score (p > 0.05). Meanwhile, the bleeding severity assessments in all patients indicated 100% decreases in both median bleeding frequency score (3.0 [2.8, 4.0] pre-CAES versus 0.0 [0.0, 0.0] post-CAES, p < 0.05) and median bleeding intensity score (2.0 [2.0, 2.0] pre-CAES versus 0.0 [0.0, 0.0] post-CAES, p < 0.05; Table 2).

The two dimensions of the bleeding assessment—namely, bleeding intensity dimension (bleeding intensity score) and bleeding frequency dimension (bleeding frequency score)—exhibited a certain inconsistency among the patient cohorts. In patients with excellent bleeding outcomes (n = 66), a strong positive correlation was observed between the bleeding intensity score and bleeding frequency score before CAES (r = 0.758, p < 0.001). However, in patients with non-excellent bleeding outcome (i.e., good or poor outcome related to persistent bleeding, n = 16), the correlation was weaker, with Spearman coefficients of r = 0.430 (p > 0.05) pre-CAES and r = 0.621 (p = 0.010) post-CAES.

Risk factors for persistence of bleeding after CAES

A total of 14 baseline characteristics (independent variables) were included in univariate logistic regression analysis to explore the potential risk factors for non-excellent treatment outcome on bleeding (i.e., bleeding persistence, the dependent variable). These variables encompassed age, BMI, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, and constipation), pre-CAES HDSS domain scores (bleeding frequency, prolapse, pain, soiling, and itching score), pre-CAES bleeding intensity score, number of injection sites, and dosage of polidocanol. It revealed that a higher pre-CAES bleeding intensity score might be the only potential risk factor for persistence of bleeding (non-excellent outcome) at 3 months after CAES (OR = 2.887, 95% CI [1.106, 7.536], p < 0.05; Fig. 3) in this study.

Secondary outcome: treatment outcome on prolapses and other symptoms

Of the 82 patients, a 75.0% decrease of median HDSS prolapse score (4.0 [1.0, 4.0] pre-CAES versus 1.0 [0.0, 3.0] post-CAES, p < 0.001; Table 2) was recorded after CAES therapy. Among them, 67 patients presented with symptom of prolapse before CAES (at baseline). At 3 months after CAES procedure, their treatment outcomes for prolapse were as follows: excellent (complete resolution) in 35.8% (24/67), good (post-CAES 2.0 [1.0, 2.0] versus pre-CAES 4.0 [3.0, 4.0], p < 0.05; Table 3) in 22.4% (15/67), and poor in 41.8% (28/67). Accordingly, the overall effective rate for the treatment response on prolapse was 58.2%. Furthermore, among all the 82 patients with grade III HDs, regardless of subjective symptoms of prolapse, 45.1% (37/82) demonstrated an improvement in HD grades from grade III pre-CAES to grade II post-CAES; the remaining patients (54.9%) retained their grade III status.

The HDSS soiling score also showed a significant decrease after CAES therapy (2.0 [1.0, 3.0] pre-CAES versus 0.0 [0.0, 2.0] post-CAES, p < 0.05; Table 2). However, there was no significant alteration in HDSS pain score or itching score before and after CAES treatment (both p > 0.05; Table 2), which may be related to the low baseline scores of these symptoms in the included patients.

Secondary outcome: post-CAES bleeding recurrence in the follow-ups

During the 24-month follow-up after CAES, bleeding recurrence was reported in 20 of the 79 patients (25.3%) who initially responded to the treatment (Fig. 4). Among these 20 patients with recurrence, 55.0% (11/20) experienced recurrence within the first 6 months.

Among the patients with bleeding recurrence (n = 20), four underwent surgical intervention and subsequently achieved excellent outcomes. In contrast, three patients who underwent additional CAES treatment had poor outcomes and are currently receiving traditional Chinese medicine treatment. The remaining 13 patients did not receive any further intervention.

Risk factors for bleeding recurrence

To explore risk factors for post-CAES bleeding recurrence (dependent variable) in the follow-ups, a total of 20 post-CAES variables at baseline (pre-CAES) and 3 months (independent variables), including age, BMI, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, and constipation), pre- and post-CAES HDSS domain scores (score of bleeding frequency, prolapse, pain, itching, and soiling), bleeding intensity score, number of injection sites, and dosage of polidocanol, were selected in the univariate logistic regression analysis.

It revealed several potential risk factors (all p < 0.05; Fig. 5), including age, comorbidity of constipation, pre- and post-CAES HDSS bleeding frequency score, pre-CAES HDSS prolapse score and soiling score, and pre- and post-CAES bleeding intensity score. However, to minimize overfitting, a limited number of variables (n = 2, both p < 0.005) that showed the strongest and most consistent clinical and statistical associations with bleeding recurrence (events = 20) were entered into the following multivariate logistic regression analysis (EPV = 10 [20/2]) using a forward stepwise selection method[21]. It revealed that pre-CAES bleeding intensity score (OR = 3.981, 95% CI [1.347–11.761], p = 0.012) and post-CAES bleeding frequency score (OR = 3.803, 95% CI [1.480–9.775], p = 0.006) were potential independent risk factors for post-CAES bleeding recurrence (Fig. 5).

Even so, this analysis of risk factors is exploratory. Given the limited sample size and single-arm retrospective study design, the risk factors are presented as hypothesis-generating findings that require validation in larger, prospective cohorts before they can be considered confirmatory.

Safety evaluation

No procedure- or drug-related adverse events occurred during CAES. Further, no severe post-CAES complications, such as severe bleeding (requiring blood transfusion or emergency intervention, or hemodynamic instability), severe infection (abscess or sepsis), external thrombosis, urinary retention, perforation, or sexual dysfunction, were recorded. Post-CAES pain was reported in 15.9% (13/82) of patients; this was mild and self-limiting within 1 day in 12 cases, while one patient experienced severe pain that resolved after 10 days of oral analgesia.

Discussion

CAES treatment has emerged as one of the most widely accepted and cost-effective EIS for treating symptomatic internal HDs in recent years. This single-arm retrospective study evaluated the outcomes of CAES using a long needle in patients with bleeding-predominant grade III internal HDs. The key results included the following: (1) at 3 months after CAES, the treatment outcomes assessment showed that the overall efficacy rate was 85.4%, with effective rates of 96.3% for bleeding and 58.2% for prolapse. (2) The cumulative bleeding recurrence rate was 25.3% (20/79) during the 24-month post-CAES follow-ups. (3) Exploratory logistic regression analysis indicated that a high pre-CAES bleeding intensity score was a potential risk factor for the non-excellent treatment outcome on bleeding (i.e., persistent bleeding); meanwhile, a higher pre-CAES bleeding intensity score and a higher post-CAES bleeding frequency score were potential independent risk factors for post-CAES bleeding recurrence. (4) No severe complications were recorded.

EIS therapy is widely recommended for low-grade (grade I and II) internal HDs. In recent years, technological advancements have led to its increasing acceptance and recommendation for grade III internal HDs in certain regions [9, 10]. Furthermore, a few small sample studies have reported sclerotherapy for grade IV internal HDs [22], despite a high recurrence rate. However, surgical interventions (e.g. hemorrhoidectomy), rather than sclerotherapy, remain the recommended approach for high-grade (grade III and IV) internal HDs in guidelines of most countries [11, 23]. A few previous studies investigating the efficacy and safety of CAES for grade III internal HDs were limited by small sample sizes within subgroups. A retrospective study by Xie et al. [13] included 201 patients with internal HDs, of whom 24.9% (50/201) had grade III and 5.0% (10/201) had grade IV HDs; it reported symptom improvement in 87.5% of patients after a single CAES. Our study, which enrolled 82 patients with grade III HDs, demonstrated a comparable overall efficacy rate of 85.4%.

CAES, as a modified sclerotherapy for internal HDs, facilitates ideal visualization and complete exposure of the surgical field via a transparent cap attached to the colonoscope. This, combined with the use of a long needle, enables precise injection of liquid sclerosant into the base of internal HDs with accurate control over the angle, direction, and depth [24–26]. Lobascio et al. [22] reported sclerotherapy under anoscope for grade II and III HDs using a short injection needle, which indicated an overall success rate of 78.8% after a single sclerotherapy session and up to 86.0% following a secondary sclerotherapy session. Our study demonstrated an excellent overall efficacy rate of 85.4% and an effective rate for bleeding of 96.3% after a single CAES session, with a rebleeding rate of 25.3% over a follow-up of 24 months. Although, as a single-arm (uncontrolled) retrospective study, the efficacy rate of this study cannot be directly compared with those of other studies using sclerotherapy, it might suggest that long-needle CAES therapy may be an effective treatment option for managing bleeding in grade III internal HDs. Ultimately, high-quality randomized controlled trials are warranted to further validate the advantages of long-needle CAES with liquid sclerosants, particularly for high-grade internal HDs.

The sclerosant employed in this study was 1% liquid lauromacrogol (10 ml:100 mg), the domestically manufactured equivalent of polidocanol. Its use is explicitly recommended by the Chinese Expert Consensus on Hemorrhoid Injection Therapy [27] and the CAES protocol [28], which specifically advocate for the injection of the liquid (original) lauromacrogol. Currently, the optimal concentration of polidocanol for hemorrhoids remains unstandardized, with clinical practice utilizing concentrations ranging from 1% to 3%. Although higher concentrations (e.g., 3%) are preferred by more surgeons, 1% polidocanol/lauromacrogol has demonstrated sufficient hemostatic efficacy and a favorable safety profile in numerous clinical studies [13, 14, 25, 29–31]. Regarding formulation, while foam sclerotherapy offers superior tissue coverage with lower volumes and has gained increasing attention [29, 32], the liquid formulation remains a standard, effective, and widely adopted approach. It eliminates the need for on-site preparation, ensures dosing accuracy, and circumvents potential complications arising from foam instability or uneven distribution. Given the significant variations in real-world clinical practice, high-quality randomized controlled trials are warranted to compare different concentrations and formulations to optimize procedural strategies for hemorrhoid sclerotherapy.

Unlike previous studies that evaluated bleeding focusing on a single dimension (either bleeding frequency or intensity) [4, 5, 33], our study paid attention to both dimensions, which exhibited a certain degree of inconsistency between them. A strong positive correlation was observed between the bleeding intensity score and bleeding frequency score in patients with excellent bleeding outcomes but not in patients with non-excellent outcomes (i.e., persistent bleeding). Furthermore, the high bleeding intensity score and high bleeding frequency score were explored as potential risk factors for bleeding persistence and recurrence, respectively. These findings suggest that both dimensions of bleeding might be considered in treatment response assessments and risk factor evaluations.

This study explored potential risk factors for persistent bleeding and rebleed after CAES in patients with grade III internal HDs. Although the risk factor analysis was intended as an initial exploratory investigation into potential predictors within our single-arm retrospective study with medium samples, it might provide preliminary insights for optimizing treatment strategies. The exploratory logistic regression analysis suggested that the elevated bleeding-related symptom scores, including the pre-CAES bleeding intensity score and post-CAES bleeding frequency score, were significantly associated with non-excellent treatment response to bleeding (i.e., persistent bleeding) or post-CAES bleeding recurrence. Accordingly, surgery may be a preferable treatment option over CAES for patients with grade III HD with these potential risk factors. Alternatively, CAES could serve as a bridging therapy prior to surgery. These findings are supported by existing literature: A prospective multicenter cohort study on polidocanol foam sclerotherapy for grade I–III hemorrhoids identified a high baseline Sodergren symptom severity score as a key risk factor for recurrence [34]. Furthermore, an international multivariate analysis by Godeberge et al. [35] confirmed constipation as a significant risk factor, attributing this to repeated straining during defecation, which elevates rectal vascular pressure. In our study, constipation also emerged as one of the potential risk factors for recurrent bleeding (OR = 4.250, p = 0.015) by univariate regression analysis. However, constipation was excluded from further multivariate logistic regression analysis to mitigate the risk of overfitting, given the limited number of recurrent events (n = 20).

EIS has been established as a repeatable minimally invasive treatment with a favorable safety profile; however, severe life-threatening complications, including abdominal compartment syndrome, septic infections, rectal necrosis, rectovaginal fistula, severe postoperative hemorrhage, perforation, and external thrombosis requiring additional surgery have been reported [7]. In the present study, none of these severe complications occurred. The most frequent adverse event was mild postoperative anal pain at the injection site, which was typically self-limiting. The low complications rate observed may be attributed to the enhanced visualization and precise injection afforded by the CAES technique, which utilizes a transparent cap and long needle to minimize operative trauma and reduce the risk of injection-related ulcers [22, 26].

This single-center retrospective study evaluated the efficacy of CAES therapy using a single injection of liquid polidocanol via a long needle for grade III internal HDs. There were several limitations in the study: (1) The sample size, primary assessment horizon for efficacy, and follow-up duration were limited. The limited sample size and number of positive events resulted in the risk factors being interpreted only as exploratory signals. (2) The study exclusively enrolled patients with grade III internal HDs, precluding the inclusion of HD grade as a variable in the risk factor analysis. (3) As a single-arm retrospective study, CAES was not directly compared with other minimally invasive therapies, such as EBL or combination regimens, which limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding optimal treatment strategies. (4) The treatment efficacy was assessed by the alteration of HDSS, on the basis of symptom frequency, which may be partly arbitrary and may not align with patient-perceived success thresholds.

In the future, multicenter, prospective, randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-ups [23] are warranted to further validate the efficacy and safety of CAES for high-grade internal HDs.

Conclusions

CAES utilizing a long needle for liquid polidocanol injection may represent a potentially effective and safe therapeutic option for bleeding control in grade III internal HDs. The exploratory logistic regression analysis suggested that elevated pre- and post-CAES bleeding scores (encompassing both frequency and intensity dimensions) might be potential risk factors for persistent or recurrent bleeding following the CAES procedure.
