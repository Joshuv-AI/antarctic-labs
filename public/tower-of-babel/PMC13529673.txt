Molecular mechanism of pore formation by Plasmodium Perforin-like Protein 2
Authors: Yu Zhang, Lijie Zhong, Yun Song, Mingcheng Guo, Keli Ren, Tingting Yang, Yixin Huang, Ilia Sirotkin, Gangshun Yi, Fang Jiao, Peijun Zhang, Robert J C Gilbert, Tao Ni, Xiulian Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria-causing Plasmodium parasites must pass through several host cell types to complete their life cycle. This cell traversal is facilitated by perforin-like proteins (PLPs), among which PLP2 is essential for erythrocyte rupture by gametocytes. However, the mechanism by which PLP2 forms pores is not yet understood. Here, we combine cryo-electron microscopy and tomography to reveal the structural basis of Plasmodium vivax PLP2-mediated membrane attack. PvPLP2 assembles on lipid bilayers into heterogeneous arc- and ring-shaped pores with variable stoichiometries. Among them, we determine the structure of a 17-subunit pore complex in which the pore-forming MACPF domains form the central β-barrel, while the peripheral Apicomplexan PLP C-terminal β-pleated sheet (APCβ) domains anchor the complex to the membrane surface. A disulfide-stabilized mutant captures an intermediate pre-pore complex prior to membrane insertion, delineating the structural transitions that underpin β-barrel deployment. Functionally, PvPLP2 acts preferentially on the inner leaflet of the erythrocyte membrane, a specificity driven by its affinity for negatively charged lipids. Together, these findings establish the pore-formation pathway for a key Plasmodium virulence factor and provide a structural framework for rational design of transmission-blocking agents that prevent gametocyte egress.Subject terms: Cryoelectron tomography, Cryoelectron microscopy, Parasitology

Introduction

Plasmodium is the causative agent of malaria, which remains a global threat. The parasite’s life cycle involves multiple hosts, transitioning from mosquitoes to humans during a mosquito’s blood-feeding process1. As Plasmodium parasites alternate between intracellular and extracellular stages, they must enter and exit host cells during transition phases, making egress a vital component of the life cycle for these intracellular pathogens. In the mammalian hosts, individual parasites invade host erythrocytes for both asexual multiplication and differentiation into male and female gametocytes, which serve as the precursor cells of Plasmodium gametes2,3. Male and female gametocytes develop inside the parasitophorous vacuole (PV) in the erythrocytes and are subsequently released into the bloodstream. Egress of asexual merozoites and sexual gametocytes from erythrocytes requires rupture of both the PV membrane (PVM) and the erythrocyte membrane (EM). Efficient release of asexual daughter cells and gametes from host erythrocytes is necessary for further development4.

Understanding how the PV and EM are ruptured is essential to dissect the mechanism of Plasmodium egress and transmission. Throughout their complex life cycles in mammalian and insect hosts, apicomplexan parasites rely on a family of pore-forming proteins known as perforin-like proteins (PLPs). These proteins are critical mediators of parasite-host cell interactions, including cell traversal, invasion, and egress5,6. Mechanistically, PLPs are sequestered within the parasite’s secretory organelles and are rapidly secreted during parasite motility, where they are thought to form pores in the target membrane to facilitate these processes7,8. Toxoplasma gondii TgPLP1 permeabilizes the PV membrane and host plasma membrane during exit5. Plasmodium species express five distinct PLPs (PLP1-5), with PLP1 and PLP2 primarily involved in the mammalian host stage, and PLP3-5 in the mosquito stage of the parasite life cycle9–12. Notably, PLP1 is essential for the motility and cell traversal of Plasmodium sporozoites, facilitating migration from the dermal inoculation site to the liver, the subsequent invasion of hepatocytes, and the evasion of phagocytic immune cells13,14. Another key member, Plasmodium protein PLP2, is essential for gametocyte egress from host erythrocytes. Genetic ablation of PLP2 in P. berghei and P. falciparum prevents the perforation of the erythrocyte cell membrane, trapping the gametocyte and consequently blocking male gamete exflagellation1,15,16. Consequently, transmission of PLP2(-) parasites to the mosquito is reduced.

Plasmodium perforin-like proteins are members of the perforin branch of the Membrane Attack Complex/Perforin (MACPF)/Cholesterol-Dependent Cytolysin (CDC) superfamily, a group of evolutionarily conserved pore-forming proteins. The MACPF/CDC superfamily represents an ancient homologous group of pore-forming proteins that function primarily in cellular attack or defence17. Typically, they are secreted by bacterial or mammalian immune cells to form lytic pores in target cell membranes. The pore-forming process is driven by a conserved structural rearrangement within the MACPF/CDC domain18. In its soluble monomeric state, the MACPF domain displays a central, bent β-sheet flanked by α-helical bundles. Upon membrane binding and oligomerization, these α-helices undergo a conformational shift, transforming into transmembrane β-hairpins that collectively form a large β-barrel pore. Key members of this family include perforin-1 in cytotoxic lymphocytes19, perforin-2 for which a wide range of activities has been identified20, the complement system21, and various toxins produced by diverse organisms22.

The molecular pathway to pore-formation by MACPF/CDC proteins has been studied extensively23. Their stringent regulation of oligomerization and pore formation is critical to prevent off-target activity, and these regulatory mechanisms are remarkably diverse. For example, perforin-1 binds to target cell membranes via its Ca2+-dependent C2 domain19, whereas perforin-2 preferentially associates with negatively charged lipid bilayers and forms pores upon acidification20,24. In the complement system, the membrane attack complex (MAC) forms pores via sequential assembly of C5b–C8 on target membranes followed by C9 polymerization25; this process is receptor-independent, protease-activated, and modulated by membrane properties and host inhibitors such as CD5921,26. In the gasdermin (GSDM) family, a central effector of pyroptosis, pore formation also requires proteolytic activation by inflammatory caspases or granzymes to release the pore-forming domain, followed by its binding to anionic membrane lipids27.

The apicomplexan PLPs, including Plasmodium PLPs, contain a conserved central MACPF domain, a distinct and conserved Apicomplexan PLP C-terminal β-pleated sheet (APCβ) domain, and a variable N-terminal domain6. It is widely assumed that these PLPs form pores to compromise host membrane integrity during the parasite life cycle, but the direct evidence and mechanistic details have remained elusive. Specifically, the molecular basis for PLP2 recruitment to the erythrocyte cell membrane and its subsequent oligomerization into a functional pore is unknown. We previously reported structures of Toxoplasma gondii PLP1 (TgPLP1) in its monomeric and hexameric forms by X-ray crystallography28,29, prior to target membrane insertion.

Here, we investigate the mechanism of Plasmodium vivax PLP2 (PvPLP2) assembly on a native membrane bilayer. We resolve cryo-EM structures of PvPLP2 in both its mature pore conformation and a disulfide-locked pre-pore state. This work delineates the pore-formation pathway for PvPLP2 and reveals key structural features that define a conserved mechanism for the pre-pore-to-pore transition among apicomplexan perforin-like proteins.

Results

Structural heterogeneity of PvPLP2 pores on liposomes

Perforin-like proteins in Plasmodium are thought to perforate target membranes during parasite egress. Building on our previous structural and biochemical analysis of the APCβ domains from Plasmodium vivax PLP1 (PvPLP1) and PvPLP2, which reveals that PvPLP2 preferentially binds anionic lipids30, we evaluated the pore-forming activity of full-length PvPLP2 on liposomes composed of negatively charged lipids at pH 7.5 (LPA: lysophosphatidic acid; POPS: phosphatidylserine, and E. coli total lipid extract). PvPLP2 was recombinantly expressed in HEK293T or HEK293F cells as a secreted protein, purified, and assessed by liposome dye-release assays and negative-staining electron microscopy, which confirmed robust pore formation (Suppl. Fig. 1a; Suppl. Fig. 8a, b). We then characterized PvPLP2 assembly on liposomes of different lipid compositions by cryo-electron tomography (Supplementary Table 1). The PvPLP2 pore completeness was observed to vary with lipid composition: pores formed on E. coli total lipid extract membranes exhibited relatively greater completeness (ring 72.6%) compared to LPA and POPS liposomes (ring 37.47%) (Fig. 1a, c; Suppl. Fig. 1b–e). Interestingly, the membrane stability under the electron beam was itself composition-dependent: POPS membranes were most susceptible, exhibiting pronounced beam-induced bubbling upon irradiation, whereas liposomes prepared from E. coli total lipid extract were comparatively resistant and remained largely intact even at cumulative doses of ~120 e − /Å2 (Suppl. Fig. 2). Given its superior stability and ability to support pore formation, we focused our analysis on the E. coli liposome dataset (Suppl. Fig. 1b–d; Suppl. Fig. 3). Subtomogram averaging (STA) and classification revealed significant heterogeneity in pore architecture. Despite analysing a large dataset (Supplementary Table 1), the consensus map resolved to only ~8 Å to depict an overall arc-shaped structure but precluded detailed analysis of inter-subunit interfaces (Fig. 1b–f; Suppl. Fig. 3). This resolution limit is largely attributed to the inherent structural flexibility and variable completeness of the pores on the membrane.

Cryo-EM structure of liposome-bound PvPLP2 pore

To mitigate conformational and compositional heterogeneity and obtain a near-atomic structure of the PvPLP2 pore complex, we performed cryo-EM single-particle analysis (SPA) on pores assembled directly on liposomes (Supplementary Table 1). Using the pore complex density map from STA as the template (17-subunit ring), we identified pore complexes on the liposome surface (Suppl. Figs. 4, 5). Consistent with cryo-ET STA and classification analysis, PvPLP2 pores exhibited varying degrees of openness and stoichiometry, ranging from arc to closed rings comprising 16 − 20 subunits (Suppl. Fig. 4b). We focused on the most abundant pore assembly, which comprised 17 subunits in a ring conformation. Iterative classification and refinement yielded a ~ 4 Å resolution map with C17 symmetry (Suppl. Fig. 4). Subsequent symmetry expansion followed by focused 3D classification and local refinement on a four-subunit sub-region further improved local structure features, resulting in a final map at 3.64 Å resolution (Suppl. Fig. 5). The density reveals the MACPF domain in the pore conformation, displaying two β-hairpins (four β-strands) that span the membrane (Fig. 2b, c). The pseudo-threefold symmetric APCβ domain is well resolved, enabling complete atomic modeling of the apicomplexan perforin-like protein captured in its pore state on a lipid bilayer. The N-terminal domain is predicted disordered (Suppl. Fig. 6), remains structurally unresolved, and is not required for pore formation (Suppl. Fig. 8c and f).

The C17 PvPLP2 pore adopts a ring-shaped mushroom-like architecture with an outer diameter of 255 Å, an inner lumen of 76 Å, and an overall height of 127 Å (Fig. 2a, b). The extra-membranous cap rises ~85 Å above the lipid bilayer. The transmembrane β-barrel comprises 68 β-strands (4 per protomer), forming a continuous hydrogen-bonded sheet (Fig. 2c). The stability of the PvPLP2 pore is maintained by extensive inter-subunit contacts involving both the MACPF and APCβ domains. The transmembrane β-barrel is primarily stabilized by the lateral MACPF-MACPF interfaces, while the periphery of the extra-membranous cap is secured by contacts between neighbouring APCβ domains. The APCβ domain stands on the membrane surface, with its triangular core oriented approximately perpendicular to the membrane bilayer (Fig. 2b), in agreement with prior mutational and molecular dynamics analyses of the APCβ-membrane interface30. This basic surface is at the APCβ base complemented by specific hydrophobic and positively charged lysine clusters, which are positioned to interact directly with lipid headgroups (Fig. 2d), consistent with our previous results showing that neutralization mutations at these sites impair membrane recognition30. Electrostatic and hydrophobic surface analyses reveal pronounced positive potential at the base of APCβ and along the membrane-proximal rim of the β-barrel (Fig. 2e; Suppl. Fig. 5e), complementary to anionic membranes.

Comparison with other pore-forming proteins

PvPLP2 pore complexes display an overall similar architecture to canonical CDCs and vertebrate MACPF family pores31. The 17-mer PvPLP2 ring has a comparable inner diameter to perforin-2 pores ( ~ 75 Å)20,32, but it is substantially smaller than the CDC pores (e.g., pneumolysin33–35, suilysin36, and perfringolysin O37, inner diameters ~25-30 nm), while gasdermin pores can also reach large diameters depending on oligomeric state38–41, typically similar in size to CDCs (Suppl. Fig. 7). Unlike vertebrate MACPF proteins such as perforin-119,42,43 and perforin-224,32, which possess auxiliary domains (e.g., EGF-like modules) bridging the MACPF and C-terminal domains, apicomplexan PLPs feature a direct link of the MACPF domain to the APCβ domain. The position of the PvPLP2 membrane-targeting APCβ domain differs significantly. In CDCs (e.g., pneumolysin) and in perforin-2, the C-terminal domains are located near the rim of the β-barrel. In contrast, the APCβ domain of PvPLP2 is displaced laterally by ~7 nm, creating a much wider peripheral scaffold (Fig. 2b; Suppl. Fig. 7a).

Beyond its overall architecture, the distortion of the membrane bilayer around these pore forming proteins provides another key point of distinction for PvPLP2. Measured directly on liposomes by cryo-ET and STA, the PvPLP2 barrel inserts into the membrane at a near-perpendicular angle ( ~ 0° tilt), in stark contrast to the substantial lift given to the membrane bilayer by perforin-220,32 ( ~ 30°) and the Membrane Attack Complex44 (~15°) (Suppl. Fig. 7c). These distinct orientations in protein/membrane relationships likely represent alternative strategies for managing biophysical challenges like hydrophobic mismatch and domain-imposed constraints.

PvPLP2 pre-pore-like oligomers in solution

Different to mammalian perforin-2, in which pre-pore and pore states can be both captured45, PvPLP2 was observed to form only pore-forming states when added to anionic liposomes. We hypothesized that the absence of the PvPLP2 pre-pore may be due to a rapid pre-pore to pore transition on membranes. To capture a pre-pore-like assembly, we employed a similar strategy to the previous perforin-2 work, in which mild acidification promotes efficient oligomerization20,24. Under such mild acidic conditions (pH 5.5), PvPLP2 activity is only modestly reduced as shown in the sulforhodamine B dye-leakage assay, and cryo-ET corroborated efficient pore formation on anionic membranes (Suppl. Fig. 8a, b and e). To determine whether PvPLP2 forms soluble pre-pore assemblies like perforin-2 does, we performed single-particle cryo-EM at pH 5.5 and pH 7.5. PvPLP2 remains monomeric at neutral pH (7.5), and it self-assembles into small oligomers under acidic conditions (pH 5.5). Two-dimensional (2D) classification revealed locally curved oligomers comprising stacks of two, three, or four PvPLP2 subunits at pH 5.5 (Fig. 3a; Suppl. Fig. 9a-b), reminiscent of pre-pore arcs described for mouse perforin-2 in solution, a concentration-dependent assembly20. Although preferred orientation limited high-resolution reconstruction, 3D reconstruction of these oligomers revealed a pre-pore-like architecture (Fig. 3b). Rigid-body fitting of an AlphaFold-predicted MACPF domain reveals that the pre-pore-like arc comprises a lateral stack of MACPF domains. In this conformation, the two sets of transmembrane hairpin α-helices (TMH1 and TMH2, Fig. 3b) are poised to transition into a membrane-spanning β-sheet upon pore formation (Fig. 3c); APCβ domains are not visible and this is probably due to the lack of interaction with the MACPF domain, and/or the absence of a membrane support. The curvature of MACPF arcs and inter-subunit spacing and relative azimuthal positioning closely match that observed for membrane-inserted pores on liposomes (Suppl. Fig. 11a, b). Thus, the solution structure of these pre-pore-like oligomers represents an early intermediate of PvPLP2 oligomerization, which is likely further stabilized upon engagement with the target membrane.

A disulfide-bond locked pre-pore intermediate

To capture the pre-pore assembly on a membrane, we introduced specific disulfide bonds into the MACPF domain in the pre-pore-like conformation to prevent the transmembrane helices (TMHs) from converting into β-strands31,46, following the strategy used for perfringolysin O and pleurotolysin47,48. Importantly, these newly introduced cysteine residue pairs must contain suitable Cβ–Cβ distances and geometries which would satisfy the disulfide bond formation49. Yet, the interface among pre-pore subunits should be maintained to allow oligomer assembly. Specifically, residue V584C/T792C or K585C/K793C was engineered to lock TMH1 with the central β-sheet (Suppl. Fig. 10a), and residue K735C/E771C to lock TMH2 within the Helix-turn-helix (HTH) motif within the same protomer (Fig. 3c). Consistent with this design, the disulfide-bonded PvPLP2 mutants failed to exhibit efficient pore formation (oligomerization) upon incubation with liposomes, as evidenced by the lack of high-molecular-weight oligomers in SDS-PAGE analysis (Suppl. Fig. 10b–d). To verify whether these disulfide-locked mutants could trap a pre-pore intermediate on membranes, we performed cryo-ET and subtomogram averaging. The TMH2-locked K735C/E771C mutant assembled into arc-like structures on the liposome surface, similar in top-view to the mature pore (Fig. 3d, yellow arrowheads). However, side-view images provided unambiguous evidence of a pre-pore state: the underlying membrane remained fully intact, in stark contrast to the perforated membrane observed beneath the wild-type pore (Fig. 3d, e, compared with Fig. 1b). Despite the limited resolution, subtomogram averaging revealed a pre-pore intermediate state where the MACPF and APCβ domains were in a relatively similar position to the pore state (Fig. 3i, j; Suppl. Fig. 11c). More importantly, the reconstruction map exhibits a relatively thick, segmented MACPF density rather than the thin circular β-barrel in the mature pore conformation (Fig. 3f, g). This feature provides direct structural evidence that in the state that has been captured the TMHs remain folded and are not fully extended or inserted into the membrane to form a continuous barrel, prior to membrane perforation.

To better interpret the pre-pore reconstruction, we computationally modeled the transition by fitting a MACPF core domain (lacking TMHs) into the density map. By generating a series of intermediates that simulate the gradual opening of the central β-sheet by morphing, we identified a conformation that best fit into our map (Fig. 3j; Suppl. Fig. 11c). In this model, the central sheet is already splayed open to a state nearly identical to that of the mature pore. This result strongly suggests that the opening of the central β-sheet is a distinct step that can occur before the final deployment of the TMHs, particularly when the HTH-TMH2 rearrangement is physically constrained by the disulfide bond in the K735C/E771C mutant.

The minimal structural rearrangement between the pre-pore and pore states of PvPLP2 suggests a pore-forming mechanism similar to other MACPF proteins (for example, Pleurotolysin48) but distinct from perforin-2, which requires a dramatic 180° rotation of its MACPF domain for membrane insertion24,50. By contrast, PvPLP2 undergoes a relatively subtler conformational change (Suppl. Fig. 11). This small energetic barrier likely explains why the transient intermediate pre-pore could only be captured using a disulfide lock. We propose that this structural conservation has a critical mechanistic consequence: it enables sequential, incremental pore growth on target membrane. Because the major inter-subunit interfaces (both MACPF-MACPF and APCβ-APCβ) remain largely unchanged during the transition, new subunits can add onto a pre-existing, partially formed pore (Suppl. Fig. 11). Based on this structural framework, a nascent small arc-pore can serve as a template for the direct binding and conversion of subsequent monomers, allowing the arc-pore to grow to a near complete pore. Such a mechanism, where new subunits can seamlessly add to an already-inserted pore, would be sterically impossible for systems like perforin-2 that require large-scale domain rotations during insertion.

PvPLP2 forms functional pores on the inner leaflet of the erythrocyte membrane

Given that membrane lipid composition has effects on, and may dictate, pore-forming activity, we investigated the action of PvPLP2 on its physiological target, the erythrocyte. Surprisingly, despite its known role in erythrocyte rupture during gametocyte egress, our recombinant PvPLP2 failed to bind or lyse intact erythrocytes (Fig. 4a), a finding that contrasts with some previous reports16,51. Since PvPLP2 acts during gametocyte egress from within the erythrocyte, we hypothesized that it may only selectively target the cytoplasmic (inner) membrane leaflet from the erythrocyte interior52. Furthermore, the erythrocyte plasma membrane exhibits pronounced lipid asymmetry. Anionic phospholipids, such as phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylinositol (PI), along with phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), are almost exclusively sequestered in the inner (cytoplasmic) leaflet. Conversely, zwitterionic lipids, including phosphatidylcholine (PC) and sphingomyelin (SM), are predominantly exposed on the outer leaflet52. To test this, we transiently permeabilized the erythrocyte membranes with saponin to deliver PvPLP2 to the cell interior and assessed membrane association. Only after saponin treatment could PvPLP2 associate with the erythrocyte membrane, as revealed by confocal fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 4b, c), indicating PvPLP2 binding from the erythrocyte interior. To better visualize pore formation on the erythrocyte membrane, we performed cryo-ET directly with PvPLP2-bound erythrocytes. The erythrocyte was penetrated with perfringolysin O (PFO), a cholesterol-dependent cytolysin that forms 25–30 nm pores, to deliver recombinant PvPLP2 to access the inner membrane leaflet53. The resulting erythrocyte ghosts revealed PFO and PvPLP2 pores with distinct features (Fig. 4b, d–f). Subtomogram averaging and classification confirmed the architectures of both pore types (Fig. 4d, Suppl. Fig. 12). Additionally, PvPLP2 formed pores when incubated with lysed-resealed inside-out erythrocyte vesicles prepared via sonication (Suppl. Fig. 13). This was evidenced in some cases by its co-localization on the same vesicle membrane with flotillin - a naturally cytoplasmic membrane-localized protein54 (Suppl. Fig. 13f, magenta arrowhead). Taken together, these results demonstrate that PvPLP2 selectively forms pores on the inner membrane leaflet of erythrocytes, consistent with both the asymmetric distribution of negatively charged lipids across the erythrocyte membrane52 and its role in membrane disruption during gametocyte egress.

Based on these findings, we propose a model for PvPLP2-mediated erythrocyte rupture during gametocyte egress (Fig. 5). In gametocytes, PvPLP2 has been reported to localize in egress vesicles (EVs), which are discharged following the abundant secretory organelles, osmiophilic bodies secretion55. The released PvPLP2 is then trafficked into the host erythrocyte cytoplasm. There, soluble PvPLP2 monomers or small oligomers recognize and bind to the exposed, negatively charged lipids of the inner erythrocyte leaflet, forming an initial pre-pore assembly. This is followed by a subtle conformational change that converts the pre-pore to an inserted pore, a transition with a low energetic barrier. Since the intersubunit interfaces are structurally similar between the pre-pore and pore states, this nascent pore can then act as a template for the incremental growth of the ring. The final mixture of arc- and ring-shaped pores compromises membrane integrity, leading to erythrocyte lysis and the release of the mature gametocyte.

Discussion

A comprehensive understanding of how host membranes are ruptured during Plasmodium transmission is vital for identifying targets for malaria control. Our work provides direct structural evidence of pore formation by an apicomplexan PLP, demonstrating that PvPLP2 has a strong preference for the negatively charged lipids of the inner erythrocyte leaflet. This specificity, mediated by the generic charge complementation between APCβ domain and lipids (Fig. 2d, e), ensures stringent spatiotemporal control over pore formation. The strict spatiotemporal regulation of PvPLP2 during Plasmodium egress is likely further governed by compartmentalization and targeted delivery. Prior to discharge, PvPLP2 is sequestered within parasite secretory vesicles. This is analogous to the regulation of mammalian perforin, which is prevented from premature polymerization by calreticulin and specific calcium/pH levels in secretory granules. Such a mechanism allows the parasite to selectively disrupt the host cell membrane from within, while safeguarding its own secretory vesicles from accidental lysis, a critical feature for successful egress. During natural infection, parasites must traverse the parasitophorous vacuolar membrane (PVM) prior to targeting the erythrocyte membrane. Although PLP2 is dispensable for PVM rupture15, its potential contribution to this initial breach, along with its precise route of delivery to the erythrocyte cytoplasm, remains a critical area for future study.

In vitro, PvPLP2 forms soluble pre-pore-like oligomers at acidic pH, yet it perforates anionic membranes efficiently across pH 5.5–7.5, with only modest reduction at pH 5.5 (Suppl. Fig. 8a, b). We exploited this mildly acidic pH in vitro to stabilize soluble pre-pore-like oligomers in the absence of membranes, enabling high-resolution cryo-EM analysis of an otherwise transient assembly intermediate. The conformational transitions resolved here, such as the opening of MACPF β-sheet and the refolding of TMHs into transmembrane β-hairpins, mirror canonical MACPF/CDC mechanisms and are not intrinsically dependent on mild acidification. Determining whether PvPLP2 encounters locally acidified microenvironments in vivo, and how pH and lipid composition synergistically tune the kinetics of pore formation, remain important avenues for future research.

Unlike perforin-2, which forms stable pre-pore oligomers and undergoes a dramatic 180° domain rotation upon insertion20, PvPLP2 undergoes a much subtler pre-pore-to-pore transition, similar to MAC and perforin-119,21. To dissect this transition, we engineered a disulfide lock that trapped a key intermediate48, which revealed that the central MACPF β-sheet can splay open before the transmembrane hairpins (TMH1/TMH2) refold and insert (Fig. 3i, j; Suppl. Fig. 11c). This mechanistic decoupling, combined with the structural similarity between the pre-pore and pore interfaces, supports a model of sequential, incremental growth. In this model, new subunits can be added directly to an existing pore arc, a process sterically hindered in systems requiring large-scale rotations, or lesser but still significant conformational change. Guided by our pre-pore model in solution, future disulfide designs targeting TMH1 alone and both TMHs together should enable stepwise trapping of early, mid, and late intermediates, clarifying the order and coupling of latch movement, sheet rotation, and TMH refolding.

The differential prevalence of arcs in POPS and LPA membranes versus E. coli lipid extracts likely reflects lipid-dependent thermodynamic and kinetic effects on pore assembly. Mechanistically, the propensity to form arcs versus complete rings can be understood through the kinetic relationships between (i) initial oligomer formation (nucleation), (ii) membrane insertion, and (iii) subsequent subunit addition up to completion of a full ring of subunits (oligomeric growth). In pure POPS or LPA membranes, strong electrostatic interactions drive rapid PvPLP2 binding. If the subsequent nucleation and membrane insertion rates outpace oligomeric growth, available monomers would be rapidly depleted, yielding a high prevalence of kinetically trapped, incomplete arc pore assemblies. In this scenario, both the fluidity of the membrane and its permissiveness of membrane insertion would be critical factors in the balance of arc or ring assemblies. Conversely, E. coli total lipid extracts are compositionally complex and contain a lower overall density of anionic lipids. The bulkier lipid headgroups and increased membrane rigidity would likely create both a reduced level of membrane fluidity and a higher thermodynamic barrier for initial PvPLP2 insertion. Consequently, the initial membrane insertion rate – in particular – should be slowed (as membrane insertion only requires a minimally insertion-competent arc of subunits). However, once an initial membrane insertion event successfully occurs, cooperative subunit addition would drive the arc-to-ring transition ultimately to completion, making full ring pores the dominant observed species. Furthermore, post-insertion membrane mechanics and pore-edge energetics may play critical roles in stabilizing these structures. We consistently observe spatial clusters of PvPLP2 pores, suggesting a positive feedback mechanism wherein initial pore formation induces local membrane defects or alters local curvature and tension, lowering the energetic barrier for adjacent PvPLP2 insertions. Overall, rapid nucleation and localized clustering may together contribute to arc pore formation.

As the Plasmodium perforin-like protein is structurally defined both in vitro and in situ, PvPLP2 exhibits the conserved MACPF core and APCβ domain shared with Plasmodium PLP1 and PLP3–5, though its extended N-terminal domain remains unresolved, likely due to conformational flexibility30. The N-terminal region of PvPLP2 and its orthologues across different Plasmodium species are predicted to be largely disordered and do not form a distinct folded domain. This contrasts structurally with complement MACPF proteins (C6–C9), which possess well-defined N-terminal Thrombospondin type 1 (TSP1) and Low density Lipoprotein Receptor Type A (LDLRA) domains56,57 (Suppl. Fig. 6), and with perforin-1 and perforin-2, which lack any additional N-terminal region preceding the MACPF core19,24,43. Although the N-terminal domain is not required for PvPLP2 pore formation in vitro, it may play a role in membrane binding together with the C-terminal APCβ domain, as has been demonstrated previously for TgPLP158. Determining how other PLPs are regulated to form functional pores under their native conditions will be essential for understanding subfamily function across the Plasmodium genus and the broader apicomplexan lineage. Key future challenges will be to determine the pore-forming capabilities and membrane specificities of other PLPs, which act on distinct membranes in different life-cycle stages. Inhibiting PvPLP2’s ability to oligomerize or insert into the inner erythrocyte membrane could effectively trap gametocytes inside host cells, preventing parasite transmission. The recent success in developing inhibitors against other pore-forming proteins, such as the gasdermin family, validates this approach59. The detailed structural and mechanistic understanding of PvPLP2 presented here provides the essential foundation for structure-based drug design efforts aimed at creating a class of transmission-blocking antimalarials.

Protein expression and purification

The sequence of Plasmodium vivax perforin-like protein 2 (PvPLP2; PlasmoDB: PVX_123515) was codon optimized (by Thermo Fisher, GeneArt) for expression in mammalian HEK 293 T (ATCC, CRL-11268) or HEK293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A14635). The full-length recombinant protein (residues 25-1104) was purified as described previously30. Briefly, 1 liter of HEK293F cells was transfected with 1.2 mg PvPLP2 plasmids using 4 mg PEI MAX (Polysciences, 24765-1). Three days after transfection, cell medium was harvested by centrifugation at 7000 × g for 20 min, and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. The filtered medium was supplemented with 5 mM imidazole and loaded to a 5 ml HisTrap Excel column (Cytiva, 17-3712-06). Bound protein was eluted with an imidazole gradient (20–500 mM), and PvPLP2-containing fractions were identified by SDS–PAGE. The pooled fractions were buffer-exchanged and further purified by cation-exchange chromatography using a HiTrap SP HP column (Cytiva, 17-1152-01), followed by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column (Cytiva, 28-9909-44) equilibrated in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, and 150 mM NaCl. Purified PvPLP2 was concentrated to 1.75 mg/ml, then aliquoted, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and were stored at −80 °C until further use. The disulfide mutants and N-terminal truncation mutant (residues 460–1104) were purified in a similar way.

Thermal shift assay

Thermal shift assay (TSA) was performed on a Bio-Rad real-time PCR (qPCR) system. The final reaction volume was 50 µl containing 3 µg PvPLP2 and 3× SYPRO Orange dye (Life Technologies S-6650). The protein sample was distributed across buffers with a pH range of 4.0–9.5 in 0.5 unit increments, as well as in serial concentrations of NaCl (0 mM, 100 mM, 200 mM, and 500 mM). The fluorescence was recorded during a linear temperature ramp from 25 to 98 °C at 1 °C increments (74 steps). The melting temperature Tm (the midpoint of the unfolding transition) was calculated from the melting curve.

Sulforhodamine B liposome leakage assay

POPC (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine), POPS (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoserine) and POPE (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine) were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids Inc. Sulforhodamine B liposome leakage assays were conducted as described60. Briefly, a total of 1 mg dried lipids mixtures (40%POPC/30%POPE/30%POPS) was rehydrated with 0.5 ml of different solubilisation buffers, respectively (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5 or 50 mM citric pH 5.5 supplemented with 150 mM NaCl and 50 mM sulforhodamine B (Sigma-Aldrich, S1402-5G)). After extrusion, the unencapsulated sulforhodamine B dye was removed by two sequential buffer exchanges using a disposable PD 10 desalting column (Cytiva, 17085101). For the assay, the dye-encapsulated liposome was diluted 25 times. The fluorescence of sulforhodamine B was monitored using an excitation wavelength of 585 nm and an emission wavelength of 610 nm. The fluorescence before adding the proteins was used as Ft0, then 5 μl of protein was added to 45 μl liposomes, and the fluorescence was continuously recorded every 30 s for 60 minutes (Ftn). The addition of buffer served as a negative control for liposome stability over time, while the addition of 2.5% SDS (5 μl) to induce complete sulforhodamine B dye release provided the 100% leakage value as Ft100. The percentage of liposome leakage was calculated as: leakage (%) = (Ftn – Ft0) / (Ft100 – Ft0) × 100. Each leakage assay was performed at least three times.

Hemolysis assay

One milliliter of sheep whole blood (Ittabio, YT2633-3, 241203) was washed four times by centrifugation at 956 × g for 10 min each and resuspension in 10 ml of 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove plasma and the buffy coat. The isolated red blood cells (RBCs) were then resuspended in PBS to a final volume of 25 ml. For the assay, 250 µl aliquots of the RBC suspension were incubated with an equal volume (250 µl) of PBS (negative control), 2% Triton X-100 (positive control), PFO (16 µg/ml) or PvPLP2 (35 µg/ml), as well as a combination of both PFO and PvPLP2 proteins, respectively. Each test condition was performed with four replicates at room temperature. Following 30 min incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 min to remove cell debris, and 100 µl of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate for absorbance measurement at 545 nm using a Perkin Elmer Victor 3 microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Wellesley, MA, USA). The hemolysis percentage was calculated as: hemolysis (%) = (OD (test) − OD (negative control)) / (OD (positive control) − OD (negative control)) × 100. Data were plotted by GraphPad Prism 10.

Sample preparation of liposome-bound PvPLP2 for cryo-EM/ET

To prepare PvPLP2 oligomers on membranes, liposomes composed of 100% E. coli total lipid extract, 100% POPS (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoserine) and 40%POPC/30%POPE/30% Egg-PA (L-α-phosphatidic acid, LPA) (Avanti Polar Lipids Inc) were prepared at a concentration of 5 mg/ml in HBS (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl), respectively. In brief, a total of 2.5 mg lipids (dissolved in chloroform) was mixed and dried under a nitrogen stream and further desiccated under vacuum for 1 h. The lipid film was then rehydrated with 0.5 ml of HBS buffer, followed by vigorous vortexing and 3–5 freeze–thaw cycles. The resulting mixture was extruded 13 times through a 100 nm polycarbonate membrane (Whatman) using a mini-extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids Inc.). The liposomes were diluted to 2.5 mg/ml, and gently sonicated on ice at 10% amplitude (5 s total; 1 s on, 9 s off). The suspension was then centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove large aggregates, and the supernatant was collected and adjusted to 2 mg/ml in HBS for downstream experiments. To form PvPLP2-liposome complexes, PvPLP2 (final concentration of 0.65 mg/ml) was added to the freshly prepared liposomes (final concentration of 1.25 mg/ml) and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. PvPLP2 addition to liposomes induced visible aggregation, evidenced by turbidity; therefore, the liposome: PvPLP2 ratio was optimized each time to yield PvPLP2-bound liposomes without excessive aggregation on cryo-EM grids. The PvPLP2 NTD truncation and PvPLP2K735C/E771C mutant-bound E. coli liposomes were prepared in a similar manner.

For samples intended for cryo-ET, 6 nm colloidal gold fiducials (Aurion, 206.033) were added to the PvPLP2-liposome mixtures before plunge freezing. No gold fiducials were added for PvPLP2-E. coli liposome sample prepared for single particle cryo-EM. A 3.5 μl PvPLP2-bound or PvPLP2 mutant-bound liposome sample was applied onto Lacey carbon grids (AGS166-3, Agar Scientific) which were freshly glow-discharged for 45 s at 15 mA with PELCO easiGlowTM Glow Discharge Cleaning System. The grid was blotted for 3.5 s with a blot force of 0 in 100% humidity at 20 °C and plunged into liquid nitrogen-cooled ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cryo-ET data collection and subtomogram averaging of liposome-bound PvPLP2

For E. coli liposome-bound wild-type PvPLP2 and PvPLP2K735C/E771C mutant, tilt series were acquired using a Titan Krios G4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 300 kV, equipped with a Falcon 4i detector and a Selectris X energy filter (20 eV slit). Data were recorded at a nominal magnification of 81,000 × with a pixel size of 1.571 Å using SerialEM61 with the PACE-tomo script62, allowing up to 15 µm image-beam shift between target areas. For E. coli liposome-bound wild-type PvPLP2, 1267 tilt series were collected using a dose-symmetric scheme over a tilt range of either ±45° in 3° increments or ±40° in 4° increments, with an exposure dose of 4 e−/Å² per tilt image and a defocus range of −2 to −3 µm. For the PvPLP2K735C/E771C-mutant dataset, 322 tilt series were acquired over a range from −60° to 60° at 3° increments, with an exposure dose of 3 e−/Å² per image and a defocus between −3 and −4 μm. For LPA and POPS liposome-bound PvPLP2, tilt series were acquired on a Titan Krios G2 with a post GIF Gatan K3 detector with 20 eV slit width in CDS mode. For these samples, 127 (LPA) and 70 (POPS) tilt series were collected at a magnification of 64,000 × (pixel size: 1.34 Å) over ±60° in 3° steps, with an exposure dose of 1 e−/Å² per tilt image and a defocus between −3 to −6 µm. For E. coli liposome-bound PvPLP2 ΔNTD mutant sample, tilt series were collected on a Glacios 2 Cryo-TEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a Falcon 4 detector. A total of 15 tilt series were acquired at a nominal magnification of 73,000× (2 Å/pixel) over a ± 60° range in 2° increments, with a dose of 2 e−/Å2 per tilt movie and a defocus range of −3 to −4 μm. The detailed data collection parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table1.

The cryo-ET data processing and subtomogram averaging were conducted as previously63. For E. coli liposome-bound PvPLP2, the frames of each tilt series were motion-corrected using MotionCor264 implemented in RELION 4.065, and tilt images with abnormally low mean intensity (“dark” images) were discarded. The motion-corrected images were then assembled into tilt series and aligned by patch tracking in Etomo/IMOD (v.4.11.2466). For PvPLP2K735C/E771C datasets, motion correction, tilt-series alignment and tomogram reconstruction were all performed using AreTomo367. The resulting tilt series were imported into emClarity (v1.6.268) for CTF estimation and tomograms were reconstructed from 4× binned data and denoised with cryoCARE69 for visualization. For the E. coli liposome-bound PvPLP2 dataset, particle picking was performed via template-matching in emClarity, yielding an initial set of 334,526 subtomograms. These subtomograms were extracted, and particles were exported to RELION 4.0 for further processing. The initial 3D classification on 6× binned pseudosubtomograms identified arc-shaped and ring-shaped pore classes and further rounds of 3D classification and refinement assessed heterogeneity in pore completeness, corresponding to arc pore and ring pores. To improve the resolution, all the particles were combined, recentered with relion_star_handler to place the arc-shaped pore at the box center, and subjected to focused refinement, yielding a final map at 8.57 Å (summarized in Suppl. Fig. 3). The POPS liposome-bound PvPLP2 dataset was processed in a similar way. For the E. coli liposome-bound PvPLP2K735C/E771C dataset, particles were picked manually from denoised tomograms using the ArtiaX70 plugin in ChimeraX71. A total of 1496 particles were selected, with their z orientations predefined as perpendicular to the membrane. Their coordinates and orientations were exported to RELION 4.0 for 3D classification and refinement with 6× and 4× binned data, resulting in a final reconstruction at 28 Å resolution (summarized in Suppl. Fig. 9d, e).

Cryo-EM data acquisition and single particle analysis of PvPLP2 pore on liposome

A single-particle cryo-EM dataset for the E. coli liposome-bound PvPLP2 sample was collected on a 300 kV Titan Krios G4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a Falcon 4i detector at a magnification of 130,000× (pixel size: 0.9557 Å) and a total dose of 40 e−/Å2. In total, 27,526 movies were recorded in EER format with a target defocus range of −1 to −1.6 μm using serialEM. Due to the aggregation of PvPLP2-liposome samples, acquisition points were added manually from medium-magnification montages (6500 ×) and data were collected with image-beam shift limited within 10 µm. Single particle analysis (SPA) was performed using cryoSPARC (v4.2.0, v4.6.2, and v4.7.1)72. After motion correction, CTF estimation, particles were picked with the template picker from 25,201 manually curated micrographs. In the first round of 2D classification, top views and side views were separated for further rounds of classification. Particles displaying a 17-subunit complete ring in top views and clear side views were retained. These particles were subjected to a non-uniform (NU) refinement followed by several rounds of 3D classification and heterogeneous refinement with C17 symmetry. Classes with near-complete rings were selected for further rounds of NU refinement and yielded a 17-mer pore complex at 4.24 Å global resolution. However, the density map displayed pronounced distortion due to preferred orientation. To address this, particles were re-picked using Topaz73 to enhance the diversity of particle orientations, and after classification and orientation rebalancing to reduce the top-view particles, a new reconstruction was generated at 4.42 Å resolution with improved map quality. Further particle polishing and NU refinement improved the overall resolution to 3.86 Å (FSC = 0.143). Subsequently, C17 symmetry expansion was performed, and the particle center was shifted to sub-particles (a smaller subunit assembly) and re-extracted, followed by local CTF refinement and local refinement using a soft mask containing 6-mer or 4-mer subunits. After 3D classification, 899,494 particles from favourable classes were selected for polishing and further local refinement, yielding a pore complex map at an overall resolution of 3.64 Å (FSC = 0.143). Local resolution estimation for both the 17-mer and the final 4-mer maps was performed in RELION 4.0. The final 4-mer density map was further processed with EMReady74 to improve interpretability. The complete single-particle analysis pipeline is summarized in Suppl. Figs. 4, 5.

Cryo-EM single particle analysis of soluble-state PvPLP2 at pH 5.5 and pH 7.5

Cryo-EM grids of soluble PvPLP2 protein at pH 5.5 or pH 7.5 were prepared by applying 3 μl of 0.8 mg/ml protein onto a gold grid (UltrAuFoil® R 1.2/1.3 300 mesh), which was glow discharged for 75 s at 25 mA. After 2 s incubation, the grid was blotted for 3 s with a blot force of 3 at 4 °C and 100% humidity before plunge-freezing into liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data were collected on a Titan Krios G4 microscope at a magnification of 175,000× (0.7393 Å/pixel) with defocus values from −1 to −2 μm. A total of 11,279 and 2,908 movies were acquired using EPU with a total dose of 40 e−/Å2, respectively. Image processing was performed in CryoSPARC (v4.2.0, v4.6.2, and v4.7.1) and acquired movies were subjected to patch motion correction and CTF estimation. Micrographs were curated to exclude those with out-of-range average defocus (0.9–2.5 μm), CTF fit resolution above 7 Å, and relative ice thickness higher than 1.06, resulting in 9010 and 2351 high-quality micrographs, respectively. Initially, particles were picked using blob picking followed by 2D classification. In the pH 5.5 dataset, multiple oligomeric particle classes (classes 1–4) were identified, whereas no oligomeric classes were observed in the pH 7.5 dataset. Subsequent processing therefore focused on the pH 5.5 dataset. Particles from this dataset were re-picked by iterative template-based particle picking using selected 2D class averages as templates. After two additional rounds of 2D classification, a total of 1,372,245 particles binned at 2× were selected for heterogeneous refinement with C1 symmetry. Particles displaying clear three-subunit PvPLP2 features were selected and re-extracted using a box size of 189 Å. To address preferred orientation issues, the Cryo-EM Image Evaluation Foundation (Cryo-IEF)75 was employed to rank the top 50,000 particles based on quality. Subsequent non-uniform (NU) refinement, local CTF refinement, and polishing yielded a reconstruction with a global resolution of 3.27 Å (FSC = 0.143, cFAR=0.01). Subsequently, an ab initio reconstruction was performed using only high spatial frequency information76, resulting in a 3-mer PvPLP2 MACPF domain map at an overall resolution of 3.31 Å (FSC = 0.143, cFAR 0.22) after NU refinement. The resulting map was further enhanced with EMReady74 to improve interpretability.

Confocal microscopy

Red blood cells (RBCs) were prepared by washing four times in HBS buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl) as described above. To assess membrane binding, 4 × 106 RBCs were incubated with 17 μg of recombinant His-tagged PvPLP2 for 30 min at room temperature, either in the presence or absence of 0.01% (w/v) saponin to permeabilize membranes. To minimize nonspecific binding, 1% (w/v) BSA was supplemented in all buffers throughout the procedure. Following the incubation, cells were washed with HBS and then incubated with a primary anti-His antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA1-21315, ZH397571, dilution 1:500) in HBS containing 0.01% saponin for 20 min at room temperature. After washing, cells were labelled with donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-21202, 2563848, dilution 1:1,000) for 30 min at room temperature. Prior to imaging, the buffer was exchanged with fresh HBS without saponin to minimize ongoing membrane permeabilization. Fluorescence images were subsequently acquired using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope.

Visualization of PvPLP2 pore on erythrocyte by cryo-ET

Red blood cells isolated from a 250 μl fresh mouse whole blood sample were diluted in PBS to a final volume of 500 μl. To prepare the RBC ghosts, 1 μl of 3.2 mg/ml PFO protein was added to 250 μl of the RBC suspension and incubated for 5 min at room temperature (RT) to induce cell lysis. The resulting RBC ghosts were pelleted by centrifugation (15,294 × g 10 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in 1 ml PBS. This wash step was repeated three times to remove hemoglobin. The final RBC ghosts were resuspended in 20 μl PBS and incubated with 3.5 μl of PvPLP2 protein (final concentration of 0.26 mg/ml) for 30 min at RT. The samples were then plunge frozen on freshly glow-discharged lacey carbon grids (AGS166-3, Agar Scientific), using a Leica EM GP2 automatic plunge-freezer (Leica). A 3.5 μl of the mixed sample was applied to the carbon side of the grids and 3 μl of PBS was added to the backside of the grids. The grids were back-blotted for 7–8 s at 30 °C with 95% humidity and immediately plunge frozen in liquid ethane. The tilt series were collected on a Titan Krios G4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a Falcon 4i detector and a Selectris X energy filter (20 eV slit). A total of 427 tilt series were acquired at a nominal magnification of 53,000× (2.417 Å/pixel) over a ± 51° or ±48° range in 3° increments, with a dose of 3 e-/Å2 per tilt movie and a defocus range of −3 to −5 μm.

Raw EER movies were motion-corrected in RELION 4.0, and the identified dark tilt images (abnormal low mean intensity) were removed. Motion-corrected images were then assembled into individual tilt series and aligned with AreTomo277. Tomograms were reconstructed from 4× binned data and denoised using cryoCARE with default parameters. The cryo-ET subtomogram averaging was conducted as WT PvPLP2-bound E. coli liposome data. CTF estimation and particle picking were performed via template-matching in emClarity (v1.6.268), using pneumolysin (EMD-4118) and PvPLP2 map (reconstructed from our subtomogram averaging) as the template, respectively. A total of 37,900 PvPLP2 and 68,016 PFO subtomograms were extracted and exported to RELION 4.0 for further processing. Several rounds of 3D classification using 6× and 4× binned data resolved distinct arc-shaped and ring-shaped pore classes for both proteins. The selected classes were subsequently refined at bin2, yielding reconstructions at ~30 Å resolution for both datasets (Suppl. Fig. 12).

To visualize the three-dimensional distribution of PFO and PvPLP2 pores on the erythrocyte membrane, the denoised tomograms were first processed in Amira (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The side-view profile of the red blood cell membrane was segmented using intensity-based thresholding combined with manual refinement. Due to the missing-wedge effect, the top view of the membrane is not resolved in the tomogram. As both PFO and PvPLP2 are known to associate with the membrane surface, a planar top-view membrane model was manually reconstructed in Amira for reference purposes, guided by the spatial distribution of the erythrocyte membrane protein densities. Segmented side- and top-view membrane models were combined and visualized alongside the denoised tomogram in ChimeraX using the ArtiaX plugin. To determine the positions and orientations of PFO and PvPLP2 pores on the erythrocyte ghost, the coordinates and orientations of all candidate particles from the above-described template matching were then mapped back onto the denoised tomograms for manual curation using ChimeraX via ArtiaX. During this step, false positives were discarded, and the positions and orientations of true-positive particles were refined to accurately match the local density.

Visualization of PvPLP2 pore on erythrocyte vesicles by cryo-ET

Erythrocyte vesicles were prepared as described previously54 with minor modifications. Sheep whole blood (20 ml; Ittabio, YT2633-3, 260205) was washed in ice-cold PBS containing protease inhibitor cocktail (P8340; 1:1000) by centrifugation at 2000 × g. Cells were lysed in 30 ml hemolysis buffer (1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 5 mM NaCl, nuclease (ACROBiosystems, BEE-N3116-10KU), protease inhibitor cocktail) and centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 10 min to obtain erythrocyte “ghosts”, which were washed four times with hemolysis buffer and resuspended in 30 ml GPMV buffer (10 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 140 mM NaCl, 10 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl₂). Following probe sonication, vesicles were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 180,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C in a P32ST rotor over a 4 ml 12% (w/v) sucrose cushion. The pellet was gently resuspended in 200 μl GPMV buffer, briefly sonicated in a water bath, clarified by low-speed centrifugation, and applied to the top of a step sucrose gradient prepared as described previously54. Gradients were centrifuged at 180,000 × g for 14 h at 4 °C in a TH-641 rotor. The 35–45% sucrose fraction was collected, and vesicles were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 180,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C in the same rotor and finally resuspended in 20 μl HBS buffer.

To prepare the sample for cryo-ET, 2 μl erythrocyte vesicles were incubated with 5 μl of PvPLP2 protein (final concentration of 1.27 mg/ml) for 30 min at RT. The samples were then plunge frozen on freshly glow-discharged lacey carbon grids (AGS166-3, Agar Scientific), using a Leica EM GP2 automatic plunge-freezer (Leica). A 3.5 μl of the mixed sample was applied to the carbon side of the grids and incubated for 10 s. Next, 1.5 μl of PBS was added to the backside of the grids, followed by back-blotting for 5 s at 10 °C with 100% humidity, and the grids were immediately plunge-frozen in liquid ethane. The tilt series were collected on a Glacios 2 Cryo-TEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a Falcon 4 detector. A total of 63 tilt series were acquired at a nominal magnification of 73,000× (2 Å/pixel) over a ± 60° range in 2° increments, with a dose of 2 e−/Å2 per tilt movie and a defocus range of −3 to −4 μm. Motion correction, tilt-series alignment and tomogram reconstruction were all performed using AreTomo367 using local patch alignment. All 4× binned tomograms were denoised with CryoCARE69.

Model building and validation

The PvPLP2 pre-pore MACPF domain (residues 460–832) and APCβ domain (residues 833 to 1104) structures were predicted with AlphaFold 378. The pre-pore MACPF domain was docked into the cryo-EM map of soluble-state PvPLP2 in ChimeraX, and intermediate MACPF conformations (excluding TMHs) were generated by morphing in ChimeraX. The best-fitting intermediate (with highest correlation), combined with the predicted APCβ domain, was fitted into the cryo-ET map of liposome-bound PvPLP2K735C/E771C. For the membrane-inserted PvPLP2 pore model, the 4-mer consensus-pore map at an overall resolution of 3.64 Å was used. The predicted APCβ domain structure was manually fitted into the density map and adjusted using ISOLDE79 implemented in ChimeraX. The pre-pore MACPF domain minus TMH1 and 2 was first fitted into the equivalent region of the pore map, and the transmembrane β-hairpins were manually rebuilt using Coot80 followed by real-space refinement in Phenix81. The resulting 3-mer model (comprising the APCβ domain and pore-forming MACPF domain) was subject to further inspection and refinement in Coot and Phenix81. The complete protomer was then manually fitted into the C17 ring map in ChimeraX. The final model was validated using Phenix real-space refine and wwPDB Validation Service; the refinement parameters and model statistics are summarized in Suppl. Table 1.
