Anatomical structure of pulmonary vessels and bronchi in the right upper lobe based on three-dimensional reconstruction
Authors: Wu Yan, MengXuan Wang, YongSheng Li, LiJuan Xu, JiXing Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to evaluate the anatomical patterns of the right upper lobe pulmonary segments through three dimensional reconstruction and to describe the frequency of observed variant types.MethodsThree-dimensional reconstruction of images of the pulmonary structures was performed in 67 patients with ground-glass pulmonary nodules who were due for segmentectomy in our hospital, from August 2021 to June 2025.ResultsIncidentally, the reconstructed models were used for the statistical analysis of the anatomical structures in the right upper lobe. In terms of results, the most common anatomical variant of the bronchus right upper lobe was trifurcate (51/67). This was followed by bifurcate (16/67). Bifurcation was the most common configuration (36/67) in the right upper lobe pulmonary artery followed by trifurcation (16/67) and singled branching (8/67). The most frequent configuration of the right upper lobe pulmonary veins was anterior vein + central vein (42/67), followed by anterior vein + posterior vein (7/67) and isolated central vein (7/67). Furthermore, a set of 12 rare variations were also observed, which includes 4 bronchial cases, 2 pulmonary artery cases, and 7 pulmonary vein variations cases.ConclusionThe lung has complex anatomical structures with various variations. The surgeon must have a proper knowledge of the segmental anatomy of the area of the operation. The study offers a descriptive reference of anatomical variations that may assist in preoperative planning and intraoperative orientation for right upper lobe segmentectomy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-03953-6.Keywords: Right upper lung, Segmentectomy, Three-dimensional reconstruction, Pulmonary vascular anatomy

Introduction

Lung cancer is still one of the most common cancers and causes of cancer deaths in China and the world [1, 2]. While treatment choices for lung cancer have expanded significantly over time, surgical resection remains the principal approach [3, 4]. The use of CT scans on a large scale and better public awareness about preserving one’s health have significantly increased the diagnosis of early lung cancer which is often ground-glass nodules, compared to earlier years [5]. As a result, thoracic surgery widely performs anatomic segmentectomy as a surgical procedure. This main purpose of technique is highly anatomical identification, requiring accurate bronchial as well as blood vessels division to the level of segmental and preferably also sub-segmental levels. Thoracic surgeons face considerable challenges due to the configuration of the structures at this level. Pulmonary segment anatomy and variations have not been much studied historically due to the high cost of available anatomical specimens. As a result, pulmonary segments have had increasing difficulty supporting growing individualized and refined surgical demands. In addition, the technical difficulty and complexity for thoracoscopic segmentectomy are significantly higher than that for thoracoscopic lobectomy since variations in the pulmonary vessels and bronchi complicate the thoracoscopic technique further.

Right upper lobe of lung is the most common site for lung cancer Anatomical variations of the region are rather common and complex making thoracoscopic right upper lobe segmentectomy much more complicated. Anatomical variations of the right upper pulmonary vessels and bronchi are important for planning and undertaking thoracoscopic right upper segmentectomy. Consequently, the present study used software Mimics 21.0, three-dimensional reconstruction software, for performing preoperative three-dimensional reconstructions of the chest-enhanced CT data in 67 patients with right upper pulmonary nodules in our centre. This study condenses the anatomic peculiarity discovered in the right upper pulmonary arteries, veins and bronchi of these 67 cases. While three-dimensional reconstruction is increasingly used in clinical practice, systematic classification of anatomical variations—especially rare types—remains limited in the literature. This study provides a structured description of such variations in the right upper lobe, which may serve as a reference for preoperative planning, intraoperative orientation, and surgical education.

Materials and methods

Since August 2021, patients with pulmonary nodules in our department have been screened according to specific inclusion criteria. For those scheduled to undergo anatomical segmentectomy, routine preoperative chest enhanced CT scans were performed. The scanning equipment utilized was a 256-slice spiral CT, and three-dimensional reconstructions were carried out using Mimics 21.0 software based on the CT images. By June 2025, a total of 67 cases had been reconstructed. Data on patient characteristics (including sex, age, location and size of lesion and perioperative complications and reconstruction) was recorded, and anatomical structures of the right upper lobe analysed statistically.

The desirables are as follows: (1) Primary lesion positioned in right upper lobe and measuring at least 6 mm. (2) The proposed segmental resection margin must be at least 2 cm from the tumor or equal to tumor diameter. (3) CT scans must show no evidence of hilar or mediastinal lymph node metastasis. (4) Major organ functions, including heart, lung, liver and renal, must be sufficient to tolerate the surgery. The exclusion criteria were: (1) preoperative 3D reconstruction shows that the location of the lesion is in the central area of the lobe, and the operator considers it inappropriate for segmental resection; (2) distant organ metastasis; (3) previous right upper lobe lung surgery and radiotherapy; (4) the patient refuses to carry out segmental resection; (5) history of pulmonary tuberculosis; (6) other malignant tumors.

Results

The data reconstructed of patients with pulmonary nodules consisted of 67 cases. Of these, 29 were male and 38 were female aged between 24-year-old and 77-year-old (mean age, 53.51 ± 11.38-years). The lesions in diameter from of 3 to 20 mm (mean diameter: 9.77 ± 4.20 mm). Out of all the lesions studied, it was determined that 28 cases (41.79%) involved the apical segment of the right upper lobe. Furthermore, 18 cases (26.87%) involved the posterior segment of the right upper lobe. Lastly, 21 cases (31.34%) involved the anterior segment of the right upper lobe. All of the 67 patients had successful surgery. 9 of the cases developed complications post-surgery. All the complications were pulmonary infections. They were resolved after symptomatic treatment. Atelectasis, pulmonary air leakage, incision infections and other perioperative complications were not seen. With regards to the bronchial branching pattern, 63 cases (94.03%) of the primary and secondary branched normal. Four cases of variations were noted. In one case (1.50%), the apical and anterior segmental bronchi of the right upper lobe originated separately from the main bronchus, resulting in a trifurcation at the carina (Fig. 1). In another instance (1.50%), the bronchus of the anterior segment right upper lobe was given rise from the bronchus of the right middle lobe (Fig. 1). Moreover, there were two cases (2.98%) of the right upper lobe bronchus showing two main branches (Fig. 2).

According to the number of branches from the right upper lobe bronchus, we classified them into bifurcation type and trifurcation type (Fig. 3). The most common type of trifurcation was present in 76.12% (51 cases out of 67). Out of these, 43 had the following types of branching: type B1 apical segmental bronchus; type B2 posterior segmental bronchus; and type B3 anterior segmental bronchus. Furthermore, there were three instances with a branching pattern whereby the B1a (apical subsegmental bronchus), B2 (posterior segmental bronchus), B1b (posterior subsegmental bronchus of the apical segment) and B3 (anterior segmental bronchus) arose from a single trunk. Of the 67 cases treated, 23.88% (16 cases) were of the bifurcated type. The specimens in this category mostly had B1a and B1b originating from B2 and B3 (13 cases) respectively.Furthermore, there were two cases with a common trunk of the apical anterior segmental bronchus (B1 + B3) and posterior segmental bronchus (B2), and one case where the posterior subsegmental bronchus of the posterior segment (B2a) and the anterior subsegmental bronchus of the posterior segment (B2b) arose separately from the apical segmental bronchus (B1) and anterior segmental bronchus (B3).

The branching patterns of the pulmonary arteries exhibit distinct characteristics at the segmental level. Specifically, while the branches of the pulmonary arteries run alongside the bronchial branches, their main trunks do not accompany each other, leading to significant differences in their branching patterns. According to established nomenclature, the portion of the right superior pulmonary artery that arises anterior to the right superior lobar bronchus is termed the anterior trunk. This trunk typically consists of a single branch, although it may occasionally have two branches, designated as the superior anterior trunk and the inferior anterior trunk, respectively. After the anterior trunk divides into two branches, the pulmonary artery continues downwards as an interlobar artery which gives branches off to the right superior lobe. A posterior ascending branch is defined as one that goes posteriorly and superiorly to supply the posterior segment. Conversely, if it runs anteriorly and superiorly to supply either the anterior segment or the apical segment, it is referred to as the anterior ascending branch.

Based on the occurrence of the aforementioned branching patterns, six types were identified in this study: (1) trunchous anterior, (2) trunchous anterior + posterior ascending branch, (3) trunchous anterior+ anterior ascending branch, (4) truncus arteriosus anterior superior+trunous inferior anterior artery, (5) trunchous anterior+ anterior ascending + posterior ascending branches, and (6) truncus arteriosus anterior superior+trunous inferior anterior artery+posterior ascending. The specific statistical data are shown in Table 1. Among them, 7 cases (10.45%, 7/67) exhibited significant variations: 2 cases had the lateral subsegment of the posterior segmental artery (A2b) originating from the right inferior pulmonary artery, 1 case had the posterior subsegment of the posterior segmental artery (A2a) originating from the right inferior pulmonary artery, 3 cases had the anterior segmental artery (A3) or its branches originating from the right middle pulmonary artery, and 1 case had the lateral subsegment of the anterior segmental artery (A3a) originating from the right inferior pulmonary artery and the medial subsegment of the anterior segmental artery (A3b) originating from the right middle artery (Fig. 4).

The pulmonary venous drainage pattern exhibits a unique branching system, with the pulmonary veins running between segments or subsegments independently of the pulmonary arteries or bronchi. According to previous studies, the venous trunk that courses anterior to the right upper lobe bronchus is referred to as the anterior vein. The vein that traverses between the posterior and anterior segments of the right upper lobe is designated the central vein, while the vein that runs posterior to the right upper lobe bronchus is identified as the posterior vein.

In this study, all 67 cases drained into the right atrium. Six drainage patterns were observed in the right superior pulmonary vein (Fig. 6): (1) anterior vein + central vein (62.69%, 42/67); (2) anterior vein + central vein + posterior vein (5.97%, 4/67); (3) central vein alone (10.45%, 7/67); (4) anterior vein alone (8.96%, 6/67); (5) anterior vein + posterior vein (10.45%, 7/67); and (6) anterior vein + posterior vein + inferior vein (1.49%, 1/67). Most of these venous branches drained into the right superior pulmonary vein, except for one special drainage pattern of the posterior vein: in one case (1.49%), the posterior vein of the right superior lung coursed through the posterior hilum and drained directly into the atrium (Fig. 5).

Among the 51 cases of trifurcated tracheal types, the arterial distribution pattern of anterior trunk + posterior ascending branch was the most common, observed in 21 cases (41.18%, 21/51), followed by anterior trunk + anterior ascending + posterior ascending branch in 9 cases (17.65%, 9/51) and superior anterior trunk + inferior anterior trunk in 8 cases (15.69%, 8/51). However, among the 16 cases of bifurcated tracheal types, the predominant patterns were anterior trunk in 6 cases (37.5%, 6/16) and anterior trunk + posterior ascending branch in 5 cases (31.25%, 5/16).

Among the 51 cases of trifurcated tracheal type, the predominant venous drainage pattern was anterior vein + central vein, observed in 32 cases (62.75%, 32/51). Similarly, among the 16 cases of bifurcated tracheal type, the main venous drainage pattern was also anterior vein + central vein, seen in 10 cases (62.5%, 10/16).

Discussion

The British Thoracic Society defined the international nomenclature of pulmonary segments used today in 1950 [6]. Nonetheless, the use of this terminology in current clinical practice does not accurately match the pulmonary anatomy of individual patients. This is because it only pick out the most prevalent and characteristic types of branching present in the population, omitting other kinds & variations. Consequently, it does not provide accurate guidance for the execution of segmentectomy. Unlike the lobectomy where surgical handling occurs on relatively fixed anatomical structures, the surgeon needs to have high-level skills for managing the complex anatomical variations that can occur within the pulmonary segments- most commonly the right upper lobe. A systematic three-dimensional reconstruction analysis that includes CT images of 67 patients undergoing right upper lobectomy was carried out to thoroughly elaborate on the anatomical variation of the bronchi, arteries and veins of the right upper lung. The findings provide important references to safe and accurate anatomical segmentectomy.

The studies show high variability in anatomical structure of right upper lung. At the bronchial level, 94% of patients had normal lobar and segmental branching, although anomalies were rare, including the formation of a common trunk for the apical and anterior segmental bronchi, the independent origin of the apical segmental bronchus from the right main bronchus, and anterior segmental bronchus arising from the right middle lobe bronchus (incidence rate: 5.97%). This is in the same line as observed in Nagashima et al. (2015). In a larger-sample 3D CT study, they noted that even if the predominant pattern is the trifurcation pattern of the right upper lobe bronchus, bifurcation and other complex variations accounted for more than 30% of the cases and diverse variation patterns were observed [7]. The ‘target segment’ bronchial structures to be identified and tackled during surgery are determined directly by these variations. If these variations are not recognized in advance of the operation, they may easily result in incorrectly transecting or leaving behind residual tissue.

The pulmonary artery variations are pretty complex that mostly cause significant danger. As a classic pattern of anterior trunk + posterior ascending branch were only 35.82%. So does that mean that there are other patterns? There does seem to be. Patterns including separate superior and inferior anterior trunks, independent origins of anterior ascending branches. It is important to note that about 10.5% of cases demonstrated “aberrant” arteries. These are branches originating from the interlobar artery or from the right inferior pulmonary artery. They could either be posterior or anterior segmental branches. According to Shimizu et al., these aberrant arteries or additional arteries that arise in a location other than their normal origin pose the greatest risk of bleeding during segmentectomy. The hidden path of aberrant arteries makes it vulnerable to inadvertent damage while dissecting the intersegmental plane [8]. Therefore, in order to prevent disastrous intraoperative bleeding, preoperative three-dimensional reconstruction clarifying the origin, path, and relationship of each target segmental artery with the structures around is important.

The importance of the anatomical study of pulmonary veins is also paramount, as they are important landmarks to define intersegmental planes and margins of resection. In this study, we elaborate six patterns of venous drainage, among which, the “anterior vein + central vein” was the most common one, accounting for 62.69%. A clinically significant finding is rare cases (exceeding 1.49%) of posterior veins draining into the atrium. If this abnormal drainage is not properly identified and is accidentally cut during surgery it can lead to serious complications like hemorrhage of the atrial wall or air embolism. According to Oizumi et al. segmental pulmonary veins are not parallel to the arteries or bronchi and are normally road signs for intersegmental fissures. These veins should be clearly identified anatomically for correct division of the intersegmental planes to prevent injuring adjacent segmental venous drainage and limiting the risk of post-operative pulmonary edema or infarction [9].

Surgeons have relied on so-called textbook anatomy and intraoperative exploration to deal with anatomical variations for long. However, complex variations can be difficult and dangerous to manage under the narrow two-dimensional view offered by thoracoscopy. Three-dimensional construction technology, using enhanced CT Mimics, is suitable for this study and addresses the challenges outlined above. Through this technology, it is possible to have a personalized three-dimensional (3D) image created from a regular two-dimensional (2D) image. Users can rotate and view these 3D images from various angles. It also displays the tree-like structures formed by bronchi and blood vessels, the dimensions between them, and differences. The clinical value of this technology can be understood at several levels. First of all, they facilitate “surgical rehearsal”. The surgeons can repeatedly study the models pre-operatively to plan the most appropriate anatomical sequence, transection sites of the vessels, and the possible “pitfalls”, making the surgery “precise and goal-directed operations” as opposed to “exploratory discovery”. Secondly, they increase determination of “intersegmental planes”. The artistically designed three-dimensional models help create an intuitive three-dimensional understanding of the course of target segment veins; when used in conjunction with Indocyanine green fluorescence staining technology, they help achieve more accurate identification and division of intersegmental planes. Lastly, for educational purposes, customized 3D models are very useful to help train young surgeons understand anatomical variants along with enhancing spatial reasoning. Numerous studies have shown the substantial application value of three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction in pulmonary segmentectomy. As illustrated by Chen-Yoshikawa et al. research, preoperative 3D reconstruction can reduce operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and conversion rate to thoracotomy. This is even more pronounced on more complex lung segments like the right upper lobe [10].

The findings are not intended to replace individualized 3D reconstruction but to complement it by providing a systematic catalogue of possible variants

There are limitations to this study. First, The sample size of 67 cases is relatively small. Therefore, the incidence rates reported may not fully represent rarer variants in the general population. Multicenter, large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate and extend our findings. Furthermore, this research is a single-center retrospective imaging study. Future investigations should consist of multicenter, large-sample prospective studies incorporating 3D reconstruction models and intraoperative navigation. In addition, long-term follow-up on patients’ oncological outcomes and quality of life is need for higher-level evidence.It should be emphasized that improved anatomical knowledge does not directly equate to reduced complications; future prospective studies are needed to link presurgical identification of variations with clinical outcomes.
