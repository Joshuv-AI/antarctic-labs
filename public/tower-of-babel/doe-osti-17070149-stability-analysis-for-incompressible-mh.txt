Summary
We verify the magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) solver in Nek5000/RS, which is based on the spectral
element method, against linear stability analysis. We consider MHD flow in a square duct with
electrically conducting walls of finite thickness, with dimensionless wall thicknesses tw /a from 10−2
to 10−1 , Hartmann numbers Ha = 30–500, and Reynolds numbers Re = 103 –104 . Linear stability
analysis of the laminar base flow predicts the growth rate of small perturbations, and we test whether
nonlinear Nek5000/RS simulations reproduce it. The growth rates computed by Nek5000/RS agree
with the linear stability predictions. We also find that, for a fixed wall conductance ratio, the flow
becomes more unstable as the wall thickness increases.

 ii

1 Background
1.1    MHD in Nek5000/RS

The main aim of this study is to provide a verification benchmark for the magnetohydrodynamics
(MHD) capabilities of Nek5000/RS [1, 2]. In our previous works [3, 4], Nek5000/RS was shown
to converge exponentially to exact steady base-flow solutions of laminar MHD duct flow as the
polynomial order is increased. The natural next step is to verify its accuracy for unsteady flow.
In this study, we use stability analysis of the MHD base flow to predict the growth rate of small
perturbations, and we test whether the nonlinear Nek5000/RS simulations reproduce this growth
rate.

1.2 Square duct MHD flow

A series of linear stablity analysis has been done for square duct MHD flow [5, 6, 7]. We are
primarily concerned with the case of finite conducting wall. For the case of finite conducting wall,
previous study assumed a thin wall boundary condition. This enforce a surface Laplacian condition
on the fluid boundary, which avoids having to solve the equation inside the solid domain. However,
this approximation could deviate from actual experiments or simulation that actually have a solid
domain, for example, in this paper where we run a NekRS simulation that compute the solid domain
and do not utilize a surface Laplacian boundary condition.

4.2 Finite conducting wall

We sample the parameter space to roughly map out the marginal Reynolds curve. We started out
with a coarse grid, and start refining closer to the marginal curve by marching along the curve.
These samples are shown in Figure 3 and Figure 4.
 Note that there are a few cases denoted here that should have been unstable instead of stable.
This is due to the Arnoldi iteration not finding an unstable mode. With an appropriate shift,
however, this problem will be mitigated as a good shift will help Arnoldi converge to the unstable
mode extremely fast. During the coarse sampling, shift is chosen to be arbitrarily σ = 0.05, which
is not very optimal. We use a better strategy while marching along the marginal curve, which set
the shift to be equal to the unstable eigenvalue closest to the current location.
 The Arnoldi iteration seems to struggle obtaining accurate result for thinner wall thickness.
We weren’t able to obtain good result for W = 0.001, as the residual in eigenvalue solve is not
within tolerance, as well as instability behavior is not consistent in the parameter space. As the
wall get thinner, the jump in conductivity at the wall increases, which makes the system more
ill-conditioned.
 Using these sampled points, we can map out the marginal curve which is shown in Figure 5.
The marginal curve seems consistent with known literature [7]. We see that as wall thickness
increases, the the marginal Reynolds number lowers.

4.3 Nek5000/RS Verification

The inductionless MHD modeling in NekRS is covered in a companion report [4].
 We picked out cases which has only one unstable eigenmode and use that as an initial condition
in Nek simulation. The results, shown as error in simulation’s growth rate vs growth rate predicted
by linear stability analysis, are plotted in Figure 6 and Figure 7.
 The results show that for Ha = 15, the error in growth rate can lower to 0.1%, and improve at
higher polynomial order. For higher Ha, the error worsen, however it can still maintain under 1%
error at Ha = 100.
 The high error is not indicative of the accuracy of neither Nek or stability analysis. It merely
shows the difference between linear and nonlinear equation. Regardless, we have shown that Nek
can achieve accuracy that can capture the growth rate of small perturbations down to 1%.

5 Conclusion
We have verified the MHD solver in Nek5000/RS, which is based on the spectral element method,
against linear stability analysis of MHD flow in a square duct with electrically conducting walls of
finite thickness. The study covered dimensionless wall thicknesses tw /a from 10−2 to 10−1 , Hartmann
numbers Ha = 30–500, and Reynolds numbers Re = 103 –104 . For each configuration, linear stability
analysis of the laminar base flow was used to predict the growth rate of small perturbations, and
nonlinear Nek5000/RS simulations, initialized with the base flow and a small perturbation, were
compared against this prediction.
 Across the parameter range considered, the growth rates computed by Nek5000/RS agreed
with the linear stability predictions. This agreement extends earlier verification, which established
exponential convergence to exact steady solutions, to unsteady flow. It shows that the solver
accurately captures the time-dependent coupling between the flow, the electric current in the

 11

fluid, and the current in the conducting walls, which is the regime relevant to liquid-metal blanket
applications.
 The results also reveal a physical trend: for a fixed wall conductance ratio, the flow becomes
more unstable as the wall thickness increases. Since the wall conductance ratio combines the wall’s
thickness and conductivity, this indicates that thin-wall models, which characterize the wall by the
conductance ratio alone, may not fully capture the stability of flows with thick conducting walls.
 The present comparison is restricted to the linear growth phase of small perturbations in a
square duct. Future work will extend the verification to the nonlinear saturation of the instabilities,
to higher Hartmann numbers and other duct geometries, and to additional wall configurations or
multiphysics coupling. Together with the exact steady solutions, these cases will form a benchmark
suite for verifying MHD solvers across both steady and unsteady regimes.
