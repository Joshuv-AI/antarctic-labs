Toward precision immunotherapy in recurrent/metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma: lessons from the FOCUS trial
Authors: Christoph Schultheiss, Tobias Andermatt, Anna Brandt, Mascha Binder
Published: 2026

We thank Flores and colleagues for their thoughtful commentary on the randomized phase 2 FOCUS trial evaluating the human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT)-derived peptide vaccine UV1 in combination with pembrolizumab in recurrent/metastatic (r/m) programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1)-positive head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) (1). We broadly agree with the key conclusions and interpretations raised by the commentators and appreciate the opportunity for constructive academic discussion.

FOCUS was designed to address a major unmet need in r/m HNSCC. Although programmed death-1 (PD-1) blockade has improved outcomes in a subset of patients, most derive no durable benefit, and many are not candidates for chemotherapy intensification because of comorbidities or impaired functional status. In the trial, 75 patients were randomized to pembrolizumab alone (n=25) or pembrolizumab plus UV1 (n=50). The study did not meet its prespecified primary endpoint: the 6-month progression-free survival (PFS) rate was 42% with pembrolizumab alone and 31% with the combination, with no meaningful differences in median PFS or overall survival between arms (2). UV1 was generally safe and feasible, with similar adverse-event rates across groups. Consistent with the vaccine’s mechanism of action, hTERT-specific T-cell responses were detected in the peripheral blood of the majority of patients.

We agree with the commentators that higher vaccine doses could, in principle, enhance immunogenicity and potentially improve clinical outcomes. However, the reference to the 700 µg dose should be interpreted in the context of the small phase I dose-finding study in non-small cell lung cancer from which this observation originates (3). That study included only six patients per cohort and was not designed to establish a definitive dose-response relationship. Importantly, subsequent clinical development of UV1 has not moved toward the highest tested dose. Instead, subsequent studies combining UV1 with immune checkpoint inhibitors have employed doses in the range of 300–400 µg together with granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), based on a comparative analysis of three UV1 trials demonstrating that this dose range was associated with the highest capacity to elicit durable hTERT-specific T-cell responses (4).

We also welcome the commentators’ focus on immune mechanisms and agree that peripheral immunogenicity alone does not guarantee effective tumor control. UV1 was developed based on the concept that vaccine-primed CD4+ Th1 cells can coordinate antitumor immunity through several complementary mechanisms. These include licensing antigen-presenting cells via CD40-CD40L interactions, providing helper cytokines such as interleukin-2 (IL-2), sustaining and diversifying tumor-reactive CD8+ T-cell responses, and shaping the tumor microenvironment (4). In addition, hTERT-specific vaccination is known to also induce cytotoxic CD4+ T cell responses (4). Such multifunctional CD4+ T-cell responses may therefore contribute both helper and direct effector activity within the antitumor immune response. More broadly, CD4-driven immune programs are increasingly recognized as important determinants of responsiveness to checkpoint blockade, including through CD4-myeloid cell interaction networks that influence intratumoral immune states. In this context, we agree that future vaccine trials in HNSCC should integrate tumor tissue analyses such as spatial immune profiling, assessment of T cell infiltration and positioning, and evaluation of exhaustion marker patterns alongside peripheral immune-monitoring to better connect vaccine-induced responses with intratumoral biology and clinical outcomes—if possible. It is important to acknowledge that the acquisition of post-treatment tumor samples in the r/m HNSCC setting is often not feasible for ethical and logistical reasons, as additional tissue collection may be difficult to justify in the absence of a routine diagnostic indication. The introduction of perioperative pembrolizumab in HNSCC may help to overcome this limitation, as both pre-treatment (diagnostic biopsy) and post-treatment (surgical resection) specimens can become available within standard clinical workflows (5).

Taken together, it is difficult to attribute the outcome of the FOCUS trial solely to vaccine dose or the magnitude of peripheral immune responses. Biological heterogeneity within r/m HNSCC is likely to be a major contributing factor. Differences between tumor subsites, particularly HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancers versus HPV-negative disease, as well as variability in antigen presentation, T cell infiltration, and stromal exclusion, may substantially influence responsiveness to immunotherapy. Such heterogeneity can obscure subgroup-restricted signals in phase 2 trials. In this context, we agree with the commentators that PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS), while clinically used to guide pembrolizumab treatment, represents an incomplete biomarker of tumor-immune dynamics. Also in the FOCUS cohort, quantitative PD-L1 levels in the tumor or tumor microenvironment were not associated with treatment benefit, and CPS alone did not explain the observed variability in outcomes.

To better understand determinants of response to pembrolizumab, we subsequently performed translational analyses in the combined FOCUS cohort, assessing systemic cytokine profiles, while analyses of the (pre-treatment) tumor microenvironment are ongoing (6). These studies identified a consistent high-risk immune phenotype characterized by systemic inflammation and limited adaptive immune diversity. Elevated plasma interleukin-6 (IL-6), increased levels of soluble TIM-3 (sTIM-3) and CD25 (sCD25), as well as a more oligoclonal peripheral T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire, were associated with poor clinical outcomes on pembrolizumab. Notably, several of these associations were more pronounced after pembrolizumab induction than at baseline, suggesting that early on-treatment immune dynamics may provide particularly informative biomarkers of therapeutic response.

These findings provide potential avenues for future trial design in r/m HNSCC. Patients lacking high-risk inflammatory or immune-dysregulation features may represent a biomarker-enriched subgroup likely to derive benefit from pembrolizumab monotherapy—an important consideration for frail patients. Conversely, patients characterized by elevated IL-6, markers of checkpoint-molecule shedding such as sTIM-3, and restricted TCR repertoire diversity may require rational escalation strategies targeting inflammatory signaling pathways or additional inhibitory checkpoints. IL-6-JAK/STAT signaling has been implicated in tumor progression and T cell dysfunction, and experimental data support a role for IL-6 signaling in resistance to PD-(L)1 blockade, strengthening the biological plausibility of these observations (7).

Together, these findings emphasize the importance of deeper biological characterization of patients receiving immune checkpoint blockade in r/m HNSCC. The identification of inflammatory and adaptive immune features associated with poor outcomes highlights the potential value of integrating systemic and tumor-derived biomarkers into future trial designs. Such approaches may enable improved patient stratification and help guide rational combination strategies aimed at overcoming resistance to immunotherapy.
