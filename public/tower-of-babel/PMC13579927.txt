Beyond microprocessor knees: exploring the potential of fully powered prosthetic legs
Authors: Kyle R. Embry, Chandrasekaran Jayaraman, Chen Yang, Shenan Hoppe-Ludwig, Matt McGuire, Jonathan Mendley, Chaithanya K. Mummidisetty, Brian E. Lawson, Kiana Mohammadian, Jasmine Hunt, Apurva Reddy, Michael Goldfarb, Keith E. Gordon, Arun Jayaraman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPowered knee and ankle prostheses generate mechanical energy to replicate biological leg function, yet direct comparisons with more commonly prescribed microprocessor-controlled knee prostheses are limited by methodological variability and small sample sizes.MethodsTwelve adults with transfemoral or knee disarticulation limb loss or difference who regularly used microprocessor knees were enrolled in a crossover study. After device fitting and an average of 10 training sessions with a powered knee and ankle prototype, participants completed a comprehensive battery of biomechanical, physiological, and functional assessments. Tests were repeated using each participant’s prescribed prosthesis following a two-month washout period to reduce carryover effects. Paired statistical comparisons were used to evaluate differences across key outcome measures.ResultsParticipants walked slightly slower and exhibited marginally reduced swing time symmetry when using the powered prosthesis. However, there were no significant differences in metabolic energy cost between devices, despite the significant increase in weight of the powered prosthesis. This may suggest that the powered leg provided sufficient assistance to compensate for its added weight, but did not yield improvements in walking speed, gait symmetry, or functional endurance.ConclusionsUse of this powered knee and ankle prosthesis resulted in comparable physiological effort and postural control to microprocessor knees, but did not improve outcomes like walking speed, symmetry, or endurance. Continued development of comparative testing protocols for prototype componentry could represents a critical step in optimizing PKA design and validation for improved clinical outcomes.Trial registration This study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT03204513; Date: 06/27/2017).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12984-026-02042-2.

Introduction

Despite recent advancements in medical and artificial limb technologies, people with limb loss or difference (PLL) at the transfemoral level continue to face many challenges. Compared to able-bodied populations, transfemoral prosthesis users have slower gait speed [1], asymmetrical gait that can cause degenerative musculoskeletal complications [2], and chronic back pain that can significantly affect their daily activities [3]. Furthermore, increased energy expenditure while walking [4] limits social activities and overall quality of life. Together, these challenges present severe limitations to mobility. Even compared to transtibial (below knee) PLL, transfemoral prosthesis users have a much lower likelihood of achieving community and functional household mobility [5]. Limited mobility and fear of falling can also lead to decreased participation in community and social activities [6], which is associated with additional comorbidities and other health concerns [7].

Microprocessor knees (MPKs) are one option for people with a transfemoral limb loss, as they replicate many functions of the human leg by using sensors to detect the phases of gait and appropriately adjust their stiffness and damping. MPKs show several marked improvements compared to mechanical prosthetic legs, including an increase in self-selected walking speed [8], decrease in energy expenditure [9], reduction in stumbles and falls [10], and improvements in quality-of-life factors [11]. However, MPKs are not capable of generating net positive mechanical work, which is an important function of the human leg during everyday activities.

In contrast to commonly used passive componentry, including both microprocessor controlled and mechanical components, powered knee-ankle (PKA) prostheses can exert net-positive mechanical work and may improve mobility and outcomes of transfemoral prosthesis users. To date, many different PKA prototypes have been developed, and each provides power at the knee and ankle to replicate the biomechanical functions of the leg, though they vary significantly in terms of mechanical design and control architecture [12–19]. The ability of a PKA to provide net positive work has significant potential to improve mobility and decrease compensations during many tasks. While PKA’s provide significant improvements in terms of power delivery, that advantage often comes at the cost of weight and control complexity. PKA prototypes tend to have more distal mass than a MPK, and adding distal mass to an unpowered prosthesis is associated with slower and less symmetrical gait that results in increased metabolic cost to the user [20, 21]. Similarly, while the ability to provide powered assistance provides significantly more options for control, it also increases the complexity of the human-machine interaction. Consequently, PKA prototypes introduce challenges for comparative clinical testing and complicate the personalization and tuning processes for individual users. Despite these hurdles, PKAs represent a paradigm shift in prosthetic technology, though achieving consistent clinical impact requires addressing methodological challenges related to these developmental devices. For example, recent PKA and MPK studies have demonstrated varied approaches to acclimation and training protocols. Some studies have used minimal supervised training, such as 30–60 min of in-clinic acclimation [22, 23] before allowing users to take devices home or evaluating functional outcomes after only a single training session [24]. These approaches, while demonstrating the feasibility of shorter acclimation periods, often lack standardized training protocols and rely on subjective assessments of user readiness (e.g., user-reported comfort and prosthetist satisfaction) rather than objective performance metrics [25]. Additionally, studies evaluating PKA prototypes have shown promise in achieving more normative joint kinematics with untrained users [26] yet continue to emphasize the need for expert-driven, device-specific parameter tuning for each user and test condition, substantially increasing preparation time and limiting scalability [13]. Building on these recent studies that highlight gaps in acclimation protocols and resource-intensive tuning requirements, we evaluate our PKA prototype using a standardized training and testing methodology with functional benchmarks.

In this study, we present the results of a randomized crossover comparison of PKA and MPK devices with 12 participants. Materials and methods

Study overview

Twelve participants with a transfemoral or knee disarticulation amputation were assessed across a wide range of clinical, biomechanical, and user-centered outcome measures. All participants in this study were everyday users of an MPK and were a K3 or K4 functional classification. All tests in the predicate prosthetic devices will be referred to collectively as the “MPK condition,” while tests in the PKA prototype are referred to as the “PKA condition.”

These participants were extensively trained to become proficient with the non-commercially available PKA prototype device, the Vanderbilt Gen3 Powered Leg [26], before outcomes testing (see Fig. 1). Our hypothesis was that the power generated in the knee and ankle of this prototype device would lead to better gait outcomes than the predicate MPKs, despite the difference in mass, as demonstrated in our prior case series [27]. Participants’ prosthesis weight increased by an average of 3.1 kg while using the PKA, compared to the MPK condition, including the total weight of the prostheses and all componentry, see Supplementary Table S1 “Prosthetic Componentry” for details.

During both testing conditions (using an MPK or PKA), participants underwent a series of standardized assessments that can be grouped into four categories: (1) walking speed (2), symmetry (3), endurance, and (4) user preference. The order of testing conditions (seven participants starting with MPK, five participants with PKA) and outcome assessments were randomized with the two sets of assessments separated by a two-month-minimum washout period to mitigate carryover effects. Performance metrics from these assessments were compared to evaluate the PKA’s impact on gait quality relative to each participant’s MPK condition. This comparison enabled a direct evaluation of functional tradeoffs between the PKA prototype and MPK prostheses by reflecting real-world conditions, minimizing confounding effects, and systematically assessing functional performance alongside user satisfaction metrics. The PKA’s impact on gait quality, user experience, and practical functionality was isolated by anchoring each comparison to the participant’s predicate device performance. Participants

The participant cohort (10 males, 2 females) had a mean age of 44.4 years (σ = 12.5), average height of 1.79 m (σ = 0.09), and average weight of 84.3 kg (σ = 12.8) with their own prosthesis. All participants were transfemoral PLLs with no prior experience using PKAs for their daily use prostheses, mitigating potential bias from prior device-specific adaptations. Details of the MPK components are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Detailed demographics and amputation history are provided in Supplementary Table S3. This study was approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board (STU00200842), Chicago, IL.

PKA controller description

The PKA prototype uses a hierarchical state-machine controller [26]. The top-level controller, called the supervisory controller, chooses between the modes the prosthetic leg can perform (in this study, modes included Sitting, Standing, and Stepping). There are knee and ankle angle thresholds required to switch from one mode to another, and the threshold values are tunable to match the user. In addition to the mode switching parameters, the controller also specifies a cadence ratio, which dictates the duration of the next swing phase, given a measurement of the previous stance phase. This parameter was used to encourage the swing phase to keep pace with the gait of the user.

Within each mode, the prosthesis uses impedance-based control, emulating a spring/damper system with parameters appropriate for each mode and phase of the gait cycle. Prosthetic device evaluation, fitting and adjustments

Each participant, already experienced with their MPK device, underwent a preliminary check by a Certified Prosthetist to ensure optimal fit and comfort with his or her predicate MPK. Any necessary adjustments were made to avoid discomfort during walking.

The PKA device required individualized fitting for each participant. Participants’ own sockets were evaluated for compatibility for use with the PKA by a certified Prosthetist; if the socket could be used safely, aligned appropriately, and was comfortable, it was utilized in the study (n = 2). For those that were not suitable (n = 10), the participant’s socket was duplicated and adjusted to allow proper use with the PKA. Predicate socket shape was largely maintained in an effort to minimize differences. Modifications included reduced volume for improved suspension, differing distal socket connections/alignment, and adding of ischial containment to a clinical subischial socket. Additionally, to ensure secure suspension, some participants used auxiliary suspension (TES belt) (n = 6). All participants using elevated vacuum in their own prostheses were switched to suction, as the PKA did not allow for additional componentry to be utilized. Participants used shoe lifts in order to equalize leg length when the prototype componentry did not allow for the adjustability needed. Fittings were typical to a clinical prosthetic appointment and included evaluation, socket fittings, as well as static and dynamic alignment. Because this study occurred over the course of months, participants may have had volume fluctuations that required use of varying suspension methods across time, including change of sock ply/gel underliner or auxiliary TES belts. Prosthetic set up during testing is outlined in Table S1. The primary aim was to differentiate between prosthetic knee and ankle componentry; non conformity of the socket allowed for optimal safety, comfort and function of distal componentry for the user.

Parameter tuning, device acclimation and training

Following fitting, the PKA’s control parameters (including stiffness, damping, equilibrium positions, and state switching rules) were tuned for each subject in a manner similar to [26]. This process, aimed at achieving a natural and comfortable gait, involved optimizing various parameters, such as the ankle dorsiflexion angle that would initiate a step, torque force at the ankle, and prosthetic alignment during stance. Participants engaged in multiple walking trials at varying speeds to ensure the device behaved as expected. This was determined by the experience of the study clinicians and engineers, as well as participant feedback. The final tuning parameters for each participant are detailed in Supplementary Table S4 “Controller Parameters.”

Device training

Participants underwent comprehensive training sessions with study clinicians (both a CPO and a PT) for both devices regardless of randomized order. Training was similar to clinical practice of fitting a new prosthesis, only of longer duration, and included an engineer for assistance as necessary when training was performed with the PKA. To mitigate potential carryover effects from prior MPK use and establish a standardized baseline for PKA performance, training ensured familiarity with assessment procedures and consistency across both devices. Each participant completed an average of 10 sessions, with each session lasting approximately 2 h, over a period of 2–3 months, focusing on biomechanical improvement, compensation reduction, and mastery of practical tasks like donning/doffing, walking on different surfaces, and sit-to-stand transitions. The training program was tailored to each user’s ability and included indoor walking, outdoor walking, and treadmill sessions. The PKA prosthesis was used exclusively in controlled laboratory, or adjacent environments, and was not permitted for unsupervised or at-home use. Consequently, PKA acclimation was limited to supervised sessions and was shorter than users’ real-world exposure to their standard MPK, representing a potential confounding factor that may underestimate PKA performance relative to devices used in daily life. However, the structured training duration provided in this study remained longer and more intensive than acclimation periods reported in several recent prosthetic evaluation studies [23–25], supporting that adequate familiarization time was provided prior to testing despite the lack of home use. Only upon meeting a series of competency functional benchmarks, as evaluated by our CPO and PTs (see Supplementary Document S1 “Study Training Objectives”), were participants deemed ready for device assessments. Competency was confirmed when participants were able to safely and independently perform each functional task over at least three sessions, two of which were consecutive. After each device outcome measure assessment session, each participant underwent a washout period of a minimum of two months. No device training took place during the washout periods.

Back pain surveys

The Quebec Back Pain Disability Scale (QBPDS) served as a tool to identify pre-existing back pain and monitor any changes in pain levels throughout the washout period. The Quebec Back Pain Disability Scale shows high test-retest reliability and is recommended as an outcome measure for clinical trials [28].

Outcome measures

Our comprehensive gait assessments spanned a wide range of aspects that are crucial for prosthetic leg usability. From this group of assessments, there were four types of outcome measures: Speed, Symmetry, Endurance, and Personal Preference (Fig. 2). Testing order blocks were randomized across participants to mitigate sequence effects. Protocols for each assessment are available in Appendix A. When available, interpretation was based on minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs), which represent validated, population-specific thresholds for meaningful functional change. When MCIDs were not available, we used minimal detectable change (MDC) values derived from population-specific reliability data to ensure that observed differences exceeded measurement error. Outcomes were also interpreted using statistical testing (t-tests and linear mixed-effects models) to identify significance differences, which have not been formally validated for clinical interpretability in this population.

Speed

Walking speed is one of the most important measures of functional gait in any population [29]. Our assessments resulted in four measures of walking speed (10MWT-FV, 10MWT-SSV, TOP, TC) that varied in terms of required distance, intensity (self-selected or fastest safe velocity), and terrain.

Symmetry

Unilateral limb loss creates asymmetric gait compensations to accommodate for asymmetry in anatomy [30–32]. These gait asymmetries can lead to overuse injuries in the intact leg and chronic lower back pain [33, 34]. Therefore, we analyzed the PKA’s impact on spatiotemporal symmetry.

Spatiotemporal symmetry

Bilateral swing time and step length were measured during the 10 MWT at both SSV and FV, using a GAITRite pressure sensitive mat (GAITRite Gold, CIR Systems, West Conshokocken, PA, USA). Endurance

Walking endurance is an important metric to determine the functional capacity for gait, and potential for community mobility. Our assessments measured multiple aspects of walking endurance. Firstly, we conducted a 6MWT, which tests how far a participant can travel within six minutes. We also conducted an increasing speed treadmill test (ISTT), in which endurance was tested by assessing how long a participant could keep walking on a treadmill that gains speed at regular intervals. Please see Appendix A for full protocol. During both the 6MWT and ISTT, participants wore a COSMED K4b2 oxygen sensor (COSMED Srl, Rome, Italy) to measure O2 consumption. For each test, we calculated the normalized O2 consumption (called O2 cost), in units of mL of O2 per kg/m for every minute of the test.

Metabolic data during the ISTT were collected over two-minute intervals at each speed. This approach is supported by prior work demonstrating that O₂ cost derived from short-duration treadmill bouts is highly correlated with steady-state measures obtained over longer durations, while substantially reducing participant burden in populations with limb loss [35]. Accordingly, two-minute sampling intervals were considered sufficient to provide estimates of metabolic cost across conditions.

Personal preference

User preference for a prosthetic device is a key factor for establishing long-term use [36]. To measure user preference, we conducted the Quebec User Evaluation of Assistive Technology survey (QUEST). This 12-item survey is designed to determine a participant’s degree of satisfaction with an assistive device and has shown satisfactory performance in terms of test-retest stability, alternate-form equivalence, and internal consistency [37].

Statistical analysis

After recording all the outcome measures above for both the MPK and PKA conditions, we analyzed the results for normality using Shapiro-Wilk tests. Since all outcomes were normally distributed, we proceeded to compare the effects of prosthesis type (MPK vs. PKA) on the outcomes. Paired t-tests (two-tailed) or linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were performed for all outcomes, and the statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. LMMs were used for outcomes to account for the repeated-measures crossover design and to adjust for potential demographic and clinical confounders. The results of each outcome indicate which test was used. Results

Quebec Back Pain Disability Scale (QBPDS)

Our findings revealed that the majority of participants reported minimal disability due to back pain. Specifically, only Participants 2, 3, 7, and 11 exhibited QBPDS scores exceeding 14 at any point during the study, suggesting they experienced some degree of back pain disability. Furthermore, statistical analysis using a paired t-test revealed no significant difference in back pain ratings between the use of MPK and PKA devices (t = 1.883, p = 0.084), as detailed in the Supplemental Table S2, “Quebec Back Pain Disability Scale Overall Scores”, which presents scores during the assessment day for each device.

Speed

Four assessments were conducted that measure walking speed in a laboratory or practical environment. These tests included the 10-meter walk test (10MWT) at a self-selected velocity (SSV) and fastest safe velocity (FV), the Timed Outside Pavement test (TOP) and Timed Crosswalk test (TC).

After accounting for multiple comparisons, all assessments exhibited statistically significant differences between PKA and MPK except for the 10MWT-SSV (LMM: p = 0.089) (Fig. 3). Participants walked faster with MPK compared to PKA in TOP (0.17 m/s faster; LMM: p < 0.00001), TC (0.25 m/s faster; LMM: p < 0.00001), and 10MWT-FV (0.19 m/s faster; LMM: p < 0.00001). Note that recent studies have suggested 0.21 m/s as the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for self-selected PLL walking speed [38]. Only the self-selected walking speed trial, the 10MWT-SSV (0.10 m/s faster with MPK, LMM: p = 0.089), did not exceed this threshold. All others were conducted at a fastest safe speed, for which there is no established MCID in the PLL population. See Table 1 for regression coefficients and Table S5a for all measurements.

Further analysis on 10MWT - FV, via a paired t-test, showed significant differences between devices in cadence (t = 7.256, p < 0.001). The PKA condition significantly lowered cadence compared to the MPK (113.51 ± 5.35 vs. 125.21 ± 9.33). Interestingly, the PKA and MPK had nearly the same prosthetic-side step length (MPK: 85.17 ± 6.01 cm vs. PKA: 86.62 ± 7.20 cm, t = -1.112, p = 0.29). However, the intact limb when using the PKA had a significantly shorter step length compared to MPK (MPK: 89.50 ± 8.66 cm vs. PKA: 83.84 ± 7.03 cm, t = 2.928, p = 0.014). Analysis on step time showed that both the prosthetic (MPK: 0.51 ± 0.42 s vs. PKA: 0.58 ± 0.29 s, t = -9.338, p < 0.001) and intact limb (MPK: 0.46 ± 0.04 s vs. PKA: 0.48 ± 0.03 s, t = -2.464, p = 0.031) had increased step time with PKA, which led to the decreased cadence.

Symmetry

Spatiotemporal symmetry

Step length and swing time were analyzed for gait symmetry, at both SSV and FV. After accounting for multiple comparisons, both swing time symmetry at SSV and FV showed a significant difference (LMM: p = 0.009 for both conditions) between MPK and PKA (Fig. 4), with PKA performing worse. (See Table 2 for regression coefficients and Table S5b for all measurements.) There has been no MCID of spatiotemporal symmetry established for this population. Participants exhibited a more symmetrical swing time when walking at SSV using MPK (0.85 ± 0.04) compared to PKA (0.80 ± 0.08). A further comparison of the swing time showed that there was no difference for the intact limb (MPK: 0.39 ± 0.04s vs. PKA: 0.39 ± 0.04s), while there was in the prosthetic limb (MPK: 0.46 ± 0.03s vs. PKA: 0.49 ± 0.04s).

Endurance

6MWT distance travelled

To analyze the effect of each device on walking endurance, we analyzed the distance travelled in six minutes using both devices. As shown in Fig. 5, there was a statistically significant difference (LMM: p = 0.0003) between PKA and MPK in the 6MWT total distance. The participants walked 58.33 m further using MPK than PKA. However, this difference did not exceed the minimal detectable change (MDC) for PLLs on the 6MWT, 147.5 m [39].

Max speed on the treadmill

On average, the participants reached a max speed of 1.36 ± 0.22 m/s using PKA, while the max speed using MPK was 1.41 ± 0.32 m/s. There was no significant difference between the max speed using these two devices (LMM: p = 0.432) (Fig. 5).

Metabolic efficiency

The net O2 consumption showed no significant differences between MPK and PKA (LMM: p = 0.378) for the 6MWT (Fig. 6). Participants showed similar metabolic efficiency using both devices in the 6MWT (MPK: 0.16 ± 0.03 mL/kg/m vs. PKA: 0.16 ± 0.02 mL/kg*m). There is no MCID for 6MWT O2 cost in this population.

The O2 cost using both devices were also compared during the ISTT. To analyze this data, we only analyzed O2 cost at the speeds that all participants were able to perform: 0.39, 0.61, and 0.81 m/s, and followed best practices for metabolic measurements during short walking bouts [40, 41]. The net O2 cost was high at 0.39 m/s, and decreased somewhat as participants increased in speed. This may represent that 0.39 m/s was below our participants’ comfortable (and efficient) walking speeds. There was no significant difference between MPK and PKA in O2 cost at any of the ISTT speeds that all participants could complete: 0.39 m/s (LMM: p = 0.767), 0.61 m/s (LMM: p = 0.321), or 0.81 m/s (0.496) (Fig. 6). The participants showed similar metabolic efficiency at the 0.81 m/s speed (MPK: 0.17 ± 0.05 mL/kg/m vs. PKA: 0.17 ± 0.04 mL/kg/m) using these two devices on the treadmill. Note that the long duration between outcome measurement days in the two device conditions may have reduced the interclass correlation between metabolic cost measurements [42, 43].

Personal preference

The Quebec User Evaluation with Assistive Technology (QUEST) survey was used to assess the participants’ satisfaction with MPK and PKA, with a score of one indicating “not satisfied” and five indicating “very satisfied.” On average, the PKA scored lower in the QUEST (PKA: 3.55 ± 0.78 vs. MPK: 4.49 ± 0.51), see Fig. 7. Discussion

Using a standardized crossover framework with extensive training and washout, this study offers a robust evaluation of a powered knee–ankle prosthesis relative to commercially available microprocessor knees. While the powered device successfully maintained metabolic efficiency despite increased mass, it did not confer clinically meaningful improvements in walking speed, symmetry, or endurance during level-ground ambulation. Together, these results emphasize the need for rigorous, clinically grounded testing paradigms to compare prostheses with fundamentally different functionality.

Overall, the MPK provided statistically significant increases in maximum walking speed and walking distance during the 6MWT, but neither SSV walking speed or 6MWT distance exceeded the relevant MCID/MCD for those tests, indicating no clinically meaningful differences. No significant differences were observed across almost all other metrics. These results are interesting in comparison to prior work investigating the effect of increasing the mass of a transfemoral prosthesis [21]. This prior work found that increased distal mass on a prosthesis has a statistically significant reduction in walking speed, decreases symmetry, and increases metabolic cost of walking. Our results agreed that the heavier PKA prototype provided lower walking speeds, and lower symmetry (but only in the case of swing time symmetry), but metabolic differences were insignificant. This may suggest that the PKA was able to deliver sufficient power during gait to maintain energetic efficiency, even in the presence of weight constraints, an important insight for future prosthetics research and development.

To encourage a fair comparison between the MPK and PKA devices, participants underwent one of the most thorough PKA training protocols in prosthetics research to date. After an average of 10 training sessions, our participants demonstrated proficiency using the PKA device independently for tasks directly aligned with the study’s outcome measures. Proficiency was systematically evaluated using a competency checklist administered across multiple days. However, users were unable to bring the PKA device home, and still had significantly more hours of use with the MPKs. Familiarity and acclimation in a prosthetic leg is a necessary prerequisite to utilizing the device to its fullest, an important factor that is often overlooked in methodologies used to evaluate and compare prototype devices [23–25, 44].

Speed

Walking speed significantly decreased when using the PKA for all fastest, safe speed trials. Linear mixed-effects model results confirmed that no demographic or clinical covariates showed significant effects on walking speed outcomes. However, there were no statistically or clinically significant differences in self-selected walking speed. Self-selected gait speed is a much more common clinical metric than fastest safe walking speed, and in this metric, both devices performed about equally.

Symmetry

The symmetry outcomes were nearly equivalent between both device types. The swing time symmetry was the only significant difference, wherein the MPK provided a greater degree of symmetry for both self-selected and fastest walking conditions. In this case, the MPK and PKA had nearly identical intact leg swing times, but the PKA had longer prosthetic leg swing time, which decreased the symmetry value. These results are very consistent with a general decrease in speed while using the PKA during fastest safe walking speeds.

Endurance

Our results showed that participants in MPKs walked farther in the 6MWT than when they used the PKA, but this difference was only statistically significant, not clinically. However, as the oxygen cost did not increase between device conditions (even after accounting for differences in speed), we believe that this decrease in distance was mostly limited by the reduced maximum walking speed in the PKA, not because of increased metabolic effort on behalf of the participants. The results of the increasing speed treadmill test were similar: the MPK-condition was able to reach higher walking speeds but used the equivalent amounts of oxygen at each speed. Linear mixed-effects model results showed that height and etiology had statistically significant impacts on O2 cost with taller participants consuming less oxygen during 6MWT and the participant (n = 1) with a congenital amputation required more O2 during the ISTT. These differences reflect baseline variation in biomechanical abilities that cannot be attributed to the devices used in this study, but should be considered within the population.

Personal preference

Overall, participants preferred using the MPK devices to the PKA. This was somewhat expected, as the MPK is their everyday-use device and are very familiar to them, while the PKA was never used in their home environment. Most validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROM) for PLL are for devices that users are able to take home and use in their own environment to develop an opinion about the device’s usefulness. In this study and many evaluating prototypes, the experiences are more controlled and narrow. However, user perceptions are still important to understanding what features are most and least liked about a device in order to direct future development. As such, scores for certain domains of the QUEST, such as Ease in Adjusting, Durability, and Ease of Use were likely to be biased towards the MPK. All questions on the QUEST received better scores for the MPK than PKA on average. Paired t-test comparisons conducted for each QUEST subcategory demonstrated statistically significant differences favoring the MPK device for Dimensions, Weight, Adjusting, and Safe/Secure, as well as for the overall QUEST score, whereas differences in Durability, Ease of Use, Comfort, and Effectiveness did not reach statistical significance. The three aspects of the PKA that caused the greatest decrease in QUEST score were the Weight, Dimensions, and Adjusting. This clearly demonstrates that participants were dissatisfied with the mass and size of the PKA, as well as the somewhat complicated process to adjust and tune the PKA to their individual needs. The aspect that decreased in score the least was Comfort, which aligns with our observations that once the device was adjusted to an individual, they generally felt secure enough with wearing the device to help obtain a natural gait with it.

Guidelines for future work

The following guidelines, tailored to researchers, clinicians, as well as those involved in design and development (e.g., engineers and prosthetists), might inform future studies and improve comparisons of conventional prostheses and powered prosthetic prototypes.

For engineers and prosthetists, it is essential to prioritize individualized adjustment of wearable components as these modifications optimize weight distribution, joint biomechanics, and user comfort. Researchers and clinicians should implement standardized training protocols and functional benchmarking prior to device testing to ensure participant proficiency and minimize adaptation-related variability. Outcome measures must be conscientiously selected to assess overall functional performance as well as quality of life, using validated instruments. Validated measures for PLL are not always available and much work needs to be done in this aspect, including defining subpopulations such as transfemoral limb loss or high functioning PPL. For engineers and prosthetists engaged in design and development, recent research has emphasized the integration of easily configurable control systems that allow for precise, user-specific adjustment of control parameters to accommodate user needs and real-world conditions [45–47]. Notably, newer powered prostheses than the PKA used in this study, such as those incorporating advanced algorithms, lower weight, reduced inertia, and self-adjusting joints, are demonstrating substantial improvements in mobility, comfort, and adaptability, as seen in next-generation devices like the Utah Bionic Leg [22] While these advancements are expected to yield superior performance compared to earlier models, it is essential that they undergo rigorous, standardized testing methodologies to ensure that functional outcomes and quality of life measures are validated across diverse user populations. Additionally, when evaluating device performance under variable speed conditions, it is important to acknowledge the inherent limitations of treadmill-based testing in replicating the complexity and variability of real-world ambulation. Methods that better capture functional performance in diverse environments should be implemented.

Limitations

Despite structured acclimation and training, participants’ exposure to the PKA prosthesis remained limited to supervised, task-specific sessions and did not reflect typical daily use. In contrast, participants continued to use their prescribed MPKs in real-world settings throughout the study, including during the washout period. This imbalance in real-world exposure may have limited the effectiveness of the washout in reducing carryover effects and may have biased outcomes in favor of the MPK condition.

Additionally, participants could not be blinded to device type due to the inherent physical and functional differences between prostheses, which may have influenced user perceptions and subjective outcomes. Finally, all participants were high-functioning (K3–K4), and therefore the findings may not generalize to individuals with lower functional levels within the broader population of people with limb loss.

The physical design of the PKA introduced several constraints that may have influenced both participant selection and study outcomes. The build height of the device limited inclusion of individuals with certain residual limb lengths (e.g., long transfemoral limbs or knee disarticulations) and required adjustments to prosthetic configuration. These adjustments included socket modifications, changes in suspension strategy, and the use of contralateral shoe lifts to achieve appropriate alignment, all of which may have introduced variability in performance outcomes.

Socket fit and suspension could not be fully standardized due to the investigational nature of the device. While efforts were made to preserve each participant’s original socket shape through duplication and to maintain primary suspension methods when possible, iterative adjustments were required over time to accommodate alignment needs, volume fluctuations, and comfort. These included changes in sock ply, liners, auxiliary suspension (e.g., TES belts), and other clinical modifications. Although such variability reflects real-world prosthetic care, it also represents a potential confounding factor that could not be fully controlled.

Additional limitations arose from the use of a single PKA configuration across all participants. Device components, including foot size, were not individualized (limited to the available sizes: 26 and 28), which may have resulted in suboptimal fitting for some users. The overall weight of the PKA also necessitated increased suspension in many cases and may have negatively affected comfort, confidence, and user perception. Over the duration of the study, some participants also experienced body weight changes requiring further socket adjustments, introducing additional variability.

Several methodological factors should be considered when interpreting these results. First, most patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are designed for devices used in daily life and may not fully capture user experience with laboratory-restricted prototype systems, potentially limiting their interpretability in this context.

Second, the extended time interval between testing sessions for each device condition may have reduced measurement consistency for certain outcomes. As noted in the Methods, shorter testing intervals are typically preferred for assessing metabolic cost to minimize variability due to temporal changes in physiology or performance.

Finally, this study focused exclusively on level-ground walking, which does not fully represent the range of functional tasks relevant to community mobility. While level walking is a clinically important benchmark, a key potential benefit of powered prostheses lies in more demanding activities such as stair ascent, incline walking, and sit-to-stand transitions. The PKA prototype used in this study was not capable of reliably performing these tasks due to limitations in control strategy, actuator capacity, and system stability. Consequently, these activities were excluded from evaluation, which may underestimate the broader functional potential of powered systems. Future studies should incorporate these higher-demand tasks to better assess the full capabilities of next-generation powered prostheses and ensure alignment with real-world mobility needs.

Summary

The PKA prototype used in this study demonstrated lower maximum walking speed, 6MWT distance, and a decrease in swing time symmetry. The results are consistent with prior work in adding distal mass to a prosthesis, with the notable exception that metabolic cost did not increase in the PKA. This may imply that the power provided by the PKA offset the metabolic costs of its increased weight. We also found that the PKA slightly increased the step length on the prosthetic leg during FV walking and significantly decreased walking cadence.

Despite all these factors, the PKA prototype used in this study provided similar functional outcomes in all other clinical tests. Our participants consistently identified the weight of the PKA as the greatest limiting factor of the prototype. In the future, we believe that reducing the mass and inertia of powered legs should be a high priority for translational research. Several research programs are developing hybrid powered legs, which save motor and battery weight by relying on passive dynamics for most tasks and only providing powered assistance during high-demand tasks like stair ascent or sit-to-stand transitions [48, 49]. We believe that this approach is very promising, given the current limits of motor torque density and battery energy density. Lighter, faster powered legs with appropriate control architecture to harness their advantageous power generation have significant potential to improve patient outcomes.

This study presented the results of clinical testing of an early-stage PKA prototype, emphasizing the use of subject proficiency benchmarks that directly correspond to functional tests. Future work should maintain this rigor while reducing device weight, refining adjustability (to optimize push-off power), and improving control parameter adaptability (to reduce tuning complexity). Incorporating improved variable cadence testing will further validate PKAs against real-world mobility needs. These results offer important direction for future development of powered prosthetic legs aimed at improving real-world mobility outcomes.
