Amount and bioavailability of dissolved organic carbon leached from decomposing bamboo wood organs under manganese addition in a subtropical moso bamboo forest
Authors: Jiawen Xu, Jun Deng, Ziying Zhang, Gong Zhang, Ting Li, Hanchang Zhou, Lin Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractManganese (Mn) is regarded as a crucial factor regulating wood litter decay and property change. However, the effect of Mn on wood litter-leached dissolved organic carbon (DOC) characteristics during decomposition remains unexplored in forests. We collected four wood organs (culm base, rhizome, culm root, and culm) from Phyllostachys edulis (moso bamboo) in a subtropical moso bamboo forest in southern China, and combined Mn addition in the field and laboratory leaching experiment to determine the influence of Mn on the quantity and bioavailability of DOC leached from decomposing bamboo wood organs. For each organ type, Mn addition generally increased DOC amount, DOC aromaticity, DOC molecular weight and DOC: dissolve total phosphorus (DTP) ratio, but decreased DOC bioavailability in the bamboo wood organ leachates over 360-day exposure in the field. Moreover, bamboo wood organ-leached DOC bioavailability negatively correlated with corresponding DOC aromaticity, DOC molecular weight, and DOC: DTP ratio, but exhibited no relationship with corresponding DOC: dissolved total nitrogen (DTN) ratio in the leachates. These observations highlight the pivotal role of Mn in regulating bamboo wood organ-leached DOC amount, and suggest that Mn addition influences bamboo wood organ-leached DOC bioavailability via its effect on DOC quality and phosphorus availability in the leachates in subtropical moso bamboo forests.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09255-6.Keywords: Aromatic DOC, Leaching, Lignin, Stoichiometry, Wood litter

Introduction

Dissolved organic carbon (DOC), a highly labile component of soil organic matter, serves as the primary energy source for soil heterotrophic microorganisms [1, 2], and therefore plays a pivotal role in carbon cycle in forests [3, 4]. Because the vast majority of plant biomass ultimately returns to soil as senescent tissue, soluble organic matter leached decomposing litter is regarded as a major DOC source in forest soils [5, 6]. Wood organs contribute the necessary components for forest litter stock [7, 8], and thus producing a considerable amount of soluble materials in forest soils [9]. Nevertheless, most research has focused on leaf litter, and the factors controlling wood litter-leached DOC amount and bioavailability remain poorly understood in forests [10], especially for monocots such as bamboos (Poaceae, subfamily Bambusoideae) [8]. Considering that bamboos are predominant components and contribute greatly to carbon and nutrient cycles in many (sub) tropical ecosystems [11, 12], these knowledge gaps will constrain our understanding of plant litter-leached DOC fate and associated dynamics in these ecosystems.

In forest soils, manganese (Mn) is a required element in the process of Mn peroxidase synthesis [13, 14], and thus regulates recalcitrant organic carbon composition and associated physical properties of decomposing litter [15, 16]. For example, Mn enrichment can accelerate lignin biodegradation and increase the release of inner carbon source during litter decomposition [17, 18]. Furthermore, the stimulating effects of Mn on litter decomposition rate have been frequently observed in different ecosystems [16–19]. During litter leaching process, the amount of DOC leached out is governed by litter physical properties (e.g., tissue density and specific surface area) and organic carbon composition [1, 10]. Consequently, by promoting lignin degradation and altering litter structure, Mn availability could be an overriding regulator of litter-leached DOC amount. To date, however, we are not aware of empirical studies that directly test how Mn availability influences decomposing litter-leached DOC amount in forests.

Litter-leached DOC bioavailability is often influenced by the DOC component and C: N:P stoichiometry in the leachates [20, 21], both of which are controlled by litter chemistry properties [22, 23]. Previous studies have observed that Mn peroxidase could depolymerize litter lignin with aromatic structure into simple organic compounds and thus increase the amount of aromatic carbon in the leachates in forests [24]. On the contrary, enhanced lignin decomposition can stimulate release of labile carbon fractions (e.g., cellulose, hemicelluloses and starch) and nutrient matter in the litter [25, 26], which would increase the availability of energy and nutrient in the litter leachates. Accordingly, increased Mn availability may exert a substantial impact on litter-leached DOC bioavailability by changing the chemistry traits of the litter leachates [18]. However, few empirical works have been implemented to determine the effect of Mn availability on the bioavailability of DOC leached from decomposing litter in forests.

Phyllostachys edulis (moso bamboo) is an indispensable timber resource in southern China, which cover over 4.67 million hm2 of forest area and occupy 73% of the total bamboo-forest area in China [27]. Here, we collected four wood organ types from moso bamboo including culm base, rhizome, culm root, and culm from a subtropical moso bamboo forest in southern China. Then, we combined field Mn addition experiment and laboratory leaching experiment to investigate the amount and chemical composition of DOC leached from decomposing bamboo wood organs, and quantified the bioavailability of such DOC conducting a 28-day standard incubation experiment. Our aim was to identify how Mn addition affects decomposing bamboo wood organ-leached DOC amount and bioavailability in moso bamboo forests in southern China. We proposed the following hypotheses: during decomposition process, (1) Mn addition would increase bamboo wood organ-leached DOC amount due to enhanced lignin decay, and (2) Mn addition would reduce bamboo wood organ-leached DOC bioavailability because of increased aromatic carbon contents in the leachates.

Materials and methods

Study site

Collection and decomposition of litters were performed in a moso bamboo forest (28°72′77″N, 115°68′80″E), which is located within the Chayuanshan plantation, Meiling National Forest Park, Nanchang city, Jiangxi Province, China. The site belongs to the zone with subtropical monsoon climate, and mean annual temperature and precipitation was 18.8 °C and 1560 mm, respectively [28]. In this site, the culm density of moso bamboo is generally 2150–2450 plants per hectare. The mean diameter at breast height was 9.6 ± 2.2 cm, and the average height was 12.7 ± 4.9 m. The logging management mainly involves digging bamboo shoots every spring. Notably, in this study, the collection of litters and the related experimental treatments have obtained the permission from the governing department of Chayuanshan plantation. During the litter decay periods, mean monthly soil temperature and water content at the depth of 10 cm were obtained from an adjacent weather station in Nanchang Urban Ecosystem Research Station.

Litter sampling and field-exposure experiment

In September 2023, we randomly set up five forest patches (20 m × 20 m) with the same management pattern as replicates to collect litters. In each plot, we selected 10 bamboo individuals with 3 years old and used a saw to obtain fresh (Without treatment) bamboo wood organs (i.e., culm base, rhizome, culm root, and culm) at one time in February 2024. For each organ type, litters from the same patch were evenly mixed, oven-dried at 65 °C, and segmented into two groups. One group was regarded as initial litter treatment, and the other one was applied to field-exposure experiment.

In April 2024, we established 10 plots with area of 2 m × 2 m in this moso bamboo forest, and randomly arranged five pairs of control and Mn addition (2 g Mn m− 2 year− 1) treatments in these plots. The distance between each plot was controlled to be at least 10 m. For Mn-addition plots, we used MnSO4 solution to evenly spray to the floor twice a year. Meanwhile, the same volume of deionized water likewise was sprayed to the control plots. To ensure a strictly controlled and comparable experimental environment, we performed the litter decay experiment at the soil surface of such plots. Moreover, the collected litters from the five forest patches were assigned to the 10 plots (five pairs of control and Mn addition treatments) on a one-to-one block basis. For each organ type, oven-dried litters (30 g) were placed in the litter bags with 1.0 mm mesh. This study prepared a total of 120 litterbags (4 organ types × 2 treatments × 3 sampling dates × 5 replicates). To avoid interference caused by the micro-environment within plot, litterbags were randomly positioned on the soil surface, and the distance between each one was controlled to be at least 15 cm. In late April 2024, we placed the litterbags, and retrieved them after 90, 180, and 360 days of field exposure. For initial and field-exposure treatments, litters were oven-dried at 65 °C, and then were cut into two parts by the knife. A part of litter was milled for analyzing litter traits, and other part of litter was prepared for obtaining leachates. Litter lignin concentration was measured on an automatic fiber analyzer (F2000, Hanon Instruments, Jinan, China). For decomposing litter, the mass and lignin remaining was expressed as the percentage of initial litter content, respectively.

DOC extraction and measurement

Litter-leached DOC was extracted basing on a short-term leaching method [29]. To suppress microbial degradation, DOC was extracted from the litter at 4 °C [30]. Litter leachates were prepared by immersing 10-g oven-dried litter in deionized water (500 mL) in lightproof glass bottles for 24 h, and then the obtained solutions were filtered through the 0.7-µm filter (Whatman GF/F). In litter leachates, the concentrations of DOC and dissolved total nitrogen (DTN) were analyzed on a C/N analyzer (multi N/C 2100 S, Analytik Jena, Germany), dissolve total phosphorus (DTP) concentration was determined with the procedure according to Ebina et al. [31]. In this study, DOC, DTN, and DTP amounts were calculated based on specific substance amount in the leachates and the remaining litter mass (Ding et al. 2021), and represented the net release of DOC, DTN, and DTP in the solution, which includes both physically leaching and microbial generation in situ [42]. The SUVA254 value (i.e., the specific absorbance at 254 nm) was obtained via dividing the DOC absorbance at corresponding wave length measured on ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (UV600SC, Jinghua Instruments, Shanghai, China) by the DOC concentration [32]. Moreover, the S350−400 value was obtained by fitting the absorption spectrum within wavelength range of 350–400 nm into an exponential decay function [33]. Generally, SUVA254 values serve as index of DOC aromaticity, which exhibiting positive correlations with the content of aromatic DOC [32–34]; S350−400 value acts as index measuring DOC molecular weight, and the greater value indicates a lower molecular weight [33].

In this study, a 28-day laboratory incubation experiment was performed to quantified litter-leached DOC bioavailability [35]. To prevent microbial from excessive growth, the prepared leachates were diluted with deionized water to below 20 mg DOC L–1. Moreover, we obtained microbial inoculums by soaking 5-g fresh humus collected from each forest patch in deionized water (1000 mL) in lightproof glass bottles for 12 h at 20 °C. Each DOC treatment received litter leachates (50 mL) and inoculums (1 mL) in a 500-mL glass bottle. Meanwhile, five same glass bottles were established as blanks, including deionized waters (50 mL) and inoculums (1 mL). All treatments were aerobically incubated at 20 °C in the light-avoiding environment. After 28-day incubation, we retrieved glass bottles to measure DOC concentration, and calculated DOC concentration by subtracting the blank value from the treatment value. Litter-leached DOC bioavailability (%) was expressed as the proportional loss of DOC concentration over the incubation.

Statistical analyses

Mn addition effects on litter leachates properties were calculated with the following formula [18]:

Mn addition effects (%) = (LMn – LControl) / LControl × 100

where LMn and LControl are the litter leachates properties under Mn addition and control treatments, respectively.

In this study, all data analyses were conducted in R (4.1.1), and the statistically significant was set as P < 0.05. Before statistical analyses, the normality and variance homogeneity of the data were examined respectively using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test. Meanwhile, we verified the homogeneity of the initial litters (i.e., no significant differences in initial litter traits among the five patches) (Table S3). Therefore, patch was not included as a random factor in subsequent models. Linear mixed models were performed to assess the responses of litter leachates properties to organ type, Mn addition treatment, decomposition time in the field, and their interactions with the ‘nlme’ package. Specifically, organ type, Mn addition treatment, and decomposition time were considered as independent factors to evaluate their main and interactive effects. A paired t test was applied to compare the significance of the differences in litter-leached DOC amount and lignin remaining in litter between control and Mn addition treatment with the “agricolae” package. The relationship between DOC bioavailability and litter leachates properties was examined by linear and/or nonlinear regression analysis. A single-sample t test was used to define the significant differences between the Mn addition effects and zero [18]. The positive and negative values differed significantly from zero, indicating stimulatory and inhibitory effects, respectively. Using the “multcomp” package, Tukey’s HSD test was applied to examine differences in litter leachates properties across treatment types and decomposition times, as well as to compare Mn addition effects among organ types.

Results

After 180 and 360 days of decay, bamboo wood organ under Mn addition treatment had lower mass remaining and lignin remaining than that under control treatment, regardless of organ type (Table 1 and Table S1). Organ type, Mn addition and litter decomposition time all had significant influences on bamboo wood organ-leached DOC amount (Table 2). Irrespective of organ type, wood organ of bamboo in the Mn addition treatments generally had greater DOC amount than that in the control at each decomposition time (Fig. 1). Thus, Mn addition generally had stimulatory effects on bamboo wood organ-leached DOC amount during decomposition (Table 3). However, such Mn addition effects were significantly affected by organ type and decay time (Table S2). Among four organ types, Mn addition had the greater impact on the rhizome after 90 and 180 days of decay, and on the rhizome and culm after 360 days (Table 3).

Organ type, Mn addition, and litter decomposition time all exhibited significant effect on the properties of bamboo wood organ leachates (Table 2). Generally, regardless of organ type, lower DOC: DTP ratio (Fig. 2E, F, G and H), lower SUVA254 value (Fig. 3A, B, C and D), greater S350−400 value (Fig. 3E, F, G and H), and greater DOC bioavailability (Fig. 4) were observed for the leachates of bamboo wood organ under the control treatment than that under the Mn addition treatment over the decomposition time in the field. Therefore, significant Mn addition effects were observed for these leachates properties, although no consistent effect was found on the DOC: DTN ratio (Fig. 2A, B, C, D; Table 3). After 90 days decay, Mn addition generally had the greater stimulatory effects for DOC: DTP ratio and SUVA254 value and greater inhibitory effects for S350−400 value and DOC bioavailability on rhizome and culm root than on the other two organ types (Table 3 and Table S2). For each treatment type, there were similar temporal dynamics in bamboo wood organ-leached DOC amount throughout the decay period in field (Fig. 1). Specifically, bamboo wood organ-leached DOC amount remarkably decreased before 90-day decomposition; decreased at a substantially slower rate and even stabilized from 90-day to 180-day decay; had a weak increase in the following decomposition period (Fig. 1). In general, DOC: DTP ratio (Fig. 2E, F, G and H), and SUVA254 value (Fig. 3A, B, C and D) were lowest in the leachates of initial litter and gradually increased as decomposition time under both control and Mn addition treatment, regardless of organ type. Moreover, an opposite changing pattern was observed for S350−400 value (Fig. 3E, F, G and H) and DOC bioavailability (Fig. 4) in the leachates of bamboo wood organ. While the DOC: DTN ratio of rhizome leachates followed a similar trend to the DOC: DTP ratio, those of the other three organs showed no distinct temporal dynamics (Fig. 2A, B, C and D).

After 28-day incubation, bamboo wood organ-leached DOC bioavailability respectively exhibited negative relationships with DOC: DTP ratio (Fig. 5B) and SUVA254 value (Fig. 5C), and positively related to S350−400 value in the leachates (Fig. 5D). Nevertheless, litter-leached DOC bioavailability did not showed significant relationship with the DOC: DTN ratio in the leachates (Fig. 5A).

Discussion

Over 360 days of decomposition in the field, bamboo wood organ under Mn addition treatment often leached greater amount of DOC than that under control treatment (Fig. 1). Accordingly, our results generally supported the first hypothesis, and indicating that Mn addition can increase the amount of soluble organic carbon in decomposing bamboo wood organs. In the present study, the stimulating effects of Mn addition on DOC amount of decomposing bamboo wood organs would be accounted for the following mechanisms. First, Mn addition can stimulate lignin decomposition [15, 16], which would enhance the formation of lignin-derived soluble compounds and increase the release of inner soluble organic matter in the bamboo wood organ [24, 36]. Meanwhile, Mn addition-induced acceleration of lignin degradation can increase the relative surface area of bamboo wood organ contacting the waters, and hence raise DOC leaching [37]. Consistent with previous evidences that Mn availability regulates litter decomposition in forest ecosystems [16–18], the positive effects of Mn on DOC amount, in the present study, demonstrates that Mn addition can also promote wood litter decay via enhancing leaching process in these ecosystems.

In this study, Mn addition effects on litter-leached DOC amount varied with organ types. Across 360 days of decay, rhizome and culm generally had greater stimulatory effects than the other two organ types (Table 3). Compared with the culm base and culm root, the rhizome and culm generally possess higher lignin concentrations (Table S3), thereby rendering them more responsive to Mn addition. In fact, previous studies also observed that Mn application exerted a more pronounced stimulatory effects on the decomposition of lignin-rich litter than that of lignin-poor litter in forests [16–18]. Considering the crucial role of lignin degradation in regulating litter leaching process, Mn addition had greater stimulatory effects on DOC amount of rhizome and culm than that of culm base and culm root during decomposition. However, the pronounced stimulatory effects of Mn addition on culm did not become apparent until after 360 days of decay (Table 3). This delayed response can be largely ascribed to the specific chemical composition of the culm. In this study, compared across the four organ types, the culm displayed exceptionally high C: N and C: P ratios (Table S3), which implies a prolonged phase of microbial nutrient immobilization before rapid lignin degradation [38, 39]. Accordingly, compared with the rhizome, the Mn addition-induced stimulation of DOC amount in the culm only emerged after 360 days of decay.

Irrespective of decay time, DOC: DTP ratio in the leachates of bamboo wood organ was generally lower under control treatment than under Mn addition (Fig. 2E, F, G and H). Previous studies have reported that the production of soluble phosphorus rather than soluble carbon was depended on litter element concentration [29, 40]. Accordingly, Mn addition-induced variations of DOC: DTP ratio in the leachates would be primarily accounted for the varied response of organic compounds differing in element content to Mn addition across the litter degradation processes [10]. During litter leaching, the vast majority of phosphorus is released in the form of inorganic orthophosphate which is likely to be readily consumed by microorganisms and roots [29]. Meanwhile, Mn addition was conducive to the formation and release of bamboo wood organ-leached DOC in this study. Accordingly, Mn addition increased DOC: DTP ratio in the leachates of decomposing bamboo wood organs. These observations highlight the key role of Mn availability in regulating C: N:P stoichiometry in wood litter leachates in forest soils.

In this study, bamboo wood organ under Mn addition treatment generally had greater SUVA254 value (Fig. 3A, B, C and D) and lower S350−400value (Fig. 3E, F, G and H) in the leachates than that under control treatment. These results indicate that Mn addition increases DOC aromaticity and molecular weight in the leachates of bamboo wood organ [21, 34]. In general, organic carbon composition of litter leachates is primarily influenced by litter chemistry traits [22, 41]. Previous studies have confirmed that the degradation of lignin could enhance the production of lignin-derived DOC with high aromaticity and complexity during leaching process [36, 42]. Over decomposition time in the field, we also found generally lower mass remaining and lignin remaining of bamboo wood organs under Mn addition treatment than under control treatment (Table 1 and Table S1). Therefore, Mn addition-induced acceleration of lignin degradation would explain the greater aromaticity and molecular weight of DOC in the leachates of bamboo wood organ compared with control treatment in this study.

Over 28-day incubation, Mn addition generally reduced bamboo wood organ-leached DOC bioavailability, regardless of exposure time in the field (Fig. 4), thereby supporting our second hypothesis. Generally, in the litter leachates, microbial degradation of DOC is constrained by both nutrient availability and chemical composition [10, 20]. Our data showed that DOC bioavailability exhibited a positive relationship with S350−400 value (Fig. 5D), and negatively correlated with the DOC: DTP ratio (Fig. 5B) and SUVA254 value (Fig. 5C), respectively, indicating the predominance of the phosphorus availability and DOC quality in driving the variations of bamboo wood organ-leached DOC bioavailability. Compared with control treatment, bamboo wood organ under Mn addition treatment leached DOC with greater aromaticity (Fig. 3A, B, C and D), molecular weight (Fig. 3E, F, G and H) and DOC: DTP ratio (Fig. 2E, F, G and H). Therefore, bamboo wood organ under Mn addition treatment had lower DOC bioavailability in the leachates than that under control treatment. These results imply that the Mn availability regulates bamboo wood organ-leached DOC bioavailability predominantly via altering both DOC quality and phosphorus availability in subtropical moso bamboo forests.

Notably, Mn addition exerted stronger stimulatory (DOC: DTP ratio and SUVA254 value) and inhibitory (S350−400 value and DOC bioavailability) effects on the rhizome and culm root than on other organs after 90 days of decay (Table 3). Given that C: N:P stoichiometry and DOC quality in the litter leachates were primarily controlled by the soluble C compounds in this study [10, 36], the inconsistent patterns would be explained by the inter-organ differences in DOC traits. After 90 days of decay, both of rhizome and culm root had greater Mn addition effects on DOC amount than culm base and culm (Table 3). Therefore, the higher magnitude of Mn-induced increase in lignin-derived DOC amount directly led to elevated Mn addition effects on DOC: DTP ratios, DOC aromaticity, and DOC molecular weight of rhizome and culm root relative to those of culm base and culm. Moreover, such changes of phosphorus availability and DOC quality would account for the greater Mn addition effects on DOC bioavailability of rhizome and culm root than those of culm base and culm in this study.

Since a large amount of DOC released from initial litter, DOC amount decreased dramatically as the decomposition progresses of bamboo wood organs in the field (Fig. 1). Similar to this study, Don and Kalbitz [42] observed that leaf litter emitted a high pulse of DOC under initial condition. Kalbitz et al. [43] also found that initial leaf litter released the larger amount of DOC relative to decomposing litter. Generally, the content of soluble organic compounds (e.g., starch and soluble sugars) is highest in initial litter during decay process, and is gradually exhausted after decomposition period [44]. Furthermore, the rapid depletion of the DOC amount during the initial 90 days (May to late July) coincided with the high-temperature and high-moisture conditions (Fig. S1), which would synergistically accelerate both physical leaching and microbial metabolism. In contrast, the decline trend of DOC amount was substantially weakened and even disappeared from 90-day to 180-day decay (Fig. 1). This dynamic is likely driven by the rapid exhaustion of the initial DOC pool, coupled with a concurrent decline in precipitation during this phase (Fig. S1). These observations indicate that the release dynamics of litter-leached DOC are primarily regulated by the initial availability of labile carbon fractions and seasonal hydrothermal fluctuations.

Interestingly, the extracted quantities of bamboo wood organ-leached DOC weak increased from 180-day to 360-day decomposition (Fig. 1). That is in agreement with the study about leaf litter in temperate forests, in which the amount of soluble compounds significantly decreases as the decomposition in the early phase and then slowly increases in subsequent decomposition stage [43, 44]. In the present study, the increasing DOC amount of bamboo wood organ in relative late-stage decomposition would be accounted for the following mechanisms. First, the lignin degradation can stimulate the release of lignin-leached DOC in such decomposition stage [24, 42]. Second, continuous decomposition can increase the contact area between the bamboo wood organ and waters during leaching [36]. In addition, decreased soil temperature and moisture levels likely constrain both the microbial consumption and physical leaching of DOC in litter under field conditions (Fig. S1). Thus, our observations further substantiate that lignin degradation, coupled with environmental constraints, acts as a fundamental driver governing the dynamics of DOC in decomposing litter in forest ecosystems.

In the leachates of bamboo wood organs, the DOC bioavailability gradually decreased with increasing decomposition time in the field (Fig. 4). Don and Kalbitz [42] also observed a reduced trend of leaf litter-leached DOC bioavailability during decay process. In the leachates, initial bamboo organ had greatest nutrient availability (i.e., lowest DOC: DTP ratios) and DOC quality (i.e., lowest DOC aromaticity and molecular weight) among different decay time, and gradually decreased with increasing decomposition time (Figs. 2E, F, G and H and 3). Given the tight linking between the microbial degradation of DOC and the availability of energy and nutrients [20, 21], the temporal variations of DOC composition and C: N:P stoichiometry in the leachates would account for the changing pattern of bamboo wood organ-leached DOC bioavailability over decay time in the field.

While our study provides novel insights into the Mn-driven regulation of amount and properties of DOC leached from decomposing bamboo wood organ, certain methodological constraints inherent to field experiments should be acknowledged. First, the 1.0-mm mesh litterbags used to prevent physical fragment loss effectively excluded soil macrofauna. This isolation is strictly necessary to capture the specific degradation signals driven by enzymatic hydrolysis process excluding the confounding effects of macrofauna physical fragment, but our findings specifically reflect microbe- and mesofauna-driven processes and cannot be extrapolated to natural conditions. Second, regarding chemical quantification, the standard proximate analysis used represents apparent lignin, which increasingly incorporates recalcitrant microbial necromass as litter decay progresses. Although this accumulation may partially mask the absolute degradation rate of native plant lignin, the significant relative differences observed between treatments robustly demonstrate how Mn addition alters overall recalcitrant carbon dynamics. Therefore, future studies integrating varied mesh sizes with advanced techniques (e.g., stable isotope tracing and microbial biomarker) are highly recommended to precisely disentangle synergistic faunal contributions and distinguish litter- and microbial-derived lignin during litter decomposition under Mn enrichment.

Conclusion

Combining field Mn addition and laboratory leaching experiment, we observed that bamboo wood organ under Mn addition treatment leached greater DOC quantities with lower bioavailability than that under control treatment during decomposition process in bamboo forests. The tight correlations between DOC bioavailability and DOC: DTP ratio, SUVA254 value and S350−400 value indicate that Mn enrichment alters decomposing bamboo wood organ-leached DOC bioavailability predominantly via its effect on phosphorus availability and DOC quality in the leachates. Considering the vital contribution of decomposing bamboo wood organ-leached soluble organic carbon to soil DOC, these results will be conducive to improve our understanding of the Mn function in controlling carbon and nutrient cycles in subtropical moso bamboo forests.
