Prevalence and associated factors of liver fibrosis and steatosis in Gansu, China: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Junhong Yang, Xeuni Ma, Bin Peng, Min Zhang, Chengbo Yang, Yan Yang, Jianping Ji, Weidong Ren, Anxing Zhang, Xinjian Yang, Deqiang Luo, Liang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe epidemiology of chronic liver disease in China is undergoing a major transition, shifting from a predominance of viral hepatitis to metabolic-associated liver disease. However, population-based data from Northwest China remain limited. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and independent associated factors of liver fibrosis and steatosis in the general population of Gansu Province, China.MethodsWe conducted a community-based cross-sectional study across three regions in Gansu Province (Huining, Jingtai, and Jingyuan), enrolling 5,043 eligible participants between January and December 2025. All participants underwent anthropometric measurements, laboratory testing, and transient elastography (TE), and completed a structured questionnaire. Liver fibrosis and steatosis were assessed using the liver stiffness measurement (LSM) and ultrasound attenuation parameter (UAP), respectively. Fibrosis was staged as F0 (LSM < 7.3 kPa), F1 (7.3 ≤ LSM < 7.85), F2 (7.85 ≤ LSM < 10.0 kPa), and F3-4 (LSM ≥ 10.0 kPa). Steatosis grades S1 (mild), S2 (moderate), and S3 (severe) were defined using UAP cutoff values of 244, 269, and 296 dB/m, respectively. Independent associated factors were identified using multivariate logistic regression analysis.ResultsAmong 5,043 participants (median age 46 years; 59.8% female), significant fibrosis (≥ F2) was observed in 5.18% (95% CI: 4.56–5.79), and advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis (F3-4) in 1.67% (95% CI: 1.31–2.02). The prevalence of liver steatosis (≥ S1) was 50.45% (95% CI: 49.07–51.85), including 26.87% with moderate-to-severe steatosis (≥ S2) and 7.16% with severe steatosis (S3). Multivariate analysis identified HBV infection (OR 3.19, 95% CI: 1.72–5.92), obesity (OR 5.14, 95% CI: 4.14–8.42), diabetes (OR 3.55, 95% CI: 2.02–6.25), elevated ALT (OR 3.86, 95% CI: 2.96–7.60), and decreased PLT (OR 6.45, 95% CI: 2.03–20.52) as independent factors cross-sectionally associated with advanced fibrosis. Severe steatosis was independently associated with obesity (OR 17.62, 95% CI: 13.50-23.01), diabetes (OR 1.73, 95% CI: 1.17–2.55), hypertension (OR 1.50, 95% CI: 1.14–1.96), dyslipidemia (OR 1.36, 95% CI: 1.04–1.78), decreased PLT (OR 4.16, 95% CI: 1.46–11.84), elevated ALT (OR 2.17, 95% CI: 1.46–3.22) and male sex (OR 1.70, 95% CI: 1.18–2.46).ConclusionsHepatic steatosis was prevalent in this community population of Gansu Province, whereas clinically significant or advanced liver fibrosis remained relatively modest and was predominantly concentrated within high-risk metabolic and viral subgroups. These findings highlight the urgent need for integrated metabolic and viral screening strategies to enable early identification and prevention of progression to advanced liver disease in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28333-w.

Introduction

Chronic liver disease represents a major and escalating global health burden, accounting for approximately two million deaths annually worldwide. Of these, nearly one million deaths are attributable to cirrhosis and a further 600,000-900,000 to primary liver cancer, together representing ~ 3.5% of all global mortality [1, 2]. Despite advances in antiviral therapy and vaccination programs, the global burden of liver-related morbidity and mortality continues to rise, underscoring the urgent need for effective population-level prevention and early detection strategies [3].

Liver fibrosis and hepatic steatosis are central pathological processes underpinning the progression of chronic liver disease across diverse etiologies. Fibrosis, characterized by excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix proteins, represents the final common pathway leading to cirrhosis, portal hypertension, hepatic decompensation, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [4]. Hepatic steatosis, characterized by abnormal triglyceride accumulation within hepatocytes, is the hallmark of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Under the recent global multi-society consensus nomenclature, the term MASLD has officially replaced non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) to better reflect the underlying cardiometabolic pathophysiological drivers and eliminate potentially stigmatizing language [5]. MASLD is now recognized as the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide, affecting approximately 30–40% of the adult population and up to 60–80% of individuals with obesity or type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [6–9]. Importantly, numerous longitudinal studies have demonstrated that fibrosis stage, rather than steatosis or steatohepatitis alone, is the strongest determinant of liver-related and all-cause mortality in MASLD [10, 11].

The epidemiology of liver fibrosis and steatosis has undergone a profound shift over recent decades. In Asia, chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection has historically been the dominant driver of advanced liver disease [12]. However, the widespread implementation of neonatal HBV vaccination and effective antiviral therapy has altered the etiological landscape. In parallel, the global epidemics of obesity, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome have fueled a dramatic rise in MASLD, rendering metabolic dysfunction a leading contributor to hepatic steatosis and an increasingly important cause of progressive fibrosis, even in regions traditionally dominated by viral hepatitis [6, 13]. Large population-based studies from Europe, North America, and urban regions of China have consistently demonstrated strong associations between steatosis, fibrosis, and cardiometabolic risk factors including central obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and T2DM [14–17].

Despite this growing body of evidence, substantial knowledge gaps persist regarding the burden and determinants of liver fibrosis and steatosis in rural Chinese populations. Most large-scale epidemiological studies have focused on urban centers or hospital-based cohorts, which may not adequately reflect the socioeconomic conditions, lifestyle patterns, dietary habits, and healthcare access characteristic of rural communities [14, 15]. Northwestern China, including Gansu Province, has experienced rapid nutritional and epidemiological transitions, potentially creating a unique ‘dual-burden’ scenario in which persistent HBV infection coexists with a rapidly increasing prevalence of metabolic dysfunction [18]. However, robust community-based data describing the prevalence, severity distribution, and associated factor profiles of liver steatosis and fibrosis in these settings remain limited.

Early identification of liver injury in asymptomatic individuals is critical for preventing progression to advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis. Transient elastography (TE) has emerged as a validated, noninvasive modality capable of simultaneously assessing liver stiffness measurement (LSM) and ultrasound attenuation parameter (UAP), enabling large-scale population screening for fibrosis and steatosis [19–21]. In the present prospective, community-based study, we applied standardized TE assessments to evaluate the prevalence and severity of liver fibrosis and steatosis in three rural communities in Gansu Province (Huining, Jingtai, and Jingyuan), and to identify metabolic, viral, and lifestyle-related factors associated with these conditions.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a community-based cross-sectional screening, which serves as the baseline evaluation of an ongoing prospective cohort study, between January and December 2025 in three counties of Baiyin City, Gansu Province, China. Participants were recruited from rural villages and residential communities within Huining, Jingtai, and Jingyuan counties via a localized public health screening network co-organized by government and primary healthcare clinics. Eligible residents were invited through community announcements to participate in a comprehensive, free chronic liver disease healthcare screening program, rather than relying on self-selected volunteers or hospital outpatients. These three neighboring counties are located within the Yellow River basin of Baiyin City, representing the typical semi-arid agricultural and pastoral transition zone of the Loess Plateau in Northwest China. Economically, these regions are characterized by a traditional agrarian economy primarily reliant on dryland farming and local animal husbandry, with a per capita disposable income that closely mirrors the median socioeconomic status of rural Gansu Province. This study was approved by Baiyin Central Hospital (No. 2025BYSZXYYLL-0001). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

To be eligible for final analysis, participants had to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged ≥ 18 years; (2) primary residents who had lived in the local area for over 6 months; and (3) successfully completed all three baseline components, including standardized anthropometric measurements, comprehensive biochemical fasting blood tests, and reliable TE scans.

Participants were strictly excluded based on the following criteria: (1) missing or highly incomplete structured questionnaire data; (2) previous clinical diagnosis of liver cirrhosis, liver cancer, or other advanced extrahepatic malignancies; (3) current pregnancy; or (4) severe anatomical or cognitive limitations that precluded reliable physical or elastography examinations. Data collection

To ensure data consistency and minimize potential measurement bias, all key assessments—including the structured questionnaire, anthropometric measurements, blood sampling, and non-invasive liver scans—were performed on the same day during a single health examination visit. Blood samples were collected in the morning via venipuncture after a strict overnight fast of at least 8 h. Anthropometric and blood pressure measurements were obtained by trained clinical staff using standardized equipment while participants were in a rested state, immediately prior to the TE examination. Demographic information, including age and sex, was collected during health examinations. Anthropometric measurements included height, weight, waist circumference, and blood pressure. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²), and obesity was defined according to Chinese criteria.

Blood samples were obtained after an overnight fast for biochemical testing, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-Glutamyl transferase (γ-GTT), total Bilirubin (TBIL), direct Bilirubin (DBIL), indirect Bilirubin (IBIL), total Protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C), red blood cell count (RBC), white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), haemoglobin (HGB), and hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). HBV infection was defined as positivity for HBsAg.

A structured questionnaire was a self-assessment scale developed by 5 investigators dedicated to questionnaire development and administered by trained investigators to collect information on marital status, educational level, medical history (including hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia), medication use, alcohol status, smoking status, and tea intake (Supplementary material: Survey Questionnaire: Prevalence and Risk Factors of Liver Fibrosis and Steatosis in Gansu, China).

Assessment of liver fibrosis and steatosis

TE was performed by trained and certified operators using ILivTouch (Hisky Medical, Wuxi, China) according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol. To minimize measurement bias, all participants underwent the examination in a supine position with the right arm maximally abducted, following a strict overnight fast of more than 8 h. The system automatically utilized multi-frequency probes tailored to the participant’s anthropometric profile. In accordance with standard quality control procedures, the equipment underwent regular professional calibration. Measurements were deemed reliable only if at least 10 valid acquisitions were completed with a success rate > 60%, and the Interquartile Range (IQR) to median ratio of LSM was strictly < 30%. For individuals with severe obesity-related measurement limitations or an initial failure rate exceeding the acceptable threshold, examinations were repeated by a senior certified specialist or recorded as technical failures. Liver fibrosis was categorized based on LSM values as follows: F0 (LSM < 7.3 kPa), F1 (7.3 ≤ LSM < 7.85 kPa), F2 (7.85 ≤ LSM < 10.0 kPa), and F3-4 (LSM ≥ 10.0 kPa). Specifically, ≥F1 indicates any fibrosis, ≥F2 significant fibrosis, and F3-4 advanced fibrosis. For liver steatosis, Median UAP cutoff values of 244 dB/m, 269 dB/m, and 296 dB/m were used to define grades S1 (mild), S2 (moderate), and S3 (severe), respectively [14].

Definitions of various subpopulations

The subpopulations in this study were assessed based on demographic, cardiovascular, and chronic liver disease risk factors, which were categorized as follows: age (< 60 years, ≥ 60 years), sex (male, female), and geographic regions (Huining, Jingtai, Jingyuan). BMI was calculated as body weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. BMI status was categorized as underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 24.0 kg/m2), overweight (24.0 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 28.0 kg/m2, obese (BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m2) [22]. According to the World Health Organization criteria for diabetes, participants with FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, self-reported diagnosis of diabetes, or use of diabetes medication were categorized as having diabetes. Participants with SBP/DBP ≥ 140/90 mmHg, self-reported diagnosis of hypertension, or use of hypertension medication were categorized as having hypertension. Participants with TC ≥ 6.20 mmol/L, TG ≥ 2.30 mmol/L, HDL-C < 1.00 mmol/L, LDL-C ≥ 4.20 mmol/L, or use of dyslipidemia medication were categorized as having dyslipidemia. Elevated ALT was identified if ALT > 40U/L. Elevated AST was identified if AST > 40 U/L. Decreased ALB was identified if ALB < 35.0 g/L. Decreased PLT was identified if PLT < 100 × 10^9/L [14]. Central obesity is defined as a waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) ≥ 0.90 in male and ≥ 0.85 in female [23]. HBV infection was identified if the participant was positive for HBsAg. In this community screening context, individuals with TE-confirmed hepatic steatosis (≥ S1) who concurrently met at least one cardiometabolic risk factor were provisionally classified under the broad umbrella of MASLD pathways. However, due to the concurrent endemicity of viral hepatitis in this geographic region, overlap with viral-associated fatty liver clinical presentations was evaluated based on parallel HBsAg positivity structures.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables with a normalized distribution or skewed distribution are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR) as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The characteristics of participants according to grades of liver steatosis and fibrosis were compared using 1-way analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate for continuous variables and X2 test for categorical variables. To determine the factors independently associated with liver steatosis and fibrosis, logistic regression models were performed. Binary logistic regression models were performed by establishing distinct cumulative thresholds for each pathological endpoint, specifically evaluating any fibrosis (≥ F1), significant fibrosis (≥ F2), advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis (F3-4), any steatosis (≥ S1), moderate-to-severe steatosis (≥ S2), and severe steatosis (S3). Preliminary univariable logistic regression analyses were conducted for all baseline characteristics; variables yielding a P < 0.10 or those with documented biological plausibility were selected as candidate covariates for the multivariable restrictions. Multicollinearity among predictors was stringently evaluated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with a VIF ≥ 5 indicating significant collinearity. To address severe collinearity and overlapping clinical definitions between BMI, general obesity, and central obesity, these variables were entered into separate, independent multivariable model blocks rather than a single simultaneous regression.

For the multivariate logistic regression and prevalence calculations, a complete-case analysis approach was primarily adopted after excluding participants with missing critical questionnaire data. There were 14 missing values for allergy history, which were filled as ‘none’, one missing value for PLT, which was filled with the mean, and one missing value for antilipemic agents, which was filled as ‘none’.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, NY, USA). The column charts were drawn by Origin 2016 software (OriginLab). A 2-tailed value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 5,639 participants were initially recruited from three community-based cohorts in Gansu, China (Huining, n = 1,815; Jingtai, n = 1,802; Jingyuan, n = 2,022). After excluding 596 participants due to incomplete questionnaire data or pre-existing cirrhosis or liver cancer, 5,043 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The median age was 46 years (IQR, 38–55), and 59.8% were female. The median LSM was 5.3 kPa (IQR, 4.5–6.3), and the median UAP was 244.3 dB/m (IQR, 218.0-270.5) (Table 1).

Baseline characteristics according to liver fibrosis and steatosis

As shown in Table 1, most participants had no fibrosis (F0, 89.1%), whereas 5.7%, 3.5%, and 1.7% were classified as F1, F2, and F3-4, respectively. Increasing fibrosis stage was associated with older age, male sex, and a higher prevalence of metabolic comorbidities, including obesity, central obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia (all P < 0.001). Serum ALT and AST levels increased progressively with fibrosis severity, while platelet counts declined. HBV infection and lifestyle factors, including smoking and alcohol status, also differed significantly across fibrosis stages (Table 1). Significant differences were observed across fibrosis grades (F0 to F3-4) regarding SBP (P < 0.000), DBP (P < 0.001), and educational level (P < 0.001). Specifically, participants with more advanced fibrosis stages tended to exhibit higher median SBP and DBP values (Supplementary Table 1).

With respect to steatosis, 50.5% of participants exhibited hepatic steatosis (≥ S1), including 23.6% with mild (S1), 19.7% with moderate (S2), and 7.2% with severe steatosis (S3) (Table 2). Increasing steatosis severity was associated with male sex, older age, higher BMI and waist-to-hip ratio, and a greater prevalence of metabolic disorders (all P < 0.001). TG and FBG levels increased, whereas HDL-C levels decreased with worsening steatosis. Lifestyle factors, including smoking, alcohol, and tea intake, also varied significantly across steatosis grades. Similarly, SBP and DBP showed significant upward trends across increasing steatosis grades (S0 to S3, P < 0.001 for both). Additionally, the prevalence of heart disease varied significantly across steatosis severities (P < 0.01). No significant differences were found for GLB, cerebrovascular disease, or kidney disease across steatosis grades (Supplementary Table 2).

The prevalence of any fibrosis (≥ F1), significant fibrosis (≥ F2), and advanced fibrosis (F3-4) in the overall population was 10.85% (95% CI, 10.00-11.70), 5.18% (95% CI, 4.56–5.79), and 1.67% (95% CI, 1.31–2.02), respectively (Table 3). Participants with metabolic disorders had a markedly higher prevalence of fibrosis. The prevalence of ≥F2 fibrosis was higher among individuals with obesity (19.21% vs. 3.27%), diabetes (13.57% vs. 4.57%), hypertension (9.41% vs. 3.71%), and dyslipidemia (8.21% vs. 3.72%) (all P < 0.001; Fig. 2B). Fibrosis prevalence increased progressively with steatosis severity; the prevalence of ≥F2 fibrosis rose from 2.08% in S0 to 24.10% in S3, and advanced fibrosis reached 9.97% in participants with severe steatosis (P < 0.001). HBV-positive participants, those with elevated ALT or AST levels, and those with thrombocytopenia had significantly higher fibrosis prevalence (all P < 0.001). Differences were also observed according to marital status and educational level (Fig. 2C-D). Highly significant differences in fibrosis prevalence were observed among different educational groups (P < 0.001 for ≥F1 and ≥F2). Participants with heart disease showed a significantly higher prevalence of ≥F1 fibrosis compared to those without (16.46% vs. 10.67%, P < 0.05). While trends toward higher prevalence were noted in participants with kidney disease and those aged > 60 years, these differences did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05). Marital status, cerebrovascular disease, and ALB levels also showed no significant association with fibrosis prevalence in this cohort (Supplementary Table 3).

Prevalence of liver steatosis in the overall population and subgroups

The prevalence of any steatosis (≥ S1), moderate-to-severe steatosis (≥ S2), and severe steatosis (S3) was 50.45% (95% CI, 49.07–51.85), 26.87% (95% CI, 25.65–28.09), and 7.16% (95% CI, 6.45–7.87), respectively (Table 4). Steatosis prevalence varied by region (P < 0.05) and was consistently higher in males than females across all grades (Fig. 3A). Participants aged > 60 years had a higher prevalence of ≥S1 steatosis than those aged ≤ 60 years (56.98% vs. 49.67%, P < 0.05).

Metabolic comorbidities were strongly associated with steatosis severity. Obesity was associated with a markedly higher prevalence of ≥S1 steatosis (89.07% vs. 45.19%), and severe steatosis (S3) was present in 40.89% of obese participants (P < 0.001). Diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia were each associated with higher steatosis prevalence across all grades (all P < 0.001; Fig. 3B). Steatosis prevalence also increased with fibrosis severity and was higher among participants with elevated ALT or AST levels. Lifestyle factors, including smoking, alcohol, and tea intake, as well as marital status and educational level, were associated with differences in steatosis prevalence (Fig. 3C-D). Significant differences in the prevalence of ≥S1 and ≥S2 steatosis were linked to marital status (P < 0.001), educational level (P < 0.001), and age > 60 (P < 0.001). Heart disease was significantly associated with ≥S2 steatosis (P < 0.01). Furthermore, decreased ALB and decreased PLT counts were significantly associated with higher steatosis grades (P < 0.05), while cerebrovascular and kidney diseases did not show significant associations (Supplementary Table 4).

Factors associated with liver fibrosis

Multivariable logistic regression analyses identified obesity, decreased PLT, elevated ALT, HBV infection, and diabetes as independent associated factors of liver fibrosis across increasing severity thresholds (Table 5). For advanced fibrosis (F3-4), decreased PLT (OR 6.45, 95% CI: 2.03–20.52), obesity (OR 5.14, 95% CI: 4.14–8.42), and HBV infection (OR 3.19, 95% CI: 1.72–5.92) were independently associated with higher odds. Elevated ALT (OR 3.86, 95% CI: 2.96–7.60) and diabetes (OR 3.55, 95% CI: 2.02–6.25) also remained significant predictors.

Discussion

In this large, community-based cross-sectional screening of 5,043 adults from rural Gansu Province, we observed that while hepatic steatosis was prevalent, the overall burden of liver fibrosis remained comparatively modest. More than half of the participants (50.45%) exhibited liver steatosis (≥ S1), while 5.18% had significant fibrosis (≥ F2), and 1.67% had advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis (F3-4). These findings highlight an underrecognized but clinically meaningful burden of chronic liver disease in rural Northwest China.

MASLD has emerged as a major global public health challenge, with pooled prevalence estimates ranging from 30% to 40% among adults worldwide. Recent meta-analyses reveal substantial regional variation: Latin America exhibits the highest prevalence (44.37%), followed by the Middle East and North Africa (36.53%), South Asia (33.83%), Southeast Asia (33.07%), North America (31.20%), East Asia (29.71%), Asia Pacific (28.02%), and Western Europe (25.10%) [8]. Another meta-analysis reported that MASLD is the most prevalent subtype of steatotic liver disease, with an overall prevalence of 33.6% in the general population; regionally, the Americas had the highest prevalence (51.1%), followed by the Eastern Mediterranean (40.8%) and Southeast Asia (35.7%) [24]. In China, a study including 22,970 participants who underwent comprehensive medical examinations reported an overall MAFLD prevalence of 28.77% (6,608/22,970), with 16.87% (1,115/6,608) of these patients exhibiting significant fibrosis, as assessed using FIB-4 [15]. Moreover, a cross-sectional, population-based nationwide study, including 5,757,335 adults from 30 provinces who underwent health check-ups between 2017 and 2022, evaluated steatosis and fibrosis using transient elastography. In this cohort, the prevalence of steatosis and severe steatosis was 44.39% and 10.57%, respectively [14]. Notably, the prevalence of steatosis observed in our cohort substantially exceeds pooled national and global estimates for MASLD, indicating a higher burden in this population. These inter-regional and inter-country differences are largely attributable to variations in obesity prevalence, dietary patterns, physical activity levels, and the burden of T2DM, which are well-recognized drivers of MASLD development [25, 26]. The relatively high prevalence observed in our study highlights the growing burden of MASLD in China and underscores the urgent need for targeted, population-specific prevention and management strategies.

Our findings are consistent with robust evidence linking MASLD to cardiometabolic risk factors. Obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia have been consistently associated with higher liver stiffness and steatosis severity in large population-based studies. Analyses from NHANES demonstrate that clustering of metabolic abnormalities exerts synergistic effects on MASLD prevalence and fibrosis risk, particularly when obesity coexists with impaired glucose metabolism or hypertension [27]. Consistent with this, central obesity, present in nearly 60% of our cohort and almost 90% of individuals with general obesity, emerged as a dominant predictor of hepatic steatosis. Disorders of glucose metabolism further amplify liver disease risk. Impaired glucose disposal and insulin resistance are closely linked to hepatic fat accumulation and fibrogenesis, and lower estimated glucose disposal rate has been independently associated with both MASLD and liver fibrosis [17]. A meta-analysis reported MASLD prevalence of 60–70% among individuals with T2DM, highlighting their heightened susceptibility [28]. In line with this evidence, participants with diabetes in our cohort exhibited markedly higher rates of significant fibrosis and severe steatosis. Beyond individual risk factors, the accumulation of cardiometabolic abnormalities confers a progressively higher burden of liver disease. Prior cohort studies have shown that obesity, (pre-)diabetes, hypertension, and increased waist circumference are among the strongest predictors of fibrosis and adverse outcomes [29]. Similarly, data from NHANES III indicate that advanced Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) stages are independently associated with a more than twofold increased risk of liver fibrosis [30]. Multicenter evidence further confirms that obese patients with T2DM carry a substantially higher prevalence of clinically significant fibrosis than their non-obese counterparts [31]. Collectively, these findings support a clear dose-response relationship between metabolic burden and liver disease severity and reinforce current recommendations for routine fibrosis screening and comprehensive metabolic assessment in high-risk populations.

Although metabolic dysfunction has emerged as the predominant driver of chronic liver disease globally, our data highlight the continued clinical importance of chronic HBV infection as a determinant of liver fibrosis in regions with high endemicity. In our study, participants with chronic HBV infection exhibited a markedly higher prevalence of significant fibrosis compared with HBV‑negative individuals (15.13% vs. 4.54%). This finding aligns with the ‘dual-hit’ hypothesis supported by recent high-quality evidence. For instance, a large population-based study by Cheng et al. demonstrated that while chronic HBV infection may paradoxically exert a ‘protective’ effect on lipid profiles and reduce the degree of hepatic steatosis, it significantly elevates the risk of liver fibrosis in patients with concurrent metabolic dysfunction [32]. This suggests that viral-induced inflammation and metabolic stress act synergistically to accelerate fibrogenesis. Furthermore, the clinical prognosis of these patients is heavily influenced by the severity of the underlying metabolic burden. As highlighted in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, the coexistence of metabolic dysfunction and chronic hepatitis B (CHB) necessitates a more granular stratification, as MASLD severity directly correlates with poorer antiviral treatment outcomes and clinical prognosis [33]. Interestingly, the relationship is not unidirectional; the metabolic environment may also modulate viral kinetics. Huang et al. reported that the presence of MASLD actually facilitates HBsAg seroclearance and seroconversion, potentially through the modulation of the intrahepatic immune microenvironment. However, this viral clearance often occurs in the context of persistent metabolic-driven liver damage [34]. As emphasized in the latest global primer on MASLD, managing metabolic comorbidities is as critical as viral suppression in preventing the progression to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, particularly in regions where both conditions are endemic [6].

Sex-specific differences were markedly evident in our study, with men demonstrating a significantly higher prevalence of both significant fibrosis and severe steatosis compared with women. This observation is consistent with the global epidemiological profile of MASLD. The incidence of MASLD is nearly double in men compared to women (5,944 vs. 3,315 per 100,000 person-years), reflecting a clear male predominance in disease susceptibility [6]. While behavioral factors such as higher rates of smoking and alcohol consumption, both of which were identified as risk factors for advanced fibrosis in our univariate analysis, contribute to this disparity, biological drivers play a central role. Tilg et al. [7] emphasizes that male sex is a fundamental risk factor (male-to-female ratio ~ 2), largely due to sex-specific fat distribution patterns and the protective effects of estrogen. Men typically exhibit greater accumulation of visceral adipose tissue, which is more pro-inflammatory and metabolically active than the subcutaneous fat predominant in premenopausal women. Furthermore, experimental and clinical evidence suggest that estrogen exerts potent anti-steatotic and anti-fibrotic effects. As highlighted in a recent study [35], estrogen suppresses adipogenesis-related genes in hepatocytes and inhibits the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from Kupffer cells, thereby preventing the activation of hepatic stellate cells. Consequently, the loss of this hormonal protection post-menopause often leads to an accelerated progression of liver fibrosis in women, narrowing the sex gap in older age groups and necessitating the implementation of sex-adjusted screening and management strategies.

We additionally observed associations between lifestyle factors, including tea consumption, and steatosis and fibrosis severity. Tea contains bioactive polyphenols with antioxidant and metabolic regulatory properties, and large population studies, such as those from the UK Biobank, have reported inverse associations between tea intake and imaging markers of hepatic fat and fibroinflammation [36]. Additionally, large-scale studies confirmed that regular tea intake is significantly associated with a reduced risk of fatty liver, likely through the improvement of lipid profile and insulin sensitivity [37–39]. However, results from a longitudinal cohort (RaNCD) indicated that higher tea consumption was not significantly linked to worsening steatosis or fibrosis risk, and paradoxically, greater tea intake was associated with a lower likelihood of improvement in hepatic steatosis over time, highlighting the complexity and potential heterogeneity of effects across populations and tea types [40]. These findings suggest that while tea consumption may confer hepatoprotective benefits, its impact is likely modified by overall dietary patterns, metabolic status, and tea type. Longitudinal follow-up of our cohort will be essential to clarify causal relationships.

Regarding non-invasive liver assessment, the implementation of the iLivTouch system in this large-scale community screening provides a standardized and validated methodological approach. Utilizing TE technology, this modality allows for the simultaneous, rapid evaluation of liver stiffness and ultrasound attenuation parameters in asymptomatic populations. In the context of epidemiological studies involving over 5,000 participants, this technique offers a distinct logistical advantage over liver biopsy due to its non-invasive nature, excellent reproducibility, and high patient acceptance. Crucially, the diagnostic accuracy of this specific system for staging fibrosis and grading steatosis has been validated against histological standards and magnetic resonance imaging proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) in recent Asian cohorts, justifying its deployment as a reliable epidemiological tool for mapping regional chronic liver disease burdens [41–42].

Several limitations merit critical consideration. First, the absence of liver biopsy, the traditional reference standard for fibrosis staging, may be perceived as a diagnostic constraint. Nevertheless, a landmark head-to-head comparison by Zhang et al. demonstrated that LSM is comparable to, and in some aspects superior to, histological fibrosis staging in predicting long-term liver-related events (LREs) in patients with MASLD. This evidence suggests that changes in LSM can serve as a reliable proxy for clinical progression or regression [43]. Second, the etiological classification of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis remains partially incomplete in this screening cohort. Chronic HBV infection was evaluated solely via HBsAg positivity, lacking granular clinical variables such as serum HBV DNA viral load, HBeAg status, duration of infection, or history of antiviral therapies. Furthermore, while alcohol consumption history was gathered qualitatively, it was not quantified into precise daily grams of ethanol intake. This measurement constraint limits our capacity to systematically differentiate overlapping advanced etiological phenotypes, such as true isolated MASLD, MetALD (metabolic dysfunction-associated altered liver disease with increased alcohol intake), pure alcohol-related liver disease, or viral-associated steatosis. Consequently, our cross-sectional attribution of the overall disease burden to combined metabolic, viral, and lifestyle elements should be interpreted with caution as hypothesis-generating, and comprehensive stratified or sensitivity analyses tracking precise replication and consumption kinetics are highly warranted in future long-term prospective follow-ups A recent study highlighted that metabolic liver diseases are highly heterogeneous; a single assessment may not capture the longitudinal fluctuations of metabolic stress [7]. Finally, the relatively short window of observation prevents a definitive assessment of LREs, such as hepatocellular carcinoma or decompensated cirrhosis. As suggested by a multicenter study [44], longer follow-up is essential to establish the predictive value of baseline liver stiffness for future clinical events. Continued monitoring of this Gansu cohort will be imperative to validate these findings and to assess the long-term impact of metabolic and viral factors on fibrosis progression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our screening data demonstrate that hepatic steatosis is highly prevalent in rural Gansu Province, whereas the prevalence of clinically significant liver fibrosis is comparatively modest and strictly concentrated within high-risk subgroups characterized by severe obesity, diabetes, or chronic HBV infection. Our findings support the implementation of TE-based screening programs in community settings to facilitate early detection and targeted intervention, particularly among individuals with obesity, diabetes, and HBV infection.
