1.1 Problem Statement

 Radiographic imaging at X-ray energies in the MeV range poses a complex problem.

Detec on of high energy X-rays depends heavily on the ability of the detec on material, in this

work the scin llator, to a enuate and ul mately stop the X-ray. A enua on is more readily

achieved by increasing material thickness, however thick scin llators are plagued with rela vely

high internal sca ering, leading to an overall decrease in spa al resolu on and image quality.

1.2 High Level Overview of Proposed Work

 This work seeks to op mize the process of high energy X-ray detec on and conversion into

1.3 Radiography

 X-ray radiography is governed by many changeable parameters, such as voltage, current,

detector speed, frame averaging, etc., but the two most basic parameters of these are voltage

and current. X-ray photons with higher voltage have more “penetra ng power”, meaning the

probability of a enua on when passing through the imaging object is lower. The current of an

x-ray beam represents the number of photons being produced. If voltage is how much energy

one photon carries, current is how many of those photons of said energy are being produced.

Output current for x-ray sources is measured in microamps or milliamps and are directly

propor onal to the amperage that is fed to the cathode, causing the electrons to liberate.

Increasing the current of the x-ray beam increases the number of photons hi ng the detector

on the other side of the object, which improves the signal of the image.

1.4 Flat panel detectors

 Radiographic imaging detectors operate via two main mechanisms: a scin lla on screen

absorbs incoming photons and transforms the x-rays into visible light with intensity that is

directly related to the energy of the absorbed photon, then a photodiode array measures the

scin llated light and digi zes the light intensity into a grey value. For 16-bit detectors, there are

65,536 grey values available to describe how bright the light is from the scin llator. This means

that total darkness is assigned to a grey value of 1 and light that reaches the maximum

detectable intensity gets a grey value of 65,536.

 Detector quality is measured by many parameters, the two most common parameters

being: modula on transfer func on (MTF) and detector quantum eﬃciency (DQE)1-6. The MTF

provides informa on on spa al resolu on, the smallest detectable object that the detector will

be able to image. DQE measures the detector’s eﬃciency of photon absorp on and energy

conversion into a readable metric based on the MTF and noise. The value of the calculated DQE

is given between 0 and 1, where 1 is how a perfect detector would perform based on

absorp on eﬃciency and spa al resolu on, and 0 is the inability of the detector to absorb and

measure any photon energy.

1.5 Scin llators

 Scin llators within the detector operate by absorbing either X-ray or gamma photons and

producing visible light from the interac on. The intensity of the visible light is propor onal to

the amount of energy imparted into the scin lla ng material.

 Commonly, imaging detectors are made of either gadolinium oxide (gadox) or cesium

iodide (CsI) scin llators, with more exo c materials available for more specialized applica ons.

CsI is normally used for lower energies, up to 150 keV, while Gadox can be used for both low

and high energy, up to the 10s of MeV range1, 3, 7. Each of these scin llators are available in

commercial oﬀ the shelf ﬂat panel radiography detectors, and each have been well

characterized for spa al resolu on, visible light yield per X-ray photon, visible light dwell me,

etc. Some other common scin llators used for imaging and research and development include

bismuth germanate (BGO), lute um oxyorthosilicate (LSO), lute um y rium orthosilicate

(LYSO), and various tungstates8.

3.4 Spa al resolu on

Spa al resolu on is greatly aﬀected by all the above metrics and indicates the dimension of the

smallest detectable feature of an image. As the TIR of a scin llator increases, the spa al

resolu on of the detector decreases because signal is lost within the scin llator, and some

signal that escapes is sca ered from its original angle. Lower SNR and CNR values show

increased levels of noise within an image, which produce higher uncertainty for the true value

of the pixels in the image. This higher uncertainty leads to a loss of smaller detectable features.

Finally, sca er is much like TIR, where the X-ray and visible light photon interac ons can cause

the detected signal to be at an angle diﬀerent than that of the incident signal, leading to a

blurring of the image, and worse spa al resolu on1, 10, 11, 16.

3.6 Burn in

 Burn in relates the change in pixel response as a func on of dose. As the detector absorbs

dose, its output signal diﬀers over me despite consistency in dose. This can be accounted for

with a calibra on curve measuring the change in response vs. dose with open-beam

radiographs. Burn in is also seen to be more reliant on total dose rather than energy of incoming

photons1, 18, however energy does play a role in the magnitude of the burn in phenomenon.

3.7 Lag

 Lag refers to the drop-oﬀ of image signal a er the photon source is no longer impac ng

the detector. A ghost image, also known as ghos ng, will appear in the subsequent frames a er

the source is oﬀ due to lag in the pixel response, then will dissipate over me. The degree of lag

dictates the speed at which the image dissipates. Lag has been shown to depend heavily on

number of frames taken per image, instead of on percent pixel satura on19, 20.
