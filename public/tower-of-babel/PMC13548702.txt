Integrated multi-omics analysis of fluoroquinolone tolerance mechanisms induced by enrofloxacin in Pasteurella multocida
Authors: Yu Qi, Jiayi Li, Muhammad Inam, Xinyu Li, Ling-Cong Kong, Hong-Xia Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global prevalence of multidrug-resistant bacteria has been rising at an alarming rate, posing a serious threat to both human and animal health. However, the mechanisms by which bacteria acquire antibiotic tolerance and subsequently develop resistance remain incompletely understood.MethodsIn this study, Pasteurella multocida, a common pathogen in the animal husbandry industry, was exposed to enrofloxacin, and genome resequencing, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses were performed to elucidate the adaptive mechanisms of P. multocida under fluoroquinolone-induced stress.ResultsCompared with the wild-type strain, the enrofloxacin-tolerant strain exhibited an extended lag phase, a prolonged logarithmic phase, reduced sensitivity to polymyxin B, reduced biofilm formation, and an elongated cellular morphology. Multi-omics analysis revealed a deletion in the dusB gene of the tolerant strain, resulting in a truncated non-functional protein. The deletion of dusB enhanced tolerance by prolonging the lag phase and reducing the growth rate. Moreover, the expression of genes in the CAMP pathway was up-regulated, and deletion of cpxR further promoted tolerance by modulating ribosome-associated genes. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses indicated activation of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle during tolerance development.ConclusionThis study identified dusB and cpxR as key genes mediating enrofloxacin tolerance in P. multocida, elucidated the association between the antibiotic tolerance, growth, and gene expression, and may provide potential targets for future strategies aimed at limiting tolerance-associated resistance development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05321-z.

Background

The emergence and development of pathogenic bacterial resistance to antibiotics pose significant challenges to human health. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) causes 1.3 million deaths annually, and it has been predicted that it could contribute to nearly 39 million deaths between 2025 and 2050 [1]. The substantial financial investment and prolonged research and development timelines associated with new antimicrobial drugs have resulted in the rate of drug resistance outpacing the development of new antibiotics [2]. Therefore, it is crucial to identify and understand the mechanisms underlying drug resistance to develop new combinations of existing drugs with better therapeutic effects and to identify novel molecular therapeutic targets.

Pasteurella multocida (P. multocida) is an important Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen that resides in the upper respiratory tract of various animal species and is responsible for several infectious diseases, including avian cholera, bovine hemorrhagic septicemia, porcine atrophic rhinitis, and pneumonia [3, 4]. Furthermore, P. multocida frequently co-infects livestock in association with pathogens such as Mycoplasma spp., bovine coronavirus, and bovine viral diarrhea virus, leading to more severe clinical manifestations and increased mortality, thereby causing substantial economic losses to the global livestock industry. Fluoroquinolones represent a major class of bactericidal antimicrobial agents used to control P. multocida infections. Among them, ciprofloxacin (CIP) is recognized by the World Health Organization as a “critically important antimicrobial for human medicine.” Enrofloxacin, a third-generation fluoroquinolone and a prodrug of ciprofloxacin, is specifically developed for veterinary applications [5, 6]. Owing to its broad-spectrum activity against Gram-negative bacteria, Gram-positive bacteria, and Mycoplasma, enrofloxacin has been extensively utilized for the treatment and prevention of bacterial diseases in livestock.

Antibiotic tolerance refers to the ability of a bacterial population to survive transient exposure to a high concentration of an antibiotic without a change in their MIC [7]. Among the various mechanisms involved in bacterial drug resistance, such as target mutations, antibiotic modifications, envelope alterations, the regulation of intracellular antibiotics concentration near the target site is one of the main mechanisms that contributes to resistance in Gram-negative bacteria [8]. Consequently, the MIC values of antibiotics typically increase in resistant bacteria. In contrast, in tolerant bacteria, the MIC remains unchanged despite their capacity to survive antibiotic exposure [7]. Bacteria can withstand antibiotic stress through several adaptive responses, including growth retardation, enhanced biofilm formation, and metabolic reprogramming, among others [9–12]. Tolerance-associated mutations often facilitate the rapid subsequent evolution of resistance. Therefore, preventing the emergence of tolerance may represent a promising strategy to delay or mitigate the development of antibiotic resistance [13].

With the widespread use of fluoroquinolones and the rising prevalence of bacterial resistance, relatively limited attention has been given to bacterial tolerance toward this class of antibiotics. Therefore, P. multocida, a pathogenic bacterium that poses significant threats to the beef cattle industry, was selected as the model organism in this study. Enrofloxacin, a fluoroquinolone widely used in veterinary clinical practice, was employed to investigate the mechanisms underlying bacterial tolerance. The primary objective of this study is to elucidate the metabolic regulatory responses of P. multocida under fluoroquinolone-induced stress, which may provide potential targets for future strategies aimed at mitigating the development of antimicrobial resistance.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The P. multocida wild-type strain Pm-3 was obtained from the Department of Veterinary Medicine, College of Animal Science and Technology. The strain was isolated from the lungs of cattle exhibiting severe respiratory disease in Jilin Province, and preserved at -80 °C. The P. multocida wild-type strain Pm-3 exhibits strong pathogenicity, with a median lethal dose (LD₅₀) of 1.8 × 106 CFU/mL in mice (95% confidence limit was 1.3 × 106–2.5 × 106 CFU/mL) [14]. The strain was stored at -80 °C. The fluoroquinolone-tolerant strain, designated Pm-3T, was derived from Pm-3 through induction of tolerance using enrofloxacin. The strain obtained after 17 consecutive passages under enrofloxacin induction was designated Pm-3T17, while the strain obtained after 19 passages was designated Pm-3T19. Both the strains exhibited tolerance to enrofloxacin. Both P. multocida Pm-3 and Pm-3T strains were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth at 37 °C under standard aerobic conditions.

Determination of Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC)

The antimicrobial susceptibility of P. multocida Pm-3 to enrofloxacin was determined using the broth microdilution method. Briefly, enrofloxacin was serially twofold diluted in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth and mixed with an equal volume of bacterial suspension containing approximately 1.5 × 10⁶ colony-forming units (CFUs)/mL in sterile, UV-irradiated 96-well microtiter plates (Corning). Following incubation at 37 °C for 18 h, the MIC was determined as the lowest antibiotic concentration that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth. Other representative antimicrobial agents were also tested using this method. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 was utilized as the quality control strain for all antimicrobial susceptibility tests, with its interpretive criteria aligned with the CLSI guidelines.

A 100 µL aliquot of bacterial culture was taken from consecutive wells of the 96-well plate corresponding to concentrations above the determined MIC and evenly spread onto BHI agar plates without antibiotics. The plates were then incubated statically at 37 °C for 48 h. The lowest antibiotic concentration that yielded no visible bacterial colonies was recorded as the MBC of the strain. All assays were performed in triplicate for each strain and antibiotic tested.

Time-kill curve assay

A single colony of Pm-3 was inoculated into BHI broth and cultured in a shaking incubator at 37 °C and 170 rpm until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.5. Subsequently, 1 mL of the culture was collected, serially diluted tenfold, and plated to determine the initial colony-forming units (CFUs). Enrofloxacin was then added to the remaining culture at final concentrations corresponding to 5×, 10×, and 20× the MIC, followed by continued incubation under the same conditions. At one-hour intervals, 1 µL aliquots of the culture were sampled, serially diluted tenfold, and plated for CFU enumeration.

Evolutionary protocol of cyclic exposure to enrofloxacin

To investigate the relationship between antibiotic resistance and tolerance, P. multocida cultures were evolved through cyclic exposure to enrofloxacin, following a slightly modified protocol described by reference [15]. An overnight culture was diluted 1:100 into 50 mL of BHI broth containing 10 µg/mL of enrofloxacin and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 170 rpm. Each day, the overnight culture was again diluted 1:100 and exposed to enrofloxacin at 10 µg/mL for 6 h. Following antibiotic exposure, the culture was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the bacterial pellet was resuspended in fresh BHI broth. The cells were washed twice to remove residual antibiotic. To minimize variability in antibiotic exposure between cycles, a consistent sub-inhibitory concentration (1% of the highest concentration, i.e., 0.1 µg/mL) was maintained, accounting for potential fluctuations introduced during the wash steps. After washing, the bacterial suspension was transferred into 50 mL of fresh BHI broth and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 170 rpm for 17 h to allow regrowth. Importantly, nearly all viable cells were carried over between cycles, thereby minimizing population drift. A portion of the regrown culture was sampled for analysis after each cycle. Following enrofloxacin treatment, the bacteria were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and CFUs were enumerated to assess bacterial survival and adaptation.

Growth curve

Single colonies of P. multocida Pm-3 (wild-type strain) and Pm-3T (enrofloxacin-tolerant strain) were inoculated into 5 mL of BHI broth and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 170 rpm for 24 h. Subsequently, each culture was diluted 1:100 into 300 mL of fresh BHI broth and incubated under the same conditions. At one-hour intervals, 3 mL aliquots of the cultures were collected, and bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using a TU-1901 spectrophotometer. Each measurement was performed in triplicate, and the mean OD600 value was calculated and recorded for analysis.

Biofilm formation assay

Biofilm formation by the P. multocida wild-type strain Pm-3 and enrofloxacin-tolerant strain Pm-3T was quantified using the crystal violet staining method. Single colonies of Pm-3 and Pm-3T were inoculated into BHI broth and cultured at 37 °C with shaking until the optical density at OD600 reached 0.5. The bacterial suspensions were then aliquoted into 96-well microtiter plates. The plates were incubated statically at 37 °C for 48 h to allow biofilm formation. After incubation, the wells were washed three times with sterile PBS to remove non-adherent cells, and the washing solution was discarded. Each well was then fixed with methanol for 15 min at room temperature, after which the methanol was discarded, and the plates were air-dried. Subsequently, 1% (w/v) crystal violet solution was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The excess stain was removed, and the wells were gently rinsed with running water until clear. The plates were inverted on clean filter paper to remove residual moisture and allowed to air dry. Finally, 200 µL of 95% ethanol was added to each well to solubilize the bound dye, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader. Each strain was tested in three biological replicates, with six technical replicates included for each biological replicate.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy for morphological analysis of Pm-3 and Pm-3T strains was performed according the method mentioned by Joeng J et al. [16]. The bacterial cultures were collected and rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (0.01 M, pH 7.2). Subsequently, both of Pm-3 and Pm-3T strain cultures were immersed in cacodylate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.0) supplemented with 5% glutaraldehyde and 0.15% (wt/vol) ruthenium red (TCI America, Tokyo, Japan). Fixed cells were resuspended in cacodylate buffer and exposed to polycationic ferritin (Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA) at a final concentration of 1.0 mg/mL for 30 min at 20 °C. The reaction was halted by a 10-fold dilution with cacodylate buffer, followed by centrifugation and three washes in the same buffer. Subsequently, the cells were immobilized in 4% agar, underwent three washes in cacodylate buffer, and were postfixed with 2% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. The postfixed samples were subjected to three washes in cacodylate buffer, dehydrated through a series of acetone washes, and further dehydrated in propylene oxide. Following dehydration, the samples were embedded in Spurr low-viscosity embedding resin. Thin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, then examined using a transmission electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 60 kV.

Microbial genome resequencing, genome assembly and ANI analysis

Chromosomal DNA was extracted from overnight cultures of the wild-type and mutant P. multocida strains using the Bacterial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified DNA samples were submitted to Novogene (Beijing, China) for microbial whole-genome resequencing. Comparative genomic analysis was conducted using the reference genome sequence of P. multocida strain Pm-3 (GenBank accession number: CP014618.1). This strain was the same isolate as Pm-3 used in the present study. The steps of data processing were as follows: (1) Discard a paired reads if either one read contains adapter contamination (> 10 nucleotides aligned to the adapter, allowing ≤ 10% mismatches); (2) Discard a paired reads if more than 10% of bases are uncertain in either one read; (3) Discard a paired reads if the proportion of low quality (Phred quality < 5) bases is over 50% in either one read. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (InDels) were detected and annotated using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) software [17].

Raw paired-end sequencing reads were quality-filtered using fastp v1.3.3 with automatic adapter detection. The filtered reads were independently assembled de novo using SPAdes v4.2.0 in isolate mode. Scaffolds shorter than 500 bp were excluded. Assembly statistics, including genome size, GC content, scaffold number and N50, were evaluated using QUAST. Genome completeness and contamination were assessed using CheckM2 v1.1.0. Bidirectional average nucleotide identity (ANI) between the parental strain and the enrofloxacin-induced strain was calculated using FastANI v1.34. The mapped-fragment proportion was calculated as the number of mapped query fragments divided by the total number of query fragments.

RNA sequencing and analysis

The wild-type P. multocida strain Pm-3, the enrofloxacin-tolerant strain Pm-3T17 and Pm-3-ΔcpxR were washed twice with sterile PBS, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use. Total RNA was extracted using the MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The purified RNA was used as a template for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis using the TransScript® One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech Co., Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting cDNA samples were submitted to Novogene (Beijing, China) for transcriptome sequencing and downstream bioinformatic analysis. Both building index of reference genome (NCBI Reference Sequence: NZ_CP014618.1) and aligning clean reads to reference genome were used Bowtie2-2.2.3. HTSeq v0.6.1 was used to count the reads numbers mapped to each gene. And then FPKM of each gene was calculated based on the length of the gene and reads count mapped to this gene. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq R package (1.18.0). DESeq provide statistical routines for determining differential expression in digital gene expression data using a model based on the negative binomial distribution. The resulting P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach for controlling the false discovery rate. Differentially expressed genes were identified using a threshold of |Log2FoldChange| > 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. We used KOBAS 3 software to test the statistical enrichment of differential expression genes in KEGG pathways. Three biological replicates were analyzed for each group (Pm-3, Pm-3T17, and Pm-3-ΔcpxR).

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

The wild-type P. multocida strain Pm-3 and the enrofloxacin-tolerant strain Pm-3T17 were collected in Eppendorf tubes and resuspended in prechilled 80% methanol with thorough vortexing. The samples were then incubated on ice and mixed by vortexing for 30 s, followed by sonication for 6 min. After sonication, the samples were centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 1 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were freeze-dried and reconstituted in 10% methanol prior to analysis. Metabolite profiling was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS) system (Vanquish UHPLC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) coupled with an Orbitrap Q Exactive™ HF or Q Exactive™ HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) at Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Samples were injected onto a Hypersil GOLD column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) and separated using a 12-min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. For positive and negative ionization modes, eluent A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and eluent B consisted of methanol. The solvent gradient was programmed as follows: 2% B (0–1.5 min), 2–85% B (1.5–3 min), 85–100% B (3–10 min), 100–2% B (10–10.1 min), and 2% B (10.1–12 min). The Q Exactive™ HF mass spectrometer was operated in both positive and negative ionization modes under the following parameters: spray voltage, 3.5 kV; capillary temperature, 320 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 35 psi; auxiliary gas flow rate, 10 L/min; S-lens RF level, 60; and auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350 °C. The raw data files generated by UHPLC-MS/MS were processed using the Compound Discoverer 3.3 (CD3.3, ThermoFisher) to perform peak alignment, peak picking, and quantitation for each metabolite. The main parameters were set as follows: peak area was corrected with the first QC, actual mass tolerance, 5ppm; signal intensity tolerance, 30%; and minimum intensity, et al. After that, peak intensities were normalized to the total spectral intensity.The normalized data was used to predict the molecular formula based on additive ions, molecular ion peaks and fragment ions. And then peaks were matched with the mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org/), mzVault and MassList database to obtain the accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results. Each group contained six biological replicates. We applied univariate analysis (t-test) to calculate the statistical significance (P-value). The metabolites with P-value < 0.05 and fold change ≥ 2 or FC ≤ 0.5 were considered to be differential metabolites. Metabolite identification and annotation were performed using the KEGG (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html).

Construction of gene deletion mutants

Approximately 700–800-bp DNA fragments upstream and downstream of the target gene were amplified by PCR and fused by overlap-extension PCR. The resulting fusion fragment was ligated into the P. multocida gene knockout vector pSHK5Ts-NgAgo to generate the corresponding deletion plasmid. The recombinant plasmid was introduced into P. multocida by electroporation. Transformants carrying the deletion plasmid were serially passaged at 28 °C, and candidate deletion mutants were identified by PCR using primers located outside the two homologous arms. Confirmed mutants were subsequently passaged at 37 °C to cure the temperature-sensitive plasmid, yielding a markerless target-gene deletion strain.

Construction of complemented strains

The complete coding sequence of the target gene was amplified and cloned into the pPBA1100 vector. The recombinant plasmid was verified by PCR and sequencing and introduced into the corresponding P. multocida deletion mutant by electroporation. Following recovery, transformants were selected on BHI agar containing kanamycin (50 µg/mL) at 37 °C. Kanamycin-resistant colonies were confirmed by colony PCR and sequencing. Positive complemented strains were cultured at 37 °C in BHI medium containing the same concentration of kanamycin for subsequent phenotypic assays.

Results

MIC and time-kill curve assay of enrofloxacin against Pm-3

The MIC of enrofloxacin (ENR) against the P. multocida strain Pm-3 was determined to be 0.5 µg/mL. To evaluate and select the exposure time and optimal concentration of ENR for tolerance induction, three multiples of the MIC—5×MIC, 10×MIC, and 20×MIC—were used for the time-kill curve assay. These concentrations exhibited varying bactericidal effects on Pm-3 over time. The bactericidal activity of ENR at 20×MIC showed a marked reduction in viable cells beginning at the 4th hour, reaching maximal killing at the 6th hour (Figure S1). Therefore, the P. multocida strain Pm-3 was treated with enrofloxacin at 20×MIC (10 µg/mL) for 6 h per cycle.

Enrofloxacin-induced tolerance in the Pm-3 strain

A modified tolerance induction protocol was employed to expose P. multocida Pm-3 to 20×MIC of enrofloxacin (Fig. 1A). The induction experiment was independently repeated three times, and all experiments showed the same overall trend. Compared with the initial strain, the number of surviving bacteria gradually increased following repeated enrofloxacin exposure. After regrowth under antibiotic-free conditions, no significant difference in the number of surviving cells was observed (Fig. 1B). When the regrown Pm-3 cultures were subsequently challenged with a high concentration of enrofloxacin, the bacterial density remained nearly unchanged after 6 h, with an OD₆₀₀ of approximately 0.05. However, this stability was disrupted around the 20th exposure cycle (Fig. 1C). At this stage, the bacterial density after 6 h of treatment increased significantly and remained at this elevated level thereafter. This change corresponded with an increase in the MIC of enrofloxacin against the Pm-3 strain—from 0.5 µg/mL to 1 µg/mL—indicating a twofold rise in resistance. Significant tolerance was defined as a condition in which the ratio of viable bacterial counts before and after antibiotic treatment was < 100, while the MIC value remained largely unchanged. The 17th cycle was identified as a critical transition point, as a marked increase in bacterial survival was observed following enrofloxacin exposure (Fig. 1D). Cultures obtained at the initial onset of tolerance and those collected prior to any change in the MIC were identified as tolerant strains and designated as Pm-3T. To minimize the potential impact of low-frequency pre-existing resistant mutants in the culture, we intentionally selected the strain from three passages prior to the observed MIC change, namely the 17th passage, for subsequent analyses.

Characteristics of P. multocida Pm-3 and tolerant strain Pm-3T

The growth curves of the wild-type P. multocida Pm-3 strain and the enrofloxacin-tolerant Pm-3T strain were established using cultures initiated from single colonies under antibiotic-free conditions. Growth initiation was observed after 3 h of incubation. Overall, the Pm-3T (tolerant) strain exhibited a slower growth rate compared to the Pm-3 (wild-type) strain, with notable differences observed across the distinct phases of bacterial growth. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, substantial variation was observed in the lag phases of the two strains. The lag phase of the Pm-3T strain extended up to approximately 6 h, whereas that of the Pm-3 strain lasted about 4 h. Similarly, the exponential phase of the Pm-3 strain was shorter than that of the Pm-3T strain, which demonstrated a prolonged linear growth phase extending up to the 17th hour. The Pm-3 strain entered the stationary phase earlier and maintained it for a longer duration, while the Pm-3T strain exhibited a gradual and sustained increase in cell density before reaching the stationary phase at a relatively later time point.

To determine whether P. multocida strains tolerant to enrofloxacin exhibit altered susceptibility to other bactericidal agents, MICs and MBCs of ampicillin, ceftriaxone, gentamicin, and polymyxin B were determined for both the wild-type (Pm-3) and tolerant (Pm-3T) strains. As shown in Fig. 2B, the MICs of most bactericidal agents against P. multocida remained unchanged, except for polymyxin B. The tolerant strain Pm-3T exhibited reduced sensitivity to polymyxin B, with both the MIC and MBC values increasing by eightfold compared to the wild-type strain. Additionally, the MBC of ampicillin against the tolerant strain increased fourfold. No significant differences in the MBC values of ceftriaxone or gentamicin were observed between the wild-type and tolerant strain.

Biofilms are composed of extracellular polymeric substances secreted by bacterial cells, forming a physical barrier that protects persister cells from host immune defenses and facilitates bacterial survival under antibiotic stress. In our biofilm assays comparing the wild-type Pm-3 and enrofloxacin-tolerant Pm-3T strains, we observed that, contrary to expectations, biofilm formation by the Pm-3T strain was significantly reduced compared to that of the wild-type Pm-3 (Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that enrofloxacin-induced tolerance in P. multocida is not associated with enhanced biofilm formation.

TEM was performed on both the wild-type (Pm-3) and enrofloxacin-tolerant (Pm-3T) strains following the induction of fluoroquinolone tolerance to examine morphological alterations. As shown in Fig. 2D, distinct morphological differences were observed between the two strains in response to enrofloxacin exposure. The Pm-3T cells exhibited a noticeably thinner and elongated morphology, whereas the wild-type Pm-3 cells maintained a predominantly round or oval shape. These findings are consistent with previous reports that alterations in bacterial cell morphology, particularly elongation, can lead to resistance or tolerance to antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin [18–20].

Multi-omics approach and functional analysis

To further investigate whether notable genomic alterations occurred in P. multocida during continuous enrofloxacin exposure, comparative whole-genome resequencing was performed. Previously, the whole genome of the wild-type P. multocida strain Pm-3 had been sequenced, and the resulting data were deposited in the NCBI database (GenBank accession number: CP014618.1). In the present study, this genome was utilized as the reference blueprint to analyze both the continuously cultured Pm-3 strain and the enrofloxacin-induced passage strain. The analysis specifically focused on unique differential mutations exclusively present in the tolerant strain. Mutations shared between the tolerant strain and the continuously cultured Pm-3 strain were presumed unrelated to tolerance and were therefore excluded from further analysis. We performed bidirectional ANI analysis using the de novo assemblies of the parental and enrofloxacin-induced isolates. After removal of scaffolds shorter than 500 bp, the assemblies were 2,329,439 bp and 2,329,294 bp, respectively, with GC contents of 40.19% and N50 values of 186,534 bp. CheckM2 estimated 100% completeness for both assemblies, with contamination estimates of 0.22% and 0.23%. FastANI yielded values of 99.9920% and 99.9991% in the two comparison directions, with mapped-fragment proportions of 99.7354% and 99.8675%, respectively. These results support the very high genome-wide similarity of the parental and induced isolates. Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis identified two nonsynonymous SNPs unique to the tolerant strains. The first was located in the trkH gene, which encodes the putative Trk potassium uptake system protein (protein accession WP_414057686.1), resulting in a substitution of serine with phenylalanine at position 317 (S317F) (Figure S2). The second occurred in the sapD gene, encoding the peptide ABC transporter ATP-binding protein (protein accession AAK02998), leading to a tyrosine-to-cysteine substitution at position 323 (Y323C) (Figure S3). We next examined insertion and deletion (InDel) mutations. Two InDels were detected in the tolerant strain: one in the sapB gene (Figure S4), which encodes the peptide ABC transporter permease protein, and another in the dusB gene, which encodes tRNA-dihydrouridine synthase. Notably, a 10-base pair deletion in dusB introduced a frameshift mutation, resulting in the deletion of amino acids 100–102 (cysteine, proline, and alanine, respectively) and substitution of the lysine codon at position 103 with a premature stop codon. This truncation likely produces a non-functional protein (Fig. 3A).

Gene function is inherently dependent on gene expression. To elucidate transcriptional changes associated with tolerance, transcriptome sequencing was performed on the wild-type P. multocida strain Pm-3 and the tolerant strain. Analysis of differentially expressed genes based on threshold criteria of |log2(FoldChange)| > 1 and padj < 0.05 revealed that a total of 181 genes were up-regulated and 236 genes were down-regulated in the tolerant strain compared with the wild-type strain. Differentially expressed genes were annotated and mapped to metabolic pathways using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; http://genome.jp/kegg/). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that, compared with the wild-type strain, the Cationic antimicrobial peptide (CAMP) resistance was significantly altered in the tolerant strain (Fig. 3B). Within this pathway, the acrAB operon, which encodes a multidrug efflux pump responsible for antibiotic extrusion, was notably up-regulated, a finding consistent with its established role in antimicrobial tolerance [21–24]. However, the marked up-regulation of cpxR, a response regulator involved in envelope stress and signal transduction, remains mechanistically unclear and warrants further investigation [25].

In addition, we analyzed the metabolic differences among the bacterial strains. The wild-type strain Pm-3 and the tolerant strain were subjected to liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis, with six biological replicates per strain. Principal component analysis demonstrated distinct clustering between the tolerant and wild-type strains, indicating substantial metabolic divergence (Fig. 3C). Compared with the wild-type strain, a total of 207 differentially abundant metabolites were identified in the tolerant strains, including 113 up-regulated and 94 down-regulated metabolites (Fig. 3D). These differential metabolites were significantly enriched in key metabolic pathways, including the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, riboflavin metabolism, and purine metabolism (Fig. 3E). An integrated analysis combining transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets revealed that the TCA cycle was the most significantly affected pathway. Within this cycle, the expression levels of several key genes—gltA, acnB, and frdABCD—were markedly up-regulated, whereas the levels of the corresponding metabolites α-ketoglutarate and fumarate were significantly decreased (Fig. 3F).

dusB and cpxR genes play an important role in enrofloxacin tolerance in P. multocida

Potential candidate genes associated with enrofloxacin tolerance were selected based on next-generation sequencing and LC-MS/MS analyses. Genes with significantly altered expression levels were selected based on the multi-omics analyses. The selected genes were deleted in the wild-type strain Pm-3 using the pSHK5Ts-NgAgo gene-knockout plasmid through homologous recombination. The resulting mutants were then used to evaluate the bactericidal efficacy of enrofloxacin. Except for dusB and cpxR, the genes katA, glgP, focA, and napB exhibited significant transcriptional alterations. Deletion mutants were successfully generated for six genes (dusB, katA, glgP, focA, napB, and cpxR). First, we determined the MIC and bactericidal efficacy of enrofloxacin against the mutant strains. After 6 h of enrofloxacin exposure, deletion of katA, glgP, focA, and napB did not significantly affect the bactericidal efficacy of enrofloxacin against Pm-3 (Fig. 4A). However, deletion of dusB and cpxR markedly reduced the bactericidal efficacy of enrofloxacin. After reintroduction of the corresponding genes, the bactericidal activity of enrofloxacin against the complemented strains was restored to a level similar to that observed in the wild-type strain, with no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05) (Figure S5). Compared with the wild-type strain, the MIC, MBC and mutant prevention concentration (MPC) values of enrofloxacin for the deletion mutants remained unchanged (Fig. 4B).This indicates that loss of dusB and cpxR significantly enhanced the tolerance of P. multocida Pm-3 to enrofloxacin.

It has long been recognized that conditions reducing bacterial growth rates increase tolerance to various antibiotics [26, 27], as the activity of many antimicrobial agents depends on active cell growth. The growth curves of the wild-type strain Pm-3 and its gene-deletion mutants, Pm-3-ΔdusB and Pm-3-ΔcpxR, were monitored over 20 h. Compared with the wild-type strain, Pm-3-ΔdusB exhibited delayed growth during the early stage, suggesting that deletion of dusB may prolong the lag phase. In contrast, Pm-3-ΔcpxR showed earlier growth initiation. (Fig. 4C).

The increased tolerance to enrofloxacin observed in the dusB deletion mutant appears to be associated with its reduced growth rate. However, the mechanism underlying the enhanced tolerance in the cpxR deletion mutant remains unclear. CpxR functions as a response regulator in the Cpx two-component system, where phosphorylated CpxR (CpxR-P) acts as a transcription factor to modulate the expression of target genes. To elucidate the potential mechanism, we analyzed the transcriptomic profile of the cpxR deletion mutant. Both the wild-type Pm-3 strain and the cpxR deletion mutant were exposed to either a high concentration of enrofloxacin or no antibiotic for six hours, followed by transcriptome sequencing. In the absence of antibiotic pressure, transcriptomic analysis showed that the expression levels of 316 genes were increased and those of 300 genes were decreased in the cpxR deletion mutant compared with the wild-type strain (Fig. 4D). Under enrofloxacin pressure, the expression levels of 189 genes were increased and those of 189 genes were decreased in the cpxR deletion mutant relative to the wild-type strain. Notably, upon enrofloxacin exposure, a subset of genes showed concordant expression changes in both the ΔcpxR mutant and the enrofloxacin-induced tolerant strain compared with the wild type. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that these shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were predominantly enriched in the ribosome pathway (Fig. 4E), indicating that the increased tolerance of the cpxR deletion mutant may be related to changes in the expression of ribosome-associated components.

Discussion

Fluoroquinolones (FQs), a class of clinically important bactericidal antimicrobials, possess a unique mechanism of action distinct from other bactericidal drugs [28]. However, it remains unclear whether bacterial adaptive responses under FQ stress differ mechanistically from those induced by other antibiotics. In particular, the development of FQ tolerance in P. multocida—a pathogen of major concern in the livestock industry—has not been characterized. Compared to the well-studied model organism Escherichia coli, P. multocida exhibits more fastidious nutritional requirements, slower growth rates, and distinct metabolic activities. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of FQ-induced tolerance in P. multocida is of significant biological and clinical relevance.

We adapted the bacterial tolerance induction protocol described by Ofer Fridman et al. [15], using enrofloxacin—a third-generation FQ used exclusively in veterinary medicine—for successive induction of tolerance in P. multocida strain Pm-3. Prior to induction, the MIC of enrofloxacin against P. multocida Pm-3 was determined in BHI broth, followed by time-kill assays. Because FQs exhibit concentration-dependent bactericidal activity, to minimize the confounding effects of dosage, high concentrations of enrofloxacin were employed in subsequent experiments. This approach ensured that bacterial killing reached a saturation level at high antibiotic concentrations, making the outcome primarily dependent on exposure duration rather than drug concentration [29]. After three weeks of continuous induction, an enrofloxacin-tolerant strain was obtained. Similar induction using ampicillin against Escherichia coli was previously reported by Ming Jiang et al., but in contrast, P. multocida required a longer exposure period to develop tolerance to enrofloxacin. The tolerant strain did not exhibit altered susceptibility to five other bactericidal agents—ampicillin, ceftriaxone, gentamicin, polymyxin, and ciprofloxacin. Interestingly, while the MBC of both FQs remained unchanged, the MBCs for ampicillin, gentamicin, and polymyxin showed variation. Cross-resistance among FQs is well documented, and extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production has been reported to confer resistance to other antibiotic classes such as aminoglycosides and FQs. Our findings suggest that although FQs themselves may not directly induce cross-resistance to unrelated antibiotic classes, prolonged exposure to enrofloxacin can diminish the bactericidal efficacy of other antimicrobials. This enhanced tolerance provides a transient survival advantage, allowing time for the accumulation of resistance-conferring mutations. Furthermore, the tolerant strain exhibited slower growth, which may reduce the bactericidal efficiency of antibiotics whose action depends on active bacterial metabolism [30]. Collectively, these adaptive changes enable the bacterium to better endure antimicrobial pressure. However, this induced tolerance was transient; following one week of storage at − 80 °C and subsequent subculture, the strain lost its tolerance, displaying susceptibility levels comparable to the wild-type strain upon antibiotic exposure. In addition, the multi-omics analysis was performed using one independently induced evolutionary lineage, and the results may therefore have been influenced by random mutations, bottleneck effects, or culture adaptation. Although the induction experiment was independently repeated three times and all experiments showed the same overall trend, this limitation should not be overlooked. We will further optimize the induction strategy in future studies.

Transfer RNA (tRNA) is an essential component of the protein translation machinery. Within tRNA molecules, dihydrouridine (D) plays a critical role in maintaining structural flexibility and stability [31]. The reduction of uridine to dihydrouridine is catalyzed by a large family of flavoproteins known as dihydrouridine synthases (Dus) [32]. Prokaryotic organisms typically possess three dus genes—dusA, dusB, and dusC [33]. Among these, DusB is considered the ancestral form, from which DusA and DusC likely arose through gene duplication events [32]. DusB is an FMN- and NADPH-dependent tRNA dihydrouridine synthase that reduces uridine at position 17 in the tRNA D-loop to dihydrouridine (D17), thereby increasing tRNA conformational flexibility and maintaining global translation efficiency and ribosomal reading-frame fidelity. Beyond D17 modification, DusB exerts an independent redox-regulatory function that modulates the cellular NADP⁺/NADPH balance, proteostasis and cysteine metabolism, thereby promoting bacterial tolerance to oxidative stress, including hydrogen peroxide exposure. Accordingly, loss of DusB broadly impairs codon decoding, disrupts proteome homeostasis and increases susceptibility to oxidative stress [32, 34, 35]. In the fluoroquinolone-tolerant P. multocida strain, our study identified a truncation in the dusB gene spanning approximately two-thirds of its coding sequence, resulting in the loss of a full-length functional DusB protein. Deletion of dusB in P. multocida led to a markedly slower growth rate, smaller colony morphology, and decreased bactericidal activity of enrofloxacin, while simultaneously enhancing tolerance. This phenomenon may be attributed to reduced metabolic activity resulting from the loss of dihydrouridine synthase function, which could impair translational efficiency and cellular energy metabolism. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this tolerance-associated phenotype require further investigation.

At the transcriptional level, the differentially expressed genes between the tolerant and wild-type P. multocida strains were significantly enriched in the Cationic Antimicrobial Peptide (CAMP) resistance pathway. Among these, the transcriptional regulator cpxR was markedly upregulated. The CpxA/CpxR two-component regulatory system is known to play a critical role in mediating envelope stress responses and virulence regulation in various Gram-negative bacteria [36]. Whole-genome sequencing analyses have demonstrated that the development of resistance in numerous clinically isolated or experimentally evolved strains is frequently associated with mutations in the cpxA/R genes [37]. Moreover, the CpxAR system directly influences bacterial antibiotic susceptibility by modulating efflux pump expression or altering outer membrane permeability [38]. CpxA, an inner membrane histidine kinase, functions as a sensor that autophosphorylates in response to envelope stress and transfers the phosphate group to CpxR, the cytoplasmic response regulator. Phosphorylated CpxR (CpxR ~ P) undergoes conformational changes that enhance its DNA-binding affinity, thereby regulating the transcription of downstream target genes [25]. Previous studies have shown that CpxR can influence Salmonella Typhimurium susceptibility to colistin. Moreover, deletion of cpxR increased the susceptibility of V. alginolyticus to kanamycin, midecamycin and high salinity when compared to WT [39]. In our study, CpxR was found to play a similar role in P. multocida, potentially contributing to enrofloxacin tolerance through transcriptional regulation of stress response or membrane-associated genes.

Significant alterations in metabolic activity were observed in the FQ-tolerant strain. Integrated analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic data revealed marked enrichment of genes and metabolites associated with the TCA cycle. As a central hub of bacterial metabolism, the TCA cycle plays a pivotal role in maintaining cellular bioenergetics and metabolic homeostasis [40, 41]. Alterations in central carbon flux within the TCA cycle can modulate bacterial susceptibility to antimicrobial agents [10]. For instance, TCA cycle activity has been shown to influence aminoglycoside (tobramycin) susceptibility and tolerance in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, where fumarate stimulates TCA cycle activation, enhances cellular respiration, and increases the proton motive force [42]. In contrast to these findings, our study demonstrated that the TCA cycle was upregulated in enrofloxacin-induced tolerant P. multocida, despite a significant decrease in fumarate levels. Previous research suggests that the metabolic state of persister cells may depend on the nature of the selective pressure. For example, persisters selected by β-lactam antibiotics (cell wall synthesis inhibitors) exhibit distinct metabolic profiles compared to those selected by gyrase inhibitors such as ciprofloxacin or nalidixic acid. Therefore, similar metabolic adaptations may occur in fluoroquinolone-tolerant P. multocida. Additionally, we observed a significant increase in thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) levels in the tolerant strain. TPP, in conjunction with adaptive mutations such as those in oxcA (encoding oxalyl-CoA decarboxylase), can promote the detoxification of oxalyl-CoA [43]. Hence, the elevated TPP concentration observed here may serve an analogous protective function, though the precise mechanism requires further investigation.

Currently, there is a lack of comprehensive studies on antibiotic tolerance in P. multocida, a pathogen that poses a significant threat to animal husbandry. In this study, we employed an integrated multi-omics approach to investigate the genomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic characteristics of FQ-tolerant P. multocida. Our analyses revealed several adaptive responses distinct from those observed in other bacterial species. These findings provide valuable insights into the potential molecular mechanisms underlying fluoroquinolone tolerance in P. multocida and may facilitate the development of effective strategies to mitigate both antibiotic tolerance and resistance.
