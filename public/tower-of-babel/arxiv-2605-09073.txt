Smoothing Out the Edges: Continuous-Time Estimation with Gaussian Process Motion Priors on Factor Graphs
Holmes, Connor, Lilge, Sven, Guo, Zi Cong, Dellaert, Frank, Barfoot, Timothy D

Abstract
Continuous-time state estimation is gaining in popularity due to its abilities to provide smooth solutions, handle asynchronous sensors, and interpolate between data points. While there are two main paradigms, parametric (e.g., temporal basis functions, splines) and nonparametric (Gaussian processes), the latter has seen less adoption despite its technical advantages and relative ease of implementation. In this article, we seek to rectify this situation by providing a new simplified explanation of GP continuous-time estimation rooted in the language of factor graphs , which have become the de facto estimation paradigm in much of robotics. To simplify onboarding, we also provide three working examples implemented in the popular GTSAM estimation framework.

Chapter 1
Introduction

In this article, we consider the problem of estimating a continuous-time trajectory from discrete measurements. This is a common problem in robotics, computer vision, and many other fields where we have sensors that provide measurements at discrete time intervals, but we would like to query the state of a system at any point in time.

Specifically, we will discuss using continuous-time estimation based on
GP
(
GP
) regression. This topic is not itself new and therefore this article should be considered mostly of tutorial value. Its contributions beyond the existing literature are:

•

We provide new insights on the connections between continuous-time
GP
regression and factor graphs.
In particular, we show that querying the trajectory at any time of interest can be accomplished by an application of the factor-graph elimination algorithm. This leads to a novel way to do covariance interpolation and new insights into interpolating measurement states.

•

We implement
GP
-based continuous-time estimation in the popular
GTSAM
(
GTSAM
) framework and apply it on three tutorial examples.
This allows us to demonstrate the use of continuous-time estimation in a practical setting, including how to handle high-rate and asynchronous measurements.

•

We release the code and the datasets used in the examples open source.
This will hopefully make adoption by others easier and allow for further experimentation and development in this area.

In the rest of this chapter, we motivate the continuous-time estimation problem, discuss the specific type of continuous-time estimation we consider, and provide an overview of related work.

1.1
What is Continuous-Time Estimation?

Figure
1.1
illustrates the general problem in which we are interested. We have a trajectory
\mathbf{x}(t)
that we would like to estimate, but we only have measurements
\mathbf{y}_{k}
at discrete times
t_{k}
. We would like to be able to query the trajectory at any time of interest,
\tau
.

Figure 1.1
:
We consider a trajectory to be a continuous function of time. Measurements
\mathbf{y}_{k}
occur (possibly asynchronously) at discrete times
t_{k}
yet we would like to be able to query (estimate) the trajectory state
\mathbf{x}(\tau)
at any time of interest,
\tau
.

There are four main advantages to continuous-time estimation over its discrete-time counterpart:

•

We can easily handle high-rate and asynchronous measurements.
This is accomplished by assigning each measurement to the trajectory at its time of occurrence,
t_{k}
.

•

We can query the trajectory at any time of interest,
\tau
, not just at the measurement times,
t_{k}
.
This is useful for applications where we might use one sensor to estimate the trajectory but another to build a map, for example. It is also useful in control where we might want to query the trajectory at a high rate to compute control inputs.

•

The continuous-time representation can impose smoothness on the estimated trajectory.
Often the object to which our sensors are attached is moving smoothly through the world because it is governed by physics, and we can use this knowledge to regularize the solution through the choice of motion prior.

•

The continuous-time representation can be used to overcome observability issues.
For sweeping-while-moving sensors such as rolling-shutter cameras or spinning lidars, the trajectory is often under-constrained by the measurements alone since each pixel or point is acquired from a unique pose. Our motion prior serves to regularize the solution and can be physically motivated, such as a constant-velocity prior or a constant-acceleration prior.

The ability to separate the roles of
measurement times
,
estimation times
, and
query times
is an important property of continuous-time estimation. We will next discuss the two main paradigms for continuous-time estimation, parametric and nonparametric, and why we focus on the latter in this article.

1.2
Parametric vs. Nonparametric

A common way to perform continuous-time estimation is to use a
parametric
representation of the trajectory, such as a spline or temporal basis functions. In this case, we assume that the trajectory can be represented by a finite number of parameters, and we estimate these parameters from the measurements. For example, we might use a representation such as

\mathbf{x}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{n}(t){\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},

(1.1)

where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}
are the coefficient parameters to be estimated and
\mathbf{B}_{n}(t)
are known temporal basis functions. We receive some discrete-time measurements
\mathbf{y}_{k}
at times
t_{k}
, given by the linear measurement function,

\mathbf{y}_{k}=\mathbf{C}_{k}\mathbf{x}(t_{k})+\mathbf{n}_{k},

(1.2)

with
\mathbf{n}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{R}_{k})
measurement noise. We can then estimate the parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}
by minimizing the residuals between the measurements and the predicted values from the trajectory,

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{C}_{k}\mathbf{x}(t_{k})\|^{2}_{\mathbf{R}_{k}}+\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\|^{2}\right)\\
=\min_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K}\|\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{C}_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mathbf{B}_{n}(t){\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{R}_{k}}+\sum_{n=0}^{N}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\|^{2}\right),

(1.3)

where we have substituted in the trajectory from (
1.1
).
The second term
\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\|^{2}
regularizes the problem by penalizing the magnitude of the parameters,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}
(i.e., minimum description length prior).
Stacking all the quantities up into vectors/matrices, we can write this problem cleanly as

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\left(\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}+\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}\right),

(1.4)

The solution to this least-squares problem is the solution to the following linear system of equations:

\left(\mathbf{B}^{T}\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{I}\right){\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbf{B}^{T}\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{y}.

(1.5)

One challenge with parametric methods is to choose the right basis functions
\mathbf{B}_{n}(t)
, which can be difficult in practice; too few and the trajectory does not have enough flexibility to fit the data, too many and the solution becomes overfit, although the regularization term can help with this.

The state at the measurement times is
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{B}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and so we can manipulate the linear system of equations above to become

\left(\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{K}^{-1}\right)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{y},

(1.6)

where
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{T}
can now be interpreted as a prior or regularizer term on the state
\mathbf{x}
.
1
1

1

Note that the
\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{x}
term in (
1.6
) corresponds exactly to the regularization term on
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in (
1.4
).

The leap from parametric to
nonparametric
comes from the observation that we do not actually need the individual basis functions, only their inner products in order to construct
\mathbf{K}
. As such, we can instead select a
kernel function

\mathbf{K}(t_{k},t_{\ell})
that defines the inner product between two points in time, and we can write the
kernel matrix
as

\mathbf{K}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}(t_{k},t_{\ell})\end{bmatrix},

(1.7)

which is block-wise populated with evaluations of the kernel function at all pairs of measurement times. This substitution is known as the
kernel trick
.

There are a number of technical advantages of the nonparametric approach over the parametric one. Selecting a kernel function rather than the individual basis functions provides more representational power and is inherently regularized without having to introduce and tune a minimum-description-length prior. The kernel function essentially builds in smoothness to the system and we can therefore also use it to determine what is going on between the measurements; this is the essence of
GP
regression
[
42
]
. In general, the inverse kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}^{-1}
may be dense. However, with the right choice of kernel function, we will show that it can be
block-tridiagonal
, which is a key advantage of our specific
GP
approach as it means large computational savings. Moreover, the kernel function can be chosen intuitively to represent a physically motivated motion prior, such as a constant-velocity or constant-acceleration prior, which is useful for many applications in robotics and computer vision. Finally, there is also a well-known connection between the sparsity pattern of the left-hand side of (
1.6
) and the structure of the equivalent factor graph that we will discuss in more detail in Chapter
3
.

Chapter 2
Gaussian-Process Regression

An inherent limitation of classical discrete-time optimization problems in robotics is that each state of interest must be represented
explicitly
as a variable. This can lead to complications when downstream robotics tasks, such as motion planning or control, require trajectory and uncertainty information at
arbitrary
times that cannot be known prior to solving the estimation problem
[
38
]
. Similar failings occur when performing estimation of
smooth shapes
(
e.g.
, for continuum robotics
[
33
]
). These issues can be readily handled by
continuous-time
estimation, which processes measurements at given times to estimate a trajectory that can be queried at
any
time by a downstream process.

In this chapter, we show how
GP
regression – a popular method for function approximation that fits with the Bayesian estimation paradigm
[
42
]
– can be used to carry out
continuous-time
estimation. Here, we primarily follow
[
8
]
to provide a background on
GP
regression and set the stage for the factor graph interpretation that will be the subject of the next chapter. Readers who are already familiar with the details of
GP
regression can safely skip this chapter.

2.1
Brief Derivation of GP Regression

We begin by considering systems with a continuous-time
GP
prior and a discrete-time, linear measurement model:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\mathcal{GP}(\check{\mathbf{x}}(t),\check{\mathbf{P}}(t,t^{\prime})),\qquad t_{0}<t,t^{\prime},

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{k}\mathbf{x}(t_{k})+\mathbf{n}_{k},\qquad t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{K},

(2.1b)

where
\mathbf{x}(t)
is the state,
\check{\mathbf{x}}(t)
is the mean function,
\check{\mathbf{P}}(t,t^{\prime})
is the covariance function,
\mathbf{y}_{k}
are measurements,
\mathbf{n}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{R}_{k})
is Gaussian measurement noise, and
\mathbf{C}_{k}
is the measurement model coefficient matrix. The state at
t=0
is defined by a prior,
\mathbf{x}(0)\sim\mathcal{N}(\check{\mathbf{x}}_{0},\check{\mathbf{P}}_{0})
. The first equation defines a
Gaussian process
that captures our prior belief about the likelihood of all possible trajectories that the state could take. Accordingly,
\check{\mathbf{P}}(t,t^{\prime})
is a
kernel function
that describes the covariance between the state at two different times,
t
and
t^{\prime}
. Figure
2.1
illustrates this conceptually.

Figure 2.1
:
In
GP
regression, we begin with some prior over trajectories, including mean and covariance functions. After incorporating measurements, we obtain a posterior distribution over trajectories that can be queried at any time.

We consider that we want to query the state at a number of times (
\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\ldots<\tau_{J}
) that may or may not be different from the measurement times (
t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{K}
).
The joint density between the state (at the query times) and the measurements (at the measurement times) is written as

p\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}\\
\mathbf{y}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}\\
\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{x}}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau\tau}&\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}\mathbf{C}^{T}\\
\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}^{T}&\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{C}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}(t_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{x}(t_{K})\end{bmatrix},\quad\check{\mathbf{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{x}}(t_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\check{\mathbf{x}}(t_{K})\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{x}_{\tau}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}(\tau_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{x}(\tau_{J})\end{bmatrix},\quad\check{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}=\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{x}}(\tau_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\check{\mathbf{x}}(\tau_{J})\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}_{K}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{C}=\mbox{diag}\left(\mathbf{C}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{C}_{K}\right),\quad\mathbf{R}=\mbox{diag}\left(\mathbf{R}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{R}_{K}\right),

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{P}}=\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{P}}(t_{i},t_{j})\end{bmatrix}_{ij},\quad\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}=\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{P}}(\tau_{i},t_{j})\end{bmatrix}_{ij},\quad\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau\tau}=\begin{bmatrix}\check{\mathbf{P}}(\tau_{i},\tau_{j})\end{bmatrix}_{ij}.

In
GP
regression, the matrix,
\check{\mathbf{P}}
, is known as the
kernel matrix
and comprises evaluations of the kernel covariance function between all pairs of measurement times. Factoring (
2.2
) via the Schur complement,

1

we can show that

p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}\biggl(\underbrace{\check{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}+\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}\mathbf{C}^{T}(\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{C}^{T}+\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{x}})}_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau},\;\mbox{\small mean}},\biggr.\\
\biggl.\underbrace{\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau\tau}-\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}\mathbf{C}^{T}(\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{C}^{T}+\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}^{T}}_{\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau\tau},\;\mbox{\small covariance}}\biggr),

(2.3)

for the density of the predicted state at the query times, given the measurements.

The expression simplifies further if we take the query times to be exactly the same as the measurement times (i.e.,
\tau_{k}=t_{k},K=J

\check{\mathbf{P}}=\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}=\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau\tau},

(2.4)

and then we can write

p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}\biggl(\underbrace{\check{\mathbf{x}}+\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{C}^{T}(\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{C}^{T}+\mathbf{R})^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{x}})}_{\hat{\mathbf{x}},\;\mbox{\small mean}},\biggr.\\
\biggl.\underbrace{\check{\mathbf{P}}-\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{C}^{T}(\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{C}^{T}+\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{C}\check{\mathbf{P}}^{T}}_{\hat{\mathbf{P}},\;\mbox{\small covariance}}\biggr).

(2.5)

We can rearrange this using the
matrix-inversion lemma
so that

p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}\biggl(\underbrace{\left(\check{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}+\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\right)^{-1}\left(\check{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\check{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{y}\right)}_{\hat{\mathbf{x}},\;\mbox{\small mean}},\biggr.\\
\biggl.\underbrace{\left(\check{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}+\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\right)^{-1}}_{\hat{\mathbf{P}},\;\mbox{\small covariance}}\biggr).

(2.6)

Further rearranging the mean expression, we have a linear system for
\hat{\mathbf{x}}

\left(\check{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}+\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\right)\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\check{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\check{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{y}.

(2.7)

The solution to this linear system gives us the estimate of the state at the measurement times.

If, after solving for the estimate at the measurement times, we later want to query the state at some other times of interest (
\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\ldots<\tau_{J}
), we can use the classic
GP

[
42
]
to do so:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}+\left(\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}\check{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\right)(\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\check{\mathbf{x}}),

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau\tau}+\left(\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}\check{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\right)\left(\hat{\mathbf{P}}-\check{\mathbf{P}}\right)\left(\check{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}\check{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}\right)^{T}.

(2.8b)

This is linear interpolation in the state variable (though not necessarily equivalent to linear interpolation in time).

In general, this
GP
approach has complexity
O(K^{3}+K^{2}J)
since the initial solve is
O(K^{3})
(for an arbitrary kernel function such as squared exponential) and the query is
O(K^{2}J)

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&&\\
\mathbf{A}_{K-1,0}&\mathbf{A}_{K-1,1}&\mathbf{A}_{K-1,2}&\cdots&\mathbf{1}&\\
\mathbf{A}_{K,0}&\mathbf{A}_{K,1}&\mathbf{A}_{K,2}&\cdots&\mathbf{A}_{K,K-1}&\mathbf{1}\end{bmatrix},

and we have simplified notation by writing
\mathbf{A}_{k,\ell}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(t_{k},t_{\ell})
.

Substituting our specific prior into (
2.7
), the linear system that we need to solve becomes

\left(\mathbf{A}^{-T}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}+\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\right)\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{A}^{-T}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{y},

(2.16)

which as yet does not seem to offer any advantage over (
2.7
). However, it turns out that
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
has the special form

\mathbf{A}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{1}&&&&&\\
-\mathbf{A}_{1,0}&\mathbf{1}&&&&\\
&-\mathbf{A}_{2,1}&\mathbf{1}&&&\\
&&-\mathbf{A}_{3,2}&\ddots&&\\
&&&\ddots&\mathbf{1}&\\
&&&&-\mathbf{A}_{K,K-1}&\mathbf{1}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.17)

Since
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
has only the main diagonal and the one below non-zero, and
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}
is block-diagonal, the inverse kernel matrix
\check{\mathbf{P}}^{-1}=(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}^{T})^{-1}=\mathbf{A}^{-T}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}
is exactly block-tridiagonal. Since
\mathbf{C}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{C}
is block-diagonal, it means the left-hand side of (
2.16
) is also block-tridiagonal. This means that we can solve the linear system in
O(K)
time instead of
O(K^{3})
for a dense inverse kernel matrix. This has been made possible by our specific choice of kernel function, built from an
LTI

SDE
.

We can also view (
2.16
) as the solution to the following least-squares optimization problem:

\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mbox{arg}\min_{\mathbf{x}}||\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{A}^{-1}\,\mathbf{x}||^{2}_{\mathbf{Q}}+||\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}||^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}.

(2.18)

Or, breaking it down by time steps,

\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mbox{arg}\min_{\mathbf{x}}||\check{\mathbf{x}}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{0}||^{2}_{\check{\mathbf{P}}_{0}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}||\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{A}_{k,k-1}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{v}_{k,k-1}||^{2}_{\mathbf{Q}_{k,k-1}}\\
+\sum_{k=0}^{K}||\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{C}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}||^{2}_{\mathbf{R}_{k}}.

(2.19)

It is clear from this expression that solving at the measurement times can be carried out using classical, discrete-time state estimation tools. Note that from the factor graph perspective presented in the next Chapter
3
, each of these terms accounts for exactly one factor.

3.1
A Brief Introduction to Factor Graphs

Figure 3.1
:
A simple factor graph for a classic estimation problem. The unary factor on
\mathbf{x}_{0}
represents the prior knowledge about the initial state, the binary factors represent the motion model, and the remaining unary factors represent the measurements. In practice, we can have much more complicated factor graphs with loops.

Typically in state estimation, we are interested in computing a posterior density representing the likelihood of a state,

p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y}),

(3.1)

where
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{K})
is the state at times
t_{0},\ldots,t_{K}
,
\mathbf{v}=(\check{\mathbf{x}}_{0},\mathbf{v}_{1,0},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{K,K-1})
is a set of inputs/commands, and
\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{y}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{K})
is a set of measurements. In most practical scenarios, we can factor this posterior density into a product of factors, each of which depends on only a subset of the variables in the problem. For example, we might have

p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y})\propto\underbrace{p(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\check{\mathbf{x}}_{0})}_{\phi(\mathbf{x}_{0})}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\underbrace{p(\mathbf{x}_{k}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v}_{k,k-1})}_{\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})}\prod_{k=0}^{K}\underbrace{p(\mathbf{y}_{k}|\mathbf{x}_{k})}_{\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{k})},

(3.2)

where each
\phi(\cdot)
and
\varphi(\cdot)
is a
factor
of the posterior density corresponding to motion priors and measurements, respectively. The factors can be
unary
(involving one variable),
binary
(involving two variables), or higher-order (involving more than two variables).

The normalization constant associated with this factorization is typically expensive to compute so we often satisfy ourselves with finding the
\mathbf{x}
that maximizes the posterior density, or equivalently the
\mathbf{x}
that minimizes the negative log-likelihood cost:

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x})=-\log\phi(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k-1})-\sum_{k=0}^{K}\log\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{k}).

(3.3)

This is a common approach in estimation, referred to as

MAP
(
MAP
)
estimation.

We can visually understand the factors by assembling them into a
factor graph
, which is a bipartite graph with two types of nodes: variable nodes and factor nodes. The variable nodes represent the variables in the problem, such as
\mathbf{x}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{K}
, and the factor nodes represent the factors, such as
\phi(\mathbf{x}_{0})
,
\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})
, and
\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{k})
. The edges connect variable nodes to factor nodes, indicating which variables are involved in each factor. This graphical representation allows us to see the dependencies between variables and factors in a very intuitive way. Figure
3.1
shows a simple factor graph for a classic discrete-time estimation problem.

Figure 3.2
:
Simple example of the elimination algorithm. The left shows the factor graph before elimination and the right shows the result of eliminating
\mathbf{x}
from the factor graph, resulting in a new factor and a conditional density for
\mathbf{x}
.

Aside from the benefits to intuition, factor graphs also lay the groundwork for efficient computation via the
elimination algorithm
. The key idea behind the iterations of this algorithm is shown in Figure
3.2
: we eliminate one variable at a time, and update the remaining factors in the graph accordingly. This update includes generating new factors between neighbouring, non-eliminated variables and identifying
conditional dependencies
of the eliminated variables (indicated by arrows in the factor graph).
Once all variables have been eliminated, we are left with a structure – the so-called
Bayes net
– that allows for efficient recovery of the optimal posterior density.

In the Appendix, we provide additional details about factor graphs, including the elimination algorithm (Section
A.1
), show standard manipulations of factor graphs (Section
A.2
), and apply the factor graph approach to linear, discrete-time estimation (Section
A.3
). We encourage readers that are not familiar with factor graphs to first read this appendix before proceeding to the next section.

In this section, we discuss querying the trajectory at some time(s) of interest,
\tau

||\mathbf{x}_{\tau}-\mathbf{A}_{\tau,k-1}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}-\mathbf{v}_{\tau,k-1}||^{2}_{\mathbf{Q}_{\tau,k-1}}+||\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{A}_{k,\tau}\mathbf{x}_{\tau}-\mathbf{v}_{k,\tau}||^{2}_{\mathbf{Q}_{k,\tau}}\\

(3.10)

where
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\tau}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\tau}
are the mean and covariance of the Gaussian conditional density for

Critically, the new binary factor between the measurement states,
\mathbf{x}_{k-1}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}

We want the posterior estimate for the query state,
p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y})

p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})=\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\tau},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\tau})

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau},\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}\right)

(3.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})p(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k}|\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{x}_{k-1}d\mathbf{x}_{k}.\qquad

p(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k}|\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}\\

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}

(3.13a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\tau}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\tau}+\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\tau}&{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\tau}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{k-1}&\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{k,k-1}^{T}\\

\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}_{\rm op}\mbox{Exp}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\approx\mathbf{T}_{\rm op}\underbrace{(\mathbf{I}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\wedge})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{approx.}\\

(4.3)

\approx\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{f}\left((\mathbf{I}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\wedge})\mathbf{T}_{\rm op}^{-1}\mathbf{p}\right)=\underbrace{\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{T}_{\rm op}^{-1}\mathbf{p})}_{\mathbf{e}_{\rm op}}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{p}}\bigg|_{\mathbf{T}_{\rm op}^{-1}\mathbf{p}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\wedge}\mathbf{T}_{\rm op}^{-1}\mathbf{p}\\

Representing uncertainty on Lie groups is also facilitated by the exponential map and a Lie algebra. We would like to have a Gaussian distribution on the Lie group. To accomplish this we define a Gaussian distribution on the Lie algebra, and then map it to the Lie group as follows:

\mathbf{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mathbf{T}},\hat{\mathbf{P}})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathbf{T}=\hat{\mathbf{T}}\,\mbox{Exp}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\quad{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\hat{\mathbf{P}}).

(4.5)

In other words, we define a zero-mean Gaussian distribution on the Lie algebra, and then map it to the Lie group using the exponential map and the closure property of the Lie group. This allows us to represent uncertainty on the Lie group in a way that is consistent with the geometry of the Lie group.

Chapter 5
Robotics Examples in GTSAM

GTSAM
is a widely used C++ library that leverages factor graphs to solve large-scale optimization problems in robotics and computer vision efficiently. One of the key contributions of our work is the introduction of Gaussian-process-based continuous-time (GP-CT) estimation into the existing
GTSAM

5.1
GTSAM Primer

GTSAM
provides a wide range of factor implementations that can be combined to construct arbitrary factor graphs.
In practice, users typically rely on existing factors or develop new ones tailored to their specific application.
Each factor is associated with a set of keys, which index the state variables in the factor graph.

A typical
GTSAM
workflow proceeds as follows:

1.

Define the factors and their associated symbols, indicating to which states each factor relates.

2.

Add the factors to a factor graph object.

3.

Specify initial values for all states involved in the graph.

4.

Pass the factor graph, initial values, and solver parameters to an optimizer (e.g., Gauss-Newton or Levenberg-Marquardt).

5.

Extract the estimated means from the optimizer, and optionally compute the associated covariances.

The implementation of our continuous-time framework within
GTSAM
maintains this workflow, as it mainly introduces new factor types that integrate seamlessly with the existing architecture.
In addition, we provide functionality to compute the mean and covariance of continuous-time states at arbitrary query times, which may differ from the discrete estimation times defined in the primary optimization problem.

5.2.2
Interpolating States with Measurements

We can treat a subset of the variables as interpolated states and use the methodology outlined in Section
3.3
and Appendix
B
to eliminate them from the optimization, while still accounting for the effect of factors that are defined on these states.
As mentioned in the preceding chapters, this allows the practitioner to reduce the number of variables in a given optimization problem, a key reason for using continuous-time estimation.

The independence approximation introduced in Appendix
B
allows us to continue to treat each factor defined on these states separately (rather than lumping them together).
Effectively, each factor defined on an interpolated state,
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
, can be mapped to a new factor on the bordering states, removing the need to actually solve for the interpolated states.

Our implementation allows a user to convert any
GTSAM
factor graph into an equivalent graph where all the interpolated states have been removed from the optimization.
The core element of this implementation is new interpolation factor
WnoaInterpFactor
that can be wrapped around any
GTSAM
factor defined on one or more interpolated variables.
This ‘wrapper’ factor automatically maps the errors and Jacobians to the bordering states via the
WNOA
interpolation equations and the chain rule.
The resulting factor is defined only with respect to non-interpolated variables.

Mathematically, our wrapper factor implements the conversion from a measurement factor defined on an interpolated state,
p(\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n}})
, to a measurement factor defined the bordering states (see (
B.10
)), but with two key generalizations. First, the development shown in Appendix
B
applies to variables defined in a vector space, but our implementation can be used with variables defined on Lie groups, using the development shown in Chapter
4
for continuous-time systems on Lie groups. Second, Appendix
B
is demonstrated for unary factors on the interpolated variables, but our wrapper factor implementation can be applied to any kind of
GTSAM
factor. As we show below, our framework can be applied to factors defined on any number of interpolated and non-interpolated variables.

Given the interpolated factor graph, we now show optimizing and using after-main-solve querying to extract solutions.

5.2.4
Speeding up Computation

The above implementation can introduce computational overhead because each wrapper factor processes interpolation independently.
As a result, factors relying on the same interpolated state repeatedly recompute its mean and the associated Jacobians from the boundary states.
To avoid this redundancy, we introduce the
WnoaFactorGraph
, a derived class of the
GTSAM

NonlinearFactorGraph
that has been modified to tracks all interpolated and estimated states.
During construction, the graph receives a mapping from each interpolated state to its boundary states.
The convenience function,
interpolateFactorGraph
, has a second template argument that determines the type of factor graph that it returns. By default, the template parameter is set to
NonlinearFactorGraph
, but it can also be set to
WnoaFactorGraph
to obtain a factor graph of this form.

The
WnoaFactorGraph
overrides the
error
and
linearize
methods to compute and cache interpolated state means and Jacobians once per iteration, then pass them by reference to all wrapper factors.
Wrapper factors simply retrieve these cached values during their own evaluations.
This eliminates redundant computation and yields substantial runtime improvements, particularly when multiple wrapper factors share the same interpolated states. Moreover, the existing
GTSAM
optimizers do not need to be modified to leverage these overridden methods.
Listing
6
shows an example usage of this functionality.

We can achieve additional computational speed-up by omitting the computation of the
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\tau}\mathbf{C}^{T}
term in the measurement covariance computation of our wrapper factors in (
B.10
), since the computation of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\tau}
ends up being costly.
We discuss the implications of omitting this terms with respect to accuracy and computational speed later in this section.

Lastly, we note that it may be feasible to approximate the Lie group Jacobians as identity when the relative pose change between consecutive boundary states is small.
This approximation can further reduce computational cost during derivative evaluation, while typically sacrificing only minimal accuracy and preserving overall convergence behavior.

In particular, interpolation is used to reduce the number of estimated states in the factor graph relative to a ‘full’ graph, which also includes the interpolated states.
While high-level descriptions are provided in the following subsections, readers are encouraged to explore the dataset examples themselves, which are available in the open-source repository associated with this paper,

5.3.1
1D: Giant Glass of Milk Dataset

In our first example dataset, we investigate a linear one-dimensional problem, in which a mobile robot drives back and forth in a straight line.
The experimental setup is shown in Figure
5.2
, consisting of the robot, two fixed guiding rails as well as a large cylinder resembling a
giant glass of milk
.
Both the robot and the cylinder are equipped with reflective markers, which are tracked by a ten-camera motion-capture system, providing ground-truth positions with millimeter-level accuracy.
Additionally, the robot is equipped with a laser rangefinder (Hokuyo URG-04LX) that provides 681 range measurements over a 240° horizontal field of view, centered straight ahead, as well as wheel odometers for measuring the robot’s speed.

Figure 5.2
:
A mobile robot driving between rails. It is equipped with an odometer to measure translational speed and a laser rangefinder to measure the range to the large white cylinder.

During data collection, the robot was driven back and forth along the two guiding rails for 20 minutes, logging laser rangefinder and odometer data at 10 Hz, while ground-truth positions were recorded at 70 Hz.
Moreover, the distance between the robot and the cylinder was extracted from each rangefinder scan by exploiting the cylinder’s circular shape.
This eliminates the need to process the raw laser scans directly, allowing the use of a simplified range measurement instead.
Given the known location of the cylinder, the range measurement can be treated as a noisy observation of the robot’s position.
The noise of each sensor was characterized using the ground-truth data provided by the motion-capture system.

For this, we set our problem up using

5.3.4
Key Takeaways and Guidelines

We have demonstrated practical examples of the proposed continuous-time estimation framework, implemented in
GTSAM
, across several datasets.
In the following, we outline practical guidelines to help readers set up their own estimation problems:

•

Interpolation of states without measurements requires
WNOA
motion model factors between states, but can be computed in a post-processing step that does not affect the runtime or accuracy of the main solve.

•

Interpolation of states with measurements can reduce the number of variables in the linear system solve at each iteration, but increases the time required for linearization of the resulting interpolated factors. Where possible in our implementation, we have used shared memory and parallelization to reduce this linearization cost.

•

Interpolation can substantially reduce computation while preserving accuracy, provided the interpolation intervals are not too large and the motion generally adheres to the chosen prior.

•

When sufficient computation is available, solving the full factor graph, i.e., including a state at every measurement time during optimization, remains the best option.

•

Using the “simplified noise model” (see Section
5.3.2
) for factors on interpolated states reduces computation, but comes at the cost of degraded accuracy of the mean estimate and overconfidence at estimation times compared to the full-graph solution. On the other hand, the “full noise model” increases computation time, but maintains higher accuracy at the estimation times and may be beneficial in scenarios where only the accuracy at a specific time (e.g., a single timestamp within a LiDAR scan) matters.

Conclusion and Outlook

We have revisited the
GP
approach to continuous-time estimation, through the lens of factor graphs. We hope the new insights this provides will lead to wider adoption of the approach, as well as new applications. To this end, we also provided implementations of the approach in
GTSAM
that we hope will be useful to the community.

Looking forward, we see several interesting directions for future work:

•

For computational reasons, we employed a sequence of local
GP
between our estimation times when working on Lie groups. While this works well in practice, it is not as elegant as the original linear-Gaussian theory, sometimes leading to artifacts in the estimated trajectory. Perhaps there are other ways to formulate the problem that would allow us to work with a single
GP
over the entire Lie group. For example, we are currently investigating the use of differential flatness to formulate the problem in a way that would allow us to work with a single
GP
over the entire Lie group along the lines of
[
27
]
who used a similar idea with parametric continuous-time estimation.

•

It could be of interest to explore the use of multi-dimensional
GP
to model more complex systems such as dynamic continuum robots
[
50
]
. This would require careful consideration of the kernel functions and the structure of the factor graph.

•

Our approach still requires the user to select the estimation times, which is currently done in a heuristic way. It would be interesting to explore how to automatically select the estimation times based on the data, perhaps using a data-driven approach or a more principled method based on information theory.
[
4
]
, for example, used a wavelet approach in the parametric setting.

Happy estimating!

A.1
The Elimination Algorithm

While it is possible to minimize the cost function in (
3.3
) directly using matrix algebra, we here lean into the factor-graph representation and present the general
elimination algorithm
, an equivalent way to solve the minimization problem. The key idea is to eliminate one variable at a time, updating the factors accordingly. Once we have eliminated all variables, our factor graph has been transformed into another graph called a
Bayes net
; there is then a simple procedure to compute the desired posterior density from the Bayes net.

Mathematically, the elimination algorithm starts from a factor graph and ends with a new factorization of the posterior in the form

p(\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{s}_{0})p(\mathbf{x}_{1}|\mathbf{s}_{1})\cdots p(\mathbf{x}_{K})=\prod_{k=0}^{K}p(\mathbf{x}_{k}|\mathbf{s}_{k}),

(A.1)

where
\mathbf{s}_{k}
is the
separator
for
\mathbf{x}_{k}
, defined as the set of variables on which
\mathbf{x}_{k}
is conditioned after it is eliminated.
Without loss of generality, here
k=0\ldots K
is the order in which the variables have been eliminated.

Once all the conditional densities are computed, we can work backwards from
K
to
0
to compute the posterior densities for each variable. This operation is well-defined because there are can be no (directed) cycles in the resulting graph; a consequence of the fact that each variable’s separator cannot include any variables that have already been eliminated.

Each step of the elimination algorithm consists of three operations: (i) select a variable to eliminate, (ii) multiply all factors involving that variable together to form the
product factor
, and (iii) factor the product factor into a
conditional density
on the eliminated variable and a
residual factor
on the separator variables. The new factor is then added to the factor graph, and the eliminated variable is removed from the graph. This process is repeated until all variables have been eliminated.

In a bit more detail, consider the example factor graph on the left of Figure
A.1
. Suppose we select
\mathbf{x}
as the variable to eliminate, then the product factor is given by

\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=\phi(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}_{1})\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s}_{2}),

(A.2)

where
\mathbf{s}_{1}
and
\mathbf{s}_{2}
are two other variables that share factors with
\mathbf{x}
; the separator for
\mathbf{x}
is then
\mathbf{s}=\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2}\}
. The product factor is then factored into a conditional density on
\mathbf{x}
and a new factor on the separator variables:

\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})=p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s})\,\phi(\mathbf{s}).

(A.3)

The key to this last factorization is that
\phi(\mathbf{s})
has no dependence on
\mathbf{x}
; it is the factor that would result if
\mathbf{x}
were
marginalized
from the factor graph. After all variables have been eliminated, we are left with a graph with only directed arrows called the Bayes net.

Figure A.1
:
Example of the elimination algorithm. The left shows the factor graph before elimination and the right shows the result of eliminating
\mathbf{x}
from the factor graph, resulting in a new factor on the separator variables and a conditional density for
\mathbf{x}
.

The elimination algorithm is quite powerful in that it can be applied to any factor graph. However, it is particularly useful for problems with a lot of structure, such as those arising in robotics, as we can exploit the sparsity of the factor graph in the various operations. Here, we will only need to work with factors and densities that are Gaussian, and we use the next section to show that the elimination algorithm can be carried out in closed form when the factors are Gaussian. This will allow us to carry out estimation efficiently and effectively.

A.2
Gaussian Factors, Elimination, Fusion

Now that we understand factor graphs and the general strategy for carrying out estimation, we show that when our factors are Gaussian we can carry out elimination in closed form. We know that a Gaussian density has the form

p(\mathbf{x})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}||\mathbf{x}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}||^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\right),

(A.4)

where
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
is the mean value and
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
is the covariance.
After taking the negative logarithm, we will only need the
||\mathbf{x}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}||^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
part, which forms a term in the negative-log likelihood cost that we are seeking to minimize; we will often then label our factors on our factor graphs using this cost term for simplicity. We may also sometimes write
p(\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
as a shorthand for Gaussian densities.

In general, we can use the following relationship – based on the Schur complement – to carry out elimination when all the factors are Gaussian:

\underbrace{\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\mathbf{s}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{x}\\
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{s}\end{bmatrix}\right\|^{2}_{\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{xx}&{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{xs}\\
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{sx}&{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{ss}\end{bmatrix}}}_{-\log p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s})}\\
=\underbrace{||\mathbf{x}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{x}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{xs}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{ss}^{-1}(\mathbf{s}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{s})||^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{xx}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{xs}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{ss}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{sx}}}_{-\log p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{s})}+\underbrace{||\mathbf{s}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{s}||^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{ss}}}_{-\log p(\mathbf{s})}.\\

(A.5)

Here
\mathbf{x}
is the state to be eliminated and
\mathbf{s}
is the separator state. The term on the left is the (negative log of the)
product factor
in a standardized form. The second term is the (negative log of the)
conditional density
and the third term is the (negative log of the)
residual factor
on the separator. This expression can be used to carry out any Gaussian elimination, including grouped-state elimination.

\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{x}
. This is a specific example of the general Gaussian elimination formula in (
A.5

For example, consider the situation on the left of Figure
A.2

\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{x}

\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{k,k-1}=\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{k}\left(\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{k-1,f}\mathbf{A}_{k,k-1}^{T}\check{\mathbf{P}}_{k,f}^{-1}\right)^{T}

(A.9)

and can be calculated during the backward pass. The computational complexity of the

O(K)
, in other words, linear in the length of the trajectory.

Appendix B
Interpolating Multiple States with Measurements

Discrete-time state estimation often runs into trouble when dealing with measurement data from many unsynchronized, high-rate sensors,
e.g.
,
lidar
,
radar
,
imu
and rolling shutter cameras. The naive approach of defining a state for every measurement may be untenable in practice due to the size of the resulting optimization problem. Methods to aggregate measurement data across time can lead to tractable problems, but also introduce motion distortion errors
[
48
]
.
On the other hand, continuous-time estimation offers a principled alternative to the approximations that are typically required to aggregate high-rate sensor data.

In this chapter, we show that continuous-time estimation can be used to include the
effect
of measurements without needing to explicitly define a state variable at their associated measurement times. Instead, we can use
GP
interpolation to reformulate the associated measurement cost terms as a function of a
reduced set of states
that are included in the optimization problem. The size of this reduced set then leads to a clear trade-off between the accuracy and compute time.

We will see that the factor graph lens continues to bring clarity to the underlying mechanisms of this approach, as well as shedding new light on approximations that are commonly used in practice. However, in contrast to the previous chapter, we now consider the case where interpolated states
do
have measurements associated with them.

In the remainder of this chapter, we show that the elimination operations and some suitable approximations that will lead to a practical approach to dealing with asynchronous, high-frequency sensor data.

B.1
Grouped Interpolation

The core idea of this chapter is shown in Figure
B.1
: we wish to interpolate a group of states,
\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{N}}
, to reduce the size of the estimation problem to be solved. In the context of high-rate or asynchronous sensor applications, these states are introduced to register the exact measurement time of the data.

In the previous chapter, we saw that interpolation of a continuous-time variables is equivalent to including the variable in the factor graph and subsequently eliminating it. By extension, eliminating a group of states en masse leads to the factor graph in the bottom left of the figure. The measurements associated with the interpolated variables are collected into a single factor connecting the bordering states,
\mathbf{x}_{k-1}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}
. The resulting conditional density is expressed over the joint variable
\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}
, meaning it includes the full covariance between all the eliminated states. If the cross-covariances are not required, we can drop these and only compute individual marginal conditional densities as in the bottom right of the figure.

Figure B.1
:
To reduce the number of states in the factor graph during the main solve, we can eliminate several states at once, even if they have measurements associated with them. An example of this is classic IMU preintegration.

Grouped-state Gaussian elimination is handled by the general formula in (
A.5
). If we do not want the full covariance between the eliminated states, we can compute only the desired diagonal blocks of
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{xx}-{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{xs}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{ss}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{sx}
, resulting in the marginal conditional densities for each of the individual eliminated states.

In our context, we may not even care about solving the problem at the interpolated states since they were introduced only to account for certain measurements. In this case, we can just drop the conditionals from the factor graph entirely.
1
1

1

We may not want to do this in nonlinear factor graphs, where the interpolated states can still serve as linearization points for the remaining factors.
We are left with a single, reduced factor between the states of interest,
\mathbf{x}_{k-1}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}
. The remaining sections will show how this factor can be further decomposed.

B.2
Decomposing the Residual Factor

An important remaining question is whether there is additional structure to be exploited in the residual factor between the bordering states,
\mathbf{x}_{k-1}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}
, after the grouped-state elimination. It turns out that this factor can actually be split into two factors; one corresponding to our prior model and the other corresponding to the measurements on the eliminated states.
To see why this is true, consider that the probability density function of the original factor graph in Figure
B.1
can be factored into a term that contains only measurement factors and a term that contains only motion-model prior factors,

p(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v})\propto p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}})p(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v}).

(B.1)

In the original factor graph, the measurement and motion model terms are further factored as follows:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{n=1}^{N}p(\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n}}),

(B.2a)

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{k}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{N}},\mathbf{v}_{k,\tau_{N}})\prod\limits_{n=2}^{N}p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n}}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n-1}},\mathbf{v}_{\tau_{n},\tau_{n-1}})

(B.2b)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\;p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1}}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v}_{\tau_{1},k-1}).

However, given a motion prior of the type discussed in this article, it turns out that the second term can be alternatively factored as

p(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v})=p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v})p(\mathbf{x}_{k}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v}_{k,k-1}),

(B.3)

where

p(\mathbf{x}_{k}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v}_{k,k-1})=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{A}_{k,k-1}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}+\mathbf{v}_{k,k-1},\mathbf{Q}_{k,k-1}\right),

(B.4)

and, as above, the conditional density,
p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v})
, can be found by applying the general formula in (
A.5
).

The residual factor,
\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})
, is obtained by marginalizing the full joint distribution with respect to the interpolated variables:

\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})=\int p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}})p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v})p(\mathbf{x}_{k}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v}_{k,k-1})\,d\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}.

(B.5)

The rightmost term,
p(\mathbf{x}_{k}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v}_{k,k-1})
, can be pulled out of the integral and corresponds to a motion-prior factor between bordering states,
\mathbf{x}_{k}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k-1}
. The remaining terms in the integral correspond to the measurement distribution conditioned on the border states. Thus, our original factor in Figure
B.1
can be broken into two parts:

\phi(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})=\underbrace{\int p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}})p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v})\,d\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}}_{\phi_{y}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})}\,\underbrace{p(\mathbf{x}_{k}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v}_{k,k-1})}_{\phi_{v}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})}.

(B.6)

The resulting factor graph from this factorization is shown in Figure
B.2
in the bottom left.

Figure B.2
:
Following on to Figure
B.1
, we can exactly decompose the residual factor into two factors: a measurement factor
\phi_{y}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})
and a motion-prior factor
\phi_{v}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})
(bottom left). By making a further approximation, we can drop the cross-covariances between the interpolated states, resulting in a factor graph that can be solved as if the measurements were independent (bottom right).

At this point, it is worth noting that, even though the original measurement distribution,
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}})
, can be factorized into individual measurements, our new measurement distribution cannot be similarly factorized. This is because the interpolated states,
\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}
, are fully correlated variables.
2
2

2

This correlation arises due to the fact that these variables are assumed to come from the same trajectory in time. Their distributions are linked by their dependence on the bordering states.
When we eliminate these states in the factor graph, we necessarily obtain a new (large) lumped measurement factor,
\phi_{y}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})
, that cannot be further separated without making additional approximations.

B.3
Independence Approximation

To keep the batch optimization lightweight, we may want to treat each measurement as a separate factor, even after marginalization of the interpolated states. This can be accomplished by dropping the cross-covariances between the interpolated variables. In other words, we can make the approximating assumption that the distribution of the
\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}
variables are independent when conditioned on the bordering states:

p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1:N}}|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v})=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{N}p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n}}|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v}).

(B.7)

The exact form of the individual distributions was derived above in Section
3.2.2
and is given by

p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n}}|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v})=\mathcal{N}\biggl(\underbrace{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\tau_{n}}+\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\tau_{n}}&{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\tau_{n}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}\\
\mathbf{x}_{k}\end{bmatrix}}_{\check{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau_{n}}},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\tau_{n}}\biggr),

(B.8)

In turn, our lumped measurement factor can now be broken into factors corresponding to individual measurements:

\displaystyle\phi_{y}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{N}\phi_{y_{n}}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k}),

(B.9a)

\displaystyle\phi_{y_{n}}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int p(\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n}})p(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n}}|\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{v})d\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n}}.

(B.9b)

Assuming that the measurements are linear,
p(\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n}})=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{C}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{n}},\mathbf{R}_{n}\right)
, it is fairly straightforward to show that these measurement factors have the following distribution:

\phi_{y_{n}}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\mathbf{x}_{k})=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{C}_{n}\check{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau_{n}},\mathbf{C}_{n}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\tau_{n}}\mathbf{C}_{n}^{T}+\mathbf{R}_{n}\right).

(B.10)

The interpretation of this distribution is straightforward: the mean is obtained by applying the measurement model to the bordering states after mapping through the motion model to the measurement time. The covariance of the measurement is inflated proportionate to the temporal distance from the bordering states to measurement times, accounting for the uncertainty of the model.

Dropping the cross-covariance allows us to re-separate the measurements factors even after marginalization. This approximation also treats a set of correlated measurements as if they were independent. In practice, this typically does not affect the mean estimate, but can lead to overconfident estimates (i.e., artificially reduces the estimated posterior covariance).

Many prior works in continuous-time estimation – including the
STEAM
framework – further approximate the measurement distribution by removing the
\mathbf{C}_{i}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\tau_{i}}\mathbf{C}_{i}^{T}
term from the covariance
[
15
]
. This has the effect of removing any dependence of measurement certainty on the time that measurement was observed.

Our full procedure in this chapter is summarized in the factor graphs of Figure
B.2
: the final factor graph no longer requires separate states at each measurement time. Moreover, the new factors correctly account for the measurement times of the data, providing a principled way to deal with high-rate or asynchronous sensor data without needing to define a state for each measurement time.

Although we considered unary prior factors on the interpolated states above, the derivation shown here can be easily extended to factors that involve other variables. For instance, a binary factor between a landmark variable and the interpolated variable would lead to a ternary factor between the bordering variables and the landmark variable.
