The synergistic antimicrobial effect of tetrasodium EDTA and chlorhexidine against Enterobacterales isolates from hospital carriers
Authors: Niloofar Kiaheyrati, Fatemeh Fardsanei, Maliheh Charkhchian, Mahnaz Mohammadi, Amir Javadi, Farhad Nikkhahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital-acquired infections are a major concern in healthcare settings, often involving Gram-negative bacilli that affect both patients and healthcare personnel. This study aimed to assess the antimicrobial effects of chlorhexidine and EDTA, alone and in combination, on Gram-negative bacilli isolated from carriers, along with their antibiotic resistance profiles and biofilm-forming abilities.MethodsThis cross-sectional study collected 390 samples from hospital personnel, patients and environmental surfaces to investigate Gram-negative bacilli. Antibiotic susceptibility, biofilm formation, and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of chlorhexidine, with and without EDTA, were also determined. PCR also was used to detect biocide resistance genes (qacE, qacEΔ1, CepA).ResultsA total of 120 Gram-negative bacilli were isolated and subsequently confirmed through biochemical tests. Klebsiella pneumoniae was the predominant bacterial species, accounting for 70% (n=84). Background

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) cause hundreds of millions of patients to be infected annually worldwide, resulting in extended hospitalization, elevated healthcare expenses, and a substantial burden of morbidity and mortality [1]. In recent years, a marked rise in hospital-acquired infections has been observed, closely associated with the escalating prevalence of multidrug-resistant pathogens. Hospital environments serve as optimal reservoirs for the proliferation and transmission of pathogenic microorganisms, including bacterial and yeast species [2, 3]. Microbial flora transmission via hospital personnel’s hands and intimate patient contact has been identified as a plausible method of nosocomial pathogen spread. Hand washing is recognized as the most crucial method for reducing the incidence of infections acquired in hospitals [4].

Hospital medical facilities are also viewed as a double-edged sword. A lot of medical devices that are used to treat illnesses can potentially lead to serious medical issues known as nosocomial infections [5, 6]. Gram-negative bacilli (GNB) cause more than 50% of hospital-acquired infections. The ESKAPE pathogens, including Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp., represent the most formidable nosocomial threats due to their high prevalence, multidrug resistance, and association with HAIs. Of these, K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp. are Gram-negative bacilli notorious for their ability to evade antibiotics through mechanisms such as extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production, carbapenem resistance, and biofilm formation [7–9]. These organisms frequently colonize medical devices, ICU surfaces, and the hands of healthcare workers, facilitating persistent outbreaks in clinical settings. Their resilience underscores the urgent need for novel antimicrobial strategies, such as synergistic disinfectant combinations, to curb their transmission. The efficacy of biocides is influenced by multiple factors, including concentration, bacterial physiological state (biofilm vs. planktonic), and the presence of biocide resistance genes [10, 11]. Given these considerations, there is legitimate concern that improper biocide usage, such as application at excessively high or suboptimal concentrations, or repeated exposure to subinhibitory concentrations may promote the selection of biocide-tolerant strains. This tolerance can compromise biocide effectiveness and potentially contribute to cross-resistance with antibiotics, ultimately driving the emergence of MDR pathogens. A prominent mechanism contributing to biocide resistance is the production of efflux systems associated with qac genes (qacE and its mutant variant qacE∆1) and the CepA gene [12, 13]. Conversely, studies demonstrate that chelating agents such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) can potentiate the antimicrobial activity of biocidal compounds. As a well-characterized metal chelator, EDTA exerts its effects through two primary mechanisms: (1) disruption of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) structure in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, and (2) modulation of membrane permeability, thereby enhancing bacterial susceptibility to antimicrobial agents [14]. Moreover, EDTA’s capacity to sequester cations (Mg2+, Ca2+, and Fe3) can disrupt bacterial biofilms, preventing and lowering the risk of biofilm development and colonization [15]. These properties position EDTA as a promising adjuvant for antimicrobial formulations targeting Gram-negative pathogens.

While the antimicrobial activity of various disinfectants has been widely investigated, most studies have focused on clinical infection isolates rather than carrier-derived strains. The evaluation of isolates obtained from hospital carriers, including healthcare workers and environmental surfaces, may offer valuable information regarding the circulation of resistant and biofilm-producing bacteria within healthcare systems. Accordingly, this study was designed to comprehensively assess the role of hospital personnel and environmental surfaces as potential reservoirs of Gram-negative nosocomial pathogens. The current investigation sought to assess the biofilm formation ability of isolates using a microtiter plate assay, in addition to identifying the presence of efflux pump genes (qacE, qacEΔ1, and CepA) through conventional PCR techniques among the isolates.

Methods

Sampling

This cross-sectional study was conducted between January 2023 and November 2024. Overall, 390 samples; 160 samples from 80 personnel and 150 samples were collected from 75 hospitalized patients in various departments, including the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), dialysis, and emergency wards, and 80 samples from the hospital environment and devices were collected. In this study, informed consent was obtained from all study participants, including hospital personnel and patients, before sample collection. All participants received adequate information regarding the study objectives and procedures and voluntarily signed consent forms prior to enrollment.

Sampling from carriers was conducted using sterile swabs moistened with saline solution, which were used to collect specimens from the anterior region of both nostrils and from the hands, particularly the nails. Samples were also collected via swabs from hospital equipment that had been in contact with patients, such as beds and bedding, as potential indirect carriers.

Bacterial isolation and identification

Sampling Swabs were maintained in TSB medium and subsequently transported to medical microbiology laboratories, where they were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The samples were cultured on blood agar, Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar (Merck, Germany), and MacConkey (MAC) agar (Merck, Germany) to isolate a diverse range of microorganisms. Subsequently, further biochemical assays were conducted, including oxidase, catalase, urease, carbohydrate utilization tests, motility assessments, and staining methods such as gram staining. Ultimately, the strains that exhibited Gram-negative bacilli characteristics, tested negative for oxidase, and belonged to the Enterobacteriaceae family, yet could not be identified using the IMViC test were confirmed through the API-20E test.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antibiotic susceptibility tests for Enterobacterales isolates were performed using the Kirby-Bauer Disk diffusion method, according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute 2024 (CLSI) guidelines. Cefotaxime (30 µg), Cefotaxime/ clavulanic acid (30 µg), Ceftazidime (30 µg), Ceftazidime/clavulanic acid (30 µg), Cefoxitin (30 µg), Cefepime (30 µg), Meropenem (10 µg), Tetracycline (30 µg), Levofloxacin(5 µg), Gentamicin (10 µg) and Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (25 µg), were used in this study for Enterobacteriaceae isolates. The isolates were categorized as resistant, intermediate, and susceptible based on zone of inhibition in accordance with CLSI, 2024. Colistin sensitivity was also determined using the colistin broth dilution elution (CBDE) method according to CLSI guidelines. MDR was defined as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories. The combined test method also was used to identify ESBL/AmpC-producing strains [16].

Identification of AmpC and ESBL-producing strains

To identify these resistance phenotypes, the Combined Test method was employed in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI 2024) guidelines [16]. Ceftazidime (30 µg), Ceftazidime/Clavulanic Acid, Cefotaxime (30 µg), Cefotaxime/Clavulanic Acid, and Cefoxitin were used for this purpose. Isolates exhibiting a zone of inhibition diameter for the cephalosporin disk combined with clavulanic acid that was ≥ 5 mm larger than that of the cephalosporin disk without clavulanic acid were phenotypically identified as ESBL-producing bacteria. Additionally, Screening for AmpC β-lactamase production is often initiated by cefoxitin resistance, followed by confirmatory phenotypic tests such as inhibitor-based assays using boronic acid. Boronic acid acts as a reversible inhibitor of AmpC enzymes, and when combined with antibiotic disks, an increase in the zone of inhibition compared to the antibiotic alone suggests AmpC production. This method has been shown to be a practical and effective phenotypic assay for detecting AmpC β-lactamase producers among Enterobacteriaceae isolates [17]. E. coli ATCC 25,922 was used as the ESBL-negative control strain, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700,603 was used as the ESBL-positive control strain.

Briefly, the isolates were incubated at 37 °C for 20 h after being cultured on trypticase soy agar (TSA). Two colonies were selected and inoculated into 400 µL of Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer, heated at 100 °C for 10 min, and then cooled down on ice for 15 min. Biofilm formation

The formation of biofilms was studied using a 96-well microtiter plate technique. Bacterial cultures were grown in a TSB medium containing 5%sucrose and then adjusted to a standard concentration (0.5 Mc Farland) before being added to each microplate well. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 18 to 24 h. Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 and Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) medium were the positive and negative controls, respectively. Following three quick washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and cell fixation with methanol 95% solution, the plate was stained with 200 µL of 1% crystal violet (CV) for 15 min. The dye was taken out, and the wells were rinsed three times with PBS. Subsequently, the biofilms formed were dissolved with 200 µL of 33% acetic acid for 30 min. A microtiter plate reader (BioTek, Epoch, USA) was used to measure the optical absorbance at 570 nm (OD570, ODC570). Biofilms were categorized into four groups based on the ODc value: OD ≤ ODc (non-biofilm formation), ODc < OD ≤ 2 ODc (weak-biofilm formation), 2 ODc < OD ≤ 4 ODc (medium-biofilm formation), and OD > 4 ODc (strong-biofilm formation) [24].

Disinfectant susceptibility testing

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC₉₀) is defined as the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agent that inhibits 90% of bacterial growth. Briefly, the experimental design and MIC-MBC procedures were adapted from the protocol outlined by Tamar Leshem et al. [10]. The checkerboard microdilution method was performed in a 96-well plate to evaluate the interaction between EDTA and chlorhexidine (CHG), with EDTA arranged in columns and CHG in rows. Each received 100 µL of EDTA, 100 µL of CHG, and 100 µL of a bacterial suspension (~ 10⁵ CFU/mL), prepared by diluting a 0.5 McFarland standard 1:100, following CLSI guidelines [16]. After incubation at 37 °C for 18–24 h, the wells were assessed for growth inhibition to determine whether the combination was synergistic, additive, or antagonistic [20]. The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) is the concentration that eradicates ≥ 99.9% of microorganisms. A 100 µl sample from the wells that represented the MIC and wells treated with the CHG concentrations just below and just above the MIC was transferred to a Muller Hinton agar in order to establish MBC, as advised by the ASM recommendations [24]. The concentration that killed more than 99.9% of the colonies was identified by counting the colonies after incubation (24 h, 37 °C).

Chlorhexidine time–concentration assay

A microdilution-based exposure assay was conducted to assess the time- and concentration-dependent bactericidal activity of chlorhexidine. Chlorhexidine digluconate solutions were prepared at concentrations of 5%, 2.5%, 1.25%, 0.62%, 0.31%, 0.15%, 0.07%, 0.03%, 0.01%, 0.009%, 0.004%, and 0.002%. Test bacterial suspensions were adjusted to ~ 105 CFU/mL and exposed to each concentration of chlorhexidine for predetermined contact times: 5 min, 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h at room temperature. After each contact time, 100 µL of the samples were plated on Muller Hinton agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Bacterial growth was checked by observing whether colonies appeared or not [25].

Investigation of detergent resistance genes

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted to identify the presence of the qacE, qacΔE, and CepA genes using primer sequences listed in Table 1. The PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to assess the characteristics of the study. Pearson chi-square or Fisher’s exact test and McNemar’s test was used to determine significant differences between proportion. P values of < 0.05 were considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed by using SPSS version 16.0 statistical software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Bacterial isolation and identification

Out of 390 samples collected from carriers, a total of 120 Gram-negative bacilli were isolated and subsequently confirmed through biochemical tests. Among the 120 collected samples, 87 were isolated from the nasal and nail swabs of hospital staff and patients, identified as direct carriers.

The remaining 33 samples were collected from the hospital’s environment, representing indirect carriers. Among the total number of direct carriers, 49.5%were female and 50.5% were male, with ages ranging from 26 to 90 years. Klebsiella pneumonia was the predominant bacteria species 72.5%(n = 87) followed by Escherichia coli 14.2%(n = 17). The hands exhibited the highest level of contamination, both in terms of the quantity and the variety of isolated organisms. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The resistance patterns of the tested 5 antimicrobial categories, which consist of 10 antimicrobial agents, against all Gram-negative bacilli, are detailed in Fig. 1. Based on the results, the highest antibiotic resistance was observed against Cefoxitin 36.7% (n = 44), followed by Tetracycline at 33.3% (n = 40) and Third-generation Cephalosporins and Trimethoprim/Sulfamethoxazole at 22.5% (n = 27). The highest antibiotic susceptibility was observed for Cefepime, Gentamicin and Meropenem at 18.3% (n = 22), 16.6% (n = 20) and 16.6% (n = 20) respectively. Additionally, the Colistin Broth Disk Elution (CBDE) method was employed to assess resistance to Colistin. It was found that 16.6% (n = 20) of the samples exhibited resistance to Colistin, with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) exceeding 4 µg/ml. Based on the present findings, 34 (28.3%) isolates were multidrug-resistant (MDR).

Detection of β-lactamase-encoding genes

Phenotypic analysis revealed that 12.5% (15/120) of the isolates exhibited extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production. Notably, all ESBL-positive isolates harbored the blaTEM gene (15/15), as confirmed by molecular analysis. 60% (9/15) and 46.6% (7/15) of the isolates carried the blaSHV and blaCTX−M genes respectively. All ESBLs producers and Cefoxitin resistant GNB were screened and confirmed for AmpC production. Of all tested Gram-negative bacilli, 21% (25/120) were found to be AmpC beta-lactamase producers. Molecular characterization revealed all AmpC-producing strains carried the blaDHA gene, while 72%(18/25) isolates additionally carried the blaCIT resistance determinant. Among the isolates, 21.6% (26/120) of strains were resistant to carbapenems. Of these, 9 strains harbored the blaNDM−1 gene, 4 carried the blaIMP gene, 3 carried the blaVIM gene, and 10 harbored the blaOXA−48 gene. None of the strains carried the blaKPC gene. Disinfectant susceptibility testing

A statistically significant difference in chlorhexidine (CHG) susceptibility was observed among the various bacterial species tested (P ≤ 0.05). Among these, Escherichia coli isolates demonstrated the highest sensitivity to CHG, whereas Klebsiella pneumoniae exhibited the lowest levels of susceptibility. To evaluate the interaction between chlorhexidine and EDTA, a checkerboard assay was performed. All confirmed Enterobacterales isolates were included in the synergistic analysis to avoid selection bias and to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the antimicrobial interaction. The FICI values varied between 0.2 and 1.06 among the tested isolates. Based on these results, synergy (FICI ≤ 0.5) was observed in 106 (88.3%) of the isolates, an additive effect (0.5 < FICI ≤ 1) in 9 (7.5%), and an indifferent effect (1 < FICI ≤ 4) in 5 (4.2%). No antagonism (FICI > 4) was detected in any of the isolates. Detailed data including MIC values, MBC results, and dilution percentages for both agents are presented in Tables 5 and 6.

Chlorhexidine time–concentration assay

Chlorhexidine concentrations of 0.31% and above indicated complete bacterial inhibition at all points assessed (from 5 min up to 4 h), indicating rapid and sustained antimicrobial activity at these concentrations. Intermediate concentrations (0.07% and 0.15%) showed a clear time-dependent killing pattern, suggesting that prolonged exposure enhances the efficacy of submaximal concentrations. Interestingly, at lower concentrations of chlorhexidine (e.g., 0.03%, 0.01%, and 0.009%), bacterial growth was completely inhibited after 1 and 2 h of exposure.

However, growth was observed again after 4 h of exposure at the same concentrations (Fig. 4).

Investigation of detergent resistance genes

PCR was performed to determine the correlation between reduced susceptibility with qacE, qacEΔ1 and CepA genes as specific resistance genes to biocides. The qacE gene was seen in 20 (16.6%), the qacEΔ1 gene was detected in 11 isolates (9.1%), while the CepA genes were found in 15 isolates (12.5%) of Gram-negative bacilli. Furthermore, 8 isolates (6.6%) were found to simultaneously harbor qacE and CepA, while 7 isolates (5.8%) carried both qacE and qacEΔ1 genes.

Discussion

Hospital-acquired infections are a significant concern in healthcare settings and are among the most common complications observed in hospitalized individuals. These infections can be transmitted to hospitalized patients and individuals in close contact with them, including healthcare personnel and visitors [26]. Additionally, discharged patients may serve as carriers, which might distribute the pathogens into the community. The prevalence of Gram-negative species on the hands of personnel in various global investigations ranges from 21% to 86%. The current study has reported a 30.76% frequency of Gram-negative species, which is consistent with the global prevalence reports [27]. Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most frequently isolated Gram-negative species, accounting for 72.5% of the total Gram-negative isolates. Among the tested antibiotics, Cefoxitin exhibited the highest resistance rate (36.7%), followed by Tetracycline (33.3%). Third-generation cephalosporins and Trimethoprim/Sulfamethoxazole showed equal resistance levels at 22.5%, indicating a concerning trend in multidrug resistance patterns. Although the overall level of resistance was not alarmingly high, a notable concern arises from the strong biofilm-forming capacity, with 58.3% of the isolates exhibiting strong biofilm production. This percentage exceeds that reported by Aimen Fatima et al. [28]. It is important to emphasize that the isolates were obtained from asymptomatic carriers. These individuals, despite not being infected themselves, have the potential to act as reservoirs and facilitate the transmission of resistant bacteria to vulnerable patients within the healthcare setting.

In healthcare centers, It may be transmitted through direct contact with contaminated skin or shared surfaces, particularly when healthcare professionals fail to perform proper hand hygiene after examining an infected patient [29, 30]. Microorganisms are thought to survive and grow in the cracks, chips, or peeling sections of artificial nails, where they are protected from the antimicrobial effects of handwashing with soap or alcohol-based disinfectants [31]. Nurses with artificial nails harbored greater numbers of gram-negative rods both before and after handwashing than nurses with natural nails. Although hand hygiene is widely recognized as an effective method for reducing the transmission of microorganisms, the presence of artificial nails may compromise its efficacy [32]. The majority of female staff members who tested positive were found to be wearing artificial nails, which may have contributed to the transmission of infection at the hospital level. This is particularly concerning given the inadequate hand disinfection practices observed and the limited use of sterile gloves during clinical procedures. Considering the high prevalence of Gram-negative bacteria among hospital staff, the effectiveness of disinfectants in reducing microbial transmission becomes a critical point of concern. The results regarding the effect of chlorhexidine alone on isolates obtained from carriers demonstrated that the MIC₉₀ was generally 0.009% (90 µg/ml). At this concentration, all bacterial growth was effectively inhibited. However, the bactericidal effect of chlorhexidine was observed at a higher concentration, specifically at 0.019% (190 µg/ml), indicating the threshold required for complete bacterial eradication. According to the findings of this study, the combination of chlorhexidine with EDTA exhibited a synergistic effect, resulting in a significant reduction in both MIC and MBC values. Specifically, the MIC decreased from 0.009% to 0.002%, and the MBC was reduced from 0.019% to 0.004%. A notable shift in MIC distribution was observed toward lower CHG concentrations in the combination treatment. While no isolates exhibited MIC at the lowest tested concentrations of 0.0006% and 0.0003% when exposed to CHG alone, 18.3% and 17.5% of isolates, respectively, exhibited MIC at these concentrations in the presence of EDTA. The observed synergistic impact of chlorhexidine and EDTA indicates a feasible approach to augment antibacterial activity, especially against resistant Gram-negative pathogens. The marked reduction in MIC and MBC highlights the potential of this combination in optimizing disinfection protocols in healthcare settings while minimizing potential toxicity due to lower effective concentrations. The present findings are consistent with a study conducted by Raana Kazemzadeh et al. in Iran, showing significant reductions in both MIC and MBC values [3]. Furthermore, studies on A. baumannii showed enhanced bactericidal effects against biofilm producers, reinforcing the robustness of this synergistic strategy [33].

The results demonstrated that chlorhexidine concentrations of 0.31% and above rapidly and consistently inhibited bacterial growth. Intermediate concentrations (0.07% and 0.15%) exhibited a time-dependent killing effect, with increased efficacy upon prolonged exposure. If economic considerations necessitate a reduced concentration, the contact duration must be substantially extended. At lower concentrations (0.03% and below), complete bacterial inhibition was observed after 1 to 2 h; however, bacterial regrowth occurred after 4 h. This unexpected regrowth pattern can be attributed to several possible factors; chlorhexidine may become inactivated or degraded over time, especially in culture media or in the presence of organic matter. As a result, its effectiveness decreases and bacteria begin to grow again. Also, prolonged exposure at sub-MIC concentrations can promote early biofilm development. Even minimal biofilm formation may act as a protective barrier, reducing chlorhexidine penetration and allowing bacterial survival and regrowth after several hours. Therefore, disinfecting surfaces and hospital environments with chlorhexidine at the beginning of each work shift is recommended to ensure continuous microbial control. These findings underscore the critical role of both antimicrobial concentration and exposure duration in maintaining effective bacterial inhibition. Although chlorhexidine demonstrates strong bactericidal activity at higher concentrations, its stability and sustained efficacy at lower doses warrant further investigation, particularly under clinical and environmental conditions.

Biocide usage is significantly less controlled than antibiotic use, raising worries about the development of biocide tolerance and the potential involvement of these compounds in fostering the evolution of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria. Concerns about acquired bacterial resistance, which is mostly caused by the acquisition of genes that confer tolerance, are raised by the regular use of CHG [10].

The qacE gene and related qac genes frequently encode efflux pumps, which are mechanisms that can assist microbes in the expulsion of antimicrobial agents such as chlorhexidine. Isolates with qacE or qacEΔ1 tend to have higher MICs to chlorhexidine compared to gene-negative isolates. In the present study, isolates harboring the qacE, qacEΔ1, and CepA genes exhibited notably higher MIC values to chlorhexidine compared to gene-negative isolates. This suggests a potential role of these genes, particularly qacE, in reduced susceptibility to chlorhexidine, likely via efflux mechanisms. This finding is consistent with previous reports in K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa, showing a statistically significant association between CepA/qac genes and elevated chlorhexidine MICs [34, 35]. Interestingly, isolates that produced substantial biofilms and were positive for qac/CepA genes showed the greatest reduction in susceptibility, indicating a combined effect of biofilm-mediated protection and efflux-mediated resistance.

However, the frequency of biocide resistance genes, including qacE, qacEΔ1, and CepA was found to be low among the isolates in our study. This low prevalence is likely due to the fact that this disinfectant is not commonly used in the hospital setting. This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the experiments were conducted in vitro, which may not fully replicate the complex conditions of the in vivo clinical environment, including host immune responses or tissue penetration of biocides. Additionally, molecular typing of the isolates was not performed, which could have provided further insight into the clonality and transmission patterns of the strains. Although molecular typing of the isolates was not performed in this study, it is planned as part of future investigations to better understand the clonality and transmission dynamics of resistant strains within healthcare environments. Lastly, the long-term efficacy and residual activity of the CHG–EDTA combination were not assessed, particularly under conditions simulating real-world environmental or clinical use.

Conclusion

Although economic and logistical challenges may restrict the routine use of chlorhexidine in some hospital settings, our findings suggest that even low concentrations of this agent may exert measurable antimicrobial activity against hospital-associated Gram-negative bacteria in short exposure periods under in vitro conditions. Notably, this study included isolates from patients, healthcare personnel, and the hospital environment, providing a more comprehensive assessment of microbial contamination compared with previous reports.

These results indicate that chlorhexidine could be considered a cost-effective disinfectant option in healthcare settings. However, to fully validate and extend these findings, further studies involving in vivo models and clinical investigations are necessary. Moreover, hospital personnel and the hospital environment can serve as direct and indirect carriers in transmitting infections among patients. Therefore, identifying these factors and providing education on the proper use of disinfectants are crucial steps in reducing hospital-acquired infections.
