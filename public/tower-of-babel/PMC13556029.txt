Links between falls in hospitals and professional fulfilment, burnout, work environment and organisational culture: a scoping review
Authors: Veethika Nayak, Marina B. Pinheiro, Bethan Richards, Luiza Rampi Pivotto, Catherine Sherrington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare staff wellbeing is critical to deliver quality patient-centred care. Burnout, lack of professional fulfilment, poor work environment, and an unsupportive organisational culture – impact healthcare delivery. However, their relationship with hospital patient falls – a frequent adverse outcome remains underexplored.ObjectiveThis review aimed to: (1) map the evidence investigating the relationship between staff wellbeing concepts (burnout, professional fulfilment, work environment and organisational culture) and patient falls in hospitals; and (2) determine the extent of consideration of staff wellbeing in existing hospital falls prevention trials.MethodsReview followed Joanna Briggs Institute methodology [1]. PubMed, MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase and PsycINFO were searched from inception to July 2024. Cochrane database was searched to identify existing hospital falls prevention interventions trials. Studies mentioning at least one staff wellbeing concept, and patient falls were included.ResultsOf 11,358 records, 78 studies were included. Most were observational (n = 54), nurse-focused (n = 67), and in acute settings (n = 69). Twenty studies investigated association between staff wellbeing and patient falls– mostly cross-sectional (n = 18), only two longitudinal. Burnout was associated with increased falls; job satisfaction showed a protective role. Better work environment reduced falls. Organisational culture findings were mixed – group culture and better leadership reduced falls, while toxic leadership and development culture increased falls. Variability in definitions and measurement tools led to conceptual overlaps. No Cochrane trials considered staff wellbeing in fall prevention interventions.ConclusionEvidence linking staff wellbeing and patient falls is emerging but limited. Critical gaps include inconsistent conceptualisation, a lack of – longitudinal, multidisciplinary focused intervention research integrating these concepts. Embedding staff wellbeing into fall prevention strategies is essential.Clinical trial registration numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14976-3.

Background

Healthcare workforces play the central role in the healthcare system [2]. According to World Health Organization, the global health workforce was estimated to be approximately 65 million as of 2020 [3]. This comprises a wide range of professionals, including nurses, doctors, allied health workers, and other non-clinical staff. They all are essential for delivering quality care. The demand for healthcare professionals continues to grow, driven by ageing population and the rising prevalence of chronic diseases [4, 5]. By 2050, individuals aged 60 and over are expected to reach 2.1 billion, up from 1 billion in 2020 [6], significantly increasing care needs for healthcare professionals and services in the future and meaning many more individuals will risk being affected by falls [4].

In the context of growing demand, however, healthcare workforce shortages are a significant issue. The Global Strategy on Human Resources for Health (GSHRH): workforce 2030, suggests a health workforce shortage of 10.2 million by 2030 [2, 3]. Key contributors to this shortage include burnout [7, 8], poor working conditions [9], and job dissatisfaction [10] among healthcare professionals.

Healthcare staff wellbeing is critical to deliver high-quality and patient centric care [11]. Wellbeing is a complex and multidimensional concept, with no universally agreed definition or conceptualisation. Although several theoretical and operational models of wellbeing have been proposed across disciplines, there remains limited consensus regarding its boundaries and measurement [12]. Wellbeing has broadly been described as how individuals feel, function, and evaluate their lives both personally and socially [13]. In healthcare, wellbeing is increasingly recognised as being influenced by professional demands, organisational resources, workplace experiences and patient care outcomes [11, 14, 15].

This review is informed by the Conservation of Resources (COR) Theory, which suggests that individuals strive to obtain, retain, and protect valued resources such as energy, time, social support, health, and status [16]. From this perspective, in healthcare settings, burnout, professional fulfilment, work environment and organisational culture represent interconnected but distinct resources-related factors that are both modifiable and commonly referenced in wellbeing and patient safety literature as critical resources that affect the staff performance, care quality and patient outcomes, including patient falls [14, 15, 17, 18]. These constructs were selected due to their recognised influence on teamwork, communication, and safety practices, all of which are addressed in fall prevention research [14, 19–23]. However, definitions and boundaries of these constructs are not consistently applied across the literature, with considerable conceptual overlap- particularly between burnout, professional fulfilment, organisational culture and work environment. This lack of conceptual clarity presents challenges in interpretation, which this review aims to address.

Burnout is defined by the WHO as “occupational distress resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed” [24], and is increasingly prevalent among healthcare professionals [25]. It manifests as emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, and reduced professional efficacy [26–28]. Burnout has serious consequences, including impact on nurse’s health and mental wellbeing [29], increased intention to leave the profession, higher turnover [8], medication errors and reduced quality of care [30, 31].

Burnout and wellbeing are related but distinct constructs [22, 32, 33]. Policy and public health organisations, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [34] defines wellbeing as “the presence of positive emotions, satisfaction with life, fulfilment, and positive functioning” [35]. Any imbalances in the personal and professional demands can lead to burnout, which creates a negative feedback loop [28]. On the other hand, professional fulfilment is a sense of intrinsic satisfaction, and purpose, efficacy, happiness and control at work, and inversely related to burnout and can serve as a protective factor [36, 37].

Organisational culture and work environments also play a crucial role in shaping healthcare staff experiences. Although work environment and organisational culture are closely related and often overlapping constructs, work environment generally refers to day-to-day physical, social and organisational conditions under which healthcare staff work, while organisational culture reflects the broader shared values, norms, beliefs, and behaviours within an organisation [38–41]. Factors such as excessive workloads, bureaucracy, poor communication, underappreciation and lack of support, contribute to burnout and dissatisfaction [42, 43]. Unlike other workplaces, healthcare settings are unique as the staff is exposed to sufferings, deaths, social inequities and ethical dilemmas, which can be emotionally overwhelming and challenging, especially when organisational support is insufficient [44].

Workplace or organisational culture has significant implications for patient safety. The Australian commission on Safety and Quality in Healthcare defines patient safety culture as “a pattern of individual and organisational behaviour, based upon shared beliefs and values that continuously seeks to minimise patient harm, which may result from the process of care delivery” [45, 46]. Poor working conditions and unit-level stress are associated with adverse patient outcomes, including medical errors, infections, and patient falls, further increasing staff stress [47, 48].

Patient falls are one of the most common adverse events in hospital settings [49–51]. Falls can occur at any age, however older adults (65+) are at a higher risk, with risk increasing with advanced age [52]. In Australia, 47,551 fall-related injuries in hospitals were reported in 2020–2021 [53], up from 34,000 incidents reported in 2015–2016 [54]. Falls often result in severe injuries, prolonged hospital stays [55], additional treatment needs, increased financial and workload burden on staff and healthcare systems [56].

Prevention and management of patient falls typically relies on multifactorial prevention strategies, including falls risk screening, exercise-based interventions, medication review, environmental modifications, assistive technologies, patient and staff education, organisational system level changes, and coordinated multidisciplinary care [57–61]. While falls prevention remains a key patient safety priority, prevention strategies should be considered within the broader context of patient care, as some prevention strategies may have other adverse consequences if they restrict mobility and reduce patient independence [62, 63]. This highlights the importance of balanced, patient-centred, multidisciplinary approach to falls prevention. Healthcare staff play a central role in identifying patients at increased risk of falling, assessing individual risk factors, and implementing prevention strategies, making effective falls prevention heavily dependent on sustained staff engagement and multidisciplinary collaboration [64–66]. Successful implementation often requires staff to work proactively rather than reactively, supported by adequate staffing, leadership, equipment and safe environment, ongoing professional development, effective communication and teamwork across disciplines [64, 65].

Given the global ageing trends, falls and fall related injuries are expected to rise further, necessitating timely prevention strategies [67] – requiring additional healthcare resources [56], further increasing the resource demands on healthcare staff and system. These ongoing demands may contribute to staff stress, frustration, reduced confidence in ability to manage falls, and workload pressures, particularly where staffing, communication, training and organisational support are limited [64]. Broader healthcare literature has linked such workforce pressures with poorer staff wellbeing and patient safety outcomes [14], highlighting a potential interplay between staff wellbeing and patient falls outcomes.

In summary, the growing demand for healthcare professionals reflects Australia’s evolving demographic and healthcare landscape [4]. Research exploring the link between staff burnout, professional fulfilment, work environment, organisational culture, and patient falls in hospital setting remains limited. Therefore, understanding this interplay is crucial, as falls are an additional burden on healthcare systems, especially amid workforce shortages and increasing healthcare demands. This review aims to examine existing literature to understand the association between these concepts to inform future falls prevention strategies. Throughout this review, we refer to burnout, professional fulfilment, workplace environment, and organisational culture collectively as “concepts of wellbeing”. This umbrella term is used to avoid repetition of the concepts while capturing the interconnected elements.

The primary objective of this scoping review was to describe and analyse the existing literature regarding the relationship between (a) staff burnout, professional fulfilment, organisational culture, work environment, and (b) patient falls in hospital settings, aiming to map the evidence, identify the gaps and highlight the role of healthcare staff wellbeing in patient safety and falls.

Methods

We conducted a review following JBI scoping review methodology [68]. A protocol for this scoping review was prepared in advance and published on the Open Science Framework website [69].

Patient and Public Involvement Statement:

Patients and public were not directly involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination of this scoping review, as we examined previously published literature. However, the objective and research questions were built on the authors’ clinical experience and understanding of the routine challenges faced by healthcare staff and patients in hospital settings.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria were established using the PCC (Population, Concept, and Context) framework, as recommended by the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis [1].

Population

This scoping review included the studies that focused on patients aged 18 years or older admitted to a hospital. There was no limit to health conditions among hospital patients.

Concept

We examined the concepts of staff wellbeing – burnout, professional fulfilment, organisational culture, and work environment. To ensure a more inclusive approach, we also considered studies that used related terms or addressed these concepts in a broader context. We included studies that used closely related synonyms or interchangeable terms for key concepts as the exact concepts of interest were not always explicitly mentioned, but these studies still aimed to capture similar themes or objectives. For example, terms like “job satisfaction” was considered in place of “professional fulfilment” and “stress” or “compassion fatigue” were used as close concepts to “burnout”.

The definitions and relevance of burnout and professional fulfilment have been detailed in the introduction section. In brief, burnout is characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and reduced professional efficacy [26–28], while professional fulfilment encompasses purpose, satisfaction, and control at work [37].

For organisational culture, we adopted the widely accepted and most recently cited definition, describing it as “an organisational management concept which deals with the attitudes, beliefs, and perception of employees relative to the principles and practices adhered to by the institute” [38] and “which have considered valid, and therefore to be taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think and feel in relation to those problems” [39].

Whereas work environment refers to physical, psychological, and social conditions under which healthcare staff works every day. This includes factors such as staffing levels, workload, interpersonal relationships, teamwork, communication, leadership, and support systems [11, 40, 41].

To examine of these concepts across the healthcare workforce in a broader way, studies using any measurement tools to assess these major concepts of interest were also considered. Measurement tools like Stanford Professional Fulfillment Index [37], Maslach Burnout Inventory [39, 70] and any other measurement tools were eligible for inclusion.

For inclusion in this review, studies must investigate either professional fulfilment, burnout, organisational culture, or workplace environment among any staff working in a hospital setting, including both clinicians (e.g. nurses, doctors, allied health) and non-clinicians (e.g. ward clerk, cleaners, administration).

Context

We included studies that investigated falls in hospital settings. This includes all inpatient care settings, emergency departments, short stay and rehabilitation facilities. Studies from any geographical location were eligible for inclusion.

Data source and search strategy

To address the first objective, an extensive literature search was conducted across five major databases — PubMed, MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, and PsycInfo — from inception to July 2024.

Search terms, informed by screening of these trials, abstracts, and reference lists, included combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, key words, and synonyms for “professional fulfilment”, “burnout”, “organisational culture”, “work environment’’, “falls” and “hospital setting”. A Medline search was conducted initially to pilot test the search strategy (please refer to Supplementary Fig. 3. Example of search strategy- Ovid Medline Search history for an example of the search strategy).

To address the second objective, Cochrane database was searched to identify systematic reviews on falls prevention in hospital settings. Relevant reviews were reviewed to identify whether individual studies reported any consideration of staff wellbeing concepts in the trials. We searched the whole paper for each included trial using the similar key words and synonyms for wellbeing concepts to understand any consideration of wellbeing concepts.

Data extraction and synthesis of results

The search results obtained from all the electronic databases were imported to an online research screening software program for systematic reviews ‘Covidence’ and duplicates were removed by the first author. Two independent reviewers (VN and LP) screened the titles and abstracts of all the identified studies. The final sample of relevant studies was then assessed for full text by the same reviewers, independently, against the eligibility criteria. One reviewer extracted data, which were checked by the second reviewer. We also extracted data regarding the type of relationship examined between wellbeing constructs and falls (e.g., correlational, predictive or regression-based associations), including whether mediating or causal relationships were explored, where such information was reported in the included studies. An existing data extraction template from Covidence was modified in excel spreadsheet to fit with the review’s objectives. We classified studies into three groups, that:

Results

To answer the first objective, which examined the volume of evidence investigating the relationship between wellbeing concepts and patient falls in hospital settings, the initial database search yielded 11,358 results. After removing duplicates, and screening titles and abstracts, 194 studies were eligible for full text review. After full text review, both the authors agreed on a final sample of 78 studies that were relevant considering the objective of this review (Refer to Supplementary Fig. 1. PRISMA flow diagram for further details and reasons for exclusion at each stage).

To address the second objective, to understand the extent of consideration of wellbeing concepts incorporated into falls prevention trials in hospital settings, the Cochrane search returned 24 trials included in the systematic review of falls prevention interventions in hospital settings updated until 2018. However, none of the included trials in Cochrane explicitly considered staff wellbeing concepts in hospital falls prevention trials, and hence no further data was extracted from these trials.

Therefore, remainder of the results section refers to objective 1 only:

Characteristics of the included studies

Of 78 studies included, majority (n = 69) were conducted in acute hospital settings, while seven studies were conducted in specialized care settings, such as military healthcare, neurocritical care, intensive care, cardiac care, maternity care and oncology units. Additionally, two studies focused on rehabilitation settings.

In terms of the study design, most of the studies were observational (n = 54), while 16 were experimental studies. Six studies were systematic reviews, and two were literature reviews.

The primary participants in these studies were predominantly registered nurses (n = 67). Eleven studies extended beyond nurse-only participants, however, several still involved nursing leadership roles such as chief nursing officers (CNOs), chief nursing educators (CNEs), nursing unit managers (NUMs), and directors of nursing (DONs). Only eight studies included broader multidisciplinary team participation, involving professionals such as physicians, unit clerks, physiotherapists, pharmacists, and other clinicians (Refer to online Additional File 1- Appendix 2 for full reference list). However, non-nursing professionals were generally included alongside nursing staff in broader staff surveys, and profession-specific findings were not reported separately. In many of these studies, staff wellbeing related constructs (e.g., job satisfaction, organisational culture) were measured descriptively or to assess intervention success, rather than examining their association with falls, while studies that explored associations did not report findings by professional group.

Mention and measurement of staff wellbeing concepts

The final set of 78 studies mentioned at least one staff wellbeing concept — burnout, professional fulfilment, work environment, or organisational culture — alongside patient falls in hospital settings (Full reference list is available in Additional File 1. Of these, 8 studies focused on falls and only briefly mentioned a wellbeing concept without measuring it, while 7 studies focussed on wellbeing but briefly mentioned patient falls without further examination (Fig. 1).

To understand the extent of consideration of staff wellbeing in falls prevention initiatives, we further extracted data from the studies that measured both wellbeing concepts and falls. A total of 70 studies measured at least one concept of wellbeing, and 71 measured falls in hospitals. Sixty-eight studies measured both – at least one wellbeing concept alongside falls (Fig. 1). However, most of them only measured falls as part of a broader category of adverse patient outcomes, with only limited focus on falls specifically. Falls were often examined in relation to other factors like skill mix, staff shortages, and working hours, etc., or as a metric to evaluate the success of their interventions.

Association of each concept with falls

In 68 studies that measured wellbeing concepts and falls, 20 studies explored associations between at least one wellbeing concept and falls in hospital settings (Fig. 1). These 20 studies primarily examined associative relationships between staff wellbeing constructs and patient falls using a range of analytical approaches, including correlation and statistical models (e.g., odds ratios, incidence rate ratios, and regression coefficients). No studies explicitly examined mediating or causal pathways. These 20 studies were conducted across a diverse range of countries. The majority were from the United States (n = 10), followed by studies from South Korea (n = 2), and one each from Saudi Arabia, Thailand, Belgium, Philippines, Oman, Greece, Switzerland, and a broader Middle Eastern region.

Analysis of the frequency of each concept suggested, most studies measured and associated nurse work environment or practice environment (n = 14), followed by job satisfaction (n = 7) and burnout (n = 6) with falls. Four studies measured and associated organisational culture, and one study associated compassion satisfaction with falls (Fig. 1).

Dugan (1996) found moderately strong association between burnout and patient falls, suggesting higher nurse stress levels correlated with increased patient falls, putting burnout as a potential risk factor for adverse patient outcomes [71]. Similarly, Nantsupawat (2016) found that dimensions of burnout – high depersonalisation among nurses was associated with 2.06 times increase, whereas low personal accomplishment was linked to 1.61 times increase in the odds of reporting falls [9].

Van Bogaert (2014) demonstrated that components of burnout, such as higher emotional exhaustion (adjusted OR: 1.25) and higher depersonalisation (adjusted OR: 1.40), were significantly associated with increased patient falls [72]. However, personal accomplishment did not show any significant association with falls. While, Alharbi (2020) examined burnout and compassion satisfaction with patient falls across four hospitals but could not establish a clear trend due to inconsistently distributed data [73]. Overall, while some studies suggested a strong link between burnout and falls, inconsistencies in findings highlight the need for further research.

Professional fulfilment and falls

There were no studies that specifically measured or linked professional fulfilment to patient falls. As professional fulfilment reflects positive dimensions of work-related wellbeing, conceptually related constructs such as job satisfaction and job dissatisfaction were included as proxy measures to provide insight into this domain in relation to patient falls.

Several studies reported mixed findings on the association between nurses’ job satisfaction and fall rates (Table 2). Choi (2013) found a significant relationship, specifically, for each one-unit increase in job satisfaction, patient falls decreased by 5.9% [74]. Similarly, Alvarez (2007) reported, units with higher overall job satisfaction had lower fall rates [75].

Hanrahan (2010), emphasising on the role of leadership, found that better management skill was significantly associated with fewer patient falls with injuries [78]. Similarly, Brewer (2006) reported that group culture (a supportive, family-like environment) was inversely related, while developmental culture (innovation and individual-focused environment) was positively associated with falls with injury [79].

Conversely, Labrague (2021) highlighted a strong association between increased perceptions of toxic leadership in nurse managers (NMs) and higher nurse-reported patient falls [80].

Work environment and falls

Several studies examined the link between the nurse work environment and patient falls, with mixed findings. Overall, a positive work environment, characterised by supportive management, strong teamwork, continuous education programs, was associated with fewer falls in multiple studies (Table 4). Cho (2016) found a 32% lower incidence of falls with injury in better work environments [81], while Smith (2019) reported that improving work environment score by one standard deviation reduced the odds of falls with injury by 50% (p < 0.01) [82]. Taylor (2012), using unit-level Safety Attitudes Questionnaire domains reflecting workplace conditions and staff perception, found no significant association between perceptions of management and patient falls, although higher registered nursing hours per patient day (RNHPPD), were associated with fewer patient falls [77]. Van Bogaert (2014) also highlighted that unit level work environment, specifically hospital management and organisational support, significantly lowered the odds of patient falls [72].

Some studies found the role of teamwork and workplace culture in patient safety. Al Sabei et al. (2021) found a weak but significant negative correlations between nursing practice, environment, teamwork, and falls [83]. Prezerakos (2013) reported that a non-favorable work environment significantly increased fall rates [84].

However, some studies found no direct effect of work environment on falls. Almotairy (2022), Ausserhofer et al. (2012), Breckenridge-Sproat 2012, all reported that there was no statistically significant association between work environment and falls [85–87]. Similarly, Swiger 2018, found no significant association after controlling the key variables [88].

Kang (2014) identified that hospital level nurse practice environment factors such as continuous education programs significantly reduced falls [89]. Falls reporting and rationing of care was also examined by one study, Blegen (2004), which found that though a strong quality management process improved falls reporting, but fear of administrative response to falls incidents often led to underreporting [76].

Measurement tools

The analysis of measurement tools used in 20 studies that examined the association between wellbeing concepts and falls revealed considerable variation in instruments used to measure similar concepts. An additional file shows this in more details including all the measurement tools used (Refer to Supplementary Table 1. Summary of measurement tools used in the studies examining association in Additional File 1).

The most commonly used tool (Supplementary Fig. 2) was the Practice Environment Scale of the Nursing Work Index (PES-NWI), used in total nine studies to measure work environment or nursing practice environment [81–88], of which one study [78] used PES-NWI to measure organisational culture.

Two studies utilised the Nursing Quality Indicators (NDNQI) to measure job satisfaction [74, 75], while one study [76] used a modified 25-item Brayfield-Rothe Tool, which also measured work environment. The Safety Attitudes Questionnaire (SAQ) was used in one study [77] to assess both job satisfaction and work environment.

For burnout, the Maslach Burnout Inventory Human Service Survey (MBI-HSS) was the most frequently used, appearing in two studies [9, 72]. The Professional Quality of Life Scale (ProQOL-5) measured both burnout and compassion satisfaction in one study [73], while Dugan (1996) used the Stress Survey and Stress Continuum Scale (SCS) to assess burnout [71].

For work culture, different instruments were applied. Van Bogaert (2014) used the Nursing Work Index Revised (NWI-R) [72], while Kang (2014) used the Korean General Inpatients Unit Nursing Work Index (KGU-NWI) [89].

Lastly, organisational culture was measured using various tools. The Hospital Culture Instrument [90] was applied in one study [79], while the Toxic Leadership Behaviors of Nurse Managers Scale (ToxBH-NM) [80] and SAQ [77] were also used in other studies.

Study designs of included studies

Out of 20 studies that examined the association between wellbeing concepts and patient falls, only 2 studies had longitudinal design, while 18 studies were cross-sectional. This reflects a predominant method design preference for cross-sectional design and scarcity of longitudinal or interventional studies investigating this association.

Discussion

This scoping review examined the relationship between staff wellbeing concepts and patient falls in hospital settings. In doing so, this study also sought to clarify the definitions and boundaries of these concepts. However, a key challenge that emerged was the lack of standardised definitions, particularly for wellbeing itself, making it difficult to determine what constitutes wellbeing and how different studies conceptualised and measured these constructs.

Among the 78 studies included, most studies focused on nursing staff, reflecting a significant gap in understanding the impact of these concepts on broader multidisciplinary teams involved in falls prevention. This nursing focus may partly reflect the central role nurses play in continuous patient monitoring, falls risk assessment, incident reporting and implementation of prevention strategies. However, as falls are multifactorial in nature, existing literature suggests that effective falls prevention requires coordinated multidisciplinary care [91]. Interestingly, despite the wide recognition that falls prevention is a shared priority, healthcare professionals are often uncertain about the distribution of roles and responsibilities in falls prevention, alongside challenges such as competing priorities, workforce pressures, hierarchical tensions and communication barriers [91, 92]. This may contribute to falls prevention being viewed primarily through a nursing lens, limiting the exploration of multidisciplinary wellbeing perspective. Of the 78 studies, only 20 examined direct associations, primarily through correlational rather than interventional approaches, while none examined staff wellbeing as part of falls prevention interventions. Additionally, none of these studies specifically explored this link in Australian context, highlighting the need for country-specific research.

Among these studies, burnout was generally associated with increased fall rates, while professional fulfilment (assessed through job satisfaction), showed mostly protective effects. A positive work environment, consisting of quality improvement education, better management and leadership was consistently associated with fewer falls. In contrast, the influence of organisational culture was less consistent, with group culture linked to reduced fall rates, while toxic leadership and development culture linked to elevated fall risk.

The observed association may reflect several pathways through which staff wellbeing could influence the implementation of falls prevention in hospitals. Burnout, low job satisfaction, poor teamwork, or inadequate organisational support may reduce staff capacity to maintain patient surveillance and monitoring, falls risk screening, provide timely assistance, support supervised mobilisation, deliver patient education, and consistently implement multifactorial falls prevention strategies [14, 64, 65]. In contrast, supportive work environment, adequate staffing, and a positive team culture may act as important enablers of effective falls prevention at the bedside.

However, establishing the direction of relationship is difficult. The included studies primarily examined associative relationships using correlational and regression-based analysis predominantly within cross-sectional study designs. No studies explicitly examined causal or mediating relationships. A major challenge was the scarcity of longitudinal and interventional studies investigating this association, which limited the ability to understand the direction of the relationships between falls and staff wellbeing.

Another challenge across the literature was lack of conceptual clarity. Conceptual overlap between “organisational culture” and “work environment” was common, with studies using similar tools (e.g., PES-NWI) to assess related constructs (Refer Supplementary Table 1 in Additional File 1). PES-NWI was predominantly used to assess work environment or nursing practice environment, however, its occasional application to measure related constructs such as organisational culture may reflect conceptual overlap between these domains. As PES-NWI captures both day-to-day practice environment characteristics and broader organisational characteristics such as leadership, staffing, participation in decision-making, interprofessional relationships, some overlap in measurement of related constructs is expected [93–95]. Similarly, there was a lack of conceptual clarity between compassion satisfaction and compassion fatigue, and their relationship to burnout and secondary traumatic stress (STS), further limiting consistent measurement approaches and comparability of the studies.

The challenges around conceptual ambiguity were also reflected in the lack of standardisation in measurement tools used to assess wellbeing related concepts. Studies employed a wide variety of instruments (Refer Supplementary Table 1 Additional File 1), often using different tools to measure the same concept. For instance, burnout was measured using instruments ranging from the Maslach Burnout Inventory to the ProQOL-5, while some tools, such as ProQOL-5, captured multiple related constructs. This variation, along with the use of proxy indicators (e.g., stress for burnout, job satisfaction for professional fulfilment), limits comparability across studies. It also made it challenging to distinguish between what authors intended to measure versus what was actually assessed, raising concerns about selection of the right tools to capture the intended constructs. This urges the need to prioritise the development of a universally accepted, holistic definition of wellbeing and establish comprehensive tools to measure them.

Strengths and limitations

The key strength of this review is that it is the first review known to the authors that brings together the concepts like burnout, professional fulfilment, work environment and organisational culture, and patient falls. This review highlighted an important yet underexplored relationship between staff wellbeing and patient safety.

However, there are some limitations. Despite the use of synonyms and closely related terms, the use of interchangeable terms for the concepts of interest may have led to some relevant studies being missed. Conceptual overlaps between wellbeing concepts posed challenges in interpretation. Lack of standardised definitions and measurement frameworks for wellbeing-related concepts, led to inconsistencies in study findings. And lastly, lack of longitudinal studies limited our understanding of the direction of relationship– whether poor staff wellbeing leads to increased falls, or if falls contribute to poor wellbeing.

Conclusion

This review highlights a critical gap, urging the need to explore how interventions aimed at improving staff wellbeing such as reducing burnout, increasing job satisfaction, and creating a supportive work environment and organisational culture can contribute to better falls outcomes. While existing studies recognise links between the wellbeing related concepts and patient safety, only a few have integrated staff wellbeing and patient falls as dual priorities. Staff wellbeing may act as important enabler or barrier to implementation of best practice falls prevention, influencing staff capacity for risk screening, patient surveillance, timely response, supervised mobilisation, patient education, and multidisciplinary teamwork. Given the multidisciplinary nature of hospital care, future research should extend beyond nursing staff to consider the wellbeing of the broader healthcare team and its collective impact on patient falls. Falls prevention interventions should explicitly incorporate staff wellbeing as a core intervention component. Addressing these gaps will not only help in enhancing the accuracy and comparability of future research but also contribute to more effective strategies that support both healthcare staff wellbeing and falls prevention in hospital settings.

Practice and policy impact

The findings urge the need for integrated, multidisciplinary focused, longitudinal research that embeds staff well-being concepts into patient falls prevention initiatives in hospital settings. Future falls prevention policies should consider healthcare staff well-being as part of fall prevention strategies, supported by clear definitions and standardised measurement tools.
