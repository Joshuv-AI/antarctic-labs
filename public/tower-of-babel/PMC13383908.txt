All‐Inside Primary Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction Using a Combined Polyester Synthetic Graft and Gracilis Tendon Autograft: Modified Technique of a Hybrid 4 × 2 Graft Preparation
Authors: Ion Bogdan Codorean, Edna Vasiliu, Stefania Tanase, Sebastian Culman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnterior cruciate ligament reconstruction is a frequently performed procedure in arthroscopic surgery, with rising rates paralleling the incidence of anterior cruciate ligament ruptures worldwide. Synthetic grafts, especially hybrid constructs combining artificial devices with autologous tissue, offer biomechanical advantages and potential faster recovery. This article describes a modified technique for primary all‐inside anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction using a 4 × 2‐strand hybrid graft composed of a tubular synthetic device combined with the gracilis tendon. This technique aims to deliver a stronger graft, optimizing stability while preserving bone and reducing operative time. The step‐by‐step surgical method, graft preparation, tunnel creation, and fixation are detailed, with emphasis on biomechanical and clinical benefits for professional athletes and high‐demand patients.

Description of a technique for anterior cruciate ligament all‐inside reconstruction using a 4 × 2 hybrid graft composed of a tubular synthetic device and the gracilis tendon. Gracilis tendon is passed through the synthetic scaffold with midpoints aligned, and both ends are reinforced with high‐strength nonabsorbable sutures, ensuring a minimum graft length of 6 cm. The construct is mounted on tibial and femoral suspension loops to create a 4 × 2‐strand graft. Tensioning, equalization, and buried‐knot suturing are performed, and the graft is soaked in vancomycin solution. Tibial and femoral tunnels are prepared according to graft diameter: the femoral tunnel in isometric position and the tibial tunnel anatomically using a dowel‐harvesting technique. The graft is advanced through the tibial tunnel, femoral fixation is confirmed arthroscopically, and tibial fixation is achieved with dowel reinsertion. Second‐look arthroscopy at 12 months shows graft integration with satisfactory filling around the synthetic scaffold, while magnetic resonance imaging at 8 months confirms a well‐positioned, continuous, and appropriately matured construct. Reconstruction of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) remains a key procedure in arthroscopic surgery, given the increasing incidence of ACL ruptures in both orthopaedics and sports medicine. 1 , 2 Synthetic grafts have recently garnered significant attention due to their biomechanical strength and potential advantages over traditional autografts. 3 , 4 The main advantages of ACL synthetic graft or hybrid grafts focus on providing a greater biomechanical strength which can lead to a faster recovery through hamstring, patellar tendon or quadriceps spearing, and rapid weight‐bearing. Other benefits include a lower risk of ACL failure due to the lack of ligamentization, less operative time, avoiding donor site morbidity, and potentially less arthrofibrosis. These features make a synthetic graft the optimal option for professional athletes or high‐demand patients but not restricted only to this group of patients. 5

Synthetic grafts can be prepared in multiple ways. They can either be used alone or augmented with biological tissue, and they can be used with different fixation systems such as cortical buttons or interference screws, giving the surgeon the possibility to choose the technique he/she is most comfortable with.

Among the synthetic grafts approved and available on the market today, JewelACL (Xiros, Leeds, UK) is a tubular tissue graft sparing device that overcomes the need to take any tissue from hamstrings tendons. If, however, a tissue graft is still desired, JewelACL may also be used in a partial tissue graft sparing or hybrid graft, by inserting 1 hamstring tendon through the tubular mesh. 6 This artificial graft matches the maximum natural ACL stiffness, and the ultimate tensile strength is higher than that of semitendinosus. 6

The recommended technique for JewelACL graft preparation with or without biological augmentation implies folding the tubular device in 2, suturing, and fixating it isometrically with a cortical button on the femoral side and with an interference screw on the tibial side. 6

This article details a modified step‐by‐step all‐inside technique of primary ACL reconstruction and hybrid graft preparation incorporating the Silva method using solely cortical fixation systems both on the femoral and tibial sides, with autologous bone grafting of the tibial tunnel at the end. 7 , 8 The particularity brought through this technique is the preparation of the hybrid graft which delivers a 4 × 2‐strand graft instead of 2 × 2 standard strands, thus providing a thicker and stronger final graft.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Positioning and Arthroscopic Evaluation

The patient is positioned supine after spinal anesthesia. A tourniquet is applied. One lateral post is fixed on the surgical table for the valgus moment of the knee, and 2 foot rolls are added to hold the lower extremity at 90° and 120° of knee flexion, respectively. Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals are created (Figure 1), and arthroscopic examination is then performed, confirming ACL rupture. During examination, the medial aspect of the lateral femoral condyle is inspected and prepared, and the intercondylar notch and lateral femoral condyle depth are routinely measured.

Portal placement, planned incision for graft harvest, and left lower extremity positioning.

Hybrid Graft Preparation

Hamstring tendons are inspected before harvesting, and the thinnest one is usually chosen. Generally, the thinnest one is the gracilis tendon (GT). GT is then measured and thoroughly cleaned from muscle tissue; loose fibers or remaining adhesions are removed (Figure 2).

GT graft before (top) and after (bottom) preparation. (GT, gracilis tendon.)

The tendon is passed through the tubular synthetic device (JewelACL, Xiros) with the “half” mark of the artificial ligament placed at the half of the GT (Figure 3a,b, Video 1). The hybrid graft is verified and matched and placed on the graft preparation station. The final 4 × 2‐strand graft length is always planned to be at least 6 cm long. It is then positioned and sutured according to measurements at extremities covering 2 cm each, with high‐strength sutures (No. 2 FiberWire, Arthrex, Naples, FL), making sure the sutures pass equally through the artificial device and through GT (Figure 4, Video 1). The remaining artificial mesh is then cut.

(a) Half mark of GT corresponding with JewelACL half mark. (b) GT is passed through the tubular synthetic device using the specially made holes. (GT, gracilis tendon.)

Extremities are sutured with No. 2 FiberWire (Arthrex).

The following steps closely follow the Silva technique (Video 1). One end of the hybrid graft is passed first through the tibial cortical fixation loop (ToggleLoc XL inline 20 mm, Zimmer Biomet), resulting in a 2 × 2‐strand graft (Figure 5). The suspension wires of the button are always placed at the “half” mark of the artificial device. The free, sutured ends of the 2 × 2‐strand graft are then passed through the femoral suspension loop (ToggleLoc inline 13 mm, Zimmer Biomet), and the graft is folded again at half, resulting in the final 4 × 2‐strand new graft (Figure 6).

Two‐by‐two‐strand graft through the tibial loop.

Four‐by‐four‐strand graft through the femoral loop.

The femoral button is loosely placed on the tensioning poles of the station, and one of the free sutured ends is placed through the tibial button loop. The remaining FiberWires of the free ends are knotted, paying attention to avoid the tearing of the cut (free) ends of the artificial ligament. The strands are then equalized. The final touch of this technique is to pass the now knotted ends of the graft through the other 2 strands in order to hide the FiberWire knots within the clean strands, making sure at all times that all 4 × 2 strands are equal in length and tension (Figure 7).

Silva technique of folding and knotting the 4 × 2‐strand graft.

The buttons’ suspension wires are calibrated, the buttons are placed loosely on the station poles, and 1 more row of knots is performed over the clean strands’ loop for extra stabilization. While positioned and tensioned at 100 N, the graft is marked at 2 and 2.5 cm on the tibial end and 1.5 and 2 cm on the femoral side, according to the planned length of the graft ends to be inside the tunnels. Two rows of sutures are placed on the tibial side of the graft and 2 on the femoral side. Each stitch must pass through each strand, and the suture limbs are wrapped once more around the graft. On completion, the final stitch will result in a buried knot technique. The sutures are placed according to the aforementioned marking of the graft (Figure 8).

Marking and suturing the graft while tensioning the graft at 1 N.

The graft is soaked in vancomycin and saline solution for at least 5 min, until fixation. Diameters are now measured at both femoral and tibial ends, and once the length of the femoral tunnel is known, the femoral interosseous distance (flip distance) is marked on the femoral suspension loop with a button in preflipped position.

Femoral and Tibial Tunnel Placement and Drilling

Meanwhile, femoral and tibial tunnels are prepared. Femoral tunnel is drilled in an isometric position, 9 using a specific guide according to graft diameter. Through an accessory medial portal, a K‐wire is first introduced, maintaining the knee at 120° of flexion; then, a 4.5 mm drill is used. Femoral tunnel length is now measured, and the final drill is used according to graft diameter, inside‐out, for 20 mm, making sure to leave at least 1 cm distance until the cortex. The tibial tunnel is prepared using the specific guide set between 55° and 60° depending on the patient's anatomy in order to obtain a longer tunnel (>30 mm). This is important to ensure optimal tightening of the suspensory mechanism‐graft construct. The tibial guide is always set and placed respecting hamstring insertion, anterior to the medial collateral ligament insertion. Under the scope view, a K‐wire is introduced anatomically into the tibial ACL footprint. If satisfied, the K‐wire is then fixed in the femur. A first drill is used according to the diameter of the graft only for the cortex of the tibia. A cannulated plugger (Zimmer Biomet) is then used to harvest a bone dowel of the same dimension as the diameter of the graft. The tunnel is finalized using a second drill until viewed on the scope exiting the tibial plateau (Figure 9).

Bone dowel harvested from the tibial tunnel (left knee at 90°).

Graft Passage and Fixation

The graft is passed through the tibial tunnel, pulled with the knee at 90° of flexion until the femoral button flips. Flipping is checked with the scope. Graft is further pulled, under scope visualization, until proper placement within the femoral tunnel, according to marking of the graft (Figure 10). Tibial fixation is performed with the knee extended and externally rotated. After the tibial button is in final position, the bone dowel previously harvested is now introduced back into the tibial tunnel (Figure 11). Stability is checked through a few rounds of flexion‐extension‐hyperextension. It is mandatory to check for graft impingement in the femoral notch throughout the entire range of motion as this may cause loose ends of the artificial device that may lead to synovitis or ultimately graft failure. If a narrow notch is present, a notchplasty is done routinely.

AL arthroscopic view (left knee) of the hybrid ligament passage and fixation, after proper notchplasty, throughout flexion‐hyperextension to infirm impingement. (AL, anterolateral.)

Bone dowel placed back into the same tibial tunnel (left knee at 90°), after graft fixation.

Postoperative Rehabilitation and 12 Months Postoperative Aspect of 4 × 2 Graft

Postoperative protocol depends on the meniscus or cartilage status. The standard protocol for simple ACL reconstruction with synthetic graft consists of immediate, gradual weight‐bearing, and no limitation of range of motion. A brace is recommended more for the physical and phycological comfort of the patient. If a meniscal suture was performed simultaneously, a restriction of 90° of flexion is implied. Early rehabilitation focuses on full extension and quadriceps activation exercises. A continuous passive motion device is used until they start rehabilitation in a recommended specialized center. Return to sports is typically allowed at 4 to 6 months for nonpivoting noncontact sports and 6 to 9 months for pivoting contact sports. 10 In our practice, all patients are advised to take a “safety return to sports” test before returning to the field. Regular follow‐up is initially scheduled at 7, 14, and 21 days to inspect and dress the postoperative wound and then at 3, 6, and 12 months for dynamic monitoring of patients.

Hybrid or simple synthetic grafts were used in over 80 patients since 2023 in the first authors’ practice. Second‐look arthroscopy 12 months after surgery in a complex case with multiligament knee reconstruction (ACL, posterolateral corner, and medial meniscus suture) showed excellent integration of ACL hybrid graft and satisfactory filling around the artificial device with ligament‐like tissue, added to the already existing GT within (Figure 12). Magnetic resonance imaging taken 8 months after surgery of the same case showed the neo‐ACL to be well‐positioned, continuous, and appropriately matured (Figure 13).

Intraoperative AL arthroscopic view of a hybrid 4 × 2 graft used in a primary ACL all‐inside reconstruction in a different patient's right knee (left) and second look 12 months after surgery (right) in a complex multiligamentous knee reconstruction showing good integration of the graft and satisfactory filling around the artificial device added to the already existing gracilis tendon within. (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament; AL, anterolateral.)

MRI obtained 8 months postoperatively from the same patient with multiligamentous right knee reconstruction, showing a well‐positioned, continuous, and appropriately matured neo‐ACL. No signs of synovitis or tunnel widening were observed. (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.)

DISCUSSION

The use of synthetic grafts has shown in many studies to provide equal or even more short‐ to long‐term stability of the knee and better patient‐reported outcomes, with similar or lower failure rates, which are essential for professional athletes and high‐demand patients. 5 , 11 , 12 , 13 At the moment, there is still some degree of scepticism among orthopaedic specialists; however, the use of artificial grafts is inevitably spreading as there is high interest and demand especially among professional athletes and high‐demand patients. 5 , 14 Nonetheless, a consensus of synthetic grafts is yet to arise.

Xiros's JewelACL device can be seen and used as an entire new ACL all by itself or as a brace. Its fibers are braided so that they do not tear or untangle after cutting the free ends, making it less likely for the device to loosen or unfold inside the knee joint and potentially cause synovitis. The nets of the device can act like a matrix and stem cells can migrate and supposedly produce collagen; 3 however, the device not being bioabsorbable, the ligamentization process does not need to be waited for and patients can return to physical activities at the same level as before injury depending on their rehabilitation progress. Evidently, spearing of hamstrings, patellar tendon, quadriceps tendon, or any other autograft also speeds up the rehabilitation period.

Among other benefits, the use of synthetic devices can be further applied in ACL revisions in case of lack of autografts or allografts and can also be a good fit for low‐demand or elderly patients who cannot commit to a very long or intense rehabilitation process.

According to authors and in line with over 20 years of cumulated experience in knee sports injuries, this 4 × 2‐strand hybrid graft preparation technique is a good solution to spear the stronger hamstring tendon, but to also biologically augment the graft, make it thicker and stronger, and optimal for professional athletes and high‐demand patients. The technique is user friendly, given the device's tubular configuration and proper marking. After a short learning curve, this technique can reduce the operative time, which can be essential in long and complex knee surgeries, such as ACL reconstructions with meniscus repair, cartilage repair, and multiple ligament reconstructions. Moreover, the integration and stability of the graft is further provided by an all‐inside technique, as bone is partially preserved in both tunnels, making it the optimal choice for athletes or high‐demand patients (Tables 1 and 2).
