Background

Microfluidic platforms offer an efficient approach for analyzing and manipulating cellular and biomolecular processes [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. In these systems, a dispersed phase is introduced into an immiscible continuous phase to generate droplets that function as isolated microreactors for encapsulating biological or chemical samples, thereby enabling precise monitoring and analysis [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Their capabilities to produce and manipulate small reaction volumes, and excellent control over reaction conditions, droplet microfluidic platforms have been successfully applied to drug screening, enzyme engineering, biofuel production, gene transfer, and, more recently, green chemistry [[9], [10], [11]]. Beyond laboratory research, droplet microfluidic technologies are increasingly used in commercial applications including molecular diagnostics, pharmaceutical screening, and single-cell sequencing. However, broader application remains limited by challenges related to sustainable materials and maintaining biologically relevant operating conditions.

Fluorinated oils have been widely used as the continuous phase because of their chemical stability and compatibility with biological samples [12]. However, growing concerns regarding the health and environmental risks associated with per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) have driven the search for more sustainable alternatives [13].

Many biological and biochemical assays performed in microdroplets also require precise thermal control to preserve native molecular and cellular behavior [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]].

However, most studies have focused on maintaining a uniform temperature throughout the microfluidic device or within the generated droplets, while the independent effects of the dispersed- and continuous-phase temperatures on droplet formation have been poorly studied.

Building on our recent work demonstrating emu oil, an animal-derived fatty acid oil, as a sustainable alternative to fluorinated oils [23], this study addresses this gap by systematically investigating the influence of phase-specific temperature control on droplet formation regimes. By establishing the relationship between the temperature of each fluid phase and droplet formation characteristics, this work provides practical insights for the design of sustainable microfluidic platforms operating under physiologically relevant conditions. The findings are particularly relevant to temperature-sensitive biological applications, including the polymerization of encapsulated intracellular biofilaments, such as microtubules, and their interactions with therapeutic agents.

Method details

In this work, we engineered a method that enabled controlled variation of the temperature of the two fluid phases used for microdroplet generation. The objective of this research is to achieve a quantitative understanding of how independent thermal conditions in the dispersed and continuous phases influence droplet formation characteristics and reproducibility, particularly in sustainable alternatives to fluorinated oils.

The experimental setup followed our previously reported configuration [23]. Following the methodology reported in similar microfluidics studies, deionized water containing dye (in this case black dye 0.5% (v/v)) was used as the dispersed phase to facilitate visualization of droplet formation [24]. The centrifuged and filtered emu oil (commercially purchased from Botanical Beauty, purchased through Amazon Inc., Seattle, WA, USA) used as the continuous phase. Two syringe pumps (Infusion ONE, New Era Pump Systems, Inc., Farmingdale, New York, NY, USA) were part of the system to introduce the fluids through connected tubing into the microchannels. A T-junction microchannel (The microfluidic 285 chip, microfluidics ChipShop GmbH, Jena, Germany) was used for droplet generation and was mounted on the stage of an optical upright brightfield microscope (AmScope Microscopes, Irvine, CA, USA). The generated droplets were observed using a microscope-mounted high speed camera (FLIR-Teledyne Imaging—with frame rate of 55 fps, Wilsonville, OR, USA)equipped with Spin View software [Computer software, version 4.2.0.88, Teledyne DALSA Inc., Waterloo, ON, Canada), recorded in real time, and subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software (version number 1.54p,Rasband, W.S., ImageJ, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Temperature control element: The goal of the experiment was to independently adjust the temperature of each fluid at the microchannel entry point. To achieve this, the PTFE tubing connecting the syringe pumps to the microchannel inlets was routed through a heating pad (Pump Accessories / Thermo-Kinetic Heater Control Unit / HEATER-KIT-1LG, NEW ERA, Pump System Inc., East Farmingdale, New York) positioned close to the channel entrance. By adjusting the temperature of the heating pad, the temperature of the fluids could be increased before entering the microchannel. Two separate heating pads were installed along the tubing connecting the oil and water syringes to the microchannel entrances. The temperature of each syringe, and consequently the fluid flowing through the associated tubing and heating pad, could be controlled independently. This setup enabled the temperature of the dispersed and continuous phases to be varied independently or simultaneously. In this study, the temperature of each phase was increased individually as well as together, and the resulting effects on microdroplet formation were characterized. The schematic view of the experimental setup. The tubing connecting each syringe to the microchip entrance passed through a separate heating pad, enabling independent control of the temperature of each phase before it entered the microchannel.

Although significant heat loss occurred over the short distance between the heating pad and the channel inlet due to the low thermal conductivity of the PTFE tubing, the thermal properties of the fluid phases, and exposure of the tubing to ambient conditions, the system still enabled reliable control of fluid temperature. The baseline (room temperature) of the fluids was approximately 23 °C and could be increased to about 25 °C by raising the heating pad temperature from 23 °C to 83 °C in 20 °C increments. The fluid temperature at the microchannel entrance was measured using a digital thermometer (thermometer basic type K/J, Sper Scientific 800004, Sper Scientific Direct, Environmental Measurement Instruments, Scottsdale, AZ, USA).

Viscosity Measurement: The temperature of the fluid phases at the microchannel inlet was approximately 23 °C under ambient conditions and was increased to 25 °C during the experiments. The viscosity of water under this condition decreases from 0.9321 mPa·s at 23 °C to 0.8900 mPa·s at 25 °C, corresponding to an approximate 4.5% reduction [25].

The viscosity of the filtered emu oil was measured by RheoSense Inc. (Rheo Sense Inc., San Ramon, CA, USA) using an m-VROC II® instrument equipped with a B05 chip (depth ≈ 50 µm, Pmax ≈ 42 kPa). Measurements were conducted over a temperature range of 23 to 25 °C in 0.5 °C increments. The viscosity decreased from 0.054 mPa·s at 23 °C to approximately 0.049 mPa·s at 25 °C, corresponding to an approximate 9.3% reduction (supplementary materials).

Visualization and statistical analysis: The microfluidic chip was mounted on the stage of the microscope and observed using a 4 × objective lens. The microchannel had a width of 0.08 mm and a depth of 0.14 mm. As the width of the microchannel was smaller than the diameter of the microdroplets, the droplets were produced in the squeezing regime. Following droplet formation, microscopic videos were recorded for 2–3 min. This duration ensured the collection of a sufficient number of microdroplets for subsequent analysis.

The recorded videos were then analyzed using ImageJ software, and the size of each droplet was measured manually. A sufficient number of droplets was measured to obtain an adequate sample size for statistical analysis. The collected measurements were used to determine the mean droplet size and standard deviation (SD) for each experimental condition. Based on the number of droplets analyzed, as well as the mean values and standard deviations of each sample, statistical comparisons were performed using a t-test with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Method validation

Droplet formation at room temperature was observed when the flow rate of the continuous phase (Emu oil) was 30 µL/h and that of the dispersed phase was 50 µL/h. These conditions were used as the baseline for droplet formation while the temperature of each phase, individually and simultaneously, was increased.

The overall experiment consisted of three phases: (a) increasing only the temperature of the dispersed aqueous phase, (b) increasing only the temperature of the continuous oil phase, and (c) increasing the temperatures of both phases simultaneously. Each phase was performed on three separate days, and microscopic videos were recorded following the droplet formation. The overall experiment was repeated three times, except for Phase (c), which was repeated twice because the system transitioned rapidly to the jetting regime at elevated temperatures. The recorded videos were analyzed independently, and the corresponding data were collected and processed separately. The numerical values of the droplet sizes’ mean showed only minor variations among the different phases of repeated experiments, with the differences primarily reflected in the standard deviations. Nevertheless, all experiments exhibited the same overall behavior, with increasing temperature resulting in larger droplets and a consistent transition from the squeezing to the jetting regime.

It is notable that, when the temperature of the dispersed phase was increased, the droplet size grew from 0.19 ± 0.01 mm to 0.46 ± 0.1 mm, corresponding to a 130% increase, and then system consequently transitioned into the jetting regime after this thermal threshold. However, the decrease in the dispersed phase flow rate to 35 μL/h caused the system to shift back from the jetting to the dripping regime, producing droplets with a size of 0.35 ± 0.04 mm (n = 10).

Similarly, increasing the temperature of the continuous phase resulted in an increase in droplet size from 0.28 ± 0.02 mm to 0.67 ± 0.14 mm, representing approximately a 140% increase. Compared to the case where only the dispersed phase was thermally manipulated, the transition to the jetting regime occurred at a lower temperature when the continuous phase was heated. Again, the system returned to the dripping regime at the dispersed flow rate of 35 μl/h with the droplet size of 0.62 ± 0.09 (n = 10).

When the temperatures of both phases were increased simultaneously, the droplet size increased from 0.30 ± 0.01 mm to 0.74 ± 0.13 mm (approximately 150% increase) under smaller thermal variations recorded by the heating pad. In this case, the transition to the jetting regime occurred at even lower temperature changes compared to when only one phase was heated. Simultaneously increasing the temperature of both fluid phases at constant flow rates resulted in a significant increase in breakup time compared to heating each phase individually.

In microdroplet platforms, the capillary number, one of the key parameters, is defined as Cac=ηcUγ, where γis the interfacial tension between the continuous and dispersed phases (mN/m), and ηc is the dynamic viscosity of the continuous phase. The capillary number is inversely correlated with droplet size.

Increasing the temperature of the dispersed phase increases the molecular mobility of water molecules and weakens the hydrogen-bond network, consequently reducing the viscosity and allowing the water to flow more easily. Because the temperature of the continuous phase was independently controlled, heating the dispersed phase primarily altered the properties of water while producing only minimal changes in the surrounding oil. Consequently, only a slight increase in droplet size is observed. This change does not significantly affect the capillary number; as a result, the transition to jetting is only slightly accelerated. In contrast, increasing the temperature of the oil influences droplet breakup by increasing the molecular mobility of the oil molecules leading to reducing both the oil viscosity and the oil–water interfacial tension. The lower viscosity decreases the associated squeezing stress acted by the continuous phase. Also, the reduction in interfacial tension makes droplet deformation easier. As a result, the dispersed phase elongates into the oil shell further before pinch-off, producing larger squeezed droplets and leading to an earlier transition to the jetting regime. Finally, when the temperature of both phases increases simultaneously, these effects combine, leading to a rapid increase in droplet size and a significantly accelerated transition from the squeezing to the jetting regime.

Limitations

In the effort to identify environmentally safe microdroplet systems, fatty acid-based oils, such as emu oil, represent a promising alternative to fluorinated and other synthetic oils. However, because microdroplets function as microreactors, they often need to operate under biologically relevant or reaction-specific conditions, such as elevated temperatures required for certain biochemical reactions or polymerization processes. While temperature can be essential for enabling these processes, it also plays a significant role in influencing droplet morphology and stability.

This study demonstrates the droplet formation parameters of emu oil as a function of temperature changes in the dispersed phase, the continuous phase, and both phases simultaneously. The results show that increasing temperature accelerates the transition toward the jetting regime, with the transition occurring progressively faster when heating is applied first to the dispersed phase, then to the continuous phase, and most rapidly when both phases are heated together. In addition, this shift is accompanied by an increase in droplet size.

These findings build upon our previous work on droplet formation using fatty acid–based oils and highlight the critical role of temperature under conditions where phase-specific thermal control is possible. Despite these insights, the long-term stability of such microdroplets remains an open question and warrants further investigation.
