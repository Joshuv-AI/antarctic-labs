PSF-Driven
Spatiotemporal Blending in Fluorescence
Lifetime Imaging Microscopy and Its Mitigation via Mean-Shift Super-Resolution-Based
Masking

Mario González-Gutiérrez, Diana M Vázquez-Enciso, Nicolás Mateos, Wonsang Hwang, Esley Torres-García, Haydee O Hernández, Jenu V Chacko, Iván Coto Hernández, Pablo Loza-Álvarez, Christopher Wood, Adan Guerrero

Abstract

Fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) enables
quantitative
mapping of molecular environments in living systems with high biochemical
specificity. However, spatial overlap dictated by the diffraction-limited
point spread function (PSF) causes a mixing of temporal signals: photons
from neighboring emitters collected within the same pixel yield composite
decay profiles, generating apparent intermediate lifetimes that can
be mistaken for variations in the local molecular environment. We
introduce a workflow that applies mean-shift super-resolution (MSSR)
to raw intensity data to generate intensity-derived spatial masks
prior to phasor-based lifetime analysis. The method is computationally
efficient and preserves decay kinetics because it operates on intensity-derived
spatial information rather than modifying temporal data. In U2OS cells
labeled with spectrally-overlapping fluorophores, phasor analysis
reveals an intermediate lifetime population localized at PSF-overlap
interfaces, consistent with optical mixing rather than intrinsic lifetime
heterogeneity. MSSR-derived masking suppressed this mixed population
while preserving the distribution of similar phasor coordinates in
the phasor plane for each fluorophore. Simulations of strictly monoexponential
fluorescence decay emitters further show that blended lifetime decay
profiles are present at separations up to 4σ, indicating that
conventional spatial resolution criteria can underestimate lifetime
cross-talk. Application of this workflow to three-component FLIM showed
also a reduced overlap of pixel distributions in phasor plots while
maintaining distinct lifetime signatures. Ultimately, the primary
value of MSSR-based spatial refinement lies in leveraging improved
spatial resolution to effectively isolate the temporal profiles of
mixed fluorophore populations preserving the integrity of FLIM measurements.

Introduction

Fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy
(FLIM) maps the spatial
distribution of fluorescence decay kinetics and thereby reports on
biochemical and biophysical states that are often inaccessible to
intensity-only imaging. By probing excited-state relaxation dynamics,
FLIM enables contrast based on environmental sensitivity and molecular
interactions even when fluorophore concentration, excitation inhomogeneity,
or optical path variations confound intensity measurements. FLIM implementations
are commonly classified into time-domain (TD-FLIM) and frequency-domain
(FD-FLIM) modalities.
−

 TD-FLIM estimates lifetimes from the distribution of photon arrival
times after pulsed excitation, most often using time-correlated single-photon
counting (TCSPC) with single-photon detectors (including photomultiplier-based
and single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD)-based detectors) or using
widefield time-gated detection, and recent advances include analog
approaches that record fluorescence waveforms.
−

 FD-FLIM, in contrast, derives lifetimes from the
phase shift and demodulation of fluorescence emission signals relative
to a temporally modulated excitation signal. FLIM has found extensive applications in medical imaging and in
biochemical and physicochemical research, enabling quantitative assessments
of parameters such as pH, viscosity, and other environmental or material
properties.
,−

In FLIM measurements, uncertainty in the temporal characterization
of fluorescence decays can arise from several distinct sources. First,
the instrument response function (IRF) limits the temporal resolution
with which photon arrival times can be resolved, imposing an instrumental
broadening of the measured decay. Second, the stochastic nature of
photon detection introduces shot-noise-limited uncertainty, particularly
under low-count regimes. Third, and central
to the present work, diffraction-limited imaging induces spatial convolution
through the optical point spread function (PSF), such that photons
emitted by neighboring fluorophores are collected within the same
pixel. This spatial overlap produces composite decay signals that
manifest as apparent lifetime mixtures that do not correspond to intrinsic
photophysical interactions.
,
 We refer to this effect
as PSF-induced temporal blending (TB).

Independent of acquisition
modality, lifetime estimation can be
performed using fitting or non-fitting approaches. Fitting methods
include weighted least squares estimation (WLS), maximum likelihood
estimation (MLE), and Global and Bayesian fitting. Non-fitting routines
include rapid lifetime determination (RLD), Center-of-mass methods
(CMM), Integral extraction methods, Laguerre Expansion, and the Phasor
approach. More recently, machine learning
and deep learning strategies have been explored to improve estimation
accuracy and throughput, and to provide denoising capabilities.
−

The phasor method provides a graphical representation of fluorescence
decay kinetics by mapping each pixel to phasor coordinates (G,S).
In this framework, monoexponential decays lie on the universal semicircle,
which represents the geometric locus of phasor coordinates corresponding
to lifetimes ranging from zero to infinity (for a given modulation
frequency). Multiexponential decays fall inside this semicircle, where
their position corresponds to the vector sum of the phasors of the
individual lifetime components, weighted by their relative contributions.
Intermediate phasor positions can reflect either genuine biochemical
heterogeneity within the sampled volume or linear combinations of
distinct lifetime components. This mixing property is useful for unmixing
and segmentation, but it also implies that intermediate lifetimes
can arise from optical cross-talk when photons from multiple emitters
are pooled within a single spatial bin.
−

As introduced above, PSF-induced temporal blending
(TB) leads to
spatially mixed decay signals that can bias lifetime estimation in
diffraction-limited conditions. A direct strategy to mitigate TB is
to increase spatial resolution so that neighboring emitters are better
separated prior to lifetime extraction. Super-resolution techniques
can be broadly classified into three categories. The first comprises
deterministic approaches such as Stimulated Emission Depletion (STED)
and Structured Illumination Microscopy (SIM).
−

 STED has been integrated with lifetime measurements through multiple
strategies, including time-gated STED, Separation of Photons by Lifetime
Tuning (SPLIT-STED), STED-FLIM combined with phasor analysis, and
TauSTED, which exploit depletion-induced lifetime gradients to refine
contrast and spatial information.
−

 The second category comprises stochastic localization-based super-resolution
methods collectively referred to as Single-Molecule Localization Microscopy
(SMLM). Representative SMLM implementations include Photoactivated
Localization Microscopy (PALM) and Stochastic Optical Reconstruction
Microscopy (STORM), which reconstruct super-resolved images by localizing
temporally separated single-emitter signals over many acquisition
frames.
−

 Lifetime-resolved variants, including confocal fluorescence-lifetime
SMLM and time-correlated SMLM, further increase molecular specificity
by assigning lifetime values to localized molecules using TCSPC-based
detection.
,

The third category comprises
computational approaches, including
deconvolution methods that attempt to reverse optical blurring using
an estimated PSF, as well as fluctuation-based super-resolution methods
such as cross-cumulant Superresolution Optical Fluctuation Imaging
(XC-SOFI), Bayesian analysis of Bleaching and Blinking data (3B),
Entropy-based Superresolution Imaging (ESI), and Super-Resolution
Radial Fluctuations (SRRF). Addressing
TB specifically, Bastiaens and Squire developed an iterative constrained
deconvolution method based on the Tikhonov–Miller algorithm
(ICTM), processing temporal information through Fourier components
that are trigonometric analogs of the phasor coordinates (G,S).
,
 While effective in relevant contexts, many computational super-resolution
strategies require large frame stacks, substantial computation, or
temporal processing steps that may introduce artifacts or confounds
when applied to FLIM, particularly when preservation of decay kinetics
is essential.

Mean-shift super-resolution
(MSSR) offers a distinct opportunity
for FLIM because it operates on intensity information to enhance spatial
separability without directly altering the fluorescence decay kinetics. In the present workflow, MSSR is applied to
an average (or integrated) intensity projection of the FLIM data set
to generate an intensity-derived spatial mask with improved separability
and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Phasor coordinates are then computed
from the unmodified time-resolved FLIM data within the masked regions,
thereby suppressing PSF-driven photon mixing while conserving the
lifetime signatures. Accordingly, we developed and validated a method
that couples MSSR-derived spatial masks with phasor analysis, demonstrating
that improved spatial separability can reduce temporal blending in
FLIM while maintaining decay fidelity in complex specimens.

Results

The central hypothesis of this work is that
intermediate apparent
lifetime signatures can arise from optical mixing rather than intrinsic
fluorescence heterogeneity. A brief conceptual foundation in phasor
analysis is therefore provided to support interpretation of the results
(Supporting Information, Figure S1). In
phasor space, monoexponential fluorescence decays map onto the universal
semicircle, whereas multicomponent decays and mixtures of fluorescence
signals occupy positions within the semicircle according to the linear-combination
rule. Consequently, when photons emitted by distinct monoexponential
species are collected within the same diffraction-limited observation
volume, the resulting phasor lies at an intermediate position between
the corresponding end point lifetimes.

Spatial Convolution Drives Temporal Blending in Lifetime Imaging

To experimentally illustrate how temporal blending (TB) emerges
at the cellular scale, we analyzed a fixed-cell specimen in which
two distinct subcellular structuresmitochondria and microtubuleswere
labeled with spectrally overlapping fluorophores. Alexa Fluor 532
(AF532; TOM20) and Alexa Fluor 555 (AF555; α-tubulin) have closely
spaced emission maxima (553 and 580 nm), rendering the two structures
indistinguishable in single-channel confocal intensity imaging (a). In this reference
specimen, both dyes are immobilized via immunolabeling on their respective
targets, and no direct biochemical coupling between labels is expected.
The system therefore approximates two independent lifetime species;
under this condition, intermediate apparent lifetimes are predicted
to arise predominantly from optical photon pooling when diffraction-limited
point spread functions (PSFs) overlap.

Mean-Shift Super-Resolution Mitigates Temporal Blending in Fluorescence
Lifetime Imaging Microscopy

The baseline temporal blending
(TB) signature established above,
defined as a spatially patterned intermediate lifetime class concentrated
at structural interfaces, led us to explore a strategy that increases
spatial separability without direct manipulation of decay kinetics.
Mean-shift super-resolution (MSSR) operates on the spatial intensity
landscape and can reduce effective PSF overlap through redistribution
of signal density toward local maxima, thereby improving emitter separability
in diffraction-limited data.
,,
 In the present workflow, MSSR is used as a spatial prior that defines
where photons are likely to originate, after which lifetime information
is interpreted from the original time-resolved measurements. This
ordering is essential: spatial processing is applied to an intensity
projection to derive a mask, and phasor coordinates remain derived
from the unmodified time domain FLIM data, preserving the decay information
that underpins the phasor representation.

A practical aspect
of this implementation is that the masking step
uses an intermediate-bounded output from MSSR rather than the final
remapped intensity image. Specifically, the procedure intentionally
omits the last MSSR intensity remapping (normalization step) and instead
uses the intermediate matrix that is naturally bounded between 0 and
1, denoted here as (
PMSSRn
). This quantity behaves as a probability-like
spatial support map, i.e., values near 1 concentrate around likely
emitter locations, whereas low values occupy background and overlap
regions. Thresholding 
PMSSRn
 therefore yields binary masks that progressively
restrict analysis to spatial regions with high localization likelihood,
a property exploited here to mitigate mixed-pixel signatures while
maintaining the end point lifetime classes identified previously.

The expected effect of MSSR on separability is illustrated using
a two-emitter model at the Sparrow limit (two Gaussian emitters separated
by 2σ). In the raw summed profile, strong overlap yields a single
broadened maximum and poor resolvability at the midpoint (a). After MSSR processing,
the probability-like output becomes increasingly bimodal as MSSR order
increases, enabling threshold selection that isolates high-support
cores of each emitter while suppressing the overlap region (b). The corresponding
two-dimensional maps illustrate that, across threshold levels, raw
data retain substantial overlap, whereas MSSR0 and MSSR progressively concentrate probability density
into separated lobes (c). These model results define the operational principle used
in the cellular experiment. applies the same 
PMSSRn
-thresholding logic outlined in to generate a spatial
mask from a FLIM intensity projection, with the goal of suppressing
overlap-dominated pixels that are predicted to underline intermediate
phasor positions.

To test this prediction in the cellular specimen,
MSSR was applied
to an intensity projection of the FLIM acquisition to generate 
PMSSR1
, which was thresholded to produce a binary
mask. The mask was then applied to the phasor-coordinate images (G,S)
computed from the original time-resolved FLIM data, so that only spatially
supported pixels contributed to the phasor plot and to the corresponding
pseudo-color maps (b). If MSSR preprocessing had distorted decay kinetics, such
distortion would manifest as a systematic displacement of the end
point phasor clusters. Instead, the masked analysis preserves the
end point loci while altering the spatial sampling
of pixels that enter the analysis (compare a versus b), consistent with conservation of the underlying
lifetime signatures. This invariance was quantified by computing phasor-cluster
centroids before and after masking and measuring their displacement
as a Euclidean distance in (G,S) space; centroid shifts were minimal
(0.0066 for the whole cell and 0.0064 for ROI 1) and remained well
below a conservative no-distortion bound of 0.05 phasor units selected
to exceed fluctuations expected from photon noise and finite sampling
(a,b; Methods).
In parallel, the spatial maps show improved delineation of microtubule-like
and mitochondria-like structures within the ROI, accompanied by an
apparent reduction in the number of pixels assigned to the intermediate
mixed class (b, ROI 1). Consistent with reduced mixing, the phasor clusters obtained
after masking exhibit increased compactness, which was quantified
using an ellipse-area ratio defined as the cluster ellipse area after
masking divided by the area before masking, which remained below unity
for end point clusters (Methods). Because reduced phasor cluster area
reflects diminished pooling of heterogeneous decays within single
pixels, the combined behavior of minimal centroid displacement and
reduced ellipse area supports the conclusion that MSSR-derived probability
masking mitigates TB-associated mixing signatures while preserving
the end point lifetime components of the specimen. In other words,
it reduces intermediate apparent lifetimes attributable to photon
mixing.

In Silico Characterization of Diffraction-Induced Temporal Blending
Evidenced as Phasor Mixing

To systematically characterize
TB, we developed an in silico model composed of two independent, noninteracting
fluorophores. This simulation isolates and quantifies the contribution
of point spread function (PSF) overlap to TB at controlled spatial
separations (). The model is applicable to both time-domain FLIM (TD-FLIM, a) and frequency-domain
FLIM (FD-FLIM, Figure S3) modalities.

Building on an initial frequency domain approximation
of signal
mixing, we implemented a more general
simulation framework that explicitly accounts for PSF-induced mixing
prior to phasor extraction. This refinement avoids a coarse-grained
representation of temporal behavior and enables the same simulation
backbone to be used in both the TD-FLIM and FD-FLIM regimes. Using
PhasorPy, time-resolved fluorescence
signals were generated for two fluorophores with distinct lifetimes
of 1 ns and 4 ns. The temporal information was initially assigned

4 ns) at pixel (i
1,j
1) and fluorophore 2 (short-lived, 1 ns) at pixel (i
2,j

image stack with dimensions (x, y, t) was then constructed, where each frame t represents a discrete time point within the simulated
excitation cycle. To model spatial blurring, the fluorophore pixels

Mean-Shift Super-Resolution Addresses Spatiotemporal Resolution
in Three-Component FLIM Images

In the preceding sections,
we demonstrated that masking FLIM images with MSSR-enhanced intensity
maps mitigates temporal blending arising from the spatial overlap
of two independent fluorophore PSFs. Here, we extend this approach
to a more complex three-component system and show that the same strategy
reduces lifetime mixing originating from the spatial overlap of three
distinct fluorescent species.

These findings establish that mean-shift
super-resolution enables
spatial refinement of FLIM images without perturbing the underlying
lifetime information, even in complex, multi-component systems. The
persistence of stable phasor centroids alongside reduced lifetime
mixing supports a spatialrather than temporalorigin
of Temporal Blending.

Discussion

Temporal blending in FLIM has been approached
from multiple theoretical
and computational perspectives, yet a unified interpretation that
cleanly separates spatial from temporal contributions remains incomplete.
Here, we frame TB primarily as a PSF-mediated optical mixing effect
and demonstrate that applying MSSR-derived spatial masks reduces this
mixing while preserving lifetime information on the retained pixels.
This interpretation is supported by phasor-cluster centroid stability
accompanied by a systematic reduction in the phasor cluster area.

The impact of spatial overlap on lifetime measurements was recognized
in early FLIM studies. To the best of our knowledge, the first explicit
description of this phenomenon was provided by Anthony Squire and
Philippe Bastiaens, who introduced the term temporal blurring. Although this terminology appropriately conveys
the decrease of measurement precision in FLIM, in the present work
we adopt the term temporal blending to reflect more precisely the
underlying mechanism: the mixing of photon streams from spatially
overlapping PSFs prior to lifetime transformation. In this framework,
the measured decay corresponds to a spatially weighted superposition
of multiple fluorescence decays within the diffraction-limited observation
volume. Importantly, TB can mimic genuine lifetime heterogeneity arising
from environmental variations, molecular interactions, or conformational
dynamics, thereby complicating the interpretation of the FLIM measurements.
,

It is essential to distinguish this effect from fundamentally
different
phenomena such as diffraction in time. However, PSF-induced temporal blending represents a classical, instrumentally
induced effect arising from optical diffraction, diffraction in time
is a quantum-mechanical phenomenon associated with the time–energy
uncertainty principle and describes the transient behavior of massive
particles governed by the Schrödinger equation when encountering
temporal boundaries. Despite the similarity
in terminology, this effect is unrelated to the optical measurement
process considered here.

Early deconvolution-based strategies,
such as the Tikhonov–Miller
reconstruction introduced by Squire and Bastiaens, sought to mitigate spatial blurring by applying regularization
independently to each Fourier component (mean, real, and imaginary
frames) to deblur FLIM images by minimizing a Tikhonov functional. While effective at sharpening images, regularization
performed directly on time-varying components can differentially reshape
harmonics under low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and/or elevated background,
which may distort phasor geometry and bias lifetime estimation.

A complementary analytical framework was introduced by Yuan et
al. through the FMCE, which describes
how, in FD-FLIM, multiemitter measurements may become dominated by
the strongest emitter. In that model, amplitude-dependent capture
biases the measured lifetime toward the brightest fluorophore, producing
smoothly varying lifetime distributions and spatial mislocalization
artifacts. To mitigate this effect, Yuan et al. proposed a hybrid
strategy in which spatial resolution is enhanced using super-resolution
radial fluctuations (SRRF) on an intensity image and subsequently
combined with the lifetime map. Although powerful, this approach typically
relies on multiframe reconstruction and temporal interpolation. Additionally,
it can be computationally demanding, because SRRF benefits from large
frame counts for optimal performance.

Unlike this strategy,
the present work does not reconstruct additional
temporal information or modify the lifetime-estimation procedure.
Instead, MSSR is applied exclusively to the intensity (DC) image prior
to phasor transformation, where it generates an intensity-derived
spatial discrimination mask that emphasizes emission maxima while
suppressing low-intensity neighborhoods, where PSF overlap contributes
to signal mixing. Phasor coordinates are subsequently computed from
the retained pixel set without temporal interpolation, resampling,
or manipulation of the fluorescence decay information. Consequently,
MSSR does not alter the fluorescence decay associated with individual
retained pixels; instead, it modifies the subset of pixels contributing
to the phasor distribution by preferentially excluding the overlap-dominated
regions. Therefore, the global phasor distribution is not expected
to be identical before and after the masking. Rather, the expected
outcome is the preservation of the end point phasor coordinates together
with a reduction in the relative weighting of intermediate mixed-pixel
populations, as spatially induced signal mixing is mitigated upstream
of the phasor transformation.

Analysis of both two- and three-component
FLIM shows that phasor-cluster
centroids remain practically invariant before and after MSSR masking,
supporting the preservation of the intrinsic lifetime information.
Simultaneously, the marked reduction in phasor-cluster dispersion
and the improved separation between components indicate that the MSSR
suppresses overlap-driven mixing without introducing synthetic decay
components. This behavior distinguishes MSSR from approaches that
act directly on time-resolved data or implicitly modify the temporal
content through reconstruction. Notably, in the most challenging three-component
system, MSSR enhances the separation of AF555 and DAPI populations,
reducing the extensive mixing observed in diffraction-limited FLIM
and yielding more symmetric, well-resolved phasor distributions.

The experimental systems investigated in this study were deliberately
selected as controlled proof-of-concept specimens since immobilized
fluorophores associated with distinct subcellular structures allow
PSF-mediated optical mixing to be evaluated independently of biochemical
interactions. Extension of the proposed workflow to more complex biological
settings, including live-cell imaging and heterogeneous specimens,
is expected to introduce additional sources of variability, such as
sample motion, photobleaching, dynamic changes in fluorophore brightness,
heterogeneous labeling density, and molecular interactions, all of
which may influence the interpretation of MSSR-masked phasor distributions.
Nevertheless, the method is expected to remain most effective in applications
where the primary objective is to reduce spatially induced signal
mixing between adjacent structures, exhibiting distinguishable lifetime
signatures. Conversely, when lifetime heterogeneity is intrinsic to
the retained pixels, MSSR-FLIM should not be interpreted as a complete
unmixing strategy but rather as a complementary approach for improving
the spatial specificity of phasor-based lifetime analysis.

From
a practical standpoint, the MSSR-derived masking strategy
inevitably introduces a trade-off between the spatial discrimination
and signal retention. By preferentially excluding low-probability
pixels, the mask reduces the contribution of overlap-dominated regions
to the phasor distribution, but it may also remove weak fluorescence
structures, particularly in photon-limited data sets or specimens
with low labeling density. This limitation should be considered in
the broader context of FLIM, where lifetime estimation is fundamentally
constrained by photon statistics, irrespective of the analysis method
employed (1-3). Consequently, although MSSR has been shown to remain
robust under low signal-to-noise conditions for image reconstruction, overly stringent masking may reduce the number
of pixels available for phasor analysis and thus diminish the representation
of low-intensity structures.

The threshold selection should
therefore balance the reduction
of overlap-induced mixing against the preservation of a sufficient
signal for reliable lifetime estimation. Rather than relying on a
single threshold value, we recommend evaluating threshold sensitivity
by jointly monitoring the retained pixel fraction, the stability of
the end point phasor populations, the reduction of intermediate mixed-pixel
contributions, and the structural consistency of the reconstructed
image. Such an empirical evaluation allows the masking level to be
adapted to the characteristics of each data set while preserving the
benefits of MSSR without unnecessarily compromising biologically relevant
low-intensity features.

Additionally, since the MSSR-derived
mask is constructed exclusively
from the intensity image, its performance is inherently influenced
by factors that affect fluorescence intensity, including fluorophore
brightness, labeling density, excitation or detection inhomogeneity,
optical attenuation, background signal, photobleaching, and, naturally,
true variations in fluorophore abundance. Consequently, these factors
may influence which pixels are retained for phasor analysis and, therefore,
modify the statistical representation of lifetime populations within
the final phasor distribution. This behavior should not be interpreted
as a modification of the underlying fluorescence decay kinetics but
rather as an inherent consequence of any intensity-based selection
strategy. Accordingly, MSSR-FLIM is intended to reduce PSF-mediated
signal mixing and improve the spatial specificity of lifetime analysis
rather than to provide an unbiased quantification of fluorophore abundance
without appropriate experimental controls.

The present approach
is specifically designed to mitigate PSF-mediated
spatial mixing arising from the diffraction-limited overlap of the
neighboring fluorescence sources. Consequently, the observed improvement
in lifetime discrimination originates from reducing the contribution
of overlap-dominated pixels to the phasor analysis rather than from
separating the intrinsically mixed fluorescence decays. Pixels that
genuinely contain multiple lifetime components, e.g., because distinct fluorophores coexist within the same diffraction-limited
region or because unresolved subpixel lifetime heterogeneity is present,
remain represented by their corresponding composite decay after masking.
In other words, the MSSR-FLIM approach proposed in this work was not
evaluated as a method for decomposing multicomponent fluorescence
decays within a single pixel. Nevertheless, dedicated phasor methodologies
based on higher-order harmonic analysis have been developed for this
purpose, highlighting that these approaches
and MSSR-FLIM address complementary aspects of lifetime analysis,
the former decomposes multicomponent fluorescence decays within individual
pixels, whereas the latter mitigates PSF-mediated spatial mixing prior
to phasor analysis.

The in silico characterization further shows
that TB persists even
when fluorophores are spatially resolvable under conventional diffraction-based
criteria. Significant phasor mixing remains detectable at emitter
separations as large as 4σ, intensifies near the 2σ Sparrow
limit, and reaches a maximum at intermediate separations around 1.6σ
under the simulated conditions. At complete overlap (0σ), the
phasor distribution collapses toward the intensity-weighted linearly-combined
phasor of the overlapping lifetimes. These effects emerge despite
the presence of strictly non-interacting emitters, having monoexponential
relaxation dynamics demonstrating that apparent lifetime heterogeneity
can arise purely from PSF-mediated spatial blending.

The Richardson-Lucy
comparison highlights that reducing the statistical
dispersion of the phasor distribution does not necessarily eliminate
intermediate phasor populations. Although the centroid displacement
remained within the acceptance criterion, MSSR-FLIM performed better
since Richardson-Lucy provided a 2.8 times larger centroid displacement.
On the other hand, although the ellipse area became markedly more
compact, a continuous bridge between the end point populations persisted
in phasor space. These observations suggest that phasor compactness
and the continuity of intermediate populations describe complementary
aspects of the distribution and should therefore be interpreted independently.
In summary, under the conditions presented in this work, Richardson–Lucy
primarily reduces the statistical dispersion of the phasor distribution,
whereas MSSR-FLIM preferentially reduces the continuity of intermediate
phasor populations associated with PSF-mediated optical mixing.

To provide additional insight into how MSSR masking influences
the relative weighting of lifetime populations in phasor space, we
performed a fractional contribution analysis of lifetime components,
shown in Figure S5 for the two-color FLIM
case. Conventionally, fractional contributions are interpreted as
the relative amplitudes or pre-exponential factors from the fluorescence
decay components,

of each component to the detected signal. Before masking, the distribution
exhibits a broad transition between the two reference fluorophore
populations, with a pronounced shoulder near equal fractional contributions
(∼0.5). As the MSSR masking threshold increases, this intermediate

instance, a 4% threshold yields approximately two-fold decrease in
the steepness of the transition suggesting a better separation of
phasor lobules; thus, enriching pixel populations associated with
the dominant lifetime components (see Table S3, Supporting Information). Together, these observations are consistent
with a reduced representation of pixels containing mixed fluorescence
contributions, which are expected to arise predominantly from spatial
overlap within the diffraction-limited image.

Importantly, this
redistribution should not be interpreted as a
modification of the fluorescence decay kinetics of the retained pixels.
Instead, it reflects a redistribution of the relative weightings of
the pixel populations contributing to the phasor analysis. Likewise,
the increased representation of pixels with high fractional contributions
near the 0.75 region of the plot is consistent with the preferential
retention of pixels that are more likely to belong to the corresponding
signal source after thresholding. Consequently, the masking strategy
inherently represents a trade-off between reducing overlap-dominated
pixels and excluding low-probability pixels, particularly under photon-limited
conditions. Although the observed redistribution may also reflect
the influence of temporal blending on the fractional contribution
distributions, a quantitative relationship between these distributions
and the degree of signal overlap remains to be established. Developing
such a theoretical framework could provide a physically motivated
criterion for selecting the masking threshold according to the degree
of the overlap. However, the development of this theoretical description
is beyond the scope of the present work.

It is important to
emphasize that this scenario represents a controlled
boundary condition in which the geometric–optical contribution
to TB is isolated through idealized assumptions. In practical biological
systems, fluorophores within a diffraction-limited volume may undergo
interactions such as Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET),
where intermolecular distance plays a critical role. However, the
absence of characteristic FRET signatures in the phasor plots supports the conclusion that such interactions
do not significantly contribute under the conditions examined here.
More generally, the separation thresholds at which TB becomes significant
will depend on multiple experimental factors, including signal-to-noise
ratio, background signal, relative emitter brightness, sampling conditions,
and the specific PSF model.

It is instructive to compare this
approach with super-resolution
strategies based on higher-order correlations, such as antibunching-based
microscopy introduced by Schwartz and Oron. In that framework, spatial resolution is enhanced by exploiting
the nonclassical photon statistics of fluorescence emission, effectively
narrowing the PSF via higher-order intensity correlations. While conceptually
distinct, both approaches highlight the central role of spatial statistics
in overcoming diffraction-induced averaging. However, antibunching-based
methods require photon-number-resolving detection and high photon
fluxes, whereas MSSR operates within the classical intensity domain
and can be readily applied to standard FLIM data sets without specialized
hardware or acquisition schemes.

While MSSR mitigates temporal
blending by increasing spatial separability
in the intensity domain prior to lifetime estimation, complementary
strategies have been developed to address statistical uncertainty
in phasor space. Methods such as pawFLIM and its generalized extension, Binlets, apply a translational-invariant Haar-wavelet transform directly
on the phasor images. Unlike MSSR, which sharpens spatial structures
to prevent optical mixing, pawFLIM acts as an adaptive spatial binning
tool that sacrifices spatial resolution in homogeneous regions to
dramatically improve molecular precision under low-photon conditions,
guided by the explicit covariance (uncertainty) of each phasor. A
combined strategy in which MSSR is applied upstream to reduce PSF-induced
mixing, followed by adaptive denoising in phasor space, could provide
a unified framework that simultaneously addresses spatial overlap
and overcomes shot-noise limitations.

Based on these results,
we propose a mechanistic interpretation
in which TB arises from PSF-weighted spatial averaging imposed by
the imaging system prior to lifetime estimation. When neighboring
emitters fall within overlapping diffraction volumes, their photon
contributions are aggregated at the pixel level, yielding apparent
decays that reflect spatially weighted superposition rather than a
true molecular lifetime distribution at a single location. The persistence
of phasor mixing at separations up to 4σ from the in silico
characterization supports this geometric–optical origin and
motivates mitigation strategies that reduce effective spatial averaging
before the lifetime transform. While this interpretation is supported
by the present simulations and experimental observations, further
validation across diverse fluorophore systems, imaging modalities,
and noise regimes will be required to establish its generality.

Conclusions

This study identifies and addresses a fundamental
limitation in
FLIM, namely, the intrinsic coupling between spatial resolution and
temporal accuracy caused by temporal blending. Through combined in
silico modeling and experimental validation, we demonstrate that this
effect is driven by optical-PSF-mediated spatial averaging, which
produces apparent lifetime mixing even for emitters showing strictly
monoexponential fluorescence decay.

By applying mean-shift super-resolution
(MSSR) as a spatial pre-processing
step prior to phasor transformation, we show that TB can be substantially
mitigated without introducing lifetime artifacts. MSSR acts as a probabilistic
spatial filter operating exclusively on intensity information, thereby
preserving the intrinsic fluorescence decay kinetics. Phasor analysis
confirms this preservation through practically invariant centroid
positions and reduced phasor cluster spread corresponding to reduced
temporal mixing. In contrast to iterative deconvolution or temporally
interpolative super-resolution approaches, MSSR enhances spatial resolution
without distorting the phasor geometry or decay information. Another
strength of MSSR is its capability of enhancing spatial resolution
within one frame of microscopy images, thus significantly lowering
the computational resources required to process the data.

Overall,
the MSSR-FLIM framework offers a conceptually robust and
computationally efficient strategy for improving spatial resolution
while preserving temporal fidelity. These results also highlight the
phasor approach as an effective tool to diagnose and quantify the
influence of spatial filtering on lifetime data. The methodology is
immediately applicable to live-cell FLIM, super-resolution workflows,
and lifetime unmixing, offering a generalizable strategy to overcome
spatial–temporal trade-offs in modern fluorescence microscopy.

Data Set, Specimen Preparation, and Labeling Strategy

Primary experimental analyses were performed on time-domain confocal
FLIM data acquired from a standardized multicolor FLIM slide containing
fixed human osteosarcoma U2OS cells (GATTA-Cells 3C FLIM; GATTAquant).
The sample comprises three spectrally distinct labels: nuclear DNA
labeled with DAPI, mitochondria labeled by TOM20 immunolabeling with
Alexa Fluor 532, and microtubules labeled by α-tubulin immunolabeling
with Alexa Fluor 555. Samples were mounted in ProLong Diamond antifade
medium and sealed.

The immunolabeling strategy anchors fluorophores
to distinct macromolecular structures, supporting a working model
of spatially interlaced yet non-interacting fluorescent species at
the scale of labeled structures. The same slide was used for two-color
(Alexa Fluor 532/Alexa Fluor 555) and three-color (including DAPI)
FLIM experiments. All channels were processed independently by using
an identical phasor-analysis framework.

FLIM Image Acquisition

Time-resolved confocal FLIM
images were acquired on a custom microscope platform (iRT-FLIM-001;
Intek Scientific). Excitation was provided by a 515 nm pulsed laser
(Prima; PicoQuant) operating at a 25 MHz repetition rate. Images were
collected using a 60× objective (UPLXAPO60; Olympus), a 0.5 Airy
unit pinhole, and a pixel size of 54 nm. Emission was filtered to
550–614 nm using a bandpass filter (FF01-582/64-25; Semrock)
and detected with a photomultiplier tube (H10720-01; Hamamatsu Photonics).
For each pixel, the acquisition yielded a time-domain fluorescence
decay sampled over a fixed nanosecond-scale temporal window. A modulation
of 50 MHz (2nd harmonic) was used for the calculation of G and S phasor
coordinates.

Selected data sets
were additionally acquired on a Leica TCS SP8 STED 3X platform equipped
with a FALCON-FLIM module (photon-counting hybrid detectors, galvanometric
scanning, and a 63× high-numerical-aperture oil-immersion objective).
The acquisition frequency for this system was 78 MHz. These data sets
were analyzed with the same conceptual workflow (phasor-based segmentation
with optional MSSR-derived spatial masking); instrument-specific acquisition
parameters are detailed in the corresponding subsection.

Phasor Computation and Data Handling

Phasor analysis
followed the standard FLIM phasor formalism and the Weber identities.
Time-domain decays were converted to per-pixel phasor coordinates
(G, S) and mean intensity (DC component). Dual modulation frequencies

accelerated on CUDA-enabled GPUs when available (LifetimeXplorer v1.10.9).
For downstream Python-based processing, DC images were imported from
TIFF files (e.g., *_mean.tif) and (G,S) matrices from CSV (e.g., *_real.csv,
*_imag.csv) using tifffile and pandas, respectively.

For the

Frequency Conventions, Harmonic Support, and Calibration

Downstream phasor operations followed explicit frequency parametrization
PhasorPy conventions. A fundamental frequency of 50 MHz (0.05 GHz)
was used. Harmonic support was enabled by specifying a harmonic index
(e.g., harmonic = 1 for the fundamental). When reference-based calibration

Intensity Thresholding and Spatial Stabilization of Phasor Maps

To suppress noise-driven phasor scatter, low-count pixels were
excluded before visualization, segmentation, and lifetime extraction.
Thresholding was applied to the DC image to generate a binary support
mask that removed background and low-count regions. Unless otherwise
stated, (G,S) and derived lifetime quantities were computed from the
original decay data. Representative preprocessing parameters included
a minimum intensity threshold (mean_min = 7) and optional Gaussian
smoothing (scipy.ndimage.gaussian_filter, sigma = 1 pixel). To reduce
salt-and-pepper noise in the phasor coordinate maps while preserving
structural boundaries, repeated median filtering with intensity weighting
was applied by the use of the in-built PhasoPy’s function phasor_filter_median­(image,
G, S; size = 3, repeat = 3) corresponding to a 3 × 3 spatial
median filter iterated three times and weighted by the smoothed mean

Three-Component TD-FLIM Phasor Analysis and MSSR Masking

A three-component cellular FLIM data set was analyzed in phasor space
to evaluate MSSR-mediated suppression of Temporal Blending in regions
of multi-structure spatial overlap. The specimen was collected with
a Leica TCS SP8 STED 3X platform equipped with a FALCON-FLIM module
(photon-counting hybrid detectors, galvanometric scanning, and a 63×
high-numerical-aperture oil-immersion objective), corresponds to the
same fixed-cell multicolor FLIM slide used throughout this work, including
the DAPI channel for three-color TD-FLIM acquisitions; each spectral
channel was processed independently using the same phasor formalism.
The region of interest (ROI) reported in was selected in the diffraction-limited
image to include spatial overlap among nucleus (DAPI), microtubules
(AF555), and mitochondria (AF532), thereby maximizing the prevalence
of mixed pixels for downstream evaluation.

Phasor coordinates
were computed directly in the processing notebook using the PhasorPy
(phasorpy) Python library. Time-tagged photon data were imported from
PicoQuant.ptu files and organized as time-resolved image stacks with
axes (x, y, t), where t indexes the TCSPC histogram bins over the
80 MHz excitation period. Pixel-wise phasors (G, S) and the mean intensity
(DC component) were obtained by Fourier decomposition at the selected
analysis harmonic using the PhasorPy implementation, yielding spatial
maps of DC, G, and S and a corresponding phasor distribution in the
(G, S) space.

Instrument-related phase shifts and timing offsets
were corrected
by reference calibration using a homogeneous lifetime standard acquired
under the matched acquisition settings. A homogeneous solution of
ATTO 488 (200 nM) was used as the calibration reference. The reference
phasor was computed from this standard within the same PhasorPy workflow
and used to calibrate experimental phasors by aligning the measured
reference phasor to the theoretical phasor expected for the ATTO 488
reference lifetime at an 80 MHz modulation frequency. The resulting
calibration transform was applied to the three-component cellular
data set prior to phasor visualization, cursor-based segmentation,
and MSSR-masked reanalysis.

To suppress background and low-count
pixels, intensity thresholding
was applied to the DC image to generate a binary support mask that
was propagated to the G and S maps prior to visualization and segmentation.
Spatial stabilization of the phasor maps was performed by median filtering
of G and S using a small kernel (3 × 3) with repeated application.
Three-component behavior was assessed by the emergence of a triangular
phasor distribution. End point populations were identified by cursor
selection near each vertex of the triangle, and reciprocity mapping
was used to project phasor selections back into image space for visualization
of the spatial distribution of each component and mixed regions.

For MSSR-assisted mitigation of temporal mixing, mean-shift super-resolution
(MSSR) was applied to the mean-intensity
image to generate an MSSR-derived probability-like map. The MSSR output
was rescaled to [0, 1], and a fixed threshold (8% in ) was applied to create a binary
MSSR mask that restricts analysis to high-likelihood emitter regions
prior to phasor transformation and segmentation. The MSSR mask was
applied to the ROI data set, and phasor distributions were recomputed
from the masked pixels to evaluate changes in cluster compactness
and mixing signatures. Cluster compactness was quantified using an
ellipse area ratio, defined as the area of a covariance-based confidence
ellipse fitted to a cluster after MSSR masking divided by the area
before masking. Centroid displacement was quantified as the Euclidean
distance between pre- and postmasking cluster centroids in (G, S)
space. These metrics were used to confirm reduced phasor dispersion
(reduced mixing), together with centroid stability (preservation of
intrinsic decay signatures).

Richardson-Lucy Deconvolution

Richardson-Lucy deconvolution
was performed using the built-in function from the skimage library
from Python. A user function was defined to produce a Gaussian kernel
(generate_gaussian_psf­(sigma, size), and the sigma parameter was defined
as a quarter of the standard deviation from the Gaussian functions
to produce the simulated PSFs. On the other hand, the kernel size
was defined as a 2x2 Gaussian kernel.

To perform the Richardson-Lucy
deconvolution, the richardson_lucy function was used importing from
the skimage.restoration library. The number of iterations was set
to 43, chosen as the maximum number of iterations before the ellipse
ratio analysis of its phasor cluster yielded nan.

Fractional Contribution Analysis

A fractional contribution
analysis from the phasor plot was performed by using the PhasorPy
library. To perform this analysis, the following libraries were imported:
phasor_component_fit, phasor_component_fraction, and phasor_component_graphical
from the phasor.component library. The lifetime components were defined
within a list as in component_lifetimes = [1.5, 2.3], and the values
used from the lifetime components were obtained from the GattaQuant
webpage. To transform these lifetime signatures to phasor space, the
function phasor_from_lifetime from PhasorPy was used before fractional
analysis.

Brightness and Contrast

To improve the visualization
of the FLIM-MSSR analysis, the brightness and contrast of the pseudo-color
FLIM images were adjusted by linearly rescaling the display intensity
range prior to rendering. Brightness was controlled by shifting the
center of the displayed intensity interval using a defined variable
center = (new_vmin + new_vmax)/2, whereas contrast was adjusted by
proportionally expanding the displayed dynamic range about this center
through the user-defined variable span = (new_vmax - new_vmin) * contrast.
In the images presented here, brightness and contrast scaling factors
of 1.5 and 2.5, respectively, were applied uniformly to all pseudo-color
images to improve the visualization of lifetime distributions. These
adjustments affected only the image display and were not used for
quantitative analysis or phasor calculations.

Software, Libraries, and Computational Environment

Experimental data processing, visualization, phasor operations, filtering,
MSSR masking, and model fitting were performed in Python using numpy,
pandas, scipy (including scipy.ndimage and scipy.optimize), skimage,
matplotlib, tifffile, PhasorPy (phasorpy), and napari_superres. Simulation
plotting additionally used Cmcrameri for colormap selection. Phasor
coordinate computation from time-domain decays was performed in LifetimeXplorer
v1.10.9 with GPU acceleration when available, exporting mean intensity
and (G, S) matrices for Python-side processing.
