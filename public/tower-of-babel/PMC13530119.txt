Development of high-performance sustainable epoxy bio-composites reinforced with banana fibre and marine shell-derived calcium carbonate filler
Authors: A Arunkumar, P Prabhu, P Manoj Kumar, N Parthipan, M Venkatasudhahar, M Sathyanathan, Dawit Tafesse Gebreyohannes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, the development of sustainable banana fiber-epoxy composites reinforced with bio-waste derived calcium carbonate (CaCO3) from marine shells of Turbinella pyrum has been investigated. The purpose was to evaluate the combined effect of natural fibre reinforcement and inorganic filler loading on mechanical, thermal and environmental performance. NaOH treated banana fibres (30 wt%) were embedded in a bisphenol-A epoxy matrix with varying content of CaCO3 (0–20 wt%) and composites were prepared by hand lay-up followed by compression moulding. Mechanical results show that a moderate filler addition significantly improves the performance, with the composite with 10 wt% of CaCO3 (S10) showing the highest tensile strength (55 MPa) besides better flexural strength and impact resistance. These improvements are due to the better bonding at the fibre-matrix interface and uniform filler dispersion. The hardness increased with filler content due to matrix stiffening effect. FTIR results revealed improved stability and higher char residue for filled composites. Differential scanning calorimetry analysis indicated an increase in glass transition temperature at optimal filler loading. Water absorption test confirmed lower moisture absorption and biodegradation of S10 in comparison to control. SEM and SEM-EDX analysis showed a homogeneous filler distribution and strong interfacial interaction. The results were highly significant (p < 0.001) as revealed by ANOVA statistical analysis and S10 was the best formulation as revealed by Tukey post-hoc grouping. Overall, the best combination of mechanical strength, thermal stability and environmental durability was obtained for 10 wt% CaCO3, which makes these composites suitable for lightweight sustainable applications.Keywords: Banana fibre reinforced epoxy composites, Bio-waste derived calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), Turbinella pyrum shell powder, Natural fibre hybrid composites, Mechanical and thermal properties, SEM–EDX characterization, Sustainable composite materialsSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

There are increasing concerns about the environment due to the use of synthetic polymers and the continuous depletion of fossil resources. This has led to a surge in research focusing on sustainable and bio, based composite materials1. Natural fibre, reinforced polymer (NFRP) composites are one of the green materials that have attracted a lot of attention because they have a low density, come from renewable sources, are biodegradable, and have competitive specific mechanical properties2. Among natural fibres, flax and other lignocellulosic reinforced composites have been found to provide excellent structural performance in polymer matrices3.

The dynamic mechanical behaviour and viscoelastic properties of natural fibre composites have been deeply analyzed and discussed in reviews, which highlight the key role of fibre-matrix interaction in the determination of stiffness and damping properties4. Hybridization of cellulosic and synthetic fibres has been proven to enhance the strength-toughness balance and structural reliability of natural fibre composites5. Eco, friendly chemical treatments, among other surface modification techniques, have considerably improved fibre-matrix adhesion while reducing interfacial defects, as demonstrated in studies6.

Inorganic fillers incorporation like calcium carbonate has been a successful way to get better stiffness and thermal resistance of epoxy composites7. Systems with natural filler-reinforcements not only help to keep dimensions stable but also give a mechanical boost8. Some of the waste fillers that come from agriculture like husk and shell grains have proved their ability to improve the load transfer and structural efficiency in thermoplastic matrices8. The mechanical properties of laminated natural fibre composites are mainly determined by the type of fibre used and the quality of the interface bonding9. Nano filler along with hybrid composite systems offer more ways for reinforcement and also make the microstructural quite stable10. Besides that, sustainable cellulose Nano fibrils are reported to increase composite strength and environmental compatibility at the same time11. Hybrid fibre composites which are made of both natural and synthetic reinforcements perform better mechanically when subjected to multi, axial loading12.

The use of natural fibre composites in the industry has been spurred by their versatility in making light structural and semi, structural components13. Hybrid basalt, based composites have additionally shown the potential of mixing natural and inorganic reinforcements14. Environmental durability, especially in the case of seawater exposure, is still the main issue that affects the long, term performance of materials15. With the help of breakthroughs in nano-cellulose, reinforced polymer systems, the researchers have been able to achieve good results in terms of stiffness and thermal stability16.

Research on natural glass hybrid composites has unveiled superior impact strength and structural integrity17. Breakthroughs in nano-cellulose reinforced polymer nano-composites show increased barrier and reinforcement effects18. Studies on basalt fibre, reinforced polymer composites have covered their mechanical performance and durability19. Hybrid epoxy systems for marine environments have illustrated that inorganic reinforcements help to minimize environmental degradation20. Extensive reviews on natural fibre, based composites point to their increasing importance in sustainable material engineering21. Research on green polymer composites is continuously growing, combining bio, based fibres and mineral fillers to achieve better functional properties22. Calcium carbonate fillers, especially, have been revealed to have a positive effect on the thermal stability and mechanical rigidity of polymer composites23. Interfacial modification is still crucial to enhance fibre compatibility and to lower moisture, induced degradation24. Alkali treatment of natural fibres has revealed better mechanical properties and an increased efficiency of stress transfer25. Processing parameters have a major effect on the performance and structural integrity of composites26. The fibre surface treatment can also lower water absorption and improve dimensional stability27.

Life, cycle assessments indicate that natural fibre composites are environmentally less damaging than traditional synthetic systems28. The thermal degradation of lignocellulosic material generally involves decomposition of hemicellulose, cellulose, and lignin through multi, stage mechanisms29. Indeed, combining fibre, reinforced epoxy composites with ceramic fillers has resulted in mechanical and dielectric performance enhancements under optimized filler loading conditions, according to recent studies30. Previous studies have demonstrated that the addition of particulate fillers into polymer composites can lead to a drastic improvement in mechanical performance to a large extent with the help of systematic optimization and statistical validation. Multi-response optimization techniques like orthogonal experimental design and grey relational techniques have been shown to be effective for determining the best material formulations in the presence of conflicting performance requirements. These techniques allow for the simultaneous consideration of tensile, flexural and impact characteristics and the use of analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post-hoc comparisons provide an assurance of statistical soundness. The observed enhancements at medium filler loadings, and then decreasing at higher level due to agglomeration and stress concentration effects are well accepted phenomena in particulate reinforced systems31–34. On the other hand, the use of bio-based and waste-derived fillers has become very popular in the fabrication of sustainable composite materials with improved performances. When properly dispersed in the matrix, these fillers can improve interfacial adhesion, thermal stability and overall physical strength of composites. But increased filler levels lead to microstructural disorder that mitigates improvements. Our results are in agreement with above that best hybrid composite has higher strength, thermal and durability properties due to good dispersion of filler and strong bonding between fibre and matrix. This attitude stresses the importance of the controlled filler content and the use of statistical methods to optimize the combination of strength, stability and environmental friendliness in hybrid composites35–37.

Natural fiber reinforced epoxy composites and calcium carbonate filled systems have been extensively studied, but the majority of studies are restricted to evaluating individual properties, rather than an integrated multi-response framework. Particularly, very few studies have focused on the combined effect of banana fibre reinforcement and bio-derived CaCO3 filler on mechanical performance, considering simultaneously statistical validation and optimization. Most current studies report only the trends in tensile, flexural or impact properties without establishing a common relationship between filler loading, interfacial behaviour and the overall composite performance. In addition, there is no quantitative optimization approach based on ANOVA and post-hoc analysis to find statistically significant differences between composite formulations. This gap limits the determination of an optimal filler loading to enhance the strength without compromising the structural integrity, especially in sustainable hybrid composite systems.

The current work focuses on the development of banana fibre reinforced epoxy composites with marine shell derived CaCO3 filler and systematically evaluating and optimizing the mechanical performance of the composites in view of the above-mentioned limitations. Materials and methods

Materials

Banana fibre (BF) was the main reinforcement picked up as it is a natural fibre with a very high cellulose content (60–65%), low density, it is biodegradable, and has good specific mechanical properties2,13. Fibres were obtained from local agro, waste and cleaned to get rid of the dirt before subjecting it to chemical treatment. The mean fibre diameter was 80–250 μm, varying with extraction conditions.

A standard commercial bisphenol-A-based epoxy resin (LY 556 or equivalent) with amine hardener (HY 951 or equivalent) was utilized as the matrix material. Epoxy systems have been extensively used in structural composite applications because of their great adhesion strength, dimensional stability, chemical resistance, and good mechanical performance5,13. The resin and hardener were weighed out to the ratio the manufacturer recommended of 10:1 (by weight) and combined.

Turbinella pyrum (marine shell) used in this study was obtained from a commercial supplier (M/s Sea Shells, St. John’s Exim, Tamil Nadu, India). The calcium carbonate (CaCO3) filler was made from the obtained Turbinella pyrum marine shell waste. The marine shells were first washed, dried in an oven at 100 °C, and then crushed mechanically. Afterward, the material was ground into a fine powder by using a ball mill. The powder was then passed through a sieve to get a particle size less than 75 μm that was suitable for a homogeneous mixture with the matrix environment. The marine shell powder mainly contains calcite/aragonite crystalline CaCO3, which is a source of properties like high stiffness, thermal stability, and thermal barrier characteristics7,23. Generally, the decomposition of CaCO3 is above 700 °C, which results in more char during the thermal degradation process23,29. Fibre surface treatment

Banana fibres were first treated with alkali to increase fibre-matrix interfacial adhesion and at the same time get rid of the fibres’ naturally hydrophilic nature. Natural fibres have components like hemicellulose, lignin, pectin, and surface waxes which can obstruct the formation of a good bond with polymer matrices2,24. To make the fibres more compatible, they were put into a solution of 5 wt% sodium hydroxide (NaOH) for 4 h at room temperature. The fibres were washed with distilled water until the pH was neutral and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h to completely remove any moisture before making the composites.

In this research, using alkali (NaOH) surface treatment method, the issue of fibre matrix compatibility has been resolved. In fact, this method is a very effective and at the same time most common way of modifying lignocellulosic fibres. It removes amorphous constituents like hemicellulose lignin surface impurities and so on. Therefore, in addition to increasing the surface roughness, it also reveals the cellulose microfibrils which are responsible for enhanced mechanical interlocking with the epoxy matrix. The literature shows that these changes would make the interfacial adhesion and stress transfer more effective in natural fibre composites2,24,25. Besides, NaOH treatment changes the banana fibre’s hydrophilic character by decreasing the number of accessible hydroxyl groups, which among other things would reduce the moisture-induced interfacial degradation and support the dimension stability27.

Alkali treatment eliminates amorphous constituents like hemicellulose and lignin and thus the surface becomes rougher as a result of the protruding cellulose fibrils which allow the epoxy matrix to form a mechanical interlocking better25,26. Besides, the treatment makes the fibre thinner and more hydrophilic, which will accommodate a better stress transfer at the time of mechanical loading24. Additionally, the surface modification lowers moisture absorption through a lesser number of hydroxyl groups and an improved encapsulation of the fibre in the matrix27. This kind of chemical treatment is very beneficial, according to numerous researchers, as it significantly improves the tensile, flexural, and durability properties of natural fibre composites6,25.

Preparation of shell powder

Marine shell waste (Turbinella pyrum) was sourced from shorelines and utilized as a natural calcium carbonate filler. Marine shell waste was utilized as natural calcium carbonate filler. First, shells were washed several times with distilled water to wash off organic matter, salt, and impurities sticking to the shells and then dried at 100 °C for 24 h to remove any moisture. Dried shells were manually broken with a jaw crusher and then ground in a ball mill to get a fine powder. The powder was passed through a mesh to get a uniform particle size below 75 μm for the epoxy matrix to be uniformly dispersed. In the present study, the CaCO3 filler based on the marine shell was further left chemically untreated on the surface, as the inherent physicochemical properties of its surface were considered to be sufficient to warrant a strong effective contact with the epoxy matrix. The shell powder is mainly composed of calcite/aragonite CaCO3 which has inherently high surface energy and polar functional groups that can lead to a good interfacial affinity with thermoset matrices especially when well dispersed. Researchers have reported that, for example, micron-sized CaCO3 fillers increase the stiffness, thermal stability and load transfer efficiency potentials of polymer composites even without surface modification and agglomeration is minimized by proper processing7,23. The powder had a particle size range of about 20–75 μm with an average particle size of about 48 μm. In this paper, mechanical stirring and a fixed particle size (< 75 μm) were used for homogeneous dispersion, a very important factor influencing filler-matrix interaction and the overall performance of the composite8,23. The study also aims to develop a cost-effective and environmentally sustainable composite system by reducing the processing complexity and chemical use, such as not carrying out any additional chemical treatments. The better mechanical, thermal and vapor resistance properties especially at the optimum filler loading (10 wt%) along with the SEM-EDX revealing uniform distribution of filler, indicate that the as received shell derived CaCO3 was sufficiently compatible with the epoxy matrix in itself, which is also consistent with the findings of other studies on particulate-filled epoxy systems7,23.

Composite fabrication

Banana fibre-reinforced epoxy composites with Turbinella pyrum shell powder as filler were produced by a combination of hand lay, up and compression molding. Hand lay, up is the most common manufacturing method of natural fibre composites because it is simple, cheap, and well adapted to particulate fibre hybrid systems5,13. In the pre-treatment stage, alkali, treated banana fibres were chopped to the same length, and then they were dried in an oven at 60 °C to get rid of moisture absorbed by the fibres. To obtain proper crosslinking and therefore better mechanical properties, the two components of the epoxy resin and hardener were mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (10:1 by weight)5. Shell powder was introduced as a filler in the epoxy matrix in different weight percentages (0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 wt%) and was mechanically stirred at speed (500–700) rpm for 15–20 min to break down the filler agglomerates and ensure uniform dispersion. The dispersion of CaCO3 particles must be done properly to avoid their agglomeration and to achieve effective stress transfer within the matrix7,23. Banana fibres were laid evenly in a steel mold (300 mm × 300 mm × 3 mm), and the presence of the filler loaded epoxy mixture was gently poured so that the fibres were completely wetted. To remove entrapped air bubbles and to improve fibre-matrix impregnation, manual rolling was done. Besides good interfacial bonding, the absence of voids is essential for obtaining high mechanical properties of natural fibre composites2,24.

The lay, up was afterward compression, molded using a pressure of 3–5 MPa for 24 h at room temperature in order to get uniform thickness and consolidation. The post, curing was done at 80 °C for 2 h to improve the crosslink density and thermal stability of the epoxy network13. A well, cured system will have a higher glass transition temperature and better dimensional stability7. Composite laminates were taken out of the mold after the curing process and were cut into standard specimens as per ASTM standards for tensile, flexural, impact, thermal, and water absorption testing. The deliberate change of the shell powder content allowed for the assessment of filler loading effects on mechanical, thermal, and durability properties in line with the methods of hybrid natural fibre composites5,23. Mechanical testing

The mechanical performance of banana fibre/shell powder reinforced epoxy composites was assessed to determine the effect of variation in filler loadings on the strength, stiffness, and impact resistance of the composites. Testing of mechanical properties was carried out at room temperature in a laboratory setting under controlled conditions. In order to confirm the reproducibility of results, for each composite formulation (S0–S20), a minimum of five specimens were tested, and the mean values were reported. Tensile tests were performed according to ASTM D638 using a Universal Testing Machine (UTM). Specimens of the dog, bone shape were prepared Type I geometry, and the tests were performed at a crosshead speed of 2 mm/min. Tensile strength, Youngs modulus, and elongation at break were extracted from the stress-strain plots. Test results of tensile properties reveal fiber-matrix interfacial bonding and how efficiently the load is transferred between the two components within the composite system2,24.

Flexural properties were assessed by performing a three, point bending test according to ASTM D790. Rectangular samples were subjected to testing with a span, to, depth ratio of 16:1 at a crosshead speed of 2 mm/min. Flexural testing is very crucial for determining the bending stiffness and load, bearing capacity of hybrid composites that are used for semi, structural applications5,13. Impact resistance was determined by using the Izod impact method in line with ASTM D256. Notched samples were tested so as to find the energy uptake at fracture. Impact performance is a composite’s capability to resist immediate loading and crack propagation, which is mainly dependent on filler dispersion and fibre pull, out mechanisms7,23. Surface hardness was checked by using a Shore D hardness tester as per ASTM D2240. Several readings were taken at different spots on each sample to check for the consistency, and the average value was recorded. Hardness tests show the degree of resistance to surface indentation and are related to the efficiency of filler reinforcement and matrix stiffness23. Thermal analysis

The thermal stability and transition behavior of banana fibre/shell powder reinforced epoxy composites were studied by Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA), Derivative Thermogravimetry (DTG), and Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC). Thermal analysis helps understand degradation pathways, thermal resistances, and glass transition characteristics of hybrid composites. TGA was done in an inert nitrogen environment from 30 to 800 °C with a 10 °C/min heating rate. The weight loss versus temperature curve was used to determine onset decomposition temperature (Tonset), peak decomposition temperature (Tmax), and residual char content. Natural fiber composites generally show multi, stage decomposition: moisture evaporation (< 120 °C), hemicellulose and cellulose degradation (250–350 °C), and epoxy matrix degradation (350–450 °C)29. Addition of calcium carbonate filler improves thermal stability by functioning as a heat barrier and increasing residual char formation7,23.

DSC test was carried out from 30 to 250 °C with a 10 °C/min heating rate to measure the glass transition temperature (Tg). The Tg is an indicator of the crosslink density and molecular mobility of the epoxy resin. It is well known that moderate filler addition raises Tg as a result of segmental motion restriction whereas excessive filler leads to Tg decline due to agglomeration, induced heterogeneity13,23. Optimization using the Taguchi method and Tukey post-hoc analysis

In this study, to ensure the statistical reliability and efficient evaluation of parameters, the Taguchi method combined with Tukey post-hoc analysis was used for the optimization of mechanical performance. The Taguchi method, using orthogonal array design, allows for a systematic study of the effect of filler loading on several mechanical responses with a reduced number of experimental trials. To further confirm the significance of the observed variations, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s posthoc test to determine statistically distinct groups among the composite formulations. The results confirm that the S10 composite is a separate homogeneous group with significantly improved tensile, flexural, and impact properties compared to other compositions, thus confirming its optimal performance. This combined approach offers a robust framework to identify optimal processing conditions and material composition by combining design of experiments with rigorous statistical comparison. Similar approaches have been successfully applied in composite and manufacturing optimization studies, resulting in improved reliability of multi-response decision making and parameter selection31–34.

Results and discussion

Tensile properties

The Figs. 2 and 3 show the tensile strength and tensile modulus of banana fibre, reinforced epoxy composites loaded with the Turbinella pyrum shell, derived CaCO3 filler (0–20 wt%) respectively. Figure 2 shows that the tensile strength was going up from about 42 MPa for the neat composite (S0) to the highest point of around 55 MPa at 10 wt% filler content (S10), after which a slow drop was noticed for the samples with higher filler contents (S15 and S20). The improvement at the beginning of the use of shell powder in the range 5–10 wt% is due to the better fibre-matrix interfacial adhesion and the improved capacity of the fibres to carry the load Besides, the evenly distributed CaCO3 particles serve as rigid microscopic reinforcements in the epoxy matrix, limiting the local deformation and thus helping to avoid the formation of micro voids, which eventually enhances the composite’s ability to carry the load7,23. Additionally, banana fibre alkali treatment which roughens the surface and eliminates the amorphous components that contribute to the removal of the film forming substances on the surface, results in improved wettability and interfacial bonding with the epoxy matrix, thus leading to better tensile properties2,24,25.

The maximum tensile strength found at a 10 wt% filler concentration points to a fibre-filler-matrix interaction that is evenly balanced, allowing a sufficient level of inorganic reinforcement without breaking the continuity of the matrix. Particulate, filled epoxy systems with moderate CaCO3 loadings also showed similar improvements in tensile properties due to increased stiffness and interfacial compatibility which ultimately result in superior mechanical performance7,23. Nevertheless, at filler incorporations of more than 10 wt%, the tensile strength diminished owing to particle agglomeration and stress concentration effects. Very high levels of inorganic materials can break the continuity of the matrix, lower the degree of fibre wetting, and cause microstructural heterogeneity that leads to premature crack initiation and propagation under tensile loading8,23. The further increase of brittleness at high filler contents also weighs in the reduction of the strength. Tensile modulus trend in Fig. 3 reflects the mentioned observations. Youngs modulus showed an increase at 10 wt% (4.2 GPa) filler loading, thus extending the stiffening effect of the composite which, in turn, is caused by the rigid character of CaCO3 particles. However, at higher filler loadings, a handful of fluctuations were noticed in turn, with modulus values still being at the level of or even slightly higher than the control composite, thus confirming the stiffening effect of inorganic filler7,13. The rise in stiffness due to polymer chain immobilization, as well as good filler-matrix interaction, agrees with the results of studies on hybrid natural fibre composites23.

In general, the tensile results clearly show that 10 wt% shell powder (S10) is the optimal level of reinforcement, resulting in the highest tensile strength and increased stiffness. The trend observed emphasizes the significance of properly controlled filler dispersion in realizing great mechanical properties while minimizing the negative effects of agglomeration at higher loading levels.

SEM morphological analysis

SEM was used to study the fracture surface morphology of banana fibre, reinforced epoxy composites with different contents of Turbinella pyrum shell, derived CaCO3 filler (0–20 wt%) as shown in Fig. 4. The failure mechanisms and interfacial characteristics were understood from the morphology. SEM images visually confirm the quality of fibre, matrix bonding, distribution of filler, crack growth routes, and how different microstructural features influence the mechanical properties.

SEM images of the banana fibre/shell powder reinforced epoxy composites fracture surfaces: (a) S0 depicting fibre pull-out and interfacial voids, (b) S10 showing better fibre encapsulation and more uniform CaCO3 dispersion, and (c) S20 illustrating particle agglomerates and regions of localized stress concentration.

The control composite (S0) showed prominent fibre pull, out, interfacial gaps, and microvoids through the fractured surface (Fig. 4a). These features imply a rather weak interfacial adhesion between banana fibres and the epoxy matrix. The formation of voids and debonded fibre areas indicates that the stress transfer during tensile and bending is not very effective. This is the reason for the lower mechanical performance of S0 as has been explained in Tensile properties section. In S0, crack propagation was largely along the fibre-matrix interface, thus interfacial debonding was the main failure mechanism. Natural fibre composites having less interfacial modification also have similar fracture characteristics2,24. Moreover, the S10 composite panel (Fig. 4b) illustrated a marked enhancement in fibre-matrix interaction. SEM images showed that the fibres were well, encapsulated and the CaCO3 particles were relatively evenly spread in the epoxy matrix. The fracture surface was uneven and consistent with cohesive failure, which is indicative of higher energy dissipation during crack propagation. Lesser fibre pull, out and fewer interfacial voids testify to better interfacial adhesion due to alkali treatment and proper filler incorporation. The existence of dispersed CaCO3 particles facilitates mechanical interlocking and crack deflection mechanisms, thus improving stress transfer efficiency and resulting in better tensile, flexural, and impact properties7,23. The enhanced morphology seen in S10 is in line with the structure-property correlation that was previously outlined.

With an increase in filler loading (S20), the SEM pictures (Fig. 4c) showed the presence of particle agglomeration in a few spots and micro cracks. Even though fibre encapsulation was still quite good, there were clusters of CaCO3 particles within the matrix that act as potential stress concentration areas. These agglomerates interrupt the continuity of the matrix and facilitate brittle fracture under loading conditions. The fracture surface of S20 exhibited comparatively smoother areas than S10 which suggests that there was less plastic deformation and, consequently, lower energy absorption capacity. Too much inorganic content can cause microstructural heterogeneity, which results in early crack initiation and a slight decline in tensile and flexural strength8,23. On the whole, SEM morphological analysis reveals that the performance of composites is mainly determined by interfacial bonding and the quality of filler dispersion. Among the composites, S10 has the most uniform microstructure with the least amount of voids and a very even distribution of the filler, which leads to better mechanical integrity and resistance to cracks. Meanwhile, the control sample is a victim of interfacial weaknesses, and in the case of S20, the use of too much filler causes the formation of agglomerates that end up leading to heterogeneity. The mechanical, thermal, moisture, and biodegradation tests results that have been obtained are also very consistent with these morphological observations, thus the structure-property relationship in hybrid natural fibre composites is well supported7,23,24.

SEM–EDX analysis of S10 composite

Moreover, to validate the incorporation and distribution of filler within the optimized composite, Energy, Dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis combined with SEM was conducted on the S10 sample containing 10 wt% Turbinella pyrum shell, derived CaCO3 filler as depicted in Fig. 5. SEM-EDX not only provides qualitative but also quantitative data on elemental composition and the spatial distribution of the elements within the composite matrix. SEM-EDX analysis of the S10 composite validates the successful incorporation of marine shell derived CaCO3 in the structure. From the EDX spectrum the peaks assigned to Ca, O and C were identified as significant peaks. The C signal was due to the carbon matrix primarily, and the banana fiber additives. The O signal was from the network present of the polymer and also the carbonate groups. The Ca peak gave a good indication of the ability to stabilize the mineral within the matrix and results similar to those found in calcium carbonate reinforced polymer composites7,23. The signals seen here were in agreement with the expected chemical composition of CaCO3 and demonstrated the mineral was not degraded.

SEM-EDX spectrum and elemental mapping of the S10 composite revealing the dominant peaks of C, O, and Ca, which confirms the successful incorporation and uniform dispersion of the CaCO3 particles derived from the shell in the epoxy matrix.

In addition, the elemental map shows that the calcium is distributed relatively uniformly throughout the matrix which is indicative of a good dispersion of the CaCO3 filler at the 10 wt% level. The ability of the filler to be effectively dispersed and the minimization of agglomeration is important for maximizing interfacial interaction between the three phases (fibres, matrix and filler). No large calcium/callium conglomerates are visible which suggests the paste processing employed was effective at mixing and dispersing the composite. This uniform dispersion is important for increasing the stress transfer efficiency, limiting crack growth and improving the thermal stability30 of the S10 composite which can be seen to have excellent mechanical and durability properties compared with other hybrids. These results are comparable with previous research showing effective dispersion of CaCO3 filler increases the properties of hybrid polymeric composites, such as structural integrity7,23,28. Moreover, the atomic percentage analysis reveals that the ratio of Ca content to C and O is Ca, containing balanced, which indicates that filler loading has been optimally done without the excessive dominance of the inorganic phase. The absence of significant agglomeration in S10 is the basis of its heat resistance improvement (higher residual char and delayed degradation onset), increased Tg (~ 98–100 °C), decreased water uptake (3.10%), and lower biodegradation rate (4.1% after 45 days). In sum, SEM-EDX results provide a clear indication that 10 wt% CaCO3 loading is sufficient to obtain fibre-matrix-filler synergistic effect through a very uniform dispersion of the inorganic phase and good interfacial compatibility. The microstructural regularity in S10 accounts vividly for its top, notch thermo, mechanical characteristics and environmental endurance. These results back up the previous studies on calcium carbonate reinforced epoxy composites, where the structural efficiency and stability were significantly improved through controlled filler dispersion7,23,28.

Flexural properties

The plots for flexural strength and flexural modulus of banana fibre reinforced epoxy composites containing varying degrees (0–20 wt%) of Turbinella pyrum shell powder are shown in Figs. 6 and 7, respectively. In the context of semi, structural applications, flexural strength is a key property since the materials will be subjected to transverse loading and bending stresses. Figure 6 clearly shows that the flexural strength rose from around 55 MPa for the control composite (S0) to nearly 62 MPa for the composite with 5 wt% shell powder (S5), suggesting greater load, bearing capacity after the first phase of filler incorporation. This change has been explained by a better fibre-matrix interfacial bonding combined with the firmer effect of the CaCO3 particles scattered evenly in the epoxy matrix. The addition of hard inorganic particles leads to the limitation of matrix deformation under bending stress and also the improvement of stress transfer efficiency between fibre and matrix7,23. The same type of increase in the bending behavior of particulate, filled epoxy systems has been observed as a result of better interfacial adhesion and less void content5,13.

Flexural strength of banana fibre/shell powder reinforced epoxy composites (S0–S20).

Flexural modulus of banana fibre/shell powder reinforced epoxy composites (S0–S20).

At 10 wt% filler loading (S10), the flexural strength of the sample didn’t drop much and remained fairly well with respect to the control. In contrast, the flexural modulus (Fig. 6) got to its peak value (4.5 GPa). The rise of the flexural modulus is a kind of proof of the hardening effect of CaCO3 particles, which therefore increase the ability of the material to resist mart elastic deformation under bending loads. The better modulus at moderate filler loading is attributed to the immobilized polymer chain segment as well as the closer internal adhesion between the phases, which is in line with the findings of calcium carbonate, filled polymer composites7,23. Besides that, alkali, treated banana fibres also help the fibre-matrix compatibility to be improved, which makes the stress distribution under flexural loading more efficient2,24. However, when the filler content is higher (S15 and S20), a significant drop in the flexural strength of the material is visible, especially in the case of S15. The reason for this is that the particles first tended to stick together and then the mixture became more and more brittle due to the addition of too much inorganic stuff. A cluster of filler particles leads to the formation of local stress concentration regions, thus giving rise to crack initiation and propagation under bending loads8,23. Also, too much filler breaks the matrix continuity and thus doesn’t let the fibres get wetted properly, which means the composite after all cannot support bending stresses. The flexural modulus may still be somewhat higher than that of the control sample at moderate filler levels, but overloading results in slight decreases due to microstructural heterogeneity.

In general, the flexural behavior reveals that adding a moderate amount of shell powder (5–10 wt%) will improve the bending strength and stiffness of the material, whereas a high amount of filler will weaken the structure. The findings are very clear that 10 wt% shell powder is the best level of reinforcement for achieving a good combination of strength and stiffness under flexural loading. The tendency agrees well with the literature on hybrid natural fibre composites, where properly dosed particulate reinforcement can enhance mechanical properties, and over, dosing leads to particle agglomeration and performance decline5,7,23.

Impact properties

The changes in the impact strength of banana fibre-reinforced epoxy composites with different amounts of CaCO3 filler from Turbinella pyrum shells (0–20 wt%) are shown in Fig. 8. Impact performance indicates how well a composite can absorb energy when subjected to a sudden load and is determined by the quality of fibre-matrix bonding, the dispersion of the filler, and the way cracks propagate. From Fig. 8, it can be seen that impact strength goes up from around 3.5 kJ/m2 for the control composite (S0) to a peak of nearly 4.8 kJ/m2 at 10 wt% filler content (S10). The reason for the improvement at moderate filler levels (S5–S10) is that interfacial adhesion between alkali, treated banana fibres and the epoxy matrix was strengthened, and at the same time, the CaCO3 particles were uniformly dispersed. Well, distributed hard filler particles serve as areas where cracks deviate, thus facilitating the process of energy release during fracture, and at the same time, the propagation of cracks is avoided7,23. In addition, strengthened fibre-matrix adhesion limits fibre pull, out and intensifies fibre bridging mechanisms that together help to increase the extent of energy absorption under impact loading2,24. The existence of these conjunctions in natural fibre, based hybrid composites has been confirmed by experiments in which moderate particulate reinforcement leads to increased crack resistance and toughness of impact5,17.

Impact strength of banana fibre/shell powder reinforced epoxy composites (S0–S20).

The highest impact strength that was recorded at 10 wt% shell powder shows that there is the best synergy between the fibre, filler, and matrix. The stress redistribution and crack pinning mechanisms are well, activated in this case. The incorporation of fine CaCO3 particles leads to the increased complexity of the crack paths which means that more energy is needed for the fracture to propagate7,23. On the other hand, when the filler is over 10 wt% (S15 and S20), the impact strength is weakened. This drop is mainly caused by the formation of particle agglomerates and the increased brittleness that comes with too much inorganic content. Aggregated CaCO3 particles become stress concentration locations, which make it easier for cracks to start under sudden loadings8,23. Besides, a higher amount of filler limits the plastic deformation of the matrix, thus, lowering the composites capacity to take impact energy. The decline in toughness with a higher filler content matches the well, known behavior of particulate, filled thermoset composites, where too much filler results in greater stiffness but less impact resistance7,23.

In brief, the data suggest that the introduction of moderate amounts of shell powder (10 wt%) gives a big boost to the impact performance, whereas too much filler loading brings down the toughness because the agglomerates make the material brittle. The trend that is found here is in good agreement with the previous studies of hybrid natural fibre composites, which show that the effective dispersion of the filler is vital for the impact resistance to be at its best5,7,23.

Hardness behaviour

The Shore D hardness values of banana fibre, reinforced epoxy composites with different amounts of Turbinella pyrum shell, derived CaCO3 filler (0–20 wt%) are given in Fig. 9. Hardness is one of the essential surface characteristics that indicate resistance to the penetration of an indenter and localized plastic deformation, and it is thus strongly tied to the matrix rigidity, filler reinforcement efficiency, and interfacial bonding quality. From the graph in Fig. 9, it is clear that the Shore D hardness increased steadily from around 78 for the control composite (S0) to a peak of about 87 at 15 wt% shell powder loading (S15). The fact that the hardness rises with filler addition (S5, S10) can be explained by the stiff character of CaCO3 particles, which made the epoxy matrix stiffer and limited the deformation that was localized to the application of the load7,23. The presence of well, dispersed inorganic particles reduces polymer chain mobility and increases resistance to indentation, resulting in better surface hardness13. Moreover, alkali, treated banana fibres help to improve fibre, matrix interfacial bonding, which thus results in a more robust structure and surface resistance2,24.

Shore D hardness of banana fibre/shell powder reinforced epoxy composites (S0–S20).

The maximum hardness obtained at 15 wt% filler loading reveals that increasing the amount of inorganic material increases the rigidity of the composite due to the higher volume fraction of filler. The same trends have been found in calcium carbonate filled polymer composites, where the hardness is increased proportionally with the filler loading as a result of the higher capacity of the material to bear the load and the stiffening of the matrix7,23. On the other hand, at 20 wt% filler loading (S20), the hardness was slightly decreased compared to S15. This slight drop might be explained by particle agglomeration and microvoid formation as an excessive filler content. Agglomerated particles would be less able to distribute the load evenly and thus, would create weak spots within the matrix, which would slightly reduce the surface resistance8,23. However, the value of the hardness at the higher filler loading is still higher than that of the control composite, thus the reinforcing effect of CaCO3 has been proved.

In general, the hardness results verify that the inclusion of shell powder contributes to the increase in surface stiffness until a certain optimum level is reached. The steady rise of the Shore D hardness with the addition of filler clearly indicates the stiffening effect of the bio, derived CaCO3 particles, whereas too much loading can lead to a microstructural heterogeneity which in turn might slightly influence the surface performance. These results align with the ones in earlier studies of particulate, reinforced epoxy composites, in which increased filler content is shown to improve indentation resistance as a result of enhanced matrix stiffness and decreased polymer mobility7,13,23.

Hemicellulose has amorphous structure and degrades at lower temperature. Then cellulose rapidly degrades to produce rapid mass loss. The neat composite (S0) shows an earlier degradation onset which indicates a lower thermal stability compared to the filled systems of CaCO3. Derivative thermogravimetric curves (DTG)

The derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves (Fig. 11) show the rate of mass loss as a function of temperature and provide a better resolution of the thermal degradation behaviour of the composites. The DTG profiles present distinct multi-stage degradation features, as expected for lignocellulosic fibre reinforced epoxy systems. The first stage (120 °C) is related to the evaporation of absorbed moisture and low-molecular-weight volatiles. Then there is a major degradation step between roughly 250–350 °C, mainly related to the decomposition of hemicellulose and cellulose components of banana fibres. The secondary degradation stage, observed in the range of 350–500 °C, is attributed to the breakdown of lignin and the epoxy network matrix. This kind of multi-stage thermal behaviour has been reported widely in natural fibre reinforced polymer composites29.

The comparison of the DTG peak positions (Tmax) indicate a substantial shift towards higher temperatures for the S10 composite, which reflects a good improvement in the thermal stability. Such a change arises from improved fibre-matrix interfacial bonding and evenly distributed CaCO3 particles that serve as thermal barriers and consequently slow down the degradation. In addition, the lower peak intensity and slower degradation rate for S10 mean that its thermal decomposition stability is enhanced. On the other hand, the S20 composite shows a slight peak broadening which can be related to the filler agglomeration and heterogeneous microstructure resulting in non-uniform degradation behaviour. Similar observations have been reported for particulate-filled epoxy composites where an optimum filler loading improves the thermal resistance but excessive filler loading causes microstructural irregularities7,23.

The addition of CaCO3 filler derived from shells into the composites resulted in a gradual decrease in water absorption up to a 10 wt% loading level. This decrease can be credited to the improvement in fibre-matrix interfacial bonding and the lowering of porosity due to the even filler distribution. On the other hand, at higher filler loadings (S15 and S20), a minor rise in water absorption was noted ompared to S10, however, the figures were still below those of the control composite. The soil burial biodegradation behaviour of banana fibre epoxy composites (S0, S10 and S20) is shown in Fig. 16. Conversely, the CaCO3 filled composites possess a decreased degree of biodegradation, with S10 showing the lowest weight-loss (~ 4.1%) of the samples tested. The decrease in degradation rate is observed to be the result of ‘better fibre encapsulation, lower porosity and restricted diffusion of moisture through the matrix due to the addition of well-dispersed inorganic particles and enhanced filler-matrix interaction. Conclusion

In the present work, banana fibre reinforced epoxy biocomposites incorporated with marine shell derived CaCO₃ filler were developed and their mechanical, thermal, moisture and biodegradation performance were systematically evaluated. The results demonstrate that the addition of moderate amount of inorganic filler plays a key role in controlling the structure-property relationships of the hybrid composite system. The addition of filler resulted in a significant improvement in mechanical performance. The tensile strength increased from 42 MPa (S0) to 55 MPa at 10 wt% CaCO3 (S10), while the flexural strength and modulus reached 70 MPa and 4.5 GPa, respectively. The impact resistance was increased from 3.5 to 4.8 kJ/m², indicating better energy absorption mechanisms such as crack deflection and interfacial strengthening. The thermal analysis further proved the improvements. The TGA showed delayed degradation onset and increased char formation. The DSC showed an increase of the glass transition temperature from the value of approximately 90 °C to the value of approximately 98–100 °C, implying restricted mobility of the polymer chain due to improved filler-matrix interaction. Water absorption and biodegradation were also lowered (4.85–3.10% and 6.8–4.1% respectively) which is indicative of improved fibre encapsulation and lowered porosity. The SEM images showed better fibre-matrix adhesion, lower pull-out and uniform filler dispersion in S10, whereas S0 showed interfacial voids and S20 had defects due to agglomeration, supporting these results. SEM–EDX analysis confirmed the uniform distribution of CaCO3 in the matrix. The results show that, from an optimization point of view, 10 wt% CaCO3 is the most efficient filler loading among the studied compositions, providing the best compromise between mechanical performance, thermal stability and environmental resistance without leading to agglomeration-related disadvantages. Higher filler content (20 wt%) caused reduction in some of the properties due to microstructural inhomogeneity. The S10 formulation showed the best normalized mechanical properties, with tensile strength (55 MPa), flexural strength (70 MPa) and impact strength (4.8 kJ/m²) at the maximum values of all the formulations studied. The improvement is due to the better interfacial bonding between the fibre and matrix and the filler is uniformly dispersed. ANOVA (p < 0.001) and Tukey HSD grouping confirmed the statistical significance of these improvements with S10 consistently forming a distinct superior group. The introduction of diffusion and degradation models rationalizes the increased environmental stability of the well-designed composite (S10) by reducing moisture permeability and biodegradation kinetics, which highlight the role of CaCO3 filler in the improvement of barrier mechanisms and fibre-matrix encapsulation. The overall results indicate that the developed composites have the potential of utilizing a combination of banana fibre and waste-derived CaCO3 to develop lightweight and sustainable composites for semi-structural and eco-engineering applications and underline the importance of optimized filler loading for superior composite performance.
