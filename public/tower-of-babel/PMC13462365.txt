Hip resurfacing arthroplasty with cemented highly cross-linked all polyethylene acetabular components
Authors: James W. Pritchett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHip resurfacing arthroplasty (HRA) is needed for certain patients, such as those with in situ metal work and abnormal anatomy, and/or other needs that are not well-served by total hip replacement (THR). Over the last 20 years, almost all HRAs have been performed using Metal-on-Metal (MoM) bearings, but metal wear debris generated over time has now ended their continued use. Cemented highly cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) acetabular bearings have shown excellent survivorship for use in THR and the aim of this study is to determine if this also applied to HRA.MethodsThis prospective cohort study evaluated extending the use of cemented XLPE acetabular bearings to HRA. There were 122 HRA patients who received a cemented XLPE acetabular component. They were followed for a mean 15 years (range, 10–20 years). There were 65 women (53%) and 57 men (47%). The mean age at surgery was 49.2 years (range, 21–68 years) the mean body mass index (BMI) was 26 kg/m2. Clinical outcomes and survivorship were determined. Analyses of implants retrieved during revision or postmortem were performed to evaluate consequences of wear debris.ResultsThe 10-year survival rate for 122 hips was 93.5% [95% confidence interval (CI): 91.7–98.1%] for all-cause revision. Six acetabular bearings failed, requiring revision for: loosening [2], polyethylene wear [2], and dislocation [2]. Four additional well-functioning XLPE bearings were retrieved postmortem. Two well-functioning cemented acetabular bearings were also retrieved; one was an explant for an infection after 9 years and the other was retrieved during revision to THR because of femoral head avascular necrosis at 6 years. Analysis of the 12 retrievals found signs of limited anterior/superior wear in all bearings along with minimal wear in the dome of 10 bearings. Only two retrieved bearings had more than 50% of the anterior/superior area worn and these were after 15 and 20 years of use. Two of the retrieved implants had no signs of wear.ConclusionsCemented all XLPE acetabular bearings produced successful clinical outcomes initially in all patients. Six (4.9%) patients required revision for acetabular failure. Anterior/superior wear was found in the retrievals, but dome wear was limited. Cementing an XLPE acetabular component is a reasonable approach in HRA, but since it does not allow adjunct screw fixation that a metal-backed two-piece component can offer, it is not a solution for every HRA.

Introduction

Background

Since 2005, most surgeons have used Metal-on-Metal (MoM) for hip resurfacing arthroplasty (HRA). These implants usually work well but put patients at risk for adverse reactions to metal wear debris (1). Highly cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) is an alternative to MoM implants for HRA. Cementless modular, metal-backed acetabular bearings are the most common way to perform total hip replacement (THR), but this has not been a common practice for HRA (2-7). HRA has used one-piece acetabular bearings.

Cemented all-polyethylene acetabular bearings with XLPE have shown excellent survivorship for THR. Because of its greater wear resistance, XLPE acetabular bearings are now being used in HRA on a limited basis. The technology for HRA necessitates that many sizes of implants must be available, and polyethylene implants for HRA have only been available from a few manufacturers. In addition, the polyethylene used for HRA is much thinner than what is used for THR. Many resurfacing patients have hip dysplasia, which further limits the acetabular bone available for acetabular component fixation. This and the difficult exposure make HRA technically demanding. Also, the larger femoral head size used in HRA compared to THR produces greater frictional torque, which presents a challenge for both fixation and wear (5).

Legacy two-part HRA systems used a monobloc cemented all polyethylene acetabular component. They did not perform well because of wear, impingement, and loosening (3,8-10). In 2005, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) reclassified two-part resurfacing systems (monobloc acetabular component and monobloc femoral component) from Class 2 to Class 3 indicating a higher level of risk (11). A three-part system has a two-part acetabular component and a monobloc femoral component. The FDA 2005 regulatory change eliminated almost all polyethylene HRA implants. The eliminated systems were all two-part and all used cemented conventional polyethylene. These implants, as well as the instrumentation and insertion techniques, were crude by today’s standards. The three-part systems using a cementless metal acetabular shell and polyethylene liner had less loosening and performed better than the two-part systems. Three-part systems remained in limited use after 2005 (3,8,9), but with the conventional polyethylene used, they still produced unacceptable polyethylene wear.

Rationale and knowledge gap

With the advent of XLPE, there has been renewed interest in polyethylene for HRA. This coincides with less interest in MoM. It is the adverse reaction to wear debris that has reduced the interest in MoM. The wear of MoM is low compared to polyethylene (1,12,13). The femoral component of HRA has a natural secure press-fit because of the precise femoral preparation possible. The femoral component can be placed with or without cement. There are advantages to both methods of fixation. Cementing the femoral component increases the contact area between implant and bone and provides immediate secure fixation. This has been a common and successful method for HRA. However, cement transfers more load through the stem to the surrounding bone, which increases stress shielding. This leads to periprosthetic bone resorption and can result in latent aseptic loosening. Cementless HRA systems have less femoral stress shielding and avoid the thermal effects of cement on the femur. In addition, cementless systems avoid late breakdown of the cement.

Cementing an all-polyethylene acetabular component has several advantages: (I) immediate implant security; (II) lower cost; (III) no intraoperative in vivo component assembly; (IV) no assembly instruments; and (V) no deformation of thin acetabular metal shells. A two-piece acetabular shell and liner, however, has its own advantages: (I) supplemental fixation with screws is possible; (II) independent exchange of the bearing surface during revision for infection or wear is possible; and (III) direct visualization through the shell assures complete seating of the implant.

Objectives

This study reviewed the records of all patients who underwent HRA using XLPE cemented acetabular bearings at Swedish Medical Center. The hypothesis was that XLPE bearings would result in superior implant survival because of their greater resistance to the wear compared with conventional polyethylene. This study asked the following questions about cemented all-polyethylene bearings for HRA: (I) what are the clinical outcomes? (II) what is the implant survivorship? and (III) what are the complications, including wear and loosening? This article is presented in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://aoj.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/aoj-2026-0016/rc).

Methods

This prospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The Institutional Review Board of Swedish Medical Center (IRB No. 5190S-1) approved the study. All patients provided their consent for participation and publication of the results. Participant personal identity and any identifying images were protected. All the information was collected prospectively, as all patients undergoing HRA at Swedish Medical Center are enrolled in an HRA database. All patients receiving HRA between October 2000 and March 2015 were included in the study. The primary outcome measure was implant survival, with revision for any reason as the endpoint. The secondary outcome measure was wear of the retrieved implants. Clinical outcomes were assessed preoperatively and postoperatively using Harris Hip Score (HHS) (14) and the University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) activity score (15).

Patients

There were 122 patients who received cemented all-XLPE bearings for their HRA. These patients were the basis for the study. The mean patient age was 49.2 years (range, 21–68 years), 65 (53%) of patients were women, and 57 (47%) were men. The mean body mass index (BMI) for all patients was 26 kg/m2 and the mean follow-up period was 15 years (range, 10–20 years). From a clinical judgment standpoint, all patients were able to receive either a cemented or a cementless HRA. The indications for the HRA were osteoarthritis (n=49), arthritis from dysplasia (n=49), avascular necrosis (n=7) and post-traumatic arthritis (n=17). Table 1 shows the dimensions of the acetabular implants and the corresponding number of patients who received each size. The femoral implant bearing surfaces were metal using cobalt-chromium for 53 patients and ceramic-coated titanium for 69 patients. The femoral implants were cementless for 100 patients and cemented for 22 patients. Two patients requested a cemented polyethylene acetabular component, and 120 patients received the cemented acetabular component because the cementless metal-backed acetabular component was not available in the necessary dimension. There were no medical or surgical indications contributing to the decision to use all polyethylene bearings.

All patients in this study received a cemented XLPE BP prosthesis (Endotec, Inc., Fullerton, CA, USA). The polyethylene was 4 mm GUY 1020 with 7.5 mRad and sterilized with ethylene oxide. Local and systemic complications and the indications for revision surgery were collected. The acetabular component used was 3 mm smaller than the last reamer used to allow for a cement mantle. The cement used was Simplex T (Stryker, Mahwah, NJ, USA). All procedures were performed with the patient in the lateral position using the posterior approach. A transosseous soft-tissue repair of the capsule and external rotators was performed during closure.

Radiographic evaluations

Two blinded independent observers analyzed all the radiographs (John McDermott and Briana Arnold). The intraobserver and interobserver reliability were 0.96 and 0.94, respectively. Cup inclination and anteversion angles were measured, as were femoral component inclinations with respect to the preoperative femoral neck/shaft angle. The radiographs were analyzed for signs of component wear, bone loss, loosening, stress shielding, and impingement (Figures 1,2).

Retrieval analyses

There were 12 retrieved implants collected (8 during revisions, 4 postmortem) and assessed using optical scanning and a digital coordinate measuring machine to determine wear, damage, and delamination (13).

Statistical analysis

The power analysis, assuming a precision of 10% with a 95% confidence interval (CI) determined 96 to be the minimum required number of patients. Baseline characteristics were reported as means for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Survival analysis was performed with revision for any reason as the endpoint (16). Subgroup analyses to investigate the association between age, gender, and femoral head size on implant survival was not possible since there were only six failures. Analyses were performed using Stata 13.1 software (StataCorp, LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and the level of significance was set at P<0.05.

Results

The mean follow-up was 15 years (range, 10–20 years). No patients were lost to follow-up. By the end of the follow-up period, 8 patients (7%) were deceased. At 10 years, the acetabular implant survival was 93.5% (95% CI: 91.7–98.1%), with all-cause revision as the endpoint. The mean HHS was 92 (range, 86–100) and the mean UCLA activity score was 8 (range, 4–10). The mean acetabular component inclination was 40° and the mean anteversion angle was 15°. The mean thickness of the acetabular cement mantle was 3 mm (range, 1–5 mm).

Complications and revisions

There was one local wound infection that was resolved with local wound care and antibiotics. There were 8 (6.5%) revisions. Six were for acetabular failure: loosening at years 8 and 9 (n=2), pain with polyethylene wear (n=2) both at year 10, and dislocation at years 5 and 8 (n=2). There were no other patients with radiographic signs of impending failure from wear or loosening. There were also two well-functioning implants revised, one for infection at year 9 and one for revision to THA due to avascular necrosis of the femoral head at year 6. With just six failures, it was not possible to discern any patient characteristic, implant size, or component position factors that could influence the possibility of failure or success.

Retrieved component analysis

There were 12 acetabular bearings retrieved (8 during revision and 4 postmortem). Ten showed signs of wear limited to the anterior/superior areas consistent with the upward and lateral wear pattern of their native hip; the two dislocation cases also showed edge loading at the posterior edge consistent with the posterior dislocation and reduction. Two revisions were performed because of pain with polyethylene wear. Both retrieved implants had 2 mm of wear in the anterior/superior area. The wear at the dome was 1 mm or less for all retrievals. The cement remained attached to the polyethylene in all but one of the retrieved implants.

Discussion

This study found a high rate of implant survival and good clinical results using a cemented all XLPE acetabular component in HRA procedures. There has been a marked improvement in HRA outcomes with the lower wear of XLPE implants compared to conventional polyethylene (3,8,9,10,17). Also, there were fewer failures from loosening and impingement in this series compared with first-generation cemented conventional polyethylene HRA.

Key findings

Analysis of the retrieved bearings found wear in the highest stress areas anteriorly and superiorly for the XLPE. This is not unexpected, as XLPE is vulnerable to edge loading, and when used at just 4 mm in thickness and at these very large dimensions. It should be noted that the patients were very active, as indicated by their high UCLA activity scores.

Cementless one or two-piece acetabular bearings are used currently for most HRA procedures. The thickness of the cement mantle (3 mm) in this study is similar to the thickness of the metal backing that is used to support the cementless application (2-7,12,18).

Strengths and limitations

The primary strengths of this study are that no patient was lost to follow-up and that there were 12 retrievals available for analysis. Limitations of the study are: (I) the follow-up is midterm, with a mean of 15 years; (II) this is a single-center, single-surgeon study; and (III) there were no predefined indications for using a cemented all-polyethylene acetabular component over a cementless acetabular component. There is some resistance among surgeons to HRA because the operation is technically demanding and the patients’ expectations are high. THR is more familiar and works well, but is not always available as an option, such as in cases with in situ metal work and abnormal anatomy, and/or other needs that are not well-served by THR (19).

Comparison with similar research

The results of this study are similar to reports of cemented polyethylene acetabular bearings for THA (20). There is no comparison series for HRA using a cemented XLPE versus a cementless metal-backed polyethylene HRA. Amstutz et al. reported a few cases (2). Metal-backed two-piece cementless XLPE has worked very well for HRA. Buechel et al. (3), Pritchett (4-6,19), and Basgul et al. (21) also reported good outcomes with cementless HRA using ceramic-coated titanium.

Explanation of findings

A one-piece acetabular component may not be better than a two-piece because: (I) A one-piece acetabular component may not be covered completely because of dysplasia putting the polyethylene at risk for edge loading, loosening, and failure; (II) it is not possible to exchange the polyethylene independently if needed due to wear, infection, or traumatic damage from dislocation; and (III) there is no ability to add adjunctive fixation with screws, if needed.

Implications and actions needed

Contributing factors to the success of cemented acetabular bearings in this series include a highly experienced resurfacing surgeon, improved polyethylene, a proven acetabular component design, and the use of modern cementing techniques. Since there was only one femoral failure in this series, it was not possible to determine whether the femur should be metal or ceramic coated and cemented or cementless; further studies will be needed to determine the value of these options. An ongoing clinical trial evaluating a one-piece, XLPE acetabular component with cementless fixation imbedded into the polyethylene is showing very positive short-term results (22).

Conclusions

Cemented XLPE acetabular bearings for HRA offer successful implant survival at a mean of 15 years of follow-up. To achieve high quality results, full attention is paid to acetabular cementing technique, component sizing, and orientation. XLPE has improved the results compared with conventional polyethylene HRA (13); however, there is still a need for further improvements in HRA implant procedures.
