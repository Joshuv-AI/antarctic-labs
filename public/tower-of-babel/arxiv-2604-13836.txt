Potential Games on Unimodular Random Graphs
Eyal Neuman, Sturmius Tuschmann

Abstract
We study potential games on unimodular random graphs of bounded degree, where players interact through the underlying network. Using the unimodular measure, we define a well-posed global potential that captures both finite- and infinite-player games. A key observation is that the mass-transport principle identifies the first variation of this potential with the first-order condition of a representative (root) player. Under suitable convexity assumptions, we prove that minimizers of the potential coincide with quenched Nash equilibria, and conversely. We also establish the thermodynamic limit of the potential along weakly convergent sequences of unimodular measures. Finally, we present examples with semi-explicit equilibrium descriptions. In linear-quadratic games on unimodular graphs, equilibria are expressed in terms of the Green kernel of the simple random walk operator, while in convex settings, equilibria are characterized by solutions to nonlinear Poisson equations.

Introduction

Network games are games in which interactions between players are heterogeneous, often modeled through a graph. Each player’s action affects not only their own payoff, but also the local environment and the incentives of neighboring players. Such models arise naturally in a broad range of real-world applications, including autonomous driving
[
21
]
, dynamic production management
[
27
]
, and real-time bidding
[
42
]
. From a mathematical perspective, their analysis is challenging because it must simultaneously account for heterogeneous interactions, large populations, and nonlinear feedback effects between individual actions and the environment.

In recent years, there has been substantial progress in the analysis of static and dynamic games on dense networks using the graphon framework (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
33
,
34
,
38
,
39
,
43
]
). A central advantage of the mean-field game (MFG) paradigm is that it simplifies the analysis of large games by reducing an
n
-player interaction problem to that of a single representative player via a fixed-point condition. Following this idea,
Lacker and Soret 2023
showed that a graphon game can be recast as a standard MFG on an enlarged state space, where each player is described not only by its state variable but also by a random label encoding its position in the graphon. As a consequence, equilibria can be characterized by a single forward–backward system rather than by a continuum of coupled equations, thereby recovering a level of tractability akin to that of classical MFGs.

At the same time, most real-world networks are sparse, as players typically maintain only a limited number of interactions due to informational, economic, or technological constraints. This sparsity plays a fundamental role in shaping strategic behavior, as it restricts the range of interactions and gives rise to strong locality effects. Consequently, dense network models often fail to capture key features of large-scale systems encountered in economics, engineering, and the social sciences. In contrast, sparse network models, in which node degrees remain locally bounded, provide a more realistic description of such environments and are therefore central to the study of large-population games.

Despite their importance, the theoretical understanding of games on sparse networks remains limited. While dense graph limits are well described by graphon theory
[
29
,
30
]
, the sparse regime requires fundamentally different tools. A natural framework for sparse graph limits is local weak convergence, introduced by
Benjamini and Schramm 2001
and independently by
Aldous and Steele 2004
. This notion captures the asymptotic distribution of neighborhoods around a typical node and leads to probability measures on the space of countable, connected, locally finite, rooted graphs. In this setting, unimodular probability measures emerge as canonical limit objects, playing a role analogous to that of graphons in the dense case
[
1
,
6
]
. Their defining property is the mass–transport principle (see (
2.2
)). The framework of local weak convergence has already proved fruitful in the study of interacting particle systems on sparse graphs (see, e.g.,
[
16
,
20
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
36
,
37
,
41
]
). More recently, the authors of the present paper used this approach to analyze the convergence of Nash equilibria in games on sparse graphs
[
35
]
. Motivated by the MFG perspective, this raises the following fundamental question about network games in the infinite-graph limit:

Is there an analogue of the representative player in network games?

We answer this question within the class of potential games, a particularly tractable and extensively studied class of games. Originally introduced in the static setting by
Monderer and Shapley 1996
, potential games have received renewed attention in recent years, partly through developments in multi-agent reinforcement learning (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
12
,
28
,
31
,
40
,
46
]
) and partly through recent analytical advances in dynamic potential games
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. A game is called a potential game if there exists a global functional, called a potential, such that every unilateral deviation by a player changes this functional by exactly the same amount as it changes that player’s payoff. This variational structure makes it possible to reformulate the search for Nash equilibria as an optimization problem for the potential, thereby opening the door to powerful analytical and computational methods.

While potential games have been extensively studied in finite-player and mean-field settings, their extension to graph-based and infinite-player models raises substantial challenges. In network settings, payoffs depend on local neighborhoods, and there is generally no canonical global state variable, which complicates both the construction and the identification of potential structures. Moreover, even when local interactions admit a potential representation, it is not clear that these local structures can be aggregated into a well-defined global potential on the graph, since this would require summing contributions from infinitely many players. On the other hand, the study of potential games on infinite random graphs is particularly relevant, as it is often unrealistic to assume that each player has detailed knowledge of the entire network. Collecting exact network data may be prohibitively costly and often impossible because of proprietary or privacy constraints
[
39
]
. A more realistic approach is therefore to model the network through a probability distribution over graph realizations, such as a unimodular measure. This leads to the second question addressed in this paper:

Can one define a potential game on an infinite random graph?

The goal of this paper is to develop a general framework for potential games on unimodular random graphs, thereby bridging the theories of sparse graph limits and potential games. We consider games in which players are indexed by the vertices of a bounded-degree graph, possibly infinite, and interact through the underlying network. The players’ actions take values in a general Hilbert space, a formulation flexible enough to cover static games as well as dynamic games in either discrete or continuous time. See Section
2
for details.

In Section
3
, we introduce a potential functional
\Phi
for finite and infinite-player games, defined through the unimodular measure. Proposition
3.1
establishes that this potential is finite for all admissible actions, despite the possible presence of infinitely many players. Proposition
3.2
then identifies its first variation with the first-order condition of a representative player via the mass–transport principle. In particular, Theorem
3.5
shows that, under convexity with respect to each player’s own action, minimizers of the potential
\Phi
form quenched Nash equilibria. Conversely, if
\Phi
is itself convex, then every quenched Nash equilibrium is also a minimizer of
\Phi
. The mass-transport principle is again the key mechanism underlying this equivalence. In Theorem
3.9
, we then show that whenever a sequence of finite graphs converges in the local weak sense, the associated finite-player potentials converge to the potential of the limiting unimodular random graph, further justifying our definition of the infinite-player potential.

In Section
4
, we present examples in which the game admits semi-explicit solutions. In particular, Proposition
4.1
and Corollary
4.2
show that, for linear–quadratic potential games on unimodular random graphs whose random walk operator has spectral radius strictly less than
1
, equilibria can be expressed in terms of the Green kernel of the graph. In Proposition
4.5
, we further show that, in the nonlinear convex case, the equilibrium is characterized by a solution to the Poisson equation associated with a nonlinear Laplacian induced by the random graph realization, together with the convex component of the potential. Throughout, the mass-transport principle and its interplay with the potential play a central role in deriving these results. Sections
5
–
8
are devoted to the proofs of our results.

2
Model Setup

In this section, we set up a general framework for potential games on unimodular random graphs. We begin by introducing unimodularity, then present the game-theoretic model, and finally discuss several examples of graphs that illustrate the representative-player formulation.

2.1
Unimodular random graphs

We consider graphs
G=(V,E)
that consist of a finite or countably infinite vertex set
V
and an edge set
E
. All graphs are assumed to be simple (that is, they are undirected and have no loops or multiple edges) and locally finite (that is, the degree
\deg_{G}(v)
of each vertex
v\in V
is finite). For two vertices
u,v\in V
, denote by
d_{G}(u,v)
the graph distance between
u
and
v
, that is, the length of the shortest path in
G
from
u
to
v
. If
\{u,v\}\in E
, we write
u\sim v
and call
u
and
v
neighbors. With a slight abuse of notation, we often write
v\in G
instead of
v\in V
, and set
|G|:=|V|
for the cardinality of the vertex set.

A rooted graph
(G,o)=(V,E,o)
consists of a (simple, locally finite) graph
G=(V,E)
with a distinguished vertex
o\in V
, called the root. Given a rooted graph
(G,o)
and
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we write
B_{r}(G,o)
for the induced rooted subgraph consisting of all vertices
v\in V
whose graph distance
d_{G}(o,v)
from the root
o
is at most
r
. Two rooted graphs
(G,o)=(V,E,o)
and
(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},o^{\prime})
are said to be isomorphic if there exists a bijection
\varphi:V\to V^{\prime}
with
\varphi(o)=o^{\prime}
such that

\{u,v\}\in E\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\{\varphi(u),\varphi(v)\}\in E^{\prime},\quad\text{for all }u,v\in V.

In this case we write
(G,o)\cong(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})
and say that
\varphi
is an isomorphism from
(G,o)
to
(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})
. We denote the isomorphism class of a rooted graph
(G,o)
by
[G,o]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
denote the set of isomorphism classes of countable, connected, rooted graphs. A sequence
\{[G_{n},o_{n}]\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
is said to converge locally to
[G,o]\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
if for every
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there exists
n_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
B_{r}(G_{n},o_{n})\cong B_{r}(G,o)
for all
n\geq n_{r}
.

For
[G,o],[G^{\prime},o^{\prime}]\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
, define the corresponding metric by

d_{\ast}\big([G,o],[G^{\prime},o^{\prime}]\big):=\inf_{r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\big\{2^{-r}:B_{r}(G,o)\cong B_{r}(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})\big\}.

(2.1)

Remark 2.2
.

The metric in (
2.1
) turns
(\mathcal{G}_{\ast},d_{\ast})
into a complete and separable metric space (see
[
2
]
, Section 2.2). If one restricts
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
to graphs of uniformly bounded degree,
(\mathcal{G}_{\ast},d_{\ast})
is compact (see
[
29
]
, Chapter 18.3.1).

Similarly to Definition
2.1
, let
\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}
denote the space of isomorphism classes
[G,o_{1},o_{2}]
of countable, connected, doubly rooted graphs
(G,o_{1},o_{2})
. Let
d_{\ast\ast}
be the corresponding metric defined analogous to (
2.1
) (two doubly rooted graphs
(G,o_{1},o_{2})
and
(G^{\prime},o_{1}^{\prime},o_{2}^{\prime})
are isomorphic if there exists an isomorphism
\varphi:(G,o_{1})\to(G^{\prime},o_{1}^{\prime})
with
\varphi(o_{2})=o_{2}^{\prime}
). Assume that
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}
are equipped with their Borel
\sigma
-fields induced by
d_{\ast}
and
d_{\ast\ast}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
. We say that
\mu
is unimodular if for every nonnegative measurable function
T:\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}\to[0,\infty]
the following mass-transport principle holds:

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\in V(G)}T([G,o,v])\mu(d[G,o])=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\in V(G)}T([G,v,o])\mu(d[G,o]).

(2.2)

For a unimodular measure
\mu
, a random rooted graph
[G,o]
distributed according to
\mu
is called a unimodular random graph.

Remark 2.4
.

Unimodular measures are central in the theory of sparse graphs, as they arise as limits of locally weakly convergent graph sequences (see
[
5
]
, Section 3.2). More precisely, a sequence of finite graphs
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is said to converge locally weakly to a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
if the probability measures
\{\mu_{G_{n}}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
, obtained by choosing a root uniformly at random and considering the associated rooted connected component, converge weakly to
\mu
. We do not further formalize local weak convergence here, and only note that this notion can also be extended to random graph sequences. See
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
7
,
44
]
for further details.

In what follows, we impose the following bounded-degree assumption.

Assumption 2.5
.

Let
\mu
be a unimodular measure on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
. We assume that there exists a constant
D\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\deg_{G}(v)\leq D,\quad\text{for all }v\in V(G),\text{ for }\mu\text{-a.e. }[G,o].

(2.3)

2.2
The potential network game

Throughout, let
H
denote a separable real Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{H}
. The set of admissible actions
K\subset H
is assumed to be nonempty, closed, and convex.

A measurable map
x:\mathcal{G}_{\ast}\to H
will be interpreted as an action rule.
Given such
x
and a rooted graph
(G,o)
, define

x^{G}_{v}:=x([G,v]),\quad v\in V(G).

(2.4)

Thus, a single action rule on the space
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
induces an action profile
x^{G}=(x^{G}_{v})_{v\in V(G)}
on each countable, connected (unrooted) graph
G
. We define the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}(\mu,H)=\bigg\{x:\mathcal{G}_{\ast}\to H\text{ measurable}\,\bigg|\,\|x\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\|x([G,o])\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])<\infty\bigg\},

(2.5)

endowed with the inner product

\langle x,y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}:=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\langle x([G,o]),y([G,o])\rangle_{H}\mu(d[G,o]),\quad x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

Let

\mathcal{K}:=\big\{x\in\mathcal{H}\,\big|\,x([G,o])\in K\text{ for }\mu\text{-a.e. }[G,o]\big\}.

(2.6)

Since
K\subset H
is closed and convex,
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{H}
is also closed and convex. Next, let

f:\mathcal{G}_{\ast}\times H\to\mathbb{R}\quad\text{and}\quad h:\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}\times H\times H\to\mathbb{R}

(2.7)

be measurable functions. We interpret
f
as the private cost and
h
as the interaction cost. For a fixed countable, connected graph
G
and an action profile
\smash{x^{G}=(x^{G}_{u})_{u\in V(G)}\in K^{V(G)}}
, define the cost functional
J_{v}^{G}
of player
v\in V(G)
by

J_{v}^{G}(x^{G}):=f([G,v],x^{G}_{v})+\sum_{u\sim v}h([G,v,u],x^{G}_{v},x^{G}_{u}).

(2.8)

The corresponding game on the fixed countable, connected graph
G
will be referred to as the quenched game.

Remark 2.6
.

The abstract Hilbert space
H
can be chosen in different ways, depending on whether one wants to model static or dynamic games. In particular, our framework covers the following examples.

(i)

Static games.

One may take
H=\mathbb{R}^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, equipped with its usual Euclidean inner product.

(ii)

Dynamic games.

Let
T>0
and fix a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T},\mathbb{P})
.

•

Discrete time.

Let
\mathbb{T}:=\{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=T\}
be a finite time grid. One may choose

H=\bigg\{a:\Omega\times\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}\,\bigg|\,a(t_{j})\text{ is }\mathcal{F}_{t_{j}}\text{-measurable for every $j$, }\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=0}^{N}a(t_{j})^{2}\bigg]<\infty\bigg\},

equipped with the inner product

\langle a,b\rangle_{H}=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{j=0}^{N}a(t_{j})b(t_{j})]
for
a,b\in H
.

•

Continuous time.
One may set

H=\bigg\{a:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}\,\bigg|\,a\text{ is }\mathbb{F}\text{-prog.~measurable, }\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}a(t)^{2}dt\bigg]<\infty\bigg\},

equipped with the inner product

\langle a,b\rangle_{H}=\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{T}a(t)b(t)dt]
for
a,b\in H
.

Remark 2.7
.

The restriction to action profiles induced by action rules as in (
2.4
) is a central feature of the present framework. It ensures consistency under rerooting: whenever two players
u,v\in V(G)
have indistinguishable rooted positions in the graph

G
, in the sense that the rooted graphs
(G,u)
and
(G,v)
are isomorphic, they must choose the same action, that is,
x_{u}^{G}=x_{v}^{G}
. For graphs with nontrivial automorphisms, this can lead to a substantial reduction in complexity, since vertices lying in the same orbit of the automorphism group necessarily carry the same action under any admissible rule. For highly symmetric graphs, the analysis of the infinite-player game may therefore reduce to a finite-dimensional problem, as illustrated in Examples
2.12
,
2.13
, and
2.15
below.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{K}
. We say that
x
is a quenched Nash equilibrium if

J_{o}^{G}(x^{G})\leq J_{o}^{G}(a,x^{G}_{-o}),\quad\text{for all }a\in K,\text{ for }\mu\text{-a.e. }[G,o],

(2.9)

where
x^{G}_{v}=x([G,v])
for all
v\in V(G)
, and
(a,x^{G}_{-o})
denotes the action profile obtained from
x^{G}
by replacing the root action
x^{G}_{o}
by
a
.

In other words, for
\mu
-a.e. rooted graph
[G,o]
, the root player cannot lower their cost by a unilateral deviation, holding fixed the actions prescribed by the rule
x
at all other vertices.

Remark 2.9
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite connected graph with
|V|=n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
G
induces a unimodular measure
\mu_{G}
on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
by choosing the root uniformly at random:

\mu_{G}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in V}\delta_{[G,v]}.

(2.10)

In this case, an action rule
x\in\mathcal{K}
induces an action profile
x^{G}=(x_{v}^{G})_{v\in V}
on the finite player set
V
. The quenched Nash equilibrium condition
(
2.9
) holds if and only if

J_{v}^{G}(x^{G})\leq J_{v}^{G}(a,x^{G}_{-v}),\quad\text{for all }a\in K,\ v\in V.

We now impose the structural assumptions on the cost functionals in (
2.8
) that make this game a potential game.

Assumption 2.10
.

Assume the following on the cost functionals defined in (
2.7
) and (
2.8
).

(i)

For
\mu
-a.e.
[G,o]
, the map
a\mapsto f([G,o],a)
is Gâteaux differentiable on
H
.

(ii)

For
\mu
-a.e.
[G,o]
and every neighbor
v\sim o
, the map
a\mapsto h([G,o,v],a,b)
is Gâteaux differentiable on
H
for every
b\in H
. Moreover, there exists a measurable map

\psi:\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}\times H\times H\to\mathbb{R},

(2.11)

such that for
\mu
-a.e.
[G,o]
and every neighbor
v\sim o
, the map
(a,b)\mapsto\psi([G,o,v],a,b)
is Gâteaux differentiable on
H\times H
, and for all
a,b\in H
,

\nabla_{1}\psi([G,o,v],a,b)=\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a,b),

(2.12)

\nabla_{2}\psi([G,o,v],a,b)=\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],b,a).

(2.13)

(iii)

There exist constants
L_{f},L_{h}\geq 0
such that for
\mu
-a.e.
[G,o]
, every neighbor
v\sim o
, and all
a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}\in H
,

\|\nabla f([G,o],a)-\nabla f([G,o],a^{\prime})\|_{H}\leq L_{f}\|a-a^{\prime}\|_{H},

(2.14)

and

\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a,b)-\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\|_{H}\leq L_{h}(\|a-a^{\prime}\|_{H}+\|b-b^{\prime}\|_{H}).

(2.15)

(iv)

There exists a reference point
a_{\ast}\in H
such that

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}|f([G,o],a_{\ast})|\mu(d[G,o])<\infty,\quad\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\|\nabla f([G,o],a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])<\infty,

(2.16)

and

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}|\psi([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})|\mu(d[G,o])<\infty,

(2.17)

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])<\infty.

(2.18)

Remark 2.11
.

Assumptions (
2.12
)–(
2.13
) encode the exact potential property of
Monderer and Shapley 1996
in differential form at the level of a single edge
\{o,v\}
. Equivalently, in difference form, for all
a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}\in H
,

\psi([G,o,v],a,b)-\psi([G,o,v],a^{\prime},b)=h([G,o,v],a,b)-h([G,o,v],a^{\prime},b),

(2.19)

and

\psi([G,o,v],a,b)-\psi([G,o,v],a,b^{\prime})=h([G,v,o],b,a)-h([G,v,o],b^{\prime},a).

(2.20)

That is, a unilateral deviation at
o
changes the interaction cost
h
by exactly the corresponding change in the functional
\psi
when its first argument is varied, see (
2.19
). Reversing the perspective along the edge
\{o,v\}
, a unilateral deviation at
v
changes the interaction cost
h
by exactly the corresponding change in the functional
\psi
when its second argument is varied, see (
2.20
).

2.3
Examples of graphs and associated representative players

We illustrate the representative-player formulation with several basic examples. Here, the key point is that admissible action profiles in (
2.4
) are induced by action rules
x:\mathcal{G}_{\ast}\to H
, so that players with isomorphic rooted neighborhoods always choose the same action.

Example 2.12

(Cycle graphs)
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
C_{n}
be the cycle graph with vertices

V(C_{n})=[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\},

and let
\mu_{C_{n}}
denote the corresponding unimodular measure on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
from (
2.10
). Since
C_{n}
is transitive, all the rooted graphs
(C_{n},k)
for
k\in[n]
are isomorphic. Consequently, every action rule induces a constant action profile on
C_{n}
. In other words, although the game on
C_{n}
has
n
players, only a single player type appears in the representative-player formulation.
As
n\to\infty
, the local weak limit of
\{C_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is the unimodular measure concentrated on the two-way infinite path
P\cong\mathbb{Z}
rooted at an arbitrary vertex (see
[
29
]
, Chapter 19, Example 19.3). Again, this limit graph is transitive, so the infinite-player game also has only one representative player (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1
:
Left: the
n
-cycle
C_{n}
on
[n]
(for
n=30
). Right: its local weak limit, the two-way infinite path
P\cong\mathbb{Z}
with a root (red).

The next example shows that passing to the infinite-player limit can reduce the dimensionality of the game significantly.

Example 2.13

(Boxes in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
)
.

Fix
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
G_{n}
be the nearest-neighbor box graph on

V(G_{n})=[n]^{d}:=\{1,\dots,n\}^{d},

where two vertices
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
and
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})
are adjacent if and only if there exists a unique index
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
such that
|u_{i}-v_{i}|=1
and
u_{j}=v_{j}
for all
j\neq i
. Let
\mu_{G_{n}}
be the associated unimodular measure on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
from (
2.10
). For
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\in[n]^{d}
, define

\delta_{i}(v):=\min\{v_{i}-1,n-v_{i}\},\quad i=1,\dots,d.

Then the isomorphism class
[G_{n},v]
of
(G_{n},v)
is uniquely determined by the multiset

\{\delta_{1}(v),\dots,\delta_{d}(v)\}.

Each
\delta_{i}(v)
takes values in
\{0,\dots,\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-1\}
, so the number of representative players is exactly the number of multisets of size
d

The above examples demonstrate the strength of the representative-player formulation. By identifying players with isomorphic rooted neighborhoods, it can reduce the dimensionality of network games substantially, often even to a single representative player. We now turn to the construction of a global potential that allows us to systematically exploit this idea. Throughout, it is helpful to keep the examples in mind as concrete illustrations.

3
Main Results

This section contains the main analytical results of the paper. We show that the game admits a potential functional whose first variation recovers the representative-player first-order condition. This variational structure yields a characterization of quenched Nash equilibria via minimizers of the potential and allows us to study the thermodynamic limit under weak convergence of unimodular measures.

3.1
The potential functional

Under Assumptions
2.5
and
2.10
, define the potential functional
\Phi:\mathcal{H}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

\Phi(x):=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\bigg[f\big([G,o],x([G,o])\big)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\sim o}\psi\big([G,o,v],x([G,o]),x([G,v])\big)\bigg]\mu(d[G,o]),\quad x\in\mathcal{H}.

(3.1)

The factor
1/2
in (
3.1
) compensates for the fact that each undirected edge is counted twice. The following proposition shows that
\Phi
is well-defined.

Proposition 3.1
.

Under Assumptions
2.5
and
2.10
, the potential functional
\Phi
from (
3.1
) is finite on
\mathcal{H}
.

The next proposition contains an important structural observation of the framework. Owing to the potential property in Assumption
2.10
, the first variation of
\Phi
can be identified with the first-order expression of the representative (root) player in the game.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.5
and
2.10
hold, and let
x,{y}\in\mathcal{H}
. Then
\Phi
is Gâteaux differentiable at
x
, and

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\Phi(x),y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\bigg\langle\nabla f([G,o],x([G,o]))+\sum_{v\sim o}\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],x([G,o]),x([G,v])),\,{y}([G,o])\bigg\rangle_{H}\mu(d[G,o]).

Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
are proved in Section
5
. Motivated by Proposition
3.2
, define the representative-player first-order operator
F
on
\mathcal{H}
by

F(x)([G,o]):=\nabla f([G,o],x([G,o]))+\sum_{v\sim o}\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],x([G,o]),x([G,v])).

(3.3)

Under Assumptions
2.5
and
2.10
, the operator
F:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is well-defined. Indeed, arguing as in the proof of Proposition
3.1
, one can show that
F(x)\in\mathcal{H}
. For a general closed, convex set
K
, the associated representative-player first-order condition is the variational inequality

\langle F(x),z-x\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq 0,\quad\text{for all }z\in\mathcal{K}.

(3.4)

In the unconstrained case where
K=H
, we have
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}
. Then (
3.4
) reduces to the unconstrained representative-player first-order condition

F(x)=0.

(3.5)

Remark 3.3
.

The first-order condition (
3.4
) (resp. (
3.5
)) is formulated in terms of the root player (or representative player), analogous to the representative player in mean-field games. As illustrated in Examples
2.12
,
2.13
, and
2.15
, this viewpoint can substantially reduce the dimensionality of the corresponding equilibrium conditions. As we shall see, the potential functional
\Phi
captures these conditions in a unified way and thereby provides a convenient tool for characterizing quenched Nash equilibria.

3.2
Minimizers and quenched Nash equilibria

We now connect the variational problem for the potential functional
\Phi
to the notion of quenched Nash equilibria.

Assumption 3.4
.

For
\mu
-a.e.
[G,o]
and every family
(b_{v})_{v\sim o}\subset H
, the map

a\mapsto f([G,o],a)+\sum_{v\sim o}h([G,o,v],a,b_{v})

is convex.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.5
,
2.10
, and
3.4
hold, and let
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{K}
. Consider the following statements:

(i)

x^{\ast}
is a minimizer of
\Phi
over
\mathcal{K}
.

(ii)

x^{\ast}
solves the representative-player first-order variational inequality (
3.4
).

(iii)

x^{\ast}
is a quenched Nash equilibrium.

Then
\text{(i)}\implies\text{(ii)}\iff\text{(iii)}
. If, in addition,
\Phi
is convex on
\mathcal{K}
, then also
\text{(ii)}\implies\text{(i)}
.

Theorem
3.5
is proved in Section
6
.

Remark 3.6
.

Theorem
3.5
shows that minimizers of the potential functional
\Phi
are quenched Nash equilibria under convexity in the own action. Conversely, under the additional assumption that
\Phi
is convex, quenched Nash equilibria also minimize
\Phi
. The key mechanism behind this correspondence is that the mass-transport principle (
2.2
) and the potential property (
2.12
)–(
2.13
) identify the first variation of
\Phi
with the root player’s first-order condition.

3.3
The thermodynamic limit

Weak convergence of unimodular measures naturally arises from local weak convergence of graph sequences (see Remark
2.4
). It is therefore important to understand how the potential functional behaves under such convergence. In view of Theorem
3.5
, this also provides information on the behavior of quenched Nash equilibria along locally weakly convergent graph sequences.

Recall that
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
(resp.
\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}
) is the space of isomorphism classes of countable, connected, rooted (resp. doubly rooted) graphs equipped with the topology induced by
d_{\ast}
(resp.
d_{\ast\ast}
).

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of probability measures on
\mathcal{G}_{*}
, and let
\mu
be another probability measure on
\mathcal{G}_{*}
. The sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges weakly to
\mu
, denoted

\mu_{n}\,\xrightarrow{w}\,\mu,

if for every bounded continuous function
\theta:\mathcal{G}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{*}}\theta([G,o])\,\mu_{n}(d[G,o])\,\longrightarrow\,\int_{\mathcal{G}_{*}}\theta([G,o])\,\mu(d[G,o]),\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Assumption 3.8
.

Let
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be unimodular probability measures on
\mathcal{G}_{\ast}
such that
\mu_{n}\xrightarrow{w}\mu
, and assume that all
\mu_{n}
are supported on graphs with uniform degree bound
D\in\mathbb{N}
as in (
2.3
). Assume further that
f:\mathcal{G}_{\ast}\times H\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi:\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}\times H\times H\to\mathbb{R}
from (
2.7
) and (
2.11
) are continuous. Finally, suppose there exist
\varepsilon>0
and a reference point
a_{\ast}\in H
such that

\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}|f([G,o],a_{\ast})|^{1+\varepsilon}\mu_{n}(d[G,o])<\infty,\quad\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\|\nabla f([G,o],a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2+\varepsilon}\mu_{n}(d[G,o])<\infty,

(3.6)

and

\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}|\psi([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})|^{1+\varepsilon}\mu_{n}(d[G,o])<\infty,

(3.7)

\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2+\varepsilon}\mu_{n}(d[G,o])<\infty.

(3.8)

Theorem 3.9
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.5
,
2.10
(i)–(iii), and
3.8
hold. Let
x\in\mathcal{H}
be an action rule satisfying

\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\|x([G,o])\|_{H}^{2+\varepsilon}\mu_{n}(d[G,o])<\infty,\quad\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\|x([G,o])\|_{H}^{2+\varepsilon}\mu(d[G,o])<\infty,

(3.9)

for the same
\varepsilon>0
as in Assumption
3.8
. Then

\Phi_{n}(x)\longrightarrow\Phi(x),\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

where
\Phi_{n}
denotes the potential functional (
3.1
) with
\mu
replaced by
\mu_{n}
.

Theorem
3.9
is proved in Section
7
.

Remark 3.10
.

The convergence result in Theorem
3.9
applies to Examples
2.12
,
2.13
, and
2.15
, namely cycle graphs, boxes in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and random
D
-regular graphs. For the latter two examples, the local weak limit has a substantially simpler representative-player structure than the corresponding finite graphs, and in fact reduces to a single representative player. This shows that the thermodynamic limit may lead to a significant reduction in dimensionality when analyzing the corresponding potential functional and equilibrium conditions.

4
Examples

We first consider a static linear–quadratic game, which fits our framework and leads to a linear Poisson equation for quenched Nash equilibria. Under an assumption on the simple random walk operator associated with the graphs in the support of
\mu
, this allows the derivation of an explicit solution via the corresponding Green kernel. We then turn to a more general nonlinear potential game, where we characterize the equilibrium through a nonlinear Poisson equation.

4.1
Linear–quadratic potential games and random walks on graphs

Assume that
\mu
is a unimodular measure with
\deg_{G}(o)>0
for
\mu
-a.e.
[G,o]
, such that Assumption
2.5

, and let

h:\mathcal{G}_{\ast\ast}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad h([G,o,v],a,b):=\frac{1}{2}(a-b)^{2}.

(4.2)

Set also

(4.3)

Define the simple random walk operator

(Px)([G,o]):=\frac{1}{\deg_{G}(o)}\sum_{v\sim o}x([G,v]).

For the choice (

4.1
is proved in Section
8
. Next, we study under which conditions the Poisson equation (

[

.

Proof of Proposition

.

Recalling (

, and write

x_{o}:=x([G,o]),\quad x_{v}:=x([G,v]).

Let

be as in Assumption

(iv). First, we prove that the first term in (

) is integrable. Consider the path given by

\gamma:[0,1]\to H,\quad\gamma(t):=a_{\ast}+t(x_{o}-a_{\ast}),

g(t):=f([G,o],\gamma(t))

a\mapsto f([G,o],a)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{g(t+s)-g(t)}{s}

(5.2)

|f([G,o],x_{o})|\leq|f([G,o],a_{\ast})|+\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\nabla f\big([G,o],a_{\ast}+t(x_{o}-a_{\ast})\big)\right\|_{H}\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}dt.

(5.3)

Next, by (

\left\|\nabla f\big([G,o],a_{\ast}+t(x_{o}-a_{\ast})\big)\right\|_{H}\leq L_{f}\,t\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}+\|\nabla f([G,o],a_{\ast})\|_{H}.

(5.4)

Substituting (

|f([G,o],x_{o})|\leq|f([G,o],a_{\ast})|+\frac{L_{f}}{2}\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}+\|\nabla f([G,o],a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}.

(5.5)

Integrating (

) with respect to
\mu
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}|f([G,o],x_{o})|\mu(d[G,o])

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}|f([G,o],a_{\ast})|\mu(d[G,o])+\frac{L_{f}}{2}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])

(5.6)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\|\nabla f([G,o],a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}\mu(d[G,o]).

The first term on the right-hand side of (
5.6
) is finite by (
2.16
). The second term is finite since
x\in\mathcal{H}
. Finally, the third term is finite by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
2.16
). Hence

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}|f([G,o],x_{o})|\mu(d[G,o])<\infty.

(5.7)

Next, we prove that the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.1
) is integrable. By (
2.12
), for any
a,b\in H
,

\|\nabla_{1}\psi([G,o,v],a,b)\|_{H}=\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a,b)\|_{H}.

Using (
2.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{1}\psi([G,o,v],a,b)\|_{H}

(5.8)

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a,b)-\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}+\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}

\displaystyle\leq L_{h}\big(\|a-a_{\ast}\|_{H}+\|b-a_{\ast}\|_{H}\big)+\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}.

Similarly, by (
2.13
) and (
2.15
),

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{2}\psi([G,o,v],a,b)\|_{H}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],b,a)\|_{H}

(5.9)

\displaystyle\leq L_{h}\big(\|a-a_{\ast}\|_{H}+\|b-a_{\ast}\|_{H}\big)+\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}.

Now consider the path given by

\gamma:[0,1]\to H\times H,\quad\gamma(t):=\big(a_{\ast}+t(x_{o}-a_{\ast}),\,a_{\ast}+t(x_{v}-a_{\ast})\big).

Arguing exactly as in (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), we obtain from the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\displaystyle\psi([G,o,v],x_{o},x_{v})-\psi([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})

(5.10)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\psi\big([G,o,v],\gamma(t)\big),\,x_{o}-a_{\ast}\right\rangle_{H}dt+\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\nabla_{2}\psi\big([G,o,v],\gamma(t)\big),\,x_{v}-a_{\ast}\right\rangle_{H}dt.

Hence, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle|\psi([G,o,v],x_{o},x_{v})|

\displaystyle\leq|\psi([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})|+\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla_{1}\psi([G,o,v],\gamma(t))\|_{H}\,\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}dt

(5.11)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla_{2}\psi([G,o,v],\gamma(t))\|_{H}\,\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}dt.

Using (
5.8
) and (
5.9
), we get from (
5.11
),

\displaystyle|\psi([G,o,v],x_{o},x_{v})|

\displaystyle\leq|\psi([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})|+L_{h}\big(\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}+\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}\big)\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}

(5.12)

\displaystyle+L_{h}\big(\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}+\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}\big)\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}

\displaystyle\leq|\psi([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})|+2L_{h}\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}+2L_{h}\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}.

Summing (
5.12
) over
v\sim o
and integrating with respect to
\mu
, we obtain

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}|\psi([G,o,v],x_{o},x_{v})|\mu(d[G,o])

(5.13)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}|\psi([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})|\mu(d[G,o])

\displaystyle+2L_{h}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])+2L_{h}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}\mu(d[G,o])

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}\mu(d[G,o]).

The first term on the right-hand side of (
5.13
) is finite by (
2.17
). For the second term, the degree bound (
2.3
) and the fact that
x\in\mathcal{H}
yield

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])\leq D\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])<\infty.

(5.14)

For the third term, the mass-transport principle (
2.2
) applied to the transport function
T([G,o,v])=\mathbf{1}_{\{o\sim v\}}\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}
and (
5.14
) give

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])<\infty.

For the fourth term, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}\mu(d[G,o])

(5.15)

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])\bigg)^{1/2},

which is finite by (
2.18
) and (
5.14
). For the last term, the mass-transport principle (
2.2
) and (
2.18
) give

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])\hskip-1.70717pt=\hskip-1.99168pt\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])<\infty,

and therefore the same Cauchy–Schwarz argument as in (
5.15
) applies. Hence

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}|\psi([G,o,v],x_{o},x_{v})|\mu(d[G,o])<\infty.

(5.16)

Combining (
5.7
) and (
5.16
), we conclude that

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\Big|f([G,o],x_{o})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\sim o}\psi([G,o,v],x_{o},x_{v})\Big|\mu(d[G,o])<\infty.

Hence the integrand in (
3.1
) is absolutely integrable, and therefore
\Phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

We continue with the proof of Proposition
3.2
, where we compute the Gâteaux derivative of
\Phi
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Recalling (
2.5
), fix
x,{y}\in\mathcal{H}
, and write

x_{o}:=x([G,o]),\quad x_{v}:=x([G,v]),\quad{y}_{o}:={y}([G,o]),\quad{y}_{v}:={y}([G,v]).

For
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, define
\Xi:\mathcal{G}_{\ast}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

\Xi_{t}([G,o]):=f([G,o],x_{o}+t{y}_{o})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\sim o}\psi([G,o,v],x_{o}+t{y}_{o},x_{v}+t{y}_{v}).

(5.17)

Then, by definition of
\Phi
in (
3.1
),

\Phi(x+t{y})=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\Xi_{t}([G,o])\mu(d[G,o]).

(5.18)

Since
a\mapsto f([G,o],a)
and
(a,b)\mapsto\psi([G,o,v],a,b)
are Gâteaux differentiable by Assumption
2.10
, for every fixed
[G,o]
we have by (
5.17
) and a similar argument as in (
5.1
),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Xi_{t}([G,o])

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla f([G,o],x_{o}+t{y}_{o}),{y}_{o}\right\rangle_{H}

(5.19)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\sim o}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\psi([G,o,v],x_{o}+t{y}_{o},x_{v}+t{y}_{v}),{y}_{o}\right\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\sim o}\left\langle\nabla_{2}\psi([G,o,v],x_{o}+t{y}_{o},x_{v}+t{y}_{v}),{y}_{v}\right\rangle_{H}.

Next, by (
2.14
) it holds for any
a\in H
that

\|\nabla f([G,o],a)\|_{H}\leq L_{f}\|a-a_{\ast}\|_{H}+\|\nabla f([G,o],a_{\ast})\|_{H}.

(5.20)

Likewise, by (
2.12
) and (
2.15
), it holds for any
a,b\in H
that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{1}\psi([G,o,v],a,b)\|_{H}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a,b)\|_{H}

(5.21)

\displaystyle\leq L_{h}\big(\|a-a_{\ast}\|_{H}+\|b-a_{\ast}\|_{H}\big)+\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H},

and similarly, by (
2.13
) and (
2.15
),

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{2}\psi([G,o,v],a,b)\|_{H}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],b,a)\|_{H}

(5.22)

\displaystyle\leq L_{h}\big(\|a-a_{\ast}\|_{H}+\|b-a_{\ast}\|_{H}\big)+\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}.

Assuming
|t|\leq 1
, it holds that

\|x_{o}+t{y}_{o}\|_{H}\leq\|x_{o}\|_{H}+\|{y}_{o}\|_{H},\quad\|x_{v}+t{y}_{v}\|_{H}\leq\|x_{v}\|_{H}+\|{y}_{v}\|_{H}.

Therefore, using (
5.19
), (
5.20
), (
5.21
), (
5.22
), and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{d}{dt}\Xi_{t}([G,o])\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+\|x_{o}\|_{H}+\|{y}_{o}\|_{H}\big)\|{y}_{o}\|_{H}+\|\nabla f([G,o],a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|{y}_{o}\|_{H}

(5.23)

\displaystyle+C\sum_{v\sim o}\big(1+\|x_{o}\|_{H}+\|x_{v}\|_{H}+\|{y}_{o}\|_{H}+\|{y}_{v}\|_{H}\big)\|{y}_{o}\|_{H}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|{y}_{o}\|_{H}

\displaystyle+C\sum_{v\sim o}\big(1+\|x_{o}\|_{H}+\|x_{v}\|_{H}+\|{y}_{o}\|_{H}+\|{y}_{v}\|_{H}\big)\|{y}_{v}\|_{H}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}\,\|{y}_{v}\|_{H}.

for a positive constant
C<\infty
not depending on
|t|\leq 1
. Using Young’s inequality and the degree bound (
2.3
), it follows from (
5.23
) that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d}{dt}\Xi_{t}([G,o])\right|

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\bigg(1+\|x_{o}\|_{H}^{2}+\|{y}_{o}\|_{H}^{2}+\sum_{v\sim o}\|x_{v}\|_{H}^{2}+\sum_{v\sim o}\|{y}_{v}\|_{H}^{2}\bigg)+2\|\nabla f([G,o],a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}

(5.24)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}+\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}

(5.25)

for a positive constant
C^{\prime}<\infty
not depending on
|t|\leq 1
. We claim that the right-hand side of (
5.25
) is integrable with respect to
\mu
. Indeed,
x,{y}\in\mathcal{H}
and by the mass-transport principle (
2.2
),

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|x_{v}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|x_{o}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])\leq D\|x\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}<\infty,

and similarly

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|{y}_{v}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|y_{o}\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])\leq D\|{y}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}<\infty,

thus the first term on the right-hand side of (
5.25
) is integrable. For the other three terms, (
2.16
), (
2.18
) and a further application of the mass-transport principle (
2.2
) give

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\|\nabla f([G,o],a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])<\infty

and

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])\hskip-1.70717pt=\hskip-1.99168pt\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}^{2}\mu(d[G,o])<\infty.

Hence the right-hand side of (
5.25
) is integrable.
Define the difference quotient

Q_{t}([G,o]):=\frac{\Xi_{t}([G,o])-\Xi_{0}([G,o])}{t},\quad t\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}.

(5.26)

Since, for every fixed
[G,o]
, the map
t\mapsto\Xi_{t}([G,o])
is differentiable by (
5.19
), the fundamental theorem of calculus yields

Q_{t}([G,o])=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d}{dr}\Xi_{r}([G,o])dr.

Hence, by (
5.25
),

|Q_{t}([G,o])|\leq\sup_{|r|\leq 1}\left|\frac{d}{dr}\Xi_{r}([G,o])\right|\leq M([G,o]),\quad\textrm{for all }t\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\},

where
M([G,o])
denotes the
\mu
-integrable right-hand side of (
5.25
).
Therefore, by the dominated convergence theorem,

\lim_{t\to 0}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}Q_{t}([G,o])\mu(d[G,o])=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\frac{d}{dt}\Xi_{t}([G,o])\bigg|_{t=0}\,\mu(d[G,o]).

(5.27)

Since by (
5.18
) and (
5.26
),

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}Q_{t}([G,o])\mu(d[G,o])=\frac{\Phi(x+ty)-\Phi(x)}{t},

(
5.27
) shows that differentiation under the integral sign for
\Phi
in (
3.1
) is justified. Consequently, by (
2.12
), (
2.13
), and (
5.19
),

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\Phi(x),y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\left\langle\nabla f([G,o],x_{o}),{y}_{o}\right\rangle_{H}\mu(d[G,o])

(5.28)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\psi([G,o,v],x_{o},x_{v}),{y}_{o}\right\rangle_{H}\mu(d[G,o])

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\left\langle\nabla_{2}\psi([G,o,v],x_{o},x_{v}),{y}_{v}\right\rangle_{H}\mu(d[G,o]).

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\left\langle\nabla f([G,o],x_{o}),{y}_{o}\right\rangle_{H}\mu(d[G,o])

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\left\langle\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],x_{o},x_{v}),{y}_{o}\right\rangle_{H}\mu(d[G,o])

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}\left\langle\nabla_{1}h([G,v,o],x_{v},x_{o}),{y}_{v}\right\rangle_{H}\mu(d[G,o]).

Now define the mass transport

T([G,o,v]):=\mathbf{1}_{\{o\sim v\}}\left\langle\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],x_{o},x_{v}),{y}_{o}\right\rangle_{H}.

(5.29)

By (
5.21
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

|T([G,o,v])|\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{o\sim v\}}\Big(L_{h}(\|x_{o}-a_{\ast}\|_{H}+\|x_{v}-a_{\ast}\|_{H})+\|\nabla_{1}h([G,o,v],a_{\ast},a_{\ast})\|_{H}\Big)\|{y}_{o}\|_{H}.

Using
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
, the degree bound (
2.3
), (
2.18
), and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we conclude that

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\in V(G)}|T([G,o,v])|\mu(d[G,o])<\infty.

(5.30)

Hence, the mass-transport principle applies (see
[
1
]
, Section 2), and gives

\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}T([G,o,v])\mu(d[G,o])=\int_{\mathcal{G}_{\ast}}\sum_{v\sim o}T([G,v,o])\mu(d[G,o]).

Thus, by (
5.29
), the last two terms in (
5.28
) are equal, and therefore (
5.28
)
reduces exactly to (
3.2
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.5

This section focuses on the proof of Theorem
3.5
. We start by proving two auxiliary lemmas.
Recall that
F
is the representative-player first-order operator, defined in (
3.3
).

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.5
and
2.10
hold, and let
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{K}
be a minimizer of
\Phi
over
\mathcal{K}
. Then

\langle F(x^{\ast}),z-x^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq 0,\quad\text{for all }z\in\mathcal{K}.

(6.1)

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathcal{K}
and define the perturbation
y:=z-x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Because
x^{\ast}
minimizes
\Phi
over the convex set
\mathcal{K}
, the function
t\mapsto\Phi(x^{\ast}+ty)
attains its minimum at
t=0
on
[0,1]
. Hence its right derivative at
0
is nonnegative, that is

\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{\ast}),y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\Phi(x^{\ast}+ty)-\Phi(x^{\ast})}{t}\geq 0.

By Proposition
3.2
,

\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{\ast}),y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle F(x^{\ast}),y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle F(x^{\ast}),z-x^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

This proves (
6.1
).
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.5
and
2.10
hold, and let
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{K}
. If

\langle F(x^{\ast}),z-x^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq 0,\quad\text{for all }z\in\mathcal{K},

(6.2)

then for
\mu
-a.e.
[G,o]
,

\left\langle F(x^{\ast})([G,o]),\,a-x^{\ast}([G,o])\right\rangle_{H}\geq 0,\quad\text{for all }a\in K.

(6.3)

Proof.

Let

A:=\left\{[G,o]\in\mathcal{G}_{\ast}:\exists a\in K\text{ such that }\left\langle F(x^{\ast})([G,o]),\,a-x^{\ast}([G,o])\right\rangle_{H}<0\right\}.

We show that
\mu(A)=0
. Suppose by contradiction that
\mu(A)>0
. Since
H
is separable and
K\subset H
is closed, there exists a countable dense subset
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset K
. Hence

A=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n},\quad A_{n}:=\left\{[G,o]:\left\langle F(x^{\ast})([G,o]),\,a_{n}-x^{\ast}([G,o])\right\rangle_{H}<0\right\}.

(6.4)

Therefore
\mu(A_{n})>0
for some
n
. Fix such an
n
, and define

z([G,o]):=\begin{cases}a_{n},&[G,o]\in A_{n},\\
x^{\ast}([G,o]),&[G,o]\notin A_{n}.\end{cases}

Since
a_{n}\in K
and
x^{\ast}([G,o])\in K
for
\mu
-a.e.
[G,o]
, we have
z\in\mathcal{K}
. Applying the variational inequality (
6.2
) to this choice of
z
, we obtain

0\leq\langle F(x^{\ast}),z-x^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{A_{n}}\left\langle F(x^{\ast})([G,o]),\,a_{n}-x^{\ast}([G,o])\right\rangle_{H}\mu(d[G,o]),

but the integrand is strictly negative on
A_{n}
by (
6.4
), which is a contradiction. Hence
\mu(A)=0
, proving (
6.3
).
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.5
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

By Lemma
6.1
, the implication (i)
\implies
(ii) holds.

Next, assume (ii), that is,

\langle F(x^{\ast}),z-x^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq 0,\quad\text{for all }z\in\mathcal{K}.

Hence Lemma
6.2
yields

\left\langle F(x^{\ast})([G,o]),\,a-x^{\ast}([G,o])\right\rangle_{H}\geq 0,\quad\text{for all }a\in K,\text{ for }\mu\text{-a.e. }[G,o],

(6.5)

Fix a rooted graph
[G,o]
in the full-measure set where (
6.5
) holds, and define

\Gamma_{[G,o]}(a):=f([G,o],a)+\sum_{v\sim o}h([G,o,v],a,x^{\ast}([G,v])),\quad a\in H.

(6.6)

By Assumption
3.4
, the map
a\mapsto\Gamma_{[G,o]}(a)
is convex, and by (
3.3
),

\nabla\Gamma_{[G,o]}(x^{\ast}([G,o]))=F(x^{\ast})([G,o]).

Therefore, by (
6.5
),

\left\langle\nabla\Gamma_{[G,o]}(x^{\ast}([G,o])),\,a-x^{\ast}([G,o])\right\rangle_{H}\geq 0,\quad\text{for all }a\in K.

(6.7)

It follows from (
6.7
) that
x^{\ast}([G,o])
minimizes
\Gamma_{[G,o]}
over
K
(see
[
4
]
, Chapter 17, Proposition 17.51, Chapter 27, Proposition 27.8). Equivalently, recalling (
2.8
) and (
6.6
) we have,

J_{o}^{G}(x^{\ast,G})\leq J_{o}^{G}(a,x^{\ast,G}_{-o}),\quad\text{for all }a\in K.

Hence
x^{\ast}
is a quenched Nash equilibrium, so (iii) holds.

Conversely, assume that (iii) holds, i.e., let
x^{\ast}\in\mathcal{K}
be a quenched Nash equilibrium. Then for
\mu
-a.e.
[G,o]
,

J_{o}^{G}(x^{\ast,G})\leq J_{o}^{G}(a,x^{\ast,G}_{-o}),\quad\text{for all }a\in K.

By (
2.8
) and (
6.6
),

J_{o}^{G}(x^{\ast,G})=\Gamma_{[G,o]}(x^{\ast}([G,o]))\leq\Gamma_{[G,o]}(a)=J_{o}^{G}(a,x_{-o}^{\ast,G}),\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathbb{R}.

Since this holds for
\mu
-a.e.
[G,o]
,
x^{\ast}
is a quenched Nash equilibrium.
∎
