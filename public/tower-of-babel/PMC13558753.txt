An expert-level vision-language model for multitask diagnostic morphology in clinical laboratories
Authors: Han Liu, Yuhan Zhu, Shunbo Li, Qinli Pu, Liping Yang, Weixian Chen, Juan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microscopic morphology in clinical laboratories is expertise-intensive, variable across observers, and difficult to scale. We present Lingjian, a vision-language model tailored for laboratory morphology. Built on Qwen3-VL-8B and trained via multistage domain adaptation on more than 400,000 laboratory images with paired text and grounding supervision, Lingjian unifies image comprehension, cell identification, morphology description, and interpretation, and target localization. Across public benchmarks and cross-domain test sets, Lingjian shows robust multitask performance. On China’s National Center for Clinical Laboratories External Quality Assessment (EQA, 2021–2025), it reached 93.0% overall accuracy, outperforming a human expert group (78.1%) and strong general models (e.g., Gemini-3 Pro, 75.3%). In a 120-case multireader multicase reader study, Lingjian's assistance improved junior-reader sensitivity for abnormal screening from 69.7% to 91.7% while maintaining high specificity (85.0% to 89.5%). Model weights and evaluation resources are released to support reproducibility.

Introduction

Morphological examination is a core technique in laboratory medicine. When clinical specimens (e.g., peripheral blood smears, bone marrow smears, body fluids, and microorganisms) are observed under a microscope, cellular morphology, structure, and distribution patterns are analyzed to support clinical diagnoses, risk stratification, and treatment monitoring. However, such examinations are highly dependent on the subjective experience of the practitioners1, resulting in prolonged training periods, significant interobserver subjectivity, and limited consistency; these issues are particularly notable in primary care settings with limited accessibility. These challenges are even more pronounced in multisample morphological image analysis (for example, practical deployments of digital morphology approaches and AI assistants remain exploratory)2.

In recent years, multimodal language models (MLMs), especially vision-language models (VLMs), have exhibited remarkable general image interpretation capabilities, leading to the development of specialized VLMs tailored for clinical and biomedical applications. The development of multimodal biomedical models (e.g., BiomedCLIP, PLIP) and medical conversational vision-language models (e.g., LLaVA-Med) has markedly advanced cross-modal understanding between medical images and text, as well as visual question answering (VQA) performance3–5. Nevertheless, in the clinical laboratory domain—especially for microscopic morphological recognition, discrimination of staining variability, and small-object detection—neither general VLMs nor specialized biomedical and medical VLMs have achieved the fundamental recognition and understanding capabilities required for practical deployment. Moreover, in this domain, a unified multimodal foundation model and systematized data resources have not yet been established, and there is no sustainable “data–model” iterative mechanism5. Compared with more mature “ecosystems” of “model–data–tools–benchmarks–applications” in radiology and pathology, a substantial gap persists in this context3,4.

Historically and in mainstream practice, convolutional neural network (CNN)-based approaches have predominated in morphology assessments in laboratory settings. Public datasets (such as the Brief peripheral blood cell image corpus and Raabin-WBC datasets) have facilitated the establishment of baseline models and reproducible experiments for fundamental tasks such as white blood cell classification and segmentation6,7. For detection and localization, traditional methods typically rely on two-stage or one-stage detectors (e.g., Faster R-CNN and the YOLO series of models) to recognize small targets such as cells, granules, and parasites. However, these approaches typically suffer from the “single-task silo” limitation: they focus solely on classification, detection, or segmentation in isolation, lack in-depth domain knowledge and reasoning capabilities, and fail to provide verbal explanations or interactive dialog functions. As a result, they struggle to meet the integrated needs of clinical teaching, quality control, and research in the “recognition–explanation–localization–interaction” framework6–8. In addition, many methods are tailored to a single smear or data source and therefore adapt poorly to the complex domain-shift challenges posed by multismear rotation, device heterogeneity, staining condition variations, and interlaboratory variability in real clinical environments6,7. Extending ensembles of such task-specific models to multitask or multiscenario deployments may demand additional architectural and training design decisions as well as careful optimization; in particular, when tasks and scenarios differ substantially in data distribution, task interference (negative transfer) may arise, and incremental updates may suffer from catastrophic forgetting9,10.

To address these gaps, we propose “Lingjian”, an all-in-one VLM for clinical laboratory medicine (Lingjian in Chinese means smart test). Within a single framework, the model integrates image understanding, cell identification, morphological analysis, and key target localization (bounding box (bbox)/grounding), supports interaction via natural language instructions, and produces interpretable analytical outputs. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

We conducted the first systematic evaluation of state-of-the-art vision-language models in the context of morphological analyses in clinical laboratory settings.

We developed Lingjian, the first VLM to achieve expert-level proficiency via a multistage knowledge injection paradigm in the context of morphological analyses in clinical laboratory settings. The proposed model integrates dialog, localization, and reasoning functions into a unified framework.

We provide an open-source model; namely, we have publicly released the model weights and evaluation scripts to foster further development.

Our data are organized into four classes, each governed by a distinct train/test rule. Class I consists of public datasets with official test sets; the official test sets are used for testing, and the remaining non-test data are used for training. Class II consists of public datasets used only for testing and never for training. Class III consists of public datasets without official test sets and is split 9:1 into training and test subsets (seed = 42). Class IV consists of one proprietary training set (~100,000 images), used entirely for training, plus three independent evaluation test sets—NCCL EQA (2021–2025), MRMC, and MPB-394—that are completely excluded from all training stages and used only for final evaluation.

Training therefore, uses the non-test portion of Class I, none of Class II, the training split of Class III, and the proprietary training set in Class IV. No test sample was used for training, validation, parameter tuning, or early stopping. The validation set used for monitoring and early stopping was split from the training data only and contains no overlap with any test set. We report results on the corresponding test sets across Class I–IV. Unless otherwise specified, inference used temperature 0, Top-P 1, and a maximum output length of 1,024 tokens with Lingjian-1.0-Chat. Classification tasks were evaluated with a closed-set prompt that casts each sample as a single-choice problem (Supplementary Note 5). For grounding, responses followed the “coordinate textualization” format and were parsed into absolute pixel coordinates, with “/bbox” appended to the user input.

On public datasets, we also compare Lingjian with recent representative methods. We report accuracy for classification tasks across Class I–III, with macro-F1 additionally reported for Class I–II where comparable; for Class III, Table 3 emphasizes accuracy to facilitate comparison with prior work. Detection and localization performance is reported as F1@0.5 under a unified protocol. AP/mAP-style ranking-based metrics are not directly applicable because Lingjian outputs discrete bounding-box coordinates without confidence scores.

Test data that are drawn from the same distribution or a distribution similar to the training data are referred to as in-distribution (ID) data. Test data that are not involved in training and exhibit significant domain differences are referred to as out-of-distribution (OOD) data.

External quality assessment

To evaluate the performance of the model in real-world clinical scenarios and compare it with human expertise, we conducted an assessment using external quality assessment (EQA) data organized by the National Center for Clinical Laboratories (NCCL) under China’s National Health Commission. The EQA program is a nationally authoritative, annual proficiency testing scheme for clinical laboratories across China. In this evaluation, morphology-related items included blood cell morphology (30 images per year), urine sediment morphology (15 images per year), and parasite morphology (15 images per year). The standard workflow of this program is as follows: After receiving the test images, each participating laboratory submits its responses at the laboratory level, after which the NCCL provides sample-level feedback in a binary “yes/no” format indicating whether each response is correct. Because NCCL EQA images can be publicly obtained, they may be present in large-scale web-crawled pretraining or post-training corpora used by general-purpose models. We therefore explicitly treat this benchmark as a public test set and control for leakage on our side: we conducted image-level decontamination between the EQA set and the Lingjian training corpus using the imagededup similarity search followed by manual verification, and did not find any matches above a predefined similarity threshold. With respect to proprietary baselines (e.g., Gemini-3 Pro and GPT-5.1) and other third-party models, their training corpora are not fully auditable; consequently, their reported EQA accuracies should be interpreted as potentially optimistic upper bounds if any benchmark images are seen during training. To promote transparency and reproducibility, we made the evaluation scripts and image set publicly available on GitHub (newfyu/EQA2021-2025), enabling replication and benchmarking of future models. Owing to the authority and standardized design of this EQA program, the dataset and evaluation protocol serve as a reliable benchmark for assessing VLMs in laboratory morphology tasks.

The original NCCL EQA set was designed at the laboratory level and functions as an “open-book proficiency test” that allows access to reference materials and collective decision-making; in routine EQA practice, a score above 80% is considered passing, and our laboratory has historically achieved scores of 95–100% under this workflow. However, such a laboratory-style protocol is not directly comparable to a single model’s stand-alone response. To obtain a controlled human baseline under a unified protocol, we adopted an individual, closed-book setting: Participants were divided into four groups (from intern students to senior experts), with five participants in each group. Each participant completed one session per day (1 hour) for five days and answered the full 300-item set. Group performance was reported as the average score within each group. Here, MLS denotes a medical laboratory scientist. We note that this closed-book protocol is intentionally stricter than routine laboratory EQA and should be interpreted as a standardized lower-bound baseline for individual proficiency under time constraints rather than as an estimate of achievable laboratory-level performance. For model testing, each model received only the provided image and task prompt (including predefined options where applicable); web search, retrieval-augmented generation, external reference lookup, and the Lingjian-Agent retrieval layer were not enabled.

As illustrated in Fig. 4B, under the strict “closed-book” testing conditions, the domain-specific Lingjian model exhibited exceptional morphological recognition capabilities, achieving an overall accuracy of 93.0% (with 95% confidence intervals shown as error bars; Wilson score intervals for model accuracies and participant-level t intervals for human cohorts, n = 5 per cohort). These results are significantly better than those of all the comparison groups: the proposed model outperformed state-of-the-art general multimodal large language models (Gemini-3 Pro: 75.3%; GPT-5.1: 43.7%; Qwen-3: 40.3%) as well as the human expert group (super MLS: 78.1%). Notably, Gemini-3 Pro showed impressive potential among the general models, performing at a level between Senior MLS (72.3%) and MLS (65.1%) practitioners. In contrast, despite their strong generalizability, GPT-5.1 and Qwen-3 performed comparably to or slightly below the level of intern students (MLS Student: 45.2%) in this specialized task, highlighting the necessity of domain adaptation for medical subfields.Fig. 4Performance benchmark on the National Center for Clinical Laboratories External Quality Assessment (NCCL EQA) 2021–2025 dataset.A shows the evaluation workflow, B shows the overall accuracy ranking with 95% confidence intervals, and C shows the heatmap of accuracy across subspecialties.

While Lingjian exhibits a clear advantage in individual comparisons, importantly, in traditional laboratory-level EQAs (which allow open-book resources and collective decision-making), laboratory composite scores typically exceed 95%. These findings indicate that although Lingjian has overcome the limitations of individual human experts, a marginal gap remains compared with human collective intelligence, suggesting room for further optimization.

To further evaluate the capabilities of the proposed model, we compared its performance across three subspecialties: blood cells, urine sediment, and parasites (Fig. 4B, C). Lingjian consistently performed well across all three subspecialties (blood cells: 91.3%; urine sediment: 92.0%; parasites: 97.3%). This uniformity suggests that the model has overcome the “specialization bias” often observed in human experts and demonstrates comprehensive morphological diagnostic capabilities.

Gemini-3 Pro performed remarkably well in parasite recognition (88.0%), outperforming some human experts; however, its accuracy decreased to 65.3% for urine sediment. This discrepancy may be attributed to training data distribution biases or the high degree of morphological variability inherent in urine sediment samples.

The performance of the human participants clearly increased in a stepwise manner with increasing experience level (Fig. 4C). However, performance fluctuated across subspecialties. Notably, even expert performance in parasite recognition was relatively limited (Super MLS: 74.7%). This is primarily attributable to the significant decline in the incidence of parasitic diseases in recent years, resulting in a scarcity of clinical samples and reduced practical exposure for clinicians, thereby hindering the accumulation of experience. This leads to unbalanced skill development across subspecialties, an unavoidable occupational characteristic of human experts.

In contrast, Lingjian (red diamond) consistently aligns closely with the central diagonal in the extreme upper-right region. Since the diagonal represents a perfect balance between two dimensions (x = y), Lingjian’s position signifies not only optimal numerical accuracy but also a high degree of balanced proficiency. The proposed model effectively addresses the knowledge barriers imposed by human role division, exhibiting the traits of an unbiased, “all-around” morphological expert.

The data points representing Gemini-3 Pro (orange diamond) are clustered near those representing the Senior and Super MLS groups in most charts, demonstrating that state-of-the-art general LLMs can be used to solve complex clinical problems under zero-shot or few-shot conditions.

Even within the Super MLS group, the recognition abilities for specific morphologies differ among individuals because of individual domain expertise. Conversely, AI models provide stable, reproducible, and standardized diagnostic suggestions. In conclusion, the results of the NCCL EQA evaluation strongly confirm that Lingjian not only surpasses individual human experts in terms of morphological recognition accuracy but also effectively overcomes the “skill silos” caused by clinical subspecialty division and the “experience gaps” resulting from changing disease spectra. The model’s superior performance in this objective, rigorous, national-level external quality assessment demonstrates its applicability for deployment in real-world clinical scenarios.

Real-world multireader multicase (MRMC) reader study

In routine clinical slide reviews, cases typically do not involve premarked regions of interest (ROIs), and readers must proactively search a large field of view to identify subtle abnormal cues. To assess whether Lingjian can provide practical assistance to junior readers under this “no-ROI-hint” setting, we conducted a real-world multireader multicase (MRMC) human–AI study across four routine microscopy contexts: peripheral blood smear, bone marrow smear, urine sediment microscopy, and stool microscopy. We included N = 120 retrospective cases, each comprising nine images (one low-power field and eight high-power fields) and a brief clinical history; the abnormal rate was 35.0% (42/120), and the reference standard was the final clinical report. Three junior medical laboratory scientists independently completed case interpretation using a unified interface. Each case followed a “read-first, then (optionally) AI revise” workflow: readers first submitted an initial diagnosis without AI; then, at the case level, the system randomized (1:1) whether Lingjian assistance was available, stratified by specimen type and ground truth. MPB-394: Extreme morphological stress testing and long-tail knowledge evaluation

The core challenge in morphology tasks in clinical laboratory settings involves managing “long-tail” samples within clinical practice. EQA samples predominantly consist of common clinical types, making it difficult to evaluate a model’s proficiency in identifying extremely rare or subtle morphological subtypes. To address this, we constructed the MPB-394 (Morphological Precision Benchmark) test set, which was designed to define the performance ceiling of AI models for clinical morphological tasks through a highly rigorous protocol.

MPB-394 encompasses 197 morphological categories (the detailed directory is provided in Supplementary Table 2), comprising a total of 394 microscopy images. Benchmark success requires the model to distinguish between features with extremely high similarity, such as accurately differentiating between the ring forms of Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax or precisely distinguishing between lymphoblasts and myeloblasts.

A correct response is not defined by a single classification label. Instead, the model must hit multiple morphological facts simultaneously, including cell morphology, developmental stage, subtype classification, and specific structures (e.g., inclusion bodies and Auer rods). The model must report these facts spontaneously without prompts; a sample is considered correctly identified only if all relevant key facts are hit.

The strict "zero-tolerance” cross-source dual-sample rule (2-out-of-2 rule) requires that each category consist of two independent samples. These samples are strictly selected from different sources to simulate real-world clinical heterogeneity. A category is only marked as “mastered” if the images from both sources satisfy the requirements for “comprehensive fact-hitting.” This rule effectively eliminates the possibility of random guessing or local overfitting, ensuring the absolute stability of the model’s knowledge system. We evaluated Lingjian and mainstream general VLMs on MPB-394. To quantify the knowledge acquisition capability of the models, we sorted categories by sample count and calculated a rolling mean (window size = 50). The Y-axis represents the average mastery of each category at specific data scales. The uncertainty gap (the difference between image-level and category-level accuracy) reveals a transitional state where the model recognizes isolated features but lacks stability. The smoothed curve for strict pass serves as the baseline for evaluating the “average performance” of the model at any given data volume. We observed an understanding threshold at ~50 samples and a mastery threshold at >300 samples. This value has an important reference value for the number of samples needed to compensate for specific features in the future.

The efficiency gap represents the deviation of specific categories from the model’s average data utilization capacity and is calculated as follows: Y = Actual Score − Expected Score. Here, the actual score is the specific test result for a category (1 for pass, 0 for fail), and the expected score is predicted by the baseline trend in Fig. 7C on the basis of that category’s training count. Categories with Y > 0 are interpreted as “fast learners”, representing distinctive features that are mastered even with minimal data. Categories with Y < 0 remain “stubborn samples” that are challenging despite large data volumes. This indicates categorical ambiguity (e.g., continuous blastic–immature transitions), where even human experts face issues such as high inherent error rates and extreme semantic similarity of microscopic features that are exceptionally small and easily confounded. For instance, small inclusion bodies often present significant identification challenges, as they can be easily confused with other intracellular granules or staining artifacts. These results suggest that the performance ceiling of the morphological AI model is determined not only by the data scale but also by the inherent semantic distinctiveness of the images.

Nevertheless, the analysis of these challenging samples helps explain the model’s consistently good performance through targeted iterations.

Discussion

This study introduces Lingjian, a laboratory-domain vision-language model designed to support routine microscopic morphology workflows, including cell and microorganism identification, fine-grained morphological description and interpretation, and evidence-linked target localization. Here, “all-in-one” refers to unifying these core tasks across representative specimen types evaluated in this work (e.g., peripheral blood and bone marrow smears, urine sediment microscopy, and parasitology samples), rather than implying exhaustive coverage of every specialized laboratory assay.

Across standardized public benchmarks and heterogeneous cross-domain scenarios, Lingjian-1.0 shows stable multitask performance, indicating that systematic domain post-training can substantially strengthen general-purpose VLMs for microscopy. In the National Center for Clinical Laboratories External Quality Assessment (EQA, 2021–2025), Lingjian achieved 93.0% overall accuracy, exceeding the human expert group (78.1%) and strong general models (e.g., Gemini-3 Pro, 75.3%). In a realistic 120-case multireader multicase reader study without ROI hints, Lingjian assistance improved junior-reader sensitivity for abnormal screening (69.7% to 91.7%) while maintaining high specificity (85.0% to 89.5%), supporting its role as a safety-oriented decision-support tool in routine microscopy workflows. Ablation studies further confirm that each training stage and supervision signal contribute meaningfully to final performance (Supplementary Note 4, Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).

Compared with classic CNN-based systems that typically address isolated tasks (classification, detection, or segmentation), Lingjian integrates recognition, explanation, interaction, and localization within a single model, enabling users to query visual evidence and clinical meaning in natural language. This unified design also helps mitigate domain shift from staining variability, device heterogeneity, and interlaboratory differences by leveraging shared representations learned across tasks and specimen types.

From an implementation perspective, Lingjian can be integrated into existing laboratory pipelines for preliminary screening, suspicious-area localization, auxiliary counting, and reporting consistency checks, and it can facilitate education and training by providing step-by-step explanations grounded in visual evidence. The model’s interactive behavior (including bidirectional visual prompting and context-aware refinement) further enables collaborative review for small or atypical findings and may accelerate data curation and quality control workflows.

Several limitations should be noted. First, scaling effects suggest that larger backbones and additional high-quality training data may further improve performance, particularly for fine-grained and long-tail categories. Second, although the autoregressive objective used in our pipeline is inherently self-supervised, the current training setup does not incorporate dedicated semi-supervised or self-supervised objectives; future work will evaluate such strategies for challenging microscopy categories11 within a curated “data–model” loop for model-assisted screening, annotation, and instruction-data generation. Third, rare morphologies and specialized settings (e.g., certain bone marrow subtypes and uncommon parasites) remain challenging and motivate targeted data enrichment. Fourth, because Lingjian-1.0-Chat was optimized through full-parameter domain post-training for laboratory morphology, general abilities inherited from Qwen3-VL may be degraded, and the model may fail on some routine non-morphology tasks and natural images; it should therefore be used as a specialized morphology model rather than as a general chatbot. Within morphology tasks, it remains able to follow domain-related instructions and produce natural-language or bbox outputs, although its response style is relatively fixed. Finally, optimizing visual-language reasoning (potentially with reinforcement learning) is an important direction for future work. In ongoing internal development, we are piloting a model-assisted data curation loop in which Lingjian-1.0 preannotates newly collected samples, senior experts audit and adjudicate difficult cases, and mismatched outputs are converted into preference-learning pairs to support frequent (approximately monthly) model updates; systematic results will be reported in the next-generation release.

Overall, Lingjian establishes a unified evaluation benchmark and an expert-level laboratory morphology VLM, and we release model weights and evaluation resources to support reproducibility and continued progress in this field.

Methods

This study developed a laboratory-scale dataset for image-text matching and localization tasks, consisting of over 300,000 images from public open-source repositories and an additional 100,000 images from internal proprietary collections. Since only existing, fully de-identified retrospective data were used, with no direct patient contact and no access to identifiable information, the study posed no risks to privacy or welfare. The study protocol, including the use of retrospective, fully de-identified image data and the MRMC reader study, was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical and Pharmaceutical College (Approval No. 2025-03) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All model training and testing were performed within a secure institutional AI infrastructure using retrospective, anonymized data only. The requirement for informed consent for the retrospective image data was waived by the same Ethics Committee because the study used existing, fully de-identified materials. The same approval also covered the MRMC reader study; the participating readers took part under institutional research oversight, and the need for written informed consent was waived by the approving Ethics Committee because the study evaluated case-interpretation performance without collecting identifiable personal health data.

The data were organized into four dataset classes (Class I–IV) according to data availability and split protocol. The detailed partition rules are summarized in Fig. 3, and all training, validation, and testing procedures described below follow that same partition scheme.

Classification of the public datasets

The proprietary dataset was meticulously assembled through a two-stage process. First, keywords and exemplar seed images for more than 1000 entities across domains, including clinical hematology, hematological disorders, body fluid morphology, bacteria, fungi, parasites, culture plates, and immunofluorescence, were extracted from internal laboratory testing workflows and educational resources. To evaluate the model’s proficiency in precision morphology applications, specifically with respect to fine-grained categories and long-tail subtypes that are often rare in routine clinical practice, we introduced the MPB-394 (Morphological Precision Benchmark) test set. This test set encompasses 197 morphological categories, comprising a total of 394 microscopy images. The grounding data are first unified to an absolute coordinate system and converted to a textual representation with a “short description + (category, coordinates) × N” format. For each sample containing bounding boxes, we design a diverse questioning system: First, we pose comprehensive questions and require the model to provide the positions of all key targets in the image at once; then, we construct category-specific questions to guide the model in category-aware selective localization in multitarget scenarios; and finally, we add negative questions that explicitly ask the model about nonexistent targets. Supplementary Note 1 provides typical examples of the grounding data format. We uniformly add a “/bbox” marker at the end of the user instructions to explicitly require the model to output bounding boxes.

To support the injection of morphological knowledge in phase two, we used a pure text corpus in this stage. The data sources were primarily open-source morphological textbooks, textbooks/manuals for interpreting test results, and open-source educational resources (such as WHO/CDC materials, OER textbooks, and NCBI Bookshelf/StatPearls chapters), from which context-only text paragraphs that are publicly reusable were collected with strict adherence to the permissions of each source.

All the texts were deduplicated, somewhat standardized, and then split into paragraph-level samples with an average length of 2000 tokens per sample, resulting in a total of approximately 20.35 million tokens across more than 10,000 samples (Chinese:English ≈ 5:1). These data were used for (i) injecting morphological knowledge and test report interpretation knowledge and (ii) providing a knowledge background for the automatic expansion of long descriptions. QA dataset (stage3_qa_dataset)

The stage 3_qa_dataset is used in Stage 3 to improve the instruction follow-up and dialog question–answering capabilities of the model. In particular, we ensured that the responses of the model were consistent with the provided images. This dataset was generated entirely automatically from the Stage 2 data, specifically from the long descriptions (stage2_longcaption_dataset) described in the long-caption dataset description above and the plain text corpus (stage2_text_dataset) described in the text-corpus description above. This dataset was constructed in batches using Qwen2.5-72B with fixed prompts (Supplementary Note 3).

On the basis of a “3–5 questions per image” strategy, we randomly covered four dimensions: identification (cells/structures/inclusions); morphological features (e.g., nuclear shape, chromatin, nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, and granules); technical information (sample type and staining method); and clinical/pathological significance directly supported by the image. The question types include open-ended questions, single-choice questions, and multiple-choice questions; the questions provide spatial references (e.g., top-left/bottom-right/within the bounding box), and distractors are derived from similar concepts in the same domain.

The output follows a unified JSON schema (id, language, type, question, options, answer, reasoning, etc.), with automatic validation of the structural legality and key constraints (answerable on the basis of the question alone, the dimension coverage, and the reasonableness of the distractors). Additionally, senior laboratory technicians conducted sample audits to iteratively refine the templates and examples.

Ultimately, approximately 200,000+ QA samples were formed (all generated from Stage 2 data). These data were used for the fine-tuning of the instruction/dialog component in Stage 3.

Data augmentation strategies

To increase model generalizability, we apply lightweight visual augmentations to the stage1_pretrain_dataset and stage2_longcaption_dataset data, including random cropping (0.8–1.2 ratio), scaling (0.5–1.5 factor), and minor color perturbations (brightness 0.1, contrast 0.05, saturation 0.05, hue 0.05), along with horizontal, vertical, and bidirectional flipping. In the text, directional words (e.g., “top, bottom”, “left”, “right”) are synchronously replaced, and a protected word whitelist (e.g., “neutrophilic left shift”) is established to avoid misreplacement. The localization data are not augmented or normalized to maintain coordinate consistency.

Model and training overview

Lingjian-1.0 is based on Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct12 and consists of the ViT, Aligner MLP, and LLM components. The ViT segments images into patches and encodes them as visual tokens; the Aligner MLP maps visual features to the LLM’s semantic space; and the LLM serves as the decoder and is responsible for generating the descriptions, performing the question—answering task, and coordinating the textual outputs.

Compared with domain-specific models trained from scratch, general VLMs that have undergone large-scale pretraining possess robust commonsense reasoning and general understanding capabilities. At the same data scale, general foundation models outperform specialized models in multidomain tasks (especially in the microscopic imaging domain)5. Moreover, general model architectures are well optimized for inference tasks and more convenient for deployment. Training a model solely with domain knowledge makes it difficult to exploit real-world knowledge. Therefore, starting with a general model is a more pragmatic approach. We adopt the general foundation model and a domain post-training approach, during which we gradually inject laboratory domain knowledge.

In Stage 1, the image–text pairs with short descriptions from stage 1_pretrain_dataset are used for alignment training, the LLM backbone is frozen, and only the ViT and Aligner components are updated to quickly align the visual distributions to the general semantic space.

In Stage 2, stage2_longcaption_dataset, stage2_text_dataset, and stage2_grounding_dataset are jointly used for multitask training, and the LLM module is unfrozen while the ViT and Aligner components are continuously optimized. This approach enables the model to acquire fine-grained expressions, background knowledge, and bbox-level localization information.

In Stage 3, stage 3_qa_dataset is primarily used to enhance the instruction-following and multiturn dialog capabilities of the model. In this stage, small amounts of Stage 1 and Stage 2 samples are mixed to mitigate model forgetting while maintaining the stable recognition and localization performance of the model. A diagram of the three-stage training process is shown in Fig. 2.

Stage 1: Domain-continued pretraining

The training data are sourced from the stage1_pretrain_dataset described above. We freeze all the parameters of the LLM and only train the vision encoder and alignment projector to reduce computational overhead and overfitting risks while ensuring that the language component maintains its general expressive capabilities.

This stage involves the implementation of the standard autoregressive approach for supervised image–text alignment. In particular, given an image and a short descriptive sentence, we minimize the cross-entropy loss of the next token, with only the ViT and Aligner layers updated on the basis of the gradients. The learning rate is set to 1e−5.

On the basis of the image–text pairs, the model learns domain-specific visual representations while the LLM parameters remain unchanged. This approach ensures the stable performance of the vision-language model, which is subsequently used in the fine-grained and localization training processes.

Stage 2: Fine-grained description and localization supervision

In this stage, three types of datasets are employed: stage 2_longcaption_dataset, stage 2_text_dataset, and stage2_grounding_dataset. In this stage, we unfreeze the LLM parameters and fully train the Aligner and ViT components, thereby realizing fine-grained bidirectional language–vision coupling while optimizing the following three objective functions:

The loss term Lcap performs autoregressive supervised learning on long descriptive texts from stage2_longcaption_dataset, enhancing the model’s fine-grained image description capabilities.

The loss term Ltxt conducts supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on text paragraphs from stage2_text_dataset, injecting rich morphological domain knowledge into the model.

The loss term Lbox performs supervised learning on coordinate annotations from stage2_grounding_dataset. A “coordinate textualization” strategy is adopted, with bounding box coordinates (xmin, ymin, xmax, and ymax) embedded as integer absolute coordinates into the response texts and supervision performed at the token level; for multitarget scenarios, aggregated outputs can be organized by category or spatial position order. The joint optimization objective is 7D2L = Lcap + Ltxt + Lbox.

After training in this stage, the resulting model is named Lingjian-1.0-Morph. This model possesses fine-grained morphological understanding and description capabilities, as well as precise bounding box-level object localization abilities.

Stage 3: Chat fine-tuning

In this phase, stage 3_qa_dataset is predominantly used to update all the model parameters. A dialog system, which includes multiple-turn interactions, is implemented. A supervised fine-tuning (SFT) strategy is adopted, incorporating both single-turn and multiturn dialogs. The formats of the questions are diverse and include open-ended, single-choice, and multiple-choice questions. Importantly, the question prompts retain spatial references (such as top-left, bottom-right, or within a specified region). In the context of multiturn dialogs, preventing the responses of previous turns from being disclosed in subsequent prompts is imperative to closely mirror typical scenarios in educational and clinical environments.

Associations between targets and evaluation metrics are next established. The training objectives include adherence to instructional guidance and compliance with statement-based directives. With the validation set, training efficacy is monitored using ROUGE-L against the reference responses. The model developed during this stage is designated Lingjian-1.0-Chat.

Training implementation details

The base model is Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct. The main software frameworks include MS-Swift 3.7.2, PyTorch 2.7.1, and Transformers 4.52.4. All training and experiments were conducted on four NVIDIA A800 80 GB GPUs.

For Stage 1, we employ a cosine annealing learning rate with 200 warmup steps. The parameters of the LLM are frozen, and only the ViT and Aligner are updated. Training is performed for 2 epochs. Note that the data were augmented by a factor of 5 in each epoch (augmentation described above). The learning rates for the ViT and Aligner components are both 1e-5. The maximum sequence length is 8,192 tokens, with images allowed up to 802,816 pixels; the images are scaled down if this threshold is exceeded. We enable the padding-free option and the Liger kernel. The DeepSpeed strategy uses ZeRO-2. The optimizer is AdamW. The batch size per card is 2, with a gradient accumulation of 2, resulting in a total effective batch size of 16.

For Stages 2 and 3, we unfreeze the ViT, Aligner, and LLM components. The learning rates are 1e-6 for the ViT and 1e-5 for the LLM. Training is performed for 2 epochs while maintaining an augmentation factor of 5. The maximum sequence length is 12,000 tokens. The other configuration parameters are largely consistent with those used in Stage 1.

We removed 1000 samples from the training set to establish a validation set using a fixed random seed approach; this validation set contains no overlap with any held-out test set in the Class I–IV partition scheme described above. ROUGE-L serves as the primary monitoring metric. Early stopping is typically triggered at the end of the second epoch.

Evaluation

Given the lack of standardized benchmarks in the clinical laboratory field, we systematically constructed a standardized test set covering most clinical laboratory scenarios on the basis of the datasets described above to systematically evaluate the morphological recognition and localization capabilities of the proposed model.

We followed the Class I–IV partition scheme summarized in Fig. 3. Evaluation was performed on the corresponding held-out or official test sets for each class, including the three independent Class IV evaluation sets (NCCL EQA, MRMC, and MPB-394).

For Class I–III classification, we used a closed-set evaluation adapter. We report accuracy across Class I–III and macro-F1 for Class I–II where comparable, whereas Table 3 emphasizes accuracy for Class III to facilitate comparison with prior work. For detection and localization tasks, we report F1@0.5 (IoU = 0.5); AP/mAP-style ranking-based metrics are not directly applicable because Lingjian outputs discrete bounding-box coordinates without calibrated confidence scores. For free-text benchmarks, we used benchmark-specific scoring rules. For NCCL EQA 2021–2025, we report sample-level binary accuracy against the official reference answers. For MPB-394, we report category-level accuracy under the strict 2-out-of-2 rule. For the MRMC reader study, human reviewers graded predictions against the reference-standard final clinical report, and we report sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F1.

Implementation details of the closed-set evaluation adapter, including option mapping, output parsing, and the exact prompt template, are provided in Supplementary Note 5. Briefly, each sample is cast as a single-choice task over the dataset-specific label space, and outputs that cannot be mapped back to a valid label are counted as incorrect.

For bounding-box tasks, textual coordinates are parsed into absolute image coordinates, matched to ground-truth boxes by one-to-one assignment within each image and class at IoU=0.5, and summarized as F1@0.5. AP/mAP-style ranking-based metrics are not directly applicable because Lingjian does not output confidence scores.
