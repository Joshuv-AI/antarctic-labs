Performance evaluation of deep learning based YOLOv5 and YOLOv8 models for real time breast cancer detection in mammographic images
Authors: Nibedita Sahoo, Aurobindo Kar, Vineeta Khemchandani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer detection remains a significant challenge in medical diagnostics. Traditional diagnostic methods are time-consuming, unable to detect complex patterns in medical images, and achieve only moderate accuracy with high false-positive rates. The study aims to identify the most effective and efficient algorithm for clinical application that reduces the rates of false positives and false negatives Various image segmentation methods are available, including U-Net (Ronneberger et al. in International Conference on Medical image computing and computer-assisted intervention, Springer International Publishing, Cham, 2015), Mask R-CNN (He et al. in Proceedings of the IEEE international conference on computer vision, 2017), fully convolutional networks (FCNs) (Long et al. in Proceedings of the IEEE conference on computer vision and pattern recognition, 2015), and SegNet (Badrinarayanan et al. in IEEE Trans Pattern Anal Mach Intell, 39:2481–2495, 2017). However, some of these have complex architectures, require extensive training time, and consume significant computational resources. Beyond these methods, other well-cited studies include CNN & context aggregation, transformer-based segmentation, promptable models, and medical-focused architectures. However, all of these still struggle with issues like long-range dependencies caused by the locality of convolutions, architecture design, high computational cost, require large amounts of pre-training data, not always being optimal for domain-specific tasks, high compute and memory demands, and the need for fine-tuning for specialised high-accuracy applications that are frequently trained on relatively small datasets. Therefore, this study employs YOLO, which is a DL-based one-step object detection algorithm that greatly enhances speed, accuracy, and recognition across various categories of image processing and video processing. It also lies in its ability to perform real-time object detection and its time-sensitive applications. This study assesses the effectiveness of two cutting-edge deep learning based object detection algorithms, YOLOv5 and YOLOv8, for early breast cancer screening and detection. A total of 2,620 digitised mammography images from film, including normal, benign, and malignant cases with verified pathology, are included in the original Digital Database for Screening Mammography (DDSM) dataset. Improving patient outcomes and early detection rates depends on the efficacy of these algorithms.YOLOv5 and YOLOv8 are used in this study to detect breast cancer lesions, and their performance metrics are compared. The results for YOLOv5 show an F1 score of 0.97, a precision-recall score of 0.97, and confidence scores for precision and recall of 0.67 and 0.98, respectively. The results for YOLOv5 show an F1 score of 0.97, a precision-recall score of 0.97, and confidence scores of 0.67 and 0.98 for precision and recall, respectively. The mAP of YOLOv8 is 0.99. Therefore, YOLOv8 outperforms YOLOv5 across all assessed parameters, indicating that it is more suitable for clinical use in automated breast cancer screening and detection. With YOLOv8’s improved recall and precision, there may be a greater likelihood of accurate, early detection of breast cancer, thereby reducing false positives and negatives. Future research will refine these models for real-time use to improve comprehensive cancer screening programs and investigate their integration with other diagnostic techniques.Keywords: Breast cancer detection, YOLOv5, YOLOv8, Deep learning, Mammographic images, Performance evaluation

Introduction

Globally, Breast cancer is a significant cause of death among women, resulting in the deaths of over 40,000 women and 600 men, as reported by the American Cancer Society [1]. The disease appears in various forms based on its behaviour, including (i) benign, (ii) malignant, and (iii) normal. Benign tumours pose minimal risk and do not spread to other tissues. Whereas situ carcinoma, which is a form of malignant tumour, though treatable, impacts mammary duct lobules without metastasising. Invasive carcinoma, the most aggressive form, has the potential to metastasise [2]. In normal lesions, cells exhibit regulated growth and do not undergo uncontrolled division, as in cancer. Detection methods such as mammography, X-ray, ultrasound, positron emission tomography (PET), computed tomography (CT), temperature measurement, and MRI [3] are used solely to capture medical images. They are not capable of analysing them more efficiently. Currently, artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and deep learning (DL) have significantly enhanced the accuracy of disease diagnosis in medical image analysis [4–12]. Various image segmentation methods are available, including U-Net [13], Mask R-CNN [14], fully convolutional networks (FCNs) [15], and SegNet [16]. However, some of these have complex architectures, require extensive training time, are less accurate for fine structures, and consume significant computational resources. However, all of these still struggle with issues like long-range dependencies caused by the locality of convolutions, architecture design, high computational cost, requiring large amounts of pre-training data, not always being optimal for domain-specific tasks, high compute and memory demands, and the need for fine-tuning for specialised high-accuracy applications that are frequently trained on relatively small datasets. Therefore, this study employs YOLO, a DL-based one-step object detection algorithm that greatly enhances speed, accuracy, and recognition across various image and video processing tasks [17]. Its real-time performance, such as image or video frame processing, is highly valuable for clinical decision-making [18]. YOLO, grid-based image division [19], which uses multiple bounding boxes and class probabilities for objects. In the domain of real-time object detection, YOLOv5 and YOLOv8 introduce significant enhancements that optimise both efficiency and adaptability [20]. YOLOv5 [21], a series of YOLO models released in 2020, improves processing speed and accuracy while reducing memory consumption when processing large numbers of images. This study leverages physiological data to early detect sepsis, using inputs such as vital signs, test results, and patient demographic data. To improve the training process and fine-tune the inference probability threshold, we incorporated support vector machine (SVM) and long short-term memory (LSTM) models [22–25] and meticulously selected optimal hyperparameters through thorough evaluation [26].

The most recent version in the YOLO series [27–29] is YOLOv8, meticulously designed to push the boundaries of object detection performance. YOLOv8’s development integrates state-of-the-art concepts with proven methodologies from prior YOLO versions [30]. This results in a robust and efficient network architecture tailored for real-time object recognition. YOLOv8 demonstrates its suitability for constrained resources through its adept utilisation of deep learning algorithms and its ability to manage large datasets with a minimal memory footprint, achieving exceptional computational efficiency [31]. This study aims to evaluate YOLOv8’s efficacy in breast cancer detection using a vast dataset of real-world photos. The primary contribution lies in proposing an enhanced YOLOv8 variant tailored for breast cancer detection [32], featuring crucial adjustments to refine object classification and localisation precision. Specifically, these enhancements involve integrating an object-ness-aware DFL (distribution focal loss) loss function. Moreover, a batch size of 16 accelerates inference and training without compromising accuracy. The transition of YOLOv8 from an anchor box approach to anchor-free detection with the simplification of Non-Maximum Suppression(NMS) [33]. Moreover, its ability to overcome generalisation challenges relative to other object detection models makes YOLOv8 a noteworthy innovation [33, 34]. In summary, our study significantly enhances object detection accuracy. It provides valuable insights into developing efficient DL architectures tailored for identifying cancer diseases such as breast cancer, even in challenging scenarios.

Proposed methodology

Dataset

The curated breast imaging subset-digital database for screening mammography(CBIS-DDSM) dataset is used in this study. The DDSM data is converted to the CBIS-DDSM format. The original DDSM includes 2,620 digitised film mammography images, including normal, benign, and malignant cases with verified pathology. The DDSM is essential for developing and evaluating decision support systems due to its broad scope and verified ground truth. The images in this collection have undergone decompression, conversion to the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format, and been labelled with training data, pathological diagnoses, bounding boxes, and updated ROI segmentation.

Data preparation

To guarantee YOLOv5 [55, 56] and YOLOv8 [57, 58] compatibility, the CBIS-DDSM dataset was prepared through the following series of steps:

Step 1: YOLO Format Conversion

To make the DICOM images compatible with YOLOv5 and YOLOv8, they are converted to PNG or JPG format. After the bounding box annotations are converted to the YOLOv5/YOLOv8 format, text files containing the bounding box coordinates normalised for each image are required.

Step 2: Enhancement of Data

Various data augmentation techniques [59], such as rotation, scaling, and flipping, are applied to enhance the model’s robustness and reduce overfitting. Here, the bounding boxes are rotated to keep the object labels aligned. It helps to detect the lesions. We set the rotation angle to (− 15°, + 15°). Scaling the range from 0.5 to 1.5 for different breast sizes. In our study, a horizontal flip of 0.5 is applied to mammography images because of the left-right orientation of the breasts. Image augmentation libraries such as imgaug and albumentations are employed to facilitate this procedure. The entire dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in a 70:20:10 ratio, each containing a representative distribution of normal, benign, and malignant samples.

Model selection

The YOLO family of object detection algorithms transformed real-time detection by reframing the task as a single regression problem that simultaneously predicts bounding box coordinates and object classes, as suggested by ref. [60].

Early versions, such as YOLOv1–v3, demonstrated high inference speed but faced challenges in small-object detection and localisation accuracy [60–62]. Subsequent versions, such as YOLOv4, incorporated improvements to backbone architectures, data augmentation, and training strategies to achieve a better trade-off between speed and accuracy [63]. But the community-driven extensions, YOLOv5 released by Ultralytics in 2020, have become one of the most widely adopted versions due to its PyTorch implementation, transfer learning support, and multiple model sizes (n, s, m, l, x), making it highly scalable across resource-constrained and high-performance environments [64].

Despite the advances of YOLOv6 and YOLOv7, they still struggle to locate small, concealed objects in complex settings [45]. Building on these advances, YOLOv8, again released by Ultralytics in 2023, introduced further enhancements, including an anchor-free detection head, task flexibility (supporting detection, segmentation, and classification), and improved training pipelines. Compared with YOLOv5, YOLOv8 offers higher accuracy for small-object detection, a simpler model configuration, and more efficient deployment, while retaining the real-time performance characteristic of the YOLO family [65].

YOLOv5 model

Ultralytics developed YOLOv5 [66] as a state-of-the-art object detection model utilising CSPDarknet as its backbone, a refined version of Darknet that employs Cross-Stage Partial (CSP) connections, which split feature maps into parallel paths to improve feature extraction efficiency while reducing computational load [67].Expanding upon the YOLO series [68], which is well-known for its effectiveness in real-time object identification.YOLOv5 also integrates the Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast (SPPF) module, which pools features at multiple scales and concatenates them to create a rich multiscale representation [67]. This enables the network to capture both fine and coarse details, improving its ability to detect objects of varying sizes. In addition, YOLOv5 applies various data augmentations, including Mosaic, copy-paste, random affine, MixUp, HSV adjustments, and random horizontal flipping [67]. Within the YOLOv5(s: small, m: medium, l: large, x: extra-large) models family, the YOLOv5l variant is well-suited for mammography applications [64]. Although YOLOv5s offers the advantages of high speed and low computational demand, it suffers from reduced accuracy and limited ability to detect small, low-contrast lesions, particularly in BC screening [69, 70]. YOLOv5m offers a reasonable trade-off between accuracy and efficiency; however, it may still underperform when precise localisation is required.

In contrast, YOLOv5x is highly accurate and computationally intensive, making it less practical for medical imaging environments where hardware resources are often constrained. Therefore, YOLOv5l provides an optimal trade-off among sensitivity, precision, and computational cost, enabling robust detection of subtle abnormalities without incurring high computational cost. The literature reports comparable findings, demonstrating that YOLOv5l outperforms smaller variants in medical imaging by achieving higher mAP and recall, particularly for small-object recognition [64]. YOLOv8 model

YOLOv8, introduced by Ultralytics, supports multiple tasks, including image classification, pose estimation, instance segmentation, and oriented object detection, and features an improved backbone similar to YOLOv5, replacing the CSPLayer with the more efficient C2f module for improved feature fusion and detection accuracy [67].

The YOLOv8 model’s parameters are optimised using a single loss function that combines localisation, confidence, and classification losses. It is recognised that the loss function used in YOLOv8 differs from those used in previous iterations of YOLO [51], as shown in Fig. 1, although the precise formulation remains confidential to the YOLOv8 developers.

Compared with the YOLOv5 model family, the YOLOv8 model variants are better suited to mammography-based tumour detection [74]. YOLOv8 introduces several architectural improvements, including an anchor-free detection head, distribution focal loss (DFL) for bounding box regression, and streamlined model variants (n: nano, s: small, m: medium, l: large, x: extra-large), which collectively enhance both accuracy and generalisation. YOLOv5, which relies on an anchor-based prediction strategy and a focus layer, simplifies network design, reduces computational overhead, and improves convergence stability. Compared to YOLOv5, YOLOv8 offers quantifiable improvements in mAP and recall while retaining competitive inference speed, which is crucial for medical imaging, where sensitivity and accurate localisation of small lesions are essential. Moreover, its multitask capability, which supports detection, segmentation, classification, and pose estimation within the same framework, makes YOLOv8 more flexible for clinical research [74].

The architecture of the YOLOv8 model

Our model provides a detailed description of its design and implementation, highlighting its applicability to real-time disease detection. YOLOv8 emerges as a cutting-edge object detection algorithm, surpassing its predecessors [77]. As the eighth iteration, it boasts unparalleled precision and efficiency, particularly excelling in real-time detection. This makes it versatile across applications like video surveillance, autonomous vehicles, and robotics. Its adaptability supports a range of hardware configurations, ensuring optimal performance even with large datasets.

YOLOv8 distinguishes itself [78, 79] with an anchor-free approach, enhancing robustness by eliminating anchor boxes and minimising false positives. It compacts the model and custom loss function to improve computational efficiency and accuracy. The custom loss function allows the model to learn more effectively. Since the custom loss function achieves higher accuracy when tuned to the dataset, it also improves convergence speed. Inspired by the spatial pyramid pooling network(SPP-Net), it streamlines the architecture and introduces Mix-up data augmentation, solidifying YOLOv8 as a significant upgrade in object detection algorithms. This efficient DL model consists of three primary building blocks: backbone, neck, and head.

Step 1: Backbone

A modified version of the cross-stage partial darknet (CSPDarknet53) backbone is integrated into the YOLOv8 architecture, enhancing feature extraction and model efficiency. On the other hand, instead of the C3 (which consists of 3 convolution modules and n bottlenecks) modules utilised in YOLOv5, YOLOv8 adopts cross-stage partial fusion (C2f) modules, which reduce redundancy and are efficient in real-time object detection. This update aims to maintain the module’s low computational cost while enhancing its feature extraction capacity. The C2f module combines elements from the C3 module with the Efficient Layer Aggregation (ELAN) structure from YOLOv7. This combination enhances the model’s overall performance and efficiency. The components of each convolution module are a sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) activation function, batch normalisation, and a convolution layer. The optimiser is set to stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with OneCycleLR as the scheduler, due to its higher accuracy and faster training. In YOLOv8, this scheduler is mainly applied to reduce overfitting. The depth parameter (n) includes extra padding layers and determines the number of bottleneck stacks. The characters k, s, and p in Fig. 6 stand for kernel, stride, and padding, respectively.

Step 2: Neck

Integrating characteristics retrieved from various network tiers is made easier by the Backbone’s three distinct depth connections to the Neck (Stages 2, 3, and 4). After that, the Head is the target of these combined traits. Path aggregation networks (PANs) are employed by Neck [80] to minimise information loss from repeated convolutions. The feature pyramid network (FPN) structure is created. Lower-level features are first upsampled from top to bottom within the FPN structure, preserving their spatial information. By employing convolutional modules to downsample from the bottom up, the PAN structure, in contrast, enables higher-level features to incorporate additional positional information. Like the Backbone, the Neck in our architecture utilises the FPN structure for direct up-sampling, substituting the C2f module for the ConvModule, which in YOLOv5 is the C3 module. In the PAN-FPN architecture, the Neck’s output channels correlate to the Backbone’s.

Step 3: Head

The head is the final element of the network, responsible for making predictions, such as bounding boxes, objectness scores, and class probabilities. It also utilises the features from the backbone and neck to generate predictions. It includes tasks like instance segmentation, localisation, and classification. By analysing the neck’s features, the head generates predictions for each object candidate. The aforementioned improvements are intended to mitigate the effects of class imbalance and to improve precision and recall measures.

Anchor free detection

In contrast to earlier iterations, YOLOv8 employs an anchor-free method that predicts an object’s centre without needing pre-established anchor boxes. This change tackles issues with the distribution of anchor boxes. Predictions are made more efficiently, and the non-maximum suppression(NMS) process proceeds more quickly with the removal of anchor boxes. Result discussion and performance analysis

This section presents a comparative evaluation of the performance of the YOLOv5 and YOLOv8 models using key metrics, including F1-score, precision, recall, confidence scores, and mAP. We employed model pruning to reduce the overall model complexity and improve inference efficiency. Specifically, the model applied structured pruning to avoid the irregular sparsity patterns produced by unstructured pruning. Here, the pruning ratio for our model was 20% based on observation and experiment, resulting in improved model size and inference speed (18-30ms) without significantly affecting accuracy. Experiments with higher pruning percentages resulted in lower mAP50 and recall. We chose the YOLOv8 model for its superior precision and robustness in early breast cancer detection. A carefully organised mammography image dataset is used to train the YOLOv8 algorithm. The model was trained for 25 epochs, enabling it to learn and refine YOLOv8’s higher predictive capabilities.

Conclusion and limitations

This study states that YOLOv8 significantly outperforms YOLOv5 in automated BC detection using the CBIS-DDSM dataset. With superior metrics in F1-confidence, precision-recall, and mAP, YOLOv8 shows greater promise for clinical applications, potentially improving early detection and reducing diagnostic errors. These findings underscore the importance of adopting advanced object detection algorithms in medical diagnostics. Future efforts will focus on real-time optimisation and integration with other diagnostic tools to enhance the robustness and clinical efficacy of these models in breast cancer screening.

Future work will optimise YOLOv8 for real-time clinical application to enhance its usability in routine screenings. Additionally, integrating YOLOv8 with other diagnostic tools, such as MRI and ultrasound, will be explored to create a comprehensive breast cancer detection system. Further research will also involve testing these models on larger and more diverse datasets to validate their generalizability and robustness. Investigating the use of advanced preprocessing techniques and data augmentation to improve detection accuracy and reduce false positives and negatives will ultimately enhance patient outcomes in breast cancer diagnostics.

The study’s limitations concern real-time optimisation, integration with other diagnostic tools, and extensive validation across diverse datasets to enhance the model’s robustness and clinical efficacy in breast cancer screening. Secondly, implementing YOLOv8 in real-time clinical workflows may require substantial computational resources and infrastructure that may not be readily available in low-resource clinical environments. Thirdly, the investigation findings differentiate between cancerous and non-cancerous lesions. It does not classify the tumor subtypes or grades, which are critical for personalised treatment planning.
