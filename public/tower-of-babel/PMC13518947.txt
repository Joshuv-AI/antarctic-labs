Challenges of conventional iterative all-atom and coarse-grained multiscale molecular dynamics
Authors: Hung N Do, Joe McKenzie, S Gnanakaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, we evaluate the biomolecular dynamics behaviors when conventionally iterating between all-atom (AA) and coarse-grained (CG) molecular dynamics (MD) simulations over multiple cycles. We implemented the workflow to iterate between AA and CG in OpenMM, namely the iterative multiscale MD (iMMD) simulation workflow. In particular, we aim to identify practical applications for iterating between AA and CG simulations in a conventional manner without any constraints or model modifications. We evaluate the iMMD workflow on four representative systems, spanning folding of two soluble proteins and protein-protein as well as protein-lipid interactions of two membrane proteins. We observe that iteration between AA and CG representations could help the soluble proteins exit undesirable metastable states to fold, resulting from random protein structural distortions due to cycling. Consequently, the most reliable use of iterative AA and CG simulations appears to be to accelerating complex lipid mixing for membrane-bound protein systems rather than sampling protein conformational space. Our work explores the practical usages and limitations for iterative AA and CG simulations using readily available AA and CG force fields. Introduction

For decades, all-atom molecular dynamics (AA MD) has been a powerful computational technique for exploring biomolecular dynamics at an atomistic level1. However, conventional MD (cMD) simulations are computationally intensive. Despite the advances in computing hardware2,3, many biological processes of interest cannot be sufficiently sampled in a reasonable amount of time due to their large conformational landscapes and high energy barriers4.

Several strategies have been developed over the last few decades to address the challenges facing cMD5. One such strategy involves creating enhanced sampling methods that smooth the free energy landscapes and accelerate transitions from one conformational state to another6–17. Another strategy simplifies representations of biomolecules to facilitate conformational landscape sampling18–20. In particular, coarse-grained (CG) models depict biomolecules as beads that represent groups of atoms. By reducing the degrees of freedom in MD simulations, CG simulations can be performed at increased speeds and over much longer timescales compared to AA simulations, albeit at the expense of atomistic details21,22.

Both AA and CG simulations have their strengths and weaknesses. As a result, they have been combined over one or multiple iterations with constraints for conformational sampling of different biomolecular components in reasonable amounts of simulation time as AA simulations excel at sampling of local dynamics in refined temporal resolutions and CG simulations perform better for larger complexes and longer timescales19,23–35. In particular, the PACE framework establishes a hybrid model of representations where the protein conformations are sampled at united atom resolutions while other components of the simulation systems are simulated at CG resolutions36,37. Initial works utilizing PACE showed that the method could simulate proper protein and peptide folding36,37, and further refinements improved parameters related to backbone hydration and interactions involving charged side chains to increase its applicability38. A recent PACE application proved that 20-µs hybrid-resolution simulations of PIEZO2 could reveal a tension-dependent two-step opening mechanism of the protein39. Meanwhile, the multiscale enhanced sampling (MSES) method accelerates AA simulations by coupling them to CG models while removing the resulting bias through Hamiltonian replica exchange40, whereas serial workflows may exploit CG simulations to sample multiple conformers to be reconstructed and used as initial structures to initiate AA replica-exchange MD simulations27. Furthermore, it is worth mentioning bottom-up CG models, which provide a statistically grounded route to derive effective CG interactions for CG simulations using AA reference data through force matching, relative entropy minimization, and ultra-coarse-graining41–43. These models are particularly parameterized to recover structural and thermodynamic behaviors of biomolecules at given the CG resolution41–43 and widely applied to study membrane-protein interactions, nucleosome aggregation and chromatin organization, and protein conformational dynamics44–46. More recently, Lopez et al. connected Martini 2.246 CG and CHARMM36m48 AA simulations in automated feedback loops to simulate the dynamics of the large membrane-associated protein assemblies19, and machine learning (ML) has been employed by Ingolfsson et al. in their multiscale workflow to account the differences between serial continuum, CG, and AA simulations20. These works demonstrated the potential benefits of combining AA and CG simulations by retaining AA details at key biomolecular components while allowing sampling of large system sizes and timescales in reasonable simulation times, especially when coupled with advanced backmapping protocols49–52.

As described previously, most workflows combining serial AA and CG simulations have been carried out over only one iteration27,39. Heavy constraints or model modifications were employed during hybrid AA and CG simulations that were performed over multiple iterations19,20,30,31. In this work, we set out to examine the dynamics behaviors of biomolecules when AA and CG simulations were iterated over multiple cycles in a conventional manner (i.e., without constraints or model modifications) (Fig. 1). We implemented the iterative multiscale molecular dynamics (iMMD) workflow in the OpenMM53 simulation engine to evaluate its usefulness and practicality (Fig. 1). We evaluated iMMD on four model biomolecular systems, including two globular proteins (the fast-folding variant of Trpcage and the Z-matrix protein of Lassa mammarenavirus (LASV)) and two membrane proteins (the Torpedo nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) and the CXCR4s in complex with the REGN7663 Fabs in the heterogeneous cholesterol/phosphatidylcholine (CHOL/POPC) lipid bilayers.We only ran the serial simulations long enough to observe our processes of interest, including protein folding, lipid equilibration, and protein-protein association.

Scheme of the conventional iterative multiscale molecular dynamics (iMMD) workflow in OpenMM for evaluation. Given an initial PDB structure, iMMD automatically sets up the simulation system in aqueous solution or simple membrane lipid bilayer. The all-atom (AA) MD simulation is then carried out, using the CHARMM36m as the force field parameter set. The final frame of the AA simulation is converted to its coarse-grained (CG) representation using the Martinize2 framework for protein and backward python script for the other components of the simulation system. The CG MD simulation is then performed to facilitate the transitions from one conformational state to another, with MARTINI 3 as the force field parameter set. The final frame of the CG simulation is converted back to its AA representation using the mstool python package for the protein, common lipids, water, and ions and backward python script for the uncommon lipid molecules. The AA-CG-AA cycles are repeated to continue the iMMD simulation.

Results

Evaluation of iMMD on folding of Trpcage

We performed iMMD simulations starting from the unfolded conformation of the fast-folding variant of Trpcage in explicit solvent and observed that iterating between AA and CG simulations could fold the protein within two AA MD simulation iterations, for a total of 2 µs, and one short CG MD simulation iteration of 15 ns (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Iteration between AA and CG simulations appeared to result in an approximate seven-fold speed up compared to mere cMD of fast-folding Trpcage in explicit solvent54. Starting from its extended conformation in the “Unfolded” state (Fig. 2a), Trpcage first formed a short helix from residue Y3 to L7 at ~ 630 ns into the first AA MD simulation iteration in state “S1” (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1a), whose Cα-atom root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) was ~ 5.1 Å compared to the 2JOF55 PDB structure. The “S1” state was stabilized by a weak hydrogen bond between the N backbone atom of residue A4 and the O backbone atom of residue S11. Consequently, Trpcage could not overcome the “S1” state and stayed in that conformation for the remainder of the first AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1a). The final conformation of Trpcage in the first AA MD simulation was converted to its CG representation, and the short 15 ns CG MD simulation transitioned Trpcage from state “S1” to state “S2” by breaking the weak hydrogen between residues A4 and S11 (Fig. 2b). However, it should be noted here that the transition between AA and CG representations randomly resulted in a strong perturbation that helped the simulation system escape a persistent metastable state to arrive at the folded state (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). However, it should be noted here that the transition between AA and CG representations may result in a random configuration due to unexpected perturbations. That may facilitate the system’s escape from persistent metastable states to reach the folded state (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). At least two measures need to be done to evaluate these transitions. First, an evaluation of sampled protein conformations and their interactions with ions and water must be carried out to ensure that no unphysical or energetically incompatible configurations continued from CG to AA. Second, an in-situ evaluation should be implemented during the entire iMMD trajectory to monitor the states and transitions of the system to detect “drift” towards a non-physical state . In state “S2”, the long loop between residues K8 and P18 was no longer restricted by the interaction between residues A4 and S11, leading to the fast transition into state “S3” at ~ 50 ns into the second AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 1b). The Cα-atom RMSD of state “S3” compared to the 2JOF55 PDB structure was ~ 3.0 Å, and Trpcage fully formed its major helix from residue Y3 to D9 in this state (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 1b). At ~ 150 ns and especially ~ 360 ns into the second AA MD simulation iteration, state “S3” transitioned into the “Folded” state (Fig. 2b), whose Cα-atom RMSD was ~ 0.8 Å compared to the 2JOF55 PDB structure. As Trpcage gradually folded, the radius of gyration decreased from ~ 8–14 Å in state “S2” to ~ 8 Å in state “S3” and eventually ~ 6.5-7 Å in the “Folded” state (Fig. 2c). The α-helical proportion between residues Y3 and D9 increased from ~ 0.7 at the end of the first AA MD simulation iteration to 1.0 through most of the second AA MD simulation iteration (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating the complete formation of the major α-helix. Meanwhile, the proportion of native contacts compared to the 2JOF55 PDB structure increased from ~ 0.4 at the end of the first AA MD simulation iteration to ~ 0.9 at ~ 160 ns into the second AA MD simulation iteration as Trpcage folded (Fig. 2d). Trpcage stayed in the “Folded” state for the rest of the second AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 2b and d).

However, as the simulation system transitioned into random states during the AA-CG cycling, iterating from the second CG MD simulation and back to the third AA MD simulation iteration unfolded Trpcage (Fig. 2c and d, Supplementary Figs. 1c, d, and 2). The radius of gyration surged to ~ 8–14 Å at the beginning of the third AA MD simulation iteration and fluctuated between ~ 8 Å and ~ 10 Å throughout the simulation iteration (Fig. 2c). The α-helical content between residues Y3 and D9 dropped to zero in the first ~ 60 ns of the third AA MD simulation iteration, before fluctuating between ~ 0.6 and 1.0 from the ~ 80 to ~ 800 ns mark and finally dropped to zero in the last ~ 200 ns of the iteration (Supplementary Fig. 2). Seemingly, Trpcage was not able to properly refold in the third AA simulation iteration, as indicated by the high Cα-atom RMSDs of residues Y3-P18 (Supplementary Fig. 1c) and reducing proportion of native contacts compared to the 2JOF55 PDB structure, from ~ 0.8 at the end of the second AA simulation iteration to only ~ 0.3 at the end of the third AA simulation iteration (Fig. 2d). In other words, the tertiary structure of Trpcage was not properly formed in the third AA MD simulation iteration. Eventually, the major helix between residues Y3 and D9 of Trpcage distorted at ~ 760 ns into the third AA MD simulation iteration, and Trpcage returned to its “Unfolded” state (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d).

Overall, these observations indicated that repeated cycling between AA and CG simulations introduced uncontrolled transitions into random conformational states (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). These random events resulted from protein structural distortions might help reach desirable protein conformations (AA1-CG1-AA2) or lead to undesirable outcomes (AA2-CG2-AA3), thus any states sampled from the cycling should be examined carefully before biological interpretations.

Evaluation of iMMD on folding of the Z-matrix protein of the LASV

We observed that iMMD was able to capture the folding of the helical portion of LASV Z-matrix protein, a peptide of 49 residues in size, starting from its extended conformation in solution after seven AA MD simulation iterations, for a total of 8 µs, and six CG MD simulation iterations of 100 ns each (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 3, 4). Again, while iterating between AA and CG simulations could fold the ordered region of the protein (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 3, 4), the conformational transitions resulted from random structural distortions should not be considered advantageous enhanced sampling and need careful examinations.

Here, as the LASV Z-matrix protein primarily existed as disordered loops, except residues L51-L58 which formed a helix, we primarily paid attention to the Cα-atom RMSD of residues L51-L58 of the LASV Z-matrix protein compared to the 2M1S56 PDB structure, their radius of gyration, their α-helical contents, and their proportions of native contacts compared to the 2M1S56 PDB structure to determine the folding of the LASV Z-matrix protein (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 3, 4). During the first six AA and CG MD simulation iterations, the LASV Z-matrix protein mostly existed as disordered loops, with minor α-helices and β-sheets formed at random timepoints, but no notable α-helical formation was observed between residue L51 and L58 (Fig. 3c and d and Supplementary Figs. 3, 4). The LASV Z-matrix protein started the sixth AA MD simulation in the disordered loop form of state “S1”, whose Cα-atom RMSD of residues L51-L58 compared to the 2M1S56 PDB structure was ~ 3.8 Å (Fig. 3a). The LASV Z-matrix protein transitioned from the “S1” to the “S2” state at ~ 370 ns into the sixth AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 3a). In state “S2”, the Cα-atom RMSD of residues L51-L58 was ~ 3.2 Å, and β-strands were formed between residues Y48-N52 and I66-P70 (Fig. 3a). The LASV Z-matrix protein fluctuated between states “S1” and “S2” during the last ~ 200 ns of the sixth AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 3a).

The Z-matrix protein started the seventh and final AA MD simulation in state “S3”, whose Cα-atom RMSD of residues L51–L58 was ~ 3.7 Å (Fig. 3b). This state was primarily a disordered loop, with a minor α-helix formed between residues L56–L58 (Fig. 3b). State “S3” of the LASV Z-matrix protein transitioned into state “S4” at ~ 500 ns into the seventh AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 3b). The “S4” state of the LASV Z-matrix protein showed a Cα-atom RMSD of residues L51–L58 of ~ 3.3 Å, and it had β-strands formed between residues C50-N52 and I66-K68 (Fig. 3b). A minor α-helix was observed between residues T55-L58 (Fig. 3b). As we transitioned from the “S3” to the “S4” state, the α-helix extended from residues L56–L58 to residues T55-L58. The “Folded” state of the LASV Z-matrix protein was first observed at ~ 900 ns and stably at ~ 1250 ns into the final AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 3b). In this state, the Cα-atom RMSD of residues L51–L58 of the LASV Z-matrix protein compared to the 2M1S56 PDB structure was ~ 0.8 Å, and the α-helix extended to cover residues C53-L58 (Fig. 3b). Residue N52 was in a turn, while residue L51 was mostly loop-like.

As the LASV Z-matrix protein gradually folded, the radius of gyration decreased from its fluctuation between ~ 6-7.5 Å at the beginning of the seventh AA MD simulation iteration to ~ 5 Å at ~ 500 ns into the simulation iteration and remained between ~ 5–6 and Å till the end of the simulation iteration (Fig. 3c). The α-helical proportion between residues L51–L58 increased from 0 to ~ 0.5 at ~ 525 ns into the seventh AA MD simulation iteration (representing state “S4” with half of the residues from L51 to L58 being helical) (Supplementary Fig. 4). The α-helical content between residues L51-L58 peaked at ~ 0.8 at ~ 994 ns into the AA MD simulation iteration (illustrating the transition from the “S4” to the “Folded” state) and fluctuated between ~ 0.5 and ~ 0.8 for the remaining of the seventh AA MD simulation iteration (Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, iMMD was able to form most of the native contacts between residues L51 and L58 of the LASV Z-matrix protein compared to the 2M1S56 PDB structure (Fig. 3d). In particular, the proportion of native contacts between residues L51 and L58 of the LASV Z-matrix protein compared to the 2M1S56 PDB structure was found to reach 1.0 at the ~ 530 ns mark, between ~ 1400 ns and ~ 1525 ns, and between the ~ 2240 and ~ 2350 ns marks of the seventh AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 3d).

Evaluation of iMMD on lipid equilibration on membrane-bound Torpedo nAChR

CG MD simulations with MARTINI forcefield are known to facilitate lipid equilibration in heterogeneous lipid bilayers compared to AA MD simulations due to their flattened free energy surfaces resulting from simplified molecular representations57. Here, we would like to examine the dynamics behaviors of membrane protein systems in heterogeneous membrane bilayers while iterating between AA and CG simulations. We employed the Torpedo nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) in the CHOL/POPC lipid bilayer as a model system (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 5–8).

Proposed usage for conventional iMMD for equilibration of the heterogenous lipid bilayers. The equilibrated membrane lipid bilayers from the CG MD simulation iteration can be backmapped to their AA representations and combined with the final AA protein conformation prior to the CG MD simulation iteration using mstool to proceed to the next AA MD simulation iteration.

In our first test, we iterated between 100 ns AA MD and 100 ns CG MD simulation iterations, with the whole simulation system (including the Torpedo nAChR and CHOL/POPC lipid molecules) cycled between their AA and CG representations using the Martinize2 framework58, backward59python script, and mstool49python package (Supplementary Fig. 5). However, after two AA-CG-AA cycles, we observed that the secondary structures of the ligand-binding domain of the Torpedo nAChR in the solution became completely distorted, similar to the behaviors observed for Trpcage and LASV Z-matrix proteins (Supplementary Fig. 5e), even with the strong position and force restraints applied in the CG MD simulations. Here, the structural distortions appeared more obvious due to the long CG simulation lengths (Supplementary Fig. 5), and the extent of this distortion might be reduced using shorter CG MD simulations. However, long CG MD simulations are required for the purpose of lipid equilibration in the heterogenous lipid bilayers.

Therefore, we proposed a strategy to separate the sampling of protein and lipid configurations by using CG simulations to facilitate the sampling of lipid configurations and AA simulations to sample protein conformations (Fig. 4). In particular, the CG MD simulations would be used only to facilitate the equilibration of the complex lipids in the membrane (Fig. 4). After each CG MD simulation iteration, the CG equilibrated lipid bilayers would be backmapped to their AA representations using the mstool49 and/or backward59python script and combined with the final AA protein conformation prior to the CG MD simulation iteration to replace the CG-sampled protein conformation in order to continue the iMMD simulation (Fig. 4). The assembly (consisted of the AA-backmapped CG-equilibrated lipid bilayers and final AA protein conformation from the previous iteration) would be subjected to energetic minimization to resolve the possible clashes between the proteins and lipids before the next AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 4).

Using the proposed strategy (Fig. 4), we ran a second test and performed six AA MD simulations of 75–250 ns in length iterated by five CG MD simulations of 1 µs each in between on the Torpedo nAChR in the CHOL/POPC lipid bilayer. The secondary structure contents of the nAChR, including the proportions of the α-helices and β-sheets, were monitored in each AA MD simulation iteration (Supplementary Fig. 6), and we observed no difference in the proportions of the α-helices and β-sheets of the nAChR across the AA MD simulation iterations. In other words, no distortion of secondary structures took place in the nAChR as expected while we iterated between the AA and CG simulations using the proposed protocol (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). Using the LiPyPhilic60 python package, we calculated the lipid diffusion coefficients within the heterogeneous lipid bilayers across the first five AA and CG MD simulation iterations (Supplementary Fig. 8). The lipid diffusion coefficients were found to be higher, of 5–9 folds, during the CG MD simulations compared to the AA MD simulation iterations (Supplementary Fig. 8). In particular, the lipid diffusion coefficients during the first five AA MD simulation iterations were calculated to be ~ 3.5⋅10− 7 ± 5.4⋅10− 8, ~ 1.7⋅10− 7 ± 2.2⋅10− 8, ~ 1.3⋅10− 7 ± 1.3⋅10− 8, ~ 1.4⋅10− 7 ± 1.4⋅10− 8, and ~ 1.3⋅10− 7 ± 1.3⋅10− 8 nm2/ns, respectively, while the CG lipid diffusion coefficients were calculated to be ~ 1.8⋅10− 6 ± 2.5⋅10− 7, ~ 1.3⋅10− 6 ± 1.9⋅10− 7, ~ 1.2⋅10− 6 ± 1.7⋅10− 7, ~ 1.1⋅10− 6 ± 2.0⋅10− 7, and ~ 1.1⋅10− 6 ± 1.7⋅10− 7 nm2/ns (Supplementary Fig. 8). The enhanced motions expectedly resulted from the flattened free energy surfaces coming from the employed MARTINI 318 force field. Therefore, the CG MD simulations enhanced lipid motions within the membranes compared to the AA MD simulations.

Evaluation of iMMD on association of two membrane-bound CXCR4 receptors

Lastly, we evaluated iMMD on simulating the binding of two REGN7663 Fabs to two CXCR4 receptors in the heterogeneous CHOL/POPC lipid bilayer. Here, we aimed to test the behaviors of iMMD in simulating the associations of proteins in solution (i.e., REGN Fab binding to the CXCR4) and in the membrane (i.e., the dimerization of the two CXCR4 receptors). Since there is currently no dimeric structure of CXCR4s in complex with REGN7663 Fabs (with 8U4R61 being the monomeric structure, 8U4S61 being the trimeric structure, and 8U4T61 being the tetrameric structure of CXCR4s in complex with REGN7663 Fabs), these simulations should only be treated as demonstrations for protein-protein association evaluations within iMMD rather than validated binding models.

We performed six AA MD simulation iterations of 125–500 ns each in length iterated with five CG MD simulation iterations of 1 µs each and observed the binding of two REGN7663 Fabs to the extracellular domains of the two CXCR4 receptors and the dimerization of the two CXCR4 receptors (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 9–11). Here, we employed the strategy shown in Fig. 4, using the CG MD simulations to facilitate lipid equilibration and the AA MD simulations to sample the protein-protein association. It is possible that when mapping MARTINI water bead (representing four atomistic water molecules) may show gaps in small cavities and interfacial regions 62. However, since we only used the lipid configurations sampled from the CG simulations to proceed with AA simulations further during our evaluation, with waters and ions regenerated between iterations using fixed box dimensions (Fig. 4 and Methods section), such issues should be diminished during our iterations.

In the initial configuration of the first AA MD simulation iteration, the two REGN Fabs were located 20 Å away from the extracellular pockets of the CXCR4, and the CXCR4 receptors were also located 20 Å away from each other (Fig. 5b). Towards the end of the first AA simulation iteration, we already observed from 10 to 20 contacts between the REGN-Fab-1 and CXCR4-1 as well as from 15 to 25 contacts between the REGN-Fab-2 and CXCR4-2 (Fig. 5a), indicating the two REGN Fabs approached the CXCR4s (Fig. 5c). No contact was observed between the two CXCR4 receptors throughout the first AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 5a and c). As we transitioned into the first 1µs CG MD simulation iteration for lipid mixing and back to the second AA MD simulation iteration, we began to observe minor contact formation between the two CXCR4 receptors at the ~ 53 ns and ~ 68 ns marks (Supplementary Fig. 9a). However, it was not until the end of the final AA simulation iteration, i.e., the sixth AA MD simulation iteration, that we observed consistent contact formations between the two CXCR4 receptors (Fig. 5d and f and Supplementary Figs. 9, 10). In particular, we observed from 1 to 4 contacts from the ~ 300 ns mark towards the end of the AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 5d). At this stage, notable contacts were formed between each REGN-Fab and CXCR4 receptor, with between 35 and 102 contacts observed for REGN-Fab-1 and CXCR4-1 and up to 67 contacts observed for REGN-Fab-2 and CXCR4-2 (Fig. 5d). Moreover, we also observed up to 67 contacts between the two REGN-Fabs (Supplementary Fig. 11), which could be the driving force for the CXCR4 dimerization captured in our iMMD simulation due to the simultaneous increases in number of contacts between the two REGN-Fabs and two CXCR4s observed towards the end of the sixth AA MD simulation iteration (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 11). Here, we cannot distinguish between the observed sampling of Fab-Fab and Fab-CXCR4 interactions that are due to accelerated diffusion in CG simulations or from the AA-CG cycling and require further validation before biological interpretations.

Lastly, we also calculated the lipid diffusion coefficients during the first five AA-CG MD simulation iterations of this simulation system (Supplementary Fig. 12). The lipid diffusion coefficients from the first to fifth AA MD simulation iterations were found to be ~ 1.0⋅10− 6 ± 5.5⋅10− 8, ~ 7.0⋅10− 7 ± 5.5⋅10− 8, ~ 4.7⋅10− 7 ± 3.8⋅10− 8, ~ 4.0⋅10− 7 ± 3.7⋅10− 8, and ~ 3.6⋅10− 7 ± 3.3⋅10− 8 nm2/ns, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 12). The CG lipid diffusion coefficients from the first to fifth AA MD simulation iterations were calculated to be ~ 3.9⋅10− 6 ± 1.7⋅10− 7, ~ 3.2⋅10− 6 ± 1.7⋅10− 7, ~ 3.1⋅10− 6 ± 1.7⋅10− 7, ~ 2.9⋅10− 6 ± 1.6⋅10− 7, and ~ 3.0⋅10− 6 ± 1.7⋅10− 7 nm2/ns (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Discussion

In this work, we examined the biomolecular dynamics behaviors when conventionally iterating between AA and CG MD simulations without constraints or model modifications (Fig. 1). The resulting workflow was called iterative multiscale molecular dynamics (iMMD) (Fig. 1) and was performed on four different simulation systems, spanning soluble protein folding to membrane protein, for the evaluation purpose.

AA MD simulations remain the gold standard for exploring biomolecular dynamics at atomistic details. However, they are computationally expensive due to the sampling required to address the high dimensionality involved. The high energy barriers in the AA MD simulations also prevent smooth transitions from one conformational state to another. CG MD simulations allow for the sampling of biomolecular configurations due to their simplified representations of biomolecules57. However, they lack the atomistic detail provided by AA MD simulations. CHARMM36m48 and MARTINI 318,33,63 are two commonly used force fields in AA MD and CG MD simulations, respectively. Here, it should be noted that the two force fields were not systematically derived from another, thus consistency between the two models could not be guaranteed. This limitation should not apply for specially developed bottom-up coarse-graining models that explicitly derive CG interactions from AA representations41–43. Therefore, we iterated between CHARMM36m48-based AA simulations and MARTINI 318,33,63-based CG simulations in OpenMM53 coupled with mstool49 and backward59 for backmapping to determine if conventional iMMD could have any practical uses in expanding conformational sampling. For their own uses and testing purposes, users should be able to easily adapt this workflow using other AA64,65 and CG41–43 force fields and backmapping tools49–52 in other simulation engines such as GROMACS66. Here, the iterative simulations were only run long enough to observe our processes of interest, including protein folding, lipid equilibration, and protein-protein association .

Overall, we found two practical uses for conventional iMMD. The first one involved cycling between AA and CG simulations to transition a simulation system out of undesirable metastable states, resulting from protein structural distortions during the AA-CG iterations (Figs. 2 and 3 and Supplementary Figs. 1–4). However, this should be used with extreme caution as certain conformational transitions may result from random structural distortions and in turn should not be treated as a controlled enhanced sampling strategy. Furthermore, any protein conformations obtained during the AA-CG-AA cycling should be carefully examined for physical validity before any further usages or biological interpretations. In our evaluating iMMD simulations, short iterative CG simulations between AA simulations were observed to transition fast-folding Trpcage as well as LASV Z-matrix protein out of undesirable states for the two soluble proteins to fold (Figs. 2 and 3 and Supplementary Figs. 1–4). However, they could also unfold soluble proteins and get them back into undesirable states, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 5. In fact, it would be likely for the simulation systems to drift into unphysical states under indefinite AA-CG cycling, making these conventional iMMD impossible to converge. Also, regardless of soluble or membrane-bound protein systems, the mapping between MARTINI water bead (representing 4 atomistic waters)62 and AA water molecules leads to pathologicaloutcomes in small cavities and interfacial regions. We suggest two in-situ evaluations that could help toalleviate some of these concerns. First, an evaluation of sampled protein conformations and theirinteractions with ions and water must be carried out to ensure that no unphysical or energeticallyincompatible configurations continued from CG to AA. Second, an in-situ evaluation should beimplemented during the entire iMMD trajectory to monitor the states and transitions of the system todetect “drift” towards a non-physical state.

The second use of iMMD, the ability to simulate large protein systems in complex membranes, appears to be the most practical and reliable approach. Here, the conventional iteration between AA and CG simulations can be used to perform lipid equilibration of the membrane, covering regions surrounding the embedded proteins as well as those farther away during CG simulations, while allowing sampling of protein conformations only during AA simulations. Here, when you go from CG to AA, protein structural distortions are avoided by discarding CG protein conformations and replacing them with those from the previous AA run (Fig. 4). This usage was practical as the simplified representations and flattened energy surface during CG simulations should allow for faster sampling of lipid configurations57 (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 12), while avoiding structural distortion resulting from AA-CG-AA cycling.

In conclusion, we examined the conventional workflow to iterate between AA and CG MD simulations with commonly used force fields, CHARMM36m48 and MARTINI 318,33,63. The workflow developed for our evaluation purpose can be found at https://github.com/lanl/iMMD. While two practical uses were found for conventional iMMD, researchers should exercise extreme cautions when deciding to iterate between AA and CG MD simulations without constraints or model modifications.

Materials and methods

Workflow of the conventional iterative multiscale molecular dynamics (iMMD)

The current implementation of iMMD is in the OpenMM simulation package53,67 due to its python interface. The initial input for iMMD can be a simple atomistic PDB structure of the protein of interest if the simulation system is in an aqueous solution or only involves common membrane phospholipid molecules, including dilauroyl phosphatidylcholine (DLPC), dilauroyl phosphatidylethanolamine (DLPE), dimyristoylphosphatidylcholine (DMPC), dioleoylphosphocholine (DOPC), dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC), palmitoylphosphatidylcholine (POPC), and pamlmitoylphosphatidylethanolamine (POPE) (Fig. 1). iMMD in OpenMM can then automatically embed the protein in a solvated simulation system with water and ions and/or in a phospholipid membrane of choice. If the simulation system involves non-phospholipid molecules, the user should set up simulation systems outside iMMD and provide the topology and coordinate files to start iMMD simulations.

The AA simulation involves an initial short energetic minimization, followed by equilibration with the constant number, volume, temperature (NVT) and constant number, volume, pressure, temperature (NPT) ensembles, and finally a production simulation with NPT ensemble (Fig. 1). The common CHARMM36m force field48 is used in the AA simulation. The energetic minimization was carried out using a root-mean-square force tolerance of 1.0 kJ mol− 1 nm− 1 with a maximum of 5000 iterations before equilibration. The settings for the AA simulation use periodic boundary conditions, with bonds containing hydrogen atoms restrained using the SHAKE68 algorithm. In addition, iMMD uses the Particle-Mesh Ewald summation69 with a cutoff of 9.0 Å for long-range interactions to calculate the electrostatic interactions. The Monte Carlo barostat70, with the default pressure of 1 atm and temperature of 310 K, is used for solution simulation systems, while the Monte Carlo membrane barostat70, with the default pressure of 1 atm, surface tension of 0 atm nm, and temperature of 310 K, is used for simulation systems involving membranes. For soluble protein simulations, the X, Y, and Z directions are set to scale by the same amount (or isotropic). Meanwhile, for membrane protein simulations, the X and Y directions of the membrane are set to scale by the same amount, whereas the Z direction of the membrane is allowed to vary freely (or semi-isotropic). The Langevin Middle Integrator71, which combines Langevin dynamics with the LFMiddle discretization72, is used by default in the atomistic simulations, with a temperature set at 310 K, friction coefficient of 1 ps− 1, and timestep of 2 or 4 fs (coupled with hydrogen mass repartitioning73.

The final frame of the AA simulation is obtained and converted into its coarse-grained (CG) representation, using the Martinize2 and Vermouth framework58 for the proteins and the backward59python script for lipids and other molecules (Fig. 1). The Insane74python package may be used to solvate the simulation system with the box dimension kept the same as in the AA simulation. The default settings for AA to CG conversion of the proteins using Martinize258 uses side chain corrections, neutral termini, backbone position restraints, with a position restraint force constant of 2500 kJ/(mol nm2), DSSP75 to determine the secondary structures, and the protein elastic network76, with an elastic bond force constant of 1000 kJ/(mol nm2) and elastic bond lower and upper cutoffs of 0 Å and 9 Å. The CG simulation is performed following the same protocol as the AA simulation: starting with an energetic minimization, followed by equilibration with NVT and NPT ensembles, and finally a production simulation with NPT ensemble (Fig. 1). For CG simulations, the energetic minimization was also carried out using a root-mean-square force tolerance of 1.0 kJ mol− 1 nm− 1 with a maximum of 5000 iterations before equilibration. The popular MARTINI 3 force field18,77 is used for the CG simulation. The default settings for the CG simulation treat the long-range interactions with reaction field using a cutoff of 11 Å and a relative permittivity of 1577. The settings for barostat are identical to those from the AA simulations70. The Langevin Middle Integrator71 is also used for the CG simulations, with a default temperature of 310 K, collision frequency of 10 ps− 1, and timestep of 20 fs.

The final frame of the CG simulation is then converted to its AA representation, using the mstool49python package to backmap the protein and common lipid molecules, including DOPC, POPC, DPPC, DMPC, DSPC, DOPE, POPE, DOPS, POPS, DOPG, POPG, DOPA, POPA, SAPI, PI2A, PI2B, PI3A, PI3B, PI3C, and cholesterol (CHOL) (Fig. 1). The backward python script59 may be used to backmap uncommon lipid types and other molecules (Fig. 1). The CG-backmapped AA simulation system is automatically resolvated and ionized by OpenMM53, with the box dimensions kept fixed to previous iterations. When mstool is used, the software included a relaxation step within itself involving the Reduced Nonbonded Energy Minimization (REM) workflow49. The resulting AA structure was further minimized in OpenMM53 using a root-mean-square force tolerance of 1.0 kJ mol− 1 nm− 1 with a maximum of 5000 iterations before equilibration, as described above. The next AA simulation is performed, and the AA-CG-AA cycles are repeated.

Simulation system set up and analysis

We evaluated iMMD on four representative model systems, including two globular proteins in an aqueous environment (i.e., the fast-folding variant of Trpcage and Z-matrix protein of the LASV) and two membrane bound proteins (i.e., the Torpedo nAChR in a heterogenous CHOL/POPC lipid bilayer, and binding of two REGN7663 Fabs to dimerizing CXCR4s in a CHOL/POPC lipid bilayer). We prepared the simulation systems of fast-folding Trpcage and LASV Z-matrix protein starting from the extended conformations of the 2JOF55 and 2M1S56 PDB structures, respectively. The solution simulation systems were automatically prepared by iMMD through the solvation of the proteins in 0.15 M NaCl solutions (Fig. 1). Since the model membrane-bound systems of Torpedo nAChR and CXCR4s in complex with REGN7663 Fabs involved a non-phospholipid molecule (i.e., CHOL), we needed to embed the proteins in the heterogeneous lipid bilayers by the CHARMM-GUI webserver78,79 prior to solvating the assembly in 0.15 M NaCl solutions in iMMD. We prepared the simulation systems of Torpedo nAChR and CXCR4s in complex with REGN7663 Fabs starting from the 4AQ980 and 8U4S61 PDB structures, respectively. The missing regions within the 4AQ980 PDB structure were modeled using the SWISS-MODEL webserver81. The 8U4S61 PDB structure is a trimeric structure of CXCR4s in complex with REGN7663 Fabs, and we took two REGN7663 Fabs and two CXCR4 receptors from the PDB structure to prepare our simulation system. The two REGN7663 Fabs were removed from the extracellular pockets of the CXCR4 receptors and placed 20 Å away from the extracellular mouths of the CXCR4s, and the two CXCR4s were placed 20 Å away from each other. As described in the Results section, the simulations of two CXCR4s with two REGN7663 Fabs should only be treated as demonstrations for evaluations rather than validated binding models. We primarily used the AmberTools82 along with the MDAnalysis83,84python package in analyzing the simulation trajectories obtained from the iMMD simulations. We used a definition of ≤ 9 Å between Cα atoms of at least three residues apart to define contacts.
