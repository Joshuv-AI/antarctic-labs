Effect of acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) pretreatment on white noise-induced stress model in rats
Authors: Deniz Aktaran Bala, Ibrahim Akyazi, Evren Eraslan, Feraye Esen Gursel, Sibel Akyol, Nuran Dariyerli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnvironmental noise is a non-specific stressor that can activate stress-response pathways and increase the production of reactive oxygen species, contributing to oxidative stress in the brain. Limbic regions such as the amygdala and hippocampus play key roles in stress processing and are particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage. Acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) has been reported to possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine whether ASA pretreatment provides protective effects against white noise-induced oxidative stress and inflammation, both systemically and in brain tissue. In the study, ALT (Alanine aminotransferase), creatinine, glucose, total protein, HDL (high-density lipoprotein), urea, uric acid, plasma albumin, cholesterol, and cortisol levels were measured from plasma samples. Malondialdehyde (MDA) and ascorbic acid (vitamin C) levels were measured as an antioxidant from brain tissue homogenates of the whole brain, cortex, cerebellum, amygdala, and hippocampus. interleukin 1β (IL-1β), IL-2, IL-6, granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GMCSF), interferon gamma ( IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), IL-4 and IL-10 levels were measured from plasma samples.ResultsIn the present study, it was found that the MDA level in the amygdala tissue was significantly lower in the animals in ASA + stress group which underwent ASA pretreatment compared to the other groups (p ≤ 0.05). There was no statistically significant difference in MDA levels in the amygdala tissues of the other three groups. A significant decrease was observed in ASA control group. MDA values in cortex tissue compared to stress control group (p ≤ 0.05). There was no significant difference between the groups regarding MDA levels of other brain regions. When whole brain tissue homogenates were examined in the ASA pretreatment groups, a significant decrease was observed in the vitamin C levels of the animals in ASA + stress group compared to the other groups. (p ≤ 0.05).There was no statistically significant change in IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, GMCSF, IFN-γ, and IL-10 levels. It was observed that TNF-α levels increased in the groups given aspirin, and this increase was significant in ASA control group compared to stress control group (p ≤ 0.05). In the study, there was no significant change in plasma biochemical parameters ALT, creatinine, glucose, total protein, HDL, urea, and uric acid evaluations. However, plasma albumin levels were found to be higher than in control group (p ≤ 0.05). Plasma cholesterol levels were found to be decreased in stress control group compared to control group (p ≤ 0.05). When plasma cortisol levels were examined between the groups, a significant increase was observed in stress control group compared to control group (p ≤ 0.05).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that acetylsalicylic acid may influence selected oxidative stress-related parameters in specific brain regions following white noise exposure. However, the observed effects were limited and not consistent across all biochemical and inflammatory markers examined. Further studies are needed to clarify the optimal dose and duration of ASA treatment for controlling stress-related effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05658-7.

Introduction

Stress is a condition triggered by psychological, physiological, or environmental stressors, in which homeostasis is threatened [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines stress as a state of anxiety or mental tension resulting from a difficult situation. Globally, stress-related conditions such as depression and anxiety lead to the loss of approximately 12 billion working days each year, representing an estimated economic cost of nearly 1 trillion USD annually [2]. These figures highlight the substantial impact of stress on public health worldwide. Studies examining the effects of physiological and psychological stress on health date back many years. Various findings have been noted in these stress studies on humans and animals [3–5]. This suggests that stress on living things is not experienced in the same way, and its impact on physical and mental health is quite complex [6]. Stressors are internal or extrinsic stimuli that activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system, leading to a physiological change [7].

A stressful situation leads to an overproduction of free radicals, which in turn causes oxidative stress, i.e., an imbalance in the oxidant/antioxidant system [8, 9]. Free oxygen radicals are physiological byproducts of aerobic cell metabolism [10]. Although these unstable compounds are formed physiologically during normal metabolic events, they can damage cellular lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids in the DNA strand when the antioxidant balance is disturbed [11].

Noise has become a major source of stress in modern society, affecting people's health and animal welfare. It is generally accepted that noise has the potential to act as a non-specific stressor [12]. White noise is a broadband acoustic stimulus containing a wide range of frequencies presented simultaneously and continuously. In experimental studies, controlled white noise exposure is frequently used as a standardized auditory stimulus to model environmental noise exposure in rats [13].Noise generated by sounds exceeding 90 dB in intensity is also among these stress factors [12]. Many studies have been conducted on the effects of noise-induced stress. For example it has been reported that oxidative stress occurs in living beings exposed to such acute or chronic noise [14].

The brain, which is the foundation of the central nervous system, perceives, evaluates and responds to environmental stressors and is also the target organ for stress hormones [15–17]. The brain is more sensitive to oxidative damage compared to other organs due to its richness in polyunsaturated fatty acids, relatively weak defense mechanisms, and high levels of oxygen consumption [18]. This situation also makes the brain a target for all types of noise-induced stress. Studies have shown that oxidative stress increases in plasma and brain tissue in rats exposed to noise-induced stress [9, 19, 20]. Although acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) is one of the oldest drugs in clinical use and has been extensively studied, the mechanisms underlying its multifaceted biological effects have not yet been fully clarified [21]. When the balance between oxidants and antioxidants in biological systems shifts towards oxidants, oxidative stress occurs [22]. In vivo biomolecular damage is initiated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and is defined as oxidative stress [23, 24]. However, studies have shown that ASA reduces oxidative stress by acting as a free radical scavenger with its antioxidant properties, and some of its multifaceted activity has been attributed to this property [25–27]. ASA is the oldest and most widely used non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) drug with analgesic, antipyretic, and antiplatelet effects [28]. Studies have shown that acetylsalicylic acid is effective in daily use for preventive purposes for diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, cerebral ischemia, colon cancer, and Alzheimer's due to its multifaceted therapeutic activity [21, 29–31].

This study aims to determine whether ASA pretreatment provides protective effects against white noise–induced oxidative stress and inflammation, both systemically and within brain tissue. A review of the literature indicates that studies investigating this relationship remain limited. Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were determined as a marker of oxidative stress, while ascorbic acid (vitamin C) levels were evaluated as an indicator of antioxidant status in tissue homogenates. In addition, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine, glucose, total protein, HDL, urea, uric acid, plasma albumin, cholesterol, and cortisol levels were analyzed as biochemical parameters. Furthermore, the effects of stress and ASA were investigated in different brain regions, including the cortex, cerebellum, amygdala, and hippocampus.

The levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, GMCSF, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, were measured from the collected plasma samples in the present study. The anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 and IL-10 were also detected.

The findings of the present study may contribute to the clarification of the effects of acetylsalicylic acid, which is recommended for daily use for protection against many diseases ranging from cardiovascular diseases to Alzheimer's, on noise-induced stress, which is a very important problem for both human health and animal welfare.

Materials and methods

Animals and housing conditions

The studies were conducted on 24 male Sprague Dawley rats (purchased from the Institute of Experimental Medicine of Istanbul University, Istanbul, Turkey) weighing 250 ± 10 g. The animals were housed in groups of 4 in polycarbonate cages with sawdust bedding (four rats/cage) with at least 250 cm2 of housing space per animal and were given standard pellet rat chow (supplemented with ASA for two different groups) and water ad libitum. Animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions (22 ± 3 °C ambient temperature, relative humidity of 60% ± 5%, artificial 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle).

Istanbul University Local Ethics Committee approved all experimental procedures. (Approval date: 26.04.2012).

Animal groups and the procedures

ASA treatment

Animals in the ASA control and ASA + stress groups were fed ASA (PMI Feeds, Inc., St. Louis, MO, USA)-supplemented feed for 2 months under standard laboratory conditions. The ASA dose to be applied was determined in light of information obtained from the literature, and the feed additive rate was calculated to provide 150 mg/kg ASA per day, considering the body weight and feed consumption of the animals [32].

These human doses were scaled according to Kleiber's rule [33] as follows: dose (rat)/dose (human) = BW0.25 (rat)/BW0.25 (human) (BW = body weight) [34].

Taking into account the daily food consumption of the rats, corresponding amounts of ASA were added to the standard rat feed material prior to pelleting.

During these two months, the other groups were kept in the same environment and fed with standard food. At the end of the two-month ASA pretreatment, animals in groups 3 and 4 were subjected to stress. As a stress treatment, animals were taken to an isolated sound cabin for 4 h between 11:00 and 15:00 every other day and exposed to 100 dB white noise. Animals in groups 1 and 2 were simultaneously kept in a second cabin with the same characteristics without being exposed to sound stress. The stress treatment was continued every other day for 15 days (8 sessions in total).

Sample collection and analyses

At the end of this period, euthanasia was performed by taking blood from the animals by cardiac puncture under general anesthesia (xylazine/ketamine, 10/75 mg/kg). Blood samples obtained from rats were collected into EDTA-coated tubes. Complete blood count analysis was performed using an automated hematology analyzer (Medonic 570, Boule Medical, Sweden). Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate plasma, which was then collected and stored at − 80 °C until further biochemical analysis [35].

Biochemical parameters such as ALT, creatinine, glucose, total protein, HDL, urea, uric acid, albumin, cholesterol and cortisol levels were examined in the obtained plasmas. Biochemical parameters were measured using spectrophotometric methods with commercial kits (Randox Laboratories, UK; catalog no. 562616).

After euthanasia, brain tissue was removed via necropsy. After the brain tissues were frozen, they were placed in brain matrix produced for rats (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA, catalog no.69026-C) and cut into 2-mm thick slices (− 2.76 to − 4.76 mm from the bregma) using brain matrix razor blades (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA, catalog no.70933–70). With the purpose of examining the interaction of stress and ASA in different brain regions, the cortex, cerebellum, amygdala, and hippocampus were removed with a 2-mm diameter punch tool from one hemisphere according to the brain atlas of Paxinos and Watson [36]. The other hemisphere was used as a whole brain. All tissues obtained were homogenized. Brain tissues were homogenized in TNG-T buffer using a rotor–stator homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and the supernatant fluid was used for biochemical analyses [37]. As indications of oxidative stress, levels of ascorbic acid (vitamin C) and MDA were assessed from the homogenates. The thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS) assay was used to determine the MDA level. The method of Yoshioko et al. [38] was used to quantify plasma TBARS. The interaction of two thiobarbituric acid molecules with one MDA molecule served as the basis for the experiment. A colorful compound with a maximum absorbance of 532 nm was created as a result.

In the presented study, the levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, GMCSF, IFN-γ,TNF-α were examined from the plasma samples obtained. Similarly, the levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 and IL-10 were measured. Cytokine levels were determined using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Invitrogen, Camarillo, CA, USA) in tissue homogenates and plasma samples.

Statistics

A 2 × 2 factorial analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to examine the main effects of ASA administration and stress exposure, as well as their interaction, on the outcome measures. Interaction effects were of particular interest because they indicate whether ASA treatment modified the biological responses induced by stress exposure. For each parameter, the ANOVA yielded three effects: the main effect of stress exposure, the main effect of ASA administration, and their interaction (Stress × ASA). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Tukey HSD test following significant ANOVA results. The data were assumed to follow a normal distribution based on the experimental design and precedent in similar published studies; formal normality testing was not performed due to the small sample size (n = 6 per group), which is acknowledged as a limitation. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 17.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level was set at p ≤ 0.05. Complete ANOVA results including F values, degrees of freedom, exact p-values, and partial eta squared (η2p) for all parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the potential protective effects of ASA pretreatment against white noise–induced stress. Our findings suggest that ASA pretreatment exerts differential effects across brain regions with respect to oxidative stress parameters. In particular, ASA administration was associated with reduced MDA levels, a marker of lipid peroxidation, and alterations in vitamin C levels in the examined tissues. In addition, stress exposure is known to trigger changes in circulating inflammatory cytokines during both acute and chronic stress conditions [39]. In our study, TNF-α levels were significantly higher in the ASA control group compared with the stress control group. This finding indicates that ASA administration under the present experimental conditions may influence inflammatory responses. However, the mechanisms underlying this observation cannot be determined from the current data. Although ASA has been reported to exhibit antioxidant properties, including scavenging reactive oxygen species and reducing oxidative stress [40–42], the relationship between these effects and the increase in TNF-α observed in the present study remains unclear. Therefore, this result should be interpreted cautiously, and further studies are required to clarify the mechanisms involved. [27]. Qiu et al. [43] in their study, examining the protective effects and mechanism of aspirin on focal cerebral ischemia–reperfusion in rats, reported that aspirin reduced the content of MDA, an indicator of oxidative stress, and lipid peroxides in the brain tissue on the occluded side. In line with our findings another study that examined the protective effects of Xuesaitong and aspirin against cerebral ischemia and reperfusion injury. The study found that the combination decreased MDA levels and, in contrast to our findings, decreased TNF-α levels in the plasma, cerebral cortex, and hippocampus of rats [44]. TNF-α is a proinflammatory cytokine that regulates the production and release of inflammatory mediators [45]. There are widespread studies that stress can cause brain damage [43, 46]. De Cristóbal et al. [46], reported that acute (6 h) stress exposure in rats caused an increase in plasma glutamate and brain TNF–α, induction of oxidative indicators in the brain and a decrease in brain ATP levels, and aspirin blocked all effects. Contrary to our study findings, there are also findings in other stress studies that aspirin's effectiveness is directed towards decreasing TNF–α [27, 47, 48]. In addition, a report that compiles studies on how aspirin and other cyclooxygenase inhibitors affect the course of multiple myeloma disease mentions a two-phase dose response They reported that high aspirin doses inhibit TNF-α production, but low doses can increase TNF-α production [48]. Considering these results, the increase in TNF-α levels in the aspirin-only group compared to the stress-only group in our study may be due to the dose of aspirin that we applied.

In our study, it was observed that vitamin C levels decreased in the stress and ASA + stress group compared to the other groups. Vitamin C is a powerful antioxidant, acts inside and outside the cell, and provides a protective effect against many diseases, including oxidative imbalances that occur for various reasons in the brain and other tissues [49]. Contrary to our findings, many studies have reported the ability of vitamin C to reduce oxidative damage in the brain or other tissues [50, 51]. Some studies also find that vitamin C acts as a pro-oxidant (reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, free radicals) due to the environment in which it is found [49, 52]. Vitamin C is a major antioxidant involved in cellular defense against oxidative stress. In the present study, vitamin C levels were decreased in the ASA + stress group. Although vitamin C has been reported to exhibit pro-oxidant properties under certain conditions [53], such as in the presence of transition metals, the mechanism underlying the observed decrease in vitamin C levels cannot be determined from the present data. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, and further studies are required to clarify its biological significance.

Activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis is a fundamental component of the physiological stress response. Therefore, circulating glucocorticoids, which are the end products of this axis, are commonly used as biochemical markers of stress [54]. In rodents, corticosterone is widely accepted as the primary glucocorticoid hormone [55]. Nevertheless, cortisol has also been reported to serve as an indicator of stress, since the synthesis and release of both hormones are regulated by similar mechanisms within the HPA axis [56, 57]. Previous experimental studies have also measured plasma cortisol levels in rats and used them as indicators of stress. In the present study, cortisol levels were measured because the analytical methods available in our laboratory are optimized for cortisol-specific analysis. However, the use of cortisol instead of corticosterone should be regarded as a limitation of this study.

In our study, the increase in cortisol levels in the stress exposed group was found to be statistically significant compared to the control group. Noise exposure activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and stimulates the release of stress hormones. [58, 59]. There are many studies showing that noise increases steroid levels [20, 60]. Similarly, according to a study by Lee et al. [58], cortisol levels a crucial sign of stress went up during the first 15 days. The increase in cortisol levels in the stress group compared to the control group shows the effectiveness of the noise-induced stress we applied. The absence of a significant change in cortisol levels in the other ASA + stress group shows that aspirin treatment may have an effect on cortisol levels. Guan et al. [61] reported that aspirin administration significantly reduced the corticosterone increase caused by the forced swim test. In our study, we couldn’t find the significant effect but aspirin treated groups corticosteron levels were not significantly different than those of control and stress groups.

In our study, it was determined that the albumin value, increased significantly in stress and ASA + stress group. Changes in the function and molecular structure of plasma albumin have been reported in many diseases, including liver disease [62]. It is argued that possible changes in albumin also affect the progression of diseases associated with oxidative stress [63, 64]. Bito et al. [63], tried to verify whether denatured albumins generally increase under oxidative stress conditions and reported that oxidative stress increased the levels of denatured albumin (D2 and D3 albumin) in circulating blood in rats.

In the study, it was observed that cholesterol levels decreased in other groups compared to the control group. This decrease was found to be significant in the stress group. In a study examining the effects of stress and exercise on plasma corticosterone, plasma cholesterol and aortic cholesterol levels in rats, running wheel training was performed for 3 h immediately after different stress sessions. Stressed animals that were allowed to run were found to have lower plasma corticosterone levels than stressed animals that were not allowed to run. In general, aortic cholesterol levels of stressed animals were lower than those of non-stressed animals [65]. There are reports in many studies that stress increases cholesterol levels [66, 67]. Whether or not an organism can control a stressful event is an important variable that determines the effect of the event on physiology and behavior. However, many behavioral and physiological variables are more negatively affected by uncontrolled stress. In this respect, the possible mediators of stress-induced cholesterol increases and the fact that these mediators may be insensitive to stress control are among the discussed reports [68].

Overall, the findings of the present study indicate that ASA pretreatment was associated with changes in selected oxidative stress-related parameters following white noise exposure. The most prominent finding was a reduction in MDA levels in the amygdala, whereas most biochemical and inflammatory parameters did not show significant or consistent treatment-related changes. In addition, the decrease in whole-brain vitamin C levels and the increase in TNF-α levels observed in ASA-treated animals suggest that the biological effects of ASA under stress conditions may be complex and require cautious interpretation. Therefore, the results should be considered limited to the specific parameters and brain regions examined in the present study. Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying these observations and to determine the dose-dependent effects of ASA under environmental stress conditions.

Study limitation

A limitation of the present study is that oxidative stress was evaluated using a limited number of biochemical markers. The assessment was restricted to TBARS (MDA) and vitamin C levels due to the availability of analytical kits in our laboratory. Therefore, additional oxidative stress parameters, such as antioxidant enzyme activities (e.g., superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase) or other lipid peroxidation markers, were not included in the analysis. Future studies incorporating a broader panel of oxidative stress biomarkers would provide a more comprehensive biochemical evaluation.
