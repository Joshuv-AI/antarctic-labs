Identification of high-risk SNPs in SLC22A transporter genes: their potential role in PCOS and metformin uptake
Authors: R. Harini, B. Usha, R. Satish, Pai Akshatha, T. M. Vijaya kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polycystic ovary syndrome is a prevalent, heterogeneous endocrine and metabolic disorder affecting approximately 10% of women of reproductive age, often characterized by hyperandrogenism, anovulation, and insulin resistance. Metformin, a first-line treatment for type 2 diabetes, is also widely prescribed for PCOS to reduce insulin resistance and induce ovulation. However, therapeutic response varies significantly across individuals, partly due to genetic variations in solute carrier (SLC) transporters. The organic cation transporter family, encoded by SLC22A1 (O15245), SLC22A2 (O15244), and SLC22A3 (O75751), facilitates metformin uptake into hepatocytes and renal excretion. Using an in silico pipeline, Align GVGD, PolyPhen-2, PROVEAN, PANTHER, PhD-SNP, and the ensemble predictor Meta-SNP, we screened 14 deleterious nonsynonymous SNPs. Solvent accessibility values were derived using NetSurfP, indicating that L42R (RSA: 0.54, ASA: 96.2 Å2) and F422S (RSA: 0.49, ASA: 85.7 Å2) are surface-exposed and likely to influence protein interaction. In contrast, R175L (RSA: 0.07, ASA: 18.4 Å2) and T275M (RSA: 0.06, ASA: 21.1 Å2) were buried and may impact local folding. ΔΔG predictions using MuPro ranged from −0.01745 to −1.99395, consistently indicating decreased protein stability. Structural models were built in SWISS-MODEL and validated via MolProbity scores (<1.5), ERRAT (>85%), and PROSA Z-scores (−6.8 to −8.2). Functional enrichment of the PPI network (STRING-DB) highlighted significant roles in drug transmembrane transport (GO:0006857, p = 2·3e-06), organic cation transport (GO:0015695, p = 4·7e-05), and xenobiotic metabolism pathways. These mutations may impair metformin pharmacokinetics and contribute to inter-individual therapeutic variability. While this study is limited by its computational design, the findings identify high-priority SNPs for future experimental validation, offering a foundation for precision pharmacogenomics in PCOS management.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome is a multifaceted endocrine disorder affecting approximately 10% of females, characterized by irregular or absent ovulation, elevated androgen levels, and the presence of polycystic ovaries [1], ]. Beyond its impact on reproduction, PCOS is associated with long-term metabolic disturbances, including diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease [2]. Physiologically, ovarian theca cells which are specialized cells found in the ovary, surrounding each developing ovarian follicle, play a pivotal role in supporting follicular development and facilitating the maturation of oocytes. Remarkably, studies have shown that ovarian theca cells derived from individuals with PCOS exhibit heightened insulin sensitivity, contrasting with the insulin resistance observed in peripheral tissues [3]. This dysregulation in insulin responsiveness within ovarian theca cells focuses on the complex interplay between endocrine dysfunction and reproductive abnormalities in PCOS. The theca cells in individuals with PCOS exhibit hyper-responsiveness to insulin stimulation, leading to ovarian hyperthecosis which is a condition where theca cells become excessively active producing large amounts of androgens [4]. Concurrently, insulin resistance in peripheral tissues androgen production within the theca cells, contributing to the manifestation of PCOS symptoms. Furthermore, the heightened sensitivity of theca cells to gonadotropin stimulation by gonadal steroids contributes to the hyperandrogenic state observed in PCOS. These dysregulations commonly result in anovulation and menstrual irregularities, which are prevalent concerns among women of reproductive age [3, 4].

Metformin, an off-label medication utilized as an insulin sensitizer in patients with PCOS, exhibits multifaceted effects on metabolic and endocrine parameters. It exerts notable influences such as the reduction of elevated insulin levels, androgens, and circulating free testosterone levels, alongside elevating Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin and Insulin-like Growth Factor-Binding Protein levels [5]. Its primary mechanism involves the suppression of hepatic glucose production. Additionally, Metformin has been observed to augment SHBG synthesis and enhance parameters related to menstrual regularity, ovulation, conception, and live birth rates. Nonetheless, the therapeutic efficacy of Metformin varies among PCOS patients, with approximately 30% exhibiting non-responsive outcomes to the treatment [6].

The Organic Cation Transporter proteins, integral constituents of the SLC22 family, serve as pivotal mediators in the transmembrane transport of organic cations [7]. Metformin, a widely prescribed pharmaceutical agent for the management of diverse metabolic disorders, including PCOS, is notably recognized as a substrate for the liver-specific OCT1 and the kidney-abundant OCT2. These transport proteins, encoded by the genes OCT1 (SLC22A1) and OCT2 (SLC22A2) respectively, significantly influence the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of metformin [8]. Metformin belongs to the biguanide class of antihyperglycemic agents, primarily used in the management of type 2 diabetes and widely prescribed for PCOS [9]. Unlike other antidiabetic drugs, it does not induce insulin secretion but rather reduces hepatic glucose output and enhances insulin sensitivity. Its clinical utility hinges on effective cellular uptake, which is mediated by OCTs, particularly those encoded by the SLC22A gene family [10]. Consequently, single nucleotide polymorphisms within these transporter genes may alter drug transport, influencing metformin’s bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. Understanding the functional consequences of these genetic variants is vital for optimizing individualized metformin therapy, especially in PCOS patients who already present with metabolic heterogeneity. This pharmacogenomic insight lays the foundation for personalized medicine approaches in endocrine-metabolic disorders [10, 11].

Pharmacoresistance to metformin remains a pressing concern in clinical endocrinology and reproductive medicine. Classified as a biguanide, metformin functions primarily through activation of AMP-activated protein kinase, reducing hepatic gluconeogenesis and improving peripheral insulin sensitivity. Its cellular uptake and distribution are predominantly mediated by the solute carrier transporter proteins, particularly SLC22A1 (OCT1), SLC22A2 (OCT2), and SLC22A3 (OCT3), which are highly polymorphic and tissue-specific. Genetic variations in these transporters can drastically influence metformin’s absorption, hepatic uptake, renal clearance, and intracellular retention [12]. For instance, SNPs in SLC22A1 have been shown to reduce hepatic metformin uptake, leading to diminished AMPK activation and therapeutic response [13]. This interindividual variability contributes to poor glycemic control, especially in patients with PCOS, a condition marked by both endocrine and metabolic irregularities. Moreover, clinical studies in women with PCOS have revealed differential metformin responses associated with SLC22A variants, pointing toward a pharmacogenomic basis for therapy resistance [14]. Understanding these transporter-related SNPs is critical not only for therapeutic optimization but also for advancing personalized medicine strategies in polygenic disorders like PCOS.

Scientific investigations have delineated the structural and functional attributes of OCTs, identifying a minimum of three distinct subtypes: OCT1, OCT2, and OCT3. Remarkably, the genetic loci for OCT1 and OCT2 are closely clustered on chromosome 2q26, whereas OCT3‘s gene is positioned on chromosome 6q27 [15]. This comprehension of the genetic architecture of OCTs and their involvement in metformin transport yield crucial insights into the intricate mechanisms governing individual variations in response to metformin treatment among patients with PCOS [16].

Genetic variants within genes encoding SLC22A transporters may exert influence on transporter functionality, thereby contributing to the observed interindividual variability in drug disposition [17]. The advent of genomic technologies, epitomized by the monumental Human Genome Project, has facilitated the comprehensive mapping of the human genome. Through the utilization of abundant genetic data derived from this initiative, researchers have delved into the intricacies of genes, genetic variants, and their prevalence within the population [18, 19]. The advancement of genomic research has propelled the field of pharmacogenomics and pharmacogenetics, leading to the identification of genetic markers, primarily in the form of single nucleotide polymorphisms [18, 20]. These markers play a crucial role in modulating treatment efficacy and safety by influencing drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics [20, 21], including polymorphisms in genes encoding transporter proteins such as SLC22As. The human genome exhibits an impressive degree of homology, with approximately 99.9% identical DNA sequences shared globally, while the remaining 0.1% encompasses individual variations [20]. These variations arise from random mutations, with SNPs being the most prevalent type, characterized by a single base substitution in the alleles. SNPs are distributed throughout the genome at a frequency of 1 in 1,000 base pairs, with clinically significant SNPs typically localized within coding regions [19, 20]. Previous studies have demonstrated that nonsynonymous single nucleotide polymorphisms (nsSNPs) account for approximately 50% of mutations associated with various genetic disorders [20, 21]. Structural and functional analysis of nsSNPs holds promise for advancing personalized medicine based on genomic variability. However, the precise impact of nsSNPs on the pathogenesis of diseases related to SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 proteins remains poorly understood. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the functional implications of nsSNPs in SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 using a range of bioinformatics tools and servers.

Materials & methods

Retrieval of OCT1–OCT3 nsSNPs

The reference protein sequences for the three human Organic Cation Transporters, SLC22A1 (UniProt Accession ID: O15245), SLC22A2 (UniProt Accession ID: O15244), and SLC22A3 (UniProt Accession ID: O75751), were retrieved in FASTA format from the UniProt Knowledgebase (UniProtKB) [https://www.uniprot.org/] [22]. These sequences were used as the basis for downstream in silico analysis of nonsynonymous single nucleotide polymorphisms. To obtain the complete list of nsSNPs associated with the above genes, the NCBI dbSNP database [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/] [23] was queried using the corresponding Gene IDs and Accession Numbers. Only validated SNPs located within the coding regions (i.e., exonic nonsynonymous variants) were selected for further structural and functional assessment. The collected variants were further annotated with genomic position, nucleotide change, amino acid substitution, and dbSNP rsIDs for consistent reference across analyses.

Identification of deleterious SNPs

To assess the functional implications of nonsynonymous single nucleotide polymorphisms identified in SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3, we employed a multilayered bioinformatic prediction strategy. This included six independent yet complementary tools, each based on distinct algorithmic principles, to ensure robustness, sensitivity, and cross-validation of deleterious variant predictions. Variants that were consistently predicted to be pathogenic across all tools were prioritized for downstream structural and functional modeling.

In this study, we utilized six distinct bioinformatics tools, including Align GVGD (http://agvgd.hci.utah.edu/), PolyPhen-2 (Polymorphism Phenotyping v2) (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/), PANTHER cSNP (Protein Analysis Through Evolutionary Relationship-CodingSNP) (http://pantherdb.org/tools/csnpScoreForm.jsp), PROVEAN (Protein Variation Effect Analyzer) (http://provean.jcvi.org/index.php), and PhD-SNP (Predictor of Human Deleterious Single Nucleotide Polymorphism) (https://snps.biofold.org/phd-snp/phd-snp.html), to predict the functional effects of all SNPs associated with SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 retrieved from dbSNP [24, 25].

The Align GVGD [26], a semi-quantitative tool, integrates the degree of evolutionary conservation at specific codon positions from. It classifies substitutions into a continuum of classes ranging from C0 (least likely to interfere with protein function) to C65 (most likely to interfere). In our study, we considered only those variants scoring C45 and above as potentially function-altering, reflecting a high probability of detrimental structural consequences.

PolyPhen-2 [http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/] combines sequence conservation, 3D protein structure, and known functional annotations to predict the impact of amino acid substitutions on protein function. It uses a naive Bayes classifier to compute a score between 0.0 and 1.0, where values above 0.85 are interpreted as “probably damaging”, 0.15 to 0.85 as “possibly damaging”, and below 0.15 as “benign”. Only substitutions classified as “probably damaging” were retained for further evaluation.

PANTHER [https://www.hsls.pitt.edu/obrc/index.php?page=URL1100614269] uses the PSEP (position-specific evolutionary preservation) algorithm, which estimates how long a particular amino acid residue has been preserved in the evolutionary history of that protein family [27]. Variants affecting residues preserved for over 450 million years were considered “probably damaging”, those between 200 and 450 million years as “possibly damaging”, and residues conserved for less than 200 million years were categorized as “probably benign”. In this study, only variants mapped to highly conserved residues were selected.

PROVEAN [http://provean.jcvi.org] [28] determines the likely impact of amino acid substitutions or indels based on sequence homology clustering. It assigns a continuous score, where values ≤ −2.5 suggest a deleterious effect, and values > −2.5 indicate neutrality. The PROVEAN scoring approach hinges on comparing the similarity of protein sequences with and without the mutation across homologs, allowing it to flag substitutions that disrupt evolutionary conservation. We used the −2.5 threshold as a stringent criterion for damaging predictions.

PhD-SNP [https://snps.biofold.org/phd-snp/phd-snp.html] [29] is an SVM-based classifier trained on a curated dataset of disease-related and neutral mutations. It uses protein sequence, mutation site, and altered residue as input and outputs both the classification (disease vs. neutral) and a confidence score. Mutations with scores above 0.5 were considered disease-associated in our pipeline. The strength of PhD-SNP lies in its ability to recognize complex non-linear relationships between sequence features and phenotypic outcomes.

To strengthen the reliability of deleterious SNP predictions, we employed Meta-SNP [30] [https://snps.biofold.org/meta-snp/], a robust meta-predictor that integrates the outputs of multiple individual classifiers into a single ensemble model. Meta-SNP evaluates concordance among these algorithms and generates a consolidated pathogenicity score along with a binary classification (disease vs. neutral). In our analysis, a Meta-SNP score greater than 0.5 was interpreted as indicative of pathogenicity.

Only those nsSNPs that were consistently classified as deleterious or disease-associated by all five primary tools, and further reaffirmed by Meta-SNP were considered high-confidence pathogenic candidates. These variants were subsequently shortlisted for structural modeling and functional impact analyses, thereby ensuring a stringent, consensus-based approach to SNP prioritization.

Minor allele frequency Retrieval

To evaluate the population-level relevance of the selected high-risk nsSNPs in SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3, we employed the Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) tool available at Ensembl.org [31]. This tool integrates information from multiple genomic databases, including gnomAD [32], to provide annotations such as allele frequency, protein position, codon change, and predicted consequence. Minor allele frequencies (MAF) were extracted for each variant across different ethnic groups including African (AFR), American (AMR), East Asian (EAS), South Asian (SAS), Non-Finnish European (NFE), and others.

Structural & Topological mapping

To determine the structural context of the identified nsSNPs within the SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 proteins, we used protein annotations obtained from UniProtKB (https://www.uniprot.org/) [22], which provides experimentally validated and computationally inferred transmembrane topology. These domain annotations, including cytoplasmic, extracellular, and helical (transmembrane) regions, were cross-verified using the InterPro protein family classification tool (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) [33]. Each variant’s position was mapped manually to determine its domain localization, thereby helping infer its possible structural and functional implications.

Effect of nsSNPs on protein stability

The nsSNPs have the potential to substantially alter both the structural stability and function of proteins. Therefore, assessing the stability of deleterious nsSNPs is crucial and serves as a key parameter in such analyses. MuPro (http://mupro.proteomics.ics.uci.edu/) is a computational tool designed to predict alterations in protein stability by examining protein structures or sequences. It calculates shifts in protein stability, as indicated by corresponding Delta Delta G values, resulting from mutational changes [34].

Effect of nsSNPs on solvent accessibility

NetSurfP-2.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/NetSurfP/) is a computational tool utilized to predict surface accessibility, secondary structure, disorder, and phi/psi dihedral angles of amino acids within protein sequences. The fasta sequences corresponding to SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 were submitted for analysis, elucidating both buried and exposed regions within the protein structures [25].

Protein modeling and structure prediction of SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3

The three-dimensional structures of both native and mutated forms of SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 were generated using the SWISS-MODEL (http://swissmodel.expasy.org/) to assess and compare their structural stability. SWISS-MODEL [35] employs a hierarchical approach to protein modeling, refinement, and functional annotation by utilizing relevant templates from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). The web server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) utilizes the updated UniProtKB proteome for homology modeling, with input queries comprising FASTA sequences. Metrics provided include Ramachandran plot analysis, QMEAN, and Molprobity scores for structural validation [35]. PROCHECK [36] (https://servicesn.mbi.ucla.edu/PROCHECK/) assesses stereochemical quality, while ERRAT (https://servicesn.mbi.ucla.edu/ERRAT/) evaluates overall model quality based on characteristic atomic interactions using predicted models in PDB format [37]. ProSA-web (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php) refines and validates experimental protein structures, employing predicted structures in PDB format for model quality estimation [38].

Prediction of protein-protein interaction

Preserving protein interactions is vital for maintaining system homeostasis. The STRING (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins) database (https://string-db.org/cgi/inputsessionId=croExd7fYyEe&input_page_active_form=single_sequence) integrates protein-protein interaction data from diverse sources, encompassing physical interaction databases and curated biological pathway knowledge [39]. Wild-type protein sequences of SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 were utilized as input for analysis, enabling visualization of their molecular interactions with other proteins.

Functional enrichment analysis

To investigate the biological roles and interconnectivity of SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3, we conducted a functional protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis using STRING and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment. Each protein’s top 10 high-confidence interactors (score > 0.40) were retrieved. GO terms were annotated for both biological processes and molecular function, and enrichment significance was calculated using a hypergeometric test corrected for multiple testing [40].

Pathway enrichment analysis

To gain a deeper understanding of the physiological roles and systemic effects of the SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 transporter networks, pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Reactome database [41]. Each protein’s high-confidence interaction partners (as identified via STRING) were mapped to curated human pathways. This analysis allowed the identification of biologically meaningful clusters in which these transporters participate. The top significantly enriched pathway for each protein were selected based on adjusted p-values and gene ratios. These enriched pathways revealed distinct but overlapping roles for the SLC22 family members.

Results

Retrieval of SNPs associated with SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3

Analysis revealed various genetic variations within each gene. Specifically, SLC22A1 exhibited 7 inframe deletions, 1 unframe indel, 3 inframe insertions, 2 initiator codon variants, 12,563 introns, 576 missense mutations (nsSNPs), 290 non-coding transcript variants, and 261 synonymous mutations. Similarly, SLC22A2 displayed 2 inframe deletions, 1 inframe insertion, 3 initiator codon variants, 14,226 introns, 510 missense mutations, and 202 synonymous mutations. Lastly, SLC22A3 showcased 3 inframe deletions, 1 inframe insertion, 4 initiator codon variants, 33,392 introns, 505 missense mutations, 2724 non-coding transcript variants, and 250 synonymous variants. Although this study primarily focused on missense SNPs for downstream functional impact assessment, the predominance of intronic variants merits closer attention. Intronic polymorphisms, while residing in non-coding regions, are increasingly recognized for their functional and regulatory roles. These variants can influence pre-mRNA splicing by affecting canonical splice donor or acceptor sites or introducing cryptic splice sites, potentially resulting in exon skipping or intron retention. This is particularly relevant to transporters like SLC22 family proteins, where correct splicing is essential for the expression of full-length, functional proteins.

Moreover, intronic SNPs may modulate transcriptional regulation through their impact on transcription factor binding motifs and enhancer/silencer elements located within introns. This can alter gene expression patterns, tissue specificity, and response to external stimuli. For example, intronic variants in the genes have been shown to affect drug transporter expression levels, thereby altering pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Another critical consideration is the role of intronic variants in forming haplotype blocks. These may co-occur with exonic missense or synonymous SNPs and could serve as genetic proxies in linkage disequilibrium studies. In the case of SLC22A3, which had the highest intronic burden, such haplotype-linked effects could influence complex traits, including drug response variability, as the gene is known to modulate neurotransmitter uptake and xenobiotic transport.

Although we did not perform functional validation of intronic variants in this study, the substantial presence of these polymorphisms suggests that future integrative analyses, such as expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) mapping, splicing assays, or transcription factor occupancy studies

Align GVGD analysis categorized a significant proportion of SNVs within each gene (SLC22A1: 55.17%, SLC22A2: 52.38%, SLC22A3: 57.69%) as exhibiting a high likelihood of functional impact (Class 65), suggesting potential significant consequences. Additionally, a notable number of SNVs within each gene (SLC22A1: 44.83%, SLC22A2: 47.62%, SLC22A3: 42.31%) were deemed less likely to have a functional impact. PolyPhen-2 analysis predicted a considerable proportion of SNVs within each gene as probably damaging (SLC22A1: 34.48%, SLC22A2: 42.83%, SLC22A3: 42.31%), with additional SNVs classified as possibly damaging or benign. PANTHER analysis highlighted a considerable proportion of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) within each gene as likely to be deleterious (SLC22A1: 48.28%, SLC22A2: 42.86%, SLC22A3: 42.31%), accompanied by additional SNVs classified as possibly damaging or benign. PROVEAN analysis indicated that a subset of SNVs within each gene was linked to diseases, ranging from 42.83% to 61.90%, while the remaining SNVs were considered neutral. PhD-SNP analysis unveiled varying percentages of SNVs associated with disease across the three genes, predominantly categorized as diseased, with a minority classified as neutral.

Utilizing a composite approach integrating five prediction tools, we identified a total of 4, 3, and 6 significant nsSNPs as potentially damaging, deleterious, and disease-associated, respectively. Subsequent analysis aimed to assess the structural and functional implications of these nsSNPs on the SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 proteins (refer to Table 1). Notably, mutations such as L42R, R175C, R175L, and F422S in SLC22A1, R404C, G373D, and E227A in SLC22A2, and R212C, G259R, T275M, R348W, R407C, and P499S in SLC22A3 were predicted to exert damaging effects, being deemed deleterious and associated with disease.

Among the analyzed missense variants in the SLC22 family, a subset, including R175C, R175L, R404C, R212C, and R348W, were consistently predicted to be deleterious and disease-associated across all five computational tools employed (Align GVGD, PolyPhen-2, PANTHER, PROVEAN, and PhD-SNP). To strengthen the predictive validity, these variants were further cross-validated using the META-SNP meta-predictor, which integrates outputs from multiple algorithms into a consensus score. Notably, these high-risk variants exhibited META-SNP scores above 0.89 and Reliability Index (RI) values of 7 or 8, underscoring their strong likelihood of functional disruption and pathogenic potential. Variants such as R175C, R175L, R404C, and R212C displayed high pathogenicity scores across nearly all tools and attained a high RI value of 8, indicating consistent agreement among the predictors and high confidence in their deleterious nature. These mutations are particularly noteworthy because they show strong conservation (high Align GVGD values), damaging effects on protein function (PolyPhen scores > 0.65), significant evolutionary impact (PANTHER > 0.95), and substantial alterations in protein behavior based on sequence homology (PROVEAN scores < −5). Furthermore, high META-SNP scores (e.g., >0.89 for R175C and R404C) affirm the cumulative deleterious potential of these mutations using meta-predictive models (Fig. 2).

On the other end, variants like T275M and G373D, despite being flagged by some tools, exhibit lower META-SNP scores (<0.70) and RI values of 3, suggesting a lower likelihood of being strongly disease-associated. These SNPs might still play regulatory roles or act as modifiers but may not independently confer high functional disruption. The convergence of high-impact predictions across independent tools, coupled with strong reliability indices, provides robust evidence supporting the prioritization of these mutations for downstream experimental validation and clinical correlation. This integrative approach enhances confidence in identifying biologically relevant, deleterious polymorphisms that may contribute to disease susceptibility or altered transporter function.

MAF profiling of functionally prioritized nsSnps

The inclusion of population-specific MAF data enables an enhanced understanding of the selective pressures acting upon these variants and helps differentiate between common polymorphisms and potentially deleterious rare mutations. A considerable proportion of the analyzed variants were found to have extremely low global allele frequencies (MAF < 0.001). Such rarity in the general population may reflect negative or purifying selection, whereby deleterious alleles are removed over generations due to their impact on protein function and overall fitness. For example, the L42R substitution in SLC22A1 (rs778919765) exhibits a global MAF of 7.12 × 10− 5, with low but detectable frequencies in non-Finnish European and East Asian subgroups. This extremely low frequency, coupled with its consistent prediction as damaging across multiple bioinformatic tools (e.g., PolyPhen-2, PROVEAN, PhD-SNP), suggests that the variant may compromise the function of the OCT1 transporter and thus has been evolutionarily conserved. Similarly, the F422S variant in SLC22A1 (rs780250888) also exhibits a low MAF of 2.17 × 10− 5, further supporting its potential pathogenic. Topological localization and functional implications of identified nsSnps

To elucidate the structural and functional consequences of the high-priority nsSNPs identified in this study, each amino acid substitution was meticulously mapped onto the topological architecture of the corresponding protein (SLC22A1, SLC22A2, or SLC22A3). The L42R variant in SLC22A1, localized within the first transmembrane helix (residues 22–42), is of particular interest. This region is critical for substrate entry and structural anchoring. The substitution of a small, nonpolar leucine with a bulky, positively charged arginine may disrupt the tight packing of helices or induce charge repulsion, potentially impairing substrate affinity or ion coupling, both of which are essential for effective organic cation transport.

Similarly, F422S, also in SLC22A1, maps to the ninth transmembrane domain (403–423). Phenylalanine’s large aromatic side chain contributes to membrane-embedded helix stability; its replacement with serine introduces a polar side chain that may affect helix hydrophobicity and disrupt transporter gating or ligand translocation dynamics. In SLC22A3, the R212C variant lies within the third transmembrane helix (182–202). The replacement of arginine with cysteine, a residue susceptible to oxidation and disulfide bonding, could interfere with helix-helix interactions or alter membrane integration, potentially affecting substrate selectivity or transporter kinetics.

Conversely, several variants were found in cytoplasmic or extracellular loops, which, although not directly involved in substrate transport, are integral to transporter folding, trafficking, and conformational modulation. For example, the R175L and R175C mutations in SLC22A1 reside in a short cytoplasmic linker (171–176). Arginine’s loss may impair charge-dependent folding signals, while the introduction of cysteine could result in aberrant disulfide bridges or misfolding under oxidative stress. In SLC22A2, the G373D mutation is situated in the sixth extracellular loop (370–375). Glycine, a flexible residue often found in turns or loops, is substituted with a negatively charged aspartic acid. This may create steric hindrance or introduce electrostatic interactions, potentially altering extracellular domain dynamics or protein-protein interactions relevant to transporter regulation or cell surface stability.

P499S, a mutation in SLC22A3, is embedded within the final (12th) transmembrane helix (498–518). Proline residues in helices often serve as kinks that facilitate bending and helix mobility. Their substitution can rigidify or destabilize transmembrane domains. Given that this terminal helix is essential for closing the conformational transport cycle, its disturbance may have profound implications on transporter recycling and ion-coupling fidelity. These structurally contextualized observations suggest that variants within transmembrane helices are likely to exert direct biophysical impacts on substrate translocation, while those in loop regions may modulate transporter biosynthesis, folding, or regulatory mechanisms. Prediction of protein stability of the selected nsSnps in SLC22A1, SLC22A2 and SLC22A3

Protein stability is a critical determinant of proper folding, structural integrity, and function. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), especially nonsynonymous SNPs (nsSNPs), can lead to amino acid substitutions that disrupt the delicate thermodynamic balance governing protein folding and stability. MuPro is a machine learning-based tool used to predict changes in protein stability upon point mutations using both sequence and structure-based features. It computes a ΔΔG (Delta Delta G) value that reflects the change in Gibbs free energy associated with a mutation. A negative ΔΔG indicates a decrease in protein stability, whereas a positive value suggests increased stability.

In this study, all nsSNPs shortlisted from Table 1 for SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 were subjected to MuPro analysis to evaluate the structural consequences of these substitutions. The obtained ΔΔG values ranged from −0.01745 to −1.99395, suggesting that all analyzed mutations are predicted to reduce protein stability to varying extents (Table 5). Notably, SLC22A2 variant E227A (rs137885730) showed the most significant reduction in stability with a ΔΔG of −1.99395, followed by L42R in SLC22A1 and P499S in SLC22A3. These findings are functionally relevant, as decreased protein stability often correlates with a higher likelihood of protein misfolding, aggregation, proteasomal degradation, or loss of function. Prediction of protein-protein interaction

SLC22A1 PPI network

The PPI network for SLC22A1 (OCT1) reveals strong functional associations with transporters and enzymes involved in drug disposition, nucleoside trafficking, and membrane transport specificity. The most prominent interaction is with SPNS3 (score: 0.892), a sphingolipid transporter implicated in cell signaling and lipid mediator transport, suggesting a potential link between SLC22A1 activity and lipid signaling pathways. Another critical partner is CERS2 (0.867), which catalyzes ceramide synthesis; this may reflect overlapping roles in membrane remodeling or cell stress response due to xenobiotic transport.

Interactions with SLC28A1 (0.866) and SLC28A2 (0.817), both nucleoside cotransporters, highlight the convergence of purine and pyrimidine uptake systems, possibly indicating shared transport substrates such as antiviral nucleoside analogs (e.g., AZT, ddC). The presence of SLC47A1 (0.860) and SLC47A2 (0.802), key MATE transporters, underscores the central role of OCT1 in phase III drug elimination and intracellular cation clearance mechanisms. Associations with ABCG2 (0.810) and ABCB1 (0.723) further reinforce the multidrug resistance and detoxification theme. These ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters mediate efflux of xenobiotics, often co-regulated with OCT1 expression in hepatic tissue. Furthermore, SLCO1B1 (0.795) and SLCO1B3 (0.743), both organic anion transporters involved in statin and hormone uptake, suggest that SLC22A1 may operate in parallel or compensatory transport modules (Fig. 5a). This interaction landscape points to SLC22A1 as a pharmacologically pivotal transporter within hepatic and renal drug clearance systems, intersecting multiple uptake-efflux circuits that coordinate drug absorption, bioavailability, and toxicity mitigation (Fig. 5a).

SLC22A2 PPI network

The interaction profile for SLC22A2 (OCT2) strongly overlaps with pathways regulating renal drug excretion, metal ion homeostasis, and neuroendocrine signaling. SLC47A1 (0.902) and SLC47A2 (0.885) emerge as top interaction partners, reflecting the tight cooperation between OCT2 and MATE transporters in renal epithelial cells for clearance of cationic drugs like metformin and creatinine. This interplay is central to the apical-basolateral exchange that governs drug elimination across the proximal tubule. Notably, IGF2R (0.887), involved in lysosomal trafficking and insulin-like growth factor modulation, suggests OCT2 may interface with metabolic and immune regulation networks. Similarly, EHMT2 (0.737), a histone methyltransferase, implies possible epigenetic regulation of transporter expression or chromatin interactions, an emerging area in transporter biology (Fig. 6a). The presence of SLC6A2 (0.696), a norepinephrine transporter, and SLC31A1 (0.706), a high-affinity copper transporter, reveals OCT2’s role in neurochemical balance and metal ion trafficking. These interactions are suggestive of broader physiological relevance beyond renal clearance, possibly including modulation of neuronal signaling or trace element availability (Fig. 6a).

Interactions with SLCO1B1 (0.682) and SLCO1B3 (0.680) again indicate functional crosstalk between organic anion and cation pathways. The inclusion of RSC1A1 (0.668), a regulator of SLC22A2, further validates the integrity of the network and supports known transcriptional feedback mechanisms. Collectively, the PPI network underscores SLC22A2’s dual role in both pharmacokinetic processing and systemic homeostasis, highlighting its relevance in drug-drug interactions and precision medicine strategies for renal transporter function (Fig. 6a).

SLC22A3 PPI network

The SLC22A3 (OCT3) PPI network illustrates a clear expansion from classic drug transport roles to encompass neurotransmitter regulation and epigenetic crosstalk. IGF2R (0.906) and EHMT2 (0.799) are prominent interactors, indicating potential involvement in transcriptional control, lysosomal enzyme trafficking, and immune cell modulation. This may relate to the broad tissue expression profile of SLC22A3, including liver, brain, and heart (Fig. 7a).

Highly ranked functional associations with SLC6A2 (0.825), SLC6A3 (0.665), and SLC6A4 (0.720), all neurotransmitter transporters for norepinephrine, dopamine, and serotonin, suggest OCT3 plays a supporting or modulatory role in synaptic reuptake mechanisms. This aligns with literature implicating OCT3 in stress hormone transport and CNS monoamine clearance, especially under high-concentration or saturation conditions (Fig. 7a). Further connections with SLC29A4 (0.731) and SLC47A1/47A2 reaffirm OCT3’s broader capacity as a polyspecific organic cation transporter, handling xenobiotics, neurotransmitters, and metabolic byproducts. RSC1A1 (0.669) also appears here, indicating conserved regulatory inputs across the OCT family. Functionally, this network situates SLC22A3 at the crossroads of neuromodulation, detoxification, and gene-environment interaction, with implications for its involvement in CNS disorders, psychopharmacology, and pharmacogenomic variability (Fig. 7a).

Discussion

The emergence of genotypic and phenotypic variability among individuals is rooted in genetic mutations, which contribute to diversity both within and across populations. Importantly, since SNPs are classified according to allele frequency thresholds within a population, they are by nature population-based variants. In order to comprehend their biological and clinical implications, population genetic background is therefore crucial. At the most granular level, single nucleotide changes known as Single Nucleotide Variants (SNVs) represent a substitution of one nucleotide for another at a specific position in the genome. When these changes occur with a minimum population frequency of 1%, they are classified as SNPs [41]. This threshold-based distinction between SNVs and SNPs is not merely semantic but carries biological and clinical relevance, particularly in identifying markers for disease susceptibility, drug response, and evolutionary conservation [26].

SNPs, a primary source of genetic variation in the human genome, play a pivotal role in modulating protein function and serve as crucial markers for elucidating various diseases [24]. SNPs can reside in coding or non-coding regions of the genome. Within protein-coding sequences, SNPs may be silent (synonymous), missense (nonsynonymous), or nonsense. Among these, nonsynonymous SNPs are of particular interest due to their capacity to alter amino acid sequences, thereby potentially affecting protein folding, function, stability, or interactions. Such changes can result in gain-of-function, loss-of-function, or dominant-negative effects [25]. In our study, we specifically focused on these nsSNPs within the SLC22A gene family, which encode for OCT proteins integral to the pharmacokinetics of therapeutic agents like metformin, a frontline drug in PCOS management. Despite their significance, the identification of functional SNPs poses a considerable challenge. To mitigate this challenge, various computational methods have been devised to pinpoint promising and potentially significant variants prior to experimental validation in vitro or in vivo [26]. In this context, an in-silico approach emerges as a practical means to discern deleterious SNPs from neutral ones, leveraging specific algorithms tailored for this purpose. Moreover, the comprehensive impact of polymorphisms, encompassing both functional and structural alterations, can be scrutinized through numerous public databases [24–26].

SNPs can also exert regulatory effects by influencing transcriptional activity, splicing patterns, or mRNA stability. Variations in promoter regions or transcription factor binding sites may modulate gene expression, further impacting downstream phenotypic traits. In the context of PCOS, a complex endocrine-metabolic disorder, even subtle genomic perturbations can tip the balance of insulin signaling, steroidogenesis, or follicular development [13].

Despite their biological importance, distinguishing deleterious from neutral SNPs remains a major challenge. To address this, we adopted an in-silico pipeline, integrating computational tools like Align-GVGD, PolyPhen-2, PROVEAN, PhD-SNP, and PANTHER. These tools harness parameters such as evolutionary conservation, sequence homology, structural topology, and machine learning algorithms to evaluate the functional consequences of each variant.

In this study, we utilized an in-silico prediction model to identify the most deleterious nsSNPs associated with SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3. Our analysis incorporated five prediction tools. Due to the varying parameters employed by each algorithm, we focused on mutations that were consistently identified as damaging, deleterious, and disease-associated across all algorithms. Specifically, we identified L42R, R175C, R175L, and F422S mutations in SLC22A1, R404C, G373D, and E227A mutations in SLC22A2, and R212C, G259R, T275M, R348W, R407C, and P499S mutations in SLC22A3 as high-risk nsSNPs. These mutations were consistently predicted to be deleterious by all SNP prediction algorithms, warranting further analysis.

In protein biology, the function, activity, and regulation of a protein are greatly influenced by the structural stability of the molecule. Any decrease in protein stability can trigger protein degradation, misfolding, and aggregation, ultimately leading to functional impairment. To assess the impact of the aforementioned nsSNPs on the stability of SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3, we utilized MuPro. The Delta G values, ranging from −0.01745 to −1.99395, indicated that these nsSNPs are likely to decrease protein stability, potentially contributing to protein dysfunction.

Using NetsurfP, we evaluated the solvent accessibility and stability of all 4, 3, and 6 variants in SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3, respectively. Determining protein stability is a complex task, as it involves multiple factors. The physiochemical properties of amino acid residues, including polarity, charge, and hydrophobicity, significantly contribute to the overall stability of the protein molecule. Research has demonstrated that protein stability correlates with the presence of hydrophobic residues on its surface, which restrict the accessibility of water molecules to the protein.

Understanding the population-specific prevalence of SNPs is vital in dissecting the genetic susceptibility landscape for complex disorders like PCOS. In this study, we examined deleterious variants in the SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 genes, placing special emphasis on their MAFs across diverse ancestral populations using aggregated data from repositories such as gnomAD and ALFA. Striking patterns were observed in the distribution of high-risk alleles. For example, multiple SNPs, including rs768905186 (R175C/L) and rs778599126 (R212C), showed significantly higher MAFs in South Asian populations (0.004–0.006 range) compared to East Asians or Europeans, where frequencies often remained below 0.001. This enrichment suggests that individuals of South Asian ancestry may be at an elevated genetic risk due to a higher background prevalence of deleterious nsSNPs affecting organic cation transporters. Similar patterns, although with less magnitude, were also observed in African and Latino cohorts.

The SLC22 family is involved in drug disposition and detoxification pathways and their variants have been linked to altered transporter efficiency and metabolic imbalances, features commonly reported in PCOS pathology. Ethnic-specific polymorphism distributions imply that the penetrance or expressivity of PCOS-related traits could be partially modulated by ancestry, possibly contributing to variability in clinical phenotypes and treatment response observed across global populations. In addition to ancestry-linked variation, the heterogeneous nature of PCOS itself further complicates this relationship. PCOS is not a uniform disorder but a syndrome with varying manifestations, including hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and metabolic derangements. These features often cluster differently based on both genetic makeup and environmental context, making population genetics an indispensable dimension in PCOS research. For instance, insulin resistance, often more pronounced in South Asian and Hispanic PCOS populations, may be exacerbated by deleterious variants in genes involved in xenobiotic metabolism, such as the SLC22 family. Moreover, the interpretation of functional SNPs should not be made in isolation. SNPs with relatively low individual pathogenicity may exert synergistic effects in haplotype configurations, particularly in regions rich in intron and regulatory variants, which we found to be highly represented. For example, in SLC22A3, more than 33,000 intronic variants were observed, raising the potential for complex regulatory networks involving transcription factor binding, alternative splicing, or epigenetic modulation, which could differ by ancestry due to linkage disequilibrium patterns [42].

Taken together, our findings emphasize that population context is indispensable in interpreting SNP impact, particularly for polygenic conditions like PCOS. Future studies should incorporate haplotype structure, regulatory annotation, and gene–environment interactions, while also prioritizing underrepresented populations in genomic datasets. Functional validation of these computational predictions across ancestries, possibly through transcriptomic and proteomic integration, is warranted to illuminate their full biological and clinical relevance.

The mutations within the SLC22A1 gene have led to varied effects on the exposure of amino acid residues on the protein surface. Specifically, the variants L42R and F422S, along with their corresponding wild-type counterparts, were observed to be exposed on the surface. In contrast, the variants R175C and R175L, along with their respective wild-type forms, were found to be buried within the protein structure. To fully appreciate the implications of these substitutions, it is essential to understand how the chemical nature of amino acids contributes to protein structure. Proteins are folded in such a way that hydrophobic (nonpolar) residues are typically buried inside the core, avoiding water, while polar or charged residues are exposed on the surface where they can interact with the aqueous environment. This arrangement contributes to the protein’s thermodynamic stability and functional surface topology.

The substitution of Leucine (a nonpolar amino acid) with Arginine (a polar amino acid) is likely to modify the surface characteristics of the protein. This transition from a nonpolar to a polar residue could potentially influence protein stability by affecting hydrophobic interactions. Arginine carries a positively charged guanidinium group at physiological pH, and when introduced into regions normally buried within the protein’s hydrophobic core, it can cause local unfolding or destabilization. This is due to unfavorable energetics from exposing a charged group to a nonpolar environment. Conversely, when exposed on the surface, Arginine may form new electrostatic interactions or hydrogen bonds, altering the local structural dynamics or interaction potential with ligands and other macromolecules.

The replacement of Phenylalanine (a nonpolar amino acid) with Serine (a polar amino acid) on the protein surface alters the hydrophobicity of the exposed area. This substitution may impact the protein’s interactions with other molecules due to changes in surface properties. Phenylalanine’s large aromatic ring contributes to hydrophobic stacking and stabilizing van der Waals interactions. Serine, on the other hand, introduces a hydroxyl group that can form hydrogen bonds. Therefore, this switch not only changes the chemical interaction profile but may also subtly alter surface geometry, which is critical in membrane transporters like SLC22A1, where substrate recognition and gating depend on surface-exposed motifs.

Additionally, the substitution of Arginine (a polar amino acid) with Cysteine (another polar amino acid) at position 175 (R175C) and the substitution of Arginine with Leucine (a nonpolar amino acid) at the same position (R175L) affects the buried area within the protein structure. These substitutions may modify the local structure and stability of the protein, potentially influencing its overall function and interaction with other molecules. Cysteine, though polar, carries a reactive sulfhydryl group capable of forming disulfide bonds. In the cytosolic environment (typically reducing), these groups remain unpaired and may create local strain if introduced in a hydrophobic, crowded region. Leucine, being hydrophobic, may be more tolerated in buried cores but still lacks the side-chain flexibility and charge of Arginine, thus disrupting any ionic interactions Arginine previously contributed to. Both substitutions have the potential to alter internal packing density, leading to misfolding, reduced protein half-life, or impaired function.

In the case of membrane transporters like OCT1 (SLC22A1), even small perturbations can compromise conformational switching required for substrate translocation.

In SLC22A3, three variants (G259R, R348W, and R407C) and their corresponding wild-type counterparts were identified as exposed on the protein surface, potentially affecting interactions and stability. These substitutions involve changes from nonpolar to polar or vice versa, thus altering the surface characteristics of the protein. Glycine at position 259, due to its small size and lack of a side chain, offers flexibility in loop or turn regions. Its substitution with a large, charged Arginine introduces steric hindrance and positive charge, potentially disrupting loop dynamics or solvent-exposed interactions. R348W replaces a basic Arginine with a bulky aromatic Tryptophan, likely impacting local curvature and hydrophobicity. Such changes may influence substrate recognition or the positioning of extracellular loops crucial for transporter function.

Conversely, three variants (R212C, T275M, and P499S) and their respective wild-type counterparts were found buried within the protein core. These buried variants likely impact local conformation and stability, with substitutions involving polar or nonpolar amino acids influencing the characteristics and interactions within the buried region of the protein structure. Threonine to Methionine substitution (T275M) introduces a thioether-containing side chain in place of a hydroxyl-bearing residue. This not only reduces hydrogen bonding potential but can also alter packing due to Methionine’s bulk and hydrophobic nature. Proline at position 499, known to induce rigid kinks in helices, if replaced or mutated, could have major repercussions on helix orientation or folding initiation sites. particularly in transmembrane segments.

Additionally, several of these mutations lie near predicted substrate-binding sites or gating residues, based on UniProt annotations and homology modeling. Mutations at these positions could impair metformin recognition or passage, leading to reduced drug uptake and therapeutic inefficacy. Taken together, these substitutions do not simply change the primary sequence but have the potential to reshape local microenvironments, alter transport kinetics, and influence membrane orientation. Therefore, the high deleteriousness predictions across multiple tools are structurally and chemically justified, and these insights warrant in vitro validation to confirm transporter impairment.

Understanding cellular processes relies heavily on comprehending protein-protein interaction networks. STRING serves as a pivotal tool in filtering and evaluating functional genomics data, offering an intuitive platform for interpreting the structural, functional, and evolutionary properties of proteins. In this study, utilizing this database unveiled the interactions of SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 with other proteins, which likely participate in various pathways. Disruption of these pathways may consequently lead to the onset of diseases. Protein-protein interaction network analysis reveals distinct interactions involving SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3, all implicated in polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). SLC22A1 exhibits interactions with proteins linked to lipid metabolism (CERS2), nucleoside transport (SLC28A2, SLC28A1), and drug resistance (ABCG2, ABCB1), indicating potential roles in these processes relevant to PCOS. SLC22A2 interacts with proteins involved in neurotransmitter transport (SLC6A2), lysosomal function (IGF2R), and metal ion transport (SLC31A1), suggesting its participation in neurotransmitter signaling, lysosomal function, and metal ion homeostasis, potentially influencing PCOS-related symptoms. SLC22A3 interacts with proteins associated with neurotransmitter transport (SLC6A4, SLC6A3, SLC6A2), lysosomal function (IGF2R), and nucleoside transport (SLC29A4), highlighting its potential involvement in neurotransmitter signaling, lysosomal function, and nucleoside transport, potentially affecting PCOS pathophysiology. These interactions collectively suggest that dysregulation of SLC22A transporters and their interactions with various proteins may contribute to the development or progression of PCOS.

The PPI networks constructed for SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3 revealed distinct yet interconnected biological roles with potential relevance to PCOS pathophysiology. For SLC22A1, interacting partners were significantly enriched in pathways related to xenobiotic and drug transport, particularly those involved in Drug ADME and bile secretion. This aligns with the known role of SLC22A1 in mediating metformin uptake, suggesting that genetic variations in this gene could influence drug pharmacokinetics in PCOS patients. Moreover, the observed enrichment in neurotransmitter transport and blood-brain barrier pathways indicates potential neuromodulatory impacts relevant to the neuroendocrine axis dysregulation observed in PCOS.

SLC22A2 displayed enrichment in biological processes such as norepinephrine uptake, L-arginine transport, and xenobiotic detoxification, with Reactome pathways pointing to bile salt and organic ion transport. Given the metabolic and endocrine disturbances in PCOS, including altered sympathetic tone, insulin resistance, and elevated androgens, variants in SLC22A2 may influence disease phenotype by modulating substrate specificity or transport efficiency across key tissues like the liver and kidneys.

In contrast, SLC22A3‘s PPI network was enriched in neurotransmitter reuptake and clearance pathways, especially those related to serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. These monoaminergic systems are critical for hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis regulation and mood stability, both frequently affected in PCOS. The gene’s strong association with Na+/Cl− -dependent neurotransmitter transporters further underscores its neuromodulatory potential. Together, these findings suggest that the SLC22 gene family is embedded within broader physiological networks impacting not only drug disposition but also hormonal balance, metabolic regulation, and neuroendocrine signaling, core aspects of PCOS pathology.

Previous research on pharmacogenetics gives us a useful way to understand how our computer-based results in the SLC22 gene family might be used in real life. There is more and more proof that genetic variation in organic cation transporters is a major cause of differences between individuals in insulin sensitivity, glucose homeostasis, and the effectiveness of metformin as a treatment for metabolic disorders. It was this situation that Fizalah et al.‘s study shed light on how SLC22A1 affects the insulin response in people with type 2 diabetes mellitus. They found SNPs 156T > C and 1222A > G in both groups of responders and non-responders. They also saw that changes at positions 181T > C and 1222A > G caused structural changes in OCT-1, which was expected from the computer simulations. These changes then affected the transport of metformin in the lab. The change in the activation of AMPK, a marker of metformin action [10], added to the evidence of this effect.

These results are important for this study because they show a connection between computer expectations about structure and functional effects seen in experiments. Fizalah et al. demonstrated that expected changes in OCT1 structure were not just theories; they were actually reflected in differences in how well the transporter worked and how metabolic signals were sent further down the line. This similarity strongly supports the biological plausibility of our computational method. Several prediction algorithms agreed on a subset of nsSNPs that are likely to make SLC22 transporters less stable or change how they move or conform. In the same way that changes in the structure of OCT1 affected the uptake of metformin and the activation of AMPK in the study mentioned, the variants we found may also have an effect on how drugs are moved around and how metabolism is controlled in PCOS.

Similarly, differences in how people react to metformin have been linked to variations in other members of the SLC22 transporter family. This shows that pharmacogenetic effects are specific to genes and variants. In a different study, Rizvi et al. looked into how OCT1 (rs628031) and OCT3 (rs2292334) genes affect how people with type 2 diabetes respond to metformin. Their research showed that there was no significant link between the OCT1 (rs628031) gene and how well these patients responded to metformin. On the other hand, they saw a strong link between the OCT3 (rs2292334) polymorphism and metformin response, which suggests that it might be useful for figuring out how a patient will react to metformin treatment [43]. These different results show an important idea that is related to our research: different types of transporter variants don’t have the same clinical or functional effects. The effect of a specific SNP depends on its structural context, how it affects protein stability, and how it affects substrate interaction or transport kinetics. This supports the idea behind our multi-tool prioritization approach, which was to cut down on false positives by choosing only the nsSNPs that were consistently predicted to be harmful across different algorithms. By doing this, we raise the chance that the variants we’ve identified are biologically significant candidates and not just harmless polymorphisms.

Genetic differences in SLC22A transporters cause metformin to work differently in different people, but genetics alone can’t fully explain how PCOS shows up in different people. A lot of nonsynonymous variants don’t fully affect the gene’s function, and their effects are only likely to be clinically important when other hormonal, metabolic, or external factors are present.

Changes in SLC22A3, which is found in both the brain and other tissues, may affect both the transport of xenobiotics and the removal of neurotransmitters. But neurobehavioral symptoms like anxiety or sadness, which are common in PCOS, probably can’t just be caused by genetic differences. They may also need other factors like stress, hyperandrogenism, or insulin resistance to work together. So, the variants found in this study should be seen as risk-modifying or pharmacogenomic factors rather than deterministic drivers of PCOS. This shows how important it is for studies that combine genetic, hormonal, metabolic, and clinical data.

It’s important to note that the studies mentioned were done on people with type 2 diabetes, but the mechanisms are the same for people with PCOS, where insulin resistance and changed metabolic signaling are key traits. Metformin is still one of the most important treatments for PCOS, but there is still a lot of variation in how well it works for different people. Because of this, the work by Fizalah et al. and Rizvi et al. provides a clinically useful example of how differences in OCT transporters can affect how drugs work and how metabolism works. Putting our computer-based predictions into this well-known pharmacogenetic framework builds on previous experimental and clinical observations. It also shows how these findings apply to the endocrine-metabolic landscape of PCOS, where differences in transporter function may also affect how well treatments work and how different people with the disease are.

Conclusion

The present study investigates the complex relationship between genetic variations in SLC22A transporters, namely SLC22A1, SLC22A2, and SLC22A3, and their impact in PCOS and metformin response. Employing an extensive in-silico analysis utilizing five prediction tools, we identified several mutations, including L42R and F422S in SLC22A1, R404C, G373D, and E227A in SLC22A2, and G259R, R348W, and R407C in SLC22A3. These nsSNPs consistently demonstrated deleterious effects across multiple algorithms, highlighting their significance in the context of PCOS and metformin pharmacokinetics. These genetic variations, especially those categorized as high-risk nsSNPs, have the potential to significantly impact the structural integrity and functional dynamics of SLC22A transporters. Consequently, they may play a crucial role in cellular processes relevant to the pathophysiology of PCOS and the response to metformin treatment. Although the in-silico prediction approach utilized in this study is robust, it serves as an initial phase in identifying potentially deleterious nsSNPs. Subsequent experimental validation is essential to confirm their functional significance. Moreover, integrating genetic data with other omics datasets, such as transcriptomics and metabolomics, holds promise in offering a more holistic comprehension of the molecular mechanisms implicated in PCOS and metformin response. This integration could lead to the emergence of personalized therapeutic strategies customized to individual genetic profiles.
