Biochar-mediated improvements in soil fertility and wheat (Triticum Aestivum L.) productivity in the Indo-Gangetic Plains
Authors: Gaurav Sharma, Rajeev Kumar Gupta, Eiji Nishihara, Kazuyuki Inubushi, Shigeto Sudo, Sachiko Hayashida, Chandra Mohan Mehta, Munirah Abdullah Al–Dosary, Saud Alamri, Maqsood Ul Hussan, Manzer H. Siddiqui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe annual burning of 500–900 million tons of crop residues releases nearly 1.5 billion tons of CO₂ equivalents and severely degrades soil quality, causing 25–30% losses in soil organic carbon and 40–60% reductions in nitrogen. This unsustainable practice increases fertilizer requirements by 15–20% to sustain crop yields. Converting crop residues into biochar offers a sustainable alternative that mitigates air pollution, improves soil fertility, enhances carbon sequestration, and increases agricultural productivity.AimThis study evaluated the effect of biochar application on soil properties, wheat growth, and yield performance.MethodsA two-year field experiment was conducted on wheat (PBW 824) at the Agriculture Research Farm of Lovely Professional University, Punjab, India. Treatments included a control, recommended fertilizer doses, nitrogen and phosphorus alone, and nitrogen and phosphorus combined with rice husk or rice straw biochar applied at 5, 10, and 15 t/ha.ResultsBiochar application significantly improved crop performance, with the most pronounced effects observed under RSB & RHB doses at 5 t/ha. Compared to non-biochar treatments, this rate increased plant height (12.8%), tillers (49.8%), leaf area index (43.3%), crop growth rate (25.4%), spike length (34.2%), grains per spike (40.6%), test weight (19.6%), biological yield (17.2%), grain yield (32.8%), and harvest index (13.4%).ConclusionsApplication of biochar at 5 t/ha is an effective and sustainable strategy for enhancing soil physical properties, nutrient availability, plant growth, and wheat productivity, while improving soil moisture retention and reducing soil hardness.

Introduction

The rapid increase in the world population, projected to reach 9.7 billion by 2050, is placing unprecedented pressure on crop production systems to generate more food. Wheat, as one of the most important staple cereals globally, plays a vital role in ensuring food security. However, ensuring sustainable wheat production poses significant challenges, particularly in managing crop residues. After harvest, large quantities of rice residues remain in the fields, creating logistical and agronomic challenges for farmers. Poor management of these residues not only delays the establishment of subsequent crops but also leads to environmental degradation, especially when they are burned—a common practice in many developing countries. With rising food demand and limited arable land, it is essential to develop sustainable solutions for crop residue management to maximize soil fertility and maintain long-term agricultural productivity.

Open burning of crop residues is a widespread yet harmful practice, especially in South Asia, where mechanized harvesting results in significant amounts of straw left in the fields. These burning releases substantial quantities of greenhouse gases (GHGs), such as carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O), thereby contributing to climate change. Additionally, open burning results in the loss of critical nutrients, with approximately 80% of nitrogen, 25% of phosphorus, and 21% of potassium lost. Air pollution from burning residues poses severe health risks, leading to respiratory illnesses and smog formation. Despite these adverse effects, many farmers continue to burn residues because of a lack of affordable, practical alternatives.

One potential solution is the pyrolysis of crop residues to produce biochar, a stable, carbon-rich material. Unlike direct residue addition, which may temporarily immobilize nitrogen and hinder crop growth, biochar significantly improves soil fertility by enhancing nutrient retention, water-holding capacity, and microbial activity. Research indicates that applying biochar can increase soil organic carbon (SOC), thereby significantly improving long-term soil health. Furthermore, soils treated with biochar exhibit enhanced cation exchange capacity (CEC), thereby improving nutrient availability for crops. Given the pressing need to increase agricultural productivity while mitigating environmental degradation, biochar presents a promising approach for sustainable residue management.

In wheat cultivation, biochar has shown immense potential to boost yields and improve soil health. Studies indicate that applying optimal biochar rates (5–20 t/ha) can increase wheat grain yields by 10–25% due to improved nitrogen use efficiency and water retention. A meta-analysis by Jeffery reported an average yield increase of 13% with biochar application, with the greatest benefits observed in degraded, nutrient-poor soils. Moreover, biochar’s porous structure enhances root growth, thereby improving nutrient uptake and increasing biomass yield. As global food demand continues to rise, incorporating biochar into wheat production systems may become a crucial factor in promoting sustainable agricultural intensification.

Currently, the impact of biochar on crop productivity remains inconsistent, influenced by biochar type and properties, as well as experimental factors such as soil type, crop species, application timing, and environmental conditions. Therefore, further investigation into the effects of biochar on crop yields in specific site conditions is essential. This research aims to (1) evaluate the impact of biochar application on soil properties and (2) analyze its effects on the growth and yield attributes of wheat. In addition, we hypothesize that application of biochar in optimum amounts (5 t ha⁻¹) will improve soil fertility and maximize production of wheat (Triticum aestivum L.). It is anticipated that this study will contribute to establishing evidence-based information to help develop sustainable residue management practices, thus enhancing agricultural productivity and sustainability.

Materials and methods

Source of planting material

The Wheat (Triticum aestivum L., variety PBW 824) used in the study is a released, commercially cultivated variety obtained from Punjab Agricultural University as a seed source. The variety is well characterized and maintained under standard institutional records.

Experimental site description

The research trial was conducted at the agricultural research farm of Lovely Professional University in Jalandhar, Punjab, from 2022 to 2024. The location’s coordinates are latitude 31°14’30.5” N and longitude 75°41’52.1” E. The soil characteristics of the experimental site are detailed in Table 1, indicating that the area has a sandy loam soil texture. In November, daytime temperatures at the site reach approximately 27 °C, while nighttime temperatures drop to around 13 °C, accompanied by minimal rainfall. The winter period, spanning from December to February, is notably cool, with temperatures generally ranging from 10 °C to 21 °C and very little rainfall. As March and April approach, temperatures rise steadily, reaching highs of 34–36 °C and lows of 18–21 °C, with low humidity. The meteorological data for this period are shown in Fig. 1.

Experimental design

A two-year field experiment was conducted in Punjab from 2022 to 2024 under irrigation to evaluate a wheat cropping system. The long-duration PBW 824 wheat matures in 148–156 days. It was sown manually on 22 November and harvested on 24 April, with 22.5 cm between rows. The experiment used a randomized complete block design (three replications) with nine treatments: T1 (absolute control), T2 (100% recommended NPK fertilizers—RDF), T3 (100% NP), T4–T6 (T3 plus rice husk biochar at 5, 10, and 15 t ha⁻¹, respectively), T7–T9 (T3 plus rice straw biochar at 5, 10, and 15 t ha⁻¹, respectively). In T3 to T9, potassium was omitted to assess the effect of biochar-supplied potassium on soil fertility and uptake. EDX analysis verified potassium in biochar. Excluding external potassium enabled the assessment of potassium release from biochar. RDF (NPK) was used as a standard for common fertilization. Each plot was 5 × 5 m. Plots were separated by 1 m buffers, with a 5 m buffer around the site. Two 1 m-wide irrigation channels ran between replications for even water distribution.

Biochar preparation and characterization

Rice straw and husk were the feedstock used for the production of biochar in this research. Biochar was produced from rice husks and rice straws using a Kon-Tiki open-conical kiln, which operates by creating a flame curtain during pyrolysis under limited-oxygen conditions. First, pyrolysis was conducted at temperatures between 450 and 650 °C. The process duration depended on the biomass: 2.5 to 3 h for rice husks, and 25 to 35 min for rice straws. The thermal properties of these feedstocks were initially analyzed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), in which samples were heated from 30 °C to 600 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere to monitor mass loss and decomposition patterns. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was also utilized to analyze heat flow and phase transitions as the samples were heated from 30°C to 440°C, giving information about their thermal stability and transformation profiles. Biochar was produced at the research farm [1] after this, and pH and EC were analyzed. To examine the temporal changes, residual biochar samples were collected after the trial’s completion, and the soil attached to the biochar was gently removed. Fresh and residual biochar samples were subjected to thorough surface characterization using FESEM (field emission scanning electron microscopy) JSM-7610 F PLUS microscope (Oxford Instruments X-Max N, Japan) at an operating voltage of 15–25 kV. EDS analysis was performed with an OXFORD X-Max N system (Japan) to determine the elemental composition of the biochar samples.

Soil analytical procedures

Before sowing and after harvesting, soil samples were analyzed. Soil samples were collected from the 0–20 cm layer, air-dried, and sieved through a 2 mm mesh before analysis. Electrical conductivity (EC) and soil pH were analyzed using a HANA digital pH and EC meter [2]. Soil organic carbon was determined using the Walkley-Black method [3], and available nitrogen was determined through the Kjeldahl method [4]. Potassium was determined using Flame emission spectroscopy [5], whereas soil phosphorus and sulphur were determined by colorimetric methods [6]. Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS) was used to determine the levels of micronutrients, including iron, copper, manganese, and zinc. The soil hardness was measured at depths of 2, 5, 10, and 15 cm using a Yamanaka-type soil hardness tester (Model No. PK-704, Fujiwara Seisakusho Co., Ltd., Japan). The tester is equipped with a spring graduated to 8 kg at 40 mm displacement. For each depth, the probe was plunged vertically, and the spring displacement (x) was noted. Quantification of plant traits

Major agronomic parameters of wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) were examined systematically throughout the study. Plant height was measured on selected plants per plot, from the basal node to the apical meristem, using a calibrated measuring tape. Node, internode diameter and internode distance were measured by using a digital vernier calliper. Chlorophyll index was determined from the top leaf using a SPAD meter (model 502). For the determination of leaf area index (LAI), leaves were removed, and leaf area was measured by a leaf area meter; LAI is calculated by using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{LAI}=\frac{\text{leaf area}\:\mathrm{cm}^{2}}{\text{ground area}\:\mathrm{cm}^{2}}$$\end{document}

Crop growth rate (CGR) was estimated using the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{CGR}=\frac{\mathrm{W}_{2}-\mathrm{W}_{1}}{\mathrm{A}\times{\left(\mathrm{T}_{2}-\mathrm{T}_{1}\right)}}$$\end{document}

where W1 and W2 are the dry biomass at T1 and T2, respectively.

The tiller number and effective tiller were counted in a one-meter running row length. The spike length was measured with a measuring tape. Grains per spike were counted manually. Test weight was measured by counting and weighing 1000 grains using a precision weighing balance. To calculate the biological yield, one square meter of crop was cut from each plot from a uniformly matured area. Grain yield was calculated from the biological yield after manual threshing. Statistical analysis for experimental data

The two-year mean data were analyzed using R Studio software with Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) at a significance threshold of p < 0.05 to identify differences between group means. OriginPro software was used to construct graphical representations of the data, facilitating easier visual examination of the findings. Furthermore, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used in OriginPro to investigate correlations among variables and to minimize dimensionality, revealing underlying patterns within the dataset.

Thermogravimetric analysis and differential scanning calorimetry were performed on rice straw and husk (Fig. 2). The curves showed that as temperature increased, there was a loss in weight (%). DSC curves represent the heat flow as a function of temperature change for both rice straw and husk feedstocks. This information will help optimize biochar production conditions and characterize the energy-release profiles of these feedstocks.

Temporal dynamics of biochar properties

Fresh rice husk biochar had moderate levels of carbon (35.8%), oxygen (30.8%), and silica (17.9%), along with notable levels of calcium (15.1%) and phosphorus (1.7%). After aging, carbon rose to 74.6%, while oxygen and silica dropped to 14.8% and 0.43%. The aged biochar also shows more nitrogen (1.4%) and traces of manganese (1.6%) and cobalt (0.6%). In contrast, calcium and phosphorus levels decrease over time. In comparison, fresh rice straw biochar contained more carbon (51.7%) than fresh rice husk biochar. Among the main components, oxygen (21.2%), silica (13.2%), and potassium (6.8%) were present in decreasing order of abundance. Upon aging, the carbon content of straw biochar decreases, while the oxygen content increases to 27.5%. The silica percentage also rises to 15.9% in the residual straw biochar. Conversely, the potassium and aluminum contents decrease during aging, while magnesium and aluminum remain at low levels, as shown in Fig. 3.

Biochar impact on soil nutrient dynamics

Soil pH is a crucial factor that influences nutrient availability and the overall health of the soil. Biochar application resulted in a marginal elevation in soil pH, although the observed variations were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Pre-sowing soil pH is 7.51, while the maximum pH at harvest is recorded under the RSB dose of 15 t/ha (7.69), followed by T5 (7.68). The results of the soil electrical conductivity (EC) show a significant interaction, as shown in Fig. 5. As biochar treatment was applied, the soil electrical conductivity (EC) generally increased across all biochar treatments compared to the control treatments. The electrical conductivity (EC) of soil is significantly affected by biochar application. Pre-sowing soil EC is 0.19, while the maximum EC recorded after harvest is in T9 (0.20). In Fig. 5, the data show that soil organic carbon is significantly affected by biochar application. Pre-sowing, soil organic carbon is 0.46 while the maximum organic carbon recorded after harvest in RSB and RHB dose of 15 t/ha, i.e., T9 (0.67), followed by T6 (0.62) and T5 (0.6).

Influence of biochar on wheat growth indices

Different biochar treatments influenced the agronomic parameters of wheat in the present study; these data are shown in Fig. 8. A significant impact of biochar was observed on plant traits, including plant height, number of tillers, LAI, CGR, and node and stem diameter. ANOVA at p < 0.05 revealed that the maximum plant height was recorded under RSB & RHB doses at 5 t/ha, i.e., T4 & T7 (12.8%), followed by T5 (9.3%) compared with the control treatment (T2). The RSB treatment at 5 t/ha showed relatively higher node and stem diameters, i.e., T7 (21.1%) and T8 (15.6%), compared to T2. Recorded the same for stem diameter, i.e., T7 (25.5%), followed by T8 (17.9%), as compared to T2. The number of tillers was significantly enhanced with biochar application. The maximum number of tillers was revealed under T4 (49.8%), followed by T7 (47.1%). LAI and CGR in the wheat crop are essential indicators of crop health. The results for LAI and CGR followed the same trend. The highest LAI was observed under T4 (43.3%), followed by T7 (37.9%). The same CGR revealed the highest under-treatment of T4 (25.4%) and T7 (15.9%) compared to the control treatment.

Discussion

The effect of biochar on wheat production was evaluated at different application rates. Prior to application, biochar samples were prepared under identical conditions and characterized. Subsequently, applying 5 t ha⁻¹ biochar improved soil fertility and yielded more than both untreated controls and higher rates (10 t ha⁻¹ and 15 t ha⁻¹). These findings align with previous research, demonstrating biochar’s role in reducing agricultural waste combustion.

Dynamics of biochar ageing in soil

The EDX data revealed that residual biochar in soil alters the elemental composition, depending on the feedstock. Rice husk biochar exhibited a higher carbon content after aging, which may be attributed to the selective loss of volatile organic compounds and the concentration of relatively more stable constituents. While rice straw biochar shows a reduction in carbon content over time, this may result from oxidation reactions and the leaching of soluble carbon fractions. In addition, the elemental changes observed could contribute to the concept of nutrient cycling and availability in the soil-plant system [7]. Changes in phosphorus, potassium, and other key elements can indicate biochar’s ability to absorb, retain, and release nutrients, thereby affecting soil fertility and crop productivity [8]. Additional evidence for these changes comes from EDX analysis. It showed that the carbon content increased sharply. This increase mostly happens because there are fewer minerals, so carbon makes up a larger share. The EDX results also show that the silicon signal in the fresh rice husk biochar is almost gone in the old samples. This means much of the Sio2 has dissolved and washed away from the soil. When ash is removed, most of the original minerals are removed, leaving behind more stable carbon. This carbon build-up from minerals being washed out matches results for plant material with a lot of ash [9].

Modulation of soil physicochemical properties through biochar

Biochar amendment has been recognised for its diverse effects on soil health, including its impact on soil physical and chemical properties. The hardness of soil and the development of porosity after the addition of biochar can be directly attributed to the porous nature of biochar [10]. This morphology promotes greater aeration and water penetration into the soil, thereby increasing the soil’s moisture content. Our results align with those of [10]. Additionally, as reported in our study, the soil pH increases slightly after biochar application. The alkalinity of most biochars increased soil pH and had the potential to enhance soil electrical conductivity. This promotes the nutrient availability and plant uptake. Biochar plays a vital role in increasing soil organic carbon [11]. It is a valuable tool for carbon sequestration and long-term soil fertility [12]. As reported in our study, biochar application significantly influences the dynamics of macro- and micro-nutrients in soil, including N, P, K, S, Ca, and Mg [13, 14]. These findings highlight the interactions between biochar and soil nutrients, which affect the solubility, availability, and plant uptake of nutrients [15–17].

Biochar-induced alteration in plant growth

The results indicate that biochar application increases chlorophyll levels. By improving the soil’s physical and chemical properties, biochar enhances biological productivity, thereby elevating chlorophyll levels. These higher chlorophyll concentrations likely enable crops to absorb more sunlight and increase photosynthetic efficiency, potentially leading to higher yields and healthier plants [18]. Secondly, phosphorus is the crucial macronutrient involved in many plant processes, including energy transfer and the synthesis of DNA and RNA. Increased P availability through biochar promotes root development, increases plant height, and enhances overall plant growth [8]. The increased node and internode diameters in our study indicated enhanced vascular development, facilitating efficient water and nutrient transport throughout the plant. The improved growth of xylem and phloem is evidenced by increased node and internode diameter. The higher LAI indicates a greater photosynthetic area, allowing the plant to capture more light. The enhanced CGR indicates that the plant can efficiently use resources to produce biomass. These factors are correlated with more nutrient supply and vascular development, resulting in higher LAI and CGR [19].

Wheat productivity enhancement through biochar application

The favourable impact on wheat yield attributes, such as spike length, grain per spike, biological yield, grain yield, and test weight, may be due to several factors. This may be due to better soil structure, water-holding capacity, and nutrient supply [20]. As shown in our results, biochar reduces soil hardness and increases moisture content, thereby favouring root growth and, consequently, increasing the number of tillers [21]. Biochar increases the concentration of available nitrogen, thereby enhancing grain filling and more effectively allocating resources to the grain [22].

Principal component analysis

PCA was conducted to reduce the dataset’s dimensionality and identify key factors affecting soil properties and plant growth (Fig. 11). The eigenvalue analysis indicated that the first two components accounted for 74.4% and 9%, respectively. The loading plot for PC1 showed positive loadings for most plant and soil parameters, except for soil hardness, iron, and copper. High positive loadings were observed for plant height, chlorophyll content, number of tillers, leaf area index, crop growth rate, number of productive tillers, anthesis, days to maturity, spike length, grains per spike, biological yield, grain yield, straw yield, test weight and harvest index. This suggests that PC1 primarily represents overall plant productivity and soil fertility, with higher values indicating more favourable growth conditions. PC2 exhibited strong positive loadings for soil moisture, potassium, and copper. This suggests that PC2 may represent a gradient of soil moisture and nutrient availability. Together, these findings highlight the complex interplay between soil properties and plant growth (Fig. 12). PC1, representing overall plant productivity and soil fertility, is the dominant factor driving variability in the dataset. Soil moisture, potassium, and copper levels, as represented by PC2, also play significant roles in plant growth and development. Understanding these relationships helps targeted interventions to optimise soil conditions and improve crop productivity [23, 24].

Correlation

The strong negative correlation between soil hardness and various plant traits suggests that increased soil hardness may inhibit root development and nutrient transport, thereby limiting overall wheat productivity. The positive correlation among soil moisture, pH, EC, OC, and plant attributes indicates that these soil traits play a vital role in increasing crop productivity. For instance, ideal soil moisture ensures proper hydration and nutrient uptake, while adequate soil pH facilitates nutrient availability [25]. Similarly, higher EC was associated with higher nutrient concentrations and OC, reflecting soil fertility. These findings underscore the strong relationship between soil fertility and crop productivity [26]. Optimising soil conditions by addressing soil hardness, ensuring adequate moisture, maintaining optimal pH, and providing balanced nutrient supply is vital for maximising crop productivity [23].

One-way ANOVA

The ANOVA revealed significant differences across soil and plant traits, indicating that biochar has a substantial impact on wheat productivity. Biochar’s impact on soil attributes, such as modifying pH and EC, demonstrated its potential to change soil chemistry, which can influence soil nutrient availability [27]. The improvement in soil OC, N, P and K aligns with the concept that biochar can improve soil fertility [7]. Furthermore, improvements in plant height, node diameter, chlorophyll index, and LAI indicated that biochar had a positive effect on vegetative growth. The increased number of productive tillers, spike length, grain per spike, test weight, biological yield, grain yield, and harvest index indicate that biochar amendment can enhance wheat productivity [28].

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of biochar amendments and found that biochar positively influences soil fertility and crop productivity. We concluded that applying both rice husk and straw biochar at a rate of 5 tons per hectare improves soil moisture, reduces soil hardness, and maintains soil nutrient status. This leads to enhanced nutrient availability, improved fertilizer use efficiency, and a decreased environmental impact. Biochar promotes plant growth and increases the Leaf Area Index (LAI), allowing plants to capture more sunlight and produce more photosynthate. The increase in Crop Growth Rate (CGR) suggests higher overall biomass and wheat yield. Specifically, wheat grain yield significantly increased under the application of rice straw biochar at 5 tons per hectare (T7), showing a 32.8% increase, followed by T4 with a 26.3% increase compared to the control treatments. Biochar amendments represent an effective strategy for intensifying wheat production while potentially improving soil fertility by enhancing nutrient availability. To encourage the adoption of biochar, clear policy frameworks regarding its use and associated technologies in subsistence farming systems are essential.
