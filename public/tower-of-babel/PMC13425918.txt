Soft-tissue reconstruction timing in ballistic lower extremity open fractures: impact on osteomyelitis and union
Authors: Ali Aydilek, Erkan Sabri Ertaş, Mustafa Kara, Begüm Aslantaş Kaplan, Mustafa Aydın, Deniz Çankaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBallistic lower extremity open fractures represent a distinct subset of high-energy injuries characterized by extensive soft-tissue destruction, contamination, and complex wound biology. Although early soft-tissue reconstruction is widely advocated, a clinically relevant timing threshold specific to ballistic trauma remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of soft-tissue reconstruction timing on osteomyelitis and bone union in ballistic lower extremity open fractures.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 56 adult male patients with high-energy ballistic lower extremity open fractures classified as Gustilo–Anderson type IIIB–IIIC, treated between 2016 and 2024. These injuries represent a severe form of extremity trauma, frequently encountered in military-related or complex trauma settings. Demographic characteristics, vascular injury, reconstruction timing, debridement burden, definitive fixation method, and clinical outcomes were recorded. Patients were stratified according to reconstruction timing. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to determine the optimal temporal threshold for predicting osteomyelitis, and logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors.ResultsOsteomyelitis developed in 23 patients (41.1%). ROC analysis identified 14.5 days as the optimal threshold for soft-tissue reconstruction (AUC 0.773), with reconstruction beyond this cutoff significantly increasing osteomyelitis risk (58.8% vs. 13.6%). Multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that increasing time to soft-tissue reconstruction was independently associated with osteomyelitis risk (OR 1.043 per day, p = 0.011). Bone union was achieved in 75% of patients, and no significant association was observed between reconstruction timing and time to union. Prolonged union was instead associated with a greater number of pre-reconstruction debridements.ConclusionDelayed soft-tissue reconstruction beyond approximately two weeks may be associated with an increased risk of osteomyelitis in ballistic lower extremity open fractures. These findings suggest that earlier provision of vascularized coverage, following adequate serial debridement, can be beneficial, while highlighting the importance of considering wound biology rather than relying solely on fixed temporal thresholds. Bone healing, in contrast, appears to be more closely related to initial injury severity than to reconstruction timing.

Introduction

Lower extremity open fractures, particularly those involving the tibia, remain among the most challenging injuries in orthopedic traumatology due to the tenuous regional blood supply and limited soft-tissue envelope [1]. These injuries frequently result from high-energy mechanisms and often require a coordinated orthoplastic approach to achieve both skeletal stabilization and durable soft-tissue coverage [2]. Unlike conventional blunt trauma, ballistic open fractures exhibit a distinct pathophysiological profile driven by temporary and permanent cavitation effects, whereby high kinetic energy transfer creates a broad zone of injury with microvascular disruption, muscle necrosis, and deep contamination [3, 4].

From a biological standpoint, early vascularized soft-tissue coverage has been regarded as a critical determinant in the management of Gustilo–Anderson Type IIIB fractures. Vascularized tissue provides metabolic support to compromised bone by supplying oxygen, nutrients, and immune components, facilitating revascularization within the Haversian system and reducing the risk of persistent infection [5]. In the absence of timely coverage, exposed bone becomes susceptible to desiccation, bacterial colonization, chronic osteomyelitis, and eventual nonunion [5, 6].

The optimal timing for such reconstruction has remained a subject of debate since the seminal work of Godina, who advocated microsurgical soft-tissue coverage within 72 h to minimize infection and flap failure [7]. Although contemporary wound management strategies—including negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT)—have challenged these strict temporal constraints and extended the effective window for intervention to approximately 7–10 days, ballistic trauma presents unique logistical and physiological barriers [8, 9]. In conflict or resource-limited settings, the need for serial debridements, systemic stabilization, and delayed evacuation frequently contributes to unavoidable delays [10].

Despite the increasing prevalence of ballistic limb injuries in certain regions, there remains a paucity of data defining a clinically meaningful temporal threshold beyond which delayed reconstruction adversely affects infection and union outcomes. While early flap coverage has been associated with lower deep infection rates, the specific timing parameters applicable to ballistic trauma are yet to be fully clarified [11]. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of reconstruction timing on osteomyelitis and bone union in ballistic lower extremity open fractures and to identify a clinically relevant temporal cutoff to optimize limb salvage outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

Following approval from the Institutional Review Board of Gülhane Training and Research Hospital, a retrospective cohort study was conducted including patients treated for high-energy ballistic-induced lower extremity open fractures between 2016 and 2024. The study comprised 56 adult patients with Gustilo–Anderson type IIIB–IIIC open fractures who underwent soft-tissue reconstruction (Fig. 1). Three patients excluded due to flap failure additionally had severe vascular compromise and a history of repeated vascular surgical procedures. The cohort consisted of high-energy ballistic injuries related to military mechanisms. All injuries were attributable to high-energy ballistic mechanisms, defined as projectile injuries associated with an estimated muzzle velocity exceeding 2000 ft/s (≈ 600 m/s) [12].

Surgical protocol and soft-tissue management

Upon admission, all patients were evaluated according to standard trauma protocols and underwent initial surgical management consisting of extensive irrigation, meticulous debridement, and temporary skeletal stabilization, most commonly achieved with external fixation.

Concomitant vascular injuries were managed as surgical emergencies, with vascular repair performed prior to orthopedic stabilization in all cases. Vascular injuries included popliteal artery disruption, superficial femoral artery injury, anterior or posterior tibial artery injury, and combined arteriovenous injury. Repair techniques consisted of reverse saphenous vein interposition grafting, primary repair, and temporary vascular shunting followed by delayed definitive reconstruction. Prophylactic four-compartment fasciotomy was performed in all patients with vascular injury. Serial debridements were subsequently performed in the operating room to remove devitalized tissues and establish an adequately vascularized wound bed suitable for reconstruction.

Soft-tissue decision-making was conducted through an orthoplastic approach involving coordinated consultation between orthopedic trauma and plastic-reconstructive surgery teams. Definitive skeletal fixation was performed with intramedullary nailing, plate–screw osteosynthesis, or circular external fixation. The sequencing of reconstruction and fixation varied across patients and was determined based on local soft-tissue conditions and overall clinical status, such that fixation was performed either prior to, concurrently with, or following soft-tissue coverage.

Soft-tissue coverage modalities were categorized into three reconstructive classes: (1) free microsurgical flaps, (2) pedicled muscle-based flaps, and (3) local rotational fasciocutaneous flaps. Primary STSG/FTSG was not utilized as the definitive method in cases involving exposed bone or tendon. In selected cases, delayed STSG/FTSG was applied following flap transfer to achieve epidermal resurfacing when required.

All patients received standardized perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis upon admission, consisting of intravenous first-generation cephalosporin (cefazolin 2 g) combined with an aminoglycoside (gentamicin 5 mg/kg) for Gustilo–Anderson Type III injuries, in accordance with established open fracture management guidelines. Antibiotic prophylaxis was initiated in the emergency department and continued for up to 72 h following the initial debridement, or for 24 h after each subsequent surgical intervention. For patients with documented penicillin allergy, clindamycin (900 mg IV) was substituted. Empirical antibiotic regimens were modified based on intraoperative deep tissue culture results obtained during serial debridements. In cases where polymicrobial or resistant organisms were identified, targeted antibiotic therapy was initiated in consultation with the infectious disease service.

Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) was applied in all patients as a standard bridging modality between the initial debridement and definitive soft-tissue reconstruction. NPWT was administered at a continuous negative pressure of − 125 mmHg using polyurethane foam dressings, with dressing changes performed every 48–72 h coinciding with serial debridement sessions.

Data collection and grouping

Patients were stratified into three groups according to the timing of soft-tissue reconstruction: (1) acute group (≤ 10 days), (2) subacute group (11–30 days), and (3) late group (> 30 days). This stratification was based on established clinical timeframes: the acute phase corresponds to the recommended coverage window of 7–10 days described in contemporary orthoplastic guidelines [8], the subacute phase reflects the interval during which serial debridement and wound bed optimization are typically completed; and the late phase represents cases in which reconstruction was delayed beyond standard protocols, often due to logistical constraints inherent to conflict-related settings.

Demographic variables (age and sex), fracture-related characteristics (anatomical location and Gustilo–Anderson classification), and surgical parameters (number of pre-reconstruction and post-reconstruction debridements, type of definitive fixation, and time to definitive fixation) were recorded for all patients.

The primary outcome measures were the development of osteomyelitis and time to radioclinical union. Osteomyelitis was diagnosed according to the confirmatory criteria of the Fracture-Related Infection (FRI) consensus definition [13, 14], based on clinical findings (persistent wound drainage or sinus tract formation), microbiological evidence, and histopathological confirmation. At least one confirmatory criterion, such as sinus tract formation, intraoperative purulence, or concordant deep tissue cultures with evidence of osseous involvement, was required for diagnosis. The term “osteomyelitis” was used as the primary endpoint to specifically represent bone-related infection within the broader FRI framework.

Radiographic union was defined as bridging callus across at least three of four cortices on orthogonal radiographs, while clinical union was defined as the ability to bear full weight without pain at the fracture site.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows. Descriptive data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum), as appropriate. The distribution of variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons of continuous variables among the three groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed by the Mann–Whitney U test for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Correlations between variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal temporal threshold for predicting osteomyelitis. Univariable logistic regression analysis was conducted for all candidate variables to estimate crude odds ratios, and variables demonstrating statistical significance (p < 0.05) were subsequently entered into a revised multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of osteomyelitis and to calculate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In this model, time to soft-tissue reconstruction (modeled as a continuous variable), number of debridements after reconstruction, and vascular injury were included as covariates. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics and injury characteristics

A total of 56 male patients with high-energy ballistic lower extremity fractures were included, with a mean age of 26.36 ± 3.93 years (range, 20–36 years). All fractures were classified as Gustilo–Anderson type IIIB (n = 35, 62.5%) or type IIIC (n = 21, 37.5%). Fracture localization was as follows: tibial shaft (41.1%), femoral shaft (19.6%), distal tibia (14.3%), distal femur (12.5%), and proximal tibia (12.5%). Injuries predominantly involved the left side (53.6%). Vascular injury was documented in 21 patients (37.5%). Initial orthopedic management consisted primarily of external fixator (EF) application in 91.1% of cases, followed by splint immobilization (7.1%) and circular external fixation (ECF) (1.8%). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort are summarized in (Table 1; Fig. 2). The mean follow-up duration was 18.4 ± 8.7 months (median 16.5; range 6–42 months). All patients were followed for a minimum of 6 months, with 89.3% (50/56) achieving at least 12 months of follow-up.

Surgical and reconstructive details

The mean number of debridements performed prior to definitive soft-tissue reconstruction was 4.46 ± 3.79 (median, 4; range, 0–21). Definitive soft-tissue coverage was performed at a mean of 27.34 ± 22.18 days following injury (median, 25; range, 2–120). After reconstruction, patients underwent an additional mean of 3.48 ± 3.34 debridements (median, 2; range, 0–11). Definitive orthopedic fixation was performed at a mean of 51.46 ± 36.34 days after injury (median, 55; range, 1–145). Among the 42 patients who achieved union, the mean time to bone union was 6.48 ± 2.89 months (median, 6; range, 3–13).

With respect to reconstruction timing, 13 patients (23.2%) underwent acute coverage (≤ 10 days), 21 patients (37.5%) underwent subacute coverage (11–30 days), and 22 patients (39.3%) underwent late coverage (> 30 days). Definitive soft-tissue coverage was exclusively flap-based. Free flaps were used in 16 patients (28.6%), pedicled muscle-based flaps in 21 patients (37.5%), and local rotational fasciocutaneous flaps in 19 patients (33.9%). Primary STSG/FTSG was not employed as a standalone reconstructive modality; however, delayed STSG/FTSG following flap transfer was required in 16 patients (28.6%) to obtain epithelial closure.

Regarding definitive orthopedic management, intramedullary nailing was the most commonly utilized method (44.6%), followed by plate–screw osteosynthesis (25.0%), circular external fixator (17.9%), and monolateral external fixator (12.5%). Bone union occurred in 42 patients (75.0%) and nonunion in 14 patients (25.0%), while osteomyelitis developed in 23 patients (41.1%) (Table 2).

Osteomyelitis outcomes

Osteomyelitis was identified in 23 patients (41.1%) (Fig. 3). A significant association was detected between the timing of soft-tissue reconstruction and the development of osteomyelitis (p = 0.04). Patients who underwent late reconstruction (> 30 days) exhibited markedly higher osteomyelitis rates (68.2%; 15/22), and this rate differed significantly from both the acute (≤ 10 days) and subacute (11–30 days) groups (23.1% and 23.8%, respectively; both p < 0.01). No significant difference was observed between the acute and subacute groups (p > 0.05).

Proportion of osteomyelitis according to time to soft-tissue reconstruction (acute, subacute, and late groups), demonstrating increased infection rates with delayed reconstruction

Patients who developed osteomyelitis had a longer time to soft-tissue reconstruction (35.52 ± 25.44 vs. 23.61 ± 18.66 days; p = 0.007) and required a higher number of post-reconstruction debridements (4.61 ± 3.46 vs. 2.70 ± 3.07; p = 0.006). In contrast, no significant differences were identified regarding the number of pre-reconstruction debridements (p = 0.100) or the timing of definitive orthopedic fixation (p = 0.325). No significant associations were found between osteomyelitis and definitive orthopedic fixation method (p > 0.05), fracture location (p > 0.05), or the type of soft-tissue reconstruction (free flap, pedicled muscle-based flap, or local fasciocutaneous flap) (p > 0.05). However, the presence of vascular injury was significantly associated with the development of osteomyelitis (p = 0.014) (Table 3).

ROC curve analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis identified reconstruction timing as a significant predictor of osteomyelitis (AUC: 0.773, 95% CI 0.570–0.855, p = 0.007). The optimal threshold for reconstruction timing was determined to be 14.5 days according to the Youden index (Sensitivity: 87.0%, Specificity: 67.6%). This value represents a statistically derived cutoff rather than a biologically predetermined inflection point. Patients undergoing reconstruction after 14.5 days demonstrated a markedly higher rate of osteomyelitis (58.8%) compared to those reconstructed within 14.5 days (13.6%) (Fig. 4; Table 4).

Logistic regression analysis

In univariable logistic regression analysis, time to soft-tissue reconstruction (days) (OR 1.034, 95% CI 1.003–1.066, p = 0.034), number of debridements after reconstruction (OR 1.222, 95% CI 1.025–1.456, p = 0.025), and presence of vascular injury (OR 4.062, 95% CI 1.291–12.780, p = 0.017) were significantly associated with the development of osteomyelitis. Other variables, including age, fracture localization, definitive fixation method, number of pre-reconstruction debridements, time to definitive fixation, and flap type, were not significantly associated with osteomyelitis (all p > 0.05). Variables found to be significant in the univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that time to soft-tissue reconstruction (days) remained a significant independent predictor of osteomyelitis (OR 1.043, 95% CI 1.011–1.087, p = 0.011). In addition, the number of debridements after reconstruction (OR 1.296, 95% CI 1.049–1.578, p = 0.015) and the presence of vascular injury (OR 7.765, 95% CI 1.446–24.101, p = 0.013) were identified as independent risk factors for osteomyelitis (Table 5). These findings indicate a dose–response relationship, whereby each additional day of delay in soft-tissue reconstruction is associated with a 4.3% increase in the odds of osteomyelitis, independent of other covariates.

Bone union outcomes

Bone union was achieved in 42 patients (75.0%), with a mean union time of 6.48 ± 2.89 months. No significant association was identified between the timing of soft-tissue reconstruction and time to union. In contrast, a significant positive correlation was observed between the number of pre-reconstruction debridements and prolonged union time, indicating that a greater debridement burden may delay bone healing (Table 6).

When analyzed according to definitive fixation method, time to union differed significantly among groups (p = 0.036). Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that delaying soft-tissue reconstruction beyond a 14.5-day threshold in ballistic lower-extremity open fractures constitutes an independent risk factor for osteomyelitis and is associated with progressively increasing infection risk. This observation provides a temporal framework for the long-standing debate on early versus late soft-tissue reconstruction in high-energy ballistic trauma.

Historically, Godina advocated performing microsurgical reconstruction within the first 72 h to minimize the risks of infection and flap failure [7]. However, this recommendation predates the widespread adoption of negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) and contemporary aggressive debridement protocols [15]. In current orthoplastic practice, the ‘safe window’ for reconstruction has been extended to 7–10 days due to the beneficial effects of NPWT in reducing exudate, controlling bacterial burden, and promoting granulation tissue formation [8, 10].

These findings align with accumulating evidence supporting a dose–response relationship between reconstruction delay and infection. A meta-analysis of 43 studies confirmed that free-flap coverage within 72 h significantly reduces both flap failure and infection rates [16]. Tiongco et al. reported a 50% reduction in infection with coverage within 7 days across 973 patients [9]. In a nationwide analysis of 1,970 open lower extremity fractures, each additional day of delay in flap coverage increased the odds of fracture-related infection by approximately 10% (OR 1.107, 95% CI 1.062–1.154), supporting a dose–response pattern consistent with our continuous-variable analysis, although the lower magnitude observed in our cohort may reflect differences in injury severity and wound management protocols [17]. Conversely, time to coverage was not independently associated with chronic osteomyelitis when standardized NPWT protocols were applied, suggesting that protocolized wound management may attenuate, but not eliminate, the consequences of delayed reconstruction [11].

Traditionally, the subacute period (7–21 days) has been regarded as a ‘high-risk’ phase for reconstruction due to the peak inflammatory response and progressive fibrosis [11]. In our cohort, the identification of an approximately two-week threshold in ballistic injuries suggests that reconstruction timing should be evaluated not solely on chronological duration, but in conjunction with wound readiness for definitive coverage. This concept aligns with the literature emphasizing the importance of wound bed preparation and infection control prior to closure [18]. The 14.5-day threshold likely reflects both biological and systemic factors: from a biological standpoint, progressive biofilm maturation and fibrotic tissue organization create an increasingly hostile environment for vascularized tissue integration [19]. from a systemic standpoint, the inherent logistical constraints of conflict settings shift reconstruction into the subacute phase. Our data suggest that protocolized wound management, including serial debridement and NPWT, can safely extend the reconstruction window beyond the classical 72-hour paradigm, but not indefinitely. This is consistent with the marked rise in infection rates observed beyond the 14.5-day threshold in our cohort (13.6% vs. 58.8%; Table 4) [6, 20]. The continuous-variable analysis further supports this finding, demonstrating a progressive increase in infection risk with each additional day of delay, rather than an abrupt step-change at 14.5 days.

Ballistic trauma differs from civilian open fractures due to its unique tissue biology. The phenomenon of “temporary cavitation” causes damage beyond the visible permanent tract, leading to microvascular disruption, muscle necrosis, and delayed tissue non-viability [21]. Consequently, defining true tissue viability in ballistic injuries often requires serial and radical debridement [2]. In our cohort, a mean of 4.46 pre-reconstruction debridements was performed, highlighting the necessity of a cautious and staged approach to achieve a healthy granulation bed for definitive coverage.

In this study, vascular injury was identified as a risk factor for osteomyelitis in ballistic open fractures. Temporary cavitation affects not only muscle and bone but also the microvascular network, resulting in hypoperfusion and impaired immune response [22–24]. This biological environment impairs bacterial clearance and facilitates biofilm formation [23]. The requirement for post-reconstruction debridement was also found to be an independent risk factor for osteomyelitis. This finding likely reflects residual contamination, biofilm persistence, or inadequate perfusion at the wound site [25, 26]. Thus, the number of post-reconstruction debridements may serve as an indicator of persistent infection rather than merely procedural burden. Notably, osteomyelitis risk appeared to be influenced not only by reconstruction timing but also by the microbiological and biological condition of the wound bed.

Interestingly, no significant difference in osteomyelitis rates was observed among patients reconstructed using free, pedicled, or local flaps. This observation supports contemporary orthoplastic principles suggesting that infection control is determined less by flap type and more by adequate debridement, sufficient perfusion, and appropriate timing.

Another notable finding of this study was the absence of a significant relationship between reconstruction timing and fracture union. Although recent meta-analyses suggest that early soft-tissue reconstruction may reduce nonunion rates [9, 27], our data indicate that the number of pre-reconstruction debridements was the main determinant of delayed union. This supports the concept that bone healing is primarily influenced by the severity of initial trauma and degree of periosteal stripping rather than reconstruction timing alone [5, 28]. Meanwhile, patients treated with circular external fixation demonstrated significantly prolonged union times (p = 0.002), likely reflecting a selection bias in which external fixation is preferentially used for segmental bone loss and complex injury patterns.

The exclusively ballistic nature of our patient population is an important contextual factor, as logistical delays and evacuation pathways in military or conflict environments often shift reconstruction into the subacute phase, reducing the practicality of early reconstruction. The experience of our center in managing ballistic trauma, combined with the “bridging” role of negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) in preserving the wound bed, may have contributed to the safe utilization of an approximately two-week window for reconstruction, consistent with the 14.5-day ROC-derived threshold.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, limited sample size, and single-center nature. A subset of patients had follow-up durations shorter than the 12-month minimum recommended by the FRI Consensus Group. Detailed subclassification of ballistic mechanisms was not consistently documented, precluding stratified analyses. NPWT was uniformly applied as part of the institutional protocol, and its duration was not systematically recorded, limiting evaluation as an independent variable. The ROC-derived 14.5-day threshold was identified in a single-center cohort without internal or external validation, and the relatively wide confidence interval reflects statistical uncertainty and a potential risk of overfitting. Nevertheless, the homogeneous high-energy ballistic etiology, restriction to Gustilo–Anderson IIIB–IIIC injuries, and consistent application of orthoplastic protocols represent important methodological strengths. Future prospective, multicenter studies with standardized documentation are warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides exploratory insights into the timing of soft-tissue reconstruction in ballistic lower extremity open fractures. The findings suggest that, within contemporary wound management paradigms, the biological readiness of the wound bed may be more relevant than strict chronological thresholds. When supported by systematic and adequately thorough debridement, soft-tissue coverage within an approximate two-week interval appears achievable without a clear increase in osteomyelitis risk. These observations highlight the potential value of a patient- and wound-specific, flexible, and biologically guided orthoplastic approach in the management of ballistic extremity trauma. Given the exploratory design of the study, these findings should be interpreted with caution and validated in larger, prospective cohorts. Furthermore, they are specific to high-energy ballistic Gustilo–Anderson IIIB–IIIC injuries and may not be generalizable to civilian blunt-mechanism open fractures, which differ substantially in wound characteristics, contamination profiles, and reconstructive considerations. Taken together, we believe that this study may contribute to the evolving understanding of timing strategies in complex ballistic injuries and to inform more nuanced, biology-driven clinical decision-making.
