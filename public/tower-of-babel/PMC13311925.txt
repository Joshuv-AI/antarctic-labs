Differential toll-like receptor signaling pathways shape IL-1β-dependent inflammatory responses in chicken macrophages
Authors: Seungyoon Roh, Tina Sørensen Dalgaard, Ngo Thuy Bao Tran, Tae Sub Park, Cheol-Heui Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Macrophages are primary regulators of the innate immune system, integrating pattern recognition receptor (PRR) signaling and cytokine production to shape early inflammatory processes. However, the molecular mechanisms governing macrophage-driven responses in chickens remain poorly understood, despite their importance in controlling major infectious diseases. Among these pathways, interleukin (IL)-1β functions as a central mediator of inflammatory signaling, yet its integration with Toll-like receptor (TLR) pathways in chicken macrophages remains unclear. Here, lipoteichoic acid (LTA)-Toll-like receptor (TLR)2, poly(I:C)-TLR3, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-TLR4 pathways were investigated in the chicken macrophage-like cell line HD11 and an IL1β-deficient HD11 mutant, with PBMC-derived macrophages included for comparison. HD11 cells exhibited minimal responsiveness to LTA–TLR2 stimulation, whereas LPS–TLR4 stimulation elicited a limited response primarily characterized by strong induction of IL-1β and IL-8 but minimal or undetectable IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, IL-18, and interferon (IFN)-β. In contrast, poly(I:C)–TLR3 stimulation induced IL-1β, IL-18, and IFN-β with limited induction of other cytokines. A genetically engineered CRISPR/Cas9 IL1B knockout (KO) HD11 model has been established to evaluate molecular interactions of the inflammatory response, which appears to be modulated by IL-1β in a TLR ligand-specific context. The present study highlights distinct pattern recognition profiles on chicken macrophages that may offer a molecular basis for IL-1β-targeted signaling networks and immunomodulation in chickens.

Introduction

The global poultry industry is a fast-growing agricultural sector, supplying essential high-quality protein sources such as chicken meat and eggs. With rising international demand (Cai et al., 2024), the economic value of poultry production has grown substantially, and disruptions carry far-reaching consequences for food security and trade stability (Kleyn and Ciacciariello, 2021). Expansion coincide with persistent threats from infectious diseases, including avian influenza (Raphael et al., 2025), infectious bronchitis (Sun et al., 2021), and salmonellosis (Matulova et al., 2012). These pathogens highly transmissible, cause severe morbidity or mortality, and in some cases pose zoonotic risks, resulting in flock losses, movement restrictions, and significant economic burdens across the production chain (Raphael et al., 2025). The capacity to rapidly and effectively initiate immune responses is critical for disease outcomes in poultry, underscoring the importance of understanding the cellular and molecular mechanisms that regulate these responses.

In chickens, like in mammals, innate immune responses are the front line of defense during early infection, with macrophages playing a key role in antimicrobial defense (Barton, 2008). They perform key functions including phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and cytokine and chemokine production that shape downstream immune responses (Chen et al., 2023). Chicken macrophages, while sharing features with mammalian ones, display distinct receptor usage and downstream signaling pathways (Keestra et al., 2013), highlighting the necessity of avian-specific research. Despite its pivotal role in inflammatory amplification loops, the molecular architecture governing cytokine hierarchies in chicken macrophages remains incompletely defined.

Effective macrophage activation relies on recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns particularly by Toll-like receptors (TLRs). Avian TLR2, TLR3, and TLR4 are important innate immune pathways that respond to Gram-positive bacteria (TLR2), Gram-negative bacteria (TLR4), and viral double-stranded RNA (TLR3), respectively, resulting in early inflammatory cytokine production (Barjesteh et al., 2014). These streamline frameworks could allow us to investigate pathogen diversity, cellular localization, and signaling pathway differences in a controlled experimental setting, which is critical for protective immunity against infectious diseases in poultry. TLR2 is known to mainly recognizes bacterial lipoproteins and lipoteichoic acid (LTA), signals predominantly through MyD88-dependent pathways (Yu et al., 2018). TLR3 function as the main endosomal sensor for double-stranded RNA and its synthetic analog poly(I:C), activating TRIF (TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-beta)-dependent pathways that induce IFN-β production and regulation of antiviral gene expression (Hong et al., 2021). TLR4 primarily recognizes lipopolysaccharide (LPS) derived from the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria. Despite sharing features with mammalian systems, TLR4 in chickens exhibits species-specific differences in adaptor utilization and signaling kinetics, which could affect the balance between MyD88- and TRIF-dependent transcriptional outputs (Kannaki et al., 2015; Keestra and van Putten, 2008).

After TLR activation in mammals, adaptor-mediated signaling leads to NF-κB and interferon regulatory factors, driving the transcription of proinflammatory mediators (Brown et al., 2011). Among these, interleukin (IL)-1β is a key inflammatory cytokine produced by macrophages, resulting in positive-feedback loops that enhance and sustain inflammatory signaling (Aggeletopoulou et al., 2024; Alomar et al., 2016).Mammalian IL-1β promotes the expression of chemokines, antimicrobial effectors, and inflammatory mediators, including IL-8, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2) (Kim et al., 2010). Hence, the regulatory functions of IL-1β are well characterized in mammals; however, its role in shaping cytokine hierarchies and effector programs in chicken macrophages is not yet fully understood. Chickens lack several essential TRIF-dependent components, including TRAM and IRF3 (Brownlie and Allan, 2011; Cheng et al., 2019) and a functional RIG-I orthologue (Magor et al., 2013). This suggests that avian macrophages may rely on distinct signaling strategies, potentially favoring MyD88-dependent pathways.

Although IL-1β has been implicated in inflammatory amplification, its causal role within specific TLR signaling pathways in chicken macrophages remains unclear, necessitating approaches to dissect its contribution. The present study aimed to define the regulatory role of IL-1β in representative pattern-recognition receptor (PRR) pathways by analyzing TLR2 (LTA), TLR3 (poly(I:C)), and TLR4 (LPS) signaling in the HD11 chicken macrophage-like cell line, and IL-1β-deficient HD11 cells. This framework aimed to determine how cellular context and IL-1β dependency shape inflammatory and antiviral responses in chicken macrophages.

Materials and methods

Animal experiment

Fertile eggs from White Leghorn chickens were obtained from the research animal facility at Seoul National University (Pyeongchang, Korea) and incubated at 37°C for 3 weeks. Newly hatched chicks were raised under conventional husbandry conditions and provided feed and water ad libitum without feed additives or vaccination for the first two weeks. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Seoul National University (IACUC No. SNU-251106-4).

Cell preparation

Peripheral blood was collected aseptically from the brachial wing vein of 10- to 16-week-old chickens in sodium heparin blood collection tubes (Becton, Dickinson and Company, 367871) and diluted 1:1 with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Cytiva, SH30022.01). The diluted blood was layered onto Ficoll-Histopaque 1083 (Sigma-Aldrich, 10831), then centrifuged at 400 × g for 20 min at room temperature without brake. Following centrifugation, the plasma was discarded, and the cloudy layer with mononuclear cells was collected into a separate tube. PBMCs were washed twice with complete medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% Antibiotic–Antimycotic) by centrifugation at 400 × g for 10 min at room temperature.

The cells were resuspended in complete medium, and 2 × 107 cells were plated into each well of sterile, flat-bottom 6-well plates. Monocytes were enriched by plastic adherence by incubating PBMCs for 2 h at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ in incubator. Non-adherent cells were gently removed, and adherent cells were maintained in complete medium for 2 more days at 37°C in humidified 5% CO₂ incubator to allow monocyte derived macrophages to develop and remove thrombocytes. This basic protocol for monocyte derived macrophages was previously published by others (Feng et al., 2017; Kaspers et al., 1993).

Cell culture

HD11 chicken macrophages, a cell line derived from chicken bone marrow and transformed by avian leukemia virus MC29 (Beug et al., 1979), were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Cytiva, SH30022.01) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Antibiotic–Antimycotic (Gibco, 15240062). The cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and were passaged using Trypsin–EDTA (Gibco, 15400054) once they reached approximately 80% confluence.

IL1B gRNA vector design

A pUC-Amp plasmid backbone was used to generate a CRISPR–Cas9 guide RNA expression construct targeting the chicken interleukin-1 beta (IL1B) gene. The exon sequence of IL1B was obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu). A guide RNA (gRNA) was designed on exon 1 based on the presence of a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence (TGG). The selected targeting sequence was 5′-ATGGCGTTCGTTCCCGACC-3′. The gRNA expression cassette was placed under the control of the U6 promoter and terminator. Expression of Cas9 was achieved using a CMV-Cas9-2A-GFP expression plasmid purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Transfection vectors into HD11 cells

HD11 cells were transfected with the IL1B gRNA vector and a Cas9 expression plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000001), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until approximately 70–80% confluence. Prior to transfection, the cells were washed twice with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), and the culture medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, without Antibiotic–Antimycotic. For complex formation, 7.5 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 was mixed with 250 μL Opti-MEM (Invitrogen, 31985062) in one tube, and 10 μL of P3000 reagent was mixed with 250 μL Opti-MEM together with 2.5 μg of gRNA plasmid and 2.5 μg of Cas9 plasmid in a separate tube. Both solutions were combined and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The resulting mixture was added dropwise to each well. After 5 h of incubation at 37°C, the cells were washed three times with DPBS and transferred to complete medium containing 10% FBS and Antibiotic–Antimycotic. GFP-positive cells expressing Cas9 were isolated the following day by cell sorting (FACSAria, BD Biosciences).

FACS sorting and single cell cloning

Following transfection with the gRNA and Cas9 expression plasmids, GFP-positive cells were isolated by using cell sorter (FACSAria). The sorted GFP-positive cells were seeded into 12-well plate for expansion. Then, single-cell cloning was performed by depositing one cell per well into 96-well plates, and monoclonal colonies derived from individual cells were cultured until sufficient cell numbers were obtained for downstream analysis.

Genomic PCR

To sequence genomic DNA from single-cell derived HD11 colonies, gene-specific primers targeting the IL1B locus were designed. The forward primer was located in the intronic region upstream of exon 1 (5′-CGTGCTGCACATACGAAACT-3′), and the reverse primer was located within exon 1 (5′-CCCTCCCATCCTTACCTTCT-3′). Genomic PCR was performed with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 60°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s. A final extension step was carried out at 72°C for 5 min, and reactions were held at 4°C until further analysis.

T-vector cloning and sequencing data analysis

Purified PCR products were cloned into a T-vector using the pGEM®-T Easy Vector System I Kit (Promega, A1360). Ligation reactions were set up by combining 5 μL of 2× ligation buffer, 1 μL of pGEM-T Easy vector, 1 μL of T4 DNA ligase, and 3 μL of purified PCR product, followed by incubation overnight at 4°C. The ligation mixture was transformed into DH5α chemically competent E. coli. After plasmid preparation and sequencing, the mutant genotypes were analyzed using NCBI BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi).

Flow cytometry analysis

PBMCs were cultured as aforementioned and then separated into whole, suspension, and adherent fractions prior to staining. The cells were washed with PBS and incubated with mouse anti-chicken monocyte/macrophage-PE (KUL01; SouthernBiotech, 8420-09) and mouse anti-chicken MHC class II-FITC (2G11; SouthernBiotech, 8350-02).

Following the staining, the cells were washed and resuspended in 10% FBS/PBS for analysis. Flow cytometric acquisition was performed, and cell populations were gated based on forward scatter area (FSC-A) and side scatter area (SSC-A) profiles to exclude debris and non-cellular events. MRC1 (PE) and MHCII (FITC) expression was analyzed, and adherent double-positive populations with higher FSC and increased expression of the two markers were identified and defined as PBMC-derived macrophages (Peng et al., 2020).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Purified HD11 cells and PBMC-derived macrophages were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well in 6-well culture plates and stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Salmonella Typhimurium, 1 μg/mL), lipoteichoic acid (LTA, Staphylococcus aureus, 10 μg/mL), or polyinosinic–polycytidylic acid [poly(I:C), 2 μg/mL] with lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000001) at the indicated final concentrations. The concentrations of TLR ligands were selected based on previous studies and standard experimental conditions to ensure effective cytokine induction without apparent cytotoxic effects (Haddadi et al., 2015; Hong-Yan et al., 2023). Following stimulation, cells were incubated for 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C. At each time point, the cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 μg of total RNA with the SuperScript™ III First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen, 18-080-051), following the recommended protocol. Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer3Plus (https://www.primer3plus.com), and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted with an EvaGreen-based Real-Time PCR Kit (Solgent, SRH91-R250) on a CFX Duet Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). The primer sequences used for quantitative real-time PCR are listed in Table 1. Expression levels were normalized to chicken glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal reference gene. GAPDH expression remained stable across all experimental conditions. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method after normalization to the GAPDH transcript levels (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). All qRT-PCR reactions were carried out in triplicate. PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide. DNA fragments were visualized under ultraviolet illumination using a transilluminator. Western blot

HD11 cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer, and total protein was collected by following the recommended protocol. Protein concentration was determined using a bovine serum albumin (BSA)-based colorimetric protein assay reagent (Bio-Rad, 5000001). The samples were mixed with 6× Laemmli SDS sample buffer (Thermo Scientific, J61337) and heated at 100°C for 5 min to denature proteins. Equal amounts of protein were separated by electrophoresis on 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad, 1620112). After transfer, membranes were blocked in TBST supplemented with BSA for 2 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Primary antibodies used included mouse anti–β-actin antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-47778) and rabbit anti–cIL-1β polyclonal antibody (Invitrogen, PA1-84913). After incubation, membranes were washed five times with TBST and were incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for one hour at room temperature. Goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP (Bio-Rad, 1706516) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L)-HRP (Invitrogen, 31460) were used as secondary antibodies. After five additional washes with TBST, protein bands were visualized using Clarity™ Western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad, 1705061) and imaged with a ChemiDoc™ XRS+ imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.1; GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent biological replicates (n = 3), with each sample measured in technical triplicates. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. When two independent variables were evaluated, two-way ANOVA with Šidák’s post hoc test was conducted. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001, and p < 0.0001.

Results

Ligand-specific induction of inflammatory cytokines in PBMC-derived macrophages

Understanding the cytokine response patterns in chickens elicited by distinct PRR stimuli in macrophages is essential for defining early innate immune dynamics, particularly given their relevance in host defense against infectious diseases (Wang et al., 2021). Prior to assessing ligand-specific cytokine responses, the expression of TLRs relevant to the applied stimuli was examined. As shown in Fig. S1, all macrophages used in the current study exhibited detectable expression of TLR2, TLR3, and TLR4, as well as TLR1, which functions as a heterodimeric partner of TLR2 (Keestra et al., 2013). These results indicate that both HD11 cells and monocyte derived chicken macrophages possess the necessary cognitive receptors required to respond to stimulation by LTA, poly(I:C), and LPS. To investigate functional responses induced by distinct PRR pathways in chicken, macrophages derived from PBMCs were used, and macrophage enrichment was confirmed by increased MRC1-positive populations in the adherent fraction (Fig. S2A).

To define cytokine induction triggered by TLR4 activation, PBMC-derived macrophages were stimulated with LPS, and the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, IL-18, and TNF-α was analyzed (Fig. S2B). LPS stimulation induced a rapid and strong upregulation of IL-1β, peaking at approximately 6 h and declining thereafter, with IL-6 showing a similar pattern. IL-12 and IL-18 were moderately induced, whereas TNF-α remained low. These results indicate that TLR4 activation is characterized by dominant IL-1β and IL-6 responses.

In contrast, LTA stimulation primarily induced a transient IL-1β response at early time points (1–3 h), with minimal changes in other cytokines (Fig. S2C), suggesting a more restricted inflammatory profile downstream of TLR2. These results suggest that TLR2 activation in PBMC-derived macrophages primarily elicits a strong IL-1β response, without distinct induction of the additional inflammatory cytokines.

To examine cytokine induction by RNA-sensing pathways, PBMC-derived macrophages were stimulated with poly(I:C) (Fig. S2D). Poly(I:C) stimulation resulted in significant induction of IL-1β, peaking at 3–6 h, along with moderate IL-18 expression, while IL-6, IL-12, and TNF-α remained near baseline, indicating selective activation of IL-1β–IL-18–associated responses. These results indicate that poly(I:C)-mediated TLR3/RNA-sensing pathways induce an inflammatory response characterized by involvement of IL-1β and IL-18.

It is clear that PBMC-derived macrophages exhibit ligand-specific inflammatory cytokine responses downstream of distinct PRR pathways, with IL-1β consistently and most prominently induced as a central inflammatory output. These data demonstrated that innate inflammatory responses in PBMC-derived chicken macrophages are dominated by IL-1β, with additional cytokines produced in a ligand-dependent manner.

HD11 cells with dominant IL-1β-driven cytokine responses

Next, to enable controlled mechanistic studies, we evaluated whether the immortalized chicken macrophage-like cell line HD11 recapitulates key features of TLR-induced cytokine responses observed in PBMC-derived macrophages. HD11 cells represent a widely used model for avian macrophage biology, although some differences from primary cells have also been documented (Ijaz et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2018). To directly compare cytokine responses between primary and immortalized macrophages, HD11 cells were stimulated with LPS, LTA, or poly(I:C) using identical conditions as previously described.

To assess cytokine induction downstream of TLR4, transcript levels of cytokines were examined in HD11 cells stimulated with LPS. Similar to the primary PBMC-derived macrophages, HD11 cells exhibited a rapid induction of IL-1β, with expression significantly elevated and peaking at approximately 6 h, followed by a decline toward baseline levels by 12–24 h (Fig. 1A). In contrast, IL-12, IL-18, and TNF-α mRNA levels remained near the baseline across all the time points measured. Interestingly, IL-6 transcripts were not reliably detected above the baseline (Fig. S3). Following LTA stimulation, IL-1β levels in HD11 cells did not show induction comparable to that observed in PBMC-derived macrophages (Fig. 1B). Levels of other proinflammatory cytokines, IL-12, IL-18, and TNF-α mRNA, remained near baseline. Consistent with the results obtained from LPS stimulation, IL-6 transcripts were not observed above baseline levels in HD11 cells treated with LTA. In response to poly(I:C) stimulation (Fig. 1C), HD11 cells showed an increase in IL-1β expression, peaking at approximately 12 h before declining by 24 h. IL-18 also displayed a significant induction, mirroring to the expression pattern of IL-1β. It is intriguing that IL-12 and TNF-α did not show notable induction and remained near baseline across the time course. As observed in the other stimulation groups, IL-6 transcripts consistently low or undetectable. These findings indicate that poly(I:C) stimulation in HD11 cells is associated with a robust induction of IL-1β and IL-18.

Despite stimulation with the selected TLR ligands, IL-6 amplification was not detected in HD11 cells (Fig. S3). While primary macrophages exhibited a distinct melting peak at approximately 90°C consistent with specific IL-6 amplification, HD11 samples showed only lower-temperature peaks (<80°C) overlapping with the non-template control, suggesting the absence of specific amplification (Fig. S3A). Gel electrophoresis further confirmed successful amplification of GAPDH, whereas no IL-6 amplicons were detected using four independent primer sets (IL-6_1–4) (Fig. S3B), suggesting that the absence of IL-6 detection in HD11 cells is not due to failure of PCR amplification or primers.

CRISPR-Cas9 mediated gene KO in HD11 cells

Since among the inflammatory cytokines examined, IL-1β showed the most consistent and prominent changes in response to TLR2, TLR3, and TLR4 stimulation in PBMC-derived macrophages (Fig. S2) and HD11 cells (Fig. 1), an IL-1β knockout (IL1B KO) HD11 cell line was generated to investigate how the absence of IL-1β affects inflammatory responses. After IL-1β knockout by CRISPR-Cas9, sequencing analysis confirmed that the IL1β KO#2 clone contained two deletion mutations consisting of a 1-bp G deletion and an independent 4-bp CCCG deletion located at the CRISPR cut site (Fig. 2A). Translation of the edited sequence into its predicted amino acid sequence revealed a frameshift mutation leading to a premature stop codon (Fig. 2B). To functionally validate the loss of IL-1β expression at the protein level, cell lysates from WT and IL-1β KO cells were analyzed by Western blotting. Consistent with the sequencing results, ∼29 kDa IL-1β protein was detectable in WT cells, whereas no IL-1β band at the expected site was observed in the KO cells (Fig. 2C). The observed molecular weight is consistent with the predicted size of uncleaved chicken IL-1β and represents the full-length pro-IL-1β form. Effects of Il-1β deficiency on LPS-induced inflammatory responses in HD11 cells

To determine whether the absence of IL-1β alters the induction of other primary inflammatory cytokines in HD11 cells, WT and IL-1β-deficient HD11 cells were stimulated with LPS, and the expression of IL-12, IL-18, and TNF-α was analyzed. Results from PBMC-derived macrophages are shown for conditions showing significant differences, while conditions lacking substantial changes are not omitted for clarity. Both WT and IL-1β KO cells exhibited no notable changes in IL-12, IL-18, and TNF-α mRNA expression at any of the time points measured, with overall expression profiles remaining close to baseline levels (Fig. 3A-B). At later time points (6–24 h), IL-12 and IL-18 transcripts showed a modest increase only in IL-1β KO cells, while WT cells remained near the baseline (Fig. 3B). In IL-1β KO cells, the induction of IL-12 and IL-18 was limited compared with the strong LPS response observed in PBMC-derived macrophages (Fig. 3B). Discussion

In chickens, TLR2 signaling predominantly depends on a MyD88-dependent axis (Brownlie and Allan, 2011), whereas TLR4 engages both the MyD88 and TRIF adaptors (Keestra and van Putten, 2008), and TLR3-poly(I:C) sensing involves the TRIF pathway (Lee et al., 2020). Given that IL-1β–IL-1R signaling is also primarily mediated through MyD88-dependent pathways, these differences in adaptor usage across TLR pathways may influence how IL-1β signaling intersects with, or feeds back into, TLR-induced responses. Consistent with this framework, the present study demonstrates that IL-1β is not a universal amplifier of inflammation in chicken macrophages but acts as a context-dependent regulator within pathway-specific modules. Under TLR2 activation, IL-1β may contribute to enhanced induction of PTGS2, consistent with its known role in NF-κB–dependent inflammatory signaling (Tominari et al., 2021). By contrast, under TLR4-LPS or TLR3-poly(I:C) stimulation, IL-1β deficiency was associated with increased PTGS2 expression at later time points, suggesting that IL-1β may exert differential regulatory effects depending on the upstream signaling pathway, potentially enhancing or restraining inflammatory outputs. This aligns with previous studies demonstrating that IL-1 family cytokines are central regulators of innate immune responses and are integral to the mode of action of vaccine adjuvants (Munoz-Wolf and Lavelle, 2018).

Evidence indicates that IL-1 signaling can produce divergent outcomes depending on the cellular and pathological context, supporting the notion that a single cytokine may have distinct effects under different conditions (Evavold and Kagan, 2022). In addition, distinct TLR agonists are known to induce qualitatively different transcriptional programs and immune polarization profiles. This supports the idea that IL-1β-mediated modulation may vary according to the upstream signaling pathways engaged, rather than acting as a uniform downstream amplifier (Gnjatic et al., 2010). These observations provide a conceptual framework wherein IL-1β functions as a context-dependent regulator of inflammatory signaling in avian macrophages. Importantly, mRNA expression levels do not necessarily reflect protein abundance, as protein levels are regulated by multiple layers including translation efficiency, degradation, and temporal delays between transcription and protein synthesis (Liu et al., 2016). Therefore, the transcriptional changes observed in this study should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect time-dependent regulatory dynamics or post-transcriptional buffering effects rather than direct functional outcomes. Taken together, while further studies are necessary to define the precise mechanisms involved, this model has significant implications for understanding innate immune regulation in chickens. It may also inform future studies in disease biology, immunology, and veterinary applications.

From a methodological standpoint, these findings indicate that HD11 is a powerful tool for mechanistic examination, particularly for uncovering pathway-specific regulatory logic and analyzing IL-1β-dependent feedback architecture. HD11 results should be contextualized with primary macrophage data to avoid overgeneralization. Previous studies have demonstrated that the origin of macrophage, along with distinction between immortalized and primary cells, substantially influence their phenotypic and functional responses to stimuli (Graf et al., 2024). Although both cell types respond to TLR2, TLR3, and TLR4 stimulation in qualitatively similar directions, there are significant differences in the magnitude, temporal structure, and hierarchical organization of cytokine networks. This discrepancy suggests that innate immune responses in chickens may vary depending on the cellular context of the responding macrophage population. These differences may be associated with intrinsic cellular properties of each system, potentially including differences in transcriptional regulation (Li and Yang, 2025), although the underlying mechanisms were not directly examined in this study. The integration of both cell-lines and primary cells ensures that mechanistic insights are grounded in physiological relevance, while allowing controlled manipulation of signaling modules.

While this study offers a comprehensive understanding of TLR2, TLR3, and TLR4-driven inflammatory processes and IL-1β-dependent regulation in chicken macrophages, it is critical to recognize a number of significant limitations. These restrictions originate from molecular gaps unique to chickens, incomplete understanding of IL-1 signaling pathway, and the constraints of the experimental systems used, such as lacking appropriate antibodies.

The molecular mechanisms linking TLR signaling to IL-1β activation in chickens remain incompletely defined. In contrast to mammals, where the inflammasome-dependent processing of IL-1β is well characterized, the identity and functional relevance of inflammasome components in chickens are still ambiguous. Consequently, the activity IL-1β in this study was inferred primarily from transcriptional analysis and genetic deletion, without direct assessment of cytokine processing, secretion, or receptor-mediated signaling.

This study primarily utilized the HD11 macrophage-like cell line, an immortalized and virus-transformed system, which may exhibit altered transcriptional characteristics in comparison to primary macrophages (Yang et al., 2025). Although PBMC-derived macrophages were included to partially address this limitation, further validation using additional primary cells and in vivo systems will be necessary to confirm our findings.

Addressing these gaps will enable to progress beyond the correlative observations presented and build mechanistically validated, chicken-specific models of IL-1β function that account for both pathway architecture and cellular context.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that inflammatory and antiviral transcriptional programs in chicken macrophages are organized according to the specific stimulus and cell type involved. Activation of TLR2, TLR3, and TLR4 led to unique response patterns; primary PBMC-derived macrophages exhibited broad and rapid cytokine production, whereas HD11 cells showed more restricted responses. Importantly, IL-1β regulates downstream inflammatory mediators in a stimulus-specific manner. IL-1β, upon TLR2 stimulation, promotes the expression of inflammatory genes, such as PTGS2. Under TLR3 and TLR4 stimulation, IL-1β deficiency led to altered or enhanced expression of specific inflammatory mediators like IL-8, PTGS2, and iNOS.

Collectively, IL-1β–IL-1R signaling is integrated into pathway-specific regulatory networks and may influence the hierarchical organization of cytokine responses, rather than acting solely as a linear amplifier. Functional divergence between primary macrophages and the HD11 cell line highlights the importance of model selection in chicken immunology research.
