Antiepileptic drug therapy in companion animals: New perspectives on clinical pathology monitoring
Authors: Narges Lotfalizadeh, Saeed Nazifi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntiepileptic drugs (AEDs) are widely used in companion animal medicine and are used long-term to treat a variety of epilepsy conditions. The use of these drugs can cause changes in routine tests.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the broad dimensions of hematological and biochemical changes induced by various AEDs in dogs and cats. Also, the distinction of these changes from actual pathologic alterations was considered.MethodsIn this review, relevant articles were searched and selected from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. Then, data related to the type of AEDs and hematological and biochemical changes in dogs and cats were extracted and qualitatively analyzed.Key findingsAEDs in dogs and cats can cause significant changes in laboratory parameters. The most common biochemical changes were liver-related, especially elevated liver enzymes, which in dogs were often adaptive. In contrast, in cats, these changes were less common but were of greater clinical significance. Hematological changes were less common, but in some cases, they included anemia or cytopenias. It was also found that the severity and type of changes depended on the drug type, treatment duration, and animal species. Ultimately, most of these changes were mild and manageable, but in some cases, they required further investigation or adjustment of treatment.ConclusionBased on the pattern of changes, their severity, the type of drug used, and the patient's clinical status, it is possible to distinguish between true pathological changes and drug-induced changes to a large extent. Conducting targeted studies and developing more precise protocols could improve this distinction and increase the accuracy of clinical decisions.Keywords: Antiepileptic drugs, Clinical pathology, Companion animals, Monitoring

Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the most commonly diagnosed persistent neurological conditions in companion animals‚ particularly dogs‚ and is one of the most common small animal clinical disorders encountered by veterinarians (Charalambous et al., 2021)․ The prevalence of epilepsy in dogs has been reported to range from approximately 0․5% to 5% based on the population studied and case definitions․ Epilepsy is characterized by recurrent unprovoked seizures secondary to the abnormal and synchronous electrical discharges of a cluster of neurons and can be classified as idiopathic‚ structural, or unknown (Chandler, 2006; Charalambous et al., 2014)․

Long-term seizure control in dogs and cats is generally achieved with antiepileptic drugs (AEDs)․ There are multiple AEDs employed in veterinary medicine‚ including older drugs such as phenobarbital and potassium bromide‚ and newer AEDs including levetiracetam‚ zonisamide‚ and imepitoin (Charalambous et al., 2026; Green & Lowrie, 2022)․ In monotherapy for canine idiopathic epilepsy, the greatest evidence of efficacy is with phenobarbital and imepitoin, according to systematic reviews․ Potassium bromide and levetiracetam are also frequently used as monotherapy or adjunctive therapy (Charalambous et al., 2014, 2018; Skovola et al., 2021)․

Although AEDs are generally effective‚ they may produce a range of side effects․ These may be divided into clinical side effects‚ such as sedation, gastrointestinal issues‚ as well as hematology and biochemistry changes that may occur with long-term use of antiepileptic drugs․ Laboratory values may be affected by drug toxicity‚ metabolic adaptation‚ or progression of the underlying disease process‚ and require routine laboratory monitoring throughout AED therapy (Charalambous et al., 2018; Munguia et al., 2024)․ While research have looked at how well and safely antiepileptic medicines work in dogs and cats, the information we have on how these drugs affect blood and chemical levels remains scattered across different clinical trials. Moreover, the database is far more substantial in dogs than in cats, and thorough syntheses concentrating specifically on laboratory alterations related to AED therapy in companion animals remained limited (Charalambous et al., 2018).

Long-term AED therapy is regularly monitored for possible clinicopathological side effects such as raised activities of liver enzymes‚ metabolic effects, and less commonly hematological effects such as bone marrow suppression․ These have been reported with all commonly used AEDs‚ in particular phenobarbital․ Even if these alterations persist for the entire course of therapy and are asymptomatic‚ laboratory evaluation should be performed on a routine basis in animals receiving treatment (Charalambous et al., 2018; Scott et al., 2021)․

Most of the time, blood monitoring in the clinical setting involves a complete blood count‚ a serum biochemical profile, and periodic serum drug levels․ The blood tests measure therapeutic drug levels and identify any adverse effects‚ thereby allowing for any necessary treatment adjustments to be made․ Consequently‚ laboratory monitoring is considered an integral part of long-term management of companion animal epilepsy cases treated with antiepileptic drugs (Charalambous et al., 2014, 2018)․

Although antiepileptic drugs are among the most commonly prescribed medications in veterinary practice‚ the clinicopathological consequences of antiepileptic drug therapy in dogs and cats remain sparsely documented․ A majority of studies have focused on clinical efficacy or seizure control‚ whereas hematological and biochemical alterations have been less frequently reported (Charalambous et al., 2014, 2016)․ The majority of available literature and data is based on canine studies‚ and it must be noted that literature to provide information about drug-induced laboratory changes in cats is lacking․ Since some of the studies had small sample sizes‚ and being retrospective in nature‚ the clinical importance of these changes can be difficult to ascertain (Charalambous et al., 2016;;Dohány et al., 2023)․ Consequently, a comprehensive synthesis of currently available evidence regarding laboratory changes associated with AED therapy in companion animals is still lacking.

AEDs are increasingly being used to treat epilepsy in companion animals and it has been shown that AEDs can cause a variety of clinicopathological changes․ Monitoring of these alterations is critical in the early detection of adverse effects, optimization of therapeutic management, and improvement of long-term patient outcomes. Recognizing drug-induced laboratory changes is essential for appropriate interpretation of diagnostic tests and prevention of misdiagnosis or unnecessary treatment modifications. This review complements existing literature on antiepileptic therapy by focusing specifically on drug-induced clinicopathological changes and their implications for laboratory monitoring in companion animals. The purpose of this review is to summarize the common hematological and biochemical abnormalities that occur with treatment with the most commonly used primary AEDs in veterinary medicine‚ including phenobarbital‚ potassium bromide‚ levetiracetam‚ and zonisamide․ This review also discusses common laboratory abnormalities associated with adjunctive AEDs and the possible clinical importance of these abnormalities․

Methods

Search strategy

Literature searches were conducted to identify studies that investigated the adverse effects of AEDs on veterinary medicine. Searches were conducted in April 2026 using electronic databases such as PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. The literature search covered publications from 2000 to April 2026, encompassing early foundational and recent studies in the field, and the last search was performed on April 29th. It was possible to search using combinations of the following keywords: “Veterinary medicine,” “Epilepsy,” “Antiepileptic drugs,” “Adverse effect,” “Biochemical,” “Hematological,” “Dog,” “Cat,” “Companion animals,” “Medications,” and combined using Boolean operators (AND/OR), including search strings such as (“epilepsy” AND “anticonvulsant OR antiepileptic drugs” AND “dog OR cat”), as well as drug-specific terms (e.g., “phenobarbital”, “potassium bromide”, “levetiracetam”, “zonisamide”, “imepitoin”, “gabapentin”). Animal studies (dogs and cats) were included in the study. Comparative purposes and possible translational applications were discussed with reference to human studies.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they comprised original research articles, clinical trials, and retrospective or prospective studies evaluating the use of AEDs in dogs and/or cats, as well as relevant treatment guidelines and therapeutic protocols. Eligible studies were required to report hematological and/or biochemical laboratory parameters associated with AED therapy and to investigate commonly used agents such as phenobarbital, potassium bromide, levetiracetam, zonisamide, and other adjunctive drugs. Conference proceedings, narrative reviews, and basic science studies were also screened and included when they provided relevant mechanistic or pharmacological information related to antiepileptic drug effects. Studies published in languages other than English were excluded. Studies without accessible full text were also excluded when sufficient data could not be extracted.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized format in which information on study characteristics, including species, sample sizes, type of antiepileptic drugs used, treatment duration, and follow-up times, was recorded. All relevant laboratory data reported were extracted. These included hematological findings (red blood cell indices, white blood cell counts, and platelet counts) and biochemical measurements (activities of hepatic enzymes, kidney function parameters, lipid profile, and electrolyte levels), in addition to all results related to drug-induced effects and adverse reactions. The extracted data were subsequently analyzed qualitatively to highlight common trends, specific drug-related effects, and species-related discrepancies.

Antiepileptic drug therapy in companion animals

First-line antiepileptic drugs

Phenobarbital has been used in dogs for several decades‚ and is the custom first-line AED for‌ idiopathic epilepsy (Löscher, 2022)․ Phenobarbital is documented as reducing the length‌ and frequency of seizures for idiopathic epilepsy‚ and trials of phenobarbital monotherapy in dogs confirm seizure control in most dogs with idiopathic epilepsy‚ with success rates of between 60% and 90% (Boothe et al., 2012)․ Long-term phenobarbital use is generally well tolerated; however‚ periodic analysis is required to monitor for‌ possible hepatotoxicity and bone marrow suppression (Charalambous et al., 2018; Mansoor et al., 2025)․

Primidone is an antiepileptic medicine that has been used for more than a century and has historically been given to dogs. However, phenobarbital has primarily replaced primidone as the preferred medication due to concerns over its safety. The liver is responsible for the pharmacological transformation of primidone into phenobarbital and phenylethylmalonamide (PEMA). Phenobarbital is the principal agent that is responsible for the majority of the anticonvulsant effects that are associated with primidone (Ghattas et al., 2026; You et al., 2026). Primidone is not recommended in clinical practice due to associated adverse effects, and its utilization has significantly declined in favor of its safer alternative, phenobarbital (Michelucci & Pasini, 2015; Parker, 2025).

Another widely used first-line and adjunctive therapy for canine epilepsy is potassium bromide‚ especially if there is not a satisfactory response‌ to monotherapy with phenobarbital․ Evidence suggests that potassium bromide can achieve effective seizure control either as a primary treatment or in combination with phenobarbital, especially in refractory canine epilepsy (Gouveia et al., 2024; Royaux et al., 2017; You et al., 2026). Its application in cats, however, is constrained due to the elevated risk of side effects and inconsistent pharmacokinetics (Charalambous et al., 2018).

Both phenobarbital and potassium bromide should be considered as first-line alternatives for treating canine patients, according to systematic evaluations of AEDs in companion animals (Gouveia et al., 2024; Parker, 2025). The relative lack of high-quality data for feline patients is another issue that is brought to light by these findings (Charalambous et al., 2018). The therapeutic relevance of phenobarbital and potassium bromide as fundamental therapy for the management of idiopathic epilepsy in companion animals is further strengthened by these findings. It is important to follow up the animals with adequate laboratory testing to ensure that these medications are both safe and effective (Charalambous et al., 2026; Ribeiro et al., 2022).

Second-line and adjunct antiepileptic drugs

In the management of epilepsy in companion animals, it is customary to employ various second-line and adjunctive therapies alongside first-line antiepileptic medications. Due to its favorable pharmacokinetic profile, rapid onset of action, and relatively low hepatotoxic risk, levetiracetam is increasingly adopted as an adjunctive treatment in canine and feline patients. Clinical studies have demonstrated that levetiracetam effectively diminishes seizure frequency in individuals resistant to first-line medications. Additionally, it is often advised for short-term perioperative seizure control (Bhatti et al., 2015; Hazenfratz & Taylor, 2018).

In addition, zonisamide is a further AED that is effective in the treatment of refractory idiopathic epilepsy diagnosed in dogs. The fact that zonisamide is capable of acting as an anticonvulsant across a variety of conditions and that it has a low incidence of adverse effects makes it a viable choice for use in the second line of treatment. It is possible to use it as a monotherapy in some situations, or it may be considered in conjunction with phenobarbital or potassium bromide (Bhatti et al., 2015; Budde & McCluskey, 2023; Muñana, 2013a).

One of the key reasons that gabapentin and pregabalin are employed in the treatment of epileptic seizures is due to their anticonvulsant and neuromodulatory properties. Even though their efficacy as monotherapy in the treatment of epilepsy is limited, it has been noted that both medications, when combined with conventional AEDs, provide additional control over seizures. Both gabapentin and pregabalin are particularly beneficial in the treatment of patients who are susceptible to the negative effects of conventional AEDs, as well as in the treatment of animals that concurrently suffer from neuropathic pain (Bhatti et al., 2015; Budde and McCluskey, 2023; Kriechbaumer et al., 2022).

A thorough assessment of feline epilepsy offers more support for the use of levetiracetam, zonisamide, gabapentin, and pregabalin as adjunctive treatment. The study emphasizes the generally acceptable safety profiles of these drugs; there is a paucity of high-quality clinical evidence in felines compared to canines (Charalambous et al., 2018; Hazenfratz & Taylor, 2018). The second-line and adjuvant AEDs expand the treatment options for veterinarians treating refractory epilepsy in companion animals. This has enabled veterinarians to create customized therapy regimens that optimize seizure management while minimizing adverse effects (Cook & Hechler, 2024).

Newer and alternative antiepileptic drugs

As a consequence of the pharmaceutical industry's discovery and introduction of more antiepileptic drugs, the treatment choices that are accessible for the management of epilepsy in companion animals have been expanded in recent years (Gristina et al., 2023). This has been the case because of the growing number of available treatments. Imepitoin is a relatively new antiepileptic medication that was developed primarily for use in veterinary medicine, particularly in conjunction with the treatment of idiopathic epilepsy in dogs (Muñoz Amezcua et al., 2024). It is a relatively recent therapy, which is one reason it has attracted considerable interest among these treatments.

When it comes to the benzodiazepine binding site of the gamma-aminobutyric acid A (GABA_A) receptor, imepitoin is a partial agonist that has a low affinity for the receptor. Through the modulation of inhibitory neurotransmission in the central nervous system, it is responsible for the anticonvulsant actions that it exerts (Rundfeldt et al., 2014). Through the use of clinical studies, it has been demonstrated that imepitoin is effective in reducing the number of seizures that occur in dogs who have just been diagnosed with idiopathic epilepsy. Additionally, it has the potential to produce seizure control that is comparable to that of common drugs used in the first line of treatment, such as phenobarbital (Tipold et al., 2015). There are several factors that have contributed to the increasing utilization of imepitoin in the area of veterinary neurology (Muñoz Amezcua et al., 2024; Rundfeldt et al., 2015; Tipold et al., 2015). One of these factors is the fact that imepitoin has been associated with a favorable safety profile and fewer severe side effects in contrast to certain traditional antiepileptic medications.

Imepitoin has been the subject of randomized controlled clinical studies, which have shown that it is very tolerable over the course of long-term treatment and that it significantly reduces the frequency of seizures experiencing seizures (Charalambous et al., 2026; Gallucci et al., 2017). Based on these data, it appears that this medicine might be an advantageous choice for the treatment of epilepsy in dogs (Rundfeldt et al., 2015). Furthermore, systemic investigations of antiepileptic medications have demonstrated that imepitoin possesses the potential to be a viable therapeutic option, particularly in circumstances where traditional drugs are either not suitable or are not well tolerated (Charalambous et al., 2014).

The management of epilepsy in companion animals is currently being explored, and there are a variety of therapeutic strategies that are now in the process of being developed. Imepitoin is one of the methods in this category. Some examples of these improvements include the creation of novel antiepileptic medications, the refinement of existing pharmacological therapies, and the enhancement of multimodal therapy regimens for epilepsy that is resistant to treatment (Bhatti et al., 2015). Brivaracetam, a next-generation analog of levetiracetam, exhibits approximately 10–30 times higher affinity for its receptor, which may translate into faster brain penetration, improved potency, and potentially better tolerability compared to levetiracetam. However, available veterinary data remain limited, and its clinical utility in small animal epilepsy has not yet been established (Jukier et al., 2024; Platt, 2014; Podell, 2013b).

Lacosamide and rufinamide, both sodium channel modulators, have demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetic properties in experimental studies, although clinical evidence in small animals remains limited (Hussar & Bilbow, 2009; Lumley et al., 2024; Platt, 2014; Podell, 2020). Emerging antiepileptic agents such as cenobamate, carisbamate, and padsevonil have been developed for the management of drug-resistant epilepsy in humans. However, their use in veterinary medicine remains limited. Accordingly, data regarding their safety and laboratory effects in companion animals are currently lacking (Löscher et al., 2026).

Although some of these treatments have demonstrated encouraging results, supporting the routine therapeutic use of these drugs remains insufficient. The studies underline the importance of undertaking additional controlled clinical trials to better describe the efficacy and safety profiles of more current treatment options (Charalambous et al., 2014, 2016).

Hematological alterations associated with antiepileptic drugs

Mechanisms of drug-induced hematological changes

Drug-induced hematological abnormalities are a significant adverse consequence that can be attributed to a wide range of pharmacological substances, including antiepileptic medications that are utilized in veterinary medicine. These modifications can be brought about by several different processes, the most prevalent of which are immune-mediated cytopenias, oxidative damage to erythrocytes, and suppression of bone marrow (Renard & Rosselet, 2017; Weiss, 2012).

Bone marrow suppression is one of the key processes that contribute to drug-induced hematological toxicity. This suppression can reduce the production of one or more blood cell lineages. In situations like these, medications can directly harm hematopoietic stem cells or alter the normal function of the bone marrow microenvironment. This procedure has the potential to result in cytopenias, which include anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and, in extreme circumstances, pancytopenia (Lyraki & Wilson, 2020). There is a correlation between bone marrow abnormalities and the use of antiepileptic medications, notably phenobarbital, in both dogs and cats. Hematological changes, including anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia, have been described in clinical and retrospective research in animals that have been receiving long-term phenobarbital treatment; nevertheless, it appears that these occurrences are very unusual (Dohány et al., 2023; Oberholster, 2021; Scott et al., 2021). There have been reported instances of severe bone marrow suppression that resulted in pancytopenia, which suggests that the medicine may have caused an idiosyncratic toxic response in six or seven cases (Lyraki & Wilson, 2020; Oberholster, 2021).

Blood cells can also be destroyed by immune-mediated processes, which is another key pathway. In situations like these, drug molecules or their metabolites may adhere to cell membranes or plasma proteins and behave as haptens. This triggers an immune response, which in turn leads to the production of antibodies directed against blood cells. Immune-mediated hemolytic anemia, neutropenia, or thrombocytopenia are all potential outcomes that might result from this mechanism (Renard & Rosselet, 2017; Scott et al., 2021; Weiss, 2012). Individual variability in immune response and drug metabolism is reflected in the fact that drug-induced immunological cytopenias are frequently unanticipated and may appear after varying amounts of time have passed since the animal was started on the medication (Renard & Rosselet, 2017).

Hematological abnormalities can also be caused by oxidative damage to erythrocytes, the third mechanism contributing here. Certain medicines and their metabolites can stimulate the production of reactive oxygen species, which can damage erythrocyte membranes and hemoglobin. Oxidative damage can compromise the integrity of red blood cells, eventually resulting in hemolysis (Gibson et al., 2019; Paika et al., 2025). This particular mechanism has been characterized in a variety of drug-induced hemolytic conditions, and it is especially pertinent in circumstances in which antioxidant defense mechanisms are overloaded (Paika et al., 2025).

In general, drug-induced hematological changes may result from multiple processes that overlap. These mechanisms may involve defective hematopoiesis, immune-mediated destruction, or oxidative damage to blood cells simultaneously. It is important to differentiate bone marrow suppression from immune-mediated cytopenia and oxidative damage, as the underlying mechanisms have distinct clinical implications (Scott et al., 2021; Weiss, 2022). When it comes to interpreting laboratory abnormalities and determining the likelihood of adverse drug responses in companion animals that are undergoing long-term pharmacological therapy, having a solid understanding of these processes is absolutely necessary (Renard & Rosselet, 2017; Weiss, 2012).

Alterations in red blood cell parameters

Among the hematologic abnormalities linked to pharmacological therapy, drug-induced changes in the properties of red blood cells (RBC) are among the most commonly observed. Several symptoms, including anemia, structural anomalies in erythrocytes, and differences in reticulocyte responses, may indicate these abnormalities (Dohány et al., 2023). The presence of such data frequently suggests the existence of underlying processes, such as defective erythropoiesis, immune-mediated destruction of erythrocytes, or direct toxic effects on red blood cells (Behling-Kelly, 2022; Oberholster, 2021; Paika et al., 2025; Schultze & Ramaiah, 2022).

The presence of anemia is frequently indicated by a reduction in the hematocrit of the blood, the concentration of hemoglobin, and the number of erythrocytes (Thrall et al., 2022; Weiss & Wardrop, 2011). Anemia is defined by a decrease in the total number of erythrocytes that are circulating in the blood. Drug-induced anemia can develop through several different mechanisms. These include inhibiting the production of erythrocytes in the bone marrow, causing hemolysis through the immune system, or causing direct toxic damage to erythrocytes (Behling-Kelly, 2022; Maquet et al., 2024; Schultze & Ramaiah, 2022).

When pharmacological drugs interfere with the proliferation or differentiation of erythroid precursors, the production of adult erythrocytes may be reduced. Certain circumstances may lead to the occurrence of this. The production of drug-dependent antibodies that target erythrocyte membranes has been identified as the cause of immune-mediated hemolytic anemia that is generated by certain medicines (Maquet et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). As a consequence, laboratory abnormalities are induced, which are defined by a lower hematocrit and higher markers of erythrocyte degradation (Behling-Kelly, 2022). In veterinary medicine, anemia is a prevalent condition commonly identified in canines and felines. By conducting clinical examinations of systemic disorders or drug-induced toxicity, erythrocyte indices and hematologic profiles are evaluated simultaneously (Ahmadi‐hamedani et al., 2025; Lippi et al., 2024; Scott et al., 2021).

There is a strong correlation between drug-induced hematologic diseases and morphological abnormalities of erythrocytes in the patient's blood. Blood smears might be examined under a microscope to detect any abnormalities. For example, spherocytes, echinocytes, acanthocytes, schistocytes, and other membrane irregularities might fall into this category of abnormalities. There is a possibility that these abnormalities are indicative of immune-mediated hemolysis, oxidative damage, or structural instability of the erythrocyte membrane (Behling-Kelly, 2022; Schultze & Ramaiah, 2022; Thrall et al., 2022). To prevent morphological changes that may result from interactions between medications and membrane lipids or proteins, it is vital to take immediate action. The shape of erythrocytes will be altered as a result of these interactions, and the flexibility of the cells will be reduced, which will ultimately lead to an increase in the likelihood of premature destruction (Behling-Kelly, 2022). Taking this into consideration, the assessment of the morphology of erythrocytes is an essential component of hematologic assessment in both human and veterinary medicine (Harvey, 2011; Thrall et al., 2022; Weiss & Wardrop, 2011).

To determine whether the patient has regenerative or non-regenerative anemia, it is essential to first measure the patient's reticulocyte count. Erythropoiesis is often increased in the bone marrow as a response to increased erythrocyte breakdown or bleeding. This, in turn, results in an increased circulation of reticulocytes in the bloodstream (Behling-Kelly, 2022; Tripathi, 2025). The occurrence of reticulocytosis, in conjunction with anisocytosis and polychromasia, is indicative of a vigorous regeneration response to the loss of erythrocytes (Thrall et al., 2022; Tripathi, 2025). Every one of these three symptoms can be present at the same time. Anemia is a condition that is characterized by the suppression of bone marrow or the impairment of erythropoiesis, which may be the consequence of drug-induced toxicity that affects erythroid precursors (Ahmadi‐hamedani et al., 2025; Schultze & Ramaiah, 2022). It is possible to diagnose both of these disorders based on the absence of a significant reticulocyte response in anemia. It is crucial to conduct a thorough evaluation of reticulocyte counts, morphology, and erythrocyte indices in order to differentiate between the many routes that are responsible for drug-induced erythrocyte abnormalities (Behling-Kelly, 2022; Thrall et al., 2022).

Alterations in leukocyte profiles

In leukocytic adverse effects, which can include leukopenia, neutropenia, and a range of inflammatory responses, changes in leukocyte profiles that are induced by pharmacological drugs are a key component (Tripathi, 2025; Ward, 2025). Several different inflammatory reactions can cause these changes. These problems may present in various ways, including direct suppression of bone marrow, immune-mediated death of leukocytes, or manipulation of inflammatory pathways. These are only some of the possible mechanisms. It is vital to be aware of these variances in order to understand hematological data and identify potential adverse pharmacological responses in clinical practice (Curtis, 2017; Tripathi, 2025).

Leukopenia is a condition that occurs when there is a decrease in the total amount of white blood cells (WBC) in the circulation through the body. This fall in white blood cell count may result from decreased leukocyte production in the bone marrow, increased peripheral destruction, or sequestration of leukocytes within tissues (Thrall et al., 2022; Weiss & Wardrop, 2011). In most cases, drug-induced leukopenia is associated with the inhibition of hematopoietic precursors, notably in the myeloid lineage. Several drugs have the capacity to affect the proliferation and differentiation of bone marrow progenitor cells, which can result in a reduction in the creation of leukocytes (Tripathi, 2025). These medications can reduce the number of leukocytes produced. Leukopenia is a disorder that may be identified during routine hematologic examinations in the area of veterinary medicine. It is also a condition frequently studied in relation to clinical findings and other hematologic abnormalities (Stockham & Scott, 2024; Weiss & Wardrop, 2011). There are three factors that can influence the severity and duration of leukopenia: the underlying cause, the therapy dose, and the individual's susceptibility (Tripathi, 2025). It is conceivable for these factors to alter over the course of the disease.

Several drug-induced leukocyte disorders are among the most common in clinical practice. Neutropenia, which is defined by a drop in circulating neutrophil counts, is one of them. Direct toxic effects on myeloid progenitor cells, immune-mediated neutrophil death, or increased peripheral consumption during inflammatory processes are all potential causes of this phenomenon (Curtis, 2017; Ward, 2025). Drug-induced neutropenia and agranulocytosis are well-documented in both human and veterinary medicine, and they might quickly present themselves after being exposed to particular medicines (Andrès et al., 2011; Curtis, 2017). Drug-dependent antibodies have the potential to target neutrophil surface antigens in some circumstances, which can lead to the rapid death of circulating neutrophils (Curtis, 2017). Patients who suffer from severe neutropenia may be more susceptible to opportunistic infections as a result of compromised innate immune responses.

Leukopenia and neutropenia are not the only adverse effects of leukocyte distribution and function. Drug-induced inflammatory responses can activate immunological pathways, resulting in the recruitment of leukocytes, the generation of cytokines, and changes in leukocyte trafficking (Hsu et al., 2021; Pichler, 2019). These reactions may manifest as differences in leukocyte differentials or changes in leukocyte counts, both of which are indicators of the activation of inflammatory pathways (Harvey, 2011; Thrall et al., 2022). Leukocytosis is one potential manifestation of these responses. Drug hypersensitivity reactions can increase inflammatory responses via immune-mediated pathways, leading to systemic inflammatory symptoms and hematologic abnormalities (Pichler, 2019). Therefore, it is essential to evaluate leukocyte profiles, which include total white blood cell counts, leukocyte differentials, and the clinical context, to identify any drug-induced inflammatory and hematologic effects (Thrall et al., 2022).

Platelet abnormalities

Platelet abnormalities pose significant challenges for therapeutic and diagnostic purposes, especially when studying the adverse hematologic consequences of antiepileptic or other long-term therapies (Mansoor et al., 2025). These conditions often fall into two main categories: thrombocytopenia, which is defined as a quantitative drop in platelet count, and platelet dysfunction, which is defined as a qualitative impairment of platelet activity (Badulescu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026).

When blood platelet counts drop below the usual range, a medical condition known as thrombocytopenia sets in. An elevated risk of bleeding is a common symptom of this illness. Adverse effects on platelet production and survival, as well as immune-mediated processes, are two potential pathways that might lead to drug-induced thrombocytopenia (Marini et al., 2022). When drug-dependent antibodies form, they accelerate platelet removal from the blood, a condition known as immune-mediated thrombocytopenia. This occurs after the drug binds to specific surface epitopes on the platelet. It is common for platelet counts to rebound rapidly once the offending drug is stopped (Danese et al., 2020; George & Aster, 2009).

The symptoms of drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP) usually appear between a few days and a few weeks after starting a new medication. One of the most prevalent mechanisms is complement activation, which causes platelet death; another is the binding of drug-dependent antibodies to glycoprotein complexes such as GPIIb/IIIa, which leads to opsonization and phagocytosis (Bakchoul & Marini, 2018; George & Aster, 2009). Non-immune mechanisms might include direct suppression of megakaryocyte maturation or acceleration of platelet turnover. The presence of cytotoxic medications or therapies that damage the bone marrow may make these processes more noticeable (Danese et al., 2020). The clinical identification of DITP is of the utmost importance because irreversible spontaneous bleeding, including mucosal or cerebral hemorrhage, can occur from abrupt and severe thrombocytopenia if left untreated (Danese et al., 2020; George & Aster, 2009).

Even in the absence of substantial thrombocytopenia, pharmaceuticals can affect platelet function through qualitative processes. Platelet dysfunction can manifest in several ways, including impaired adhesion, altered aggregation responses, or compromised signal transduction pathways. On the other hand, if only quantitative signs are evaluated, this qualitative flaw may go unnoticed. This is because it frequently occurs independently of the platelet count. There are several medications that have the ability to alter the activation receptors of platelets or the intracellular signaling pathways, which ultimately results in a reduction in the body's hemostatic function despite the presence of normal platelet levels (Badulescu et al., 2025; Lordkipanidzé, 2016). One example of this is antiplatelet medications, which have the potential to interact with other medications that are administered concurrently to companion animals (Rajashekaraiah & Berikai Ananthakrishna, 2023).

In the context of clinical practice, platelet dysfunction and thrombocytopenia are two conditions that must be taken into consideration if there is a fluctuation in the platelet count or an increased tendency for bleeding after extended pharmacological therapy (Bakchoul & Marini, 2018; Rajashekaraiah & Berikai Ananthakrishna, 2023). Enhanced diagnostic accuracy and the ability to differentiate between quantitative and qualitative abnormalities can be achieved by the utilization of serial platelet counts, the evaluation of peripheral blood smears, and, in cases where they are available, platelet function tests. The discontinuation of the suspected medicine and the complementary therapy frequently result in the patient's rehabilitation. On the other hand, failing to identify these influences may result in hemorrhage results that is catastrophic or diagnostic procedures that are not essential (George & Aster, 2009).

Drug-specific hematological effects

When it comes to companion animals, the hematological effects of AEDs might differ depending on the treatment and the species (Portuheis, 2025). It is important for clinical monitoring to consider these effects. Although the majority of AEDs have positive safety profiles, there are a few that have been connected to hematologic adverse effects. These adverse effects need clinical awareness and regular monitoring of complete blood counts (CBC).

Phenobarbital

Phenobarbital continues to be a primary maintenance antiepileptic medication for both cats and dogs. A systematic evaluation of AED safety in canine subjects indicates that phenobarbital possesses a well-defined spectrum of side effects. These include idiosyncratic hematologic manifestations such as blood dyscrasias. They may present as anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and pancytopenia (Bersan et al., 2014; Charalambous et al., 2016). Retrospective research assessing phenobarbital-induced hematological abnormalities in idiopathic epileptic dogs indicated a prevalence of approximately 4.2% for such abnormalities, including neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and pancytopenia, which occurred even at therapeutic doses; the issue went away after phenobarbital was stopped (Bersan et al., 2014). Phenobarbital therapy in cats has been correlated with a significant incidence of cytopenias: a retrospective analysis of 69 cats revealed that 76.8% experienced cytopenias, with neutropenia being the most prevalent (60%), followed by leukopenia (49.3%), thrombocytopenia (24.1%), and anemia (20.3%); the majority were classified as mild to moderate (Charalambous et al., 2018; Dohány et al., 2023). These data highlight the necessity of routine hematology surveillance in patients on long-term phenobarbital treatment.

Potassium bromide

Potassium bromide, a primary anticonvulsant in dogs, has a robust safety profile supported by several clinical investigations. Potassium bromide is typically linked to negative neurological and gastrointestinal consequences in canine populations, rather than main hematologic cytopenias (Charalambous et al., 2016; Gouveia et al., 2024). Systematic studies do not emphasize hematologic dyscrasias as prevalent with potassium bromide monotherapy; nonetheless, when bromide is used alongside phenobarbital or other AEDs, vigilant monitoring is essential due to potential synergistic effects that may indirectly influence CBC values (Charalambous et al., 2016; Potschka et al., 2015).

Levetiracetam

Levetiracetam is a commonly prescribed adjunctive AED in dogs and cats due to its favorable safety and pharmacokinetic profile (Mastrocco et al., 2024). Systematic study indicates that levetiracetam generally exhibits a reduced overall incidence of adverse effects compared to phenobarbital and potassium bromide, with the majority of reported adverse effects being neurological or gastrointestinal rather than hematologic in character (Charalambous et al., 2016). Clinical data demonstrate that it is well tolerated even at elevated doses, and overt hematologic toxicity has not been a significant characteristic in canine populations (Charalambous et al., 2016; Green & Lowrie, 2022). Nonetheless, individual variability in cases requires regular CBC assessments during prolonged treatment.

Zonisamide

The use of zonisamide, a sulfonamide derivative, as either monotherapy or adjunctive treatment is increasingly favored. Nonetheless, although research on the hematologic effects of zonisamide is limited, the available information suggests that the incidence of adverse hematologic consequences is generally minimal. A limited number of studies have shown that the most common side effects in canine populations are lethargy and gastrointestinal issues. This is true even if the quantity of research is very limited. Conversely, hematologic cytopenias have also been observed in association with idiosyncratic responses or polytherapy with various AEDs (Charalambous et al., 2016). Despite the limited availability of data in cats, zonisamide is generally regarded as well tolerated when administered at the recommended dosages (Collinet & Sammut, 2017).

Other AEDs

Information about other AEDs, such as gabapentin, pregabalin, and felbamate in veterinary species is limited, and the evidence for notable hematologic side effects is either inadequate or derived from isolated case reports rather than comprehensive assessment (Charalambous et al., 2016; Mikscha et al., 2016). Despite this, it is important to exercise caution, especially when these medications are administered in conjunction with the first antiemetic medications (Di Cesare et al., 2023).

In conclusion, potassium bromide and phenobarbital have the most extensively documented hematologic effects, whereas levetiracetam and zonisamide are generally considered to be better-tolerated (Charalambous et al., 2024; Chen‐Allen, 2019) (Table 1).

Biochemical alterations associated with antiepileptic drugs

Mechanisms of drug-induced biochemical changes

AEDs are widely used in companion animals for the management of idiopathic and structural epilepsy. Although generally effective, chronic administration of these drugs can lead to significant biochemical alterations, particularly affecting hepatic function, due to mechanisms such as enzyme induction, hepatotoxicity, and metabolic adaptation (Charalambous et al., 2016; Dhungana et al., 2022; Sasaki & Yokoi, 2018).

Hepatic enzyme induction

Phenobarbital is a robust inducer of hepatic cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzymes and is the antiemetic that is utilized in the first-line treatment of canine and feline patients most frequently. Enzyme induction results in increased levels of blood liver enzymes, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), which are indicative of increased metabolic activity rather than direct hepatic damage in most patients (Charalambous et al., 2016; Hermans et al., 2022). According to research conducted on dogs, continuous phenobarbital treatment has been shown to considerably raise ALP and ALT levels, with elevations occasionally surpassing twice the upper reference range. However, in animals that are otherwise healthy, the majority do not exhibit any symptoms of the condition. According to retrospective investigations, similar patterns are observed in cats; however, the amplitude of enzyme increase is frequently less evident, which may be a reflection of species-specific changes in the inducibility of hepatic enzymes (Charalambous et al., 2016; Hermans et al., 2022; Mahon et al., 2024). On the other hand, zonisamide and levetiracetam exhibit minimal hepatic enzyme induction, which makes them more suitable for individuals at risk of hepatotoxicity (Mahon et al., 2024; Schwartz et al., 2011).

Drug metabolism and hepatotoxicity

Hepatotoxicity might result from both intrinsic and idiosyncratic causes. Phenobarbital-induced centrilobular hepatocellular enlargement and enzyme induction are two instances of dose-dependent and predictable intrinsic toxicity (Dreifuss & Langer, 1987; Maronpot et al., 2010). Although idiosyncratic reactions are unusual, they can cause clinically severe hepatopathy and abnormal serum biochemistry (Schwartz et al., 2011).

The combined metabolic burden of the parent drug and its active metabolites is most likely the source of this phenomenon (Spina et al., 2026). Some of the laboratory abnormalities that have been described in cases of chronic liver injury include elevations in liver enzymes, most notably ALT and ALP, as well as possible changes in serum proteins. Primidone is currently only seldom advised in clinical practice due to these risks, and its usage has substantially dropped in favor of safer alternatives. This is because there is no clear therapeutic advantage associated with the use of primidone (Bhatti et al., 2015; Muñana, 2013a; Podell et al., 2016).

Zonisamide has been linked to hepatotoxicity in case reports involving both cats and dogs. Because of this hepatotoxicity, which is characterized by high levels of bilirubin, ALT, and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), it is occasionally essential to stop the medication (Mahon et al., 2024; Podell et al., 2016; Schwartz et al., 2011). However, close monitoring is required to avoid the development of liver failure (Hermans et al., 2022; Mahon et al., 2024). Although the hepatotoxic effects are usually reversible after the medicine is discontinued, close monitoring is always required.

Metabolic adaptations

Prolonged AED therapy may induce systemic metabolic changes, indicative of the liver's capacity to manage escalating xenobiotic metabolism. Prolonged use of phenobarbital has been demonstrated to increase hepatic microsomal enzyme activity, potentially altering the pharmacokinetics of concomitant medications and endogenous substrates, including hormones and vitamins (Charalambous et al., 2016; Dreifuss & Langer, 1987). These adjustments may manifest as changes in blood protein, cholesterol, and bilirubin concentrations. Regular monitoring often reveals mild biochemical abnormalities (Dhungana et al., 2022). In some cases, the effects of additive enzyme induction or metabolic interactions may be exacerbated by the combination treatment with multiple AEDs (Mahon et al., 2024; Podell et al., 2016).

It is critical to identify these metabolic changes in the context of therapeutic intervention. Recognizing the mechanisms of enzyme induction and hepatotoxicity allows veterinarians to distinguish between mild adaptation changes and early signs of liver injury (Charalambous et al., 2016; Dreifuss & Langer, 1987; Podell et al., 2016). This allows veterinarians to intervene promptly and minimize the probability of negative consequences.

Hepatic biochemical alterations

The long-term use of AEDs in companion animals can result in substantial changes in the liver's metabolic processes. These modifications are mostly evidenced by the activity of hepatic blood enzymes. These alterations may come from hepatic enzyme induction, medication metabolism, or idiosyncratic hepatotoxicity. The expression of these changes differs according to the species and the particular AED utilized (Maronpot et al., 2010).

ALP is significantly impacted by enzyme-inducing AEDs, particularly phenobarbital in dogs. This elevation can continue even during long-term therapy without causing any structural damage to the liver (Charalambous et al., 2016; Mahon et al., 2024). Elevations that are extremely high or changes that occur simultaneously in other enzymes may be an early indicator of hepatotoxicity and require for more investigation (Bunch et al., 2026; Schwartz et al., 2011).

Upon activation or injury to hepatocytes, the enzyme ALT is released into the circulation. This enzyme resides in the liver's cytoplasm. Even without apparent hepatopathy, prolonged dosing of phenobarbital in dogs often results in a little elevation of ALT levels (Bunch et al., 2026; Charalambous et al., 2016). This increase is generally a result of subclinical hepatocellular adaptability rather than damage associated with clinical significance. This is the typical circumstance in most cases. ALT increases in felines are not as consistent as those seen in humans; just a small percentage of cats administered phenobarbital had marginally increased levels. The occurrence of these negligible increases is generally linked to elevated medication concentrations (Hermans et al., 2022).

A less liver-specific enzyme than ALT, AST can also be found in muscle and other tissues in addition to the liver. When there is no evidence of genuine hepatocellular damage, continuous phenobarbital treatment does not reliably raise AST levels, according to the majority of studies (Bunch et al., 2026; Charalambous et al., 2016). Consequently, if AST levels are elevated in conjunction with ALT and ALP levels, this may be an indication of genuine hepatocellular injury rather than merely an increase in enzyme levels.

Cholestatic or hepatobiliary stress causes a rise in the amount of gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), which is an enzyme that is connected with the membrane. Phenobarbital treatment may cause a slight or temporary increase in the level of glutathione in dogs (Mahon et al., 2024). Increases in GGT and ALP that occur together are characteristic of enzyme induction, but high GGT levels that occur alone may indicate involvement of the biliary system (Schwartz et al., 2011).

Total bilirubin levels in animals administered AEDs generally stay stable, unless in cases of hepatic dysfunction or cholestasis (Hermans et al., 2022). In most instances, significant hyperbilirubinemia is associated with overt hepatotoxicity rather than enzyme induction for its own sake. The incidence of this illness is exceptionally rare. Regular monitoring is essential due to infrequent instances of idiosyncratic medication responses, such as those related to zonisamide, which have been linked to elevated bilirubin levels in canines (Hermans et al., 2022; Podell et al., 2016).

Routine blood biochemistry monitoring is necessary for animals undergoing prolonged AED medication. Monitoring of ALT, ALP, AST, GGT, and bilirubin levels is essential during this procedure. Patterns of enzyme elevation can distinguish between benign adaptive changes due to enzyme induction and clinically relevant hepatotoxicity. This is due to the clinicians' ability to differentiate between the two. This allows them to adjust dosages safely and to choose drugs administered correctly. Despite the relatively mild impact of contemporary medications like levetiracetam and zonisamide on liver enzymes, phenobarbital remains the AED most frequently linked to enzyme induction (Hermans et al., 2022; Mahon et al., 2024; Podell et al., 2016).

Renal and metabolic alterations

In the context of small animal medicine, the use of AEDs over extended periods may be associated with renal and systemic metabolic changes. Routine biochemical examinations can discover these changes. Some examples of these modifications include shifts in serum urea, serum creatinine, and electrolyte balance (Bhatti et al., 2015). Furthermore, renal impairment, changed renal excretion mechanisms, and metabolic adaptations due to drug pharmacokinetics and interactions may be responsible for these alterations (Gim et al., 2021). These changes may be a reflection of not just the direct effects of the medication, but also of the drug itself.

The levels of serum urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine, respectively, are considered to be among the most significant indices of renal excretory function. Considerable increases in these markers may suggest a reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR), which is associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD) or acute kidney injury (AKI). A drop in GFR is connected to both of these conditions (Selin, 2025). In the context of the administration of AEDs, some medications or combinations can either disclose or exacerbate the renal impairment that is already present. For example, a recent veterinary investigation indicated that epileptic dogs with pre-existing chronic kidney disease who were given levetiracetam displayed more frequent increases in BUN and serum creatinine concentrations following the beginning of treatment in comparison to dogs that did not have renal illness (Gim et al., 2021). The significance of this result lies in the fact that it most likely exemplifies the consequence of reduced renal clearance in CKD. In addition, it emphasizes the importance of comprehensive renal monitoring when using AEDs, which are highly reliant on renal excretion (Charalambous et al., 2024; Gouveia et al., 2024; Selin, 2025).

Furthermore, the elimination half-life of levetiracetam can be prolonged in individuals with reduced kidney function, which might result in elevated plasma concentrations and potentially a larger metabolic burden on the kidneys (Gim et al., 2021). Levetiracetam is mostly removed unaltered in the urine. The pharmacokinetic profile of levetiracetam highlights the need for personalized dosing and renal panel monitoring in dogs and cats with a history of renal disease. This is despite the fact that levetiracetam itself has not been linked to any direct nephrotoxicity (Charalambous et al., 2024; Gim et al., 2021).

While potassium bromide, which is similarly excreted through the kidneys, may potentially be influenced by compromised renal function, there is minimal evidence that it directly causes clinically significant increases in serum urea or creatinine in the absence of co-morbidities (Gouveia et al., 2024; Nichols et al., 1996). This is because potassium bromide is eliminated through the kidneys. There have been classic veterinary case reports that have shown that bromide elimination may be delayed, and bromide toxicosis may arise in animals that have renal insufficiency (Munguia et al., 2024). When bromide is administered to animals that have a history of renal illness, it is suggested that caution be exercised (Gouveia et al., 2024; Nichols et al., 1996).

Changes in electrolyte levels are another possible metabolic alteration that may arise from prolonged use of AEDs. Several routes exist via which AEDs may indirectly alter electrolyte balance. This occurs despite the infrequent reporting of electrolyte profiles in most clinical epilepsy studies. Phenobarbital is an anticonvulsant medication that stimulates hepatic enzymes. These AEDs may modify systemic protein synthesis and hormone metabolism, thereby affecting the kidneys' ability to regulate sodium, potassium, and chloride, especially in patients who are dehydrated or under metabolic stress (DeClementi, 2025; Muñana, 2013b). In particular, animals taking potassium bromide may experience a substantial chloride imbalance. This is because bromide and chloride compete for renal tubular reabsorption, which might alter serum chloride and affect the kinetics of bromide elimination (Muñana, 2013b). Furthermore, it is essential to take caution, particularly when treating patients who are also suffering from metabolic or renal diseases, despite the fact that overt electrolyte alterations in dogs and cats that have been generated entirely by AEDs have not been regularly observed (Sanders, 2025).

Electrolytes such as potassium and sodium must be incorporated into the biochemical monitoring procedures conducted during the extended administration of an AED. This is because these electrolytes may be extremely sensitive to changes in the homeostasis of the system. In order to ensure that animals are not at risk for clinical manifestations such as cardiac arrhythmias, muscular weakness, or neurologic dysfunction, it is essential to do regular evaluations of the levels of sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, and calcium. This is because substantial electrolyte imbalances can put animals at risk for these clinical manifestations (Munguia et al., 2024).

In clinical practice, it is recommended that routine monitoring of renal parameters (urea/BUN, creatinine) and electrolytes be undertaken before starting AED therapy and at frequent intervals thereafter. It is of utmost importance to consider this when dealing with animals that already have renal disease, elderly patients, or patients receiving polytherapy (Bhatti et al., 2015; Kimram et al., 2025). The early diagnosis of metabolic and renal irregularities enables medical professionals to adjust prescription dosages, enhance hydration and food intake, and, if necessary, switch to other AEDs with more favourable renal elimination profiles. When it comes to the treatment of epilepsy in dogs and cats, adapting AED therapy to the specific renal and metabolic status of the individual patient can improve both patient safety and the therapeutic effectiveness of the treatment (Charalambous et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025) (Fig. 1).

Alterations in lipid and energy metabolism

AEDs can influence lipid and energy metabolism in companion animals, leading to clinically relevant changes in conditions such as cholesterol, triglyceride (TG), and glucose levels. The AEDs can cause these changes. These side effects are specific to the medication, and they may differ across dogs and cats depending on the metabolic capacity of the animal as well as the hepatic enzymes that are triggered by the medication.

It is hypothesized that the processes responsible for the hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia observed in dogs following continuous phenobarbital therapy include enhanced hepatic microsomal enzyme activity and increased lipid synthesis (Charalambous et al., 2016; Podell et al., 2016). These conditions have been observed in dogs. In a study that looked at the effects of long-term phenobarbital therapy on epileptic dogs, it was discovered that a significant percentage of patients had elevated levels of serum cholesterol and TG (Charalambous et al., 2016; Sheth, 2004). Even though the fact that it is not very usual to observe comparable abnormalities in cats, there have been instances of mild hyperlipidemia occurring on occasion. Species-specific differences in lipid metabolism and hepatic enzyme activity underlie this phenomenon (Hermans et al., 2022).

When it comes to other AEDs, such as zonisamide and levetiracetam, their lipid profiles are typically not significantly affected. In most studies, it has been demonstrated that dogs treated with zonisamide maintain stable levels of cholesterol and triglycerides; however, there have been rare instances of transient increases (Smith et al., 2022). It would appear that levetiracetam is typically metabolically inert concerning the maintenance of lipid homeostasis in both dogs and cats (Barnes Heller et al., 2018; Gim et al., 2021).

The applications of AEDs have the potential to influence the pathway through which glucose is metabolized in the body. There is an association between the administration of phenobarbital and potassium bromide to dogs and mild hyperglycemia. The potential reason for this is that the activation of hepatic gluconeogenic pathways or alterations in insulin sensitivity are responsible for this (Charalambous et al., 2016; Podell et al., 2016). In spite of the fact that cats are often less susceptible to variations in glucose levels that are brought about by AEDs, there have been documented instances of stress-associated hyperglycemia that have taken place during treatment (Hermans et al., 2022). During the majority of instances, levetiracetam and zonisamide do not have a substantial impact on the levels of glucose that are present in the blood when the patient is fasting (Barnes Heller et al., 2018; Djani et al., 2024; Sheth & Montouris, 2008).

Even while these metabolic irregularities are often mild, they have the potential to have clinical ramifications, particularly in animals that already have metabolic disorders such as diabetes mellitus or obesity (Charalambous et al., 2016; Hermans et al., 2022). This is especially true in cases where the animals are obese. It is recommended that patients on chronic AED therapy undergo routine monitoring of blood glucose, triglyceride, and cholesterol levels (Naveen, 2016; Rudick, 2024). This procedure is performed to identify early metabolic complications and adjust treatment accordingly. Knowing species differences helps clinicians evaluate test results and avoid misattributing small metabolic changes to diseases (Barnes Heller et al., 2018; Charalambous et al., 2016; Hermans et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2022).

Drug-specific biochemical effects

Among companion animals, the biochemical effects of AEDs are very drug-specific and have the potential to influence hepatic, renal, and metabolic parameters. When it comes to the safe and long-term care of epileptic dogs and cats, as well as the monitoring of any adverse responses, having a solid understanding of these consequences is necessary (Podell et al., 2016). Clinicians can distinguish between adaptive alterations and actual toxicity when they are aware of the biochemical consequences specific to a medicine. In particular, for phenobarbital and potassium bromide, it is suggested to do routine monitoring of ALT, ALP, AST, GGT, bilirubin, BUN, creatinine, and electrolytes (Baird-Heinz et al., 2012; Moore, 2014). There are several factors that impact the metabolic reaction to AEDs, including individual variability, variations across species, and concomitant medication (Charalambous et al., 2016).

Phenobarbital

In both dogs and cats, phenobarbital is considered to be a traditional first-line AED. The use of the drug over an extended period is associated with induction of hepatic enzymes, resulting in elevated blood levels of ALP, ALT, AST, and occasionally GGT in dogs (Charalambous et al., 2016; Podell et al., 2016). It is common practice to regard increases in ALT and ALP as adaptive rather than suggestive of real hepatotoxicity, particularly when they are not accompanied by clinical indications of liver disease (Podell et al., 2016). In general, cats treated with phenobarbital have liver enzyme increases that are less severe and less consistent. However, high phenobarbital concentrations can cause modest changes in ALT levels (Hermans et al., 2022). It has also been documented that both species have had isolated instances of idiosyncratic hepatopathy (Charalambous et al., 2016; Hermans et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2022). Phenobarbital has been associated with endocrine alterations, including decreased serum thyroid hormone concentrations, and may also interfere with the interpretation of adrenal function tests due to hepatic enzyme induction rather than true endocrine dysfunction (Gaskill et al., 2000; Müller et al., 2000b).

Potassium bromide

Potassium bromide is commonly used in dogs, either as monotherapy or as an adjuvant, due to its versatility and effectiveness. Neither therapy differs in this regard. On the other hand, potassium bromide does not undergo the process of hepatocellular metabolism and has a modest impact on the activities of liver enzymes (Baird-Heinz et al., 2012; Gouveia et al., 2024). This is in contrast to phenobarbital, which undergoes this process. On the other hand, prolonged treatment with potassium bromide may result in electrolyte abnormalities, the most apparent of which is hyperchloremia, as well as minor changes in renal indicators, such as BUN and creatinine (Baird-Heinz et al., 2012; Gouveia et al., 2024). There have been instances of pancreatitis that have been linked to potassium bromide, even though direct hepatotoxicity is an exceedingly rare occurrence. It has been proven that this leads to an indirect effect on metabolic profiles (Gouveia et al., 2024).

Zonisamide

Zonisamide is a sulfonamide-derived antiepileptic that is often well tolerated. A significant number of dogs and felines continue to exhibit normal liver biochemical indicators even after extended medication treatment (Baird-Heinz et al., 2012; Smith et al., 2022). There have been reports of modest, transient elevations in ALT or ALP that typically do not involve clinical hepatopathy (Baird-Heinz et al., 2012). These reports have only been discovered in extremely rare instances. Despite this, idiosyncratic hepatotoxicity can occur as a result of the sulfonamide makeup of the drug, particularly in individuals who are vulnerable to these effects (95). Since the liver of cats metabolizes zonisamide only to a limited extent, the risk of enzyme induction is reduced compared with that of phenobarbital (Smith et al., 2022).

Levetiracetam

Since levetiracetam has minimal hepatic metabolism, it is advised for use in animals that have compromised liver function (Barnes Heller et al., 2018; Gim et al., 2021). This is because levetiracetam is rather safe for use in these animals. When it comes to dogs and cats, levetiracetam has a very low likelihood of affecting ALT, ALP, or AST levels. On the other hand, there have been cases of dogs with chronic renal disease exhibiting slight increases in their BUN and creatinine levels. The most likely explanation for these increases is that they are the product of altered renal excretion rather than hepatotoxicity (Gim et al., 2021; Muñana et al., 2015). Levetiracetam is a preferred choice as a second-line or adjunct antiemetic medicine in patients who are at risk for liver damage (Barnes Heller et al., 2018; Muñana et al., 2015). This is due to the biochemical safety profile of levetiracetam.

Other AEDs

Benzodiazepines, including diazepam and midazolam, are widely used as antiepileptic agents for the acute management of seizures and status epilepticus in both dogs and cats (Blades Golubovic & Rossmeisl, 2017; Bray et al., 2021; Charalambous et al., 2021). However, their role in long-term seizure control is limited, particularly in dogs, due to the rapid development of tolerance. From a laboratory perspective, benzodiazepines are generally associated with minimal hematological or biochemical alterations during short-term use. Rarely, idiosyncratic hepatotoxicity has been reported, especially in cats receiving oral diazepam, which may manifest as increases in liver enzymes and, in severe cases, acute hepatic necrosis (BVSc et al., 1996; Park, 2012; van Beusekom et al., 2015). Consequently, while benzodiazepines are essential for emergency seizure control, their contribution to chronic laboratory alterations is limited compared to other AEDs (Bhatti et al., 2015; Dewey & Da Costa, 2015; Plumb, 2018).

Only a small amount of information from veterinary trials is known for other AEDs, such as gabapentin and pregabalin. Research suggests that there is little impact on hepatic enzymes, despite the fact that comprehensive metabolomic exams are still in their early phases (Castel & Abuelo, 2025; Moore, 2014). Even though the biochemical safety profiles of more recent AEDs are often more favorable when compared to those of phenobarbital, it is nevertheless suggested that careful monitoring be conducted during long-term drug use (Castel & Abuelo, 2025; Moore, 2014) (Table 2).

Species-related differences in laboratory alterations

It is vital to have an understanding of the species-specific metabolic and pharmacokinetic variances that exist between cats and dogs in order to correctly interpret the laboratory modifications that are brought about by AEDs (Wang & Zang, 2023). These variations are characterized by differences in biochemistry and pharmacokinetics. As a consequence of these alterations, enzyme induction, drug-metabolism pathways, and biochemical processes are all affected. This leads to a variety of adjustments in the laboratory, which in turn have important repercussions for clinical practice (Jukier; Mastrocco et al., 2024).

The hepatic drug metabolism pathways of dogs and cats differ markedly, one of the most significant pharmacological distinctions between the two species. Cats have deficiencies in a number of phase II conjugation enzymes, the most well-known of which are uridine 5′ diphospho-glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs), which are important for glucuronidation activities in the liver (Court, 2013; Wang & Zang, 2023). In contrast to dogs, cats do not have essential UGT isoforms such as UGT1A6 and UGT1A9, which results in a reduced capacity for glucuronidation and a delayed clearance of medications or metabolites that are dependent on these pathways (Wang & Zang, 2023). Cats also have a lower capacity for glucuronidation than dogs do. Cats typically metabolize several xenobiotics in a manner that is different from that of dogs (Court, 2013).

An example of a metabolic process specific to a species is the metabolism of phenobarbital, an AED that is widely administered. Phenobarbital is an effective inducer of hepatic microsomal enzymes in dogs, and it does this by using CYP450 pathways throughout the body. This causes an increase in the activity of liver enzymes in the blood, particularly ALP and ALT, even when the medicine is provided for considerable amounts of time without producing any visible hepatic conditions (Hermans et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2024). Through the mechanism of enzyme auto-induction in canine patients, it is well known that phenobarbital can accelerate not only its own metabolism but also that of other medications. Additionally, this capability results in changes in biochemical markers, which are seen following treatment for an extended period of time (Müller et al., 2000a).

However, phenobarbital does not consistently induce the same amount of hepatic enzyme activity in cats as it does in dogs. The induction of liver enzymes was either low or inconsistent, even after chronic therapy, according to the findings of retrospective investigations that assessed the influence of phenobarbital on blood biochemistry in epileptic cats. These investigations were conducted based on the findings of previous studies. In addition, the majority of epileptic cats maintained consistent ALP and ALT levels relative to their initial values (Hermans et al., 2022). According to these findings, enzyme auto-induction is less likely to account for the hepatic response in felines to phenobarbital. This is probably due to the fact that the expression and control of enzymes differ from species to species (Hermans et al., 2022; Marsh et al., 2021).

In practical applications, the disparate responses to enzyme induction should be considered significant. Elevations in ALP and ALT that are associated with phenobarbital in dogs may be suggestive of adaptive enzyme induction rather than intrinsic liver injury. This is because phenobarbital is connected to both of these indicators. When interpreting blood biochemistry findings, it is essential to consider the circumstances under which the data were obtained (Kumar et al., 2024; Müller et al., 2000a). Studies carried out on canines have revealed that administering phenobarbital over a lengthy period of time can significantly raise levels of ALP and ALT without causing any discernible histopathologic symptoms of structural liver damage. Taking this into consideration, it may be concluded that the treatment is more likely to be an adaptive reaction than a hepatopathy (Balana Pedrol & Seth, 2023).

In cats, however, elevated liver enzymes during phenobarbital therapy should be interpreted with greater caution, as they may be less attributable to adaptive enzyme induction and more suggestive of underlying hepatocellular stress or pathology (Hermans et al., 2022; Marsh et al., 2021). Because cats metabolize certain drugs more slowly than dogs, particularly those requiring glucuronidation, biochemical responses in feline patients may differ even with the same dosing regimens (Court, 2013).

Clinicians must recognize that enzyme induction patterns and metabolic capabilities differ fundamentally between species, affecting laboratory interpretation. In dogs, elevated liver enzymes in the context of phenobarbital therapy may not necessarily indicate hepatotoxicity but rather a metabolic adaptation to chronic drug exposure (Balana Pedrol & Seth, 2023; Kumar et al., 2024). Regular monitoring of liver profiles is still recommended, but elevations in dogs may be more benign compared to similar changes in cats.

In cats, biochemical changes such as increased ALT or ALP during AED therapy may warrant more thorough investigation for hepatic disease, since species differences reduce the likelihood that such elevations are purely due to enzyme induction (Hermans et al., 2022). Additionally, cats’ reduced capacity for glucuronidation means they may accumulate certain drugs or metabolites, further modify their biochemical profiles and increase susceptibility to dose-related adverse effects (Court, 2013). Understanding these species-specific differences enhances clinical decision-making when interpreting laboratory findings in epileptic dogs and cats, enabling more individualized and safer AED management strategies.

Laboratory monitoring during antiepileptic therapy

Evidence-based medicine plays a central role in the management of epilepsy in companion animals, with therapeutic and monitoring decisions increasingly relying on robust clinical evidence and validated studies. Consensus statements and international guidelines, particularly those developed by the International Veterinary Epilepsy Task Force (IVETF), provide structured and disease-specific recommendations that translate available evidence into practical guidance for antiepileptic drug selection, therapeutic stratification, and standardized monitoring protocols in companion animal epilepsy (Bhatti et al., 2015; Charalambous et al., 2024; Potschka et al., 2015).

According to the consensus guidelines of IVETF, the primary goal of antiepileptic drug (AED) therapy is to achieve complete seizure freedom, defined as the absence of seizures for a period exceeding three times the longest pretreatment interictal interval and for at least three months (Berendt et al., 2015; Potschka et al., 2015). However, the guidelines acknowledge that complete seizure control may not be achievable in all patients. In such cases, partial therapeutic success may still represent a clinically meaningful outcome, including the prevention of cluster seizures or status epilepticus, a substantial reduction in seizure frequency compared with the pretreatment period, or a decrease in seizure severity (Berendt et al., 2015). When either complete seizure freedom or partial therapeutic success is achieved, continuation of the current treatment regimen with ongoing long-term evaluation is recommended. Conversely, failure to achieve meaningful clinical improvement may warrant reassessment and modification of the antiepileptic treatment strategy (Charalambous et al., 2024; Potschka et al., 2015; Volk, 2015).

Regular laboratory monitoring is necessary to assess treatment effectiveness, identify medication toxicity, and guide dosage modifications when AEDs are administered long-term to companion animals. It is common practice for monitoring procedures to incorporate hematologic, biochemical, and therapeutic drug concentration evaluations at predetermined intervals during the course of therapy (Muñana et al., 2015; Plumb, 2018). This is because many AEDs are processed hepatically or removed renally. Current laboratory monitoring recommendations for antiepileptic therapy are largely derived from long-term studies of phenobarbital and potassium bromide (Bhatti et al., 2015; Hermans et al., 2022; Müller et al., 2000a). For newer antiepileptic drugs, however, longitudinal data evaluating hematological and biochemical changes remain scarce, limiting the development of evidence-based monitoring protocols (Charalambous et al., 2018, 2016). The implementation of standardized monitoring protocols in veterinary epilepsy management could improve clinical decision-making by facilitating the differentiation between adaptive laboratory alterations and true drug-induced toxicity.

Baseline laboratory evaluation

Before initiating treatment with AEDs, it is essential to perform a thorough baseline laboratory evaluation. This is done to identify any pre-existing abnormalities that may influence the selection of medication or increase the likelihood of experiencing adverse side effects. There are several components that are included in the suggested baseline investigations (Berendt et al., 2015; Plumb, 2018). These include a CBC, a serum biochemistry profile, an electrolyte panel, and a urinalysis. The interpretation of any future adjustments to the laboratory may be dependent on these characteristics, which provide essential reference values when used.

Due to the fact that medications like phenobarbital undergo significant hepatic metabolism and have the potential to stimulate liver enzyme activity or cause hepatotoxicity during chronic therapy, it is important to pay special attention to hepatic biomarkers such as ALT, ALP, AST, bilirubin, albumin, and BUN (Castel & Abuelo, 2025; Jukier, Gross et al., 2024). Patients who are suspected of having liver dysfunction may be advised to undergo additional functional tests, such as measuring the concentration of bile acid before and after meals, in order to assess the hepatic reserve before the initiation of pharmacological therapy (Castel & Abuelo, 2025; Hermans et al., 2022). This is done to determine whether the patient can tolerate the medication. Additionally, baseline renal function measurements, such as serum creatinine and BUN, are highly relevant, particularly when administering drugs with renal clearance pathways, such as bromide or levetiracetam (Muñana et al., 2015). These medications are administered to patients who have a history of renal dysfunction. It is important to establish baseline renal parameters to facilitate the detection of later renal abnormalities during prolonged therapy (Kharouba et al., 2025; Muñana et al., 2015; Skovola et al., 2021).

Recommended monitoring intervals

The pharmacokinetics of the specific AED that is being administered, in addition to the animals' capacity to keep their seizures under control, are the factors that decide the frequency of monitoring (Platt & Garosi, 2025). When measuring the serum drug concentrations of phenobarbital, which continues to be one of the most widely used first-line AEDs in dogs and cats, it is recommended that the measurements be taken approximately two to three weeks after the beginning of therapy, when steady-state concentrations are reached, and again two to three weeks after any dosage adjustment (Castel & Abuelo, 2025; Jukier et al., 2024; Plumb, 2018). It is advised that clinically stable patients undertake first follow-up monitoring three months after the commencement of their therapy, and then every six to twelve months after that (Castel & Abuelo, 2025). Once-daily phenobarbital administration has been shown to provide effective seizure control in cats with presumptive idiopathic epilepsy, with most cats maintaining stable control over long-term follow-up without requiring adjustment of dosing frequency (Mojarradi et al., 2023).

During the course of long-term phenobarbital medication, it is recommended that periodic hematologic and biochemical tests be carried out in addition to monitoring the concentration of the medicine. In rare instances, chronic dosing might lead to hepatotoxicity or bone marrow dyscrasia (Jukier, Gross et al., 2024; Plumb, 2018). Hepatic enzyme induction, in particular, increased ALP activity, can also be a consequence of prolonged administration of AEDs in dogs. Monitoring liver enzyme activity and hepatic function indicators is therefore critical for detecting early drug-induced toxicity. Phenobarbital administration in cats has not consistently been associated with the marked hepatic enzyme induction typically observed in dogs, with most biochemical parameters remaining within reference intervals during long-term treatment (Hermans et al., 2022).

There are a variety of monitoring strategies that are applied for this medicine because of the long half-life of potassium bromide, which is around two weeks in animal subjects. In the majority of instances, serum bromide concentrations are examined either after the loading phase has been finished or after a certain number of weeks of therapy in order to confirm that therapeutic levels have been reached (Dewey & Da Costa, 2015; Hermans et al., 2022). Once seizure control has been achieved, it is recommended that bromide concentrations be monitored frequently and clinical status be evaluated. This is because high-dose bromide can cause seizures itself. Newer antiepileptic drugs are generally considered to have more favorable safety and tolerability profiles compared with traditional agents (Boothe & Perkins, 2008; Skovola et al., 2021). Levetiracetam, zonisamide, imepitoin, and gabapentin are increasingly incorporated into long-term antiepileptic treatment protocols in dogs and cats, although standardized monitoring recommendations vary between agents (Charalambous et al., 2024, 2018). For levetiracetam, imepitoin, and gabapentin, current evidence suggests that extensive routine laboratory monitoring is not universally indicated, and long-term follow-up is mainly guided by clinical response, seizure control, and individual patient factors, particularly in cases of prolonged administration or adjunctive polytherapy (Charalambous et al., 2026; Engel et al., 2017; Hazenfratz & Taylor, 2018; Mastrocco et al., 2024; Rundfeldt et al., 2015; Volk et al., 2008). In contrast, zonisamide may require periodic biochemical monitoring during long-term use, with emphasis on liver-related parameters depending on clinician judgment and patient-specific risk factors (Djani et al., 2024; Miller et al., 2011; Smith et al., 2022). Despite their growing clinical application, evidence-based and universally accepted monitoring intervals for these newer AEDs remain limited, and current recommendations are largely extrapolated from short- to mid-term clinical studies and expert consensus (Green & Lowrie, 2022; Griffin et al., 2022; Gristina et al., 2023). It is strongly recommended that periodic clinical and biochemical assessments be conducted throughout long-term treatment, particularly when these drugs are co-administered with enzyme-inducing agents such as phenobarbital (Mahon et al., 2024; Platt & Garosi, 2025) (Table 3).

Parameters that should be routinely assessed

Therapeutic drug concentrations

The measurement of serum AED concentrations is absolutely necessary in order to accomplish the highest possible level of therapeutic efficacy while simultaneously reducing the potential for adverse effects. The commonly recommended therapeutic serum phenobarbital concentration range of approximately 15–35 µg/mL is generally applied across dogs and cats, although interpretation in feline patients may rely more heavily on clinical response due to more limited species-specific pharmacokinetic data (Jukier et al., 2024). This range is referred to as the phenobarbital concentration range. Dosages that are higher than this threshold have the potential to increase the risk of hepatotoxicity (Charalambous et al., 2024; Jukier et al., 2024; Plumb, 2018). The determination of trough concentrations is important for the purpose of advising adjustments to dose in circumstances when seizure control is insufficient or when adverse effects arise.

Hematologic parameters

It is recommended that the CBC test be performed regularly since certain AEDs have been associated with unusual hematologic adverse effects. These side effects include anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and other bone marrow ailments (Plumb, 2018). Monitoring erythrocyte indices, leukocyte counts, and platelet levels enables early diagnosis of these uncommon but clinically important conditions. Differentiation between bone marrow suppression and is clinically crucial, as immune-mediated cases may require immunosuppressive therapy, unlike direct marrow toxicity (Archer & Fellman, 2025; Santos et al., 2024; Whitley & Day, 2011).

Hepatic biomarkers

ALT, ALP, AST, bilirubin, and albumin should all be included in routine biochemical monitoring since these measures show how well the liver and the hepatocellular structure are functioning (Boothe & Perkins, 2008). Increasing the amount of ALP activity that occurs as a consequence of microsomal enzyme stimulation is a common consequence of long-term phenobarbital treatment, particularly in dogs (Castel & Abuelo, 2025; Plumb, 2018). The presence of progressive rises in ALT or contemporaneous changes in bilirubin or bile acid concentrations, on the other hand, may be indicative of genuine hepatocellular damage rather than merely enzyme induction (Boothe & Perkins, 2008; Castel & Abuelo, 2025; Hermans et al., 2022).

Renal and metabolic parameters

Measurement of BUN, creatinine, and electrolyte concentrations should be added to periodic monitoring regimens. These parameters are especially important for individuals who are undergoing potassium bromide therapy or who have a renal disease that was present before treatment. The assessment of electrolytes may also be of use in determining metabolic abnormalities that are linked with the treatment of AEDs on a long-term basis (Dewey & Da Costa, 2015; Skovola et al., 2021).

The results of laboratory tests should always be interpreted in conjunction with clinical characteristics such as seizure frequency, neurological condition, and potential medication tolerance. Mild biochemical changes that do not manifest clinically may indicate pharmacologic adaptation. On the other hand, increasing abnormalities or combined modifications across multiple parameters may warrant a dosage adjustment, a medication substitution, or additional diagnostic evaluation (Castel & Abuelo, 2025; Platt & Garosi, 2025).

Endocrine biomarkers

Endocrine assessment should be considered prior to starting an AED, especially if an underlying endocrinopathy is suspected or if significant endocrine comorbidities exist (Fischer, 2022; von Klopmann et al., 2006). It could be particularly pertinent to perform thyroid function tests as a number of AEDs (specially phenobarbital) are known to affect serum thyroid hormone concentrations, causing lowered serum total thyroxine (TT4) and free thyroxine (fT4) with potentially increased serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) (Gaskill et al., 2000, 1999). These changes may reflect either true thyroid dysfunction or drug-induced alterations in thyroid hormone metabolism and protein binding, which can complicate the interpretation of laboratory results during long-term therapy (Kantrowitz et al., 1999). Therefore, baseline thyroid function testing would facilitate interpretation when differentiating pre-treatment hypothyroidism from treatment-induced changes in follow-up assessments.

Also, while definitive clinically relevant adrenal dysfunction is not proven in AED receiving dogs, phenobarbital and other enzyme-inducers may interfere with adrenal function testing, such as ACTH stimulation tests and low-dose dexamethasone suppression, via pharmacokinetic reasons or modification of corticosteroid metabolism (Chauvet et al., 1995; Müller et al., 2000b). Incorporating baseline thyroid and, when clinically indicated, adrenal function testing prior to AED initiation may improve the ability to distinguish pre-existing endocrine abnormalities from subsequent drug-induced clinicopathological changes, thereby enhancing the accuracy of long-term monitoring and clinical decision-making in dogs and cats receiving antiepileptic therapy.

Pancreatic enzymes

Pancreatic biomarkers may also be considered in selected patients prior to initiation of antiepileptic therapy, particularly in dogs with a history of pancreatitis, gastrointestinal disease, hyperlipidemia, or previously increased pancreatic enzyme activity (Albarracín et al., 2015). Phenobarbitone and potassium bromide therapy have been linked to elevated pancreatic lipase concentrations (PLI) and a potentially increased risk of pancreatitis in some dogs (Albarracín et al., 2015; Gaskill & Cribb, 2000; Kluger et al., 2008). Therefore, an initial baseline measurement of pancreatic markers would aid interpretation of later abnormalities and help differentiate primary pancreatic disease from a treatment-related problem.

Clinical interpretation of laboratory abnormalities

Interpretation of clinical pathologic changes in patients receiving AEDs requires a systematic approach, as many of these drugs, particularly phenobarbital, can cause laboratory alterations through enzyme induction or metabolic changes without necessarily reflecting actual tissue damage (Center, 2007; Charalambous et al., 2014). Interpretation of adaptive drug induced changes versus pathologic changes is critical in making clinical decisions in these patients.

The first step after observing an abnormality is to assess the magnitude and pattern of the changes. In cases where the increases are mild and isolated (e.g., only ALP or ALT increases without other changes), a drug-induced origin is more likely. This condition is often due to hepatic enzyme induction by AEDs, which can occur in the absence of hepatocellular injury (Center, 2007; Hermans et al., 2022). In these situations, it is recommended to check additional tests such as bile acids or basic metabolic indicators (glucose, BUN, creatinine) to assess the actual function of the organ (Kimram et al., 2025).

If the results of these secondary tests are normal, the interpretation is towards an adaptive response to the drug, and only periodic monitoring is usually sufficient. In contrast, if these mild cases are accompanied by abnormalities in functional tests, the possibility of an underlying disease should be considered, and the diagnostic path changes to the branch of disease investigation (Charalambous et al., 2014; Podell et al., 2016).

On the other hand, when the changes are moderate to severe or when several parameters are involved simultaneously (multi-parameter abnormalities), the probability of true pathology is much higher. In these cases, evaluation should include clinical signs (e.g., anorexia, vomiting, jaundice, lethargy) and the use of more advanced methods such as imaging, liver biopsy, or specific renal and liver function tests (Balana Pedrol & Seth, 2023; Gaskill et al., 2005; Muñana, 2013a). The presence of clinical signs concomitant with laboratory abnormalities has greater predictive value for true disease than single laboratory changes.

A key point in distinguishing drug-induced changes from true disease is the temporal relationship: changes that occur after drug initiation and improve with dose adjustment or discontinuation are more likely to be drug-induced. In contrast, progression despite stabilization of drug dose or the development of new changes in other systems reinforces the possibility of underlying disease. Also, in dogs, phenobarbital-induced ALP elevations are very common and often benign, whereas in cats, such patterns are less common and should be interpreted with more caution (Hermans et al., 2022; Mojarradi et al., 2023; Müller et al., 2000a).

Ultimately, clinical decision-making is based on the summary of this information and may include continuing treatment with monitoring, adjusting the drug dose, or treating the underlying condition. This stepwise approach helps to avoid overdiagnosis and unnecessary discontinuation of anticonvulsant medications in some cases, while also ensuring that true cases of disease are identified and managed promptly (Charalambous et al., 2014; Muñana, 2013a) (Fig. 2).

Limitations and future research directions

The available evidence should be interpreted with caution due to methodological limitations across studies. The available evidence base is limited by differences in treatment protocols and monitoring approaches, which should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review. One of the most important limitations of studies investigating clinicopathological changes induced by AEDs in dogs and cats is the study design and data quality. Many studies have been conducted retrospectively or in case series and lack adequate control groups, making it difficult to infer a causal relationship between drug use and laboratory changes (Charalambous et al., 2014; Podell et al., 2016). In addition, small sample sizes and heterogeneity of populations in terms of breed, age, nutritional status, and co-morbidities increase variance and reduce the generalizability of results (Charalambous et al., 2014, 2016)(2,3). Potential sources of bias in the available literature may include retrospective study designs, selection bias due to non-randomized case inclusion, publication bias favoring positive findings, and heterogeneity in study populations and outcome reporting.

An important limitation is the short follow-up period in many studies, which limits the assessment of chronic complications such as hepatotoxicity or late hematological changes (Charalambous et al., 2018). Also, in many studies, serum therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) was either not performed or was not systematically linked to clinicopathological findings, making it difficult to analyze a precise dose-response relationship (Charalambous et al., 2018; Podell et al., 2016). In addition, the concomitant use of multiple AEDs (polytherapy) acts as a significant confounding factor, complicating the dissociation of the effects of each drug (Charalambous et al., 2024).

Another notable point is the paucity of data in cats compared with dogs. Most evidence for the biochemical effects of drugs such as phenobarbital has been derived from studies in dogs, and generalization to cats is uncertain (Charalambous et al., 2018). In this context, the limited availability of feline-specific data represents an important gap in current knowledge. Several key areas remain insufficiently investigated, including the long-term safety of newer antiepileptic drugs, the frequency and clinical significance of hematological and biochemical alterations, species-specific responses to therapy, endocrine and metabolic adverse effects, and the establishment of evidence-based monitoring protocols and follow-up intervals (Charalambous et al., 2018; Potschka et al., 2015). Addressing these gaps through well-designed feline studies will improve the interpretation of clinicopathological changes, support the development of standardized monitoring approaches, and ultimately enhance clinical decision-making in the management of feline epilepsy.

Also, many laboratory changes, such as elevated liver enzymes, may be due to enzyme induction rather than actual tissue damage; therefore, it is difficult to distinguish adaptive from pathological changes without additional methods, such as biopsy or advanced functional tests (Charalambous et al., 2016). To advance in this area, prospective, controlled studies with sufficient sample sizes are essential to distinguish the true effect of AEDs from confounding factors (Charalambous et al., 2014, 2016). The design of these studies should include a careful baseline, an appropriate control group, and long-term follow-up to accurately identify cumulative and chronic complications (Charalambous et al., 2016). Furthermore, given the increasing use of newer drugs such as levetiracetam, which has a better safety profile, conducting direct comparative studies between classic and new-generation drugs could help select the optimal treatment with minimal clinicopathological changes (Charalambous et al., 2024; Löscher & White, 2023). Ongoing efforts to identify agents with fewer adverse hematological and biochemical effects may help reduce the need for intensive monitoring and improve long-term treatment compliance. The shift toward safer and more targeted therapies is expected to enhance both clinical outcomes and quality of life in affected patients. Future studies in small animal practice may also consider the evaluation of additional antiepileptic agents such as brivaracetam, lacosamide, and rufinamide, particularly in refractory cases where conventional therapies fail to achieve adequate seizure control (Jukier et al., 2024; Klein et al., 2016; Lumley et al., 2024; Platt, 2014).

One novel approach is the use of advanced metabolomics and proteomics-based biomarkers that can detect subtle metabolic changes before classical changes in parameters such as ALT and ALP occur, and help distinguish adaptive changes from true damage (Charalambous et al., 2026; Perera et al., 2022; Verdoodt et al., 2025). In addition, the integration of TDM with pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic models can provide a more precise understanding of the relationship between drug levels and clinicopathological changes (Charalambous et al., 2024; Podell et al., 2016).

Another promising area for future research is the identification of novel biomarkers that may facilitate the early detection of antiepileptic drug-associated adverse effects before conventional clinicopathological abnormalities become evident. Emerging approaches, including extracellular vesicle-derived biomarker profiling, as well as circulating microRNA analysis, have shown potential in veterinary epilepsy research and may contribute to the development of more sensitive and individualized monitoring strategies (García-Gracia et al., 2024; Verdoodt et al., 2024). The validation of such biomarkers could improve the differentiation between adaptive physiological responses and clinically significant drug-induced toxicity, thereby enhancing long-term patient management and therapeutic decision-making in dogs and cats receiving antiepileptic drugs.

The growing importance of individualized treatment approaches should be emphasized in veterinary epilepsy management, as the selection and long-term use of antiepileptic drugs should not follow a uniform protocol for all animals (Chon et al., 2024). Instead, drug choice and dosing should be tailored according to the specific pharmacological profile of each antiepileptic drug, as well as patient-related factors such as age, breed, comorbid conditions (particularly hepatic, renal, endocrine, and gastrointestinal diseases), and concurrent medications that may influence drug metabolism or increase the risk of adverse effects (Pisani et al., 2017; Potschka et al., 2015; Schmidt, 2023). Furthermore, laboratory monitoring plays a central role in identifying drug-specific clinicopathological alterations, enabling timely dose adjustment or drug substitution when necessary (Bhatti et al., 2015; Podell, 2013a). This individualized, drug-oriented approach may improve therapeutic efficacy while reducing the risk of misinterpretation of laboratory changes and minimizing adverse effects during long-term antiepileptic therapy in dogs and cats. Cross-species and computational approaches have shown that integrating heterogeneous neurological data can improve seizure detection and characterization, thereby supporting the development of precision medicine frameworks and more individualized monitoring strategies in epilepsy management (Wang et al., 2025).

Conclusions

Antiepileptic drugs play a central role in the long-term management of epilepsy in dogs and cats; however, their use is frequently associated with predictable clinicopathological alterations, particularly affecting hepatic, renal, and hematopoietic systems. Regular laboratory monitoring is therefore essential not only for therapeutic drug management but also for early detection of adverse effects. For clinicians, accurate interpretation of these changes requires a structured, context-based approach that considers the time course, drug regimen, clinical status, and the magnitude of abnormalities, thereby allowing differentiation between adaptive drug effects and true pathological conditions. Despite advances in epilepsy management, important gaps remain, especially regarding long-term safety data in cats. Further advances will come from the combination of therapeutic drug monitoring, improved biomarkers and individually tailored approaches to guide optimal therapy aimed at enhancing long-term safety and efficacy of antiepileptic drug use in veterinary patients.
