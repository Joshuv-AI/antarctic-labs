Impact of SNAPPS-based teaching on diagnostic accuracy and academic achievement in pathology residents
Authors: Shraddha Khanke, Pankti Buch, Archana Buch, Charusheela Gore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & objective The SNAPPS model is a learner-centered teaching strategy widely used in clinical specialities. It promotes active participation and enhances diagnostic reasoning. However, its application in pathology residency training remains underexplored. This study aimed to assess the utility of the SNAPPS method in improving the academic performance, diagnostic reasoning, and self-directed learning of pathology residents.Materials & methodsA cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in Department of Pathology. 35 residents across three levels of training participated. The study was conducted in four phases: sensitization of preceptors, case writing using the traditional method, sensitization of residents to components of SNAPPS -Summarize, Narrow, Analyze, Probe, Plan, and Self-directed learning; and case presentation under SNAPPS guidance. Residents’ performances were evaluated through standardized SNAPPS checklists, multiple-choice question (MCQ) tests (pre- and post-intervention), and structured feedback forms. Data were analysed using Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test.ResultsPost-test MCQ scores demonstrated significant improvement across all academic years. First-year residents showed modest but statistically significant gains (p < 0.001). Second-year residents exhibited consistent improvement (p = 0.004), while third-year residents demonstrated the greatest learning gains (p < 0.0001). SNAPPS checklist scores improved from median of 3 to 4 for first five SNAPPS components, while sixth component increased from median of 1 to 2 (p < 0.0001). Feedback analysis showed > 90% positive responses for most items, although time-related concerns were noted.ConclusionSNAPPS can serve as an effective structured teaching tool in postgraduate pathology education, as it enhances academic performance and clinical reasoning and promotes active participation.Keywords: SNAPPS, Pathology residency, Clinical reasoning, Postgraduate medical education, Learner-centred teaching

Introduction

Medical education has progressively shifted from teacher-centred to learner-centred teaching models that foster active engagement and critical thinking among residents. One such teaching model, SNAPPS—an acronym for Summarise, Narrow, Analyse, Probe, Plan, and Self-directed learning—has demonstrated success in improving case presentations, diagnostic reasoning, and self-directed learning among residents in clinical specialities [1–3]. The SNAPPS model, initially developed for outpatient teaching, has been successfully adapted across multiple clinical disciplines [1].

Pathology residency training faces distinct challenges. In clinical settings, residents interact directly with patients; however, pathology residents must develop the ability to connect theoretical knowledge with practical skills when working with histopathology specimens and patients’ clinical data to make diagnoses, this includes interpretation of gross findings, considering various differential diagnoses and narrowing down to definite diagnosis after microscopic examination along with immunohistochemistry. For example, while evaluating a soft tissue tumor, residents are expected to identify relevant gross features, interpret histomorphology, narrow differential diagnoses, and recommend appropriate immunohistochemical markers to reach a definitive diagnosis. Hence, structured case discussions are essential for building clinical reasoning and decision-making abilities in the residents [4, 5]. While SNAPPS has been widely studied in other specialities such as internal medicine, pediatrics, surgery and psychiatry [1–3, 6], its formal application in pathology training has not been thoroughly explored.

The present study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the SNAPPS model in postgraduate pathology training, in enhancing residents’ clinical reasoning, diagnostic accuracy and self-directed learning.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted in the Department of Pathology at a tertiary care center in western Maharashtra.

Study participants

We have 14 residents per year. Out of 42 residents, 7 residents were on district residency posting and were excluded from the study. A total of 35 pathology residents participated in the study - first-year (n = 12), second-year (n = 9), and third-year (n = 14). Participation of the residents was voluntary. Two trained preceptors supervised the sessions. As the study was conducted in the department of pathology of a medical college, convenience sampling was used.

Study duration- 6 months

Data collection method

The study was conducted in four structured phases:

1. Sensitization of Preceptors: Preceptors were introduced to the SNAPPS model through instructional videos, handouts, and prior literature. To improve uniformity in assessment, the preceptors jointly reviewed sample case presentations and discussed scoring criteria using the SNAPPS checklist before the study began. Calibration exercises were performed to minimise inter-observer variability in scoring.

4. Case Presentation (SNAPPS-based): After one week of preparation, residents presented the same cases under SNAPPS headings. Preceptors evaluated presentations using the SNAPPS checklist. The SNAPPS checklist included the six components, S- summarise the case, N- narrowing down the differential diagnoses, A- analyse the differential diagnoses, P- Probing the preceptor, P- Plan management of patient/ ancillary investigations for patients, S- Self-directed learning. Each SNAPPS component was assessed using a 5-point scoring scale. A score of 1 indicated minimal or poor performance with limited demonstration of the component, whereas a score of 5 indicated excellent performance with clear and structured application of the SNAPPS element. Intermediate scores represented varying levels of competency and completeness. A post-test MCQ and a structured feedback survey (10-item Likert scale) for residents were administered. The resident feedback survey used a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree. Responses with scores of 4 or 5 were categorized as positive responses, score 3 as neutral response, and scores of 1 or 2 as negative responses.

Results

Preceptors documented residents’ performance for each case, and individual case presentations were treated as the unit of analysis. Overall, residents showed noticeable improvement across all steps of the SNAPPS model after the intervention (Fig. 1).

Bar chart revealing the pre- and post-SNAPPS scores of residents' performance

Summarize the case

Before SNAPPS training, most residents gave lengthy, unfocused case summaries. After the intervention, their presentations became more structured and concise, emphasising the key clinical data, gross findings, and histopathology features. The median checklist score for this step increased from 3 to 4, reflecting more precise and more organized case summaries.

Narrow the differential

Before the intervention, residents often generated broad and sometimes poorly connected differential diagnoses. Following the implementation of SNAPPS, residents were able to prioritise differentials based on clinicopathological correlation. The median score improved from 3 to 4, with third-year residents showing the most consistent improvement (p < 0.0001).

Analyze the differential

Before SNAPPS, residents were not oriented to provide differential diagnoses for cases. After SNAPPS training, residents provided two to three differential diagnoses for a case based on clinical history, gross and microscopic findings, and were able to justify their findings. Checklist scores rose from 3 to 4, indicating stronger diagnostic reasoning.

Probe the preceptor

This area showed one of the most significant changes. Before the intervention, residents rarely asked probing or clarifying questions. After SNAPPS sensitisation, they actively engaged with preceptors, asking questions about pathology, ancillary tests, and diagnostic pitfalls in the case. The median score increased from 3 to 4, and residents reported feeling more confident in directing their own learning.

Plan management / next steps

In the pre-SNAPPS phase, residents’ planning was often limited to stating the likely diagnosis. Post-intervention, they began discussing relevant next steps, such as recommending immunohistochemistry, molecular studies, or further clinical correlation. Median scores improved from 3 to 4, with senior residents showing the clearest gains.

Self-directed learning

Self-directed learning remained the most challenging area for residents. Before the intervention, very few engaged in learning beyond the cases assigned to them, which was reflected in a low median score of 1. After the SNAPPS training, however, residents began to recognise their own knowledge gaps, seek out additional resources, and share what they had learned during presentations. Although the median score rose to 2 (p < 0.0001), many residents noted that time constraints continued to limit how deeply they could explore topics on their own.

MCQ performance

Residents’ knowledge improvement was assessed through pre-test and post-test MCQs (Fig. 2).

The results of the Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test indicate statistically significant improvements in post-test scores across all academic years. For 1st-year students, the improvement was modest but statistically significant (P < 0.001). Among 2nd-year students, a more pronounced and consistent gain was observed, supported by a significant P-value of 0.004. The greatest learning gains were seen in 3rd-year students, whose scores improved substantially with a highly significant result (P < 0.0001)

The post-test MCQs result demonstrated statistically significant improvements across all three academic years. Among first-year residents, the post-test scores showed a modest but consistent increase compared to baseline and this improvement was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Second-year residents demonstrated a more pronounced gain than the first-year residents; the higher post-test scores suggested a better integration of theoretical knowledge with practical reasoning (p = 0.004). The third-year residents showed the most substantial improvement, with a marked increase in their post-test scores compared to pre-test performance, yielding highly significant results (p < 0.0001). Overall, these results indicate that although the SNAPPS model benefited residents at all levels, its impact was strongest among senior residents—likely because their prior exposure to case materials enabled them to apply structured reasoning more effectively.

Discussion

The six steps SNAPPS model was developed based on cognitive learning (Bordage) and reflective practice theory (Osterman and Kottkamp) [1]. The SNAPPS model has been effectively used in clinical specialities and is a time-saving teaching model in busy outpatient and inpatient settings. In the field of pathology, there are subspecialties such as hematopathology and histopathology where the SNAPPS model can be used effectively [7]. Pathology differs from medicine and surgery in that the pathologist does not have patient interaction and serves essentially as a diagnostic consultant. The pathologist’s diagnosis will determine the patient’s further treatment plan. Hence, a case-based approach in which the resident can correlate clinical and pathological findings, narrow down 2 to 3 differential diagnoses, and plan further IHCs or molecular testing is essential for developing clinical reasoning. This teaching framework can also allow preceptors to provide more focused education to the residents without taking additional valuable time.

This study highlighted the successful utility of the SNAPPS model for case presentations in pathology residency. Each of the SNAPPS components has essential implications for pathology residency. We further discuss the present study findings of each element of SNAPPS.

Improvement in knowledge and clinical reasoning

The positive change in MCQ scores and overall reasoning reflected the educational value of SNAPPS. Similar improvements have been documented in earlier studies involving postgraduate learners [2, 3, 6]. The enhanced performance among senior residents may be due to their greater baseline familiarity with pathology case workups, which aligns with established clinical reasoning frameworks.

Strengthening case presentation and communication

Residents displayed more structured and concise case presentations, supporting findings from SNAPPS-based educational interventions in other specialities [2, 3, 8, 9]. The ‘Summarize’ component helped residents present cases in a concise and organised way by focusing on relevant clinical history of the case and key morphologic findings, and correlating the clinical history with the gross and microscopic findings. The ‘Narrow’ and ‘Analyze’ steps encouraged residents to move beyond descriptive reporting and develop logical differential diagnoses supported by clinicopathological correlation. Emphasis on narrowing differentials based on gross and microscopic findings and on explaining the reasoning helped them transition from descriptive to analytical thinking, which is critical for pathology reporting practice. For example, for a soft tissue mass case, residents were given the clinical history. Residents probed the preceptor for the Imaging findings, FNAC findings if available (Probe). They note down the gross findings of the specimen like soft or firm, greyish white, fleshy, presence of areas of hemorrhage or necrosis etc. Depending on the microscopic findings, and integrating the clinical, gross and microscopy, residents wrote down the differentials (Summarise, Narrow). Then they analzed the differentials (Analyze). Then to confirm the diagnosis, residents wrote down the IHC panels (Plan).

Encouraging active learning and engagement

After SNAPPS training, residents actively asked questions, explored uncertainties, and engaged in academic dialogue—an effect consistent with previous literature showing that SNAPPS promotes a shift from passive to active learning [1, 10]. The “Probe” element particularly encouraged deeper inquiry. For example, after narrowing down the differentials on the basis of gross and microscopy, residents did ask the questions like how to select the IHCs for a particular case, how to interpret the IHCs and come to the final diagnosis.

Promotion of self-directed learning

Although the improvement in self-directed learning was modest, it mirrors challenges noted in previous studies, in which learners required time and practice to identify knowledge gaps independently. Residents can direct their own learning through the use of journal articles, books, and slide study sets, to augment their knowledge and deepen their understanding of similar cases with variations [7].

Implications for pathology education

Pathology relies on structured interpretation, critical thinking, and clinical correlation. SNAPPS provides a systematic method that aligns well with these competencies. Its potential to improve clarity, reasoning, and engagement suggests substantial value in incorporating it into pathology teaching programs.

Feasibility of SNAPPS in pathology training

Although many residents thought that SNAPPS is time-consuming, the educational benefits observed in this study suggest that the additional time investment of the residents may be worthwhile. The structured nature of SNAPPS improved diagnostic reasoning, active participation, and academic engagement among residents. Furthermore, as learners and preceptors become more familiar with the SNAPPS, the time required for implementation may decrease. In pathology training, where residents routinely participate in detailed case discussions and slide review sessions, SNAPPS can be incorporated into existing academic activities rather than requiring separate teaching sessions. Therefore, despite the initial increase in preparation time, the long-term educational benefits may outweigh the practical limitations. The residents also agreed that even if SNAPPS is time consuming, they did find it beneficial, and with practice they will get used to it and will require lesser time for case presentations in future.

Limitations of the study

The single-centre design and smaller sample size were the limitations of the study. Similar limitations are reported in other SNAPPS studies [2, 6]. A larger multicentric SNAPPS study should be conducted to assess its utility in enhancing case presentation and clinical reasoning among residents and to determine whether any modifications are needed. Another limitation is the absence of a control group or comparison with another structured teaching model such as the One-Minute Preceptor (OMP) method. Therefore, the relative effectiveness of SNAPPS compared with other teaching strategies could not be assessed. An additional limitation of the study was the potential for performance bias. Since the baseline assessment of the residents was conducted before SNAPPS sensitization, and the same cases were used during both phases of the study, prior exposure to the material may have partially contributed to the improved performance observed.

Conclusion

This study is the first to explore the role of SNAPPS among pathology residents. SNAPPS can serve as an effective structured teaching tool in postgraduate pathology education as it enhances academic performance, clinical reasoning, and promotes active participation.
