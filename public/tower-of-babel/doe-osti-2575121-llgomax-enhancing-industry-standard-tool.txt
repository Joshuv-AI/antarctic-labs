Executive Summary
In 2018, the Advanced Research Project Agency – Energy (ARPA-E) launched the Grid Op-
timization Competition (GOC) [1], a series of competitive challenges intended to accelerate
innovation in decision support software used to schedule power grid operations, making
them as efficient as possible, while respecting operational constraints of power equipment
and operational security. This report covers the participation of the LLGoMAX team—a col-
laboration of the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and ECCO International,
Inc.—in Challenge 3 of the competition.
 Challenge 3 focused on the security-constrained alternating-current unit commitment
(SCACUC) problem, a large-scale mixed-integer nonlinear optimization problem solved, ap-
proximately, by power system operators across the globe, at least on a daily basis. The
variant of SCACUC used in Challenge 3, set by the GOC Administrator Team, modeled
most features encountered in current electricity markets, with additional complicating fea-
tures intended to represent the electricity markets of the future.
 The LLGoMAX team developed an innovative approach that adapted existing commer-
cial software tools in the area to be effective for the SCACUC setup of Challenge 3. First, we
implemented the additional features of power elements in Challenge 3 that are not present
in current power grid. Then, we built from the traditional iterative approach, which con-
sists of solving a linearized (mixed integer linear) approximation of SCACUC in sequence
with a power flow evaluation routine and contingency analysis, by replacing its power flow
routine with an advanced power flow routine, which solves a nonlinear constrained optimiza-
tion problem using state-of-the-art techniques in the field, instead of a nonlinear system of
equations as current tools. Crucially, this new power flow routine is capable of optimizing
controls that can only be taken as fixed in current tools. Finally, we interfaced our advanced
power flow routine, with its additional controls, with the routines to generate and solve the
linearization of the SCACUC problem.
 Using the outlined approach, the LLGoMAX team was ranked 7th in overall on Challenge
3. Further, our technique is perfectly compatible with current electricity market specifica-
tions and requirements, allowing a potential transfer to industry within the medium term,
which could significantly increasing the efficiency of power grid operations and result in
savings for final US electricity consumers.

1 Introduction
The ARPA-E Grid Optimization Competition (GOC) is a series of challenges aimed at over-
hauling and modernizing grid software. ARPA-E GOC Challenge 1, opened from November
2018 until December 2019, focused on improved solutions for the near-real-time security-
constrained alternating-current optimal power flow problem (SCACOPF) [2]. In near-real-
time SCACOPF only generator production and voltage setpoints can be controlled, the
remaining variables are either fixed or implied by the physics of the network and/or the
automatic control systems. ARPA-E GOC Challenge 2 [3], opened from December 2020
until December 2021, focused on the next-interval (minutes-ahead) SCACOPF problem,
which includes additional control variables—such as transformer tap settings and network
topology modifications—but it also simplifies the modeling of control systems.
 This report summarizes the execution of the present award by our team LLGoMAX as
participant of the ARPA-E GOC Challenge 3 [4]. Challenge 3 focused on the security-
constrained alternating-current unit commitment problem (SCACUC), a standard problem
in electricity markets which within Challenge 3 took three flavours (in three, separate,
Divisions): Real-Time Market (Division 1, 15 minutes ahead of real time), Day-Ahead
Market (Division 2 ), and Week-Ahead Advisory (Division 3 ).
 Our team’s approach was to build on solutions currently used by industry for SCACUC.
This approach had the goal of advancing software tools used by industry in ways that are
compatible with electricity market structures, so that our advances can be transferred to
industry in the short to medium term. In the following sections we outline how we achieve
the milestones and deliverables of our project; our activities, challenges encountered, and
solutions; outputs; and current follow-on funding opportunities.

2 Accomplishments and Objectives
A number of tasks and milestones were laid out in Attachment 1, the Technical Milestones
and Deliverables, at the beginning of the project. The actual performance against the stated
milestones is summarized in Table 1.
 The formulation of SCACUC used in this project was set by the ARPA-E GOC Ad-
ministration team [5]. In a nutshell, this formulation sets as control variables: on/off
status, production setpoint, and voltage setpoints of generators; consumption level of con-
sumers; connected/disconnected status, tap settings, and angle shift settings of transform-
ers; connected/disconnected status of lines; and shunt step settings. The formulations
constraints model generators considering typical unit commitment restrictions, alternating-
current power flow constraints for the base case (normal operating conditions), and direct-
current (approximate) power flow constraints for contingencies (emergency conditions).
 The ARPA-E GOC Administration team also set the instances against which approaches
of teams participating in Challenge 3 were benchmarked [6]. These datasets presented
multiple differences with datasets encountered in practice, which caused our first attempts
at adapting industry solutions for the competition to fail. We outline our major technical
enhancements to address the issues found and how we implemented them in the next section.

3 Project Activities
Our starting assumption for Challenge 3 was that current industrial tools would have com-
petitive performance in the competition instances, and that we will improve from such
performance by enhancing the industrial sequential linearization methodology [7] as the
competition advanced. This assumption was proven wrong early in Competitive Event 1
due to four main factors:

 1. The formulation of SCACUC in Challenge 3 did use traditional slack bus or distributed
 slack buses to accommodate system loses, instead Challenge 3 used a full alternating-
 current formulation where losses were not separated from transacted power. This
 difference initially led to highly unbalanced dispatches being passed to a power flow
 solver which had many difficulties in balancing power as a results.

 2. Challenge 3 formulation imposed hard bounds on voltage magnitudes but soft limits
 on power balance. Industrial tools do almost the opposite; they impose hard power
 balance but only targets or soft bounds, which makes sense in practice as voltage
 magnitude violations are physically possible but power imbalances are not. This caused
 performance issues as the industrial power flow solver worked towards a solution that,
 often, was infeasible within the context of the competition.

 3. Challenge 3 instances, in general, let all consumers be completely flexible, while in
 reality only a small fraction of consumers are such. This caused problems in the
 industrial sequential linearization approach, as usually only generators would modify
 the linearization point, but in Challenge 3 all generators and consumers could do so.

 4. Multiple branches (power lines and transformers) in Challenge 3 instances had un-
 realistic parameters. In particular, branches had very large impedances or physically
 impossible thermal limits. Large impedances caused converge issues in the industrial
 power flow engine, designed to work with small angles found in practice. Physically
 impossible thermal limits, on the other hand, often lead to voltage collapses during
 the iterative linearization process.

 In preparation for Competitive Events 1 and 2, the LLGoMAX team tried multiple strate-
gies to make the industrial sequential linearization approach competitive. Among them, we
used the flexibility of consumers to assign slacks in the power flow engine (addressing fac-
tors 1 and 3), projected variables with hard bounds before outputing solutions (addressing
factor 2), and limited the maximum voltage differences across branches to be within what
is observed in practice (addressing factor 3). These strategies did improve the performance
of our approach, but were unable to handle all instances or provide the robustness required
to be competitive in Challenge 3.
 As result, prior to Competitive Event 2 we decided to pivot our approach in a more
promising direction: continue using sequential linearization but paired with a new power
flow engine capable of handling all the non-traditional SCACUC features in Challenge 3
while respecting requirements of traditional operations. We designed and implemented a

Projection-Based Optimal Power Flow (PBOPF) engine which takes the dispatch point com-
puted by the linearized (industrial) unit commitment solver and finds the closest feasible
solution to it, within the domain of the power flow equations, with respect to a weighted L1
distance function. The developed power flow engine does not require slack bus constructs,
works with or without power slacks, and can handle soft or hard bounds on voltage mag-
nitudes. After PBOPF returns, we collect violated transmission constraints and generate
shift factors for the sequential linearization in an identical manner as done by industrial
approaches, iterating between unit commitment with shift factors and PBOPF.
 An initial implementation of the PBOPF was submitted for the Competitive Event 3,
whereas its interfacing with our industrial unit commitment solver and its parallelization was
only finished by the Final Event (Competitive Event 4), where team’s final rank was 7th,
overall.
