High resistance of Anopheles gambiae s.L. populations to public health insecticides in Ghana: contribution of domestic and agricultural pesticide use
Authors: Stephina Adjoa Yanney, Anisa Abdulai, Christopher Mfum Owusu-Asenso, Yaw Akuamoah-Boateng, Abdul Rahim Mohammed Sabtiu, Isaac Kwame Sraku, Maxwell Gesuge Machani, Simon Kwaku Attah, Yaw Asare Afrane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria elimination efforts in sub-Saharan Africa are increasingly threatened by the spread of insecticide resistance in Anopheles gambiae sensu lacto (s.l.). The widespread use of insecticides in vector control and pesticides in agriculture has intensified resistance development in malaria vectors. This study examined the drivers of high insecticide resistance in Anopheles mosquitoes in Ghana’s Coastal and Sahel Savannah ecological zones.MethodsAnopheles larvae were collected from eight sites across the Coastal Savannah (Tuba, Opeibea, Nima, and Teshie) and Sahel Savannah (Kpalsogu, Libga, Taha, and Kulaa) zones and reared to adults. WHO insecticide susceptibility intensity assays were performed on 3–5-day-old female Anopheles gambiae s.l. to assess phenotypic resistance. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) technique was used to identify species within the An. gambiae s.l. and detect mutations at voltage-gated sodium channels (Vgsc- 1014F, Vgsc-1014S, Vgsc-1575Y) and acetylcholinesterase G119S-Ace-1 mutation. Alongside larval sampling, a survey was conducted to assess household use of insecticide-based vector control products and pesticide application in agriculture.ResultsAnopheles gambiae s.l. from the Coastal zone showed high resistance intensity to pyrethroids (Mortality rate: 50 to 85.9% at 10x). In the Sahel zone, moderate resistance intensity (Mortality rate: >98% at 10x) was observed. High resistance intensity to pirimiphos-methyl (80–90% at 10x) was recorded in vectors from Tuba and Opeibea. The prevalence of L1014F-kdr mutation was high (range: 0.81 to 1) in all study sites, while L1014S and N1575Y kdr mutations were present at low to moderate frequencies (range: 0 to 0.39). Household surveys revealed widespread use of pyrethroid-based mosquito control products (88.10%), including aerosol sprays (25.33%) and coils (53.95%). Farm surveys showed extensive application of pesticides containing organophosphates (16.2%), carbamates (11.29%), neonicotinoids (25.7%), and diamides (5.4%).ConclusionThis study reveals widespread pyrethroid and organophosphate resistance in Anopheles gambiae s.l,. which could likely be associated with intensive insecticide use in public health and agriculture. With resistance escalating, continuous surveillance and enhanced resistance management strategies are essential to sustain malaria control efforts in Ghana.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13746-0.

Introduction

Vector control plays a crucial role in reducing malaria transmission and is an essential element of malaria control and elimination strategies [1]. Malaria transmission reductions in sub-Saharan Africa are largely due to the scale-up of insecticide-based tools, such as long-lasting insecticide-treated nets (LLINs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS). However, increasing insecticide resistance in malaria vectors threatens malaria elimination efforts in Ghana. Resistance to multiple insecticide classes, including pyrethroids and organophosphates, has been reported, driven by long-term use of LLINs and IRS [2–6]. Aside from insecticides used in public health, other factors, such as agricultural pesticides and environmental contaminants, have been shown to mediate insecticide resistance in malaria vectors. [7, 8].

Target-site resistance and metabolic resistance are the main mechanisms of insecticide resistance in malaria vectors that have been extensively studied and reported in sub-Saharan Africa [9]. The continuous exposure to insecticides has led to the selection of resistance alleles in malaria vectors [9, 10]. Knockdown resistance (kdr) mutations in the voltage-gated sodium channel (VGSC) gene, such as L1014F, L1014S, and N1570Y kdr mutation have been found to confer pyrethroid resistance in African malaria vectors [3, 11]. The L1014F-kdr mutation has been found in high frequencies, almost nearing fixation in some mosquito populations in Ghana [3, 12]. The L1014F-kdr mutation is widespread and predominant in West and Central Africa, while the L1014S-kdr mutation is more common in Central and East Africa, though it is also rapidly spreading in West Africa [3, 9, 11, 13, 14]. The N1575Y kdr mutation has also been found to increase pyrethroid resistance when in association with the L1014F-kdr mutation [15].

The extensive use of both public health insecticides and agricultural pesticides has intensified selection pressure on local malaria vector populations, accelerating the development of insecticide resistance and threatening the effectiveness of current malaria control interventions [16]. Improper or frequent use of insecticide-based personal protection products, such as coils, sprays, and repellents, further contributes to this resistance phenomenon, particularly in areas with limited indoor residual spraying (IRS) or bed net coverage [17]. Moreover, many agricultural pesticides share active ingredients with public health insecticides, resulting in repeated and prolonged exposure of mosquito populations to similar chemical compounds. This overlap amplifies selective pressure and speeds up the evolution of resistance [7, 18]. Consequently, malaria vectors sampled from agricultural areas are increasingly exhibiting high levels of resistance to insecticides used in public health programs [4].

Therefore, it is imperative to strengthen insecticide resistance surveillance of local malaria vectors in Ghana to support evidence-based decision-making on resistance management strategies and the deployment of appropriate vector control tools across different ecological zones. This study investigated the intensity of insecticide resistance in Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations across the Coastal and Sahel savannah ecological zones of Ghana, and the potential influence of household mosquito control practices and agricultural pesticide use.

Methods

Study sites

The study was conducted at eight sites, with four located in the Coastal Savannah and four in the Sahel Savannah ecological zones of Ghana. The Coastal Savannah sites, located in southern Ghana, include Opeibea (5°35′46.42″N, 0°11′01.43″ W), Tuba (5°30‘46“N, 0°23’20” W), Teshie (5°35‘0“N, 0°5’0” W), and Nima (5°35‘0“N, 0°12’0” W). The Sahel Savannah sites, located in northern Ghana, include Libga (9°35′32.26′′N, 0°50′48.8′′W), Kpalsogu (9°33′45.2″N, 1°01′54.6″ W), Kulaa (9°26′59″N, 0°43′44″ W), and Taha (9°26’08.3“N 0°47’02.9” W) (Fig. 1). Tuba and Opeibea are sites where irrigated vegetable farming takes place with permanent use of pesticide application to safeguard crops, creating all year-round mosquito breeding habitats and sub-lethal exposure of these mosquitoes to insecticides. Nima and Teshie are residential areas within the city of Accra. Nima is characterized by slums and overcrowded structures on poorly demarcated plots while Teshie has standard housing on well-demarcated plots but poorly managed drainage and sanitation systems. Irrigated farming with high usage of pesticides is practiced in Kpalsogu and Libga throughout the year, creating suitable breeding habitats for Anopheles gambiae s.l. Taha, Kulaa, and Pagaza are rural sites in the Tamale district of Northern Ghana.Fig. 1Map of Ghana showing the study sites

Map of Ghana showing the study sites

Multiple mass LLIN distribution campaigns have been implemented by the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) between 2014 and 2023 in all the study sites, involving pyrethroid-only nets, and PBO-based LLINs since 2022. In Kpalsogu in the Sahel zone, indoor residual spraying (IRS) has been conducted annually since 2008, initially using pyrethroids and later pirimiphos-methyl (2016–2019), and subsequently clothianidin-based formulations (from 2020 onwards).

The coastal savannah has a tropical savannah climate with temperatures ranging from 23 °C to 33 °C and an average annual precipitation of 787 mm with a bimodal rainfall pattern with a major rainy season from April to June and a short rainy season from October to November. The Sahel savannah experiences a unimodal rainfall from May to November, with an annual average of around 900 mm. The long dry season from December to April sees temperatures rise to 42 °C, while the mean annual temperature is around 28 °C.

Larval sampling and raising in the insectary

Anopheles larvae were collected with WHO standard dippers from various larval habitat types, including irrigation linings, ponds, dug-out wells, swamps, drainage ditches, hoof/foot prints, and puddles into larval bowls. Larvae collected within sites in the Coastal zone were transported to the insectary at the Department of Medical Microbiology, University of Ghana Medical School, Korle-Bu, Accra, and those collected in the Sahel zone were transported in large plastic larval rearing trays to the insectary of the US President’s Malaria Initiative (PMI) Project Office in Tamale. At the insectary, field-collected Anopheles larvae were fed daily with TetraMin ® fish meal (Tetra Werke, Melle, Germany). Larvae were raised into adults under controlled standard insectary conditions (temperature: 27 ± 2 °C, relative humidity: 80 ± 10%). Emerged adults were fed on a 10% sugar solution until use in WHO susceptibility bioassays.

WHO resistance intensity assays

Anopheles gambiae s.l. adults that were 3–5 days old were exposed to insecticide impregnated papers with discriminating concentrations of deltamethrin [1x (0.05%), 5x (0.25%), 10x (0.5%)], permethrin [1x (0.75%), 5x (3.75%), 10x (7.5%)] and pirimiphos-methyl [1x (0.25%), 5x (1.25%), 10x (2.5%)] following the WHO tube bioassay guidelines [19]. The assays were conducted under standard temperature of 27 °C ±2 °C and relative humidity of 75% ± 10%. Controls carrying oil-treated papers were also included in the setup. Knockdown rates were recorded every 10 minutes for 1 hour. After the 1-hour exposure, mosquitoes were transferred into holding tubes and fed on 10% sucrose solution. Mortality rate was recorded after a 24-h recovery period, and resistance was determined according to the WHO guidelines on insecticide susceptibility [19]. The Kisumu susceptible An. gambiae strain served as the primary test control to validate the quality of impregnated papers. Dead and alive mosquitoes were stored in absolute ethanol for later DNA analysis.

Molecular identification of Anopheles gambiae s.L.

A subset of 642 adult mosquitoes from the insecticide susceptibility assays was randomly selected and their DNA extracted using previously described alcohol precipitation, as previously described by Zur et al. [20]. All mosquitoes genotyped were those previously exposed to the 1× diagnostic concentration of deltamethrin and pirimiphos-methyl during the susceptibility tests. PCR was then run to distinguish the members of the An. gambiae s.l. using four sets of oligonucleotide primers in the PCR reaction using the protocol by Scott et al. [21]. Anopheles gambiae s.s and An. coluzzii were distinguished by PCR-RFLP using the method by Fanello et al. [22].

Detection of kdr and G119S-Ace-1 mutations in An. gambiae s.L. across the study sites

A subsample of 198 alive and 142 dead mosquitoes from the bioassay tests at 1x pyrethroid concentration (deltamethrin and permethrin) was randomly selected for genotyping of the L1014F, L1014S, and N1575Y kdr mutations. For genotyping of the G119S-Ace-1 mutation, a total of 56 pirimiphos-methyl-resistant and 86 pirimiphos-methyl-susceptible An. gambiae s.l mosquitoes exposed to 1x pirimiphos-methyl insecticide were used. The L1014F, L1014S, and N1575Y kdr mutations were genotyped using well-described protocols [14] and [15], while the G119S-Ace-1 mutation was genotyped using protocols by Weil et al. [23].

Insecticide use surveys for households in the different ecological zones

To understand the high resistance in the study sites, a survey was undertaken to determine the use of public health insecticides in the community and pesticides for agricultural use. A structured questionnaire was developed to gather detailed information on the types and usage of mosquito control products in households across the two ecological zones; Coastal savannah zone (Tuba, Ada, Nima) and the Sahel-savannah zone (Kpalsogu, Libga, Pagaza) (Additional File 2 Table 1.1). A total of 269 households were surveyed for the purpose of this study; a maximum of 50 randomly selected houses were sampled at each study site. In a smaller community of fewer than 50 houses, all houses were included in the study. A cluster-based sampling approach was employed to ensure representative spatial coverage within each study site. Each community was divided into sections or quadrants, and households were systematically selected across these areas to minimize spatial sampling bias and improve representativeness of household mosquito control practices within the study sites. The subsampling strategy was adopted to achieve balanced sampling across communities while maintaining logistical feasibility for household-level interviews and product verification. Key areas of focus included (i) types of mosquito control products used by households, preferred products, and frequency of usage; (ii) Active ingredients present in these chemical-based products were later verified by trained assistants. The heads of individual households were interviewed by the research assistant in a face-to-face interview. The data collection process took place at the homes to gather relevant information on the type and frequency of mosquito control products used. Interviews were conducted in the local language to facilitate understanding and accurate responses.

Pesticide use surveys in farms in the different ecological zones

Similarly, to understand the contribution of pesticides used in irrigated agricultural sites to the high insecticide resistance observed in the study sites, a questionnaire survey was undertaken. To do this, structured questionnaires were used to obtain detailed information on the types of agricultural pesticides and herbicides used by farmers, the frequency of usage, and the active ingredients of the pesticides used (Additional File 2 Table 1.2). The study was undertaken in study sites with irrigated agriculture, where mosquito larvae were collected to check for insecticide resistance phenotypes and genotypes. These sites were Tuba and Ada in the coastal zone of southern Ghana, and Kpalsogu and Libga in the Sahel zone of Northern Ghana. The study was explained to the farmers, and verbal consent was obtained from each participant. A total of 100 farmers were recruited into the study, 20 farmers from Tuba, 26 farmers from Ada, 34 farmers from Kpalsogu, and 20 farmers from Libga respectively. The questionnaires were administered using face-to-face interviews within the farms. Interviews were done in the local dialect to facilitate understanding and accurate responses, and later translated and transcribed into English.

Data management and statistical analysis

Mortality rates and insecticide susceptibility status were determined using the WHO guidelines: mortality rates of ≥98%, 90–97%, and ˂90% indicate susceptibility, suspected resistance, and confirmed resistance, respectively [19]. The intensity of resistance was also inferred from the WHO protocol: low resistance intensity (resistant at 5x insecticide concentration); moderate resistance intensity (resistant at 5x but susceptible at 10x concentration), and high resistance intensity (resistant at 10x concentration) [19]. The kdr and Ace-1 mutation frequencies were calculated according to the Hardy-Weinberg formula. Allele frequencies were determined using the formula: Allele frequency = (2 × number of homozygotes + number of heterozygotes) / (2 × total number of mosquitoes tested) [24]. Generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with binomial error distribution were used to assess associations between household insecticide use and mosquito resistance phenotype. The study site was included as a random effect to account for clustering, site-level heterogeneity, and non-independence of observations collected within the same community. Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were employed to assess associations between kdr mutations and phenotypes and across ecological zones in genotypic analyses, with odds ratios serving as measures of effect size. Statistical significance was determined at a probability threshold of less than 0.05. All data collected from the questionnaires were reviewed for completeness and consistency before analysis (Additional File 2, Table 1.1 and 1.2). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Results

Malaria vector species present in the study sites

Out of the 642 Anopheles gambiae s.l mosquitoes analyzed, 628 were successfully identified at the sibling species level (Table 1). Among these, Anopheles coluzzii was the most abundant species, accounting for 49.68% (312/628), followed by Anopheles arabiensis at 26.43% (166/628), Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto at 22.29% (140/628), and hybrids of An. coluzzii and An. gambiae s.s. at 3.7% (10/628). An. arabiensis was absent in the Coastal savannah but was present in high numbers in the Sahel savannah (Table 1). In the Coastal savannah, An. coluzzii predominated in Teshie, Tuba, and Nima, while An. gambiae s.s. was the most common in Opeibea. In the Sahel zone, An. coluzzii was the dominant species in Kpalsogu and Kulaa, whereas An. arabiensis was most abundant in Taha and Libga.

Phenotypic resistance in Anopheles gambiae s.L

A total of 5,832 Anopheles gambiae s.l. mosquitoes were used in the WHO intensity assays. Insecticide resistance profiles varied across all sites and ecological zones. For the Sahel savannah zone, pyrethroid resistance was observed at 1x and 5x insecticide concentration across all the sites. High resistance intensity to deltamethrin was observed in Kpalsogu [1x (30%), 5x (68%) and 10x (94%)], Libga [1x (28%), 5x (58%) and 10x (85%)] and Kulaa [1x (69%), 5x (89%), 10x (96%)] while moderate resistance intensity was observed in Taha [1x (78%), 5x (85%), 10x (99%)](chi square (χ2) = 64.360, degree of freedom (df) =3, p-value (P) < 0.001) (Fig. 2a). Moderate resistance intensity to permethrin (mortalities < 90% at 1x and 5x insecticide concentrations) was observed in Libga, Taha and Kulaa except Kpalsogu, where high resistance intensity was observed (mortalities < 90% at 1x, 5x and 10x insecticide concentrations) %)] (χ2 = 39.416, df = 3, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2b). Vectors from Kpalsogu and Libga exhibited moderate resistance intensity (mortalities < 90%) to pirimiphos-methyl at 1x and 5x insecticide concentrations. However, vectors from Taha and Kulaa were found to be resistant at 1x and susceptible at 5x, signifying low resistance intensity. High resistance intensity to pyrethroids (deltamethrin and permethrin) was observed in Opeibea [1x (18.75–26.25%), 5x (36.08–60.71%) and 10x (50–85.94%)], Tuba [1x (25–51.25%), 5x (35–72.94%) and 10x (62.5–81.25%)], Nima [1x (10–31.4%), 5x (36.25–55.95%) and 10x (57.5–76.25%)], Teshie [1x (12.79–13.75%), 5x (20.45–67.5%) and 10x (67.5–85%)] (Figs. 2d & 2e). Anopheles gambiae s.l. from Tuba (<90% mortalities at 10x) and Opeibea (<90% mortalities at 10x) in the coastal zone showed high intensity of resistance to pirimiphos-methyl. However, mosquitoes from Teshie and Nima exhibited moderate resistance intensity (<90% mortalities at 1x and 5x). There were significant differences in mortalities to pirimiphos-methyl at x1 insecticide concentrations across all the coastal sites (χ2 = 13.778, df = 3, p = 0.003) (Fig. 2).

A multivariable generalized linear mixed-effects model, adjusted for household insecticide use and site-level clustering, demonstrated that insecticide type was a significant predictor of resistance phenotype. Compared with deltamethrin as the reference insecticide category, exposure to pirimiphos-methyl was associated with significantly lower odds of resistance phenotype (OR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.09–0.19, p < 0.001), indicating comparatively higher susceptibility to this insecticide. In contrast, permethrin exposure was not significantly associated with resistance phenotype (OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.53–1.06, p = 0.107). The random effect variance for site was low (0.097), suggesting limited between-site variability in resistance phenotype after accounting for fixed effects (Additional file 3, Table S1).

Frequency of kdr mutations and its association with phenotypic resistance

A subset of 340 pyrethroid-resistant An. gambiae s.l mosquitoes obtained from the phenotypic assays were genotyped for the L1014F, L1014S, and N1575Y kdr mutations. The L1014F kdr mutation was detected with a high allelic frequency (range: 0.81 to 1) in both the pyrethroid-resistant and susceptible groups of mosquitoes across all the study sites. Low allele frequencies of the L1014S-kdr mutation were observed, with significantly lower frequencies in the Coastal savannah (range: 0 to 0.02) compared to the Sahel savannah (range: 0.0 to 0.19) (χ2 = 28.36, df = 1, p < 0.001). In addition, there were very low frequencies of the N1575Y kdr mutation (range: 0 to 0.18) with no significant difference between the coastal and Sahel savannah study sites (χ2 = 5.84, df = 1, p > 0.05). No significant association was observed between the presence of the kdr mutations and resistant phenotypes within the sites except Nima, where the presence of the N1575Y kdr mutation was significantly associated with resistance phenotypes (p = 0.038) (Table 2). G119S-Ace-1mutation frequencies in An. gambiae s.l mosquitoes across the study sites

Allele frequencies of the G119S-Ace-1 mutation ranged from 0.24 to 0.88, with the highest frequency occurring in An. gambiae mosquitoes from Opeibea (0.88) in the coastal savannah. Mosquitoes from Libga had the lowest frequency of the G119S-Ace-1 mutation (0.24) (Table 3). A significant difference was observed in the frequency of the G119S-Ace-1 mutation in An. gambiae s.l mosquitoes between the coastal and Sahel savannah zones of Ghana (χ2 = 26.62, df = 1, p < 0.001). A significant difference in the frequency of the G119S-Ace-1 mutations was observed across phenotypes (χ2 = 8.42, df = 1, p < 0.05). Species-stratified analysis (Additional file 1, Table S3) showed variability in G119S-Ace-1 mutation frequencies across Anopheles coluzzii, Anopheles gambiae s.s., and Anopheles arabiensis, indicating that site-level differences may be influenced by underlying species composition. Frequency of genetic mutations in the Anopheles gambiae sibling species

All Anopheles gambiae s.l. mosquitoes genotyped showed very high allele frequencies for the L1014F-kdr mutation (range: 0.94 to 0.96). The frequency of the L1014S-kdr mutation [Allele frequency (F) = 0.04 -0.06] and the N1575Y-kdr mutation [F = 0.03–0.08] was low across all species. Discussion

Increasing insecticide resistance in local malaria vectors threatens to reverse the gains made towards malaria control and elimination in Ghana. The widespread use of public health insecticides and agricultural pesticides in Ghana and other parts of sub-Saharan Africa suggests that insecticide resistance may escalate steadily with time [5, 11]. This study documents patterns of insecticide resistance in malaria vector populations across multiple sites in Ghana and indicates the potential role of insecticide-based vector control and agricultural pesticide use as factors that may contribute to observed resistance patterns. Moderate to high intensity resistance to pyrethroids and organophosphates was observed in sites from both the coastal and Sahel ecological zones of Ghana. Several studies have associated the recurrent use of insecticides to be the main cause of insecticide resistance in malaria vectors, with other factors, such as insecticides used for pest control, playing a significant role in resistance [25–28]. The high insecticide resistance intensity observed in An. gambiae s.l. populations in Ghana have serious implications for current control efforts, as they can reduce the efficacy of insecticide-based vector control tools such as IRS and LLINs. The multivariable generalized linear mixed-effects model demonstrated that mosquitoes exposed to pirimiphos-methyl had significantly lower odds of resistance phenotype compared with the deltamethrin insecticide, suggesting comparatively higher susceptibility to this insecticide across the study populations. This finding indicates that pirimiphos-methyl-based interventions may still retain operational effectiveness and could be useful in insecticide resistance management and rotational vector control strategies in Ghana.

Overall, the L1014F-kdr mutation was detected at extremely high frequencies in all Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations. Household and farm surveys showed widespread and intensive use of insecticide-based products and agricultural pesticides, which may be associated with the increasing insecticide resistance observed in local An. gambiae s.l populations in Ghana.

Anopheles coluzzii was the most abundant species in the coastal zone, followed by An. gambiae s.s. However, in the Sahel zone, An. arabiensis and An. coluzzii were the most abundant species. An. coluzzii is known to preferentially breed in permanent larval habitats such as irrigated fields [29]. This could explain why it was the dominant species found in the coastal study sites. An. gambiae s.s. and An. arabiensis, on the other hand, tend to breed in rain-dependent and more temporary larval habitats; hence, their distribution may differ with seasons [30]. An. arabiensis was found only in the Sahel savannah zone, consistent with findings from other studies that showed that this species is mostly adapted to arid areas like the Sahel savannah ecological zone in Ghana [30, 31]. Anopheles gambiae/ Anopheles coluzzii hybrids were found in only the coastal zone, but in low proportions, as reported in other studies from Ghana [3, 32].

Although high levels of insecticide resistance were observed across all study sites, variations were evident between ecological zones. In particular, differences in resistance intensity between coastal and Sahelian sites may suggest that the implementation of vector control strategies and ecological variability may influence resistance patterns, beyond the mere presence of insecticide pressure. The observed variation in pyrethroid resistance across sites may be influenced by differences in species composition, as sites dominated by Anopheles arabiensis showed comparatively lower resistance than those where Anopheles coluzzii and Anopheles gambiae s.s. were more prevalent, potentially reflecting known differences in vector behaviour and insecticide exposure [3, 18].

High frequencies of the L1014F-kdr mutation were found in all An. gambiae s.l. populations genotyped in all study sites. This may be an indicator of how wide the mutation has spread in the local vector population [3, 12, 24]. The L1014F- kdr mutation has been associated with pyrethroid resistance [9, 10, 14]. Nevertheless, phenotypic resistance could have also been mediated by multiple mechanisms, including metabolic enzymes [33]. Several studies in Ghana have reported high frequencies of the L1014F-kdr mutation in Anopheles gambiae s.l. in Ghana [3, 12, 24]. In contrast, low frequencies of the L1014S and N1575Ykdr mutations were observed in the An. gambiae s.l populations from the study sites. Although these mutations were found at low frequencies, the presence of the L1014S kdr mutations in An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes in Ghana show how rapidly insecticide resistance is spreading across Africa [11]. The occurrence of the N1575Y-kdr mutation in mosquito populations could also be an indication of an increased selection pressure. However, the N1575Y-kdr mutation, which has been reported to enhance resistance conferred by the L1014F-kdr mutation, was detected at low frequency in this study and therefore is unlikely to have played a major role in the observed reduced response to pyrethroids. [15]. The G119S-Ace-1 mutation, although it has a lower frequency as compared to the L1014F-kdr mutation, is of particular interest as it has been linked to organophosphate resistance [34]. The increase in insecticide resistance mutations in malaria has negative implications for malaria transmission, as these may indirectly compromise the potency of vector control measures, especially LLINs and IRS [1, 35].

Findings from this study show the widespread usage of mosquito control products such as coils and aerosol sprays in households. However, bednets, despite their proven effectiveness in reducing malaria cases, were less frequently used [36]. Pyrethroids are used in LLINs and the findings of the current study also indicated that this class of insecticides are also the main active ingredient in aerosol sprays, mosquito coils, and repellents encountered during the survey [37]. Frequent exposure of malaria vectors to pyrethroid-based products, including insecticidal sprays and treated materials, can create selective pressure that may contribute to the development of insecticide resistance. Other studies have also attributed increasing pyrethroid resistance to the extensive use of pyrethroid-based mosquito control products [17, 38, 39]. A study in southern Ghana also found insecticide resistance in Culex to be associated with household mosquito control strategies [40]. The multivariable generalized linear mixed-effects model further supported this observation, showing that households reporting aerosol spray use had increased odds of resistance phenotype after adjusting for insecticide type and site-level variation, although the association was not significant. This finding suggests that repeated household exposure to insecticide-based products may contribute to local selection pressure and potentially sustain resistance in mosquito populations. This finding may imply that pyrethroid-based vector control tools may fail in these areas and might derail the efforts towards malaria elimination in Ghana.

The findings of this study on pesticide and herbicide usage in agricultural settings reveal critical knowledge in the potential public health impacts and implications for insecticide resistance, particularly among malaria vectors. The frequent and unregulated use of pesticides and herbicides containing organophosphates, pyrethroids, neonicotinoids, and carbamates may have contributed to the observed insecticide resistance in the mosquito population. These findings align with patterns observed in agricultural areas with documented cases of insecticide resistance [16, 18, 34]. The co-application of pesticides and herbicides in agricultural practices with different chemical classes, which are similar to those used in mosquito control in public health, may increase selection pressure and may contribute to the increase of insecticide-resistant alleles in mosquito populations [41]. It is possible that pesticides with active ingredients such as pyrethroids and organophosphates applied on crops could leach into mosquito breeding pools inadvertently, resulting in the development of insecticide resistance [42, 43].

While this study provides important baseline information on phenotypic and target-site resistance in malaria vectors across ecological zones, it did not include synergist bioassays or tests with newer insecticide classes such as neonicotinoids. A key limitation of this study is the potential confounding effect of species composition within the Anopheles gambiae s.l. complex on Ace-1 mutation frequency estimates. Although species-stratified results are presented (Additional file 2, Table 3), limited sample sizes within some species-site strata precluded robust statistical adjustment for species effects. Consequently, observed differences in Ace-1 frequencies across sites may partly reflect underlying variation in species composition rather than solely environmental or resistance-related factors. Although a multivariable generalized linear mixed-effects model was performed to assess associations between household insecticide exposure and resistance phenotype, incomplete covariate representation across all study sites limited the inclusion of additional environmental and ecological variables in the final model. Consequently, residual confounding from ecological variation, species composition, agricultural practices, and other environmental exposures may still have influenced the observed associations. Furthermore, the absence of Piperonyl butoxide (PBO) assays limits details on the contribution of metabolic mechanisms. Resistance monitoring should be expanded to include neonicotinoid (clothianidin), chlorfenapyr, broflanilide, and isocycloseram to better understand emerging susceptibility patterns and potential cross-resistance with existing insecticide classes. Incorporating these newer insecticides into resistance monitoring strategies will provide valuable data to inform the selection and deployment of novel vector control tools.

Conclusion

The high pyrethroid and organophosphate resistance observed in An. gambiae s.l populations may be associated with the widespread use of mosquito control products in households and agricultural pesticides in the study sites. These findings, supported by the multivariable analysis, suggest that domestic and agricultural insecticide exposure may potentially influence local resistance patterns in malaria vectors. It is crucial to develop an integrated approach, such as using new insecticide classes, rotating current insecticides, and using alternative non-insecticide-based control tools to manage the growing challenge of insecticide resistance in Ghana and elsewhere in West Africa.
