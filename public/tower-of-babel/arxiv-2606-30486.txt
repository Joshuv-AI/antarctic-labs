Introduction, related work and main results

1.1
Notation and definitions

Let us begin with introducing some piece of notation that will be fixed throughout the text.
Given a positive integer
n
, we denote by
[n]
the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and, given a finite set
F
, we denote by
|F|
its cardinality.
For
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, we denote by
\binom{[n]}{k}
the collection consisting of all subsets of
[n]
having
k
elements.

If
X,Y
are two random variables, we write
X\sim Y
when they have the same distribution.
Unless mentioned otherwise, every random variable
X
that will be considered takes values in a
finite
set
\mathcal{S}_{X}
. We denote by
p_{X}
the
probability mass function
of the random variable
X
, that is,
p_{X}(x)=\mathbb{P}(X=x),x\in\mathcal{S}_{X}
.
A
mode
of the random variable
X
is a value
x^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{X}
such that
p_{X}(x^{*})\geq p_{X}(x)
, for all
x\in\mathcal{S}_{X}
.

Given positive integers

n,k
such that
n\geq k\geq 1
and a positive integer
i\in[n]
, we denote by
\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
a hypergeometric random variable which counts the number of black marbles in a sample without replacement of size
k
from an urn that contains
i

black and
n-i
white marbles.
Formally, if
H\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
then

\mathbb{P}(H=j)=\frac{\binom{i}{j}\binom{n-i}{k-j}}{\binom{n}{k}}\,,\,\text{ for }\,j\in\{\max\{0,k-(n-i)\},\dots,\min\{k,i\}\}\,.

Given a vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
x_{(1)}\geq\cdots\geq x_{(n)}
denote the coordinates of
\mathbf{x}
in decreasing order.
Given two vectors
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
\mathbf{x}

is majorized

by
\mathbf{y}
if it holds

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}x_{(j)}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}y_{(j)}\,,\,\text{ for all }\,\ell=1,\ldots,n-1\,,\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{(j)}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{(j)}\,.

We write
\mathbf{x}\prec\mathbf{y}
when the vector
\mathbf{x}
is majorized by the vector
\mathbf{y}
.
A function
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
defined on a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
Schur concave on
A
if for every
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in A
for which
\mathbf{x}\prec\mathbf{y}
we have
f(\mathbf{x})\geq f(\mathbf{y})
. We refer the reader to
[
10
]
for further details and references on the theory of majorization.

The
Shannon entropy
of
a random variable
X
, taking values in a finite set
\mathcal{S}_{X}
, is defined as

\mathbf{H}(X)\,=\,-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{X}}\mathbb{P}(X=x)\cdot\log(\mathbb{P}(X=x))\,,

where
\log
denotes the binary logarithm, and with the usual convention that
0\log(0)=0
. We refer the reader to
[
4
]
for a standard reference on the topic.
The
binary entropy function
is defined as
h(x)=-x\log(x)-(1-x)\log(1-x)
, for
x\in[0,1]
. Note that
h(x)
is equal to the Shannon entropy of a Bernoulli random variable of mean
x\in[0,1]
, denoted
\text{Ber}(x)
.
It is a well known fact that the binary entropy function is concave.
Moreover, it is a well known fact (see
[
10
, p. 101]
) that
the Shannon entropy of
X
is Schur-concave on the set
\{(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}:\sum_{i}p_{i}=1\}
, for all
n
. In other words, if
X
and
Y
are two random variables such that
p_{X}\prec p_{Y}
, then it holds

\mathbf{H}(X)\geq\mathbf{H}(Y)
.

1.2
Related work and main results

In this article we shall be concerned with an entropic analogue of the Manickam-Miklós-Singhi (MMS) conjecture, which is a statement about the sum in a sampling without replacement from a finite, zero-mean, population.

More concretely, fix positive integers
n\geq 2
and
k<n
. Throughout the text, we denote by
\mathcal{N}_{n}
the family consisting of all
multisets
P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
of real numbers whose sum is equal to zero.
We view the elements of
\mathcal{N}_{n}
as populations from which we sample
k
elements without replacement. Formally, let
P\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
be fixed and choose
\mathbb{I}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
uniformly at random.
Let

X_{P}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}}x_{i}

be the sum of the
k
elements that are sampled without replacement from
P
.
What is a sharp lower bound on the probability that
X_{P}
is non-negative?

The following has been conjectured almost forty years ago by Manickam and Miklós
[
8
]
and by Manickam and Singhi
[
9
]
, driven by questions concerning the so-called first distribution invariant of the Johnson scheme.

Conjecture 1

(MMS,
1987
)
.

If
n\geq 4k
, then for any
P\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
it holds that

\mathbb{P}(X_{P}\geq 0)\,\geq\,\frac{k}{n}\,.

If the conjecture is true, then the result is sharp as can be seen by considering the population
P_{1}=\{1,\frac{-1}{n-1},\ldots,\frac{-1}{n-1}\}
, consisting of one element that is equal to
1
and
n-1
elements
that are all equal to
\frac{-1}{n-1}
, which clearly satisfies

\mathbb{P}(X_{P_{1}}\geq 0)=k/n
.

One particular line of research, which has attracted considerable attention (see
[
2
,
6
,
11
,
12
]
, and references therein), focuses (for fixed
k
) on upper bounds on the minimal integer
N_{k}
having the property that for all
n\geq N_{k}
and all
P\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
it holds
\mathbb{P}(X_{P}\geq 0)\geq k/n
.
The MMS conjecture asserts that
N_{k}\leq 4k
, and
the best current upper bound on
N_{k}
, due to Pokrovskiy
[
11
]
, is
N_{k}\leq 10^{46}k
.
In other words, if
n\geq 10^{46}k
then the MMS conjecture holds true.

Let us remark that
Conjecture
1
is not true without the assumption
n\geq 4k
.
For instance, when
n=3k+1
, the population

P_{3k-2}=\{\frac{1}{3k-2},\ldots,\frac{1}{3k-2},\frac{-1}{3},\frac{-1}{3},\frac{-1}{3}\}
, consisting of
3k-2
values that are equal to
\frac{1}{3k-2}
and three values that are equal to
\frac{-1}{3}
, is such that it holds
\mathbb{P}(X_{P_{1}}\geq 0)>\mathbb{P}(X_{P_{3k-2}}\geq 0)
, as can be easily verified, and so the above-mentioned population
P_{1}
is not minimal in this case.
The following statement is a refinement of Conjecture
1
that covers all non-trivial instances of the parameters
n
and
k
.

Conjecture 2

(Aydinian, Blinovsky
[
3
]
,
2012
)
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer. Then
for any
k\in[n-1]
and any
P\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
it holds that

\mathbb{P}(X_{P}\geq 0)\,\geq\,\min_{i\in[n-1]}\mathbb{P}(H_{i}\geq ik/n)\,,

where
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
.

The hypergeometric random variable
H_{i}
in Conjecture
2
corresponds to the sum of a sampling without replacement of size
k
from the population

P_{i}=\left\{\frac{1}{i},\ldots,\frac{1}{i},\frac{-1}{n-i},\ldots,\frac{-1}{n-i}\right\}\,,

(1)

consisting of
i\in[n-1]
values that are equal to
\frac{1}{i}
and
n-i
values that are equal to
\frac{-1}{n-i}
.
In this case, if we denote by
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
the number of nonnegative elements in a sample of size
k
without replacement from
P_{i}
, we have

X_{P_{i}}=\frac{1}{i}\cdot H_{i}-\frac{1}{n-i}\cdot(k-H_{i})=\frac{n}{i(n-i)}\cdot\left(H_{i}-\frac{ik}{n}\right)\,,

and so
\mathbb{P}(X_{P_{i}}\geq 0)=\mathbb{P}(H_{i}\geq ik/n)
.
In other words, provided the Aydinian-Blinovsky conjecture holds true, the populations
P_{i},i\in[n-1]
, defined in (
1
), appear as extreme instances of the MMS conjecture.
Note that Conjecture
2
, if true, reduces the problem of obtaining a sharp lower bound on
\mathbb{P}(X_{P}\geq 0)
to the one of minimizing the “tails”
\mathbb{P}(H_{i}\geq ik/n)
over
i\in[n-1]
. Let us note that the latter optimization problem can be solved under the assumption
n\geq 8k
(see
[
1
]
) but, to the best of our knowledge, its solution is not known for smaller values of
n
.

In this article we investigate the above-mentioned question from an entropic point of view.
Fix positive integers
n\geq 2
and
k<n
, and let
P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
.
If
X_{P}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}}x_{i}
, where
\mathbb{I}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
is chosen uniformly at random,
what is a sharp lower bound on the Shannon entropy of
X_{P}
?

To make the problem interesting, we have to assume that the population

P\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
is non-trivial, i.e., not all elements
x_{i}
are equal to zero. Under this assumption, we deduce the following entropic analogue of the MMS conjecture.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer. Then
for any
k\in[n-1]
and any
P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
such that
\sum_{i\in[n]}|x_{i}|>0
it holds that

\mathbf{H}(X_{P})\,\geq\,\mathbf{H}(\text{Ber}(k/n))\,,

where
\text{Ber}(k/n)
is a Bernoulli random variable of mean
k/n
.
The inequality becomes an equality when
P=\{1,\frac{-1}{n-1},\ldots,\frac{-1}{n-1}\}
.

The proof of Theorem
1
relies on the following entropic analogue of the Aydinian-Blinovsky conjecture.

Theorem 2
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer. Then
for any
k\in[n-1]
and any
P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
such that
\sum_{i\in[n]}|x_{i}|>0
it holds that

\mathbf{H}(X_{P})\,\geq\,\min_{i\in[n-1]}\,\mathbf{H}(H_{i})\,,

where
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
.

In other words, the populations
P_{i},i\in[n-1]
, defined in (
1
), are extreme instances of the “entropified MMS conjecture”.
We provide two proofs of Theorem
2
.
The first proof combines ideas from Sperner theory and the theory of majorization, and provides more than desired.
Let us remark that an interplay between Sperner theory and the theory of majorization has been reported in
[
7
]
, where entropy inequalities are derived for sums of independent integer-valued
random variables. Our setting is different, as we are dealing with sums of dependent random variables.

A bit more precisely, our approach is to consider the family
\binom{[n]}{k}
as a ranked poset, which we refer to as the
sign-split poset
, having the Strong Sperner Property.
It turns out (see Lemma
1
below) that each value attained by the random variable
X_{P}
corresponds to an antichain in this sign-split poset, whose Strong Sperner Property (Lemma
2
) readily implies that the probability mass function,
p_{X_{P}}
, of
X_{P}
is majorized by the probability mass function,
p_{H_{i}}
, of a hypergeometric random variable
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
, for some
i\in[n-1]
(Lemma
3
).
This guarantees that, for any Schur-concave function
f
, it holds

f(p_{X_{P}})\geq f(p_{H_{i}})
. In particular, the latter holds true for the Shannon entropy and provides the first proof of Theorem
2
.
As a byproduct of the aforementioned majorization between the probability mass functions, we deduce the following Littlewood-Offord-type result for
X_{P}
.

Theorem 3
.

For any
k<n
and any
P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
such that
\sum_{i\in[n]}|x_{i}|>0
it holds that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}(X_{P}=x)\,\leq\,\max_{i\in[n-1]}\,\mathbb{P}(H_{i}=m_{i})\,,

where
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
and
m_{i}
is a mode of
H_{i}
.

We also provide a second proof of Theorem
2
, which is of a more elementary nature since it avoids completely Sperner theory and Schur-concavity. It exploits the appealing properties of the Shannon entropy, and draws inspiration from Lubell’s well-known proof of the LYM inequality; so it may be of independent interest.

Finally, to prove Theorem
1
, observe that Theorem
2
reduces the problem of minimizing
\mathbf{H}(X_{P})
to the one of minimizing the entropies
\mathbf{H}(H_{i})
over
i\in[n-1]
and, unlike the setting of the MMS conjecture, we are able to solve the latter optimization problem (Lemma
4
)
using a coupling argument and the appealing properties of the hypergeometric law.

Organisation

In Section
2.1

2.2

2
The sign-split poset

2.1
Preliminaries from Sperner theory

As already mentioned,
our main results employ ideas from Sperner theory of partial ordered sets (posets), which we briefly discuss here and refer the reader to
[
5
]
for an excellent textbook on the topic.

Recall that a
finite poset
is a pair
Q=(X,\leq)
, where
X
is a finite set equipped with a partial order relation
\leq
which is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive. We write
p\leq q
for two comparable elements
p,q
of the poset, and
p<q
when
p\leq q
and
p\neq q
.
An element
q\in X
is said to
cover
the elements
p\in X
, and is denoted
p\lessdot q
, if

p<q
and
p<q^{\prime}\leq q
implies
q=q^{\prime}
.
An element
p\in X
is called
maximal
if
p\leq q
implies
q=p
.
Similarly, an element
p\in X
is called
minimal
if
q\leq p
implies
q=p
.

A
chain
in a poset
Q
is a subset of pairwise comparable elements, i.e., a set
C\subseteq X
of the form

C=(p_{1}<p_{2}<\cdots<p_{m})
, for some integer
m\geq 1
.
The integer
m
is the
length
of the chain.
A chain
C
in a poset
P
is called
saturated
if it has the form
C=(p_{1}\lessdot p_{2}\lessdot\cdots\lessdot p_{m})
.

Given a positive integer
\ell\geq 1
, an
\ell
-
Sperner family
in a poset
Q
is a subset
S\subseteq X
such that
|S\cap C|\leq\ell
, for all chains
C
of
Q
. A
1
-Sperner family is referred to as an
antichain
.
In other words, an
\ell
-
Sperner family
is a subset of
X
which does not contain a chain of length
\ell+1
.

A chain
C
in a poset
Q=(X,\leq)
is
maximal
if there is no chain
C^{\prime}
in
Q
, which is different from
C
, such that
C\subseteq C^{\prime}
.
If every maximal chain of the poset has the same length, then the poset is called
graded
. In this case we can define a unique
rank function

r:X\to\{0,1,\ldots\}
from
X
to the natural numbers which satisfies

r(q)=r(p)+1
whenever
p\lessdot q
, and is such that every minimal element has
the same
rank and every maximal element has
the same
rank. The
rank
of an element
p\in X
is the value
r(p)
, and the
rank of the poset
is defined as
r(Q)=\max\{r(p):p\in X\}
. The
minimum rank
of
Q
is the integer

\mu(Q)=\min\{r(p):p\in X\}
. A
ranked
poset is a poset in which a rank function can be defined.

The
i
-
th level set
of a ranked poset
Q=(X,\leq)
is the set

N_{i}(Q)=\{p\in X:r(p)=i\}
, for integer
i\geq 0
.
Note that, by definition, each element
p\in X
belongs to the level set
N_{r(p)}(Q)
.
The
i
-
th Whitney number
is the size of the
i
-th level set, denoted
W_{i}(Q)
. Hence
W_{i}(Q)=|N_{i}(Q)|
.

A ranked poset
Q=(X,\leq)
is said to have the

\ell
-
Sperner property
if the maximum size of a

\ell
-Sperner family in
Q
equals the sum of the
\ell
largest Whitney numbers
of
Q
. The ranked poset
Q
has the
Strong Sperner property
if it has the
\ell
-Sperner Property for all integers
\ell\geq 1
.

Given an element
p
of a poset
Q=(X,\leq)
, we define the
upper shadow
of
p
to be the set

\nabla(p)=\{q\in X:p\lessdot q\}\,.

The
upper shadow
of a set
A\subseteq X
is defined as
\nabla(A)=\{q\in X:p\lessdot q,\text{ for some }p\in A\}
.

A ranked poset
Q=(X,\leq)
, with rank function
r
, is said to have the

normalized matching property (NMP)
if it satisfies

\frac{|A|}{W_{i}(Q)}\leq\frac{|\nabla(A)|}{W_{i+1}(Q)}\,,\,\text{ for all }A\subseteq N_{i}\,\text{ and all }\,i\in\{\mu(Q),\ldots,r(Q)-1\}\,.

Briefly, a ranked poset with the NMP property is referred to as a
normal poset
. The following well-known result will be employed in the proof of Theorem
2
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
Q=(X,\leq)
be a normal poset. Then
Q
has the Strong Sperner Property.

Proof.

See
[
5
, Theorem 4.5.1 and Corollary 4.5.3]
.
∎

2.2
Normality of the sign-split poset

In this section we associate a ranked poset to each non-trivial population from

\mathcal{N}_{n}
. More precisely, let
n\geq 2
and
k\in[n-1]
be fixed integers, and consider a multiset
P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
such that
\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i\in[n]}|x_{i}|>0
.
Consider the following partition of the index set:

A=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\geq 0\}\quad\text{ and }\quad B=\{i\in[n]:x_{i}<0\}\,.

Note that every element
F\in\binom{[n]}{k}
can be partitioned as

F=F_{A}\cup F_{B}
, where
F_{A}=F\cap A
and
F_{B}=F\cap B
.
We define a partial order on the collection
\binom{[n]}{k}
via the relation

F\leq G\quad\iff\quad F_{A}\subseteq G_{A}\,\text{ and }\,G_{B}\subseteq F_{B}\,.

Then
Q=(\binom{[n]}{k},\leq)
is a poset, which will be referred to as the
sign-split poset
corresponding to
P
.
We turn
Q
into a ranked poset by defining the rank of an element
F\in\binom{[n]}{k}
to be equal to

r(F)=|F_{A}|\,.

The
i
-th level of
Q
is the set
N_{i}:=\{F\in\binom{[n]}{k}:|F_{A}|=i\}
, and therefore the
i
-th Whitney number of the sign-split poset is equal to

W_{i}:=\binom{|A|}{i}\binom{n-|A|}{k-i}\,,\,\text{ for }\,i\in\big\{\max\{0,k-(n-|A|)\},\ldots,\min\{k,|A|\}-1\,\big\}\,.

The proofs of our main results are based on the following observations.

Lemma 1
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer and fix
k\in[n-1]
.
Let also
P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be such that
\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i\in[n]}|x_{i}|>0
.
Then, for any real number
x
, the collection

\mathcal{F}_{x}=\left\{F\in\binom{[n]}{k}\,:\,\sum_{i\in F}x_{i}=x\right\}

is an antichain of the sign-split poset corresponding to
P
.

Proof.

Suppose that the conclusion is not true. Then there exist
F,G\in\mathcal{F}_{x}
such that
F<G
.
Hence, using the fact that
x_{i}<0
, for
i\in B
, we deduce that

0=\sum_{i\in G}x_{i}-\sum_{i\in F}x_{i}=\sum_{i\in G_{A}\setminus F_{A}}-\sum_{i\in G_{B}\setminus F_{B}}x_{i}\,>\,0\,,

which is a contradiction.
∎

The following result is the main ingredient of our approach.

Lemma 2
.

The sign-split poset
Q=(\binom{[n]}{k},\leq)
has the Strong Sperner Property.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
4
that it is enough to show that
Q
is normal. To this end, we have to show that
for all
i\in\{\max\{0,k-(n-|A|)\},\ldots,\min\{k,|A|\}-1\}
and all
\mathcal{F}\subseteq N_{i}
it holds

\frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{W_{i}}\leq\frac{|\nabla(\mathcal{F})|}{W_{i+1}}\,.

(2)

Let
i
and
\mathcal{F}
be as above. We count pairs

(F,G)
such that
F\in\mathcal{F}
,
G\in\nabla(\mathcal{F})
and
F\lessdot G
.
For every
F\in\mathcal{F}
there are exactly
(|A|-i)\cdot(k-i)
sets
G\in\nabla(\mathcal{A})
such that
F\lessdot G
; those obtained by replacing one element of
F_{B}
with one element from
A\setminus F_{A}
.
Hence the total number of pairs
(F,G)
is equal to

|\mathcal{F}|\cdot(|A|-i)\cdot(k-i)
.

On the other hand, for every
G\in\nabla(\mathcal{F})
there are at most
(i+1)\cdot(n-|A|-k+i+1)
elements
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F\lessdot G
; those obtained by replacing an element of
G_{A}
with an element from
B\setminus G_{B}
.
Hence it holds

|\mathcal{F}|\cdot(|A|-i)\cdot(k-i)\leq|\nabla(\mathcal{F})|\cdot(i+1)\cdot(n-|A|-k+i+1)\,.

The latter inequality and the fact that
W_{i}=\binom{|A|}{i}\binom{n-|A|}{k-i}
imply that the desired inequality (
2
) holds true, and the result follows.
∎

3
Proofs of the main results

Throughout this section, we let the integers
n\geq 2
and
k\in[n-1]
be fixed, and we also fix a population

P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
which satisfies

\sum_{i\in[n]}|x_{i}|>0
.

Choose
\mathbb{I}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
uniformly at random and set
X_{P}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{I}}x_{i}
.
Let
\mathcal{X}_{P}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\}
be the set of real numbers for which it holds
\mathbb{P}(X_{P}=s_{j})>0
, for
j\in[m]
. Note that
m\geq 2
.
For
j\in[m]
, let

\mathcal{F}_{j}=\left\{F\in\binom{[n]}{k}\,:\,\sum_{l\in F}x_{l}=s_{j}\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad f_{j}=|\mathcal{F}_{j}|\,.

Then it holds

\mathbb{P}(X_{P}=s_{j})=\frac{f_{j}}{\binom{n}{k}}\,,\,\text{ for }\,j\in[m]\,.

Let
p_{X_{P}}=(f_{1}/\binom{n}{k},\ldots,f_{m}/\binom{n}{k})
be the probability mass function of
X_{P}
.
For
i\in[n-1]
, let
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
and
let
p_{H_{i}}
be its probability mass function.
In the following lemma we show that the probability mass function of
X_{P}
is majorized by the probability mass function of a hypergeometric random variable.

Lemma 3
.

There exists
i\in[n-1]
such that
p_{X_{P}}\prec p_{H_{i}}
.

Proof.

Let
Q=(\binom{[n]}{k},\leq)
be the sign-split poset corresponding to
P
.
Recall that in the definition of the sign-split we partition the indices of the elements in the population into the sets

A=\{j\in[n]:x_{j}\geq 0\}\quad\text{ and }\quad B=\{j\in[n]:x_{j}<0\}\,.

Set
i=|A|
, and note that
i\in[n-1]
.
Let

a=\max\{0,k-(n-i)\}
and
b=\min\{k,i\}
, and observe that the non-empty levels of
Q
are
N_{a},\ldots,N_{b}
, where
N_{j}=\{F\in\binom{[n]}{k}:|F\cap A|=j\}
.
Then the Whitney numbers of
Q

are equal to

W_{j}=|N_{j}|=\binom{i}{j}\binom{n-i}{k-j}\,,\,\text{ for }\,j\in\{a,\ldots,b\}\,.

Set
d=b-a+1
, and let
W_{(1)}\geq\cdots\geq W_{(d)}
be the Whitney numbers of
Q
in decreasing order.
Since every maximal saturated chain of

Q
has one element in each level, and the sum
\sum_{r\in F}x_{r}
strictly increases along chains, it follows that
the random variable
X_{P}
takes at least
d
distinct values. Thus
m\geq d
. We extend the
sequence
W_{(1)},\ldots,W_{(d)}
by setting
W_{(l)}=0
for
d<l\leq m
. Let
\mathbf{w}_{i}
denote the vector
(W_{(1)},\ldots,W_{(m)})
.

Now let
f_{(1)}\geq\cdots\geq f_{(m)}
be the coordinates of the vector

\mathbf{v}_{P}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
in decreasing order.
Note that it holds
\sum_{j\in[m]}f_{(j)}=\sum_{j\in[m]}W_{(j)}=\binom{n}{k}
.
We now claim that, for all

\ell\in[m-1]
, it holds

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}f_{(j)}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}W_{(j)}\,.

To see the claim, note that Lemma
1
implies that each
\mathcal{F}_{j}
is an antichain of the sign-split poset. Fix
\ell\in[m-1]
,
and assume that the integers
f_{(1)}\geq\ldots\geq f_{(\ell)}
are the cardinalities of the families
\mathcal{F}_{s_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{s_{\ell}}\subseteq\binom{[n]}{k}
, respectively.
Then the collection
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{F}_{s_{1}}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{F}_{s_{\ell}}
is a subset of the sign-split poset which does not contain a chain of size
\ell+1
.
Since the sign-split poset has the Strong Sperner property, by Lemma
2
, the claim follows.

In particular, we conclude that the vector

\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{P}
is majorized by the vector
\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\cdot\mathbf{w}_{i}
.
The result follows upon observing the the former vector is the probability mass function of
X_{P}
, while the latter is the probability mass function of
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
.
∎

Note that Lemma
3
immediately implies Theorem
3
. Moreover, since the Shannon entropy is a Schur-concave function of the probability mass function, Theorem
2
follows immediately from Lemma
3
,
and we are thus left with proving Theorem
1
.
The following lemma, combined with Theorem
2
, completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
k\in[n-1]
. Then for all
i\in[n-1]
it holds

\mathbf{H}(H_{i})\geq\mathbf{H}(\text{Ber}(k/n))\,,

where
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
and

\text{Ber}(k/n)
is a Bernoulli random variable of mean
k/n
.

Proof.

The result is clearly true when
n=2
, so we may assume that
n\geq 3
.
Note that the probability mass function of
H_{1}
is equal to
(\frac{k}{n},1-\frac{k}{n})
, and therefore it holds

\mathbf{H}(H_{1})=\mathbf{H}(\text{Ber}(k/n))
.
Note also that for each
i\in[n-1]
it holds

k-H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,n-i,k)
. Hence
k-H_{i}\sim H_{n-i}
and so we have
\mathbf{H}(H_{i})=\mathbf{H}(H_{n-i})
.
In particular, we may assume that
i\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, and it is therefore enough to show that

\mathbf{H}(H_{i+1})\geq\mathbf{H}(H_{i})\,,\,\text{ for }\,i\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\,.

(3)

We prove (
3
) via a coupling argument.
More concretely, let
\mathbb{I}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
be chosen uniformly at random and, for each
i\in[n-1]
, consider the random variable

Z_{i}=|\mathbb{I}\cap[i]|\,.

Note that
H_{i}\sim Z_{i}
, for all
i\in[n-1]
, and therefore it is enough to show that

\mathbf{H}(Z_{i+1})\geq\mathbf{H}(Z_{i})\,,\,\text{ for }\,i\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\,.

(4)

Observe that it holds

Z_{i+1}=Z_{i}+\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}\,,

where
\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}
denotes the indicator of the event
\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}
.
In other words, if we know the pair
(Z_{i},\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}})
then we can recover
Z_{i+1}
, and if we know the pair

(Z_{i+1},\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}})
then we can recover
Z_{i}
.
This implies that

\mathbf{H}(Z_{i},\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}})=\mathbf{H}(Z_{i+1},\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}})

which, using the chain rule, can be equivalently written as

\mathbf{H}(Z_{i})+\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}\mid Z_{i})=\mathbf{H}(Z_{i+1})+\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}\mid Z_{i+1})\,.

Hence, in order to prove (
4
), it is enough to show that

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}\mid Z_{i})\geq\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}\mid Z_{i+1})\,,\,\text{ for }\,i\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\,.

(5)

Let
i\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1
be a positive integer. Note that

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}=1\mid Z_{i}=j)=\frac{\binom{i}{j}\binom{n-i-1}{k-j-1}/\binom{n}{k}}{\binom{i}{j}\binom{n-i}{k-j}/\binom{n}{k}}=\frac{k-j}{n-i}\,.

Hence, if
h(\cdot)
denotes the binary entropy function, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}\mid Z_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}\mid Z_{i}=j)\cdot\mathbb{P}(H_{i}=j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}h\left(\frac{k-j}{n-i}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}=j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(h\left(\frac{k-Z_{i}}{n-i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(h\left(\frac{Z_{n-i}}{n-i}\right)\right)\,,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
k-Z_{i}\sim k-H_{i}\sim H_{n-i}\sim Z_{n-i}
.
Similarly, we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}=1\mid Z_{i+1}=\ell)=\frac{\binom{i}{\ell-1}\binom{n-i-1}{k-\ell}/\binom{n}{k}}{\binom{i+1}{\ell}\binom{n-i-1}{k-\ell}/\binom{n}{k}}=\frac{\ell}{i+1}\,,

and therefore we may write

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}\mid Z_{i+1})=\mathbb{E}\left(h\left(\frac{Z_{i+1}}{i+1}\right)\right)\,.

Summarizing the above, it follows from (
5
) that it is enough to show that

\mathbb{E}\left(h\left(\frac{Z_{n-i}}{n-i}\right)\right)\geq\mathbb{E}\left(h\left(\frac{Z_{i+1}}{i+1}\right)\right)\,,\,\text{ for }\,i\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1\,.

(6)

We prove a bit more. Namely, we show that the sequence

E_{m}:=\mathbb{E}\left(h\left(\frac{Z_{m}}{m}\right)\right)\,,\text{ for }\,m=1,\ldots,n-1

is non-decreasing. The desired inequality (
6
) then follows upon observing that the assumption
i\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1
implies that
n-i\geq i+1
. To this end, let
m\in[n-2]
and note that, since
Z_{m+1}=Z_{m}+\mathbf{1}_{\{m+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}
, we may write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{Z_{m}}{m}\mid Z_{m+1}=\ell\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{Z_{m+1}-\mathbf{1}_{\{m+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}}{m}\mid Z_{m+1}=\ell\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{m}-\frac{1}{m}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{1}_{\{m+1\in\mathbb{I}\}}=1\mid Z_{m+1}=\ell)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{m}-\frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{\ell}{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{m+1}\,.

Since the binary entropy function is concave, the conditional Jensen inequality yields

\mathbb{E}\left(h\left(\frac{Z_{m}}{m}\right)\mid Z_{m+1}=\ell\right)\,\leq\,h\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{Z_{m}}{m}\mid Z_{m+1}=\ell\right)\right)=h\left(\frac{\ell}{m+1}\right)\,.

In particular, this implies that

E_{m}=\mathbb{E}\left(h\left(\frac{Z_{m}}{m}\right)\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(h\left(\frac{Z_{m}}{m}\right)\mid Z_{m+1}\right)\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(h\left(\frac{Z_{m+1}}{m+1}\right)\right)=E_{m+1}\,,

as desired.
∎

4
An elementary proof of Theorem
2

In this section we provide a proof of Theorem
2
that avoids Sperner theory and majorization. The proof draws inspiration from Lubell’s proof for the LYM inequality.
Briefly, the idea is to show that drawing from the random variable
X_{P}

is equivalent to first choosing a maximal chain of the sign-split poset uniformly at random, then choosing a
k
-set of the selected chain with probability proportional to its rank and finally adding the elements
of this
k
-set.
It turns out that the function that maps the rank of the selected
k
-set to the sum of its elements is injective, and allows to bypass the use of Schur-convexity.
The Sperner theory is bypassed upon observing that a uniformly chosen maximal chain in the sign-split poset is completely determined by two random permutations of the sign-split sets.

Second proof of Theorem
2
.

Consider a partition the indices of the elements in the population into non-negative and negative elements:

A=\{j\in[n]:x_{j}\geq 0\}\quad\text{ and }\quad B=\{j\in[n]:x_{j}<0\}\,.

Set
i=|A|
, and note that
i\in[n-1]
.
Now choose a random permutation of the elements in
A
, say
a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}
, and, independently, a random permutation of the elements in
B
, say
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-i}
.
For every
j\in\{\max\{0,k-(n-i)\},\dots,\min\{k,i\}\}
, consider the (random) set
C_{j}=A_{j}\cup B_{k-j}
, where

A_{j}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{j}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad B_{k-j}=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k-j}\}\,.

Here, we set
A_{0}=B_{0}=\emptyset
.
Now consider the (random) collection
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{j}\}_{j=\max\{0,k-(n-i)\}}^{\min\{k,i\}}
.
Observe that each set
C_{j}
is uniformly distributed on the collection

\mathcal{B}_{j}
consisting of all
F\in\binom{[n]}{k}
for which

|F\cap A|=j
.

We claim that the map
f:\{\max\{0,k-(n-i)\},\dots,\min\{k,i\}\}\to\mathbb{R}
with

f(j)=\sum_{l\in C_{j}}x_{l}
is strictly increasing. Indeed, since
C_{j+1}=(C_{j}\setminus\{b_{k-j}\})\cup\{a_{j+1}\}
, it holds that

f(j+1)-f(j)=\sum_{l\in C_{j+1}}x_{l}-\sum_{l\in C_{j}}x_{l}=x_{a_{j+1}}-x_{b_{k-j}}>0\,.

Now let
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
be independent of the collection
\mathcal{C}
, and note that for a given
F\in\mathcal{B}_{j}
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(C_{H_{i}}=F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(C_{H_{i}}=F\mid H_{i}=j)\cdot\mathbb{P}(H_{i}=j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(C_{j}=F)\cdot\mathbb{P}(H_{i}=j)=\frac{1}{\binom{i}{j}\binom{n-i}{k-j}}\cdot\frac{\binom{i}{j}\binom{n-i}{k-j}}{\binom{n}{k}}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\,.

This implies that
C_{H_{i}}
is uniformly distributed on
\binom{[n]}{k}
, and therefore we conclude that

X_{P}\,\sim\,f(H_{i})=\sum_{l\in C_{H_{i}}}x_{l}\,.

Since
f
is injective and
H_{i}
is independent of
\mathcal{C}
, we deduce that

\mathbf{H}(f(H_{i})\mid\mathcal{C})=\mathbf{H}(H_{i}\mid\mathcal{C})=\mathbf{H}(H_{i})\,.

Hence, since conditioning does not increase entropy, it holds that

\mathbf{H}(X_{P})=\mathbf{H}(f(H_{i}))\geq\mathbf{H}(f(H_{i})\mid\mathcal{C})=\mathbf{H}(H_{i})\,,

and the result follows.
∎

5
Concluding remarks

In this article we presented an “entropified version” of the MMS conjecture. Our approach is based on Lemma
3
, which guarantees that the probability mass function of
X_{P}
is majorized by the probability mass function of a hypergeometric random variable
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
. The Schur-concavity of the Shannon entropy then implies that an “entropified version” of the Aydinian-Blinovsky holds true,
which reduces the problem to the one of minimizing
the Shannon entropy of the extremal random variables
H_{i}
, for
i\in[n-1]
.

Let us remark that our approach may be employed for any Schur-concave function of the probability mass function. For instance, the
Rényi entropy
of a random variable
X
, taking values in the set

\mathcal{S}_{X}
and having probability mass function
p_{X}
, is defined as

\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}(X)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\cdot\log\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}_{X}}(p_{X}(x))^{\alpha}\right)\,,\,\text{ for }\,\alpha\in(0,\infty)\,.

It is a well knwon fact that the Rényi entropy is a Schur-concave function of the probability mass function, and therefore Lemma
3
yields the following analogue of the Aydinian-Blinovsky conjecture in this setting.

Corollary 5
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer. Then
for any
k\in[n-1]
and any
P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{N}_{n}
such that
\sum_{i\in[n]}|x_{i}|>0
it holds that

\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}(X_{P})\,\geq\,\min_{i\in[n-1]}\,\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}(H_{i})\,,

where
H_{i}\sim\text{Hyp}(n,i,k)
.

We are unable to solve the optimization problem
\min_{i\in[n-1]}\,\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}(H_{i})
, but our numerical experiments suggest that
\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}(H_{1})
is minimal.
