Quantum Speedup for Network Coordination via Fourier Sparsity
Vinayak Dixit

Abstract
Network coordination—synchronising traffic signals, scheduling trains, assigning communication slots—requires minimising pairwise costs across coupled systems. These problems are NP-hard yet share a common Fourier-sparse structure exploitable by quantum algorithms. We introduce the Fourier Network Coordination problem (Fourier-NC), unifying eight application domains. For abelian and dihedral groups, classical sparse Fourier transforms match quantum in the same oracle model, limiting the advantage to at most polynomial. The genuine separation emerges for the symmetric group S k S_{k} : a conditional super-exponential speedup of k ! → poly ⁡ ( k ) k!\to\mathrm{poly}(k) for class-function costs with non-trivial minimisers. When the minimising conjugacy class is structurally determined, the problem lies in NP ∩ \,\cap\, BQP and is conditionally outside P (Corollary 6.5 ), placing it in the intermediate complexity regime alongside integer factorisation and graph isomorphism. We formalise the abelian index α ( G ) = [ G : A max ] \alpha(G)=[G:A_{\max}] as the structural invariant governing the quantum–classical gap and identify a three-regime complexity trichotomy: abelian ( α = 1 \alpha=1 , classical sFFT suffices), nearly abelian ( α = d max \alpha=d_{\max} , polynomial advantage), and strongly non-abelian ( α ≫ d max \alpha\gg d_{\max} , super-exponential advantage).

Introduction

Many real-world systems require coordinating timing or ordering across a network: synchronising traffic lights so vehicles hit green waves, scheduling trains to minimise passenger waiting, assigning communication slots to avoid interference, or sequencing signal phases to maintain coordinated vehicle flow. These problems share a common mathematical structure—each pair of neighbours pays a cost that depends only on their
relative offset or ordering
—and that structure is precisely what a quantum computer can exploit. The general problem is NP-hard (we prove this via reduction from MAX-CUT, Theorem
5.1
), but its Fourier-sparse structure enables exponential quantum speedup for an important subclass—specifically, frustration-free instances whose cost functions have polynomially bounded Fourier dynamic range (a condition verified for all engineering domains considered here; see Propositions
D.1
–
D.2
).

1.1
Intuitive overview

Consider four traffic lights along a corridor, each cycling through a 60-second period. Each must choose a start-time offset; if adjacent lights differ by 15 seconds, vehicles hit a “green wave.” With
60^{100}
possible assignments for a 100-intersection network, brute-force search is hopeless—but the delay function between each pair of lights is
smooth and periodic
, describable by two or three Fourier components. The Quantum Fourier Transform identifies these components in one sweep, collapsing the search from exponential to
O(mr)
modes. For cyclic offsets (
\mathbb{Z}_{C}
), classical sparse Fourier transforms match this advantage; the genuine quantum speedup emerges for
orderings
over the symmetric group
S_{k}
, where
k!
possibilities and no known classical sFFT yield a conditional
k!\to\mathrm{poly}(k)
separation. The algorithm requires frustration-free networks (Definition
3.3
); frustrated instances receive bounded approximations (Proposition
J.1
).

3
The Fourier-NC Framework

The central idea is simple: each node in a network must choose a setting (a time slot, a phase angle, a channel) from a finite set of
C
options arranged in a cycle. Neighbouring nodes pay a cost that depends only on the
difference
between their settings. The question is: what assignment minimises the total cost?

Notation.

Let
G=(V,\mathcal{A})
denote a network with
n=|V|
nodes and
m=|\mathcal{A}|
edges.
\mathbb{Z}_{C}=\mathbb{Z}/C\mathbb{Z}
is the cyclic group of order
C
;
D_{C}
the dihedral group of order
2C
;
S_{k}
the symmetric group on
k
elements.
\hat{f}(k)
denotes the Fourier coefficient of
f
at frequency
k
, and
r
is the Fourier sparsity (number of non-zero coefficients). For
S_{k}
,
\lambda\vdash k
indexes irreducible representations with dimension
d_{\lambda}
;
p(k)
is the number of partitions of
k
;
\chi^{\lambda}
is the character of irrep
\lambda
.

For each edge
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
, a cost function
f_{ij}\colon\mathbb{Z}_{C}\to\mathbb{R}
maps offset differences to costs. The global cost is
H(\mu)=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}f_{ij}\!\bigl((\mu_{i}-\mu_{j})\bmod C\bigr)
. The
Fourier-NC problem asks
:
\operatorname*{minimise}H(\mu)
over
\mathbb{Z}_{C}^{n}
.

Decision version (Fourier-NC Decision).
Given a threshold
\tau
, decide whether there exists
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{C}^{n}
with
H(\mu)\leq\tau
. Since a candidate
\mu
can be verified in polynomial time, Fourier-NC Decision is in NP; combined with Theorem
5.1
, it is NP-complete.

The key observation is that in all eight application domains (Sec.
2
), the cost functions are
smooth and periodic
, so their Fourier transforms are
sparse
—only a handful of frequency components matter. This is what makes the problem tractable for a quantum computer.

Definition 3.1

(
r
-Sparse Delay Function)
.

A cost function
f_{ij}
is
r
-Fourier-sparse if its DFT has at most
r
non-zero coefficients, i.e.

\hat{f}_{ij}(k)=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{x=0}^{C-1}f_{ij}(x)\cdot\omega_{C}^{-kx},

(1)

where
\omega_{C}=e^{2\pi i/C}
, and
|\{k:\hat{f}_{ij}(k)\neq 0\}|\leq r
.

Three conditions characterise Fourier-NC applicability:
(C1)

Group domain
—variables in
G^{n}
(
G=\mathbb{Z}_{C},D_{C},S_{k}
);
(C2)

Pairwise relative structure
—cost
H=\sum f_{ij}(g_{i}^{-1}g_{j})
, so the transform factorises edge-by-edge;
(C3)

Fourier sparsity
—each
f_{ij}
has
r\ll|G|
non-zero coefficients. All eight domains in Sec.
2
satisfy (C1)–(C3); when any condition fails, only generic algorithms (Grover, QAOA) apply.

3.1
Fourier Factorization

Theorem 3.2

(Fourier Factorization)
.

If each
f_{ij}
is
r
-sparse, then the DFT of
H
over
\mathbb{Z}_{C}^{n}
has at most
O(mr)
non-zero coefficients.

Proof sketch (full proof in Appendix
C
).

By character orthogonality on
\mathbb{Z}_{C}
, the single-edge DFT
\hat{H}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
vanishes unless
k_{\ell}=0
for all
\ell\neq i,j
and
k_{j}\equiv-k_{i}\pmod{C}
, reducing to
\hat{H}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})=\hat{f}_{ij}(k_{i})
. Since each
f_{ij}
has at most
r
non-zero coefficients, each edge contributes at most
r
non-zero frequency vectors. Summing over
m
edges gives at most
mr
potentially non-zero modes.
∎

3.2
Frustration-freeness

Definition 3.3

(Frustration-free and frustrated graph)
.

For each edge, let
D^{*}_{ij}=\arg\min_{d}f_{ij}(d)
. The graph
G
is
frustration-free
if there exists a selection
d^{*}_{ij}\in D^{*}_{ij}
for every edge and an assignment
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}_{C}^{n}
with
\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}\equiv d^{*}_{ij}\pmod{C}
for every edge; equivalently, the
holonomy

h_{\gamma}=\sum_{(i,j)\in\gamma}d^{*}_{ij}\bmod C
vanishes for every cycle
\gamma
. Otherwise
G
is
frustrated
.

For frustration-free graphs the tree solver (Sec.
4
) is exact; for frustrated graphs the suboptimality gap is bounded by the cycle rank
\beta=m-n+1
(Proposition
J.1
, Appendix
J
). Bipartite graphs are always frustration-free (holonomy vanishes on even cycles).

4
The Quantum Algorithm

The algorithm encodes the cost function into quantum phases, then applies the QFT to extract the
O(mr)
dominant frequencies (Theorem
3.2
). We distinguish
query complexity
(oracle calls) from
gate complexity
(elementary gates); separation results use query complexity, while gate counts (Sec.
5.3
) differ by
O(\log^{2}C)
per query.

4.1
Algorithm Description

Algorithm 1: Fourier-NC Solver

Input:
Graph
G=(V,\mathcal{A})
, cycle length
C
, edge-level oracle access to cost functions
f_{ij}\colon\mathbb{Z}_{C}\to\mathbb{R}
(or equivalently, their Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{ij}(k)
, which can be computed classically in
O(C\log C)
per edge).

Output:
Optimal offset vector
\mu^{*}
.

Step 1.
Allocate
n
quantum registers, each of
\lceil\log_{2}C\rceil
qubits. Total:
n\cdot\lceil\log_{2}C\rceil
qubits.

Step 2.
Prepare uniform superposition:
|{+}\rangle^{\otimes n}=(1/\sqrt{C^{n}})\sum_{\mu}|\mu\rangle
.

Step 3.
Apply phase oracle:
U_{H}|\mu\rangle=e^{2\pi i\,H(\mu)/K}|\mu\rangle
, where
K=P\cdot m\cdot\max_{(i,j)}\|f_{ij}\|_{\infty}
with
P=\mathrm{poly}(n,m,r)
ensures all phases satisfy
H(\mu)/K\leq 1/P
, placing the oracle in the
linearisation regime
(see Fig.
2
a for the high-level circuit). In the edge-level implementation (Sec.
4.2
), each edge oracle
U_{ij}
contributes a phase
e^{2\pi i\,f_{ij}(\mu_{i}-\mu_{j})/K}
, so
U_{H}=\prod_{(i,j)}U_{ij}
.

Step 4.
Apply inverse QFT over
\mathbb{Z}_{C}^{n}
.

Step 5.
Measure to obtain frequency vector
\mathbf{k}^{*}
.

Step 6.
Classical post-processing: solve system of linear congruences over
\mathbb{Z}_{C}
to reconstruct
\mu^{*}
from Fourier modes.

O(r\log^{2}C)
.

4.2
Phase Oracle Construction

The phase oracle
U_{H}=\prod_{(i,j)}U_{ij}
factorises over edges. Each
U_{ij}
computes
d=(\mu_{i}-\mu_{j})\bmod C
via a quantum subtraction circuit (
O(\log^{2}C)
gates per edge), then applies
r
controlled-phase rotations using the Fourier synthesis
f_{ij}(d)=\sum_{k\in S_{ij}}\hat{f}_{ij}(k)\omega_{C}^{kd}
, with
|S_{ij}|\leq r
(
O(mr\log^{2}C)
gates total). The inverse QFT decomposes as
n
independent QFTs over
\mathbb{Z}_{C}
(
O(n\log^{2}C)
gates). Total:
O\!\bigl((mr+n)\cdot\log^{2}C\bigr)
gates per repetition; see Fig.
2
(b).

4.3
Correctness

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
p_{\min}=\min\{|\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}/\|\hat{H}\|^{2}:\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})\neq 0\}
denote the minimum non-zero Fourier weight. Algorithm 1 outputs the optimal offset with probability
\geq 1-\delta
using
T=O(P^{2}/p_{\min}\cdot\log(mr/\delta))
raw measurements, where
P=\mathrm{poly}(n,m,r)
is the linearisation parameter (Step 3). If additionally
p_{\min}\geq 1/(mr\cdot n)
(a condition that can be verified by classical preprocessing; see Remark below), the total gate complexity is
O\!\bigl(\mathrm{poly}(n,m,r,\log C,\log(1/\delta))\bigr)
.

Proof sketch (full proof in Appendix
B
).

The proof proceeds in three stages.
Stage 1 (Linearisation):
Setting
K=P\cdot m\cdot\max\|f_{ij}\|_{\infty}
with
P\gg 1
ensures
g(\mu)=e^{2\pi iH(\mu)/K}\approx 1+(2\pi i/K)H(\mu)
, so the DFT of
g
recovers
\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})
up to negligible error. Conditional on measuring
\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{0}
, the distribution is proportional to
|\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}
.
Stage 2 (Linear system):
Each measured non-zero mode yields a linear congruence
k_{i}(\mu^{*}_{i}-\mu^{*}_{j})\equiv\varphi_{ij}\pmod{C}
; edge-level oracle access resolves overlapping modes. Collecting congruences along a spanning tree determines all
n-1
offset differences via the Chinese Remainder Theorem.
Stage 3 (Repetition bound):
By a coupon-collector argument,
T=O(P^{2}/p_{\min}\cdot\log(mr/\delta))
raw measurements suffice to collect all
s\leq mr
non-zero modes. Under
p_{\min}\geq 1/(mr\cdot n)
, this remains
O(\mathrm{poly})
. Convergence is shown in Fig.
3
.
∎

Remark (on the
p_{\min}
condition).

The condition
p_{\min}\geq 1/(mr\cdot n)
is a
verifiable hypothesis
: a classical preprocessing step computes
p_{\min}
exactly from the
O(mr)
edge-level DFT coefficients in
O(mr\cdot C\log C)
time. The sufficient condition is that the per-edge Fourier dynamic range
\kappa=\max_{k}|\hat{f}_{ij}(k)|/\min_{k:\hat{f}_{ij}(k)\neq 0}|\hat{f}_{ij}(k)|
be polynomially bounded; we prove
p_{\min}\geq 1/\mathrm{poly}(n,m,r)
for cosine coupling (Proposition
D.1
; covers traffic NC, OPF, Kuramoto, clock sync) and piecewise-linear costs (Proposition
D.2
; covers railway PESP, TDMA/FAP, influence scheduling) in Appendix
D
. Numerical validation (Appendix
F
) confirms that
p_{\min}
exceeds the polynomial threshold by
5
–
29\times
across all tested topologies.

Oracle models.

An “oracle model” specifies what the algorithm is allowed to ask: in the
global
model, it evaluates the total cost
H(\mu)
for a complete assignment (like running a full traffic simulation); in the
edge-level
model, it queries individual link costs
f_{ij}(d)
(like measuring delay on a single road link). Whether edge-level access is practical depends on the domain: railway timetable penalties and power-flow sinusoids are given analytically, but traffic delay functions require costly per-link field experiments. For
S_{k}
, even edge-level access requires evaluating functions on a domain of size
k!
, so the quantum advantage persists regardless of the oracle model.

5.1
NP-Hardness

Theorem 5.1
.

The Fourier-NC optimisation problem (minimisation) is NP-hard, and the Fourier-NC Decision problem is NP-complete, even for
r=1
and
C=2
.

Proof sketch (full proof in Appendix
E
).

Reduce from MAX-CUT: set
C=2
and
f_{ij}(x)=\cos(\pi x)
, so
f_{ij}(0)=+1
(same side) and
f_{ij}(1)=-1
(different sides). Then
H(\mu)=m-2\cdot\mathrm{cut}(\mu)
, and minimising
H
is equivalent to MAX-CUT
[
27
]
. The cost
f_{ij}
has
r=1
, confirming hardness at minimal Fourier sparsity. NP-completeness of the decision version follows because a candidate
\mu
is verifiable in
O(m)
time. Fig.
4
illustrates the reduction.
∎

(Classical query lower bound)
.

Any classical algorithm in the global oracle model requires

returns the same value regardless of

(Fig.

Permutation Coordination over
S_{k}

The symmetric group
S_{k}
—permutations of
k
items—is where quantum
genuinely
wins. Problems over
S_{k}
arise when agents must agree on an
ordering
(ranking, scheduling, routing). Since
|S_{k}|=k!
but the largest abelian subgroup has only
{\sim}3^{k/3}
elements, no classical abelian decomposition can work, and the QFT provides a super-exponential speedup.

Intuitive example: fleet route assignment.

Consider
k
trucks and
k
delivery routes coordinated across
n
depots. Each depot assigns trucks to routes—a permutation
\sigma_{i}\in S_{k}
. Assuming a homogeneous fleet (identical capacity and type), trucks are interchangeable, so congestion between neighbouring depots depends only on the
cycle type
of
\sigma_{i}^{-1}\sigma_{j}
(how many routes stay, swap in pairs, rotate in triples), making the cost a class function. A composite cost penalising both priority violations (Kendall tau,
r=2
) and disruption (Hamming distance,
r=2
) has

k=15

6.1
The symmetric group
S_{k}

Definition 6.1

(Symmetric group)
.

Every permutation in
S_{k}
(
|S_{k}|=k!
) has a unique decomposition into disjoint cycles; the multiset of cycle lengths is the
cycle type
, a partition of
k
. Conjugacy classes of
S_{k}
are indexed by partitions of
k
, with
p(k)\sim e^{\pi\sqrt{2k/3}}
classes. The irreps of
S_{k}
are also indexed by partitions
\lambda\vdash k
; the maximum irrep dimension is
d_{\max}(S_{k})=\Theta(\sqrt{k!})
, super-exponential in
k
(see Appendix
H
for details).

6.2
Permutation Coordination problem

Definition 6.2

(Permutation Coordination)
.

Given a graph
G=(V,\mathcal{A})
with
|V|=n
agents and
|\mathcal{A}|=m
edges, each agent
i
is assigned a permutation
\sigma_{i}\in S_{k}
. Each edge
(i,j)
has a cost function
f_{ij}\colon S_{k}\to\mathbb{R}
that depends on the relative permutation
\sigma_{i}^{-1}\cdot\sigma_{j}
. The
Permutation Coordination
problem is:

\operatorname*{minimise}_{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}\in S_{k}}\;H(\sigma)\;=\;\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}f_{ij}\!\bigl(\sigma_{i}^{-1}\cdot\sigma_{j}\bigr).

A cost function
f\colon S_{k}\to\mathbb{R}
is
r
-Fourier-sparse
if at most
r
of the
p(k)
irreducible Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}(\lambda)
are non-zero.

This generalises Fourier-NC from cyclic (
\mathbb{Z}_{C}
) and dihedral (
D_{C}
) groups to the symmetric group
S_{k}
. The key structural difference is that Fourier coefficients are now
matrices
, not scalars.

6.3
Cost functions

Theorem
6.3
requires that edge cost functions be
class functions
—constant on conjugacy classes. The two principal building blocks are:
Kendall tau distance

d_{\mathrm{KT}}
, which counts pairwise inversions and has
r=2
(supported on trivial and standard irreps)
[
28
]
; and
Hamming distance

d_{H}(\sigma)=k-\mathrm{fix}(\sigma)
, also with
r=2
. A composite cost
f(\sigma)=\sum_{i}w_{i}g_{i}(\sigma)
of
\ell
such metrics has
r\leq 2\ell
. With
\ell\geq 2
and non-uniform weights, the minimising conjugacy class depends on the weight configuration and is generally not known a priori---placing composite-cost problems in the regime where quantum advantage is genuine.
1
1

1

For pure Kendall tau cost (
r=2
), the identity minimiser yields

in
O(1)

d_{\mathrm{KT}}(e)=0
and setting

6.4
Quantum algorithm

The quantum algorithm follows the same Fourier-recovery structure as the abelian case (Sec.

Theorem 6.3

(Quantum Permutation Coordination)
.

For the Permutation Coordination problem on

Proof sketch (full proof in Appendix

The proof parallels the abelian case (Theorem

6.5
Determined-minimiser instances and complexity classification

Definition 6.4

(Determined-Minimiser Permutation Coordination (DMPC))
.

A Permutation Coordination instance (Definition

) is a
determined-minimiser
instance if, for each edge
(i,j)
, the minimising conjugacy class of
f_{ij}
can be identified in
\mathrm{poly}(k)
time—for example, when the cost is a composite class-function metric whose weight configuration determines the optimal cycle type. The DMPC
search problem
asks for the optimal assignment
\sigma^{*}
; the DMPC
decision problem
asks whether
H(\sigma)\leq\tau
for a given threshold.

Corollary 6.5

(BQP membership for DMPC)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
6.3
, for a DMPC instance (Definition
6.4
), the full quantum algorithm (Fourier recovery
+
minimisation
+
spanning-tree propagation) runs in
O(m\cdot r\cdot\mathrm{poly}(k))
total time and the problem is in
BQP
. Under Assumption
6.6
, it is not in
P

k!\to\mathrm{poly}(k)
is genuine for the search problem.

Proof.

Fourier recovery:
O(m\cdot r\cdot k^{2}\log k)
queries, each specifiable in
O(k\log k)
bits and executable with
O(k^{2}\log k)
gates, so total
time
(not merely query count) is
O(m\cdot r\cdot\mathrm{poly}(k))
. When the minimising conjugacy class is known
a priori
, the
p(k)

O(n\cdot k)
. Total:
O(m\cdot r\cdot\mathrm{poly}(k))

decision
version (“does there exist
\sigma

\sigma
is verifiable in
O(mk\log k)

6.6
implies no classical algorithm can match this with polynomially many queries, hence the search problem is not in P.
∎

Remark (complexity classification of DMPC).

DMPC lies in NP
\,\cap\,
BQP and is conditionally outside P (under Assumption
6.6
). It is almost certainly not NP-hard, since NP-hard
\,\cap\,
BQP would imply the widely-disbelieved NP
\,\subseteq\,
BQP. This places DMPC in the intermediate complexity regime alongside integer factorisation and graph isomorphism. Whether DMPC
\,\in\,
co-NP remains open. A natural candidate for a co-NP witness—membership of the minimiser in a
\mathrm{poly}(r,k)

-sparse class function can require up to

distinct cycle-type parts regardless of

).

Remark (applicability).

Two situations qualify for DMPC: (i) the minimiser is uniquely determined by symmetry (e.g. the identity class for pure Kendall tau, though this case is classically trivial); (ii) domain knowledge constrains the search to
O(\mathrm{poly}(k))
candidate cycle types. Establishing that specific composite metrics admit polynomial-time minimiser identification is an open problem.

6.7
Structural classification and classical limits

We now unify the abelian–non-abelian landscape through a single structural invariant.

Definition 6.8

(Abelian index)
.

For a finite group
G
, the
abelian index
is
\alpha(G)=|G|/\max\{|A|:A\leq G,\;A\text{ abelian}\}
.

For the three groups studied:
\alpha(\mathbb{Z}_{C})=1
,
\alpha(D_{C})=2
, and
\alpha(S_{k})=k!/3^{k/3}

[
34
]
.

Proposition 6.9

(Fundamental inequality)
.

For any finite group
G
,
d_{\max}(G)\leq\alpha(G)
.

Proof.

Let
A\leq G
be abelian with
[G:A]=\alpha(G)
, and let
\rho\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
achieve
d_{\rho}=d_{\max}(G)
. Since
A
is abelian,
\mathrm{Res}^{G}_{A}\rho
decomposes into one-dimensional representations; by Frobenius reciprocity,
\rho
embeds in
\mathrm{Ind}^{G}_{A}\chi
for some
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(A)
, giving
d_{\max}(G)\leq[G:A]=\alpha(G)
.
∎

The abelian complexity trichotomy.

Proposition
6.9
yields three regimes.
Regime I
(
\alpha(G)=1
):
G
is abelian; classical sFFT
[
1
]
recovers all
mr
coefficients from
O(m\cdot r\cdot\mathrm{polylog}\,C)
edge-level queries, matching quantum.
Regime II
(
1<\alpha(G)=d_{\max}(G)
, bounded): classical coset decomposition gives polynomial cost; advantage is at most polynomial (includes
D_{C}
,
\alpha=2
).
Regime III
(
\alpha(G)\gg d_{\max}(G)\gg 1
): no efficient coset decomposition exists; quantum advantage is genuine.
S_{k}
sits firmly in Regime III.

Even in the global oracle model, classical
node-sweep
demixing extracts edge-level coefficients at cost
O(m\cdot C)
for abelian groups—polynomial, though independent of
r
. For
S_{k}
, demixing costs
O(m\cdot k!)
—exponential—while quantum runs in

.

The algorithm does not solve all Fourier-NC instances (which would imply NP

6.9
Limitations

The principal limitations are: (i) for
r
-sparse abelian instances, classical sFFT matches quantum; genuine advantage requires
S_{k}
; (ii) the
S_{k}
separation is conditional on Assumption
6.6
; (iii) polynomial runtime requires
p_{\min}\geq 1/\mathrm{poly}
, verified for standard cost functions but not adversarial instances; (iv) frustrated instances yield bounded approximation (Proposition
J.1
); (v) coherent QFT circuits of depth
O(k^{2}\log k)
may exceed NISQ capabilities; (vi) extending beyond class functions on
S_{k}
remains open.

7
Conclusion

We introduced the Fourier Network Coordination problem (Fourier-NC), unifying network coordination across 8 application domains through a shared Fourier-sparse pairwise structure. For abelian and dihedral groups, classical sFFT matches quantum for sparse instances; the genuine separation emerges for
S_{k}
: a conditional super-exponential speedup of
k!\to\mathrm{poly}(k)
for class-function costs with non-trivial minimisers, placing DMPC in NP
\,\cap\,
BQP and conditionally outside P (Corollary
6.5
).

The central structural insight is that the
abelian index

\alpha(G)=[G:A_{\max}]
governs the quantum–classical gap: when
\alpha(G)
is constant (
\mathbb{Z}_{C}
,
D_{C}
), classical sFFT suffices; when super-exponential (
S_{k}
), quantum coherence is irreplaceable. This establishes non-commutativity—not merely exponential state-space size—as the structural prerequisite for super-polynomial quantum advantage in network coordination.

Future work includes unconditional lower bounds for sparse Fourier recovery over
S_{k}
, implementation on fault-tolerant hardware, and extensions to other non-abelian groups (wreath products
\mathbb{Z}_{m}\wr S_{k}
, general linear groups
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
) along the abelian index frontier.

Appendix B
Full Proof of Theorem
4.1
(Abelian Correctness)

Proof.

The proof proceeds in three stages: coefficient recovery, linear system solution, and error analysis.

Stage 1 (Coefficient Recovery via Linearisation).
After applying
H^{\otimes n\cdot\log_{2}C}
and the phase oracle
U_{H}
, the quantum state is
|\psi\rangle=(1/\sqrt{C^{n}})\sum_{\mu}g(\mu)|\mu\rangle
where
g(\mu)=e^{2\pi i\,H(\mu)/K}
. Because the phase oracle encodes
g=e^{2\pi iH/K}
rather than
H
directly, the DFT of
g
is not the DFT of
H
—the exponential introduces all harmonics via the Jacobi–Anger expansion. To recover the Fourier coefficients of
H
, we operate in the
linearisation regime
: setting
K=P\cdot m\cdot\max\|f_{ij}\|_{\infty}
with
P=\mathrm{poly}(n,m,r)
ensures
|2\pi H(\mu)/K|\leq 2\pi/P\ll 1
for all
\mu
. Taylor expanding:
g(\mu)=1+\tfrac{2\pi i}{K}H(\mu)+O(1/P^{2})
. Taking the DFT: for
\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{0}
,
\hat{g}(\mathbf{k})=\tfrac{2\pi i}{K}\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})+O(1/P^{2})
; the zero mode is
\hat{g}(\mathbf{0})=1+O(1/P)
. Most measurement probability (
1-O(1/P^{2})
) concentrates on the zero mode
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}
, but
conditional on measuring
\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{0}
, the distribution is proportional to
|\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}+O(1/P^{2})
. Since the total error across all
s\leq mr
non-zero modes is
O(mr/P^{2})
, correct sampling requires
P\gg\sqrt{mr/p_{\min}}
; under
p_{\min}\geq 1/(mr\cdot n)
, we set
P=\Theta((mr)^{2}\cdot n)
, which remains polynomial.

Stage 2 (Linear System).
The phase oracle factorises as
U_{H}=\prod_{(i,j)}U_{ij}
(Sec.
4.2
). By Theorem
3.2
, the only non-zero Fourier modes of
H
lie on the anti-diagonal
k_{j}=-k_{i}\bmod C
for each edge
(i,j)
. When measurement yields frequency vector
\mathbf{k}
with exactly one non-zero pair
(k_{i},k_{j}=-k_{i})
, this identifies a specific edge
(i,j)
and its Fourier mode. Edge-level oracle access allows independent recovery of each
\hat{f}_{ij}(k_{i})
, so the per-edge phase
\varphi_{ij}=\arg\hat{f}_{ij}(k_{i})
is determined without ambiguity. Each measured
\mathbf{k}
reveals the linear congruence
k_{i}\cdot(\mu^{*}_{i}-\mu^{*}_{j})\equiv\varphi_{ij}\pmod{C}
. When
\gcd(k_{i},C)>1
, multiple congruences with coprime frequencies resolve ambiguity via the Chinese Remainder Theorem. Collecting congruences along a spanning tree determines all
n-1
offset differences.

Stage 3 (Repetition Bound).
Due to linearisation, each raw measurement returns the zero mode with probability
1-O(1/P^{2})
; conditional on non-zero outcome,
\Pr[\mathbf{k}]\propto|\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}/\|\hat{H}\|^{2}
. By a coupon-collector argument,
T_{\mathrm{raw}}=O(P^{2}/p_{\min}\cdot\log(mr/\delta))
measurements suffice. Under
p_{\min}\geq 1/(mr\cdot n)
, the total is
O(\mathrm{poly})
. Phase estimation is exact because phases take discrete values in
\{0,\ldots,C{-}1\}
.
∎

Proof of the Fourier Factorization Theorem

Proof.

By linearity,
\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{H}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
, where
\hat{H}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})=C^{-n}\sum_{\bm{\mu}}f_{ij}(\mu_{i}{-}\mu_{j})\,\omega_{C}^{-\mathbf{k}\cdot\bm{\mu}}

\ell\neq i,j

\mu_{\ell}
gives
\sum_{\mu_{\ell}}\omega_{C}^{-k_{\ell}\mu_{\ell}}=C\cdot\delta_{k_{\ell},0}

d=\mu_{i}{-}\mu_{j}
,
t=\mu_{j}

\hat{H}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{C^{2}}\sum_{d}f_{ij}(d)\,\omega_{C}^{-k_{i}d}\sum_{t}\omega_{C}^{-(k_{i}+k_{j})t}\\
=\delta_{k_{j},-k_{i}}\cdot\hat{f}_{ij}(k_{i}).

r

Appendix D
p_{\min}
Bounds for Engineering Cost Functions

Proposition D.1

(
p_{\min}
bound for cosine coupling)
.

Let each edge cost function have the form
f_{ij}(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r^{\prime}}a_{ij,\ell}\cos(2\pi\ell\,x/C)
with
a_{ij,\ell}>0
, and let
\Delta=\max_{i}\deg(i)
. Then
p_{\min}\geq 1/(\Delta\cdot m\cdot r^{\prime}\cdot\kappa^{2})
, where
\kappa=\max a/\min a
is the dynamic range. For sparse networks (
\Delta=O(1)
) with
\kappa=O(\mathrm{poly}(n))
, this gives
p_{\min}\geq 1/\mathrm{poly}(n,m,r)
.

Proof.

The DFT of
a\cos(2\pi\ell x/C)
gives
|\hat{f}(\ell)|=a/2
, so
\min|\hat{f}|^{2}\geq(\min a)^{2}/4
. By Theorem
3.2
, at most
\deg(i)
edges share vertex
i
, so
\|\hat{H}\|^{2}\leq\Delta\cdot m\cdot\sum_{\ell}(\max a_{\ell})^{2}/4
. Dividing yields the bound.
∎

Proposition D.2

(
p_{\min}
bound for piecewise-linear costs)
.

For piecewise-linear periodic costs with total variation
V_{f}
, Fourier coefficients satisfy
|\hat{f}(k)|=O(V_{f}/k^{2})
. Truncating to
r^{\prime}
harmonics gives dynamic range
\kappa\leq(r^{\prime})^{2}
, yielding

Proof.

Appendix G
Full Proof of Theorem
6.3

Proof.

The proof proceeds in three stages, paralleling the abelian proof but with matrix-valued Fourier coefficients.

Stage 1 (Quantum: Fourier coefficient recovery via linearisation).

By the Peter–Weyl theorem,
f(\sigma)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}d_{\lambda}\,\mathrm{tr}(\hat{f}(\lambda)\,\rho^{\lambda}(\sigma))
. Group elements are encoded in
O(k\log k)
qubits via the Lehmer code. The QFT over
S_{k}

[
29
]
maps
|\sigma\rangle\mapsto\sum_{\lambda}\sqrt{d_{\lambda}/k!}\sum_{p,q}\rho^{\lambda}_{pq}(\sigma)|\lambda,p,q\rangle
in
O(k^{2}\log k)
gates. Linearisation (
K_{ij}=P\cdot\|f_{ij}\|_{\infty}
) gives
\hat{g}(\lambda)\approx\delta_{\lambda,\mathrm{triv}}+(2\pi i/K_{ij})\hat{f}_{ij}(\lambda)+O(1/P^{2})
. For class functions,
\hat{f}(\lambda)=c_{\lambda}I_{d_{\lambda}}
, so the linearised coefficients are scalars. This stage is inherently quantum (Assumption
6.6
).

Stage 2 (Classical: optimisation and propagation).

Given the
r
active irrep labels and coefficients
c_{\lambda}
, evaluate
f_{ij}(\sigma)=\sum_{\lambda}c_{\lambda}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma)
on
p(k)
conjugacy-class representatives (
e^{O(\sqrt{k})}
evaluations). Propagate the global optimum along a spanning tree.

Stage 3 (Complexity accounting).

Total quantum query complexity:
O(m\cdot r\cdot k^{2}\log k)
. Classical minimisation adds
O(m\cdot r\cdot p(k))
operations; since
p(k)\sim e^{\pi\sqrt{2k/3}}
, total time is
e^{O(\sqrt{k})}
—far below
O(k!)
.
∎

Appendix I
On Extremal Conjugacy Classes

A natural route to establishing DMPC
\,\in\,
co-NP (Remark
6.5
) would be to show that the minimiser of an
r
-sparse class function on
S_{k}
always lies in a
\mathrm{poly}(r,k)
-sized “extremal” subset of conjugacy classes. We tested the candidate definition that extremal classes are those whose cycle type has at most
r
distinct part sizes. For each
(r,k)
pair with
r\in\{2,3,4\}
and
k\in\{3,\ldots,25\}
, we generated
10{,}000
(
k\leq 15
) or
5{,}000
(
k>15
) random
r
-sparse class functions, evaluated them on all
p(k)
conjugacy classes, and recorded the minimiser.

Results.

The candidate definition holds trivially for
k\leq 5
(the extremal set covers all classes). For
k\geq 6
, counterexamples appear: at
(r{=}2,\,k{=}25)
,
47\%
of trials have minimisers outside the extremal set, with cycle types exhibiting up to
5
distinct part sizes. The maximum number of distinct parts in minimisers grows as
\Theta(\sqrt{k})

independently of
r
. At threshold
\lceil\sqrt{k}\rceil
, the qualifying set covers
{\geq}94\%
of all conjugacy classes across all tested
k
, offering no useful restriction.

Conclusion.

No
\mathrm{poly}(r,k)
-sized characterisation of the minimising class is apparent from the data. The co-NP membership question for DMPC therefore remains open and may require fundamentally different techniques (e.g. algebraic certificates rather than combinatorial witnesses).

Appendix J
Frustration Gap and Hybrid Approach

Proposition J.1

(Frustration gap bound)
.

Let
G=(V,\mathcal{A})
have cycle rank
\beta=m-n+1
, and let
\Delta_{\max}=\max_{(i,j)}\bigl(\max_{d}f_{ij}(d)-f_{ij}(d^{*}_{ij})\bigr)
. The tree-solver output
\mu_{T}
satisfies
H(\mu_{T})-H(\mu^{*})\leq\beta\cdot\Delta_{\max}
.

Proof.

The tree solver achieves
\min_{d}f_{ij}(d)
on all
n-1
tree edges. Each of
\beta
non-tree edges incurs at most
\Delta_{\max}
excess cost. Therefore
H(\mu_{T})\leq\sum_{\mathrm{all}}f_{ij}(d^{*}_{ij})+\beta\cdot\Delta_{\max}\leq H(\mu^{*})+\beta\cdot\Delta_{\max}
.
∎

For small
\beta
, quantum Fourier recovery plus classical enumeration over
C^{\beta}
residual configurations is exact when
\beta=O(\log n/\log C)
.
