Impaired cellular trafficking of a thrombomodulin mutant causes severe bleeding, thrombosis, and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome∗†
Authors: Joanna Gemel, Zhour Barnawi, Anna R Dahlgren, Anthony Chang, Shireen Hashmat, Jordan A Shavit, Gabrielle Lapping-Carr, Eric C Beyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•We report a novel THBD mutant (C256S) that causes bleeding, thrombosis, and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome.•THBD-C256S has a defect in the cellular trafficking/export pathway.

Introduction

In rare cases, patients pose significant diagnostic and management decisions, because they paradoxically develop life-threatening bleeding and thrombosis. These competing clinical problems have few known causes including thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) and disseminated intravascular coagulation. Thrombotic microangiopathies comprise a group of distinct disorders including thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS). Thrombocytopenic purpura results from the deficiency or inhibition of the metalloproteinase ADAMTS13 (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin type 1 motif, member 13), which cleaves high-molecular-weight multimers of von Willebrand factor.1,2 HUS often occurs secondary to infection with Escherichia coli (especially serotype O157:HT) that produces Shiga-like toxin.3 Atypical HUS (aHUS) denotes HUS not caused by this infection.4 It results from uncontrolled activation of the complement system, leading to endothelial injury. The damaged endothelium provides a critical interface for inciting bleeding, clotting, inflammatory responses, and immune activation.5,6 Many aHUS cases have a genetic cause due to mutations of complement regulatory or activation proteins including complement factor H (CFH), complement factor I, membrane cofactor protein (CD46), thrombomodulin (THBD), complement factor B, or complement component 3 (C3).7,8 Acquired abnormalities (CFH autoantibodies) have also been described.9

In this report, we describe a novel THBD mutation identified in a patient with a broad spectrum of abnormalities including bleeding, thrombosis, and aHUS. Our characterization of endothelial cells from a patient expressing this mutation may help to explain the loss of THBD functions.

Materials and methods

Primary culture of endothelial cells

The patient’s parents provided written informed consent, and the patient assented to tissue procurement protocol, IRB20-1683. A fragment of adipose tissue was retrieved during a catheter replacement procedure. Endothelial cells were affinity isolated at the University of Chicago Organoid and Primary Culture Research Core using Dynabeads CD31 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc, Waltham, MA) that were precoated with antibodies directed against CD31 (platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1 [PECAM-1]). The isolation followed the protocol described by Hewett.10 The patient (mutant) endothelial cells were used at passage 4 for all experiments, because they tended to dedifferentiate with further passages.

For control (wild type) endothelial cells, we used human dermal microvascular endothelial cells (CC-2543; Lonza, Allendale, NJ). For all experiments, wild-type cells were used at passage 9/10 when they exhibited robust expression of endothelial proteins including PECAM-1, vascular endothelial (VE)–cadherin, and THBD. Mutant and wild-type endothelial cells were maintained in endothelial growth medium (EGM-2MV Bullet Kit; Lonza) and incubated in a cell culture incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2. Cells were grown on tissue culture plates that had been precoated with 0.1% gelatin to increase adherence.

Antibodies

THBD was detected using a rabbit monoclonal antibody (ab109189; Abcam, Cambridge, MA) at 1:1000 dilution for immunoblotting and 1:100 for immunofluorescence. For immunoblotting, PECAM-1 was detected using sheep polyclonal antibodies (AF806; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) at 1:1000 dilution. For immunofluorescence, PECAM-1 was detected using a 1:100 dilution of a mouse monoclonal antibody (sc-376764; Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc, Dallas, TX) specific for an epitope between amino acids 699 and 727 of mouse PECAM-1 that also reacts with the human protein. VE-cadherin was detected using a mouse monoclonal antibody (sc-9989; Santa Cruz) specific for amino acids 768 through 784 of human VE-cadherin. The antibody was diluted 1:2000 for immunoblotting and 1:100 for immunofluorescence. 58K Golgi protein was detected with a 1:1000 dilution of a mouse monoclonal antibody (G2404; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO).

Cyanin-3 (Cy3)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG), Cy3-conjugated goat anti-sheep IgG, AlexaFluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG, and horseradish peroxidase–conjugated goat anti-rabbit, anti-mouse, or anti-sheep IgG antibodies were obtained from Jackson ImmunoResearch (West Grove, PA). They were used as secondary antibodies for immunofluorescence or immunoblotting.

Immunofluorescence microscopy and immunoblotting

Immunofluorescence microscopy was performed as previously described.11 Immunoblotting of cell homogenates was performed as previously described.12 The protein concentrations of cell homogenates were determined spectrophotometrically by Coomassie brilliant blue dye binding reagent (Bio-Rad, Richmond, CA).13 Details of both immunofluorescence microscopy and immunoblotting are included in the supplemental Methods.

Enzymatic deglycosylation of proteins was performed using an Enzymatic DeGlycoMx deglycosylation kit (50355826; QA Bio Inc, Palm Desert, CA). Cell lysates containing 10 μg (wild type) or 25 μg (mutant) of protein were boiled for 5 minutes in denaturation solution (to increase the extent of subsequent enzyme digestion). After brief chilling on ice, the samples were digested at 37°C overnight with a mixture of glycosidase enzymes to remove both N-linked and O-linked oligosaccharides from glycoproteins.

Plasma membrane proteins were biotinylated and isolated by avidin affinity using a Pierce cell surface protein isolation kit (89881; Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL).

Plasmid construction and zebra fish embryo microinjection

Zebra fish thbd complementary DNA was cloned into a plasmid driven by the ubiquitin (ubi) promoter14 and containing mCherry regulated by the cryaa promoter in a Tol2-based plasmid.15 The Q5 site-directed mutagenesis kit (New England Biolabs) was used to replace the orthologous cysteine (in the wild-type protein) with a serine (p.C217S in zebra fish, which is referred to as p.C256S for convenience). Primers were designed with NEBaseChanger (forward, GATTATAAGTctGTTAAAAACGGCC; reverse, ACATCCACCATCAAAAAC); 100 ng of either the wild-type or mutant plasmid was combined with 300 ng of transposase15 and injected into thbd−/− incrosses at 25 pg plasmid and 75 pg transposase per single-cell embryo. thbd−/− fish have a 1253–base pair deletion, resulting in a null allele (Q. Y. Zhao and J. A. Shavit, unpublished data). A portion of the embryos were left uninjected as controls.

Laser-mediated venous endothelial injury in zebra fish

Zebra fish experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the University of Michigan animal care and use committee. At 3 days after fertilization, laser-mediated venous endothelial injury was performed, as previously described.16,17 The larvae were anesthetized with tricaine and mounted on a glass coverslip in 0.8% low-melting-temperature agarose. Endothelial injury was performed by an observer blinded to condition using a pulsed nitrogen dye laser system (Andor Technology), targeting the endothelium of the posterior cardinal vein at 5 somites caudal to the anal pore with 99 pulses. An inverted microscope (Olympus IX73) with a 20× objective was used to visualize occlusion, and time to occlusion was measured up to 2 minutes. The injected fish were screened for red fluorescence in the eye to confirm plasmid expression. The data were analyzed using a Kruskal-Wallis test followed by pairwise comparisons with a false discovery rate correction (GraphPad Prism).

Results

Clinical case presentation

A 17-year-old adolescent boy of Middle Eastern origin, presented to the emergency department at Comer Children’s Hospital (The University of Chicago) with acute life-threatening epistaxis (see computed tomographic scan in Figure 1A). His initial hemoglobin was 13 g/dL and dropped by >7 g/dL in 24 hours despite massive transfusions of platelets, fresh frozen plasma, and cryoprecipitate. The patient required emergent sphenopalatine embolization to stop the bleeding.

This coagulopathy was associated with a normocytic anemia and profound thrombocytopenia (see course of laboratory values in Figure 2; supplemental Table 1). His hemoglobin nadired at 6.1 g/dL and the platelet count nadired at 30 000/μL. The lowest fibrinogen was <30 mg/dL, D-dimers were significantly elevated at >20 μg/mL, and his maximum International Normalized Ratio was 4.2. He received multiple blood and plasma products (Figure 2). Initial blood smears showed some schistocytes, no spherocytes, and a reticulocyte count of 1.9%. Two days after admission, Coombs testing (direct and indirect) was negative, and haptoglobin was 54 mg/dL.

Graphical depiction of the patient’s laboratory values and transfusions with blood products during 3 episodes encompassing major clinical events or complications. INR, International Normalized Ratio; pRBC, packed red blood cell transfusions.

The patient’s past medical history included no history of significant epistaxis, or other bleeding complications, including when he underwent circumcision or dental procedures. However, when the patient was aged 2 years, a fall onto a soft surface had resulted in an intracranial hemorrhage, requiring surgical evacuation and transfusion of multiple blood products. No diagnostic evaluation was performed at the time, and he recovered fully from that event. Neither the parents nor the patient’s siblings have had any bleeding or clotting episodes.

The patient subsequently developed severe hypertension and acute renal failure, which warranted hemodialysis. Kidney biopsy revealed severe TMA with focal cortical necrosis (Figure 1B-C), suggestive of aHUS. Consistent with this diagnosis, ADAMTS13 function was within the normal range and complement C4 was mildly decreased at 15 mg/dL, whereas complement C3 levels were normal. The patient initiated peritoneal dialysis; he underwent 3 plasma exchanges; and eculizumab therapy was initiated.

The patient remained critically ill for 1 month after this admission. During that time, he developed thromboses of the left iliac and common femoral veins, a hemorrhagic renal cyst, and abdominal skin necrosis in proximity to a peritoneal dialysis catheter site (Figure 1D). A panel of hypercoagulability laboratory tests was performed, but the results showed no abnormalities (values included: protein C activity, 156%; and protein S activity, 83%). Anticoagulant treatment was initiated with a heparin drip and then transitioned to long-term prophylaxis using apixaban after venous thromboses resolved. As shown in supplemental Table 1, his “baseline” levels at the time of discharge (while taking apixaban) included normal platelet count and fibrinogen level but persistently elevated D-dimers. He continued daily peritoneal dialysis and eculizumab infusions every 2 weeks as an outpatient. He remained on peritoneal dialysis for ∼6 months. His renal function showed partial improvement, and he regained some urine output.

Subsequently, the patient underwent unrelated, deceased donor kidney transplant. Transplant surgery was complicated by profound postoperative hemorrhage. This exceptional bleeding was not isolated to the surgical sites, but occurred throughout the abdominal cavity, including on serosal surfaces. For the first 3 days, Doppler scan results showed normal arterial and venous blood flows in the transplanted kidney, but on the fourth postoperative day, reversal of venous flow was noted. Simultaneous right femoral and common iliac vein thromboses were noted. The interventional radiologists attempted to perform a thrombectomy but were unable to cannulate the renal vein of the transplanted kidney. Postoperatively, the patient developed thromboses in his fingers (Figure 1E) and toes as well as necrosis of the skin of his nose (Figure 1F). Low-dose argatroban was initiated together with aggressive replacement using fresh frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate, platelets, and packed red blood cells. Supportive care, eculizumab, and plasmapheresis were followed by gradual hematologic improvement, with no further thromboses, but kidney function did not recover. The patient remained anuric on dialysis.

Over the subsequent 2 years, the patient has been maintained on eculizumab (or an alternative complement inhibitor), and daily direct anticoagulant (apixaban). He started on hemodialysis for end-stage kidney disease, but he developed several episodes of catheter-related thrombosis and required central-line replacements. Arteriovenous fistula was not an option due to the risk of bleeding/thrombosis and aHUS recurrence. He subsequently transitioned to peritoneal dialysis. Before any operative procedures, he receives prophylactic blood and plasma transfusions with packed red blood cells, platelets, cryoprecipitate, and fresh frozen plasma to achieve targets of hemoglobin >7 g/dL, platelets of >50 000, fibrinogen of >150 mg/dL, and International Normalized Ratio of <1.2.

After the kidney biopsy findings, the patient had genetic testing for a panel of genes associated with aHUS/TMA. No substitutions were identified in C3, CD46, complement factor B, CFH, complement factor I, or diacylglycerol kinase-epsilon. The only abnormality detected was a homozygous variant of THBD (THBD): c.767G>C (p.C256S). This variant has not been reported previously, and it is not listed in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD). Parents refused genetic testing of additional family members; however, they did endorse that they were first cousins. THBD is a multifunctional, integral membrane protein (Figure 3A) encoded by the THBD gene that is present in all endothelial cells and contributes to regulating diverse processes including bleeding, thrombosis, complement activation, and inflammation.18, 19, 20 The variant was initially reported to be of “uncertain significance.” However, as shown in in Figure 3B, this substitution would replace a cysteine in the first epithelial growth factor–like domain in the protein, preventing the formation of 1 of 3 disulfide bonds in this domain. We hypothesized that this change might substantially disrupt the secondary and tertiary structures of the protein. Therefore, we pursued a series of experiments to examine the behavior of the THBD protein in this patient’s cells.

Characterization of the mutant THBD in a zebra fish model

We tested the hypothesis that this mutant had disrupted the function of THBD using a zebra fish model. In normal (wild type) zebra fish larvae, laser-mediated venous endothelial injury rapidly causes vascular occlusion (Figure 4). We have recently developed thbd-null fish; many of them display an increased time to occlusion (Zhao and Shavit, unpublished data) (uninjected thbd-null in Figure 4). The increased time to occlusion observed in thbd mutants is consistent with the disseminated intravascular coagulation observed due to loss of other anticoagulants in zebra fish including antithrombin III and protein C.21,22 Plasmids with either wild-type or mutant (zebra fish mutation orthologous to p.Cys256Ser) thbd were injected into offspring from thbd-null zebra fish incrosses. Laser-mediated venous endothelial injury was performed on the injected larvae at 3 days after fertilization as well as uninjected siblings. There was a significant difference between the 3 groups (P = .009) with the wild-type plasmid partially rescuing the null phenotype. The C256Sthbd plasmid injections were not significantly different from uninjected thbd-null larvae (Figure 4), providing supportive data that the mutant is nonfunctional.

Larvae injected with the wild-type plasmid were significantly different from uninjected or injected with the mutant plasmid. The uninjected control and mutant plasmid groups had a similar median time to occlusion and no significant difference. Characterization of THBD in the patient’s endothelial cells

While the patient was undergoing a surgical procedure to remove a central venous catheter, we obtained a small amount of adipose tissue that was adherent to the catheter. We used affinity purification to isolate endothelial cells. Primary cultures of these cells were established, and they were used for several studies. We used primary cultures of human dermal microvascular endothelial cells as controls.

We performed immunoblots to detect and quantify THBD in homogenates from the patient’s endothelial cells and compared them with wild-type cells (Figure 5A). Levels were significantly lower in the patient’s endothelial cells than in controls. We had to load the gels with large quantities of total protein to detect THBD in homogenates of the patient’s cells.

Immunofluorescence microscopy was used to localize THBD in the patient’s endothelial cells (Figure 5B) and in wild-type controls. In the patient’s cells, only a modest amount of THBD was detected in cells that were not permeabilized with Triton X-100, implying that most of the protein was localized intracellularly. In control cells, staining was also increased by detergent permeabilization, suggesting that a substantial fraction of the wild-type protein is also localized within the cytoplasm.

We hypothesized that the cytoplasmically localized THBD might represent protein that was retained within the cellular biosynthetic pathway. As a plasma membrane protein, THBD should be sequentially processed through multiple cellular compartments including the rough endoplasmic reticulum, the endoplasmic reticulum–Golgi intermediate compartment, and the Golgi apparatus. When we performed double-label immunofluorescence using antibodies directed against both THBD and a Golgi-resident protein (58K), we found extensive colocalization (Figure 5C), implying that much of the cytoplasmic THBD was present within the Golgi apparatus.

In addition to differences in levels, immunoblots showed that the electrophoretic mobilities differed between THBD from wild-type and mutant (patient) cells (Figure 5A,D). The major component in the wild-type sample migrated with a molecular mass of ∼100 kDa. In contrast, the major band in the mutant/patient sample migrated faster with an apparent molecular mass of ∼89 kDa (similar to a minor band in the wild-type sample). Since much of the mutant protein was retained in the Golgi apparatus (the site for processing oligosaccharides on secreted and membrane glycoproteins), we hypothesized that the apparent size differences might reflect incomplete processing of the sugars on the mutant protein. We tested this hypothesis by enzymatically treating homogenates of both patient and control cells with a mixture of glycosidases to remove both N- and O-linked oligosaccharides. This treatment reduced both wild-type and mutant proteins to an apparent molecular mass of ∼82 kDa (Figure 5D). Because the deglycosylated proteins migrated similarly, the major differences likely resulted from the sugar modifications.

We also used cell-surface biotinylation to examine the abundance of THBD that was exposed on the surface of the patient’s cells (Figure 6). We found that a large fraction of the THBD in control endothelial cells was accessible to cell surface biotinylation, but the biotinylated fraction was dramatically reduced in the patient’s cells (Figure 6A; 36% in control vs 5% for the patient). In contrast, a different endothelial membrane protein (PECAM-1) was efficiently biotinylated in both patient and control cells (Figure 6B; 50% in control vs 36% in patient cells).

Reduced levels of other endothelial proteins in the patient’s cells

It has been suggested previously that THBD expression can influence the expression/levels of other major proteins in endothelial cells.20 Therefore, we examined the levels and distribution of PECAM-1 and VE-cadherin. Immunoblots showed that the levels of PECAM-1 were much lower in homogenates of the patient’s cells than in control endothelial cells (∼30-fold less; Figure 7A). VE-cadherin was undetectable in the patient’s cells, even when gels were loaded with 50-times as much protein as for the control cell homogenate (Figure 7A). Immunofluorescence micrographs showed that PECAM-1 was localized in a rather similar distribution around the surfaces of both patient and wild-type endothelial cells (Figure 7B). Although VE-cadherin was abundantly detected around the plasma membranes of control endothelial cells, very little or no VE-cadherin was detected by immunofluorescent examination of the patient’s cells (Figure 7C).

Discussion

This report presents the clinical description of a patient homozygous for a previously unknown THBD mutation (THBD-C256S). THBD accomplishes several different apparently opposing functions. By serving as a receptor for thrombin, THBD acts as a natural anticoagulant through activation of protein C. Thrombin binding to THBD alters thrombin’s activity, decreasing conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin and activation of platelets and coagulation factors V and VIII.18,19,23 THBD is so abundant in the microvasculature that it sequesters most of the thrombin generated under ambient conditions.20 Thrombin binding to THBD enhances thrombin-mediated activation of protein C by >1000-fold.23 Activated protein C complexes with protein S, and the complexes catalyze the proteolytic inactivation of factors Va and VIIIa. Studies in mice with endothelium-specific THBD loss highlight its anticoagulant role; they develop severe thrombosis and die as a result.24 Thrombin binding to THBD also promotes activation of thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI), which blocks the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin, thus inhibiting fibrinolysis.25

THBD’s additional roles include regulating innate immunity, complement activation, inflammation, and cell proliferation.26,27 Complement regulation by THBD may be mediated, in part, by TAFI, because TAFI can also use C5a and C3a as substrates.28 TAFI can also inactivate other proinflammatory mediators, including bradykinin and osteopontin. Many effects of THBD are mediated by activated protein C, which interferes with thrombin-induced proinflammatory activities. Many of these protective effects of activated protein C are mediated by proteolytic cleavage of protease activated receptor 1.29 THBD can directly elicit anti-inflammatory signaling responses in endothelial cells by downregulating expression of cell adhesion molecules and proinflammatory cytokines; these effects may be mediated by regulation of PTEN/AKT/FOXO1 signaling.30

Previous studies have reported a significant number of THBD variants and disease-associated mutations. Most commonly, autosomal-dominant mutations are associated with deep vein and arterial thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and myocardial infarction, likely reflecting reduced activation of protein C.31, 32, 33, 34 Missense mutations (including P495S, P501L, and D486Y) have been identified in ∼5% of patients with aHUS.35 Profound bleeding is less commonly associated with THBD mutations, but one such mutation of a nearby cysteine (C265S) had extensive bleeding similar to our patient. The defects present in our patient appear quite different from the unusual THBD variant (THBD c.1611C>A, p.C537Stop) that has been linked to bleeding associated with “shedding” of a truncated THBD protein.36 Genetic mouse models have been prepared (with mutations in different regions of the THBD protein), and they differentially recapitulate some of these abnormalities.37 Although previous studies have identified amino acid substitutions within THBD that are associated with bleeding, thrombosis, or aHUS, our patient is remarkable, because he survived with all of 3 of these challenging complications.

In our patient, the THBD-C256S mutation prevents formation of 1 of 3 critical disulfides within the first epithelial growth factor–like domain; this causes a major disruption of the secondary and tertiary structure of the protein. Our biochemical and cellular studies support this prediction. When expressed in THBD-null zebra fish, the mutant protein exhibited no functional activity. In contrast, wild-type THBD showed significant rescue. As in other zebra fish studies using this assay, rescue was incomplete due to variable expression levels and mosaicism.21,22,38, 39, 40, 41 Although the THBD polypeptide is produced by the patient’s endothelial cells, it is largely retained in the biosynthetic pathway (with little or none on the cell surface), and it is incompletely modified by glycosylation. The severely reduced levels in the patient may reflect degradation of the abnormal protein through cellular quality control proteolysis.

The major consequence of these abnormalities is that this patient has severely reduced levels of functional THBD available on the surface of his endothelial cells. Because they are important functions mediated by THBD, it is interesting to evaluate how this variant affects protein C activation, thrombin binding, or TAFI-mediated complement regulation. We had initially planned to use the patient’s endothelial cells to perform such evaluations. However, because our cell biology studies showed that his cells have massively reduced THBD at the cell surface (nearly null), we did not perform such experiments. We anticipate that they would not have differed from cells displaying no THBD. In previous studies, null cells or cells transfected with similar THBD mutants have not affected protein C activation or enhanced complement inactivation significantly beyond background. Thus, his physiology should be functionally similar to THBD-null mice and zebra fish. Interestingly, the thbd-null fish show substantial phenotypic variability in the vascular occlusion model (“uninjected” in Figure 4). Temporal variability in our patient may explain his ability to survive until he had an injury. Unlike some patients harboring THBD mutations that alter a single functional domain and only a subset of THBD functions,42,43 all known THBD roles are affected in our patient. Perhaps it is most surprising that he has survived as well and as long as he had previously with only a few major episodes of bleeding, clotting, and aHUS.

The absence of THBD is not the only abnormality present in our patient’s endothelial cells. He also has reduced levels of other major endothelial membrane proteins, including PECAM-1 and VE-cadherin. We suspect that this reflects a disruption of multicomponent membrane protein complexes as has been observed previously.12 Alterations of these other major proteins suggest that the reduction/absence of THBD in our patient may also affect other aspects of endothelial biology (like barrier function).

Although our patient has had multiple life-threatening complications, we have arrived at a regimen that has been keeping him in a generally stable state of health. Like other patients with aHUS, a mainstay of the management of our patient is the use of eculizumab to inhibit complement activity.6,44 He also receives a direct acting oral anticoagulant (apixaban) to prevent thrombosis. Any surgical procedures are anticipated by aggressive replacement with platelets, fresh frozen plasma, and cryoprecipitate. His most significant current problem is renal failure requiring chronic dialysis.

The potential role of soluble THBD in this patient warrants speculation. Although THBD primarily functions as an endothelial membrane protein, it can also be released into circulation after proteolytic cleavage of its extracellular domain. Given the markedly reduced membrane-bound THBD observed in this patient, it is unlikely that significant levels of soluble THBD would be produced. Consistent with this, we were unable to detect soluble THBD in the culture medium of the patient’s cells (data not shown). However, a number of clinical trials have suggested the utility of recombinant soluble THBD for treatment of patients with diseases such as sepsis- or cancer-associated disseminated intravascular coagulation.27,45,46 Additionally, a case report of another patient with a point mutation in THBD, that caused defective thrombin binding, showed evidence that soluble THBD had substantial efficacy.43

Conclusions

The homozygous THBD-C256S mutation leads to severe bleeding, clotting, and aHUS. Loss of Cys256 disrupts a critical disulfide bond in THBD, altering structure and function. The mutant protein is incompletely processed and rarely appears at the cell surface. The reduced cell surface levels of endothelial THBD may explain altered activation of complement and protein C. The availability of patient endothelial cells for cell biology studies have provided useful insights; however, our studies were limited by our inability to directly measure changes in thrombin binding, protein C activation, or complement regulation.
