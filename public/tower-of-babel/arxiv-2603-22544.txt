Density of Visible Lattice Points on Hyperplanes and their Intersections
Finnley Goss, Kelly McKinnie

Abstract
A lattice point x → = ( x 1 , … , x n ) ∈ ℤ n {\vec{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n} is said to be visible if the line segment between x → {\vec{x}} and the origin contains no other lattice point. In this paper, we compute the asymptotic density of visible lattice points on hyperplanes and their intersections. In particular, we show that the hyperplane a → ⋅ x → = b \vec{a}\cdot{\vec{x}}=b in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} has visible point density J n − 1 ​ ( b ) / b n − 1 J_{n-1}(b)/b^{n-1} where J J is the Jordan totient function. We extend this basic result to find the density of visible points on the intersection of hyperplanes and to the density of k k -th power free points. Finally, for a fixed dimension n n , we consider the closure of the set of all possible densities that occur.



1
Introduction

A lattice point
{\vec{x}}=(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is said to be visible if the line segment between
{\vec{x}}
and the origin intersects no other lattice points. Equivalently, a lattice point is visible if and only if
\gcd({\vec{x}})=\gcd(x_{1},...,x_{n})=1
. The density of visible lattice points in a bounded,
n
-dimensional region is given by the ratio of visible to total lattice points contained in the region. The asymptotic density of visible lattice points in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is defined to be the limit of this bounded density as the diameter approaches infinity. It has long been known that the asymptotic density of visible lattice points in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is
{1}/{\zeta(n)},
where
\zeta
is the Riemann-Zeta function (
[
23
]
, see also the classic references
[
4
]
and
[
13
]
).

Numerous generalizations of the classic notion of density of visible points exist in the literature (e.g.,
[
12
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
8
]
). In this paper we define and calculate the asymptotic density of visible lattice points which lie on hyperplanes and their intersections. In our main result, Theorem
3.3
, we provide a formula for the density on any such domain. The proof uses
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
transformations of the domain, the Smith Normal Form and the sieve methods found in
[
18
]
adapted to our setting. Let
a_{i},b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\gcd(a_{i})=1
. In Corollary
3.4
we show that the asymptotic density of visible points on the hyperplane
a_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}x_{n}=b
is
J_{n-1}(b)/b^{n-1}=\prod_{p|b}1-{1}/{p^{n-1}}
where the
J
is the Jordan totient and the product is over all primes dividing
b
.

In Section
4
, we generalize the arguments used in Theorem
3.3
to compute the density of
k
-visible lattice points on the same domains. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, a lattice point
{\vec{x}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is
k
-visible
if
p^{k}\nmid\gcd(x)
for all prime numbers
p
. For
k=1
, these are the visible lattice points. For
k=2
, these are the lattice points with square-free greatest common divisors.

Lastly, in Section
5
we consider the set of asymptotic densities of all hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For
n=2
, the densities of lines are all numbers of the form
\varphi(b)/b
for
b\in\mathbb{Z}
. These numbers are dense in
[1/\zeta(1),0]=[0,1]
by
[
27
]
. In Theorem
5.2
we show that for
n>2
the set of densities of visible points on hyperplanes is not dense in the interval
[1/\zeta(n-1),1]
.

1.1
Preliminaries

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a nonempty set. To study the asymptotic density of visible lattice points in
\Delta\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
we define the following subsets of
\Delta
:

\displaystyle\Delta(r)

\displaystyle=\Delta\cap[-r,r]^{n}

(1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(r)

\displaystyle=\Delta(r)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}

(2)

\displaystyle\Delta_{b}(r)

\displaystyle=\{{\vec{x}}\in\Delta_{1}(r):\gcd({\vec{x}},b)=1\}

(3)

so that
\Delta_{1}(r)
is the subset of lattice points in
\Delta(r)
and
\Delta_{b}(r)
is the subset of those lattice points that are coprime to
b
, that is,
\gcd({\vec{x}},b)=\gcd(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},b)=1
. In particular,
\Delta_{0}(r)
is the set of visible lattice points in
\Delta_{1}(r)
. We use
|\cdot|
to indicate the size of a set.

The asymptotic density of visible lattice points in
\Delta
, if it exists, is defined to be

\rho(\Delta)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{|\Delta_{0}(r)|}{|\Delta_{1}(r)|}.

Taking
\Delta=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the classic result is
\rho(\mathbb{R}^{n})=1/\zeta(n)
(
[
23
]
). In this paper we concern ourselves with
\rho(\mathcal{H})
, where
\mathcal{H}
is the intersection of finitely many hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For brevity, we drop the adjectives ‘asymptotic’ and ‘lattice’ and simply refer to
\rho(\Delta)
as the density of visible points in
\Delta
.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem 2.1 of

.

Let
\Delta

\mathbb{R}^{n}

\omega\geq 1

\Delta_{0}

\Delta

\left||\Delta_{0}|-\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\Delta)}{\zeta(n)}\right|=O\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\omega\log\omega,&\textrm{if }n=2\\
\omega^{n-1},&\textrm{otherwise}\end{array}\right)

where the constant implied by the

.

The diameter of a set is the largest distance between points in the set and as noted in
[
18
, (2)]
,
\mathrm{vol}(\Delta)=O(\omega^{n})
. The classic density theorem mentioned above (
[
23
]
) is obtained from Theorem
1.1
by taking
\Delta=[-r,r]^{n}
with
\mathrm{vol}(\Delta)=(2r)^{n}
, diameter
\omega=2r\sqrt{n}
and
|\Delta_{1}|=(2r+1)^{n}
. Dividing both sides of the equation from Theorem
1.1
by
(2r+1)^{n}
and letting

shows the density of visible points is
\rho(\mathbb{R}^{n})=1/\zeta(n)
.

In Section
2
, Theorem
2.1
, we extend Theorem
1.1
to give the density of points coprime to a fixed integer
b
rather than the density of all visible points. The constant replacing
\zeta(n)
in the equation is related to the Jordan totient of
|b|
, a variant of Euler’s totient function. This generalization is used to prove the density of visible points on all hyperplanes and their intersections in Theorem
3.3
.

For
n,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, the Jordan totient function,
J_{n}(b)
, is defined to be the number of integer
n
-tuples selected from a complete set of residues modulo
b
with
\gcd
coprime to
b
(
[
10
, pg 155 - 174]
,
[
21
]
). The Jordan totient function has closed formula

J_{n}(b)=\sum_{d|b}\mu(d)\frac{b^{n}}{d^{n}}=b^{n}\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n}}

where the product is over all distinct primes dividing
b
(see e.g.,
[
4
, Ch 2, exercise 17]
).

Though we provide a rigorous argument in Section
3
, we first present here a heuristic argument using the Jordan totient as to why the density of visible points on
\mathcal{H}
, the hyperplane defined by
x_{1}=b
, should be
J_{n-1}(b)/b^{n-1}
. By its above definition,
J_{n-1}(b)
is the number of visible lattice points in
\{b\}\times[0,b-1]^{n-1}\subset\mathcal{H}
. Translations of this set by linear combinations of
b\vec{e}_{i}
for
2\leq i\leq n
, where
\vec{e}_{i}
is the
i
-th unit normal basis vector, form a regular tiling of
\mathcal{H}
. Each tile has exactly
J_{n-1}(b)
visible lattice points and
b^{n-1}
total lattice points, meaning they each have a ratio of visible to total lattice points equaling
J_{n-1}(b)/b^{n-1}
. Therefore it is expected that the density of visible points in
\mathcal{H}
is

\rho(\mathcal{H})=\frac{J_{n-1}(b)}{b^{n-1}}=\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n-1}}.

We prove this result for hyperplanes of the form
x_{1}=b
in Lemma
3.1
and for all hyperplanes
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}=b
in Corollary
3.4
and a corresponding result for intersections of hyperplanes in Theorem
3.3
.

Note here that we only concern ourselves with hyperplanes that are defined by
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{x}=b
with
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\gcd(\vec{a})=1
. If
\gcd(\vec{a})\neq 1
and
\gcd(\vec{a})\nmid b
, then
\mathcal{H}
contains no integral points and
\rho(\mathcal{H})
does not exist. If
\gcd(\vec{a})\mid b
, then divide the equation by
\gcd(\vec{a})
and proceed.

A generalization and some lower bounds

Theorem
2.1
generalizes Theorem
1.1
, allowing us to measure the density of points in a given domain that are coprime to a fixed integer
b
rather than visible (coprime to every integer). We make use of the well-known identity

\sum_{d|b}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{n}}=\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n}}

(4)

(
[
4
]
)
as a finite version of the Euler product for
1/\zeta(n)
used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
\Delta
be a convex domain of full dimension in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with diameter
\omega
. As in Section
1
,
\Delta_{1}
is the set of integral points in
\Delta
and
\Delta_{b}\subseteq\Delta_{1}
the subset of points coprime to
b
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For
b\neq 0
,

\left||\Delta_{b}|-\left(\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n}}\right)\mathrm{vol}(\Delta)\right|=O(\omega^{n-1})

where the product is over all distinct primes dividing
b
.

Note that if
b=0
then
\Delta_{b}=\Delta_{0}
is the subset of visible points in
\Delta
and the product in the statement of Theorem
2.1
is infinite and equal to
1/\zeta(n)
. The theorem holds in this case by Theorem
1.1
, except for
n=2
, where the bound is
\omega\log\omega
. The proof of Theorem
2.1
is somewhat simpler than that of Theorem
1.1
because the product is finite but otherwise follows nearly identically.

Proof.

The proof follows the argument of
[
18
, proof of 2.1]
. For any
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, let
\Lambda_{d}=\Delta_{1}\cap(d\mathbb{Z})^{n}
be the subset of points with each component divisible by
d
. Then

\displaystyle|\Delta_{b}|=\sum_{d|b}\mu(d)|\Lambda_{d}|.

(5)

To compute
|\Lambda_{d}|
for
d|b
let
\Delta/d=\{{\vec{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:d{\vec{x}}\in\Delta\}
so that
|\Lambda_{d}|=|(\Delta/d)_{1}|
.
\Delta/d
is a convex domain of full dimension and diameter
\omega/d
. Hence by Lemma
1.2
we have

\displaystyle O(\omega^{n-1})=O((\omega/d)^{n-1})

\displaystyle=\Big||(\Delta/d)_{1}|-\mathrm{vol}(\Delta/d)\Big|=\Big||(\Delta/d)_{1}|-\mathrm{vol}(\Delta)/d^{n}\Big|.

(6)

Use (
4
), (
5
), (
6
) and the triangle inequality to sum on the divisors of

\displaystyle\left||\Delta_{b}|-\left(\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n}}\right)\mathrm{vol}(\Delta)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{d|b}\mu(d)|\Lambda_{d}|-\sum_{d|b}\frac{\mu(d)}{d^{n}}\mathrm{vol}(\Delta)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{d|b}\mu(d)\left(|\Lambda_{d}|-\mathrm{vol}(\Delta)/d^{n}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{d|b}|\mu(d)|\Big||(\Delta/d)_{1}|-\mathrm{vol}(\Delta)/d^{n}\Big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{d|b}|\mu(d)|O(\omega^{n-1})=O(\omega^{n-1}).

(7)

∎

In Section
3
, the convex domains of Theorem
2.1
are hyperplane sections of
n
-dimensional cubes or transformations of them by
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. While the combinatorial type of the polyhedra which is the intersection of a
n
-cube and a hyperplane can be complicated, a general formula for the volume is known (
[
11
]
) and there is also a formula for the number of lattice points in the intersection (
[
1
]
). We need something similar but more basic in order to use Theorem
2.1
to attain the density of visible points on hyperplanes; we need a lower bound on the size of
\Delta_{1}(r)=\mathcal{H}\cap[-r,r]^{n}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, where
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a fixed hyperplane. This lower bound is Corollary
2.3
. It follows from Lemma
2.2
which gives a lower bound on the number of integral points on intersections of hyperplanes and is used again in the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq s
, be hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by equations
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}x_{j}=b_{i}
and set
A=(a_{ij})\in M_{s,n}(\mathbb{Z})
. Let
\Delta(r)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{s}\mathcal{H}_{i}\cap[-r,r]^{n}
and
\Delta_{1}(r)\subset\Delta(r)
the subset of integral points. Let
r_{0}
be the smallest positive integer such that
\Delta_{1}(r)\neq\emptyset
. For
r\geq r_{0}
,

\displaystyle|\Delta_{1}(r)|\geq\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{r-r_{0}}{c}\right\rfloor+1\right)^{n-m}

(8)

where
m=\mathrm{rank}(A)
, the null-space of
A
is spanned by
\vec{v}_{j}=(v_{j1},\ldots,v_{jn})
for
0\leq j\leq n-m
and
c=\max(\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}|v_{jk}|)_{k=1}^{n}
. In particular,
|\Delta_{1}(r)|=\Omega(r^{n-m})
.

Proof.

Let
{\vec{p}}\in\Delta_{1}(r_{0})
. All other integral points on
\bigcap_{i=1}^{s}\mathcal{H}_{i}
are of the form
{\vec{p}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}c_{j}\vec{v}_{j}
for
c_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
. When
|c_{j}|\leq\left\lfloor\frac{r-r_{0}}{c}\right\rfloor
for all
j
, the
k
th component of
{\vec{p}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}c_{j}\vec{v}_{j}
has bounded magnitude:

\displaystyle|p_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}c_{j}v_{jk}|

\displaystyle\leq|p_{k}|+\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}|c_{j}||v_{jk}|

\displaystyle\leq r_{0}+\left\lfloor\frac{r-r_{0}}{c}\right\rfloor\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}|v_{jk}|

\displaystyle\leq r_{0}+r-r_{0}=r

That is,
{\vec{p}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}c_{j}\vec{v}_{j}\in[-r,r]^{n}
, showing the lower bound in the lemma. For
r\gg r_{0}
,
\left\lfloor\frac{r-r_{0}}{c}\right\rfloor^{n-m}\geq\left(\frac{r-r_{0}}{c}-1\right)^{n-m}=\frac{1}{c^{n-m}}(r^{n-m}+\cdots)
, hence
|\Delta_{1}(r)|=\Omega(r^{n-m})
.
∎

In the case of only one hyperplane there is a nicer formulation for the constant
c
from Lemma
2.2
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by equation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}=b
. Let
\Delta(r)=\mathcal{H}\cap[-r,r]^{n}
and
\Delta_{1}(r)\subset\Delta(r)
the subset of integral points. Let
r_{0}
be the smallest positive integer such that
\Delta_{1}(r_{0})\neq\emptyset
. For
r\geq r_{0}
,

|\Delta_{1}(r)|\geq\left\lfloor\frac{r-r_{0}}{\sum|a_{i}|}\right\rfloor^{n-1}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume
a_{1}\neq 0
. The row space of

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}&-a_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
a_{3}&0&-a_{1}&\cdots&0\\
&&\ddots&&\\
a_{n}&0&0&\cdots&-a_{1}\end{pmatrix}

(9)

is a basis of the null-space from Lemma
2.2
, so that
c=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_{i}|
satisfies the property of the
c
from Lemma
2.2
. Since the rank of
A
is 1, the corollary follows.
∎

3
Density of Visible Points on Intersections of Hyperplanes

Although Theorem
3.3
ultimately subsumes the result, we begin this section with Lemma
3.1
which demonstrates the proof technique using the simplest hyperplane:
x_{1}=b
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
b\in\mathbb{Z}
. Theorem
3.3
gives the density for arbitrary intersections of hyperplanes and the proof follows from arguments similar to those in Lemma
3.1
, Lemma
3.2
, and the results of Section
2
. Several corollaries with simple formulas for the density of visible points follow. All hyperplanes and intersections of hyperplanes are assumed to contain at least one integral point and all defining equations
\vec{a}\cdot{\vec{x}}=b
assume
\gcd(\vec{a})=1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{Z}
and let
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the hyperplane
x_{1}=b
, the density of visible points on
\mathcal{H}
is

\rho(\mathcal{H})=\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n-1}}.

Note this value is equal to
\displaystyle\frac{J_{n-1}(|b|)}{|b|^{n-1}}
when
b\neq 0
and
1/\zeta(n-1)
when
b=0
, as expected from the discussion in Section
1
.

Proof.

For
b=0
,
\mathcal{H}=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and this is the classic result that the density of visible points in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is
1/\zeta(n-1)
, which matches the Euler product given in the lemma. For
b=\pm 1
, the density is 1 because every integer point on the hyperplane is visible and the lemma holds because the product is empty. It is left to show the lemma for
b>1
since the density will be the same for
\pm b
.

Fix
b>1
, set
\Delta(r)=\mathcal{H}\cap[-r,r]^{n}
,
\Delta_{1}(r)\subset\Delta(r)
the subset of integral points and
\Delta_{0}(r)
the subset of visible points. For
r\geq b
, the diameter of
\Delta(r)
is
\omega_{r}=2r\sqrt{n-1}
so
O(\omega_{r}^{n-2})=O(r^{n-2})
. Therefore, by Theorem
2.1
,

\left||\Delta_{0}(r)|-\left(\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n-1}}\right)\mathrm{vol}(\Delta(r))\right|=\left||\Delta_{0}(r)|-\left(\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n-1}}\right)(2r)^{n-1}\right|=O(r^{n-2}).

Since
|\Delta_{1}(r)|=(2r+1)^{n-1}
, we see that

\rho(\mathcal{H})=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{|\Delta_{0}(r)|}{|\Delta_{1}(r)|}=\left(\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n-1}}\right)\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{(2r)^{n}}{(2r+1)^{n}}=\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n-1}}.

∎

To make the calculation of the density of visible points on arbitrary hyperplanes and their intersections easier, we act on them by
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. Lemma
3.2
notes that the action of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
preserves visibility of integral points. Though certainly well known, we include a proof here for completeness.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
{\vec{x}}
be a point in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Then
\gcd({{\vec{x}}})=\gcd(A{\vec{x}})
for any
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is generated by transvection matrices
T_{ij}
for
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
with 1’s on the diagonal and in the
(i,j)
entry, and 0’s elsewhere (
[
9
]
). Moreover,
\gcd(T_{ij}{\vec{x}})=\gcd(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i}+x_{j},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})=\gcd({\vec{x}})
. Therefore, for any
A\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
,
\gcd(A{\vec{x}})=\gcd({\vec{x}})
. If
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
with
\det(A)=-1
, then
A=D(DA)
with
D
the diagonal matrix with entries
[-1,1,\ldots,1]
and
DA\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
. Since
D
has no effect on the
\gcd
,
\gcd(A{\vec{x}})=\gcd(D(DA\vec{X}))=\gcd(DA{\vec{x}})=\gcd({\vec{x}})
.
∎

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{s}
be
s
hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a common integral point
{\vec{p}}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and equations
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}x_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}p_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq s
. Set
A
to be the
s\times n
matrix
A=(a_{ij})
so that
{\vec{x}}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{H}_{i}
iff
A{{\vec{x}}}=A{{\vec{p}}}
.

By the existence theorem for the Smith Normal Form (e.g.,
[
22
, Thm. 1.11]
) there exist matrices
U\in\mathrm{GL}_{s}(\mathbb{Z})
and
V\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
so that
UAV=D
where
D
is a
s\times n
matrix whose off diagonal entries are 0 and whose diagonal entries,
d_{i}
, are the invariant factors of the matrix
A
. That is,
d_{i}\mid d_{i+1}
and
\prod_{j\leq i}d_{j}=\gcd(i\times i\textrm{ minors of }A)
(
[
22
, Def 1.6]
). Since
\gcd(a_{ij})=1
, we have
d_{1}=1
and also
d_{i}=0
for all
i>m
where
m
is the rank of
A
.

Since
{\vec{x}}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{s}\mathcal{H}_{i}
if and only if
A{\vec{x}}=A{\vec{p}}
, using the SNF matrices above, we see that
{\vec{x}}\in\bigcap\mathcal{H}_{i}
if and only if
DV^{-1}{\vec{x}}=DV^{-1}{\vec{p}}
. Consider the change of variables
{\vec{y}}=V^{-1}{\vec{x}}
and note that by Lemma
3.2

5
Structure of Density Sets

We conclude with an investigation into the structure of the set of possible densities of visible points on hyperplanes for a fixed dimension. For each
n\geq 1
, define the density set to be

D_{n}=\bigg\{\prod_{p|b}1-\frac{1}{p^{n}}:b\in\mathbb{Z}\bigg\}\subset\left[\frac{1}{\zeta(n)},1\right].

D_{n}
is also the range of the Jordan totient quotient
{J_{n}(b)}/{b^{n}}
, which
by Corollary
3.4
is the set of possible densities of visible lattice points on a hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Note that a density of
{1}/{\zeta(n)}
is achieved by hyperplanes of the form
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{x}=0
and a density of
1
is achieved by hyperplanes of the form
\vec{a}\cdot{\vec{x}}=\pm 1
. We are interested in
D_{n}
and its closure,
\overline{D}_{n}
, in the interval
\big[{1}/{\zeta(n)},1\big]
.

For
n=1
,
\big[{1}/{\zeta(n)},1\big]=[0,1]
. The densities of lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are of the form
\prod_{p|b}1-p^{-1}=\frac{\varphi(b)}{b}
. Not every rational density will occur because when in lowest terms these rational numbers will all have square free denominators, so there is no line with density
1/4
, say. However, Schinzel and Sierpiński (
[
27
]
, see also
[
25
, pg. 38]
) proved that the set
\left\{{\varphi(b)}/{b}\,|\,b\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is dense in
(0,1)
, so
\overline{D}_{1}=[0,1]
and therefore, for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
we can find a line with asymptotic density as close as we like to
\alpha
.

.

Using the greedy algorithm, the first three primes used in the approximation of

are

, giving the three approximations

. Set

. By Corollary

is used so that the

Theorem 5.2
.

For

1-\frac{1}{2^{n}}<\prod_{p\neq 2}1-\frac{1}{p^{n}}

In particular,

is not dense in the interval

Proof.

Since elements of
D_{n}
look like

, the fact that the intersection of the two sets is empty is easy to see; if
2|b
then
\prod_{p|b}1-1/p^{n}\leq 1-1/2^{n}
and if

then
\prod_{p|b}1-1/p^{n}

and hence greater than it.

To show the inequality, multiply both sides by

\zeta(n)<(1-1/2^{n})^{-2}

In particular, it is enough to show the following bound on the tail of

\sum_{k\geq 3}\frac{1}{k^{n}}<\frac{1}{2^{n}}+\sum_{k\geq 3}\frac{k}{2^{n(k-1)}}.

(14)

This is true for all

\sum_{k\geq 3}\frac{1}{k^{n}}<\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{n}}\,dx=\frac{1}{n-1}\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}

) decreases the lower bound:

\sum_{q>p}\frac{1}{q^{n}}\geq\frac{n}{p^{n-1}(\log p^{n-1}+1)}-\frac{\pi(p)}{p^{n}}

(17)

In Theorem
5.4
and Lemma
5.5
we start with (
17
) and then use different upper bounds on
\pi(x)
to show
a_{p}\leq_{q>p}a_{q}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

For
n\geq 2
,
\overline{D}_{n}
is a finite union of compact intervals.

Proof.

By
[
26
, Thm.2]
for
x\geq e^{3/2}\approx 4.5
,
\pi(x)<x/(\log x-3/2)
. Putting this into (
17
) we get for
p\geq 17

\sum_{q>p}\frac{1}{q^{n}}\geq\frac{n}{p^{n-1}(\log p^{n-1}+1)}-\frac{1}{p^{n-1}\log p}=\frac{\log p-\frac{3}{2}n-1}{p^{n-1}(\log p^{n-1}+1)(\log p-\frac{3}{2})}.

(18)

We can now compare
\sum_{q>p}a_{q}\geq\sum_{q>p}\frac{1}{q^{n}}
with
a_{p}\leq\frac{1}{p^{n-1}}
:

\frac{1}{a_{p}}\sum_{q>p}a_{q}\geq\frac{\sum_{q>p}\frac{1}{q^{n}}}{1/{(p^{n}-1)}}\geq\frac{(p^{n}-1)(\log p-\frac{3}{2}n-1)}{p^{n-1}(\log p^{n-1}+1)(\log p-\frac{3}{2})}.

Since the right hand side goes to infinity as
p\to\infty
there exists a prime
p_{n}
so that for all
p\geq p_{n}
,
a_{p}\leq\sum_{q>p}a_{q}
.
∎

We conclude by explicitly computing the gaps in the set
D_{2}
, that is the set of possible densities of planes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
D_{2}
is shown in Figure
3
along with the endpoints of the empty intervals.

Blue points are the values of
J_{2}(b)/b^{2}
for all
b

\overline{D}_{2}
is given in Theorem

.

Lemma 5.5
.

Fix
n=2
so that
a_{p}=-\log(1-1/p^{2})
. For every prime
p\geq 5
,
a_{p}\leq\sum_{q>p}a_{q}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
5.4
, it is enough to show
\sum_{q>p}1/q^{2}\geq 1/(p^{2}-1)
for all
p\geq 5
. By
[
26
, Cor.1]
,
\pi(x)<{1.25506x}/{\log(x)}
. Putting this into (
17
) we get, for
p\geq 17
,

\displaystyle\sum_{p<q}\frac{1}{q^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{p(\log p+1)}-\frac{1.25506}{p\log p}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{p(\log p+A\log p)}-\frac{1.25506}{p\log p}

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{.2}{p\log p}.

Here
A=2/1.45506-1
is chosen so that
A\log p\geq 1
for
p\geq 17
so that inequality (
19
) holds. It is not hard to check that
.2/(p\log p)>1/(p^{2}-1)
for all
p\geq 17
, so the desired inequality holds for all
p\geq 17
. That leaves the primes
p=5,7,11,13
. It is easiest to check
\exp(a_{p})\leq\exp(\sum_{q>p}a_{q})
by hand for these primes. For example, in the
p=5
case this is equivalent to checking
(1-1/4)(1-1/9)(1-1/25)^{2}=384/625\geq 6/\pi^{2}=1/\zeta(2)
.
∎

The inequality from Lemma
5.5
fails to hold for
p=2,3
because
(1-1/4)^{2}<6/\pi^{2}
and
(1-1/4)(1-1/9)^{2}<6/\pi^{2}
. As found in the “First Gap Lemma” (
[
7
, 21.9]
) these inequalities tell us exactly where the gaps are in
D_{2}
.

Theorem 5.6
.

\overline{D}_{2}=\big[\frac{6}{\pi^{2}},\frac{2}{3}\big]\cup\big[\frac{27}{4\pi^{2}},\frac{3}{4}\big]\cup\big[\frac{8}{\pi^{2}},\frac{8}{9}\big]\cup\big[\frac{9}{\pi^{2}},1\big]

Proof.

By Lemma
5.5
, for
p\geq 5
,
a_{p}\leq\sum_{q>p}a_{q}
. Thus, by
[
17
]
every element in the interval
[0,\sum_{q\geq 5}a_{q}]
is a subsum of the series
\sum_{q\geq 5}a_{q}
. Exponentiating, we see that the partial products of
\prod_{q\geq 5}(1-1/q^{2})
are dense in
[\prod_{p\neq 2,3}1-1/p^{2},1]=[9/\pi^{2},1]
and hence
\overline{D}_{2}\supset[9/\pi^{2},1]
.

By adding
a_{2}
to the subsums of
\sum_{q\geq 5}a_{q}
, we see that every element in
[a_{2},a_{2}+\sum_{q\geq 5}a_{q}]
is a subsum of the series
\sum a_{q}
. In particular,

\overline{D}_{2}\supset\big[\prod_{p\neq 3}1-1/p^{2},3/4\big]=\big[\frac{27}{4\pi^{2}},\frac{3}{4}\big]

Adding
a_{3}
or
a_{2}+a_{3}
to the interval behaves accordingly, showing
\overline{D}_{2}
contains the sets. On the other hand, any element in
\overline{D}_{2}
is in one of these sets. Which one is determined by whether or not the product includes the primes 2 and 3.
∎
