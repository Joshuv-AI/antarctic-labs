ABSTRACT
This report supplements the Verification of Empire-Cable SAND report[1] by expanding on the use
of Xyce to simulate the coupling to a transmission line cable model. While Empire-Cable solves its
governing equations on a high-order, finite-element mesh with an an implicit-in-time formulation,
Xyce must use a first order graph for the circuit and explicit-in-time approach to be compatible
with non-linear electrical device models[2]. Thus, given the different solution methodologies in
Xyce as compared to Empire-Cable, the convergence rates are expected to be different but the
overall quality of the solution should be the same. The original four canonical problems studied
in the Empire-Cable verification report are replicated here running in Xyce using transmission
line modeling parameters from the verification report. Overall, agreement between the codes is
excellent with Xyce’s convergence rates being limited mostly to first order due to the circuit network
approximation of a transmission line being a first order approximation.

1. INTRODUCTION

The Empire-Cable verification report established the accuracy and convergence rate of Empire-
Cable on a set of transmission line coupling problems that have analytic solutions. [1]. Empire-Cable
uses finite-elements in the spatial domain and an implicit time-integration formulation. This allows
Empire-Cable to solve problems at a high-order of accuracy and achieve second-order convergence
with mesh refinement.
Xyce is an electrical circuit simulator that follows the electrical design pattern of describing electrical
circuits with discrete parts such as resistors, diodes and transistors. In such a framework the circuit
topology is a graph which describes the connectivity of the electrical models. The individual
electrical models implement the physics dictating how they respond to voltage and current. This
formulation is well known within the circuit design community and is represented as a system of
differential-algebraic equations (i.e. DAE’s) [3, 4, 5].
Since the circuit network is a discrete graph of connected devices, it cannot be refined in the same
way that a finite-element mesh can nor can high-order approximations to derivatives be done on a
graph of circuit nodes. Thus, circuit simulators operate in a largely first-order spatial domain. In
this report, the circuit model of the transmission line can be refined by implementing it as more
sub-components. This greatly helps with the spatial resolution of the transmission line but it is still
only a first-order approximation in the spatial domain.
Because the physics being modeled by the electrical components is often non-linear, the DAE
formulation does not easily allow for implicit time integration. Time integration algorithms in
Xyce use variable time stepping coupled with a predictor/corrector step-error-control algorithm to
maintain accuracy within specified tolerances. In highly periodic circuits, Xyce can operate at up
to 5th order in time, but such periodicity is rare in real circuits.
Given the differences in how Empire-Cable and Xyce formulate and solve the transmission line
coupling problems within this document, it is expected that they will have different convergence
behavior. In these problems Xyce will be operating largely at first order while Empire-Cable will
be largely at second order. Default time-integration and solver tolerances are used in Xyce to best
capture how Xyce would normally be used.
In the following sections this work will cover how the transmission line equations are implemented
in Xyce and then dive into the four problems studied in the Empire-Cable verification report.

2.1. Theoretical notes

The process of homogenization in Empire-Cable produces two particular matrices stored in a matrix
market format, the Capacitance matrix C and Inductance matrix L, and a vector stored in matrix
market format, the line source vector Y, which are used as inputs to the transmission line subcircuit
generator script. Two notes should be made here.
First, the Y source term appears on the same side as the time derivative of the voltage term in
the Empire-Cable formulation of the Telegrapher’s equations, whereas the B current source term
appears on the opposite side of the time derivative of the voltage term in the Xyce modified nodal
analysis equations. Thus, the transmission line generator script supplies the factor of −1 in the
source terms of the subcircuit, due to the choice of B current sources oriented from ground to the
conductor; note that no such factor would be necessary if the B current source was chosen to be
oriented from each conductor to ground. This sign convention is tested explicitly in the fourth of
the four verification problems reported.
Second, and more notably, the capacitance matrix produced by Empire-Cable through homoege-
nization is what is commonly referred to as the Maxwell capacitance matrix, in order to distinguish
it from the circuit capacitance matrix. The entries of this Maxwell capacitance matrix, however,

3. NUMERICAL VERIFICATION

The Empire-Cable verification report documents four cable modeling problems that have analytic
solutions. For each problem as solution was simulated and then the relative error in voltage, 𝜖 𝑣
and current, 𝜖𝑖 is calculated compared to the analytic solution. See equations 2.20, 2.40, 2.41
in [1]. Additionally, the rate of change in relative error when the transmission line is more finely
discretized can be used to calculate a convergence rate 𝑝 as in equation 2.2 and 2.3 in [1].

In the following sections each of the following problems are modeled in Xyce:

 • Problem 1: 1D planewave, single inner conductor with outer shield

 • Problem 2: 1D planewave branched single inner conductor with outer shield

 • Problem 3: Two inner conductor with outer shield, planewave

 • Problem 4: Two inner conductor with outer shield and line current source

For each problem, the transmission line is modeled at four resolutions with relative error and
convergence rates calculated. In each convergence study the lines are modeled with 200, 400, 800
and 1600 segments. In the convergence plots, the segment size is plotted on the x-axis. Thus,
the order of the data is the 1600 segment simulation followed by the 800 segment simulation,
etc. Additionally, Xyce has two time integration methods, trapezoid and Gear, both are used to
document their performance. As the transmission line resolution is refined, the maximum allowed
time-step is also reduced at the same rate – for every doubling of the number of segments in the
transmission line, the maximum time step is halved.

3.5. Limiting Time Integration Order

In the prior simulations the time integration algorithm is a variable order scheme. Specifically,
based on the truncation error for the prior steps, the time integrator may increase the order of the
algorithm to second or higher. For very smooth signals, even at small time steps the time integration
order can rise above 1. Thus, as a final test of the convergence for these problem, all of them were
re-run with the time integration order fixed at 1. Table 3-6 shows the results:
In all case the convergence rate is near 1.0. Thus it is the variable order of the time integration
scheme that allows for very high convergence rates above 1.0 in the earlier results. Fixing the time
integration order at 1 with a largely first order spatial model of the transmission line yields first
order convergence as the system is refined.

4. CONCLUSIONS

This work demonstrates that modeling a transmission line as a circuit model for electro-magnetic
coupling problems is useful and can be done at high accuracy. Additionally, converting what would
be piecewise-constant sources to piecewise-linear or fixing the time integration order can greatly
stabilize convergence rates. The problems investigated here have been incorporated into Xyce’s
regular, nightly testing suite and the scripts and full test problems are part of the Commodore git
repository for preservation and maintenance.
