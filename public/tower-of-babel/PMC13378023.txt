Helminth proteins recapitulate key structural motifs of MIF to facilitate immunomodulation of host receptors
Authors: Jacob A Orkwis, Ramu Manjula, Elias J Lolis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryParasite-derived homologs of the cytokine macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) function as virulent factors during parasitic infection. Recent evidence suggests MIF-like products from multicellular helminthic species can be targeted to ameliorate parasite burden. Here, we identify a broad contingent of hypothetical MIF-like proteins from genomic helminth repositories and perform structure analysis to validate conserved homology. Further, we employ a diverse subset of MIF-specific assays to establish cross-species functionality of helminth MIF proteins, including native enzymatic activity, binding to cognate receptor CD74, direct interactions with human MIF, and signaling through chemokine receptors CXCR2 and CXCR4. We demonstrate that MIF-like proteins retain a preserved architecture but are capable of diverse physiological outcomes due to small changes in key components of the conserved MIF structure. This work simultaneously provides a mechanistic understanding of helminth virulence upon infection, while broadly examining the potential to neutralize MIF-like proteins for protection against various pathological species.Subject areas: Biological sciences

Introduction

The human protein macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) was first identified in 1966.1 Generally regarded as a pro-inflammatory mediator of the mammalian immune system, systemic MIF expression has been implicated as a driver of various disease pathologies, including sepsis,2 rheumatoid arthritis,3 atherosclerosis,4 and various cancers.5,6 Recent work has elucidated a diverse “superfamily” of MIF-like proteins appearing in various evolutionary taxonomies. In particular, numerous parasite species secrete structural homologs to MIF, or the human orthologue D-dopachrome tautomerase (DDT), that function as immunomodulatory cytokines upon the infection of a host species.7,8,9 Similarly, some parasites express structurally orthologous proteins to other major inflammatory human cytokines, or associated receptors, such as tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFαR10) and transforming growth factor (TGFβ11). In many instances, the mechanistic interplay between parasite protein orthologues and the host immune response is strikingly unclear. There is a general, emerging hypothesis that parasite-derived MIF proteins modulate the initial host immune response to sustain infection.12,13,14 By contrast, it has been shown that host MIF is a critical component of innate immune responses to helminthic Heligmosomoides polygyrus12 and filarial nematode Brugia malayi parasite infection.14 Due to dueling evolutionary pressures, the counter-regulatory signaling effects between parasite-derived MIF and human MIF (hMIF) must be further examined.

Apart from a lack of direct mechanistic insights, the evolutionary significance of cross-kingdom structural homology is likewise undescribed. Adding to the complexity, recent work on plant-derived MIF/DDT-like proteins (MDLs) has elucidated a capacity of MDL proteins to stimulate human chemokine (and non-cognate MIF) receptors CXCR2 and CXCR4, exemplifying a cross-kingdom protein-protein symbiosis that augments the native inflammatory functionality of hMIF.15 Preliminary evidence also points to interactions of some parasitic MIF orthologues, such as the malaria-causing Plasmodium falciparum (PfMIF), in stimulating CXCR2 and CXCR4 via simple experimental models.16 While hMIF has long been an elusive target for drug development, there is evidence that the targeting of parasitic MIF protein may effectively ameliorate parasite burden and induce prophylactic protection against infection. For example, helminthic Ancylostoma ceylanicum MIF (aceMIF) enzymatic activity can be specifically targeted by small molecule inhibitors to identify potential anti-helminthic remedies.17,18 Additional work has likewise established a prophylactic capacity for protozoan MIF-like proteins, in which the amelioration of murine malaria-causing Plasmodium berghei can be achieved by neutralizing its secreted MIF homolog.19

Here, MIF-like sequences were identified by the examination of deposited databases in comparison to existing MIF proteins. H. polygyrus was selected as an optimal model for helminthic immunomodulatory byproducts due to its extensive characterization in infections of rodent models and corresponding gastrointestinal dysregulation.20 Necator americanus, a soil-borne infective helminth parasite, has likewise received attention for use in the immunomodulation of irritable bowel disease symptoms.21 Both helminthic species impart a significant disease burden on developing healthcare systems, where gold-standard therapeutics are often inadmissible for extended use, are difficult to administer effectively, and present a risk of adaptable parasite resistance at the population level.22,23 Therefore, identification of novel anti-helminthic targets is necessary, while characterization of potential antigen agents such as helminth MIF (helMIF) should also be prioritized.24,25

This work begins to decipher the structural homology of a broader range of MIF-like proteins from multicellular, helminthic parasite species (helMIFs), N. americanus (naMIF), and H. polygyrus (hpMIF). Using modified biochemical and biophysical characterization techniques that were originally developed for hMIF, this approach establishes that helminthic parasite-derived protein orthologues can interact with mammalian host receptors to promote physiologically relevant outcomes, including direct hMIF-helMIF interactions, helMIF—human CD74 interactions, and stimulation of human chemokine receptors CXCR4 and CXCR2. These data ultimately demonstrate that the structural homology in the MIF superfamily supports a conserved function across taxa to signal with mammalian host receptors, but key variations to MIF active sites, allosteric regulatory motifs, and chemokine-binding motifs explain the diverse biochemical outcomes possible.

Results

helMIFs are homologous to hMIF and retain key catalytic pocket residues

The distinctive MIF architecture was conserved across each of the N. americanus and H. polygyrus helMIF structures (Figure 1A; Table S1). This homology was maintained despite variances in primary sequences, where N. americanus MIF1 (naMIF1) and H. polygyrus MIF1 (hpMIF1) shared 73.95% identity, while naMIF2 and hpMIF2 shared 81.74% identity. By contrast, the inter-species identity between numerically mismatched parasitic orthologues was only 25.22% (for naMIF1 and hpMIF2) and 23.48% (for naMIF2 and hpMIF1), respectively (Table S2). The conserved homology among helMIF proteins is predominantly reflected by the distinctive orthosteric site, where “primary” catalytic residues Pro-1, Lys-32, and Ile-64, those which bind directly to ISO-1 through hydrogen bonds in co-crystal structures, are ubiquitously conserved across helMIF proteins (Figure S1A). Interestingly, non-primary catalytic residues of hMIF-those which do not bind directly to substrates but exhibit hydrogen bonds, stacking interactions, and van der Waals forces with side-chains to stabilize the active site-are also indirectly implicated in binding to the MIF inhibitor ISO-1 ((S,R)-3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-4,5-dihydro-5-isoxazole acetic acid methyl ester26), such as Tyr-95, and are present in some helMIF proteins, including naMIF2 and hpMIF2, but not all. Whereas Asn-97, which is likewise a critical binding residue for ISO-1, is ubiquitously replaced in all known helMIF domains (Figure S1A). From a tertiary perspective, the “primary” catalytic residues are conserved in the monomeric subunit directly interacting with ISO-1 (Chain A), while “non-primary” residues interact through an adjacent monomer (chain C).

Despite overall homology, minor differences in the structural alignment of helMIF proteins were consistent with variances in amino acid sequence (Figures 1B and S1B; Table S2). Namely, the alignment of intra-species pairs with less than 40% identity (naMIF1 and naMIF2 or hpMIF1 and hpMIF2) is illustrated by an RMSD of 0.823 and 0.923, respectively, while the structural alignment of interspecies proteins (naMIF1 and hpMIF1, naMIF2 and hpMIF2) exhibits an RMSD of 0.548 and 0.480, respectively (Table S2).

There are 14 residues that are ubiquitously conserved between hMIF and each of the four helMIFs (Figure 1C), 12 of which are also conserved in the sequence of DDT. As these residues are implicated in different functional outcomes, it is notable that the architecture of conserved motifs is consistent across species in regions that describe the MIF catalytic site, allosteric modalities, and the central solvent channel (Figure 1C). Further, the tertiary structure of two α-helices transposed opposite five successive β-strands describes a bona fide structure model for most MIFs and microbial enzymes 4-oxalocrotonate tautomerase (4-OT) and 5-(carboxymethyl)-2-hydroxymuconate isomerase (CHMI) (Figures 1D and S1C).27

helMIF structure homology is maintained in the catalytic pocket but diverges at central solvent channels

Previous crystallographic analysis has identified allosteric regulators of MIF catalysis that function through interactions with water molecules permeating the distinctive central solvent channel. Enzymatic activity is thus indirectly amendable by the morphology and physicochemical properties of the central cavity, where larger channels with negatively charged residue motifs reduce the tautomerization of MIF substrates.28 Here, the electrostatic potential of the naMIF1 and hpMIF1 solvent channels was similar to hMIF, while naMIF2 and hpMIF2 displayed central channels with starkly negative electrostatic potential (Figure 2A). Further, while the size and geometry of naMIF1 and hpMIF1 central channels were likewise emblematic of hMIF, naMIF2 and hpMIF2 saw much broader, deeply defined surface residues dispersed around the channel (Figure 2A), with a clear surface-accessible channel identified in naMIF2.

In contrast to the allosteric divergence identified in the central solvent channel, the residue interactions governing the primary catalytic pocket are well preserved. The Pro-1 base that facilitates the tautomerization of non-physiological substrates has emerged as one of the few keystone residues in the expanded MIF proteome. An atomic-level examination of modeled helMIF proteins reveals specific variations in the predicted hydrogen bonds that stabilize the relevant residues and bound HPP (Figures 2B and S2A), as well as variances in adjacent catalytic pocket residues that have been identified as regulators of MIF enzymatic activity, including Met-2, His-62, Tyr-95, Asn-97, and Val-106 (Figures 2C and S2B). Namely, hMIF exhibits two bonds of similar length between HPP, Lys-32, and Ile-64. By contrast, each of the predicted helMIF contacts suggests promiscuity is possible between the same HPP regions and either Lys-32 or Ile-64, with large variances in predicted length. The overall pocket size varies only slightly between each structure model, while physicochemical properties of allosteric regulators, primarily in the electrochemical depictions of the central solvent channel in naMIF2 and hpMIF2, are much more divergent within the helMIF proteome (Figure 2A).

Specific side-chain residue interactions between hMIF and surrounding water molecules within the solvent channel have likewise been identified to facilitate the described allosteric modulation of the catalytic pocket. Hydrophobic interactions between Pro-1 and Tyr-99, as well as hydrogen bonds facilitated by Met-2 and His-62 in conjunction with water molecules accessing the central solvent channel, coordinate to regulate MIF function.28 NaMIF1 and hpMIF1 both share a Met-2 residue, but all helMIFs lack a His-62, a Phe-99, and associated π-stacking (Figures 2D and S1A). It is intriguing that naMIF1 and hpMIF1 present a similarly aromatic Phe-99 residue in place of Tyr-99, while naMIF2 and hpMIF2 present a non-aromatic Val-99. In contrast to MIF, DDT is less susceptible to allosteric regulation, in part due to a divergent Phe-2-Ser-62-Phe-99 motif.29 Similarly, naMIF1 and hpMIF1 retain a Met-2-Glu-62-Phe-99 motif, while naMIF2 and hpMIF2, by contrast, display a Val-2-Lys-62-Val-99 motif (Figure 2D). In all helMIFs, the Met-2 or Val-2 residue forms shorter hydrogen bonds to either Glu-62 or Lys-62, respectively (Figure 2D). However, the existence of a Phe-99 in conjunction with a charged Glu-62 or Lys-62 ablates the possibility of His-62 and Tyr-99 π-π interactions that stabilize adjacent β-strands in the MIF backbone. While the MIF catalytic pocket is remarkably conserved across helMIFs, the variable allosteric residue patterns identified in non-mammalian MIF orthologs must be assessed via enzymatic assays to establish the importance of the described evolutionary divergences.

helMIFs demonstrate partial enzymatic activity

As expected, helMIF catalysis was reduced compared to hMIF (Figure 2E). NaMIF1, which exhibited allosteric regulatory dynamics and catalytic pocket morphology that were most similar to hMIF, demonstrated higher tautomerase activity than other helMIF proteins. Similar to hMIF, helMIF HPP concentrations reached a saturation point, wherein 2.5 mM HPP bound to hMIF had relatively less activity than concentrations between 1.0 and 2.0 mM. Each helMIF, by contrast, peaked in activity between 0.5 and 1.0 mM (Figure 2E).

Our previous work showed helminthic aceMIF exhibiting a Vmax and Km that were 3.4- and 6-fold less than hMIF, respectively, with a substrate specificity index nearly 1.7-fold higher.17,18 Here, all helMIFs exhibit at least a 3.4-fold reduction in Vmax and an 8-fold reduction in Km (Table S3). While naMIF1 exhibits the closest kinetics to aceMIF, relative to hMIF, hpMIF1 and hpMIF2 also show substrate specificity indices close to the human protein. These data are consistent with previous assessments of partial activity in non-mammalian MIF tautomerase assays.

helMIFs displace hMIF from cognate receptor sCD74

Past work has implicated Tyr-36, Lys-66, and Asn-109 in binding to CD74.30 Intriguingly, Tyr-36 and Lys-66 both feature variants that are consistent in H. polygyrus and N. americanus MIFs (Figure S1A). In naMIF1 and hpMIF1 (as well as DDT), Tyr-36 is replaced by Arg-36, while Lys-66 is replaced by Ser-66. NaMIF2 and hpMIF2, by contrast, also see a similar conserved mutation in which Lys-66 is replaced by Gly-66 in both species, and Asn-109 is replaced by Gly-109. Overall, Asn-109 from hMIF is conserved in all helMIFs except hpMIF1, Tyr-36 is conserved in both naMIF2 and hpMIF2, and Lys-66 is ubiquitously replaced.

In 3-dimensional space, some helMIF residue mutations present with nearly identical side-chain occupancy to the hMIF motif, such as the transposition of Ser-36 (hpMIF1) with Tyr-36 in (hMIF) or Gly-109 (hpMIF1) and Asn-109 (hMIF) (Figure 3A). However, the ablation of the Lys-66 residue in all helMIF models reflects a much more significant difference, whereby the hMIF Lys-66 is longer than any of the substituted residues in the same location (Figure 3A). It is possible that the altered presentation of Lys-66 allows helMIF binding to CD74 via an “angular” mechanism, similar to DDT. Such a phenomenon would be akin to the conserved allosteric motif divergences that were described in both helMIFs and DDT.

The ability to bind CD74 was assessed via a modified compound-capture ELISA assay using immobilized sCD74 as bait for biotin-hMIF in competition with helMIFs. At low concentrations of immobilized receptor, all helMIFs demonstrate partial ability to displace hMIF in a dose-dependent manner, albeit to a lower extent than hMIF itself or anti-CD74 (Figure 3B). The displacement of hMIF from sCD74 only occurs at concentrations of helMIF greater than 0.1 μM, where hMIF itself and anti-sCD74 displace hMIF at much lower concentrations. As the concentration of sCD74 increases to 2.6 and 26 ng μL−1, this effect wanes (Figures 3B and S2C). At sCD74 concentrations closer to the physiological equilibrium that would be expected between hMIF and sCD74 (2.6 ng μL−1), the overall displacement of hMIF becomes more variable with increasing doses of competitor, but all helMIF competitors are capable of displacing hMIF from sCD74 at greater concentrations (Figure S2C). Finally, at the highest concentrations of sCD74 (26 ng μL−1), low concentrations of MIF instead augment the binding of hMIF to sCD74 (Figure S2C). With increased MIF concentrations, at the highest concentrations of sCD74, all MIFs recapitulate their displacement of hMIF from sCD74, while hMIF and anti-CD74 do so most effectively (Figures 3B and S2C).

hMIF and helMIFs interact directly on magnetic pulldown

Some members of the MIF superfamily demonstrate remarkable synergism with hMIF receptors. This was observed, most notably, in the formation of higher-order, oligomeric complexes between MDLs and hMIF to promote a super-agonistic signaling effect.15 Direct interactions between parasite MIFs and hMIF have likewise been observed, but in less sophisticated systems.16

Given this peculiar cross-kingdom interaction, we probed the ability of each of the helMIFs to directly interact with hMIF through modified pulldown assays. Interestingly, each of the selected helMIFs demonstrates a capacity to bind to hMIF in a standard biotinylated bait/pulldown system (Figures 4A–4D). However, the specific structural facets governing direct MIF/MIF, or MIF/orthologue, interactions are still absent.

helMIF signal through chemokine receptors CXCR2 and CXCR4

MIF has been implicated in non-cognate receptor interactions that promote deleterious outcomes in numerous diseases. While the relationship between hMIF and either CXCR2 or CXCR4 has been described to promote monocyte and T cell recruitment in humans, recent work has begun to establish a conserved capacity within the MIF superfamily to promote similar signaling outcomes.31,32,33,34 It is therefore prudent to assess individual protein-receptor signaling modalities to examine the interaction in isolation, a demand that is critical due to the lack of MIF-CXCR2 and MIF-CXCR4 structural models.

Previous work has employed peptide spot array analysis, site-directed mutagenesis, and a CXCR4-dependent leukocyte migration assay to identify an Arg87-Leu88-Arg89 motif that extends hMIF’s N-like loop to one of the two canonical binding sites of CXCR4.35 The amino acid sequence of the helMIFs, notably, ubiquitously retains the Leu-88 motif but is lacking either of the flanking Arg-87/89 groups (Figure S1A). The N-like loop of hMIF is also conserved, with the majority of helMIF residues between residues 67 and 81 experiencing pairwise substitutions when the original hMIF motif is absent (Figure 5A). Similar to hMIF, the N-like loop region is ubiquitously presented in all helMIFs as surface-exposed residues circumscribing the catalytic pocket. Further similarities exist to hMIF, where the Leu-88 residue in each helMIF is positioned within the direct vicinity of the N-like loop (Figure 5A). It is believed that the RLR motif functions by extending the N-like loop in 3-dimensional space to facilitate binding to CXCR4 motifs.

The modified CY12946 strain emits a downstream luminescence signal inextricably tied to MAPK signaling, which can be induced by either endogenously administered cognate ligand CXCL12 (by subcloning into yeast shuttle vectors, Figures 5B and 5C) or exogenously administered protein agonist at excess concentrations (Figure 5D). This model was initially developed to isolate MIF-chemokine receptor interactions in a standardized system and has since been adopted for the identification of MIF-like proteins and MIF mutants that interact with chemokine receptors.

As expected, hMIF promoted CXCR4 signaling to a greater extent than CXCL12 (Figure 5D). Surprisingly, multiple helMIFs exhibited greater signaling outputs compared to hMIF. NaMIF1 consistently demonstrated signaling outcomes with over 100-fold greater RLU than hMIF. Further, naMIF2 and hpMIF1, which are relatively dissimilar structures, demonstrate a similar capacity to one another in promoting CXCR4 signaling (Figure 5D). It was likewise notable that hpMIF2 signaling, while comparable to exogenous CXCL12, was markedly reduced relative to other helMIFs, despite strong structural similarity to naMIF2 (Figures 5D and S1B).

All helMIFs promoted CXCR4 signaling in a dose-dependent manner, with each orthologue exhibiting sub-maximal signaling to excess concentrations (Figures 5D and S3A). ISO-1 treatment, when administered at varying concentrations relative to protein administration, inhibited MIF signaling in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 5E), where saturation was achieved at large concentrations of inhibitor (Figure S3B). AMD3100 was administered to regulate CXCR4 and showed that sub-molar concentrations (0.01×) imparted an inhibitory effect on the signaling of all helMIF proteins, except hpMIF2, which exhibits the least amount of native signaling capacity (Figure S3C).

Due to the remarkable synergism displayed between plant-derived MIFs (MDLs) and hMIF, we examined whether a similar phenomenon occurred between helMIFs and hMIF, in part providing context to an otherwise undescribed molecular mechanism of action. However, combinatorial assay of the 5 proteins (hMIF and 4 helMIFs) demonstrated a veritable lack of synergistic signaling, and in some cases reduced effect (Figure S4).

High-affinity hMIF interactions with CXCR2 have been characterized as a determinant of monocyte and T cell infiltration, thereby implicating hMIF-related chemokine signaling in numerous inflammatory conditions.36,37 It has been suggested that hMIF, as a chemokine-like factor, may bind CXCR2 with a similar mechanism to CXCL8, via a “two-site” model in which a pseudo-ELR motif works in conjunction with an N-terminal flexible loop to stimulate the binding of MIF to extracellular loops (ELs) 2 and 3. Further, R11A and D44A mutations ablate MIF/CXCR2 binding, corresponding downstream signaling, and leukocyte recruitment.36

Structurally, Arg-11 and Asp-44 are replaced in helMIF orthologues in a pairwise fashion, in which numerically matched protein pairs (naMIF1 and hpMIF1, naMIF2 and hpMIF2) with high sequence identity emerge with the same “mutation” at both residues. Specifically, Asp-44 in hMIF is replaced by Gly-44 in naMIF1 and hpMIF1 but retained by both naMIF2 and hpMIF2 (Figure S1A). Similar to hMIF, all helMIF proteins feature motif substitutions at positions 11 and 44 that present as surface-exposed residues circumscribing the central solvent channel (Figure 6A). Once more, all helMIFs promoted significantly greater signaling of CXCR2 in the yeast-reporter system than hMIF (Figure 6B). Notably, the signaling pattern roughly followed that of CXCR4, with the exception that hpMIF2 signaling was more comparable to hMIF. NaMIF1, again, exhibited a relative signaling output that was 100-fold greater than any other helMIF, while naMIF2 and hpMIF1 likewise presented signals consistently 10-fold greater than that of hMIF (Figure 6B).

Discussion

MIF functionality has been implicated in a diverse subset of physiological outcomes, including protein catalysis, regulation of mammalian immunity, and the pathology of numerous diseases and inflammatory conditions. These disparate outcomes are attributable, in part, to a physiological promiscuity relating to a unique molecular profile; MIF is simultaneously regarded as an enzyme, a cytokine with chemokine-like function, and a systemic regulator of immune cells. The expansion of the MIF superfamily, which now includes orthologues identified in plants, parasites, and bacteria, likewise reflects the expansive niche MIF fulfills. MIF-like proteins identified in both simple, unicellular protozoan parasites as well as more complex multicellular parasites, such as helminths, are often contextualized along with other parasite-derived immunomodulators.38,39 Parasite-derived orthologues tend to mimic signaling cytokines such as TNFαR and TGFβ, which natively contribute to cell survival, growth, differentiation, death, and microenvironmental formation in mammalian organisms, and facilitate additional roles in the response to tissue damage, immune regulation, and tumor suppression.40,41

The existence of non-mammalian cytokine orthologues is likely an evolutionary outcome to promote the virulence of infective species by subverting the native immune response of the host.42 However, the molecular profile of parasite excretory and secretory products is often complex. For example, helminth excretory and secretory (HES) products confer robust, sterile immunity to helminth infection in mouse models, but are comprised of a number of small molecules and metabolites that are largely uncharacterized, thus preventing the use of HES in antigen design.43,44 Therapeutically, many parasite-derived products impart acute physiological outcomes that may be appreciable for biomedicine, such as the ability of recombinant cytokine orthologues such as TGFβ to induce alternative Th2-immune responses that aid in the treatment of autoimmune disorders, including irritable bowel diseases.45,46

Native MIF functionality is still not totally understood due to a lack of co-crystal structural models examining MIF with its receptors CD74, CXCR2, and CXCR4. Nonetheless, some groups have used MIF peptide arrays and site-directed mutations to identify residues that contribute to binding.30,35,36,47 For instance, it has been established that the Pro-1 base of MIF, responsible for tautomerase activity, is also implicated in promoting the signaling of CD74.48 However, it has been shown through P1G mutants that MIF/CD74 binding can still occur, even in the absence of tautomerase activity and subsequent downstream CD74-associated MAPK signaling.30,49 Due to similarities of helMIF catalytic pockets, but vast divergences in catalytic activity, it is likely that the changes to allosteric sites are more complicit in altering physiological signaling. Notably, naMIF1 and hpMIF1, which exhibited significantly greater enzymatic activity, feature central solvent channels with similar physicochemical properties to hMIF.

While the catalytic activity of MIF is the initial focal point for the analysis of variants, emerging evidence suggests that partial signaling ability to chemokine receptors is likely a conserved MIF feature as well. Unexpectedly, however, many of the parasite-derived orthologues exhibit even stronger signaling effects to chemokine receptors than hMIF, in contrast to the subdued effects seen in parasite-derived MIF catalysis or stimulation of CD74.50 Further, recent evidence showed that CXCR2 and CXCR4 signaling by hMIF can be symbiotically upregulated by the addition of plant-derived MDLs, which form higher-order oligomeric complexes to promote markedly stronger signaling effects.15 Recently, one group has also demonstrated a capacity to use MIF as a building-block module for advanced, self-assembling computational protein design scaffolds (T33-28, PDB 4NWR),51 demonstrating immense potential for the MIF superfamily in the formation of tertiary protein complexes.

Despite observable cross-kingdom synergy, the preceding evolutionary pressures are unclear. Plant- and parasite-derived products should incur different signaling exigencies upon interaction with mammalian host organisms—whereby plants would likely benefit from products inducing pro-inflammatory signals, while parasites would likely benefit from anti-inflammatory effects. From this perspective, it is intriguing that the helMIFs do, in fact, signal strongly through CXCR2 and CXCR4 but do not appear to possess any real synergism with hMIF or capacity to upregulate CD74 signaling. Multiple helminth species, including Trichinella spiralis and AceMIF, produce MIF-like proteins that bind CD74 and promote macrophage activation thereafter.17,18,52 Further, the localization of hMIF, CD74, and CXCR2/4 at cell membranes intuitively suggests that parasite MIFs interacting with CD74 may likewise demonstrate signaling through chemokine receptors, but the evidence suggesting as much is absent.

Some protozoan-derived products, such as Trypanosoma cruzi recombinant protein P21, have demonstrated a capacity to bind CXCR4 on macrophages to stimulate immune cell chemotaxis and incur anti-oncogenic effects.53 Thus far, experimental evidence has only identified helminth-derived products that directly neutralize CXCR2-specific ligands, such as the S. mansoni chemokine-binding protein (smCKBP)-mediated neutralization of CXCL8.54 Broadly, an expansive catalog of individual helminth-derived products have been described to suppress inflammatory responses in simple models, including remodeling of T-regulatory cell (Treg) networks that are likely derivative of an impedance to the CXCL12 signaling axis.55 In that sense, it is possible that the strong signaling of helMIFs to chemokines is meant to displace cognate hMIF-, CXCL8-, or CXCL12-induced stimulation, but direct mechanistic insights remain the primary bottleneck to understanding this complexity.

The selection of two helminth species provides MIF-like products that are divergent from the primary sequence of hMIF but still retain structurally implicated residues for chemokine binding. The binding mechanism between MIF and CXCR4 was previously described by a 3-residue motif, Arg87-Leu88-Arg89.35 It is notable that Leu-88 is one of only 14 hMIF residues that are ubiquitously conserved in the helminth species described here, as well as a broader cohort of predicted helminth-derived MIFs. With associated functional assays displaying robust signaling effects on CXCR4 and CXCR2, the structural conservation of Leu-88 further elucidates the importance of the few ubiquitously conserved MIF residues. By contrast, the N-like loop itself, interestingly, shows little sequence identity between CXCL8, hMIF, or any of the helMIFs. The robust ability of helMIFs to signal through such receptors with only partially retained residues suggests there are more precise assessments needed to fully depict the structural insights in hMIF binding to chemokine receptors, as well.

The structure analysis also presented suggests a redundancy in MIF genetics that promotes pleiotropic outcomes emerging from generational evolutionary pressures. The robust, cross-kingdom structural homology that exists despite large discrepancies in primary sequences suggests that MIF structures are dependent on a few key residue motifs, which can be recapitulated in the proteome of simple organisms. By preserving these keystone motifs, parasites and other organisms mimic the MIF proteome with partial functionality, while the mutable intermediary amino acid sequences between keystone residues can be consistently amended by evolutionary pressures to ultimately prevent epitope recognition and humoral-mediated eradication by host organisms. This phenomenon is exemplified by the P. falciparum antigen PfEMP1, which diverges to a var gene ontology of nearly 60 variants.56 While MIF-like proteins feature a more conservative gene ontology and are less characterized for antigenic potential than PfEMP1, parasite MIFs (and other virulence factors) may have evolutionarily converged to a similar strategy of immunomodulatory redundancy.

Successful targeting of non-mammalian MIF orthologues may establish a paradigm for exploiting MIF-like proteins in numerous additional species, including intracellular blood-based parasites of the Leishmani family,57,58 agricultural fungal pathogens Magnaporthe oryzae,59 and even environmental threats such as Loingulodinum polyedra.60 By examining multiple MIF-like sequences from a diverse parasite cohort and establishing the importance of structural motifs with explicit functional outcomes, this work further unravels the immense pleiotropy of hMIF and the expanded MIF superfamily.

Limitations of the study

HelMIF structures with co-crystal models (HPP, ISO-1) were not solved within the purview of this manuscript. Further structural insight is necessary to better understand MIF interactions with the receptors CD74, CXCR2, and CXCR4, a problem that persists for both mammalian MIFs and parasitic orthologues.

Method details

Protein identification, expression, and purification

Two MIF-like proteins were identified in both H. polygyrus (hpMIF1, hpMIF2) and N. americanus (naMIF1, naMIF2) based on homology of deposited genomic nucleotide databases to existing MIF proteins in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database as well as WormBase ParaSite (UK MRC). Predicted amino acid transcript sequences61,62,63,64,65,66,67,68 were aligned by Clustal Omega Multiple Sequence Alignment (v1.2.4, EMBL-EBI,69,70 RRID:SCR_001591) and visualized with MView (v1.67,71 RRID:SCR_024129). The cDNA templates corresponding to each amino acid sequence (Figure S1A) were synthesized and subsequently subcloned into Escherichia coli (E. coli) expression vector pRSET_A by GeneArt custom gene synthesis services (Thermo Scientific). Constructs were transformed into electrocompetent BL21(DE3) Gold cells (Invitrogen) by heat shock at 42°C followed by expression in recovery medium.72 For large-scale purifications, seed cultures were inoculated in Luria Broth (Miller’s) media (LB, RPI) with appropriate antibiotic selection and grown overnight at 37°C with consistent shaking at 180 RPM. Cultures were diluted 1:100 and grown until OD600nm reached 0.6, before addition of 1.0 mM Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and further growth for 3.5 h. Resulting cultures were pelleted by centrifugation at 4500 RPM for 25 min at room temperature in an RC-3B refrigerated centrifuge (Sorvall). Proteins with theoretical isoelectric points (pI) greater than 7.7 were resuspended in 20 mM Tris, 20 mM NaCl, pH 7.4 (hMIF, hpMIF1), those with a pI below 7.7 (naMIF1, naMIF2, hpMIF2) were resuspended in 20 mM Tris, 20 mM NaCl, pH 8.0, and His-tagged variants of each protein were resuspended in 50 mM HEPES, 500 mM NaCl, pH 6.8.

E. coli cultures were lysed by addition of 1 U μL−1 DNAse I (Roche), one tablet of cOmplete™, Mini, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche), and 1× CellLytic™ Bacterial Cell Lysis Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich). Cultures were subjected to three passages through an Emusiflex C3 high-pressure homogenizer (Avestin). Insoluble material was pelleted by centrifugation at 12,000 RPM for 25 min at 4°C in an Avanti J-E centrifuge (Beckman Coulter). Soluble fractions were clarified and used for subsequent purification.

Human MIF and helMIF proteins were purified according to standard MIF purification schema, as described.26 Briefly, clarified lysates were subjected to sequential anion (Cytvia HiTrap QP FF) and cation (Cytvia HiTrap SP FF XL) exchange columns and eluted with a 0.5 M linear gradient of NaCl. Fractions were pooled, concentrated, filtered, and subjected to additional purification on Superdex S75 16/60 GL gel filtration columns (Cytvia).

The cDNA depicting the extracellular, soluble domain of CD74 (sCD7473−232)73 with a 6X-his tag was ligated to T7 expression vector Pet28a (+) (Novagen). His-tagged helMIF proteins and sCD74 were subsequently expressed in BL21(DE3) Gold cells and induced with IPTG, as described. Lysates were purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography using a 0.5 M linear gradient of imidazole. Resulting fractions were further purified by Superdex S75 16/60 GL gel filtration columns. Resulting protein fractions were examined on SDS-Page gels and pooled where purity exceeded 95%, before additional concentration and storage at −20°C. Protein concentration was estimated by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) plate-based assay where each sample was serially diluted and estimated with 4 replicate assay developments (Pierce).

Crystallography and structure determination

Protein samples were concentrated to at least 10 mg mL−1. All helMIF proteins were screened against Crystal Screen HT, Index Screen HT, and PegRX HT deep well blocks (Hampton). Preformulated screens were dispersed onto 96-well plates using an NT8 Drop Setter Crystallization Robot (Formulatrix, v.4.0) and subsequently imaged and analyzed via a RockImager 1000 Crystal Plate Automated Imaging System with UV-visualization (Formulatrix) processing in conjunction with Rock Maker Protein Crystallization Software (Formulatrix, v3.17.7.1). All helMIF proteins produced crystals under multiple formulations. Crystals were obtained for data collection from 60%v/v Tacsimate™ (naMIF1), 1.26 M sodium phosphate (NaH2PO4) with 0.14 M potassium phosphate (K2HPO4) (naMIF2), 0.1 M sodium acetate (CH3COONa) 4.5 pH with 25%w/v polyethylene glycol (PEG) 3350 (hpMIF1), and 0.8 M sodium succinate (C4H4Na2O4) (hpMIF2), where all chemicals were obtained from pre-made Hampton crystallization screens.

X-ray diffraction reflections of crystals were collected on the AMX-17 or FMX-17 beamline from the Brookhaven National Synchrotron Light Source (NSLS-II). Raw data was processed using the standardized NSLS-II autoProc (v1.0.5,74 RRID:SCR_015748) and FastDp (v1.6.2) pipelines, where reflections were indexed by XDS (v20220820,75 RRID:SCR_015652), assessed with pointless (v1.12.14,76 RRID:SCR_014218) and scaled with aimless (v0.7.9,77 RRID:SCR_015747) through the Collaborative Computational Project No.4 platform (CCP4, v.8.0.010,78 RRID:SCR_007255). Molecular replacement was performed using Phaser (v2.8.3,79 RRID:SCR_015747) with human MIF (PDB 1MIF or 3DJH) or predicted alphafold2 structure (AF-A0A183FYG9-F1) used as search models. 2|Fo| - |Fc| and |Fo| - |Fc| Fourier transformed maps were generated to display the electron density of each structure and the model generated from Phaser was refined through iterations of RefMac5 (v5.8.0419,80 RRID:SCR_014225) and manual model building in Coot (v0.9.8.95,81 RRID:SCR_014222) to determine the final structure. All structures were analyzed with MOLPROBITY (v4.5.1,82 RRID:SCR_014226) to ensure proper quality of generated models. Visual representations were created in PyMOL (Schrödinger LLC, v3.0.0, RRID:SCR_000305), whereby surface renderings, alignments, and predicted bonds were calculated. Electrostatic potential was calculated by the Adaptive Poisson-Boltzmann Solver (APBS, RRID:SCR_008387) plugin and represented by a surface-coded color gradient.83 Additional structure figures were rendered by UCSF Chimera (v1.18, RRID: SCR_004097) or UCSF ChimeraX (v1.10.1, RRID: SCR_015872). Alignments of produced models were produced by ChimeraX matchmaker.84

hMIF biotinylation

MIF protein was biotinylated using a biotin protein labeling kit (Roche) by adapting the manufacturer’s recommendation. Briefly, human MIF was diluted to 1.0 mg mL−1 in 1× PBS. D-Biotinoyl-ε-aminocaprioic acid-N-hydroxy-succinimide ester (Biotin-7-NHS solution, 20 mg mL−1) was diluted 1:10 in DMSO and added to the 1.0 mg mL−1 hMIF at a 1:5 M ratio (0.133 mg Biotin-7-NHS). A Sephadex G-25 column was equilibrated with 5 mL of biotin equilibration buffer before incubation with 6 consecutive 5 mL washes of 1× PBS and the reaction mixture of hMIF-biotin-7-NHS was applied in concert with an additional 1.5 mL of 1× PBS. Biotinylated hMIF was eluted with 2.5 mL of 1× PBS. Fractions were analyzed on SDS-page gel and pooled where biotin was successfully conjugated to hMIF.

MIF catalysis and sCD74 compound capture assay

For all biological assays, hMIF and helMIF proteins were further purified for removal of high affinity endotoxins (lipopolysaccharide, LPS) as previously described.85 Briefly, endotoxin-free phosphate buffered saline (pH 7.4) was incubated with hMIF or helMIF protein and 1% Triton X-114 (VWR). This mixture was cycled through 3-iterations of 30 min and 10-min incubations at 4 deg C and 37 deg C, respectively. Triton X-114 and LPS were removed by centrifugation and the supernatant (containing endotoxin-free MIF) was subjected to an additional purification step on S75 16 60 columns. Resulting protein that was used for assays was further dialyzed overnight in endotoxin-free PBS (pH 7.4) before concentration.

In vitro functionality assays were adapted from those designed to evaluate hMIF. Enzymatic activity was assessed as described.18 Briefly, the tautomerase activity of helMIFs was established by dilution of non-physiological substrate 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvic acid (HPP, Sigma-Aldrich) to 50 mM in 500 mM ammonium acetate, pH 6.0, and incubation overnight at 4°C to ensure equilibration of keto-enol compositions. helMIF proteins were then diluted to 50 nM in 424 mM boric acid before plating into a 96-well UV transparent bottom half-area plate (Corning, NY). HPP was added at variable concentrations between 0 and 2.5 mM and the formation of the borate-enol complex was detected by assessment of absorbance at 306 nm (ABS306nm) for at least 90 s on an M200 Infinite Plate Reader (Tecan). Initial velocities and corresponding non-linear regressions were calculated based on the rate of change occurring from 5 to 90 s. All data points were subjected to at least 4 replicate trials.

Modified compound-capture assays were adapted from protocols designed to evaluate the ability of hMIF to displace biotinylated-hMIF from the soluble form of its cognate physiological receptor CD74 (sCD74(73–232)).18,73 Briefly, competitive proteins (helMIFs, hMIF, and anti-sCD74) were dissolved to 10 mM in DMSO. EIA/RIA high-binding immunoassay 96-well plates (Costar 3690) were coated with varying concentrations (0.03 ng μL−1, 2.6 ng μL−1, 26 ng μL−1) of sCD74 in 1× PBS, pH 7.2, before overnight incubation in the dark at 4°C. Plates were washed with 20 mM tris-buffered saline Tween 20, pH 7.4 (TBST) and subsequently blocked with SuperBlock™ (PBS) Blocking Buffer (Thermo Scientific) before an additional overnight incubation. Compounds were further diluted to variable concentrations (0.0056 μM, 0.056 μM, 0.56 μM, and 5.6 μM) in 20 mM TBST and then immersed in an equal-volume solution of 2.2 ng μL−1 biotinylated-hMIF in 20 mM TBST. The resulting mixture was incubated with sCD74-coated plates overnight at 4°C in the dark. Final binding was detected by development of the biotinylated complex with addition of 1:1000 diluted (0.5 ng μL−1) phosphatase-labeled streptavidin (Thermo Scientific) and incubation for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Plates were washed again with 20 mM TBST, developed by dissolution of 2 4-Nitrophenyl phosphate disodium salt hexahydrate tablets (PnPP, Thermo Scientific) in 10 mL of diethanoloamine substrate buffer (Thermo Scientific), and incubated in PnPP-buffer solution for an additional 20 min. Plates were read at 405 nm (Abs405nm) on an M200 Infinite Plate Reader (Tecan, RRID:SCR_020543) with I-Control (v.3.9.1.0, RRID:SCR_024562), where resulting signals were adjusted for both blank background and relative to a negative control of DMSO signal detected in the absence of competitive compounds.

Magnetic pulldown of helMIFs

Binding of hMIF to helMIF protein was first assessed by incubation of equimolar quantity of biotinylated hMIF (biotin-hMIF) and corresponding helMIF overnight at 4°C. A volume of hydrophilic, streptavidin magnetic beads (NEB) corresponding to 30 μg of biotinylated protein per 1 mg of beads (4 mg mL−1 stock) was subjected to a Magnetic Separation Rack (NEB), per manufacturer recommendations. Suspension buffer was removed, and beads were subjected to three subsequent washes and magnetic separation of pulldown-equilibration buffer (1× PBS, pH 7.4, 0.01% w/v BSA, 0.05% Tween 20). Biotin-hMIF was mixed with the individual helMIFs and incubated along with the beads for 2 h. Supernatant was removed from the incubation and beads were once more subjected to three washes of equilibration buffer and corresponding magnetic separation. Resulting pulldowns were incubated with equal parts Laemilli buffer (BioRad) and separated by SDS-page electrophoresis on a NuPAGE Bis-Tris Mini Protein Gel (4–12%, Invitrogen). Gels were transferred to pre-equilibrated polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes using a Mini Trans-Blot Cell (BioRad) operating for 1 h at 100 V on ice. Membranes were blocked in 3% BSA in 1× TBST overnight and incubated in 1:1,000 rabbit anti-hMIF (R&D Systems) and 1:2,000 mouse anti-6XHIS primary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific) dilutions, respectively, for 1 h. Secondary antibodies were diluted 1:20,000 in 3% BSA and incubated with the corresponding primary antibody (Goat anti-rabbit, IRDye 800CW, LiCOR, Goat anti-mouse, IRDye 800CW, LiCOR) for 1.5 h at 4°C in the dark. Membranes were again washed in 1× TBST before development on an Odyssey CLX imager (RRID:SCR_014579) using LiCor Image Studio (v.6.1).

S. cerevisiae chemokine signaling system

A strain of Saccharomyces cerevisiae (CY12946) was engineered to conjoin chemokine receptor-induced MAPK signaling to β-galactosidase (β-Gal) reporter expression (lacZ), as described.86 The system was constructed to examine human chemokine receptors by replacing the native yeast pheromone receptor Ste2. Engineered genetic modifications inextricably tie stimulation of the human receptors to downstream yeast MAPK signaling that can be detected by the expression of β-Galactosidase and subsequent development of resulting luminescence signal.

Here, shuttle vectors encoding β-Gal (CP1584, TRP-) and CXCR2 or CXCR4 (CP4181, LEU-) were sequentially transformed to CY12946 using a modified lithium acetate procedure (TRAFO87) with selection by corresponding dropout media. Cells containing the plasmids CP1584 and CP4181 were grown in complete minimal dropout media (TRP-LEU-) at 30°C and 180 RPM for 72 h until reaching saturation (OD600nm = 1.30). A sample of this culture was diluted to 0.01 OD600nm in fresh media. Recombinant MIF protein was added86 and cultures were grown for an additional 1.5 h under the same conditions. Inhibition with MIF enzymatic inhibitor ISO-1 and AMD3100, a CXCR4 inhibitor, was performed in a dose-dependent assay under the same conditions. β-Gal expression was quantified by incubating equal volume solution of β-Glo reagent (Promega) with lysed cells for an additional 30 min, following manufacturer recommendations. The resulting luminescence signal was detected on an M200 Infinite microplate reader (Tecan, RRID:SCR_020543).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed in Prism (v10.4.1, GraphPad), whereby representative bar and scatterplots depict mean ± SEM. Significance tests were determined by One-Way or two-Way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc comparison of means where ∗p < 0.1, ∗∗p <0 .01, ∗∗∗p <0 .001, ∗∗∗∗p <0 .0001. Figures were collated from Prism in Photoshop (RRID:SCR_014199), with additional annotations and schematic representations depicted by Illustrator (RRID:SCR_010279).
