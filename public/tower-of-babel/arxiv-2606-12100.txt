Quasi-linear Time Multiplication of Sparse Polynomials with Integer Coefficients
Qiao-Long Huang \institution Shandong University, Yichuan Cao \institution State Key Laboratory of Mathematical Sciences, Academy of Mathematics and Systems Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences; University of Chinese Academy of Sciences, Ruichen Qiu \instref 2, Xiao-Shan Gao \instref 2 \Cauthor

Abstract
Sparse polynomial multiplication is a fundamental problem in computer algebra and the theory of computation, and the development of a quasi-linear time output-sensitive multiplication algorithm has been posed as an open challenge. In this paper, a counterexample is provided to a previously claimed solution to this open problem for integer coefficients. By employing the existing quasi-linear modular-black-box interpolation algorithm, we are able to provide an algorithm with quasi-linear bit complexity for the integer coefficients setting. Furthermore, in the case of coefficients over a finite field, we obtain an algorithm whose bit complexity is linear in the number of terms, the logarithm of the degree, and the logarithm of the size of the finite field.

Introduction

Polynomial multiplication is a fundamental algebraic operation that appears in many scientific and engineering computations. Consequently, it is also a basic issue in computer algebra and theory of computation
von zur Gathen and Gerhard 2013
;
Jin and Xu 2024
;

The classical multiplication algorithms for dense polynomials

;
Toom 1963
;
Cook and Aanderaa 1969
;
Schönhage and Strassen 1971
;
Cantor and Kaltofen 1991

In practice—and in computer algebra systems like Maple and Mathematica—polynomials are usually represented in a sparse format

For a sparse univariate polynomial
f
of degree

nonzero terms and whose coefficients have a maximum absolute value of

\text{size}(f)=O(T(\log D+\log H))=O(T\log(DH)).

The challenge in sparse polynomial multiplication is that the product is output-sensitive; that is, the size of the result cannot be deduced solely from the sizes of the inputs can can be exponential in the input size, as shown by the following example.

Example 1

(
Grenet 2026
)
.

Let

,

Note that

and

have the same support. However,

has

terms.

Therefore, the dense multiplication algorithms fail to accurately reflect the true complexity of the sparse setting, and meaningful sparse multiplication algorithms must be output-sensitive.
Open Problem 1 in

calls for the development of a quasi-linear, output-sensitive algorithm for sparse polynomial multiplication over an arbitrary ring

Develop an algorithm to multiply two sparse polynomials

Open Problem 2.
Develop an algorithm to multiply two sparse polynomials
f,g\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]\setminus{\mathbb{Z}}

\widetilde{O}(T\log(DH))

D=\max(\deg(f),\deg(g))

Since

3.1
Supplied Output-sparsity Multiplication

The integer result answers the supplied-output-sparsity form of Open
Problem 2. The problem asks for an algorithm to multiply two sparse
polynomials
f,g\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
using

\widetilde{O}\bigl(T\log(DH)\bigr)

D=\deg(fg),\qquad H=\max(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty},\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\infty},\left\lVert fg\right\rVert_{\infty}),\qquad T=\max(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{0},\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{0},\left\lVert fg\right\rVert_{0}).

Here
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{0}
denotes sparsity and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}

denotes the largest absolute coefficient. Changing the logarithm base changes
only absolute constants, which are absorbed by the soft-O notation.

Theorem 5

(Supplied output sparsity)
.

Assume that the sparsity parameter
T
in Open Problem 2 is supplied to the
algorithm. There is a Monte Carlo algorithm which returns the sparse
representation of
fg
with probability at least
2/3
and uses

\widetilde{O}\bigl(T\log(DH)\bigr)

Source theorem used.

The proof uses Theorem 3.4 of Giorgi, Grenet, Perret du Cray, and

implementation of the black-box probes also uses Fact 3.2 of the same
paper
Giorgi et al. 2022

5.

Give

, and sparsity bound

6.

When

Proof.

Put
h=fg
. Since
D=\deg h\geq 2
, the product is nonzero and nonconstant,
and, because

The sparse inputs therefore determine the degree bound

First consider the possible monomial case
T=1
. Then both inputs have one
term, say
f=ax^{r}
and
g=bx^{s}
. The algorithm returns

The coefficient multiplication costs
\widetilde{O}(\log(H))

and writing the exponent costs

It remains to treat

This implies

\log(DH_{0})=O(\log(DH)).

, and sparsity bound

For any modulus

\mathcal{B}_{h}(\theta,m)=\left(\sum_{a\in A}f_{a}\theta^{a}\right)\left(\sum_{b\in B}g_{b}\theta^{b}\right)\bmod m.

It remains to account for the cost of answering the structured probes used by
Theorem 3.4. In an interpolation round with residual sparsity bound
U
, the
algorithm asks for values of

, where

is its current partial

1,\omega,\omega^{2},\ldots,\omega^{2U-1}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{F}_{q},

The values of

are handled internally by the interpolation algorithm.
We supply the values of

by evaluating

and
g

and multiplying the corresponding values in the ambient ring.

Let
P(x)=\sum_{e\in E}c_{e}x^{e}
be either input polynomial, with

This is a transposed-Vandermonde product. Fact 3.2 of

For the shifted list, set
\alpha=1+q^{k}
. For every exponent

\alpha^{e}=(1+q^{k})^{e}\equiv 1+eq^{k}\pmod{q^{2k}},

because all binomial terms of order at least two are divisible by

Hence

which is again the same transposed-Vandermonde evaluation problem, now with

bit operations in the rings used by Theorem 3.4.

Therefore, in one interpolation round, all probe values for both inputs and

\widetilde{O}((\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{0}+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{0}+U)\log p)

The residual bounds

is
O(T)
. The fixed input sparsities
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{0},\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{0}\leq T
are
paid in only a logarithmic number of rounds, which is absorbed by the soft-O
notation. Thus the total cost of all black-box probe answers, pointwise

\widetilde{O}\bigl(T\log(DH_{0})\bigr)=\widetilde{O}\bigl(T\log(DH)\bigr).

The non-probe work of Theorem 3.4 and the cost of writing the sparse output are
within the same bound.

All degree, height, and sparsity preconditions for Theorem 3.4 have now been
met:

D\geq\deg h,\qquad H_{0}\geq\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\infty},\qquad T\geq\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{0}.

Consequently, outside the failure event allowed by Theorem 3.4, the
interpolation algorithm returns the sparse representation of
h=fg
. This
is exactly the stated Monte Carlo guarantee. The supplied-
T
theorem does
not claim a certified or Las Vegas algorithm.

3.2.1
Probability Analysis

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be the desired overall error bound.
Define
\delta=\varepsilon/2
and let
k_{\max}
and
r
be as in the algorithm, with

\beta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2(k_{\max}+1)r}.

Let
T=\max(\|f\|_{0},\|g\|_{0},\|fg\|_{0}))
.
There exists a unique
k^{*}
such that
2^{k^{*}-1}<T\leq 2^{k^{*}}
(or
k^{*}=0
if
t^{*}=1
).

Correct guess.

For the correct guess
t=2^{k^{*}}\geq T
, the base multiplication algorithm (Algorithm 1) succeeds with probability at least
2/3
.
Repeating it
r
times independently, the probability that all trials fail is at most

(1-2/3)^{r}=(1/3)^{r}\leq 2^{-r}\leq\delta.

Hence, with probability at least
1-\delta
, at least one trial produces the correct product
fg
.

Verification error.

The verification algorithm (Algorithm 2) with error bound
\beta
returns
True
for an incorrect candidate with probability at most
\beta
.
There are at most
(k_{\max}+1)\cdot r
verification calls in total. By the union bound, the probability that
any
incorrect candidate passes verification is at most

(k_{\max}+1)\cdot r\cdot\beta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Total failure probability.

The algorithm fails if either:

•

the correct guess never produces a correct candidate (probability
\leq\delta=\varepsilon/2
), or

•

an incorrect candidate passes verification and is output (probability
\leq\varepsilon/2
).

By the union bound, the overall failure probability is at most
\varepsilon
. Therefore,

{\mathbb{P}}[\text{output}=fg]\geq 1-\varepsilon.

3.2.2
Complexity Analysis

Base multiplication.

For a guess
t=2^{k}
, if
2^{k}\leq T
, we may use the true sparsity
T
as an upper bound for the complexity; if
2^{k}>T
, the complexity is bounded by
\widetilde{O}(2^{k}(\log D+\log H))
.

Verification.

Similarly, when
2^{k}\leq T
, the verification complexity can be bounded using
T
as
\widetilde{O}(T(\log D+\log H)\hbox{\rm{polylog}}(1/\beta))
; when
2^{k}>T
, it is bounded by
\widetilde{O}(2^{k}(\log D+\log H)\hbox{\rm{polylog}}(1/\beta))
.
Given

1/\beta=O\!\left(\frac{\log T\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\right),

substituting this yields the complexity in terms of the original parameters:

\widetilde{O}\!\left(T(\log D+\log H)\cdot\operatorname{polylog}\!\left(1/\varepsilon\right)\right),

and, when
2^{k}>T
,

\widetilde{O}\!\left(2^{k}(\log D+\log H)\cdot\operatorname{polylog}\!\left(1/\varepsilon\right)\right).

Average-case complexity analysis.

Consider the moment when the guess
2^{k}
first exceeds the true sparsity
T
, i.e.,
k=k^{*}+1
.
If the algorithm stops at this step, the total computational cost is

C_{k^{*}+1}=\widetilde{O}\!\bigl(T(\log D+\log H)\cdot\operatorname{polylog}\!\left(1/\varepsilon\right)+2^{k^{*}+1}\cdot r\cdot(\log D+\log H)\bigr).

If it instead stops at
k=k^{*}+2
, then all previous guesses (including
k=k^{*}+1
) must have failed to produce a correct candidate that passes verification.
The probability that a given guess fails to stop is at most
\varepsilon/2
, so the probability of reaching step
k^{*}+2
is at most
\varepsilon/2
.
More systematically, let
p
be the failure probability per guess step. Then the probability that the algorithm first stops at
k=k^{*}+s
is at most
(\varepsilon/2)^{s-1}
.

Thus the expected cost is bounded by

\mathbb{E}[\text{cost}]\leq T(\log D+\log H)\cdot\operatorname{polylog}\!\left(1/\varepsilon\right)+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\bigl((\varepsilon/2)^{s-1}\bigr)\cdot\widetilde{O}\!\bigl(2^{k^{*}+s}\cdot r\cdot(\log D+\log H)\operatorname{polylog}\!\left(1/\varepsilon\right)\bigr).

Since
2^{k^{*}+s}=T\cdot 2^{s}
, the geometric series converges:

\mathbb{E}[\text{cost}]\leq\widetilde{O}\!\left(T\cdot(\log D+\log H)\cdot\operatorname{polylog}\!\left(1/\varepsilon\right)\cdot\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{s-1}\right).

As
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(\varepsilon)^{t-1}=\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}=O(1)
.
Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[\text{cost}]=\widetilde{O}\!\left(T\cdot(\log D+\log H)\cdot\operatorname{polylog}\!\left(1/\varepsilon\right)\right).

This shows that the average-case bit complexity matches the worst-case bound up to constants, and the geometric decay ensures that the contribution from large
k
is negligible.

Hence, the algorithm achieves quasi-linear bit complexity in the input size without prior knowledge of
T
, with an arbitrarily high success probability
1-\varepsilon
.

Multivariate case.

Using the Kronecker substitution
x_{i}\mapsto X^{D^{i-1}}
(with
D
larger than any exponent), a multivariate multiplication
fg
reduces to a univariate multiplication of two polynomials of degree
\leq D^{n}
.
Applying the univariate algorithm and substituting back yields the same guaranties.
The bit complexity becomes

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(T\cdot(n\log D+\log H)\operatorname{polylog}\!\left(1/\varepsilon\right)\right),

where
T=\max(\#f,\#g,\#fg)
,
D
bounds the total degree, and
C
bounds the coefficients.
This is optimal for integer coefficients up to polylogarithmic factors.

Theorem 7

Remark 8
.

In the finite field setting

, the same framework in Section
3.2
applies to generate Theorem
7
to the case where the output sparsity is not provided.
There exists a sparse multiplication algorithm that assumes a known sparsity bound

4.1
Interpolation Ingredients

In this section, we present the components of sparse polynomial interpolation that are employed in the algorithm.
The algorithm uses derivative images in the sparse-interpolation style of

Huang 2019
;
Giorgi et al. 2021

self-contained: derivative images generate a short candidate list containing
the true support, and ordinary cyclic images then filter that list.

Put
B=\max\{D_{\rm out}+1,2\}
and

L_{D}=\log\max(D_{\rm out}+1,2)
. If

is finite, a prime

isolates

is congruent to

modulo

\ell

Lemma 9

(Prime isolation sampler)
.

Let
S\subseteq\{0,\ldots,D_{\rm out}\}
, let
|S|\leq M
, and let

0<\eta<1
. There is a Monte Carlo sampler using

k=O(\log(\max(M,2)/\eta))

primes, each of size
\widetilde{O}(ML_{D})
, such that with probability at least

1-\eta
, every
e\in S
is isolated in
S
by at least one sampled

Proof.

Choose

N=\lceil 8\max(M,2)\lceil\log_{2}B\rceil\rceil

and sample

k=C\lceil\log(\max(M,2)/\eta)\rceil

primes independently and uniformly from the first
N
primes, where
C
is
an absolute constant large enough for the probability estimate below. Fix

e\in S
. If a sampled prime
\ell
fails to isolate

, then

\ell\mid(e-e^{\prime})
for some
e^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{e\}
. For fixed
e^{\prime}
, the
nonzero difference
e-e^{\prime}
has absolute value less than

, so it has at
most
\log_{2}B
distinct prime divisors. Therefore at most

(M-1)\log_{2}B
among the first

primes are bad for this fixed

.
By the definition of

, a uniformly sampled prime from this list is bad
for

with probability at most

. Hence the probability that all

sampled primes are bad for

is at most

, and the constant

C
makes this at most

. A union bound over all

gives the claimed probability. The

\widetilde{O}(ML_{D})
by the standard prime-number estimate for the growth of the

N
-th prime

.
∎

Lemma 10

(Derivative candidate generation)
.

Let

P=\sum_{e\in S}c_{e}x^{e},\qquad S\subseteq\{0,\ldots,D_{\rm out}\},

over
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}
, with
\operatorname{char}(K)>D_{\rm out}
. Suppose

are known. One can form a candidate set

with
|C_{\ell}|\leq|S|
, and

\ell
has its exponent in

.

Proof.

For each residue
r

in
P_{\ell}
is nonzero, let

be the coefficient of

in

in

into

when

There is at most one inserted candidate for each nonzero bucket. Each nonzero

, so the number of inserted

Lemma 11

(Candidate filtering)
.

Let
P=\sum_{e\in S}c_{e}x^{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]

\widetilde{O}(\log(\max(|C|,2)/\eta))

If

to the known set

∎

4.2
Proof of Theorem
7

For a prime
\ell
, define

f_{\ell}=f\bmod(x^{\ell}-1),\qquad g_{\ell}=g\bmod(x^{\ell}-1).

Because reduction modulo
x^{\ell}-1
is a ring homomorphism, the product image
is

h_{\ell}=f_{\ell}g_{\ell}\bmod(x^{\ell}-1).

Also define

f^{\prime}_{\ell}=f^{\prime}\bmod(x^{\ell}-1),\qquad g^{\prime}_{\ell}=g^{\prime}\bmod(x^{\ell}-1).

The formal derivative satisfies
(fg)^{\prime}=f^{\prime}g+fg^{\prime}
, and reducing this identity
modulo
x^{\ell}-1
gives

h^{\prime}_{\ell}=f^{\prime}_{\ell}g_{\ell}+f_{\ell}g^{\prime}_{\ell}\pmod{x^{\ell}-1}.

Thus
h_{\ell}
and
h^{\prime}_{\ell}
are obtained from the sparse inputs by folding

f,g,f^{\prime},g^{\prime}
to length
\ell
, performing dense cyclic products, and adding
the two derivative-product terms. No inverse of
g_{\ell}
is used.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

If
f=0
or
g=0
, return zero. Otherwise let
S=\operatorname{supp}h
. Apply
Lemma
9
to the unknown set
S
, with
M=T
and
failure budget
\delta/2
. The sampler itself uses only
T
,

D_{\rm out}
, and
\delta
. For each sampled prime
\ell
, compute

h_{\ell}
and
h^{\prime}_{\ell}
by the cyclic product and derivative formulas above,
then use Lemma
10
to form
C_{\ell}
. Let

C=\bigcup_{\ell}C_{\ell}.

On the isolation event from Lemma
9
, every element of

S
lies in
C
. Also each
C_{\ell}
has size at most
|S|\leq T
, and
the number of sampled primes is
O(\log(T/\delta))
, so

|C|=\widetilde{O}(T\,\hbox{\rm{polylog}}(1/\delta))

with no additional visible power of
L_{D}
.

Run Lemma
11
on the known set
C
with failure budget

\delta/2
, using ordinary product images
h_{\ell}=f_{\ell}g_{\ell}
. If both
the generation-stage and filtering-stage isolation events occur, the output is
exactly
h
. The union bound gives total failure probability at most

\delta
.

It remains to record the cost. For one modulus
\ell
, folding the sparse
inputs and, during candidate generation, their formal derivatives costs

\widetilde{O}(T)
field operations plus exponent bookkeeping with
L_{D}
-bit
exponents. Dense cyclic multiplication of length
\ell
costs

\widetilde{O}(\ell)
operations over the coefficient algebra by the
Cantor–Kaltofen dense multiplication theorem
Cantor and Kaltofen 1991
.
Lemma
9
samples primes of size
\widetilde{O}(TL_{D})
in the

\widetilde{O}(|C|L_{D})
in the filtering stage. The number of

and

\widetilde{O}\!\left(TL_{D}\,\hbox{\rm{polylog}}(1/\delta)\right)

field operations under the separated-variable convention. In an explicit

\widetilde{O}\!\left(TL_{D}\log q\,\hbox{\rm{polylog}}(1/\delta)\right).

∎

5
Conclusion

We first provide a counterexample to a key result of
Giorgi et al. 2020
, thereby invalidating the quasi-linear sparse polynomial multiplication claim.
By applying the MBB interpolation algorithm, we can derive a quasi-linear algorithm for multiplying sparse polynomials with integer coefficients.
Finally, we introduce a sparse polynomial multiplication algorithm defined over the large characteristic finite field, which attains a bit complexity of
\widetilde{O}(T\log D\log H)
.
