Executive Summary
Between 2020 and 2024, the Inclusive Shared Solar Initiative (ISSI) served as a forum
for three states to navigate the design and development of LMI-accessible community
solar projects. A collaboration between the National Association of State Energy
Officials (NASEO) and National Energy Assistance Directors Association (NEADA), ISSI
provided financial and technical assistance to teams of State Energy Offices and State
LIHEAP Agencies in Minnesota, Wisconsin, and the District of Columbia. The key goals
guiding ISSI were to develop four new community solar frameworks in three new states
and create greater momentum among state governments, community solar developers,
and the finance community in scaling and investing in community solar projects that are
affordable and accessible to LIHEAP households. Ultimately, in addition to developing
and launching four new shared solar projects serving LMI subscribers, the ISSI state
partners pioneered community solar policy and program design strategies that
increased protections for low-income consumers, reduced barriers to enrollment and
project development, and fostered inter-program coordination.

ISSI offers examples and precedents from three diverse states, representing varied
electricity market and policy arrangements, that can help inspire further action in
accelerating the delivery of solar to households and people who participate in state low-
income programs. Prior to the start of ISSI in 2020, only a few states had begun to fully
explore the intersection between LIHEAP and LMI community solar. The framework,
relationships, and precedents cultivated by ISSI have catalyzed actions and
opportunities for advancing low-income community solar, not just in the partner states
but also across the country. Since its establishment, ISSI has served as a forum to
examine how interagency collaboration and program coordination between State
Energy Offices and LIHEAP Agencies can address barriers to solar access, protect
consumers, and prioritize investments. Through regular convenings and demonstrating
new models for LMI community solar, the ISSI State Implementation Partners emerged
as leaders in this space, helping inform one another, federal agencies, private sector
partners, and other states of ways to maximize access to the benefits of community
solar for LIHEAP households. ISSI has advanced new concepts and precedents that
advance the state of the art in low-income-centric community solar literature, both
through reports and resources developed directly in support of ISSI and its partners as
well as by helping to inform federal investigations and efforts (for instance, the Solar
Energy Technologies’ Office “Low Income Connector” tool; the Department of Health
and Human Services’ Information “Memorandum on Community Solar and LIHEAP
Considerations”; and the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development’s
guidance on “Treatment of Community Solar Credits on Tenant Utility Bills”).

The findings and principles of ISSI will have lasting impact because State and Territory
Energy Offices across the country continue to pursue LMI-accessible community solar
policies, programs, and projects. Thanks to initiatives from the U.S. Department of
Energy, U.S. Environmental Agency, and states themselves, State Energy Offices can
be inspired by ISSI to continue applying the concepts of affordability, accessibility, and
interagency coordination to their shared solar and storage initiatives.

Background
Community solar allows households, regardless of ownership status or roof suitability,
to access cost savings and other benefits often associated with solar energy. Also
known as shared solar or solar gardens, community solar arrangements enable
customers to subscribe to a “share” of a solar power project in their community or utility
territory, without installing rooftop solar panels on-site. As such, it is often the only viable
solar option for households that may not be able to afford or are otherwise unable to
invest directly in rooftop photovoltaic options.

Unfortunately, community solar has remained largely out of reach for lower-income
households. While the U.S. community solar market has grown significantly over the
last decade, as of December 2022 low- to moderate-income community solar
represented just 2% of the overall market based on capacity.1 A growing body of
literature has found that subscription costs, contract terms and fees, and customer
outreach and acquisition strategies can impede LMI households from accessing and
benefiting from community solar. Additionally, policy and regulatory uncertainty resulting
in high capital costs may stall projects and reduce profit margins for developers and
investors, limiting their ability to deliver benefits to subscribers.

State laws, policies, and program designs create and shape the market for community
solar and can play an influential role in eliminating barriers to access for lower-income
households. When designed with underserved subscribers in mind, community solar
offers states an opportunity to reduce costs for households with vulnerabilities such as
high energy burdens, i.e., where energy costs exceed 6% of gross income, while
supporting clean energy and grid resilience policies and goals.

As of August 2024, 23 states and the District of Columbia had passed community-solar
enabling legislation that establishes a mandate for community solar and/or to develop
programs that support or incentivize its expansion.2,3 A subset of state policies have
included provisions that lower costs and other barriers to participation for lower-income
subscribers (referred to in this report as “LMI-accessible community solar”. Seventeen
states, including the District of Columbia, have legislation requiring or encouraging LMI
participation in community solar development or have community solar pilots under way
that include LMI customers. Some of these states have employed carve-outs that
mandate that a certain percentage of subscribers or proportion of project capacity or
output be reserved for LMI households. Others promote low-income subscriber
participation by offering discounted or waived subscription rates, requiring LMI
consumer protections, and/or providing financial incentives to developers, for example.

Community solar projects that include LMI customers rely on grants or public benefit
funds to cover or mitigate the costs to the developer and subscribers. While these
models are effective in making shared solar options more affordable to LMI participants,
the subsidy limits the scalability of the programs. In response to this obstacle, ISSI
aimed to advance strategies that increase the scalability of LMI community solar
programs through targeted interagency (State Energy Office and LIHEAP Office)
engagement, involvement, and coordination, and through the streamlining of LMI

energy programs and resources. Prior to the start of ISSI, only a few states had begun
to fully explore the intersection between LIHEAP and LMI community solar. Since its
establishment in 2020, ISSI has served as a forum to examine how interagency
collaboration and program coordination between State Energy Offices and LIHEAP
Agencies can address barriers to solar access, protect consumers, and prioritize
investments.

Throughout ISSI, the project team relied heavily on literature, concepts, and experts in:
 • LIHEAP program design: Tapping into expertise offered by project partner
 NEADA and its membership of energy assistance offices, ISSI was heavily
 informed by the federal and state structures, processes, and metrics that govern
 how LIHEAP funds are allocated and distributed. Through NEADA, the project
 team gained direct access to states’ unique LIHEAP rules and systems,
 informing technical assistance and brainstorming with the State Implementation
 Partners. In addition to NEADA’s work scanning state LIHEAP program plans,
 federal literature, reviews, and resources on LIHEAP through HSS enabled the
 project team to explore opportunities to integrate community solar considerations
 into sometimes-complex LIHEAP processes and strucures.
 • Principles of low-income-accessible community solar: Building on existing
 literature and experience from community solar programs, particularly the
 National Consumer Law Center’s important research into “Principles for
 Protecting Low-Income Community Solar Subscribers4,” the ISSI team
 developed a suite of principles and considerations that can inform state policy
 and program design, including: establishing a minimum proportion of community
 solar project or program capacity for LMI subscribers; offering financial incentives
 to community solar providers who meet LMI participation thresholds; discounting
 or waiving subscription rates for LMI subscribers; require consolidated billing by
 utilities; and eliminating up-front costs, cancellation fees, and minimum
 commitment periods, along with other consumer protections.
 • Effective stakeholder engagement: ISSI was designed to prioritize community
 needs and voices in the development of the State Implementation Partners’ plans
 and investments in community solar. The ISSI team leveraged key resources,
 such as the Spectrum of Community Engagement to Ownership5, and contracted
 with Green for All to provide information and training to the state partners. These
 resources in turn informed the extensive stakeholder engagement that the
 agencies in Minnesota, Washington, DC, and Wisconsin undertook to inform their
 participation in ISSI.
 • Costs and benefits of shared solar: Throughout ISSI, each state partner had to
 engage decision-makers to advance the development of their pilot community
 solar projects. A common objection to the development of LMI-accessible
 community solar projects and programs stemmed from their potential to
 contribute to inequities in electric system costs, a dynamic commonly known as
 “cross-subsidization” or “cost-shifting.” Working through the Minnesota
 Department of Commerce, the ISSI team engaged researchers at the University
 of Minnesota’s Chan Lab, a leading expert on this topic, to collect and analyze
 data to document how community solar can help utilities’ bottom line and provide

 Page 6 of 23

 benefits for all customers. The Chan Lab utilized real data from one of the ISSI
 projects to demonstrate that community solar can help avoid demand charges,
 which can make up a significant portion (between 30 and 50% —of the amount
 some utilities pay to their wholesale provider. joins a growing body of research
 across the United States examining the electric system costs and benefits of
 distributed solar projects and, beyond the grid, their societal impacts. A 2024
 analysis from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory for Frankfort, Kentucky,
 found that meeting a portion of the city’s electricity demand through 11.54
 megawatts of solar production would reduce costs borne by the power supplier,
 the Kentucky Municipal Energy Agency, by over $1,000,000 annually by
 decreasing aggregate peak demand.6 A 2023 study by the Brattle Group found
 that both ground-mounted and rooftop community solar and storage projects,
 particularly in urban and suburban settings, provide net value across grid, land
 use, workforce, and resiliency value streams.7 In a national modeling study
 commissioned by Local Solar for All, the Coalition for Community Solar Access,
 and Vote Solar, Vibrant Clean Energy, LLC, came to similar conclusions: that
 distributed energy resources can lower costs across the entire electricity system
 (ranging from $301 billion to $473 billion by 2050) and result in net-new jobs,
 without sacrificing system reliability and resource adequacy,8 thus benefiting all
 ratepayers regardless of income or rate class.

Ultimately, ISSI helped to advance new concepts and precedents that advance the state
of the art in low-income-centric community solar literature. Through regular convenings
and demonstrating new models for LMI community solar, the ISSI State Implementation
Partners emerged as leaders in this space, helping inform one another, federal
agencies, private sector partners, and other states of ways to maximize access to the
benefits of community solar for LIHEAP households. ISSI led to the development of
reports and resources by project partners (see “Products” section below), and helped to
inform federal investigations and efforts (for instance, the Solar Energy Technologies’
Office “Low Income Connector” tool; the Department of Health and Human Services’
Information Memorandum on Community Solar and LIHEAP Considerations”; and U.S.
Department of Housing and Urban Development’s guidance on “Treatment of
Community Solar Credits on Tenant Utility Bills”).

Project Objectives
The end goals of the ISSI project were to develop four new community solar
frameworks in three new states and create greater momentum among state
governments, community solar developers, and the finance community in scaling
and investing in community solar projects that are affordable and accessible to
LIHEAP households.

In the context of broader national and state level clean energy goals, the projects
facilitated by the ISSI State Implementation Partners represent just a small portion of the
important work being done by the federal government, states, localities, the private
sector, and communities to deliver solar energy and economic benefits to people in
 Page 7 of 23

financial need. However, the framework, relationships, and precedents cultivated by ISSI
have catalyzed more significant program and project alignments across the country.
ISSI offers examples and precedents from three diverse states that can help inspire
further action in accelerating the delivery of solar to households and people who
participate in state low-income programs. The foundation built by ISSI and its NASEO’s
unique ability to continue disseminating findings from ISSI aligns well with the National
Community Solar Partnership goals of enabling community solar systems to power the
equivalent of five million households by 2025 and create $1 billion in energy savings for
subscribers, represents a 700% increase in community solar deployment from 2020
levels.

According to the project Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO), to reach the
aforementioned end goals, the project team worked to advance the following objectives
in each of the three project budget periods:

 Budget Period 1:
 • Develop and publish a suite of starter materials to inform states about ISSI
 concepts;
 • Select three State Implementation Partners to develop pilot programs under ISSI,
 through a competitive Request for Proposals (RFP) process; and
 • Initiate a Peer Exchange Group between the State Implementation Partners and
 additional interested states (Observer States).

 Budget Period 2:
 • Developed template materials to support each state in initiating and implementing
 their LMI community solar pilots; and
 • Host three Peer Exchange Meetings and broadened awareness of ISSI among
 SEO and EAA networks.

 Budget Period 3:
 • Support the launch of four LMI community solar pilots in the State
 Implementation Partner states;
 • Connect ISSI state partners with financial institutions and capital providers active
 in LMI community solar;
 • Engage stakeholders in over twenty states on the results of the pilot programs
 completed in Tasks 1 and 2; and
 • Provide additional deep-dives to at least two other states that express interest in
 pursuing similar pilot programs as a result of learning about ISSI.

COMPLETE - STATE IMPLEMENTATION PLANS. Subtask 2.1 involved the
development of ISSI State Implementation Plans. The ISSI team worked one-on-one
with each state to identify key aspects of their plan approach, including regulatory and
policy challenges and opportunities for LI/LMI participation in the pilot projects, a

COMPLETE – INCLUSION OF COMMUNITY SOLAR IN RELEVANT STATE PLANS.
Subtask 2.2 requires the State Implementation Partners to incorporate their work on
LMI community solar into relevant plans and proposals (such as LIHEAP plans) on an
as-needed basis. Each State Implementation Partner has assessed their current need
for such action, and taken appropriate steps to gain buy-in from both State Energy
Office and LIHEAP Office leadership. A description of the changes and decision-maker
engagement conducted by each state so far includes:
 • The Minnesota LIHEAP Program has engaged the U.S. Department of Health
 and Human Services (HHS) to incorporate changes to its LIHEAP policy, which
 will enable the state’s benefits structure to more effectively improve access to
 LMI community solar opportunities. These modifications will allow LIHEAP funds
 to cover community solar subscription costs on behalf of EAP-qualified
 households, and also allow the inclusion of CSG subscription costs as a cost of
 energy purchase when calculating the energy assistance benefit payments to
 EAP program participants. As a result of Minnesota’s participation in ISSI, these
 changes have been implemented, and now allow the state to fully account for
 community solar subscription costs in calculating LIHEAP benefit payments.
 • The District of Columbia Department of Energy and Environment (DOEE)
 conducted stakeholder engagement to identify a first site for its ISSI-supported
 community solar and storage pilot project: the Faunteroy Community Enrichment
 Center in Ward 7, in a lower-income neighborhood of the District. The site was
 approved for a resilience hub grant in April 2021. Additionally, DOEE has had
 continual engagement with the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban
 Development (HUD) to request a waiver that would prevent HUD from reducing
 utility allowances and other benefits for residents of HUD-assisted housing that
 participate in community solar programs. In the following year, HUD approved
 the DOEE waiver request9 and would proceed to provide a national-level waiver10
 enabling residents of HUD-assisted housing to access cost-saving community
 solar subscriptions without inducing a rent increase or utility allowance
 adjustment.
 • The Wisconsin Office of Energy Innovation and the Division of Energy Housing
 and Community Resources have exchanged data and strategies to implement a
 new electric rate structure and LIHEAP benefits package to ensure LMI
 subscribers of community solar projects achieve a cashflow-positive experience.
 These explorations fed into the rate structure in its pilot programs with local
 community action agencies and rural electric cooperatives.

Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
ISSI has helped inform state-level LIHEAP policy, program, and process adjustments.
The ISSI team in the Minnesota Department of Commerce enacted a change to its
LIHEAP program in 2020 that now enables beneficiary households to use LIHEAP
dollars for community solar garden subscriptions. Wisconsin is considering a similar
policy approach following a conversation between the Minnesota and Wisconsin
LIHEAP offices facilitated by the ISSI project team. Additionally, Washington, DC team
adopted an opt-out model that automatically subscribes LIHEAP households to cost-
saving Solar for All projects. Both of these measures are helping other states explore
changes to their LIHEAP programs to enable energy burden reductions and more
streamlined funds usage through community solar programs.

ISSI innovations have helped inform federal efforts. Since the launch of ISSI, DOE and
the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) have launched the Low-Income
Clean Energy Connector, a digital tool that makes community solar with verified savings
and strong consumer protections more accessible to households participating in
government-run low-income support programs. Early lessons learned from the ISSI
State Implementation Partners have helped the Connector project team identify best
practices in the LMI community solar space, including effective state agency

                                      Page 19 of 23

coordination and strategies to ensure participation in a community solar program does
not negatively impact LIHEAP benefits for low-income households. Additionally, in June
2023, HHS’s Office of Community Services (OCS) sent an Information Memorandum to
LIHEAP grant recipients highlighting the benefits of community solar for low-income
households and clarifying how LIHEAP funds can be used in relation to community
solar. The memorandum 1) confirmed that LIHEAP funds can be used for solar energy
use (as they are in Minnesota) through new and existing electric payment mechanisms,
such as community solar subscription fees, and 2) provides LIHEAP grant recipients
with recommendations to consider when utilizing LIHEAP funds for community solar
subscriptions. Finally, in response to engagement from ISSI project partners and other
advocates, the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development issued guidance in
2022 that enabled residents of HUD-assisted housing to access cost-saving community
solar subscriptions without inducing a rent increase or utility allowance adjustment.

ISSI has modeled constructive State Energy Office-LIHEAP Office partnerships in
community solar beyond New York. While the original basis for ISSI lies in the
partnership established between the New York State Energy Research and
Development Authority (NYSERDA), the State Energy Office, and the New York Office
of Temporary and Disability Assistance (ODTA), which administers LIHEAP, the three
State Implementation Partners in ISSI have helped model and expand this type of inter-
agency partnership. For many states, New York is not always relatable due to its large
budget and the state’s general public support for clean energy and low-income
programs. With State Energy Office-LIHEAP Office pairs coming together around
shared goals in DC, Minnesota, and Wisconsin, other State Energy Offices and LIHEAP
Offices have a more diverse array of examples to inspire similar partnerships in their
unique state contexts.

ISSI has facilitated analysis on ways that low-income programs intersect with
community solar. Through the Peer Exchange Group meetings, the ISSI team has
elevated research and facilitated discussions on streamlined enrollment and low-income
benefits protection in LMI community solar projects. Additionally, as energy
technologies continue to evolve, the ISSI team has encouraged LIHEAP agencies to
adopt a more holistic view of the costs and benefits of different sources of energy; this is
especially relevant in states where community solar subscription fees may not appear
on LIHEAP households’ utility bills, which is often the main method that LIHEAP
agencies use to calculate households’ benefits.

ISSI has promoted states’ sharing of firsthand experiences navigating the expansion of
low-income-centric programs. In particular, the State Implementation Partners have
shared strategies and insights on ways to identify and contextualize cost-shifting/cross-
subsidization narratives that may impact public and utilities’ perspectives on LMI
community solar projects.

Project Team and Roles
ISSI was led by subject matter experts at NASEO and NEADA and its initial stages
were supported by a diverse, volunteer-led Advisory Group. The project team also
included three teams of State Implementation Partners, representing pairs of State
Energy Offices and state LIHEAP Agencies committed to exploring strategies to expand
LMI access to community solar through the development of LMI community solar pilot
projects.

The ISSI Core Team, composed of NASEO and NEADA staff, provided overall program
oversight and budget management; developed research, technical assistance offerings,
and other resources to support State Energy Office and LIHEAP Office understanding of
community solar; coordinated the ISSI Advisory Group, State Implementation Partners,

                                      Page 21 of 23

and Peer Exchange Group; and used its platforms to increase visibility and
understanding of ISSI goals and activities among external stakeholders.

The ISSI Advisory Group, which was particularly active in Budget Period 1 to support
the ISSI Core Team in designing and launching the initiative, included volunteer
representatives from State Energy Offices, LIHEAP Offices, utility associations, solar
developers, capital providers, community-based organizations, and SETO. The
Advisory Group met quarterly in Budget Periods 1 and 2 to to provide insights grounded
in their policy, market, and community experiences and expertise, and served as an
sounding board in the development of technical assistance for the ISSI team.

The State Implementations Partners, composed of State Energy Office-State LIHEAP
Office teams in Minnesota, Washington DC, and Minnesota, committed to specific ISSI
goals and processes in order to receive financial, technical, and peer-exchange
assistance opportunities. By Budget Period 3, the State Implementation Partners served
as ambassadors of ISSI for external audiences, including other interested State Energy
Offices and LIHEAP Offices. Each State Implementation Partner was afforded the
flexibility to engage additional partners in their state to support their pilot project
development and research.
