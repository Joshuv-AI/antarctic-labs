The Hisashi technique: Making a “pent roof” for safe and curative lung segmentectomy
Authors: Hideki Ujiie, Masaoki Ito, Hana Oiki, Shota Fukuda, Mai Nishina, Shuta Ohara, Masaya Nishino, Akira Hamada, Masato Chiba, Toshiki Takemoto, Yasuhiro Tsutani
Published: 2026

Hisashi technique schematic showing the pent roof created via precise proximal division.

Central Message

The Hisashi technique creates a protective pent roof that preserves proximal bronchovascular structures and stabilizes stapler insertion, enabling safe, margin-secure segmentectomy.

Evidence from Japanese Oncology Group 0802/West Japan Oncology Group 4607L1 and Cancer and Leukemia Group B 1405032 established segmentectomy as the standard of care for small peripheral non–small cell lung cancer. However, segmentectomy is technically demanding, requiring adequate margins and preservation of proximal bronchovascular structures to ensure optimal postoperative lung expansion.

Traditionally, surgeons expose the intersegmental vein (V. inter) proximal root to identify the plane. However, without creating a parenchymal “pent roof,” the distal vein and proximal bronchovascular structures remain vulnerable to compression or injury during parenchymal division. Especially in deep operative fields, stapler insertion becomes unstable because of the thickness and elasticity of the undivided parenchyma, requiring multiple firings and potentially compromising functional outcomes and surgical margins.

In cases with deep intersegmental planes or incomplete fissures, blind stapling carries an inherent risk of kinking the proximal V. inter or the preserved segmental bronchus. The Hisashi technique standardizes the creation of a “buffer zone” to visually and physically protect these structures.

To address these challenges, we developed the Hisashi technique (“pent roof” in JapaneseE1). By lifting a controlled parenchymal layer (the pent roof) away from the V. inter and adjacent structures, this technique protects these structures and provides a stable pathway for intersegmental plane division regardless of tumor location. Unlike conventional vein-guided segmentectomy, the Hisashi technique intentionally elevates a proximal parenchymal roof to create a space that protects bronchovascular structures and stabilizes stapler insertion during deep parenchymal division.

Technique

1.Identification: The V. inter is identified at the hilum and traced distally depending on the tumor location and the anticipated intersegmental plane. The vein may be partially or fully skeletonized if its course is clear. The Hisashi technique is defined by forming a pent roof through accurate proximal division to reveal the intersegmental boundary. The “pent roof” is specifically defined as the protective parenchymal layer elevated from the vein (V. inter) and adjacent bronchus during proximal division, rather than the layer left directly on these structures.

2.Pent roof formation: After dividing the responsible segmental bronchus, the stump is gently elevated, exposing the natural plane. Using this traction, the surgeon proceeds distally along the V. inter, dividing the parenchyma in a peeling manner to create a controlled surface. During division of the proximal one-third, the anatomical boundary is followed to form the pent roof. Proximal division is performed using monopolar electrocautery at a power setting of 30 W. For tumors with ambiguous boundaries, indocyanine green may be used adjunctively to confirm the intersegmental line before creation of the pent roof. This procedure ensures accurate proximal separation with a natural parenchymal surface on which the V. inter becomes clearly visible (Figure 1).

The pent roof preserves the proximal bronchovascular structures, prevents compression, provides a stable stapling platform by reducing the bulk of tissue the stapler must initially compress, and facilitates consistent deep-margin division with fewer firings.

3.Peripheral extension for margin acquisition: Peripherally, the resection line is not constrained by anatomy. For tumors approaching the plane, parenchymal division may extend into the adjacent segment to achieve adequate margins. Extension is limited to the minimum necessary depth, avoiding removal of entire adjacent subsegments, thereby preserving postoperative lung function.

Discussion

Tumors abutting the intersegmental plane often require extended segmentectomy.3 Oizumi and colleagues4 demonstrated that dividing “across the vein” ensures adequate margins when anatomically correct. The Hisashi technique builds upon and standardizes this approach via proximal pent roof formation and controlled non-anatomical extension distally.

Nomori and colleagues5 demonstrated preserved pulmonary function when resection volume was minimized. The Hisashi technique enhances functional preservation through precise proximal division and targeted peripheral extension, avoiding unnecessary subsegmental removal.

Formation of the pent roof creates a clean and anatomically correct proximal surface with the V. inter clearly visible, thereby preserving the anatomical relationships among the vein, pulmonary artery branches, and remnant bronchus to support postoperative expansion.

Although air leaks are a common concern with cautery, the Hisashi technique limits cautery to the less vascular proximal hilum and uses the resulting flat surface to optimize subsequent staple line integrity.

Compared with conventional division using staplers only, use of electrocautery to create the pent roof theoretically introduces a potential risk of minor air leaks from the exposed proximal parenchymal surface. However, by restricting cautery use to the less vascular, fibrous proximal hilar region and using the resulting flat surface as a stable platform, the subsequent stapler application is optimized, thereby minimizing the overall clinical risk of prolonged air leak.

As demonstrated in Figure E1, the Hisashi technique prevents unintentional kinking with minimal staple line, leaving the preserved segmental bronchus and proximal V. inter fully patent and uncompressed postoperatively. This feature may be particularly beneficial in patients with narrow segmental angles or deep intersegmental planes.

Postoperative computed tomography scans demonstrated that the Hisashi technique prevented unintentional kinking with minimal staple line (circle), leaving the preserved segmental bronchus (arrowheads) and proximal intersegmental vein (arrow) fully patent and uncompressed postoperatively.

Furthermore, the visual “platform” provided by the pent roof is especially beneficial in robotic-assisted thoracic surgery to compensate for the absence of tactile sensation, ensuring high reproducibility regardless of the surgical approach.

By providing a stable proximal stapling platform, the pent roof reduces stapler firings and postoperative air leakage and increases reproducibility across video-assisted, robot-assisted, and open approaches.

Conclusions

The Hisashi technique (Video 1) forms a pent roof through precise proximal division, revealing the true intersegmental boundary as a natural parenchymal surface, preserves proximal bronchovascular structures, allows controlled nonanatomical distal extension to secure margins, minimizes parenchymal loss, and may facilitate margin-secure resection while preserving lung parenchyma.

Video 1

The Hisashi technique. This video demonstrates the Hisashi technique for a robotic right upper lobe S1 segmentectomy, including a preoperative computed tomography scan of the S1 tumor. The lung parenchyma is divided along the intersegmental plane using monopolar electrocautery. After dividing the segmental bronchus, the stump is gently elevated to expose the natural plane. Using this traction, the surgeon proceeds distally along the intersegmental vein (V. inter), peeling the parenchyma to create a controlled surface. Proximal division forms the “pent roof,” with distal nonanatomical extension selectively applied to secure adequate margins. Following pent roof formation, the distal parenchyma is divided by a stapler to ensure surgical margins and prevent air leakage. The pent roof preserves the proximal bronchovascular structures, prevents compression, and provides a stable stapling platform by reducing the bulk of tissue the stapler must initially compress, thereby facilitating consistent deep-margin division with fewer firings. Upon completion, the pent roof remains as an accurate proximal surface with the V. inter exposed, confirming correct anatomical division. This approach combines proximal accuracy and distal flexibility, enabling margin-secure resection while minimizing parenchymal loss.
