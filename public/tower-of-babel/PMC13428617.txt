Do Patients Value the Service Provided by Physicians Who Overbill? A Willingness‐to‐Pay Study Using French Survey Data
Authors: Karine Lamiraud, Radu Vranceanu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOutpatient specialist physicians in France can work either in the regulated sector, where they charge the regulated fee, or in the unregulated sector, where they set their own fees above the regulated fee. This context provides a unique opportunity to estimate the extent to which patients value services provided under balance billing. In January 2021, we carried out a contingent valuation method (CVM) survey on a sample of 1051 individuals who were representative of France's adult population. Results highlighted that willingness‐to‐pay (WTP) for a specialist consultation in the unregulated sector was higher than for a consultation in the regulated sector, with a significant WTP difference of €4.30 (almost 15% of the regulated fee). This difference was significant across all respondent subgroups (i.e., gender, age, education level, marital status, income, health status, insurance coverage, and area of residence) and was not fully driven by differences in income. Moreover, this preference for the unregulated sector was associated with the perception that specialists in this sector provide better quality medical care, better quality non‐medical services, and that making an appointment with them is easier. Finally, we estimated the patient net welfare gain generated by specialist consultations in the unregulated sector in France at approximately €165 million per year. This is the first study to provide an estimation of patients' benefits associated with balance billing.Keywords: balance billing, contingent valuation method, subjective value, two‐sector healthcare market, WTP

Background

In countries with dual healthcare systems, the cost of baseline treatment is fully reimbursed by basic insurance, while patients must make out‐of‐pocket payments for the incremental costs associated with non‐basic care; examples of the latter include treatments not covered in the basic insurance package, better‐quality services, the opportunity to choose a specific physician, and the possibility to access more innovative treatments (Barros and Martinez‐Giralt 2002; Brekke and Sørgard 2007; Barros and Siciliani 2011; Olivella 2003). Balance billing, overbilling, and bulk‐billing are interchangeable terms used in the literature to describe situations in which physicians are legally allowed to charge patients an extra payment on top of the regulated fee (Glazer and McGuire 1993; Kifmann and Scheuer 2011; Savage and Jones 2004; Jelovac 2015; Besancenot et al. 2023). For consistency, we used the term ‘balance billing’ throughout the remainder of this article.

Many studies in healthcare industrial organization have analyzed balance billing as a form of price discrimination. Early theoretical models depicted physicians as monopolists offering homogenous services, able to price discriminate based on patients' willingness‐to‐pay (WTP) out‐of‐pocket fees (J. B. Mitchell and Cromwell 1982; Zuckerman and Holahan 1991; Savage and Jones 2004). In these models, balance billing primarily increased physicians' income at the patients' expense. Feldman and Sloan (1988) suggested that capping extra fees could lead to a decline in the quality of the service provided. Later models addressed the price‐quality choice in a monopolistic competition framework (e.g., Glazer and McGuire 1993; Kifmann and Scheuer 2011; Jelovac 2015; Gravelle et al. 2016; Besancenot et al. 2023). Lamiraud and Vranceanu (2025) developed a directed search model of the French idiosyncratic two‐sector market for specialist physician care, revealing that, in equilibrium, higher fees in the unregulated sector were associated with a higher likelihood of finding a specialist.

All these models seeking to explain how physicians who charge extra fees decide on these fees assume, either explicitly or implicitly, that a broad measure of patients' utility—net of out‐of‐pocket payments and any other costs (such as congestion or poor quality of amenities) from consulting a physician in the unregulated sector under balance billing—should exceed the utility of consulting a physician in the regulated sector for free (with fees typically covered by insurance). 1

While intuitive reasoning supports this assumption, no empirical study to date has verified it. Calcoen and Van de Ven (2019) pointed out that the value added for patients of consultations in the unregulated sector in Belgium and France is largely undocumented. This is confirmed by Dormont and Péron (2016), who noted that some patients in France prefer to consult specialists who balance bill, but did not provide an explicit measure of this utility gain.

The present paper seeks to fill this gap in the empirical literature by investigating whether patients assign a higher utility to consulting a specialist in the unregulated sector compared to consulting one in the regulated sector, using an original set of French data. France provides a unique setting to study this question as specialist physicians providing outpatient care can work exclusively in the regulated sector, charging regulated fees determined by the National Health Insurance, or in the unregulated sector where they can set their own fees under some loose guidelines from national health authorities. Furthermore, patients in France can freely choose to consult specialists in either sector.

In order to assess patients' benefits, we carried out a contingent valuation method (CVM) survey in a representative sample of the French adult population. Respondents were exposed to a hypothetical scenario where participants had to pay the consultation fee for a specialist in full out‐of‐pocket (i.e., no reimbursement for consultations in either sector). In this context, they were asked to express their WTP for a specialist consultation for both sectors. Data analysis investigated whether personal characteristics explained the heterogeneity between the two sectors in terms of WTP. The association between patient perceptions and WTP levels was also investigated in both sectors. Finally, the results from our study provide an approximate estimate of the patient welfare gain associated with consultations of specialist physicians in the unregulated sector.

When well‐functioning markets exist, preference estimation can rely on observed choices and the revealed preference framework, whereby consumers' budget allocations across bundles allow the inference of underlying utility. 2 However, when goods are provided free of charge or outside standard market mechanisms, such as regulated or publicly funded provision, market data cannot identify marginal WTP directly because there is no price variation to reflect individual trade‐offs. This is typically the case for public goods such as the quality of the environment, for which no market exists. Furthermore, in sectors characterized by price regulation or centralized allocation, such as healthcare, transaction prices simply serve as a transfer and accounting device and do not necessarily reflect marginal costs of the providers and marginal utilities of the consumers.

In such contexts, scholars often rely on stated preference methods, such as CVM, to determine consumer valuations of a given good or service. CVM is a survey‐based, direct approach to eliciting WTP through the use of hypothetical scenarios. It has been widely used in health economics to estimate the monetary value of medical technologies, pharmaceutical products, regulatory policies, and health insurance schemes (see surveys in Diener et al. 1998; Klose 1999; Steigenberger et al. 2022). A recent example arises from the COVID‐19 pandemic, during which numerous studies sought to estimate individuals' WTP for vaccines that were still under development, whose effectiveness was uncertain, and whose provision was expected to be publicly funded (Catma and Varol 2021; Cerda and García 2021; Das et al. 2021; Costa‐Font et al. 2023; Brinkmann et al. 2025). Despite the absence of a market price, such valuations were highly relevant from a welfare perspective as they back rigorous cost‐benefit analysis.

While CVM provides a way to elicit preferences in the absence of markets, it is subject to well‐known biases, including protest bias (expression of an objection to some aspect of the survey or the policy context rather than true valuation), hypothetical bias (overstated WTP in non‐binding scenarios), and anchoring or starting‐point effects, that can distort welfare estimates (R. C. Mitchell and Carson 1989; Arrow et al. 1993; Carson 2000; Carson and Groves 2007). Such biases can be reduced by careful survey design and data analysis, yet results should be interpreted with care.

Section 3 presents our WTP study and the empirical methods used. Sections 4 and 5 respectively present and discuss the results, while Section 6 shares our conclusions.

Balance Billing in the French Healthcare System

With its two‐sector structure, France showcases an original organization of the market for outpatient specialist consultation services.

On the supply side, outpatient specialist physicians in France are self‐employed. They are paid on a fee‐for‐service basis and can choose to work either in the regulated market (called sector 1) or the unregulated market (called sector 2); this choice is exclusive and permanent. Sector 1 specialists must charge a fixed regulated fee per consultation, 3 while sector 2 specialists can charge extra fees above the regulated fee. However, recent regulations and incentives seek to limit the fees in sector 2. Between 2012 and 2022, the share of specialists in sector 2 rose from 42% to 53% (Dixte and Vergier 2022; DREES 2023).

On the demand side, patients can freely choose to consult sector 1 or sector 2 specialists. Generally, those referred by their registered GP for a specialist consultation 4 are fully reimbursed if they see a sector 1 specialist except for a two‐euro copayment. 5 Referred patients who choose to consult a sector 2 specialist must make higher out‐of‐pocket payments.

More specifically regarding health insurance coverage, France's National Health Insurance (NHI) system provides public, compulsory, universal health insurance. In addition to this basic coverage, 95% of the French population have complementary insurance (Barlet et al. 2019). Three types of complementary contracts exist: corporate contracts provided by firms for their employees on a compulsory basis (i.e., employees cannot opt out), individual contracts negotiated and paid on an individual basis, mostly by self‐employed people, and CSS 6 which is means‐tested complementary health insurance provided by the state granting low‐income people virtually free access to health care. Hereafter, we shall refer to CSS as state‐provided complementary health insurance or SPCI.

In general, co‐payments/co‐insurance under the NHI system can be reimbursed by complementary health insurance. However, a small number of specific co‐payments, including the two‐euro co‐payment for a physician visit (see above), cannot be covered by complementary health insurance contracts, and therefore represent out‐of‐pocket payments for patients. One exception is SPCI enrollees who are exempt from the two‐euro co‐payment. Balance billing amounts cannot be reimbursed by complementary insurance for amounts exceeding twice the regulated sector 1 fee. Moreover, coverage of balance billing amounts differs according to the type of complementary insurance. Specifically, corporate contracts provide greater coverage, while half of the individual contracts taken out do not cover balance billing at all (Barlet et al. 2019). Physicians cannot balance bill SPCI beneficiaries (Dormont and Gayet 2021).

For the specific case of a specialist consultation, basic insurance reimburses 70% of the regulated price minus the two‐euro co‐payment. Complementary insurance covers the cost‐sharing amount (i.e., the remaining 30% of the regulated price but not the two‐euro co‐payment). Complementary insurance may also cover a share, but not all, of potential higher fees charged by physicians in sector 2. Finally, SPCI beneficiaries can access both sector 1 and sector 2 specialist services free of charge. Based on Choné et al. (2019), the average out‐of‐pocket payment for a consultation with a sector 2 specialist in 2014 was between 0 and 18 euros for all patients.

Note that most French patients (except for SPCI beneficiaries, who are exempt from advance payment) initially pay the full price of the visit and are reimbursed afterward. Access to specialist care is relatively widespread in France, with 49% of the population having visited a specialist physician in 2019 (OECD 2019).

Methods

The CVM Study

One key objective of this study is to determine the patients' utility gain (if any) from consulting a specialist physician in the unregulated fee sector (sector 2) compared with the regulated fee sector (sector 1).

To do so, we implemented a CVM study. The relevance of the CVM in this context stems from the absence of a genuine market: sector 1 prices are administratively regulated, and consultation fees are generally covered (in full for sector 1 and to a large extent for sector 2, aside from a 2 euro co‐payment). Furthermore, as people living in France are familiar with the choice between sector 1 and sector 2 specialists (Jelovac 2015; Besancenot et al. 2023; Lamiraud and Vranceanu 2025) and typically make advance payments when visiting a physician, the use of CVM to elicit patient preferences for specialist visits in each sector is a valid approach.

We therefore commissioned the market research institute OpinionWay to administer a computer‐assisted questionnaire on a representative sample of the French adult population on 4 January 2021. Segmentation criteria were age, gender, socio‐professional category, type of residency and region. A target sample size of n = 1000 was selected to ensure satisfactory sample power.

To ensure that participants had a common level of knowledge about specialist supply, they were reminded before the survey that patients in France can freely choose between consulting a specialist in sector 1 or in sector 2. They were also told that sector 1 specialists charged a fixed regulated fee per consultation of 30 euros while those working in sector 2 could choose to set their fee above this regulated price. It was made clear that patients who consult sector 1 physicians have very low copayments (only 1 euro at the time of the study) after reimbursement by basic and complementary insurance, while persons consulting sector 2 physicians in general have to pay larger out‐of‐pocket payments.

After providing this introductory information, the respondents were asked to imagine that they had to consult a specialist physician, while assuming that both sector 1 and sector 2 specialists were available in their areas of residence.

First, respondents had to state whether they preferred to consult a sector 1 or a sector 2 specialist physician or whether they were indifferent under the existing reimbursement rules (the fee in sector 1 is in general covered in full, while for consultations of physicians in sector 2 there is often a variable amount to be paid out‐of‐pocket).

Then respondents were asked to imagine that financing mechanisms for specialist consultations had been changed and that specialist consultations were no longer reimbursed by basic and complementary insurance. Hence, patients would have to pay the visit fee in full out‐of‐pocket, regardless of the sector. Given this hypothetical situation, respondents were asked to report their maximum WTP—that is, the maximum amount they can accept to pay for a specialist consultation in either sector 1 or sector 2. A payment card approach was used to facilitate answers. This method is a valid alternative to asking dichotomous choice questions (Donaldson et al. 1997). The proposed bidding ranges (in euros) were: 0–1, 1–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–100, more than 100. Three thresholds were chosen to reflect the one‐euro copayment, and regulated consultation prices: 25 euros for a GP visit, and 30 for a specialist consultation. Other thresholds were chosen to reflect real average fees charged in sector 2 (Choné et al. 2019).

For both sectors, respondents were then asked to what extent they valued (i) the quality of medical care, (ii) the quality of non‐medical services, and (iii) the ease in getting an appointment. Quality was coded for each of the three dimensions as a dichotomous variable, taking the value 1 when high, and 0 when low.

In the final section of the questionnaire, information concerning health status and complementary coverage was collected as well as socio‐demographic information. The survey recorded the household's net income as a categorical variable with eight categories. We aggregated this information into three monthly income categories: lower than 1500 euros, between 1500 and 3500 euros, and higher than 3500 euros. The threshold for the lowest category was defined based on France's regulated minimum wage. Information about household income was missing for 96 individuals. We checked to ensure that the WTP distribution for these participants was not significantly different from the WTP distribution in the full sample. To avoid losing too many observations, we implemented an ordered probit estimation to predict missing income values 7 in a robustness check.

The survey also elicited respondents' individual attitudes towards risk using the self‐reported tolerance‐to‐risk scale introduced by Dohmen et al. (2011). Participants were asked to report “On a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 means “not at all willing to take risks” and 10 means “very willing to take risks,” how willing are you to take risks in general?”.

In Table 2, looking at the coefficients of the covariates included in X ij , most of the WTP results are in line with previous studies. Older patients (i.e., > 50 years) were significantly more likely to express greater WTP for a specialist visit than their younger counterparts, which is consistent with a greater demand for health care services in this age group.

Respondents with a higher income as well as those with a higher education level were willing to pay significantly more for a specialist consultation. This is consistent with an income effect for a normal good and with previous studies, which highlighted that people with a higher socioeconomic status value care from specialist physicians (Doorslaer et al. 2004).

Those suffering from a chronic disease indicated higher WTP, but this was only significant at the 10% level. The other covariates related to health status (self‐assessed health and hospitalization during the previous year) were not significantly associated with WTP for specialist consultations. This may reflect two opposing effects which result in health variables not being significant. On the one hand, respondents with poorer health status may have a higher demand for specialist healthcare services. On the other hand, good health may be correlated with higher socioeconomic status, which is associated with a higher WTP for specialist care.

Respondents covered by corporate complementary contracts were willing to pay significantly more for a specialist consultation than those with no complementary coverage. This likely reflects the higher socioeconomic status of individuals covered by corporate contracts. No significant differences in WTP were observed between respondents without complementary coverage and those with an individual contract, nor between respondents without complementary coverage and SPCI beneficiaries. This suggests that respondents understood the hypothetical change in the financing mechanism for specialist consultations described in the CVM study, in which consultations were no longer reimbursed by complementary insurance.

There were no significant differences in WTP for specialist consultations between male and female respondents. Moreover, marital status, number of children living in the household, and individual risk aversion were not significantly associated with WTP for specialist consultations.

The coefficient on the sector 2 dummy variable (α) was positive and significant for all specifications in Table 2, after controlling for covariates. As we can see, the coefficient is fully stable across all specifications and equals €4.30 (columns 1–9). This value can be interpreted as the mean difference in WTP (expressed in euros) per person between a specialist consultation in sector 2 and a specialist consultation in sector 1, and represents 14.30% of the regulated specialist fee of 30 euros. The coefficient on the sector 2 dummy variable was significant and equal to 17.14 euros in the subsample of enrollees who declared a higher WTP for a consultation with a sector 2 specialist (Model 10). This provides an estimate of the average value associated with a sector 2 consultation among individuals who prefer a sector 2 specialist over a sector 1 specialist.

We conclude that the WTP for a consultation with a specialist physician in sector 2 is significantly larger than for a specialist in sector 1.

As an upshot of all these, the analysis of heterogeneous effects shows that all subgroups of respondents valued sector 2 specialist consultations significantly more than sector 1 consultations.

Discussion

In this section, we present some robustness checks and provide some interpretation of our results.

Robustness Checks

First, we checked whether some individuals might have expressed protest answers (Dziegielewska and Mendelsohn 2007). As noted in Section 4.1, no respondent selected the lowest WTP category ([0–1]), suggesting the absence of protest zeros. However, Figure 1 shows that 67.2% of respondents were willing to pay less than 30 euros for a consultation with a sector 1 specialist, and 57.4% for a consultation with a sector 2 specialist. Moreover, 54.4% of respondents reported a WTP below 30 euros for both types of consultations, and 30.7% reported a WTP below 25 euros in both scenarios. Given that respondents were reminded that the regulated price for a sector 1 consultation is 30 euros, we examined whether these relatively low stated WTP values could reflect protest behavior. Specifically, we estimated probit models to analyze the characteristics of respondents reporting a WTP below 25 euros and below 30 euros for both sector 1 and sector 2 consultations. The results are presented in Table 4 (Models 1 and 2, respectively). Respondents with a lower education level, those in the lower income category, those without complementary health insurance, and those not suffering from a chronic disease were significantly more likely to be willing to pay less than 25 euros (Model 1) and less than 30 euros (Model 2). These characteristics are consistent with lower ability to pay or lower expected need for specialist care, suggesting that these responses reflect genuine preferences rather than protest behavior.

To further assess the impact of potential protest answers, we conducted additional analyses on specific subsamples. The first of these (Model 3) excluded enrollees without complementary coverage, as these individuals may be more likely to object to the hypothetical payment mechanism. In a context where most individuals in France are covered by complementary insurance—consistent with public policy objectives—those without such coverage may expect improvements in their financial protection rather than increased out‐of‐pocket payments. This may, in turn, increase the likelihood of protest responses. The second subsample excluded all SPCI respondents, as these persons cannot be balance billed by law (Model 4). The third subsample (Model 5) excluded both SPCI beneficiaries and participants without complementary coverage. In these models, the coefficient on the sector 2 variable was slightly larger than in previous estimations, respectively, 4.59, 4.48 and 4.80. While this pattern could partly reflect the exclusion of potential protest responses, this is also consistent with the fact that those models were run on wealthier subsamples of the study population.

Overall, these analyses suggest that our main results are not driven by protest responses. If anything, any remaining bias would likely lead to an underestimation of the additional benefit associated with consultations with sector 2 specialists.

Second, to further test the robustness of stated preferences, we investigated the consistency between the preferences for sector 1 and sector 2 specialist physicians implied by respondents' WTP values and their explicit rankings based on a direct preference question asking whether they preferred to consult a sector 1 or sector 2 specialist under the existing reimbursement rules (see Section 3.1). More specifically, we computed the percentage of fully consistent answers between implicit and explicit preferences, that is the percentage of respondents for whom the implied WTP ranking (higher WTP for sector 2, higher WTP for sector 1, or the same bidding range for sector 1 and sector 2) exactly matched the explicit ranking (respectively, preference for sector 2, preference for sector 1 or indifference between the two sectors). Considering the match between respondents' implicit and explicit preference for sector 1 and sector 2 specialist physicians, 94% of the respondents provided fully consistent responses. The remaining responses correspond to cases in which respondents declared a preference for either sector 1 or sector 2 in the explicit ranking question but selected the same bidding ranges for both sectors when answering the WTP questions. Although the WTP questions did not provide differentiated answers between the two sectors for these respondents (likely due to the use of ranges to collect WTP), these responses can be interpreted as partially consistent answers. Overall, this very high level of consistency is quite reassuring concerning the robustness of stated WTP values collected in this study, as they match the preferences expressed under the existing reimbursement rules.

Third, we investigated the possibility that participants anchored their WTP in both sectors around the regulated fee of 30 euros. To do so, we computed the average WTP in both sectors. Based on our benchmark specification in column 1 of Table 2, the estimated average WTP for a sector 1 and a sector 2 consultation was, respectively, 28.66 (± 10.39) euros and 32.96 euros (± 10.39). The overall average WTP was 30.81 euros, which is close to the 30 euro regulated fee for a specialist consultation at the time of the survey.

However, our results show substantial dispersion in stated WTP on both sides of the regulated fee. We have already discussed responses below 30 euros. In addition, as noted in Section 4.1, 32.8% (respectively, 42.6%) of respondents were willing to pay more than 30 euros for a sector 1 (respectively, sector 2) consultation. Among respondents with a WTP above 30 euros for a sector 1 consultation, 19.4% preferred sector 1 over sector 2, compared with 9.0% in the full sample. Similarly, among those with a WTP above 30 euros for a sector 2 consultation, 55.3% preferred sector 2 over sector 1, compared with 30.0% in the full sample. These patterns suggest that WTP values reflect respondents' preferences and that participants engaged meaningfully with the valuation task.

Although we cannot rule out the possibility that a regulated price in sector 1 may have induced some anchoring around 30 euros, this does not undermine our main finding—that the WTP for a specialist consultation in sector 2 is significantly larger than that for a consultation in sector 1. As our primary variable of interest is the difference between WTP across the two sectors, any anchoring around a common reference point would likely affect both scenarios similarly, thereby mitigating concerns that anchoring drives our results.

Finally, we note that data were collected in January 2021, during a particularly tense period for the French healthcare system, as the country was experiencing the second wave of the Covid‐19 crisis, with approximately 400 deaths per day attributable to the infection (Worldometer data). The heightened risk of contamination and repeated lockdowns increased anxiety and reduced well‐being in the general population (Joannès et al. 2023; Andersen et al. 2021). This additional medical stress may have raised individuals' WTP for a specialist consultation. However, clinical practice was similarly affected by Covid‐related restrictions across both sectors, and is therefore unlikely to bias the comparison across sectors.

Cost‐Benefit Analysis: The Patients' Perspective

Our results provide evidence that the unregulated fee sector generates positive private value for patients and is associated with a positive utility differential relative to sector 1 consultations. Simple calculations help provide additional intuition for this finding.

Multiplying the mean WTP difference per person (i.e., €4.30) by the number of consultations with sector 2 specialist physicians (1.05 × 68 million), 13 we obtain a rough estimate of the total patient surplus generated by sector 2 consultations of approximately €307 million per year.

At the same time, patients consulting specialists in sector 2 face higher fees that are not fully covered by health insurance (in contrast to sector 1 consultations). In 2022, the average household out‐of‐pocket payment for specialist care in France—defined as the share of fees not covered by basic and complementary insurance—represented 10.3% of total specialist care expenditure (DREES 2023), corresponding to approximately €142 million.

The additional value associated with sector 2 consultations (€307 million) therefore exceeds the additional costs borne by patients (€142 million), implying an annual net welfare gain of about €165 million. This comparison can be seen as an additional robustness check of our estimates, as it is consistent with the observed demand for sector 2 specialist services.

CVM Versus Revealed‐Preference Approach

So far, we have justified that the use of the CVM to assess patient preferences for specialist consultations in each sector is a valid approach in our context. An alternative approach would be to analyze observed consultation choices and prices using insurance claims data from the NHI system. However, in our setting, this approach faces important limitations due to the heterogeneous geographic distribution of specialist physicians. Patient choices may be constrained by the limited availability of sector 1 or sector 2 specialists in certain areas (Anguis et al. 2021), or even by the absence of both in so‐called “medical desert” or underserved areas (i.e., geographic areas with insufficient medical supply). By contrast, CVM allowed us to abstract from such supply‐side constraints by asking respondents to assume that both sector 1 and sector 2 specialists were available in their areas of residence (see Section 3.1). Moreover, because physician fees are largely reimbursed, observed prices do not necessarily reflect patients' marginal WTP, which limits the relevance of revealed‐preference approaches in this setting.

Conclusion

In many countries, national health systems include a regulated sector offering basic health care services for a regulated fee, and an unregulated sector, where physicians can charge an extra fee on top of the regulated one. Many theoretical studies have analyzed the welfare implications of this dual organization, taking in particular into account price and quality differentiation, signaling effects, congestion effects, and profits for the medical profession. However, it is not clear whether patients value consultations in the unregulated sector more than consultations in the regulated sector, or are simply compelled to accept balance billing because they cannot access regulated (i.e., lower fee) services.

France, with its two‐sector organization of the outpatient specialist care market, provides a perfect setting to test this “higher value assumption” associated with the balance billing sector. To do so, we developed a CVM study, where participants in a representative survey of the French adult population were asked to report their WTP for consultation in the two sectors, under the assumption that both sector 1 and sector 2 specialist physicians were available in their areas of residence and there was no reimbursement for either sector, that is that they had to pay the consultation fee in full out‐of‐pocket.

The results show a significant positive difference of €4.30 per person in favor of the unregulated sector, representing almost 15% of the regulated fee. This positive difference was significant across all subgroups defined by gender, age, education level, marital status, income, health status, insurance coverage, and area of residence. Having said that, the youngest respondents (aged 18–30) valued consultations with sector 2 specialists less than older respondents, while high‐income individuals value sector 2 specialist consultations more than other income groups. Other results show that the preference for a consultation in the unregulated sector was positively related to the perception of better quality of medical care, greater ease in getting an appointment, and, to a lesser extent, better quality of non‐medical services. Finally, we estimated the overall patient welfare gain associated with sector 2 consultations at approximately €165 million per year.

Because CVM compares predetermined scenarios based on a snap‐shot view of the market, our conclusions should be interpreted with caution. More specifically, from our estimated gain alone, we cannot infer how patient welfare would evolve if, for instance, only one sector and one pricing scheme existed, as in that case, we would observe substantial resource reallocations and changes in the terms of supply and demand of medical services (Besancenot et al. 2023; Lamiraud and Vranceanu 2025).

One might argue that the net gain in patient welfare from consulting specialists in sector 2 is relatively small and therefore does not, in itself, justify the additional complexity of maintaining the two‐sector organization in France. However, it is very probable that our estimate of the net gain is closer to the lower end of the possible range, as discussed above. Furthermore, retaining sector 2 along the sector 1 means providing patients with more choices, something that the CVM analysis we performed here could not capture as respondents had to assume that both sectors exist.

Maintaining sector 2 is also important because thanks to the larger medical profits involved, it attracts more skilled individuals to the medical profession. If these larger profits came at the expense of patient utility, this would cast serious doubts about the usefulness of maintaining sector 2. Our findings suggest that the sector 2 generates positive utility gains for patients. Note that our estimations are performed under the assumption that patients have a genuine choice between sector 1 and sector 2 specialist physicians. This choice may be achieved only if the government monitors the supply of care to ensure territorial diversity and equal access to specialist care in both sectors.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to provide evidence that the unregulated physician sector in France offers positive net value to patients. In this respect, it is relevant not only in the French context, but also for other countries where the cost of baseline treatment is fully reimbursed by basic insurance, and patients pay out‐of‐pocket for non‐basic care. Future studies in these other countries could implement similar contingent valuation analyses or infer benefits from actual consumption behaviors.
