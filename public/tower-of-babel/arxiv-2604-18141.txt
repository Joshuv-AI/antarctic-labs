Abstract
Timely and accurate monitoring in geofencing scenarios is challenging when relying on ultra-low power Internet of Things devices (IoTDs) powered by energy harvesting (EH). This is mainly because frequent wake-ups for data acquisition and data uploading may quickly deplete their limited energy buffer. Conventional grid-like IoT deployments overlook these limitations and merely rely on continuously powered sensing. Herein, we propose an energy-aware geofencing framework for camera-equipped EH IoTDs deployed around a protected area and its surrounding perimeter zone. The framework integrates a directional sensing power model with an operational representation of EH, sensing, sleeping, and reporting, accounting for the limited field-of-view (FoV) and distance-dependent detection confidence of the IoTDs. Device activity is controlled by the coverage-providing access point, which hosts a mobile edge host and a facility geocencing system to ensure timely and reliable detection under tight energy constraints. Reinforcement learning is used to determine IoTD placement, enabling earlier intruder detection than uniform grid-based deployments. Numerical results show that the proposed coordinated sensing and reporting configuration achieves frugal geofencing with fewer devices, while concurrently improving detection timeliness and dependability.

Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT)is a key element of modern digital transformation, allowing for the seamless integration of physical objects into the digital ecosystem. It connects a variety of IoT devices (IoTDs)equipped with sensors, actuators, computing, and communication modules, enabling them to collect, exchange, and respond to data across different environments

]
. However, with projections exceeding 38 billion connected IoTDsby 2029, the prevailing reliance on non-rechargeable batteries becomes increasingly incompatible with scalability and sustainability, driving frequent maintenance interventions and higher operational costs
[

In this context, Ambient IoT (A-IoT)envisions large-scale deployments of battery-free or battery-assisted IoTDsoperating at
\mu

[

[

. As deployments expand and underpin safety and mission-critical infrastructure, stringent requirements on availability, reliability, and latency further intensify. This makes the design of energy-aware sensing and reporting policies a central problem for dependable A-IoToperation
[
1

Barrier coverage is a key aspect of collaborative sensing for access control along a boundary, where a moving target must be detected by the barrier formed by the IoTDs. Recent works have introduced quality-aware methods to capture performance degradation in harsh environments and sensor faults
[
7
,
8

. Deployment strategies, including grid-based and application-specific designs, can reduce the sensor density required to achieve high reliability with respect to random deployment
[

To address coverage gaps caused by offsets, overlapping views, or failures, prior works have considered sensor mobility

, optimal IoTDselection
[

Despite these advances, most existing approaches assume that IoTDscan operate continuously or with relatively high duty cycles. In general, they do not account for the intermittency of the IoTDsoperating with EH, directional sensor field of view (FoV)constraints, and/or the joint design of sensing and reporting schedules within a unified A-IoTframework. Thus, ensuring reliable barrier coverage with energy-constrained IoTDsremains a pending challenge
[
16
]
.

To maintain performance under strict energy budgets, IoTDsand/or the network should intelligently decide when to sleep, sense, and report. Thus, an intelligent deployment must be complemented with dynamic duty cycling and adaptive sensing schedules to sustain network availability. Herein, we focus on an IoTD-based geofencing deployment for accurate and early intrusion detection with frugal use of on-device energy, signaling, and computing resources. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We present a geofencing system for low-power camera-equipped IoTDs, where a protected area (or asset) is surrounded by an outer perimeter zone that allows for early intruder detection before they cross the boundary.

•

We propose a directional sensing‑power function that fuses distance decay and sensor (camera) FoVmasking to quantify object‑detection confidence over time. We also model energy-aware operation, including sensing, sleeping, reporting, and stochastic EHdynamics.

•

We formulate a geofenced area protection problem in which the access point (AP)provides geofencing system control by dynamically adjusting device activity, reporting, and sensing orientation to balance detection reliability, timeliness, and energy sustainability.

•

We assess the proposed intelligent and exploration-based IoTDdeployments (applying a Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based approach) and sensing configurations. We demonstrate how target mobility-aware control facilitated by APcontrol signaling can decrease the necessary device density (and, therefore, system cost), while maintaining high reliability and timely detection.

II
System Model

We consider the geofencing scenario shown in Fig.
1
, where a radio APprovides coverage to a set of
N
IoTDsdeployed in an area of interest, such as a warehouse or industrial site. Spatially, the monitored setup consists of two concentric regions: (i) a protected geofenced area, which represents the facility or perimeter that needs to be secured, and (ii) an outer perimeter zone that acts as an early detection buffer around it. The deployed IoTDsare arranged so that their directional sensing sectors collectively create a barrier around the geofenced area, while also extending some coverage into the perimeter zone. The outer perimeter zone serves as an early-warning area where approaching intruders are detected first. This enhances the chances of timely identification and classification before they enter the protected area at time
t_{\mathrm{g}}^{(o)}
. This capability allows for proactive alarms starting from the moment an intruder enters the outer perimeter at time
t_{\mathrm{in}}^{(o)}
and enables earlier adjustments to device duty cycles and reporting decisions.

Fig. 1:
System model for energy-aware geofencing intrusion detection. A set of camera-equipped IoTDswith directional sensing capability is deployed around the protected geofenced area, while an outer zone provides an early detection region for approaching intruders before boundary crossing. The AP-MEHcollects local detection reports (red dashed line), predicts trajectories, and dynamically adapts sensing and reporting decisions (wake-up requests, green dashed line) according to task relevance and device energy availability.

Each IoTDis equipped with an optical camera
of a given FoV(
\Delta\theta
, assumed identical for all IoTDcameras), vision depth and rotation capability, a EHmodule, an energy buffer, and a wake-up radio (WuR). Moreover, it includes application-related hardware, such as a data storage element which, apart from a data buffer, hosts a lightweight Computer Vision (CV) model which is trained to detect different object classes (e.g., humans, vehicles, warehouse inventory, etc.), along with a radio frequency (RF)communication module for the needed communication with the APand a low-power microcontroller.

During IoTDoperation, the CV model is used in inference mode, ingesting the locally generated camera video frames. A MEH of given processing, memory, and storage resource capability is deployed in the vicinity of the AP. The MEH hosts a facility geocencing system (FGS), composed of a Controller that regulates the operation of the IoTDstowards fulfilling the geofencing goal in an energy-aware manner. To effectively control the deployment’s operation, we assume that the Controller receives information by each deployed IoTD, either periodically (i.e., the energy buffer level), or on an event-based fashion (e.g., video feed interruption and restoration time). Apart from the Controller, the FGSincorporates a fusion center (FC)to obtain and process the local detection output data from all IoTDs, when they are uploaded via the AP. In terms of network deployment flexibility, the APis assumed to be permanently fixed at a particular location, whereas the IoTDscan be easily placed at different locations of the area of interest to best address the geofence entry detection requirements.

Detected objects are reported to the APtogether with some metadata, including IoTDstatus information, useful to the Controller. Based on such uplink signaling to the AP, the received information reaches the MEH, where the FGSis hosted. Then, within the FGS, the FCfuses detection reports and predicts remaining intruder trajectories, and, subsequently triggers the Controller to reconfigure the most relevant IoTDsaccording to task relevance and energy availability. As a result, the Controller instructs the APto issue event-driven wake-up requests to selected IoTDsthrough their WuRinterface. These requests are triggered when an approaching intruder is detected, and the predicted trajectory indicates that additional sensing support is needed from sleeping IoTDs. In this way, only the most relevant IoTDsare activated, which improves timely detection, while avoiding unnecessary energy consumption.

II-A

Sensing power function

Fig.
2
shows an IoTDwith an (azimuth) angular sector of
\Delta\theta=\theta_{\max}-\theta_{\min}
degrees, which is determined by both
\theta_{\min}
and the FoV. Additionally, the figure includes an object with an already performed (up to time instant
t
) and planned trajectory that needs to be detected, as well as the involved geometrical and time parameters that will be explained in what follows.

Fig. 2:
Detection sector of an IoTD(e.g., camera) showing the object trajectory, entry and exit points, along with the angular and radial constraints.

Assuming that time is measured in transmission time intervals (TTIs), the sensing function
p(r_{t},\theta_{t})
captures the detection confidence of the target within one observation window (TTI), under the assumption that the target remains fixed during that interval.

Since the targeted object is not detectable when present at an azimuth angle outside the camera FoVof the given IoTD, i.e., outside the angular interval
[\theta_{min},\theta_{max}]
, we need to consider the following angular masking function

I(\Delta\theta_{t})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\theta_{o}\in[\theta_{min},\theta_{max}]$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1)

Given that the targeted object is within the IoTDcamera FoV, we propose a decaying function of the distance between the IoTDand the targeted object, which nulls at distance
r_{max}
. This captures the detection capability of the IoTD, which may be based, for example, on a CV algorithm applied for object recognition. Such algorithms result in a maximum number of false negatives (practically not detecting any object) when these targeted objects are located at distances beyond a maximum distance within which detection is feasible. Herein, for a given TTIperiod, we assume that the distance-dependent sensing function is modeled as follows

p(r_{t})=\begin{cases}\exp{(-\eta r_{t}}),&\text{if $r_{t}\in[0,r_{\text{max}}]$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

Thus, taking into account both distance and angular domains (equivalently, working with polar coordinates), the sensing power function for a given time interval of duration of one TTIis given by

p(r_{t},\Delta\theta_{t})=p(r_{t})I(\Delta\theta_{t}).

(3)

The detection probability for an object trajectory entering the detectability area of a given IoTDat time instance
t_{in}
and leaving it at time instance
t_{out}
, when measured at time
t\in[t_{in},t_{out}]
, is

p_{\mathrm{obj}}=\max_{t\in[t_{\mathrm{in}},t_{\mathrm{out}}]}p(r_{t},\Delta\theta_{t}).

(4)

It should be noted that, for a given IoTD, in case of non-detection of an object for the time when the object is within the detectability area of the IoTD, the detection task concludes unsuccessfully, i.e., the context of this task is not retained until the previously non-detected object re-enters the IoTDcamera FoV. In the latter (object return) case, the IoTDwill launch a new detection task.

II-B

Successful object detection

Upon detecting an intruder, the IoTDsupload a detection update to the AP, optionally accompanied by a classification label (e.g., “human”, “vehicle”, or “animal”). The FCcollects the updates from the IoTDsand declares a successful object detection as soon as at least one valid detection report is received. A detection report is deemed valid when the sensing confidence satisfies the detection condition
p_{\mathrm{obj}}\geq p_{\mathrm{th}}
. After that point, the network no longer needs to adapt sensing, wake-up, and uplink reporting decisions to that object’s motion, since the object has already been detected and identified.
If continuous object tracking until departure from the protected area is also required, then the sensing and reporting policy should continue adapting to the target trajectory over time. However, extending the problem from detection and recognition to continuous tracking is outside the scope of this paper and is left for future work.

II-C

Energy considerations

We adopt a normalized, discrete EHmodel with battery capacity
C_{\max}
and independent Bernoulli energy arrivals for each IoTD. At each TTI, each IoTDindependently harvests up to
P_{\mathrm{B}}
units with probability
\lambda
, and harvested energy is stored up to
C_{\max}
, while overflow is discarded.
1
1

1

The proposed model can adapt to different EHsources (e.g., solar, RF). In that case, probability
\lambda
can be modeled as dependent on context parameters, for instance, the time-of-day, the IoTDdistance from the RFsignaling source, etc. Such modeling and its accuracy verification are left for future work.
On the energy consumption side, transmission, wake-up operation, and camera reorientation are actions consuming
P_{\mathrm{Tx}}
,
P_{\mathrm{WuR}}
, and
P_{\mathrm{rot,bin}}
units per TTI, respectively. If the energy buffer level is insufficient for the selected action, the action is unsuccessful, and the device remains unavailable until enough energy is accumulated again.

III
Problem formulation & proposed solution

The goal of the FGSis to detect each target as early as possible within the outer perimeter zone, ideally before it crosses the geofenced boundary. Let
t_{\mathrm{in}}^{(o)}
denote the time at which intruder
o
first enters the outer zone,
t_{\mathrm{g}}^{(o)}
the time at which it crosses the geofenced boundary, and
t_{\mathrm{det}}^{(o)}
the time of the first valid detection report for that intruder. For each intruder
o
, detection is classified as early if
{t_{\mathrm{det}}^{(o)}<t_{\mathrm{g}}^{(o)}}
, late if
t_{\mathrm{det}}^{(o)}\geq t_{\mathrm{g}}^{(o)}
, and missed if no valid detection report is produced while the intruder remains within the monitored region.

We define the object-level detection error as

E_{\mathrm{obj}}(o)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{early detection},\\
\mu_{1},&\text{late detection},\\
1,&\text{misdetection},\end{cases}

(5)

where
\mu_{1}\in[0,1]
penalizes late detection less severely than a complete miss. Note that
E_{\mathrm{obj}}(o)
is evaluated during training and simulation using ground-truth object trajectories. In deployment, the AP executes the learned policy online based only on the available network state information.

Maximizing timely detection while minimizing undetected intrusions depends on the energy availability, position, and FoVorientation of the deployed IoTDs. Activating more IoTDsand increasing their duty cycles can reduce blind spots, but it also increases sensing, reporting, and reconfiguration energy consumption. Conversely, aggressive energy saving may leave parts of the perimeter insufficiently monitored or prevent low-energy IoTDsfrom reporting valid detections. Therefore, the design objective is to jointly configure sensing activity, event-driven wake-up, and camera orientation to minimize missed and late detections while preserving long-term device availability.

III-A

Problem formulation

Let
\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)=\left[\delta_{1}(t),\delta_{2}(t),\ldots,\delta_{N}(t)\right]
denote the FGS-issued sensing policy to be applied at TTI
t
, where
\delta_{j}(t)\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether IoTD
j
should be (remain or change its state to, if different) inactive/sleeping or active/sensing, respectively. Given this policy, the APissues event-driven wake-up requests to selected IoTDs, the camera FoVsof which are predicted to, at least, partly cover the remaining intruder trajectory. Likewise,
\boldsymbol{\Delta\theta}(t)=\left[\Delta\theta_{1}(t),\Delta\theta_{2}(t),\ldots,\Delta\theta_{N}(t)\right]
denote the FoVorientation vector of the deployed IoTDs. Since camera reorientation is energy-consuming,
\boldsymbol{\Delta\theta}(t)
is updated less frequently than
\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)
.

Then, the joint IoTDactivation and camera rotation optimization problem can be stated as

\min_{\boldsymbol{\delta}(t),\,\boldsymbol{\Delta\theta}(t)}\frac{1}{\alpha T}\sum_{\forall o}E_{\mathrm{obj}}(o),

(6)

where
T
is the total number of analyzed TTIsand
\alpha
is the expected number of intruders per TTI. The goal is to optimize the activation and orientation policies to minimize the average intruder detection error, favoring early detection and penalizing late detections and complete misses.

III-B

RL-based proposal

The FGSmust repeatedly adapt its decisions to time-varying object trajectories, partial sensing outcomes, and EHconditions. For this reason, we adopt a centralized RL-based approach, where the FGSController acts as the decision agent and dynamically selects the sensing states and camera orientations of the most relevant IoTDsin order to balance system availability and detection reliability and timeliness. In its capacity, the APacts as a policy enforcement entity.

At TTI
t
, the system state is defined as
\mathcal{T}(t)=\left\{\mathcal{T}_{j}(t)\right\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}}
, with per-device state

\mathcal{T}_{j}(t)=\left\{\delta_{j}(t),\,\Delta\theta_{j}(t),\,p(r_{t},\Delta\theta_{t}),\,(x_{j},y_{j}),\,B_{j}(t)\right\},

(7)

where
(x_{j},y_{j})
is the position of IoTD
j
, and
B_{j}(t)
is its energy buffer level at time
t
.
Given the observed state
\mathcal{T}(t)
, the FGSController selects the activation vector
\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)
and, when required, updates the orientation vector
\boldsymbol{\Delta\theta}(t)
. Hence, the learned policy is
{\pi^{*}:\mathcal{T}(t)\rightarrow\left(\boldsymbol{\delta}(t),\boldsymbol{\Delta\theta}(t)\right),}

to maximize the long-term expected reward.
Moreover, to transform the desired objective in (
6
) to the RLdomain, we define the reward function as

r(t)=\left(1-\frac{\boldsymbol{\delta}(t)\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}}{N}\right)+\mu_{2}\mathrm{Cov}(t)-\alpha\mu_{3}E_{\mathrm{obj}}(t),

(8)

where
\mathrm{Cov}(t)
denotes the fraction of the perimeter effectively covered by active IoTDs, and
\mu_{2},\mu_{3}>0
are weighting factors. The first term promotes energy efficiency by penalizing unnecessary activations, the second rewards spatial coverage, and the third penalizes late detections and missed detections through the intruder error in (
5
). In this way, the FGS, via the APlearns to activate and orient only the most trajectory-relevant IoTDs, while preserving energy resources for sustained geofencing operation.

IV-C

Early detection and latency at the 99.9% reliability

Reliability alone is insufficient for intrusion prevention. Late detections, even if eventually successful, may not allow enough time to trigger protective actions. Therefore, we next evaluate the timeliness at the reliability-feasible operating point
N=N_{\min}(\tau)

In contrast, the RL-based approach sustains timeliness more robustly as

By prioritizing IoTDsthat are closer to the predicted intruder route and adjusting FoVto reduce blind spots, the RL-based approach increases the probability that an intruder is observed soon after arrival despite longer sleep cycles. Moreover, the reliability-constrained choice

implicitly captures a second-order trade-off: at very large

V
Conclusion

This paper introduced a geofencing framework for camera-equipped EHIoTDs. By combining a directional sensing model, an energy-aware operation model, and RL-based control, the approach facilitated earlier and more reliable intrusion detection within the outer perimeter while using fewer IoTDsthan a static grid. Results showed that the RL method lowers the necessary IoTDdensity, improves detection timeliness, and ensures higher energy availability across IoTDs.
