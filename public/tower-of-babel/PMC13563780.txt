Exploring brain activity differences in functional constipation using fNIRS: a case-control study
Authors: Weishuyi Ruan, Fangchao Wu, Zhiping Liao, Zhaoqing Li, Zhihui Huang, Da Wang, Gulinazi Rouzi, Yan Hao, Jinna Hu, Jingyi Wang, Ying Zhang, Jianhua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFunctional constipation (FCon) involves central nervous system dysregulation, particularly in brain regions responsible for visceral sensation, emotional processing, and autonomic control. Using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), we examined brain activity alterations in FCon patients to elucidate brain-gut interactions.MethodPatients with constipation meeting Rome IV criteria were recruited from Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, while healthy controls were enrolled from the local community. The cortical regions of interest (ROIs) included the prefrontal cortex (PFC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), broca’s area, primary motor cortex (M1), supplementary motor area (SMA)/premotor cortex (PMC), and primary somatosensory cortex (S1). Participants were monitored during resting state and three defecation-related tasks: imagined defecation, simulated defecation and anal sphincter contraction. Functional connectivity (FC) strength and regional brain activation (HbO concentration changes) were compared between groups and across tasks.Result64 patients and 64 healthy controls (50% females each) were included. There was no significant difference in average FC between groups (P = 0.757). In both groups, simulated defecation and anal sphincter contraction activated most ROIs more strongly than imagined defecation (P < 0.05). In healthy controls, anal sphincter contraction elicited significantly lower HbO than simulated defecation in the left Broca’s area (P < 0.05); no significant differences between these two tasks were found in the FCon group. No significant between-group differences in HbO were found during any task. During imagined defecation, FCon patients showed a trend toward lower HbO across most ROIs, most notably in the right M1, with comparable levels in the right PFC. During simulated defecation, a similar trend of lower HbO in sensorimotor ROIs was observed alongside numerically higher HbO in the right PFC/DLPFC. During anal sphincter contraction, they showed numerically higher HbO across all ROIs. Progression curves further revealed a linear increasing pattern in FCon patients across tasks, contrasting with task-appropriate modulation in healthy controls.ConclusionThis first fNIRS study reveals task-specific cortical dysregulation in FCon. Although between-group differences were not statistically significant, these trends and dynamic patterns identify sensorimotor and prefrontal cortical targets for future neuromodulation therapies.Trial registration ChiCTR2500104882.Keywords: Functional constipation, Functional near-infrared spectroscopy, Imagined defecation, Simulated defecation, Anal sphincter contraction

Introduction

Functional constipation (FCon) is a prevalent functional gastrointestinal disorder (FGID) defined by persistent and difficult defecation in the absence of structural abnormalities [1]. FCon significantly impairs patients’ quality of life, leading to psychological distress, reduced work productivity, and increased healthcare utilization. Despite its high burden, the underlying neural mechanisms of FCon remain incompletely understood, prompting growing interest in neuroimaging research [2].

Defecation is a complex physiological process involving coordinated interactions between the autonomic and somatic nervous systems, under the hierarchical control of brain [3]. Emerging evidence suggests that FCon involves central nervous system dysregulation, particularly in brain regions responsible for visceral sensation, emotional processing, and autonomic control [4]. Neuroimaging techniques have been used progressively to study the functional and structural alterations of the brains of FCon patients, providing critical insights into these mechanisms [5, 6].

For instance, Liu et al. found patients with FCon had decreased brain functional connectivity involving the thalamus, rostral anterior cingulate cortex (rACC), and supplementary motor area (SMA), which indicated that FCon was associated with abnormalities in the thalamo-cortical network [7]. Another study revealed reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) in the white matter tracts connecting the thalamus to key limbic and sensorimotor regions, including the amygdala (AMY), hippocampal gyrus (HIPP), precentral gyrus (PreCen), postcentral gyrus (PostCen), SMA, and precuneus [8]. These findings demonstrate impaired structural connectivity in FCon. Despite fMRI’s superior spatial resolution, its high operational costs limit its feasibility for large-scale or longitudinal FCon studies. Additionally, its supine position requirement reduces ecological validity for defecation-related tasks, an issue that can be addressed by fNIRS, which allows seated measurements.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) has emerged as a promising neuroimaging tool for investigating brain-gut interactions in gastrointestinal disorders [9, 10]. Unlike traditional modalities, fNIRS offers a portable, cost-effective, and motion-tolerant approach to measuring cortical hemodynamic responses [11]. This non-invasive technique detects changes in oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) concentrations, providing real-time insights into neural activity during visceral stimulation [12] and cognitive tasks [13]. In recent years, fNIRS has expanded significantly in gastrointestinal research, particularly in the study of FGIDs. A growing body of evidence suggests that FGIDs are associated with dysregulation of the brain-gut axis, with abnormal activation in brain regions responsible for visceral sensation, emotional processing, and autonomic control. For example, Ryo et al. investigated brain activity in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) in irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and functional dyspepsia (FD) patients using fNIRS, and found that increased activity in the PFC of patients with FD [9]. This research group also combined fNIRS with visual reality environment, demonstrating that brain activity in the ventrolateral PFC (VLPFC) increased while activity in the dorsolateral PFC (DLPFC) decreased under stressful psychosocial conditions induced in the VR space in IBS patients [10]. These findings are consistent with fMRI studies, yet fNIRS offers the advantage of greater ecological validity, as it allows for measurements in more natural settings, such as sitting or postprandial states.

In our study, the design of motor paradigms is critical for fNIRS experiments aimed at investigating brain function in patients with FCon. To comprehensively assess cortical activation patterns, we have developed three task-based paradigms focusing on motor imagery, simulated defecation effort, and pelvic floor muscle rehabilitation. Specifically, the paradigms include imagined defecation [14], simulated defecation [15], and anal sphincter contraction [16].

This study aims to utilize fNIRS to monitor real-time cortical functional changes in patients with FCon during these three distinct task states. As part of our team’s onging research on neurofunctional rehabilitation in pelvic floor disorders, this investigation represents a critical component in our series of brain functional explorations of pelvic floor dysfunction. Under the leadership of Dr. Jianhua Li, who pioneered the concept of brain-computer interface (BCI)-based pelvic floor rehabilitation therapy [17], this work specifically focuses on identifying aberrant brain region targets in FCon patients. The anticipated findings are expected to [1] provide novel theoretical insights into the central neurological mechanisms underlying constipation, and [2] establish a scientific foundation for developing precision neuromodulation therapeutic strategies, ultimately facilitating the clinical translation of BCI-based interventions for this patient population.

Specifically, we anticipated that patients with FCon would show altered cortical activation patterns compared to healthy controls during defecation-related tasks, with potential differences across the three paradigms (imagined defecation, simulated defecation, and anal sphincter contraction) due to their varying sensorimotor and cognitive demands.

Method

Subjects

This clinical study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital (No.20250470). Patients with constipation were recruited from the Sir Run Run Shaw hospital, and healthy controls were from local community.

Inclusion criteria for the FCon group

1.Met the Rome IV diagnostic criteria for functional constipation. 2.Presented with a variety of major bowel habit subtypes, including slow-transit constipation, outlet obstruction, or a combination thereof. 3.Demonstrated the ability to comprehend the pre-trial instructions provided by researchers and to adhere strictly to the study protocols.

Exclusion criteria (applied to both FCon patients and healthy controls)

(1) A history of loose stools that are infrequent and occur without the use of laxatives. (2) Diagnosis of Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) or Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) (e.g., Crohn‘s disease, ulcerative colitis). (3) A history of traumatic brain injury or any known neurological disorder. (4) Current use of centrally acting medications (e.g., psychotropics, opioids) that may influence neurological function. (5) Presence of severe organic diseases (e.g., cardiac, pulmonary, renal, or hepatic failure), active infectious diseases, or other systemic disorders that may impair cerebral function. (6) Presence of severe dermatological conditions on the scalp that would interfere with fNIRS sensor placement. (7) Pregnancy.

Experimental procedure

All subjects were asked to sit in an individual room with a natural light, remain still and quiet. After 5 min, the first fNIRS scan was performed for seven minutes while the subjects were asked to thinking nothing. Subsequently, three defecation-related paradigms were administered in sequence: imagined defecation, simulated defecation (Valsalva maneuver), and anal sphincter contraction. Each paradigm was thoroughly explained and demonstrated to the subjects before recording. The task design followed an event-related block structure, optimized for capturing the cortical hemodynamic response measured by fNIRS. Each trial consisted of a 6.5-second active task period followed by an 11.5-second rest period, and this cycle was repeated five times per paradigm. The 6.5-second task duration was selected to be sufficient for the initiation and execution of the respective motor or cognitive task while remaining within the typical window for a robust hemodynamic response. The 11.5-second inter-trial interval allows for the hemodynamic signal to return near baseline, minimizing carry-over effects between trials [18, 19]. Between each paradigm, the subjects were asked to rest for 5 min. All subjects underwent the experimental procedure in the fixed order depicted in Fig. 1.

Defecation task paradigm

Acquisition of fNIRS

NirSmartII-3000 A equipment (Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co.,Ltd.,China) was used to continuously measure the concentration changes in brain oxygen hemoglobin considered as cortical activation. The near-infrared light was conveyed at wavelengths of 730 nm and 850 nm, with a sample rate of 11 Hz. A total of 39 channels were built by 19 light sources and 13 detectors for fNIRS measurement. A 3D digitizing system was used to determine the MNI coordinates for each participant [20, 21]. The cortical regions of interest (ROIs) mainly observed in this study were the prefrontal cortex (PFC, Brodmann area (BA) 10), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC, BA 9), broca’s area (BA44), primary motor cortex (M1, BA4), supplementary motor area (SMA) and premotor cortex (PMC, BA6), primary somatosensory cortex (S1, BA1-3). All channels were showed in Fig. 2.

The cortical regions of interest (ROIs) mainly observed in this study were prefrontal cortex (PFC, BA10: Ch 6,7,8,10,20,21,22), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC, BA 9: Ch 5,9,17,19,23,24), broca’s area (BA44: Ch 3,4,11,12,18,25), primary motor cortex (M1, BA4: Ch 16,31,36,38), supplementary motor area (SMA) and premotor cortex (PMC, BA6: Ch 2,13,14,27,28,29,37,39), primary somatosensory cortex (S1, BA1-3: Ch 1,15,26,30,32,33,34,35)

The cortical ROIs were selected a priori based on their well-established or hypothesized roles in the supraspinal control of defecation, pelvic floor motor function, and visceral-affective processing. The PFC and DLPFC were included for their central role in cognitive control, stress response, and the modulation of visceral sensation, which are often dysregulated in functional gastrointestinal disorders [22]. The M1, SMA, and PMC constitute the core cortical motor network responsible for planning and executing somatic motor outputs, including those to the pelvic floor [23]. The S1 was selected for its involvement in processing mechanosensory feedback from the anorectal region [3]. Broca’s area, traditionally associated with speech, was also monitored given its potential involvement in complex motor planning and its proximity to the lateral premotor cortex [24].

fNIRS pre-processing and statistical analysis

The fNIRS signals were preprocessed and analyzed using NirSpark software (Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co., Ltd.,China). The quality of the data was assessed, and poor-quality signals were rejected. Motion artifacts in channels were corrected using a spline interpolation algorithm. Any signal change beyond 5 standard deviations of the entire time series was considered as a motion artifact to ensure data quality. Then, the raw data were band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 0.2 Hz to remove physiological noise (e.g., respiration, cardiac activity, and low-frequency signal drift). The filtered signals were converted to relative concentration changes of HbO and HbR based on the modified Beer–Lambert law.

For task-related activation, the hemodynamic response function (HRF) was set with an initial time of 0 s and an end time of 18 s, with a 2-s baseline prior to the initial time. The HbO concentrations for each block were averaged to generate block-averaged results. Additionally, for dynamic trend observation, progressive activation curves of all ROIs were plotted across the three tasks. Mean HbO activation and SEM were calculated, and curves were generated using GraphPad Prism. The x-axis represented three tasks, and the y-axis indicated mean HbO levels. Intra- and inter-group activation trends were further compared.

Resting-state local activity analysis

To compare resting-state local activity between groups, the mean HbO concentration during the 7-minute resting-state scan was calculated for each fNIRS channel and for each predefined ROI (by averaging the channels within each ROI). Group comparisons (FCon vs. healthy controls) were performed using independent t-tests for normally distributed data or Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons.

Resting-state functional connectivity analysis

To comprehensively assess potential network alterations, functional connectivity (FC) was analyzed at three hierarchical levels using the 7-minute resting-state data. At the channel level, pairwise Pearson correlations were calculated between all fNIRS channels. At the ROI level, channels were grouped into predefined ROIs, their time series averaged to create a representative signal for each ROI, and pairwise correlations between these ROI-level signals were computed to generate an ROI-to-ROI FC matrix. Finally, a global average FC value was derived for each subject by averaging the correlation coefficients from the ROI-level matrix. Group comparisons were performed separately at each level (channel, ROI, and global average) using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests as appropriate, with FDR correction for multiple comparisons.

Results

Participants characteristics

A total of 69 patients and 66 healthy controls were included. Five patients and two healthy controls were excluded due to extensive motion artifact or poor data acquisition. Finally, 64 patients (mean age was 46.70 ± 15.93) and 64 healthy controls (mean age was 33.90 ± 12.23) were included, 32 females (50%) in each group. The FCon group was significantly older than healthy group. The duration of symptoms in patients ranges from 6 months to 40 years. There were no adverse events during the trial.

Resting-state local activity

To compare resting-state local brain activity between groups, the mean HbO concentration during the 7-minute resting-state scan was calculated for each fNIRS channel and for each ROI. After FDR correction for multiple comparisons, no significant difference was found between patients with FCon and healthy controls in any channel (Fig. 3A) or ROI (Fig. 3B).

The average FC (functional connectivity) of healthy control group was 0.743 ± 0.262, and the average FC of FCon group was 0.696 ± 0.227. After FDR correction for multiple comparisons, no individual ROI pair or channel pair showed a significant between-group difference. Task-related cortical activation

Compared FCon group to healthy group in three tasks

Across all three defecation-related paradigms—imagined defecation, simulated defecation, and anal sphincter contraction—no statistically significant differences in cortical activation (HbO concentration) were found between patients with FCon and healthy controls after correction. The following descriptions are therefore based on the inspection of group-averaged data to illustrate observed numerical trends and patterns.

Imagined defecation

Compared to healthy controls, patients with FCon exhibited a general reduction in cortical activation during imagined defecation, most notably in the right M1. This attenuated activation was, however, not observed in the right PFC, which showed comparable activity levels between groups. None of these regional differences reached statistical significance (Fig. 5).

The largest numerical difference between groups was observed in the right M1

Simulated defecation

As with imagined defecation, no between-group differences during simulated defecation reached statistical significance after correction. However, an inspection of the group-averaged data suggested a dissociative pattern in the FCon group. Patients tended to show numerically higher HbO concentrations in the right PFC and DLPFC, alongside numerically lower concentrations in most other regions. The most notable reductions were observed in the bilateral M1 and the right S1 (Fig. 6).

Anal sphincter contraction

In contrast to the other two paradigms, analysis of the group-averaged data for the anal sphincter contraction task showed a tendency for numerically greater cortical activation across all ROIs in FCon patients compared to healthy controls. The most notable numerical increases were observed in the left M1, left SMA/PMC, and left S1. As with the previous tasks, these between-group differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 7).

Compared three tasks in healthy group

In healthy control group, brain activation was significantly stronger during simulated defecation than during imagined defecation (P < 0.05) in the following ROIs: the right PFC, left DLPFC, bilateral Broca’s area, bilateral M1, bilateral SMA/PMC, and bilateral S1. No significant difference was found in the left PFC or right DLPFC.

Similarly, brain activation was significantly stronger during anal sphincter contraction than during imagined defecation (P < 0.05) in bilateral PFC, bilateral DLPFC, right Broca’s area, left M1, bilateral SMA/PMC, and bilateral S1. The activation in the left Broca’s area and right M1 did not differ significantly between these two tasks.

When directly comparing simulated defecation to anal sphincter contraction, the latter elicited significantly weaker activation (P < 0.05) specifically in the left Broca’s area.

Compared three tasks in FCon group

In FCon group, brain activation was significantly stronger during simulated defecation than during imagined defecation (P < 0.05) in the following ROIs: the bilateral PFC, bilateral DLPFC, bilateral Broca’s area, left S1. No significant difference was found in bilateral M1 and SMA/PMC, right S1. Brain activation was significantly stronger during anal sphincter contraction than during imagined defecation (P < 0.05) in all ROIs. When comparing simulated defecation to anal sphincter contraction, no significant difference was found in all ROIs.

Dynamic ROI activation patterns across tasks

The progression curves of brain activation across the three tasks revealed distinct dynamic patterns between the healthy control and constipation group (Fig. 8).

Progression curves of HbO activation across three tasks in all ROIs

In the healthy control group, ROIs exhibited two main response patterns: (1) Continuous increasing pattern: HbO concentrations in bilateral PFC, right DLPFC, and right SMA/PMC showed a linear increase with task demand from imagined defecation to anal sphincter contraction. (2) Peak-then-decrease pattern: All other ROIs (left DLPFC, bilateral Broca’s area, bilateral M1, left SMA/PMC, bilateral S1) reached their activation peak during simulated defecation, followed by a decrease during the anal sphincter contraction task.

In the constipation group, all ROIs except bilateral Broca’s area showed a consistent progressive increase in HbO across the three tasks, reflecting a widespread linear response pattern, although the increase from simulated defecation to anal sphincter contraction did not reach statistical significance.

Group comparisons showed two key findings: (1) The curves of right PFC and right DLPFC were nearly parallel between groups, with the constipation group exhibiting slightly higher activation throughout the tasks. (2) In most other ROIs, the curves were parallel during the “imagined to simulated defecation” phase (with lower HbO in the constipation group) but crossed over during the “simulated defecation to contraction” phase. Specifically, activation decreased in the healthy group while it continued to increase in the constipation group, leading to higher HbO levels in the constipation group during the anal sphincter contraction task.

Discussion

The act of defecation, although a ubiquitous human experience, requires the coordinated actions of the anorectum, colon, pelvic floor musculature, and the enteric, peripheral and central nervous systems [25]. In the past, research on brain function in FCon patients has been relatively limited, with most studies employing fMRI to investigate alterations in brain networks among FCon patients [26]. When rectal distension occurred in healthy volunteers, activation was found in the anterior cingulate gyrus, the insula, the thalamus and the supplementary motor area bilaterally [23]. Another fMRI study showed that rectal distension induced brain activity in FCon patients, including the anterior cingulate cortex, DLPFC, inferior parietal lobule, and putamen [8]. Franco reviewed previous studies and concluded that higher brain centers influencing and controlling defecation include the ACC, PFC, insula, amygdala, the stria terminalis, and hypothalamus [27].

While our anticipated group differences did not reach statistical significance, the observed numerical trends and dynamic patterns provide valuable exploratory insights.

Prefrontal cortex (PFC) and DLPFC are related to emotion and stress, which modulates neuroendocrine and autonomic function [28]. Prefrontal cortex (PFC) plays a key role in the perception of painful stimuli from viscera. In the previous rat experiment, Gibney et al. found that colorectal distension would induce PFC activity [29]. The central stress circuitry-comprising the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus, amygdala, and periaqueductal gray-received input from the PFC to generate a stress response. Dysregulation of PFC would induce abnormal defecation, functional dyspepsia and irritable bowel syndrome [30]. Leila et al. found simulated defecation in functional defecatory disorders was associated with the activation of PFC [31]. Stimulation of DLPFC prompted the activation of brain functional areas and improved neuroplasticity. Li et al. used repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) to stimulate left DLPFC in a Parkinson’s disease patient and effectively improved constipation symptoms [32]. The result of this study suggests that PFC and DLPFC served as neural regulatory targets in the central control of constipation.

In addition, primary motor cortex (M1) ,supplementary motor area (SMA) and premotor cortex (PMC) were regulated by DLPFC, integrated descending cortical inputs to coordinate somatic motor outputs. The activation of M1 and SMA was thought to regulate the activation of pelvic floor muscle. The stimulation of M1 and SMA by TMS could induce pelvic floor muscle contraction [33]. The reduced activity of M1 and SMA in FCon patients hinted these two ROIs were important neural regulatory targets linked to treat constipation.

Primary somatosensory cortex (S1) was associated with visceral sensation during rectal distension and contraction [34]. Normal defecation involves activation of anorectal mechanoreceptors responsive to pressure and stretch [35]. Thus the lower activity of S1 zone in FCon patients may be related to reduced anorectal mechanoreceptors response to rectal distension. In our resting-state analysis, no significant between-group differences were found in local activity (channel or ROI level) or functional connectivity after FDR correction, suggesting that baseline cortical function is largely preserved in FCon patients, with task-specific challenges revealing the observed dysregulations.

While existing fMRI studies have advanced our knowledge of defecation-related brain activity, the evidence for these regional functions largely comes from such traditional neuroimaging approaches. However, these methods face inherent methodological limitations, most notably the obligatory supine position during scanning. This posture is a critical constraint, given that defecation is a biomechanically posture-dependent process for which seated positioning is physiologically favorable [36]. Consequently, there remains a significant gap in understanding the cortical dynamics of defecation under physiologically relevant (seated) conditions.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) has gained prominence in neuroscience research due to its portability, operational convenience, and robust resistance to motion artifacts [37]. Importantly, its compatibility with seated testing directly addresses the aforementioned methodological gap, allowing participants to engage in simulated defecation and anal contraction tasks in a natural, biomechanically appropriate posture. However, few studies have utilized fNIRS to investigate brain activity in these patients. To the best of knowledge, this study firstly utilizing fNIRS to explore cortical functional differences in patients with FCon. As part of our ongoing research, this study systematically employs three defecation-related experimental paradigms—imagined defecation, simulated defecation, and anal contraction—within an fNIRS framework, thereby extending our investigation of the neural correlates of FCon and contributing to a more comprehensive theoretical understanding of its underlying central mechanisms. This functional task-based approach is critical because FCon is a disorder of sensorimotor execution. By measuring brain activity during simulations of the impaired behavior, we move beyond identifying static neural correlates to characterizing the specific dynamic dysfunction that occurs when patients attempt to defecate.

Imagined defecation constitutes a form of motor imagery, which holds significant importance in neuroscience research [38], particularly in BCI studies [14]. This paradigm provides a theoretical foundation for future applications of volitional control in defecation behavior through neural signals. Therefore, we incorporated the imagined defecation paradigm in our experiment. Specifically, our findings of task-specific cortical dysregulations highlight potential neural targets for such BCI-based interventions, where patients could learn to modulate these aberrant activities via neurofeedback to potentially restore normal brain-gut communication.

In the paradigm of simulated defecation, the subjects were asked to do valsalva maneuver (breath-holding and forced exertion), contraction of the abdominal musculature and diaphragm would induce rectal distension, which mimics the common actions during actual defecation, aiming to observe cortical activity changes during the process [15]. Anal sphincter contraction is a frequently used maneuver in pelvic floor muscle training and also occurs spontaneously after defecation. Previous study prompted the cerebral cortical representation of anal sphincter contraction and found external anal sphincter contraction was associated with multifocal fMRI activity in sensory/motor, anterior cingulate, prefrontal, parietal, occipital, and insular regions [16].

The observed activation patterns suggested a clear dissociation between tasks. During imagined and simulated defecation, patients showed a tendency for reduced engagement of motor (M1) and sensory (S1) cortices while appearing to maintain activity in right prefrontal regions (PFC/DLPFC). In contrast, the simple anal contraction task was associated with a trend toward normalized or even enhanced activation across these areas. This pattern of results aligns with established neuroimaging evidence that pelvic floor and anal sphincter contractions consistently recruit the primary motor cortex (M1) and supplementary motor area (SMA) [16, 39, 40]. Together, this task-specificity indicates that the core deficit in FCon is not a general motor impairment, but rather a potential failure to adequately recruit sensorimotor networks specifically in the complex, viscerally-loaded context of defecation. The observed preservation of right PFC/DLPFC activity during defecation tasks may reflect maladaptive cognitive monitoring or stress processing that further disrupts this sensorimotor integration. Thus, our findings provide a potential neural rationale for anal sphincter training: its therapeutic effect may stem from repeatedly engaging and potentially strengthening this otherwise hypoactive motor cortical circuitry, thereby inducing beneficial central nervous system plasticity.

The progression curve analysis (Fig. 8) provides novel insights into the dynamic cortical responses across the three defecation-related tasks. In healthy controls, two distinct response patterns emerged: a “continuous increasing pattern” (bilateral PFC, right DLPFC, right SMA/PMC) and a “peak-then-decrease pattern” (most sensorimotor ROIs). This task-appropriate modulation indicates that healthy individuals can flexibly allocate neural resources according to task demands. Consistent with this interpretation, Neshatian et al. found that simulated defecation in healthy controls activates regions of primary and supplementary motor cortices, somatosensory cortices, and prefrontal areas, supporting the notion of flexible network engagement during defecation-related tasks [31].

In contrast, patients with FCon exhibited a strikingly different ‘linear increasing pattern’ across all ROIs except bilateral Broca’s area. This pattern suggests a failure to down-regulate cortical activation when task demands decrease, which may reflect compensatory over-recruitment or reduced neural efficiency. Zhu et al. reported that patients with FC exhibit weaker effective connectivity from sensorimotor regions (SMA, precentral gyrus) to limbic areas, suggesting abnormalities in sensory and behavioral regulation [6]. Our crossover phenomenon—where FCon patients showed lower activation during simulated defecation but higher activation during anal contraction—further underscores this dynamic dysfunction.

The nearly parallel curves of the right PFC/DLPFC, with consistently higher activation in FCon patients, support the interpretation of compensatory cognitive monitoring. However, this pattern differs from findings in IBS, where Aizawa et al. demonstrated reduced right DLPFC activation and impaired DLPFC-to-pre-SMA connectivity during cognitive tasks [41].This distinction suggests that while prefrontal dysregulation is common across FGIDs, its manifestation may be disorder-specific.

Notably, bilateral Broca’s area was the only region in the FCon group that did not follow the linear increasing pattern, instead showing a peak-then-decrease pattern similar to healthy controls. Iacoboni et al. demonstrated that Broca’s area plays a key role in complex motor planning and action imitation [24].Its preserved dynamic regulation in FCon patients may indicate that higher-order motor planning functions remain intact, whereas primary sensorimotor execution regions (M1, SMA, S1) are disproportionately affected—a dissociation between planning and execution circuits that has not been previously reported in FCon.

Together, these dynamic patterns advance our understanding of FCon from a static description of regional differences to a mechanistic characterization of how cortical responses evolve across task sequences—a critical step toward identifying state-dependent biomarkers for neuromodulation.

This study has several notable limitations. First, a key limitation of this study stems from the technical constraints of fNIRS, which measures cortical hemodynamics and cannot assess subcortical structures. Therefore, our findings are confined to cortical dysfunction in FCon and do not address the potential role of deeper regions such as the amygdala, hypothalamus, or brainstem. Importantly, this cortical focus aligns with the study’s translational goal: the identified cortical dysregulations represent direct and accessible targets for non-invasive neuromodulation therapies. Second, we did not employ objective measures such as surface electromyography (EMG) or anorectal manometry to verify the specific engagement of pelvic floor muscles during the anal sphincter contraction and simulated defecation tasks. While participants followed standardized instructions, the lack of biofeedback means we cannot definitively quantify muscle effort or rule out compensatory abdominal contractions, which may influence the interpretation of the associated cortical activation patterns. Third, as reported in the Results, no statistically significant differences in cortical activation reached significance after correction, which may be attributable to substantial interindividual variability in HbO responses among participants, which could have limited the statistical power to detect between-group effects. Thus, a larger sample size is required to achieve statistically significant differences between the two patient groups. Fourth, this study lacked quantitative clinical severity scores, which precludes correlating the observed neural dysregulations with individual symptom severity. This limits the direct clinical interpretability of our findings for personalizing neuromodulation targets. Future work integrating such clinical phenotyping is essential. Finally, stratifying constipation patients into distinct subtypes (e.g., slow-transit vs. outlet obstruction) could reveal subtype-specific differences in brain functional alterations and enhance the clinical relevance and mechanistic insights of our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this first fNIRS study provides evidence of task-specific cortical dysregulation in functional constipation. Analysis of group-averaged data revealed three key patterns: reduced sensorimotor activation (M1, SMA/PMC, S1) during imagined/simulated defecation, enhanced sensorimotor activation during anal sphincter contraction, and preserved right prefrontal engagement across tasks. Progression curves further demonstrated a fundamental difference in dynamic responses: healthy controls showed task-appropriate modulation, whereas FCon patients exhibited a linear increasing pattern across tasks, indicating a failure to down-regulate cortical activation when task demands decrease. Although between-group differences did not reach statistical significance, these trends and dynamic patterns identify specific sensorimotor and prefrontal cortical targets for future non-invasive neuromodulation therapies.
