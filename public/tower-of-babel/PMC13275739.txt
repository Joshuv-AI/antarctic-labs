Benralizumab as a steroid-sparing rescue therapy in ANCA-negative eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis: real-world experience from three cases and review of the literature
Authors: Agata Sebastian, Joanna Kosałka-Węgiel, Mateusz Puchala, Katarzyna Życińska, Bogdan Kolarz, Edyta Bieniasz-Pawlik, Mariusz Korkosz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is a rare small-vessel vasculitis driven by type 2 inflammation, frequently associated with severe asthma, eosinophilia, and multiorgan involvement. Despite conventional immunosuppressive therapy, many patients remain steroid-dependent or refractory. Benralizumab, an anti-IL-5 receptor monoclonal antibody inducing rapid eosinophil depletion, has emerged as a promising therapeutic option. We present a real-world case series of three patients with severe, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA)-negative EGPA treated with benralizumab. Clinical manifestations, laboratory parameters, organ involvement, eosinophil kinetics, glucocorticoid use, and treatment outcomes were retrospectively analyzed. All patients exhibited marked peripheral eosinophilia, severe asthma or respiratory involvement, and multisystem disease. Prior glucocorticoid and immunosuppressive therapy failed to achieve sustained disease control or steroid sparing. Benralizumab administration resulted in rapid, profound, and sustained eosinophil depletion in all cases. Significant clinical improvement was observed, including improved asthma control, resolution of sinonasal and cutaneous manifestations, and better quality of life. In one patient with predominant skin involvement, clinical response was evident within hours after the first dose. In another patient, complete resolution of cardiac lesions documented on cardiac MRI was observed after six months of benralizumab therapy. Glucocorticoids were discontinued in two patients and reduced to a low maintenance dose in one. No serious adverse events occurred during follow-up. This exploratory real-world case series suggests that benralizumab may represent a promising steroid-sparing therapeutic option in selected patients with severe, steroid-dependent, ANCA-negative eosinophilic EGPA. The observed rapid eosinophil depletion and early clinical improvement should be interpreted as preliminary findings requiring confirmation in larger prospective studies.Keywords: Antibodies, Antineutrophil cytoplasmic, Churg-Strauss syndrome, Eosinophilia, Interleukin-5, Vasculitis

Introduction

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is a small-vessel vasculitis characterized by multisystem involvement, most commonly asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis, cardiac involvement, and eosinophilia. The central pathogenetic mechanism is type 2 inflammation driven by interleukin 5 (IL-5), which promotes eosinophil maturation and survival [1]. Benralizumab, an afucosylated monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-5 receptor α subunit, induces rapid and near-complete eosinophil depletion via antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, providing a biologically plausible therapeutic option for EGPA beyond systemic glucocorticoids and conventional immunosuppression [2].

Initial experience with benralizumab in EGPA came from small open-label studies, followed by larger trials demonstrating reduced eosinophil counts, improved asthma control, and significant glucocorticoid-sparing effects, with variable efficacy on vasculitic manifestations such as neuropathy or purpura [2–4].

In Poland, benralizumab has been reimbursed for EGPA since July 2025. Prior to this, access was limited and required additional hospital funding. In this article, we present a retrospective real-world case series of first EGPA patients treated with benralizumab at our centers. Cases were retrospectively identified and analyzed based on clinical documentation, laboratory data, imaging findings, treatment history, and follow-up assessments. All presented patients demonstrated an anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA)-negative eosinophilic phenotype characterized predominantly by respiratory and eosinophilic organ manifestations.

To provide clinical context for the presented cases, a focused literature review was performed using the PubMed and SCOPUS databases. The search included English-language publications available up to December 2025 using combinations of the following keywords: “EGPA”, “eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis”, and “benralizumab”. Publications including case reports, clinical studies, treatment guidelines, reviews, and meta-analyses addressing benralizumab treatment in EGPA were screened for relevance. Articles not related to EGPA, non-English publications, duplicate records, and studies lacking clinical relevance to benralizumab therapy were excluded. Due to the heterogeneity and limited number of available studies, the review was designed as a focused narrative review rather than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Since 2014, fewer than 100 publications have addressed benralizumab use in EGPA. The case series and literature review were performed according to the CAse-BAsed REview sTandards (CABARET) [5].

Case 1

An 18-year-old woman with bronchial asthma and chronic pansinusitis diagnosed at age 12 presented with severe respiratory symptoms, nasal polyposis, skin lesions, and marked eosinophilia. Despite repeated functional endoscopic sinus surgeries and long-term inhaled and systemic glucocorticoid therapy, disease control remained poor. Laboratory testing showed eosinophilia (3,000/µL), elevated eosinophilic cationic protein and IgE, with negative ANCA and exclusion of secondary causes of eosinophilia.

Spirometry with bronchodilator testing demonstrated severe, non-reversible airway obstruction (FEV₁/FVC: 72.46%, FEV₁: 1.53 L, 47% predicted; 0.05 percentile). There was no significant bronchodilator response. The Asthma Control Questionnaire (ACQ-7) score was 4.14 points, indicating poorly controlled asthma.

During systemic glucocorticosteroid therapy, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) revealed 10% eosinophils. Histopathology of bronchial mucosa showed inflammatory infiltrates rich in eosinophils (up to 10 cells/high-power field [HPF]).

Imaging revealed extensive sinus opacification, progressive pulmonary infiltrates, and cardiac involvement with late gadolinium enhancement on MRI (Fig. 2). Histopathology from bronchial and sinonasal tissue demonstrated dense eosinophilic infiltration. Gastrointestinal biopsies from the duodenum, pre-pyloric region, and gastric body were unremarkable. Lower esophageal biopsy showed lymphoid infiltrates and up to 3 eosinophils/HPF in vascular papillae, without epithelial invasion. Upper esophageal biopsy revealed similar findings with up to 4 eosinophils/HPF.

Late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac MRI in patient No. 1

The patient fulfilled the 2022 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for EGPA (13 points). Cyclophosphamide was started and discontinued due to adverse effects and fertility concerns. Benralizumab (30 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks) was initiated under the Rescue Drug Access Program. Within six months, the patient reported marked improvement in dyspnea, sleep quality, and sinonasal symptoms, with no recurrence of skin lesions. Additionally, follow-up cardiac MRI performed after 6 months demonstrated resolution of previously observed late gadolinium enhancement.

Systemic glucocorticoids were discontinued. Eosinophils were rapidly depleted (0 cells/µL by day 7). Lung function improved (FEV₁ from 47% to 59% predicted), and asthma control improved (ACQ-7 from 4.14 to 0.57). Benralizumab was well tolerated.

Case 2

A 68-year-old man with a long history of smoking presented with recurrent skin lesions, fever, joint pain, asthma-like symptoms, and marked eosinophilia. Over several years, he received multiple alternative diagnoses and treatments, including topical and systemic corticosteroids, antihistamines, antimicrobials, dupilumab, and therapies for presumed Crohn’s disease. Despite treatment with mesalazine and infliximab, systemic inflammation and eosinophilia persisted. Comorbid conditions included dermatitis and depression. Markedly severe skin symptoms and pruritus led to suicide attempts.

In 2024, he was hospitalized with severe pruritic erythroderma (Fig. 3), fever, joint pain, and pleuritis. Laboratory tests revealed eosinophilia (3,500/µL), extremely elevated IgE (> 5,000 IU/mL), and high inflammatory markers. Skin biopsy showed eosinophilic vasculitis. Extensive diagnostic evaluation excluded malignancy, infections, and other systemic diseases. He met the EULAR/ACR classification criteria for EGPA (13 points).

Severe pruritic erythroderma before benralizumab treatment in patient No. 2

Conventional therapy with glucocorticoids, antibiotics, antiparasitic agents, and antihistamines was ineffective. Benralizumab was initiated. A marked early reduction in erythema and skin infiltration was observed within 4 h after the first benralizumab administration, documented by clinical photography (Fig. 4). Eosinophils were fully depleted, and IgE levels decreased by more than 50%.

Complete resolution of skin lesions after benralizumab treatment in patient No. 2

Case 3

A 47-year-old woman with asthma and recurrent sinusitis presented with severe cardiac involvement, including heart failure, marked eosinophilia (12,240/µL), and elevated cardiac biomarkers. Imaging revealed diffuse pulmonary infiltrates, sinusitis, and impaired left ventricular function. Histopathology confirmed eosinophilic inflammation. ANCA were negative, and myeloproliferative disease was excluded. She met the EULAR/ACR criteria for EGPA (13 points).

Initial treatment with high-dose glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide led to improvement in pulmonary and cardiac findings. Maintenance therapy with azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, and later methotrexate was complicated by recurrent disease flares and persistent glucocorticoid dependence. Cardiac MRI revealed chronic scarring, and an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was placed.

Between 2022 and 2024, repeated attempts to taper glucocorticoids resulted in exacerbations with persistent eosinophilia and sinonasal disease. Benralizumab (30 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks) was initiated in December 2024. By February 2025, eosinophils were completely depleted, inflammatory markers normalized, and glucocorticoids were tapered to 5 mg/day. The patient reported improved physical performance and reduced sinonasal symptoms, with sustained remission through mid-2025.

Discussion

In this article, we present three cases of patients with EGPA that highlight the therapeutic potential of benralizumab in the management of this rare and heterogeneous disease. In two female patients, the disease had a long-standing, refractory course, with severe bronchial asthma, recurrent rhinosinusitis, marked eosinophilia, and multiorgan involvement. In all cases, very high peripheral blood eosinophilia and the absence of ANCA autoantibodies were observed, suggesting an eosinophilic, ANCA-negative phenotype of EGPA. This phenotype is characterized by frequent involvement of the lungs, paranasal sinuses, skin, gastrointestinal tract, and heart [1], which was present in all described patients to varying degrees. Interestingly, previous studies suggested that hypereosinophilia at diagnosis may neither reflect current disease activity nor predict relapse risk in systemic immunosuppressive drug-naïve patients with EGPA, despite theoretical assumptions linking eosinophil burden with disease severity [6].

Conventional treatment, including glucocorticoids and various immunosuppressive agents, did not allow sustained disease control or effective reduction of steroid doses. In contrast, the introduction of benralizumab resulted in rapid and profound eosinophil depletion and clear clinical improvement – better asthma control, resolution of sinonasal and cutaneous symptoms, and improved quality of life – and enabled tapering of glucocorticoids while maintaining disease remission. Notably, in the patient with predominant skin involvement, the early clinical improvement was observed within hours after administration. These observations suggest that benralizumab may represent a potentially useful therapeutic option in selected patients with severe, steroid-dependent, and treatment-refractory eosinophilic EGPA; however, conclusions regarding efficacy and safety remain limited by the small retrospective nature of the presented case series.

An important observation in our real-world case study was the rapid and sustained depletion of eosinophilia during benralizumab therapy. The therapeutic effect was evident both shortly after treatment initiation and over long-term follow-up. This observation may reflect differences in the mechanism of action between benralizumab and mepolizumab; however, direct comparative conclusions cannot be drawn from the present case series. Although both mepolizumab and benralizumab are monoclonal antibodies targeting the interleukin-5 (IL-5) pathway, they differ in their mechanisms of action on eosinophils. Mepolizumab binds directly to IL-5, neutralizing its activity and preventing interaction with the IL-5 receptor (IL-5R) on eosinophils. This reduces eosinophil maturation, activation, and survival, but does not result in complete elimination of these cells [7–9]. Patients typically show a substantial, yet incomplete, reduction in eosinophil counts in blood and tissues.

Benralizumab, in contrast, binds to the α subunit of the IL-5 receptor (IL-5Rα) on eosinophils and basophils. In addition to blocking IL-5 signaling, it induces antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) via natural killer (NK) cells, resulting in rapid, near-complete, and sustained eosinophil depletion, often observed within a few days of treatment initiation. This pharmacological difference may contribute to the rapid and profound eosinophil depletion observed during benralizumab therapy [8]. In a retrospective study by Ghassemian et al., complete eosinophil depletion was achieved in all cases treated with benralizumab, whereas mepolizumab produced this outcome in only 31% of patients (although both agents effectively reduced eosinophil counts below the upper limit of normal) [10]. In retrospective analyses, benralizumab has been associated with rapid eosinophil depletion; however, interpretation of comparative efficacy remains limited by differences in study design and patient populations. In our observations, this rapid onset was particularly evident in the patient with predominant cutaneous manifestations—complete eosinophil depletion was achieved already after the first dose. These observations suggest that benralizumab may be considered in selected patients in whom rapid eosinophil depletion is clinically desirable. Benralizumab and mepolizumab also differ in dosing regimens, which is clinically relevant. In EGPA, mepolizumab is administered at 300 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks, whereas benralizumab is given in a simpler regimen: 30 mg subcutaneously every 4 weeks [7].

In the MANDARA trial comparing benralizumab with mepolizumab, the safety profile of both agents was favorable and consistent with prior observations; however, close monitoring was emphasized for patients with active vasculitis and those receiving combined immunosuppressive therapy [2]. In this small retrospective series, no serious adverse events related to benralizumab were observed during the available follow-up period. Emerging prospective data and head-to-head efficacy comparisons continue to refine the position of benralizumab in EGPA treatment, including its role in induction or maintenance therapy, in relapsed or refractory disease, and in patients with advanced respiratory involvement. Current evidence suggests that benralizumab should be considered as a steroid-sparing option in selected patients with EGPA, particularly those with severe eosinophilic asthma, while taking into account individual vasculitis activity and comorbidities.

The chronic, multiorgan nature of EGPA is associated not only with substantial somatic burden, but also with a significant impact on mental health and quality of life. In our case series, depressive symptoms were clearly documented in one patient, in whom predominant, severe, and chronic skin involvement, intense pruritus, and systemic symptoms led to marked psychosocial impairment, including suicide attempts. Increasing evidence indicates that beyond effective symptom control and eosinophil reduction, benralizumab contributes to meaningful improvements in quality of life in patients with EGPA and severe eosinophilic asthma. Clinical studies in EGPA have demonstrated significant improvement in quality of life assessed with the Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire (AQLQ), with mean scores increasing from 4.15 to 5.50 over 48 weeks of therapy, representing a clinically meaningful change, along with favorable results in the SNOT-22 score reflecting reduced rhinosinusitis-related symptoms [11]. Similar findings have been reported in registration trials and real-world analyses in severe eosinophilic asthma, in which benralizumab improved AQLQ and ACQ-6 outcomes, and in patients with concomitant nasal polyposis also reduced symptom severity on the SNOT-22 scale [12, 13]. These results emphasize that the benefits of benralizumab extend beyond clinical disease control and include meaningful effects on daily functioning and patient well-being. The quality-of-life aspect is undeniably important when selecting therapy in clinical practice. Effective treatment targeting eosinophilic mechanisms may also indirectly improve mental health by reducing disease activity, alleviating debilitating symptoms, and limiting adverse effects associated with long-term glucocorticoid therapy.

One of the key challenges in EGPA management remains the need for long-term glucocorticoid use, which carries a high risk of dose-related adverse effects. The introduction of benralizumab represents an important therapeutic advance in this setting. Clinical trials and real-world observations indicate that this agent enables significant steroid dose reduction and, in some patients, complete discontinuation. In the MANDARA study, more than 40% of patients treated with benralizumab were able to discontinue glucocorticoids entirely after one year, and this effect was maintained over longer follow-up [2]. Similar outcomes were observed in patients previously treated with mepolizumab who switched to benralizumab, as well as in populations with severe eosinophilic asthma [4, 14]. Emerging evidence from case reports also suggests that benralizumab may be effective in patients with EGPA refractory to mepolizumab therapy [15]. In the study by Nanzer et al., 67.9% of patients with EGPA no longer required oral glucocorticoids for disease control, with no significant difference between ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative subgroups [16]. In our series, benralizumab enabled complete glucocorticoid withdrawal in two patients, whereas in one patient prednisone was maintained at a dose not exceeding 5 mg/day. Dose tapering was initiated early—either at the start of therapy or from the second month of treatment.

Cutaneous involvement is one of the more common manifestations of EGPA and may include a variety of lesions, such as maculopapular rashes and purpura [1]. The literature includes reports suggesting that benralizumab may be an effective therapeutic option for such manifestations. Bandla et al. described a young patient with EGPA in whom benralizumab led to rapid remission of skin lesions [17]. A similar effect, with rapid resolution of pruritus and erythema, was observed in our patient. Beneficial effects have also been reported in other eosinophilic dermatoses, such as Wells syndrome, in which benralizumab induced complete remission [18]. However, paradoxical reactions have also been described, including the development of eosinophilic fasciitis during benralizumab therapy [19], indicating the need for continued safety monitoring in this patient group.

Although our study provides real-world experience with benralizumab therapy in patients with severe ANCA-negative EGPA and highlights differences between benralizumab and mepolizumab in terms of mechanism of action and the speed and depth of eosinophil depletion, while also drawing attention to quality-of-life improvements and reduction of steroid-related toxicity, it has important limitations. These include the small number of cases, the lack of long-term follow-up, and limited data on the durability of remission and the safety profile in a broader patient population. Importantly, the present study was not designed as a head-to-head comparison between biologic agents, and therefore conclusions regarding relative clinical efficacy should be interpreted cautiously. Further prospective studies, including direct comparisons with mepolizumab, are needed to better define the optimal place of benralizumab in EGPA treatment.

Conclusion

The presented cases provide preliminary real-world observations suggesting that benralizumab may be a promising rescue and steroid-sparing therapeutic option in selected patients with severe, eosinophilic, ANCA-negative EGPA refractory to conventional immunosuppressive therapy. All cases share a common pathogenic mechanism, a similar clinical profile, and an analogous therapeutic response despite considerable differences in age, sex, and the dominant organ manifestations. However, the limited number of patients and relatively short follow-up do not allow definitive conclusions regarding the long-term safety profile of benralizumab in EGPA. The most important observations include rapid, profound, and sustained eosinophil depletion and clear clinical improvement, including asthma control, resolution of rhinosinusitis and cutaneous symptoms, and improved quality of life. The speed of benralizumab action appears particularly relevant. Our patients’ experiences suggest that beneficial clinical effects and the ability to reduce glucocorticoid doses can be achieved early in the course of treatment. Given the limited number of patients and retrospective observational design, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in larger prospective studies.
