Tumor-educated macrophages promote cytokine-driven lung colonization in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: María Rosado-Sanz, Nuria Martínez-Alarcón, Michaël H Meel, Sergio Rius-Perez, Zoey J Tolboom, Carmen Salvador-Coloma, Rebeca Burgos-Panadero, Esther Coronado, Guillermina Montoliu, Eloi Casals, Anna Esteve-Codina, Francisco Ripoll, Ana Santaballa, Jaime Font de Mora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) remains one of the most lethal breast cancer subtypes, driven by early dissemination and resistance to therapy. Here, we reveal a macrophage-centered cytokine circuit that fuels TNBC metastasis and immune evasion. Tumor cells reprogram naïve macrophages into tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) that secrete CCL3, CCL4, CXCL2, and IL-1β, collectively promoting epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, migration, transendothelial invasion, and lung colonization. These TAMs, in turn, convert naïve CD4⁺ T cells into FOXP3⁺ Tregs, establishing a self-reinforcing immunosuppressive niche. Pharmacological inhibition of CCR5, CXCR2, and IL1R1 with maraviroc, navarixin, and anakinra, respectively, disrupted this cytokine axis, suppressing metastatic colonization in vivo. Our findings reveal that blocking cytokine receptor signaling disrupts the pro-metastatic crosstalk between macrophages and TNBC cells, offering a clinically actionable strategy to restrain metastasis and overcome therapy resistance in TNBC.KEYWORDS: Triple-negative breast cancer, tumor immune microenvironment, IL-1beta, CCL3, CCL4, CXCL2

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2 expression on tumor cells. Compared to other breast cancer subtypes, TNBC is more aggressive and has a greater propensity for metastasis. It is also highly heterogeneous, with diverse molecular and genetic alterations leading to varied responses to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). 1 , 2 The Miller‒Payne grading system is a well-established and commonly used method for measuring NAC response, serving as an independent predictor of disease-free and overall survival. 3 Achieving pathological complete response (pCR) has long been the primary short-term goal of preoperative chemotherapy owing to its strong association with an excellent prognosis. 4 , 5 However, TNBC patients with residual disease (RD) after NAC experience significantly worse survival outcomes compared to other breast cancer patients. 6 Thus, identifying the biological programs that drive poor NAC responses and metastatic recurrence remains a critical unmet need.

The progression of TNBC is closely linked to mechanisms of immune evasion and immunotolerance. Poor responders to NAC exhibit distinct immunosuppressive profiles within the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and in peripheral blood. 7 Here, we extend these observations by focusing on macrophage-driven cytokine networks within the tumor microenvironment and their functional impact on lung colonization and immune suppression. High macrophage infiltration is consistently associated with poor prognosis in TNBC and contributes to early immunosuppressive remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. 8 , 9 Tissue-resident macrophages, which are abundant in early TNBC lesions prior to angiogenesis, 10 play a central role in shaping this microenvironment. Emerging evidence suggests that macrophages and TNBC cells engage in reciprocal signaling loops that promote tumor progression, yet the molecular nature and functional consequences of this crosstalk, particularly those shaping early metastatic competence independently of adaptive immune effector function, remain incompletely defined. Recent studies highlight cytokine signaling and immune checkpoint upregulation as contributors to immunosuppression, 11 but the specific mediators, their origin, and their impact on metastatic competence have not been mechanistically resolved.

The immune landscape of the TIME reveals that M2-like macrophages are enriched in tumors with poor response, whereas a decrease in regulatory T cells (Tregs) is observed in well-responding tumors. 12 The increased infiltration of M2-like macrophages associated with elevated Treg levels in TNBC 13 suggests a complex interplay between these cells, contributing to the signaling networks within the tumor niche that shape the tumor immune response.

Furthermore, the interplay between macrophage-derived cytokines and the acquisition of stable pro-metastatic traits by TNBC cells has not been systematically addressed using integrated functional, transcriptomic, and in vivo models.

Here, we investigate the molecular determinants of the NAC response and the immune-driven mechanisms of metastasis in TNBC, with a focus on interactions among tumor cells, macrophages, and T cells. We show that poor NAC responder patients display an immunosuppressive phenotype enriched in M2-like macrophages. Our mechanistic analyses revealed that TNBC cells reprogram macrophages into a cytokine-secreting TAM phenotype that drives TNBC migration, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), transendothelial invasion, and metastasis. Tumor-educated macrophages also induce FOXP3⁺ Treg differentiation, while tumor-associated T cells reinforce macrophage M2 polarization, establishing a reciprocal immune amplification loop that promotes metastasis. Importantly, cytokine receptor antagonists targeting CCR5, CXCR2, and IL1R1 were used here as pharmacological tools to interrogate the functional relevance of this cytokine network, revealing that disruption of macrophage-driven signaling attenuates migratory and metastatic traits in vitro and in vivo. Together, these findings reveal a macrophage-centered cytokine network that fuels TNBC progression and provide a mechanistic framework that may inform future therapeutic strategies, including rational combinations with immunotherapy in more immunocompetent settings.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided informed consent for the use of biospecimens. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hospital Universitari i Politècnic La Fe (Valencia, Spain) under protocol number 2015/0362. Animal procedures were approved by the Animal Experimentation and Welfare Committee of the University of Valencia (“Comité de Experimentación y Bienestar Animal”, protocol 3658598) and corresponding regional authorities (protocol 2024-VSC-PEA-0229). In vivo imaging was performed at the University of Valencia facilities in collaboration with Hospital La Fe due to the availability of specialized IVIS equipment. The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, in accordance with the European Directive 2010/63/EU and American Veterinary Medical Association guidelines.

Study design and biopsy collection

This prospective study included early or locally advanced TNBC patients diagnosed between May 2015 and June 2017 at Hospital Universitari i Politècnic La Fe (Valencia, Spain). The eligibility criteria included histologically confirmed TNBC; ER- and PR-negative status (<1% positive nuclei); HER2 negativity (0–1 + by immunohistochemistry or absence of amplification); age ≥18 y; ECOG performance status 0–2; eligibility for neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC); no prior systemic treatment or surgery; absence of metastatic disease; and written informed consent.

The cohort consisted of biologically female patients (mean age 50.34 ± 12.66 y; range 27.44–75.13 y; mean weight 68.5 ± 12.3 kg). Demographic and clinical parameters were recorded at baseline. All enrolled participants completed the study, and no patients were lost to follow-up. As this was an exploratory study, no formal power calculation was performed, and the sample size reflected all eligible patients recruited during the study period.

Participants underwent baseline evaluation, including lymph node assessment before NAC. Group allocation into responders and poor responders was determined retrospectively using the Miller–Payne classification (Supplementary Table 1). Investigators conducting the data analysis were blinded to the group assignments. No randomization was performed.

Core biopsies were obtained before NAC, paraffin-embedded for histological diagnosis, and processed for RNA isolation using the RNeasy FFPE Kit (QIAGEN). The RNA was hybridized to Clariom S Pico arrays (Affymetrix) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Patients received standard anthracycline- and taxane-based NAC regimens, most commonly anthracycline-based combinations (AC or FEC) followed by weekly paclitaxel. In some cases, taxanes were administered as docetaxel, and a subset of patients received carboplatin in combination with paclitaxel. Patients with residual disease following NAC (non-responders) were subsequently treated with the adjuvant capecitabine in accordance with clinical practice at the time.

Animal studies

Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with Directive 2010/63/EU and Spanish Royal Decree 53/2013 and were approved by the Conselleria de Agricultura, Agua, Ganadería y Pesca of the Generalitat Valenciana (protocol 2024-VSC-PEA-0229, tipo 2).

The in vivo study was performed in two sequential phases. An initial pilot phase (n = 3 mice per group), as required by the ethics committee, was conducted to demonstrate feasibility and assess whether macrophage-educated tumor cells displayed enhanced colonization capacity compared with uneducated controls. Based on consistent results, the study was subsequently expanded to include therapeutic intervention arms, increasing the total number to 7 mice per group. Data shown in Figures 1 and 5 include all animals from both phases.

Overall, seven 8-week-old female B-NDG mice (InVivo) per group were used. This sample size is consistent with exploratory xenograft studies designed to detect large biological effects and support mechanistic interpretation rather than provide definitive estimates of therapeutic efficacy. Immunodeficient mice were selected to isolate tumor cell-intrinsic and macrophage-driven cytokine signaling independently of adaptive immune responses.

Mice were injected via the tail vein with 2 × 10⁵ MDA-MB-468-Luc cells in 100 μL PBS. Tumor cells were preconditioned for 3 d in conditioned medium (CM; 1:3 dilution with fresh medium) derived from 3-day macrophage–tumor cocultures (macrophage-educated cells). The control mice received uneducated MDA-MB-468-Luc cells.

For bioluminescence imaging, the mice received intraperitoneal D-luciferin (150 mg/kg), and images were acquired 20 min later using an IVIS Lumina X5 system (PerkinElmer; 1-min exposure). Luminescence was quantified using the manufacturer’s software. During the first 5 d after tumor cell injection, the mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of maraviroc (20 mg/kg) and anakinra (25 mg/kg). Control mice received vehicle.

Immunohistochemistry

At the endpoint, the mice were euthanized, and the lungs were collected, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 3 μm. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in Tris–EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and nonspecific binding was blocked with normal serum or BSA.

The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CK8/18 (clone 5D3, Abcam, RRID: AB_778810; 1:100), Ki67 (clone MIB-1, Dako, RRID: AB_2142367; 1:200), E-cadherin (clone NCH-38, Dako, RRID: AB_2335716; 1:100), Vimentin (clone V9, Sigma-Aldrich, RRID: AB_477627; 1:200), β-catenin (clone β-catenin-1, Millipore, RRID: AB_94699; 1:100), and CD163 (clone 10D6, Leica Biosystems, RRID: AB_2134029; 1:100) antibodies.

After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, staining was visualized using DAB substrate (Dako). The sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, mounted, and imaged using a Leica DMD108 digital microscope.

Cell culture

MDA-MB-468 (RRID:CVCL_0419), MDA-MB-231 (RRID:CVCL_0062), A549 (RRID:CVCL_0023), H1299 (RRID:CVCL_0060), PC3 (RRID:CVCL_0035), LNCaP (RRID:CVCL_0395), U2OS (RRID:CVCL_0042), and THP-1 (RRID:CVCL_IR09) cells were obtained from the ATCC. BT-549 (RRID:CVCL_1092) cells were provided by Dr. Roger Gomis (IRB, Barcelona), and BT-20 (RRID:CVCL_0178) cells by Dr. J. Climent (Hospital Clínic, Valencia). MDA-MB-468-Luc (RRID:CVCL_C8XW) cells were purchased from Ubigene Biosciences. HUVECs (RRID:CVCL_2959) were provided by Dr. Sepúlveda (IISLaFe, Valencia) and maintained in EGM-2 medium (CC-3162). BT-549, BT-20, H1299, PC3, LNCaP, U2OS, and THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma). MDA-MB-468 and MDA-MB-231 cells were maintained in DMEM (HyClone) supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination (Applied Biological Materials, G238) and genetically authenticated by SNP arrays (CytoScan HD, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Macrophage polarization and coculture

THP-1 monocytes were differentiated into M0 macrophages by incubation with 150 nM PMA (Sigma, P8139) for 24 h, followed by 24 h in RPMI medium. M2 polarization was induced with IL-4 (20 ng/mL; R&D Systems, #204-IL) and IL-13 (20 ng/mL; R&D Systems, #213-ILB). 14 Macrophages were seeded in transwell inserts (24-well format, Falcon 353104; 1.0 μm pores) at 5 × 10⁴ cells/insert and used for coculture assays.

Cell migration assay

MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-231, BT-549, and BT-20 cells were seeded in 48-well plates (1.2 × 10⁵ cells/well). Wounds were generated using a 200 μL pipette tip, and the cells were incubated in DMEM containing vehicle (0.1% DMSO), maraviroc (20 nM), navarixin (20 nM), anakinra (2 μM), or combinations. TAM-conditioned medium (50 μL) or fresh RPMI was added. Images were acquired at 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h using a Leica DMi8 microscope. Migration was quantified using ImageJ (Wound_healing_size_tool.ijm) and expressed as a percentage of wound closure.

Code availability

No custom code was generated. Deconvolution analyses were performed using CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). Migration analyses were conducted using ImageJ/Fiji (https://fiji.sc/).

Cell invasion assay

PET transwell inserts (8 μm pores) were coated with Geltrex™ (0.12 mg/mL). CFSE-labeled TNBC cells (10⁴) were seeded in the upper chamber, and complete RPMI or CM was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h, invading cells were quantified using a Promega GloMax system.

Endothelial cell migration assay

HUVECs (5 × 10⁴) were seeded on PET inserts (8 μm pores) and grown to confluence in EGM-2 medium. CFSE-labeled tumor cells were added to the monolayer, and CM (200 μL) was added to the lower chamber. After 48 h, migrated cells were quantified using an Agilent BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader.

T cell extraction, coculture studies and Treg differentiation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donors by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. Naïve CD4⁺ T cells were purified by negative selection using Dynabeads Untouched Human CD4 T cells (11346D) followed by depletion of CD25⁺ cells (#11157D). CD25⁻CD4⁺ T cells were cultured in RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ and activated using CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (#11161D) at a 1:2 bead-to-cell ratio. 15 Induced regulatory T cells (iTregs) were generated by culturing CD25⁻CD4⁺ T cells with TGF-β1 (5 ng/mL), IL-2 (100 IU/mL), ATRA (100 nM), and rapamycin (100 ng/mL), together with CD3/CD28 Dynabeads, for five days. 15 Naïve T cells were cultured for five days under three conditions: alone, in two-phase coculture with MDA-MB-468 cells, or with conditioned medium from macrophages exposed to MDA-MB-468 cells (TAM-CM).

Conditioned media analysis

Cytokine profiles were analyzed using biotin-labeled antibody arrays (Human L-493 and L-507, RayBiotech). Assays were performed by RayBiotech Laboratories. Cytokine levels in conditioned media and patient plasma were quantified by ELISA for CCL3 (EH0230-48T), CCL4 (EH0067-48T), CXCL2 (EH3178-48T), and IL-1β (MEA563Hu-48T) (Cloud-Clone Corp. and Fine-Test).

Protein extraction and immunoblotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer and quantified by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins (40 μg) were separated by 8%–12% SDS‒PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Hybond-P, Amersham) overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were probed with antibodies against CD163 (Invitrogen, PA5-109327), MMP9 (Invitrogen, MA5-41640), and Vimentin (Sigma-Aldrich, HPA001762), and detected using ECL reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare). GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-365062; RRID: AB_10847862) served as a loading control.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy® Mini Kit (Qiagen) and quantified by NanoDrop. cDNA was synthesized from 100 ng RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems) and stored at 4 °C or −80 °C until use.

ddPCR

Gene expression was quantified by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR). Each 20 μL reaction contained 2 × ddPCR Supermix (no dUTP), 20 × FAM-labeled target probe mix (CDH1, CDH2, FOXP3, TWIST1, IL10, IL6, MMP9, IDO1, CXCL10, CTLA4, PD-L1, PD-L2, VEGFA, CD163, TGFB1, and VIM), and 20 × HEX-labeled ACTB probe. Droplets were generated using the QX200™ Droplet Generator and amplified in a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). The droplets were read on a QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad) and analyzed with QuantaSoft™ software. Gene expression was calculated as the fractional abundance relative to that of ACTB, whose expression remained stable during coculture assays (Supplementary Figure 1).

mRNA library preparation and sequencing

Libraries were prepared using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Poly(A) + RNA was purified, fragmented, reverse-transcribed, ligated to indexed adapters, and PCR-enriched. Library quality was assessed by fluorometry and an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Sequencing was performed on a NextSeq 550 (Illumina) using single-end 75 bp reads.

mRNAseq analysis

The reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) using STAR v2.7.8a with ENCODE parameters. 16 Gene quantification was performed with RSEM v1.3.1 using GENCODE v47 annotation. 17 Count matrices were analyzed in R (v4.5.2) using DESeq2 (v1.50.1). 18 Low-count genes (rowSums < 10) were filtered, and genes with adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1.5 were considered differentially expressed. Visualization was performed with ggplot2 (tidyverse v2.0.0). 19

Macrophage signatures were derived from published macrophage marker genes, 20-22 and T-cell signatures were derived from published T-cell gene signatures 23-27 (Supplementary Table 2). Z-scores were calculated from mean logCPM values (n = 2 per condition) and heatmaps generated using pheatmap (v1.0.13). 28 These analyses were intended to illustrate pathway- and signature-level trends. Gene Ontology (Biological Process) enrichment was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.18.1) 29 with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. ENSEMBL IDs were mapped via org.Hs.eg.db, and enriched terms were visualized using dotplot (top 20 categories).

Flow cytometry

Macrophages and CD4⁺ T cells were harvested after coculture with MDA-MB-468 cells or TAM-CM and washed in PBS. The macrophages were stained with HLA-DR PerCP-Cy5.5 (552764) and CD163-APC (eBioGHI/61). T cells were stained with CD4-FITC (555346), CD25-APC (555434), and FOXP3-PE (560046). Fluorescence-minus-one controls were included for gating. Data were acquired on a BD FACSCanto II using FACSDiva software, collecting ≥10,000 events per sample.

Statistical analysis

Data were obtained from a minimum of three independent experiments and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test, and two-group comparisons using unpaired two-tailed t-tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001). Analyses and visualization were conducted using GraphPad Prism v10.4.2.

Results

Immunosuppressive transcriptional programs and macrophage-driven colonization define poor responders in TNBC

To identify biological programs associated with therapeutic failure in TNBC, we performed parametric gene set enrichment analysis (PAGE) in our institutional cohort (HULAFE) and validated the results in an independent dataset (GSE106977). In both cohorts, tumors from poor responders displayed enrichment of transcriptional programs associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, including regulatory T-cell activity and inflammatory immunosuppressive macrophage polarization. Poor responders also exhibited enrichment of extracellular matrix remodeling, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, migration, invasion, and cytokine/chemokine signaling pathways (Figure 1A, B).

CIBERSORTx-based deconvolution revealed a predominance of M2-like inflammatory immunosuppressive macrophages and reduced M1 and memory CD4⁺ T cells (Figure 1C,D), which is consistent with a protumorigenic, immunosuppressive niche known to impair antitumor immunity and promote disease progression. 12 These features support a hybrid inflammatory-immunosuppressive TAM state rather than a classical M2 phenotype.

To functionally model tumor–macrophage interactions, we evaluated whether TNBC-derived soluble factors reprogram macrophages and whether macrophage education alters tumor cell behavior (Figure 1E). Exposure to macrophage-derived factors significantly increased TNBC cell migration, invasion, and transendothelial crossing in vitro (Figure 1F–H), findings reproduced in MDA-MB-231 cells (Supplementary Figure 2).

MDA-MB-468 cells were selected for in vivo experiments owing to their low basal migratory and colonization capacity, enabling detection of macrophage-induced acquisition of metastatic competence, which would be less discernible in highly migratory cell lines such as MDA-MB-231. Intravenous injection of macrophage-educated TNBC cells resulted in increased lung colonization compared with uneducated controls (Figure 1I, J), accompanied by elevated tumor burden and lung nodules (Figure 1K) and increased hCK8/18 expression (Figure 1L). Although exploratory, these in vivo experiments consistently supported the conclusion that macrophage-mediated tumor education enhances colonization capacity.

Together, these data provide integrated computational and functional evidence linking immunosuppressive TIME features in poor responders to enhanced TNBC colonization capacity in vivo.

In vitro coculture defines the molecular features of immunosuppressive TAMs

To investigate the mechanisms underlying immunosuppression in poor responders, we established an in vitro coculture model between TNBC cells and THP-1-derived macrophages. ddPCR analysis revealed that cocultured macrophages upregulated M2-associated genes (CD163, IL10, MMP9, VEGFA, and PD-L2) together with M1-associated markers (IL6 and HLA-DR), which is consistent with the acquisition of a mixed TAM-like phenotype. 30 Tumor cells concurrently acquired immunosuppressive and mesenchymal features, including increased IL6, PD-L1, CDH2, MMP9, and TWIST1 expression.

Morphological analysis revealed prominent lamellipodia and filopodia in cocultured TNBC cells and elongated projections in macrophages (Figure 2A), reproduced across multiple TNBC lines (Supplementary Figure 3).

Immunoblotting confirmed increased CD163 and MMP9 in TAMs and elevated vimentin in cocultured tumor cells (Figure 2B). Flow cytometry demonstrated increased CD163 and HLA-DR expression in cocultured macrophages (Figure 2C, D).

RNA-seq analysis after 3-day coculture revealed increased mesenchymal and immunosuppressive gene expression in tumor cells and a strong M2-like transcriptional shift in macrophages (Figure 2E). Despite enrichment of M2-associated genes (CD163, IL10, VEGFA, TGFB1, MARCO, and ARG1), classical M1 markers (CD86, CD80, and IL-1β) remained moderately upregulated, confirming a mixed TAM phenotype consistent with TAM plasticity in vivo. 31

Analysis using curated macrophage gene signatures 20 , 21 , 32 , 33 demonstrated enrichment of pro-angiogenic, poor-prognosis, and M2c/d programs in tumor-educated macrophages, while cytokine-polarized macrophages displayed a predominant M2a phenotype as described35 (Supplementary Figure 4A). The enriched biological processes included chemotaxis and chemokine signaling pathways (Supplementary Figure 4B), supporting the acquisition of tumor-supportive transcriptional programs. In contrast, cytokine-polarized M2 macrophages displayed a predominantly M2a phenotype, as described previously, 34 with a pronounced enrichment of the breast cancer-associated TAM signature (Supplementary Figure 4A).

Crosstalk with macrophages induces IL-1β, CCL3, CCL4, and CXCL2 secretion and enhances TNBC migration

To identify soluble mediators driving TNBC cell migration, we performed cytokine array profiling of conditioned media (CM) from TNBC cells, macrophages, and cocultures. CCL3, CCL4, CXCL2, and IL-1β were selectively detected in coculture CM (Figure 3A, B; Supplementary Figure 5), indicating induction by tumor–macrophage crosstalk. ELISA confirmed the significantly increased secretion of these cytokines following coculture (Figure 3C). Two-phase crosstalk assays demonstrated marked upregulation of CCL3, CCL4, CXCL2, and IL-1β transcripts in macrophages (Figure 3C), identifying macrophages as the principal source.

RNA-seq analysis further revealed a chemokine-enriched transcriptional program in tumor-educated macrophages, including upregulation of CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CXCL2, IL6, VEGFA, IL1R2, and CXCL1 (Figure 3D). These data define a coordinated chemokine module induced by tumor–macrophage interaction. We next evaluated whether this macrophage-derived cytokine axis also influences adaptive immune responses.

Tumor-associated macrophages promote Treg differentiation and reinforce immunosuppressive crosstalk

Given the association between poor response to NAC and increased Treg abundance in TNBC, 13 we investigated how tumor–macrophage crosstalk influences T-cell differentiation. Exposure to TAM CM, but not direct coculture with tumor cells, significantly increased FOXP3 and IKZF4 expression 35 (Figure 4B), indicating macrophage-dependent induction of regulatory programs. In contrast, naïve T cells cultured with tumor cells (TATs) alone did not acquire a Treg phenotype. Proliferation assays revealed that TATs and TAM CM-exposed T cells displayed increased proliferative capacity, whereas naïve T cells and iTregs did not (Figure 4C; Supplementary Figure 6), suggesting that FOXP3 induction alone is insufficient to confer full suppressive function.

We next examined the impact of TATs on macrophage polarization. Coculture with TNBC cells induced a TAM-like phenotype characterized by CD163 and IL-10 expression (Figure 4D). CM from TATs further enhanced expression of immunosuppressive and checkpoint-related markers, including IL-6, IDO1, PD-L1, and PD-L2 (Figure 4D), indicating reciprocal reinforcement of macrophage polarization. Functional assays showed that only CM from TAMs or TAT-educated macrophages promoted TNBC migration (Figure 4E; Supplementary Figure 7). This effect correlated with elevated secretion of CCL3, CCL4, CXCL2, and IL-1β (Figure 4F,G). In contrast, pharmacologically polarized M2 macrophages and iTregs produced lower cytokine levels and did not induce comparable migratory responses (Figure 4E–G).

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that TATs exhibited mixed effector phenotypes resembling Th1/Th2/Th17 profiles, whereas TAM CM-exposed naïve T cells showed preferential enrichment of effector Treg signatures (Figure 4F). T cells lacking effector differentiation displayed increased levels of exhaustion markers, indicating divergence toward regulatory or dysfunctional states.

Together, these data demonstrate that macrophage-derived signals are required for Treg differentiation and that tumor-associated T cells reciprocally reinforce macrophage immunosuppressive programs, establishing a self-sustaining cytokine-driven immune niche.

Pharmacological inhibition of cytokine signaling blocks TNBC migration and lung colonization

To determine whether cytokine signaling is required for TAM-induced tumor cell motility, we pharmacologically inhibited CCR5 (maraviroc), IL-1R (anakinra), and CXCR1/2 (navarixin). These inhibitors were tested individually and in combination in migration and invasion assays.

Blockade of cytokine receptors significantly reduced TAM CM-induced migration (Figure 5A; Supplementary Figure 8). Maraviroc and anakinra showed the strongest inhibitory effects, whereas navarixin displayed more modest activity. The combined treatment did not substantially exceed the effect of maraviroc and anakinra alone; therefore, subsequent experiments focused on this combination. Although anakinra exerted a more pronounced effect, the combination with maraviroc was retained to account for the complementary inhibition of interconnected cytokine signaling pathways. Similar inhibition was observed in highly motile TNBC lines, including MDA-MB-231, BT-549, and BT-20 (Figure 5B; Supplementary Figures 9–10).

None of the inhibitors exhibited cytotoxicity at the concentrations tested (Supplementary Figure 11). In macrophages, cytokine inhibition reduced coculture-induced CD163 expression, indicating partial reversal of TAM polarization (Figure 5C).

TAM CM markedly increased the expression of EMT and invasion markers CDH2, VIM, and MMP9 in TNBC cells, as assessed by ddPCR (Figure 5D). Maraviroc and anakinra showed differential effects on EMT marker expression, with partially overlapping inhibitory activity across markers. While maraviroc primarily reduced CDH2 and MMP9 levels, anakinra modestly affected vimentin and significantly decreased MMP9 levels. The combined treatment did not result in clear synergistic inhibition but allowed broader targeting of EMT-associated markers. Cytokine measurements confirmed the effective suppression of CCL3, CXCL2, and IL-1β secretion (Supplementary Figure 12). Although CCL4 secretion persisted, migration remained inhibited, indicating that blockade of multiple signaling axes is sufficient to disrupt TAM-mediated motility.

Crosstalk assays in lung, prostate, and osteosarcoma cell lines demonstrated similar macrophage polarization, cytokine induction, and migration responses, which were likewise inhibited by maraviroc and anakinra (Supplementary Figure 13), indicating that this signaling axis extends beyond TNBC.

Analysis of patient samples revealed that circulating cytokine levels did not correlate with clinical outcome (Supplementary Figure 14A), whereas intratumoral IL-1β expression was elevated in poor responders (Supplementary Figure 14B). RNA-seq further showed increased cytokine expression in tumor cells following coculture (Supplementary Figure 14 C), supporting a locally reinforced signaling loop.

We next evaluated therapeutic efficacy in vivo. Mice injected with TAM CM-educated TNBC cells displayed markedly increased lung colonization. One-week administration of maraviroc and anakinra significantly reduced the metastatic burden, and pre-treatment during macrophage education further enhanced this effect (Figure 5E). The bioluminescent signal in Figure 5E is not restricted to the thoracic region and appears more broadly distributed. Bioluminescence quantification was performed using a defined region of interest (ROI) over the thoracic area, ensuring that the reported signal reflects lung-associated tumor burden. Comparable inhibition following in vivo treatment and pre-treatment indicates that cytokine signaling is required for both acquisition and maintenance of enhanced colonization capacity.

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed increased CD163 and vimentin expression in the lungs of mice receiving educated cells, which is consistent with TAM polarization and EMT induction (Figure 5F). These features were attenuated by cytokine inhibition. The quantification of metastatic nodules and dispersed tumor cells confirmed robust suppression of lung colonization following treatment (Figure 5G; Supplementary Figure 15). Consistently, metastatic lesions derived from educated cells exhibited elevated CD163, VEGFA, and MMP9 expression, as well as increased expression of mesenchymal markers, which were reduced by therapeutic intervention (Supplementary Figure 16).

Discussion

Our study identifies a macrophage-centered cytokine loop as a key driver of enhanced colonization capacity and immunosuppression in TNBC. M2 macrophage polarization and the conversion of naïve T cells into Tregs are pivotal processes in shaping the TIME. Our study highlights the distinct roles of TAMs and TATs in TNBC: TAMs drive tumor migration and lung dissemination, whereas TATs reinforce immunosuppression and help sustain the macrophage-driven cytokine niche.

In the present work, in vivo experiments were intentionally designed as exploration validation assays rather than definitive preclinical efficacy studies. The use of immunodeficient mouse models enabled focused dissection of macrophage-driven cytokine signaling in the absence of adaptive immune rejection, thereby isolating the contribution of tumor‒macrophage interactions to enhanced colonization capacity. Accordingly, group sizes were selected to detect large biological effects and to support mechanistic interpretation, rather than to establish therapeutic superiority or clinical efficacy. Importantly, the tail-vein injection model primarily evaluates tumor cell survival and colonization in the lung rather than spontaneous metastatic dissemination from a primary tumor. Therefore, this model does not recapitulate the early steps of metastasis, such as local invasion and intravasation. Our in vivo data should be interpreted in this context as evidence for enhanced colonization and persistence in the lung microenvironment rather than for full metastatic progression. All mechanistic experiments were performed using THP-1-derived macrophages; therefore, validation in primary human macrophages and in vivo lineage-specific models will be required to confirm translational relevance.

Given that immune checkpoint blockade currently represents the standard of care for TNBC, extending these findings to combinatorial strategies involving anti-PD-1 therapy will require evaluation in immunocompetent or humanized models, which will be the focus of future investigations. In addition, the use of immunodeficient models does not allow in vivo evaluation of TAM-driven Treg differentiation and its contribution to metastatic progression, which will require validation in immunocompetent or humanized systems.

Recent spatial analyses have shown that specific macrophage populations colocalize with metastasis-initiating TNBC cells, 36 supporting our findings that a macrophage–tumor crosstalk generates a cytokine microenvironment that facilitates dissemination. Cytokines are known to modulate tumor progression, immune evasion, and metastatic potential. 37 These spatial observations are consistent with our in vivo results showing that TAM-derived factors promote a pro-colonization phenotype even prior to dissemination.

Pre-exposure of TNBC cells to TAMs CM markedly enhanced colonization capacity in vivo, demonstrating that TAM-derived factors induce durable functional changes in tumor cells. While macrophage-derived factors induce durable functional changes in tumor cells, whether these effects reflect stable epigenetic reprogramming remains to be determined. Cytokine receptor inhibitors significantly reduced lung colonization even when administered after tumor cell injection, indicating that this signaling axis remains active during metastatic colonization. Blocking cytokine receptors during the TAMs CM education phase further amplified this effect, preventing TNBC cells from acquiring colonization competence. Together, these findings show that TAM-derived cytokines are required for both the initiation and maintenance of lung colonization.

This dual requirement highlights sustained macrophage-tumor cytokine crosstalk in the lung and suggests that disruption of this interaction could both prevent metastatic establishment and impair growth at secondary sites. Cytokine receptor inhibitors effectively block tumor cell migration and have low clinical toxicity, making them attractive candidates for future metastasis-prevention strategies. Given their well-established safety profiles, these inhibitors are realistic candidates for future clinical evaluation in early-phase TNBC studies. However, clinical translation requires validation in spontaneous metastasis models and comprehensive combination safety studies.

Our immunohistochemical analyses further demonstrated that TAMs CM exposure increases M2 macrophage polarization (CD163⁺) and induces EMT (increased vimentin), providing mechanistic insight into how TAM-derived cytokines shape both the immune and phenotypic landscape of TNBC cells to promote dissemination.

Macrophage-secreted IL-1β emerged as a key driver of TNBC invasiveness, which is consistent with reports implicating IL-1β in metastatic colonization via Wnt activation. 38 However, our work reveals that IL-1β does not act in isolation but rather as part of a coordinated cytokine module including CCL3/4 and CXCL2 that amplifies metastatic traits. IL-1β also induces PD-L1 upregulation, 11 an effect we observed to be further intensified in the presence of TATs. TATs also promoted IL-6 secretion, previously associated with enhanced TNBC invasiveness. 39 Together, these findings position cytokine-driven macrophage–tumor crosstalk as a key contributor to immune suppression and provide a mechanistic basis for combining cytokine blockade with immunotherapy. Although we did not evaluate tocilizumab, its combinatorial activity with maraviroc in preclinical cancer models 40 supports future exploration. Consistent with our in vivo findings, dual cytokine receptor inhibition (anakinra and maraviroc) significantly reduced the metastasis of TAMs CM-educated TNBC cells, supporting the rationale for multi-cytokine targeting. Our data also suggest the therapeutic potential for combining anakinra with maraviroc, particularly in a TIME enriched with TAMs and TAT-reprogrammed macrophages. Maraviroc has previously been shown to potentiate anti-PD-L1 therapy, 41 underscoring the importance of CCR5 in immunosuppressive signaling and its broader relevance for TNBC treatment combinations. Our data suggest that IL-1β primarily acts within the tumor microenvironment rather than as a systemic biomarker. Recent work has shown that circulating soluble CD163 and CD14⁺CD16⁺ monocytes may reflect systemic macrophage activation and correlate with the neoadjuvant chemotherapy response in patients with breast cancer, 42 suggesting that the integration of circulating macrophage markers with tumor-based analyses may further refine patient stratification in future studies.

Blockade of PD-1 with pembrolizumab improves OS when added to NAC, 43 highlighting that poor NAC responders benefit substantially from immunotherapy. Emerging trials with PD-L1 inhibitors such as durvalumab are also shown encouraging results. 44 , 45 These clinical data underscore the importance of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in determining therapeutic response and provide a relevant clinical context for our mechanistic findings.

Our findings reveal that cytokine receptor signaling is a central determinant of enhanced colonization capacity in TNBC, particularly in tumors with an immunosuppressive TIME and poor NAC responses. Pharmacological inhibitors such as maraviroc and anakinra not only prevent initial tumor-cell education but also disrupt the ongoing crosstalk required for metastatic colonization. By delineating a macrophage-centered cytokine network that licenses both immune suppression and metastatic behavior, this study establishes a conceptual framework to inform future strategies aimed at limiting TNBC dissemination and enhancing therapeutic responsiveness.
