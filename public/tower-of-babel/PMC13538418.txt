Adaptive metabolic reprogramming conserves energy status in Antarctic giants
Authors: Piero Calosi, Lauric Feugere, Fanny Vermandele, Paulina Bruning, Mathieu Millour, Bertrand Genard, Ignacio Garrido, Luis Miguel Pardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polar marine invertebrate giants are proposed to have emerged from the greater availability of environmental oxygen, overcoming the viscosity of cold water and avoiding oxygen poisoning. However, molecular evidence on their metabolic adaptations is lacking to date. Consequently, we characterised the metabolome profiles of a number of marine Antarctic giants and their regular-size relatives exposed acutely in the laboratory either under mean seasonal conditions or elevated temperature. Giants from very distinct taxa share the differential utilisation of metabolic pathways involved in energy production, suggesting an adaptive convergence of metabolic reprogramming to meet the challenge of possessing larger bodies and facing harsh polar conditions. Further, we show that giants are not just larger regular-size species, as indicated by a breakpoint in the allometric relationship for metabolomics scores. Finally, giants do not appear to be more sensitive to ocean warming when compared to their regular-size relatives, all species tested showing no short-term metabolomics reprogramming under elevated temperatures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56044-0.

Introduction

Gigantism is one of the great paradigms of evolutionary biology. It is defined as the tendency of some species to become much larger than their “regular-size” relatives1–3. Extant marine invertebrate giants are found in the cold waters of deep-sea and polar regions4–6. Gigantism in the marine realm is observed across numerous taxonomic groups, including for example amphipods, isopods, pycnogonids, nemerteans, ophiuroids, asteroids and tunicates4,7,8. Specifically for polar gigantism, the traditional view on this phenomenon is that lower temperatures (resolving in slower developmental rates) and the greater availability of oxygen made possible the evolution of giant forms within taxa7,9. In fact, giants appear to have evolved low metabolic rates, low maintenance costs and slow growth (e.g.,7,10,11), to meet the challenge to live in extreme environments with low resources. A more recent view argues that larger body sizes help overcome the greater viscosity of cold water, whilst balancing the opposing risks of asphyxiation and oxygen poisoning12. All things considered, our current understanding of polar giants’ physiology is extremely limited, as it is largely based on the investigation of the relationship between oxygen availability in sea water and species’ body mass13–17. Surprisingly, beyond the investigation of the allometry of metabolic rates, few other aspects of the comparative physiology of giants have been characterized so far (e.g., respiratory pigment,18,heat critical thermal limits,19–21). Hence, we crucially lack a cellular and molecular perspective to help further acquire an in-depth understanding of the adaptive physiology of giants compared to regular-size species. Comparatively speaking, we have a much deeper understanding of the cellular, molecular, life history and physiological adaptations of polar regular-size ectotherms to low temperatures22–26 when compared to giants.

Depicting the responses of organisms at different levels of the biological hierarchy is capital to understand both ecological and evolutionary phenomena27. Therefore, it is paramount to comparatively characterize the cellular response of marine invertebrate polar giants and their regular-size relatives if we are to acquire a critical understanding of the way these organisms function and have evolved and, in turn, predict their response to future ocean changes28,29,30,31. This will also provide an underpinning to known whole-organism responses. In this sense, using a metabolomics approach can help shed light on how physiological systems work and have evolved32–35. In fact, metabolomics captures the end products of genetic, transcriptional and protein activity, depicting directly and from a functional perspective an organism’s physiological condition, as metabolomics is seen as the “closest link to the phenotype” (e.g.,36). Furthermore, marine invertebrates may possess a greater plasticity at the level of their cellular responses30,31,37,38 and cells function over broader temperature ranges (e.g.,39), when compared to the whole-organism level. In addition, polar marine species are known to possess extremely limited whole-organism plasticity compared to lower latitudes ones19 ,20,40. However, no information exists to date on their molecular plasticity. Employing a comparative metabolomics approach can improve our extant understanding of the physiological implications of being a polar ectothermic giant. This information can help us shedding light on the evolution of their energy metabolism, which underpins metabolic rates (see33), ultimately contributing toward our understanding of how gigantism has evolved to respond to the challenge of living under extreme environmental conditions7,12. In turn, the question remains: is there a physiology of giants?

Furthermore, the current climatic trend and predictions41,42 urge us to identify eco-evolutionary and taxonomic groups that are more vulnerable (and tolerant) to ongoing environmental changes19,20,43. If there is a physiology of giants, does it matter in light of rapid ongoing environmental changes? In other words, do giants possess a greater vulnerability to ocean change drivers due to a lesser ability for metabolic rewiring?13–20.

Consequently, using a targeted metabolomics approach, we aimed to characterize the energy metabolome of Antarctic giants, and regular-size species from the same taxa, exposed to an acute elevated temperature under laboratory conditions. We opted to work on shallow polar Antarctic giant and regular-size invertebrates, as: (i) they are relatively specious, (ii) they can be found across different taxonomic groups and phyla, (iii) their phylogenetic relationships are relatively well known, (iv) they are relatively easy to collect in shallow waters via scientific diving (despite the challenge to dive in Antarctic waters and at times remote areas), and (v) specimens of a given species can be found in large enough numbers. Considering that gigantism is a taxon-specific relative state130, “giants” can be defined as species that are at least twice as large as the mean body size in its genus44 or in the top 5% in terms of body size within its taxon and for a particular habitat7. Specifically, we exposed under laboratory conditions specimens of giant and regular-size species of amphipods, pycnogonids and ophiuroids, that were opportunistically collected around King George island (Antarctica) and exposed under laboratory conditions to two temperatures: one mimicking seasonal seawater conditions for the Antarctic current summer, and one an acute elevated temperature condition that Antarctic species may face with the progression of global changes45,46. Based on the extant literature, (i) giants show lower metabolic rates when compared to smaller species within the same taxon7,11, (ii) according to hypometric scaling, energy substrate synthesis decrease with body size47,48, (iii) there is a strong relationship between metabolomics profiles and metabolic rate which was shown in different populations of a marine ectotherm33, (iv) giants are proposed to possess a greater sensitivity to global warming and deoxygenation compared to smaller species on both modern empirical and mass extinction data7,18,49,50, and a lower transcriptional plasticity to acute thermal stress51. Therefore, we hypothesize that, when compared to their regular-size relatives, giants (H1) possess distinctive metabolic profiles, (H2) generally show lower concentrations of aerobic energy metabolites and lower levels of their energy status (i.e., [ATP]:[ADP], Adenylate Energy Charge (AEC) and Total Adenine Nucleotides)(TAN), (H3) show a shallower scaling exponent for metabolome, and (H4) possess a lesser ability for cellular metabolic reprogramming when exposed to elevated temperature.

Material and methods

Species selection

Specimens were collected around the Instituto Antártico Chileno (INACh) Antarctic Research Station Profesor Julio Escudero at Bahia Fildes (George Island, Antarctica -62° 11′ 60.00" S, 58° 56′ 59.99" W; Fig. 1A), where the experiment was carried out. Species were selected opportunistically based on the set of species available in relatively large numbers during the Austral summer 2023 (February 2023), whilst ensuring the collection of adult individuals of both giants (G) and regular-size species, according to De Broyer44 definition and multiple sources18,52,17,53, within the same taxonomic group (see Phylogeny and species information in Fig. 1B, Table S2 and “Species information” in Dataset SD1). We were able to collect a total of nine species (i.e., four G and five R) belonging to three different taxonomic groups: Amphipoda, Pycnogonida and Ophiuroidea. The species we defined as giants were already suggested to be giant forms within their taxon: Bovallia gigantea52, Paracerodocus miersi18, Ammothea carolinensis17 and Ophionotus victorae53.

Specimens’ collection, transport and maintenance

Adult specimens were collected during scuba dives via hand-picking or suction dredge sampling using an airlift device54,55 around Bahia Fildes by the IDEAL project’s Antarctic Scuba Diving Team at depths between 5 and 15 m, and field temperature around 0—2 °C56. Specimens were carefully placed under water in plastic bags filled with sea water to protect them from mechanical stress and damage during handling and transport, guarantee good oxygenation and to avoid air exposure during the underwater transportation. Once at the surface, collection bags were rapidly and delicately transferred on board of the research vessel and placed inside a cooler, which was also filled with sea water to guarantee further thermal stability and protection from mechanical stress during transportation to the research facility. Once back at the Escudero Research Base research station, specimens were immediately transferred to the Aquarium facility and placed in holding tanks equipped with aerators, using jars filled with sea water. Here, specimens’ identity was determined individually using keys from57,58 for the amphipods,59,60) for the pycnogonids, Martynov & Litvinova61 and Bell62 for the ophiuroids, and then they were haphazardly transferred inside one of the aquaria of our experimental systems at conditions comparable to those found in the field: 1 °C, salinity 34–35, 94–95% O2 saturation. Environmental parameters were measured with calibrated handheld multimeters (ProDSS, YSI, Yellow Springs, OH, USA). Before the experiments started, specimens’ condition and vitality were monitored for approx. 24–48 h. Specimens were then transferred from the holding tanks to aquaria using glass jars filled with sea water, to prevent air exposure and thermal shock. Water quality was maintained via a gentle continuous bubbling and partial daily water changes with fresh sea water of the same temperature of that of the system. Environmental conditions (temperature, salinity and O2 saturation) were monitored regularly throughout the day to ensure they remained constant and adjusted as needed.

Experimental design, system and protocol

We performed an orthogonal laboratory experiment (see Fig. 1C) aiming at testing whether giant and regular-size species differ in their: (H1) metabolomics profiles, (H2) energy status in terms of [ATP]:[ADP] AEC and TAN, and (H4) abilities for metabolomics reprogramming in response to acute elevated temperature. We exposed specimens to either control (1 °C) or an elevated temperature condition (5 °C) for 3 d, maintaining salinity between 34–35 and O2 saturation between 94–95% (see Table S1). We chose 1 °C as the mean temperature in the area and 5 °C which was shown to cause the complete loss of essential biological activities in Antarctic molluscs63 and death in some polar fish species64, and is highly stressful temperature for many Antartic species128. Furthermore, whilst they have not yet evolutionarily experienced a temperature of 5 °C, Antarctic marine species may experience such temperatures in future with increasing oceanic temperatures and extreme climatic events46, Davies et al., 2006). During this period, specimens were not fed to maintain water quality and avoid the influence of the specific dynamic action response on organisms’ metabolism. We ran four consecutive experiments, characterized by comparable environmental conditions (Table S1), to accommodate for the arrival of different species at different times during the sampling period, as well as space restrictions in our experimental systems (Fig. 1). The experimental system consisted of four large plastic basins (W: 63.0 cm, D: 45.0 cm, H: 20.3 cm), each containing four white plastic aquaria (W: 17.2 cm, D 16.4 cm, H: 16.2 cm), for a total of 16 aquaria. Basins were filled with freshwater to serve as Bain-Marie to maintain thermal stability within the aquaria. Two basins were maintained at 1 °C (1.1 ± 0.2 °C, 2.0%; mean ± SE, Coefficient of Variation) and two basins at 5 °C (5.1 ± 0.7 °C, 1.9%) using aquarium heaters (HS-100, SOBO, Xiaolan, China). Each aquarium was equipped with an air stone to guarantee continuous gentle aeration and water mixing. Aquaria were moved at regular intervals between basins maintained at the same temperature to ensure even variation of temperature conditions. Seawater quality was maintained as described above during the 3 d of experimentation. Three-four specimens were introduced in each aquarium, for a total of six-eight specimens per temperature per species, and 12–16 specimens per species in total. Finally, survival was monitored multiple times a day.

Specimens’ samples conservation and transport

At the end of the exposure time, specimens were individually transferred to plastic containers filled with sea water from their respective experimental systems, and thus at the same temperature of exposure. Using forceps to avoid warming during handling, specimens were individually rapidly blotted dry on towel paper, weighed in a pre-weighed sterile plastic container (1.5, 15 or 50 mL according to specimens’ sizes) and immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples were kept in a -80 °C freezer at the INACH research station and transferred by dry shipper prechilled with liquid nitrogen to the IDEAL Center in Punta Arenas (Chile), where they were stored in a -80 °C freezer. Finally, samples were shipped in a dry shipper prechilled with liquid nitrogen to the Marine Ecological and Evolutionary Physiology Laboratory at the University of Québec (Rimouski, QC, Canada) where they were stored in a -80 °C freezer until they were prepared for analyses.

DNA extraction, PCR, and genomic sequencing

DNA extraction was performed on 20–30 mg tissue samples, from specimens different from those used in our experiment collected during the same mission, using a kit (E.Z.N.A. Stool DNA kit, Omega Biotek Inc., Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality of the DNA extracts was validated visually on 1.0% agarose gels, and the DNA concentration (ng μL-1) was measured on a Nanodrop 1000. The amplification of the barcoding region of the mitochondrial Cytochrome C Oxidase subunit I (COI) gene was conducted via Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) using the standard primer pair LCO1490/HCO219865. Each PCR reaction was carried out in a volume of 12 μL reaction volume containing 6 μL of AmpliTaq Gold™ Fast PCR Master Mix (5 U μL-1), 0.6 μL of each primer (10 mM), 1 ng of DNA, and sterile water to reach the final volume. PCR steps were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 7 min; 35 cycles of denaturation (95 °C for 30 s), annealing (57 °C for 30 s), and extension (72 °C for 30 s), followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were visualized on agarose gels and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform at the AUSTRAL-omics core facility (Universidad Austral de Chile, Valdivia, Chile).

Sequences were assembled into contigs, edited, and aligned using Geneious 6.0.5 software66. Alignments and base composition of nucleotide sequences were analyzed in MEGA 7.067, utilizing MUSCLE with default settings. All sequences were deposited in the public GenBank database, and the accession numbers are listed in Table S2 and Dataset SD1.

Phylogenetic analyses were performed using Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI). ML analyses were conducted with IQ-TREE68 through the IQ-TREE web server, while BI analyses were carried out using MrBayes 3.2.7a69. The best-fit model of nucleotide substitution (K3Pu + F + I + G4) was determined using jModelTest 2.1.3 and applied to both ML and BI analyses. For ML analysis, nodal support was assessed using nonparametric bootstrapping (BS) with 1000 pseudo-replicates70. For BI analyses, posterior probabilities (PP) were calculated using the Metropolis-coupled Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm, with four chains run for 10 000 000 generations, sampling every 1 000 generations. The first 10% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in, and the PP values were calculated based on the fraction of trees supporting each clade. Phylogenetic trees were visualized using FigTree v.1.4.3 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree) and edited for presentation in Adobe Illustrator and Inkscape.

Sample pre-processing

Three specimens per experimental tank per species were selected for a sample size of six specimens per species per treatment, 12 specimens in total per species. The three largest specimens were selected to ensure having sufficient biomass for analyses, particularly for the smaller size species, whilst limiting the size bias between treatments. Briefly, the sample pre-processing consisted of grinding and sub-sampling, freeze-drying and homogenising whole-body specimens, as the comparison of different organs was considered problematic due to the profound differences in Bauplan among species from different taxa as well as biomass limitations in some of the regular-size species, weighing their dry mass and subsampling to obtain a < 30 mg dried homogenised sample for metabolite extraction. The protocol was adjusted to each species’ estimated dry mass (see Supplementary Methods for further information). All dried homogenised samples were in the range of 1.08 mg to 30.86 mg before metabolite extractions. Samples were always stored at -80 °C until metabolite extraction.

Metabolites extraction and quantification

Dried homogenised samples were randomised prior to metabolite extractions, blind to the experimenter. Metabolites were extracted at room temperature following a protocol optimised by Les Laboratoires Iso-Biokem Inc. (Rimouski, Quebec, Canada; see Supplementary Methods for details). Metabolites were quantified by targeted metabolomics by Les Laboratoires IsoBioKem Inc, with a modified protocol from32, as described in Supporting Information. Briefly, a total of 51 target metabolites from the energy metabolism were quantified using a high-performance liquid chromatograph (HPLC, 1260 Infinity II,Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) coupled with a mass spectrometer (6470B Triple Quadripole, Agilent Technologies). For each sample, 10 µL were eluted through an InfinityLab Poroshell 120 HILIC-Z column (2.7 μm, 100 × 2.1 mm, Agilent Technologies with a InfinityLab Poroshell 120 HILIC-Z guard column) at 30 °C with a flow rate of 500 and 250 µL min-1 for the positive and negative modes, respectively. Data was processed by Les Laboratoires IsoBioKem Inc using the MassHunter QQQ quantitative (Quant-my-Way) software (version 10.1, Agilent Technologies).

We accounted for sample-to-sample differences by expressing metabolite data as ng of compound per mg of sample dry mass. Missing values were imputed using MetImp 1.2 online tool71,72 using the “missing not at random” (MNAR) method with the “quantile regression imputation of left-censored data” (QRILC) preconised for targeted metabolomics. Metabolites with > 30% missing data across all groups were removed from the analysis. This step removed eight compounds (Cis-Aconitate, Acetyl-CoA, Octopine, Oxaloacetate, Glucose-6-phosphate, NADPH, Succinyl-CoA, and D-Fructose-1,6-biphosphate), yielding a final dataset of 43 metabolites across 108 samples. The raw data was normalised by applying a log2-transformation and a range scaling using the MetaboAnalystR R package v4.0.073,74. The adequate normalisation of data was assessed using the PlotNormSummary and PlotSampleNormSummary functions from MetaboAnalystR R package. The Gaussian profile of the data was further validated via additional distribution density graphs and Quantile–Quantile plots. The homoscedasticity of the data was confirmed visually through boxplots and statistically through Levene’s tests. All data is available in Dataset S1.

Metabolomics statistical analyses

All metabolomics statistical analyses were conducted in R v4.3.375 using the RStudio 2023.12.0 + 369 software. We provide classification of P values based on the “language of evidence” by Muff et al.76: with “little/no” (P > 0.1), “weak” (P ≤ 0.1), “moderate” (P ≤ 0.05), “strong” (P ≤ 0.01) and “very strong” (P ≤ 0.001) evidence. Effect sizes were based on Cohen77. A False Discovery Rate (hereafter, Padj) correction of 5% false positives was used for multiple testing. All statistical tests are two-sided.

The statistical analysis aimed to investigate the metabolomics profile in function of three categorical fixed variables, in isolation and their interaction: Temperature treatment (control and elevated), Gigantism (giant and regular-size species) and Taxon (taxonomic group). The multivariate analysis of the whole targeted metabolome was performed using permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) on the Euclidean distance with 999 permutations via the adonis2 function from the vegan R package78. The level of statistical evidence among PERMANOVA model terms’ was reported using the sequential term-by-term method using the “by = term” argument. The overall statistical evidence of the PERMANOVA model was reported using the “by = NULL” argument. The analyses were conducted first across all taxa and then per taxon. The addition of the covariates (experimental tank, specimen whole-body wet mass and sample extract dry mass, and species within amphipoda; all as fixed variables) to the models that included the relevant main variables of interest (treatment, gigantism, taxon) was determined using a second-order Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) selection via the fit_models function from the AICcPermanova v0.0.2 R package79. Candidate models (Dataset S1.3-S1.4) that had comparatively low ΔAICc (< 2 relative to the lowest AICc value) were further compared to remove covariates with evidence that were weak to no to follow a parsimony principle. The multivariate heteroskedasticity was tested using the betadisper and permutest functions from the vegan R package78.

The multivariate metabolomics profiles were visualised using principal component analyses (PCA) via the prcomp R function. To validate that metabolomics differences were truly not due to body mass according to H3, we investigated for the presence of a potential breakpoint in the relationship between body mass and the first PCA component (PC1) score for amphipod species. Breakpoint analyses were conducted using the segmented R package80, with a Davies’ test for the presence of one breakpoint and a p-score test for the nonlinearity hypothesis, with at least moderate evidence. Likewise, we investigated the relationship between the first three principal components (PC1, 2 and 3) scores and the gigantism status using Wilcoxon tests from the rstatix R package v0.7.281. Particularly within Amphipoda, we aimed to disentangle the effects of gigantism to that of “species” by representing the metabolome profiles both per species and by gigantism status.

Next, we performed univariate analysis through the limma R package v3.56.282 to identify differentially abundant metabolites (SDM) displaying at least moderate evidence (i.e., Padj ≤ 0.05) between giants and their regular-size relatives. In limma, we used the same model identified during the PERMANOVA AICc selection procedure to account for relevant covariates. We also explored the energy budget of giants and regular-size relatives using as proxies the ATP/ADP ratio, Total Adenylate Nucleotides (TAN, Eq. 1) and the Adenylate Energy Charge (AEC, Eq. 2).1TAN = [ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AEC = \frac{[ATP] + \frac{1}{2}[ADP]}{TAN}$$\end{document}

For each of the energy budget metrics, the best model was determined as having the lowest AIC value, and no superfluous covariate (i.e., only covariates with at least moderate evidence identified by a F test from the drop1 function were kept). The performance R package v0.12.483 was used to test the normality and homoscedasticity assumptions, following a transformation from the BestNormalize R package v1.9.184 when necessary. A Wilcoxon test from the rstatix R package was used if the assumptions were not satisfied. If relevant covariates were identified, the covariate-residuals were modeled as a function of the gigantism status.

Last, we explored the pathways associated with SDM. We used the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 web server73,74 with default parameters (hypergeometric test; relative-betweenness centrality for the topology measure; all compounds in the Homo sapiens KEGG database as the reference metabolome) for the metabolite set KEGG pathway enrichment of SDM. Homo sapiens metabolome was favoured because it is well-annotated with a high metabolite match mapping percentage that would not be biased towards a specific marine taxon. Pathways with at least moderate evidence (Padj ≤ 0.05) were considered biologically relevant. A metabolome network was built for each taxon using the FELLA R package85,86 with the reference network from the universe of metabolites from Limulus polyphemus (lpol) (Atlantic horseshoe crab) as a chelicerate relative for the Pycnogonida taxon, Hyalella azteca (hazt) as an amphipod relative for the Amphipoda taxon and Asterias rubens (arun) (European starfish) as an echinoderm relative for the Ophiuroidea taxon. Networks were built using the hypergeometric diffusion test and limited to 100 nodes. The final network was annotated using the network statistics retrieved using the Analyse Network tool (with the option “as a directed graph”) from the Cytoscape v3.9.1 software87 with software cross-talk using the RCy3 R package v2.20.288. Interactive networks were built with the visNetwork R package v2.1.289 using the forceAtlas2 network physics90 to position highly-connected nodes at the center. All data are available on Zenodo (Calosi et al.129).

Results

Survival

Only two individuals of the amphipod species Bovallia gigantea (G) and Schraderia gracilis (R) escaped from the aquaria and were found dead in the large plastic basins filled with freshwater, used as Bain Marie. Therefore, no specific mortality pattern related to either the temperature treatments employed or the gigantism status of species (i.e., giants versus regular-size species) emerged.

Whole targeted metabolome of giants across all taxa

The metabolomic profiles varied largely depending on Taxon and Gigantism, but not Temperature, when all taxa were considered together (Fig. 2A-C; ESM1). The lack of a temperature effect on the metabolome was further confirmed separately for each taxon and for each species individually (Fig. S1). We found very strong evidence supporting the hypothesis that giants from different taxa show different metabolomics profiles, as indicated by the presence of an interaction between the terms Taxon and Gigantism (Table 1A).

Furthermore, strong evidence was found for metabolomics profiles differing both among taxa and between giants and regular-size species, with the term Taxon showing the largest effect size (Table 1A). Consequently, further analyses were undertaken separately for each taxon, which also enabled us to consider Species within Amphipoda. In addition, as we reported ‘very small evidence’ that Temperature (alone or combined with other factors) affected metabolomics profiles (Table 1A), this term was removed from further analyses.

Taxon-specific whole targeted metabolome of giants

The metabolomic profiles of giants and regular-size species consistently differed within all taxa investigated, with centroids visibly showing no sign of overlap (Fig. 2D-F, Table 1B-D, ESM2). This pattern was also found for Amphipoda when all species were considered separately (Fig. 3A, Table 1B). The fact that strong and very strong evidence, respectively, was found for a difference in PC1 and PC2 scores between giants and their regular-size species, further confirmed that there was a clear difference in metabolomics profiling between giants and regular-size species across taxa (Fig. 3B-D). In fact, we found very strong evidence supporting the hypothesis that giants and regular-size species possess distinctive metabolomics profiles, separately, for all three taxa investigated, with medium to large effect sizes (Table 1 B-D).

Finally, the presence of a breakpoint in the relationship between amphipod species body mass versus PC1 scores, that clearly separates regular-size species from giants, confirmed further that the metabolomics differences reported here were not based solely on body mass per se (Fig. 3E), but rather depend on the term Gigantism.

Giants’ differentially abundant metabolites and energy status

Of the 43 detected metabolites, we identified 30, 22 and 17 differentially abundant metabolites (SDM) associated with Gigantism in Amphipoda, Pycnogonida and Ophiuroidea, respectively (Fig. 4A-D, Dataset S1.5). Of these SDM, eight were shared among taxa, with L-Aspartate decreasing and AMP increasing in all giant species investigated (Fig. 4A-J). Furthermore, giants from all three taxa showed ATP levels comparable to those of regular-size species (Fig. 4A-C, Dataset S1.5). We found very strong evidence for elevated ADP levels only in Ophiuroidea giant species (Fig. 4B), which led to a lower ATP/ADP ratio in this taxon, when compared to Amphipoda and Pycnogonida. The latter taxa showed comparable ATP/ADP ratios between giants and regular-size species (Fig. 5).

There was no evidence in Amphipoda and Ophiuroidea for giants and regular-size species’ mean AEC to differ (Fig. 5A,C), whilst we found strong support for the Pycnogonida giant species having a lower mean AEC when compared to the regular-size species (Fig. 5E). Metabolomic pathways and networks of gigantism

Taxon-specific pathways associated with gigantism-related SDM (Fig. 4D-F) showed that seven, seven, and ten pathways were differentially utilised by giants versus regular-size species in Amphipoda, Pycnogonida and Ophiuroidea, respectively (Fig. 4K; Dataset S1.6).

Giants of all three taxa shared among each other the differential utilisation of four pathways, that are involved in amino acid metabolism (‘Alanine, Aspartame and Glutamate metabolism’, hsa00250; ‘Glycine, Serine and Threonine metabolism’, hsa00260; and ‘Arginine biosynthesis’, hsa00220) and the ‘TCA cycle’ (hsa00020). However, taxon-specific giant-associated pathways also emerged. Amphipoda and Ophiuroidea giants showed unique differential utilisation of one and three unique pathways, respectively, whilst Pycnogonida giants shared with either Amphipoda or Ophiuroidea some pathways that were not found in the other taxon (Fig. 4K; Dataset S1.6).

Taxon-specific network analyses confirmed the presence of shared pathways utilisation among giants of the three taxa investigated, when compared to their relative regular-size species (Fig. 4 G-I, ESM3; Datasets S1.7-S1.9). Particularly, giants showed an alteration of the betaine-related pathways (‘betaine degradation: betaine → pyruvate’, M00975; and/or ‘betaine metabolism: betaine → glycine’, M00974) as important network modules shared between all taxa. The enrichment of these pathways is associated with an increase in betaine in Pycnogonida and Amphipoda, whilst it decreased in Ophiuroidea (Fig. 4 G-I, ESM3). Furthermore, both Amphipoda and Ophiuroidea giants, but not Pycnogonida, showed putative changes to the ‘mTOR signaling pathway’, notably associated with a decrease in L-leucine in both taxa (Fig. 4 G-I, ESM3). Unsurprisingly, some pathways utilisation was not shared among giants. In Pycnogonida and Ophiuroidea, gigantism was linked to a unique cluster, that for Pycnogonida was the ‘β-Alanine metabolism’ (lpol00410) and ‘Alanine, Aspartate and Glutamate’ (lpol00250; albeit shared by all three taxa in the KEGG enrichment analysis; Fig. 4 D-K, ESM3), whilst for Ophiuroidea was the ‘C4 dicarboxylic acid cycle, NADP—malic enzyme type’ (M00172; Fig. 4I, ESM3). Finally, pyruvate was among the nodes with the highest numbers of neighbours in all three taxa. Pyruvate levels were lower in both Amphipoda and Pycnogonida giants but increased with gigantism in Ophiuroidea (Fig. 4A-C, G-J, ESM3, Dataset S1.8).

Discussion

Is there a “physiology of giants”? The simple answer is “it appears so”! Here we report, for the first time, compelling evidence supporting the notion that marine polar invertebrate giants from very distinct taxa share the utilisation of a number of metabolic pathways involved in energy production, suggesting a convergent adaptive reprogramming of their metabolism. This has likely emerged from the need for giants to meet the challenge to maintain homeostasis and promote cell repair and growth despite possessing larger bodies and experiencing harsh polar conditions. Interspecific comparisons within Amphipoda help showing that the patterns we report are not merely caused by allometry, further strengthening the evidence for the existence of specific metabolic adaptations underpinning the evolution of polar gigantism in marine invertebrates. In other words, not only is there a physiology of giants, but giants are not just larger regular-size species. Unsurprisingly, we also report differences in how giants from different taxa utilise metabolome pathways and energy status. Finally, we find no evidence for short-term metabolomic reprogramming ability under elevated temperatures in any of the polar species investigated, allowing us to reject our hypothesis that giants are more metabolically sensitive to ongoing ocean warming when compared to their regular-size relatives. Here below we discuss the metabolic implications of being a marine giant and trace ways forward to further explore the evolution of marine polar gigantism from a mechanistic perspective.

We validate our hypothesis (H1) that marine polar giants from different taxa possess a common and distinctive metabolomics signature, consisting in the adaptive reprogramming of multiple pathways, when compared to regular-size species. This includes specifically pathways related to amino acid metabolism and biosynthesis, betaine degradation and metabolism and the TCA cycle.

Amino acid metabolism and biosynthesis, and betaine degradation and metabolism pathways are intimately interconnected with the TCA cycle, directly or via the urea cycle, as they are involved in the production of intermediates entering the TCA cycle91, thus maintain an efficient metabolism and ATP production92,93. Likewise, the decrease of L-Aspartate in giants could be a sign that this amino acid is catabolised to sustain the TCA cycle to generate ATP94, and/or of the utilisation of partial anaerobiosis for the production of AMP95. In the copepodite stages IV and V of the Arctic copepod Calanus glacialis96 exposed to elevated seawater pCO2, the catabolism of amino acids appears to be a primary mechanism for the production of TCA cycle intermediaries97. The TCA cycle is central to cellular respiration, and primarily responsible for oxidative phosphorylation for energy production in the mitochondria. Efficient metabolic processes are essential for sustaining homeostasis and other physiological functions, which is likely to be particularly true in cold environments.

The fact that ATP production is maintained in giants, and the TCA cycle emerges as one of the enriched pathways, confirm that the aerobic energy metabolism is broadly speaking maintained, and that the metabolomics reprogramming underpinning this pattern likely represents a key adaptation in gigantism in polar environments. This feature is likely to have evolved to meet the energy requirement of having to face simultaneously the challenges to possess a larger body size, cope with cold temperature and protect the organism from the opposing risks of asphyxiation and oxygen poisoning12. Amino acids can be used to buffer high energy phosphate in invertebrates and also act as a shuttle mechanism for ATP production98,99, as reported in the stage I larvae of the American lobster exposed to elevated pCO2 with an increase in phospho-L-arginine38.

Altogether, contrary to our hypothesis (H2), we conclude that marine polar giants maintain overall an energy status (defined via [ATP], [ATP] / [ADP] and AEC) that is comparable to that of regular-size species, although some peculiarities are observed (i.e., elevated AMP and TAN). In fact, we show that all giants appear to possess increased AMP levels, which in the pycnogonid and ophiuroid giants relate to an increase in TAN. Higher TAN levels can be found in cells exposed to stressful conditions (e.g., hypoxia), but classically via ATP resynthesis100 and not AMP as we report here for marine polar giants. However, higher TAN levels can relate to increased biosynthesis activity and protein turnover that are commonly observed in rapidly growing cells or tissues101–103. Both these mechanisms could explain the enhanced amino acids metabolism (amino acids being the building blocks for cell growth) we report, and could both be at play. Unfortunately, no evidence on mitochondrial efficiency and protein synthesis rates and turnover exist (to our knowledge) for polar giants, with the exception of the giant Antarctic isopod Glyptonotus antarcticus104,105, both representing interesting avenues to continue shedding light on the evolution of giants’ physiological systems.

The network analyses show that differential utilisation of betaine-related pathways appears to be a key feature of gigantism. Betaine is used in organisms to help maintain cellular integrity. More specifically, on one hand betaine promotes the osmo-regulatory function under stressful conditions (incl. cold) as it acts as an osmolyte. Across the tree of life betaine acts as an osmolyte, and the increase in the expression of gene related to betaine metabolism in marine organisms have been associated to osmoregulation and stress response106–108. Equally, in the first and pre-metamorphic larval stages (stage I and III, respectively) of the American lobster Homarus americanus (H. Milne Edwards, 1837) an increase in betaine concentration was observed when larvae were exposed to elevated seawater pCO2, suggesting this intracellular organic osmolyte is used to compensate for osmotic and acid–base challenges caused by hypercapnia38. Interestingly, osmolytes (such as glycine-betaine) may play an important role in cold adaptation in Antarctic echinoderms109. Betaine also works as a stabilizer for protein structures under freezing/near-freezing conditions protecting the cell from cold temperature stress, and as cryoprotector preventing damages from ice formation110,111. It also possibly plays a role as an antioxidant against reactive oxygen species112.

Finally, network analyses identified the mTOR signaling pathway as a key component of polar marine invertebrates gigantism. The mTOR signaling pathways plays a crucial role in several functions, including for example the regulation of growth and cell renewal, metabolism (including mitochondrial metabolism) and the allocation of energy reserves, nutrient sensing and stress responses in many organisms, including marine invertebrates32,113–118. In the abalone Haliotis discus hannai, mTOR signaling is modulated by amino acids like Arginine119. In giants, the increase in amino acids metabolism may therefore be linked to the modulation of this pathway central to growth and signaling. More specifically, the decrease in Leucine we report in the pycnogonid and ophiuroid giants may be responsible for the inhibition of the mTOR pathway. The mTOR pathway is also involved in cancer promotion, and its inhibition results in cancer suppression120. Interestingly, cancer suppression is linked to increased life span in mammals121,122 and gigantism in whales123. We suggest that the modulation of the mTOR signaling pathway may have also been implicated, among other mechanisms, in the evolution of gigantism in marine polar invertebrates. With this in mind, we hypothesise that Peto’s Paradox [which postulates that larger animal species, having more cells, should have a greater incidence of cancer when compared to smaller-size ones, but instead show lower risk of cancer mortality; see124 may also apply to marine polar invertebrate giants. This idea will have to be formally tested in future studies.

If gigantism is simply related to possessing a larger body mass, one might expect the relationship between body mass and metabolites abundance to be linear, and independent of gigantism status; based on the fact that both metabolic rates and metabolomic profiles vary linearly with body mass33,125. However, this is not the case, as in amphipods we report the presence of a threshold (at 0.19 g), validating H3, based on the species we investigated, for the relation between body mass and the PC1 of the metabolome. Furthermore, an allometric relationship exists for regular-size species, but not for giants. The fact that amphipod giants’ PC2 scores are most similar, relative to regular-size species, is another indication that the physiological system of giants has evolved along a different trajectory than that of regular-size species, and that the metabolomics patterns observed in giants cannot be solely explained by allometry. Furthermore, if metabolome profiles were to be dictated by the phylogenetic history of species, the amphipod B. gigantea would have presented a profile more similar to that of its regular-size species of amphipods we investigated, instead both giant species B. gigantea and P. miersi show comparably higher similar metabolomics profiles in comparison to the regular-size species.

Conclusion

We report, for the first time, the existence of distinctive metabolic features common to marine polar invertebrate giants from different taxa that revolve around the adaptive reprogramming of their energy metabolism, validating H1 but invalidating H2. We show that giants’ metabolomic profiles do not follow allometry (partially validating H3), and suggest that the mTOR signaling pathway modulation may be linked to gigantism, also in marine invertebrates, although this will need to be formally tested elsewhere. Finally, against H4, we show that at the metabolomic level polar marine invertebrates do not possess short-term plasticity, confirming what reported for whole-organism responses19,20. Giants do not appear, from our study, to be more sensitive to ocean warming when compared to regular-size species, in line with Chapelle & Peck7 and Spicer and Morley18 for amphipods (c.f.,50c for pycnogonids). Instead, both giants and their regular-size relatives show a lack of (at least on the short-term) metabolomic plasticity, this confirming Antarctic species’ vulnerability to ongoing environmental changes (c.f.,19,20). Of course, the patterns and candidate pathways emerging from our work will require further validation, for instance through biomarker quantification, using a greater replication level, accounting for seasonality, and testing it on a broader phylogenetic basis: including for example nemerteans, tunicates, sponges, starfish and isopods. However, their giants are much larger (in absolute terms) than those of the taxa investigated here, and represent a greater challenge for whole-body dry freezing and subsampling in remote areas often not equipped with appropriate equipment, or to be shipped in liquid nitrogen/dry ice from remote areas. It is also important to consider that polar invertebrates differ greatly in terms of growth rates, embryonic development, and metabolic rate responses from phylogenetically-related species from other climate regions7,10, and that generalisation to giants vs. regular size species systems from lower latitude or different habitats (e.g., deep sea) should be made with caution. Most importantly, our finding will need to be further elucidated at different levels of the biological hierarchy27. In this sense, recent advances in both analytical techniques and bioinformatics will be instrumental in helping shed further light on the evolution of giants from a cell physiology perspective, particularly implementing a multiomics and multilayers approach126,127.
