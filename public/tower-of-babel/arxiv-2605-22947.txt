Entanglement-facilitated macroscopic cluster formation in quantum many-body dynamics
Xiao Wang, Alexander Yosifov, Aditya Iyer, Jinzhao Sun

Abstract
The capacity of a quantum many-body system to preserve global information—encoded in the non-local correlations—is a prerequisite for robust quantum computing. Unlike local degrees of freedom, large structures offer inherent resilience to noise, but their stability is often compromised by dynamical fragmentation and local excitations. In this work, we investigate under what initial conditions the quantum dynamics can sustain system-size cluster structures by examining false-vacuum decay dynamics in a 2D quantum Ising model. We find that while product states rapidly fragment into uncorrelated domains, initial-state entanglement suppresses the proliferation of true-vacuum bubbles and stabilises macroscopic connected clusters. We find that this passive stabilisation is not a mere consequence of entanglement entropy but rather depends on the specific pre-quench correlations. Our results establish a connection between initial-state preparation and the preservation of global structures, highlighting the role of entanglement for the passive protection of information in 2D quantum many-body simulators.

The TFLIM serve as one of the simplest minimal models because the ferromagnetic coupling generates the ordered domain structure, and the transverse field provides the quantum fluctuations necessary to build entanglement in the ground state (before quench). The longitudinal field gives symmetry-breaking toward one of the two domains, which creates the metastable FV whose decay is driven by bubble nucleation.

Surface-volume competition in 1D vs. 2D bubbles.—
In 2D, a bubble is a compact domain of reversed magnetisation, and its energy has two contributions: a positive surface term originating from the domain-wall tension
\sigma
and a negative bulk term associated with the energy-density difference
\Delta\epsilon>0
between the TV and FV. For a bubble of radius
R
, these contributions take the form

E(R)=E_{\text{surface}}(R)+E_{\text{volume}}(R)=2\pi R\sigma-\pi R^{2}\Delta\epsilon,

(2)

where the surface energy grows linearly in
R
, while the volume energy grows quadratically; the transition between these two regimes is determined by the stationary point of Eq. (
2
), i.e.,
dE/dR=2\pi\sigma-2\pi R\Delta\epsilon=0
. This competition establishes a genuine nucleation barrier in higher dimensions, defined by the critical radius
R_{c}=\sigma/\Delta\epsilon
in 2D. Bubbles with
R<R_{c}
are energetically unfavourable and tend to collapse, whereas those exceeding
R_{c}
expand.

This energetic landscape provides the physical origin for the double-well potential in Fig.
1
(a). Here, the Hamming distance acts as a coordinate for the bubble size; the ascending slope of the metastable well corresponds to the subcritical regime
R<R_{c}
, where the surface tension
\sigma
dominates. Further, the discrete energy levels
V-n\Delta_{l}
correspond to the bulk term
-\pi R^{2}\Delta\epsilon
as the bubble expands across the 2D plane. In contrast, a bubble in 1D is simply an interval of length
L
bounded by two domain walls, resulting in a size-independent surface energy of
2\sigma
. The energy functional in this case

E_{\text{1D}}(L)=2\sigma-L\Delta\epsilon
is strictly decreasing for any
\Delta\epsilon>0
. Consequently, 1D systems lack a surface-volume competition and a physical nucleation barrier. Once a kink-antikink pair is nucleated, the bubble expands without an energetic penalty. The presence of this barrier in 2D is precisely why initial-state entanglement plays such a decisive role for metastability. The state
|\text{FV}\rangle
can sample the configurations near
R_{c}
through fluctuations. Rather than relying on independent local excitations to bridge the energetic gap, the pre-existing entanglement in
|\text{FV}\rangle
drives the evolution.

Real-time dynamics.—
To investigate the role of initial correlations, we simulate the non-equilibrium evolution following a quench into the metastable regime. We quantify the relaxation through the average magnetisation

M_{z}(t)=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i}\langle S_{i}^{z}\rangle,

(3)

which measures the persistence of the initial order in the lattice, and the return probability
33

P_{\mathrm{ret}}(t)=|\langle\psi(0)|\psi(t)\rangle|^{2},

(4)

which probes the coherence loss relative to the initial state
15

3

4

Large clusters from metastable states.—
While these first-passage times quantify global stability, they do not tell us about the underlying spatial structures that protect the FV from decay in 2D. To determine how this stability emerges, in Fig.

\ket{\text{FV}}

\ket{\psi_{0}}

To further isolate the role of the initial state’s structure, we compare these results to the first excited state and a random MPS state with entanglement entropy comparable to that of
\ket{\text{FV}}

(c,d). Here we observe that the excited state replicates the rapid concentration at large

While in terms of the cluster-number density

\ket{\text{FV}}
has higher density of large clusters for both
h_{q}

\ket{\psi_{0}}

\ket{\text{FV}}
exhibits a characteristic increase, indicating that the system sustains macroscopic connected domains even under stronger quenches. This distinction is also evident in the largest-cluster distribution
P(s_{\text{max}})

Physically, these observations underscore the stabilising role of initial-state entanglement in FV decay. After the quench, the product state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
excites uncorrelated local modes, leading to the rapid formation and growth of TV bubbles that destabilise the FV. In contrast, the DMRG ground state encodes coherent superpositions of domain-wall configurations that correlate local relaxation processes and suppress dephasing, thereby extending the lifetime of the initial order
20
. From a TN perspective, the finite correlation length and structured entanglement spectrum of the DMRG state constrain the post-quench exploration of Hilbert space to more accessible timescales and delay the thermalisation
19
. This manifests as an effective separation of timescales: while the Hamiltonian imposes a linear energy cost for large-domain flips, the correlated initial state suppresses collective tunnelling processes, resulting in a long-lived metastable plateau that is only disrupted at late times via rare multi-spin events. We note here that the intermediate states differ qualitatively, indicating distinct dynamical pathways rather than mere delayed relaxation.

Spectral invariance and spatial observables.—
Our results clearly show that 2D initially entangled states retain global clusters. While the presence of long-lived plateaus in the return probability
P_{\text{ret}}(t)=|\langle\psi|e^{-i\hat{H}t}|\psi\rangle|^{2}
suggests a high degree of global coherence within the metastable regime, it is essential to distinguish between the stability of this global overlap and the physical stability of the state’s structure. Because
P_{\text{ret}}(t)
depends solely on the energy spectral distribution of the initial state
18
;
36
, that limits its utility as a probe of metastability. By expanding the initial state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{n}c_{n}|E_{n}\rangle
in the eigenbasis of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, the amplitude is given by
A(t)=\sum_{n}|c_{n}|^{2}e^{-iE_{n}t}
. It is thus easy to see that any unitary transformation
\hat{U}
that commutes with the Hamiltonian leaves
P_{\text{ret}}(t)
invariant, as such operators preserve the spectral weights
|c_{n}|^{2}
. A direct consequence of this symmetry is that the time-evolved state
|\psi(t^{\prime})\rangle=e^{-i\hat{H}t^{\prime}}|\psi\rangle
shares the same return probability as the initial state for any
t^{\prime}
, despite the fact that local observables and entanglement entropy may have evolved substantially by time
t^{\prime}

27
. This spectral invariance highlights a key limitation:
P_{\text{ret}}(t)
probes only how much the evolved and initial states overlap, but not how correlations are spatially arranged in the lattice. This realisation underscores the need for percolation-based cluster analysis
9
to faithfully characterise the passive protection.

Discussion and perspectives.—
Our work identifies a mechanism by which global information can persist under quantum many-body dynamics without active intervention. Rather than being determined by the spectral properties
18
, we demonstrate how the stability of large structures depends on both lattice dimensionality (Fig.
3
) and the correlation structure of the initial state, see Fig.
4
(a,d). In 2D, the expected presence of a nucleation barrier enables the formation of large connected domains [as in Fig.
1
(b)], but whether such domains survive the dynamics is ultimately controlled by the initial entanglement. While previous studies of FV dynamics have predominantly focused on product states, our work extends this paradigm to correlated initial states, revealing how initial quantum structures can alter the decay pathways.

From a quantum information perspective, this establishes a form of passive information protection, which does not arise from encoding into a subspace or performing active measurements, but from the dynamical suppression of the fragmentation into smaller regions. The cluster statistics, including the cluster-number densities and largest-cluster distributions (Fig.
4
), show macroscopic cluster formation. This finding indicates that correlations remain distributed across the lattice rather than being localised into local degrees of freedom. More importantly, this mechanism is not captured by
P_{\text{ret}}(t)
alone, which depends on the energy distribution of the state
36
. Instead, it is the spatial organisation of correlations (encoded in the distribution of connected clusters) that determines whether global information remains accessible. In this sense, entanglement can be interpreted as a structural constraint on the dynamics, limiting the pathways through which local fluctuations can nucleate.

These observations suggest a route toward engineering many-body systems that retain macroscopic information over long times without active control. In programmable quantum simulators, such as Rydberg atom arrays
10
;
6
, the ability to prepare correlated initial states could be used to suppress the effective proliferation of local errors by preventing their growth into system-size decay processes.
An interesting question is whether this passive protection mechanism can be paired with active control to enable real-time feedback protocols during the dynamics to actively sustain or even grow the macroscopic cluster sizes. Another question concerns how the connected-cluster statistics
s_{\max}
scale with lattice size
L
and dimension
d
; for example, whether it scales polynomially with respect to
L
, i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(L^{\alpha})
with
\alpha<d
. Addressing this is hard for classical computing as
7\times 7
simulations already approach the classical limits of MPS-based methods for real-time dynamics.
In the intermediate stage, methods based on tree TNs could extend to much larger sizes
21
;
32
, although the associated sampling procedure is less straightforward than using MPS due to the branching network structure.
This motivates the use of digital quantum computers, where the distribution of the largest connected clusters and their probabilities can be accessed from projective measurements. In this sense, the cluster statistics play a role analogous to observables in quantum sampling problems, such as Gaussian boson sampling, where the target distributions are generally believed to be classically hard to solve.
Probing the emergence and stability of large connected domains, which may point out a physically motivated task for quantum computers.
