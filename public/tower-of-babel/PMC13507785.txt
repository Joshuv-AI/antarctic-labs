Enhanced games and performance-optimized athletes: anticipating the orthopedic challenges, a narrative review
Authors: Mani Eftekhari, Rohith Ryali, Justin Luk, Atta Taseh, Vencel Juhasz, Gergo Merkely, Soheil Ashkani-Esfahani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Enhanced Games (EG) represents a controversial shift in competitive athletics by removing anti-doping regulations and allowing the sanctioned use of performance-enhancing drugs (PEDs) in pursuit of maximum performance. This unprecedented model may fundamentally alter sports culture, competition standards, and the medical landscape in which athletes train and compete. Of particular concern are the potential consequences for orthopedic and musculoskeletal health, as PED use, particularly anabolic agents and growth factors, can both enhance and compromise musculoskeletal function. Potential benefits include increased muscle mass or bone density. However, these must be weighed against heightened risks of tendon rupture, altered joint biomechanics, delayed fracture healing, and increased susceptibility to overuse or catastrophic injuries. Given the likelihood of more extreme physical demands and higher injury rates in EG participants, proactive preparation from the orthopedic and sports medicine community is essential. This narrative review focuses on the potential influences of EG on musculoskeletal care, gathering evidence on anticipated injury patterns, surgical and rehabilitative considerations, and long-term health outcomes. The goal is to provide insight for policymakers and care providers tasked with protecting athletes who choose to compete in these environments.Level of Evidence:Level V.

Introduction

The first proposed Enhanced Games(EG), a sporting event that openly encourages the use of performance-enhancing drugs (PEDs), is set for the spring of 2026 to be held in Las Vegas, United States. The event will include swimming, weightlifting, and track and field competitions. Positioned in direct contrast to the anti-doping ethos upheld by the International Olympic Committee, World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA), International Testing Agency, and National Anti-Doping Organizations, the EG explicitly rejects WADA regulations in favor of permitting PED use. Backed by numerous venture capitalists, this event has come to fruition while generating significant public interest, media coverage, and spirited new discussions on the ethos of sport.1 The use of PED is not new to competitive athletics; in fact, among US elite athletes, doping rates range from 6.5 to 9.2%, with the EG representing the first formal integration of such substances into the structural framework of a global sporting competition in modern times.2 Whether or not the EG ultimately takes place in 2026, its concept signals a shift with important implications for athlete care.

While the ethical and cultural debates are ongoing, one of the most immediate medical concerns regarding EG lies in its impact on the musculoskeletal (MSK) system. Encompassing bones, joints, tendons, ligaments, and muscles, the MSK system is often the target of performance-enhancing strategies and thus especially vulnerable to unintended consequences. Despite possible challenges, athletes may pursue enhancement for diverse reasons, including improved competitive outcomes, accelerated recovery, financial incentives, and cognitive biases such as the “false consensus effect,” in which individuals overestimate the prevalence of PED use among their peers.3,4 These motivations, coupled with the physiological demands of high-intensity competition, create a complex risk–benefit landscape for MSK health. Clinical evidence already highlights these concerns of elevated rates of musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, and endocrine morbidity that persist for years after cessation.5-7 Orthopedic surgeons, sports medicine physicians, and rehabilitation specialists must be prepared to address the potential short- and long-term consequences of enhanced performance on the human body. In particular, understanding how EG, and specifically PED, may impact the MSK system is critical, as this knowledge will guide injury prevention strategies, optimize treatment protocols, and safeguard the health of athletes competing under these unprecedented conditions.

This work aimed to synthesize the scientific literature on orthopedic implications of EG, with emphasis on potential injury patterns, surgical and rehabilitation challenges, and long-term MSK outcomes. Beyond its immediate sporting context, EG may function as a large-scale, real-world case study, 1 that could inform orthopedic guidelines and rehabilitation strategies in athlete care in both regulated and unregulated performance environments.

History of performance-enhancing drugs in Enhanced Games–specific disciplines

The first iteration of EG in 2026 is advertised to include swimming, weightlifting, and track and field events. These disciplines have consistently experienced WADA anti-doping rule violations, providing historical precedent for potential orthopedic implications of enhancing human performance.

The EG swimming competition is expected to include 50 m and 100 m freestyle and 50 m and 100 m butterfly. Doping cases have been documented in elite swimming, including periods involving anabolic-androgenic steroids (AAS) and, more recently, metabolic modulators and blood-manipulation strategies. These enhancement methods provide increased muscular power for starts and turns, the ability to race at a higher pace, and delay fatigue levels. In the 1970s, East German swimmers were systematically administered anabolic steroids through a state-sponsored doping program.8 More recently, the Federal Bureau of Investigation, alongside the United States Department of Justice, launched an investigation into the use of trimetazidine, a metabolic modulator that enhances cardiovascular efficiency, involving 23 swimmers from China who were ultimately cleared to compete at the Tokyo Olympics in 2021, prompting international scrutiny and ethical debate. 9 In swimming, rapid strength gains from AAS may outpace tendon adaptation, elevating the risk for shoulder tendon injuries and biceps–labral complex pathology, and increased training load may contribute to overuse injuries of the shoulder.10,11

Enhanced Games will comprise a snatch, clean, and jerk for the weightlifting event as well. Competitive weightlifting has had many anti-doping violations, recording the highest rate of anti-doping rule violations among Olympic sports in WADA's 2020 Anti-Doping Rule Violations Report. 12 The use of AAS has been found to increase muscle size and strength, ultimately increasing maximal pressing power.13 Notably, the re-analysis of samples from the 2008 and the 2012 Olympic Games for exogenous AAS resulted in 61 weightlifters, including 9 gold medalists, having doping violations.14In weightlifting, rapid AAS-related strength gains and heavier absolute loads are linked to clusters of tendon and muscle rupture (e.g., pectoralis major).15

Athletes in EG will be competing in the 100 m sprint and 100 m/110 m hurdles. Track and field events have had highly publicized and scrutinized cases of performance enhancement, including track star Ben Johnson’s world record time of 9.79 seconds during the 1988 Seoul Olympics and subsequent suspension for the use of the AAS stanozolol.16 Even 3 decades later, that mark would remain among the fastest recorded 100 m performances. The use of AAS is associated with overall sprinting performance with hormonal manipulations linked to substantial improvements in sprint performance.17 In sprints and hurdles, augmented peak power and training loads are associated with elevated risk of hamstring strains/avulsions and Achilles tendon injury.

Beyond the musculoskeletal system, the substances can take a toll on overall health. Specifically, long-term use of AAS appears to be associated with myocardial dysfunction and increased coronary atherosclerosis.18,19 Neuropsychiatric effects such as depression, dependence, and aggression, along with reproductive consequences of infertility, have also been well documented.20,21

Musculoskeletal effects of performance-enhancing drugs

Anabolic-driven tissue changes

Anabolic-androgenic steroids can substantially alter MSK tissues, driving rapid muscle mass hypertrophy.22 Clinical and animal studies indicate that AAS enhances muscle mass by 2-5 kg and strength by 5%-20% through androgen receptor activation.5 Although this gain in muscle functionality likely leads to stronger athletic performance, the muscle-associated tendons are not given sufficient time to remodel to adapt to the changing muscular framework, predisposing users to tendon injuries.23,24 Studies have also shown that AAS further has direct effects on tendon structure and function, with animal studies demonstrating AAS inhibition of matrix metalloproteinases, thus inhibiting tendon remodeling and repair, leading to decreased flexibility.25-28Human studies are more mixed, with some suggesting direct AAS association with collagenous mechanical properties of tendons, although others do not support this collagen crimp hypothesis.29,30

The implications of AAS usage stretch beyond mechanical imbalances and injuries as well, with numerous MSK-adjacent vascular changes attributed to AAS.31 For instance, endothelial dysfunction, increased platelet aggregation, and dyslipidemia are all known to result from steroid usage and may compromise lower-extremity MSK health through increasing the frequency of potentially fatal events like deep vein thrombosis.32-35 Large population studies report an increase in the risk of venous thromboembolism after testosterone initiation, peaking within 6 months.36 Clinical guidelines highlight erythrocytosis as a dose-related adverse effect and recommend withholding therapy and/or phlebotomy when hematocrit ≥54%.37 Reduced blood flow can delay the delivery of nutrients essential to the recovery process, slowing the repair of tendons, ligaments, and muscles, which in turn increases the likelihood of chronic MSK complications. Overall, the combination of enhanced muscle strength, impaired tendon adaptation, and vascular dysfunction reveals a highly multifaceted MSK risk profile for many EG athletes.

Common injury patterns in enhanced athletes

The predominant MSK injuries linked to AAS usage involve tendon ruptures, mostly of the upper extremities.15,23,38 Notably, pectoralis major ruptures are disproportionately reported among frequent AAS users, likely due to the emphasis on upper-body muscle hypertrophy many athletes seek to achieve through targeted training regimens.15 Lower-extremity tendon injuries are also noted to occur, although at a lower frequency, with patellar tendon injury known to be more frequent.29,30 Observational testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) reports show higher odds of Achilles injury, although confounding by indication/training load remains possible. This difference may indicate potential differential tendon adaptation due to distinct loading patterns between the upper and lower extremities, with the upper extremities being subjected to irregular and eccentric loads, whereas the lower extremities are subject to the stresses of chronic full-body loading.29,39,40 In addition, PED-driven muscle growth can create a mismatch between muscle strength and connective tissue resilience. This risk is only amplified by impaired collagen remodeling, reduced blood supply, and changes in tendon mechanical properties.

The literature on ligamentous injuries is sparse regarding AAS usage, yet the link remains possible.41 Two large retrospective studies report higher anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury rates among adults on TRT. In a matched cohort of 851 816 patients, those receiving TRT were 2 times more likely to sustain a primary ACL rupture.42 Similarly, another study including 160 839 patients found a higher 2-year ACL injury incidence after ≥3 months of TRT (0.35% vs. 0.10%).43 Although direct controlled evidence is lacking, a survey of retired National Football League players found that self-reported AAS use correlated with ligamentous and joint injuries, including disc herniations, knee ligamentous/meniscal injury, elbow injuries, neck stinger/burner, spine injury, and foot/toe/ankle injuries.41 However, this was self-reported and subject to exposure misclassification and recall bias. Furthermore, in vivo studies have demonstrated the presence of androgen receptors in ACL cells in young men, supporting the biological responsiveness of ligaments to androgens.44

Beyond commonly recognized tissue injuries, AAS usage also affects bone quality and health, with large-scale cohort data suggesting that AAS users have a more than 2-fold increased risk of fractures.45Anabolic-androgenic steroid usage is linked to a higher risk of skeletal complications, particularly in adolescent athletes, where premature closure of epiphyses can result in altered bone architecture and changes in microstructure.31,46 Similarly, regarding osteoarthritis, the relationship with AAS usage is less clear, but recent Mendelian randomization data suggest that higher serum testosterone and dihydrotestosterone levels may causally increase the risk of osteoarthritis pathogenesis, particularly of the hip.47 However, unlike systemic corticosteroid use, there is no consistent evidence that suggests supraphysiologic AAS dosages lead to decreased bone mineral density, and the adverse bone-related complications of AAS usage are linked primarily to bone growth disturbances.46,48 In sum, the anatomical locations of many injuries associated with AAS usage are predictable from knowledge of athlete-specific training regimens and, in adolescents, knowledge of epiphyseal closure patterns. Thus, heightened vigilance may enable early detection of tendon and bone injuries in performance-enhanced EG athletes.

Challenges in finding the association

There are several challenges associated with diagnosing AAS-related injuries. For example, when AAS users rupture tendons, they often lack the expected prodromal symptoms and experience tendon rupture under quite low mechanical loads, which complicates the goal of early clinical detection.23,30,38 Standardized diagnostic criteria (clinical and imaging confirmation), documentation of pre-injury symptoms, and capture of training-load metrics are needed to improve comparability across studies. Also, AAS users have notably tended to underreport or not disclose their steroid usage levels, likely due to fear of illegality and repercussions in WADA-approved sports, which further obscures clinical suspicion and reduces diagnostic accuracy.31,49 Additionally, heterogeneity in the type of AAS, doses, cycle length, timing (on/off-cycle), and polypharmacy with other PED obscures attribution to AAS alone.

The transparency of AAS and PED usage celebrated by EG could potentially offer several distinct clinical advantages to address these challenges. By openly acknowledging substance use, both athletes and physicians could more accurately determine injury risk, which would open the door to the development of targeted surveillance protocols for high-risk MSK phenotypes.31,38,46 Thus, while AAS and PED usage carries substantial MSK risk, EG’s transparency may paradoxically improve athletes’ safety by allowing for informed management.

Orthopedic management

Preventive care

Given the mentioned MSK alterations, preventive care in enhanced athletes should be focused on accurate exposure capture, progressive load management, and evidence-based strengthening. A practical baseline should include time-stamped drug exposure (compound, dose, cycle length, and polypharmacy) alongside training-load metrics, with workloads managed toward a stable acute:chronic ratio.50 For athletes on prescription testosterone, endocrine guidelines should be followed, checking hematocrit at baseline, at 3 months, then annually, and holding or adjusting therapy if hematocrit reaches >54%.37 Heightened vigilance should be maintained for venous thromboembolism in the early months after initiation.36

Furthermore, there may be a role for prehabilitation in the setting of PED, aimed at increasing tendon loading capacity and addressing muscle imbalances to proactively reduce the risk of injury. Despite the lack of specific prehabilitation protocols for those who use PEDs, literature has noted prehabilitation as a potentially effective strategy to prevent tendon rupture in athletics.51Finally, screening should prioritize functional baselines and symptom surveillance, reserving imaging for prior injury or new focal signs.

Athlete education regarding the risks of PED can encourage awareness of sequelae, potentially leading to earlier diagnosis, treatment, and better outcomes. This may also improve patient awareness of safe usage practices and prevent injuries. Increased overall athlete education on PED may encourage those who use PED to consult their physicians and allow for early, informed management before injury occurs.

Care during the event

Rapid access to imaging and surgical intervention may be important to address tendon ruptures, muscle tears, and stress fractures, which are more prevalent in PED users due to altered tissue remodeling and increased pain tolerance. Prior literature has highlighted potential roles for point-of-care ultrasound to be an effective radiation-free method to evaluate morphological and mechanical properties of muscles in the context of elite sports.52,53 Additionally, there may be a lower threshold for imaging in PED users due to increased injury risk and atypical presentations.38,54 Wearable devices offer another option to provide real-time workload data to flag acute spikes, fatigue signatures, or compensatory patterns that warrant removal from play and targeted assessments.55

Increased awareness of polypharmacy should be taken if medication is given in acute and subacute care settings. Windfeld-Mathiasen et al 45 noted that users of PED have a higher rate of poisonings, including medicinal poisonings. Given the wide variety of anabolic agents, stimulants, analgesics, and more that may be used concurrently, orthopedic care teams should closely coordinate with primary care and internal medicine clinicians for safer analgesia, anesthesia, surgery, and postoperative planning. Furthermore, institutional protocols (e.g., venous thromboembolism and infection prevention) should be applied consistently and adjusted to the athlete’s verified exposure profile.

Post-event care

Risks associated with PED use do not cease after competition, with evidence of MSK alterations even years after cessation.54 Longitudinal follow-up is essential to monitor adverse outcomes, including tendon or ligament injuries. While there are limited guidelines on how protocols should be modified at this time, follow-up schedules and testing should be tailored to individuals due to the variety of effects associated with different PEDs.

Despite the knowledge of the possible influence of PED on the MSK system, the rehabilitation protocols for EG athletes have not been investigated sufficiently.56-58 In the event of injury, programs should individualize load progression to respect slower tendon adaptation, monitor for delayed-onset tendon pain/weakness, and use function-based return-to-play criteria rather than time alone. Surgical timing, postoperative thromboembolism risk management, analgesia choices, and reloading milestones may require adjustment to the athlete’s verified exposure profile. Abrupt halting of AAS use can cause withdrawal effects, including depression, anhedonia, and libido dysfunction.54,59 Thus, athletes may choose to gradually taper off such substances.57 Long-term follow-up data show that musculoskeletal, endocrine, and psychiatric effects of AAS may extend for years after use, making follow-up necessary to continue well past the end of such competitions.54,60 Overall, given the unique risk profiles present in those who use PED, individualized approaches are encouraged throughout the entire care process.

Research and policy implications

The EG initiates a unique opportunity to study the MSK consequences of sanctioned PED in a controlled setting. Policymakers, sport governing bodies, and healthcare institutions should leverage the environment to conduct unprecedented orthopedic research and develop standardized, optimized protocols for enhanced athletes. Thus, developing international athlete registries for those using performance-enhancing substances with prospective follow-up is recommended, coupled to (1) standardized injury/illness surveillance, (2) serial health-problem monitoring, and (3) linkage to imaging, surgical, and return-to-play data. These comprehensive datasets could help clarify relationships between performance enhancement and orthopedic outcomes. Furthermore, comparative analysis with regulated athletes may help refine understandings of true risks as well as potential short- and long-term orthopedic benefits of PED. Crucially, research efforts should not only aim to advance orthopedic understanding but should also apply findings to assist the EG in the optimization of athlete health and safety protocols.

As such, the EG presents a valuable opportunity in orthopedic and medical research to study both performance and safety in regulated and unregulated competitions. Rather than viewing the EG as a controversy, this unique event should be approached as an opportunity to collect data, study unique physiological adaptations, and develop evidence-based strategies for injury prevention, surgical management, and rehabilitation. The knowledge gained from such unprecedented research can strengthen care protocols across all levels of sport.

Conclusion

The EG, while planned to entertain, might be harmful to the participants, but the clinical challenge it highlights already exists across sport. The war on drugs in sport goes on, and athletes in both unregulated and WADA-regulated athletic competitions are and will likely continue to use performance-enhancing substances. Therefore, the orthopedic community should prioritize preparedness over prohibition alone. A harm-reduction framework offers the best path to protect health in enhanced settings while generating generalizable knowledge that improves musculoskeletal safety across all levels of sport.
