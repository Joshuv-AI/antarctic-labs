Reply of the Author: “Unexplained” does not mean, “not understood”
Authors: Richard J Paulson
Published: 2026

The response to the “unexplained infertility” editorial (1) is much appreciated. Clearly, we share an interest in this diagnosis. It is easy to agree that there is considerable variability in how couples understand that diagnosis, which highlights one of the fundamental problems with the imprecise designation, “unexplained infertility.”

However, I cannot agree that the primary cause of unexplained infertility remains an enigma, and I must reaffirm my conclusion that it is primarily a mechanical problem (1). If this were not the case, fertility-enhancing procedures that merely circumvent the mechanics of egg and sperm coming together would not be successful. As noted in my editorial, the gamete intra-fallopian transfer (GIFT) procedure was often used in the past as an effective therapy for unexplained infertility (2). In GIFT, the gametes are simply juxtaposed in the transfer catheter and then injected into the end of the fallopian tube. There is no attempt to enhance gamete function in the laboratory, no modification of the uterine environment, and no correction of hormonal or immune factors. Rather, it represents a simple mechanical bypass of the processes that normally bring eggs and sperm together in the ampulla of the fallopian tube. If unexplained infertility involved factors beyond a mechanical failure of gamete interaction, this procedure would arguably not succeed.

Currently, in vitro fertilization (IVF) is the most effective therapy for unexplained infertility. Its primary function, like that of GIFT, is to bring eggs and sperm together, albeit in the laboratory. With the extensive data now available in national registries, it is reasonable to conclude that in vitro fertilization outcomes in patients with unexplained infertility are comparable to those in patients with clearly defined mechanical causes, such as blocked or absent fallopian tubes. If couples with unexplained infertility have the same likelihood of conception as those with overt tubal factor infertility, there is little justification for invoking additional, poorly defined factors to explain their lack of conception.

The conclusion that unexplained infertility is a mechanical problem is therefore not based on the desire to simplify the clinical situation or to pin the potential causes to a single factor. It is not speculation, but rather a conclusion based on decades of observations. The problem with cultivating the uncertainty of the diagnosis is that this approach opens the door to hypothetical and unproven “causes,” which then result in the marketing of unproven therapies. Examples of this effect abound and range from the sales of fertility supplements (3) to the testing of sperm DNA fragmentation, infertility immunology testing, the use of platelet-rich plasma as well as many others. As long as we call it “unexplained,” we leave room for speculation; when we recognize it as mechanical, we return to evidence.
