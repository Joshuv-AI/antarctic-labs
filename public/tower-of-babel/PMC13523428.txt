Development and implementation of a new electroconvulsive therapy service line at a major medical center
Authors: Kevin Fitzmartin, Zakir Khan, Christopher James, Adeel A. Faruki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElectroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is a highly effective and standardized psychiatric treatment, yet the operational development of a new ECT service line within large academic medical centers remains complex and underrepresented in the literature. The expansion of inpatient psychiatric services at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus in 2023 created an opportunity to develop a new ECT service line. This article describes the multidisciplinary planning, implementation, early challenges, and optimization of this service, with the goal of providing a practical framework for other institutions.MethodsA multidisciplinary team of psychiatrists, anesthesiologists, nurses, pharmacists, and information technology staff collaboratively designed and implemented the ECT service. Planning included site observations at peer institutions, development of staffing models, medication management strategies, emergency protocols, and electronic medical record optimization. Standardized workflows for pre-procedure assessment, intra-procedure management, and recovery were established and refined. This descriptive study reports operational processes and early outcomes; no statistical analyses were performed.ResultsThe ECT service was successfully launched and sustained over two years, supported by a core group of anesthesiologists, advanced practice providers, and nursing staff. Key challenges included inconsistent patient volume affecting staffing efficiency, ethical and legal complexities surrounding informed consent, and the management of patients with significant comorbidities, including pregnancy and cardiovascular disease. Targeted adaptations, such as revised staffing strategies, development of serial anesthesia consent processes, and enhanced interdisciplinary communication, improved operational efficiency and patient safety. Ongoing education, simulation training, and protocol development further supported service growth and quality improvement.ConclusionsThe establishment of a new ECT service line within a large academic medical center is feasible but requires extensive multidisciplinary collaboration, flexible workflow design, and continuous quality improvement. This article provides operational guidance for institutions seeking to develop similar services, with implications for improving access to safe, effective ECT and advancing patient-centered psychiatric care.

Background

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) remains one of the safest and most effective psychiatric treatments, particularly for individuals with severe and treatment-refractory psychiatric illnesses such as major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and catatonia. This procedure introduces an electrical current through the brain to induce a brief, controlled seizure. While the exact mechanism behind ECT remains incompletely understood, the neuroplasticity hypothesis suggests that the efficacy of treatment is linked to neuronal proliferation and the development of new neural networks [1–3]. Further research suggests that ECT acts as a modulator for neurotransmitter activity (e.g. glutamate, GABA, serotonin) in networks that are commonly dysfunctional in major psychiatric illnesses [4]. Since its introduction in the 1930s, ECT has evolved from a traditionally uncontrolled intervention performed without anesthesia termed “unmodified” ECT to a contemporary “modified” ECT that utilizes general anesthesia, neuromuscular blockade, and continuous monitoring. This refinement has maximized therapeutic effects of electrical stimulation while also ensuring the safety of the patient [4, 5]. Today, ECT is widely considered a highly standardized, controlled procedure by the American Psychiatric Association and other major psychiatric organizations around the world [1–6]. Despite the abundance of literature on ECT’s clinical significance, the establishment of an ECT service line within a large academic medical center remains a complex venture and published literature on the operationalization of a new ECT service line remains limited. Establishing this requires the integration of anesthesiology, psychiatry, nursing, and pharmacology towards the establishing of multidisciplinary workflows, staffing models, medication management systems, emergency protocols, and information technology. Because ECT bridges perioperative medicine into psychiatric treatment, its launch as a new service line proposes unique logistical challenges.

At the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus, the expansion of existing psychiatric services to a new inpatient medical tower in 2023 unveiled the opportunity to introduce a new ECT service line. In this article, we describe the process taken by the Departments of Anesthesiology and Psychiatry, alongside Nursing, Pharmacy, and Information Technology (IT), to develop, implement, and optimize this new service line. This article highlights the planning phase, early challenges, preliminary outcomes, and future directions. We aim to contribute insightful operational guidance to an area with minimal existing literature to provide a framework that other institutions can utilize for safe and effective ECT service line development. This manuscript adheres to the applicable EQUATOR guidelines [7].

Methods

Service design

The development of the new service line started with monthly, multidisciplinary stakeholder meetings with representatives from the Departments of Psychiatry, Anesthesiology, and Nursing at the University of Colorado. Since laws and regulations for ECT vary by state, the discussions at these meetings adhered to the requirements specific to the state of Colorado. In the preliminary planning phase, operations were observed at UCHealth Longs Peak Hospital and peer medical universities were consulted to better understand their approaches. While many established ECT services function with a single anesthesiologist, we recognized that having two anesthesia personnel can be beneficial, though it is neither required nor standard practice. All procedures were overseen by an anesthesiologist and paired with a second provider responsible for airway management. These providers ranged from a resident, a certified registered nurse anesthetist (CRNA), an anesthesiologist assistant (AA), a respiratory therapist (RT), or a registered nurse (RN). After assessing these staffing models, we selected either an advanced practice provider (APP), such as a CRNA or AA, or a resident to partner with an anesthesiologist [8–10].

As this was a new service at the University of Colorado, ECT was considered a novel practice to majority of the staff members, unless they had prior experience at another institution. Therefore, anesthesia staff with previous ECT experience initially volunteered for coverage of the service, and participation in the ECT team was met without stigma or reservation among staff members. A core group of approximately eight anesthesiologists and fifteen APPs was established to serve as the primary staffing pool for the new service line. To support continuity and streamline the development of workflows, residents were not assigned to ECT for the first six months of the program establishment.

Along with the notable differences in staffing models, medication stocking strategies also varied across the centers. After consultation with hospital pharmacy leadership, an in-room automated medication dispensing system was chosen to facilitate rapid case turnover and reduce medication handoffs between providers and pharmacists. Utilizing the automated medication dispensing system allowed for reduced wastage of controlled substances through real-time inventory tracking, securing access to controls, and automated documentation—further enhancing efficiency [11]. As a team, the commonly used medications for ECT were identified and approved to be stocked, including methohexital, ketamine, propofol, succinylcholine, ondansetron, and glycopyrrolate, as well as medications for emergency scenarios [12]. Succinylcholine, a depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent, is frequently used as the muscle relaxant for ECT due to its rapid-onset and short-acting effects. However, it can act as a potent trigger for malignant hyperthermia (MH) so a strategy to address this rare but devastating complication was made. If MH is suspected, a MH cart containing dantrolene, as well as other supportive medications would be retrieved and administered appropriately by the charge anesthesiologist. Methohexital, a commonly used anesthetic agent for ECT induction, presents a unique challenge for pharmacists to stock 200 mg syringe aliquots in the automated medication dispensing system and track expiration times [12, 13].

In order to ensure success, multi-disciplinary meetings with the anesthesia technicians and equipment managers were conducted. These discussions focused on identifying plans for equipment maintenance, storage, and responsibilities for stocking supplies. In case of emergency, the Psychiatry Department purchased an onsite video laryngoscope and a dedicated ultrasound for the ECT suite for patients with difficult IV access.

Another necessary measure to becoming operational was optimizing the electronic medical record (EMR) through the collaboration of the vendor’s system analysts and the department representatives from psychiatry, anesthesiology and nursing. As ECT cases require quick turnover, a system tailored to the care provided during ECT is essential to allow for efficiency. Similarly, their support was needed to set up significantly simplified notes and case scheduling templates in the EMR to ensure proper scheduling alongside the anesthesia team.

A specific area of concern was access to the ECT suite, as it was attached to a locked inpatient psychiatry unit. The additional locked door required badge access. Initially, access to the ECT suite was restricted to those directly working on the service, with members added individually to reduce the risk of unwanted patient elopements. The strict access requirements of the procedural space potentially limited emergency medical response via the Rapid Response and Code Teams. To address this issue, a large cohort of providers were granted access to this traditionally restricted behavioral health space prior to opening the service. Without multi-disciplinary planning sessions with each department’s involvement, facilitating such a change would not have been feasible.

Because many team members were inexperienced with ECT, a foundational protocol was instated based on current literature [5, 6, 8, 9]. This protocol included pre-procedural considerations, common medication usage with recommended dosage ranges, and a general sequence of events for treatments. At the initiation of this program, the University of Colorado’s robust pre-anesthesia testing (PAT) clinic was utilized to support patient safety and optimize perioperative planning. Although not a universal approach, all new outpatient ECT patients were referred to the PAT clinic prior to starting treatment, where any concerns were directly communicated to the anesthesiologist serving as ECT lead. For inpatient and time-sensitive cases, the anesthesia team promptly screened patients to determine their eligibility for ECT. This holistic approach provided a structured and adaptable framework for the early phases of the program (Figs. 1 and 2).

Workflow

Workflow development for this new service was a dynamic process, with constant adaptation toward goals of improvement and efficiency. Upon arrival, patients checked in at the front desk and were directed to the waiting room. During this period, patients were promptly escorted by a registered nurse to a pre-procedural bay, where they completed the pre-procedure assessment and interview, as well as establishing intravenous access. Then, patients filled out the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a nine-item self-reported screening questionnaire used to evaluate depression severity [14]. Following this, the psychiatry team conducted their interview and obtained consent for the procedure. Lastly, the anesthesia team met with the patient to perform their assessment and obtain anesthesia consent. This structure ensures that all clinical, psychiatric, and anesthetic evaluations are completed before moving the patient into the treatment room to reduce the risk of the patient feeling time pressured during the consent process [15, 16].

Once the patient is ready for treatment, a nurse accompanies each patient throughout the procedure and into the recovery room. Over time, this evolved into designated nursing roles in three distinct settings: pre-procedure, intra-procedure, and post-procedure. In the treatment room, a procedural timeout was conducted to confirm the patient, procedural technique, allergies, concerns, and any changes to the treatment. The patient was pre-oxygenated via bag mask valve while two channels of electroencephalogram (EEG) leads are placed by the psychiatry team in a fronto-mastoid montage [10, 12, 13]. After induction, the nurse inflated the blood pressure cuff on one ankle to 20% higher than the patient’s initial systolic blood pressure. This was done to serve as a tourniquet to monitor seizure activity on only one foot. Next, a paralytic was given with on-going hyperventilation via bag mask valve ventilation. This maneuver is commonly conducted in ECT cases to facilitate seizure induction and enhance quality and duration of ictal activity [10, 12, 13]. A foam bite block was also inserted shortly before stimulus delivery to protect oropharyngeal structures from masseter muscle activation damage. The bite block may be placed by any trained member of the anesthesia team, with preference given to the team member with the greatest experience in safe placement. Once adequate paralysis was achieved, the psychiatry team delivered an electrical stimulus. The convulsive seizure would be observed in the isolated foot with the applied tourniquet and EEG activity was monitored until the non-convulsive seizure terminated and post-ictal suppression was visualized. If there was concern that the patient may have a lower oxygen reserve, mask ventilation would proceed during the convulsive and non-convulsive seizure. Ideally, there would be no patient contact during the non-convulsive seizure to reduce motion artifact on EEG recordings. However, any oxygen desaturations would be avoided by early intervention and clear communication with the psychiatry team. In rare cases of prolonged seizure (defined as ictal activity observed for greater than two minutes), the psychiatry team would request pharmacologic seizure termination using midazolam or propofol boluses.

After verification of post-ictal suppression, the anesthesia team would control and assist the patient’s airway until respiratory drive returned. When adequate breathing is restored, the patient is transported to recovery. To expedite turnover, the anesthesiologist and APP would waste the controlled substances in the in-room automated medication dispensing system prior to leaving the room. Initially, nursing utilized the same bay for patients both pre- and post-procedure. However, it was later determined to be more efficient to designate separate bay areas for pre-procedure and post-procedure patients (Fig. 3).

Results

After two years of implementing this new service, we have navigated numerous adaptations and challenges. One of the most significant obstacles has been establishing a pool of consistent patients receiving maintenance ECT, as our daily census was heavily reliant on new referrals and consults. Due to this inconsistency in patient volume, anesthesia staffing was increasingly difficult and led to inefficient use of resources. There were periods of time when an anesthesiologist and an APP would be assigned for only a few cases per day. Unlike established programs with large maintenance therapy populations that provide consistent volume, new programs do not yet have this stability. To address this issue, a team was created for add-on off-site cases after the completion of the ECT day. As the caseloads have grown and become more consistent, we are now preparing for the next stage of development and working towards sustaining a full day’s census of cases. To ensure long-term sustainability and viability, the hospital has committed to financially supporting the program during the initial ramp-up period, including appropriate compensation.

Another ongoing challenge in integrating this new service has been navigating the ethical and legal dilemmas of administering ECT to patients who are unable to provide informed consent due to the severity of their illness. State laws governing ECT vary widely; however, many jurisdictions, including Colorado, require court authorization for patients who lack decision-making capacity—even when an available and willing power of attorney (POA) exists [17, 18]. These court orders often do not address anesthesia-specific risks. While this omission is not problematic when a reliable POA is available to consent for anesthesia, there have been instances in which the POA could not be reached on the day of the procedure. This situation creates a dilemma in which psychiatry possesses the legal authority to proceed with ECT, yet anesthesia lacks the necessary consent to administer care. To address this gap, the Department of Anesthesiology developed a serial ECT anesthesia consent form in collaboration with legal counsel. Although this approach improves consistency, it still requires consent from an authorized individual—an option that is not always available. In cases where no surrogate decision-maker has been appointed and psychiatry is operating under a court order; early and proactive interdisciplinary communication is essential to ensure optimal patient care. It is also common practice to involve an ethics consultation in these circumstances to determine whether appointing an independent physician to provide consent is appropriate. Despite these challenges, we continue to work with regional jurisdictions to improve court-ordered ECT documentation by incorporating anesthesia-specific language, with the goal of supporting a safer and more efficient patient experience.

Unique clinical scenarios have also required exceptional communication and coordination to manage effectively. For example, we have treated obstetric patients in their third trimester who requested to continue their maintenance ECT. To complete this procedure and prioritize the safety of the patient, close collaboration with the obstetrics team was required [19]. Other complex scenarios have included transfer patients from the children’s hospital for catatonia with minimal response to high-dose benzodiazepines, as well as patients on the neurology and medicine services with concerns for catatonia when an ECT trial was deemed appropriate. Some patients have presented with more serious cardiovascular comorbidities requiring detailed discussions among consulting teams to assist in risk-stratification, optimization, and timing of procedures [20, 21]. In each of these scenarios, a timely specialist consultation was crucial in shaping individualized and effective treatment plans.

With the increasing use of GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-RAs) in the United States, it became apparent that we needed a management approach for these medications due to the associated increased aspiration risk. During our rollout, recommendations regarding perioperative GLP-RA use and anesthesia were continuously evolving, shifting away from the initial standard advice to hold these medications before anesthesia. The emerging consensus favors applying clinical judgement to airway management rather than uniformly withholding the drug [22, 23]. This presents a unique challenge for ECT, where mask ventilation without a secured airway is standard practice, unlike general operating room procedures where rapid-sequence intubation is used more readily for patients with aspiration risk. To tackle this obstacle, a collaborative, multi-disciplinary approach was initiated with all stakeholders, including the ECT anesthesia team, PAT anesthesia team, and the psychiatry team. All parties worked together to develop clear, consistent recommendations for ECT patients on GLP-RAs based on the most current literature and information available.

Prior to starting ECT, cessation of GLP-RA therapy is considered through a shared decision-making process involving the patient, prescribing clinician, ECT anesthesia team, and psychiatry team. For patients who continue GLP-RA therapy without severe gastrointestinal symptoms, our pathway recommends a 24-hour clear liquid diet and pre-anesthesia gastric ultrasound before the first treatment. If gastric ultrasound is negative, subsequent treatments may proceed with a minimum 18-hour clear liquid diet, with repeat ultrasound after any reduction in fasting duration. Patients with severe symptoms, gastroparesis, prior aspiration, or retained gastric contents require individualized planning, including consideration of extended fasting, interruption of GLP-RA therapy, rapid sequence induction, or rescheduling at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist in collaboration with the patient and treating psychiatrist. These recommendations apply to patients in the maintenance phase of GLP-RA therapy and require local expertise with gastric ultrasound.

Education has played a central role in the rollout and expansion of the ECT service. Initially, efforts were focused on training the core team, especially those not familiar with ECT. To support these efforts, a reference protocol was developed onto the department’s intranet, along with a quick-reference pathway uploaded to the electronic medical record. Simulation exercises, such as mock codes and mock malignant hyperthermia crises, were conducted to enhance preparation for emergencies.

Early in the implementation process, the lead psychiatrist and lead anesthesiologist delivered a joint Grand Rounds presentation to the anesthesia department in order to establish a joint understanding of ECT practice. Moving forward, the ECT service will participate with the remainder of the department’s services at an annual morbidity and mortality (M&M) conference to ensure continued learning, innovation, and quality improvement.

Discussion

As the ECT service continues to expand, strategic planning will prioritize optimizing operational efficiency and enhancing patient-centered care. Key considerations include refining the daily schedule to balance case volume with adequate staffing, potentially incorporating flexible break structures to maintain continuous operations without compromising staff well-being. Nursing roles will be further defined to streamline pre-procedural, intra-procedural, and post-procedural responsibilities, ensuring seamless patient transitions and maximizing resource utilization. Additionally, ongoing education initiatives, including regular simulation-based training for emergency scenarios and updated protocols for managing complex patient comorbidities (e.g., GLP-RA use or high-risk cardiovascular conditions), will strengthen team preparedness [21–23]. Collaboration with other departments, such as obstetrics, will continue to expand to better accommodate unique patient needs [20]. A commitment to continuous quality improvement will guide these efforts, fostering a sustainable service that prioritizes safety, efficacy, and patient-centered care.

Conclusion

Establishing a new service within a major university health system is a rare and complex endeavor. The success of this initiative highlights the critical role of clear communication, robust collaboration, and a multi-disciplinary approach. By fostering ongoing evaluation and adaptation of processes, the ECT program has achieved significant growth and operational refinement. This dedication to continuous improvement—coupled with strong stakeholder engagement and a shared commitment to outstanding patient care—will drive the sustained development and future success of the ECT service, ultimately enhancing outcomes and access for the patients who depend on it most.
