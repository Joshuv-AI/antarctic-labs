Abstract
We extend shapelets for the analysis of galaxy images to be available in a phase space, introducing Wigner Function Shapelets (WFS) . Whereas conventional shapelets expand images separately in configuration or Fourier space using Hermite–Gaussian or Laguerre–Gaussian modes, WFS represents images directly in the four-dimensional phase space with symplectic group Sp ⁡ ( 4 , ℝ ) \mathrm{Sp}(4,\mathbb{R}) , which is quantised by a phase-space cell 2 ​ π ​ λ̄ 2\pi\lambdabar that determines a resolution limit of a telescope. WFS consists of a bilinear form of the cross-Wigner function of the Laguerre-Gaussian modes as an orthogonal and complete basis for the Wigner function of an image, carrying out SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) irreducible representations of the phase space with the Hopf tori. We introduce a scalar function 𝒲 k ​ ℓ ​ ( Q 0 , Q 2 ) \mathcal{W}_{k\ell}(Q_{0},Q_{2}) from the U ⁡ ( 1 ) × U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U}(1)\times\mathrm{U}(1) - covariant tori to a two-dimensional space of constants of motion ( Q 0 , Q 2 ) (Q_{0},Q_{2}) —the harmonic energy and axial angular momentum—thereby yielding a natural phase–space “band structure”, given a pair of winding number ( k , ℓ ) ∈ ℤ 2 (k,\ell)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2} . WFS leverage key properties of the Wigner function for image analysis: (i) it encodes full information of an image in a symmetry-preserving way; (ii) its trasport equation naturally involves with a Liouville equation at λ̄ → 0 \lambdabar\rightarrow 0 ; (iii) it admits positive/negative oscillatory patterns on ( Q 0 , Q 2 ) (Q_{0},Q_{2}) plane that can be sensitive spatial coherent structure of galaxy morphology and cosmological imprints; and (iv) systematics and noise can be manipulated as a quantum channel operation. This paper aims to bring all the formulae related to the Wigner function in the context of astrophysics and cosmology, formally organising in both terminologies of astronomy and of quantum information theory.

II
Wigner function of galaxy images

Our theme is to analyse a galaxy image in phase space with the Wigner function, thereby extracting morphological information beyond what is accessible in configuration or Fourier space separately. In this section we define the Wigner function for an image field of a measured galaxy and summarise its essential mathematical properties given the Wigner function. We clarify how the Wigner function is related to the conventional astrophysical statistics, systematics, and noises.

II.3
Systematics and noise in Wigner function

We introduce systematics and noises on a galaxy image in terms of the Wigner function. Respecting that the Wigner function is the phase-space representation of an image state

\hat{\rho}
, we describe systematics and noises with corresponding algebraic operations in quantum information theory. To begin with this subsection, we provide a generic operation that characterises systematics and noises. Then we examplify the two typical cases divided by whether the PSF is deterministic or stochastic.

A physical process acting on a state
\hat{\rho}
is represented by a
completely positive trace–preserving (CPTP) map,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:\ \hat{\rho}\ \longmapsto\ \mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}).

(51)

Any CPTP map admits a Kraus representation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{a}\hat{K}_{a}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{K}_{a}^{\dagger},\qquad\sum_{a}\hat{K}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{K}_{a}=\hat{\mathbb{I}},

(52)

where
\{\hat{K}_{a}\}
are Kraus operators.
The representation may involve a discrete or continuous index. In particular, the continuous case is define as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})=\int{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu}\hat{K}(z)\hat{\rho}(z)\hat{K}^{\dagger}(z)\,,\ \int{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu}\hat{K}(z)\hat{K}^{\dagger}(z)=\hat{\mathbb{I}}\,.

(53)

Hereafter, we do not mention whether the operation is discrite or continuous except it should be in necessity.

Given two CPTP maps
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, their product is
defined as composition,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{E}_{2}\circ\mathcal{E}_{1})(\hat{\rho})\equiv\mathcal{E}_{2}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{1}(\hat{\rho})\right).

(54)

This product is associative,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3}\circ(\mathcal{E}_{2}\circ\mathcal{E}_{1})=(\mathcal{E}_{3}\circ\mathcal{E}_{2})\circ\mathcal{E}_{1},

(55)

and closed on the space of CPTP maps.
In general, CPTP maps are not invertible, so the resulting structure is a
semigroup rather than a group.
The product of the two CPTP map is represented with the Kraus operators as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{a}\hat{K}_{a}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{K}_{a}^{\dagger},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{2}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{b}\hat{L}_{b}\,\hat{\rho}\,\hat{L}_{b}^{\dagger},

(56)

then their product is

\displaystyle(\mathcal{E}_{2}\circ\mathcal{E}_{1})(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{a,b}(\hat{L}_{b}\hat{K}_{a})\,\hat{\rho}\,(\hat{L}_{b}\hat{K}_{a})^{\dagger},

(57)

so that the composed channel has Kraus operators

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{ba}=\hat{L}_{b}\hat{K}_{a}.

(58)

For any observable
\hat{O}
, the action of a channel on expectation values
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho})\,\hat{O}\right]=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\hat{\rho}\,\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\hat{O})\right],

(59)

where
\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}
denotes the adjoint (Heisenberg–picture) map,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\hat{O})=\sum_{a}\hat{K}_{a}^{\dagger}\,\hat{O}\,\hat{K}_{a}.

(60)

This duality underlies the phase–space representation of channels used in
subsequent sections.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm PSF}(\hat{\rho})

Let us remark what is relevant to our study. We make use of the feature that the Wigner function is real but not everywhere positive; unlike a probability density, an intensity map, or a Fourier power spectrum, it shows sign-oscillations and structured zero sets that encode interference at the phase–space resolution scale set by
\lambdabar

III.3
Hopf fibration of Wigner function

We introduce a representation of the phase-space coordinate
(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})
via the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
-invariant subspace labeled by the a pair of
U
(1) invariants
(Q_{0},Q_{2})
, i.e., energy and angular momentum. We construct a compact band map that preserves the essential symplectic content while encapsulating the full four-dimensional information. This band structure exposes the sign-alternating oscillations and the web of zeros (interference) of the Wigner function
W(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})
, and we use it in the next section to construct morphology diagnostics directly from the
(Q_{0},Q_{2})
map.

The pair
(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})
therefore parametrises an invariant
two–torus
T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}\simeq U(1)\times U(1)
, i.e., a Hopf torus, embedded in the normalised phase space. Given the coordinate with the Hopf spinor
z
, we obtain several properties of the Hopf torus as follows:

Disjointness (foliation by tori).

For
(Q_{0},Q_{2})\neq(Q_{0}^{\prime},Q_{2}^{\prime})
,

\displaystyle T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}\cap T_{Q_{0}^{\prime},Q_{2}^{\prime}}=\varnothing,

(113)

since a point
z\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
cannot satisfy two distinct sets of level–set
conditions for
(|u|^{2},|v|^{2})
.

Torus harmonics as
U(1)
gauge modes.

On
T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}
the residual symmetry is
U(1)\times U(1)
acting as

(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})\mapsto(\phi_{u}+\alpha,\phi_{v}+\beta)
.
The corresponding characters (torus harmonics) are

\displaystyle\chi_{k\ell}(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})=e^{\,i(k\phi_{u}+\ell\phi_{v})},\qquad k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}.

(114)

Along the Hopf fibre
(\alpha=\beta)
,
\chi_{k\ell}
carries a gauge charge

k+\ell
; the diagonal sector
k+\ell=0
is fibre–invariant and probes base
(shape) structure at fixed
(Q_{0},Q_{2})
, while
k+\ell\neq 0
resolves coherence
along the fibre.

Averaging on the Hopf torus.

Let
d\sigma
denote the induced area element on
T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}
.
The natural normalised average of a function
f
over the torus is

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}}:=2\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi_{u}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi_{v}}{2\pi}\;f,

(115)

so that
\langle 1\rangle_{T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}}=2
.
Since the level sets of
(Q_{0},Q_{2})
are precisely the Hopf tori
T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}
,
the coarea formula yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\!f\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})=(2\pi\lambdabar)^{2}\int^{\infty}_{0}\!\mathrm{d}Q_{0}\int^{+Q_{0}}_{-Q_{0}}\!\mathrm{d}Q_{2}\;\langle f\rangle_{T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}}.

(116)

Then we define the Wigner–torus coefficients labelled by
(k,\ell)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}(Q_{0},Q_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\langle W\chi^{*}_{k\ell}\rangle_{T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}}\,.

(117)

In particular,
\mathcal{W}_{00}(Q_{0},Q_{2})=\langle W\rangle_{T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}}
and this obeys the normalisation rule

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\!W\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{\theta},\bm{p}),

(118)

\displaystyle=(2\pi\lambdabar)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}Q_{0}\int_{-Q_{0}}^{+Q_{0}}\!\mathrm{d}Q_{2}\;\mathcal{W}_{00}(Q_{0},Q_{2}),

(119)

namely, the Wigner function is the torus zero–mode.
On each Hopf torus, there is a series expansion of the Wigner function as

\displaystyle W(Q_{0},Q_{2},\phi_{u},\phi_{v})=\sum_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}(Q_{0},Q_{2})\,\chi_{k\ell}(\phi_{u},\phi_{v}),

(120)

due to the orthogonality condition
\langle\chi_{k\ell}\chi_{k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}^{*}\rangle=2\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
.

Gauge invariance of
\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}

For fixed
(Q_{0},Q_{2})
, the phases
(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})\in[0,2\pi)^{2}
parametrize the
Hopf torus and generate a natural
\mathrm{U}(1)\times\mathrm{U}(1)
action

\displaystyle u\mapsto e^{i\alpha}u,\qquad v\mapsto e^{i\beta}v,

(121)

under which
(Q_{0},Q_{2})
are invariant. Then Eq. (
120
) carries the phase factor

e^{i(k\alpha+\ell\beta)}
which comes only from
\chi_{k\ell}
, leaving
\rho_{k\ell}
invariant under arbitrary phase shifts. In the WFS, the cross–Wigner function is not
invariant but transforms covariantly as a charged field on the torus:

\displaystyle W\big[\Psi^{\rm LG}_{j,s},\Psi^{\rm LG}_{j^{\prime},s^{\prime}}\big]\mapsto\ e^{i(\Delta_{u}\alpha+\Delta_{v}\beta)}\,W\big[\Psi^{\rm LG}_{j,s},\Psi^{\rm LG}_{j^{\prime},s^{\prime}}\big]\,,

(122)

i.e. its “gauge charges” are
(\Delta_{u},\Delta_{v})
.
Recalling that the cross-Wigner function of the LG modes,
we obtain the selection rule and the explicit radial closed form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\rm LG}_{k\ell;js}=R^{(1)}_{\scalebox{0.5}{$j+s,j+s-k$}}(\sqrt{Q_{0}{+}Q_{2}})\;R^{(1)}_{\scalebox{0.5}{$j-s,j-s-\ell$}}(\sqrt{Q_{0}{-}Q_{2}})\,.

(123)

Eqs. (
123
) provides an analytic
closed form of the Hopf–torus observables
\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}
in terms of associated
Laguerre polynomials, with the entire gauge (angular) structure reduced to the
Kronecker selection rule
(k,\ell)=(\Delta_{u},\Delta_{v})
.
This is equivalent to the condition that
(j,s)
must be within the domain
-j+{\rm max}(0,k)\leq m\leq j-{\rm max}(0,\ell)
given
(k,\ell)
, which originates from the constraint
-j^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime}\leq j^{\prime}
and the selection rule. One must be careful that the domain is null at
j<({\rm max}(0,k)+{\rm max}(0,\ell))/2
. Then Eq. (
120
) is represented as

\displaystyle W=\sum_{k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j\geq\frac{{\rm max}(0,k)+{\rm max}(0,\ell)}{2}}

(124)

\displaystyle\left\{\sum^{j-{\rm max}(0,\ell)}_{s=-j+{\rm max}(0,k)}c_{(j,s),(j-\frac{k+\ell}{2},s-\frac{k-\ell}{2})}\mathcal{W}^{\rm LG}_{k\ell;js}\,\chi_{k\ell}\right\}\,.

(125)

In Fig.
1
, we plot the first eighty-one combinations of the WFS in Eq. (
124
).
We find that the simultaneous flip:
n_{u}\mapsto n_{v}
and
n^{\prime}_{u}\mapsto n^{\prime}_{v}
provides the flip
Q_{2}\mapsto-Q_{2}
, exhibiting the chiral symmetry of the WFS.

After all, for a given pair of LG modes, a single torus harmonic carries all the
gauge–covariant phase information, while
\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}
packages it into a
gauge–invariant Hopf–torus observable.

Statistical isotropy and selection rule.

Statistical isotropy of the galaxy field means invariance of the Wigner function
under a common physical rotation

(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})\mapsto(R_{\alpha}\bm{\theta},R_{\alpha}\bm{p})
with

R_{\alpha}\in SO(2)
.
In the Hopf parametrisation this induces

\phi_{u}\mapsto\phi_{u}-\alpha
and
\phi_{v}\mapsto\phi_{v}+\alpha
,
while
(Q_{0},Q_{2})
remain invariant.
Hence isotropy implies

W(Q_{0},Q_{2},\phi_{u}-\alpha,\phi_{v}+\alpha)=W(Q_{0},Q_{2},\phi_{u},\phi_{v})
for all
\alpha
.
The torus harmonics transform as

\chi_{k\ell}(\phi_{u}-\alpha,\phi_{v}+\alpha)=\chi_{k\ell}(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})\,e^{i(\ell-k)\alpha}
,
so the Wigner–torus coefficients

\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}(Q_{0},Q_{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}\langle W\chi^{*}_{k\ell}\rangle_{T_{Q_{0},Q_{2}}}

can be nonzero only if this phase is trivial for all
\alpha
.
Therefore statistical isotropy enforces the diagonal selection rule

\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}(Q_{0},Q_{2})=0
for
k\neq\ell
,
with only the spin–neutral modes surviving; for an ensemble of
statistically isotropic fields this statement holds in expectation, while
individual realisations may exhibit small off–diagonal components due to
cosmic variance, masking, or noise.

Parity symmetry.

Parity invariance corresponds to an orientation–reversing transformation

\bm{\theta}\mapsto\mathcal{P}\bm{\theta}
with
\det\mathcal{P}=-1
, under which the
canonical complex variables transform as

\tilde{\theta}\mapsto-\tilde{\theta}
and
\tilde{p}\mapsto-\tilde{p}
.
With the definitions

u=(\tilde{\theta}^{*}+i\tilde{p}^{*})/\sqrt{2}
and
v=(\tilde{\theta}+i\tilde{p})/\sqrt{2}
,
this induces

(u,v)\mapsto(-u,-v)
and therefore

(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})\mapsto(\phi_{u}+\pi,\phi_{v}+\pi)
,
while the invariants
(Q_{0},Q_{2})
remain unchanged.
The torus harmonics transform as

\chi_{k\ell}(\phi_{u}+\pi,\phi_{v}+\pi)=\chi_{k\ell}(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})\,(-1)^{k+\ell}
,
so parity invariance of the Wigner function implies

\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}(Q_{0},Q_{2})=(-1)^{k+\ell}\,\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}(Q_{0},Q_{2}).

Hence all modes with odd
k+\ell
must vanish, while even
k+\ell
modes are
unconstrained.
When combined with statistical isotropy (which enforces
k=\ell
), only
even–
k
diagonal components
\mathcal{W}_{kk}
survive, and any odd–
k
or
off–diagonal contribution signals parity violation or residual systematics.

Chiral symmetry.

Chiral (handedness–reversing) symmetry corresponds to an orientation–reversing
operation in phase space that preserves the isotropic invariant
Q_{0}
while
flipping the pseudoscalar invariant
Q_{2}
.
This is equivalent to the exchange

(u,v)\mapsto(v,u)
and hence

(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})\mapsto(\phi_{v},\phi_{u})
, while

Q_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}(|u|^{2}+|v|^{2})\mapsto Q_{0},\qquad Q_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}(|u|^{2}-|v|^{2})\mapsto-Q_{2},

reflecting the sign flip of the pseudoscalar

Q_{2}
.
Under this transformation the torus harmonics satisfy

\chi_{k\ell}(\phi_{v},\phi_{u})=\chi_{\ell k}(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})
, so invariance of the
Wigner function implies the constraint

\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}(Q_{0},Q_{2})=\mathcal{W}_{\ell k}(Q_{0},-Q_{2}).

This relation provides a sharp selection rule for chiral symmetry: any
antisymmetric component under
(k,\ell)\leftrightarrow(\ell,k)
accompanied by

Q_{2}\rightarrow-Q_{2}
directly signals intrinsic chirality or pseudoscalar
contamination in the phase–space structure.

Let us mention the edge as described by the zeros of
(u,v)
.
(i) Boundary
|Q_{2}|=Q_{0}
.
The Hopf torus collapses to a single Hopf circle.
Although
\sqrt{Q_{0}^{2}-Q_{2}^{2}}\to 0
, the torus area shrinks at the same rate, so the
coarea decomposition remains finite for integrable
W
.
(ii) Apex
Q_{0}=Q_{2}=0
.
Here
u=v=0
and
S^{3}
collapses to a point.
This set has measure zero and does not contribute to the bounded
W
; if required,

\mathcal{W}_{00}
may be defined by a smooth limiting procedure.

III.4
Morphology modes and rotation modes

Let us recall two independent charge operators using a pair of Schwinger
harmonic oscillators
(\hat{a}_{u},\hat{a}_{v})
,

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{I}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\hat{a}_{u}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{u}+\hat{a}_{v}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{v}\right),

(127)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\hat{a}_{u}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{u}-\hat{a}_{v}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{v}\right),

(128)

which generate two commuting
\mathrm{U}(1)
transformations.
Their actions on the ladder operators are

\displaystyle e^{-i\alpha\hat{T}_{0}}\hat{a}_{u}e^{+i\alpha\hat{T}_{0}}

\displaystyle=e^{+i\alpha}\hat{a}_{u},\qquad e^{-i\alpha\hat{T}_{0}}\hat{a}_{v}e^{+i\alpha\hat{T}_{0}}=e^{+i\alpha}\hat{a}_{v},

(129)

\displaystyle e^{-i\beta\hat{T}_{2}}\hat{a}_{u}e^{+i\beta\hat{T}_{2}}

\displaystyle=e^{+i\beta}\hat{a}_{u},\qquad e^{-i\beta\hat{T}_{2}}\hat{a}_{v}e^{+i\beta\hat{T}_{2}}=e^{-i\beta}\hat{a}_{v}.

(130)

Since
[\hat{T}_{0},\hat{T}_{2}]=0
, the corresponding charges can be
simultaneously diagonalised.
The two charge modes generated by
\hat{T}_{J}=\exp{(iJ(\phi_{u}+\phi_{v})\hat{T}_{0})}
and
\hat{T}_{K}=\exp{(iK(\phi_{u}-\phi_{v})\hat{T}_{2})}
brings the independent charge states. We dub
J\ (J=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots)
and
K\ (K=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots)
as morphology modes and rotational modes, respectively. It is well known that the Fourier power spectrum, corresponding to the
marginal of the Wigner function over configuration space, retains only
even
J
modes while all
K
modes survive.
This reflects the fact that the Fourier power alone does not uniquely
determine the image, and that odd morphology modes require higher-order
configuration-space correlations.
The WFS formalism makes this structure explicit: for each mode,
the morphology and rotation charges are given by

J=\Delta_{u}+\Delta_{v}
and
K=\Delta_{u}-\Delta_{v}
.

The effect PSF blurring can be understood
most transparently in the Heisenberg picture, taking into account the morphology modes and the rotational modes. Following the formulation introduced in Sec.
II.3
, we describe the decoherence of the morphology and the rotational features of a galaxy image separately for whether the PSF is deterministic or stochastic.

III.4.1
Deterministic PSF

For a deterministic PSF acting coherently on the complex field amplitude,
the forward map is a single-operator channel

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{O}=\mathcal{E}_{\rm coh}(\hat{\rho}_{I})=\hat{K}\,\hat{\rho}_{I}\,\hat{K}^{\dagger},

(131)

where
\hat{K}=K_{\rm PSF}(\hat{\bm{p}})
is the optical transfer function
(OTF) acting multiplicatively in Fourier space, equivalently, a function of
the momentum operator
\hat{\bm{p}}
.
Charge preservation is therefore controlled by commutators with
\hat{K}
.

The morphology generator
\hat{T}_{0}
does not commute with
\hat{K}
in
general,

\displaystyle[\hat{T}_{0},\hat{K}]\neq 0,

(132)

reflecting the fact that coherent PSF blur induces mode coupling among
different morphology charges
J
.
For a Gaussian PSF,
\hat{K}\propto\exp(-\varepsilon\,\hat{p}^{\dagger}\hat{p})
,
this non-commutativity is governed by pair creation and annihilation
operators
\hat{a}_{u}\hat{a}_{v}
and
\hat{a}_{u}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{v}^{\dagger}
, and its
magnitude scales with the PSF width.

By contrast, the rotation generator
\hat{T}_{2}
is preserved iff the PSF is
isotropic. Since under rotations

\hat{p}\to e^{+i\beta}\hat{p}
, rotational covariance requires

\hat{K}=f(\hat{p}^{\dagger}\hat{p})
, equivalently

\tilde{K}_{\rm PSF}(\bm{p})=\tilde{K}_{\rm PSF}(|\bm{p}|)
.
Thus,

\displaystyle[\hat{T}_{2},\hat{K}]=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{isotropic PSF}.

(133)

Anisotropic coherent PSFs generically mix different rotation charges
K
.

III.4.2
Stochastic PSF

A translationally invariant PSF corresponds to a random displacement
channel,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle=\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\Delta\,K(\Delta)\,\hat{T}(\Delta)\hat{\rho}\hat{T}^{\dagger}(\Delta)

(134)

We introduce the second moments of the galaxy image as follows. Let
\bm{\theta}_{c}
be the centroid of the image intensity
I(\bm{\theta})=|\psi|^{2}
, and define the second moments as

\displaystyle\{\bm{\mathrm{Q}}\}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\theta}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{c,i})(\theta_{j}-\theta_{c,j})I\,}{\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\theta}I\,}\,,

(188)

The first way to determine
\sigma
is the method of the second-moment matching to a circular ground state of the LG modes, i.e., the Gaussian function
\exp{[-|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{c}|^{2}/\sigma^{2}]}
. We equalise
\sigma
to the scale
\sigma_{g}
that is computed from the trace of the second moments as

\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma_{g}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}({\bm{\mathrm{Q}}})}\,.

(189)

This definition becomes exact when the galaxy image is well approximated by a Gaussian profile. Note that this way of determining the image size has been widely used in the measurement of the cosmic shear
36
;
55
.

The second way to determine
\sigma
is to infer
\sigma
from the Ground–mode maximisation. Project
\psi
onto the orthonormal LG modes
\{\Psi^{\mathrm{LG}}_{j,s}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{c};\sigma)\}
up to order
J_{\max}
. We introduce the function of
\sigma
as

\displaystyle f_{00}(\sigma)=\frac{|\psi_{00}(\sigma)|^{2}}{\sum_{j\leq J_{\max}}\sum_{s=-j}^{j}|\psi_{j,s}(\sigma)|^{2}}.

(190)

We choose
\sigma_{\ast}=\arg\max_{\sigma>0}f_{00}(\sigma)
. For a nearly-Gaussian image
\sigma_{\ast}\simeq\sigma_{g}
, for structured morphology,
\sigma_{\ast}
adapts to concentrate power in the lowest modes, improving stability for truncated expansions.
Note that we prescribe the scale cut to the image, so that the whole analysis can compute the WFS in a stable way. To make sure of these requirements, we follow a scale cut with a top-hat selection function as

\displaystyle T(\bm{\theta})=\begin{cases}1\qquad|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{c}|<r_{\rm cut}\\
0\qquad|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{c}|\geq r_{\rm cut}\end{cases}

(191)

where
r_{\rm cut}=\alpha_{\rm cut}\sigma_{g}
(or alternatively
\alpha_{\rm cut}\sigma_{*}
). In practice,
\alpha_{\rm cut}\sim 3-5
to one galaxy image, isolating the image field from the diffused surroundings.

V.2
Choice of
\lambdabar

The parameter
\lambdabar
fixes the canonical relation between the image–plane angle
\bm{\theta}

and its conjugate momentum
\bm{p}
. In practice,
\lambdabar
sets the phase–space cell area, like
(2\pi\lambdabar^{2})
that you regard as one resolvable unit: smaller cells overfit noise,
larger cells smear morphology (spirals, bars, bulges). Most importantly,
\lambdabar
provides the quantisation scale of a galaxy image, where the LG eigenmodes take part, as well as the WFS. In what follows, we argue what
\lambdabar
should be.

Our Fourier convention is

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}(\bm{k})\;=\;\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\theta}\,\psi(\bm{\theta})\,e^{-i\,\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\theta}}\,,\qquad\bm{k}\;=\;\bm{p}/\lambdabar.

(192)

Let
\sigma_{\theta}
denote a characteristic angular resolution (e.g. PSF width) and

\sigma_{p}
is a characteristic half–bandwidth in
\bm{p}
–space from the optical transfer function (OTF), which is defined as the Fourier transform of the PSF as

\displaystyle K_{\rm OTF}(\bm{p}/\lambdabar)\equiv\int\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\theta}K_{\rm PSF}(\bm{\theta})\,e^{-\frac{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{\xi}}{\lambdabar}}\,.

(193)

For a reference Gaussian,
\sigma_{\theta}\sigma_{k}=1
with
\,\sigma_{k}
the width of
\tilde{\psi}(\bm{k})
,
hence
\sigma_{p}=\lambdabar\,\sigma_{k}
and

\displaystyle\lambdabar\;=\;\sigma_{\theta}\,\sigma_{p}\;\,.

(194)

Thus matching
\lambdabar
to your instrument/data amounts to matching the resolution
\times
bandwidth product.
In the absence of additional systematic blurring—such as atmospheric turbulence for ground-based telescopes—an astronomical image reaches the diffraction limit set by the finite aperture, where any astronomical images are inevitably unresolved.
For a circular pupil of diameter
D
, the incoherent PSF is given by the Airy pattern,

I(\theta)\propto\left[\frac{2J_{1}(x)}{x}\right]^{2},\qquad x\equiv\frac{\pi D}{\lambda_{\rm phys}}\,\theta,

where
J_{1}
is the Bessel function of the first kind.
The full width at half maximum (FWHM) is determined by the solution

[2J_{1}(x_{1/2})/x_{1/2}]^{2}=1/2
,
which yields
x_{1/2}\simeq 1.6163
and hence

{\rm FWHM}=2\theta_{1/2}=\frac{2x_{1/2}}{\pi}\frac{\lambda_{\rm phys}}{D}\simeq 1.028\,\frac{\lambda_{\rm phys}}{D}.

Approximating the Airy core by a Gaussian of equal FWHM then gives

\sigma_{\theta}=\frac{{\rm FWHM}}{2\sqrt{2\ln 2}}\simeq 0.437\,\frac{\lambda_{\rm phys}}{D}.

OTF of a clear circular pupil has compact support and vanishes for angular spatial frequencies larger than

\kappa_{c}\simeq\frac{2\pi D}{\lambda_{\rm phys}},

so a characteristic momentum-space bandwidth may be taken as

\sigma_{p}\simeq\kappa_{c}/2=\pi D/\lambda_{\rm phys}.

The corresponding phase–space area is therefore

\displaystyle\lambdabar\;\equiv\;\sigma_{\theta}\,\sigma_{p}\;\simeq\;0.437\,\pi\;\simeq\;1.37\quad(\mathcal{O}(1)),

(195)

demonstrating that a diffraction-limited image naturally occupies a phase–space cell of order unity. In real observations, however, other systematics add to the diffraction limits, making the image smoother. In what follows, we write down several systematics:

Seeing–limited.

The image is further blurred by atmospheric turbulence for ground-based telescopes, reaching the seeing limit. In this case, one should take
\sigma_{\theta}
from the measured seeing FWHM and
\sigma_{p}
from the empirical modulation transfer function (MTF) half–power
or effective support; set
\lambdabar=\sigma_{\theta}\sigma_{p}
.

Radio interferometry.

Radio interferometry overcomes the atmospheric seeing limit by observing at long wavelengths and by synthesising an effective aperture from widely separated antennas.
In this case, however, the measured sky modes are no longer continuous: the interferometer samples the Fourier plane only at discrete baseline vectors, leading to the well-known problem of incomplete
uv
coverage
(
88
;
26
)
.
For an array with maximum projected baseline
b_{\max}
, the synthesised beam sets the characteristic angular resolution

\sigma_{\theta}\sim\lambda_{\rm phys}/{b_{\max}},

up to a numerical factor that depends on the detailed baseline distribution and weighting scheme.
In Fourier space, the sampled visibility function has compact support bounded by the longest baseline, implying a cutoff in angular spatial frequency

\kappa_{c}\simeq 2\pi b_{\max}/\lambda_{\rm phys}.

A representative momentum–space bandwidth may therefore be taken as

\sigma_{p}\simeq\kappa_{c}/2.

The resulting phase–space area then satisfies

\lambdabar\;\equiv\;\sigma_{\theta}\,\sigma_{p}\;\sim\;\mathcal{O}(1),

indicating that, despite incomplete mode sampling, the synthesised image produced by a radio interferometer occupies a phase–space cell comparable to that of a diffraction-limited optical system.

Additional instrumental blurring mechanisms.

Astronomical images are further degraded by several
instrumental effects that suppress high–spatial–frequency modes.
In Fourier space, these effects act multiplicatively on the OTF, while in the image plane, they broaden the effective PSF.
To leading order, these blurs are well approximated as Gaussian and may be
combined in quadrature
(
26
;
50
)
.
The first one is pointing jitter. Random telescope pointing fluctuations during an exposure are well described
by a Gaussian angular displacement with rms width
\sigma_{\theta,\rm jit.}

(
31
)
.
The resulting image is the convolution of the diffraction–limited PSF with
the jitter kernel.

Secondly, the pixel response matters in image detection. Finite pixel size introduces an additional blur corresponding to the pixel
response function.
For square pixels of angular width
p
, the image is convolved with a
top–hat kernel, yielding an OTF factor

K_{\rm OTF,pix.}=\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\kappa_{x}p}{2}\right)\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{\kappa_{y}p}{2}\right),

where
\operatorname{sinc}(x)=\sin x/x

(
26
)
.
For engineering estimates, the pixel blur is often replaced by a Gaussian
with equivalent variance,

\sigma_{\theta,\rm pix.}\;\approx\;p/\sqrt{12},

corresponding to the second moment of a square top–hat response.

Thirdly, detector charge diffusion occurs when CCD/CMOS stores the image. In CCD and CMOS detectors, photoelectrons diffuse laterally before collection,
producing an approximately Gaussian broadening of the image
with rms width
\sigma_{\theta,\rm det.}

(
42
)
.

Treating all
non-diffraction blurring mechanisms as a Gaussian in the image plane, and to
add their variances in quadrature,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\theta,\rm tot.}^{2}\;\approx\;\sigma_{\theta,\rm diff.}^{2}+\sigma_{\theta,\rm jit.}^{2}+\sigma_{\theta,\rm pix.}^{2}+\sigma_{\theta,\rm det.}^{2}.

(196)

This prescription defines the effective resolution element of the system
and naturally identifies the minimum resolvable phase–space cell.
Consequently, Wigner–space structure below this scale should not be
over–interpreted as physically meaningful.

One may determine
\lambdabar
in a data-driven way from the image. In this case we propose to make use of

\displaystyle\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\;=\;\frac{\int\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\theta}|\bm{\theta}-\bar{\bm{\theta}}|^{2}\,I(\bm{\theta})}{\int\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\theta}I(\bm{\theta})},\qquad\sigma_{k}^{2}\;=\;\frac{\int|\bm{k}|^{2}\,|\tilde{\psi}(\bm{k})|^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{k}}{\int|\tilde{\psi}(\bm{k})|^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{k}}.

(197)

With
\bm{p}=\lambdabar\,\bm{k}
we have
\sigma_{p}=\lambdabar\,\sigma_{k}
, hence enforce the Gaussian reference

\sigma_{\theta}\sigma_{k}=1
to get
\lambdabar
. With this choice,
\lambdabar=\mathcal{O}(1)
at the angular scales where galaxy morphology lives, and the Wigner
phase–space analysis resolves exactly what the instrument can support—no less, no more.

V.3
Existence of optimal recovery

In order to quantify how much morphological information is preserved under
systematics and noise, we evaluate the information–theoretic fidelity of a
channel on a certain ensemble of galaxy images, generalising the single image state for the moment. Let
\hat{\rho}_{I}
denote the
ensemble (source) state on the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{\lambdabar}
truncated by
\lambdabar
, for example obtained from a population distribution

p(\psi)
as
\hat{\rho}_{I}=\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim p}[\,|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\,]
.
The observation process (PSF, diffusion, detector noise, etc.) is a CPTP map

\mathcal{E}
, so that
\hat{\rho}_{O}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{I})
.

Let us introduce an abstract reference system
R
and a purification

|\Psi_{\hat{\rho}_{I}}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambdabar}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{R}

satisfying

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}[|\Psi_{\hat{\rho}_{I}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\hat{\rho}_{I}}|]=\hat{\rho}_{I}
.
The entanglement fidelity of a channel
\mathcal{N}
acting on the image system
is defined by

\displaystyle F_{e}(\hat{\rho}_{I},\mathcal{N})\equiv\langle\Psi_{\hat{\rho}_{I}}|(\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{R})\!\left(|\Psi_{\hat{\rho}_{I}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\hat{\rho}_{I}}|\right)|\Psi_{\hat{\rho}_{I}}\rangle.

(198)

This quantity satisfies
0\leq F_{e}\leq 1
and is independent of the particular
choice of purification. Operationally,
F_{e}
measures the fraction of Schumacher–compressible information of the source
\hat{\rho}_{I}

that is preserved by
\mathcal{N}
, including all correlations encoded in
off–diagonal mode coherences.

If
\mathcal{N}
admits a Kraus representation

\mathcal{N}(\hat{\rho})=\sum_{a}\hat{K}_{a}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{a}^{\dagger}
,
then the entanglement fidelity admits the useful expression

\displaystyle F_{e}(\hat{\rho}_{I},\mathcal{N})=\sum_{a}\big|\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{I}\,\hat{K}_{a})\big|^{2}.

(199)

For a continuous Kraus index
z
, the sum is replaced by an integral,

\displaystyle F_{e}(\hat{\rho}_{I},\mathcal{N})=\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\;\big|\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{\rho}_{I}\,\hat{K}(z))\big|^{2}.

(200)

A reconstruction (denoising/deconvolution) is itself a CPTP map

\mathcal{R}
applied to the observed state, producing a recovery of an image as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\rm rec}=\mathcal{R}(\hat{\rho}_{O})\,.

(201)

We assess performance at the ensemble level via the entanglement fidelity of
the effective channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\equiv\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E},\qquad F_{e}(\hat{\rho}_{I},\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}).

(202)

Within this framework, an information–theoretically optimal denoiser is a
recovery map that maximizes
F_{e}
(or an equivalent monotone) over an allowed
class of physically admissible reconstructions
\mathcal{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\star}\in\arg\max_{\mathcal{R}\in\mathfrak{R}}F_{e}(\hat{\rho}_{I},\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}).

(203)

The constraint set
\mathfrak{R}
may incorporate practical structure such as
symmetry–equivariance, locality in mode space, or computational tractability.

The total observation channel is the composition

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\rm noise}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\rm PSF},\qquad\hat{\rho}_{O}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{\rho}_{I}),

(204)

which remains Gaussian and translationally invariant.
In phase space, the combined kernel is again Gaussian,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\rm tot}(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})\propto\exp\!\left(-\bm{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\,\bm{\theta}-\bm{p}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\,\bm{p}\right),

(205)

with covariance matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
determined by
\beta
and the
noise strengths
\gamma_{i}
. We expand
W_{I}

and
W_{K}
in terms of the torus harmonics as

\displaystyle W_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(J,K)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\mathrm{even}}}\;\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{JK}(\bm{Q})\,\tilde{\chi}_{JK}(\bm{\phi}),

(206)

\displaystyle W_{K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(J,K)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\mathrm{even}}}\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{JK}(z;\bm{Q})\,\tilde{\chi}_{JK}(\bm{\phi})\,.

(207)

Here we define
\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\mathrm{even}}\;\equiv\;\bigl\{(J,K)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,\big|\,J\equiv K\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\bigr\}
, and
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{JK}\equiv\mathcal{W}_{\frac{J+K}{2},\frac{J-K}{2}}
and
\tilde{\chi}_{JK}\equiv\chi_{\frac{J+K}{2},\frac{J-K}{2}}

. Note that we concisely denote
\bm{Q}=(Q_{0},Q_{2})
and
\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{u},\phi_{v})
, The label
z
denotes the continous spectrum of the kernel.
The entanglement fidelity can be written as a quadratic form

\displaystyle F_{e}=

\displaystyle\sum_{(J,K)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\mathrm{even}}}\sum_{(J^{\prime},K^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\mathrm{even}}}

(208)

\displaystyle\times\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{Q}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{Q}^{\prime}\;\mathcal{W}_{JK}(\bm{Q})\,\mathcal{M}_{JK,J^{\prime}K^{\prime}}(\bm{Q},\bm{Q}^{\prime})\,\mathcal{W}^{\ast}_{J^{\prime}K^{\prime}}(\bm{Q}^{\prime}),

(209)

where we have defined the fidelity kernel as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{JK,J^{\prime}K^{\prime}}(\bm{Q},\bm{Q}^{\prime})\equiv(2\pi)^{4}\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\;\mathcal{K}_{JK}(z;\bm{Q})\,\mathcal{K}^{\ast}_{J^{\prime}K^{\prime}}(z;\bm{Q}^{\prime})\,.

(211)

Let us interpret the above formulation in more detail.
The kernel
\mathcal{M}_{JK,J^{\prime}K^{\prime}}
is a positive semidefinite operator on
the space of WFS coefficients and encodes the combined effect of the
forward imaging channel and the recovery map.
Its spectral properties determine which combinations of morphology and
rotation charges are information–bearing and which are irretrievably
lost:

•

Eigenmodes of
\mathcal{M}
with large eigenvalues correspond to
charge combinations that can be faithfully recovered.

•

Small or vanishing eigenvalues signal directions in

(J,K,\bm{Q})
–space where information is erased by PSF blurring and noise.

•

The maximal achievable entanglement fidelity is obtained by
restricting reconstruction to the support of
\mathcal{M}
.

For a stationary PSF and homogeneous noise, translational invariance
ensures diagonalisation in Fourier space, while rotational covariance
further constrains the kernel to be block–diagonal in the rotation
charge:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{JK,J^{\prime}K^{\prime}}\;\propto\;\delta_{KK^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{M}^{(K)}_{JJ^{\prime}}.

(212)

In this case, different
K
sectors are independent, and information loss
is governed primarily by mixing among the morphology charges
J
within each
rotation sector.
Isotropic PSFs preserve the
K
labels, whereas anisotropic PSFs induce
off–diagonal couplings between different
J
blocks.
Within any symmetry–equivariant class of CPTP recovery maps

\mathcal{R}
, the entanglement–fidelity–optimal reconstruction
corresponds to choosing
\mathcal{R}
such that the effective kernel

\mathcal{K}
is optimally inverted (with regularisation) on its support.
In practice this leads to Wiener– or Petz–type
72
operators that act
block–by–block in
(J,K)
space.
The WFS basis therefore provides a natural coordinate system in which the
fidelity kernel is sparse, symmetry–adapted, and directly interpretable.
The WFS decomposition does not eliminate information
loss—which is fundamentally bounded by
\mathcal{M}
—but renders it
explicit.
By diagonalising the symmetry structure of
\mathcal{M}
, it enables
recovery maps that saturate the maximum achievable entanglement fidelity
while preserving all symmetry–protected morphology and rotation modes.

V.4
Feasibility

We describe how feasible the methodology of image analysis in terms of the WFS is, aligned with the timeline of the present and the forthcoming surveys. At the level of the data compression into the Wigner function, it should be available as it shares the same procedure as the standard Fourier transform, and the mathematics behind WFS is rigorous. What is non-trivial in the WFS is to reconstruct the image of galaxies within the framework of the quantum information theory, which few have addressed similar algorithms in practical image analysis in astronomy, except a couple of conceptual proposal for future telescopes cf.
67
. Although this may sound too futuristic, it has been rapidly developing in other area of research, such as quantum image processing
45
;
21
. To this end, our methodology should be developed in astronomy, and we need to further examine how much our methodology can be advantageuous the conventional image processing in astronomy.

VI
Conclusions

We have extended the conventional shapelet analysis for galaxy images into the
Wigner Function Shapelets
(WFS). The WFS decomposes the Wigner function of the galaxy image directly in the four-dimensional phase space, making the quantum information theory in image processing available. After sorting a useful extraction of the topological information of images via Hopf fibration of the phase space, we have found a useful observable
\mathcal{W}_{k\ell}(Q_{0},Q_{2})

In addition, we have found a probe for any parity-violating signature in the ensemble average of a pair product of the Wigner function of the two galaxy images. This paper is written to provide a baseline formalism of WFS. We will validate our methodology in practical image analysis, looking into image simulations as well as real-data processing.

Appendix A
Classical limit of Wigner function and Wigner transport

We describe how the classical Liouville equation is derived from the Wigner function and the Wigner transport at the limit of
\lambdabar\rightarrow 0
, where the resolution of the system is infinitesimal. Then we conclude that the Wigner function is proportional to the radiance (or specific intensity), and the Liouville equation we obtain is nothing but the radiative transfer equation of an image.

Let us assume a semiclassical family of image–plane fields of the form

\displaystyle\psi(\bm{\theta})\;=\;a(\bm{\theta})\,\exp\!\Big[\tfrac{i}{\lambdabar}\,S(\bm{\theta})\Big],

(213)

with smooth real phase
S(\bm{\theta})
and slowly varying complex amplitude
a(\bm{\theta})
. In other words, we take a WKB approximation for the image field. The Wigner function is expressed by
a(\bm{\theta})
and
S(\bm{\theta})
as

\displaystyle W(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\lambdabar)^{2}}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\xi}\;a\!\Big(\bm{\theta}+\tfrac{\bm{\xi}}{2}\Big)\,a^{*}\!\Big(\bm{\theta}-\tfrac{\bm{\xi}}{2}\Big)\,e^{\tfrac{i}{\lambdabar}\,\Phi(\bm{\theta},\bm{p};\bm{\xi})},

(214)

with phase

\displaystyle\Phi(\bm{\theta},\bm{p};\bm{\xi})=S\!\Big(\bm{\theta}+\tfrac{\bm{\xi}}{2}\Big)-S\!\Big(\bm{\theta}-\tfrac{\bm{\xi}}{2}\Big)-\bm{p}\!\cdot\!\bm{\xi}.

(215)

Rescale the integration variable as
\bm{\xi}=\lambdabar\,\bm{y}
to obtain

\displaystyle W(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{y}\;a\!\Big(\bm{\theta}+\tfrac{\lambdabar}{2}\bm{y}\Big)a^{*}\!\Big(\bm{\theta}-\tfrac{\lambdabar}{2}\bm{y}\Big)\,e^{i\,\Psi(\bm{\theta},\bm{p};\bm{y},\lambdabar)},

(216)

where

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{\theta},\bm{p};\bm{y},\lambdabar)=\frac{S(\bm{\theta}+\frac{\lambdabar}{2}\bm{y})-S(\bm{\theta}-\frac{\lambdabar}{2}\bm{y})}{\lambdabar}-\bm{y}\!\cdot\!\bm{p}.

(217)

Executing Taylor expansion of
\lambdabar
at fixed
\bm{\theta}
and using that the difference is odd in
\bm{y}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{S(\bm{\theta}+\frac{\lambdabar}{2}\bm{y})-S(\bm{\theta}-\frac{\lambdabar}{2}\bm{y})}{\lambdabar}=\bm{y}\!\cdot\!\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S(\bm{\theta})\;+\;\mathcal{O}\!\big(\lambdabar^{2}|\bm{y}|^{3}\big),

(218)

and similarly

\displaystyle a\!\Big(\bm{\theta}\pm\tfrac{\lambdabar}{2}\bm{y}\Big)=a(\bm{\theta})\;\pm\;\mathcal{O}\!\big(\lambdabar|\bm{y}|\big).

(219)

Keeping the leading terms in phase and amplitude,

\displaystyle W(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{y}\;|a(\bm{\theta})|^{2}\,e^{i\,\bm{y}\!\cdot\!\big(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S(\bm{\theta})-\bm{p}\big)}\;+\;\mathcal{O}(\lambdabar).

(220)

Using the Fourier identity
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{y}\;e^{\,i\,\bm{y}\cdot\bm{q}}=\delta(\bm{q})
, we obtain the distributional limit

\displaystyle W(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})\;\xrightarrow[\ \lambdabar\to 0\ ]{}\;L(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})\;=\;|a(\bm{\theta})|^{2}\,\delta\!\Big(\bm{p}-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S(\bm{\theta})\Big).

(221)

Thus the Wigner function concentrates on the Lagrangian manifold

\{(\bm{\theta},\bm{p}):\bm{p}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S(\bm{\theta})\}
with weight
|a(\bm{\theta})|^{2}
.
The limit
L(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})
is a positive Radon measure on phase space: a nonnegative generalized density (possibly singular) integrable against smooth test functions.

The measure
L(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})
is the classical radiance (or specific intensity) on the image–plane phase space: it assigns power per unit projected area per unit solid angle (per unit frequency/wavelength if spectral). In fact, its
\bm{\theta}
–marginal recovers the image intensity:

\displaystyle\int L(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{p}\;=\;|a(\bm{\theta})|^{2}\;=\;\lim_{\lambdabar\to 0}|\psi(\bm{\theta})|^{2}\;\equiv\;I(\bm{\theta}),

(222)

while its
\bm{p}
–marginal gives the classical distribution over propagation directions (spatial frequencies). For a finite superposition
\psi=\sum_{n}a_{n}(\bm{\theta})\exp[iS_{n}(\bm{\theta})/\lambdabar]
one has

W=\sum_{n}W_{nn}+\sum_{n\neq n^{\prime}}W_{nn^{\prime}}
.
Each diagonal term
W_{nn}
converges to

|a_{n}(\bm{\theta})|^{2}\,\delta\!\big(\bm{p}-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S_{n}(\bm{\theta})\big)
.
The cross terms
W_{nn^{\prime}}
carry rapidly oscillating phase

\exp[i(S_{n}-S_{n^{\prime}})/\lambdabar]
and vanish in the weak limit by non–stationary–phase
(unless
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S_{n}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S_{m}
on a set of measure zero, e.g. caustics). Hence

\displaystyle W\;\rightharpoonup\;L(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})=\sum_{n}|a_{n}(\bm{\theta})|^{2}\,\delta\!\big(\bm{p}-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S_{n}(\bm{\theta})\big),

(223)

a positive phase–space measure with no residual Wigner negativity.

If
\psi
evolves under a generator with Weyl symbol
H_{W}(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})
, the exact Moyal evolution

\partial_{s}W=\{H_{W},W\}_{\rm MB}
as shown in Eq. (
11
) reduces as
\lambdabar\to 0
to the Liouville (radiative–transfer) equation

\displaystyle\partial_{s}L\;+\;\{H,L\}_{\rm PB}\;=\;0,

(224)

so radiance is transported along Hamiltonian rays. For quadratic Hamiltonian
H
this transport law already holds exactly for all
\lambdabar
. In the rest of the section, we derive that the quadratic Hamiltonian
H
is derived from the Wigner symbol
H_{W}
, completing the whole logic to be consistent.

Define the canonical momentum operator

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{p}}\;=\;-\,i\,\lambdabar\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}},

(225)

so that the commutators are
[\hat{\theta}_{i},\hat{p}_{j}]=i\,\lambdabar\,\delta_{ij}
(with
i,j\in\{x,y\}
).
Then

\displaystyle\hat{H}\;=\;-\,\frac{\lambdabar^{2}}{2}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}\;+\;\hat{V}\;=\;\frac{\hat{\bm{p}}^{\,2}}{2}\;+\;\hat{V},

(226)

If an operator
\hat{A}
has kernel
A(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})
in the angle basis, its Weyl symbol is

\displaystyle A_{W}(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})\;=\;\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\xi}\;A\!\left(\bm{\theta}+\tfrac{\bm{\xi}}{2},\,\bm{\theta}-\tfrac{\bm{\xi}}{2}\right)\,e^{-\frac{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{\xi}}{\lambdabar}}.

(227)

For
(\hat{V}f)(\bm{\theta})=V(\bm{\theta})f(\bm{\theta})
, the kernel is

V(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})=V(\bm{\theta}_{1})\,\delta(\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2})
, hence

\displaystyle(V)_{W}(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})\;=\;V(\bm{\theta})\quad\text{(exact).}

(228)

For
\hat{T}=\hat{\bm{p}}^{\,2}/2
, the kernel is

\displaystyle T(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\;=\;-\,\frac{\lambdabar^{2}}{2}\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}^{2}\,\delta(\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}).

(229)

Write
\bm{\theta}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{\theta}_{1}+\bm{\theta}_{2})
and
\bm{\xi}=\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}
. Since
\delta
depends only on
\bm{\xi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle T\!\left(\bm{\theta}+\tfrac{\bm{\xi}}{2},\,\bm{\theta}-\tfrac{\bm{\xi}}{2}\right)\;=\;-\,\frac{\lambdabar^{2}}{2}\,\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}^{2}\,\delta(\bm{\xi}).

(230)

Thus

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (T)_{W}(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})&=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\xi}\;\Big[-\,\frac{\lambdabar^{2}}{2}\,\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}^{2}\delta(\bm{\xi})\Big]\,e^{-\frac{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{\xi}}{\lambdabar}}\\
&=-\frac{\lambdabar^{2}}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\xi}\;\delta(\bm{\xi})\,\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}^{2}\,e^{-\frac{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{\xi}}{\lambdabar}}\\
&=\frac{\lambdabar^{2}}{2}\,\Big[\,\,\frac{{\bm{p}}^{2}}{\lambdabar^{2}}\Big]\\
&=\frac{\bm{p}^{2}}{2},\end{aligned}

(231)

Hence the kinetic Weyl symbol equals
\bm{k}^{2}/2
exactly. Combining the two pieces,

\displaystyle H_{W}(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})\;=\;\frac{\bm{p}^{2}}{2}\;+\;V(\bm{\theta}),

(232)

which is precisely the classical Hamiltonian of geometrical optics on
(\bm{\theta},\bm{p})
.
Because
H_{W}
is quadratic in
\bm{p}
and local in
\bm{\theta}
, the Moyal and Poisson brackets coincide exactly; thus the phase–space flow is a linear symplectic (metaplectic) map with no higher–order corrections.

Appendix B
Wigner function for polarised/coloured images

We extend the formalism for the Wigner function of a galaxy image so that it can analyse an image with polarisations/colours. Let us treat the image as a Jones field
\psi(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
and define a
2\times 2
matrix–valued cross–Wigner

\displaystyle\big[W_{ab}^{\rm pol}(z)\big]_{ij}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\lambdabar)^{2}}\!\int\!\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{\xi}\;\psi_{a,i}(\bm{\theta}{+}\bm{\xi}/2)\,\psi_{b,j}^{*}(\bm{\theta}{-}\bm{\xi}/2)\,e^{-\frac{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{\xi}}{\lambdabar}}.

(233)

For a polarization basis change
U\in{\rm U}(2)
acting on components, one has

\displaystyle W_{Ua,\,Ub}^{\rm pol}(z)=U\,W_{ab}^{\rm pol}(z)\,U^{\dagger},

(234)

so the total–intensity (scalar) cross–Wigner
\mathrm{tr}\,W_{ab}^{\rm pol}
is basis–invariant. Thus polarization changes are genuinely unitary in this framework (rotations, phase retarders, Faraday rotation in a lossless model, etc.).

Two images of the same galaxy at different wavelengths need not be related by any unitary
U
on a common Hilbert space: the sky emission, PSF, and transfer function are chromatic and typically include non–unitary effects (convolution, absorption/emission). A useful exception is a pure first–order, lossless, paraxial change that is equivalent to a symplectic squeeze. Writing the symplectic squeeze

S_{s}=\mathrm{diag}(s\,I_{2},\,s^{-1}I_{2})
with metaplectic lift
\mu(S_{s})
, one has

\displaystyle W_{\mu(S_{s})a,\,\mu(S_{s})b}(z)=W_{ab}(S_{s}^{-1}z).

(235)

In diffraction–limited optics, the core scale changes
\propto\lambda
; after non-dimensionalizing
z
so that
\lambdabar
is fixed, choosing
s\approx\sqrt{\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1}}
models the achromatic scaling between bands. Beyond this ideal case (e.g. band–dependent PSF, dust, stellar–population changes), no unitary map exists, and the cross–Wigner should be used as a comparative statistic rather than expected to obey simple covariance.

Let

Appendix F
Weak-lensing operators with Swinger two oscillators

We adopt the standard Hamiltonian–Heisenberg convention in which an
infinitesimal canonical transformation is generated by

\displaystyle\delta\hat{A}=\frac{1}{i\lambdabar}\,[\hat{A},\hat{G}],

(258)

so that the classical limit is obtained by replacing commutators with
Poisson brackets,
\delta A=\{A,G\}
.

F.0.1
Shear generator

In the circular (Schwinger) basis
(\hat{a}_{u},\hat{a}_{v})
, the gravitational
shear is generated by a quadratic operator,

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\gamma}=-\frac{i}{2}\,\gamma\,(\hat{a}_{u}^{2}-\hat{a}_{v}^{2})+\frac{i}{2}\,\gamma^{*}\,(\hat{a}_{u}^{\dagger 2}-\hat{a}_{v}^{\dagger 2}).

(259)

This yields

\displaystyle\delta\hat{a}_{u}=\gamma^{*}\hat{a}_{u}^{\dagger},\qquad\delta\hat{a}_{v}=\gamma\hat{a}_{v}^{\dagger},

(260)

which reproduces, at the phase–space level,

\delta u=\gamma^{*}u^{*}
and
\delta v=\gamma v^{*}
.
The induced action on the Wigner function is therefore a Lie transport
along the corresponding phase–space vector field.

F.0.2
Flexion generator

Flexion corresponds to the quadratic term in the lens mapping and is
implemented as a canonical transformation generated by a cubic operator.
In Cartesian variables, the universal form of the generator is

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\rm flex}=\frac{1}{2}\,D_{ijk}\,\widehat{p_{i}\,\theta_{j}\,\theta_{k}},

(261)

where
D_{ijk}
is symmetric in
(j,k)
and
\widehat{\cdots}
denotes
a Hermitian (e.g. Weyl) ordering.
This guarantees

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\theta}_{i}=\frac{1}{i\lambdabar}[\hat{\theta}_{i},\hat{G}_{\rm flex}]=\frac{1}{2}\,D_{ijk}\,\hat{\theta}_{j}\hat{\theta}_{k},

(262)

with the corresponding momentum variation fixed by canonicity.

Let us define the complex operators

\hat{\theta}=\hat{\theta}_{x}+i\hat{\theta}_{y}
and
\hat{p}=\hat{p}_{x}+i\hat{p}_{y}
,
so that
[\hat{\theta},\hat{p}^{\dagger}]=2i\lambdabar
and

[\hat{\theta}^{\dagger},\hat{p}]=2i\lambdabar
.
A real Cartesian dot product can be written as

p_{x}\delta\theta_{x}+p_{y}\delta\theta_{y}=\Re(p^{\dagger}\delta\theta)=\tfrac{1}{2}(p^{\dagger}\delta\theta+p\,\delta\theta^{\dagger})
.
Hence an equivalent universal form of the flexion generator is

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\rm flex}=\widehat{\Re(\hat{p}^{\dagger}\,\delta\hat{\theta})}=\frac{1}{2}\,\widehat{\hat{p}^{\dagger}\,\delta\hat{\theta}+\hat{p}\,\delta\hat{\theta}^{\dagger}},

(263)

with
\widehat{\cdots}
denoting a Hermitian (e.g. Weyl) ordering. We identify the following operator as

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\theta}=\frac{1}{4}\Big(2\mathcal{F}\,\hat{\theta}\hat{\theta}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\,\hat{\theta}^{2}+\mathcal{G}\,\hat{\theta}^{\dagger 2}\Big),

(264)

The associated momentum update (Weyl symbol / classical form) is

\displaystyle\delta p=-\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\theta+\mathcal{F}\theta^{\ast})\,p-\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{F}\theta+\mathcal{G}\theta^{\ast})\,p^{\ast},

(265)

with the operator expression obtained by the same Hermitian ordering.

The flexion moment as

\displaystyle\delta\theta=\frac{1}{4}(2\mathcal{F}|\theta|^{2}+\mathcal{F}^{*}\theta^{2}+\mathcal{G}\theta^{*2})\,.

(266)

We can derive the corresponding variation of the complex conjugate momentum as

\displaystyle\delta p=-\frac{\mathcal{F}}{2}(\theta p^{*}+\theta^{*}p)-\frac{\mathcal{F^{*}}}{2}\theta p-\frac{\mathcal{G}}{2}\theta^{*}p^{*}\,.

(267)

Here we denote
\mathcal{F}=[\Phi_{xxx}+\Phi_{xyy}+i(\Phi_{xxy}+\Phi_{yyy})]/2

\mathcal{G}=[\Phi_{xxx}-3\Phi_{xyy}+i(3\Phi_{xxy}-\Phi_{yyy})]/2

\Phi_{xxy}=\Phi_{xyx}=\Phi_{yxx}

\sigma=1

\sigma

\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}\sigma^{2}

providing a direct extension of the shear case.
A Hermitian universal form of the flexion generator is

\hat{G}_{\rm flex}=\widehat{\Re(\hat{p}^{\dagger}\,\delta\hat{\theta})}

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\theta}=\frac{1}{4}\Big(2\mathcal{F}\,\hat{\theta}\hat{\theta}^{\dagger}+\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\,\hat{\theta}^{2}+\mathcal{G}\,\hat{\theta}^{\dagger 2}\Big),

(268)

where
\widehat{\cdots}
denotes a Hermitian (e.g. Weyl) ordering.
Substituting the above dictionary yields

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\mathcal{G}}
