Thermal versus nonthermal ablation for treatment of great saphenous veins in patients with lower extremity superficial venous disease
Authors: Ankita Katukota, Michael Fassler, Salvatore Scali, Nicholas Osborne, Benjamin Jacobs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic venous disease is a prevalent condition that significantly impacts the quality of life of affected patients. In the last 20 years, minimally invasive treatments have become widespread and have typically been classified into thermal or nonthermal ablative techniques. However, few studies have evaluated the efficacy of these interventions, with limited comparative data available with respect to quality of life.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study of adults undergoing thermal or nonthermal ablation of unilateral, great saphenous vein (GSV)-truncal veins, identified from the Vascular Quality Initiative Varicose Vein Registry, from 2014 to 2026. Patients with nontruncal, bilateral, or non–GSV-treated veins were excluded. The primary outcome of interest and comparator was long-term quality-of-life outcomes. Fisher exact and Mann-Whitney tests were used to compare categorical and continuous variables.ResultsA total of 23,247 patients were analyzed. Ultimately, 14,277 patients undergoing 17,504 procedures met inclusion criteria, with 39% (n = 8970) of patients failing to meet criteria because of missing procedural data regarding ablation technique, laterality, or truncal vein status. Eighty-three percent of procedures were thermal ablations (n = 14,481) compared with 3023 (17%) nonthermal ablations. Nonthermal ablation patients tended to be older (61 [48-70 years vs 58 [46-68] years; P < .0001) and white (85% vs 79%; P < .0001). A higher proportion of thermal ablations occurred in hospitals (34% vs 28%; P < .0001) compared with ambulatory/office-based settings. Furthermore, thermal ablation patients differed from nonthermal ablation patients with respect to prophylactic procedural anticoagulation utilization (low–molecular-weight heparin: 9% vs 6%; P < .0001 and unfractionated heparin: 3% vs 1%; P < .0001) and rates of multiple anesthetic modalities (55% vs 17%; P < .0001) and regional/tumescent (35% vs 4%; P < .0001) utilization. Thermal patients were more likely to have above-knee GSV (89% vs 78%; P < .0001) and a larger diameter vein (7.3 [6.0-9.1] vs 6.0 [4.0-8.0] mm; P < .0001) treated. Patients in the nonthermal group had lower rates of paresthesia in the treated extremity (3% vs 1%; P = .0003) and were more likely to have improvements in Clinical, Etiology, Anatomy, and Pathophysiology scores from C3 to C1 or C3 to C0 (8% vs 6%; P < .0001). Nonthermal patients were also more likely to have pain improvements from severe to mild/moderate (3% vs 2%; P = .0008).ConclusionsThermal and nonthermal ablations were both associated with clinical improvement in chronic venous disease. However, patients treated with nonthermal ablations demonstrated greater improvement in Clinical, Etiology, Anatomy, and Pathophysiology class and pain improvement from severe to mild or moderate. These findings suggest a potential therapeutic advantage in symptom resolution and functional recovery with nonthermal ablative techniques. Further investigation regarding long-term quality-of-life measures following nonthermal ablation is warranted.

Chronic venous disease is common, present in up to 50% of adult patients.1 These conditions negatively impact patient quality of life through the burden of chronic symptomatology, wound care needs, and commensurate financial strain.2 Historically, surgery with ligation and stripping was the gold standard. Recently, the advent of endovenous ablation has become widespread, offering a minimally invasive approach with reduced surgical morbidity and increased procedural delivery through outpatient or ambulatory settings.3,4

Thermal therapies use heat to ablate the venous endothelium of affected veins and include radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and endovenous laser ablation (EVLA). More recently, nonthermal, nontumescent (NTNT) methods have been introduced, which avoid the heat-induced effects of thermal ablations in favor of mechanical or chemical closure.5 These include modalities such as mechanochemical ablation (MOCA; ClariVein), cyanoacrylate (VenaSeal; Medtronic) embolization, and foam sclerotherapy (Varithena).6 Thermal techniques have predominated over NTNT techniques for the treatment of incompetent great saphenous veins (GSVs), largely because of their efficacy and safety profiles, supported by the existence of robust 10- to 15-year follow-up data.7 However, to date, few studies have compared the efficacy of thermal and nonthermal devices. Those that exist are conflicting and inconclusive.

Overall, current evidence indicates that thermal and nonthermal ablation techniques provide similarly effective and safe treatment options for saphenous vein incompetence. A 5-year extension study of the VeClose trial demonstrated no statistical difference between cyanoacrylate closure (CAC) and RFA therapies, with comparable anatomic closure rates up to 60 months.8 Both demonstrated persistent improvement in quality-of-life scores with long-term safety between groups. CAC also specifically outperformed foam sclerotherapy and EVLA in long-term recanalization rates. With limited evidence distinctly in favor of NTNT techniques, this study showed that CAC is safe, effective, and noninferior for treatment of saphenous vein incompetence. Furthermore, Bootun et al3 showed similar closure rates and clinical quality-of-life improvements in thermal compared with endothermal techniques. Other meta-analyses indicate that vein occlusion rates following thermal vs nonthermal ablation are similar, with some showing that nonthermal techniques result in less pain and lower risk of nerve injury as well as more rapid return to normal activities and work.9,10 Nonthermal modalities also demonstrate promising short-term patency and superior time to wound healing, with CAC associated with less periprocedural pain, skin pigmentation, and tissue injury compared with EVLA, without significant differences in phlebitis, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), or ecchymosis.11 Durability varies among NTNT devices, with MOCA showing lower occlusion rates at 3 years, particularly in veins over 7 mm, compared with EVLA or RFA.12 Other comparative studies report similar safety profiles between RFA and CAC with the notable finding of faster ulcer healing across all ulcer sizes in the CAC cohort.13

Although both thermal and nonthermal ablation techniques are widely used, comparative effectiveness data remain limited and inconsistent, with existing studies constrained by small sample sizes, heterogeneous patient populations, and variable outcome measures. As a result, there is no clear consensus on how these modalities differ in real-world performance or in their impact on patient-reported outcomes. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a comparative analysis of quality-of-life outcomes following thermal vs nonthermal ablation of the GSV using the Vascular Quality Initiative Varicose Vein Registry (VQI-VVR). This large, multicenter registry provides a contemporary, practice-based cohort, enabling a more robust assessment of modality-specific outcomes and offering evidence to inform clinical decision-making.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the VQI-VVR to identify patients aged 18 years and older who underwent thermal or nonthermal ablation of a unilateral, truncal vein between 2014 and 2026. Bilateral or nontruncal venous treatments were excluded. Bilateral procedures were excluded to allow clear attribution of outcomes to a single treated extremity. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in the Supplementary Table (online only). A total of 14,277 patients undergoing 17,504 procedures were included in the final analysis. Of these, 15,298 were above-knee (AK) GSV procedures and 2206 were below-knee (BK) GSV procedures. This stratification was performed to acknowledge the distinct anatomic and clinical profiles of the two groups, as the BK GSV typically presents with smaller vessel diameters and different clinical indications compared with the AK segment. Institutional review board approval was not required.

To standardize segment classification, original VQI categories were reorganized into two consistent groups: GSV AK and GSV BK. This reclassification enabled more uniform comparisons across thermal and nonthermal treatment modalities. Of the included procedures, 14,481 (82.73%) were thermal ablations and 3023 (17.27%) were nonthermal ablations.

The primary outcome was quality of life at 9-month follow-up, assessed through changes in the clinical (C) component of the Clinical, Etiology, Anatomy, and Pathophysiology (CEAP) classification and pain scores from the time of ablation.14 There were three definitions used for CEAP improvements: C3 to C1 or C0, C2 to C1 or C0, and C6r to C5. While the Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS) was considered a longitudinal outcome measure, it was excluded from the primary analysis because of a high rate of missing data within the registry for this cohort. Consequently, CEAP classification was utilized to categorize clinical severity and assess shifts in clinical status postintervention, as it provided the most robust and complete data set for the study population.

Pain improvements were categorized as moderate or mild to none, severe to moderate or mild, and severe to none. Secondary outcomes included ulcer healing (number and duration), complications, recanalization, and work impact. Work impact was defined as improvement in the ability to work, including transitions from an unable or severely reduced ability to a moderately or mildly reduced ability, from an unable or severely reduced ability to full work ability with symptoms, and from a moderately or mildly reduced ability to no symptoms.

Complications were assessed based on predefined categories available in the VQI data set. For the cohort included in this study, the only documented complications were migraine, visual disturbance, systemic infection, transient ischemic attack, stroke, and death. For thermal ablative procedures, the rates of endothermal heat–induced thrombosis (EHIT) were recorded and classified according to the guidelines set forth by the American Venous Forum and Society for Vascular Surgery.15 EHIT 2 involves a thrombus extension into the adjacent deep vein but comprising <50% of the deep vein lumen. EHIT 3 involves thrombus extension into the adjacent deep vein, comprising >50% of the deep vein lumen. Conversely, rates of non–heat-induced venous thrombosis were recorded in patients undergoing nonthermal ablation. Complication rates were recorded at the patient level and stratified by the type of ablation therapy received, thermal or nonthermal. Each complication was analyzed for frequency and distribution between treatment groups to evaluate potential differences in adverse outcomes associated with the intervention modality.

To minimize the influence of outliers, data cleaning procedures were implemented using VQI-defined boundaries from the registry’s data dictionary. For body mass index calculations, height values were restricted to 18.9 to 110 inches and weight values to 2.99 to 227 kg. Maximal vein diameters were limited to 1 to 30 mm, and missed workdays were capped between 1 and 30 days. Values outside these ranges were excluded from the analysis.

Demographic variables included age, sex, race, ethnicity, and prior venous interventions, specifically prior phlebectomy, DVT, and varicose vein treatment on the treated side only. Race categories were consolidated into four groups: White, Black, non-White (including Asian, American Indian/Alaskan Native, and Pacific Islander), and Other/Unknown. A separate statistical analysis was conducted comparing White and non-White patients. For analysis, ambulatory centers and offices were grouped as nonhospital settings, whereas hospital inpatient and outpatient were grouped as hospital settings. Although each patient had only one side treated, procedures were initially categorized as left or right and were combined for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges. To assess differences between the thermal and nonthermal ablation cohorts, Fisher exact tests or Pearson’s χ2 tests were utilized for categorical data, and Mann-Whitney U tests were employed for continuous variables. A two-sided P value <.05 was considered the threshold for statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro software (SAS Institute, Inc).

Results

Demographics, intervention setting, and venous history

A total of 23,247 patients were analyzed. Sixty-one percent of patients (n = 14,277) met inclusion criteria, whereas 39% (n = 8970) of patients were excluded because of missing procedural data regarding ablation technique, laterality, or truncal vein status. Subgroup analysis identified that a total of 14,481 (83%) thermal ablation procedures and 3023 (17%) nonthermal ablations were performed. Use of prophylactic, periprocedural anticoagulation differed by treatment type. Thermal ablation patients more frequently received either low–molecular-weight heparin (9% vs 6%; P < .0001) or unfractionated heparin (3% vs 1%; P < .0001). Although the majority of patients in both groups had no prior venous intervention, patients undergoing nonthermal ablation had higher rates of previous ipsilateral phlebectomy (8% vs 6%; P < .0001) and varicose vein treatment (17% vs 5%; P < .0001). Rates of previous ipsilateral DVT were also higher in the nonthermal ablation cohort (6% vs 4%; P = .001).

Procedural data

Larger diameter truncal veins were treated in the thermal group with a median diameter of 7.3 [6.0-9.1] mm compared with 6.0 [4.0-8.0] mm (P < .0001). Thermal ablation patients were more likely to have AK GSVs treated (89% vs 78%; P < .0001), whereas nonthermal patients were more likely to have BK GSVs treated (22% vs 11%; P < .0001) (Table II). Anesthetic practices differed between therapies, with nonthermal procedures more likely to be performed under only local anesthetic (52% vs 5%; P < .0001), only general anesthesia (5% vs 2%; P < .0001), or without any (19% vs 1%; P < .0001). Perioperative outcomes

Postoperatively, thermally ablated patients were more likely to require compression therapy (96% vs 71%; P < .0001). Patients within both cohorts that required compression had a median duration of 7 days of therapy; however, the nonthermal group had wider variability with respect to compression therapy duration (7 [7-14] vs 7 [7-7]; P < .0001) (Table III). Significant complication rates were low across cohorts; however, patients with nonthermal ablations had higher associated rates of hematoma formation (6 [0.5%] vs 12 [0.1%]; P < .03), postoperative pigmentation (14 [1%] vs 48 [0.6%]; P < .04), and allergic reactions (mild: 10 [0.3%] vs 7 [0.1%]; P < .0001 and severe: 2 [0.1%] vs 0 [0%]; P = .005). Long-term outcomes

At follow-up, patients in the nonthermal group were more likely to have improvements in CEAP scores from C3 to C1 or C0 (8% vs 6%; P < .0001) (Table IV). Discussion

In this large, retrospective registry study using data from the VQI-VVR from 2014 to 2026, we compared outcomes between thermal and nonthermal ablation therapies in patients 18 years and older undergoing treatment of a single truncal GSV vein in the lower extremity. Significant differences in patient demographics and procedural settings suggest that treatment modality is heavily influenced by nonclinical factors. A significant association was observed between race and ablation type, with non-White patients more likely to receive thermal ablation. This pattern aligns with national VQI data showing that Black or African American patients present with more advanced venous disease, are treated more often in hospital-based settings, and experience less symptomatic improvement after ablation compared with White and Hispanic patients.16 This disparity may be exacerbated by variations in insurance coverage. A recent analysis of U.S. insurance policies reveals that nonthermal modalities, such as MOC and CAC, face significantly stricter reimbursement criteria than thermal techniques.17 Specifically, MOCA is covered by as few as 8.2% of single-state carriers, compared with nearly 90% coverage for thermal laser ablation. Such inconsistencies in coverage and the frequent requirement for 12-week “conservative therapy” trials may create disproportionate barriers for patients seeking office-based, nontumescent treatments. In addition, thermal ablations were more frequently performed in hospital settings and utilized general or regional anesthesia, whereas nonthermal procedures were predominantly office based under local anesthesia. This confirms the utility of nonthermal techniques in the ambulatory setting, avoiding the resource utilization of tumescent anesthesia and operating rooms and potentially lowering the barrier to treatment for patients who may not need to undergo sedation. This shift toward the office-based setting reflects a broader transition in venous practice, as the historical reliance on hospital operating rooms has largely been superseded by streamlined outpatient models. By bypassing the scheduling constraints and significant capital costs inherent to the operating room, office-based ablation, whether thermal or nonthermal, allows for a more efficient allocation of health care resources while avoiding the unnecessary risks of general anesthesia. The higher frequency of hospital-based thermal procedures may also impact perioperative management patterns, including the use of pharmacologic venous thromboembolism prophylaxis. This likely reflects institutional standardized protocols and an effort to mitigate the risk of EHIT.15

A critical finding of this study is the relationship between anatomic treatment zones and nerve injury. Thermal ablation was significantly more likely to be used for AK segments, whereas nonthermal ablation was utilized more frequently for BK segments (22% vs 11%). Despite thermal ablation being utilized primarily in the proximal thigh, an anatomic region where the saphenous nerve is distant from the vein, it was still associated with a significantly higher rate of paresthesias compared with the nonthermal group (1% vs 0.3%). In the AK position, the safety of thermal modalities is largely predicated on the precise administration of tumescent anesthesia into the saphenous sheath to provide a protective thermal barrier. The occurrence of paresthesia in this proximal zone likely reflects instances where tumescent volume or placement was insufficient to shield adjacent cutaneous nerves. Given that the saphenous nerve courses in close proximity to the GSV BK, thermal energy delivery in distal segments is widely recognized to carry a higher risk of nerve injury and may contribute to the more frequent use of nonthermal modalities for BK insufficiency.18 This finding is supported by Kiguchi et al, who noted that treating the entire refluxing segment is optimal for healing, a goal that can be achieved with reduced risk of neuropathy using nonthermal methods in distal segments.19 Their study compared two strategies: primary nonthermal ablation of the full-length GSV using cyanoacrylate glue or microfoam (MFA), and a hybrid approach using RFA AK with MFA for the BK remnant. Both groups achieved equivalent healing times (median, 61 days), reinforcing that extending the treatment zone to the ankle, where the hydrostatic pressure gradient is highest, is critical for clinical success. By utilizing nonthermal methods to safely treat the distal segment, clinicians can effectively address pathological perforators near the ulcer bed while avoiding the iatrogenic nerve injury associated with thermal energy delivery in the distal calf.

Both cohorts achieved durable anatomic closure and symptom relief. Patients treated with nonthermal ablations demonstrated greater improvement in CEAP class from C3 to C0/C1 and pain scores from severe to mild/moderate. This aligns with a meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials showing that CAC had the greatest reduction in venous pain after the procedure compared with thermal ablation, surgery, or sclerotherapy.20 Similarly, the MARADONA trial and a study by Bootun et al found that MOCA was correlated with significantly less postoperative pain compared with RFA.21,22 Studies have shown that even in patients with CEAP 6 venous ulcers, patients treated with CAC had a significantly shorter median time to wound healing compared with RFA, regardless of ulcer size.13 The greater improvement in CEAP classification observed in the nonthermal cohort may also be partially attributed to the extent of the treated vein segment. Unlike thermal ablation, which is often anatomically restricted to the AK segment to avoid iatrogenic nerve injury, nonthermal modalities allow for safer intervention of the distal refluxing GSV. Our data confirm a higher frequency of BK treatments in the nonthermal group (22% vs 11%), suggesting that more comprehensive truncal occlusion—addressing the entire length of the refluxing segment—is a primary driver of superior clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the superior pain reduction in the nonthermal cohort may be attributed to the absence of thermal injury and the elimination of tumescent anesthesia, which can contribute to intraprocedural discomfort.

Data showing comparable outcomes between thermal and nonthermal ablations support a recent Cochrane review of 5135 patients, which described similar long-term technical success and recurrence rates between EVLA, RFA, and MOCA through 5 years.23 Kabnick et al24 similarly found that polidocanol endovenous MFA was not statistically different from thermal ablation regarding closure rates. Other studies comparing CAC and laser for refluxing GSVs found no difference, with high occlusion rates in both groups.25 Furthermore, a meta-analysis of 10 comparative studies found that MOCA is as effective as thermal ablations for patients with varicose veins specifically.26 Morrison et al8 (VeClose extension) also demonstrated no statistical differences between CAC and RFA therapies, with comparable anatomic closure rates up to 60 months. A retrospective review by Yang et al27 evaluating 335 patients treated with CAC or RFA similarly found that CAC achieved a 100% success rate compared with 99% for RFA treatment, with reduced midterm complications, including superficial phlebitis and nerve-related symptoms. Taken together, this body of evidence shows that nonthermal techniques deliver durable closure and symptom relief comparable to thermal ablation, supporting their use as equally effective options across a broad range of patients and clinical settings.

However, other data suggest nuances in long-term durability between specific devices. A retrospective review of 232 patients comparing EVLA, CAC, and RFA showed that while early occlusion rates were comparable, RFA achieved significantly higher success rates over EVLA at 3 and 5 years, with similar long-term outcomes compared with CAC.28 In addition, Borghese et al29 found that thermal techniques such as RFA provide good functional outcomes and a lower rate of complications, noting a relatively rapid return to normal activities within 8 days. Similarly, Vuylsteke et al7 described the marked shrinkage of GSVs using an endovenous laser device, noting favorable long-term results and minimal postoperative pain. While nonthermal options remain effective, select thermal technologies may offer superior long-term vein closure and functional outcomes, reinforcing the need for tailored device selection based on patient anatomy and treatment goals.

The complication profiles differed distinctly by modality, reflecting their mechanisms of action. Thermal ablation was associated with higher rates of paresthesias and necessitated higher rates of postoperative duplex surveillance. In contrast, nonthermal techniques were associated with higher rates of inflammatory skin reactions, including pigmentation and hematoma. This is consistent with literature describing lower thermal complications for nonthermal techniques but higher rates of phlebitis or skin pigmentation, likely because of inflammatory reactions to sclerosants or cyanoacrylate adhesives.26 Specific to ulcer healing, the nonthermal cohort showed a higher rate of healing ≥3 ulcers. This is supported by O’Banion et al,13 who demonstrated that CAC-treated ulcers healed faster than those treated with RFA, likely because of the ability to safely treat the ulcer bed distally without thermal risk.

In evaluating these outcomes, it is critical to acknowledge the anatomic and clinical heterogeneity between the AK and BK GSV segments within our cohort. Morphologically, the BK GSV typically exhibits a smaller luminal caliber and involves shorter treatment lengths compared with the AK segment, variables that are recognized determinants of technical success and postoperative sequelae, such as saphenous nerve paresthesia. Furthermore, the BK cohort frequently represents a secondary treatment population in whom the proximal segment has been previously obliterated. While these differences are inherent to the anatomic segments, they remain critical confounders that must be considered when interpreting comparative longitudinal outcomes between these two distinct clinical scenarios.

Our analysis also revealed that thermal ablation patients were more likely to receive periprocedural anticoagulation than those undergoing nonthermal treatments. This finding is notable given that the thermal cohort demonstrated a lower baseline prevalence of prior DVT and previous venous interventions compared with the nonthermal cohort. This suggests that the use of anticoagulation in thermal ablation may not be strictly necessitated by patient-specific thrombotic history. Instead, it likely reflects clinician-driven prophylactic strategies aimed at mitigating the risk of EHIT, a complication unique to thermal modalities that is not a factor in nonthermal procedures.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design and the use of registry data. The large sample size, while a strength, made detecting small, statistically significant differences likely, even if they were not clinically significant. The data set did not allow for granular stratification by specific device, requiring a grouped analysis of thermal and nonthermal techniques, which may mask device-specific outcomes. We also recognize that our reliance on CEAP classification rather than VCSS is a limitation of this study. Unlike VCSS, which is specifically designed to measure longitudinal changes in venous disease severity, CEAP is a static descriptive tool. However, because of the high percentage of missing VCSS data in the VQI-VVR for this specific time frame, CEAP served as the most reliable available metric for clinical assessment. Future prospective studies with mandatory VCSS reporting would be beneficial to more granularly track symptomatic improvement. Furthermore, potential comorbidities, such as musculoskeletal pathologies, which could have been confounding factors in patients’ symptomatology, were not identifiable within the data set and therefore could not be addressed within the analyses.

Conclusions

This national registry review identifies significant improvements in quality-of-life metrics following nonthermal truncal ablation compared with thermal ablation. These findings suggest a potential therapeutic advantage in symptom resolution and functional recovery with nonthermal ablative techniques. Future studies should explore these variables and long-term recurrence rates in more detail. Comparative effectiveness research incorporating patient-reported outcomes and stratified analyses by anatomic segment and disease severity may further inform optimal ablation strategies.
