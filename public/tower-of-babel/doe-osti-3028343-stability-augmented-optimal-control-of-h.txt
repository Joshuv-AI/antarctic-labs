Executive Summary
The Stability-Augmented Optimal Control of Hybrid PV Plants with Very High Penetration of
Inverter-based Resources (SAPPHIRE) project team developed and demonstrated advanced
modeling, control, and monitoring tools to address the stability challenges of operating power
systems with high shares of inverter-based resources (IBRs).

At the system level, SAPPHIRE created a stability-constrained scheduling framework that
incorporates frequency-stability-constrained alternating current optimal power flow
formulations. This framework allows operators to co-optimize cost and stability requirements in
both island and bulk power systems. At the plant level, the project advanced grid-forming (GFM)
control technologies for hybrid photovoltaic plants (HPPs), enabling them to enhance small-
signal stability by flexibly transitioning between operating modes. The team also developed
complementary modeling of battery degradation and secondary frequency regulation to ensure
economic and reliable operation while an HPP is providing grid services.

Meanwhile, SAPPHIRE delivered significant innovations in real-time monitoring techniques to
enhance grid stability. For example, the project team developed measurement-based real-time
inertia estimation for both ambient data and probing signal approaches. These methods were
validated through simulations and power-hardware-in-the-loop experiments at the National
Laboratory of the Rockies’ Flatirons Campus. We established and successfully applied a new
oscillation event analysis framework to investigate a real 19-hertz oscillation event on Kaua‘i
Island. By combining field data, full electromagnetic transient (EMT) modeling of island grids,
and EMT-based small-signal stability analysis techniques, the project identified the root causes
and validated mitigation strategies, including converting grid-following to grid-forming
inverters.

The project team successfully conducted the following three field demonstrations in Hawaii:

 • Grid stability enhancement with GFM HPPs on Kaua‘i Island showed improved
 frequency quality after inverter conversion to GFM mode by leveraging 2 years of
 continuously collected data from the island’s grid.
 • Real-time inertia estimation was implemented and demonstrated using a photovoltaic
 (PV)-battery plant to inject inertia probing signals to Kaua‘i Island.
 • The Kaua‘i Island Utility Cooperative grid operated with extremely high solar and hydro
 energy (100%) for 3 consecutive hours, with 88.5% from solar PV and 11.5% from
 hydropower, demonstrating secure system performance enabled by GFM HPPs.
We delivered 13 peer-reviewed journal papers, 16 conference papers, and 32 invited
presentations, and we contributed to 4 industry-led technical reports from the North American
Electric Reliability Corporation, the Energy Systems Integration Group, and the Institute of
Electrical and Electronics Engineers Power System Dynamic Performance Committee. We also
generated two software records for inertia-monitoring tools.

Overall, SAPPHIRE demonstrated that HPPs equipped with advanced GFM controls can provide
essential stability services, mitigate oscillation risks, and enable reliable operation of islanded
grids powered entirely with major energy from solar and water. These outcomes provide

actionable pathways for utilities, system operators, and industry stakeholders as they plan for
high-IBR futures.

1 Background
As the penetration of inverter-based resources (IBRs)—e.g., photovoltaics (PV), batteries, and
wind—increases in modern power grids, system operational instabilities have received
increasing attention. Focusing on enhancing grid stability and enabling stability-related grid
services of hybrid photovoltaic plants (HPPs), we propose a transformative hierarchical optimal
control framework: Stability-Augmented Optimal Control of Hybrid PV Plants with Very High
Penetration of Inverter-based Resources (SAPPHIRE). Figure 1 compares the conventional
power system control framework and the proposed SAPPHIRE framework. (All figures were
generated by the report authors for this project.) The proposed solution can simultaneously
ensure system stability at the transmission system level, optimize the provision of multiple
services, and realize grid-forming (GFM) operation of individual HPPs in coordination with PV
and battery energy storage systems (BESS) at the plant level.

 Figure 1. (a) Conventional control framework of power system operation. (b) The proposed
solution is grounded in developing controllers that dynamically steer operating points (e.g., active
 power, reactive power, or operation modes) of HPPs, aggregated IBRs, and synchronous
 condensers to improve system stability.
 PV = photovoltaic, DC = direct current, OPF = optimal power flow, AC = alternating current, SOC = state of charge,
 D-ES = distributed energy storage, D-PV = distributed PV

At the transmission system level, a stability-constrained alternating current optimal power flow
(AC-OPF) scheduler is proposed to take real-time, measurement-based inertia estimates as
inputs, and the adaptive inertia requirement (or a suitable substitute) is considered as a constraint
in the scheduling model. The real-time state of charge (SOC) of each BESS is also considered in
the optimization to help the scheduler accurately estimate the availability of the fast-response
capability from the BESS and the corresponding HPP. Further, to improve grid stability, the AC
scheduler also dispatches the reactive power for synchronous condensers and HPPs. At the plant
level, the coordinated control algorithm takes the dispatch and control signals from the scheduler
and executes them to: (1) satisfy the active and reactive power requirements from the system
level by operating the HPP in GFM mode, and (2) continuously coordinate the PV and BESS in
an individual HPP to simultaneously fulfill the commitment of grid services and extend the
lifetime of the BESS. The proposed SAPPHIRE control framework should be generalized for

 1

HPPs in bulk and island systems. To demonstrate the value of HPPs providing stability-related
services, we validated the SAPPHIRE control framework in Hawaii grids, and we quantified the
related value streams based on field measurements at both the system and plant levels.

2 Project Objectives
The project team developed a hierarchical optimal control framework that leverages the fast-
response capability of IBRs, specifically HPPs—from the power system level to the individual
HPP plant/device level—to better coordinate the limited battery energy storage resources with
PV to improve system stability, reliability, and flexibility. The project objectives include the
following:

 • Objective 1. At the grid operation level, the system-level optimal control goes beyond
 the traditional minimization of production cost and further coordinates various grid assets
 to ensure the adequacy of the stability resources by modeling the fast-response capability
 of HPPs and the adaptive stability constraints based on real-time measurements and
 estimation.
 • Objective 2. At the plant/device level, advanced control technologies are developed to
 push the envelope of the existing HPP controls to optimally coordinate the PV and
 battery to meet the needs of multiple energy and stability services (e.g., providing fast
 power response and GFM operation).
 • Objective 3. Demonstrate the advanced grid-friendly control capabilities of HPPs with
 leading HPP developer AES and utility partners Kaua‘i Island Utility Cooperative
 (KIUC) and Hawaiian Electric (HECO) in Hawaii.

3 Project Results and Discussion
3.1 SAPPHIRE Framework
To achieve the project objectives, the team divided the technical work into four parts: modeling
and control, measurement, validation, and demonstration. The execution path is summarized in
Figure 2. The project is organized as follows: First, the submodules of the stability-constrained
AC-OPF are separately developed to ensure the fidelity of each model, including the HPP
modeling (Task 1.2) and the real-time inertia estimation (Task 2). Then, the submodules are
integrated into a unified optimal framework (Task 1.1). To ensure the scalability of SAPPHIRE,
use cases are tested with a large interconnection model and an island model (Task 1.3). In
parallel, plant-level controls are studied by focusing on the GFM control and the optimal
coordination of the PV and BESS under Task 3. To better assist in designing the controls for an
HPP in the demo stage, we collected operational data in Hawaii to better understand the current
operation of the HPPs and the potential risk of the island system under Task 4. Before we moved
to the demo stage, all the proposed innovative controls were validated through the power-
hardware-in-the-loop (PHIL) facilities at the National Laboratory of the Rockies’ (NLR’s)
Flatirons Campus to ensure feasibility under Task 5. Finally, Task 6 demonstrates the
practicability of the SAPPHIRE framework and controls.

 Figure 2. Execution path of the proposed project
HPPs have emerged as a key solution in optimizing the use of renewable energy resources,
improving system reliability, and enhancing economic returns. Integrating PV systems with
BESS, as shown in Figure 3, allows operators to shape output profiles, reduce variability, and
better align generation with market signals or system demand.

In this section, we first describe the general HPP modeling approach used in scheduling. We then
incorporate a BESS degradation model into the scheduling optimization to explore how it can be
leveraged to provide grid services while also extending battery lifetime. Finally, we studied how
HPPs can be effectively used to deliver multiple grid services. This section is intended to provide
streamlined guidance on generic HPP modeling and its application in providing grid services.

3.1.1 Hybrid Power Plant Modeling
Here, we present an overview of modeling approaches for two primary configurations—direct
current (DC)-coupled and AC-coupled hybrid PV-battery systems—followed by a discussion of
modeling options and how these configurations can interface with independent system operator
(ISO) market platforms.

3.1.1.1 Direct Current-Coupled Hybrid Photovoltaic-Battery System
In a DC-coupled configuration, the PV array and the BESS share a common DC bus, feeding
into a single DC-to-AC converter, as shown in Figure 3. This configuration inherently aligns the
PV and storage systems, enabling more direct and efficient power transfer between them. DC
coupling can provide several key advantages:

 • Improved energy use. Because the battery is connected to the DC side, excess solar
 generation can be captured and stored with minimal conversion losses, potentially
 improving the overall round-trip efficiency.
 • Enhanced control and visibility. A DC-coupled system allows for sophisticated power
 flow control and real-time dispatch strategies that can be optimized for various
 operational objectives, such as maximizing production during high-value market periods
 or minimizing curtailment.
 • Reduced hardware costs. By sharing conversion and protection equipment, DC
 coupling often results in fewer components and potentially lower costs, simplifying the
 overall system design.

 Figure 3. Hybrid power plant configuration (DC coupled). Figure from AES
From a modeling perspective, DC-coupled systems require detailed representation of DC bus
voltage dynamics, inverter controls, and the interplay between PV output and battery
charge/discharge states. As a result, models must accurately capture nonlinear inverter behaviors,
thermal characteristics, and battery degradation to ensure reliable performance predictions.

3.1.1.2 Alternating Current-Coupled Hybrid Photovoltaic-Battery System
In AC-coupled configurations, the PV system and the BESS each have their own inverters and
are connected to a common AC bus, as shown in Figure 4. Although this might appear less
integrated than the DC-coupled approach, AC coupling offers the following distinct advantages:

 5

 • Flexibility in system expansion. Because each subsystem (PV and BESS) can be sized
 and added independently, AC coupling enables modular upgrades and simplifies scaling
 existing assets.
 • Standardized interconnection. AC-coupled systems often use well-established
 interconnection standards and off-the-shelf components, potentially streamlining design,
 installation, and maintenance.
 • Resilience and redundancy. With separate conversion paths, the system can maintain
 functionality if one inverter or component fails, enhancing overall reliability.

 Figure 4. Hybrid power plant configuration (AC coupled). Figure from AES
From a modeling standpoint, AC-coupled solutions require a careful representation of power
quality, frequency response, and synchronization of separate inverter controllers. Load flow
analysis, transient stability assessment, and harmonic studies must be integrated with battery
operational models and PV forecasts to ensure robust system performance under various
conditions.

HPP modeling in the scheduling model is detailed in Section 3.1.2.3.

3.1.1.3 Hybrid Power Plant Modeling Options and Interface With the Independent
 System Operator Market
A challenge in HPP modeling lies in developing frameworks that not only represent the technical
and operational aspects of the hybrid configuration but also account for market participation. As
ISOs increasingly allow hybrid resources to offer services, ranging from energy arbitrage and
capacity value to ancillary services (e.g., frequency regulation and voltage support), HPP models
will need to integrate market-based dispatch decisions, price signals, and compliance with grid
codes. Key modeling options and considerations include:

 • Co-optimization of PV and storage dispatch. Models that co-optimize the use of PV
 generation and battery storage can enhance market profitability. The enhancement
 involves dynamic programming or advanced optimization algorithms to determine
 charge/discharge profiles that maximize revenue under uncertain price forecasts.
 • Stochastic and probabilistic modeling. Incorporating uncertainty in solar irradiance,
 load demand, and market prices ensures that operational strategies remain robust under
 variable conditions. Probabilistic methods and scenario-based analyses are essential for
 designing optimal strategies.

 6

 • Advanced control and forecasting tools. SOC management, inverter control algorithms,
 and forecasting tools for solar production and energy prices must be tightly integrated.
 This coordination ensures that the system can dynamically respond to changes in
 weather, market conditions, and grid requirements.
 • Integration with ISO market platforms. Models should capture bidding and settlement
 processes, enabling operators to simulate participation in day-ahead and real-time
 markets, as well as ancillary service auctions. This involves adhering to market rules,
 incorporating ISO price signals, and evaluating the revenue impacts of various bidding
 strategies.
By combining technical modeling with market-based analysis, HPP operators can identify
optimal system sizing, configuration, and dispatch strategies (Yuan et al. 2024). The interplay
between DC and AC coupling, along with sophisticated control and forecasting capabilities,
provides a foundation to unlock the maximum value from hybrid systems.

3.1.2 Battery Degradation Modeling in Hybrid Power Plants
In this section, we present a generation scheduling model that incorporates PV-battery
hybridization in the unit commitment problem (Huang et al. 2023). Moreover, the cost function
of the HPP scheduling problem considers battery degradation with adjustable weights to balance
minimizing production costs and extended battery life, particularly when providing energy
arbitrage and ancillary services. Using a realistic island system as a test system, we demonstrate
that accounting for battery degradation in the scheduling problem can significantly extend
battery life with only minor additional production costs.

 power from the VRE as well as feedback on the SOC and output of the BESS from the
 time domain simulation. It strategically allocates the AGC resources, prioritizing the use
 of VRE.
 • Multi-timescale optimal operation framework with HPP. It comprises three modules
 that engage in bidirectional interaction with HPP: HPP-integrated DAUC, HPP-integrated
 real-time economic dispatch (RTED), and the AGC controller.

3.2 Stability-Constrained Optimal Scheduling
With well-structured HPP scheduling models and system-level scheduling models with grid
services now in place, the next key challenge is incorporating stability constraints into the
scheduling framework to ensure adequate stability resources in both DAUC and real-time
operations.

To address this challenge, we performed the following research activities: (1) quantifying the fast
frequency response (FFR) capability of IBRs to guide their integration into scheduling, (2)
incorporating frequency stability metrics at the system level to enable the measurement-based
inertia or other factors to play a role in the scheduling model, and (3) addressing reactive power-
driven stability issues by transitioning from DC-OPF to AC-OPF scheduling and quantifying the
resulting benefits and current limitations.

3.2.1 Fast Frequency Response Quantification for Inverter-Based Resources

3.2.1.1 Concept of Fast Frequency Response
With the increasing share of IBRs, power systems are experiencing lower frequency nadirs
following disturbances, such as generation trips and large renewable power variations.
Considering underfrequency load-shedding control, the lower frequency nadir might trip loads
and cause a power outage. To tackle these challenges and keep the frequency nadir above the
underfrequency load-shedding thresholds, one immediate solution is to leverage the capability of
existing IBRs to provide FFR. Following the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s
definition, FFR represents the active power injected to (or absorbed from) the grid in response to
variations in the measured grid frequency, aiming to increase the frequency nadir and reduce the
initial rate of change of frequency (ROCOF). IBRs can provide three main types of FFR:

 1. Step response. As shown in Figure 9(b), the step response boosts the active power by a
 predetermined fixed amount. This type of FFR can be triggered by either a contingency-
 related event or a frequency/ROCOF threshold. As displayed in Figure 10, based on the
 European Network of Transmission System Operators for Electricity (ENTSO-E)’s
 definition (ENTSO-E 2021), the step FFR can be further divided into activation,
 sustaining, deactivation, and recovery phases.
 2. Proportional response. As shown in Figure 9(c), IBRs provide proportional response,
 𝑃𝑃𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓2, following a grid frequency drop. Note that the proportional response here is
 reminiscent of the P/f droop control, or primary frequency control, in that the IBR output
 change is proportional to the frequency deviation.

 14

 3. Derivative response. The derivative response, 𝑃𝑃𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓3 , in Figure 9(d) allows IBRs to
 provide “synthetic inertia” to power systems. To achieve this, 𝑃𝑃𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓3 is controlled so that it
 is proportional to the time derivative of the measured grid frequency.

However, the heterogeneity in different types of IBR FFR poses a serious challenge to the
operator’s decision-making and the planners’ system planning, and there is a critical need to find
some metrics that can quantify the FFR capability in a unified manner. As a result, we tackle the
challenges of FFRs via two approaches. The first approach is to study the impacts of the key FFR
parameters on the resultant frequency dynamics (see Section 3.2.1.2). This approach will mainly
focus on the first type of FFR (i.e., step response). The other approach is to provide an accurate
and efficient method to analytically predict the frequency nadir of power systems (see Section
3.2.1.3). This approach will fully consider the impacts of three types of inverter-based FFR and
provides a fast prediction speed (Dong et al. 2023).

3.2.2 Frequency Stability-Constrained Scheduling for Island Power Systems
Transporting fuel to islands is expensive, so using locally available hydro, wind, and solar
resources is often the most economic option for island power systems; however, most of these
sources are IBRs. As a result, island grids face the stability challenges associated with high IBR
penetrations much earlier—and often more severely—than large, interconnected power systems.
We addressed these challenges by proposing two methods to develop the frequency stability
constraints, and then we incorporated those constraints into unit commitment models. The first
method focuses on data-driven methods for managing stability in an island grid, whereas the
second emphasizes analytical approaches for frequency nadir estimation and constraint
development.

3.2.3 Alternating Current Power Flow vs. Direct Current Power Flow Optimal
 Control
AC-OPF is a detailed and accurate power flow model that considers both active and reactive
power, voltage magnitudes, and transmission losses, making it computationally intensive but
essential for real-time operations, voltage stability, and reliability analysis. In contrast, DC
optimal power flow (DC-OPF) is a simplified, linearized approximation that only considers
active power while neglecting voltage magnitudes, reactive power, and losses; therefore, DC-
OPF is computationally efficient and widely used in market operations, congestion management,
and fast approximations, yet with less accuracy.

In practice, DC-OPF is commonly employed in electricity markets to determine the optimal
power dispatch and pricing for fast decision-making as well as in transmission planning where
simplified assumptions about power networks are valid. Meanwhile, AC-OPF’s usage is limited
by system operators due to the computational challenges for large-scale power systems in real-
time operations.

In this project, we propose using DC-OPF for day-ahead market scheduling, followed by AC-
OPF-based optimization for real-time adjustments and optimal voltage regulation. We developed
highly efficient AC-OPF algorithms so that we can leverage AC-OPF’s accurate performance
without being constrained by its computation bottleneck.

3.3 Versatile Grid-Forming Mode Control
In recent decades, GFM control has gained significant attention from both academia and industry
because of its advantages in voltage and frequency regulation, stability enhancement in weak
grids, and enabling 100% penetration of IBRs. Typically, GFM control is designed to emulate
the characteristics of synchronous generators. Common GFM control strategies include droop
control, virtual synchronous machine (VSM) control, and virtual oscillator control.

Among the three typical types of GFM control strategies, droop control is one of the most
popular designs in academic and industrial communities. This is not only because of its easy
implementation but also because of its ability to support versatile and flexible higher-level
control, aligning with plant-level control objectives.

To meet grid requirements, such as active or reactive power dispatch and voltage or frequency
regulation, and to simultaneously maintain the GFM capability, we developed a holistic bilevel
control strategy for the GFM-based HPP. In our proposed control strategy, the plant-level
controller corresponds to the grid-side demand, and inverter-level controllers are used to
establish the local voltage and frequency. At the plant-level controller, three operation modes are
available in the active loop: power regulation, DC bus voltage regulation, and synchronization.

3.3.1 Quantitative Small-Signal Stability Analysis of Grid-Forming Inverters
To ensure the stable operation of large-scale power systems, small-signal stability analysis is
usually conducted, and the stability margin and system damping can be obtained by exploring
the dominant eigenvalues. In conventional stability analysis, network dynamics are usually
ignored, as this simplification offers a reasonable trade-off between accuracy and computational
efficiency; however, as the integration of the IBRs continues to grow, power networks exhibit
increasingly complex low- and high-frequency components, and interactions among multiple
synchronous generators, IBRs, and loads become more complicated. If the network and IBR
control dynamics are neglected, the system stability evaluation may become inaccurate or even
incorrect, which may result in unforeseen power system instabilities and unnecessary failures.

To better understand the impact of IBR integration on system stability, especially when the entire
power system moves toward a 100% IBR-dominated power system with GFM inverters, we
developed a holistic framework for the small-signal stability analysis of inverter-intensive power

3.3.2 Comparative Small-Signal Stability Analysis of Grid-Forming and Grid-
 Following Inverters in Low-Inertia Power Systems
Existing grid-connected inverters are usually operated in GFL mode, which means that the
inverters will generate the required active and reactive power to the grid by following the grid-
side phase angle through the dedicated phase-locked loop (PLL). Even though the frequency-
watt or volt-volt ampere reactive (VAR) control can be further adopted to support the frequency
or voltage support, GFL inverters cannot independently establish the voltage and frequency. As a
result, they are unable to support island operations or black-start capability. On the contrary,
GFM inverters can independently establish voltage and frequency, which makes them ideal for
use in microgrids and distribution systems and a promising solution to address the challenges
posed by high penetrations of IBRs in transmission systems.

To better understand how different inverter control strategies will affect system stability, we
conducted a comparative study between GFL and GFM inverters by using the full EMT model of
both types. Further, we explored the coupling mechanism between GFL or GFM inverters and
synchronous generators by using modal analysis and participation factor analysis. Note that the
participation factor serves as a measure of the relative participation of one distinct state variable
in the specific mode, and vice versa. A higher participation factor suggests that a certain state
variable or its corresponding system component is highly involved in one oscillation mode. The

3.3.3 Flexible Operation Mode Transition in Hybrid Power Plants for Small-Signal
 Stability Enhancement
Compared to conventional synchronous generator–based power plants, renewable-based HPPs
present more significant challenges, including susceptibility to various disturbances, low inertia
support, high-frequency control dynamics, and others. These challenges can introduce stability
issues within individual power plants, and instability may even propagate throughout the entire
system. To ensure a sufficient stability margin and minimize the negative impacts caused by
these factors, the control parameters of HPPs should be carefully designed and well-tuned;
however, traditional tuning methods have limitations because of the inherent conflict between
control flexibility and the limited number and range of adjustable parameters. To address this
issue, we developed a holistic bilevel hierarchical control framework by leveraging the flexible
controllability and high-control-bandwidth characteristics of HPPs. This framework aims to
achieve the GFM functions of HPPs while simultaneously providing additional room to adjust
the system stability margin. In this framework, we used the primary level to establish the voltage
and frequency with droop characteristics, whereas the secondary level allows for multiple plant-
level operation modes (i.e., P-Q, P-V, and isochronous modes). Such a control strategy can be
leveraged to further enhance the system stability margin, and the detailed control diagram is

3.4 Measurement-Based Real-Time Inertia Estimation
Real-time awareness of effective inertia from synchronous generators and IBRs is essential for
system operators to decide the control and coordination strategy for HPPs; however, in the case
of 80%–100% instantaneous IBR operation, fast control dynamics from IBRs and variations
from renewables can make inertia estimation increasingly inaccurate. In this project, we applied
two innovative methods to extend the state-of-the-art, real-time inertia estimation methodologies:
(1) ambient data-based high-resolution real-time inertia estimation and (2) probing signal-based
real-time inertia estimation using HPPs. Both will leverage an advanced sensor technology called
the Universal Grid Analyzer (UGA), which was developed by the University of Tennessee,
Knoxville (UTK) team.

3.4.1 Universal Grid Analyzer Deployment at Two Island Grids
UGA is an embedded microprocessor system with GPS time synchronization and Ethernet
communications capability. The UGA measures power system voltage from 57.7- to 24-volt
outlets, which are commonly located in homes, offices, factories, and substations. It takes
voltage measurements with a high sampling rate and calculates phasors, harmonics (up to the
15th order), voltage sag, swell, and signal-to-noise ratio (in decibels) of a power system voltage
signal. Locally measured results are time-stamped using a highly accurate GPS clock and
streamed to UGA servers at a reporting rate from 10 frames per second up to 120 frames per
second. This system enables researchers to monitor the dynamics of interconnected power
systems and power quality in real time. Moreover, the noise analysis function allows researchers
to analyze the measurement accuracy of phasor measurements in an actual power grid
environment.

 36

For inertia monitoring of this project, the UGA is specially designed to measure the frequency
and ROCOF at a high data reporting rate (120 frames per second), while other power quality
monitoring functions are disabled to save hardware resources. The measurement accuracy of
high-reporting-rate frequency and ROCOF data was tested in the laboratory environment. Test
results show that the new UGA has a measurement accuracy higher than the IEEE C37.118.1
standard. This accurate and high-reporting-rate frequency and ROCOF measurements provide a
good foundation for inertia estimation in the Maui and Kaua‘i Island grids.

Two 120-Hz reporting rate UGAs, 2005 at Maui Island and 2006 at Kaua‘i Island, were
successfully deployed and have been sending data to the server at UTK from June 2021 to
November 2024. Two 1,440-Hz reporting rate point-on-wave UGAs, 1,844 at Maui Island and
1,857 at Kaua‘i Island, were also deployed and have been streaming data since summer 2022.
The two 120-Hz UGAs’ data were used to detect events and statistically analyze the noise and
spikes to determine the optimal filter design and curve-fitting window size. The ROCOF
estimation values can be significantly different when using varying ROCOF window sizes;
therefore, the ROCOF window size was carefully designed based on the actual UGA
measurement data collected from the deployed units.

3.4.3 Ambient Data-Based Inertia Estimation
Traditional inertia estimation relies on ringdown/ring-up event data, which does not happen
daily. This method can only achieve a very low-resolution inertia estimation, which is not
suitable for real-time scheduling. Some previous literature has revealed that inertia information
can be extracted from the micro-oscillation characteristics of ambient data under renewable
variations and load changes; thus, we proposed a real-time inertia estimation method based on
the sensitivity of inertia to micro-oscillations measured by high-data-rate synchrophasor sensors.
High-data-rate ambient measurements of both frequency and angle from UGAs are used as
inputs, and after preprocessing the measurements and necessary correction, the real-time inertia
can be estimated at a time resolution of less than 1 second. To validate feasibility, this method
was tested through a 1-year measurement dataset in the Western Electricity Coordinating
Council (WECC) system and Hawaii grid data from both simulations and the field.

3.4.3.2.1 Method Introduction
Based on the derivation shown in Figure 37, the system inertia is proportional to both the inverse
of the ambient frequency oscillation amplitude and the ambient power integral. In practice, to
avoid the difficulty of measuring the ambient power data directly, the system load level is used
to estimate the amplitude of the ambient power changes, as the higher load will have larger
ambient changes of power.

 Figure 37. Derivation of the physical equation for ambient data-based inertia estimation
Thus, we used the total system load and measured frequency data in the inertia estimation based
on physical equations. The proposed inertia estimation includes the following three steps:

 1. Feature extraction. The amplitude of the ambient frequency oscillation is extracted from
 the synchrophasor measurement.
 2. Equation model training. The extracted ambient signal feature combined with the
 system load, as well as the historical inertia, are used to fit the equation model to learn
 the quantitative relationship between the informative features and the system inertia.
 3. Real-time application. The trained physical equation is further implemented to estimate
 the system inertia using the ambient frequency measurements and load.

3.5 Oscillation Event Analysis for Kaua‘i Island
Oscillation events pose a significant threat to the stable operation of power systems because they
can result in poor power quality, damage power system components, or cause power outages.
The integration of energy sources such as wind and solar exposes power systems to high risks of
oscillation events. This is because, unlike conventional synchronous generators, wind and solar
are typically IBRs, which have vendor-specific controllers, different synchronization
mechanisms, and introduce heterogeneous multi-timescale dynamics into power systems.

In this project, we developed a real-world oscillation event analysis framework. This framework
combines both measurement- and model-based techniques to readily identify potential oscillation
sources, replay the oscillation event with numerical simulations, unveil the underlying oscillation
mechanism, and suggest mitigation methods for a wide range of oscillation events. Leveraging
our proposed oscillation event analysis framework, we completely addressed one 18- to 20-Hz
oscillation event that happened in Kaua‘i Island on Nov. 21, 2021 (see Figure 41). We first
present our proposed oscillation event analysis framework, and then we report our key results for
the Kaua‘i Island 18- to 20-Hz oscillation event analysis.

3.5.1 Oscillation Event Analysis Framework
As summarized in Figure 42, there are six key steps in our proposed oscillation event analysis
framework. The measurement-based analyses in steps 1–3 aim to collect field data, understand
the event, and identify the oscillation sources. Next, the model-based analyses in steps 4–6
unveil the oscillation mechanism and develop mitigation methods accordingly. The six steps are
as follows:

 • Step 1. Perform an overview of the event with utilities, IBR vendors, and/or original
 equipment manufacturers.

 46

 • Step 2. Collect the field data of the subsynchronous oscillations event (e.g., low-/high-
 speed digital fault recorder data, UGA data, and supervisory control and data acquisition
 [SCADA] data).
 • Step 3. Identify the oscillation source based on measurement-based methods, such as the
 dissipating energy flow and power flow-based method.
 • Step 4. Develop the EMT model to replay the subsynchronous oscillations event. In this
 step, we can leverage parallel simulations to accelerate the simulation speed.
 • Step 5. Develop the small-signal model and apply the small-signal analysis to understand
 the root cause of the subsynchronous oscillations. Also, we can perform frequency
 scanning studies while analyzing the event.
 • Step 6. Propose mitigation methods and validate them in the EMT simulation, PHIL
 experiment, or field test.

dominated power systems (Dong et al. 2025).

3.5.4 Mitigation Methods
With the high-fidelity EMT model built in step 4, we performed detailed EMT-based parameter
sensitivity studies as well as system-level small-signal analysis (step 6) to understand the root
cause of the oscillation event and develop effective mitigation methods. Our studies showed that
the Kaua‘i 18- to 20-Hz event arose from a set of suboptimal GFL parameters and settings: a
large frequency measurement delay, an aggressive P/f droop constant, a large PLL proportional
gain, and weak grid conditions. Bearing this in mind, we developed three mitigation methods for
the Kaua‘i 18- to 20-Hz oscillation event: (1) Adopt a less aggressive P/f droop constant in IBR1
and IBR2, (2) reduce the PLL gain in IBR1 and IBR2, and (3) upgrade the GFL to GFM control.

3.6 Demonstration in the Hawaii Grid
3.6.1 Evaluation at the National Laboratory of the Rockies’ Flatirons Campus

3.6.1.1 Power-Hardware-in-the-Loop Testing Structure
The PHIL testing was conducted at NLR’s Flatirons Campus, leveraging advanced hardware
resources, including megawatt-level PV arrays, BESS, and inverters. These tests integrate real-
time simulations of detailed power grid models with physical hardware to validate key system
performance metrics under realistic conditions.

The PHIL setup operates by interfacing the simulated grid model, running on the Real-Time
Digital Simulator (RTDS), with the hardware inverter through a controllable grid interface
(three-phase voltage amplifier). The controllable grid interface tracks the voltage signals in the
simulations and interacts with the inverter hardware in real time, while the resulting current
generated by the inverter hardware is measured and transmitted back to the simulated model.
This exchange of voltage signals and current measurements forms a closed-loop system,
ensuring a dynamic interaction between the simulated and physical components.

Two key use cases have been developed using the PHIL setup to validate the:

 • Frequency stability-constrained SAPPHIRE framework for Maui Island
 • Probing signal-based inertia estimation on Kaua‘i Island.

3.6.1.3.1 Case I: Synchronous Generators
This is the base case, with only synchronous generators and no IBRs online (except for the IBR
injecting the probing signal). The ground truth inertia of the system can be calculated from the
known inertia constants provided by KIUC. The purpose of this case is to benchmark the
accuracy of the proposed estimation method on a conventional power system with no IBRs.
There are a total of four subcases—A, B, C, and D—with each subcase having a different
combination of online synchronous generators and thus different system inertia values. As shown
in Table 18, it is evident that the proposed methods were able to accurately estimate the inertia
values of the system in all four subcases with an error of less than 6%.

3.6.1.3.2 Case II: Grid-Following Inverter-Based Resources
This case has all eight synchronous generators and the two GFL IBRs online. The ground truth
inertia of the system is still unknown, as the droop response of the IBRs does not conform to the
classical dynamics of synchronous inertia; however, when nameplate data of IBRs are available,
we can extract their inertia and droop values and use them as benchmark references. In our
newly proposed inertia + droop method, the fast power contribution is separated into inertia and
droop components. Specifically, the inertia value of VSM-based GFMs is available from the
nameplate, while the droop value is available for both droop-based GFMs and droop-enabled
GFLs. So, in this study, the ground truth droop of the system is calculated from the power-
frequency droop control settings of the IBRs. The purpose of this case is to investigate the
impact of the GFL IBRs on the accuracy of the proposed estimation method. There are a total of
three subcases—A, B, and C—with different IBR droop settings. As shown in Table 18, the
proposed estimation methods were able to accurately estimate the inertia value of the system for
all three subcases with an error of less than 10%. Additionally, the proposed methods were also
able to accurately estimate the droop constant of the GFL IBRs with an error of less than 10%.

3.6.1.3.3 Case III: Grid-Forming IBRs
This test case has all eight synchronous generators and the one GFM IBR online. The GFM IBR
is controlled as a virtual synchronous generator. It has an explicit inertia value and droop
constant specified in its controls; therefore, the ground truth inertia and droop of the system are
calculated from the virtual synchronous generator control settings. The purpose of this case is to
investigate the impact of the GFM IBRs on the accuracy of the proposed estimation method. As
shown in Table 18, the proposed estimation methods were able to accurately estimate the inertia
and droop value of the system with the GFM IBR present.

3.6.1.3.4 Case IV: Synchronous Generators and Inverter-Based Resources
This test case has all eight synchronous generators, the two GFL IBRs, and the one GFM IBR
online. Similarly, the benchmark value of “inertia” and “droop” have been extracted from the
nameplate values of IBRs. The purpose of this case is to validate the accuracy of the proposed
estimation method on a system with synchronous generators, GFL IBRs, and GFM IBRs. Again,
the proposed estimation methods were able to accurately estimate the inertia and droop value of
the system, as shown in Table 18. This validates the performance of the proposed estimation
method.

3.6.2.2 Large Dataset Processing
The data preprocessing is the most complicated part of this work because of the huge dataset.
The large dataset processing comprises three aspects:

 • Data preprocessing requires tackling unstable data quality, massive data volume, and
 temporal variability through careful cleaning and reorganization (e.g., adding millisecond
 labels, removing bad frames, correcting out‐of‐order records, converting time zones, and
 applying moving‐average filters).
 • By decomposing the data often hundreds of gigabytes—into frequency and time stamp
 segments (e.g., Float32/64), the workflow enables more efficient memory usage and
 supports tasks such as frequency deviation, visualization, and ROCOF analysis.
 • Finally, using multithreaded parallel computing, transitioning from MATLAB to Python,
 and leveraging high-performance-computing resources (256 GB, 104 cores) for chunk
 preprocessing alongside a high‐memory workstation (512 GB, 56 cores) for aggregation
 reduces computation time.

3.6.2.3 One-Year Data Analysis and Results
From the results, we observed that Kaua‘i Island only kept the average frequency almost the
same from 2021 to 2022, but the minimum-maximum range and 90% range are all reduced. This
indicates that the basic frequency pattern was improved for Kaua‘i Island from 2021 to 2022. To
further prove the observations and quantify the extent of the improvement for Kaua‘i Island, we
studied the probability distribution of the frequency deviation for the four scenarios (as shown in
Figure 53).

Between 2021 and 2022, the probability of frequency variation on Kaua‘i Island within the -10-
megahertz [mHz] to 10-mHz range rose from 35.86% to 43.48%. This increase suggests that the
GFM inverters are providing effective damping for grid operations, reducing the overall
frequency variation, and improving the frequency quality. To confirm that this improvement is
not because of weather differences across years, we performed the same analysis for Maui,
revealing similar probability distributions of frequency deviation between 2021 and 2022.

In addition, Figure 53 highlights another notable difference between Maui and Kaua‘i: Kaua‘i
exhibits two distinct peaks in its frequency deviation distribution. According to KIUC, this is

 58

because the island employs two frequency set points—60 Hz during daytime and 60.1 Hz at
night to account for the disabling of large solar resources after sunset. To quantify the
improvements resulting from the conversion from GFL to GFM on Kaua‘i, the study compared
frequency deviation probabilities in four ranges: -5 mHz to 5 mHz, -10 mHz to 10 mHz, -17
mHz to 17 mHz, and -36 mHz to 36 mHz. These specific ranges reflect common deadbands used
by primary frequency control units: 17 mHz in the Electric Reliability Council of Texas and 36
mHz in both WECC and the Eastern Interconnection in North America.

4 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
4.1 Accomplishments
The SAPPHIRE project delivered a comprehensive framework of models, controls, and
demonstrations that directly address the stability and reliability challenges of operating power
systems with very high shares of IBRs.

Key accomplishments include:

 1. Development of the SAPPHIRE hierarchical control framework that integrates HPPs into
 both grid-level scheduling and plant-level operations. This framework couples stability-
 constrained AC-OPF/DC-OPF with advanced GFM plant controls, enabling HPPs to
 provide energy, frequency, and voltage support in coordination with other grid assets.
 Using the Maui grid as a test system, the chance-constrained AC-OPF scheduler was
 shown to effectively mitigate the potential voltage violation issues inherent in
 conventional DC-OPF approaches, guaranteeing 99.9% compliance within the voltage
 control range.
 In addition, the framework also incorporated a new battery degradation model,
 introducing a control parameter, K, to identify an operational “sweet spot” that extends
 BESS lifetimes to 7–8 years while maintaining low overall system costs.
 Finally, SAPPHIRE advanced methods to quantify IBR contributions to frequency
 response using both analytical and machine learning-based approaches. By embedding
 these approaches into the scheduler as stability constraints, the framework ensured a
 robust dispatch that can ensure the grid’s frequency stability, with 99.8% of frequency
 nadirs (data-driven method) and 98% (analytical method) remaining above reliability
 thresholds under the largest N-1 contingency. The stability-constrained scheduler proved
 its effectiveness in this project and has been included in HECO’s recent strategic plan.
 2. Advances in the modeling and control of hybrid plants, including detailed AC- and DC-
 coupled HPP dynamic models, battery degradation modeling for long-term economic
 operation, mode switch of HPP for enhancing grid stability, and demonstration of HPPs
 providing secondary frequency regulation across multiple timescales. Specifically, these
 advances include new methods for measurement-based real-time inertia estimation,
 including both ambient data and probing signal approaches. These methods were
 validated through simulation, PHIL testing, and the field deployment of UGA sensors on
 Maui Island and Kaua‘i Island. For the ambient data method, a machine learning
 approach achieved an average inertia estimation error of less than 5% in the WECC
 system. For islanded grids, an enhanced hybrid method applied to the KIUC and Maui
 systems achieved approximately 93% accuracy. For the probing signal method, it can
 achieve greater than 95% accuracy by using lab testing data. This work produced two
 software records and multiple publications, providing the industry with ready-to-use tools
 for inertia monitoring that can be further adapted for energy management
 system/SCADA platforms at the grid’s control room.
 3. A first-of-its-kind oscillation event analysis framework for inverter-dominated systems,
 applied to the November 2021 18- to 20-Hz oscillation event on Kaua‘i Island. The

 65

 framework combined field data, EMT modeling, oscillation source analysis, and EMT-
 based small-signal analysis to identify root causes and validate effective mitigation
 methods, including converting GFL to GFM inverters. Our studies show that the Kaua‘i
 18- to 20-Hz event arises from a set of suboptimal GFL parameters and settings: a large
 frequency measurement delay, an aggressive P/f droop constant, a large PLL proportional
 gain, and weak grid conditions.
 This work delivers practical industrial guidance on how to rapidly diagnose IBR-driven
 oscillations and provides a systematic analysis procedure—from root-cause analysis to
 event replay and mitigation through control parameter tuning or a technology upgrade.
 The study has already influenced industry practice, which is being incorporated into
 three industrial reports. Moreover, it leads to a deployable tool, called the Grid
 Oscillation Analyzer (GOAL), that is ready for integration into KIUC’s control room
 under an ongoing project funded by the Solar Energy Technologies Office that is
 directly enhancing operator situational awareness and grid stability.
In addition to the previously mentioned accomplishments, this work involved three high-impact
field demonstrations in Hawaii:

 1. The GFM projects on Kaua‘i Island demonstrate a seamless integration of GFM inverters
 into the existing power grid, significantly enhancing grid stability and reliability of the
 grid operation. The projects also proved interoperability across technologies, with the
 grid successfully operating one GFL HPP, one VSM-based GFL HPP, and one droop-
 based GFL HPP in parallel.
 Following the 18- to 20-Hz oscillation event in 2021, a second GFL plant was
 completely converted to GFM in August 2022. Analysis of 2 years of operational data
 (2021–2023) provided the first field-based quantification of GFM benefits: (1)
 frequency quality improvement: The probability of frequency variation within the ±10-
 mHz range on Kaua‘i increased from 35.9% to 43.5%. This improvement demonstrates
 that GFM inverters deliver effective damping, reduce frequency fluctuations, and
 improve frequency quality. (2) Stability enhancement during the contingency: Another
 similar plant trip event occurred in Kaua‘i on April 2, 2023, which showed that after
 converting the second GFL to GFM, it successfully avoided the grid oscillations by the
 GFM inverter as it enhanced the grid strength and provided additional damping.
 This demonstration provided valuable field data that strengthens industry confidence in
 deploying GFM inverters at scale. This ability can potentially accelerate the adoption of
 GFM technology in future grid deployments in the United States.
 2. Real-time inertia monitoring at KIUC validated the probing-based estimation for operator
 use by injecting real-power probing signals into the KIUC grid. Multiple tests and a 24-
 hour continuous demonstration were conducted from 12 p.m. on Aug. 1 to 11 a.m. on
 Aug. 2, 2024 (Hawaii local time), revealing several key insights: (1) Frequency noise,
 load inertia contributions, and synchronous generator ramping actions and IBR controls
 all affect inertia estimation accuracy. (2) Traditional inertia estimation methods can be
 varied due to uncertain and time-varying IBR contribution values, creating challenges for
 quantifying FFR from IBRs in real operations. (3) Our newly proposed inertia + droop
 method provides a pathway for more robust IBR inertia quantification.

 66

 3. This demonstration represents a major step forward, moving real-time inertia monitoring
 from theory and lab testing into real-world grid operations. It has also paved a pathway to
 use this technology in the real world. By field-testing at KIUC, the technology has given
 operators direct visibility into both system-level and device-level inertia, building the
 confidence needed for wider adoption.
 This demonstration successfully achieved secure operation of Kaua’i with 88.5% from
 solar PV and 11.5% from hydropower (total 100% renewables) for a consecutive 3 hours
 and 40 minutes. Further, it provides the evidence that GFM hybrid PV + battery plants
 (GFL HPPs) can maintain stable island operation without synchronous generation,
 directly validating GFMs’ role as critical enablers for reliable and stable grids.
All three demonstrations can provide stakeholders with real-world, long-term operational data,
thereby enhancing confidence in GFM technology’s capabilities and encouraging its wider
adoption in the power grid.

4.2 Conclusions
The project demonstrated that hybrid PV-BESS plants, when equipped with advanced GFM
controls and integrated into stability-aware scheduling frameworks, can provide essential grid
services to support reliable and stable grid operation. The ability to monitor inertia in real time,
mitigate oscillation risks, and validate secure high-IBR operation provides a practical blueprint
for utilities and system operators as they move toward a high-IBR future.

SAPPHIRE’s outcomes reduce uncertainty around the stability of high-IBR grids; accelerate the
adoption of hybrid plants as well as GFMs as stability-enhancing resources; and directly inform
utility practices, industry standards, and U.S. Department of Energy grid modernization
strategies.

5 Path Forward
5.1 Technical Pathway Forward
Building on these results, several paths forward are recommended:

 • Modeling and simulation improvements:
 o Scale up the stability-constrained AC-OPF framework for large interconnections,
 leveraging advanced computing technology to ensure real-time applicability.
 o Refine and expand the development of stability constraints to address other
 stability-related issues, such as small-signal stability, grid strength limitations, and
 voltage stability. Incorporate the latest stability metrics into production-grade
 scheduling and planning tools.
 • Grid-forming controls at scale:
 o Address remaining gaps in oscillation risk analysis for wide-area frequency
 oscillation events and the interaction of heterogeneous IBR controls, such as the
 potential oscillations among GFL and GFM, GFM and GFM, and GFM and
 synchronous machines.
 o Investigate coordinated control strategies for fleets of GFL and GFM HPPs to
 manage stability across large-scale interconnected systems.
 o Quantify the benefits of GFM controls for grid strength using improved grid
 strength metrics.
 • Enhanced real-time monitoring and mitigation technologies:
 o Explore artificial intelligence-driven approaches for real-time dynamic security
 assessment that can directly predict grid stability margins without requiring
 extensive transient simulations.
 o Advance inertia estimation algorithms to provide more accurate, real-time
 assessments of system and regional inertia, including quantifying IBR FFR
 contributions on the inertial timescale.
 o Transition the offline oscillation analysis framework into a real-time tool to help
 grid operators rapidly identify oscillation sources and deploy effective mitigation
 strategies as events occur.
 • Enhanced field validation:
 o Extend real-time inertia-monitoring deployments beyond Hawaii to other islands
 and continental grids with high inverter penetrations.
 o Expand field demonstrations with AES, HECO, and KIUC to longer-duration and
 more diverse operating scenarios, including implementing a digital twin for
 stability-assured grid operation under the Solar Energy Technologies Office-
 funded project titled “Digital Twin Addressing Multi-scale Operational Needs of
 IBR-rich Grids (DIAMOND).”
 • Industry adoption and standards integration:

 68

 o Collaborate with utilities, ISOs, and original equipment manufacturers to integrate
 SAPPHIRE-developed models, control strategies, and inertia-monitoring tools
 into industry practice
 o Contribute to ongoing standards and guideline efforts (e.g., North American
 Electric Reliability Corporation, IEEE, Energy Systems Integration Group,
 Universal Interoperability for Grid-Forming Inverters [UNIFI] Consortium) to
 ensure that lessons learned from Hawaii demonstrations inform requirements for
 GFM inverters, inertia monitoring, and oscillation mitigation.

5.2 Potential Technology Transfer and Commercialization
The SAPPHIRE project has advanced a suite of software tools, models, and field-validated
methods that are potentially transferable to the power engineering industry. The following
pathways outline how these outcomes can move from research into deployment:

 • Software tool licensing, including:
 o Inertia estimation tools. We generated software licenses for inertia estimation
 tools (e.g., ambient data and probing signal methods, SWR-25-06 and SWR-24-
 28) so that they are available for licensing and integration into commercial energy
 management systems, SCADA systems, and wide-area measurement system
 platforms.
 o Real-time grid oscillation analysis tool. The aim is to work on the software
 license for the Grid Oscillation Analyzer Tool (GOAL) and transition it from
 being an offline research tool to a real-time monitoring tool, with deployment
 planned in KIUC’s control room under the DIAMOND project.
 • Demonstrations and deployment, including:
 o A stability-constrained scheduler demonstration with KIUC. The MIDAS tool
 will be deployed in the KIUC control room as the core of a digital twin,
 demonstrated in partnership with the utility to address stability challenges and
 battery-PV coordination for high-IBR grid operation. The demonstrations and
 lessons learned will serve as replicable playbooks for other island grids—
 including Hawai‘i Island, Puerto Rico, and the Caribbean—accelerating the path
 toward secure and resilient grid operation. The pathway could be from field U.S.
 Department of Energy-funded demonstrations, to utility pilot projects, and then to
 vendor product integration.

5.3 Challenges and Lessons Learned
A fundamental understanding of IBR-dominated grids is still needed. As the share of IBRs
increases, many traditional power system concepts must be revisited, clarified, and, in some
cases, redefined. Terms such as “inertia,” “grid strength,” and “frequency,” and so on, which
have been well established in the past, now carry multiple interpretations depending on the
perspective of researchers and practitioners. This disparity can lead to confusion, wherein the
same words are used but refer to different things. For example, in traditional power systems, the
term “inertia” primarily refers to the kinetic energy stored in synchronous generator rotating
masses, but because IBRs can provide fast power responses at similar timescales, the
interpretation of “inertia” becomes more complex. Traditional methods might misrepresent IBR

 69

contributions because their “synthetic inertia” is variable and control dependent. This challenge
highlights the need to revisit and refine fundamental concepts from the customer’s perspective
(e.g., grid planners and operators), ensuring that they are consistently defined, practically
measurable, and easily understood by engineers working across both power system and power
electronics domains.

Standardization can be further strengthened for many practical engineering procedures.
At present, publications often fall short of systematically summarizing and disseminating
fundamentals, but they are essential lessons for engineers, even for similar events that have
repeatedly occurred in the field. Fortunately, industry is already taking action. For example, in
oscillation event analysis, the Energy Systems Integration Group established a task force to
summarize practical methods leveraging the frameworks proposed in SAPPHIRE. We expect
this work to yield standardized guidance on how such studies should be conducted.

The industry moves from offline analysis to online applications. Traditional power system
operations have relied on offline simulations complemented by online monitoring. With
increasing IBR integrations, several offline methods, such as oscillation source location, are now
being piloted for real-time application in control rooms, though adoption remains limited. More
sophisticated tools, such as small-signal stability analysis, require further evaluation for field
usage. A promising direction is a hybrid approach that integrates field measurements (e.g.,
SCADA) with digital twin models, though challenges remain in handling black-box models of
IBRs that bring challenges to model-based eigenvalue calculations. Under the DIAMOND
project, we will explore these applications and address the key challenges to enable broader
online adoption.

Ensuring scalability from island systems to large interconnections is needed. Island grids
serve as valuable live laboratories for testing advanced technologies, as they are relatively small
and easier to manage; however, scaling from island systems to large interconnections requires
significant additional effort and rigorous validation. For instance, although an AC-OPF-based
scheduler has proven effective for island grids, solving the AC-OPF problem within a reasonable
time frame remains a major challenge when applied to large interconnections, particularly for
operational planning and real-time operations.

In summary, SAPPHIRE has provided the technical foundation for the stability-augmented
operation of high-IBR grids. The next step is to transition these innovations from research to
practice through expanded demonstrations, further development, and deployment in U.S. utility
and ISO operations.
