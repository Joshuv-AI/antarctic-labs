Incentivizing Data Trading via Profit Reallocation


Abstract
Data trading is a central approach to data circulation, yet data markets remain far less active than expected. A primary bottleneck is the lack of effective economic incentives. Existing approaches often treat data as traditional goods, overlooking its inherent replicability and resale potential: buyers can replicate and resell data products, thereby forming transaction chains. Upstream sellers do not benefit from downstream resales and thus have limited incentives to sell. However, the impact of data resale on market performance remains insufficiently understood. To address this gap, we propose a sequential, chain-based data trading model that explicitly captures data resale. The model reflects data flows in settings such as LLM training and strategic decision-making. We integrate this model with a profit reallocation mechanism. By reallocating profits along the transaction chain, this mechanism ensures upstream sellers benefit from downstream resales. We next develop efficient algorithms, including a polynomial-time exact algorithm for the discrete model and an FPTAS for the continuous model, to compute its sequential equilibria. We theoretically show that profit reallocation expands trade and improves social welfare under certain conditions, and empirical results demonstrate that our mechanism increases transaction volume by 120.0% and social welfare by 50.4% in synthetic environments, compared with the baseline mechanism that does not reallocate profits.

2.2
Profit Reallocation Mechanism

A
profit reallocation mechanism
is formally defined by a nonnegative matrix
{\bm{t}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times(n+1)}
, which specifies how profits from trades are shared among players.
Specifically,
t_{ij}
is the proportion of profit from
trade

i
allocated to player
j

i
occurs at price
p_{i-1}
, the mechanism first charges player
i

p_{i-1}
and then reallocates this profit among players
0
to
i-1
. Player
j
receives
p_{i-1}\cdot t_{ij}
from the mechanism.
The model is therefore specified by
\mathrm{Model}=(n,{\bm{t}},\mathcal{F})
.

To ensure the proposed mechanism is well-defined and economically viable, we impose the following two natural conditions:

Definition 2.5

(Budget Feasibility)
.

The total proportion of profit redistributed from any trade
i
must not exceed the realized profit. Formally, for all
i\in[n]
,
\sum_{j=0}^{n}t_{ij}\leq 1
.

Definition 2.6

(Past Only)
.

Only players who participate in trades
prior to
trade
i
are eligible to receive a portion of the reallocated profit from trade
i
. This implies
t_{ij}=0
for all
j\geq i
.

The baseline mechanism, in which the seller (player
i-1
) retains the entirety of the profit in trade
i
, constitutes a special case where
t_{i,i-1}=1
and
t_{ij}=0
for all
j\neq i-1
.

Comparison to TBDS
[
39
]
.

Transaction-Based Data Sharing (TBDS) reallocates profits along a chain using a single parameter

when player

and
t_{i0}=\alpha^{i-1}

4
Equilibrium Comparison between Profit Reallocation Mechanisms

This section establishes key results about the equilibrium comparison between profit reallocation mechanisms.

Theorem

4.4
is our main finding: compared with the baseline mechanism (
t_{ij}=\bm{1}\{i=j+1\}
), profit reallocation mechanism expands the equilibrium trade event.

There may be multiple sequential equilibria under a mechanism
{\bm{t}}
, and some may not satisfy the simplification established in
Section

3.1
.
To make the equilibrium under
{\bm{t}}
well-defined, we consider the simplified equilibrium (
\tilde{{\bm{p}}}^{*},\tilde{{\bm{s}}}^{*}
) established by
Lemmas

3.2
and
3.3
, and require the tie-breaking rule to be independent of the mechanism
{\bm{t}}
.
This convention is primarily technical, but it is also behaviorally natural: players use the single-variable strategies characterized in
Section

3.1
rather than more complex history-dependent strategies, and their tie-breaking rules do not depend on the mechanism.
To ensure tractability, we also impose two assumptions:

Assumption 4.1

(Positivity)
.

Positivity requires
\Pr_{{\bm{v}}\sim\mathcal{F}}[v_{i}>0]=1
for all
0\leq i\leq n
.

Assumption 4.2

(Homogeneity)
.

Let
2.1
hold.
Homogeneity requires that
\forall\alpha,w,v>0
and
{i\in[n]}
, we have
F_{i}(\alpha v|\alpha w)=F_{i}(v|w)
.

The Positivity assumption states that the value generated from data always exceeds the processing cost.
The Homogeneity assumption is widely used in economic models, especially market models
[
33
, §6.2]
,
[
20
, §3.2.3]
.
It captures settings in which production output scales linearly with the input.

4.1
Local Comparison between Mechanisms

We first compare the equilibria induced by two profit reallocation mechanisms
{\bm{t}}^{1}
and
{\bm{t}}^{2}
that differ locally. This result provides a building block for comparing any profit reallocation mechanism with the baseline mechanism by connecting them through finitely many locally different mechanism pairs.

Theorem

is the main theorem of
Section

. It shows that increasing future reallocated profit and decreasing one-shot profit for player

expands the equilibrium trade event of trade

while leaving other equilibrium trade events unchanged.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let the valuation distribution satisfy
2.1
,
4.1
and
4.2
.
Fix player index

. Let

be two profit reallocation mechanisms that differ only in how revenue is allocated to player
i
.
Specifically: (a)
t^{1}_{j,i^{\prime}}=t^{2}_{j,i^{\prime}}
for all
j>i^{\prime}>i
; (b)
t^{1}_{j,i}\leq t^{2}_{j,i}
for all
j\geq i+2
; (c)
t^{1}_{i+1,i}\geq t^{2}_{i+1,i}
; (d)
t^{k}_{j,i^{\prime}}=0
for all

and

unless

Let

be the corresponding equilibria for
{\bm{t}}^{k}

, and define

as the set of valuation profiles under which player

is willing to buy:

4.2
Comparison with Baseline Mechanism

We now leverage

to compare the baseline mechanism
{\bm{t}}
(where

We prove that profit reallocation mechanism weakly expands the equilibrium trade event of each trade

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
2.1
,
4.1
and
4.2

Let

5
Experimental Evaluation

This section presents computational experiments that complement the theoretical analysis and evaluate the empirical effects of profit reallocation.
The experiments are designed to address the following questions that are not fully resolved by the theory:

(a)

To what extent do profit reallocation mechanisms improve upon the baseline mechanism?

(b)

Do these improvements persist when
4.2
and
4.1
are not satisfied?

(c)

Are the improvements robust across valuation distributions
\mathcal{F}
and chain lengths

?

(d)

Does profit reallocation adversely affect the utilities of individual players?

We construct four finite-support valuation distributions

Profit reallocation mechanisms.

from

to estimate these expectations.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presents a game-theoretic, chain-based data trading model together with a profit reallocation mechanism that aims to incentivize trades.
We develop a polynomial-time algorithm and an FPTAS for computing equilibria under the proposed mechanism.
Both theoretical analysis and empirical evaluation show that profit reallocation increases transaction volume and improves expected social welfare compared with a baseline mechanism that does not reallocate profits.

Future work includes extending the chain-based model to trees or DAGs, studying alternative solution concepts beyond sequential equilibria, such as anti-collusion and sybil-proofness, as well as extending posted-price dynamic to more general dynamics.

A line of works focus on ensuring the security, privacy, trustworthiness, and tractability of data utilization through federated learning

For instance, in training of Large Language Models (LLMs), raw data on the internet may be processed to produce clean data

[

Our study differs from these works in many aspects. First, the traded object is replicable data rather than non-replicable traditional goods, so the seller can sell the data without losing it.

Lastly, data trading often occurs on a platform rather than a social network; players typically do not know one another, making collusion unlikely to form between players.

Price Simplification.

Before describing the main algorithm, we first establish the finite search space for the seller’s optimal price.
For a fixed player

, consider the finite set
\mathbf{P_{i}}
of player
i+1
’s possible threshold values:

\mathbf{P_{i}}=\big\{\,u^{*}(i{+}1,\ell):\ \ell\in[L]\,\big\}.

(17)

A critical observation is that
\mathbf{P_{i}}
must contain player
i
’s optimal price as long as
\mathbf{P_{i}}
consists of at least one non-negative element. In the opposite case that all elements in
\mathbf{P_{i}}
are negative,
p_{i}=0
is one of the optimal prices for player
i

The reason is as follows:
(a) If all elements in
\mathbf{P_{i}}
are negative, whatever price
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
player
i
sets, player
i+1
will not buy the data from player
i
. (Recall that player
i+1
with
v_{i+1}=w_{l}
will buy the data iff

.) Therefore, player
i
always gets
0
profit from all subsequent trades. As a consequence, all prices

are indifferent to player
i
, and
w.l.o.g.

p_{i}=0
is one of the optimal prices.
(b) If some elements in
\mathbf{P_{i}}
are non-negative, let the maximum value in
\mathbf{P_{i}}
be
u^{*}(i+1,l_{0})
. It’s obvious that setting
p_{i}>u^{*}(i+1,l_{0})
is weakly dominated by setting
p_{i}\leq u^{*}(i+1,l_{0})
, as player
i
guarantees zero profit in the former case, and non-negative profit in the latter case. Therefore, there is
p^{*}_{i}\leq u^{*}(i+1,l_{0})
be player
i
’s optimal price.
In addition, increasing
p^{*}_{i}
up to the next value in
\mathbf{P_{i}}
does not affect player
i+1
’s buying action and weakly increases player

’s revenue from trade
i+1
, consequently weakly increasing player
i
’s utility.
Therefore, we conclude that there is optimal
p^{*}_{i}\in\mathbf{P_{i}}
.
This reduces the search for

from a continuous space to a discrete set of size at most

.

Backward Dynamic Program.

The high-level idea of

,
with

as the terminal condition. We also define auxiliary variable

.

means that all elements in

are negative, and consequently we have

.

means that player

with

achieves optimal utility by setting price

For each player

and each valuation

, we first check whether
\mathbf{P_{i}}
contains non-negative elements: (a) If all elements in

in

.
(b)
\mathbf{P_{i}}
contains non-negative elements, and we let

We enumerate over the

and player

\displaystyle u(i,k,k^{\prime}):=g_{i}(w_{k},u^{*}(i+1,k^{\prime})).

where

The optimal pricing strategy is then chosen by maximizing this gain function value over

\displaystyle l^{*}(i,k)\in\arg\max_{k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}}\ u(i,k,k^{\prime}),

\displaystyle u^{*}(i,k)

\displaystyle=u(i,k,l^{*}(i,k)),\ \ p^{*}(i,k)=u^{*}(i+1,l^{*}(i,k)).

We also define the expected one-shot profit as an auxiliary value, which will be used in the remaining steps:

Overall, by nesting the Forward DP within the Backward DP,

The running time is:

We first show the proof idea of

The algorithm consists of three steps: (i) discretize the continuous valuation support and construct a discretized model, (ii) compute the exact sequential equilibrium of the discretized model by

The core difficulty lies in proving a polynomial approximation bound of the output strategy profile with the discretization precision

Interval Discretization.

We select a discretization precision

To construct the induced discretized model, we define the one-step transition probabilities

Appendix D
Experimental Details

We describe how we use SciPy to learn an optimal profit reallocation mechanism. All experiments were run on CPUs with a single MacBook.

Firstly, for each player index
i
, we apply a softmax to a real vector
x_{i,0:i-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{i}
so that
t_{i,0:i-1}=\mathrm{softmax}(x_{i,0:i-1})
automatically satisfies
\sum_{j}t_{ij}=1
. This reparameterization turns the constrained problem into an unconstrained one with variables
{\bm{x}}=\{x_{ij}\}_{0\leq j<i}
.

The model representation,
Algorithm

1
, and the zero-th order optimization are all deterministic for fixed inputs: the only stochastic ingredient in the welfare objective is the estimation of expected social welfare w.r.t.
{\bm{v}}\sim\mathcal{F}
.
For each model, we draw
M=10{,}000
independent valuation profiles and average social welfare within these profiles; this yields a high-precision estimate of expected social welfare.

Then, given
{\bm{x}}
, we map it to a profit reallocation mechanism
{\bm{t}}
and call
Algorithm

1
to obtain the sequential equilibrium. We evaluate that equilibrium on the same
M
profiles to approximate the expected social welfare. The pipeline from
{\bm{x}}
to expected social welfare is denoted as
\mathrm{SW}({\bm{x}})
, which is the objective function. We then use
differential evolution
, a zeroth-order algorithm
packaged in scipy, to find the maximum point of
\mathrm{SW}({\bm{x}})
. We use
50
iterations, population size
5
, and initialize from the AVE mechanism.
The running time is approximately one minute for model with
n=5
.

Appendix E
Potential Social Impact

By incorporating profit reallocation mechanisms to real data markets, data trading is likely to become more active, consequently facilitating data circulation and data utilization.
As is suggested by experiments, The interests of a small group of participants may be affected, especially those upstream (
e.g.
, data owner) in the data transaction chain.
Additionally, this work does not directly resolve the risk (
e.g.
, data leakage, data abuse) arose from inappropriate data usage. The promotion of data trading may also increase such risks.
