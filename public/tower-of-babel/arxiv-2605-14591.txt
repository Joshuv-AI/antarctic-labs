Privacy Auditing with Zero (0) Training Run
Tudor Cebere, Mathieu Even, Linus Bleistein, Aurélien Bellet

Abstract
Privacy auditing provides empirical lower bounds on the differential privacy parameters of learning algorithms. Existing methods, however, require interventional access to the training pipeline, either to retrain multiple times or to randomize data inclusion. This is often infeasible for large deployed systems such as foundation models. We introduce Zero-Run privacy auditing, a post-hoc framework for auditing models using two fixed datasets: examples known to be training-set members and examples known to be non-members. In this observational regime, membership is no longer randomized; instead, member and non-member data often differ in distribution, so membership inference scores may reflect a distribution shift rather than algorithmic leakage. Drawing on ideas from causal inference, we formalize this confounding effect and propose two complementary corrections that yield valid privacy audits. Our first approach models the combined effect of distribution shift and algorithmic leakage as an adaptive composition, producing conservative global corrections. Our second approach conditions on observed data and adjusts pointwise membership guesses, yielding sharper instance-dependent bounds. Experiments on synthetic data and large-scale models show that Zero-Run auditing enables practical privacy evaluation when retraining or controlled data insertion is infeasible.

Introduction

As AI systems increasingly process sensitive data, mitigating privacy leakage (i.e., the inadvertent disclosure of training examples) has become a critical priority. This risk is exacerbated by high-capacity foundation models trained across various modalities
[
39
,
21
,
32
,
7

18

24

[

.

A countermeasure to these risks is
differential privacy
(DP)
[
12
]
, the de facto standard for privacy-preserving algorithms. By bounding the impact of any individual’s data on the result, DP protects against privacy attacks that infer sensitive information from released outputs. However, despite offering worst-case guarantees over the privacy leakage, its adoption in large-scale training pipelines is limited due to steep utility penalties
[
20
]
and the prohibitive cost of overhauling existing data-processing infrastructure
[

.

Motivated by these practical barriers,

offers an empirical complement to DP by lower-bounding an algorithm’s privacy parameters
[
9
,
19
,
29
,
5
]
. By probing a model’s behavior on its training data, audits can expose implementation flaws
[
40
]
, identify misconfigured privacy mechanisms
[
6
]
, or demonstrate that theoretical guarantees are tight (i.e., the auditing lower bound closely matches the DP upper bound)
[
30
,
29
]
. In contrast to worst-case theoretical upper bounds, auditing quantifies empirical leakage for a specific model architecture, dataset, and threat model, yielding deployment-relevant evidence for privacy claims, compliance, and risk assessment
[
15
]
.

While traditional privacy auditing requires repeatedly retraining models on randomized datasets
[
19
,
29
,
5
]
, recent advances condense this requirement to a single
randomized
training run
[
37
,
25
]
. Yet, even this approach fails to scale to modern AI models. Their training consumes thousands of GPU days and relies on rigid, distributed data pipelines. Large language models perfectly capture this dilemma. Despite being trained on vast web corpora loaded with sensitive data, their immense scale, opaque data mixtures, and blackbox deployments render privacy auditing nearly impossible. Consequently, researchers and regulators lack tools to verify privacy guarantees, relying on trust and the voluntary cooperation of model providers. This creates an accountability gap: the systems posing the greatest privacy risks are the most insulated from independent privacy evaluation.

Contributions.
To address this gap, we introduce a
Zero-Run
auditing framework that enables auditors to assess the privacy leakage of an algorithm
post-hoc from its release
, without modifying the algorithm or data pipeline. We ground our approach in a fundamental analogy to causal inference. Existing privacy audits based on randomized data insertion are inherently interventional—analogous to randomized controlled trials—which avoid confounding by design. In contrast, our Zero-Run framework is the observational counterpart, estimating privacy leakage from static member and non-member datasets. Since these datasets often follow different underlying distributions, this distribution shift acts as a confounding factor
[
33
,
42
]
. Guided by this analogy, our main contributions are as follows:

1.

Formalizing Zero-Run Auditing (
Section

3
).
We formalize the post-hoc auditing regime where the auditor relies on disjoint datasets of known members and non-members. We demonstrate that naive audits on such data can substantially overestimate privacy leakage: membership inference attacks can succeed merely by exploiting distributional shift rather than algorithmic leakage, causing standard procedures to wrongly attribute this spurious signal to the model.
We explicitly connect this setting to observational causal inference, where valid correction requires overlap (positivity) between the member and non-member distributions.

2.

Privacy Leakage Correction (
Sections

4
and
5
).
We extend One-Run analyses
[
37
,
25
]
to the post-hoc regime, correcting distribution shift effects on empirical privacy curves via:

•

The Composition Viewpoint (
Section

4
)
models the observed leakage as an adaptive composition of a distribution-shift mechanism and the audited algorithm, yielding a deconvolution-based estimate of privacy leakage, albeit a conservative one under severe distribution shift.

•

The Conditional Viewpoint (
Section

5
)
characterizes pointwise privacy leakage conditional on the example. We tamper an adversary’s membership guesses to remove overconfidence induced by confounding, yielding a robust, shift-dependent correction.

3.

Propensity Scores and Uncertainty Quantification (
Section

6
).

Our composition and conditional correction principles rely on
propensity scores
(the probability that an example is a member, based solely on its features), which must be estimated. We explicitly quantify uncertainty in propensity score estimation in our privacy lower bounds via bootstrapping
[
14
]
.

4.

Practical Scalability (
Section

7
).
We demonstrate that Zero-Run auditing enables scalable privacy audits of large, deployed models where retraining is computationally or logistically infeasible.

2
Background on Differential Privacy and Privacy Auditing

Differential Privacy.

Informally, Differential Privacy (DP)
[
12
,
13
]
ensures that a randomized algorithm’s output remains largely unchanged whether a single individual’s data is included or not in the input. DP is widely considered the standard for privacy-preserving data analysis because it provides a mathematically rigorous guarantee against adversaries with arbitrary side information. DP is immune to post-processing (protections cannot be degraded by further computation) and composes gracefully, enabling accurate tracking of the cumulative privacy loss across multiple analyses.

Definition 1

(
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP)
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta>0
.
A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that takes as input a dataset
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{X}
and outputs
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in\Theta
is said to satisfy
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy if, for any pair of adjacent datasets
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
(i.e., which are the same except one data sample) and any measurable set
\mathcal{E}\subset\Theta
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{E}\right)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}\right)+\delta\,.

Pure
\varepsilon
-DP corresponds to the special case where
\delta=0
.
In this work, we will also rely on the more expressive notion of
f
-DP
[
10
]
, which is based on trade-off functions that lower bound the Type II error given a Type I error of
\alpha
for testing
z\sim\mathbb{P}
versus
z\sim\mathbb{Q}
.
We denote by
T_{Z,Z^{\prime}}
the trade-off function between the distributions of two random variables
Z,Z^{\prime}
.

Definition 2

(Trade-off function)
.

For two distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
on some space
\mathcal{Z}
, the trade-off function
T_{\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}}
is the function
[0,1]\to[0,1]
defined as
T_{\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)=\inf{\left\{\beta_{\phi}\,|\,\alpha_{\phi}\leq\alpha\right\}}
, where the infimum is taken over all measurable functions
\mathcal{Z}\to{\left\{0,1\right\}}
,
\alpha_{\phi}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\phi
and
\beta_{\phi}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\phi
.

Definition 3

(
f
-DP)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}

is said to satisfy
f
-differential privacy for
f
a decreasing and convex function satisfying
f(t)\leq 1-t
for
t\in[0,1]
if, for any pair of adjacent datasets
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, we have, for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
T_{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}),\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})}(\alpha)\geq f(\alpha)
.
Equivalently, for any measurable set
\mathcal{E}
,
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{E}\right)\leq\bar{f}\big(\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}\right)\big)
, where
\bar{f}=1-f.

For two mechanisms
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, respectively
f_{1}
-DP and
f_{2}
-DP, their composition
\mathcal{A}_{2}\odot\mathcal{A}_{1}
satisfies
f
-DP, with
f
given by the convolution
f=f_{1}\otimes f_{2}
, which characterizes the optimal testing error after observing both outputs. This remains valid even if
\mathcal{A}_{2}
depends on the outputs of
\mathcal{A}_{1}

[
10
]
.
Two special cases of
f
-DP will be used throughout:
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP (
Definition

1
), summarized by parameters
(\varepsilon,\delta)
, and Gaussian DP (
\mu
-GDP,
[
10
]
), summarized by a scalar
\mu
, with respective trade-off functions
f_{\varepsilon,\delta}(p)=\max(0,1-e^{\varepsilon}p-\delta,e^{-\varepsilon}(1-\delta-p))
and
G_{\mu}(p)=\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(1-p)-\mu))
.
For
\mu
-GDP, the function
f
coincides with the false negative rate as a function of the false positive rate for the optimal test distinguishing two unit-variance Gaussians with means differing by
\mu
.
While
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP summarizes privacy using two worst-case parameters,
f
-DP and GDP characterize the full trade-off between false positives and false negatives. This finer description provides a complete view of an adversary’s inference capability and enables more expressive privacy reporting and accounting
[
17
]
.

Privacy Auditing.

An auditing algorithm takes as input an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
(e.g., DP-SGD) and a privacy hypothesis
\mathcal{H}
(e.g., “
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
f
-DP”), outputting a bit indicating whether
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
\mathcal{H}
. Standard auditing follows a three-step procedure:

(1)
a
randomized experiment
evaluating
\mathcal{A}
on a dataset
\mathcal{D}
;

(2)
a
membership inference attack (MIA)
outputting guesses
(T_{i})_{i\in[m]}\in{\left\{-1,0,1\right\}}^{m}
for the true membership
S_{i}
of
X_{i}
based on the output of the experiment, with
T_{i}=0
denoting abstention; and

(3)
an
audit evaluation
outputting
b\in{\left\{\textsc{True},\textsc{False}\right\}}
based on
\mathcal{H}
, the MIA output
T
, and the experiment’s observations (including true memberships). For an error probability
p\in[0,1]
, the audit is
p
-valid if
\mathbb{P}\left(b=\textsc{False}|\mathcal{A}\text{ satisfies }\mathcal{H}\right)\leq p
.
From such procedures, one can derive
empirical privacy parameters
—high-probability lower bounds on leakage—by identifying the largest privacy level confidently ruled out by the audit (see
Section

6
).

In step
(1)
, both
Multi-Run

[
19
]
and
One-Run

[
37
]
auditing randomize data inclusions—executing
\mathcal{A}
repeatedly for individual points
X_{i}
, or once for multiple points
(X_{i})_{i\in[m]}
, respectively. Both rely fundamentally on membership indicators
S_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
being independent, centered random variables. This randomized assignment is the core statistical assumption validating interventional audits, an assumption that our Zero-Run auditing framework explicitly eliminates.

Zero-Run Auditing.

As illustrated in
Figure

1
, our
Zero-Run auditing framework
operates as follows:

1.

(Algorithm Output & Observational Data Formulation)

The auditor observes
\theta=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}})
, the result of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
executed on a private dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
. This output might be accessible via direct inspection or indirectly through a query interface. Crucially, the auditor lacks access to the full dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
and the algorithm’s internal mechanics. Instead, the auditor relies on
observational data
: known members
\mathcal{D}_{1}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
and known non-members
\mathcal{D}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
. Let
{\left\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\right\}}=\mathcal{D}_{1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{0}
represent the combined data, with true membership indicators defined by
S_{i}=1
if
X_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
S_{i}=-1
if
X_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
. Unlike traditional randomized experiments, the membership variables
S_{i}\in{\left\{-1,1\right\}}
are not centered; rather,
S_{i}
inherently depends on the data features
X_{i}
, as
\mathcal{D}_{0}
and
\mathcal{D}_{1}
may exhibit a distribution shift.

2.

(Membership Inference)

Leveraging the accessible algorithm output
\theta
and the features
X_{i}
from the observational data, the auditor runs a Membership Inference Attack (MIA). This step produces raw membership guesses
T_{i}
for each
X_{i}
. Formally, the MIA is a mapping
(\theta,(X_{i})_{i\in[m]})\mapsto T\in{\left\{-1,0,1\right\}}^{m}
, where a guess of
T_{i}=1
indicates membership,
T_{i}=-1
indicates non-membership, and
T_{i}=0
corresponds to abstention (e.g., low confidence). Further details on the MIA meta-algorithm are provided in
Algorithm

5
(
Section

B.3
).

3.

(Propensity Estimator)
To mitigate bias in raw MIA estimates caused by distribution shifts between members and non-members, we calculate an estimated
propensity score
,
\hat{\pi}(X_{i})
. This approximates the
true propensity

\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
, defined formally as
\pi(x)=\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=1\mid X_{i}=x)
. By capturing the inherent probability that a data point belongs to the training set based solely on its features, this score isolates structural selection likelihood from the signal contained in the algorithm’s output.
Section

6
details this estimation and its induced uncertainty propagation.

4.

(Zero-Run Correction)

Finally, the framework combines the true labels
S_{i}
, the raw MIA guesses
T_{i}
, and the propensity scores
\hat{\pi}(X_{i})
to produce a statistically valid audit evaluation. This correction stage effectively disentangles the algorithm’s actual privacy leakage from the underlying data distribution shift, and stems from our main theoretical contributions (
Sections

4
and
5
).
The correction can be applied
compositionally
(yielding global bounds) or
conditionally
(yielding pointwise bounds), outputting empirical DP lower bounds.

This four-step meta-algorithm forms the backbone of our Zero-Run auditing framework. To ensure a provably valid audit, a specific assumption for the generation of datasets
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{0}
is required.

Assumption 1

(Data Generation)
.

Datasets
\mathcal{D}_{1}={\left\{X_{i}:S_{i}=1\right\}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{0}={\left\{X_{i}:S_{i}=-1\right\}}
, representing members and non-members respectively, consist of independent samples drawn from distributions
\mathbb{P}_{1}
and
\mathbb{P}_{0}
, of identical sizes
n_{1}=n_{0}
.

There are several strategies for sourcing members and non-members, depending on the use case.

Member dataset
\mathcal{D}_{1}
.
This dataset can be any subset of the training set of the audited model, such as a random subsample or a curated subset selected to maximize potential privacy leakage. Even when the complete primary dataset is unknown to the auditor, it is often possible to identify
some
included examples. For instance, when auditing large-scale models,
\mathcal{D}_{1}
may consist of public records, historical archives, or corpora published prior to the algorithm’s execution phase
[
34
,
27
,
28
]
.

Non-member dataset
\mathcal{D}_{0}
.

The most straightforward scenario occurs when a randomized test split is available, yielding a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}
with zero distribution shift. When such a split is unavailable, alternative construction strategies are necessary. Following empirical evaluations for large language models
[
28
]
, one approach is to utilize data generated
after
the algorithm’s execution timestamp, ensuring
\mathcal{D}_{0}
is strictly excluded; however, a distribution shift (
\mathbb{P}_{1}\neq\mathbb{P}_{0}
) is naturally expected here due to temporal changes. Alternatively, one can synthesize non-members from a continuous approximation of the member distribution by training a generative model on
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and sampling from it, yielding non-members with
a close distributional match (
\mathbb{P}_{0}\approx\mathbb{P}_{1}
), as seen in
Panoramia

[
23
]
. Our framework seamlessly integrates these varied choices of
\mathcal{D}_{0}
.

Remark 1
.

We emphasize that, as in
[
37
,
25
]
, our meta-algorithm does not restrict the auditor to algorithms that formally satisfy DP. Because our framework treats
\mathcal{A}
as a black box and evaluates privacy leakage purely based on its outputs and observational data, it can be applied to arbitrary algorithms, regardless of whether they were designed with formal privacy guarantees.

4
A Global Correction via Adaptive Composition

If
\mathbb{P}_{1}=\mathbb{P}_{0}
, membership is independent of the features
X_{i}
, so One-Run audits apply directly: the observed member/non-member labels behave like randomized inclusion bits. Under distribution shift (
\mathbb{P}_{1}\neq\mathbb{P}_{0}
), however, the features themselves carry information about the membership bits
S_{i}
. An auditor may therefore appear to perform well by exploiting distribution-shift signals rather than privacy leakage from the algorithm’s output.

Distribution Shift as Adaptive Composition.

We account for this baseline distribution-shift leakage by modeling the auditor’s observations—the audit dataset
(X_{i})_{i\in[m]}
and the output
\theta=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}})
—as the composition of two randomized mechanisms: a
distribution-shift mechanism

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{DS}}

(X_{i})_{i\in[m]}
from the membership bits, and the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, which outputs
\theta=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}})

Formally, let

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{Tot}}(s)=\big(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{DS}}(s),\,\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{DS}}(s),s)\big)

\textstyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{DS}}(s)\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(\mathds{1}_{\left\{s_{i}=1\right\}}\mathcal{P}_{1}+\mathds{1}_{\left\{s_{i}=-1\right\}}\mathcal{P}_{0}\big),\quad\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}}(x,s)=\mathcal{A}\big(\mathcal{D}\cup{\left\{x_{i}:s_{i}=1\right\}}\big)\,,

with
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{1}

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{DS}}
samples the audit examples from the member or non-member distribution according to their membership bits, and

runs the algorithm on the examples whose bits indicate membership, such that the following holds.

A Pointwise Correction via Conditional Auditing

Modeling distribution shift globally via

\textstyle p_{i}(\varepsilon)=\frac{e^{\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{DS}}(X_{i})}}{1+e^{\varepsilon+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{DS}}(X_{i})}}\,.

Related Work

Interventional Privacy Auditing.

9
,
19
introduced privacy auditing methods that rely on repeatedly running the audited algorithm with and without a specific data point. These
Multi-Run
techniques were later refined to obtain tighter guarantees across various settings
[

1

One-Run

(\varepsilon,\delta)

f
-DP by

. While our proof techniques draw on these foundational works, their validity inherently depends on randomized membership assignments controlled by the auditor. This interventional requirement motivates the development of
Zero-Run
auditing methods that can estimate post hoc privacy leakage from a deployed model.

Observational Privacy Auditing.

22
also audits without randomized insertion, but targets the weaker label-DP, assumes identical member and non-member feature distributions, and cannot correct for general feature-level shifts. More closely related is
Panoramia

[
23
]
, which audits
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP using non-members drawn from a generative model.
Panoramia
acts as a special case of our composition framework, which we strictly generalize in two ways: we support GDP auditing and employ milder overlap assumptions (

\delta_{\mathrm{DS}}>0

\delta_{\mathrm{DS}}=0
, this recovers
Panoramia
’s
c

c=\log((1-\eta)/\eta)
. Furthermore, rather than learning a full generative model, our approach reduces the correction problem to estimating the propensity score function, i.e., a probabilistic binary classifier.

Causal Inference and Distribution Shift.

16

Unlike causal reweighting approaches
[
16
]
or generative approximations
[
23
]

As explicited in

9
Limitations & Conclusion

Propensity Estimation Limitation.
Our zero-run framework relies on being able to properly estimate the propensity score. For natural, high-dimensional data, even under an IID assumption, this is challenging due to finite-sample estimation error, as illustrated in
Figure

2
(c): although the oracle propensity score is
0.5
, the estimated scores deviate from this value and induces additional penalization. Finding better, more reliable ways to estimate propensity scores, although orthogonal to our work, will directly benefit our framework and the tightness of our audits.

One-Run & MIA Limitation.
While our framework successfully enables post-hoc privacy evaluation, it naturally inherits the limitations of existing One-Run algorithms. First, as observed by the baseline gap in
Figure

2
, Zero-Run auditing is limited by the finite-sample inefficiency intrinsic to all One-Run methods, meaning empirical bounds will inherently sit somewhat below the theoretical limit
[
37
,
25
]
. Again, any improvement in this line of work would directly benefit our framework. Second, an audit’s success is deeply tied to the baseline strength of the underlying MIA. In heavily restricted black-box deployments, particularly against DP models
[
29
]
, relying purely on loss-based attacks, the raw attack signal is often too weak to extract meaningful bounds, independent of our shift corrections.

Auditing and falsifiability of propensity score models.

Our observational audit relies on estimated bounds for the propensity scores. Although these bounds cannot be formally certified, they can be empirically falsified: one can treat them as a hypothesis and audit them using
Theorem

C.1
, analogously to a DP claim, rejecting the propensity model when the observed evidence contradicts the claimed bounds. This test is necessarily one-sided: passing it does not prove the bounds correct, but only indicates that no violation was found with the available data and auditing power.
This motivates releasing the propensity score bounds, audit dataset, and auditing procedure together with the final audit result. A skeptic can then challenge the claimed correction for distribution shift by constructing a stronger MIA that exploits the shift beyond what the released bounds allow. Thus, while the propensity model remains unverifiable in a formal sense, openness and adversarial testing make the audit computationally falsifiable and more trustworthy in practice.

The main lesson is that post-hoc privacy auditing is possible, but only as an observational inference problem. Distribution shift cannot be ignored: it is itself a source of membership information. Propensity scores provide the right language for quantifying this source, and our results show how to convert propensity bounds into valid empirical lower bounds on algorithmic privacy leakage.

We first start by proving an upper-bound on:

\tilde{W}_{i+1}=\tilde{W}_{i}+\xi_{i+1}

Conditionally on the event
{\left\{W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right\}}
for

such that this event is not of probability 0:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{S_{i+1}=\mathrm{sign}(T_{i+1})\right\}}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i+1}=\mathrm{sign}(T_{i+1})|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i+1}=T_{i+1}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i+1}=1|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t},T_{i+1}=1\right)\mathbb{P}\left(T_{i+1}=1|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i+1}=-1|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t},T_{i+1}=-1\right)\mathbb{P}\left(T_{i+1}=-1|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{i}}}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{i+1}=1|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{1+e^{-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{i}}}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{i+1}=-1|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right)\,,

using the above computations with the adequate event
\mathcal{E}
, which leads to:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{S_{i+1}=T_{i+1}\right\}}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|t_{i+1}|}{1+e^{-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{i}}}\,.

Then, noticing that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{i+1}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{|t_{i+1}|}{1+e^{-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|t_{i+1}|}{1+e^{-\varepsilon}}\times\frac{1+e^{-\varepsilon}}{1+e^{-\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|t_{i+1}|}{1+e^{-\varepsilon}}\times\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i+1}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[|T_{i+1}|\xi_{i+1}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i+1}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right]}\,,

we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i+1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{S_{i+1}=T_{i+1}\right\}}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i+1}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\left\{S_{i+1}=T_{i+1}\right\}}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|T_{i+1}|\xi_{i+1}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t}\right]\,.

Thus,
B_{i+1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{S_{i+1}=T_{i+1}\right\}}
is stochastically dominated by
|T_{i+1}|\zeta_{i+1}
conditionally on
W_{i}
,
\tilde{W}_{i}
and
T_{\leq i+1}
. Using
37
:
W_{i+1}
is stochastically dominated by
\tilde{W}_{i+1}
, conditionally on
W_{i}
,
\tilde{W}_{i}
and
T_{\leq i+1}
.
We conclude by unrolling the induction.

Fix some

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{i}=\mathcal{D}\cup{\left\{X_{j},X_{j}=1,j\neq i\right\}}

If

Let us define the following (random, since

\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}={\left\{\theta\in\Theta:|\mathrm{PLRV}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{i}}(\theta)|\leq\gamma\right\}}\,.

We first start by proving an upper-bound on:

\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=1|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)\,,

for
\mathcal{E}
such that
\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}
is of non-null probability.
Using Bayes’ theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=1|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}|S_{i}=1,S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)\times\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=1|S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}|S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}|S_{i}=1,S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)\times\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=1|S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)}{\sum_{s\in{\left\{-1,1\right\}}}\mathbb{P}\left(\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}|S_{i}=s,S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=s|S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(X_{i})\mathbb{P}\left(\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}|S_{i}=1,S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)}{\pi(X_{i})\mathbb{P}\left(\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}|S_{i}=1,S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)+(1-\pi(X_{i})\mathbb{P}\left(\theta\in\mathcal{E}|S_{i}=-1,S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)}\,,

since
\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=1|S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)=\pi(X_{i})
and
\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=-1|S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)=1-\pi(X_{i})
.
Then, let

We have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}|S_{i}=1,S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S_{>i}}\int\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\in\mathcal{E}\right\}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{|\mathrm{PLRV}_{\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}}(\theta)|\leq\gamma\right\}}{\rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{i})}(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{S_{>i}}\int\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\in\mathcal{E}\right\}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{|\mathrm{PLRV}_{\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}}(\theta)|\leq\gamma\right\}}{\rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime})}(\theta)

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma}\mathbb{P}\left(\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}|S_{i}=-1,S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)\,,

since
\mathrm{PLRV}_{\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}}(\theta)\leq\gamma

{\rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{i})}(\theta)\leq e^{\gamma}{\rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime})}(\theta)
.
Thus, we have that
R_{i}\leq e^{\gamma}
, and:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=1|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi(X_{i})}{\pi(X_{i})+(1-\pi(X_{i}))R_{i}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi(X_{i})}{\pi(X_{i})+(1-\pi(X_{i}))e^{-\gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+\frac{1-\pi(X_{i})}{\pi(X_{i})}e^{-\gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma-\varepsilon_{i}}}\,,

where
e^{\varepsilon_{i}}=\max(\frac{\pi(X_{i})}{1-\pi(X_{i})},\frac{1-\pi(X_{i})}{\pi(X_{i})})
.
Similarly, since
|\mathrm{PLRV}_{\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime}}(\theta)|=|\mathrm{PLRV}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\prime},\mathcal{D}_{i}}(\theta)|
we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=-1|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma-\varepsilon_{i}}}\,.

As a consequence, for all
i
and event
\theta\in\mathcal{E}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=T_{i}|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=1|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},T_{i}=1,S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(T_{i}=1|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=-1|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},T_{i}=-1,S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(T_{i}=-1|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma-\varepsilon_{i}}}\,.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i}\mathds{1}_{\left\{S_{i}=T_{i}\right\}}|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=T_{i},B_{i}=1|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(B_{i}=1\right)\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=T_{i}|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+e^{-\gamma-\varepsilon_{i}}}{1+e^{-\gamma}}\times\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma-\varepsilon_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma}}\,.

Now, for any
\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma
, let:

{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}={\left\{\theta\in\Theta:|\mathrm{PLRV}_{\mathcal{D}_{i},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{i}}(\theta)|=\gamma^{\prime}\right\}}\,.

Although this event can be of null probability if the PLRV is continuous, we can still condition on
{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}

Using the same reasoning as above,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=T_{i}|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma^{\prime},i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{i}}}\,,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i}\mathds{1}_{\left\{S_{i}=T_{i}\right\}}|\theta\in\mathcal{E}\cap{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma^{\prime},i},S^{(-i)}=s^{(-i)},\bar{X}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{i}}}\times\frac{1+e^{-\gamma-\varepsilon_{i}}}{1+e^{-\gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}}}\times\frac{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}}}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{i}}}\times\frac{1+e^{-\gamma-\varepsilon_{i}}}{1+e^{-\gamma}}

since
\frac{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}}}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{i}}}\leq\frac{1+e^{-\gamma}}{1+e^{-\gamma-\varepsilon_{i}}}
for
\gamma^{\prime}\geq\gamma
.

Let
\xi_{j}(\gamma^{\prime})
be independent Bernoulli random variables of parameters
\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}}}
, independent of all the rest and coupled such that
\xi_{j}(\gamma^{\prime})\leq\xi_{j}(\gamma^{\prime\prime})
for all
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\geq\gamma^{\prime}
. Such a coupling can be obtained by sampling
(U_{i})_{i\in[m]}
i.i.d. random variables uniformly in
[0,1]
and setting:

\xi_{j}=\mathds{1}_{\left\{U_{j}\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}}}\right\}}\,.

Let
\xi_{j}(\gamma)
be independent Bernoulli random variables of parameters
\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma}}
, independent of all the rest and of the

Let then:

\xi_{j}^{\prime}=\mathds{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}\right\}}\xi_{j}(\gamma)+\int_{\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma^{\prime},i}\right\}}\xi_{j}(\gamma^{\prime})\,,

which is a Bernoulli random variable, as at most only one of the

Then, if we assume that the result holds at some

, we have that

and

such that this event is not of probability 0, if

, using the above computations and writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i+1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{S_{i+1}=T_{i+1}\right\}}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t},\bar{X}=\bar{x}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i+1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{S_{i+1}=T_{i+1}\right\}}|\theta\in{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma^{\prime},i+1},W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t},\bar{X}=\bar{x}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbb{P}\left(\theta\in{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma^{\prime},i+1}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t},\bar{X}=\bar{x}\right){\rm d}\gamma^{\prime}

using the above computations with the adequate event

.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i+1}\mathds{1}_{\left\{S_{i+1}=T_{i+1}\right\}}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t},\bar{X}=\bar{x}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|T_{i+1}|\xi_{i+1}^{\prime}|W_{i}=w_{i},\tilde{W}_{i}=\tilde{w}_{i},\bar{T}=\bar{t},\bar{X}=\bar{x}\right]\,,

leading to
\max(0,T_{i+1}S_{i+1})
stochastically dominated by
|T_{i+1}|\xi_{i+1}^{\prime}
since stochastic domination for Bernoulli random variables is equivalent to domination of means, conditionally on

,

37

Recall that:

\xi_{j}^{\prime}=\mathds{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}\right\}}\xi_{j}(\gamma)+\int_{\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma^{\prime},i}\right\}}\xi_{j}(\gamma^{\prime})\,,

so that:

where

and

\displaystyle B_{m}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|T_{j}|\left(\int_{\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma^{\prime},i}\right\}}\xi_{j}(\gamma^{\prime})-\mathds{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\notin\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,i}\right\}}\xi_{j}(\gamma)\right)

\displaystyle B_{m}^{\prime}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\int_{\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma^{\prime},i}\right\}}\big(\xi_{j}(\gamma^{\prime})-\xi_{j}(\gamma)\big)\right)\,,

\xi_{i}(\gamma)

uniform in
[0,1]

\xi_{i}(\gamma)=\mathds{1}_{\left\{U_{i}\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma}}\right\}}
and
\xi_{i}(\gamma^{\prime})-\xi_{i}(\gamma)=\mathds{1}_{\left\{\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}}}\geq U_{i}>\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma}}\right\}}
.
We are thus going to upper bound
\mathbb{E}\left[B_{m}^{\prime}|A_{m}\right]
.
First,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\in{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{\gamma^{\prime},j}\right\}}\xi_{j}(\gamma^{\prime})-\mathds{1}_{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\notin\mathcal{E}_{\gamma,j}\right\}}\xi_{j}(\gamma)\,\Big|\,\xi_{j}(\gamma)=1\right]=0\,,

since
\xi_{j}(\gamma)=1\implies\xi_{j}(\gamma^{\prime})=1
. Then, under
\xi_{j}(\gamma)=0
, we have that
\xi_{j}(\gamma^{\prime})-\xi_{j}(\gamma)
is a Bernoulli random variable of parameter:

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma}}}{1-\frac{1}{1+e^{-\gamma}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1+e^{-\gamma}}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}}}}{e^{-\gamma}}

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma}\frac{e^{-\gamma}-e^{-\gamma^{\prime}}}{1+e^{-\gamma^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\gamma}(e^{-\gamma}-e^{-\gamma^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=1-e^{\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}}\,,

Appendix D
Conditional Approximate DP Auditing
Without Tampering

In this section, we show that approximate DP can be audited in Zero-Run, without tampering.
In order to do so, instead of multiplying MIA guesses by independent Bernoulli variables in order to tamper with their confidence, we adapt the success rate, which need to take into account MIA overconfidence.
While in the pure DP setting, both results would yield equivalent results, in the approximate DP case the tampering results are stronger, as they yield milder
\delta
dependence.
However, the obtained MIA success rate bounds here are conditioned also on
T_{i}
.

Appendix F
Experimental Synthetic Distribution Shift

To rigorously evaluate the deconvolution of privacy leakage from dataset shift, we construct a high-dimensional synthetic Membership Inference Attack (MIA) environment. The procedure isolates empirical privacy leakage by controlling both the algorithmic signal-to-noise ratio and the underlying structural dataset shift.

Let
N
be the total dataset size, partitioned equally into a member set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{mem}}
and a non-member set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{non}}
, and let
d
be the feature dimensionality. We first sample a fixed global direction vector
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\bm{v}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{d}),\quad\bm{\mu}=\frac{\bm{v}}{\|\bm{v}\|_{2}}

(8)

To strictly enforce a bounded
L_{2}
sensitivity for the downstream differential privacy mechanism, we define a generative function
g(\gamma)
that draws a feature vector shifted along
\bm{\mu}
with bias strength
\gamma
, and subsequently projects it onto the unit hypersphere:

\bm{\tilde{x}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\gamma\bm{\mu},\bm{I}_{d}),\quad\bm{x}=\frac{\bm{\tilde{x}}}{\|\bm{\tilde{x}}\|_{2}}

(9)

This projection step ensures that every single data record inherently satisfies
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=1
. We construct the target model’s training set by generating members with a strong baseline bias
\gamma_{\text{base}}
:

\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{mem}},\quad\bm{x}\sim g(\gamma_{\text{base}})

(10)

For the non-members, we modulate the bias strength to simulate evaluation environments with and without structural shift, parameterized by an alignment decay ratio
\rho\in(0,1]
:

•

IID Setting (No Shift):
The non-members are identically distributed to the members using
\gamma_{\text{non}}=\gamma_{\text{base}}
.

•

OOD Setting (Dataset Shift):
The non-members are drawn using an attenuated bias strength
\gamma_{\text{non}}=\rho\cdot\gamma_{\text{base}}
. Because the shift attenuates the signal purely along the axis of the base vector
\bm{\mu}
, it creates a structural dataset gap strictly aligned with the adversary’s target objective.

We abstract the training of the machine learning model by computing a differentially private sum of the members’ feature vectors. Under the standard
add/remove
adjacency definition, the global
L_{2}
sensitivity of the sum query is strictly bounded by the maximum norm of a single vector:

\Delta_{2}=\max_{\bm{x}}\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=1

(11)

We apply the Gaussian mechanism by adding isotropic Gaussian noise calibrated to a scale
\sigma>0
:

\bm{\theta}_{\text{priv}}=\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{mem}}}\bm{x}+\bm{\eta},\quad\text{where }\bm{\eta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{d})

(12)

Releasing
\bm{\theta}_{\text{priv}}
rigorously guarantees
\mu
-Gaussian Differential Privacy (
\mu
-GDP) with a true algorithmic privacy limit bounded at
\mu_{\text{true}}=\Delta_{2}/\sigma=1/\sigma
.

The adversary attempts to distinguish members from non-members via white-box access to the target model. The attacker computes a linear test statistic against the public DP-released vector for all queried samples
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{mem}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\text{non}}
:

s_{\text{MIA}}(\bm{x})=\langle\bm{x},\bm{\theta}_{\text{priv}}\rangle

(13)

Because the structural dataset shift between members and non-members occurs along the same vector
\bm{\mu}
as the principal component of
\bm{\theta}_{\text{priv}}
, the shift systematically inflates the attacker’s observed signal (
\mu_{\text{tot}}
) independently of any true algorithmic memorization.

To explicitly isolate the true empirical leakage from the confounding dataset shift, the auditor computes propensity scores
\hat{\pi}_{i}\approx P(y_{i}=1\mid\bm{x}_{i})
using an
L_{2}
-regularized Logistic Regression across the standardized feature space. These propensity scores quantify the structural ceiling of the dataset shift (
\mu_{\text{ds}}
), allowing the auditor to cleanly deconvolve the overall observed attack strength (
\mu_{\text{tot}}
) back into the true, Zero-Run algorithmic leakage:

\mu_{\text{leak}}=\sqrt{\max\left(0,\,\mu_{\text{tot}}^{2}-\mu_{\text{ds}}^{2}\right)}

(14)
