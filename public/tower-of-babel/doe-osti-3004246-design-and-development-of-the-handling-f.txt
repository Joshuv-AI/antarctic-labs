 1. Abstract

 The Handling Frame Assembly Table is a custom designed piece of equipment developed to
support the NIF Sustainment Project. The NIF Sustainment project's goal is to refurbish the
National Ignition Facility (NIF), the world’s largest and most energetic laser, so it can continue
advancing research in fusion energy, national security, and high energy density physics. A key
aspect of this effort involves replacing all 1,728 blast shields on the NIF, which requires
specialized equipment such as the Handling Frame Assembly Table to support the production of
new blast shields. This project follows a systems engineering approach that guides the design
sequence. This report documents the design process from initial concept through final design,
including stakeholder analysis, requirements development, conceptual design, design analysis
and validation, and final design. Future work will focus on building and commissioning the
system.

 2. Background and Motivation

 The National Ignition Facility (NIF) is the world’s largest and most energetic laser, designed
to create nuclear fusion by compressing and heating small fuel targets with powerful lasers. NIF
has made significant contributions to fusion energy research, national security, and high energy
density physics, including being the first facility to achieve controlled fusion ignition. Fusion
ignition is defined as the point where fusion energy output exceeds input. After over 15 years of
operation, the team at NIF has become increasingly concerned over decline in key components
such as its amplifier systems. These systems are essential for boosting the laser’s energy to its
maximum of 2.2 MJ. One major issue within the amplifier systems is the degradation of the
sealant on the amplifier blast shields, which deposits onto the amplifier slabs, increasing light
scatter and the risk of laser-induced damage. This reduces the optics’ lifetime and performance,
making replacement critical for NIF’s continued mission.

 To maintain NIF’s high energy output, the NIF Sustainment Project aims to refurbish
many laser components, including all 1,728 blast shields and 772 laser glass slabs. The blast
shields are unique optics that require multiple processing steps, including cleaning, coating,
assembly into an LRU (Line Replaceable Unit), and testing, prior to installation onto NIF. The
original production loop for these shields was created and ran over 20 years ago, but much of the
technology and documentation has been lost, necessitating a complete redesign and rebuilding of
the process. While lessons can be drawn from other optic production loops supporting the NIF,
each system within this blast shield production loop must be custom designed due to differences
in size and function of the optic.

 A critical component of the blast shield production loop is the Handling Frame Assembly
Table. Its purpose is to assemble blast shield glass into a specialized handling frame, which is
necessary for safe transport and processing of the optic through the production loop. The system

also stores the components required for assembly of the handling frame. Key design challenges
include ensuring safety, meeting stakeholder requirements, maintaining cleanliness, and
managing risk.

 This capstone project focuses on the design development of the Handling Frame
Assembly Table, using a systems engineering approach, which is a structured, multidisciplinary
process for developing complex engineering systems from concept to operation. The project
covers stakeholder analysis, requirements development, conceptual design, final design, and
design validation. In addition, lessons learned, and future work is discussed.

 3. Objectives & Stakeholder Analysis

 Following a systems engineering approach, the first step in any project is to determine its
scope. For this project, my primary responsibility was to design a system that would assist
operators with assembling the blast shield optic into a handling frame (see Figure 3.1), while also
providing storage for the handling frame components. Once I understood the overall scope, I
identified all relevant entities (individuals or groups) with a vested interest in the project’s
outcome. Examples of stakeholders included representatives from process engineering,
production, cleanliness, interfacing systems, management, safety and health, and maintenance.
Within each group, I interviewed specific individuals to gather their unique needs.
 For example, the cleanliness group emphasized that the system must maintain both the
cleanliness of the room and the optic. The production team outlined requirements for throughput
and specified the number of operators who would use the system. Management set constraints on
the project timeline and budget, while safety and health highlighted compliance with laboratory
policies. These examples highlight only a section of the stakeholders needs. Stakeholders were
interviewed multiple times to ensure their needs were accurately captured. The needs identified
during these interviews directly informed the system requirements, which are described in the
following section.

 4. Design Requirements

 After conducting interviews with stakeholders and identifying their needs, the next step was
to translate this information into specific requirements. Requirements are criteria that guide the
design of the system, ensuring that, if met, the stakeholders’ needs will be fulfilled. These
requirements are documented and subsequently reviewed with both stakeholders and the project
team. They function as a formal agreement between the engineer and the stakeholders, outlining
the expectations for the system’s design. Stakeholders provide feedback during this process to
confirm that the requirements accurately reflect their needs.

 For organizational clarity, the requirements for this project were divided into the
following categories: Functional & Performance, Safety, Interface, Operational, and Standards &
Regulations. Each requirement includes a rationale that connects it directly to the underlying
stakeholder need. It also includes a method to which the requirement will be verified that it is
met during system commissioning. The complete list of requirements presented in the system
requirements review (SRR) is shown in Figure 4.1. The requirements are not finalized at this
stage and were developed further along with the design of the system. After the review of the
requirements with stakeholders and the project team, the committee approved the project to
proceed to the next phase.

 5. Concept Development

 After the requirements for the system have been agreed upon by stakeholders, the next part of
the systems engineering process is to create a high-level concept for a system that would meet all
requirements. While there are many requirements for the system, only a few of the requirements
drive the high-level conceptual design of the system. The overall conceptual design can be seen
in figure 5.1.

 Figure 5.1: Proposed Conceptual Design

 One of the high-level requirements for the system was to provide physical support for
operators for assembling the optic into the frame, which drove the overall system to be a table-
like structure. Another key requirement was storage of the L-frame components of the handling
frame, which heavily drove the design in a specific direction. The L-frames are the two largest
components that make up the handling frame (Figure 3.1) that surround the optic. Multiple
methods were considered such as adding slots below the table to slot the L-frames into, creating
a whole separate rack to store them, and a drawer under the table. Ultimately the drawer concept
was pursued as it maximizes operators ergonomics, saves space within the cleanroom, and
minimizes risk of damaging the frames (see Figure 5.2).

 Additional key requirements included the system’s ability to withstand seismic loading
(Requirement 3.2). This was addressed in part by adding angled supports to the table legs,
allowing the system to be securely bolted to the floor. Bolting the system to the floor is necessary
to prevent it from sliding or tipping during a seismic event, which could pose a safety risk to
operators. Pins on the tabletop were incorporated to directly support the optic, while surrounding
blocks were designed to hold the handling frame components, making assembly easier for
operators and fulfilling Requirement 2.1. Delrin was selected for these support features to
minimize the risk of scratching the optics, which is essential for maintaining optic integrity. The
overall system is constructed from aluminum, stainless steel, and Delrin to meet stringent
cleanliness requirements.

 Another important element of the conceptual design is the concept of operations, which
outlines how the system will be used in practice. This helps demonstrate the system’s
functionality in the field. The concept of operations for the system is illustrated in Figure 5.3.
The concept of operations was reviewed with the production team to ensure that it meets their
expectations.
