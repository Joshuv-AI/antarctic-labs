Can Large Language Models Derive New Knowledge? A Dynamic Benchmark for Biological Knowledge Discovery DOI: XXXXXXX.XXXXXXX Conference: ; June 03–05,2018; Woodstock, NY ISBN: 978-1-4503-XXXX-X/2018/06 CCS: Computing methodologies Natural language processing
Chaoqun Yang, Xinyu Lin, Shulin Li, Wenjie Wang, Ruihan Guo, Fuli Feng, Tat-Seng Chua

Abstract
. Recent advancements in Large Language Model (LLM) agents have demonstrated remarkable potential in automatic knowledge discovery. However, rigorously evaluating an AI’s capacity for knowledge discovery remains a critical challenge. Existing benchmarks predominantly rely on static datasets, leading to inevitable data contamination where models have likely seen the evaluation knowledge during training. Furthermore, the rapid release cycles of modern LLMs render static benchmarks quickly outdated, failing to assess the ability to discover truly new knowledge. To address these limitations, we propose DBench-Bio, a dynamic and fully automated benchmark designed to evaluate AI’s biological knowledge discovery ability. DBench-Bio employs a three-stage pipeline: (1) data acquisition of rigorous, authoritative paper abstracts; (2) QA extraction utilizing LLMs to synthesize scientific hypothesis questions and corresponding discovery answers; and (3) QA filter to ensure quality based on relevance, clarity, and centrality. We instantiate this pipeline to construct a monthly-updated benchmark covering 12 biomedical sub-domains. Extensive evaluations of SOTA models reveal current limitations in discovering new knowledge. Our work provides the first dynamic, automatic framework for assessing the new knowledge discovery capabilities of AI systems, establishing a living, evolving resource for AI research community to catalyze the development of knowledge discovery.

Our results reveal the current limitations of LLMs in discovering new knowledge:

3.
DBench-Bio

To construct a rigorous benchmark for evaluating knowledge discovery capabilities in the biology domain, we adopt a systematic pipeline comprising three distinct stages: data acquisition, QA extraction, and QA filter (see Figure
1
). This pipeline is designed to ensure the authority, recency, and high quality of our benchmark.

3.1.
Data Acquisition

To ensure the quality of the data, we adopt three selection criteria: data domain, data source, and temporal separation.
(1) For data domain, to achieve comprehensive yet representative coverage of the domain, we select based on the Journal Citation Reports (JCR) domain classification system
3
3

3

https://jcr.clarivate.com/jcr/browse-categories
.
. Using the JCR taxonomy provides a methodological advantage by relying on a standardized, widely accepted framework for categorizing scientific impact. Specifically, we identify the “Biology & Biochemistry” category within the JCR. From this parent category, we meticulously select 12 representative sub-domains to capture the field’s diversity while maintaining a manageable scope.
(2) For data source, to further ensure authority, we adapt an impact-based filter. Within each selected sub-domain, we restrict our collection to journals ranked in the Journal Impact Factor (JIF) Quartile Q1.
(3) To guarantee strict temporal separation and prevent data contamination, we crawl abstracts of target journals published only after the model’s release date.
We focus on abstracts rather than full texts, as abstracts provide dense, high-level summaries of novel insights and scientific findings, minimizing noise from extraneous experimental details.

By employing these data acquisition strategies, we construct a benchmark that supports monthly updates. As the initial implementation of this pipeline, we focus our data collection on research publications from December 2025 and January 2026.

3.2.
QA Extraction

To transform these unstructured paper abstracts into a structured benchmark format, we employ a capable LLM (
e.g.,
DeepSeek-V3.2-thinking) to extract QA pairs. Each abstract corresponds to one QA pair. We design specific prompts to instruct the model: the
Question
must represent a precise scientific inquiry, while the corresponding
Answer
must encapsulate the core conclusion of the scientific finding derived directly from the abstract.

Crucially, these QA pairs are designed to focus on knowledge discovery and high-level concepts, avoiding fine-grained details such as specific parameters or numerical values. Specifically, we prioritize causal inference (
e.g.,
“Does X regulate Y phenomenon?” or “What are the implications of Z on disease progression?”) and mechanistic explanation (
e.g.,
“How does X regulate Y phenomenon?” or “What is the mechanism behind Z effect?”). Furthermore, we ensure that the language is fluent and natural, with answers formatted as clear and concise bullet points.

3.3.
QA Filter

Acknowledging that LLMs may occasionally exhibit instruction-following failures or hallucinations, we implement a robust, multi-dimensional quality control mechanism to ensure the benchmark’s reliability. Inspired by previous work
(
7
;
19
)
, we utilize an LLM as a judge to assess each generated QA pair.

Each QA pair is assessed across three distinct dimensions, scored on a discrete scale from 1 to 5:

•

Relevance:
This metric measures the semantic alignment between the QA pair and the target biology sub-domain. Even within Q1 journals, article scopes may diverge from the specific taxonomy used for benchmark categorization. This step filters out such misaligned content.

•

Clarity:
This dimension evaluates the linguistic precision and coherence of both the question and the answer, ensuring that the question is unambiguous and the answer is articulated clearly. Notably, it screens for context-dependent phrases (e.g., “based on the text”) that would effectively degenerate the task into a reading comprehension exercise rather than an independent knowledge assessment.

•

Centrality:
To prevent the benchmark from focusing on trivial details, this metric assesses whether the QA pair corresponds to the primary scientific finding of the abstract, rather than peripheral or background information.

To filter low-quality data in the benchmark, we enforce a strict quality threshold. Only QA pairs that meet the following criteria are retained in the final dataset: Relevance
\geq
4, Clarity
\geq
5, and Centrality
\geq
5. This rigorous standard ensures that the benchmark consists exclusively of high-quality, core scientific knowledge.

Consistency with Human Experts.
Following the guidelines of Alt-test
(
2
)
, three human experts were engaged to manually assess 100 QA pairs, aiming to quantify the alignment between LLMs and human judgment. As shown in Table

3.4.
Evaluation Protocol

Following the previous work
(
7
;
19
)
, we employ an “LLM-as-a-Judge” framework to evaluate the performance of target models. The primary objective is to assess the consistency between the answers generated by the models and the ground-truth answers in our benchmark.

A judge LLM is provided with the gold standard answer and the model’s generated answer. The judge assigns a score ranging from 1 to 5 on a Likert scale, where 5 indicates a correct and comprehensive answer that fully matches the gold standard, while 1 represents a completely incorrect, hallucinated, or irrelevant response.

To ensure the credibility of the LLM assessment, we do the Alt-test
(
2
)
. Three human experts are engaged to manually evaluate the consistency between the correct answers and the reference answers. As shown in Table
1
, the winning rate surpasses 0.5, passing the statistical test. Additionally, with an advantage probability exceeding 0.8, our results provide strong evidence that LLM annotation serves as a viable substitute for human evaluator.

4.1.
Experimental Setup

4.1.1.
Evaluated Models

We evaluate the following methods on our proposed benchmark:

•

Base LLMs:
A comprehensive selection of open-source and closed-source LLMs, encompassing both reasoning and non-reasoning models. These including GPT-5.2, GPT-5, GPT-5-Mini, Gemini-3-Pro, Gemini-3-Flash, DeepSeek-V3.2, Kimi-K2, GLM-4.7, GLM-4.6, Qwen3-Max, and Baichuan-M2.

•

LLM with Tools:
A simple retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system
(
1
)
, namely a base LLM augmented with a single tool-use capability.

•

ReAct Agent:
We employ the ReAct (Reasoning + Acting) paradigm
(
32
)
, which operates on an iterative “Reason-Act” loop. In this framework, the model generates reasoning traces to plan actions, executes tool calls, and subsequently observes the results to inform the next step of reasoning until the task is resolved.

•

Agent Workflow:
A workflow-orchestrated multi-agent framework comprising specialized roles
(
15
)
: the
Planner
analyzes input queries, decomposes them into sub-problems, and plans actions; the
Tool Caller
invokes the relevant tools to collect information; the
Reasoner
synthesizes the collected information; the
Reporter
that formulates the final answer; and the
Critic
that reviews the quality of the answer.

4.1.2.
Implementation Details

For methods that involve external tool integration (specifically
LLM with Tools
,
ReAct Agent
, and
Agent Workflow
), we utilize GPT-5-Mini as the backbone model. The external tool provided is PubMed literature search. To prevent data leakage and ensure a rigorous evaluation, we strictly constrain the literature retrieval scope to publications released before the data collection phase of the benchmark. Specifically, for the December 2025 benchmark, the tool is restricted to retrieving literature dated before November 30, 2025.
Unless otherwise specified, all models are evaluated using their default inference parameters.

4.2.
Overall Results (RQ1)

To validate the robustness of our methodology, we construct instances of DBench-Bio spanning two distinct temporal snapshots: December 2025 and January 2026. The overall results, illustrated in Figure
2
, demonstrate consistent trends across both periods, leading to the following key observations:

•

Low Overall Performance.
The aggregate performance across all evaluated models remains low (The maximum Score is 5). This underscores the inherent difficulty of knowledge discovery, suggesting that current LLM models have yet to master the ability to derive new knowledge.

•

Divergent Efficacy of Thinking Strategies.
Thinking strategies yield significant improvements for certain models (
e.g.,
Kimi-K2, GPT-5, GPT-5-Mini, and GPT-5.2), but offer negligible gains for others (
e.g.,
GLM-4.6, GLM-4.7, and DeepSeek-V3.2). This disparity indicates that different models possess varying reasoning capacities when tackling new knowledge problems. Furthermore, it implies that the utility of explicit thinking strategies is not universal but depends heavily on the model’s intrinsic alignment with structured reasoning patterns.

•

Limited Utility of Tool Use.
Enabling tool use within a restricted retrieval scope failed to yield significant improvements. We attribute this to the fact that the information retrieved via tools largely overlaps with the models’ internal knowledge, thereby offering negligible information gain.

•

Effectiveness of Agent Architectures.
Both ReAct and Workflow architectures result in performance boosts. Interestingly, the performance gap between these two paradigms is marginal. This finding suggests that integrating reasoning with external tools, via either iterative planning or pre-set workflows, effectively facilitates the discovery of new knowledge.

In Figure
2
, GPT-5.2 demonstrates superior performance over the agent-based methods. This discrepancy is likely attributed to the fact that our agent-based methods utilize GPT-5-Mini as the backbone model, which is significantly less capable than GPT-5.2. To verify this hypothesis, it is essential to investigate the impact of the backbone model on the performance of agent-based methods. However, given the high costs of GPT-5.2, we randomly sampled a subset of 500 instances for this experiment. The results are shown in Figure
3
, from which we can observe that:

•

Consistent Superiority of Agent Architectures.
Agent-based methods consistently outperform their corresponding backbone models. Notably, ReAct and Workflow achieve comparable performance, with negligible differences between them.

•

Scalability with Model Capacity.
The performance of agent-based methods scales positively with the capabilities of the backbone model. This observation indicates that the agent framework acts as an effective amplifier of the LLM’s intrinsic potential. Stronger backbone models lead to more capable agents.

4.4.
In-depth Analysis (RQ3)

To investigate the contribution of basic biological knowledge to the discovery of new biological knowledge, we evaluate the base models on the biology task of MMLU-Pro benchmark
(
30
)
, which offers a rigorous assessment of foundational biological literacy and textbook-level reasoning capabilities. We then compare these results with the evaluation results on DBench-Bio, as shown in Figure
5
. We can conclude the following observations:

•

Basic Knowledge as a Prerequisite.
The mastery of basic knowledge serves as a prerequisite for discovering new knowledge. Notably, Baichuan-M2 exhibits the poorest performance on both MMLU-Pro and DBench-Bio, suggesting that deficiencies in basic knowledge retention severely hinder the capacity for new discovery.

•

The Gap Between Memorization and Discovery.
Thinking models consistently achieve accuracy exceeding 90% on MMLU-Pro with negligible variance, indicating a robust grasp of biological basics. However, their performance on DBench-Bio remains suboptimal. This discrepancy suggests that high scores on MMLU-Pro may be artificially inflated by potential data contamination. More fundamentally, it highlights the limitations of current models in knowledge composition and complex reasoning. These findings imply that genuine knowledge discovery necessitates advanced reasoning capabilities, positioning DBench-Bio as a benchmark with superior discriminative power compared to traditional static alternatives.

•

Misalignment in Model Rankings.
We observe a misalignment in model rankings between MMLU-Pro and DBench-Bio. Despite the leading performance of Gemini-3-Flash on MMLU-Pro, its relatively inferior result on DBench-Bio suggests that high proficiency in static knowledge retention does not necessarily translate to the ability to discover new knowledge.

5.
Discussion and Conclusion

5.1.
Discussion

In this section, we discuss the key implications and future directions of our study.

Performance Gap in Unknown Knowledge.

Our evaluation results highlight a critical limitation of current LLMs: while they achieve impressive results on known knowledge (high performance on MMLU-Pro), their performance degrades significantly when confronted with unknown knowledge (low performance on our benchmark). The models excel at retrieving and synthesizing established facts but struggle to derive new knowledge that post-dates their training cutoff. This distinct performance gap indicates that LLMs still possess substantial room for improvement in new knowledge discovery tasks. It underscores that current models rely heavily on memorization and pattern matching rather than possessing the capability to truly reason about and assimilate novel scientific concepts from scratch.

Generalization Ability of the Benchmark Pipeline.

Addressing the challenges above requires robust evaluation tools. The methodological pipeline proposed in this study demonstrates strong generalization ability and is not confined to the biology domain. By simply replacing the source category in the JCR system, our systematic approach, spanning data acquisition, QA extraction, and QA filter, can be easily adapted to other scientific domains. This flexibility allows for the creation of a family of benchmarks, enabling a comprehensive, cross-disciplinary evaluation of how well AI models can master and discover new knowledge in various areas of human expertise.

Building on this study, we identify several promising avenues for future research:
(1) The scope of model evaluation deserves expansion. Due to limits on time and money, we only select a representative set of models instead of testing all available SOTA LLMs. Future studies should evaluate a broader range of models to ensure a more comprehensive analysis.
(2) Future research should develop a process-level evaluation framework. As an early attempt to assess the ability of LLMs to discover new knowledge, our current evaluation is primarily based on final outcomes. However, a model that arrives at a correct conclusion through flawed reasoning or logically invalid paths would still achieve a high score. Therefore, investigating the specific processes involved in knowledge discovery and proposing evaluation metrics based on these intermediate steps is essential for future research.
(3) There is a need to develop novel methodologies to enhance LLMs’ knowledge discovery ability. Our results indicate that existing LLMs still perform poorly on knowledge discovery tasks. To address this limitation, future work should explore and implement new methods, such as specialized training strategies or architectural innovations, to improve the knowledge discovery capabilities of LLMs.

5.2.
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose DBench-Bio, a novel benchmark designed to rigorously evaluate the new knowledge discovery capabilities of LLMs within the biology domain. By establishing a systematic pipeline that extracts high-quality QA pairs from the latest academic literature and conducts multi-dimensional quality control, we provided a dynamic, contamination-free evaluation benchmark that reliably assesses complex scientific reasoning over new knowledge.
Our comprehensive evaluation reveals critical insights into the current limitations of LLMs. While existing models demonstrate strong proficiency in retrieving and reasoning over established knowledge, they face significant challenges when confronted with novel, unknown scientific findings that post-date their training cutoffs.
A key strength of our work lies in the generalization ability of its methodological framework. The pipeline we proposed is inherently domain-agnostic; by simply replacing the source category within the JCR system, it can be directly adapted to construct benchmarks for other scientific domains, such as physics, chemistry, or social sciences.
This flexibility allows for the creation of a family of benchmarks, enabling the community to track and compare AI-driven knowledge discovery progress across a broad spectrum of human expertise.
Looking forward, we hope DBench-Bio will serve as a foundational infrastructure to drive future research toward AI-driven knowledge discovery.
