Association of fat-to-muscle mass ratio with incident coronary artery disease and the potential role of metabolic signatures
Authors: Kaibo Hu, Yanjun Song, Zhangyu Lin, Zechen Liu, Hao Wang, Kefei Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe fat-to-muscle mass ratio (FMR) reflects the balance between metabolic burden and capacity. However, the associations of total and regional FMR with incident coronary artery disease (CAD) and their underlying metabolic mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsWe included 398,435 UK Biobank participants free of baseline cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or lipid-lowering therapy. Total and regional (trunk, arm, leg) FMR were quantified via bioelectrical impedance analysis. Participants were stratified by sex and classified based on FMR thresholds derived from restricted cubic splines and BMI categories. Primary outcome was incident CAD, defined as myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, or CAD mortality. Metabolic signatures were constructed using elastic net regression on 251 NMR metabolites.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 12.61 years, 10,740 incident CAD cases were documented. Higher total and regional FMR were significantly associated with increased CAD risk in both sexes. Leg FMR exhibited the strongest independent association; after mutual adjustment for BMI, it remained a significant predictor in both women (HR: 3.672, 95% CI 1.958–6.887) and men (HR: 2.682, 95% CI 1.682–4.275), whereas associations for other FMR types were attenuated. Mediation analysis suggested that metabolic signatures partially accounted for the association between FMR and CAD, explaining over 70% of the statistical effect. Furthermore, adding leg FMR to standard risk factors significantly improved risk reclassification (NRI: 0.142 for women, 0.081 for men; both p value < 0.001).ConclusionsElevated FMR, particularly leg FMR, is strongly associated with higher CAD risk independent of BMI. These findings suggest that altered body composition may reflect underlying metabolic dysregulations, and assessing leg FMR could provide additive value for risk reclassification, particularly in women.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03251-8.Keywords: Fat-to-muscle mass ratio, Coronary artery disease, Cohort study, Metabolic signatures

Introduction

As coronary artery disease (CAD) persists as the principal cause of worldwide mortality, precise metabolic profiling remains critical for effective clinical intervention [1]. However, standard assessments such as body mass index (BMI) are inadequate, as they overlook the distinction between somatic muscle and fat mass, as well as visceral distribution. These shortcomings frequently result in the "obesity paradox" and diminish predictive power [2]. To overcome these barriers, adopting advanced body composition metrics is imperative for optimizing risk categorization and clarifying the metabolic heterogeneity associated with individual cardiac risk.

The equilibrium between metabolic load (adiposity) and metabolic reserve (muscle) is increasingly identified as a cornerstone of cardiometabolic well-being. Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) offers a pragmatic and reproducible approach to quantify these tissue compartments [3, 4]. The fat-to-muscle mass ratio (FMR), derived from BIA, serves as a novel composite marker reflecting this metabolic balance [5]. Despite emerging evidence linking higher FMR values to insulin resistance and systemic metabolic dysfunction, the potential predictive role of either whole-body or segmental FMR in the onset of coronary artery disease has not yet been fully characterized [6–8].

Underlying these anthropometric variations, circulating metabolic signatures may represent the fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms linking body composition phenotypes to cardiovascular outcomes [9]. High-throughput metabolomics offers a powerful tool to capture these functional physiological states. Crucially, given the profound sexual dimorphism in body fat distribution, muscle mass, and metabolic regulation, it is essential to characterize the metabolic fingerprints of FMR within a sex-stratified framework [10]. Understanding how these specific metabolic profiles mediate the relationship between body composition and CAD could reveal distinct pathogenic pathways in men and women.

While previous studies have linked whole-body FMR to cardiovascular outcomes, two critical gaps remain. First, the regional disparities of FMR have not been systematically evaluated. Second, the precise circulating metabolic pathways driving this risk are unknown. Therefore, utilizing the large-scale prospective UK Biobank cohort, this study aims to investigate the associations of total and regional FMR with incident CAD risk and to comprehensively evaluate to what extent these associations are statistically accounted for by distinct circulating metabolic profiles.

Methods

Participants and research design

All the data utilized in our research was obtained from the electronic medical records in the UK Biobank, as a large-scale prospective cohort study with more than 500,000 UK community-dwelling adults recruited between 2006 and 2010. More details of UK Biobank can be obtained in https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/. Before the recruitment, the informed consents of all participants were collected and this project was finally approved by the National Research Ethics Service Committee North West-Haydock [11]. Moreover, in our research, we excluded participants who withdrew consent for further linkage and those with missing data of body composition in the first two round, leaving 491,234 for further selection. Subsequently, we simulated primary prevention conditions by excluding participants with known CAD (n = 16,618), diabetes (n = 29,439), stroke (n = 7771), peripheral artery disease (PAD, n = 3509), or those who reported using lipid-lowering medications during the oral interview (n = 79,859), which finally left 398,435 participants included in the main analysis. The detailed flow chart for participants selection was exhibited in Fig. S1. And the data field identifiers and variable construction used in our research were summarized in Table S1.

Evaluation for exposures

At baseline, anthropometric measurements were obtained by trained staff adhering to a standardized protocol. Using a SECA 240 stadiometer and the Tanita BC418MA bioimpedance analyzer, we measured height, weight, and regional tissue composition [12]. Fat and predicted muscle mass were assessed for the trunk and limbs. The FMR was defined as the ratio of fat to muscle mass. specifically, arm and leg FMRs were computed based on the combined tissue mass of the respective left and right extremities [13].

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomic profile

High-throughput NMR profiling (Nightingale Health, Finland) was performed on EDTA plasma from ~ 491,000 UK Biobank participants between 2019 and 2023 [9]. Data collection spanned three phases across nine spectrometers. The panel included 251 biomarkers, comprising 14 lipoprotein subclasses, fatty acids, and low-molecular-weight metabolites. Following quality control to minimize variability, metabolite concentrations were log-transformed and z-standardized for analysis.

Study endpoints

The incident CAD was defined as the primary outcome of our research based on the codes provided in Table S2. Specifically speaking, we ascertained incident CAD as the principal study outcome, comprising the initial presentation of fatal or non-fatal myocardial infarction (ICD-10 codes: I21–I23, I24.1, I25.2) or coronary revascularization procedures (OPCS-4 codes: K40–K41, K45, K49–K50, K75). Time-to-event calculations spanned from baseline assessment until the diagnosis of CAD, death, or the region-specific administrative censoring date (England: September 30, 2021; Scotland: July 31, 2021; Wales: February 28, 2018), whichever occurred earliest.

Statistical analysis

Given that previous research has highlighted gender differences in body composition, all subsequent analyses in this study were stratified by sex [14]. For baseline characteristics across all participants, continuous variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are reported as percentages relative to the total number of participants in each category. We used Cox proportional hazards regression models to evaluate the association between total or regional FMR and the incident CAD in both male and female individuals, whose results were exhibited as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) through 3 models. For the model adjustments, Model 1 was adjusted for age, and Model 2 was further adjusted for race/ethnicity, TDI, education level, household income, smoking status, alcohol intake, healthy diet score, physical activity, Hb1Ac, eGFR, and medical history. And Model 3 was a multivariable model that incorporated both FMR and BMI for adjustment, was used to explore the independent role of FMR on CAD. Additionally, the possible non-linear relationship between FMR and CAD incidence was explored via using the Cox regression models with restricted cubic spline (RCS) function (3 knots). And the reference point for RCS was defined from the median value of FMR.

To elucidate the combined impact of body size and tissue composition, we evaluated the joint association of BMI and FMR. Participants were dichotomized based on World Health Organization guidelines, using a threshold of 25 kg/m2 to distinguish between low and high BMI categories [15]. Simultaneously, a data-derived cutoff for FMR was established at the inflection point where the HR reached 1 in RCS models. These binary variables generated four distinct phenotypes: low-FMR/low-BMI (LFLB, defined as the reference), low-FMR/high-BMI (LFHB), high-FMR/low-BMI (HFLB), and high-FMR/high-BMI (HFHB). Furthermore, we conducted stratified analyses to assess potential effect modification by age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), physical activity levels (< 21 vs. ≥ 21 MET-h/week), and diet quality scores (< 3 vs. ≥ 3).

To derive metabolic proxies for whole-body and regional FMR, we applied elastic net regression to the panel of 251 standardized NMR metabolites. This hybrid approach integrates the penalty functions of both Lasso and Ridge techniques. Firstly, all metabolites were standardized to the same dimension. And the penalty intensity parameter λ of the model was calculated using the “cv.Glmnet function”. The maximum λ value within one standard deviation of the minimum mean square error was selected for subsequent iterative calculations. Furthermore, we adopted the glmnet function in the “glmnet” package (version 5.0) of R software to extract the corresponding coefficients of various metabolites in each iteration of elastic net regression. Based on the regression coefficients obtained from the elastic net regression model, the positive and negative weights corresponding to each metabolite were determined. Metabolic signature were subsequently calculated as the linear combination of metabolite concentrations weighted by their respective non-zero coefficients [16]. The predictive value of these signatures for CAD was assessed using Cox models. Furthermore, a mediation analysis investigated whether metabolic signature could explain the associations between total/regional FMR and CAD incidence. Specifically, Direct effect represented the association between FMR and CAD; indirect effect represented the association between FMR and CAD mediated by metabolic signature. The R package “Mets” (version 1.3.9) was used to measure the direct effects of and the indirect effect [17]. Meanwhile, the mediation analysis was refined by adjusting the Cox model covariates as mentioned above.

To evaluate the incremental predictive value of total or regional FMR beyond conventional risk factors, we calculated the C-index, continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). We constructed a baseline risk model using established conventional risk factors, consistent with the variables used in the SCORE2 risk assessment models to estimate the 10-year risk of cardiovascular disease in Europe and compared the above indicators with an augmented model that additionally included FMR [18]. Pairwise comparisons of ROC areas under the curve (AUC) were evaluated using DeLong’s test.

In summary, all analyses in our research were conducted in the R software (version 4.4.3) and the statistical significance was determined using a two-sided p value.

Results

Participants’ baseline characteristics

A total of 398,435 participants from the UK Biobank were included in the analyses, consisting of 167,942 men and 230,493 women. Participants were stratified by CAD status and sex for comparison. And the baseline characteristics of the above participants were exhibited in Table 1. During follow-up, 10,740 new CAD cases were diagnosed among these participants. Moreover, these CAD patients were generally older and had a higher TDI compared to NM participants in both sexes. From a socio-demographic perspective, CAD patients were more likely to be of White ethnicity, had lower education levels, and lower household income. Regarding lifestyle factors, individuals with CAD were more often current or previous smokers, reported higher alcohol intake, had lower healthy diet scores, and engaged in slightly higher physical activity levels. Cardiometabolic profiles also differed significantly: CAD participants exhibited higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure, elevated HbA1c, reduced eGFR, and higher BMI. Medical history of hypertension was more prevalent among CAD participants, while chronic liver disease showed limited association. Additionally, both total and regional FMR were significantly higher in the CAD group across all measured type. And the distributions of each FMR type were assessed visually in Fig. S2 for women and Fig. S3 for men.

Linear and non-linear relationship between FMR and incident CAD

As depicted in Fig. 1, multivariable regression revealed consistent detrimental effects of higher FMR on cardiac health. Men generally exhibited more pronounced risk elevations compared to women across all categories. For total body FMR, the hazard ratio reached 3.716 (95% CI 2.853–4.841) in males versus 1.970 (95% CI 1.565–2.481) in females after full covariates adjustment. Regional analyses mirrored this trend: trunk and arm FMRs significantly predicted incident CAD, with HRs ranging from 1.359 to 2.953. Most strikingly, the leg FMR elicited the most substantial risk increase regardless of gender, with hazard ratios peaking at 2.644 (2.048–3.413) for women and 4.489 (3.344–6.027) for men.

To isolate the specific impact of muscle-fat balance, Model 3 incorporated mutual adjustments for BMI [19, 20]. In the female cohort, this adjustment rendered the associations for global, trunk, and arm FMR statistically non-significant; notably, only leg FMR persisted as a significant risk factor (HR: 3.672, 95% CI 1.958–6.887). In contrast, men showed a different profile: while arm FMR was no longer predictive (HR: 0.726), the hazard ratios for whole-body, trunk, and leg measures remained significant (HRs: 1.933, 1.566, and 2.682, respectively), despite a reduction in effect size. Comparative performance assessment via ROC curves (Fig. 2A and B) further confirmed that all four FMR subtypes outperformed traditional BMI regarding predictive accuracy for incident coronary events, while whole body and leg FMR exhibited statistical significance in both sexes.

We employed RCS function embedded within Cox regression models to characterize the potential non-linear associations between FMR subtypes and CAD susceptibility (methodology outlined in Fig. 3A). As illustrated in Fig. 3B and C, the relationship between FMR and coronary risk significantly departed from linearity across all anatomical regions for both sexes. Notably, the trajectory for arm FMR in women followed a distinct inverted U-shaped pattern. To facilitate risk stratification, we identified specific cut-off values where the hazard ratio curve intersected unity (HR = 1). These derived thresholds were: 0.79 (whole-body), 0.72 (trunk), 0.64 (arm), and 0.73 (leg) for females; and 0.45, 0.57, 0.39, and 0.36 for males, respectively. Based on these stratifications, cumulative incidence plots (Figs. S4–S5) confirmed that participants exceeding these limits faced a markedly elevated probability of developing CAD.

Combination FMR and BMI for classifications

To further elucidate the combined effects of general adiposity and body composition balance on CAD risk, we conducted a joint analysis of BMI and whole body FMR. Participants were thus categorized into four groups, according to the criteria in Fig. 4A. As illustrated in Fig. 4B–D, joint grouping revealed distinct patterns of CAD risk associated with different combinations of BMI and FMR. Generally, the co-occurrence of HFHB signaled the worst prognosis, particularly for whole-body measures in both sexes and trunk/arm indices in men. In these scenarios, while BMI elevation alone (LFHB) was detrimental, the addition of high FMR further exacerbated susceptibility. A notable exception occurred with leg FMR in females. Here, increased BMI did not correlate with higher risk; instead, the hazard was predominantly driven by high FMR in the context of low weight (HFLB). Consequently, we summarized these relationships as 'Auxiliary' (BMI-driven, FMR-amplified), 'Synergistic' (mutual reinforcement), or 'Independent'.

Subgroup analyses

To explore potential effect modifiers in the association between FMR and incident CAD, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by age, physical activity, and healthy diet score. These factors were selected based on their established roles in modulating body composition and cardiovascular risk. Subgroup analyses were performed separately for whole body, trunk, arm, and leg FMR, with results presented in Tables S3–S6 for each FMR type.

In the age-stratified analysis, we observed notable interactions between FMR and age in relation to CAD risk. For whole body FMR (HR = 2.47, 95% CI 1.26–4.84) and trunk FMR (HR = 1.85, 95% CI 1.16–4.84), a significant interaction was detected in females, with an increased risk of CAD in those aged < 60 years, while in males, the interaction was not significant. Physical activity level also modified the association between FMR and CAD risk. But this phenomenon was only observed in the male population. In detail, a higher incident risk of CAD may be more pronounced in physically inactive individuals, with the HRs (95% CI) of 2.52 (1.06–6.01) for whole body FMR, 2.07 (1.02–4.17) for trunk FMR, 2.52 (1.95–6.71) for arm FMR, and 2.53 (1.14–5.62) for leg FMR, respectively. Regarding healthy diet score, a healthier diet may attenuate the adverse association between higher FMR and CAD risk according to the results in the subgroup analysis.

Metabolites and corresponding signatures in response to FMR

Given the crucial impact of body composition on human metabolic processes, we further explore the variations of 251 metabolites and construct the corresponding metabolic signatures in response to four FMR types. 44,179 participants were excluded due to the missing of metabolomic profiles and finally we included a total of 354,256 people for the further analyses. Table S7 showed the differences of baseline characteristics between the participants with and without metabolomic profiles. The elastic net regression on the 251 metabolites was performed to construct the metabolic signatures for the FMR of whole body, trunk, arm, and leg. As shown in Fig. 5A, a combination of 202 metabolites associated with whole body FMR, 209 metabolites with trunk FMR, 204 metabolites with arm FMR, and 216 metabolites with leg FMR were detected. Omega-6 fatty acids to total fatty acids percentage (Omega-6%) was the strongest positive metabolite associated with the FMR of whole body and leg, and the cholesteryl esters to total lipids in very large VLDL percentage (XL-VLDL-CE%) showed the strongest positive association with the FMR of arm and trunk. And the HRs (95% CI) of Omega-6% and XL-VLDL-CE% indicated their adverse role in increasing the incident risk of CAD (Fig. 5B and Table S8).

The weighted sum of the selected metabolites is considered to represent the metabolic signature corresponding to FMR, with weights equal to the coefficients from the elastic network regression. After adjusting FMR, metabolic signatures and other covariates, all the metabolic signatures in both male and female populations remained associated with an increased risk of CAD, as seen in Fig. 6A. However, only leg FMR still exhibited its risky role in the incidence of CAD in both sexes. Therefore, we hypothesize that the association between FMR and incident CAD is largely propagated through specific metabolic pathways, reflecting the physiological impact of altered body composition on systemic metabolism. The specific hypothetical diagram is shown in Fig. S6. And we utilized the mediation analysis to explore the role of metabolic signatures in increasing CAD risk from total and regional FMR. For women, the metabolic signatures mediated the associations between four FMR types and CAD with the HRs (95% CIs) of 1.43 (1.29–1.59) for whole body FMR, 1.36 (1.26–1.47) for trunk FMR, 1.42 (1.28–1.58) for arm FMR, and 1.36 (1.26–1.47) for leg FMR, respectively (Fig. 6B). Similarly, as exhibited in Fig. 6C, for men, the metabolic signatures mediated the associations between four FMR types and CAD with the HRs (95% CIs) of 1.53 (1.38–1.71) for whole body FMR, 1.59 (1.46–1.74) for trunk FMR, 1.4 (1.28–1.54) for arm FMR, and 1.2 (1.12–1.29) for leg FMR, respectively. The percentages of the mediation effect in the total effect between FMR and incident risk of CAD were all more than 70%, further emphasizing the crucial role of metabolic signatures in the risky relationship between FMR and CAD.

Sensitivity analyses

To verify the reliability of our conclusions, we performed a series of rigorous robustness checks. Initially, we repeated the regression models after removing subjects with incomplete covariate data; the outcomes mirrored the primary results (Table S9). To mitigate potential reverse causality, we further excluded incident cases occurring within the first 12 months of follow-up, yet the findings remained unaltered (Table S10). Subsequently, we re-evaluated the FMR-CAD relationship within the specific sub-cohort (n = 354, 25) possessing complete NMR metabolomic profiles. While the overall trend persisted, the statistical significance for female trunk and male arm FMRs was attenuated in this restricted sample (Table S11). However, the predictive stability of the derived metabolic signatures was fully corroborated in parallel analyses (Tables S12 and S13), thereby affirming the overall integrity of the study.

Incremental predictive value of leg FMR

According to the above analysis, we indicated the crucial role of leg FMR in the associations with CAD incidence. Therefore, we further evaluated whether adding leg FMR to a model containing conventional risk factors of SCORE2 could improve CAD risk prediction (Table 2). In female participants, the addition of leg FMR yielded a statistically significant improvement in reclassification, with a continuous NRI of 0.142 (95% CI 0.097–0.187, p value < 0.001). The C-index showed a marginal increase from 0.7397 to 0.7401 (ΔC-index = 0.0004). The IDI was negligible. Similarly, in male participants, the inclusion of leg FMR significantly improved risk reclassification, evidenced by a continuous NRI of 0.081 (95% CI 0.049–0.112, p value < 0.001). The C-index increased slightly from 0.6979 to 0.6990 (ΔC-index = 0.0011), with a small IDI of 0.0001. These results suggest that while leg FMR may not drastically alter the global ranking of predicted probabilities (as reflected by the C-index), it significantly refines the risk classification of individuals, particularly in women.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large-scale prospective cohort study to simultaneously map the regional disparities of the FMR and decode the underlying metabolomic mechanisms linking body composition imbalance to incident CAD. Over a median follow-up of 12.6 years among nearly 400,000 UK Biobank participants, we demonstrated that elevated FMR is a robust risk factor for CAD. Moving beyond traditional whole-body metrics, we revealed that leg FMR serves as the strongest and most independent predictor, outperforming BMI, particularly in women. Most strikingly, by utilizing high-throughput NMR metabolomics, we constructed FMR-specific metabolic signatures and discovered that these systemic alterations characterized by specific remnant cholesterols and fatty acids, mediated over 70% of the FMR-CAD risk. These findings not only provide precise epidemiological evidence for targeted regional body composition management but also establish a potential mechanistic roadmap explaining how metabolic burden translates into cardiovascular events.

Our findings address a significant gap in the existing literature. Previous studies have predominantly focused on single-dimensional body composition metrics such as visceral fat area (VFA), epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), or crude waist circumference estimates [21–23]. Large-scale prospective evidence directly evaluating the association between the relative balance of fat and muscle mass (i.e., FMR) and CAD hard endpoint events remains extremely limited. Recent studies conducted in the UK Biobank confirm that body fat percentage (BF%) and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) are superior predictors of CAD compared to BMI [24]. Furthermore, higher fat-free mass (FFM) is associated with a reduced risk of heart failure [25]. This aligns with our research findings, which indicate that risk stems not only from the “quantity” of fat but also depends on its “ratio” relative to functional muscle mass. The unique contribution of this study lies in modeling whole body, trunk, arm, and leg FMR separately, revealing that FMR exhibits the strongest predictive capability for risk. This regional disparity may carry profound biological significance. However, the relationship between leg FMR and CAD is likely bidirectional and complex. While diminished leg muscle mass coupled with adiposity exacerbates systemic insulin resistance, it is equally plausible that subclinical cardiovascular dysfunction or other systemic aging processes manifest early as reduced physical capacity, leading to secondary lower-limb muscle atrophy. Thus, leg FMR may serve as a comprehensive phenotypic readout of both metabolic load and early functional decline [26, 27].

Another innovation of this study lies in utilizing elastic net regularized regression to screen robust metabolic signatures of total or regional FMR from 251 NMR metabolites, successfully establishing a mediation model between FMR and CAD. Our analysis indicates that multiple metabolites, including the XL-VLDL-CE% and Omega-6%, play significant mediating roles in the association between FMR and CAD. XL-VLDL-CE% constitutes a key component of remnant cholesterol. Recent studies utilizing the Nightingale platform have confirmed that remnant cholesterol plays a central pathogenic role in the development of atherosclerosis by promoting lipid infiltration of arterial walls, endothelial dysfunction, and low-grade inflammatory responses [28, 29]. Our mediation analysis results align closely with this finding, suggesting that elevated FMR may accelerate CAD progression by exacerbating excessive hepatic VLDL production and impaired metabolic clearance, leading to the accumulation of cholesterol-rich remnant lipoprotein particles in circulation. Regarding omega-6%, its association with CAD is complex. Previous studies suggest a potential U-shaped relationship [30], where both excessively high and low levels may be detrimental to cardiovascular health. It may influence the synthesis of inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins, thereby regulating and exacerbating residual cholesterol-driven vascular inflammation [31, 32]. Although our mediation analysis suggests that metabolic signatures act as downstream effectors of FMR, we acknowledge the complex, likely bidirectional relationship between body composition and metabolic health. Insulin resistance, for instance, can promote both muscle wasting and adiposity (high FMR). However, given that skeletal muscle serves as the primary site for glucose disposal and adipose tissue acts as an active endocrine organ, it is biologically plausible that structural changes in body composition (FMR) act as a significant driver of the observed metabolic dysregulation.

This study observed a stronger association between FMR and CAD in males, potentially stemming from inherent differences between men and women in sex hormone levels, body fat distribution patterns, and muscle metabolism. Before menopause, estrogen in women helps maintain a “pear-shaped” body (where subcutaneous fat accumulates primarily in the hips and lower limbs) and promotes a lipoprotein profile relatively resistant to atherosclerosis (e.g., higher HDL and lower VLDL) [33, 34]. Men, however, naturally tend toward an “apple-shaped” physique with a higher proportion of visceral fat, making them more susceptible to the risks associated with elevated FMR. With aging, particularly after menopause in women, declining estrogen levels cause fat to redistribute toward the abdomen, potentially diminishing these gender differences. Yu et al.'s research also suggests that the hazards of high FMR vary across age groups and genders, with young men (< 50 years) and older women (≥ 60 years) representing high-risk windows [13]. This aligns with hormonal shifts and evolving physical activity levels throughout the lifespan.

In addition, to further clarify the clinical implications of our research findings, it is necessary to explore the modifying effects of lifestyle factors, particularly physical activity and nutritional quality, on the developmental trajectory of FMR-CAD. As emphasized in our subgroup analyses, the above lifestyle factors can be regarded as not only covariates but also potent effect modifiers. Physiologically, an elevated FMR typically corresponds to the phenotype of sarcopenic obesity or increased myosteatosis [35, 36]. Recent evidence indicates that hidden intermuscular fat infiltration directly contributes to systemic insulin resistance and coronary microvascular dysfunction, exacerbating cardiovascular risk independent of overall body weight [37–39]. Our data revealed that the detrimental impact of a high FMR was most pronounced among physically inactive participants, particularly males. This interaction suggests that regular physical activity can partially offset the anatomical disadvantage of a high FMR by enhancing skeletal muscle quality, improving metabolic flexibility, and restoring insulin-mediated glucose disposal, even if the absolute fat mass remains elevated [40, 41]. Similarly, nutritional awareness, proxied by the healthy diet score, emerged as a critical buffer against FMR-related risks in both sexes. Adherence to a high-quality diet significantly attenuated the CAD risk associated with elevated FMR [41]. High-quality diets with abundant dietary fibers and balanced polyunsaturated fats are well-documented to counteract systemic low-grade inflammation and modulate lipid metabolism [42]. In summary, while an elevated FMR defines a high-risk anatomical phenotype, it is highly modifiable. Targeted interventions focusing on muscle-strengthening exercises and cardiovascular-protective nutrition remain highly effective strategies to rescue the metabolic profile and prevent incident CAD in these vulnerable populations.

Overall, the findings of this study hold significant clinical and public health implications. First, they underscore the necessity of assessing body composition in cardiovascular primary prevention, particularly for identifying the high-risk “normal weight obesity” phenotype in individuals with normal BMI. Second, given the convenience, low cost, and high concordance (r = 0.80–0.93) of BIA with the gold standard dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) in the UK Biobank [43], incorporating FMR—particularly leg FMR—into routine physical examinations and large-scale population screening is entirely feasible. The focus of clinical intervention should shift from mere “weight loss” to a balanced approach of “fat reduction and muscle gain.” Improving body composition through exercise and nutritional guidance may represent a more effective primary prevention strategy for CAD. The American College of Cardiology (ACC) expert consensus has proposed using FMR thresholds (e.g., > 0.35 for men, > 0.40 for women) as a reference for initiating enhanced interventions [44]. Our research provides solid evidence to support such recommendations. Moreover, our study extends current knowledge by demonstrating the incremental predictive value of leg FMR. However, it is important to cautiously interpret the incremental predictive value of leg FMR. Because established conventional risk models via incorporating variables similar to SCORE2 already possess strong discriminative capability, the global C-index improvement observed upon adding leg FMR was marginal. However, the significant continuous NRI suggests that leg FMR's true clinical utility lies not in revolutionizing overall prediction, but rather in refining risk reclassification for specific borderline individuals, particularly women thereby aiding in more personalized primary prevention strategies [18]. This discrepancy suggests that leg FMR is particularly effective at correctly reclassifying individuals who might be misclassified by traditional risk factors alone, thereby potentially refining primary prevention strategies. Furthermore, metabolic markers such as XL-VLDL-CE% identified through elastic network screening not only deepen our understanding of pathophysiological mechanisms but also hold promise as monitoring indicators or novel therapeutic targets for drug interventions (e.g., therapies targeting residual cholesterol) in the future.

Despite the significant strengths of this study, including its large sample size, prospective design, and integration of multi-omics data, several limitations exist. First, body composition was measured using BIA, which, although extensively validated, remains less accurate than DXA or MRI and may be influenced by factors such as individual hydration status [45, 46]. Second, a critical temporal mismatch exists: NMR metabolomics data were acquired years after the baseline body composition assessment (2019–2023). Consequently, our mediation analysis models purely statistical associations rather than definitive causal pathways. We cannot establish temporal precedence between FMR alterations and metabolic shifts, nor can we rule out reverse causality where subclinical CAD lesions independently alter metabolic profiles. Third, as an observational study, despite extensive adjustment for multiple confounders, residual confounding remains possible, such as unmeasured socioeconomic factors, dietary details, or inflammatory markers [47], precluding definitive causal inference. Fourth, the study population predominantly comprised European Caucasians, necessitating caution when extrapolating findings to other ethnic groups [48].

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that FMR, particularly leg FMR, serves as a robust and accessible risk biomarker for CAD independent of traditional BMI metrics. Its pathogenic risk is partially mediated through metabolic dysregulation pathways characterized by residual cholesterol and fatty acid imbalances. These findings offer new perspectives for cardiovascular disease risk stratification and prevention strategies, emphasizing the core importance of maintaining healthy body composition, especially a lower FMR.
