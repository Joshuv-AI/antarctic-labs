Matching-adjusted indirect comparison of enzalutamide versus apalutamide with androgen-deprivation therapy in patients with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer
Authors: Arun A Azad, Bhavik J Pandya, Hemant Singh Bhadauria, Erhan Berrak, Vagia Daki, Paulo Martins, Janet Kim, Alexis Serikoff, Gianluca Baio, Andrew J Armstrong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo compare the efficacy of enzalutamide + androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT) versus apalutamide + ADT in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) using a matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC).Patients and methodsIndividual patient data from patients with ≥1 confirmed bone metastasis in ARCHES (NCT02677896; enzalutamide + ADT vs placebo + ADT; n = 971) were adjusted to match baseline characteristics of patients in TITAN (NCT02489318; apalutamide + ADT vs placebo + ADT; N = 1052). Overall survival (OS), radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS), and time to prostate-specific antigen progression (TTPP) were assessed using Cox regression with placebo + ADT as the common comparator. Hazard ratios (HRs) were reported with apalutamide as the reference.ResultsThis MAIC suggested similar estimated efficacy between enzalutamide and apalutamide for OS (HR: 0.81, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.58–1.15) and TTPP (HR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.41–1.03). Patients treated with enzalutamide + ADT had improved rPFS versus those treated with apalutamide + ADT (HR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.45–0.98), but differences were not significant in sensitivity analyses aligning assessment methods.ConclusionsThis analysis suggested broadly similar efficacy between enzalutamide and apalutamide in patients with mHSPC. Findings should be interpreted in the context of inherent MAIC limitations.KEYWORDS: Apalutamide, enzalutamide, matching-adjusted indirect comparison, mHSPC, overall survival, progression-free survival

Introduction

Clinical guidelines recommend androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT) with androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) as a first-line treatment option for metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) [1,2]. Examples of ARPIs include enzalutamide and apalutamide, both of which are approved for use in mHSPC and are associated with improved clinical outcomes in patients with mHSPC. The phase III ARCHES randomized controlled trial (RCT) (NCT02677896) showed that, compared with placebo + ADT, enzalutamide + ADT was associated with significantly improved radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS) [3] and overall survival (OS) [4–6] in patients with mHSPC with any metastases. Similarly, the phase III TITAN RCT (NCT02489318), which was restricted to patients with ≥1 bone metastasis, reported that apalutamide + ADT was associated with significantly prolonged rPFS [7] and OS [8,9] compared with placebo + ADT.

ARCHES and TITAN demonstrated improved clinical outcomes among patients treated with enzalutamide + ADT and apalutamide + ADT compared with placebo + ADT, as well as similar rates of adverse events (AEs) leading to treatment discontinuation. However, there are currently no head-to-head RCTs comparing the relative efficacy of these therapies in mHSPC. In such situations, treatments can be indirectly compared [10–14]. While unadjusted indirect comparisons assume similar distributions of treatment effect modifiers (EMs; i.e., patient or disease characteristics that may modify the relative effect of a treatment) between studies [10], matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) analyses offer the ability to re-weight individual patient data (IPD) from one study so that selected baseline characteristics can be matched to the published aggregate baseline characteristics of the comparator study population, reducing cross-trial differences and enabling indirect treatment comparisons [10,12,15].

Previous MAIC analyses in the non-metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (nmCRPC) setting have compared enzalutamide and apalutamide [13,14], but there remains a need for similar analyses in the context of mHSPC. The objective of this study was to evaluate the comparative efficacy of enzalutamide + ADT and apalutamide + ADT in patients with mHSPC using MAIC methodology.

Methods

This MAIC analysis compared ARCHES and TITAN, with placebo + ADT as the common comparator (Figure 1) [3,7]. Enrollment criteria for ARCHES and TITAN are presented in Supplementary Table S1 [3,7,16]. In both trials, ARPIs were orally administered once daily at their respective standard doses with ADT (enzalutamide: 160 mg; apalutamide: 240 mg) [3,7] in a population of adult patients with pathologically confirmed mHSPC, either de novo or after recurrence. To more closely match the study populations between the two trials, only patients in ARCHES who had ≥1 confirmed bone metastasis at baseline were included, as this was a requirement for inclusion in TITAN [7] (Figure 1). This restriction was important to help ensure the comparability of the study populations and rigor of the analysis. Patients without bone metastases may present with distant lymph node metastases only, which is associated with a better overall prognosis, or with visceral disease only, which may be associated with poorer prognosis and treatment outcomes [17,18].

MAIC method used to compare efficacy of enzalutamide + ADT versus apalutamide + ADT.

Potential EMs were identified using a stepwise approach consisting of a review of covariates common to both ARCHES and TITAN (Supplementary Table S2), a targeted literature review, consultation with clinical experts, and statistical assessment [12]; prognostic variables were excluded from the analysis to avoid loss of precision, consistent with MAIC methodology [10]. EMs were selected based on the primary endpoint of rPFS, on which the study design and statistical power were based. For consistency, and in the absence of strong clinical rationale for proceeding otherwise, the same set of EMs was applied across all MAIC efficacy outcomes, reflecting the strong association between progression-related time-to-event endpoints and OS [19,20]. This set—reflecting expert consultation, literature review, and statistical assessment—was broader than the set of EMs that only presented a significant interaction with treatment in ARCHES (Supplementary Table S3). However, as a uniform set of EMs may not fully account for distinct clinical or biological factors affecting individual endpoints, different sets of EMs were used in sensitivity analyses. Results of the statistical assessment and clinical consultation are presented in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4 [14,21]. Variables deemed to have strong effect-modifying potential on rPFS were metastatic status at initial diagnosis (de novo or recurrent) [22], disease volume [23], presence of visceral disease [18], and baseline prostate-specific antigen (PSA) before ADT treatment initiation in the metastatic setting. Variables deemed to have moderate effect-modifying potential were prior use of docetaxel [21], Gleason score [24,25], age [26,27], region [28], and race [29–32]. Although Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS) is an important prognostic factor for OS among patients with mHSPC [33,34], both ARCHES and TITAN focused only on patients with an ECOG PS of 0 or 1. Since ECOG PS did not significantly modify the hazard ratio (HR) for OS or rPFS in either trial, we did not adjust for ECOG PS in our main MAIC analysis; however, it was included in a sensitivity analysis.

All potential EMs were analyzed categorically and based on original trial stratifications and definitions from the ARCHES and/or TITAN publications, with numerical variables (e.g., age and baseline PSA) converted to categories based on predefined levels: age (<65 years, 65–75 years, ≥75 years); baseline PSA (<5 ng/mL, ≥5 ng/mL); disease volume (low, high); prior docetaxel use (yes, no); presence of visceral disease (yes, no); Gleason score ≥8 (yes, no); race (White, Asian, Black or African American, Other); region (Asia, Latin America, Europe and rest of world [North America and all other countries]); metastatic status at initial diagnosis (M1 [de novo], M0 [recurrent], MX/unknown [distant metastasis could not be assessed]); and ECOG PS (0/1). One patient in TITAN with an ECOG PS of 2 was categorized as having an ECOG PS of 1, as ARCHES did not enroll patients with an ECOG PS of 2. To achieve matching across EMs, observations with missing/unknown data in ARCHES were excluded when a “missing/unknown” category was not available in TITAN. Data imputation was not performed.

To confirm the feasibility of conducting the MAIC, we assessed potential sources of heterogeneity between the two trials in terms of study design, patient population, outcome definitions, and data availability.

Statistical analysis

MAIC analysis

After excluding patients with no bone metastases, IPD from ARCHES were adjusted to match baseline characteristics of published aggregate data from TITAN [3,4,7,8,12].

ARCHES OS interim data were immature at the time of first data cutoff, with 84/1150 (7.3%) deaths across treatment groups [3]; OS data were therefore analyzed using the final data cutoff for both ARCHES and TITAN (median follow-up: ARCHES, 44.6 months; TITAN, 44.0 months) [4,8]. Although this approach could introduce possible confounding from treatment crossover for OS, use of the final data cutoff improved comparability between trials in terms of follow-up duration and data maturity. For the rPFS and time to prostate-specific antigen progression (TTPP) outcomes, the first data cutoff was used to avoid confounding due to treatment crossover (median follow-up: ARCHES,13.6 months; TITAN, 22.7 months) [3,7]. While the method of rPFS assessment differed between the two trials—in ARCHES, rPFS was assessed via blinded independent central review (BICR), while in TITAN, rPFS was assessed locally [3,7]—we opted to use the results from the first data cutoff, as these assessment methods and results were aligned with each study’s protocol, regulatory documents, and final publications. In terms of data maturity at first data cutoff, ARCHES had 292 rPFS events (91 [15.9%] with enzalutamide [N = 574] and 201 [34.9%] with placebo [N = 576]); median TTPP was not reached [3]. TITAN had 365 rPFS events (134 [25.5%] with apalutamide [N = 525] and 231 [43.8%] with placebo [N = 527]); median TTPP could not be estimated for patients receiving apalutamide and was 12.9 months for patients receiving placebo [7].

Adjustment weights were estimated using the quasi-Newton optimization method to account for population differences in EMs [11,12]. Ultimately, the EMs adjusted for all endpoints were those with strong or moderate effect-modifying potential: age, presence of visceral disease, metastatic status at initial diagnosis (de novo or recurrent), disease volume, Gleason score, race, region, and prior docetaxel use. Although baseline PSA was identified as having strong effect-modifying potential in our initial evaluation of the EMs, this potential was only present among patients whose baseline PSA was measured before ADT exposure. As some patients in both trials may have been exposed to ADT before randomization and PSA measurement, we opted not to adjust for baseline PSA in our main analysis to reduce the risk of confounding; however, we did evaluate the impact of baseline PSA in a sensitivity analysis.

Outcomes that were deemed feasible for MAIC analysis were assessed using Cox regression models, incorporating the estimated weights for patients in ARCHES. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption was evaluated for all outcomes of interest using guidance from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) Decision Support Unit Technical Support Document 14 [35]. The weighted HRs were compared with the published HRs from TITAN.

Apalutamide + ADT was the reference treatment for all analyses, and nominal statistical significance was set at 0.05.

Unadjusted Bucher analysis

Unadjusted indirect treatment comparisons were conducted using the Bucher method [36]. This method assumes EMs between study populations are balanced and allowed us to evaluate the impact of adjustment for EMs in the MAIC analysis on relative efficacy estimates [11].

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted that followed the same approach used for the main MAIC analysis, allowing adjustment for three additional sets of EMs. The first (minimalist) set adjusted only for metastatic status at initial diagnosis (de novo or recurrent), disease volume, and prior docetaxel use. The second set included ECOG PS with the EMs from the main MAIC analysis. The third (maximalist) set adjusted for both ECOG PS and PSA at baseline with the EMs included in the main MAIC analysis.

Crossover-adjusted OS sensitivity analysis

Another sensitivity analysis was conducted to account for bias due to treatment crossover in the OS analysis. A smaller proportion of patients crossed over to intervention after study unblinding in ARCHES (n = 180, 31.3%) [4] than in TITAN (n = 208, 39.5%) [8]; however, the median treatment duration for the placebo crossover group was longer in ARCHES (23.9 months) compared with TITAN (15.4 months) [8]. The rank-preserving structural failure time model (RPSFTM) [37,38] was therefore applied to ARCHES IPD for patients who had ≥1 confirmed bone metastasis at baseline to estimate adjusted survival outcomes under a counterfactual scenario in which no crossover occurred. The inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) method [39] was used to adjust for crossover in the TITAN trial. The IPCW method could not be applied to ARCHES for the MAIC analysis as both IPCW and MAIC rely on re-weighting, and integrating these methods remains an area of ongoing methodological research [40].

Radiographic PFS sensitivity analyses

Additional sensitivity analyses evaluated outcomes based on different rPFS assessment methods to improve the comparability between the two trials. As mentioned, rPFS in ARCHES was assessed via BICR [3], while in TITAN, rPFS was assessed locally [7]. Separate sensitivity analyses therefore evaluated enzalutamide + ADT and apalutamide + ADT for significant differences in rPFS outcomes using only BICR assessment data or only local assessment data.

Demographics

Notable imbalances were identified in race and region of enrollment. The proportion of White patients in ARCHES (enzalutamide group: n = 466, 81.2%; placebo group: n = 460, 79.9%) was larger compared with TITAN (apalutamide group: n = 354, 67.4%; placebo group: n = 365, 69.3%); the proportion of Asian patients in ARCHES (enzalutamide group: n = 75, 13.1%; placebo group: n = 80, 13.9%) was smaller compared with TITAN (apalutamide group: n = 119, 22.7%; placebo group: n = 110, 20.9%); and the proportion of patients from Europe and the rest of the world in ARCHES (enzalutamide group: n = 438, 76.3%; placebo group: n = 433, 75.2%) was larger compared with TITAN (apalutamide group: n = 303, 57.7%; placebo group: n = 324, 61.5%). The proportion of Black or African American patients in ARCHES (enzalutamide group: n = 8, 1.4%; placebo group: n = 8, 1.4%) was similar to that of TITAN (apalutamide group: n = 10, 1.9%; placebo group: n = 9, 1.7%).

Clinical characteristics

Notable differences were identified in ECOG PS, prior docetaxel use, and metastatic status (de novo or recurrent) at initial diagnosis. The proportion of patients with an ECOG PS of 0 in ARCHES (enzalutamide group: n = 448, 78.0%; placebo group: n = 443, 76.9%) was larger compared with TITAN (apalutamide group: n = 328, 62.5%; placebo group: n = 348, 66.0%); the proportion of patients presenting with metastatic disease at initial diagnosis in ARCHES (enzalutamide group: n = 402, 70.0%; placebo group: n = 365, 63.4%) was smaller compared with TITAN (apalutamide group: n = 411, 78.3%; placebo group: n = 441, 83.7%); and the proportion of patients with ≥1 confirmed bone metastasis in ARCHES (enzalutamide group: n = 485, 84.4%; placebo group, n = 486, 84.5%) was smaller compared with TITAN (100%), where bone metastasis was a condition for eligibility. Lymph node was the only metastatic site for 13% of patients in ARCHES; bone was the only metastatic site for a smaller proportion of patients in ARCHES (enzalutamide group: n = 268, 46.7%; placebo group: n = 245, 42.5%) compared with TITAN (apalutamide group: n = 289, 55.0%; placebo group: n = 269, 51.0%). The proportion of patients where visceral disease was present in ARCHES (enzalutamide group: n = 64, 11.1%; placebo group: n = 64, 11.1%) was similar to TITAN (apalutamide group: n = 56, 10.7%; placebo group: n = 72, 13.7%).

Length of follow-up

ARCHES had a shorter follow-up period than TITAN at the first data cutoff (13.6 months vs 22.7 months, respectively) since OS served as the primary endpoint of TITAN [3,7]. This contributed to differences in endpoint maturity. Length of follow-up was expected to differ between trials as it is an event-driven, trial-level design feature that cannot be addressed through MAIC weighting, which targets baseline covariates. Differences in follow-up affect both the censoring mechanism and endpoint maturity, and adjusting for follow-up would require unverifiable assumptions about unobserved event times and hazard patterns, which would risk biasing the analyses. As a result, length of follow-up was not adjusted in this analysis.

Outcomes

Efficacy outcomes deemed feasible for analysis based on the results of the feasibility assessment were OS, rPFS, and TTPP. These outcomes were available in both trials and had similar definitions. In both trials, OS was defined as time from randomization to death from any cause. rPFS was defined as time from randomization to first objective evidence of radiographic disease progression or death. Disease progression was defined in both trials as the appearance of two or more new lesions on bone scan as assessed via BICR (ARCHES) or local assessment (TITAN). In both ARCHES and TITAN, TTPP was defined as time from randomization to PSA progression. PSA progression was defined as a ≥25% increase and an absolute increase of ≥2 ng/mL in PSA from the nadir [41]. The published results of these outcomes for both trials are presented in Table 3.

MAIC analysis

Matching

To match the TITAN population (N = 1052), patients in ARCHES (N = 1150) with no bone metastases (n = 179) were excluded, and remaining patients with ≥1 bone metastasis (n = 971) were re-weighted according to the pre-specified EMs. Supplementary Figure S1 shows the histograms of rescaled weight distributions for the main MAIC and sensitivity analyses. As expected, the distribution of weights was right-skewed, mostly between 0 and 2, with no evidence of extreme weights; in the main MAIC and sensitivity analyses, the maximum observed weight was less than 6. A small proportion of patients received higher weights, which suggested good overlap between ARCHES and TITAN populations. Following this adjustment, baseline characteristics associated with the pre-specified EMs were well-balanced between the two populations (Table 4; Supplementary Table S5). After re-weighting, the resulting effective sample size (ESS) was 661 (median [range] weight: 0.85 [0.12–5.19]), indicating that a substantial proportion of the original sample contributed to the weighted analyses. Before and after matching, some fluctuations and crossing were identified in the log-cumulative hazard curves for ARCHES, indicating potential violation of the PH assumption. These fluctuations can occur because of the variability introduced by weighting, but formal Grambsch–Therneau testing indicated that the PH assumption was plausible.

MAIC analysis

There were no statistically significant differences in OS (HR: 0.81, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.58–1.15; p = 0.24) or TTPP (HR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.41–1.03; p = 0.07) between patients treated with enzalutamide + ADT and patients treated with apalutamide + ADT, although analyses were directionally favorable to enzalutamide (Figure 2(A)). Patients treated with enzalutamide + ADT had a statistically significant 34% improvement in rPFS compared with those receiving apalutamide + ADT (HR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.45–0.98; p = 0.04) (Figure 2(A)).

Unadjusted Bucher analysis

The unadjusted Bucher analysis showed no statistically significant differences in OS, rPFS, or TTPP between enzalutamide + ADT and apalutamide + ADT, although all analyses were directionally favorable to enzalutamide (Figure 2(B)).

Sensitivity analyses

After re-weighting, the ESS for the first, second, and third sensitivity analyses were, respectively, 828 (median [range] weight: 1.18 [0.16–1.58]), 579 (median [range] weight: 0.82 [0.09–8.10]), and 651 (median [range] weight: 0.85 [0.10–4.62]). Results from MAIC sensitivity analyses adjusting for different sets of EMs were mostly consistent with the main results across all outcomes (Figure 3(A–C)).

Crossover-adjusted OS sensitivity analysis

Consistent with MAIC analysis results, the crossover-adjusted OS sensitivity analysis (ESS = 661, median [range] weight: 0.85 [0.12–5.19]) demonstrated no statistically significant difference between patients treated with enzalutamide + ADT and those treated with apalutamide + ADT (HR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.63–1.29; p = 0.57) (Figure 3(A)). The unadjusted Bucher analysis using the RPSFTM- and IPCW-adjusted OS estimates for ARCHES and TITAN, respectively, also showed no statistically significant difference in OS between treatments (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 0.75–1.39; p = 0.89).

Radiographic PFS sensitivity analyses

The MAIC sensitivity analysis conducted using BICR-assessed rPFS from ARCHES and TITAN [42] (ESS = 661, median [range] weight: 0.85 [0.12–5.19]) demonstrated no statistically significant difference between enzalutamide + ADT and apalutamide + ADT (HR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.49–1.12; p = 0.15) (Figure 3(B)). Similarly, the unadjusted Bucher analysis using BICR-assessed rPFS from both trials showed no statistically significant difference between treatments (HR: 0.88, 95% CI: 0.62–1.27; p = 0.51). This pattern was maintained when using locally assessed rPFS in the MAIC sensitivity analysis (ESS = 661, median [range] weight: 0.85 [0.12–5.19]; HR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.60–1.28; p = 0.49) (Figure 3(B)) and in the unadjusted Bucher analysis (HR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.65–1.26; p = 0.58).

Discussion

This study evaluated the comparative efficacy of enzalutamide + ADT versus apalutamide + ADT in patients with bone-metastatic HSPC with or without visceral and/or nodal metastases. Overall, our analyses suggested broadly similar estimated efficacy between enzalutamide + ADT and apalutamide + ADT in patients with mHSPC with ≥1 confirmed bone metastasis. After baseline adjustment, the modest reduction in ESS indicated low weight variability, adequate population overlap, and stable estimates [10]. MAIC analyses showed no significant differences in the risk of death and disease progression with enzalutamide + ADT over apalutamide + ADT. OS outcomes also support this conclusion. For rPFS, patients treated with enzalutamide + ADT showed improvement compared with patients treated with apalutamide + ADT, as reported in the first data cutoff of ARCHES and TITAN. However, the methods of rPFS assessment at first data cutoff differed between ARCHES (BICR-assessed) and TITAN (locally assessed); this may explain the variability and lack of robustness between the main MAIC analysis and BICR- and locally assessed sensitivity analyses. After revising the method of assessment to compare only BICR- or only locally assessed rPFS, this difference was no longer statistically significant. Factors other than anti-cancer efficacy, such as biological heterogeneity or safety, may impact rPFS outcomes. As treatment response may depend on microenvironmental, hypoxic, and lineage-specific contexts [43], future comparative studies may consider biomarker-guided or biologically stratified approaches [44–46]. Additionally, further head-to-head research or indirect comparisons between these treatments are needed to compare toxicities, tolerability, dose adjustments, treatment discontinuation, and patient-reported outcomes, which represent highly relevant clinical considerations when selecting ARPI therapy.

The following safety observations provide important context for future research comparing the overall risk profiles of enzalutamide and apalutamide. In ARCHES, treatment discontinuation due to AEs occurred in 7.2% of patients who received enzalutamide + ADT and in 5.2% of patients who received placebo [3]. In TITAN, 8.0% of patients who received apalutamide + ADT discontinued treatment due to AEs, compared with 5.3% in the placebo group [7]. In both trials, AEs documented among >10% of patients receiving enzalutamide or apalutamide were hot flush, fatigue, and arthralgia [3,7]. No additional AEs occurring in >10% of patients in ARCHES were documented; however, >10% of patients in TITAN reported hypertension, back pain, arm/leg pain, pruritus, and weight gain [7]. Rates of all-grade drug-induced rash, a treatment-emergent AE of special interest, were much lower among patients who received enzalutamide (2.6%) [3] than among patients who received apalutamide (27.1%) [7].

To our knowledge, this is the first MAIC analysis to compare enzalutamide + ADT versus apalutamide + ADT in the mHSPC setting, and it follows a similar MAIC analysis which showed a significantly prolonged rPFS and time to castration resistance with enzalutamide + ADT versus darolutamide + ADT [11]. Our findings differ from the results of a propensity-score-weighted retrospective causal analysis by Bilen et al. which suggested a significant improvement in 2-year OS among patients with mHSPC treated with apalutamide compared with patients treated with enzalutamide [47]; however, the survival data may have been immature due to the short length of follow-up, with authors recommending additional analyses with longer follow-up periods. Moreover, Bilen et al. utilized real-world evidence, where many baseline values were unavailable, including disease volume, pattern of metastatic spread, and PSA, which may have resulted in misclassification of disease stage and residual confounding [48]. By contrast, our MAIC analysis utilized RCT data with longer median follow-up for OS (ARCHES: 44.6 months; TITAN 44.0 months) [4,8], improving the comparability and assessment of treatment groups.

In the nmCRPC setting, Chowdhury et al. used MAIC methodology to compare the efficacy of enzalutamide versus apalutamide using interim data from the PROSPER and SPARTAN trials [13]. They showed that patients had a lower probability of improved metastasis-free survival (MFS) and OS with enzalutamide + ADT compared with apalutamide + ADT [13]. However, the Chowdhury et al. analysis was limited by immature OS data, differences in how clinical endpoints were defined between trials, and the unavailability of potential EMs that were not reported in the PROSPER trial. A subsequent MAIC by Tombal et al. sought to address these limitations by conducting analyses that adjusted for EMs and utilized more mature OS data from the trials’ final analyses [14]. Similar to our MAIC analysis, Tombal et al. showed comparable MFS, OS, and time to chemotherapy (TTC) between enzalutamide + ADT and apalutamide + ADT, with a potentially greater probability for longer MFS, OS, and TTC with enzalutamide + ADT [14].

At the first data cutoff used for the rPFS and TTPP analyses in this MAIC, ARCHES had a follow-up period approximately 9 months shorter than the follow-up period observed in TITAN. It is difficult to anticipate the implied direction of bias that this may introduce, as it may vary across outcomes, especially when considering the immaturity of time-to-event outcomes. However, the ARCHES rPFS data were consistent in direction and magnitude with the results of the ENZAMET trial (ARCHES HR: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.30–0.50; ENZAMET HR: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.33–0.49) [3,49], which had a much longer follow-up period than TITAN. This consistency should be interpreted cautiously, though, as ENZAMET assessed a related but not identical endpoint and was conducted in a different trial context. The rPFS result should therefore be regarded as nominal and method dependent.

Limitations

MAIC analyses are generally limited by cross-trial heterogeneity and unmeasured confounding, as this methodology relies on the use of observed covariates; indeed, some sources of bias in our analysis could not be fully addressed, as residual confounding may have arisen through unidentified and unmeasured EMs, especially with use of aggregate data. Other sources of confounding included discrepancies in investigational site locations, outcome assessments, and length of follow-up. Any imbalances in covariates not adjusted for in the analysis were assumed not to impose considerable biases in the estimated relative treatment effects.

As noted above, use of the ARCHES final data cutoff for rPFS and TTPP analyses was not considered appropriate due to treatment crossover and differences in rPFS assessment. Methods to equalize follow-up between trials (i.e., by extrapolating data beyond the ARCHES data cutoff or truncating data before the TITAN data cutoff) would rely on pseudo-IPD and modeling assumptions that would introduce additional bias and uncertainty. Accordingly, differences in follow-up remain a limitation of this analysis, although the consistency of findings between ARCHES and ENZAMET suggests limited impact on the estimated relative treatment effect.

Radiographic PFS events occurred in 25% of patients in ARCHES and 35% of patients in TITAN. As previously noted, the method of evaluating rPFS at the first data cutoff differed between the two trials. Because local investigators may overestimate the benefits of a novel drug, BICR confirmation is essential to reinforce locally generated findings and support further clinical development [50]. Owing to the potential bias associated with locally assessed rPFS, amplification of this bias may have occurred in the sensitivity analysis that used locally assessed rPFS from both trials.

Confounding due to treatment crossover from placebo to intervention arms was an issue in the final data cutoff for both trials. Specifically, 31.3% and 39.5% of patients crossed over to enzalutamide and apalutamide in ARCHES and TITAN, respectively. However, OS data from the first data cutoff of ARCHES were immature; very few patients had experienced the outcome by this cutoff point, so initial data were subject to a high degree of uncertainty. Thus, while some bias was introduced by treatment crossover in the final analysis dataset, use of the first data cutoff for the OS outcome was not considered feasible. Combining the RPSFTM-adjusted OS results from ARCHES and the IPCW-adjusted OS results from TITAN to perform the crossover-adjusted OS MAIC sensitivity analysis increased the uncertainty propagation due to variability introduced by the different assumptions and modeling frameworks of each method; however, these methods do not eliminate all concerns around comparability. Thus, these results should be interpreted with caution and be considered exploratory.

Formal Grambsch–Therneau testing indicated that the PH assumption was plausible; however, HRs may have been sensitive to selection bias and to censoring patterns, and should therefore be viewed as descriptive rather than definitive, consistent with a non-PH assumption. In cases where the PH assumption does not hold, the HR reflects a time-averaged relative effect over the observed follow-up period rather than a constant treatment effect over time.

Lastly, an important limitation is that this analysis focuses on efficacy and does not consider comparative tolerability, dose modification, discontinuation, cognitive effects, fatigue, rash, falls, seizures, or patient-reported outcomes, which may affect efficacy outcomes or providers’ treatment decisions. Future analyses should seek to assess differences in safety and related impacts on physicians’ decision-making process.

Conclusion

Our analysis suggested broadly similar estimated efficacy between enzalutamide + ADT and apalutamide + ADT in patients with mHSPC with ≥1 confirmed bone metastasis. In this MAIC analysis, patients treated with enzalutamide + ADT showed improved rPFS compared with those treated with apalutamide + ADT, but these differences were not statistically significant in subsequent sensitivity analyses that harmonized the rPFS assessment method. Findings from this analysis should be interpreted in the context of inherent MAIC limitations; while MAIC methodology can address observed cross-trial differences, some unobserved and unadjusted differences may remain. Overall, these findings suggest that both regimens represent viable options for the treatment of patients with mHSPC.
