Cardioprotective role of antihypertensive treatment in chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity: umbrella review of meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Aghil Rostami, Haleh Ashraf, Ali Hosseininasab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity is a major contributor to long-term cardiovascular morbidity among cancer survivors. ACE inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers and beta blockers have been proposed as prophylactic therapies; however, the robustness of existing evidence is unclear. We aimed to evaluate the strength, consistency and certainty of evidence from meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) assessing antihypertensive agents for preventing chemotherapy-related cardiac dysfunction.MethodsWe conducted an umbrella review of meta-analyses of RCTs following Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews guidelines. Systematic searches were performed in PubMed, Embase and Cochrane Database through May 2025. Data were extracted and reanalysed using random-effects models (DerSimonian-Laird if ≥10 studies; Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman if <10). Certainty of evidence was graded using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. Primary outcomes included measures of cardiac function and structure; secondary outcomes were clinical and adverse events.Results35 meta-analyses comprising 108 associations were included. Preservation of left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction was observed with ACEIs (mean difference, MD, 4.22%, 95% CI 1.56% to 6.88%; GRADE: very low) and beta blockers (MD 2.77%, 95% CI 1.40% to 4.14%; low). Beta blockers reduced ratio of the early (E-wave) to late (A-wave) velocity decline (MD 0.07, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.13; moderate). No significant effect was noted on LV dimensions or biomarkers. Beta blockers reduced incidence of heart failure (relative risk, RR, 0.29, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.34) and cardiotoxicity (RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.75), while ACEIs reduced arrhythmias (RR 0.17, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.91); certainty was low. No significant impact was detected on all-cause mortality or hypotension.ConclusionsACEIs and beta blockers appear to confer modest cardioprotective effects during chemotherapy, particularly regarding systolic function and heart failure incidence, although with predominantly low-certainty evidence. Larger, robust RCTs with standardised endpoints are required before routine prophylactic use is recommended.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251046471.

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity is a well-recognised complication of cancer treatment and a growing contributor to cardiovascular morbidity among cancer survivors. Anthracyclines, HER2-directed therapies and several targeted agents can impair cardiac function, leading to left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, heart failure, arrhythmias and treatment discontinuation.1 2 As survival from cancer improves, the prevention of cardiotoxicity has become an increasingly important clinical priority within cardio-oncology.3

Antihypertensive agents, particularly ACE inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) and beta blockers, have been proposed as prophylactic therapies because of their established benefits in supporting ventricular remodelling and haemodynamic stability.4 Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have evaluated these agents across a range of cancer populations and treatment regimens; however, the evidence remains inconsistent.510 Existing meta-analyses vary in quality, analytical methods and outcome selection, making it difficult to determine the robustness and clinical relevance of the reported effects.

To provide a comprehensive and clinically meaningful overview for cardiovascular practice, we conducted an umbrella review of meta-analyses of RCTs evaluating the cardioprotective role of antihypertensive treatments during chemotherapy. Our aim was to clarify the strength, consistency and certainty of the available evidence and to inform decision-making in the prevention of chemotherapy-related cardiac dysfunction.

Methods

We conducted and reported a tertiary study, referred to as an umbrella review, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR) guidelines.11 12 The protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration No CRD420251046471).

Literature search

Systematic searches were conducted in PubMed, Embase and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews from their inception to May 2025 without language restrictions. We included systematic reviews and meta-analyses of epidemiological studies examining the potential role of antihypertensive treatments in mitigating chemotherapy-related cardiac risks. The search strategy used MeSH terms and keywords related to the topic and study design (online supplemental material 1).

Eligibility criteria

We included systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs that reported the cardioprotective effect of antihypertensive treatment in chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity.

Systematic reviews without meta-analyses or those lacking study-specific data, such as relative risk (RR), OR, mean difference (MD) or 95% CIs, were also excluded. For studies reporting multiple outcomes, data were extracted and assessed for each outcome separately.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoints were any outcome that somehow reflected heart health or function and was reported in at least two of the original studies included in the analysis. The secondary outcomes were clinical and adverse events.

Data extraction

Data extracted from meta-analyses and the original studies included within them encompassed: first author, publication year, number of included studies, interventions (drug or drug classes), comparison, evaluated outcomes, cancer type(s), follow-up duration, participant and event numbers, estimated summary effect with 95% CI, effect metric (RR, OR, MD), effect model (random or fixed), heterogeneity metrics (I² statistic, Cochran’s Q test p value), publication bias assessments (p value of Egger’s or Begg’s test) and risk of bias (RoB) assessment scores.

Quality assessment of meta-analyses

The quality of eligible studies for inclusion in this umbrella review was assessed using the AMSTAR 2 tool that evaluates studies across 16 domains and assigns ratings of high, moderate, low or very low quality.13

Two researchers (AR and AH) independently assessed the eligibility of retrieved articles, performed data extraction, and evaluated the methodological quality of included meta-analyses. Disagreements were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus between the two authors.

Data analysis

Summary effect estimate

Summary effect estimates for each outcome were recalculated using the random-effects model. That is, the effect estimates from individual studies within each meta-analysis were extracted, and the pooled effect sizes and 95% CIs were reanalysed using the random effects model. If the number of included studies in the pooled analysis was 10 or more, we reanalysed each eligible association using the random-effects model with the DerSimonian and Laird method.14 Pooled analyses with fewer than 10 studies were reanalysed using the method of Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman.15 All calculations were conducted in R software, V.4.3.3.

Certainty of each pooled analysis of RCTs

The certainty of the evidence of each unique pooled analysis was classified as high, medium, low or very low based on the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations) system.12 16 17 As recommended by GRADE, the level of evidence was determined based on the imprecision, inconsistency of results across studies, risk of bias, indirectness of evidence and publication bias (online supplemental table 1).

Results

Characteristics of meta-analyses

Overall, 35 eligible papers were identified that included a total of 108 meta-analytical associations. The quality of the included meta-analyses was rated as high for seven meta-analyses, moderate for one, low for 10 and critically low for 16 (online supplemental tables 3 and 4). No quality assessment was performed for the van Dalen et al study due to the inclusion of only one study.18 On average, 13 original research articles were included per meta-analysis (range: 1 to 39). The median number of participants was 1508 (range: 50 to 5931). Overall, 108 unique meta-analytical associations were identified, of which 56 were statistically significant at a significance threshold of p<0.05. Of these 56 statistically significant associations between antihypertensive treatments and identified outcomes, 45 were from low or critically low-quality studies. Of the 108 reported meta-analytical associations, the most studied intervention was beta blockers (78 associations), while ARBs were the least studied intervention (36 associations). LV ejection fraction (LVEF) was the most frequently studied outcome, with 42 meta-analytical associations. Online supplemental table 3 provides the summary effects of the meta-analyses and additional details for RCTs.

Certainty of evidence on the cardioprotective role of antihypertensive treatments

LV systolic functions

Pooled analysis showed preservation of LVEF with ACEIs (MD 4.22, 95% CI 1.56 to 6.88) and beta blockers (MD 2.77, 95% CI 1.40 to 4.14) compared with control, with very low and low certainty, respectively. We also found preservation of peak systolic strain (SS) with beta blockers (MD 3.24, 95% CI 1.28 to 5.21) as compared with placebo (GRADE: low). No significant relationships were observed between antihypertensive treatment and other parameters that reflect LV systolic function (figure 2, online supplemental table 5).

LV diastolic functions

Beta blockers showed a benefit in preventing ratio of the early (E-wave) to late (A-wave) (E/A) velocity reduction in comparison to the control group (MD 0.07, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.13), with moderate certainty. However, the benefits of beta blockers and ACEIs on the preservation of other parameters following chemotherapy were not different between the two groups (figure 2, online supplemental table 5).

Cardiac anatomy

Pooled analyses of data from 19 original studies did not provide evidence of a difference in LV end systolic diameter (LVESD), LV end diastolic diameter (LVEDD) and left atrial (LA) diameter with the use of antihypertensive drugs after chemotherapy (figure 3, online supplemental table 6).

Cardiac biomarker

No evidence of a significant difference between the use of antihypertensive drugs and cardiac biomarkers like global longitudinal strain (GLS), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and cTnI between the beta blocker and placebo groups (figure 3, online supplemental table 6).

Clinical and adverse events

The effect estimates from the pooled studies did not show a significant association between antihypertensive drug use and all-cause mortality in patients treated with chemotherapy. Summary estimates of data from nine original studies showed evidence of a decreased incidence of heart failure with the use of beta blockers (RR 0.29, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.34) and ACEIs (RR 0.34, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.66). The use of beta blockers significantly reduces the risk of cardiotoxicity (RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.75) and low ejection fraction (RR 0.42, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.79), with low certainty. A pooled analysis of data also indicated a significant association between ACEI use and decreased incidence of arrhythmias (RR 0.17, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.91) in patients who undergo chemotherapy. There was no significant association between the use of antihypertensive drugs and hypotension after chemotherapy, based on a pooled analysis of data from five original studies (figure 4, online supplemental table 7).

Discussion

In this umbrella review, we synthesised the totality of evidence on the cardioprotective effects of antihypertensive agents in patients receiving potentially cardiotoxic chemotherapy. Across 108 pooled associations, ACEi, ARBs and beta blockers were frequently studied, but the certainty of the evidence supporting their cardioprotective benefits was generally low to very low. Despite statistically significant improvements in several measures of systolic and diastolic function—most notably LVEF preservation with ACEIs and beta blockers and attenuation of E/A ratio decline with beta blockers—the reliability of these findings is limited by substantial heterogeneity, small sample sizes and predominantly low methodological quality of the contributing meta-analyses.

Principal findings

Our re-analysed pooled estimates showed that ACEi and beta blockers were associated with modest preservation of LVEF, while beta blockers were associated with improvements in SS, findings that are broadly consistent with previous meta-analyses.5 6 19 Although these associations were statistically significant, the observed absolute differences in LVEF were modest and of uncertain clinical relevance, particularly in light of the low certainty of evidence and the absence of consistent effects on clinical endpoints.

A notable exception was the moderate-certainty evidence supporting the protective effect of beta blockers against early diastolic dysfunction (E/A ratio decline), an observation consistent with improvements in myocardial relaxation and diastolic filling properties demonstrated in clinical and mechanistic studies.2022 Beta blocker therapy has been associated with decreased heart failure hospitalisations in patients with diastolic dysfunction, and echocardiographic measures such as the E/A ratio have shown enhancement indicative of improved diastolic function.20 Haemodynamic studies further support that beta blockers improve both early active isovolumic relaxation and late passive myocardial relaxation, leading to reduced left ventricular end-diastolic pressures and stiffness.21 22 These findings underscore the beneficial impact of beta blockers on early diastolic dysfunction through mechanisms of more complete myocardial relaxation.

Despite frequent assumptions that antihypertensive agents reduce structural cardiac damage during chemotherapy,19 23 24 our analysis found no compelling evidence of improvements in LVESD, LVEDD or atrial dimensions. Similarly, cardiac biomarkers—including GLS, BNP and troponin—were not significantly altered by prophylactic therapy, underscoring the mechanistic uncertainty surrounding these agents in preventing myocardial injury. Nevertheless, the absence of statistically significant effects on cardiac structure and biomarkers should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of no benefit, given the limited number of trials, heterogeneous measurement methods and potential for type II error.

The generally consistent reduction in heart failure, cardiotoxicity and low ejection fraction with ACEIs and beta blockers mirrors earlier meta-analyses because these agents exert well-established biological effects—such as attenuation of neurohormonal activation, reduction of oxidative stress and prevention of adverse ventricular remodelling—that directly counteract mechanisms implicated in anthracycline-induced injury.13 2528 Most previous trials enrolled similar populations receiving similar chemotherapy regimens, which likely contributes to the reproducibility of these findings across analyses. However, the low certainty of evidence in both previous studies and our umbrella review likely explains the modest magnitude of these benefits. Many contributing RCTs were small, short and heterogeneous in baseline cardiovascular risk, timing of drug initiation, LVEF thresholds and definitions of cardiotoxicity. In particular, the apparently large RR reductions observed for heart failure outcomes should be interpreted cautiously, as they are likely influenced by sparse events in small trials and variability in outcome definitions rather than reflecting true prevention. These limitations reduce precision and contribute to inconsistent or attenuated effect estimates.

The specific association between ACEIs and reduced arrhythmia risk is biologically plausible, as ACE inhibition may attenuate myocardial fibrosis, modulate autonomic tone and improve electrophysiological stability, mechanisms supported by mechanistic and clinical literature.2931

Likewise, the absence of a mortality benefit is expected because mortality is rare in these relatively short RCTs and sample sizes are insufficient to detect differences; moreover, mortality is infrequent in relatively young oncology cohorts with preserved baseline cardiac function. The consistent lack of excess hypotension mirrors earlier safety analyses showing that low-dose neurohormonal blockade during chemotherapy is well tolerated under structured monitoring.32 Overall, these observations explain both the similarities and modest discrepancies between our umbrella review and previous cardioprotective evidence syntheses.

Strengths and limitations

This umbrella review offers the most comprehensive synthesis to date of RCT evidence on pharmacologic cardioprotection in chemotherapy. Importantly, we synthesised outcomes spanning functional, structural and clinical domains to provide a holistic view of cardioprotection.

However, several limitations merit consideration. First, the majority of included meta-analyses were of low or critically low methodological quality, constraining the confidence in summary estimates. Second, the underlying trials were generally small, often single-centre and varied in chemotherapy regimens, cardiotoxicity definitions, follow-up durations and timing of drug initiation. Third, heterogeneity in echocardiographic techniques and biomarker assays limits comparability across studies. Fourth, because cardiotoxicity profiles differ across chemotherapy classes, the lack of stratified analyses by specific agents or regimens in the underlying meta-analyses represents an important limitation and may constrain the generalisability of our findings. Fifth, the absence of consistent risk-stratified data across the underlying meta-analyses precluded assessment of differential effects according to baseline cardiovascular risk, thereby limiting the applicability of our findings to specific high-risk subgroups. Finally, the available trials often recruited low-risk populations, meaning the cardioprotective effects in patients with pre-existing cardiovascular disease or high-risk cancer therapy exposures remain unclear.

Implications for clinical practice

While the overall certainty of evidence remains limited, the consistent direction of effect across multiple cardiac outcomes supports continued consideration of ACEIs and beta blockers for high-risk patients receiving anthracyclines, HER2-directed therapy or other cardiotoxic agents. These agents are inexpensive, well-tolerated and mechanistically plausible for cardioprotection. Their apparent benefits for heart failure incidence and ejection fraction decline may justify selective prophylactic use, particularly in patients with cardiovascular risk factors, borderline baseline LVEF or prior exposure to cardiotoxic therapies.

Nevertheless, the absence of strong evidence and the modest magnitude of benefit underscore that universal prophylaxis is not currently supported. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and should not be viewed as a basis for guideline-level recommendations. Instead, integration with risk-based cardio-oncology pathways—including serial imaging, biomarker surveillance and early therapeutic intervention—may be more appropriate until larger, methodologically robust RCTs become available.

Implications for future research

Future trials should adopt harmonised definitions of cardiotoxicity, longer follow-up durations and adequately powered sample sizes to detect clinically meaningful outcomes. Comparative effectiveness studies—particularly those evaluating ACEIs versus ARBs and beta blockers with differing adrenergic profiles—are needed to refine drug selection. Additionally, emerging agents such as neprilysin inhibitors and sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors warrant rigorous evaluation given their established cardioprotective benefits in other populations. Finally, individual patient data meta-analyses may help resolve heterogeneity and allow more personalised risk stratification.

Conclusions

Antihypertensive agents, particularly ACEIs and beta blockers, show promising but low-certainty evidence for mitigating chemotherapy-induced cardiac dysfunction. While small improvements in systolic and diastolic function and reductions in heart failure incidence were observed, the overall strength and reliability of the evidence remain limited, particularly given the short duration of follow-up in most available trials, which constrains inference regarding sustained clinical benefit and long-term cardiotoxicity prevention. High-quality, adequately powered randomised trials with longer follow-up are urgently needed to establish the role of prophylactic cardioprotective therapy in modern cardio-oncology.
