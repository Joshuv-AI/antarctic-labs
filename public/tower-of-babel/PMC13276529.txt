Introduction

Revolutionary treatment medicines known as antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) combine the specificity of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with the powerful cytotoxicity of chemotherapeutic agents [1]. The three main parts of an ADC are the monoclonal antibody, the cytotoxic payloads, and the linker that binds them together covalently. By limiting systemic toxicity and increasing therapeutic efficiency, the antibody guarantees that the cytotoxic medication is precisely targeted to tumor cells [2]. The idea of “magic bullets,” first put out by Paul Ehrlich in the early 1900s, imagined chemicals that could selectively target cancer cells while avoiding healthy ones. This notion is the basis for ADC research. First licensed by the FDA in 2000 for the treatment of acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL), Gemtuzumab ozogamicin (Military was the first ADC to enter clinical oncology. There has been a plethora of ADCs authorized since then [3] that target different cancer antigens.

Monoclonal antibodies bind to antigens displayed on the surface of tumor cells, allowing ADCs to deliver lethal drugs to cancer cells with precision (Fig. 1) [4]. The ADC is taken up by the tumor cell via receptor-mediated endocytosis after it binds to the receptor. Differences in pH, enzyme function, or other biological activities rupture the linker within the cell, delivering the hazardous payload [5]. This carrier has the potential to kill cancer cells by interfering with DNA synthesis and cytoskeletal activity, or by inducing cell death. Traditional anesthesia kills healthy cells as well as malignant ones, causing serious side effects [6]. ADCs allow for a more focused approach. Adherent delivery systems (ADCs) reduce the risk of adverse effects and improve safety by directly targeting tumor cells with potent cytotoxic drugs while sparing healthy tissues. Moreover, the index of therapy is improved by the specific targeting of cancer cells, which increases medication effectiveness at lower dosages [7]. With less systemic harm, ADCs have a clear benefit over more conventional treatments. Severe adverse effects, including immunosuppression and organ damage, are often caused by chemotherapeutic drugs, which generally affect rapidly dividing cells. Many obstacles still need to be overcome for ADCs to realize their full potential. Various cancers may express antigens to various degrees, creating a diverse tumor environment (TME). This heterogeneity may make ADCs less effective than they might be, especially in solid cancers, where drug transport could be hindered by low permeability and diverse antigen presentation [9]. Factors such as cancer size and the extracellular matrix may also impede ADC uptake into cancer cells and the subsequent payload release. Additionally, ADC efficacy might be diminished by resistance mechanisms, such as increased expression of efflux pumps and reduced levels of targeted antigens. The effectiveness of ADCs also depends on the development of an ideal linker that can withstand circulation while effectively delivering the payload within a cancer cell [10].

The optimal selection of cancer-specific antigens, optimization of the linker properties, and identification of higher-quality payloads are ongoing areas of interest in the quest to improve ADCs. A lot of work is going into creating “smart linkers” that can detect changes in pH or enzyme levels and discharge their payload accordingly [11]. Furthermore, targeted treatments and immune checkpoint inhibition are two alternatives therapeutic approach that, when combined with ADCs, may enhance treatment results by overcoming resistant mechanisms [12]. Researchers are also looking at ways to improve the penetration of ADCs into solid cancers by using shorter antibody fragments, including single-chain variable fragment variants. Given that full-size antibodies are somewhat large and would have difficulty permeating cancer masses, these smaller components may be able to circumvent these limitations. In addition to destroying tumor cells, ADCs may be more effective if other payloads, such as anti-inflammatory chemicals [13], are developed.

An approach that combines immune response inhibition with the powerful cytotoxicity of an attaching medication is the creation of ADCs targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), which is one of the more attractive techniques. In PD-1-targeted ADCs, a cytotoxic payload is delivered directly to the cancer site via monoclonal antibodies that bind PD-1 [5]. These ADCs can stimulate the immune response to produce an anti-inflammatory response, thereby attacking PD-1 and potentially reversing T cell fatigue. Because it restores the immune system’s capacity to identify and eliminate cancer cells by blocking the inhibitory signal, this method is especially useful for cancers that express high levels of the programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) [14]. An effective treatment approach that may circumvent some of the drawbacks of traditional treatments is the integration of immune regulation with direct tumor cell killing. Another difficulty with conventional chemotherapy is the potential for off-target impacts and tissue damage to healthy tissues; however, ADCs selectively target PD-1 on cancer-infiltrating T cells, which may mitigate these concerns [13]. Because they enhance immune systems and cytotoxicity, PD-1 ADCs have great promise as cancer treatments.

Ultimately, several obstacles must be overcome before antimicrobial-drug conjugates can fully realize their potential as cancer treatments. These include antigen variation, microenvironment differences, and antimicrobial resistance, among others. Increasing payload delivery, investigating combined treatments, and refining ADC design are all areas where researchers are actively working to improve therapeutic results and address current limits.

PD- 1 antibody drug conjugates (ADCs)

Many cancer treatments, particularly ADCs, aim to target programmed death-1 (PD-1), an important lymphocyte checkpoint molecule that is essential for immune suppression. An ADC targeting PD-1 is formed by combining the molecule’s potent cytotoxic effects with immune system blockade. These ADCs usually include a cytotoxic drug specifically administered to a cancer site, along with antibodies that specifically target PD-1 [15]. This two-pronged approach to cancer therapy is both highly effective and safe, as it modulates the immune system while simultaneously directly eliminating tumor cells. Such ADCs can overcome T cell depletion and enhance the immune system’s anti-cancer response by targeting PD-1. As a more effective alternative to standard treatments, blocking PD 1 signaling restores immune detection and cancer elimination, which is especially promising for cancers that express high levels of PD L1 [2]. The specific benefits of PD 1 ADCs in terms of effectiveness, safety, and immune regulation should be further understood by conducting systematic comparisons with alternative treatment methods in the future. These approaches might include PD 1 immunotherapy in conjunction with standard ADC treatment or PD L1 focused ADCs.

Mechanism of action and targeting PD-1

PD 1 receptors are highly expressed in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and PD 1 specific work by targeting this domain with monoclonal antibodies. T cell fatigue and immunological repression are induced when the immune checkpoint receptor PD-1 is engaged by its ligands PD L1 or PD L2. Tumors can elude immune monitoring thanks to this connection, which speeds up the evolution of cancer. ADCs that target PD 1 exploit this process by preventing the interaction between PD 1 and PD L1, and thereby blocking PD 1 signaling in PD-1-specific immune cells, especially T cells. The cytotoxic medication is targeted to cancer cells or immune cells that express PD 1 using polyclonal antibodies. The ADC can enter the desired cell via receptor-mediated endocytosis after binding to PD 1 on the cell. Cytotoxic payload and immune modulation

A potent chemotherapeutic agent commonly binds with the PD-1 monoclonal antigens agent that targets cancer cells for killing. Some examples of such payloads involve maytansine and dolastatin, which break microtubules, and calicheamicin, which damages DNA [16]. The cytotoxic medicine is released when the ADC is treated in the endosomes or lysosomes after it has been internalized by a specific cell. This processing involves cleaving the linker between the antibodies and the payload. This mechanism causes cells to die, usually by apoptosis, by interfering with essential cellular activities, including mitosis and DNA recombination [17].

Additionally, these cytotoxic drugs can kill nearby cancer cells. The ADC didn’t specifically target that. This phenomenon is known as bystander impact.

Designing PD-1 ADCs that can target cancer cells and PD-1-deficient T cells is no easy feat. Although the main objective of the ADC is to target cancer cells that express PD-1, this process may unintentionally destroy immunological cells that express PD-1, especially activated T cells, which are vital for anti-tumor protection. This paradox highlights the need to find ways to preferentially preserve functional T cells without compromising their cytotoxic efficacy and prompts worries over possible harm to T-cells. The creation of edible contextual links that exclusively deliver loads inside the tumor surroundings (for instance, in response to tumor-specific peptidases or acidic pH) has been a current strategy to address this problem [18]. Another strategy involves using payloads with different sensitivities to cancer immunological cells (such as those involved in tumor-specific PD-R activity) [19]. Finally, PD-1 ADCs can be combined with anti-inflammatory agents to prevent T-cell depletion.

In addition to having fundamental cytotoxic actions, PD-1 ADCs may also modulate immune function. To avoid detection by your immune system, tumors may activate PD-1 on functional T cells. This molecule then attaches to PD L 1 on tumor cells, reducing the effectiveness of T cells [20]. PD 1 ADCs inhibit T cell replication. Reactivating the immune system is necessary for a successful battle against malignancies because it enhances immune responses, cytokine production, and T cell division. Furthermore, PD1 ADCs have the potential to alter the immunological dynamics of the cancer microenvironment (TME) by reducing the abundance of immunosuppressive cells, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and CD4+ T cells. This renders an optimal environment for the proliferation of antigens that target cancers [21].

Enhancing tumor specific T cell activation

The capacity to revitalize cancer specific T lymphocytes is a major benefit of PD-1 ADCs. Overexpression of immunological checkpoints, such as PD-1 and PD L1, induces immunological tolerance and cancer evasion by suppressing T cell stimulation within the tumor microenvironment (TME). This is countered by PD 1 ADCs, which activate anti-cancer immune responses by inhibiting the PD-1/PD L1 interaction and thereby avoiding T cell fatigue. This procedure promotes the maturation of storage T cells, which may confer long-lasting immunity, and also enhances T cells’ capacity to identify and eliminate cancer cells.

Also, there’s a synergistic impact when PD 1 ADCs are used with different immune checkpoint blockers like tumor necrosis factor alpha or cytotoxic T lymphocyte linked protein four. The immune-suppressive mechanisms used by tumors may be circumvented by these combined treatments, according to preclinical research [4]. This leads to better medical outcomes.

PD-1 ADCs in specific cancer types

It is the unique properties of each cancer type that determine how well PD 1 ADCs work as a therapeutic tool. The capacity of the ADC to direct the delivery of the harmful cargo is influenced by the degree of PD 1 transcription on cancer infiltrating T cells and immunological cells in the tumor microenvironment which may vary across distinct forms of cancer. The use of PD-1 ADCs shows promise as a therapy for malignancies that exhibit high levels of as such tumors are more likely to respond to PD 1 based therapies. On the other hand, supplementary approaches may be necessary to improve targeted relevance and therapeutic effectiveness in cancers that have low or inconsistent PD 1 transcription [22].

Particularly promising with PD-1 ADC therapy include malignancies that often exhibit strong PD-L1 expression, such as melanoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and non-small cell lung tumors (NSCLC). On the other the other hand, researchers are still trying to figure out whether PD-1 ADCs work in cancers with less consistent PD-1/PD-L1 expression, such as colorectal or pancreatic tumors. Improving patient selection and treatment outcomes will depend on identifying indicators of which tumor types respond to PD-1 ADCs [23].

Additionally, the tumor microenvironment (TME) is critical to the efficacy of PD-1 ADC treatment. When immune-modulation treatments like PD-1 ADCs aren’t successful, it may be because the tumor microenvironment (TME) is highly immunosuppressive in some cancer types and contains high levels of regulatory T cells (Trigs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). T cell resurrection and anti-tumor immunity may be hindered when immune repressive cells are present. To circumvent these obstacles, PD-1 ADC therapy may be tailored by understanding the makeup and dynamics of the tumor microenvironment in different malignancies. In immunosuppressive settings, PD 1 ADCs may be even more effective when combined with medicines that alter the immune milieu, such as drugs that reduce the number of regulatory T cells (Trigs) or checkpoint inhibitors targeting distinct mechanisms.

The responsiveness of Ongoing or progressed cancer patients treated with PD 1 ADCs cancer remains under investigation, along with tumor type and tumor microenvironment (TME) factors. Targeting PD 1 alone may not be sufficient to elicit long term responses in these malignancies, as their immune landscapes are generally complex and diverse [24]. To address the heterogeneity of advanced malignancies and improve overall treatment effectiveness, researchers are exploring combination treatments combining PD-1 ADCs with various biologic therapies or targeted therapies.

Many concerns remain regarding the medical benefits of PD-1 ADCs. For example, there is still no consensus on the best way to select clients based on factors such as express PD 1 on tumour cells or Trigs. Furthermore, further research is needed to determine the effects on tumour cells and the immunological microenvironment resulting from the selected cytotoxic payload. Clinical evidence of combos including PD 1 ADCs and current checkpoint blockers is currently insufficient, despite preclinical data indicating that the two may synergize. Improving biomarker techniques to identify patients who will benefit the most and optimizing ADC design to balance effectiveness and toxicity are two important issues the field must address moving forward. More and more evidence suggests that especially in tumours with immunosuppressive microenvironments, may prove to be an effective weapon in the fight against cancer [28]. Their final place in medical practice, however, will be determined by strong evidence from current and future research. Understanding the mechanism of action, security, and effectiveness of this new class of medicines is the primary goal at the moment.

The mechanisms behind the action and resistance of ADCS along with approaches to overcome resistance

Cellular uptake trafficking and payload release of PD-1 ADCs

Although there have been some obstacles, PD 1 ADCs have shown significant promise in tumour immunotherapy [34]. One of the most notable targets in immune-oncology therapy is PD-1, a checkpoint blocker that tumours use to evade the immune system. Several obstacles must be overcome before PD-1 ADCs can realize their full therapeutic promise, even though they can boost the immune system’s capacity to combat cancer. Among these obstacles are concerns about ADC toxicity and security, the rise of bacterial resistance, and the difficulty of controlling ADC pharmacokinetics and instability. By employing a monoclonal antibody (mAb) as a specific targeting vehicle, ADCs are engineered to deliver potent cytotoxic agents to targeted cells with precision. When delivered by ingestion, ADCs bypass the stomach and proteolysis acids that might break down the mAb part. The majority of antigen production must occur on tumor cells for ADCs to be effective with limited accumulation in healthy tissues. This will enable the mAb to attach selectively to malignant cells. Intracellular transport of the ADC-antigen complex occurs after antigen attachment by endocytosis mediated by the creative molecule receptor [35]. Among the three primary endocytosis mechanisms, the most common for ADC internalization is endocytosis, followed by caveolae-triggered endocytosis and finally pinocytosis. Although the antigen is necessary for clathrin and caveolae-dependent internalization, the antigen is not necessary for pinocytosis. The kind of antigen and the characteristics of the cytotoxic payload both impact the effectiveness of internalization. Inadvertent discharge of ADCs beyond the target cells, side-effect impacts, and system toxicity may occur when the binding affinity is low (KD > 10 NM) [3].

The ADC compound is transported to the earliest endosomes after internalization, where the mAb element of the group interacts with human neonatal Fc receptors (Fcrn) in acidic conditions. Recycled to the extracellular space, a portion of the ADC binds to fcrn. The ADC dissociates from Fern under neutral pH conditions [36]. Damage to cells may occur due to misdirected delivery, but this recycling process protects against it. Clinical indices may be improved by ADCs that lack an Fc tail, thereby avoiding the reusing process. Any ADCs that make it past the initial endosomes and into the last endosomes without absorbing molecules are discarded. After a final ending unites with lysosomes, the surroundings become even more acidic. The lysosome’s low pH, in conjunction Using proteases, such as plasmin and cathepsin B, enables the splitting of the antibody cytotoxic payload linker. Apoptosis and cell death are brought about by the free cytotoxic chemicals that are released into the cytoplasm as a consequence of this cleavage. How cells die depends on the type of cytotoxic warhead. Some drugs trigger cell killing by incorporating DNA, such as calicheamicins and duocarmycins, while others, including aerostations and maytansinoids, cause apoptosis by interacting with microtubule stability [37]. Several parameters, ranging from targeted antigens’ specificity to antibody selection, linker robustness, and payload capacity, affect the efficacy of ADCs in inducing cytotoxicity.

Critical development challenges for PD-1 ADCs

Problems specific to the creation of PD 1 ADCs are inherent. Although these difficulties are common to the growth of all ADCs, PD 1 ADCs have it rough due to several immune-mediated and antigen-related concerns that make it hard to get the drug to work and keep it secure for patients. Immune diversity, pharmacokinetics, linkage robustness, cancer-resistant pathways, toxicity, and immune-mediated side effects (irAEs) are among such.

Safety toxicity and immune-related adverse events (irAEs)

The growth of PD-1 ADCs is plagued by toxicity concerns, in particular given the irAEs linked to PD-1 targeting. PD-1 is an essential regulator of the immune response and is especially important for preventing autoimmunity by limiting over reactive responses. Immunity abnormalities, resulting from harmful immune reactions that affect solid tissues, may be modulated with PD-1 ADCs [38]. Colitis, viral infection, pneumonia, and skin eruptions are examples of immune-associated adverse effects that are specific to PD-1-focused treatments. These adverse effects are comparable to those of other treatments that target immunological checkpoints; they can be more severe with PD-1 ADCs because they directly influence these checkpoints. Therapy administration becomes more complicated when irAEs develop, necessitating careful tracking and, perhaps, dose modifications to reduce risks [39].

Mechanisms of drug resistance to PD-1 ADC therapy

Another significant issue is resistance to PD-1 ADCs, as may eventually create ways to avoid treatment. At several phases of the method of action, such as antigen identification, normalization, charge announcement, and subsequent apoptosis signaling, tolerance may manifest. Immunological evasion methods, in which cancers modify the activity of PD 1 or other immunological checkpoints to evade the effects of PD 1 ADCs, are often associated with tolerance to PD 1 ADCs [40]. For instance, cancers might persistently evade immune surveillance despite PD 1 suppression by down regulating PD 1 transcription or up regulating other immunological regulatory proteins as LAG-3, CTLA-4, or PD L1. Additionally, higher-exit pumps, such as those linked to multidrug resistance (MDR), may be produced by cancerous cells, which could eliminate the toxic payloads in tumor cells. after it has a chance to act [41]. Innovative approaches, such as combination therapy Using additional immune checkpoint blockers or bivalent linked antigens, are needed to address these immune evasion mechanisms, which pose a particular challenge for PD-1 ADCs.

Changes in the presentation of antigens inside cancers are another route of tolerance. One particular challenge is the variation in PD-1 expression among cancers and even within different parts of a single tumour. Variations in cancer antigen transcription may result in subpopulations of cancer cells with reduced PD-1 transcription, making them less vulnerable to the ADC [42]. The ADC’s overall efficacy may be reduced by inadequate targeting due to this variance in antigenic production. Antigen heterogeneity and variability in tumor responsiveness

One specific difficulty with PD-1 ADCs is antigen diversity. The production of PD-1 varies among cancer cells, which distinguishes it from other ADCs that target cancer-specific antigens. The KRISTINE study found that HER2 diversity in cancers significantly affected treatment outcomes; with HER2 deficient cancer patches associated with poor treatment outcomes [43]. The report evaluated T-DM1 coupled with pertuzumab. Variation in PD-1 production across tumor sites may also cause PD-1 ADCs to miss some cancer areas. Some cancers produce shortened versions of PD-1 or use extracellular molecules such as MUC4 to hide the PD-1 antigen, making it impossible for the ADC to bind its target. The therapeutic efficacy is diminished, and the development of medicines that may circumvent these variances is made more difficult by the antigenic heterogeneity.

Heregulin is only one of many tumor microenvironment proteins that may alter HER2 signaling or PD-1 interactions to cause resistance and reduce ADC effectiveness [44]. The effectiveness of PD-1 ADC treatments depends on our ability to comprehend and overcome these limitations associated with antigen diversity.

Pharmacokinetics, linker stability, and controlled payload release

The efficacy of PD-1 ADCs is highly dependent on their pharmacokinetics and stabilization. The effectiveness of ADCs depends on their ability to evade immune responses and hepatic clearance, and to remain in circulation for sufficient time to reach the tumor [45]. A specific problem with PD-1 ADCs is their linker flexibility, which must be both stable enough to prevent the payload from releasing too early when the ADC is in circulation and cleaved enough to expel the harmful agents at the target site in a cancerous environment. Reducing non-target damage and maximizing payload delivery to cancer cells for successful treatment requires an optimal compromise between retention and discharge [46].

In addition, optimizing the biological delivery of PD-1 ADCs is crucial to ensuring that sufficient treatment antibodies enter the cancer. The pharmacological factors that govern the medicine’s distribution and efficiency within the tumor itself may be influenced by factors such as the molecule’s size, the chosen payload, and the bonding process. Given that PD-1 regulates the immune system, PD-1 ADCs need to strike a careful balance among antibody stabilization, charge expulsion, and accessibility.

Immune modulation and long term immune deregulations risks

The long-term risk of immune system imbalance is a major concern specific to PD-1 ADCs. To regulate immune responses and prevent autoimmune responses, PD-1 is crucial for immunological tolerance. It is believed that PD-1 ADCs, which target PD-1, may alter immunological system function in the long term by interfering with this regulatory capacity. These alterations have the potential to cause persistent immunological stimulation, which, in turn, may heighten the likelihood of autoimmune disorders and make irAEs more severe. Continued assessment of immune responses may be necessary to avoid major consequences, which makes long term treatment of PD 1 ADC therapy more problematic [47]. Furthermore, future immune treatments may be affected by immune system interference, potentially complicating therapy protocols and ultimately making them less effective.

Tumor microenvironment effects on PD-1 ADC activity

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is an important factor in the efficacy or failure of PD-1 ADC therapy. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is rife with immune-suppressing cells and signaling chemicals that may reduce ADC efficacy. By increasing various immune barrier enzymes or activating distinct immunological systems, cancer cells can leverage the tumor microenvironment to enhance immune escape. Another way the tumor microenvironment (TME) might impair the ADC’s therapeutic activity is by changing the immune cell makeup of the cancer. This, in turn, could impact the ADC’s adherence and internalization. One way the tumor microenvironment (TME) can hinder an ADC’s efficacy in preventing tumor development is by facilitating the overexpression of immune checkpoints [48].

Furthermore, alterations in the Trans membrane environment (TME) may influence ADC absorption and endocytosis. Cancer cells in an immunosuppressed setting may lack some of the proteins needed to properly bind the ADC and digest its payload, thereby limiting therapeutic benefits [49]. To improve ADC uptake and ensure their proper performance within the intricate TME, we need creative solutions to these challenges.

Emerging approaches to counter PD-1 ADC resistance

Although significant advances have been made in antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), acquired and intrinsic resistant is still a significant challenge to clinical sustainability. There are several, partly overlapping, ways in which resistance may develop, and the field is thus converging on two concomitant solutions: one is to re-engineer next-generation ADCs, and the other is to combine ADCs with rational combination partners to recover or enhance activity.

A common pathway implicated is the drug export systems, with cancer cells increasing efflux transporters that minimize intracellular exposure to the released payload. Here, Takeaway et al. showed that the HER2-positive gastric cancer models that are resistant to T-DM1 still reacted to T-DXd, which was in line with the capability of a topoisomerase-I payload and transporter setting (including aberrant ABC-transporter patterns) to counteract efflux-induced loss of activity [9]. The Phase III DESTINY-Breast02 study provided clinical evidence of a randomized controlled trial in HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer, demonstrating that T-DXd can be effective following T-DM1, supporting the notion that ADCs can overcome resistance to another ADC.

Alongside transporter biology, physicochemical tuning of ADCs can affect intracellular retention and payload delivery. To minimize loss of functionality under conditions of efflux, linker and payload properties (especially those influencing cellular entry and trafficking) have been studied. Indicatively, hydrophilic linker designs, e.g., sulfo-SPDB-DM4 and PEG4Mal conjugates, are active in floated receptor expressing environments, which serve as an example of how linker engineering can be exploited to enhance performance in resistant environments [50]. Another difficulty is the heterogeneity of tumors, particularly when target expression is low, when spatial heterogeneity is present, or when target expression is altered during treatment. Under those conditions, the efficacy of ADC might depend on the ability of the payload to spread beyond antigen-positive cells. The mesothelin-targeting ADC anetumab ravtansine has been shown to have significant antitumor activity in heterogeneous models and is also consistent with a bystander effect, which can occur in adjacent target-negative cells. Suggestions for follow-up work included increasing the rate of effective payload release to enhance overall potency and bystander action. But the size of this effect is determined by the chemistry of payloads, linker behavior, and the architecture of the local tissue [51]. To address the problem of insufficient target density or changes in trafficking, specific and biparatopic antibody formats have been developed to enhance binding avidity, internalization, and lysosome localization. A single specific HER2 targeted ADC showed activity in T DM1-resistant and low-HER2 models, which correlated with increased receptor binding and decreased recycling, favoring a lysosome process. Building on this idea, specific targeting of HER2 and PRLR enhanced binding and uptake in preclinical HER2-positive breast and ovarian cancer models, suggesting a way to improve the performance of HER2 ADC in the presence of both receptors [52]. This design strategy has been demonstrated to be successful, as zanidatamab zovodotin (ZW49), which targets two non-overlapping HER2 epitopes, was active in a Phase I setting (ORR 31%; disease control 70%), with further study of the strategy warranted [53].

Lastly, several more recent HER2-ADCs have demonstrated significant response rates in preclinical trials. SHR A1811 is a cleavable linker, a novel topoisomerase-I payload (SHR9265), and a trastuzumab-based antibody scaffold; Phase I results have shown an ORR of 61.6% in tumors that express HER2, including responses in previously resistant patients to T-DM1 [54]. A166 also showed activity in HER2 positive metastatic breast cancer with a 70% ORR and dose-dependent of between 9.4 to 12.3 months [55]. Combinations with ADC redesign, such as immune checkpoint blockade and targeted agents, are under testing to transform the tumor microenvironment and to inhibit escape mechanisms. As atezolizumab together with T DM1 failed to enhance PFS overall, subgroup analyses in PD L1 positive disease encouraged further research in KATE3 [56]. Other options include using ADCs with TKIs and disrupting NRG1/HER3 signaling to overcome resistance to HER2 directed therapy; inhibition of this axis is synergistic with anti HER2 therapy and can resensitize highly-expressing NRG1/HER3 models [57].

Conclusions and future prospects

PD-1 ADCs are a novel immune oncology approach that combines the checkpoint blockade with cell-selective delivery of cytotoxic agents. These constructs will overcome two longstanding obstacles to cancer therapy: immune escape and therapy resistance in heterogeneous tumor microenvironments by combining immune reactivation with delivery of payloads to the target. Preclinical experiments and initial clinical observations indicate that PD-1 ADCs can exert anti-tumor effects in two ways: direct effects on tumor compartments via PD-1/PD-L1-PD-1 interactions, and depletion or regulation of tumor compartments that express PD-1, such as exhausted T cells and regulatory T cells. This synergistic mechanism of action could be especially pertinent in low-PD-L1 tumors or in tumors that are less sensitive to conventional PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, where conventional checkpoint blockade is typically ineffective. Although this promise exists, a few challenges must be overcome before PD-1 ADCs can have a far-reaching, long-term clinical impact. One of the key issues is the variability of antigens, such as interchanger differences in PD-1 expression and variability among individual immune niches within the tumor microenvironment. This heterogeneity makes it difficult to achieve uniform target engagement and consistent payload delivery. Simultaneously, resistance may occur via various biological pathways, such as antigen down regulation, changed endocytic pathways and intracellular processing, enhanced drug-efflux transporter activity, and rewiring of apoptosis or stress-response pathways. As these resistance pathways are complex and overlapping, future advancements will require patient selection based on biomarkers and longitudinal measurements of antigen and immune conditions during treatment. Design-wise, pharmacokinetics, linker, and payload-release kinetics optimization are vital. Perfect PD-1 ADCs should be stable in systemic circulation yet facilitate efficient release into the interior of the desired cellular compartment, maximizing on-target exposure and minimising off-target toxicity. There are improvements in site-specific conjugation and enhanced control of the drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR), which can lead to increased product homogeneity and a higher therapeutic index. Nevertheless, there are specific considerations for PD-1 ADCs versus tumor-antigen ADCs, as they target immune cells, which can affect bio distribution, tolerability, and downstream immune dynamics.

In the future, integrated development, a combination of next-generation ADC engineering and rational combination regimens, is likely to yield the most significant clinical benefits. Combining PD-1 ADCs with immunomodulators that restructure immunosuppressive microenvironment, targeted kinase inhibitors, or complementary checkpoint modulators could be useful in counteracting adaptive escape and enhancing complementary anti-tumor immunity. Moreover, the multispecific formats can be applied to increase target engagement, internalization efficiency, and therapeutic coverage of heterogeneous tumor-immune interfaces, as well as to enhance bystander cytotoxicity when required, using specific or biparatopic antibodies.

More development in payload classes provides a definite route to expanding therapeutic breadth as well. The ability to extend classical microtubule-targeting agents to incorporate DNA-damaging, immune-modulating, or microenvironment-reprogramming payloads might broaden their efficacy against resistant phenotypes. Likewise, the creation of linkers which react to localized tumor signals - e.g., enzyme activity or pH - can enhance selectivity of payload delivery and minimize systemic toxicity, as long as systemic stability is maintained.

Lastly, future developments in this area will be enhanced by enhanced real-time measures of the treatment biology. Liquid biopsy platforms integrating molecular imaging might enable dynamic monitoring of ADC distribution, antigen evolution, and immune activity during therapy. These tools would enable personalized dosing and combination selection and might accelerate translational learning on the intersection of ADC-mediated cytotoxicity and immune regulation. Altogether, PD-1 ADCs provide an interesting platform for expanding the scope of immunotherapy by combining checkpoint inhibition with targeted drug delivery. Although the initial results are promising, the key to success in the long-term will lie in advancing molecular design, strategies to handle heterogeneity and resistance, and the introduction of biomarker-based approaches perhaps in combination regimens - to confer sustained benefit safely, especially in immunologically cold and treatment-resistant tumors.
