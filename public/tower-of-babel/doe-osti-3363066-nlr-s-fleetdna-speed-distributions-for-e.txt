Executive Summary
We compare data sets from FleetDNA and the Bourns College of Engineering – Center for
Environmental Research and Technology (CE-CERT) to defaults in the MOtor Vehicle Emission
Simulator 3 (MOVES3) in consideration of updating speed and vehicle miles traveled (VMT)
distributions for heavy-duty source types. We categorize the data by hour of the day on
weekends and weekdays for four different road types, including freeways and other roads in
urban and rural areas. Addressing only conventional vehicles in this report, we include gasoline-
and diesel-fueled vehicles and exclude propane, hydrogen, electric, hybrid, and other advanced
fuel vehicles. The FleetDNA and CE-CERT data are resolved to individual truck vocations and
the MOVES source type, whereas the heavy-duty speed distributions in MOVES3 are aggregated
across two weight class categories and applied to several source types; this report seeks to give
insight into source- and vocation- specific vehicle behaviors.

The two data sets aggregate to form more robust results than either alone. However, even when
combined, CE-CERT and FleetDNA data are not robust for all source types, types of roads, and
days over all hours, so we give specific recommendations on which data to consider using for
incorporating into MOVES.

On freeways, vehicles spend most of their time at 50–65 miles per hour (mph) per each source
type. On other roads, vehicles tend to travel much slower, often spending the most time in the 5–
10-mph bin. Aside from combination-unit long-haul trucks, most FleetDNA and CE-CERT data
point to slower average speeds than the current MOVES defaults show. In particular, this data set
has slower average speeds for transit buses, school buses, and refuse trucks, which is expected
due to their stop-and-go duty cycles. In general, we did not observe meaningful differences in
average speed across hours of the day. For use in MOVES, we recommend using average speed
distributions calculated by source type, road type, and day type (weekday or weekend).

For VMT road type distributions, we compare road type distributions to MOVES defaults. Long-
haul combination truck road type distributions compare well to the current MOVES defaults. The
remaining source types have road type distributions with more driving on urban roads than the
MOVES defaults. The road type distributions developed in this study may be used to represent
local urban fleets and update the relative proportions on urban freeways compared to urban other
roads, but we do not recommend editing the rural-to-urban ratio of the MOVES defaults based
on primarily urban fleet data.

For VMT day distributions, we observe that many of the source types have significantly different
weekday vs. weekend splits in driving behavior. We see that combination-unit long-haul vehicles
tend to drive more on weekends, whereas refuse trucks and school buses drive more on
weekdays than predicted by MOVES. We recommend that the day type distribution for several
source types from MOVES be updated.

For VMT hour distributions, weekdays often do not show the two peaks characteristic of
MOVES defaults and tend to drive more miles in the morning hours relative to MOVES defaults.
In our conclusions, we recommend updating VMT for source types, road types, and day
combinations with sufficient data.

1 Introduction
The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) MOtor Vehicle Emission Simulator
(MOVES) models mobile sources of air toxics at small and large scales across the Unites States.
Vehicle speed distributions based on the type of vehicle, type of road traveled, and hour of the
day are inputs into MOVES. In conjunction with vehicle miles traveled (VMT) inputs, they are
used to estimate total operation time and select the relevant driving cycles from which running
operating modes and emissions are estimated. In an ongoing research effort, the National
Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) partnered with the EPA to provide default average speed data
for heavy-duty vehicles based on NLR’s FleetDNA database and the Bourns College of
Engineering – Center for Environmental Research and Technology (CE-CERT) data set from the
University of California, Riverside.

NLR’s FleetDNA is a detailed database of 1-Hz time-series commercial truck and bus data that
includes over 2,000 in-use vehicles operating over 12 million miles of driving. FleetDNA data
from conventional-fueled vehicles fall into six MOVES source types: combination-unit long-haul
trucks, combination-unit short-haul trucks, single-unit short-haul trucks, refuse trucks, transit
buses, and school buses. The CE-CERT data were developed for the California Air Resources
Board (CARB) in 2017 (Zhang et al. 2021). CE-CERT heavy-duty vehicles fall into five
MOVES source types: combination-unit short-haul trucks, single-unit short-haul trucks, refuse
trucks, transit buses, and other buses. Excluding the “other bus” source type, which contains
airport shuttle buses, the four other source types overlap with observed FleetDNA source types.
On a vocational level, CE-CERT contained some line-haul trucks that did not perform multiday
trips, and as such were classified as MOVES combination-unit short-haul source rather than
long-haul source type (Zhang et al. 2021). Only two vocations overlapped between the two data
sets: towing from the single-unit short-haul source type and drayage from the combination-unit
short-haul source type.

Using these data, we calculated average speeds, the proportion of time spent in different speed
ranges, and the proportion of distance spent driving under various MOVES categories. MOVES
categories include source type ID (the type of heavy-duty vehicle), road type ID, day ID
(weekend – 2 or weekday – 5), and hour ID. EPA uses source types to classify different vehicle
categories with similar activity. The heavy-duty vehicle source types are listed in Table 1. The
definitions of the MOVES source types are included in the MOVES3 population and activity
report (EPA 2021).

county-level speeds to obtain national average speeds by source type, road type, day type, and
hour of the day. For heavy-duty trucks, speeds were developed from two broad groups: heavy-

2 Road Network Mapping
Starting with NLR’s heavy-duty FleetDNA database, we used GPS location, engine speed, and
speed of the vehicles to categorize each second of the hertz-level driving data into one of the five
specific MOVES road classifications: urban restricted (called urban freeway), urban unrestricted
(urban other), rural restricted (rural freeway), rural unrestricted (rural other), and off-network.
Such classification is broken into three distinct calculations: (1) urban vs. rural, (2) restricted vs.
unrestricted, (3) and off-network. By comparing GPS location to the U.S. Census populated area
geographic boundary shape file and determining if the point’s location was within a geographic
area with a population of 5,000 or more per the Federal Highway Administration’s definition of
an urban area (Federal Highway Administration 2018), we classified each point as urban or rural.
The specific shape file used was found on the data.gov website and is titled
“tl_2017_us_uac10.zip” (U.S. Census Bureau 2021). The 2017 TomTom U.S. road network
database was used for identifying restricted, unrestricted, and off-network points by matching a
given point to a part of the road network. (See Table 3 for a summary of mapping to MOVES
road types.) To save computation time, the closest road link to a given point within 0.01 arc
seconds was considered the matched road link. 2 If no road links were within that radius, the point
was considered off-network. In addition to points that did not match to any road segment, those
points where the vehicle was idling for 120 seconds or longer were also classified as off-
network. Idle is defined as engine speed >200 rotations per minute (RPM) and vehicle speed <1
mile per hour (mph). Once these classifications were done, each data point was given the
appropriate road type number per the MOVES definitions.

Note that there are differences in the road network used for the FleetDNA and CRC A-100
projects. FleetDNA used the TomTom network, whereas CRC A-100 used the U.S. Census
TIGER network (DenBleyker et al. 2017). This may contribute to differences observed in the
two data sets, as mentioned later.

3 Two-Minute Segment Calculations
Next, we aggregated the driving data into targeted 2-minute segments for each vehicle
separately. We considered a vehicle to be “driving” if it was moving or had been idling for less
than 2 minutes. For a vehicle, any data that fell within the same date, hour, and road type were
strung together during driving time into 2-minute segments. For example, if a specific truck
drove on and off between 8 and 9 a.m. (hour ID 9) on January 17, 2013, on an urban
unrestricted-access road (road type ID 5) and idled for periods of 2 minutes or more in between,
the seconds when the truck was driving would be pushed together to form the 2-minute
segments. Therefore, a single segment with an elapsed time of 120 seconds could be an average
of speeds from many different points in the hour, include zero speeds during any short-duration
idling (less than 2 minutes). Any remaining driving time from the specific vehicle, road type,
date, and hour are placed in a segment with less than 120 seconds if the segment is at least 10
seconds. The time elapsed is calculated for each segment so the shorter segments can be
identified and appropriately averaged with the other segments. An example output for one
vehicle, date, road type, and 2 hours is shown in Table 4.

We performed the targeted 2-minute segment calculations in this fashion because it was
computationally efficient, and it aligns with the definition of average speed in EPA MOVES.
Average speeds in EPA MOVES are intended to be representative of the average speed of
driving cycles that are currently greater than 4 minutes in length and classified by day type
(weekend or weekday), hour of the day, road type, and vehicle type.

For the segments, the data are grouped by date to ensure that segments meant to represent
driving cycle average speeds were not aggregated across separate dates. When we examine
averages and distributions in the following analyses, the date column will be dropped upon
grouping.

4 Data Density
We discuss the data density for the FleetDNA and CE-CERT data sets to demonstrate how
combining their data can augment data coverage in certain categories. We depict both vehicle
counts and hours of data recorded per each category of source type, road type, day type, and hour
of the day. Vehicle count and driving time were utilized from results and data files in Section
5.3: Average Speed. Drilling down to this temporal and spatial resolution can make certain
categories of data too sparse to continue reporting or necessitate that results be examined with
caution in some cases.

We also note that while some individual vehicles show diversity in their day-to-day driving,
other vehicles may perform the same route day after day; this repetition may generate hours of
data for a given source type or vocation while underrepresenting the group as a whole. The
greater number of vehicles and greater unique fleets that compose a category, the more likely the
full activity usage of the source type or vocation is represented. In most cases, we analyze the
vehicle count in this section, but for transit buses, we additionally examine fleet count due to
lower-than-ideal vehicle count numbers.

4.1 FleetDNA Data Density
The FleetDNA data are segmented into many separate categories, namely SourceTypeID,
RoadTypeID, DayID, and HourID. Under this examination, the available data in some categories
are sparse. While some patterns may speak to overall trends in heavy-duty vehicle driving—such
as no school bus data on weekends or a lack of transit buses on rural roads—we will not make
recommendations for MOVES3 inputs based on categories of low coverage.

Figures 1–6 display FleetDNA data density for each available MOVES source type: the four
MOVES on-network road types and the two MOVES day types (weekday and weekend). These
plots are used to determine which categories will be shown in analysis graphs in later sections of
the report. Typically, fewer than five vehicles recorded with less than an hour of data for any
hour ID are likely to be considered too low coverage. Usually, close to 30 hours recorded and 30
vehicles driving during some hours of the day constitute a robust sample size.

The road type distributions in MOVES3 are based on national values from data used to derive
the 2017 National Emissions Inventory (EPA 2021). Because the road type distribution for
individual fleets should vary substantially whether the fleet is in an urban or rural area, and the
data we have collected for other source types than combination-unit long-haul are from urban
fleets, we do not recommend that the proportion between urban and rural be used to update the
national defaults. However, within the urban fleets, the ratio of freeway to other roads are useful
to compare to MOVES and may be suggestive of improvements to current data. The FleetDNA
data suggest relatively more driving occurs on urban other roads compared to urban freeways for
all vehicles except for combination long-haul trucks. CE-CERT tends to show more time on
urban freeways than urban other roads—with the exception of airport shuttle “other” buses.
When the two data sets are aggregated, the VMT proportion still favors urban other roads over
urban freeways.

For combination-unit short-haul trucks traveling in the combined FleetDNA and CE-CERT data
set, they spend 59% of their time on urban other roads out of the total time they drive on urban
roads. Because they only spent less than 3% on rural roads, this proportion is very similar to the
57.8% they spend on urban other roads out of all road types, but we recommend updating
MOVES based solely on the fraction spent between the two urban road types for sources other
than combination-unit long-haul. In MOVES currently, the combination-unit short-haul truck
proportion on urban freeways out of all roads is 24.4% and 25.5% on urban other roads, which
totals 50%. We recommend keeping this overall percentage at 50 but adjusting the time spent on
urban other roads to 29.5% of all roads (i.e., 59% times 50%) and urban freeways to 20.5% of
the total (41% times 50%).

Similarly, for single-unit short-haul trucks, we recommend updating the urban percentages to
36.1% on urban other roads—a slight increase over the current 34%—and 21.5% on urban
freeways to match the 62.7% that FleetDNA and CE-CERT trucks combined drive on urban
other roads compared to all urban roads.

Refuse trucks spent significantly more time on urban other roads compared to urban freeways; in
the combined data set, they spent 82.7% of urban road driving on urban other roads. To update

 65

MOVES accordingly, we would recommend 48.3% of total time on urban other roads and 10.1%
on urban freeways. This adjustment maintains the MOVES default of 58.5% on urban roads to
not bias the national defaults based on collecting data from urban refuse fleets.

For transit, the ratio of urban other road driving to urban freeway driving is roughly equal to
current MOVES defaults. MOVES gives 38.6% of driving to urban other roads out of all roads,
and to match the combined FleetDNA and CE-CERT data, this proportion would only be
changed to 38.1% and urban freeways to 26.4%.

5.3 Average Speed
In this section, we examine the average speed rather than the average speed distribution. Average
speed is not a MOVES input, but the average speed analysis was conducted to reduce the
dimensions of the data, making it easier to determine differences in the average speed and
accompanying speed distributions between days of the week, different source types, and road
types. In addition, the confidence interval was used to visually determine if there were sufficient
collected data to estimate speed distributions for each combination of source type, road type, day
type, and (in Section 5.3.1) hour of the day.

5.3.1 Average Speeds With 95% Confidence Interval by Hour
We examine average speeds by hours with 95% confidence intervals using Equation 10.

 Equation 10: Confidence Interval Calculation

 𝑠𝑠
                                               𝑥𝑥̅ ± 𝑡𝑡∝⁄2,𝑛𝑛−1 ×
 √𝑛𝑛
Where:

 𝑥𝑥̅ = average vehicle speed across vehicle speeds for the given source type, road type, and
 day ID
 s = sample standard deviation of the average vehicle speeds
 n = number of vehicles sampled for the given source type, road type, and day ID
         𝑡𝑡∝⁄2,𝑛𝑛−1 = t-statistic for a 95th percentile, ∝ = 5 and ∝⁄2 = 2.5.
For each 2-minute bin, we calculate the miles driven and we have the speed operation time. We
then sum all these values for a given vehicle and divide the total miles driven by the total elapsed
time for each vehicle, road type, day type, and hour ID to obtain a vehicle-based average for each
category. Then, we calculate and graphically display the average and confidence interval across
vehicles within each category of source type, day ID, hour ID, and road type ID. This final step
determines the parameters of the confidence interval, not the number of 2-minute bins combining
to make the vehicle-average speed. For example, if FleetDNA had five vehicles driving at 11
a.m. on weekends on rural other roads within the refuse truck source type, the confidence
interval would have (n – 1) = 5 – 1 = 4 degrees of freedom. The t-statistic for a two-tailed 95%
confidence interval is then used to calculate the confidence interval of the average speed
estimate.

Note that the confidence interval is a useful tool to understand the robustness of the data but
should be interpreted with some caution. For simplicity, we calculated confidence intervals
assuming that each vehicle average speed is an independent and random variable. NLR collects
or obtains data from various vehicle fleets, so the vehicles in the FleetDNA data set are not
random samples of the entire national fleet. For example, the 16 transit buses in the FleetDNA
data are only from three different vehicle fleets in Minneapolis, Denver, and Knoxville. Because
the transit buses in each of these fleets likely have similar behavior, by treating all 16 transit
buses as independent and random variables, our resulting confidence intervals of the national
average transit bus average speed from the FleetDNA database are likely underestimated.

5.3.2 Average Speeds With 95% Confidence Interval
Now, we will not group on hour ID when calculating the average speed across vehicles. As in the
confidence interval analysis previously described, we determine the miles driven and speed
operation time for each 2-minute bin. Summing these values for each vehicle, road, and day type
and dividing the summed miles driven by speed operation time, we obtain the vehicle-average
speed. Then, we perform the mean and confidence interval calculations across vehicles for each
category of source type, day type, and road type.

This higher level of aggregation includes more individual vehicles in each average, thus
tightening the bounds of the confidence interval. In addition, the aggregated data across days
makes it easier to visualize and make comparisons between day, road, and source types.

The average speeds with 95% confidence intervals by road, day, and source type are output to
the file AvgSpeedAndCIsHourIrrespective_(source/vocation)_(fdna/cecert/combined).csv.

We compare the FleetDNA, CE-CERT, and combined data average speeds to the default average
speeds in MOVES3 (EPA 2021). We expect differences in the commercial data sets and
MOVES3 speed values for several reasons. First, they are different vehicle samples. Second, the
FleetDNA and CE-CERT data are resolved to individual vehicles and can capture differences in
vocations and specific MOVES source types such as transit buses and refuse trucks. In addition,
different methods and data are used to map the speeds to road types, as discussed in Section 2:
Road Network Mapping. The MOVES3 average speeds were aggregated by the EPA by
combining the data with the distribution of time spent traveled across hours of the day using the
default hourly distribution in MOVES3 (HourVMTFraction table).

These comparisons are displayed graphically in Figure 94. Except for combination-unit long-

We might expect CE-CERT and FleetDNA to differ on their average speeds due to differences in
the freeway vs. other roads classification. CE-CERT data contained a column labeled freeway or
non-freeway, whereas FleetDNA freeways are equivalent to TomTom road class 0 (see Table 3)
(TomTom 2022). For FleetDNA, “other roads” include TomTom road class 1, which is defined
as “all roads of high importance, but not officially assigned as motorways, that are part of a
connection used for international and national traffic and transport” (TomTom 2022). Because
roads used for national and international travel may have quite high speed limits, we might
expect FleetDNA to have higher speeds on “other roads” compared to CE-CERT. Other possible
explanations for discrepancies in the two data set speeds—such as combination-unit long-haul on
both rural and urban other roads and single short-haul on weekday urban other roads—could be
due to the vocational composition of the source type or higher congestion or lower speed limits
on California roads. For most other instances, as shown in Figure 94, the vehicle speeds compare
well between the two data sets, especially considering they are collected on different vehicle
fleets operating in different areas of the country.

6 Conclusions
Heavy-duty average speed distributions were calculated from the FleetDNA and CE-CERT data
sets for individual source types, road types, days, and hours of the day and compared to existing
default average speeds in MOVES3. The advantage of these two data sets is that they can
provide average speeds for specific vocations and source types in MOVES. As expected, the
average speeds varied considerably for different source types and vocations.

We developed average speed distributions for the source types in MOVES by assuming the
FleetDNA and CE-CERT data sets are a representative sample of different vocations and by
weighting the activity from each vehicle according to its average usage factor. We also provided
the average speed distribution data by truck vocation, enabling MOVES users to develop average
speed distributions that represent the distribution of vehicle vocations within their modeled
fleets.

We recommend that the robust speed-derived data from combining FleetDNA and CE-CERT on
urban roads from weekday driving be considered to incorporate into MOVES. This includes the
source-type-specific speeds for refuse trucks, transit buses, and school buses, which are lower
than the current MOVES3 heavy-duty speeds. By incorporating their average speeds, MOVES
can more accurately reflect stop-and-go behavior for those source types. The speeds for
combination long-haul, combination short-haul, and single-unit short-haul could also be
considered to include in MOVES, or to characterize individual vocations within these source
types. For example, combination-unit short-haul vehicles of beverage delivery and line-haul
vocations were characterized by slower speeds than food, local, and parcel delivery trucks on
freeways, and agriculture, cement mixers, and line-haul vocations were slower than delivery
vocations on other roads. For single-unit short-haul, towing, beverage, and parcel delivery
vehicles displayed slower average speeds on freeways, while utility, street sweepers, freeway
work, and warehouse and linen delivery vehicles had faster average speeds. On other roads,
street sweepers and freeway work vehicles slowed down relative to the average on weekdays,
while towing, snow plow, and beverage delivery all had faster average speeds compared to the
weekend average but also less data and larger confidence intervals (see Figure 96). The source
type average speeds for combination-unit long-haul vehicles compare reasonably well to the
current MOVES3 default values. This comparison can serve as validation that the current
MOVES3 speed data represent this source type well.

FleetDNA and CE-CERT showed slower speeds than MOVES defaults on urban freeways for
combination-unit short-haul trucks, single-unit short-haul trucks, refuse trucks, and transit buses.
For weekdays on urban freeways, we recommend updating MOVES. For weekends,
combination-unit short-haul and single-unit short-haul could inform MOVES as well. Despite a
slightly smaller sample size, transit buses could possibly be included as well. Because both data
sets display these slower speeds, it lends credibility to the behavior. Although we do not expect
large vocational shifts on highway driving, MOVES may not account for congestion based on
when certain source types are on the road, or perhaps the data informing MOVES defaults may
include less congested roadways.

We additionally saw slower speeds for those four vocations on other urban roads. Not accounting
for unique vocations and different source types in MOVES defaults could explain this difference,

 122

because many vocations and source types perform functions on other roads such as dropping off
passengers, delivering food or parcels, or picking up trash, which slows them down.

School and other buses only had four and five vehicles, respectively, and we only recommend
updating MOVES distributions based on them if this shift more accurately reflects these source
types despite such small sample sizes.

For the rural road types, only the combination-unit long-haul and, on weekdays, combination-
unit short-haul source types had sufficient data that we recommend them to be considered for
MOVES. Combination-unit long-haul vehicles traveled slightly faster than MOVES default
speeds on urban and rural freeways and slightly slower on other roads. Combination-unit short-
haul traveled noticeably slower, possibly due to the time of day or specific roads on which they
tended to travel.

The data density section describes which source, road, and day type combinations were likely to
be considered having enough data based on containing close to 30 vehicles and a similar number
of recorded hours. For the hour analysis of data, typically a confidence interval approaching 20
mph was considered not of good quality and may need to be replaced by data from neighboring
hours, but here we recommend using the data that have been aggregated over all hours, as in
Section 5.1.4.

changing the ratio between urban freeway and other roads but keep the overall percent in urban roads as a whole the
same.

There are some limitations of the speed and mileage data derived from FleetDNA and CE-
CERT. For example, they cannot provide robust estimates of average speeds across all
combinations of source types, road types, day types, and hours of the day. In addition, some of
the average speed estimates appear uncharacteristic for specific road type and source type
combinations (e.g., single- and combination-unit short-haul and transit vehicles on urban
freeways). For FleetDNA, differences in definitions in road types between different road
networks may contribute to these observations; however, CE-CERT confirms some observed
trends and independently defines freeway vs. non-freeways classifications. In cases of
inadequate data, average speed distributions from other hours of the day, day types, or other road
types could be used in place of the existing MOVES3 average speeds where source-type-specific
vehicle speeds are deemed warranted.

FleetDNA and CE-CERT provide real-world data on source- and vocation-specific average
speeds, speed distributions, and miles driven for some road and day type combinations. The two
data sets complement each other and provide reassurance on results observed by one data set
alone. The recommended updates to speed and VMT distributions should be more representative
of individual source types and provide data for MOVES users to better characterize the heavy-
duty activity of fleets in their modeling scenarios.
