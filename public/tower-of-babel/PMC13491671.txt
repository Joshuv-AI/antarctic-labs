Intestinal SMARCA4-deficient undifferentiated carcinoma: a case series and systematic review of the literature
Authors: Wenjing Ma, Shenda Zhou, Yongta Huang, Xiaofeng Tang, Shimei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntestinal SMARCA4-deficient undifferentiated carcinoma (SMARCA4-DUC) is a rare and highly aggressive malignancy. Its clinicopathological features remain poorly defined. No standardized treatment has been established.MethodsWe report two new cases and review 18 previously published cases, providing a descriptive summary of clinicopathological, molecular, and therapeutic features.ResultsThe aggregated 20 cases included 80% males, with a mean age of 56 years. The tumors exhibited diffuse sheet-like growth of epithelioid cells, with rhabdoid morphology observed in 81.25% (13/16) of cases. Necrosis and mitotic activity were frequent. Immunohistochemistry confirmed SMARCA4 loss and variable CK/CK7 expression, while CDX-2 and CK20 were consistently negative. Ki-67 was uniformly high. Although molecular profiling data in this study were limited, TP53 mutations were identified in all three cases with available NGS data. Distant metastasis was present in 41.2% of patients at initial diagnosis. The median overall survival was 11 months (range 1.5–29 months). Chemotherapy responses were poor, but responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors were heterogeneous: two patients achieved sustained complete remission (> 18 months) on pembrolizumab, while three others experienced no benefit.ConclusionsIntestinal SMARCA4-deficient carcinoma is a rare high-grade malignancy with a poor prognosis and currently no standard treatment regimen. In this small aggregated series, two patients achieved durable complete responses to pembrolizumab monotherapy, suggesting that a subset of these tumors may benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors. However, given the inconsistent responses observed, these preliminary findings require validation in future multicenter, large-scale prospective studies.Keywords: SMARCA4, BRG1, Undifferentiated carcinoma, Intestinal neoplasms, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Undifferentiated carcinoma of the intestinal tract is a rare and highly aggressive neoplasm. It has no specific cytological or architectural differentiation, as defined by the WHO classification of digestive tumors [1]. In a subset of cases, the undifferentiated phenotype is driven by inactivation of SMARCA4. This gene encodes the core ATPase subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex [2–4]. Epigenetic dysregulation and abnormalities in interconnected signaling pathways collectively mediate the aggressive phenotype seen in SMARCA4-deficient tumors [5]. Gastric SMARCA4-deficient undifferentiated carcinoma has gained increasing attention. However, the intestinal type (arising from the small intestine and colorectum) is extremely rare and remains poorly studied. To date, only sporadic cases of this tumor have been reported. No systematic analysis of its clinicopathological, molecular, or therapeutic characteristics has been conducted [6, 7]. To address these gaps, we retrospectively analyzed two institutional cases. We also reviewed 18 previously reported cases from the English literature. This yielded a combined cohort of 20 patients. By aggregating this pooled cohort, we aim to provide a descriptive summary of clinicopathological features, treatment outcomes, and prognosis. This provides a valuable preliminary basis for further research on this rare and aggressive malignancy.

Methods

Literature search and case selection

We searched PubMed, Web of Science and Embase up to March 2026 using the following search terms: “SMARCA4” OR “BRG1”）AND “deficient” AND （“intestine” OR “small bowel” OR “duodenum” OR “jejunum” OR “ileum” OR “colon” OR “colorectal” OR “rectal”.A PRISMA flow diagram summarizing the study selection process is provided in Fig. 1.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of study selection

Inclusion criteria were: (1) immunohistochemistry or molecular analysis confirmed primary intestinal SMARCA4‑DUC; (2) English language; (3) full text available.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) non-intestinal tumors; (2) involved intestine metastases from other primaries; (3) SMARCA4 status was not confirmed or was reported as intact/wild type; (4) duplicate reports.

Statistical analysis

Kaplan–Meier analysis (GraphPad Prism 11) estimated median overall survival. Patients lost to follow-up or alive at last follow-up were censored. Due to small sample size (n = 20) and censoring, 95% CIs and subgroup comparisons were not calculated.

Case presentation

Clinical data

Two cases of primary intestinal SMARCA4 (BRG1)-deficient undifferentiated carcinoma diagnosed at the Department of Pathology, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region People’s Hospital between May 2024 and July 2025 were collected. Both patients were elderly males, aged 84 and 67 years, respectively, with recurrent abdominal pain as the initial symptom. Case 1 additionally presented with weight loss, cachexia, and anemia. Case 1: CT revealed an occupying lesion in the duodenal ampulla. Endoscopy identified a tumor in the descending duodenum and the ampulla, with surface ulceration and exudate, as well as luminal narrowing. Biopsy was obtained for pathological diagnosis. Case 2: CT showed irregular thickening of the intestinal wall at the junction of the descending colon and sigmoid colon. The patient underwent surgical resection.

Gross findings

Case 1 was a biopsy of the descending duodenum, consisting of 5 tissue fragments measuring 0.1–0.3 cm in maximum diameter. Case 2 was a descending colon resection specimen. An ulcerative tumor measuring 7.5 × 5.5 × 4.5 cm was observed on the mucosal surface of the intestinal segment. The cut surface was gray-white, firm, and poorly demarcated, infiltrating the full thickness of the intestinal wall. The serosal surface and surrounding intestinal mucosa showed no abnormalities.

Microscopic findings

Microscopic morphology of the tumors in both cases was similar. At low magnification, the tumors showed a sheet-like, diffuse growth pattern. Patchy necrosis was observed among the tumor cells (Fig. 2A). The tumor cells were medium to large, with slightly coarse chromatin. Some nuclei exhibited vacuolar features, and multiple small nucleoli were visible. Mitotic figures were easily identified (Fig. 2B).

Immunophenotype

Both cases showed deficient SMARCA4 expression but retained intact INI1 expression. Immunohistochemically, CK was positive in both cases, while CK7, CK20, CDX-2, CD56, CgA, Syn, S-100, LCA and CD34 were all negative (Fig. 2C-G). The Ki-67 proliferation index was high in both cases (approximately 80%). Mismatch repair (MMR) testing showed no deficiency, and HER2 staining scored 0 (negative). SOX2 was positive in Case 2. Due to budgetary constraints, PD-L1 expression and tumor mutational burden (TMB) were not evaluated.

Treatment and prognosis

Case 1 patient received palliative radiotherapy. Obstructive symptoms were relieved after the first treatment cycle, but the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up. Case 2 patient underwent surgical resection, with postoperative stage T4N0M0, and received traditional Chinese medicine postoperatively. At the 13‑month follow‑up, the patient showed no signs of disease recurrence.

Results of the literature search

Following exclusion of metastatic disease, two primary intestinal SMARCA4-DUC cases were identified at our institution. An additional 18 previously published cases were retrieved from the English literature [8–16], yielding a combined cohort of 20 patients.

Demographics and clinical presentation

A total of 20 patients were included in this study, with a male-to-female ratio of 4:1 and ages ranging from 34 to 84 years (median age, 56.55 years). Twelve cases involved the small intestine (including the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum), and eight cases involved the colorectum. Tumor diameter ranged from 1.0 to 10.0 cm (mean, 6.5 cm). The predominant macroscopic morphology was ulcerative type, with one case presenting as a “large infiltrative ulcer”; a few cases exhibited exophytic or mixed growth patterns. Among the 17 patients with complete staging data, 7 (41.2%) had distant metastasis (stage IV) at initial diagnosis. Metastases involved the liver, peritoneum, and brain. Six patients (35.3%) had stage III disease, and 4 (23.5%) had stage II disease. Detailed information is provided in Tables 1 and 2.

Pathological morphology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular features

Histological examination revealed a consistent solid, sheet-like growth pattern with extensive necrosis and high proliferative activity in all tumors. Focal or diffuse rhabdoid morphology, characterized by eccentric nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions, was observed in 13 cases; however, this feature was absent in the two newly added cases. Sarcomatoid components were identified in one case (Case 3). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated complete loss of SMARCA4 protein expression in all evaluable cases, while INI1 expression was retained. Cytokeratin (CK) was positive in 10 of 15 cases (66.7%), and vimentin was positive in 12 of 13 cases (92.3%). CK20 and CDX2 were generally negative, with only one case showing weak positivity. Neuroendocrine, vascular, and melanocytic markers were all negative. HER2 expression was also negative in all cases tested. Mismatch repair function was proficient in 14 of 15 cases (93.3%), with only one case demonstrating deficiency. The Ki-67 proliferation index was generally > 50% and exceeded 80% in most cases. PD-L1 expression was evaluated in two cases and found to be low. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) data were available for three cases. All three harbored TP53 mutations; two also carried CTNNB1 mutations, and one additionally carried PTEN and ATM mutations. Detailed information is provided in Tables 1 and 2.

Treatment modalities and responses

Among the 20 patients, five underwent surgery alone. Early-stage patients, such as Case 9 (stage IIC) and Case 20 (stage IIB), achieved disease-free survival (DFS) of 7 months and 13 months after surgery, respectively. In contrast, two patients with advanced disease (Case 5 and Case 17, both stage IV) experienced rapid postoperative progression and died at 1 month and 5 months, respectively. Case 1 (stage unknown) underwent surgery alone and died 3 months postoperatively.

Another seven patients received surgery combined with chemotherapy, with or without radiotherapy, targeted therapy, or anti-angiogenic therapy. Notably, only one early-stage patient (Case 4, stage IIA) achieved a DFS of 29 months after radical surgery followed by the XELOX regimen and maintenance therapy with tegafur, gimeracil, and oteracil potassium (S-1). The remaining six patients, all with advanced or locally advanced disease, developed recurrence or disease progression within 3–12 months despite receiving multi-line intensive treatments (e.g., FOLFIRI plus bevacizumab, paclitaxel plus anlotinib). Ultimately, four of these six patients eventually died. However, two exceptions were Cases 3 and 18, both stage IIIC. Both experienced distant recurrence after surgery but achieved DFS status at 5 months and 12 months of follow-up, respectively, following combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Their disease remains under short-term stable control.

Case 8 (stage IIIB) completed one cycle of GP chemotherapy and showed no disease progression at the 2‑month follow‑up. Case 11 (stage IV) received palliative care only and died 11 months after diagnosis. Case 20, treated at our center (stage not yet determined), underwent palliative radiotherapy alone; after achieving a partial response, the patient was lost to follow‑up.

Outcomes

Among the 20 enrolled patients, 19 had effective follow-up. A total of 11 patients (57.9%) died due to disease progression. Overall survival (OS) for the entire cohort ranged from 1 to 29 months. With the exception of a few early-stage patients who underwent radical resection (e.g., Case 20), most patients had a disease-free survival (DFS) not exceeding one year. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a median OS of 11 months (range, 1.5–29 months) (Fig. 3). The survival curve showed a sharp decline in survival rate during the first year after surgery, followed by a plateau at a low level. Long-term survival (> 2 years) was extremely rare, with only one patient with localized disease achieving prolonged DFS (Case 4, DFS of 29 months) after radical resection.

Discussion

The SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex plays a critical role in regulating gene transcription, DNA repair, and cell differentiation. BRG1, the protein encoded by the SMARCA4 gene, serves as the core catalytic subunit of this complex [2]. As a classic tumor suppressor gene, functional inactivation of SMARCA4 leads to the development of SMARCA4 deficient undifferentiated carcinoma (SMARCA4 DUC) and represents a key driver genetic event in the initiation and progression of various malignant tumors. To date, intestinal SMARCA4 DUC has been rarely reported. In this study, we pooled two cases from our institution and 18 previously reported cases from the literature (20 cases in total) to systematically analyze their clinicopathological features, molecular characteristics, and clinical management, thereby providing a clinical basis for future large scale studies.

Intestinal SMARCA4 DUC demonstrates a male predominance (16/21 cases, 76.19%), with a mean age at onset of 56.55 years (range, 34–84 years), similar to gastric SMARCA4 DUC [7]. The tumor occurs in both the small intestine and colorectum, with no clear site predilection in this cohort.

Histologically, all 20 tumors presented a consistent solid, sheet like growth pattern, accompanied by extensive necrosis and high proliferative activity. Although rhabdoid morphology can only be observed in a subset of cases, when present, it is often considered an important morphological clue that prompts immunohistochemical evaluation of the SWI/SNF complex [17]. Nevertheless, its occurrence is not constant and shows significant heterogeneity. Previous studies have shown that the frequency of this morphology may be closely related to the specimen type: in gastric biopsy specimens, up to 86% of cases lack this feature, whereas in gastric resection specimens, the proportion is 53%. Molecular alterations in other subunits of the SWI/SNF complex may also affect the frequency and distribution of rhabdoid features [8, 18]. In this intestinal cohort, rhabdoid morphology was observed in 81.25% (13/16) of patients, a finding that may be associated with the aforementioned mechanisms. In addition, sarcomatoid components are occasionally seen. Collectively, this tumor exhibits a wide morphological spectrum, and currently, no single definite morphological feature can reliably confirm or rule out SMARCA4 DUC.

The immunohistochemical results of this study are basically consistent with those reported by Chang et al. and Neil et al [3, 8]. In all evaluable cases, complete loss of SMARCA4 expression and retention of INI1 expression were observed, and the protein loss patterns of these two markers are usually mutually exclusive [19–22]. Current studies have shown that mutations in the SWI/SNF complex do not exert their carcinogenic effects through a complete loss of chromatin remodeling function, but rather by inducing cellular dysfunction via residual SWI/SNF activity [23]. Furthermore, other researchers have hypothesized that the simultaneous loss of the core subunits SMARCA4 and INI1 may counteract the carcinogenic effects mediated by the residual SWI/SNF complex [7].

Some cases in this study showed co expression of broad spectrum CK and vimentin, indicating that intestinal SMARCA4 DUC frequently displays a hybrid epithelial mesenchymal phenotype. This feature also poses diagnostic challenges in clinical pathology, particularly in small biopsy specimens where it may be confused with other neoplasms. Notably, CK20 and CDX2 expression was generally negative in this study, with only one case showing weak positivity. The frequent loss of gastrointestinal lineage markers (such as CK20 and CDX2) combined with loss of SMARCA4 expression may provide certain reference value for the differential diagnosis of intestinal SMARCA4 DUC from other undifferentiated tumors, which may be helpful to improve the accuracy of clinical diagnosis.

The differential diagnosis includes:(1) Melanoma: Tumor cells show variable morphology (epithelioid, spindle, plasmacytoid, etc.), with or without pigmentation. Immunostaining for melanocytic markers (S100, SOX10, HMB45, Melan A) is diagnostic. (2) Anaplastic large cell lymphoma: Contains variable numbers of “hallmark” cells; of T cell origin, with strong CD30 expression. (3) Epithelioid gastrointestinal stromal tumor: Typically occurs in the stomach and greater omentum; exhibits nested or sheet like growth. Expresses CD34, CD117, and DOG1; most cases harbor activating mutations of KIT or PDGFRA. (4) Rhabdomyosarcoma: Rare in patients over 45 years of age; common sites include the head and neck, urogenital tract, retroperitoneum, and extremities. Positive for desmin, MyoD1, and myogenin. (5) Poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma: Characterized by single cell infiltration or irregular glands with fibroproliferative stroma. Immunohistochemistry may express intestinal markers (CDX2, SATB2, CK20). (6) Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma: Displays organoid, nested, or rosette like architecture; large cells with abundant cytoplasm, high mitotic rate, and extensive necrosis. Neuroendocrine markers are detectable. (7) Intestinal undifferentiated carcinoma with INI1 loss: Characteristic rhabdoid cells (eosinophilic cytoplasm, poor cohesion, mucinous stroma). Giant cells, spindle or glandular differentiation may be observed. Co expresses CK and vimentin; loss of INI1 expression is diagnostic.

In addition to histopathological features, molecular profiling analysis helps elucidate the driving mechanisms and drug sensitivity of these tumors. Unfortunately, due to the limited number of cases with available sequencing data in this study, a statistical association with clinical outcomes could not be established. Therefore, the following observations are merely descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Available genomic data from published studies suggest that SMARCA4 deficient tumors frequently harbor co occurring mutations in tumor suppressor genes such as TP53 [24, 25]. Consistent with previous reports, TP53 mutation was the mos common co occurring genetic abnormality in the few sequenced cases of this study, while CTNNB1mutation was detected in two cases. Mutations in PTEN and ATM were also observed. These preliminary findings suggest that the pathogenesis may not be driven solely by SMARCA4 deficiency, and further investigation is needed to elucidate the cooperative genetic mechanisms underlying the highly aggressive phenotype of these tumors. Discrepancies exist between SMARCA4 immunohistochemistry (IHC) and next generation sequencing (NGS) [26]. Some tumors show loss of SMARCA4 protein expression but remain wild type at the genetic level, a phenomenon often attributed to non mutational mechanisms such as epigenetic silencing [27]. Two such cases were identified in this study, suggesting that reliance solely on NGS may have diagnostic limitations. When histomorphology and routine IHC markers are insufficient for a definitive diagnosis, SMARCA4 IHC may be prioritized. Due to the limited sample size, the findings of this study are preliminary and serve as a reference for future research on the molecular characteristics of this rare tumor. Moreover, multi omics integrated analysis and pathway based studies will help elucidate the molecular pathogenesis of rare undifferentiated tumors and provide new directions for both basic and translational research on SMARCA4 deficient undifferentiated carcinoma [28].

Similar to previous studies, the clinical manifestations of this tumor lack specificity, and most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage [29]. In the present study, the proportion of patients with stage III/IV disease was 76.5%, which may suggest high invasiveness and difficulty in early diagnosis. Given the relatively small sample size and the potential for publication bias, further large scale studies are warranted to clarify the clinical and biological characteristics of this tumor.

Currently, there is no standardized treatment guideline for intestinal SMARCA4 DUC. The main treatment modalities include surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [30, 31]. Among the six patients who underwent surgery alone, two with early stage disease achieved a maximum disease free survival (DFS) of 7 months, and two with advanced disease showed rapid postoperative progression, with the shortest survival being less than 1 month. Limited data suggest that surgery alone may provide only short term control of resectable lesions and offers limited benefit for patients with advanced disease. Clinically, surgery combined with multimodal therapy is more commonly used for locally advanced cases [32, 33]. In the present study, one early stage patient underwent radical surgery followed by XELOX chemotherapy and S 1 maintenance therapy, achieving a DFS of 29 months. The other six patients with locally advanced or metastatic disease received multi line combination therapies including targeted agents; all developed disease progression or recurrence within 3–12 months, and four of them died. Overall, surgery plus adjuvant therapy may improve outcomes for some early stage patients, but despite aggressive multi line therapy, the prognosis of advanced patients remains poor. These findings require confirmation by large sample studies.

The mechanism of chemotherapy resistance in this tumor type remains unclear. Current evidence indicates that dysfunction of chromatin remodeling complexes and molecular chaperones may be closely associated with treatment resistance in this highly aggressive undifferentiated neoplasm [34]. In this cohort, high Ki 67 expression, extensive necrosis, early metastasis, and driver gene mutations (e.g., TP53, CTNNB1 and PTEN) were observed. Due to the limited sample size, lack of a control group, and the retrospective nature of this study, we can only summarize the relevant clinicopathological and molecular features without establishing a causal relationship with chemotherapy resistance. The underlying mechanisms require further investigation through large scale clinical studies and basic experiments.

SMARCA4 deficiency can create an immunosuppressive cold tumor microenvironment, characterized by reduced intratumoral CD8⁺ infiltrating lymphocytes and increased infiltration of immunosuppressive cells such as regulatory T cells and M2 macrophages [35], thereby diminishing tumor response to PD 1 inhibitors. Aberrant activation of the Wnt/β catenin pathway caused by CTNNB1 mutations is a key mechanism driving T cell exclusion in solid tumors, inhibiting dendritic cell recruitment and attenuating CD8⁺ T cell antitumor immunity, ultimately reducing tumor sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors [36]. In this study, two patients harboring CTNNB1 mutations received PD-1 inhibitor based combination chemotherapy but achieved only transient responses; they died within six months due to liver metastasis or liver failure, without demonstrating a survival benefit. Whether the presence of CTNNB1 mutations contributed to these poor outcomes remains speculative and requires validation in larger cohorts.

The immunotherapy responses in this cohort varied markedly. Previous studies have shown that even PD L1 negative SMARCA4 deficient tumors can benefit from immunotherapy [37]; the favorable efficacy of pembrolizumab in MSI H/dMMR colorectal cancer also provides a clinical rationale for immunotherapy in such intestinal tumors [38]. One dMMR patient in this study who received sequential pembrolizumab monotherapy after chemotherapy progression achieved complete remission within 3 months, with disease free survival exceeding 19 months, consistent with the known high sensitivity of these tumors to immunotherapy. One pMMR patient achieved marked tumor shrinkage and durable complete remission lasting 18 months after pembrolizumab monotherapy, indicating that some of these tumors may benefit from immunotherapy. However, whether SMARCA4 deficiency confers unique immunogenicity to pMMR tumors remains an interesting hypothesis that requires further validation in larger cohorts with comprehensive immune and molecular profiling.

Only five patients in this study received immunotherapy, with no standardized regimen or control group; therefore, the observed clinical findings do not have general applicability and are intended only as a reference for future research. Definitive confirmation of efficacy requires large sample multicenter cohort studies and prospective trials. Beyond immunotherapy, SMARCA2 targeted strategies and multiple preclinical studies suggest that epigenetic targeted therapies, primarily the CDK4/6–EZH2 pathway, have promising application prospects, with EZH2 being a key regulator of chromatin remodeling [39, 40]. Although EZH2 expression levels were not routinely tested in this study, this molecular mechanism may offer new avenues for future individualized targeted therapy.

Currently, there is no direct targeted therapy for SMARCA4 deficiency. Clinical treatment should focus on various molecular abnormalities mediated by this deficiency, primarily including genomic stability, DNA mismatch repair status, tumor mutation burden, and immune features of the tumor microenvironment. These indicators are all biomarkers associated with the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [41]. Future research should prioritize the screening and integration of effective biomarkers to achieve precise patient stratification.

Among the 20 patients, 11 (57.9%) died of the disease, and the survival period was short. The survival curve shows that the survival rate declines sharply within the first year and then remains at a low level. This poor prognosis is similar to that previously reported for gastric SMARCA4 DUC [7] and may also reflect the strong invasiveness of SMARCA4 DUC as well as the limited efficacy of conventional treatment in this cohort. However, given the small sample size and the heterogeneity of follow up, this survival estimates should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore treatment strategies beyond traditional therapy, guided by mechanistic insights, which is also a key focus of current translational oncology research on rare and refractory cancers [42].

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, as a retrospective pooled analysis based on published case reports and small case series, it is subject to publication bias and selection bias. Case reports tend to overrepresent unusual presentations or exceptional outcomes, and our manual literature screening may have inadvertently missed eligible cases. Second, the absence of a control group limits causal attribution of the observed features specifically to SMARCA4 deficiency. Third, the sample size is small (n=20), precluding formal statistical inference, confidence interval calculation, and meaningful subgroup comparisons. The median overall survival of 11 months should therefore be interpreted with caution. Fourth, molecular profiling data were available for only three patients, so no correlation between molecular findings and clinical outcomes can be established. Fifth, treatment regimens were highly heterogeneous, and no standardized biomarker assessment was uniformly performed, limiting our ability to identify predictors of treatment response. Sixth, follow-up durations were short and inconsistent, which may affect survival estimates. In summary, these findings should be regarded as preliminary, descriptive, and hypothesis-generating. The true incidence, clinical behavior, and optimal treatment strategy for intestinal SMARCA4-DUC remain to be defined. Confirmation of the signals observed here—particularly the suggestion of immunotherapy benefit in a subset of patients—will require multicenter prospective studies with systematic biospecimen collection, standardized treatment protocols, and complete follow-up. Despite these limitations, this study assembles the available cases of this rare tumor currently documented in the literature and provides a preliminary description of its clinicopathological features, molecular profiles, and heterogeneous patterns of immunotherapy response, offering a foundation for the design of future multicenter studies and prospective trials.

Conclusions

Intestinal SMARCA4 deficient undifferentiated carcinoma is a rare and highly aggressive tumor that predominantly affects elderly males. Histologically, the tumor exhibits undifferentiated cell morphology, including epithelioid and rhabdoid features. For poorly differentiated intestinal tumors presenting with such morphology and posing diagnostic difficulties, pathologists should consider performing SMARCA4 immunohistochemistry. The median overall survival of this tumor is short, and patients are often diagnosed at an advanced or metastatic stage, with an overall poor prognosis. Currently, no optimal standard treatment exists. In this small aggregated series, two patients achieved durable complete responses to pembrolizumab monotherapy, suggesting that a subset of these tumors may benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors. However, given the inconsistent responses observed, these preliminary findings require validation in future multicenter, large-scale prospective studies.
