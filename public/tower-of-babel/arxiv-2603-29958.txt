Operator systems and positive extensions over discrete groups
Evgenios T.A. Kakariadis, Malte Leimbach, Ivan G. Todorov, Walter D. van Suijlekom

Abstract
. The extension problem asks whether positive semi-definite functions on a symmetric unital subset of a discrete group can be extended to positive semi-definite functions on the whole group. It has been known at least since the work of Rudin in the 1960s that this is closely related to the problem of finding sums of squares factorisations of positive elements in the group C*-algebra. We give an operator system perspective at these two problems explaining their equivalence: the extension property is characterised by a certain quotient map on the Fourier–Stieltjes algebra, and the factorisation property by a certain complete order embedding into the group C*-algebra. These properties are linked to the duality of the operator systems which have recently emerged from spectral and Fourier truncations in noncommutative geometry. We exemplify how one can relate certain extension problems to operator system techniques such as nuclearity and the C*-envelope.



1.
Introduction

The study of positivity of functions via sums of squares of other functions has its roots in the work of Hilbert
[
27
]
, with a corresponding factorisation question formulated by him
and becoming widely known as Hilbert’s 17th problem

[
28
]
.
In the context of classical Fourier analysis, affirmative first results and counterexamples for positive semi-definite functions defined on subsets of abelian groups were obtained by Rudin
[
49
]
.
The study of the sums-of-squares problem revealed deep and intricate connections with harmonic analysis, first in the case of abelian groups
[
25
,
4
,
10
,
49
]
and then in the non-abelian case; see e.g.
[
5
,
6
]
.

Despite the negative general results, the failure of the characterisation of positivity as an expression of sums of squares led to fruitful semi-definite optimisation hierarchies.
In the commutative case, these were first studied by Lasserre
[
35
]
and have had lasting impact in optimisation theory, while in the context of quantum information theory, sums-of-squares approximations for the problem of determining the quantum commuting value of a non-local game have been proposed with great success by Navascués, Pironio and Acín
[
42
]
.
At the heart of these optimisation hierarchies lies a constrained resolution approach, where the test set (usually convex) for a given optimisation problem is represented as an increasing union (or the closure thereof) of subsets that lead to computationally amenable estimations of the cost function.

A constrained resolution approach to noncommutative geometry has independently generated recent interest;
here, the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras in the spectral triple formalism are replaced by operator systems, as a means to describe physical systems with incomplete information
[
13
]
.
Specifically, in the group setting, one considers the state space of spectral truncations, which leads to the problem of extending partially positive semi-definite functions to globally positive semi-definite ones.
The goal of this paper is to present a unifying approach to the extension and factorisation problems in general (not necessarily abelian) discrete groups, using the formalism of operator systems, motivated by the main examples appearing in the context of noncommutative geometry
[
13
]
.

To be more precise, in the latter context, operator systems arise in two main (and complementary) ways:
on the one hand as compressions
PAP
of a
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
by a projection
P\in\mathcal{B}(H)
(e.g. a spectral projection for a Dirac operator on
H
) and, on the other hand, as certain operator subsystems of
A
.
In the case of the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
A=\mathrm{C}(\mathbb{T})
of continuous complex-valued functions on the circle, the operator systems of interest are the
Toeplitz system

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}(\mathbb{T})^{(N)}:=\{(T_{ij})_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\in\mathrm{M}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\mid T_{ij}=T_{(i+1)(j+1)}\text{ for all }i,j=0,\dots,N-1\}

(the addition is mod
N
), consisting of the
N\times N
Toeplitz matrices, and the
Fourier system

(3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}(\mathbb{T})_{(N)}:=\{f\in\mathbb{T}\mid\widehat{f}_{n}=0\text{ when }n\geq N\},

that is, the operator subsystem of
A\cong\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z})
spanned by finitely supported sequences
(\dots,0,a_{-N+1},\dots,a_{0},\dots,a_{N-1},0,\dots)
.
Using the matrix-valued Fejér–Riesz lemma it was shown that the Toeplitz and the Fourier system are unitally completely order isomorphic to each other’s operator system duals
[
13
,
20
]
.
As we will explain shortly, it seems appropriate to consider this duality of operator systems as a property of the group
\mathbb{Z}
and the subset
\{1,\dots,N\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
;
it is then natural to ask, for which discrete groups
\Gamma
and finite subsets
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
analogous results hold.

To this end, for a discrete group
\Gamma
and a finite subset
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
, we denote by
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
the
Toeplitz system
, i.e. the operator system of Toeplitz matrices

(4)

\displaystyle(T_{st^{-1}})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}\cong\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Sigma)),

and by
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Sigma\Sigma^{-1})}
the
Fourier system
, i.e. the operator subsystem of the group C*-algebra
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
spanned by
\delta_{st^{-1}}
,
s,t\in\Sigma
, where
\delta_{s}
,
s\in\Gamma
, are the canonical unitary generators of
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
.
The Toeplitz system
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
arises both as the compression of the (reduced) group
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra by the orthogonal projection
P:\ell^{2}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\Sigma)
, and as the image of the restriction map
\rho:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}
of functions in the Fourier–Stieltjes algebra
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)
to the set
\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}
.
We mainly adopt the latter point of view.
Recalling that the dual of the group
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
is the Fourier–Stieltjes algebra
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)
, it is natural to expect that the Toeplitz and Fourier systems are dual to each other if and only if the restriction map
\rho
is a quotient map.
We make this precise in terms of the involved matrix order structures by showing that the Toeplitz and the Fourier system are dual to each other if and only if every positive semi-definite Toeplitz matrix
(T_{st^{-1}})_{s,t\in\Sigma}
with matrix entries extends to a positive semi-definite function on
\Gamma
,
Theorem 4.22
.

The duality of Toeplitz matrices and sums of squares was already exploited by Rudin
[
49
]
in relating the extension problem for partially defined positive semi-definite functions to a Fejér–Riesz type factorisation property
(see
[
52
,
51
,
7
]
for extensive treatments, and
[
5
, Theorem 7.1]
for an example of the explicit use of operator system duality which is similar to our
Theorem 4.22
).
We capture the sums-of-squares concept by introducing the
sums-of-squares operator system

\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)
and we show that the Toeplitz system
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
is its operator system dual.
We infer that the Toeplitz system
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
and the Fourier system
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Sigma\Sigma^{-1})}
are each other’s operator system duals if and only if the Fourier system is completely order isomorphic to the sums-of-squares system (see
Theorem 4.22
).

Note that if the group
\Gamma
is amenable, its group
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)\cong\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\mathrm{r}}(\Gamma)
is a coamenable compact quantum group in the sense of Woronowicz
[
56
]
(with comultiplication given by
\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\mathrm{r}}(\Gamma)\ni\lambda(s)\mapsto\lambda(s)\otimes\lambda(s)\in\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\mathrm{r}}(\Gamma)\otimes\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\mathrm{r}}(\Gamma)
), and the Toeplitz and Fourier systems are the operator systems arising respectively as Peter–Weyl
[
36
]
and Fourier truncations
[
47
]
.
In this way, we provide the connection between these two types of truncations.
As a byproduct we obtain that the operator system of Toeplitz matrices is not (min,max)-nuclear, thereby simplifying the corresponding argument in
[
20
]
(see also
[
21
]
).
While the non-nuclearity of the Toeplitz system is a special instance of a general characterisation of nuclear finite-dimensional operator systems
[
30
]
, it is in fact witnessed by the Toeplitz system itself
[
21
,
20
]
.

More generally, the problem of extending positive semi-definite (operator-valued) functions is usually phrased for
positivity domains
, i.e. subsets
\Delta
of a discrete group
\Gamma
such that
e\in\Delta
and
\Delta^{-1}=\Delta
.
An operator-valued function
u:\Delta\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
is called
positive semi-definite
if the Toeplitz matrix

(5)

\displaystyle(u(st^{-1}))_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{B}(H))\cong\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Sigma,H))

is positive semi-definite, for all finite subsets
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
such that
\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}\subseteq\Delta
.
A positivity domain
\Delta
is said to have the
complete extension property
if, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, every positive semi-definite function
u:\Delta\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{n}
admits an extension to a positive semi-definite function on
\Gamma
(see
[
39
]
).
We observe that positive semi-definite functions on a positivity domain span a matrix ordered vector space
\mathrm{B}(\Delta)
which we identify as the matrix ordered vector space dual of an operator system
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
.
The latter can be built up from sums-of-squares operator systems;
in fact, we show that the operator system
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
is a colimit of a suitable diagram of sums-of-squares operator systems,
Theorem 5.11
.

Similarly to the case of Toeplitz matrices and sums of squares, we relate the extension property for positive semi-definite functions on a positivity domain to the extension property for bounded functions on a positivity domain and a factorisation property,
Theorem 5.21
.
The colimit description of the operator system
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
provides new techniques to (dis-)prove factorisation, or, equivalently, extension properties;
for instance, we discuss the positivity domain

(6)

\displaystyle\Delta=\{(0,0),(0,\pm 1),(\pm 1,0)\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}

(
Figure 8
) and show that the operator system
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
is the coproduct of two copies of the sums-of-squares systems generated by the unitaries
\delta_{0},\delta_{1}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}]
.
This allows us to compute the minimal
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-cover of the operator system
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
which turns out to be
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
.
On the other hand, the minimal
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-cover of the Fourier system
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})_{(\Delta)}\subseteq\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
generated by the same elements as the operator system
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
is
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
;
we conclude that the operator systems
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})_{(\Delta)}
and
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
are not completely order isomorphic, so the positivity domain
\Delta
from (
6
) does not have the complete factorisation and the complete extension property.

2.
Operator system preliminaries

2.1.
Order unit spaces

Let
V
be a complex
∗
-vector space and
V_{\mathrm{h}}
be the real vector space of all its hermitian elements.
A
convex cone
in
V
is a non-empty subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq V_{\mathrm{h}}
such that
[0,\infty)\cdot\mathcal{C}+\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
.
A convex cone
\mathcal{C}
in
V
is called
spanning
if
\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}=V_{\mathrm{h}}
and
proper
if
\mathcal{C}\cap(-\mathcal{C})=\{0\}
.
A complex
∗
-vector space
V
, equipped with a proper spanning convex cone
\mathcal{C}
is called an
ordered vector space
.
We often write
V^{+}:=\mathcal{C}
and call it the
positive cone
of (the ordered vector space)
V
.
The positive cone
V^{+}
induces a partial order on
V
by defining
v\leq w
if
w-v\in V^{+}
.

Given a complex
∗
-vector space
V
with a proper convex cone
\mathcal{C}
, we say that an element
e\in\mathcal{C}
is an
order unit
if, for every hermitian element
v\in V_{\mathrm{h}}
, there exists a real number
r>0
such that
re-v\in\mathcal{C}
.
Note that if
e
is an order unit then, for every
v\in V_{\mathrm{h}}
, there exists a real number
r>0
such that

(7)

\displaystyle v=re-(re-v)\in\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C},

so the cone
\mathcal{C}
is spanning.
A
∗
-vector space
V
, equipped with a proper convex cone
V^{+}
and an order unit
e_{V}
is called an
order unit space
.

For an order unit space
(V,V^{+},e_{V})
, let

(8)

\displaystyle p_{V}(v):=\inf\{r>0\mid-re_{V}\leq v\leq re_{V}\},\ \ \ v\in V_{\mathrm{h}},

and note that
p_{V}
is a semi-norm on
V_{\mathrm{h}}
, referred to as the
order seminorm
.
We note that any extension of
p_{V}
to
V
induces the same topology on
V
, referred to as the
order topology

[
45
]
.

An order unit space
(V,V^{+},e_{V})
(as well as its order unit
e_{V}
) is called
Archimedean
if, for all
v\in V
, we have that

(9)

\displaystyle re_{V}+v\geq 0\mbox{ for all }r>0\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ v\geq 0.

If
V
is an Archimedean order unit space, the order seminorm
p_{V}
is a norm and the positive cone
V^{+}
is closed in
V
for the order topology
[
45
]
.

If
V
and
W
are ordered vector spaces, a linear map
\phi:V\rightarrow W
is called
self-adjoint
if
\phi(v^{*})=\phi(v)^{*}
,
v\in V
.
If, furthermore,
\phi(V^{+})\subseteq W^{+}
, the map
\phi
is called
positive
.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}(V,W)
the cone of all positive maps
V\to W
.
If
V
and
W
are order unit spaces,
\phi
is called
unital
if
\phi(e_{V})=e_{W}
.
In this case,
\phi(V^{+})\subseteq W^{+}
implies that
\phi
is self-adjoint.
If
V
is an order unit space, unital positive linear functionals on
V
are called
states
; the set
\mathcal{S}(V)
of all states is a convex set called the
state space
of
V
.

For ordered vector spaces
V
and
W
,
a positive map
\phi:V\rightarrow W
is called an
order isomorphism
if it is bijective and
\phi^{-1}:W\rightarrow V
is positive.
An injective positive map
\phi
is an
order embedding
if
\phi
is an order isomorphism onto its image.

For an ordered vector space
V
, let
(V^{\mathrm{d}})^{+}:=\mathcal{P}(V,\mathbb{C})
be the set of positive linear functionals
V\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and by
V^{\mathrm{d}}
its complex linear span.
Then
(V^{\mathrm{d}},(V^{\mathrm{d}})^{+})
is an ordered vector space;
the state space
S(V)
is a subset of
V^{\mathrm{d}}
which, equipped with the weak
∗
-topology, is a compact (convex) space by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem.

2.2.
Matrix order unit spaces and operator systems

For a vector space
V
, we let
\mathrm{M}_{m,n}(V)
be the vector space of all
m
by
n
matrices with entries in
V
, and write
\mathrm{M}_{n}(V)=\mathrm{M}_{n,n}(V)
.
If
V
is a
∗
-vector space then
\mathrm{M}_{n}(V)
is also a *-vector space when equipped with the involution
(x_{i,j})_{i,j}^{*}:=(x_{j,i}^{*})_{i,j}
.
A
matrix ordering
on a complex
∗
-vector space
V
is a family
(\mathcal{C}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of cones such that
(\mathrm{M}_{n}(V),\mathcal{C}_{n})
is an ordered vector space for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that is
compatible

in that

(10)

\displaystyle\alpha\mathcal{C}_{m}\alpha^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{n}\mbox{ for all }\alpha\in\mathrm{M}_{n,m}\mbox{ and all }m,n\in\mathbb{N}.

We usually write
\mathrm{M}_{n}(V)^{+}
in the place of
\mathcal{C}_{n}
.
Given a matrix ordered vector space
(V,(\mathrm{M}_{n}(V)^{+})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})
, an element
e\in V
is called a
matrix order unit
if the element

(11)

\displaystyle e_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}e&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&e\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(V)

is an order unit for the ordered vector space
(\mathrm{M}_{n}(V),\mathrm{M}_{n}(V)^{+})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
A
matrix order unit space
is a matrix ordered vector space, equipped with a matrix order unit.
A matrix order unit
e
is called
Archimedean
if
e_{n}
is Archimedean for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
An
operator system
is a matrix ordered vector space, equipped with a distinguished Archimedean matrix order unit.

For a linear map
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
between matrix ordered vector spaces
X
and
Y
, we define its matrix amplification
\phi^{(n)}:\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{n}(Y)
by letting

(12)

\displaystyle\phi^{(n)}((x_{ij})_{i,j}):=(\phi(x_{ij}))_{i,j},\ \ \ (x_{ij})_{i,j}\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X).

The map
\phi
is called
completely positive
if
\phi^{(n)}:\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{n}(Y)
is a positive map for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We denote the cone of all completely positive maps
X\rightarrow Y
by
\mathcal{CP}(X,Y)
.
We sometimes use the abbreviations “cp” and “ucp” for “completely positive” and “unital completely positive” respectively.
We denote the categories of matrix ordered vector spaces with cp maps by
\mathbf{MVS}
, of matrix order unit spaces with ucp maps by
\mathbf{MOU}
and of operator systems with ucp maps by
\mathbf{OSy}
.

Let
X
and
Y
be matrix ordered
∗
-vector spaces.
A completely positive map
\phi\in\mathcal{CP}(X,Y)
is called a
complete order isomorphism
if
\phi^{(n)}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(X),\mathrm{M}_{n}(Y))
is an order isomorphism for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We say that
X
and
Y
are
completely order isomorphic
if there is a complete order isomorphism
X\rightarrow Y
, and write
X\cong Y
.
A completely positive map
\phi
is a
complete order embedding
if
\phi
is a complete order isomorphism onto its image.

Every unital self-adjoint subspace
X\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
is an operator system with positive cones defined by letting

(13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)^{+}:=\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)\cap\mathcal{B}(H^{n})^{+},

and a matrix order unit defined by letting
e_{X}:=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(H)}
.
By the Choi–Effros theorem
[
12
]
, every operator system is unitally completely order isomorphic to such a unital self-adjoint subspace of
\mathcal{B}(H)
, for some complex Hilbert space
H
.
In particular, if
A
is a unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra then every self-adjoint subspace of
A
that contains the unit is an operator system in a canonical fashion.
If
X\subseteq A
is an operator system with
1_{A}
as its matrix order unit, then every positive linear functional
\phi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
can be extended to a positive linear functional on
A
by Krein’s theorem.
More generally, every cp map
\Phi:X\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
can be extended to a cp map
A\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
by Arveson’s extension theorem
[
1
]
.

For a matrix ordered vector space
X
with ordered vector space dual
X^{\mathrm{d}}
, set

(14)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{n}(X^{\mathrm{d}})^{+}:=\mathcal{CP}(X,\mathrm{M}_{n}),\ \ \ n\in\mathbb{N}.

If
X
is an operator system, by
[
43
, Theorem 6.1]
we have the identification

(15)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{n}(X^{\mathrm{d}})^{+}=\mathcal{CP}(X,\mathrm{M}_{n})\cong\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(X),\mathbb{C})=(\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)^{\mathrm{d}})^{+}.

If
X
is a finite dimensional operator system, by
[
12
, Corollary 4.5]
, one can (non-canonically) choose an Archimedean matrix order unit for the matrix ordered vector space
X^{\mathrm{d}}
, giving it the structure of an operator system.
For (matrix) ordered vector spaces
V,W
, the adjoint
\phi^{\mathrm{d}}:W^{\mathrm{d}}\rightarrow V^{\mathrm{d}}
of every (completely) positive map
\phi:V\rightarrow W
is (completely) positive.

2.3.
Archimedeanisation

Matrix order unit spaces can be turned into operator systems as follows
[
44
]
.
Let
X
be a matrix order unit space.
Set

(16)

\displaystyle N:=\bigcap_{\phi\in\mathcal{S}(X)}\ker(\phi).

Then the identication

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/N)\cong\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)/\mathrm{M}_{n}(N)

induces a matrix order unit space structure on the quotient
X/N
.
Define

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\mathrm{arch}}:=\{

\displaystyle(x_{ij})_{i,j}+\mathrm{M}_{n}(N)\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)/\mathrm{M}_{n}(N)\mid

(19)

\displaystyle r(e_{X})_{n}+(x_{ij})_{i,j}+\mathrm{M}_{n}(N)\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)^{+}+\mathrm{M}_{n}(N),\text{ for all }r>0\},

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We sometimes write

(20)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Arch}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)^{+}):=\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\mathrm{arch}}

if explicit reference to
X
is required.
Then the triple
(X/N,(\mathcal{C}_{n}^{\mathrm{arch}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},e_{X}+N)
is an operator system, called the
Archimedeanisation
of the matrix order unit space
X
;
we denote it by
\mathrm{Arch}(X)
.
Ucp maps from the matrix order unit space
X
to operator systems
Y
uniquely factor through the Archimedeanisation;
more precisely, the following universal property holds:
There is a unique surjective ucp map
q:X\rightarrow\mathrm{Arch}(X)
such that, for every ucp map
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
with
Y
an operator system, there exists a unique ucp map
\tilde{\phi}:\mathrm{Arch}(X)\rightarrow Y
such that
\tilde{\phi}\circ q=\phi
.

2.4.
Quotients

We describe the construction of quotients in
\mathbf{MVS}
, and refer to
[
32
, Section 3]
for further details.
To this end, let
X
be a matrix ordered vector space and let
J\subseteq X
be a
∗
-invariant subspace.
Assume moreover that
J
is a
complete order ideal
, i.e. if
x\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(J)
and
0\leq y\leq x
then
y\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(J)
.
Set

(21)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+}:=\{(x_{ij}+J)_{i,j}\mid\exists y\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(J)\text{ such that }x+y\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)^{+}\}.

One readily checks that
\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+}
is a convex spanning cone for the
∗
-vector space
\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)
and that

(\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is a compatible family of cones.
For properness, as in the proof of
[
45
, Proposition 2.41]
, any element of the intersection
\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+}\cap(-\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+})
can be written as
x+\mathrm{M}_{n}(J)=-y+\mathrm{M}_{n}(J)
with
x,y\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)^{+}
.
Then there is an element
j\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(J)
such that
j=x+y\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)^{+}
;
hence
0\leq x\leq j
which implies that
x\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(J)
.
So
x+\mathrm{M}_{n}(J)=0+\mathrm{M}_{n}(J)
, i.e. the intersection
\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+}\cap(-\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+})
is trivial.
This shows that
(X/J,(\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})
is a matrix ordered vector space again, and we say it is a
quotient
of
X
.

Note that the canonical quotient map
q:X\rightarrow X/J
is cp.
If
X
and
Y
are matrix ordered vector spaces and
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
is a cp map, then
\ker(\phi)
is clearly
∗
-invariant; moreover, it is a complete order-ideal, since
x\in\ker(\phi^{(n)})=M_{n}(\ker(\phi))
and
0\leq y\leq x
imply

(22)

\displaystyle 0\leq\phi^{(n)}(y)\leq\phi^{(n)}(x)=0.

We say that the cp map
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-quotient map
if it is surjective and the induced map
\tilde{\phi}:V/\ker(\phi)\rightarrow Y
is a complete order isomorphism.

Moving to the category
\mathbf{OSy}

[
32
]
, let
X
be an operator system.
A subspace
J\subseteq X
is called a
kernel
if there exist an operator system
Y
and a ucp map
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
such that
J=\ker(\phi)

(equivalently,
J
is a kernel if and only if there is a family of states
\{\phi_{\iota}\}_{\iota\in I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}(X)
such that
J=\bigcap_{\iota\in I}\ker(\phi_{\iota})
).
Defining
\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+}
as in (
21
) and
e_{X/J}:=e_{X}+J
, the quotient
X/J
inherits the structure of a matrix order unit space from
X
.
The operator system
\mathrm{Arch}(X/J)
is referred to as an
operator system quotient
;
it possesses the universal property that every ucp map from
X
to an operator system factors uniquely and canonically through it.
A kernel
J\subseteq X
is called
completely proximinal
if

(23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+}=\mathrm{Arch}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(X/J)^{+}),\ \ \ n\in\mathbb{N};

in particular, in this case
X/J=\mathrm{Arch}(X/J)
.

2.5.
Tensor products

We recall some elements of the theory of tensor products for operator systems from
[
31
]
.
Let
X
and
Y
be operator systems and denote by
X\otimes Y
the corresponding algebraic tensor product.
An
operator system structure
on
X\otimes Y
is a family
\tau:=(\mathcal{C}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
which satisfies the following properties:

(T1)

The triple
(X\otimes Y,(\mathcal{C}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},e_{X}\otimes e_{Y})
is an operator system, denoted
X\otimes_{\tau}Y
;

(T2)

We have
\mathrm{M}_{k}(X)^{+}\otimes\mathrm{M}_{\ell}(Y)^{+}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{k\ell}
, for all
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
;

(T3)

For all ucp maps
\phi:X\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{k}
,
\psi:Y\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{\ell}
, the map
\phi\otimes\psi:X\otimes_{\tau}Y\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{k\ell}
is ucp.

An operator system structure
\tau
is
functorial
if it satisfies:

(T4)

For all operator systems
S,T
and ucp maps
\phi:X\rightarrow S
,
\psi:Y\rightarrow T
, the map
\phi\otimes\psi
is a ucp map
X\otimes_{\tau}Y\rightarrow S\otimes_{\tau}T
.

The class of compatible families of cones over
X\otimes Y
is non-empty;
indeed, one such family is formed by the cones

(24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\mathrm{max}}(X,Y):=\{A(x\otimes y)A^{*}\mid x\in\mathrm{M}_{k}(X)^{+},y\in\mathrm{M}_{\ell}(Y)^{+},A\in\mathrm{M}_{n,k\ell}\},\ \ \ n\in\mathbb{N}.

Its Archimedeanisation defines a functorial operator system structure on
X\otimes Y
, called the
maximal tensor product
and denoted by
\mathrm{max}
.
It is maximal in the sense that, for every compatible family
(\mathcal{C}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive cones over
X\otimes Y
, we have

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\mathrm{max}}(X,Y)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{n},\ \ \ n\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular, if
\tau
is any operator system structure on
X\otimes Y
then the canonical identity map
X\otimes_{\max}Y\to X\otimes_{\tau}Y
is completely positive.

Assume that
X
and
Y
are only matrix ordered vector spaces (and not necessarily operator systems).
The cones
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\mathrm{max}}(X,Y)
can still be defined in the same way.
Recall, further, that a bilinear map
\phi:X\times Y\to Z
(where
Z
is another matrix ordered vector space) is called
jointly completely positive
if

(26)

\displaystyle(\phi(x_{i,j},y_{p,q}))_{i,j,p,q}\in\mathrm{M}_{nm}(Z)^{+}

whenever
(x_{i,j})_{i,j}\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)^{+}
and
(y_{k,l})_{k,l}\in\mathrm{M}_{m}(Y)^{+}
.
It follows from the arguments in
[
31
]
that a bilinear map
\phi:X\times Y\to Z
is jointly completely positive precisely when its linearisation
\tilde{\phi}:X\otimes Y\to Z
satisfies the properties

(27)

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}^{(n)}(\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\mathrm{max}}(X,Y))\subseteq\mathrm{M}_{n}(Z)^{+},\ \ \ n\in\mathbb{N}.

The same universal property, in the case where
X
,
Y
and
Z

are operator systems, characterises the operator system
tensor product
X\otimes_{\max}Y
.

The
minimal tensor product
of two operator systems
X
and
Y
, which are by the Choi–Effros theorem unitally completely order embedded in some C
∗
-algebras
A
and
B
respectively, is the operator system structure inherited from the inclusion of
X\otimes Y
in the spatial tensor product
A\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}B
.
This tensor product is independent of the choice of inclusions
X\subseteq A
and
Y\subseteq B
.
Moreover it is a functorial tensor product and it is minimal in the sense that, for every compatible family
(\mathcal{C}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive cones over
X\otimes Y
, we have that

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{M}_{n}(X\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}Y)^{+},\ \ \ n\in\mathbb{N}.

If
X,Y
are finite dimensional operator systems, we have
canonical complete order isomorphisms

(29)

\displaystyle(X\otimes_{\mathrm{max}}Y)^{\mathrm{d}}\cong X^{\mathrm{d}}\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}Y^{\mathrm{d}}\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ (X\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}Y)^{\mathrm{d}}\cong X^{\mathrm{d}}\otimes_{\mathrm{max}}Y^{\mathrm{d}}

(see
[
31
]
and the references therein).

For two operator systems
X,Y
and two operator system structures
\sigma,\tau
on
X\otimes Y
, we say that the pair
(X,Y)
is
(\sigma,\tau)
-nuclear
if
X\otimes_{\sigma}Y\cong X\otimes_{\tau}Y
canonically.
We say that an operator system
X
is
(\sigma,\tau)
-nuclear if the pair
(X,Y)
is
(\sigma,\tau)
-nuclear, for every operator system
Y
.

2.9.
Operator spaces

We now recall some basic operator space notions, and refer the reader to
[
43
,
46
,
9
,
18
]
for further details.
An
operator space
is a complex vector space
X
equipped with a family of matrix norms
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{n}
on
\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which is compatible in the sense that:

(1)

\lVert AxB\rVert_{m}\leq\lVert A\rVert\lVert x\rVert_{n}\lVert B\rVert
, for all elements
x\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)
, and scalar matrices
A\in\mathrm{M}_{m,n}

and
B\in\mathrm{M}_{n,m}
;

(2)

\lVert x\oplus y\rVert_{m+n}=\max\{\lVert x\rVert_{n},\lVert y\rVert_{m}\}
, for all
x\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X),y\in\mathrm{M}_{m}(X)
.

A linear map
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
between operator spaces is called completely bounded (cb, for short) if

(37)

\displaystyle\lVert\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{\lVert x\rVert_{n}\leq 1}\lVert\phi^{(n)}(x)\rVert_{n}<\infty;

in this case,
\lVert\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}
is referred to as the
cb-norm
of
\phi
.
We denote the set of all cb maps
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
by
\mathcal{CB}(X,Y)
.
A cb map
\phi\in\mathcal{CB}(X,Y)
is called completely contractive (cc, for short) if
\lVert\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}\leq 1
and completely isometric if
\lVert\phi^{(n)}(x)\rVert_{n}=\lVert x\rVert_{n}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)
.
We denote the category of operator spaces, with cc maps as morphisms, by
\mathbf{OSp}
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
be a closed subspace.
Then
X
is an operator space by isometrically identifying the space of matrices
\mathrm{M}_{n}(X)
as a subspace of
\mathcal{B}(H^{n})
.
Every cb map
\phi:X\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(K)
extends to a cb map
\tilde{\phi}:\mathcal{B}(H)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(K)
with
\lVert\tilde{\phi}\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}=\lVert\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}
by
Wittstock’s extension theorem

[
55
]
.

By
Ruan’s theorem

[
48
]
, every operator space is completely isometrically isomorphic to a subspace of
\mathcal{B}(H)
, for some Hilbert space
H
, implying that
every operator system is canonically (unitally completely order isomorphic) to an operator space.
For a cp map
\phi
we have
\phi(\mathbf{1})=\lVert\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}
.
A unital linear map is cp if and only if it is cc, and it is a complete order embedding if and only if it is completely isometric.
By Wittstock’s decomposition theorem
[
54
]
, see also
[
43
, Theorem 8.5]
, every cb map on an operator system
with values in
\mathcal{B}(H)
, for some Hilbert space
H
,
is a linear combination of four cp maps.
Given an operator space
X
, its dual
X^{\mathrm{d}}
is an operator space when equipped with the matrix norms inherited by declaring the identification
\mathrm{M}_{n}(X^{\mathrm{d}})\cong\mathcal{CB}(X,\mathrm{M}_{n})
completely isometric
[
9
, 1.2.20]
.
Moreover, the matrix ordered vector space and operator space structures on
X^{\mathrm{d}}
are compatible in the sense that the proper convex cones
\mathcal{CP}(X,\mathrm{M}_{n})
span
\mathcal{CB}(X,\mathrm{M}_{n})
and are closed (for the cb-norm);
this shows that
X^{\mathrm{d}}
is a
(positively generated) matrix ordered operator space
; see
[
29
]
for further details.

Recall from
[
9
, 1.2.14]
that if
X
is an operator space and
Y\subseteq X
a closed subspace the quotient
X/Y
is an operator space, for the matrix-norms given by

(38)

\displaystyle\lVert(x_{ij}+Y)_{i,j}\rVert_{n}:=\inf\{\lVert x+y\rVert_{n}\mid y\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(Y)\}.

For operator spaces
X
and
Z
, a cc map
\phi:X\rightarrow Z
is called an
\mathbf{OSp}
-quotient map if it is onto and the induced map
\tilde{\phi}:X/\ker(\phi)\rightarrow Z
is a complete isometry (i.e. the inverse map
\tilde{\phi}^{-1}:Z\rightarrow X/\ker(\phi)
is cc).

A map
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
between operator spaces is cb if and only if
\phi\otimes\mathbf{I}^{Z}:X\otimes Z\rightarrow Y\otimes Z
is cb, for every operator space
Z

[
15
]
, see also
[
46
, Proposition 2.1.1]
.
It follows that a map
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
between operator systems is cp if and only if
\phi\otimes\mathbf{I}^{Z}:X\otimes Z\rightarrow Y\otimes Z
is cp, for every operator system
Z
.

2.10.
Complete order embeddings and quotient maps

The following proposition is central to
Theorem 4.22
and
Theorem 5.21
below.
We give a full proof of the equivalence of (1) and (3) for which we could not find a reference in the infinite dimensional case.
Note that this is in line with
[
23
, Proposition 1.8]
which implies the equivalence of (1) and (3) in the case that the operator systems
X
and
Y
are finite-dimensional.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be operator systems and let
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
be a ucp map.
The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\phi:X\rightarrow Y
is a complete order embedding;

(ii)

\phi:X\rightarrow Y
is a complete isometry;

(iii)

\phi^{\mathrm{d}}:Y^{\mathrm{d}}\rightarrow X^{\mathrm{d}}
is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-quotient map;

(iv)

\phi^{\mathrm{d}}:Y^{\mathrm{d}}\rightarrow X^{\mathrm{d}}
is an
\mathbf{OSp}
-quotient map.

Proof.

The equivalence [(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)] is well-known, see e.g.
[
9
, 1.3.3]
.
For the equivalence [(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv)] see
[
9
, 1.4.3]
;
we note that the implication [(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iv)] requires (the matrix-valued version of) Wittstock’s extension theorem to extend cb maps
X\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{n}
to cb maps
Y\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{n}
(if
X
is completely isometrically identified with its image
\phi(X)\subseteq Y
).
The converse implication uses the completely isometric inclusion of an operator space in its bidual.

[(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii)].
Assume that
\phi:X\rightarrow Y
is a complete order embedding.
In particular,
\phi
is one-to-one, so
\phi^{\mathrm{d}}
is cp and surjective.
Denote by

(39)

\displaystyle q:Y^{\mathrm{d}}\rightarrow Y^{\mathrm{d}}/\ker(\phi^{\mathrm{d}})

the canonical cp quotient map and by

(40)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}}:Y^{\mathrm{d}}/\ker(\phi^{\mathrm{d}})\rightarrow X^{\mathrm{d}}

the induced cp map with the property
\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}}\circ q=\phi^{\mathrm{d}}
.
In order to prove statement (iii), we need to show that the inverse
(\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1}
is cp.
To this end, let
x^{\mathrm{d}}\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(X^{\mathrm{d}})^{+}
.
We view
x^{\mathrm{d}}
as a cp map (denoted in the same way)
x^{\mathrm{d}}:X\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{n}
.
By assumption,
\phi^{-1}:\phi(X)\rightarrow X
is a well-defined ucp map; hence

(41)

\displaystyle x^{\mathrm{d}}\circ\phi^{-1}\in\mathcal{CP}(\phi(X),\mathrm{M}_{n}).

By Arveson’s extension theorem there exists a cp map
y^{\mathrm{d}}\in\mathcal{CP}(Y,\mathrm{M}_{n})
that extends
x^{\mathrm{d}}\circ\phi^{-1}
, i.e.

(42)

\displaystyle y^{\mathrm{d}}(\phi(x))=x^{\mathrm{d}}\circ\phi^{-1}(\phi(x))=x^{\mathrm{d}}(x),\ \ \ x\in X.

Viewing
x^{\mathrm{d}}
and
y^{\mathrm{d}}
as elements of
\mathrm{M}_{n}(X^{\mathrm{d}})^{+}
and
\mathrm{M}_{n}(Y^{\mathrm{d}})^{+}
, respectively, equation (
42
) reads
(\phi^{\mathrm{d}})^{(n)}(y^{\mathrm{d}})=x^{\mathrm{d}}
.
It follows that

(43)

\displaystyle((\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1})^{(n)}(x^{\mathrm{d}})

\displaystyle=((\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1})^{(n)}\left((\phi^{\mathrm{d}})^{(n)}(y^{\mathrm{d}})\right)

(44)

\displaystyle=((\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1})^{(n)}\circ(\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{(n)}\circ q^{(n)}(y^{\mathrm{d}})

(45)

\displaystyle=q^{(n)}(y^{\mathrm{d}})\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(Y^{\mathrm{d}}/\ker(\phi^{\mathrm{d}}))^{+}.

This shows the map
(\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1}
is cp, as claimed.

[(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i)].
Assume that
\phi^{\mathrm{d}}:Y^{\mathrm{d}}\rightarrow X^{\mathrm{d}}
is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-quotient map.
In particular,
\phi^{\mathrm{d}}
is surjective, so
\phi
is one-to-one with an inverse map
\phi^{-1}:\phi(X)\rightarrow X
.
Identifying the dual
\phi(X)^{\mathrm{d}}
of the image
\phi(X)\subseteq Y
with the quotient
Y^{\mathrm{d}}/\phi(X)^{\perp}\cong Y^{\mathrm{d}}/\ker(\phi^{\mathrm{d}})
we may view the dual map
(\phi^{-1})^{\mathrm{d}}:X^{\mathrm{d}}\rightarrow\phi(X)^{\mathrm{d}}
of the inverse of
\phi
as the inverse of the dual map of
\phi
(modulo
\phi(X)^{\perp}
), i.e.

(46)

\displaystyle(\phi^{-1})^{\mathrm{d}}=(\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1}:X^{\mathrm{d}}\rightarrow Y^{\mathrm{d}}/\ker(\phi^{\mathrm{d}}).

Note that the map
(\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1}
is cp by the assumption that
\phi^{*}
is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-quotient map.
Hence the dual map

(47)

\displaystyle((\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1})^{\mathrm{d}}:(Y^{\mathrm{d}}/\ker(\phi^{\mathrm{d}}))^{\mathrm{d}}\cong\phi(X)^{\mathrm{dd}}\rightarrow X^{\mathrm{dd}}

is cp.
Recall from
[
32
, Proposition 6.2]
that an operator system is canonically unitally completely order embedded into its bidual.
We thus consider the restriction of
((\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1})^{\mathrm{d}}
to the operator subsystem
\phi(X)\subseteq\phi(X)^{\mathrm{dd}}
;
we claim that

(48)

\displaystyle((\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1})^{\mathrm{d}}|_{\phi(X)}=\phi^{-1}.

Indeed, for
x\in X
, viewing
\phi(x)
as an element of
\phi(X)^{\mathrm{dd}}
, we have

(49)

\displaystyle((\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1})^{\mathrm{d}}((\phi(x)))=\phi(x)\circ(\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1}=\phi(x)\circ(\phi^{-1})^{\mathrm{d}}=\phi^{-1}\circ\phi(x)=x.

It follows that the map

(50)

\displaystyle\phi^{-1}=((\widetilde{\phi^{\mathrm{d}}})^{-1})^{\mathrm{d}}|_{\phi(X)}:\phi(X)\rightarrow X

is cp, and hence
\phi
is a complete order embedding as claimed.
∎

.

3.
Positive semi-definite functions and the Fourier operator system

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
H
a Hilbert space.
If
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
is a finite subset and
X\subseteq\mathcal{B}(H)
is an operator space, we denote by
\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}(X)
the set of matrices over
X
indexed by
\Sigma\times\Sigma
, identified with the space
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Sigma))\otimes_{\min}X\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Sigma)\otimes H)
.
We set
\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}:=\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}(\mathbb{C})
.
We denote by
\delta_{s}
,
s\in\Gamma
, the unitary generators of the universal group C
∗
-algebra
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
.

The set
{\rm F}(\Gamma,\mathcal{B}(H))
of all
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued functions
u:\Gamma\to\mathcal{B}(H)

is a
∗
-vector space under the operation
u^{*}(s):=u(s^{-1})^{*}
,
s\in\Gamma
.
We recall that a function
u:\Gamma\to\mathcal{B}(H)
is called
positive semi-definite
if

(51)

\displaystyle(u(st^{-1}))_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{B}(H))^{+},\text{ for all finite subsets }\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma.

Observe that for the finite set
\{e,s\}
with
s\in\Gamma
, positive semi-definiteness implies that

(52)

\displaystyle u(s)^{*}=u(s^{-1})\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ u(s)^{*}u(s)\leq\lVert u(e)\rVert u(e);

therefore
u(e)
is selfadjoint and
H^{\prime}:=\overline{u(e)H}
is reducing for
u
with

(53)

\displaystyle u(s)=\left[\begin{matrix}u(s)|_{H^{\prime}}&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right],\text{ for all }s\in\Gamma.

We denote by
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}
the cone of all positive semi-definite
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued functions, and by
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)
the linear span of
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}
inside
{\rm F}(\Gamma,\mathcal{B}(H))
.
We call
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)
the
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued Fourier–Stieltjes algebra
of
\Gamma
.
Clearly, if
H=\mathbb{C}
we obtain the usual
Fourier–Stieltjes algebra
, denoted by
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)
.

The following proposition is part of folklore; we include its proof for the convenience of the reader.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group.

(i)

If
u\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}
, then there exists a (necessarily) unique completely positive map
\phi_{u}:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)\to\mathcal{B}(H)
such that
\phi_{u}(\delta_{s})=u(s)
, for all
s\in\Gamma
.

(ii)

If
\phi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)\to\mathcal{B}(H)
is a completely positive map, then there exists a (necessarily) unique positive semi-definite function
u_{\phi}\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}
such that
\phi=\phi_{u_{\phi}}
.

Proof.

(i)
Since
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
is densely spanned by
\{\delta_{s}\}_{s\in\Gamma}
, any completely positive extension
\phi_{u}
of
u
is necessarily unique.
Hence we need to show only existence of the completely positive extension map
\phi_{u}
.
We proceed in cases.

Case 1
. Suppose first that
u(e)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(H)}
.
By
[
53
, Theorem 7.1]
, there exists a Hilbert space
K\supseteq H
and a *-representation
\pi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)\to\mathcal{B}(K)
such that

(54)

\displaystyle u(s)=P_{H}\pi(\delta_{s})|_{H},\text{ for all }s\in\Gamma.

Then the completely positive map
\phi_{u}:=P_{H}\pi(\cdot)|_{H}
is the required extension.

Case 2.
Suppose that
u(e)
is an invertible element of
\mathcal{B}(H)
.
Let
u^{\prime}\in{\rm F}(\Gamma,\mathcal{B}(H))
be the
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued function given by

(55)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(s):=u(e)^{-1/2}u(s)u(e)^{-1/2},\text{ for all }s\in\Gamma.

Then for all finite subsets
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
, setting
n:=\lvert\Sigma\rvert
, we have

(56)

\displaystyle(u^{\prime}(st^{-1}))_{s,t\in\Sigma}=(u(e)^{-1/2}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{n})(u(st^{-1}))_{s,t\in\Sigma}(u(e)^{-1/2}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{n}).

Since
u(e)
is a positive operator we get that
u^{\prime}\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}
with
u^{\prime}(e)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(H)}
.
By Case 1 we obtain a unital completely positive map
\phi_{u^{\prime}}
such that

(57)

\displaystyle\phi_{u^{\prime}}(\delta_{s})=u^{\prime}(s),\text{ for all }s\in\Gamma.

Therefore the map

(58)

\displaystyle\phi_{u}(\cdot):=u(e)^{1/2}\phi_{u^{\prime}}(\cdot)u(e)^{1/2}

is completely positive and satisfies

(59)

\displaystyle\phi_{u}(\delta_{s})=u(e)^{1/2}u^{\prime}(s)u(e)^{1/2}=u(s),

for all
s\in\Gamma
.

Case 3.
Suppose that
H
is finite dimensional and that
u(e)\neq\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(H)}
.
Set
H^{\prime}:=\overline{u(e)H}=u(e)H
and observe that
u(e)|_{H^{\prime}}
is positive and invertible.
Moreover
H^{\prime}
is reducing for every
u(s)
with

(60)

\displaystyle u(s)=\begin{bmatrix}u(s)|_{H^{\prime}}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\text{ for all }s\in\Gamma.

Therefore the
\mathcal{B}(H^{\prime})
-valued function
u^{\prime}
given by

(61)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(s):=u(s)|_{H^{\prime}},\text{ for all }s\in\Gamma,

is positive semi-definite with
u^{\prime}(e)
invertible in
\mathcal{B}(H^{\prime})
.
By Case 2, we obtain a completely positive map
\phi_{u^{\prime}}:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)\to\mathcal{B}(H^{\prime})
such that
\phi_{u^{\prime}}(\delta_{s})=u^{\prime}(s)
for all
s\in\Gamma
.
Set

(62)

\displaystyle\phi_{u}:=\phi_{u^{\prime}}\oplus 0_{H\ominus H^{\prime}},

and we get

(63)

\displaystyle\phi_{u}(\delta_{s})=u^{\prime}(s)\oplus 0_{H\ominus H^{\prime}}=u(s),

for all
s\in\Gamma
.

Case 4.
Suppose that
H
is an arbitrary Hilbert space and that
u(e)\neq\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}(H)}
.
Let

(64)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(H):=\{F\leq H\mid\dim F<\infty\}.

For every
F\in\mathcal{F}(H)
define the
\mathcal{B}(F)
-valued function

(65)

\displaystyle u_{F}:\Gamma\to\mathcal{B}(F);\quad u_{F}(s)=P_{F}u(s)|_{F}.

By Case 3, there exists a completely positive map

(66)

\displaystyle\phi_{u_{F}}:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)\to\mathcal{B}(F);\quad\phi_{u_{F}}(\delta_{s})=u_{F}(s).

Since
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
is an operator system we have

(67)

\displaystyle\|\phi_{u_{F}}\|_{\rm cb}=\|\phi_{u_{F}}(\delta_{e})\|=\|u_{F}(e)\|\leq\|u(e)\|,

for all
F\in\mathcal{F}(H)
.
We make the identification of
F
as the subspace of
H
so that every
\phi_{u_{F}}
is
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued.
The set

(68)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}:=\{\phi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)\to\mathcal{B}(H)\mid\text{ $\phi$ is completely positive and }\|\phi\|_{\rm cb}\leq\|u(e)\|\}

is compact with respect to Arveson’s BW topology (see
[
1
]
), that is, the topology of point-weak* convergence; see
[
43
, Theorem 7.4]
.
Therefore there is a subnet which converges to some
\phi\in\mathfrak{C}
.
Then we can follow the proof of
[
43
, Theorem 7.5]
to conclude that
\phi
is the requried extension.
That is, for
s\in\Gamma
and
x,y\in H
we let
F
be the finite dimensional space spanned by
x,y\in H
.
Then for every
F^{\prime}\supseteq F
we get

(69)

\displaystyle u_{F}(s)=P_{F}u(s)|_{F}=P_{F}u_{F^{\prime}}(s)|_{F},

and so

(70)

\displaystyle\langle u(s)x,y\rangle=\langle u_{F}(s)x,y\rangle=\langle u_{F^{\prime}}(s)x,y\rangle=\langle\phi_{u_{F^{\prime}}}(\delta_{s})x,y\rangle.

Since the set
\{F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}:F^{\prime}\supseteq F\}
is cofinal, we get

(71)

\displaystyle\langle u(s)x,y\rangle=\langle\phi(\delta_{s})x,y\rangle,

and thus
\phi_{u}
is the required extension of
u
.

(ii)
Let
u_{\phi}:\Gamma\to\mathcal{B}(H)
be given by
u_{\phi}(s):=\phi(\delta_{s})
.
Given
s_{i}\in\Gamma
,
i=1,\dots,k
, we have that
(\delta_{s_{i}s_{j}^{-1}})_{i,j}\in\mathrm{M}_{k}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma))^{+}
;
thus,
(u_{\phi}(s_{i}s_{j}^{-1}))_{i,j}\in\mathrm{M}_{k}(\mathcal{B}(H))^{+}
, that is,
u_{\phi}
is positive semi-definite.
Since
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
is densely spanned by
\{\delta_{s}\}_{s\in\Gamma}
and the extension
\phi_{u_{\phi}}
of
u_{\phi}
is unique by (i), we get
\phi_{u_{\phi}}=\phi
.
∎

We note that
the uniqueness clauses in Proposition
3.1

imply that the assignments

(72)

\displaystyle u\mapsto\phi_{u}\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \phi\mapsto u_{\phi}

are inverse to each other.

3.1
gives a canonical identification of the cones

(73)

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}\cong\mathcal{CP}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma),\mathcal{B}(H))

implying that the family of cones
(\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n})^{+})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is compatible;
in particular
(\mathrm{B}(\Gamma),(\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n})^{+})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})
becomes a matrix ordered vector space through this identification.

We define a norm on
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)
by

(74)

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)}:=\lVert\phi_{u}\rVert_{\rm cb}.

In the case that
H=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, this can be viewed as a norm on
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{B}(\Gamma))
and in fact the Fourier–Stieltjes algebra with the matrix norms

is the operator space dual of
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
, see
[

, Subsection 1.2.20]
.
Together with the above established matrix order structure this turns

into a dual matrix ordered operator space, as studied e.g. in
[

]
.

Definition 3.2
.

[

A non-empty subset
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
is called a

if it is symmetric, i.e.

, and unital, i.e.

.

For a subset

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}[\Delta]:=\mathrm{span}\{\delta_{s}\mid s\in\Delta\}.

If
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
is a positivity domain, the vector space
\mathrm{S}[\Delta]
will be equipped with the involution, given by
\delta_{s}^{*}:=\delta_{s^{-1}}
,

.

Example 3.3
.

If
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
is any subset, then the subset
\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}:=\{st^{-1}\mid s,t\in\Sigma\}
of

We note that not all positivity domains
have the latter form; see

.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
be a positivity domain.
The
Fourier system

\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Delta)}
associated with

∗

By definition, the positive cones of the Fourier system

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Delta)})^{+}=\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma))^{+}\cap\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{S}[\Delta]),

and the matrix order unit

of
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}|_{\Delta}:=\{u|_{\Delta}\mid u\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}\};

(79)

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)|_{\Delta}:=\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C})|_{\Delta}.

We provide a generalisation of

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group,
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
be a positivity domain and

be a Hilbert space.

(i)

If
u\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}|_{\Delta}
then there exists a (necessarily) unique completely positive map

such that
\phi_{u}(\delta_{s})=u(s)
, for all
s\in\Delta
.

(ii)

If
\phi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Delta)}\to\mathcal{B}(H)
is a completely positive map then there exists a (necessarily) unique function
u_{\phi}\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}|_{\Delta}
such that
\phi=\phi_{u_{\phi}}
.

Proof.

(i)
Let
u\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}|_{\Delta}
and let
\tilde{u}\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}
be such that
\tilde{u}|_{\Delta}=u
.
By
3.1
(i), there exists a unique completely positive map
\phi_{\tilde{u}}:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)\to\mathcal{B}(H)
such that

.
Restricting
\phi_{\tilde{u}}
to the Fourier system

yields the desired completely positive map

.
If
\tilde{u}_{1},\tilde{u}_{2}\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}
are extensions of

\phi_{\tilde{u}_{1}}(\delta_{s})=\phi_{\tilde{u}_{2}}(\delta_{s})

s\in\Delta
, which implies uniqueness of
\phi_{u}
.

(ii) Let
\phi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Delta)}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
be a completely positive map.
By Arveson’s extension theorem, we may extend
\phi
to a completely positive map
\tilde{\phi}:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)\to\mathcal{B}(H)
.
Using
3.1
(ii) we obtain a positive semi-definite function
u_{\tilde{\phi}}:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
, such that
u_{\tilde{\phi}}(s)=\tilde{\phi}(\delta_{s})
, for all
s\in\Gamma
.
Restricting
u_{\tilde{\phi}}
to
\Delta
gives the desired function
u_{\phi}:\Delta\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
.
If
\tilde{\phi}_{1},\tilde{\phi}_{2}\in\mathcal{CP}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma),\mathcal{B}(H))
are extensions of
\phi
, then
u_{\tilde{\phi}_{1}}(s)=u_{\tilde{\phi}_{2}}(s)
, for all
s\in\Delta
, which implies uniqueness of
u_{\phi}
.
∎

Similarly as in (
73
),
3.5
gives a canonical identification of the cones

(80)

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}|_{\Delta}\cong\mathcal{CP}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Delta)},\mathcal{B}(H)),

implying the following corollary.

Corollary 3.6
.

The pair
(\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)|_{\Delta},(\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n})^{+}|_{\Delta})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})
is a matrix ordered vector space.

We note that, further, the identification

(81)

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)|_{\Delta}\cong\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{CP}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Delta)},\mathcal{B}(H)))=\mathcal{CB}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Delta)},\mathcal{B}(H))

turns
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)|_{\Delta}
into a (matrix ordered) operator space.

4.
Sums of squares and Toeplitz matrices

In this section we examine Toeplitz matrices associated to finite subsets
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
and provide an explicit identification of the dual of the corresponding Toeplitz operator system, leading to an operator system characterisation of a positive extension property.
Throughout,
\Gamma
will be a discrete group and
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
a finite subset.
We begin by defining an operator system spanned by the unitaries
\delta_{st^{-1}}
in
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
, for
s,t\in\Sigma
, but whose positive matrix cones consist of those elements which admit a decomposition into sums of squares, rather than the (possibly different) positive matrix cones inherited from
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
.
The resulting operator system will be denoted by
\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)
and one may think of it as the operator system structure on the *-vector space generated by
\{\delta_{st^{-1}}\mid s,t\in\Sigma\}
, for which a Fejér–Riesz type property is enforced.

4.1.
The sums of squares operator system and the Fejér–Riesz property

The following sets will constitute the positive matrix cones for our operator system
\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)
.
Recall our notation

(82)

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}[\Sigma]:=\mathrm{span}\{\delta_{s}\mid s\in\Sigma\}\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}]:=\mathrm{span}\{\delta_{st^{-1}}\mid s,t\in\Sigma\},

and the fact that
\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}]
is a *-vector subspace of
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite subset.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, set

(83)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}y_{i}^{*}\mid r\in\mathbb{N},y_{i}=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}A_{s}^{i}\otimes\delta_{s},A_{s}^{i}\in\mathrm{M}_{n}\right\}.

By definition we have
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}])_{\mathrm{h}}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite subset.
The following identities hold:

(84)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\left\{yy^{*}\mid y=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}A_{s}\otimes\delta_{s},A_{s}\in\mathrm{M}_{n,k},k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

(85)

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{s,t\in\Sigma}A_{s,t}\otimes\delta_{st^{-1}}\mid(A_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in(\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{M}_{n}))^{+}\right\}.

Proof.

Let
y_{i}=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}A_{s}^{i}\otimes\delta_{s}\in\mathrm{M}_{n}\otimes\mathrm{S}[\Sigma]
,
i=1,\dots,r
;
then
\sum_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}y_{i}^{*}=yy^{*}
, where

(86)

\displaystyle y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{r})\in\mathrm{M}_{1,r}(\mathrm{M}_{n}\otimes\mathrm{S}[\Sigma])\cong\mathrm{M}_{n,nr}\otimes\mathrm{S}[\Sigma],

showing that
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
is contained in the right hand side of (
84
).

To show the inclusion of the right-hand side of (
84
) in the set displayed in (
85
), let
yy^{*}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
with
y=\sum_{s\in\Sigma}A_{s}\otimes\delta_{s}
, for
A_{s}\in\mathrm{M}_{n,k}
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
, as in (
84
).
Then

(87)

\displaystyle yy^{*}=\sum_{s,t\in\Sigma}A_{s}A_{t}^{*}\otimes\delta_{st^{-1}}\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ (A_{s}A_{t}^{*})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in(\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{M}_{n}))^{+},

as required.

To show that the set displayed in (
85
) is contained in
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
, consider an element of the form
x=\sum_{s,t\in\Sigma}A_{s,t}\otimes\delta_{st^{-1}}
with
A:=(A_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in(\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{M}_{n}))^{+}
.
Then there is a matrix
B=(B_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
such that

(88)

\displaystyle A=BB^{*}=\left(\sum_{r\in\Sigma}B_{s,r}(B^{*})_{r,t}\right)_{s,t\in\Sigma}.

Note that
(B^{*})_{r,t}=B_{t,r}^{*}\in\mathrm{M}_{n}
, for all
r,t\in\Sigma
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\begin{split}x&=\sum_{s,t\in\Sigma}A_{s,t}\otimes\delta_{st^{-1}}=\sum_{s,t\in\Sigma}\sum_{r\in\Sigma}B_{s,r}(B^{*})_{r,t}\otimes\delta_{st^{-1}}\\
&=\sum_{r\in\Sigma}\left(\sum_{s\in\Sigma}B_{s,r}\otimes\delta_{s}\right)\left(\sum_{t\in\Sigma}B_{t,r}\otimes\delta_{t}\right)^{*}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma).\end{split}

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite subset.
The following statements hold:

(i)

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma))^{+}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(ii)

(\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a compatible family of proper convex cones on the *-vector space
\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}]
.

(iii)

If
(\mathcal{C}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a compatible family of convex cones on the *-vector space
\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}]
such that

(89)

\left\{(\delta_{s_{i}s_{j}^{-1}})_{i,j=1}^{n}\mid s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in\Sigma\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{n},\ \ \ n\in\mathbb{N},

then
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(iv)

The element
\delta_{e}
is a matrix order unit for the family
(\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

In particular, the triple
(\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}],(\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma))_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\delta_{e})
is a matrix order unit space and its canonical inclusion into
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
is ucp.

Proof.

Item (i) follows from the fact that sums of squares are positive, while
item (ii) follows from (
85
).

(iii) We first note that

(90)

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{s_{i}s_{j}^{-1}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}\otimes\delta_{s_{i}}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}\otimes\delta_{s_{i}}\right)^{*}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma),

where the
E_{i}
are the matrix units in
\mathrm{M}_{n,1}
and
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in\Sigma
; thus the matrix
(\delta_{s_{i}s_{j}^{-1}})_{i,j=1}^{n}
is an element of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
.
Next, suppose that
(\mathcal{C}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a compatible family of convex proper cones satisfying (
89
) and let
A:=(A_{i,j})_{i,j}\in{\rm M}_{k}(\mathrm{M}_{n})^{+}
.
We have that
A\otimes(\delta_{s_{i}s_{j}^{-1}})_{i,j=1}^{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{k^{2}n}
; again by compatibility,

(91)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}A_{i,j}\otimes\delta_{s_{i}s_{j}^{-1}}\in\mathcal{C}_{n},

implying, by Lemma
4.2
, that
\mathcal{C}_{n}\supseteq\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
.

(iv)
Let
s\in\Sigma
and
A\in\mathrm{M}_{n}
, and set

(92)

\displaystyle x:=x(s,A):=A\otimes\delta_{s}+A^{*}\otimes\delta_{s^{-1}};

clearly,
x\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}])_{\mathrm{h}}
.
Write
A=V|A|
in its polar decomposition and set

,
A_{2}:=|A|^{\frac{1}{2}}

Then

(93)

\displaystyle 2\lVert A\rVert\cdot(\mathbf{1}_{n}\otimes\delta_{e})-x

(94)

\displaystyle\geq(V|A|V^{*}+|A|)\otimes\delta_{e}-(V|A|^{\frac{1}{2}}|A|^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\delta_{s}+|A|^{\frac{1}{2}}|A|^{\frac{1}{2}}V^{*}\otimes\delta_{s^{-1}})

(95)

\displaystyle=(A_{1}A_{1}^{*}+A_{2}A_{2}^{*})\otimes\delta_{e}-A_{1}A_{2}^{*}\otimes\delta_{s}-A_{2}A_{1}^{*}\otimes\delta_{s^{-1}}

(96)

\displaystyle=\left(A_{1}\otimes\delta_{s}-A_{2}\otimes\delta_{e}\right)\left(A_{1}\otimes\delta_{s}-A_{2}\otimes\delta_{e}\right)^{*}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma).

Note that a general hermitian element

is of the form

, with

as in (

Setting

, we thus obtain
r\cdot(\mathbf{1}_{n}\otimes\delta_{e})-y\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)

This shows that
(\delta_{e})_{n}
is an order unit for the ordered vector space
(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}]),\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma))
.
∎

The assumption that
\Sigma
is finite allows us to show that

is in fact an Archimedean matrix order unit for the matrix ordered vector space
(\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}],(\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma))_{n\in\mathbb{N}})
;
we show this in the next proposition, whose proof is a noncommutative and matrix version of the argument in the proof of
[

Proposition 4.4
.

The matrix order unit space
(\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}],(\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma))_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\delta_{e})
is an operator system.

Proof.

We show that the cones
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
are norm-closed in
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma))
.
Let
d=|\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}|
.
We claim that every
x\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
is a sum of at most
n^{2}d
squares in
\mathrm{M}_{n}\otimes\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}]
.
To see this, let
x=\sum_{i=1}^{r}y_{i}y_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
and assume that
r>n^{2}d
.
Since

(97)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)-\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{S}[\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma])_{\mathrm{h}},

we have

(98)

\displaystyle\dim{R}(\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)-\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma))\leq\mathrm{dim}{R}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{S}[\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma]))_{\mathrm{h}}=n^{2}d;

thus there are
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}y_{i}y_{i}^{*}=0
.
We may assume that
\alpha_{i}\leq\alpha_{i+1}
, for all
i=1,\dots,r-1
.
It follows that

(99)

\displaystyle x=\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}(1-\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{r}})y_{i}y_{i}^{*},

which completes the proof of the claim.

Now, let
x^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{2}d}y_{i}^{k}(y_{i}^{k})^{*}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, with

(100)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{k}=x\in\mathrm{M}_{n}\otimes\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}].

For every
i=1,\dots,n^{2}d
, we have that

(101)

\displaystyle\|y_{i}^{k}\|^{2}\leq\|x^{k}\|\leq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|x^{k}\|<\infty.

Hence the sequences
(y_{i}^{k})_{k}
are bounded and, since
\dim(\mathrm{M}_{n}\otimes\mathrm{S}[\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma])<\infty
, we may choose convergent subsequences
(y_{i}^{k_{j}})_{j}
and set
\lim_{j}y_{i}^{k_{j}}=:y_{i}
.
It follows that

(102)

\displaystyle x=\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{k}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{2}d}y_{i}^{k}(y_{i}^{k})^{*}=\lim_{j\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{2}d}y_{i}^{k_{j}}(y_{i}^{k_{j}})^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{2}d}y_{i}y_{i}^{*}.

This shows that
x\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
, so
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
is norm closed in
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma))
.

To see that
\delta_{e}
is an Archimedean matrix order unit, recall from
[
45
]
that the order topology is a locally convex topology, whence equivalent to the norm topology, since
\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}]
is finite dimensional.
Thus the cones
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
are closed in the order topology, and, by
[
45
, Theorem 2.30, Remark 3.4]
, this implies that
(\delta_{e})_{n}
is an Archimedean order unit for
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{S}[\Sigma\Sigma^{-1})
.
∎

Example 4.10

By the operator-valued Fejér–Riesz lemma for the circle, the subset

4.2.
The Toeplitz system

We now study the operator system of Toeplitz matrices associated to a finite subset

Definition 4.13
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group,
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite set and
H
be a Hilbert space.

(i)

A
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued Toeplitz matrix
is an operator
T=(T_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Sigma)\otimes H)
with
T_{s,t}\in\mathcal{B}(H)
, for all
s,t\in\Sigma
, such that
T_{s_{1},t_{1}}=T_{s_{2},t_{2}}
whenever
s_{1}t_{1}^{-1}=s_{2}t_{2}^{-1}
.

(ii)

A
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued Toeplitz matrix
T=(T_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}
is called
positive semi-definite
if
T\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Sigma)\otimes H)^{+}
.

The
Toeplitz system

\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
is defined as the operator system consisting of all (
\mathbb{C}
-valued) Toeplitz matrices;
by its definition,
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
is an operator subsystem of the C
∗
-algebra
\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}
of matrices indexed by
\Sigma
.
Further, the operator system of
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued Toeplitz matrices is the operator subsystem
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathcal{B}(H)
of
\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Sigma,H))
.
The
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued Toeplitz matrices
T
correspond bijectively to functions
u:\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}\to\mathcal{B}(H)
via
T_{s,t}=u(st^{-1})
,
s,t\in\Sigma
.
Therefore we can define the restriction map

(108)

\rho:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)},u\mapsto(u(st^{-1}))_{s,t\in\Sigma}.

By the definitions of the positive matrix cones over
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)
and
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
, the map
\rho
is completely positive.

Our interest in the Toeplitz system (and the notation
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
) comes from its appearance in spectral truncations in noncommutative geometry
[
13
,
38
,
36
]
and its analysis in
[
20
]
.
It is straightforward to check that

(109)

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}=P_{\Sigma}\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\mathrm{r}}(\Gamma)P_{\Sigma},

where
P_{\Sigma}:\ell^{2}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\Sigma)
is the canonical orthogonal projection.

4.3.
Duality between the SOS and the Toeplitz systems

We next show that Toeplitz operator systems are compatible with the sums-of-squares cone structure.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group,
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite set and
H
be a Hilbert space.

(i)

If
T=(T_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathcal{B}(H)
is a positive semi-definite
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued Toeplitz matrix, then the map
\phi_{T}:\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{B}(H)
, given by
\phi_{T}(\delta_{st^{-1}})=T_{s,t}
,
s,t\in\Sigma
, is completely positive.

(ii)

If
\phi:\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{B}(H)
is a completely positive map then there exists a unique positive semi-definite
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued Toeplitz matrix
T_{\phi}
such that
\phi=\phi_{T_{\phi}}
.

Proof.

(i) Let
T=(T_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathcal{B}(H)
be a positive semi-definite
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued Toeplitz matrix.
By the proof of
4.3
(iii), it suffices to check that the block operator matrix
\phi_{T}^{(n)}((\delta_{s_{i}s_{j}^{-1}})_{i,j=1}^{n})
with entries in
\mathcal{B}(H)
is positive for any
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in\Sigma
.
A direct computation gives

(110)

\displaystyle\phi_{T}^{(n)}((\delta_{s_{i}s_{j}^{-1}})_{i,j=1}^{n})=(\phi_{T}(\delta_{s_{i}s_{j}^{-1}}))_{i,j=1}^{n}=(T_{s_{i},s_{j}})_{i,j=1}^{n}.

If the
s_{i}
’s are pairwise distinct then
(T_{s_{i},s_{j}})_{i,j=1}^{n}
is positive semi-definite as a principal minor of the positive semi-definite matrix
T
.
Suppose that
s_{1}=s_{2}
and the
s_{2},\dots,s_{n}
are distinct; then we can write

(111)

\displaystyle(T_{s_{i},s_{j}})_{i,j=1}^{n}=\begin{bmatrix}T_{s_{2},s_{2}}&R\\
C&(T_{s_{i},s_{j}})_{i,j=2}^{n}\end{bmatrix}

where
R
and
C
are the first row and the first column of
(T_{s_{i},s_{j}})_{i,j=2}^{n}
, respectively.
The matrix
(T_{s_{i},s_{j}})_{i,j=2}^{n}
is positive semi-definite as a principal minor of
T
, and thus
(T_{s_{i},s_{j}})_{i,j=1}^{n}
is positive semi-definite (see for example
[
19
, Remark 5.1.1]
).
Inductively we have that
(T_{s_{i},s_{j}})_{i,j=1}^{n}
is positive semi-definite for any collection
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in\Sigma
.

(ii) Let
\phi\in\mathcal{CP}(\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma),\mathcal{B}(H))
.
Replacing
\phi_{T}
by
\phi
,
T
by
T_{\phi}
in (
110
) and reading backwards for all
s_{i}\in\Sigma
, we see that
T_{\phi}
is positive semi-definite.

The uniqueness clause follows from the fact that the assignments
T\mapsto\phi_{T}
and
\phi\mapsto T_{\phi}
are inverse to each other.
∎

Corollary 4.15
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite subset.
The map

(112)

\displaystyle\varphi:\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)^{\mathrm{d}},\ \ \ T\mapsto\phi_{T},

is a complete order isomorphism, and hence its dual map

(113)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\mathrm{d}}:\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)\rightarrow(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)})^{\mathrm{d}}

is a complete order isomorphism.
Furthermore,

(114)

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{1})=\left(\delta_{st^{-1}}\mapsto\begin{cases}1,\text{if }s=t\\
0,\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\right)\ \mbox{ and }\ \varphi^{\mathrm{d}}(\delta_{e})=\left(T\mapsto\frac{1}{\lvert\Sigma\rvert}\mathrm{Tr}(T)\right).

Proof.

The fact that the map
\varphi
is a complete order isomorphism and the computation of
\varphi
evaluated at the unit matrix follow directly from
4.14
.
Since
\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)
is finite dimensional it coincides with its double dual and thus
\varphi^{\mathrm{d}}
is also a complete order isomorphism.
To see that
\varphi^{\mathrm{d}}(\delta_{e})
is the normalised trace on the Toeplitz system, note that
\varphi^{\mathrm{d}}(\delta_{st^{-1}})(T)=\phi_{T}(\delta_{st^{-1}})=T_{s,t}
, and the claim follows.
∎

4.4.
The operator extension properties

Consider the canonical injective ucp map

(115)

\displaystyle\iota:\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma),

and the canonical surjective cp map

(116)

\displaystyle\rho:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}.

By virtue of
4.15
the restriction map
\rho
is the dual map of the inclusion map
\iota
, and hence completely contractive.
Here we will examine when this map is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-/
\mathbf{OSp}
-quotient map.

Definition 4.16
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group.
A finite set
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
is said to possess the
positive operator extension property
if, for every Hilbert space
H
, the following condition holds:

Condition (PE):  For every positive semi-definite
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued Toeplitz matrix
T=(T_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathcal{B}(H)
, there exists a positive semi-definite function
u\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}
such that
T_{s,t}=u(st^{-1})
, for all
s,t\in\Sigma
.

Similarly, we say that
\Sigma
possesses the
cp extension property
if condition (PE) holds for every finite dimensional Hilbert space
H
.

Remark 4.17
.

Note that if a finite set
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
has the positive operator extension property, it trivially has the cp extension property.
Conversely, assume that
\Sigma
has the cp extension property and let
H
be a Hilbert space.
Let
\mathcal{F}(H)
be the set of all finite rank projections in
\mathcal{B}(H)
, ordered by inclusion; clearly,
\mathcal{F}(H)
is a directed set.
Assume that
T=(T_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}
is a positive semi-definite
\mathcal{B}(H)
-valued Toeplitz matrix.
Then, for every
P\in\mathcal{F}(H)
, we have

(117)

\displaystyle(I\otimes P)T(I\otimes P)\in\left(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathcal{B}(PH)\right)^{+}.

By assumption, there exists a positive semi-definite function
u_{P}\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,PH)^{+}
such that

(118)

\displaystyle PT_{s,t}P=u_{P}(st^{-1}),\text{ for all }s,t\in\Sigma.

As
PH\subseteq H
, we may consider
u_{P}
as elements of
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}
.
By virtue of (
73
), we consider further
u_{P}\in\mathcal{CP}(C^{*}(\Gamma),\mathcal{B}(H))
, and equip the latter space with Arveson’s BW topology (see
[
1
]
), that is, the topology of point-weak* convergence.
By
[
1
]
, the BW toplogy is compact on bounded sets; thus, the net
(u_{P})_{P\in\mathcal{F}(H)}
has a BW cluster point, say
u\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)^{+}
.
If
s,t\in\Sigma
then
u(st^{-1})
is a weak* cluster point of
((I\otimes P)T_{s,t}(I\otimes P))_{P\in\mathcal{F}(H)}
, and hence coincides with
T_{s,t}
, in other words,
u
extends
T
.

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite set.
The set
\Sigma
has the positive operator extension property if and only if the restriction map

(119)

\displaystyle\rho:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)},u\mapsto(u(st^{-1}))_{s,t\in\Sigma},

is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-quotient map.

Proof.

Recall from
Subsection 2.4
that
\rho
is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-quotient map if and only if the induced map

(120)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)/\ker(\rho)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}

is a complete order isomorphism.

Assume that
\Sigma
has the positive operator and hence the cp extension property.
Then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for every positive semi-definite Toeplitz matrix

(121)

\displaystyle T=(T_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)})^{+},

there is a positive semi-definite function
u:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{n}
with
T_{s,t}=u(st^{-1})
, for all
s,t\in\Sigma
;
in other words, we have that

(\tilde{\rho}^{-1})^{(n)}(T)=u|_{\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}}\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n})^{+}|_{\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}}=\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)/\ker(\rho))^{+},

showing that the cp map
\tilde{\rho}
is a complete order isomorphism.

Conversely, if
\rho
is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-quotient map, then given a positive semi-definite Toeplitz matrix

(122)

\displaystyle T=(T_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)})^{+},

the pre-image
u:=(\tilde{\rho}^{-1})^{(n)}(T)
is an element of

(123)

\displaystyle\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)/\ker(\rho))^{+}=\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n})^{+}|_{\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}}.

That is,
u
is the restriction of a positive semi-definite function
v:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{n}
to
\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}
, so
v(st^{-1})=u(st^{-1})=T_{s,t}
, for all
s,t\in\Sigma
.
Hence,
\Sigma
has the cp extension property.
By
4.17
it follows that
\Sigma
has the positive operator extension property.
∎

Definition 4.19
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group.
A finite set
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
is said to possess the
bounded operator extension property
if, for every Hilbert space
H
, the following condition holds:

Condition (BE):  For every Toeplitz matrix
T=(T_{s,t})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\in\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathcal{B}(H)
, there exists a function
u\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)
such that
T_{s,t}=u(st^{-1})
, for all
s,t\in\Sigma
, and
\lVert T\rVert=\lVert\phi_{u}\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}
.

Similarly, we say that
\Sigma
possesses the
cb extension property
if condition (BE) holds for every finite dimensional Hilbert space
H
.

An analogue of
4.17
applies:

Remark 4.20
.

If a finite set
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
has the bounded operator extension property, it trivially has the cb extension property.
Conversely, a BW-argument analogous to
4.17
shows that the cb extension property implies the bounded operator extension property.

Proposition 4.21
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite set.
The set
\Sigma
has the bounded operator extension property if and only if the restriction map

(124)

\displaystyle\rho:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)},u\mapsto(u(st^{-1}))_{s,t\in\Sigma},

is an
\mathbf{OSp}
-quotient map.

Proof.

Recall that
\rho
is an
\mathbf{OSp}
-quotient map if and only if the induced cc map

(125)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)/\ker(\rho)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}

is completely isometric.

Assume that
\Sigma
has the bounded operator, and hence the cb, extension property.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
T\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)})
let
u\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n})
be an element of the
\mathrm{M}_{n}
-valued Fourier–Stieltjes algebra viewed as function
\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{M}_{n}
such that

(126)

\displaystyle T=(u(st^{-1}))_{s,t\in\Sigma}=\rho^{(n)}(u)\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \lVert T\rVert=\lVert\phi_{u}\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}.

Then we have

(127)

\displaystyle\lVert T\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert\rho^{(n)}(u)\rVert\leq\|u+\ker\rho^{(n)}\rVert\leq\lVert u\rVert=\lVert\phi_{u}\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}=\lVert T\rVert,

showing that
\tilde{\rho}^{(n)}
is isometric, as required.

Conversely, suppose that
\tilde{\rho}
is completely isometric and fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
By surjectivity and the assumption there exists a
u\in\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{n})
such that

(128)

\displaystyle T=\rho^{(n)}(u)\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \lVert T\rVert=\|u+\ker\rho^{(n)}\|.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
choose
v_{m}\in\ker\rho^{(n)}
such that

(129)

\displaystyle\lVert T\rVert\leq\lVert u+v_{m}\rVert<\lVert T\rVert+\frac{1}{m}.

We see that

(130)

\displaystyle\lVert v_{m}\rVert\leq\lVert u\rVert+\lVert u+v_{m}\rVert\leq\lVert u\rVert+\lVert T\rVert+1.

Hence the sequence
(v_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded, and thus so is the induced sequence of cb maps
(\phi_{v_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
with respect to the cb-norm.
Since the set

(131)

\displaystyle\mathcal{CB}_{\lVert u\rVert+\lVert T\rVert+1}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma),\mathrm{M}_{n}):=\{\phi\in\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma),\mathrm{M}_{n})\mid\lVert\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}\leq\lVert u\rVert+\lVert T\rVert+1\}

is compact in the BW-topology, we may take
v_{\phi}
induced be a BW-limit
\phi
of a subnet of
(\phi_{v_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
(and rename accordingly the net
(v_{m})
).
We see that

(132)

\displaystyle\limsup_{m}\lVert\phi_{u+v_{m}}\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}=\limsup_{m}\lVert u+v_{m}\rVert=\lVert T\rVert.

Since the maps take values in
\mathrm{M}_{n}
it follows that the BW-convergence on this bounded set coincides with the point-norm convergence, and hence

(133)

\displaystyle\phi(\delta_{st^{-1}})=\lim_{m}\phi_{v_{m}}(\delta_{st^{-1}}),

for all
s,t\in\Sigma
.
By the definition of
\rho
we obtain

(134)

\displaystyle\rho^{(n)}(v_{\phi})

\displaystyle=(v_{\phi}(st^{-1}))_{s,t\in\Sigma}=(\phi(\delta_{st^{-1}}))_{s,t\in\Sigma}

(135)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m}(\phi_{v_{m}}(\delta_{st^{-1}}))_{s,t\in\Sigma}=\lim_{m}\rho^{(n)}(v_{m})=0,

where the limit above is taken in the entrywise-topology, and thus in the norm-topology, of
\mathrm{M}_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
as
\Sigma
is finite.
Therefore
v_{\phi}\in\ker\rho^{(n)}
, from which we get

(136)

\displaystyle T=\rho^{(n)}(u)=\rho^{(n)}(u+v_{\phi})\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \lVert T\rVert=\|u+\ker\rho^{(n)}\|\leq\lVert u+v_{\phi}\rVert.

On the other hand, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathrm{M}_{k}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma))
and
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{C}^{kn}
we have

(137)

\displaystyle|\langle\phi_{u+v_{\phi}}^{(k)}(x)\xi,\eta\rangle|

\displaystyle=\lim_{m}|\langle\phi_{u+v_{m}}^{(k)}(x)\xi,\eta\rangle|

(138)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{m}\lVert\phi_{u+v_{m}}\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}\cdot\lVert x\rVert\cdot\lVert\xi\rVert\cdot\lVert\eta\rVert

(139)

\displaystyle=\lVert T\rVert\cdot\lVert x\rVert\cdot\lVert\xi\rVert\cdot\lVert\eta\rVert.

From this we get

(140)

\displaystyle\lVert u+v_{\phi}\rVert=\lVert\phi_{u+v_{\phi}}\rVert_{\mathrm{cb}}=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\lVert\phi_{u+v_{\phi}}^{(k)}\rVert\leq\lVert T\rVert,

showing that
\Sigma
has the cb extension property.
By
4.20
it follows that
\Sigma
has the bounded operator extension property.
∎

4.5.
The characterisation of the complete Fejér–Riesz property

We next show that the positive/bounded operator extension property is equivalent to the complete Fejér–Riesz property.
We refer to
Figure 1
as an illustration of the operator systems and matrix ordered vector spaces with the canonical maps which are relevant in the proof of the next result.

Theorem 4.22
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite subset.
The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The set
\Sigma
has the positive operator extension property.

(ii)

The set
\Sigma
has the bounded operator extension property.

(iii)

The set
\Sigma
has the complete Fejér–Riesz property.

(iv)

The Toeplitz system
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
is completely order isomorphic to the dual operator system of the Fourier system
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Sigma\Sigma^{-1})}
.

Proof.

By
4.15
, the dual map of the canonical inclusion map

(141)

\displaystyle\iota:\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)

is the restriction map

(142)

\displaystyle\rho:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)},u\mapsto(u(st^{-1}))_{s,t\in\Sigma},

where
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)
and
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
are at the same time equipped with the structures of the matrix ordered vector space and operator space duals of
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
and
\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)
.
Recall from
4.18
and
4.21
that the set
\Sigma
has the bounded/positive operator extension property if and only if the restriction map
\rho
is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-/
\mathbf{OSp}
-quotient map.
By
2.3
, both of these statements are equivalent to the statement that the map
\iota
is a complete order embedding, i.e. that
\Sigma
has the Fejér–Riesz property.
This shows the equivalences [(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii)].

[(iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv)]. First note that, by finite-dimensionality, the dual
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Sigma\Sigma^{-1})}^{\mathrm{d}}
of the Fourier system is an operator system, and by the Fejér–Riesz property we have
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Sigma\Sigma^{-1})}\cong\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)
, which implies the claim by
4.15
.
Conversely, if the operator systems
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}
and
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Sigma\Sigma^{-1})}^{\mathrm{d}}
are completely order isomorphic, we obtain complete order isomorphisms

(143)

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Sigma\Sigma^{-1})}\cong\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Sigma\Sigma^{-1})}^{\mathrm{dd}}\cong(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)})^{\mathrm{d}}\cong\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma),

so the one-to-one ucp map

(144)

\displaystyle\iota:\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)_{(\Sigma\Sigma^{-1})}\subseteq\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)

must be a complete order embedding, showing the implication [(iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii)].
∎

In view of Theorem
4.22
, we may only refer to the operator extension property for a subset satisfying its equivalent conditions.

4.6.
(\mathrm{min},\mathrm{max})
-nuclearity

We close the section with an application to tensor products.
Recall the spatial implementation

(145)

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)^{(\Sigma)}=P_{\Sigma}\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\mathrm{r}}(\Gamma)P_{\Sigma}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Gamma)),

where

is the canonical orthogonal projection.
It follows that if

and
\Gamma_{2}
are discrete groups, and

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2})^{(\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2})}\cong\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{1})^{(\Sigma_{1})}\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{2})^{(\Sigma_{2})},

\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\mathrm{r}}(\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2})

\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\mathrm{r}}(\Gamma_{1})\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathrm{C}^{*}_{\mathrm{r}}(\Gamma_{2})

Corollary 4.23
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be discrete groups and assume that
\Gamma_{1}
or
\Gamma_{2}

For

(ii)

the pair of Toeplitz systems
(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{1})^{(\Sigma_{1})},\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{2})^{(\Sigma_{2})})
is
(\mathrm{min},\mathrm{max})
-nuclear.

Proof.

By universality and the amenability assumption we have a canonical *-isomorphism

\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2})_{((\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2})(\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2})^{-1})}\cong\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{1})_{(\Sigma_{1}\Sigma_{1}^{-1})}\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{2})_{(\Sigma_{2}\Sigma_{2}^{-1})}.

[(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)].
Assume that
\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2}
has the operator and hence in particular the cp extension property.
Then, by

, the identity map on generators

(149)

\displaystyle\iota:\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2})\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2})

is a complete order embedding.
By the assumption that
\Sigma_{i}
has the operator, and hence the cp, extension property it follows from

that
\Sigma_{i}
has the complete Fejér–Riesz property, i.e.
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{i})_{(\Sigma_{i}\Sigma_{i}^{-1})}
is completely order isomorphic to
\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma_{i})
, for

.
By
4.7
and by (

(150)

\displaystyle\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma_{1})\otimes_{\mathrm{max}}\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma_{2})

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2})

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2})_{((\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2})(\Sigma_{1}\times\Sigma_{2})^{-1})}

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{1})_{(\Sigma_{1}\Sigma_{1}^{-1})}\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma_{2})_{(\Sigma_{2}\Sigma_{2}^{-1})}

\displaystyle\cong\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma_{1})\otimes_{\mathrm{min}}\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma_{2}),

that is, we have that the pair
(\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma_{1}),\mathrm{SOS}(\Sigma_{2}))
is

5.
Positive semi-definite functions on a positivity domain

In this section we discuss positive semi-definite operator-valued functions defined on positivity domains, addressing the question of when they admit extensions to positive semi-definite functions defined on the whole group.
We thus drop the assumption that the domain of the given function has the form
\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}
for some finite set, allowing simultaneously infinite domains.
Our analysis follows a similar path as in the previous section by first identifying the operator system predual of the matrix ordered vector space of positive semi-definite functions;
this operator system can be constructed as a colimit from sums-of-squares operator systems.
In the case of the positivity domain consisting of five points in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
as in
Figure 8
this allows us to realise the associated operator system as a coproduct, giving us access to its maximal and minimal
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-cover and allowing us to infer that there must be positive semi-definite operator-valued functions on this positivity domain which do not admit positive semi-definite extensions to the whole group
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

5.1.
The operator system
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
and the complete factorisation property

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
be a positivity domain, i.e. a subset such that
\Delta^{-1}=\Delta
and
e\in\Delta
.
We begin by defining the positive matricial cones which we will use to define an operator system structure on the
∗
-vector space

(161)

\displaystyle\mathrm{S}[\Delta]:=\mathrm{span}\{\delta_{s}\mid s\in\Delta\};\quad\delta_{s}^{*}:=\delta_{s^{-1}}.

For a
∗
-vector space
V
and a subset
\mathcal{P}\subseteq V_{\mathrm{h}}
, we denote by
\mathrm{cone}(\mathcal{P})
the
cone generated
by
\mathcal{P}
; thus,
\mathrm{cone}(\mathcal{P}):=[0,\infty)\cdot\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{P}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, set

(162)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{n}(\Delta):=\mathrm{cone}\left(\bigcup\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)\mid\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma,\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}\subseteq\Delta,\lvert\Sigma\rvert<\infty\big\}\right)\subseteq\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{S}[\Delta])_{\mathrm{h}}.

Note that, since
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
is a cone for any
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
, in order to form
\mathcal{U}_{n}(\Delta)
it suffices to take the convex hull of the union of the cones
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)
.
Since the cone generated by a union of cones is their sum, we have that

(163)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{n}(\Delta)=\sum\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\Sigma)\mid\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma,\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}\subseteq\Delta,\lvert\Sigma\rvert<\infty\big\}.

Remark 5.1
.

It is straightforward to check that
(\mathcal{U}_{n}(\Delta))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a compatible family of convex proper cones, each respectively contained in
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma))^{+}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows from
4.3
that, in fact,
(\mathcal{U}_{n}(\Delta))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the smallest such family whose
n
-th level contains the sets

(164)

\displaystyle\left\{(\delta_{st^{-1}})_{s,t\in\Sigma}\mid\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma,\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}\subseteq\Delta,\lvert\Sigma\rvert<\infty\right\}.

The element
\delta_{e}
is a matrix order unit for the family
(\mathcal{U}_{n}(\Delta))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
In particular, the triple
(\mathrm{S}[\Delta],(\mathcal{U}_{n}(\Delta))_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\delta_{e})
is a matrix order unit space and the canonical inclusion into
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
is ucp.

We write
\mathrm{UOS}_{0}(\Delta)
for the matrix order unit space
(\mathrm{S}[\Delta],(\mathcal{U}_{n}(\Delta))_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\delta_{e})
and
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta):=\mathrm{Arch}(\mathrm{UOS}_{0}(\Delta))
for its Archimedeanisation.
Thus
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
is an operator system;
its notation refers to its matricial cones being generated by unions of sums-of-squares cones.
In the next proposition, we point out that
when
\Delta
is finite, under an additional technical assumption, the matrix ordered operator space
\mathrm{UOS}_{0}(\Delta)
is in fact already an operator system (without Archimedeanizing).

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group,
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite positivity domain and let
\{\Sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a family of subsets of
\Gamma
such that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma_{i}\Sigma_{i}^{-1}=\Delta
.
Assume that for every finite set
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
with
\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}\subseteq\Delta
there exists
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\mathcal{Q}_{k}(\Sigma)\subseteq\mathcal{Q}_{k}(\Sigma_{i})
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
\mathrm{UOS}_{0}(\Delta)=\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
.

Proof.

The assumptions on the family
\{\Sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
ensure that we have

(165)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k}(\Delta)=\mathrm{cone}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{Q}_{k}(\Sigma_{i})\right),

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since each cone
\mathcal{Q}_{k}(\Sigma_{i})
is closed by
4.4
, so is
\mathcal{U}_{k}(\Delta)
, whence
\mathrm{UOS}_{0}(\Delta)
is an operator system and we have
\mathrm{UOS}_{0}(\Delta)=\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Note that the assumption in
5.2
ensures in particular that each
\Sigma_{i}
is maximal in the sense that
(\Sigma_{i}\cup\gamma)(\Sigma_{i}\cup\gamma)^{-1}\nsubseteq\Delta
for any
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\Sigma
.

Before we move to the analysis of the operator systems of the form

, we provide a motivating example;
although this example may be known to the experts, we provide sufficient operator-algebraic details in order to depict the differences with the sums-of-squares operator systems.

Example 5.4

\mathrm{M}_{n}(X^{\mathrm{d}})^{+}:=\mathcal{CP}(X,\mathrm{M}_{n})

Corollary 5.16
.

The pair
(\mathrm{B}(\Delta),(\mathrm{B}(\Delta,\mathbb{C}^{n})^{+})_{n})
is the matrix ordered vector space dual of the operator system
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
.

The identification

(228)

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}(\Delta,H):=\mathrm{span}(\mathrm{B}(\Delta,H)^{+})\cong\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{CP}(\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta),\mathcal{B}(H)))=\mathcal{CB}(\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta),\mathcal{B}(H))

endows the Fourier–Stieltjes space
\mathrm{B}(\Delta)

Definition 5.17
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group.
A positivity domain
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
is said to possess the
positive operator extension property (as a positivity domain)
if, for every Hilbert space
H
, the following condition holds:

Condition (PEPD):  For every positive semi-definite function
u:\Delta\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
, there exists a positive semi-definite function
\tilde{u}:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
such that
\tilde{u}|_{\Delta}=u
.

Similarly, we say that
\Delta
possesses the
cp extension property (as a positivity domain)
if condition (PEPD) holds for every finite dimensional Hilbert space
H
.

Definition 5.18
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group.
A positivity domain
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
is said to the
bounded operator extension property (as a positivity domain)
if, for every Hilbert space
H
, the following condition holds:

Condition (BEPD):  For every positive semi-definite function
u:\Delta\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
, there exists a positive semi-definite function
\tilde{u}:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(H)
such that
\tilde{u}|_{\Delta}=u
and
\lVert\tilde{u}\rVert_{\mathrm{B}(\Gamma,H)}=\lVert u\rVert_{\mathrm{B}(\Delta,H)}
.

Similarly, we say that
\Sigma
possesses the
cb extension property
if condition (BEPD) holds for every finite dimensional Hilbert space
H
.

Remark 5.19
.

Analogues of
4.17
and
4.20
apply, showing that the positive operator and cp extension properties, as well as the bounded operator and cb extension properties (all as positivity domains) are respectively equivalent.

Remark 5.20
.

By
5.15
the canonical ucp inclusion map
\iota:\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)\to\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
induces the canonical surjective cp restriction map
\rho:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathrm{B}(\Delta)
as its dual map.
In analogy to
4.18
and
4.21
, the positivity domain
\Delta
has the cp/cb extension property (as a positivity domain) if and only if the restriction map
\rho:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathrm{B}(\Delta)
is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-/
\mathbf{OSp}
-quotient map.

Theorem 5.21
.

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group and
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
a positivity domain.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

The positivity domain
\Delta
has the positive operator extension property (as a positivity domain).

(ii)

The positivity domain
\Delta
has the bounded operator extension property (as a positivity domain).

(iii)

The positivity domain
\Delta
has the complete factorisation property.

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of
Theorem 4.22
, note that the dual map of the canonical map
\iota:\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)\rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
is the restriction map
\rho:\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathrm{B}(\Delta)
, where
\mathrm{B}(\Gamma)
and
\mathrm{B}(\Delta)
are, at the same time, the matrix ordered vector space and operator space duals of
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\Gamma)
and
\mathrm{UOS}(\Delta)
respectively.
Recall from
5.19
and
5.20
that the positivity domain
\Delta
has the positive/bounded operator extension property (as a positivity domain) if and only if the restriction map
\rho
is an
\mathbf{MVS}
-/
\mathbf{OSp}
-quotient map.
By
2.3
, (i) and (iii) are both equivalent to the statement that the map
\iota
is a complete order embedding, that is, to (iii), in view of
5.8
.
