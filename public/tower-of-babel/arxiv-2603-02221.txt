MedFeat: Model-Aware and Explainability-Driven Feature Engineering with LLMs for Clinical Tabular Prediction
Zizheng Zhang, Yiming Li, Justin Xu, Jinyu Wang, Rui Wang, Lei Song, Jiang Bian, David W Eyre, Jingjing Fu

Abstract
In healthcare tabular predictions, classical models with feature engineering often outperform neural approaches. Recent advances in Large Language Models enable the integration of domain knowledge into feature engineering, offering a promising direction. However, existing approaches typically rely on a broad search over predefined transformations, overlooking downstream model characteristics and feature importance signals. We present MedFeat, a feedback-driven and model-aware feature engineering framework that leverages LLM reasoning with domain knowledge and provides feature explanations based on SHAP values while tracking successful and failed proposals to guide feature discovery. By incorporating model awareness, MedFeat prioritizes informative signals that are difficult for the downstream model to learn directly due to its characteristics. Across a broad range of clinical prediction tasks, MedFeat achieves stable improvements over various baselines and discovers clinically meaningful features that generalize under distribution shift, demonstrating robustness across years and from ICU cohorts to general hospitalized patients, thereby offering insights into real-world deployment. Code required to reproduce our experiments will be released, subject to dataset agreements and institutional policies.

Introduction

Clinical prediction models support numerous high-stakes decisions in healthcare, such as early warning for deterioration, mortality risk estimation, and adverse event forecasting
(
5
;
26
;
35
)
. Despite rapid progress in machine learning, real-world healthcare datasets remain difficult to model reliably due to multiple challenges, such as severe class imbalance, heterogeneous feature types, complex temporal patterns, and irregular missingness
(
10
)
.

Deep learning algorithms have shown effectiveness in learning representations from raw data in multiple domains, such as vision and language
(
19
;
29
;
30
;
27
)
. However, in clinical tabular datasets, classical machine learning models, such as tree-based models, often outperform deep learning approaches
(
10
;
25
;
9
)
. In the meantime, the deployment of such models faces strong interpretability and privacy constraints, and extensive fine-tuning remains difficult in real-world settings
(
7
)
. As a result, classical machine learning paired with manual feature engineering becomes the common approach in practice, particularly when data is limited and a distribution shift across time or location is present
(
10
)
.

Manual feature engineering is often expensive and time-consuming due to several reasons. For instance, as feature dimensionality increases, the search space of possible transformations of the features grows exponentially, making exhaustive exploration impractical
(
15
)
. Effective feature construction, therefore, requires substantial domain expertise and a significant amount of iterative experiments across tasks and cohorts to verify the effectiveness of new features. Automated feature engineering methods, such as AutoFeat and OpenFE, were introduced to facilitate these challenges
(
15
;
34
)
. These approaches typically generate new feature candidates based on a set of pre-defined operators (e.g., arithmetic combinations, nonlinear transformations) and then select features using validation performance. However, these approaches have limitations, such as a lack of clinical justification and extensive computational requirements.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) provide new insights for feature engineering in healthcare
(
29
;
33
)
. LLMs have rich prior domain knowledge embedded from the training corpora and compositional reasoning to propose clinically meaningful transformations and interactions that go beyond fixed operator templates
(
32
)
. Several early works investigated this field by prompting LLMs to generate candidate features and make selections based on validation performance, including CAAFE, FeatLLM, and OCTree
(
14
;
13
;
22
)
. Although these methods demonstrate the feasibility of LLMs contributing useful features, several limitations remain in the design. First, all existing studies use a model-agnostic design when using LLMs. In practice, the value of engineered features depends strongly on the downstream learner: logistic regression requires explicit nonlinearities and interactions, whereas tree ensembles can directly represent many such patterns
(
4
)
. Second, each feature sent to LLMs is treated as interchangeable during generation, neglecting the differences in the importance of features. Third, existing studies send names and descriptions of all features to LLMs for feature generation in every query, which leads to the prompt length growing linearly with dimensionality. Long prompts can reduce generation quality because the model must distribute attention over the entire context, which dilutes the truly informative predictive signal
(
20
)
.

To address these challenges, we propose MedFeat, an explainability-driven and model-aware feature engineering framework that uses LLMs in a way that is scalable, interpretable, and compatible with clinical constraints. Concretely, MedFeat creates features based on guidance, including feature importance and characteristics of downstream models, and uses them to focus targeted feature discovery on informative predictors
(
21
)
.

Contributions.
Our main contribution in this work can be summarized as follows, corresponding to three challenges of the existing work: First, to our knowledge, MedFeat is the first LLM-based feature engineering framework that integrates model-awareness, adapting feature proposals to the downstream learner’s representational limits. This reduces wasted evaluation budgets on features that the learner can already capture implicitly and generates features that are difficult for the model to learn due to its underlying structure. Second, MedFeat uses SHAP value-based explanation scores and rankings as generation signals, both to prioritize features during sampling and to condition the LLM prompt with structured importance information. This approach improves the model’s capabilities by boosting features that have already demonstrated predictive signals. Third, MedFeat uses an island search strategy that creates feature subsets based on their importance. This improves efficiency by bounding token usage and enhances feature quality by avoiding unfocused transformations of weak predictors. Furthermore, we incorporate success/failure memory to iteratively improve the framework across rounds and avoid sharing patient-level records by relying solely on aggregated feedback and metadata.

We evaluated MedFeat on a broad suite of clinical tabular prediction tasks spanning multiple datasets and outcomes, including settings with severe imbalance and heterogeneous missingness. Our experiments show that MedFeat consistently improves the performance of strong classical baselines, both in the practical default regime, where extensive tuning is not available, and with comprehensive optimization. We also demonstrate that MedFeat features generalize across different distributional settings, including transfer features from ICU cohorts to broader hospital populations.

Figure 1
:

MedFeat iteratively augments clinical tabular data with LLM-generated features using explainability-guided, model-aware feedback. Starting from a downstream learner trained on the original features (1), the framework computes feature importance and profiles features by type and metadata (2). It then forms importance-weighted “feature islands” by sampling a small subset of influential features (3), and prompts the LLM with the island context, model constraints, and a memory bank of past successes/failures to propose clinically meaningful transformations (4). Proposed features are validated and evaluated locally (5), accepted features are added to the dataset, and the feedback memory is updated for the next iteration. Along the process, no patient-level raw data are sent to the LLM.

Related Work

Machine Learning in Healthcare.
The developments in machine learning and the availability of hospital data have resulted in multiple new approaches to processing patient data for clinical predictions
(
3
;
17
)
. Deep learning has been successful for other modalities such as images and free text in healthcare, with applications including tumor segmentation and clinical note extraction
(
19
)
. However, it often underperforms on structured tabular prediction tasks, especially when the data contains mixed feature types
(
10
)
. Compared with curated machine learning benchmarks, healthcare tabular data is typically noisy and exhibits complex patterns
(
17
)
. These properties make generalization difficult, particularly under temporal drift and cross-site distribution shifts. In this context, classical models such as XGBoost remain strong and commonly deployed baselines due to their robustness, ease of optimization, and compatibility with interpretability requirements
(
6
;
10
)
.

Automatic Feature Engineering.
Automatic feature engineering (AFE) reduces manual feature construction by generating candidate transformations and interactions from a fixed operator library and selecting those that improve validation performance. Methods such as AutoFeat enumerate feature compositions (e.g., nonlinear transforms and pairwise interactions) and apply model-based selection to control redundancy
(
15
)
. OpenFE follows a similar operator-driven design but improves scalability via pruning and staged evaluation
(
34
)
. While effective in some settings, these approaches are limited for clinical tabular data: the search space is constrained by generic operators, candidate generation can still be expensive in wide tables, and validation-score selection is unstable under label noise and severe class imbalance. Moreover, many generated features lack clinical justification, which hinders deployment
(
1
)
.

LLM-based feature engineering replaces fixed operator libraries with LLM-proposed rules or code. Existing work primarily prompts LLMs to iteratively generate Python feature-engineering code from dataset context and retains changes when validation performance improves. Other studies, such as CAAFE and FeatLLM, extract class-discriminative rules and convert them into binary features through LLM reasoning
(
14
)
. OCTree feeds the LLM decision-tree-based reasoning summaries from prior trials to refine feature-generation rules over iterations
(
22
)
. Most existing studies rely heavily on validation metrics. In practice, this can be unstable in healthcare because validation scores often exhibit high variance due to noisy labels and class imbalance. Moreover, these approaches often ignore explainability and downstream model inductive biases. Finally, some data-centric approaches provide few-shot raw samples to LLMs to ground the generation process. While helpful for general tasks
(
13
;
12
)
, sending patient-level information is often prohibited for clinical deployment due to privacy restrictions (Table
1
).

3
Explainability-Driven and Model-aware Feature Engineering

In this section, we formally formulate the research question and introduce our framework, MedFeat, which is an iterative framework for automated feature engineering in tabular prediction tasks. MedFeat uses model explainability signals to obtain a more stable and informative optimization signal and explicitly conditions feature generation on the inductive bias of the downstream learner.

Research Question Formulation:
We study supervised clinical tabular prediction. Let
D=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote a dataset of
N
patient episodes, where each input

x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{M}

is an
M
-dimensional column feature vector and

y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}

is the corresponding label. We denote the set of column names by

C=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{M}\}
,
so that
x_{i,j}
corresponds to column
c_{j}
. In this work, we focus on binary classification tasks with
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
; though the framework extends naturally to regression.
Given a training set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
and a validation set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}
,
the objective is to find a set of feature transformations

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{K}\}

that maps the original feature space to an augmented representation and maximizes validation performance.
Each transformation
\sigma_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
produces a new one-dimensional
feature from the current representation. Applying
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
to the dataset yields the
augmented dataset

\mathcal{D}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\{(x_{i}\oplus\sigma_{1}(x_{i})\oplus\cdots\oplus\sigma_{K}(x_{i}),y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}.

Let
f_{\theta}
denote a downstream model and let

\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
denote the evaluation metric of the given prediction task.
We formalize feature engineering as the following optimization problem:

\max_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Sigma}\;\mathcal{L}\!\left(f_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\star},\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)\text{ s.t. }f_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\star}=\arg\max_{f}\mathcal{L}\!\left(f,\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right),

where
\Sigma
denotes the space of admissible feature transformations.
This objective seeks transformations that, when appended to the original features and used to retrain
the model, yield maximal generalization performance on validation data
(
22
)
.

Directly solving this optimization is computationally intractable due to the non-differentiable and
combinatorial nature of
\Sigma
, as well as the need to repeatedly retrain
f
for each candidate
transformation set. Consequently, MedFeat approaches this problem via iterative, explainability-driven
optimization, using structured feedback from the downstream model to guide the search over
feature transformations.

3.1
MedFeat Overview

MedFeat proceeds in an iterative manner with a user-defined number of iterations.
Starting from the given dataset, a baseline model is trained on

\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
, and its validation performance and feature relevance are computed.
At each iteration
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, MedFeat explores multiple candidate feature-generation
contexts, referred to as
islands
, and selects the most promising one based on validation
performance.

Concretely, at iteration
t
, MedFeat maintains a set of accepted feature transformations

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t}=\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{t-1}\}
and a corresponding baseline model

f_{\theta_{t}}
trained on the augmented dataset

\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t}
.
Feature importance scores are computed from
f_{\theta_{t}}
using SHAP values applied to
\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}

(
21
)
.
These relevance scores define a probability distribution over features that is used to construct

K
islands, each consisting of a subset of features.

For each island, MedFeat builds a model-aware prompt and queries an LLM to generate a candidate
feature transformation.
Each transformation is executed and validated for numerical correctness and evaluated by
training a new downstream model on the augmented dataset.
Among the
K
candidates, the island yielding the highest validation performance is selected.
If its performance exceeds the current baseline by a tolerance threshold, which is introduced to account for noise in the validation set, the corresponding
features are accepted and merged into the feature set; otherwise, all candidates from the
iteration are rejected.
The baseline model and feature importance scores are then updated, and the process continues until
the iteration reaches the user-defined maximum number of rounds (Figure
1
, Appendix
A
.)

3.2
Explainability-Driven Feedback

Let
f_{\theta_{t}}
denote the baseline model at iteration
t
.
We compute a feature relevance vector

s_{t}

from
f_{\theta_{t}}
using SHAP values to represent the importance of features and applied it to
\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}
.

These importance scores serve as the primary feedback signal in MedFeat.
They are used to rank features by importance, to guide the sampling of islands, and to track how
feature relevance evolves across iterations.
By relying on attribution signals rather than validation metrics alone, MedFeat obtains a more
stable and interpretable signal for guiding feature generation in noisy and imbalanced regimes.

3.3
Importance-Weighted Island Sampling

At each iteration, MedFeat constructs
K
islands, where each island corresponds to a candidate
feature-generation context.
An island is defined as a subset of features sampled from the current augmented feature space, based on the type and importance of the feature. More implementation details of island generation can be found in Appendix
D
.
Let
\tilde{s}_{t,i}
denote the normalized importance of the
i
-th feature group at iteration
t
.
Features are sampled according to the distribution

p_{t}(i)=\,\tilde{s}_{t,i}

which balances the exploitation of highly relevant features with the exploration of less prominent ones.

Each island contains
m
features and may correspond to different types of features.
By restricting each LLM call to a small island, MedFeat keeps the prompt length bounded, reduces token
usage, and focuses the LLM on localized regions of the feature space.
Multiple islands per iteration enable parallel exploration of diverse feature combinations while
retaining computational efficiency.

3.4
Model-Aware Feature Generation

For each island, MedFeat constructs a model-aware prompt that conditions feature generation on the
downstream hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
in a few-shot manner.
Let
f_{\theta_{t}}\in\mathcal{H}
denote the current baseline model.

For instance, when the downstream model is logistic regression, the decision
function is linear in the input.
In this setting, MedFeat instructs the LLMs to generate transformations

\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{M+t-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

that increase representational capacity, such as nonlinear transformations, interaction terms,
and composite features.
These transformations explicitly expand the feature map available to the model.

When the downstream model is XGBoost, which corresponds to gradient-boosted decision trees, many nonlinear patterns and interactions are already representable.
MedFeat, therefore, guides the LLM toward proposing transformations that improve robustness or capture
structures that are less directly learned by trees, such as complex temporal patterns, global statistics, and context-driven interactions while avoiding redundant remappings that yield limited
marginal benefits.

3.5
Candidate Evaluation and Selection

Each candidate transformation generated from an island is executed and applied to

\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}
.
For valid candidates, a downstream model is trained on the augmented training data, and the validation metric is computed.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{t,k}
denote the validation loss obtained from the
k
-th island at iteration
t
,
i.e.,

\mathcal{L}_{t,k}=\mathcal{L}\!\left(f_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t}\cup\{\sigma_{t,k}\}}^{\star},\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}\oplus(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t}\cup\{\sigma_{t,k}\})\right).

MedFeat selects the best-performing island as

k^{\star}=\arg\min_{k}\mathcal{L}_{t,k}.

If
\mathcal{L}_{t,k^{\star}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{t,\text{baseline}}+\beta
,
where
\beta\geq 0
is a tolerance parameter, the feature transformations generated by the island

k^{\star}
are accepted and merged into
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{t+1}
.
Otherwise, all candidate transformations from the iteration are rejected. Upon acceptance, the baseline model is retrained on the updated feature set, and new feature relevance scores are computed, closing the feedback loop.

3.6
Feedback Memory and Termination

MedFeat maintains a memory of accepted and rejected feature transformations across iterations.
Rejected features are tracked to discourage repeated low-value proposals, while accepted features
influence future relevance estimates and island sampling. This improves search behavior by reinforcing feature patterns that consistently enhance performance or importance and increasing computational efficiency by reducing redundant exploration
(
22
)
.
The procedure terminates after
T
iterations, yielding a final augmented model trained on

\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\oplus\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}
, which is then evaluated once on the
held-out test set.

Experiments

4.1
Datasets and prediction tasks

We benchmark three well-established healthcare tabular datasets spanning both longitudinal panel data and hospital electronic health records: Infections in Oxfordshire Research Database (IORD), Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) IV, and the Health and Retirement Study (HRS)
(
31
;
17
;
16
;
8
;
28
)
. Selected datasets were preprocessed following established pipelines or previous studies
(
24
;
31
;
11
)
. Each task is formulated as a supervised binary classification with labels in
\{0,1\}
and evaluated. Additional details about the datasets and the pre-processing pipeline can be found in Appendix
E
.

A key motivation for this suite is that the feature space is heterogeneous: datasets include both static covariates (e.g., time-invariant attributes) and repeated measurements at different time points with irregular missingness patterns. The selected datasets also contain a rich feature space and wide coverage of underlying demographic distributions.

4.2
Baselines and Downstream Models

We compare MedFeat against a hierarchy of baselines designed to isolate the contribution of (i) feature engineering in general, (ii) classical automated feature engineering, and (iii) recent LLM-based feature engineering methods. Concretely, we report results for training downstream learners on the raw feature set (no feature engineering), classical automated feature engineering baselines (e.g., AutoFeat
(
15
)
, OpenFE
(
34
)
), and LLM-based baselines (CAAFE
(
14
)
, OCTree
(
22
)
, FeatLLM
(
13
)
), alongside MedFeat.

To avoid confounding feature discovery with extensive hyperparameter tuning, we follow a two-regime evaluation: an
untuned
setting (default learner hyperparameters) used during the iterative feature engineering loop and a
tuned
setting where extensive hyperparameter optimization (HPO) is applied to quantify the best achievable performance and study whether feature engineering gains persist under HPO. We focus on two widely used tabular learners with complementary inductive biases: logistic regression (LR) and XGBoost (XGB). Logistic regression is widely used in clinical research, known for its explainability and lightweight nature. We then consider XGBoost, which is a strong tree-based model that demonstrates highly competitive performance against neural methods in clinical tabular data.

4.3
Implementation details

We use a stratified random split with 60% of the data as the training set, 20% for the validation set, and 20% for the held-out test set, respectively. The validation set is used both for selecting successful feature transformations and for computing explainability signals. The primary evaluation metric is the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC), with additional threshold-dependent metrics, such as the F1 score, computed using a validation-selected threshold (Youden’s
J
). We use GPT-4o to generate feature transformations as executable Python functions, with temperature
0.7

(
23
)
.

We follow the report’s default configuration for iterative search: 3 feature-engineering iterations, 2 islands per iteration, and an island size of 3, with features sampled proportional to normalized SHAP importance. We employ a winner-takes-all selection within each iteration and accept the best island if it clears a tolerance buffer
\beta
relative to the current baseline validation performance; the report uses
\beta=0.01
as a default. After acceptance, we retrain the baseline model and recompute SHAP values to close the feedback loop. Additional details on implementations can be found in Appendix
F
.

Main results

We summarize predictive performance across all datasets and tasks in Table

We repeat all experiments on three random splits of each dataset and report the averaged values and standard deviations of AUC and F1 scores, with percentage improvements computed relative to the corresponding baseline. Across these settings, we observe three consistent patterns: First, MedFeat improves the performance of the predictions against the baseline and outperforms existing methods almost all the time. Second, MedFeat demonstrates robust improvement with hyperparameter optimization, which is consistent with the model-aware design. Third, MedFeat is more reliable than existing methods, which often improve on one metric while degrading another.

In the default regime, MedFeat yields consistent improvements for both downstream models. For XGBoost (Table
2
), MedFeat achieves the best AUC on all five tasks and the best F1 on three tasks. For 24-hour inpatient mortality, MedFeat increases AUC from 0.686 to 0.740, yielding a 7.87% improvement. Ten-year mortality also improves in both AUC (from 0.689 to 0.708) and F1 (from 0.238 to 0.268). On the remaining tasks, the improvements are smaller but generally positive. Importantly, Table
2
also shows that alternative feature engineering can trade off ranking quality and threshold performance in undesirable ways; for example, some methods produce higher F1 while substantially degrading AUC, suggesting that validation-driven selection may overfit a particular operating point. In contrast, MedFeat tends to improve AUC without degrading F1, providing a more stable improvement profile across tasks. Similar patterns are shown for logistic regression, where MedFeat achieves the best AUC and F1 on three tasks, and the second best for the rest. Compared with other feature engineering methods, the logistic regression table also highlights the instability of prior approaches: OpenFE
(
34
)
frequently reduces the AUC sharply, and some LLM-based methods show weaker or inconsistent gains, whereas MedFeat is consistently competitive on AUC while improving the F1 score more reliably.

We further assessed the performance after comprehensive hyperparameter optimization on both the baseline and MedFeat-augmented models in Table
3
. For XGBoost, MedFeat’s AUC gains shrink to near zero on several tasks. However, MedFeat still provides meaningful F1 improvements in this setting, most notably on the most imbalanced and challenging tasks, such as 24-hour inpatient mortality. For logistic regression, improvements are more persistent compared to the untuned setting. MedFeat improves AUC on ICU mortality, discharge the following day, and heart failure while also enhancing F1 on every task.

A key pattern reported is that the marginal benefit of feature engineering depends on the downstream learner: improvements for XGBoost tend to diminish as HPO becomes more exhaustive (since XGBoost can recover many patterns through tuning), whereas LR retains more persistent gains because engineered features can directly expand the representational capacity of a linear predictor. This interaction supports the claim that MedFeat is not merely a validation-score search procedure but a model-aware mechanism for injecting structure that the learner would otherwise be unable (or inefficient) to represent.

We demonstrate the effectiveness through an example. Starting from a baseline XGBoost model, MedFeat identifies age and the index of multiple deprivation as informative features. In one iteration, the LLM proposes an age–deprivation interaction feature. Adding this single feature improves validation AUC from 0.736 to 0.758. After iterative refinement, the final model achieves an AUC gain from 0.669 to 0.707, and the engineered interaction becomes a top contributor in the final explanation ranking (third by SHAP importance). 4.5
Ablations

As shown in Table
4
, we perform targeted ablations that remove essential components used by MedFeat. Removing the model-awareness component consistently decreases performance across all reported settings, with drops in both AUC and F1. The effect is particularly significant on the most imbalanced task, inpatient mortality, where F1 decreases by 35.9%. This suggests that model-aware prompting is especially important for operating-point performance under extreme imbalance, where feature constructions must target the learner’s representational gaps rather than generic transformations.
Disabling island creation also reduces performance in a similar pattern. Both AUC and F1 drop on almost all tasks, while AUC experiences a smaller reduction compared to the case without the model-awareness component. For ICU mortality prediction, removing island-based sampling shows no AUC loss but still results in a moderate F1 drop. It implies that island-based sampling may improve the local quality of the score distribution near the decision boundary and in the long tail, whereas AUC averages over all thresholds and can remain similar.
Removing feature-importance guidance yields the most consistently negative effect across tasks, with AUC drops of roughly 2–3% in all three settings (-1.92%, -3.31%, -2.24%) and notable F1 reductions (-10.88%, -26.19%, -9.30%). Compared to the other ablations, this component is the most uniformly significant, which supports the design claim of MedFeat: explanation scores are not only used for post-hoc interpretation but also act as a control signal that both prioritizes the feature space and grounds the LLM’s proposals in what the model actually uses. This pattern indicates that MedFeat’s gains are driven by targeted, importance-conditioned generation (stability and relevance), with model-awareness and island sampling acting as key amplifiers when the task is scarce in signal and highly sensitive to inefficient exploration.
More details of the implementations of ablation studies can be found in Appendix
D
.

Generalizability of MedFeat among Datasets.

We examine the effectiveness and generalizability of features generated by MedFeat (Figure

Adding the transferred MedFeat features yields a consistent AUC improvement and noticeably reduces run-to-run variability. Concretely, the model without the transferred features fluctuates across runs, whereas the model with MedFeat features remains relatively flat at a higher AUC. This pattern demonstrates the effectiveness and unique advantages of LLM-based feature engineering, where external knowledge is injected into feature constructions that transfer across cohorts, thereby improving and stabilizing performance even when distribution changes.

Robustness under Temporal Shift at Deployment.

We further test robustness under temporal shift, which commonly occurs in clinical practice (Figure
3
). We mimic two scenarios when using IORD data to predict 24-hour mortality: In the first instance, we select the data before 1st January 2020 and perform feature engineering using MedFeat
(

)
. We saved the trained model and feature transformations. Then we perform transformations for data from 2020 to 2024 and make predictions using the trained model. In the second scenario, we retrain the model every year without new features, using all previous years’ data, and evaluate the performance. The ”retrained” baseline is unstable over time, with a sharp performance drop in 2021 and only partial recovery in later years. Second, the MedFeat-augmented inference curve is more stable and, in most years, achieves markedly higher AUROC than the retrained baseline. This indicates that MedFeat features can act as drift-robust representations: they preserve predictive structure that remains useful out-of-time and can reduce the need for frequent retraining to maintain performance.

From a deployment perspective, MedFeat offers a complementary pathway: instead of repeatedly re-training the model, it introduces a small set of engineered variables that encode higher-level clinical regularities expected to be more invariant than raw measurements or coding frequencies
(
18
)
. This aligns with MedFeat’s design: explanation-guided generation prioritizes features tied to the model’s most stable predictive signals, and model-aware prompting encourages constructions that capture clinically meaningful mechanisms rather than cohort-specific correlations. As a result, the system can improve stability under drift while keeping changes explainable.

5
Conclusion

We presented MedFeat, an explainability-driven and model-aware LLM feature engineering framework for clinical tabular prediction. MedFeat replaces extensive feature search with an iterative loop guided by explanation signals, uses importance-weighted sampling to keep generation compact and targeted in high-dimensional data, and adapts feature proposals to downstream model capacity. Across multiple clinical tasks and datasets, MedFeat improves performance in the realistic low-budget regime and remains competitive under extensive optimization, demonstrating robustness under distribution drift. These results suggest that MedFeat offers a practical path to scalable, interpretable, and deployable feature engineering in healthcare. For future work, the framework could benefit from clinicians’ input, and whether reinforcement learning can be used to improve performance with such signals remains a promising direction.

Impact Statement

MedFeat improves clinical tabular prediction for various outcomes by producing engineered features with lower tuning and engineering burden, which support scalable, interpretable deployment across healthcare settings.
MedFeat is also designed to be privacy-preserving by avoiding sending patient-level examples to online LLMs.

Appendix D
Additional Implementation details

Island Generation.

To account for strong collinearities, we treat repeated measurements of the same underlying variable as a temporal group when computing and averaging explainability signals for feature selection. For instance, if the feature set contains 3 heart rate measurements at different time points, we treat them as a single feature when computing SHAP values and sampling. This approach addresses the collinearity of temporal features in clinical tabular data and promotes LLMs to discover subtle patterns in time series
(
21
)
. When creating each feature island, we randomly choose whether to sample static features or aggregated temporal groups. If a temporal group is chosen, all raw measurement features will be included in that island.

MedFeat Transformation.

For each candidate transformation, we execute the generated code in a restricted environment (pandas/numpy only) and apply it
independently
to
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}
to prevent leakage. We reject invalid transformations (non-numeric outputs, infinities/NaNs beyond handling rules, runtime errors) and allow up to three retries for transient generation failures.

Downstream Model Common Setup.

Logistic regression is trained with an
\ell_{2}
penalty, a Limited-memory BFGS solver, and a balanced class weight, reflecting a setting where linear models benefit from explicit feature construction but cannot recover non-linear structure on their own. Before fitting the logistic regression model, missing values in the original data were imputed using the median in the training set. Then, the data is standardized based on the scaler fitted on the training set. XGBoost uses the
scale_pos_weight
parameter, with the ratio being the ratio between negative and positive samples. XGBoost further employs histogram-based tree methods and binary logistic as objective functions. No imputation was performed for the XGBoost model unless stated elsewhere
(
6
)
.

We introduce comprehensive hyperparameter optimization to validate the effectiveness of the framework. We use Optuna with a budget of 400 trials and an early stopping mechanism. We use a random sampler and maximize AUC. Besides the common setup mentioned above for downstream models, the search spaces following previous studies

Baseline Implementations.

In this section, we summarize the implementation and configuration of baseline feature engineering methods in our study. We implemented all baseline feature engineering methods by following their original implementations and default recommended settings and evaluated them under the same data splits, preprocessing, and downstream learners as MedFeat. For baselines that are iteration-based, we ran 3 iterations to match MedFeat’s feature-engineering rounds unless stated otherwise. We did not perform HPO for existing methods since MedFeat already demonstrated superior performance against them without HPO.

AutoFeat.

We introduced AutoFeat as a classical automated feature engineering baseline. However, most of the execution runs exceeded 12 hours and failed to return results, leading to an insufficient set of results for a fair comparison. This runtime issue has also been noted in prior work when applying exhaustive operator-based feature engineering to wide real-world tables
(
1
)
. Therefore, we excluded AutoFeat from our study due to its inapplicability.

OpenFE.

OpenFE was introduced to fit the training set once and transform the validation and test sets to prevent information leakage.

CAAFE.

We followed the original implementation of CAAFE and ran for 3 iterations. We changed the cross-validation setting in CAAFE to a single split of the training, validation, and test sets to ensure a fair comparison with MedFeat, which also enables finding the best threshold for classification. Other settings are kept as defaults in the original implementation.

OCTree.

We followed the original implementation of OCTree and ran for 3 steps. Since the released version is context-agnostic, we re-implemented the context-aware version of OCTree, following the original study configuration.

FeatLLM.

We followed the original implementation of FeatLLM and ran it with 5 ensemble queries. We performed missing value imputation for the entire dataset before execution, as required by the framework’s configuration. Few-shot data samples were removed from the prompt to ensure data privacy and safety. Since FeatLLM uses the default threshold for classification, we did not report the F1 score for comparison.

Ablation Studies Implementations.

For the framework without model-awareness, we removed the related prompt in the generation process. For the framework without island-based sampling, we chose the number of islands to be equal to 1 and the number of features in each island to be the number of features in the dataset. For the framework without feature importance, we sampled features uniformly to islands and removed the importance-related prompt in the generation process.

H.2
Newly Generated Feature Importance and Explainability.

We also inspect where MedFeat’s accepted features rank in the final model’s importance ranking (Figure
4
. We observed that accepted features from MedFeat frequently appear among the top-ranked contributors under SHAP values.

We also observed a clear model-dependent pattern. For logistic regression, MedFeat features dominate the top-10 set in several tasks. For example, they constitute roughly three-quarters of the top-10 features for heart failure and about two-thirds for discharge the next day. This aligns with MedFeat’s model-aware design: linear models cannot express nonlinear effects and interactions unless they are explicitly encoded, so well-constructed engineered features often become primary explanatory factors. For XGBoost, the proportion is generally lower but remains non-trivial, ranging from roughly one-fifth on 24-hour ICU mortality to around one-third on inpatient and 10-year mortality, and reaching about three-fifths on discharge the next day. This is consistent with the higher representational capacity of tree ensembles, which can recover many patterns from raw inputs; nevertheless, the fact that MedFeat features still frequently enter the top-10 set suggests they capture signals that are efficient for the model to use rather than redundant remappings.

Appendix I
Limitations of the Study

One potential limitation of MedFeat is that the framework relies on explanation-based signals to guide sampling and prompting. If the data has strong collinearity or noise, the importance ordering might vary across splits. This can be mitigated by combining the framework with classical feature selection methods such as L1 and L2 regularization, which can be easily integrated with MedFeat.
