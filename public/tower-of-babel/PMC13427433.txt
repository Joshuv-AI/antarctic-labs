Cryptococcus gattii CNS infection presenting as a periaqueductal “soap-bubble” lesion causing obstructive hydrocephalus with refractory CSF diversion
Authors: Anisha Mittal, Jayanthraj Gone, Alex A Dos Santos, Gaurav Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryptococcosis is a potentially systemic fungal infection that may present in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised individuals, of which Cryptococcus gattii is the rarer cause. The most severe form of cryptococcosis involves the CNS. We present a case of C. gattii infection of the CNS in a 63-year-old male presenting to the emergency department mainly for a ground level fall, declining physical and mental activity, and constitutional symptoms. Initial workup revealed sinus bradycardia, hypertension, moderate hyponatremia, and malnutritional immunocompromise. The initial brain MRI with and without contrast showed a 2.1 × 1.6 × 1.4 cm complex, multicystic, necrotic, heterogeneously enhancing lesion (“soap-bubble” appearance) occupying the left posteromedial thalamus, tectum, and periaqueductal region slightly crossing the midline with dilation of the lateral and third ventricles. Management for stroke concern was conducted, VP shunt was placed, and C. gattii was found in samples from bronchoscopy and lumbar puncture. Antifungal therapy was started, but worsening findings on repeat neuroimaging required intensive care and external ventricular drain placement with unchanged subsequent imaging findings. Despite intravenous steroids for intracranial edema and prophylaxis for seizures, the patient progressively declined overall. Ultimately, prognosis was guarded, leading to discharge with hospice care. On a whole, this case captures notable imaging characteristics of C. gattii CNS infection involving invasive interventions, which very few cases have accomplished thus far. Particularly, it highlights the phenomenon of a “soap-bubble” lesion causing obstructive hydrocephalus, which is uncommon.Keywords: Cryptococcus gattii, Perivascular spaces, Hydrocephalus, Ventriculoperitoneal shunt, Cerebrospinal fluid

Introduction

Cryptococcosis is a systemic fungal infection caused by two species: Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii [1]. C. neoformans is an opportunistic fungus that harmlessly colonizes the human airway from inhalation and is associated with infection in immunocompromised states [2]. C. gattii is a fungus native to Canada, South America, and Southeast Asia more so associated with infection in immunocompetent individuals [3]. A majority of cryptococcal infections are due to C. neoformans (95% of cases), but cases due to C. gattii (5% of cases) have been increasing recently [1]. The onset of disease is insidious given the long delay between symptomatic onset and diagnosis [2]. The most severe and common presentation involves the central nervous system (CNS) called neurocryptococcosis, which consists of meningitis and encephalitis [4]. The pathogenesis involves hematogenous spread from the lungs to the basal cisterns. From there, the fungi may spread via perivascular (Virchow-Robin) spaces to the basal ganglia, internal capsules, thalami, and brainstem [5]. Clinical features of neurocryptococcosis include fever, malaise, headache, seizures, altered mental status, nausea, vomiting, signs of increased intracranial pressure (ICP), and focal neurological deficits depending on location of fungal burden [6]. Diagnosis of cryptococcosis is confirmed by lateral flow test for antigens, direct examination using stains, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and cultures of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or blood [7]. Both species are differentiated using canavanine glycine bromothymol blue agar, in which C. gattii grows but C. neoformans does not [7]. Management of both conditions consists of short-term amphotericin B with flucytosine followed by long-term fluconazole [8].

Imaging features of neurocryptococcosis generally pertain to three areas depending on the location of infection: the meninges (meningitis), the brain parenchyma, and the perivascular spaces [9]. The former two examples tend to occur in immunocompetent hosts whereas the latter example occurs in immunocompromised hosts [10]. Neurocryptococcosis can demonstrate a variety of findings, but extremely immunocompromised patients without therapy show minimal meningeal enhancement while patients receiving therapy show more meningeal enhancement in general [9]. The range of appearance includes hydrocephalus (communicating or noncommunicating), dilated perivascular spaces (due to mucoid material), meningeal enhancement, mass lesions, miliary nodules, and choroid plexitis [10]. CT scans display nonspecific or even normal features in roughly 40% of patients [11]. The next most common findings are diffuse atrophy, hydrocephalus, mass lesions, and cerebral edema [12]. Cryptococcomas (collections of fungi, inflammatory cells, and mucoid material invading into brain parenchyma) appear as round, isointense or hypointense lesions manifesting more often as granulomas (torulomas) rather than gelatinous pseudocysts, especially in immunocompetent patients [9]. MRI is the imaging modality of choice for neurocryptococcosis as it is more sensitive to abnormalities and punctate masses [13]. T1 with contrast can show pachymeningeal and leptomeningeal enhancement and T2 FLAIR with contrast shows subarachnoid hyperintensities [9]. Cryptococcomas are hypointense on T1, hyperintense on T2 or FLAIR, and variably enhancing on T1 with contrast [14]. Dilated perivascular spaces can combine into pseudocysts to give a “soap-bubble” appearance, which is hypointense on T1, hyperintense on T2, and variable on FLAIR [9]. Choroid plexitis, usually occurring alongside an infectious rather than noninfectious process, involves unilateral or bilateral enlargement and considerable enhancement of plexuses [14]. Moreover, possible entrapment of ventricular parts can cause cystic dilation and surrounding edema [15]. Currently, there is limited research on neurocryptococcosis, particularly involving C. gattii despite neurosurgical interventions, leading to some unique radiological features.

Case presentation

A 63-year-old Caucasian male with a past medical history of tobacco use, hypertension, and symptomatic bradycardia presented to the emergency department as a transfer for neurosurgical evaluation from an outside hospital. The patient presented for multiple reasons including a ground level fall, physical inactivity, headache, altered mental status, forgetfulness, appetite loss, weight loss, dyspnea, chills, fatigue, and malaise. Initial vitals and labs showed sinus bradycardia, hypertension, moderate hyponatremia, and hyperlipidemia. Initial physical exam was significant for Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 6, obtundation, disorientation, frailty, cachexia, and slow movements to stimulation. Fundoscopic exam showed papilledema. However, no additional focal neurological deficits were noted. Initial CT imaging at the outside hospital showed dilated lateral and third ventricles (Fig. 1), a 2.0 × 1.6 cm spiculated mass in the right medial upper lung abutting the superior vena cava (SVC), and moderate colonic stool burden (not shown). The initial brain MRI with and without contrast showed a 2.1 × 1.6 × 1.4 cm complex, necrotic, heterogeneously enhancing lesion occupying the left posteromedial thalamus, tectum, and periaqueductal region slightly crossing the midline with dilation of lateral and third ventricles (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Differential diagnoses at this stage included infection (likely opportunistic), primary lung malignancy with brain metastasis, primary brain malignancy with pulmonary metastasis, or granulomatous disease. We conducted initial management typical for a stroke concern, and neurosurgery placed a VP shunt. A repeat chest CT showed a slightly enlarged right lung mass (2.6 × 1.8 cm) and VP shunt placement with minimal right anterior neck subcutaneous emphysema and right upper quadrant (RUQ) pneumoperitoneum (not shown). A repeat head CT showed the VP shunt placed through the right frontal lobe with reduced ventricular sizes, and minimal adjacent pneumocephalus (Fig. 4). Pulmonology performed a bronchoscopy with studies (including Cryptococcus antigen testing) positive for C. gattii. Hematology-oncology advised avoidance of steroids and radiotherapy given concern for immunosuppression. Interventional radiology performed lumbar puncture with CSF studies (significant for opening pressure >25 cm H2O, yellow, cloudy (translucent) appearance, protein 538 mg/dL (elevated), glucose 20 mg/dL (decreased), and white blood cell count <50 cell/mm3 with 89% lymphocytes). Cryptococcus antigen testing was positive for C. gattii and negative for malignancy. Infectious disease attributed low CD4/CD8 ratio to malnutrition despite negative HIV test, started a short-term (2 week duration) combination of amphotericin B and flucytosine with plan for high dose (400 mg for 8 weeks) followed by low dose (200 mg for 1 year) fluconazole, and recommended PET scan outpatient. Salt tablets were given for hyponatremia. The patient was upgraded to the ICU for increased unresponsiveness and after a head CT showed increased ventriculomegaly and gyro-sulcal effacement despite the VP shunt in place (Fig. 5). Neurosurgery evaluated the initial shunt for patency and placed an external ventricular drain (EVD) through the left frontal lobe with unchanged findings on follow up head CT (Fig. 6) while abdominal and pelvic CT was negative for metastases (not shown). Dexamethasone and levetiracetam were started for edema and seizure prophylaxis, respectively, with clearance from infectious disease. Neurology recommended no additional seizure diagnostics and therapy. Urology placed a Foley and added tamsulosin for urinary retention. Blood, sputum, and urine cultures grew MRSA and infectious disease added linezolid and ceftriaxone. The patient’s mentation progressively worsened with the further neuroimaging showing no significant interval improvement (not shown). Temporary nasogastric tube feeds were administered due to concern for aspiration from lack of airway protection. However, palliative care recognized the poor prognosis of the patient, who was ultimately discharged with hospice.

Discussion

We have presented a case of neurocryptococcosis, specifically involving C. gattii, in the setting of invasive interventions (including VP shunt and EVD drain). Initial exam findings indicate some evidence of features typical of CNS infection and elevated ICP despite no specific neurological deficits or seizures. The initial head CT at outside facility characterizes findings showcasing noncommunicating hydrocephalus with the disconnect in its most typical location, close to the cerebral aqueduct, leading to ductal stenosis (Fig. 1). However, it does not necessarily portray the lesion responsible for the hydrocephalus as clearly. The initial brain MRI captures the abnormality and other resulting features with higher resolution. The lesion appeared hyperintense on DWI with a corresponding high ADC value (no restricted diffusion in the central area). SWI showed no significant susceptibility with no internal hemorrhage. It describes the mass as necrotic and multicystic as having a “soap bubble” appearance, likely meaning it has gelatinous pseudocysts containing collections of dilated perivascular spaces (Fig. 2). These pseudocysts appear hypointense and hyperintense on T1-weighted BRAVO and T2-weighted sequences without contrast, respectively, which is echoed by other cases (Fig. 2). The T1-weighted sequence with gadolinium contrast describes the lesion as heterogeneously enhancing, which is also consistent with such lesions (Fig. 3). Delayed postcontrast T1 sequence showed only patchy enhancement. The T2-weighted FLAIR sequence demonstrates certain parts with increased and decreased signal with minimal edema (Fig. 3). The cystic components show signal intensity that is not completely suppressed and track the perivascular spaces. However, T2-weighted FLAIR can present a variable amount of intensity with no single consistent appearance [14]. Altogether, the features indicate that the lesion is consistent with dilated perivascular spaces rather than a tumor. The Evans index is > 0.3, the third ventricular width is >10 mm, and increased transependymal CSF flow (Fig. 3). Given the collective location and mass effect of the pseudocysts externally onto the lumen of the cerebral aqueduct causing blockage and no significant flow, the features typical of noncommunicating hydrocephalus on the CT are also replicated by the MRI (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The placement of the right frontal VP shunt led to a transient improvement of the hydrocephalus given the smaller size of the lateral and third ventricles with minimal pneumocephalus and no change in the appearance of the lesion from the repeat head CT without contrast (Fig. 4). However, the subsequent head CT showed features of worsening hydrocephalus despite no manipulation or dislodgement of the VP shunt, indicating its inefficacy likely from the intracranial fungal burden and increased protein and debris buildup after neurosurgery evaluated it for patency (Fig. 5). In addition, the head CT after placement of the left frontal EVD displayed no significant improvement of neither the hydrocephalus nor the lesion from the preoperative one (Fig. 6). The final head CT once again echoed the aforementioned findings. An additional point to note is that apart from the hydrocephalus, the close proximity of the pseudocysts to the midbrain may have contributed to the progressive decline of the patient’s alertness and consciousness [7]. Overall, this case presents most features typical of cryptococcosis in the CNS. Nonetheless, one lesion not shown in this particular case is a toruloma, which is a collection of cells and debris more so in immunocompetent patients appearing hypointense on T1-weighted, hyperintense on T2-weighted and FLAIR, and variable on T1-weighted with contrast [14]. However, given that our patient was immunocompromised despite being negative for HIV, it is less likely for torulomas to manifest in such a circumstance instead of gelatinous pseudocysts [9]. Another lesion not seen in this patient is a miliary nodule. Technically, these nodules tend to show up mostly as part of pulmonary instead of CNS cryptococcosis [15]. Though meningeal enhancement and choroid plexitis possibly with complications (i.e. entrapment of ventricular horns and the resulting cystic dilation with edema) on T1-weighted MRI with contrast are relatively common in neurocryptococcosis, they are not seen in this case [15]. This case demonstrates rare but unique imaging features of C. gattii infection involving the CNS, especially in a patient with malnutrition, immunocompromise, and invasive interventions, which not many cases have done so far. Moreover, it is likely one of the first cases to capture gelatinous pseudocysts near the periaqueductal region, leading to noncommunicating hydrocephalus among all features.

Conclusion

C. gattii is a fungus capable of causing cryptococcosis of the CNS, its most severe infectious representation, more commonly in immunocompetent individuals. However, the occurrence of neurocryptococcosis in an immunocompromised patient involving a VP shunt malfunction and an EVD drain is uncommon. This case showcases such an instance in a middle-aged male associated with uniqueradiological features including gelatinous pseudocysts near the periaqueductal area and noncommunicating hydrocephalus. Additional imaging characteristics of CNS cryptococcosis (such as choroid plexitis and leptomeningeal enhancement) are not manifested in this case, which may partly be due to the periaqueductal location of the “soap-bubble” lesion.
