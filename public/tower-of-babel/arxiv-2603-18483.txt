Precise Performance of Linear Denoisers in the Proportional Regime
Reza Ghane ∗, Danil Akhtiamov ∗, Babak Hassibi

Abstract
In the present paper we study the performance of linear denoisers for noisy data of the form 𝐱 + 𝐳 \mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z} , where 𝐱 ∈ ℝ d \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} is the desired data with zero mean and unknown covariance 𝚺 \bm{\Sigma} , and 𝐳 ∼ 𝒩 ⁡ ( 0 , 𝚺 𝐳 ) \mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}) is additive noise. Since the covariance 𝚺 \bm{\Sigma} is not known, the standard Wiener filter cannot be employed for denoising. Instead we assume we are given samples 𝐱 1 , … , 𝐱 n ∈ ℝ d \mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} from the true distribution. A standard approach would then be to estimate 𝚺 \bm{\Sigma} from the samples and use it to construct an “empirical” Wiener filter. However, in this paper, motivated by the denoising step in diffusion models, we take a different approach whereby we train a linear denoiser 𝐖 \mathbf{W} from the data itself. In particular, we synthetically construct noisy samples 𝐱 ^ i \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i} of the data by injecting the samples with Gaussian noise with covariance 𝚺 1 ≠ 𝚺 𝐳 \bm{\Sigma}_{1}\neq\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}} and find the best 𝐖 \mathbf{W} that approximates 𝐖 ​ 𝐱 ^ i ≈ 𝐱 i \mathbf{W}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\approx\mathbf{x}_{i} in a least-squares sense. In the proportional regime n d → κ > 1 \frac{n}{d}\rightarrow\kappa>1 we use the Convex Gaussian Min-Max Theorem (CGMT) to analytically find the closed form expression for the generalization error of the denoiser obtained from this process. Using this expression one can optimize over 𝚺 1 \bm{\Sigma}_{1} to find the best possible denoiser. Our numerical simulations show that our denoiser outperforms the “empirical” Wiener filter in many scenarios and approaches the optimal Wiener filter as κ → ∞ \kappa\rightarrow\infty .

Introduction and Related Works

Data denoising is a fundamental problem arising in many areas of data science, signal processing, control theory, and machine learning. Denoisers have been successfully applied to signal processing
(
14
)
, image processing
(
11
;
28
;
27
)
and generative AI
(
22
;
21
)
.

Arguably the most widely known denoiser, the Wiener Filter
(
26
;
15
)
, dates back to the 1940s. However, constructing such filter in practice requires precise knowledge of the covariance of the data
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Noting that reliable estimation of the second-order statistics requires access to plethora of samples, other works, such as
11
, attempt to circumvent such need by
learning
denoisers
directly from data
.

To provide a pertinent and opportune motivation, denoisers are used as central building blocks in modern diffusion models
(
22
;
21
)
. In the context of diffusion, the denoiser
D_{\bm{\theta}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a neural network parametrized by its weights
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, such as UNet
(
12
)
or Diffusion Transformer (DiT)
(
19
)
. Simplifying and omitting certain technical details for ease of explanation, one can illustrate the core idea of denoising diffusion models as follows. Suppose we trained the denoiser
D_{\bm{\theta}}
to learn a “good quality” approximation
D_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{z}_{i})\approx\mathbf{x}_{i}

where
\mathbf{z}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathbf{z}}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
is artificially injected noise such that the signal to noise ratio (SNR) is very low, i.e.

\displaystyle d\sigma_{\mathbf{z}}^{2}\approx\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|^{2}\gg\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

(1)

Then, under (
1
), it would be reasonable to attempt generating new samples by dropping dependence of the denoiser on
\mathbf{x}
as
\mathbf{x}\sim D_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{z})\text{ where }\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathbf{z}}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
.

Motivated by classical works on learning denoisers as well as more recent works on diffusion, we would like to initiate a systematic study of the performances of denoisers.
As a starting point, we propose to study linear denoisers trained via the least squares objective.
If the number of samples
n
and the dimensionality of data
d
satisfy
n\gg d
, it is known that the least squares solution will converge to the Wiener Filter, whose performance has been studied extensively in the literature. As such, to make the problem interesting, we assume that
n
is proportional to
d
.

In the proportional regime
n
, the number of samples, and
d
, the number of features, grow together such that
\frac{n}{d}\rightarrow\kappa
. This is different from classical works in statistics and control theory, where, usually,
d
is fixed and
n\rightarrow\infty
. Arguably, the main technical difference between the two is that one can reliably estimate the covariance of the data reliably when
n\gg d^{2}
, but, generally speaking, cannot do so if
n=\theta(d)
. The latter in particular means that the Wiener Filter cannot be recovered precisely in the proportional regime, as it is defined based on the covariance of the data.

Understanding the precise asymptotics of various statistical inference problems in the proportional regime has been the subject of study of many works in the past decade
(
23
;
18
;
10
;
16
;
25
;
13
;
17
;
4
;
2
;
8
;
3
;
7
)
. Analyzing the performances of learning algorithms in the proportional regime requires a different toolbox from classical statistical work, the main two of which have proven to be the Approximate Message Passing (AMP)
(
6
)
and the Convex Gaussian Min-Max (CGMT)
(
24
)
frameworks. For the purposes of the present work, we employ the latter (CGMT) approach.

2
Problem Setting and Preliminaries

We use bold font for vectors and matrices and normal font for scalars in this exposition. Consider the following data denoising problem:

Given
n
i.i.d data points sampled from the normal distribution
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{\Sigma})
with
an unknown
covariance
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and a noise distribution
\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}})
with a
known
covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, design a linear denoiser
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, that takes noisy data of the form
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z}
as input and “suppresses the noise” and outputs
\mathbf{W}\hat{\mathbf{x}}\approx\mathbf{x}
as accurately as possible. More formally,
\mathbf{W}
should minimize the following generalization error objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{W}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{\Sigma})}\|\mathbf{W}^{T}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}

(2)

Note that (
2
) can be simplified as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\|(\mathbf{W}^{T}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}=\text{Tr}\left((\mathbf{W}^{T}-\mathbf{I})\bm{\Sigma}(\mathbf{W}^{T}-\mathbf{I})^{T}\right)+\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{W}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{W}\right)

(3)

Minimizing (
3
) over
\mathbf{W}
directly leads to

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}=\left(\bm{\Sigma}+\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}

(4)

The denoiser (
4
) is known as the
Wiener Filter
in the literature. By construction, (
4
) achieves the optimal generalization error among all linear denoisers. However, finding (
4
) requires precise knowledge of
\bm{\Sigma}
. Recovering
\bm{\Sigma}
from data with good accuracy might not be feasible when
n
and
d
are proportional to each other, unlike the classical case
n\gg d
. As such, one natural approach to the denoising problem would be to take the
empirical Wiener Filter

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}=\left(\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}+\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}\right)^{-1}\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}

(5)

Where the empirical covariance
\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}
is defined as follows via the sample data matrix

\mathbf{X}^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}^{1}&\mathbf{X}^{2}&\ldots\mathbf{X}^{n}\end{pmatrix}\allowbreak\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
with
\mathbf{X}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
being the rows generated independently:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}}{n}\right)\mathbf{X}

(6)

Naturally, the quality of the denoiser (
5
) depends on the quality of approximation of the true covariance
\bm{\Sigma}
by the empirical covariance (
6
). The latter does not have to be good in general, as
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
has
\Theta(d^{2})
degrees of freedom and (
6
) attempts to estimate
\bm{\Sigma}
from
n=\Theta(d)
samples. As such, we would like to consider an alternative approach to minimizing (
2
) as well. The approach we propose is outlined below and is based on learning
\mathbf{W}
directly from data, but the main technicality lies in how the training data should be generated from
\mathbf{X}
.

We divide the training data
\mathbf{X}
into
N
batches
\mathbf{X}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times d},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{N}\times d}

In other words,
\mathbf{X}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times d},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{N}\times d}
are defined via
\mathbf{X}^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{T}&\mathbf{X}_{2}^{T}&\ldots&\mathbf{X}_{N}^{T}\end{pmatrix}

. Using
\mathbf{X}_{t}^{i}
to denote the
i
th row of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
, note that the data points
\mathbf{X}^{i}_{t}
and
\mathbf{X}^{i^{\prime}}_{t^{\prime}}
are independent whenever
(i,t)\neq(i^{\prime},t^{\prime})
and each row satisfies

\mathbf{X}^{i}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})\text{ for }t=1,\dots,N\text{ and }i=1,\dots,n_{t}

Furthermore, consider independent noise vectors

\mathbf{Z}^{i}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ where }\mathbf{Z}^{i}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{t}),\text{ }t=1,\dots,N,i=1,\dots,n_{t}

We construct noisy data batches
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{t}:=\mathbf{X}_{t}+\mathbf{Z}_{t},\text{ }t=1,\dots,N
and train a denoiser
\mathbf{W}_{\text{lsq}}
as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{\text{lsq}}:=\argmin_{\mathbf{W}}\Bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{X}\Bigr\|_{F}^{2}

(7)

Here,
\hat{\mathbf{X}}
is the full noisy data matrix defined as

\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{1}^{T}&\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{2}^{T}&\ldots&\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{N}^{T}\end{pmatrix}
. The main results of the present work, namely Theorems
1
and
5
in Section
3
, address the following questions:

What is the generalization error (
2
) of the denoiser
\mathbf{W}_{\text{lsq}}
found via (
7
) for given
N,n_{1},\dots,n_{t},d,\bm{\Sigma}_{1},\dots,\bm{\Sigma}_{N}
?

Focusing on the case of
N=1
and relying on Theorem
1
, we also conduct extensive mathematical (Corollary
3
) as well as numerical (Section
5
) investigations of the following questions:

What is the optimal
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}
leading to the minimal generalization error (
2
) among linear denoisers trained via (
7
) in terms of
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}
? How can we approximate this optimal
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}
algorithmically given access only to
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
? Can we outperform the empirical Wiener filter (
5
) using such an approximation of the optimal
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}
?

We will also impose the following technical assumptions that are necessary for our analyses.

Assumptions 1
.

1.

\bm{\Sigma}
is invertible.

2.

\frac{n_{t}}{d}\to\kappa_{t}>0
as
n_{t},d\rightarrow\infty
for each
t=1,\dots,N
and
\kappa=\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{N}>1
.

Our Main Contributions:

•

We characterize the generalization error of
\mathbf{W}_{\text{lsq}}
found from (
7
) for arbitrary
N,n_{1},\dots,n_{t},d,\allowbreak\bm{\Sigma}_{1},\dots,\bm{\Sigma}_{N}
satisfying Assumptions
1
in Theorem
5
.

•

In the case of
N=1
, we derive much more explicit results, which are formulated in Theorem
1
, again assuming assumptions
1
hold.

•

We use Theorem
1
to find the optimal
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}
minimizing the generalization error for the speciailized case when
N=1
and both
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}
are scalar. Perhaps surprisingly, even in this case we see that the optimal
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}
is in general different from
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}
, meaning it’s better to take different noise distributions for training and testing. The result is presented in Corollary
3
.

•

In Section
5
, we propose an algorithm for approximating the optimal
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}
for the case
N=1
and arbitrary
\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}
. This algorithm is based on Theorem
1
. We then proceed to verify that in certain cases the
\mathbf{W}_{\text{lsq}}
resulting from (
7
) indeed outperforms the empirical Wiener filter (
5
).

3
Main Results

Since we use CGMT, our results are asymptotic, meaning that we characterize the error
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{lsq}})
via certain quantities
f_{n,d}
, such that
\frac{\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{lsq}})}{f_{n,d}}\to 1
as
n,d\to\infty
and
\frac{n}{d}\to\kappa>1
. For the ease of exposition, we just write equalities within the results of this section, but keep the reference to the asymptotic regime in the formulation to avoid confusion. We would like to begin with stating the results we obtained for
N=1
, as the expressions we get in this case are more explicit and thus allow for more insight.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
N=1
and Assumptions
1
hold. Then, for the denoiser
\mathbf{W}_{\text{lsq}}
found via (
7
), its generalization error (
2
) can be characterized asymptotically as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{lsq}})=\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{n-d}\text{Tr}\Bigl[\Bigl(\bm{\Sigma}+\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\Bigr)^{-1}\Bigl(\bm{\Sigma}+\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}\Bigr)\Bigr]\Bigr)\Bigl(\text{Tr}\Bigl[\bm{\Sigma}\Bigr]-\text{Tr}\Bigl[\Bigl(\bm{\Sigma}+\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\Bigr)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\Bigr)\Bigr]\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\text{Tr}\Bigl[\Bigl(\bm{\Sigma}+\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\Bigr)^{-1}\Bigl(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}-\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\Bigr)\Bigl(\bm{\Sigma}+\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\Bigr)^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}^{2}\Bigr]

(8)

to the case
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}

Corollary 2

Under the setting of Theorem

, assume in addition that
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}

\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}

Note that taking

, i.e. assuming

We obtain another corollary of Theorem

by treating the case of scalar
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}
in greater detail:

Corollary 3
.

Under the setting of Theorem
1
, assume in addition that
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}
are scalar matrices and define:

\bm{\Sigma}=\frac{c}{d}\mathbf{I}_{d}\text{ and }\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}=\frac{c_{\mathbf{z}}}{d}\mathbf{I}_{d}

Then minimizing the generalization error (

) of the denoiser found via (

) over
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}
leads to the following optimal generalization error:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{-(c+c_{\mathbf{z}})^{2}+4(\kappa-1)^{2}cc_{\mathbf{z}}}{4(\kappa-2)(\kappa-1)(c+c_{\mathbf{z}})}&(3-2\kappa)c+c_{\mathbf{z}}<0,\quad c+(3-2\kappa)c_{\mathbf{z}}<0\\

.

Corollary

4
Proof Technique and Sketches

Both proofs rely heavily on a result known in the literature as the Convex Gaussian Min-Max Theorem (CGMT). We refer the reader to
24
;
1
for a formal detailed treatment of the CGMT, but we will outline it briefly for completeness. The goal of the CGMT is to analyze properties of solutions of the objectives of the following form, called the
Primary Optimization (PO)
objective:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{w}}}\max_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{w}+\psi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})\quad\textbf{(PO)}

(10)

Here,
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{u}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{w}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are compact convex sets,
\psi
is convex in
\mathbf{u}
and concave in
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{G}
is an i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)

matrix
. To analyze (
10
), the CGMT framework introduces another objective, called
Auxillary Objective (AO)
, where
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)

vectors
:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{w}}}\max_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{g}^{T}\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{u}\|+\mathbf{h}^{T}\mathbf{u}\|\mathbf{w}\|+\psi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})\quad\textbf{(AO)}

(11)

Consider an arbitrary set
\mathbf{S}\subset\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{w}}
. Let
\mathbf{w}_{AO}
and
\mathbf{w}_{PO}
be any minimizers of (
11
) and (
10
) respectively. Assume that
\mathbf{w}_{AO}\in\mathbf{S}
holds with probability approaching 1 (w.p.a. 1), i.e. :

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{w}_{AO}\in\mathbf{S}\right)\to 1\text{ as }n,d\to\infty

Then
\mathbf{w}_{PO}\in\mathbf{S}
holds w.p.a. 1 as well, i.e.:

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{w}_{PO}\in\mathbf{S}\right)\to 1\text{ as }n,d\to\infty

Note that, formally speaking, we should introduce sequences of PO and AO to discuss convergence in probability. However, we suppress these formalities to ease exposition and refer the interested reader to
24
;
1
for a rigorous exposition.

Sketch of the proof of Theorems
1
and
5
: Recall that the linear denoiser
\mathbf{W}_{\text{lsq}}
is defined via:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{W}}\Bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{X}\Bigr\|_{F}^{2}=\min_{\mathbf{W}}\|\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{I})+\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}

(12)

Denoting the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{W}
by
\mathbf{w}_{i}
, we can rewrite (
12
) as:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\min_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}\|\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i})+\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w}_{i}\|^{2}

(13)

Introducing a set of Fenchel dual variables
\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
i=1,\dots,d
and writing
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{G}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}
, where
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is i.i.d. standard normal, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\min_{\mathbf{w}_{i}}\max_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{G}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}(\mathbf{w}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{i})+\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w}_{i}-\frac{\|\mathbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{4}

With a slight abuse of notation we drop the indices from
\mathbf{w}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}_{i}
and rewrite:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\min_{\mathbf{w}}\max_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{G}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{e}_{i})+\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w}-\frac{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}}{4}

Let
\mathbf{u}:=\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{e}_{i})
. Using a Lagrange multiplier and leveraging Sion’s minimax theorem:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\max_{\bm{\lambda}}\min_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}}\max_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w}+\bm{\lambda}^{T}\mathbf{u}-\bm{\lambda}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{e}_{i})-\frac{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}}{4}

(14)

Treating each term of (
14
) as a separate PO with an i.i.d. standard normal
\mathbf{G}
, we arrive at the following sum of AOs:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\max_{\bm{\lambda}}\min_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}}\max_{\mathbf{v}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\mathbf{h}^{T}\mathbf{v}+\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\mathbf{g}^{T}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{w}+\bm{\lambda}^{T}\mathbf{u}-\bm{\lambda}^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{e}_{i})-\frac{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}}{4}

Denoting
\beta=\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}
and performing optimization over the direction

6
Conclusion and Future Directions

In this work we rigorously derived the precise asymptotics of a linear denoiser trained using the least-squares objective in the proportional regime. Possible future directions include investigating the expressions obtained in Theorem
5
to understand how using different batches of data may help with the performance. Another promising direction is to extend the analysis to the overparameterized regime where the number of parameters exceed the number of samples and the denoiser is trained via SGD or via a regularized objective.

While we maintained the assumption of data gaussianity throughout the present work, we suspect it is not necessary for Theorems
1
and
5
. As has been observed in the literature for many statistical inference problems, the generalization error often depends only on the first and second order statistics of the data, a phenomenon commonly referred to as Gaussian Universality (see
(
20
;
7
)
and references therein). In addition, Figure
2
suggests that a form of universality might hold for the denoisers trained through the least-squares objective too. We leave the latter as an interesting direction for future work.
