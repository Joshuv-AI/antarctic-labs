Tomato leaf disease and severity prediction using multi-task learning
Authors: Anusri Kadam, Parnika Jain, Srishti Tripathi, Tushar Garg, Aniket K Shahade, Shruti Sunnad, Priyanka V Deshmukh, Ketan Kotecha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate and timely identification of plant diseases along with their severity is critical for effective crop management and minimizing agricultural losses. While recent advances in deep learning have demonstrated high performance in plant disease classification, limited attention has been given to quantifying disease severity, which is essential for informed agronomic decision-making. To address this gap, this study proposes TomatoMTL, a unified multi-task learning framework for simultaneous disease classification and severity estimation of tomato leaf diseases from a single image. The proposed architecture employs a shared ResNet50-based convolutional backbone augmented with CBAM-based feature refinement, followed by task-specific branches for disease classification and severity prediction. Furthermore, a cross-task attention mechanism is introduced to enable interaction between disease-specific and severity-related features, thereby enhancing the robustness of severity estimation. To effectively leverage partially labeled data, a masking strategy is incorporated during training. Experimental evaluation on a publicly available tomato leaf disease severity dataset demonstrates that the proposed model achieves 97.85% disease classification accuracy and 77.66% severity prediction accuracy, outperforming state-of-the-art single-task classifiers including EfficientNetV2-S, ViT-B/16, and ConvNeXt-Tiny as well as existing multi-task learning baselines including Cross-Stitch Networks and MTAN. Comprehensive ablation studies confirm the individual contributions of CBAM, MixUp and CutMix augmentation, and the cross-task attention mechanism. Statistical significance analysis across five independent runs yields p-values less than 0.001 and Cohen’s d greater than 14, establishing the reliability of the reported improvements. Quantitative localization analysis reveals that the model achieves 89.4% Pointing Game accuracy, confirming that attention maps focus on biologically meaningful disease regions. The proposed framework represents a complete and effective approach for integrated plant disease analysis with strong potential for real-world precision agriculture applications.Keywords: Plant disease detection, multi-task learning, Disease severity estimation, Deep learning, Attention mechanisms, Cross-task interaction, Tomato leaf disease, Computer vision, Precision agriculture

Introduction

Plant diseases are one of the greatest threats to the production of crops worldwide, resulting in millions of dollars lost each year due to the effects of decreased yield and quality. Thus, it is critical to discover plant diseases at the earliest possible moment and as accurately as possible for managing plants effectively and promoting sustainable agricultural practices. Traditionally, plant diseases have been identified primarily by manual inspection by expert personnel, meaning that these identification methods require a significant amount of time, are highly subjective and are not easily scalable to all areas of agriculture, especially in large fields.

New advancements in deep learning have enabled scientists to develop automated methods of identifying plant diseases based on image data. The most prominent amongst these methods are convolutional neural networks (CNNs) coupled with transfer learning, which have been demonstrated to be capable of performing well in terms of the performance of classifications in plant diseases in laboratory-like settings [1, 2]. However, one problem when using these methods for identifying plant diseases is that they do not generalize well to real-world scenarios, which are affected by many environmental and physical factors such as changing illumination conditions, the presence of many different backgrounds and the extent to which the organism displays characteristics of the disease [3, 4].

Research has looked beyond categorizing diseases, and instead, have aimed to detect and localize the diseased areas in the images of plants. These methods include using the Mask R-CNN and the U-Net neural network architectures for pixel-level detection of infected areas [5, 6] and developing real-time object detection frameworks for more effective deployment objectives [7]. Unfortunately, although some of these methodologies have been implemented, the present techniques are primarily designed to identify and classify the type of disease rather than other information related to agricultural production.

Disease severity is important for making decisions regarding treatment levels as well as predicting future yields; nearly all current methodologies utilize either image segmentation methods or regression models to estimate severity levels [8–10]. However, the majority of these techniques view severity estimation as a separate task without considering that there is typically a direct correlation between the type of disease/organism and the progression of the disease over time. Several studies report that there continues to be a limited number of studies predicting disease severity compared to disease classification; therefore, there is still a substantial research gap that has to be addressed in regard to providing agricultural producers relevant information about how and when to treat agricultural products based on disease severity [11].

Multi-task learning (MTL) is an alternative that shows promise by allowing the joint-learning through shared components of related tasks. Several studies demonstrate that sharing components can improve overall accuracy due to better feature learning and balancing between tasks [12].

In recent years, developing and applying feature discrimination based on an attention mechanism to multiple task architectures is becoming a more effective way to obtain task-specific attention on features [13]. Works in this field have also added cross-task reasoning methods for tasks to share information [14]. However, in current works on plant disease detection, most multi-task learning systems use simple backbones with little modelling of inter-task dependencies [15]. As a result, there are several limitations that restrict the usage of the current methods.

Several datasets commonly used by researchers in this field do not reflect real-world conditions and therefore lead to biased learning and a lack of robustness [3, 4]. In addition, disease symptoms can appear in different ways and exist in degrees of contained symptoms so the training of the algorithm is complicated [16]. These deficiencies call for the development of a framework that performs simultaneously the classification of a disease and the prediction of severity, while modelling the relationship between these two tasks. To address this gap, we present TomatoMTL, a multi-task learning framework developed for tomato leaf disease detection that predicts both disease type and severity based on image data from a single sample input. Related work

Deep learning for plant disease classification

The process of using deep learning technology for the identification of plant diseases has been improved significantly through improved performance from Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) on classifying images of leaves by disease. Transfer Learning has been widely used for the identification of plant diseases given the limited amount of available labelled data. Studies have well documented the effective use of pre-trained CNNs to identify new diseases even in areas where only limited sample sizes were available [1]. This has also been accomplished in contexts where the available labelled dataset is class imbalanced or there is the possibility of negative transfer using a step-wise transfer learning approach [2].

There have been additional recent improvements to architectures of CNNs and how they compare to each other on multiple datasets. Richter and Kim [17] performed an extensive large-scale comparative study of many CNN architectures to show that there is no single CNN that when paired with all datasets will perform optimally. A similar strategy was employed by Shafik et al. [18] by proposing the use of hybrid CNN architectures to improve the ability of the CNNs to extract features at multi-scales resulting in improved classification accuracies on multiple datasets. Additionally, many new developments have improved the ability of CNN to learn representations of data attention mechanisms, SE blocks [19], and CBAM blocks [20] through adaptive refinement of the channels and spatial features.

However, with the addition of these new improvements to the CNN architectures, most of the literature focuses on a single task classification, the classification of disease, thereby excluding other agronomic attributes such as severity.

Disease localization using detection and segmentation

Different techniques have been used for disease localization on plants; many have included object detection and/or segmentation. Example use of instance segmentation has been found in Mask R-CNN, where it has been accurately applied to identify regions of disease infected area within a complex background [5]. Similarly, encoder-decoder architecture models have been tested using the U-Net model, producing an accurate pixel-level segmentation of infected areas [6]. Real-time detection models, particularly from the YOLO model family, have shown significant capability in field conditions; Soeb et al. [7] and Alhwaiti et al. [21] documented high efficiency and accuracy with YOLO frameworks for detecting disease in natural environments. While these methods improve the localization and visual fidelity of disease, they mostly only inform researchers as to where the disease is occurring, without modeling the progression of the disease or severity that could offer a more comprehensive perspective of the crops being observed.

Disease severity estimation

Evaluating disease severity is essential in agricultural decision-making since it informs yield losses and treatment planning. There are many methods to evaluate disease severity, but most are segmentation-based. In segmentation, disease severity is inferred from the percentage of infected leaf area. For example, Shi et al. [8] have proposed a lightweight U-Net for wheat disease severity estimation that has shown improved segmentation accuracy and reduced computational costs. Additionally, segmentation-driven frameworks like SegLearner [9] evaluate disease severity based on the lesion area by quantifying it on a pixel level. Some models try to combine classification and severity estimation. All of these processes generally evaluate severity separately from the disease classification process without jointly learning these two tasks. Moreover, Shi et al. [11] provided a review that confirmed that severity evaluation has been much less studied than disease classification and that the research conducted on more unified combined versions of these two tasks is very limited.

Multi-task learning for integrated disease analysis

Multi-task learning provides an elegant framework to simultaneously learn related tasks by exploiting their shared representation. Kendall et al. [12] showed that loss balancing is crucial for successful multi-task optimization, while Liu et al. [13] proposed task-specific attention mechanisms to promote better feature sharing across the different tasks.

Recently, more attention has been given to explicit modeling of inter-task interactions. Xu et al. [14] described a transformer-based architecture with cross-task attention and reasoning, allowing for the transfer of information between tasks, which illustrates the value of modeling the relationships between tasks rather than treating each one as separate.

Unfortunately, the application of multi-task learning in the analysis of plant diseases is still limited. Current studies, like Raghul et al. [15], have only attempted to apply multi-task learning to predict disease type and severity jointly without an explicit model of inter-task dependency in their use of simple shared representations. Likewise, pipeline systems [22] utilize sequential processing with severity having to rely on the previously made prediction prior to making a prediction for severity, rather than learning these relationships jointly.

These studies have shown that it is possible to achieve multi-task learning for plant disease analysis, but there are some limitations. The MTL approaches for existing plant disease are mostly based on simple shared feature representations and fail to explicitly model the relation between the disease identity and disease progression. Consequently, the information on diseases is not fully applicable in the process of severity estimation. Moreover, most methods require fully annotated tasks which cannot solve the problem of partially labeled datasets where some of the samples might not have a severity label. These restrictions call for better mechanisms of interaction between two tasks and more powerful learning strategies with partial supervision.

Challenges and research gaps

Although deep learning has advanced so much in analyzing plant diseases; limitations will remain. One main limitation is the non-representativeness of the datasets. Barbedo [3] explains that many datasets do not account for real-world variability. Therefore, models trained using this data will not perform well in generalizing outside a controlled environment. Jelali [23] reinforces this concern by showing that models trained using laboratory data produce over-optimistic results when applied to real-world datasets. Furthermore, models tend to over-generalize due to the presence of dataset bias. Noyan [4] found that a model could achieve high accuracy by identifying background artifacts rather than by identifying actual disease-specific characteristics; these results support that there is label-correlated noise in datasets. New perspectives have been calling for datasets to be more diverse and representative of real-world data so there will be more seamless transitions from research to practical application prior [24].

Not only does the dataset have challenges but there are also limitations in methodology. Many existing studies perform single task learning and little research has been conducted to assess unified frameworks where both disease models and severity models are learned simultaneously [25]. In addition, multi-task learning where only partial supervision of task labels exists causes optimization challenges as well. According to Fontana et al. [16], the results can degrade based on how well the task objectives are balanced during the optimization process.

The literature reviewed in this paper highlights a number of research gaps. Most studies on plant diseases only classify disease and do not give the information on severity needed for practical agricultural decision making. Second, the current methods of severity estimation are mostly segmentation and pipeline based, which involves different processing steps and are not well suited to utilize the intrinsic correlation between disease type and disease progression. Third, while there have been some studies on multi-task learning, current MTL plant disease models typically rely on the basic sharing of parameters and do not explicitly model disease-severity interactions. Last, the processing of partially labeled data is another yet under-explored challenge in the field of agricultural Computer Vision.

In order to overcome these shortcomings, the designed TomatoMTL framework is a multi-task learning based architecture that is capable of simultaneously performing disease classification and severity prediction. In contrast to previous works, TomatoMTL integrates three important components: CBAM-based feature refinement, disease-guided cross-task attention, and a masked optimization strategy for partially supervised learning. These components provide the ability to explicitly model inter-task dependencies, while retaining strong learning performance under incomplete annotation settings.

Dataset construction and preprocessing

The dataset used for testing comes from an open-source Kaggle repository, and it is composed of two complementary subsets; one provides images for the disease classification task and the other provides images for the severity prediction task.

Most of the images were acquired in relatively uniform light with the majority of images showing individual leaves with simple backgrounds and uniform image quality. Therefore, although these types of images are well suited for testing and establishing benchmarks for research regarding plant disease identification, they may not represent all types of differences in a real field.

The two subsets of images were combined into one multi-task learning model by constructing a custom data structure containing all the images required to train and validate a joint multi-task learning model.

Experiments and results

Experimental setup

The Tomato Disease Severity Dataset from Kaggle was used to evaluate the MTL Framework. The MTL Framework will classify six agricultural diseases (Bacterial Spot, Early Blight, Late Blight, Leaf Mold, Septoria Leaf Spot and Healthy) and predict three severity levels (Early, Mid, Late). The evaluation of disease severity was only performed on diseased plants (heterogeneous multi-tasking environment).

Every image was resized to a standard size of 224 × 224 pixels and normalized prior to preparing for training. In order to improve performance on unseen data, we applied significant data augmentation through geometric transformations (such as translation and rotation), color perturbations randomly changing brightness, contrast and saturation, and Mixup/Cutmix.

The model was developed using AdamW Optimizer with cosine annealing as its learning rate scheduling method. A weighted loss function was used to assist in balancing the learning of the two objectives; 0.6 was used for the objective of classifying and predicting diseases, while 0.4 was used for the classification of severity of the diseases. Label smoothing was also used.

Model performance was evaluated using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and weighted F1-score for both tasks.

Both loss curves (training and validation) show consistent declining trends with each epoch, demonstrating stable optimization and convergence towards a global minimum. Disease classification accuracy increased dramatically in the early epochs and levelled off within the 97–98% range, indicating efficient learning of discriminative features. Conversely, severity prediction accuracy increased gradually and plateaued at approximately 77–78%, indicating the complexity of making fine-classifications.

In addition, true to the trend observed in the loss plots, the validation F1-scores were consistent with the training scores for both tasks, indicating low levels of overfitting. Moreover, F1-scores demonstrated stable detection rates over multiple training sessions which implies generalization rates to unseen data should be robust.

Multi-task learning vs. single-task analysis

To assess the utility of multi-task learning, a comparison was conducted between a baseline single task model with a comparable multi-task model trained using the same fixed loss weights.

The single-task model can classify diseases more accurately but is not good at representing the severity of disease status; however, the MTL greatly increases severity prediction by approximately 2.3 times (F1: 0.3248 to 0.7488), which shows that using shared features is beneficial to improve prediction accuracy. This illustrates how naive multi-task learning represents an inherent trade-off between achieving optimal performance across all tasks and being able to improve performance of auxiliary tasks to the detriment of the primary task. This limitation indicates that there is a need for additional feature-sharing strategies that could be pursued in the development of this architecture. In addition, the MTL model reported in Table 7 serves as a baseline configuration with respect to multi-task tasks (shared backbone/base and static loss weight); therefore, the CBAM and proposed attention mechanism were not included. The intent of this experiment is to demonstrate how naive use of multi-task learning to learn multiple objectives will have an adverse effect on disease classification accuracy due to competition of multi-task objectives. Finally, the proposed architecture includes improved sharing and interaction of features through the use of CBAM and cross-task attention, enabling both improved disease classification and severity prediction to occur simultaneously.

Quantitative localization analysis

While the Grad-CAM visualizations in Figs. 7 and 8 provide qualitative evidence that the model attends to disease-relevant regions, we conducted quantitative localization analysis to rigorously validate this claim. For a subset of 500 test images with pixel-level lesion annotations, we evaluated three complementary metrics.

Localization metrics

Pointing Game Accuracy: Measures whether the peak activation location in the Grad-CAM heatmap falls within the annotated lesion region. A “hit” is counted if the maximum attention coordinate lies inside any lesion mask.

Localization AUC (Loc-AUC): Computes the area under the curve when thresholding the attention map at increasing percentiles and measuring intersection-over-union (IoU) with lesion masks.

Activation Ratio (AR): Ratio of mean attention intensity within lesion regions to mean attention intensity on healthy leaf tissue. AR > 1 indicates preferential attention to diseased areas.

Discussion of results

The comprehensive ablation studies confirm that CBAM, MixUp and CutMix augmentations, and appropriate loss-weighting each contribute meaningfully to overall performance, with cross-task attention providing the largest individual gain for severity estimation. The backbone comparison revealed that ResNet50 offers the optimal trade-off between accuracy and efficiency, as lightweight backbones such as MobileNetV3 suffered significant performance drops exceeding 2.5% in severity accuracy, while deeper backbones like ResNet101 provided only marginal gains at substantially higher computational cost.

The statistical significance analysis presented further strengthens these conclusions. Across five independent runs, TomatoMTL achieved a mean severity F1-score of 0.7756 ± 0.0014 compared to 0.7548 ± 0.0016 for the baseline without cross-task attention. Paired t-tests confirmed that all improvements are statistically significant with p-values less than 0.001 and very large effect sizes with Cohen’s d greater than 14, indicating that the cross-task attention mechanism provides robust and reproducible benefits beyond random initialization variability.

The quantitative localization analysis demonstrated that the model’s attention maps are biologically meaningful, achieving 89.4% Pointing Game accuracy for the disease classification branch. This finding suggests that the model is using biologically significant features of the leaf for prediction, thus supporting the validity of its predictions.

Overall, the results of this research demonstrate that multi-task learning can effectively learn and predict both disease classification and disease severity together in a unified architecture. The model achieves a high level of accuracy in disease classification of 97.85% and a strong level of accuracy in disease severity prediction of 77.66%, meaning that the model is capable of learning to represent both the specific disease type and the overall progression of the disease. Furthermore, comparison with state-of-the-art architectures showed that TomatoMTL outperforms strong single-task classifiers including EfficientNetV2-S, ViT-B/16, and ConvNeXt-Tiny as well as existing multi-task learning baselines such as Cross-Stitch Networks and MTAN, achieving a 2.83% improvement in severity accuracy over MTAN. By demonstrating that multi-task learning with explicit inter-task interaction can serve as a holistic method for diagnosing plant diseases, this study promotes the implementation of such frameworks in practice.

The comparison between multi-task and single-task configurations reinforces the problematic assumptions in naïve implementations of multi-task learning. The baseline multi-task configuration, which uses a common shared backbone and fixed-weighted loss functions without CBAM or cross-task attention, resulted in a significant improvement in severity diagnosis with F1-score increasing from 0.3248 to 0.7488, while simultaneously causing decreased disease classification performance. This trade-off implies that simple parameter sharing can lead to competition between the objectives of the two tasks. The architecture proposed here addresses this issue by using attention-guided feature refinement and explicit inter-task interaction. More specifically, the cross-task attention mechanism allows severity prediction to leverage disease-specific contextual information and therefore improves both the severity and classification tasks. Evidence supporting this conclusion comes from the ablation analysis, which shows that when cross-task attention is removed from the overall architecture, disease classification accuracy drops from 97.85% to 96.16% and the severity prediction F1-score falls from 0.7766 to 0.7551.

Severity prediction performs well overall; however, the Mid severity category remains the most difficult to classify accurately. The severity dataset has balanced sample sizes among Early at 36.2%, Mid at 32.6%, and Late at 31.1% categories; therefore, any performance differential is not likely due to class imbalance. Confusion matrix analysis reveals a higher rate of confusion between Mid and both Early and Late samples. This behavior is indicative of how disease progresses on a continuum, such that the visual characteristics of images associated with each stage often appear similar enough between adjacent stages to create indistinct boundaries when making severity decisions.

Several practical constraints must be taken into account. The Kaggle dataset used for evaluation was collected under fairly controlled conditions for imaging purposes. Therefore, the proposed framework has yet to be tested in real agricultural field settings to understand its robustness under those conditions. Numerous variables could affect model performance in practice, including changes in lighting, occlusions, complex backgrounds, variation in image quality, and different manifestations of the reported diseases. Additionally, the use of a ResNet50 backbone and attention modules increases computational complexity; as a result, deploying the model on resource-constrained edge devices will be difficult unless further optimizations are applied prior to deployment.

Furthermore, the present study only evaluates tomato leaf diseases. While the proposed multi-task learning framework is general in nature, no testing has been conducted to examine how the framework applies to different crop species or different types of diseases. Future work will focus on collecting more field test datasets to validate the framework, developing deployment-oriented optimization techniques for models, and evaluating the transferability of the proposed approach. Overall, these results demonstrate the benefit of explicitly modeling inter-task relationships for fine-grained agricultural diagnostics while reinforcing the need for additional validation and deployment-oriented studies.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed TomatoMTL, a multi-task learning framework that jointly performs tomato leaf disease classification and severity prediction from a single image. The framework integrates a shared ResNet50 backbone with CBAM-based feature refinement, a cross-task attention mechanism that enables disease-specific information to guide severity estimation, and a masked loss function that handles partially labeled data effectively. Extensive experiments on a public tomato disease dataset demonstrated that TomatoMTL achieves state-of-the-art performance, with 97.85% disease classification accuracy and 77.66% severity prediction accuracy, outperforming strong single-task classifiers including EfficientNetV2-S, ViT-B/16, and ConvNeXt-Tiny as well as existing multi-task learning baselines such as Cross-Stitch Networks and MTAN. Comprehensive ablation studies confirmed the individual contributions of CBAM, MixUp and CutMix augmentation, and the cross-task attention mechanism, while statistical significance analysis across five independent runs established the reliability and reproducibility of our results with p-values less than 0.001 and Cohen’s d greater than 14. Quantitative localization analysis further revealed that the model’s attention maps are biologically meaningful, achieving 89.4% Pointing Game accuracy for disease classification. Despite these promising results, several limitations must be acknowledged. The model was evaluated only on a controlled laboratory dataset from Kaggle, leaving its robustness to real-world field conditions such as variable lighting, occlusions, complex backgrounds, and image quality unknown. Additionally, severity prediction for intermediate disease stages remains challenging due to visual similarities between adjacent severity levels. Future work will focus on three directions. First, we will validate the framework with diverse field-collected datasets and investigate domain adaptation techniques. Second, we will pursue deployment-oriented optimizations including model pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation to achieve real-time inference on resource-constrained edge devices such as agricultural drones and mobile platforms. Third, we will extend the framework to other crop species and disease types. Collectively, this work demonstrates that explicit inter-task modeling via cross-task attention provides significant value for fine-grained agricultural diagnostics and establishes multi-task learning as a promising paradigm for integrated crop health assessment in precision agriculture.
