Thermodynamics of dynamical black holes beyond perturbation theory
Abhay Ashtekar, Daniel E. Paraizo, Jonathan Shu

Abstract
The close similarities of the three laws of black hole mechanics, discovered by Bardeen, Carter and Hawking, with the laws of thermodynamics led to the identification of a multiple of the area of the event horizon with entropy. However, developments over the past two decades have shown that this paradigm has some important limitations, especially because of the teleological nature of event horizons. After a brief review of these limitations, we will show that they can be overcome using quasi-local horizons. Specifically, the new first law applies to black holes in general relativity that can be arbitrarily far from equilibrium and refers to finite changes that occur due to physical processes at the horizon. The second law is now a quantitative statement that relates the change in the area of a dynamical horizon segment due to fluxes of energy falling into the black hole. Together, they lead one to identify black hole entropy with the area of marginally trapped surfaces in quasi-local horizons, generalizing recent perturbative findings that it should be identified not with the area of the event horizon but with the area of a marginally trapped surface inside it. 1 1 1 Dedicated to the memory of Jurek Lewandowski.

Introduction

Over fifty years ago, Bardeen, Carter and Hawking (BCH) showed that black holes in general relativity obey relations that have striking similarities with the laws of thermodynamics
31
;
49
. The conceptual economy underlying these laws is remarkable. For the zeroth and the first law, one only uses the Einstein equations satisfied by space-time geometries of stationary, axisymmetric black holes in asymptotically flat space-times. The second law, which had been discovered by Hawking two years earlier, only needed the null energy condition and asymptotic predictability in addition to the Einstein equations
58
. These findings, together with Hawking’s subsequent startling discovery that black holes emit quantum radiation
59
, have provided an anchor for investigations in quantum gravity in the subsequent decades.

Recall that the zeroth law says that surface gravity
\mathring{\kappa}
is constant on the horizon of a stationary black hole (BH) just as the temperature
T
of a body is constant in thermodynamical equilibrium. The first law of thermodynamics
\delta E=T\delta S+p\delta V+\mu\delta N
constrains the permissible changes in the extensive observables
(E,S,V,N)
of a system in the passage from one equilibrium state to a nearby equilibrium state. Similarly, the BCH first law,

\delta M=\frac{\mathring{\kappa}}{8\pi G}\,\delta A+\mathring{\Omega}\,\delta J+\mathring{\Phi}\,\delta\mathfrak{q}

(1)

governs the relation between
(M,A,J,\mathfrak{q})
of two nearby stationary, axisymmetric BHs in general relativity (GR). Here
M,J

A
, the angular velocity
\mathring{\Omega}
and the electrostatic potential
\mathring{\Phi}
refer to the horizon. (The electric charge
\mathfrak{q}
can be evaluated either at the horizon or at spatial infinity since it is conserved). Since the zeroth and the BCH first law require stationarity, the horizon in their statements can be taken to be the Killing horizon
\mathfrak{Kh}
defined by the time translation isometry. On the other hand, Hawking’s second law
\Delta A\geq 0
applies to BHs that can be far from stationarity, just as the second law
\Delta S\geq 0
of thermodynamics applies to systems that can be far from equilibrium. Space-times encompassing such dynamical BHs do not admit KHs. The
\Delta A
that features in the statement of the second law is the change in the area of cross-sections of the
event horizon
(EHs)
\mathfrak{eh}
. Since
\mathfrak{eh}
is the future boundary of the causal past of
\mathscr{I}^{+}

Together, these three laws suggest that a multiple of
\mathring{\kappa}
could be identified with the temperature of a stationary BH and a corresponding multiple of the horizon area
A
would then be identified with the BH entropy. It is clear already on dimensional grounds that this identification cannot be made in classical GR. Hawking’s detailed calculation of quantum radiation showed how
\hbar

(\hbar/2\pi)\,\mathring{\kappa}

S

The diverse developments relating black hole mechanics to thermodynamics are striking. However, as we discuss below, a closer examination reveals some conceptual tensions. They suggest that this traditional formulation of BH thermodynamics can be improved upon, especially in dynamical situations. The purpose of this paper is to present a candidate that is free of the deficiencies of the standard treatment. In this improved paradigm, the three laws of BH thermodynamics are obtained using quasi-local horizons (for a recent review, see
21
). More precisely, KHs
\mathfrak{Kh}
are replaced by Isolated Horizon Segments (IHSs)
\mathfrak{ih}

23

\mathfrak{Dh}

\mathfrak{eh}
in Hawking’s second law are replaced again by DHSs
\mathfrak{Dh}

I.1
Limitations of BCH-type frameworks

Consider first the equilibrium situations that the first law (
1
) refers to. As we already noted, the ADM quantities,
M
and
J
, that appear in the first law are evaluated as 2-sphere integrals at infinity, far from the BH. They represent the
total
mass and angular momentum of the given space-time, including contributions from what is outside the BH. If one were to consider a space-time in which there is a
physical process
that sends a BH from one equilibrium state to another, the ADM quantities would be absolutely conserved, but the mass and angular momentum of the BH itself would change. Therefore, we need to have expressions of mass and angular momentum that can be calculated directly using just the fields on the horizon, without references to their values at infinity. Indeed, in thermodynamics extensive observables that appear in the first law refer
only
to the system and not what is outside.

A second drawback of the BCH analysis is that it assumes that the entire space-time is stationary, not just the BH. By contrast, in thermodynamics one only assumes that the system under consideration is in equilibrium; not the whole universe. This suggests that a better correspondence with the zeroth and the first laws of thermodynamics would result if one assumes that only the horizon is isolated –i.e., ensuring that the BH itself is a closed system– while allowing for radiative processes outside
13
;
8
;
20
. These situations cannot be incorporated using KHs
\mathfrak{Kh}
. By contrast, IHSs
\mathfrak{ih}
are tailored to incorporate them. Hence they provide a more appropriate setting for the equilibrium forms of the zeroth and the first law.

Furthermore, it should be possible to establish an active form of the first law, governing the changes in the mass and angular momentum of the black hole due to fluxes across finite portions of the horizon. Using KHs,
\mathfrak{Kh}
, one cannot incorporate such physical processes beyond perturbation theory around stationary space-times . One has to use instead DHSs
\mathfrak{Dh}
,  across which there can be arbitrarily large fluxes of energy and angular momentum.

The third limitation concerns the identification of the entropy of dynamical BHs with the area of their EHs, suggested by the second law, and is far more serious. First, as noted above, the notion of an EH requires the space-time to admit future infinity,
\mathscr{I}^{+}

There are also spatially non-compact space-times with positive cosmological constant admitting trapped regions that can be physically thought of as representing BHs. But they do
not admit EHs
because all of
\mathscr{I}^{+}

An illustration of practical difficulties is provided by numerical simulations of binary BH mergers: one cannot use EHs in the course of a numerical evolution to locate the progenitor BHs, nor the remnant; EHs can only be identified at the end of the simulation, as an ‘afterthought’. In the investigation of conceptual issues –such as cosmic censorship in classical GR
5

I.2
Overcoming the limitations: Quasi-local horizons

Let us begin by noting that a host of difficulties immediately arises when considering fully dynamical situations in place of stationary axisymmetric ones. First, we no longer have Killing fields. What would then be the analogs of the extensive observables
M
and
J
that feature in the first law (
1
)? Since they have to refer only to the individual BHs in any given space-time, they cannot be the ADM quantities. A lack of obvious candidates is widely regarded as a key difficulty in incorporating dynamical situations beyond perturbation theory around stationary BHs (see, e.g.,
62
). The absence of direct analogs of intensive parameters
\mathring{\kappa}
and
\mathring{\Omega}
seems even more serious. Indeed, already for familiar thermodynamical systems, while extensive observables such as energy and volume are generally well-defined away for equilibrium, we do not have globally defined intensive parameters such as
(T,\,p,\,\mu)
. Severe as they seem, we will be able to surmount these obstacles using the known results on quasi-local horizons and black hole uniqueness theorems. A key element is the introduction of a new conceptual arena that intertwines dynamical black holes with those in equilibrium, enabling one to assign intensive parameters to BHs that are far from equilibrium.

The underlying ideas can be summarized as follows. Quasi-local horizons can be located without reference to
\mathscr{I}^{+}
, knowing space-time geometry only in their neighborhoods. In this sense, they are free of teleology. In the quasi-local paradigm, BHs in equilibrium are represented by IHSs
\mathfrak{ih}
. While KHs
\mathfrak{Kh}
constitute an important special case, isolated horizon segments can exist in space-times without any Killing vector. As mentioned above, they encapsulate the idea that the BH itself is isolated and in equilibrium, allowing for radiation and dynamical processes elsewhere. Nonetheless, one can use results on IHSs
23
to show that the zeroth and the first laws of BH mechanics admit a natural generalization to IHSs. In this extension, the ADM observables
(M,J)
in (
1
) that refer to the global Killing fields
\mathring{t}^{a},\,\mathring{\varphi}^{a}
are replaced by the
\mathfrak{ih}
-observables
(M_{\mathfrak{ih}},J_{\mathfrak{ih}})
, that refer to the
\mathfrak{ih}
-symmetry group
\mathcal{G}
–the analog of the BMS group at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
– and are defined using only local fields on
\mathfrak{ih}
. More importantly, one can also extend black hole mechanics to fully dynamical situations in GR in which BHs are represented by DHSs
\mathfrak{Dh}

21
. Their geometry is time-dependent because there are fluxes of energy and angular momentum across them. Our first law for DHSs will refer to changes that extensive observables associated with
\mathfrak{Dh}
undergo in response to these physical fluxes. Key differences from the BCH first law are the following:

(i) Extensive observables are defined
at

\mathfrak{Dh}
, without reference to infinity. Indeed, the ADM mass and angular momentum cannot feature in the generalization of the first law to dynamical situations, first because they are absolutely conserved, and second because changes should refer to values of these observables associated with individual BHs without reference to other far away BHs, stars, and radiation in the space-time.

(ii) The changes in physical observables are
finite
, rather than infinitesimal; and,

(iii) These changes are caused by
physical processes in space-time
, not results of infinitesimal displacements from one stationary BH to another in the space of solutions.

Nonetheless, if one restricts oneself to infinitesimal processes on a dynamical horizon segment, one recovers the first law in its familiar BCH form (but changes in values of extensive observables are again due to physical processes at the horizon). Similarly, the second law of DHSs is a quantitative relation between the change in the area of the DHS and the
local
energy flux across it
19
. It holds both for classical processes by which BHs form and grow and for the quantum process by which they evaporate. It is not just a qualitative statement
\Delta A\geq 0
,  nor a teleological effect, as with EHs. The two laws imply that in dynamical situations
BH entropy should be associated with the area of cross-sections of DHSs rather than cross-sections of EHs.

A QLH incorporates both equilibrium and non-equilibrium phases of BHs, while a DHS represents a non-equilibrium phase. In both cases, during its dynamical phase, the BH can be arbitrarily far from being in equilibrium. The NEHSs, IHSs and KHs represent equilibrium phases in an increasingly stronger sense. IHSs represent phases in the evolutionary history of the BH in which it is itself isolated and in equilibrium, allowing for fully dynamical processes and radiation in the rest of the space-time. By contrast, such processes are
not
allowed in paradigms that use KHs of stationary space-times. For example, the BCH framework assumes that the entire space-time under consideration is stationary. Other discussions of the first law are anchored in Hamiltonian frameworks. Typically, they allow for radiation on stationary backgrounds but only perturbatively (see e.g.
93
). Moreover, they assume that the background space-time admits a bifurcate horizon. As the simple example depicted in the left panel of Fig 2 (and its more sophisticated variants) show, although fully dynamical BH space-times can admit IHSs, they are unlikely to admit bifurcate horizons. In spite of this generality of IHSs, as we discuss in sections
III.1
and
III.3
, the zeroth law and the standard infinitesimal version of the first law extend to IHSs. Thus, for these laws one does not need KHs;
it suffices that the BH itself is isolated,
just as in ordinary thermodynamics it suffices that only the system under consideration is in equilibrium –and not the whole universe.

Remarks:

1. Note that QLHS
\mathfrak{h}
are 3-dimensional sub-manifolds of space-time in their own right, satisfying certain conditions on fields defined on them intrinsically. In numerical relativity, in practice they are located using a space-time foliation and finding MTSs on each partial Cauchy surface of this foliation. However, although important, this is only a tool to find them. Neither the notion of a QLH nor its properties refer to these space-time foliations, or indeed any structure away from the 3-manifold
\mathfrak{h}
.

2. Recall that the null normals to our MTSs are
future
directed. On KHs of the Schwarzschild-Kruskal space-time, the static Killing field
\mathring{t}^{a}
is generally taken to be future directed on KHs of the right region. It is then past-directed on left KHs. Because of the ‘strict stability’ requirement on MTSs we imposed in this paper, only the future KHs in both regions qualify as IHSs, and the vector field
\mathring{k}^{a}
is future directed and
\mathring{\kappa}
is positive on both of them.

III
Mechanics of Isolated Horizon Segments.

As we just discussed, IHSs represent BHs that are themselves in equilibrium, while allowing for dynamical processes arbitrarily close to them. In the first part of this section we provide a brief summary of key properties of IHSs that will be used in our thermodynamic considerations, in particular recalling from
13
that the zeroth law holds on IHSs even when they are not KHs.

The first law requires a notion of the horizon angular momentum. As we discuss in Appendix
A
, this notion is well-defined on all IHSs (indeed, on all NEHSs) even when the intrinsic metric is not axisymmetric. However, that construction involves a long detour. Therefore, for simplicity of presentation, in the second part of this section we restrict ourselves to those IHSs
\mathfrak{ih}
that admit a rotational vector field
\mathring{\varphi}^{a}
as a symmetry of the IHS geometry
(\mathring{k}^{a},\mathring{q}_{ab},\mathring{D})
and discuss angular momentum.

In the third part we extend the first law to IHSs. Using the notion of angular momentum from Appendix
A
, mentioned above, our discussion of the first law extends to general IHSs, modulo the caveat discussed in Remark 2 of section
III.2
. It paves the way to generalize the first law to fully dynamical situations, discussed in section
VI
.

III.1
Salient Properties of IHSs, Including the Zeroth Law

Let us begin with NEHSs that are more general than IHSs, and then specialize to IHSs. (For details, see
13
.)

(i) The pullback to any cross-section of the NEHS of
\mathring{q}_{ab}
provides the intrinsic positive definite 2-metric
\tilde{q}_{ab}
thereon. On every NEHS there is a well-defined 2-form
\mathring{\epsilon}_{ab}
satisfying
\mathring{\epsilon}_{ab}\mathring{k}^{b}\,\hat{=}\,0
, whose pull-back to any cross-section provides the area 2-form
\tilde{\epsilon}_{ab}
compatible with
\tilde{q}_{ab}
.

(ii) Because an NEHS is a null surface, any null normal to it satisfies
\mathring{k}^{a}\nabla_{a}\mathring{k}^{b}\,\hat{=}\,\mathring{\kappa}\,\mathring{k}^{b}
. The acceleration
\mathring{\kappa}
is referred to as
surface gravity
.

(iii) While the definition only asks that a QLH should admit a foliation by MTSs,
every
cross-section of an NEHS is both expansion-free and shear-free.

(iv) The space-time Ricci tensor satisfies
R_{ab}\mathring{k}^{a}v^{b}\,\hat{=}\,0
, for all vector fields
v^{a}
tangential to the NEHS. Therefore by Einstein’s equations
T_{ab}\mathring{k}^{a}v^{b}\,\hat{=}\,0
: there are no matter fluxes across NEHSs. Since
\sigma_{ab}
also vanishes on each MTS, it follows that there is no flux of gravitational energy either.

(v) The Weyl tensor satisfies
C_{abcd}\,\mathring{k}^{a}v_{1}^{b}\mathring{k}^{c}v_{2}^{d}\,\hat{=}\,0
for all vector fields
v_{1}^{a},v_{2}^{b}
tangential to the NEHS. Hence
\mathring{k}^{a}
is a repeated principal null direction of the Weyl tensor and, in the Newman Penrose notation,
\Psi_{0}\,\hat{=}\,\Psi_{1}\,\hat{=}\,0
and
\Psi_{2}\,\,\hat{=}\,\,\frac{1}{2}\,\big(C_{abcd}+i{}^{\star}C_{abcd}\big)\,\mathring{k}^{a}\mathring{\underline{k}}^{b}\mathring{k}^{c}\mathring{\underline{k}}^{d}
is gauge invariant, i.e. insensitive to the the choice of the cross-section used to define
\mathring{\underline{k}}^{b}
, as well as the rescaling freedom in
(\mathring{k}^{a},\mathring{\underline{k}}^{a})
.

(vi) The canonical derivative operator on the NEHS satisfies
\mathring{D}_{a}\mathring{k}^{b}\,\,\hat{=}\,\,\mathring{\omega}_{a}\mathring{k}^{b}
. The surface gravity can be expressed in terms of this 1-form as
\mathring{\kappa}\,\,\hat{=}\,\,\mathring{\omega}_{a}\,\mathring{k}^{a}
. Under rescalings
\mathring{k}^{a}\mapsto e^{\alpha}\,\mathring{k}^{a}
we have
\mathring{\omega}_{a}\mapsto\mathring{\omega}_{a}+\mathring{D}_{a}\alpha
. Thus
\omega_{a}
is a connection 1-form (on a vector bundle over
\mathfrak{ih}
whose fibers are spanned by
(\mathring{k}^{a},\mathring{\underline{k}}^{a})
.) Its curvature is given by
{\rm Im}\,\Psi_{2}
:
\mathring{D}_{[a}\mathring{\omega}_{b]}={\rm Im}\,\Psi_{2}\,\mathring{\epsilon}_{ab}
.

Let us now turn to IHSs
\mathfrak{ih}
. The first major difference from NEHSs arises directly from the fact that
\mathring{k}^{a}
is a symmetry of the full
\mathfrak{ih}
-geometry including the derivative operator
\mathring{D}
. As we noted in section
II
, while every null normal
\mathring{k}^{a}
satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{\mathring{k}}\mathring{q}_{ab}\,\hat{=}\,0
, a generic NEHS does not admit any null normal
\mathring{k}^{a}
that Lie-drags
\mathring{D}
, i.e., satisfies
[\mathcal{L}_{\mathring{k}},\mathring{D}_{a}]v^{b}\,\hat{=}\,0
. Hence the passage from an NEHS to an IHS is a genuine restriction that has an important consequence: the available rescaling freedom in the choice of the null normal is drastically reduced. If an NEHS is an IHS with respect to two null normals
\mathring{k}^{\prime\,a}
and
\mathring{k}^{a}
, then
\mathring{k}^{\prime\,a}\,\hat{=}\,e^{\mathring{\alpha}}\,\mathring{k}^{a}
for some
constant

\mathring{\alpha}
. (For details, see
9
.) Therefore one introduces an equivalence class
[\mathring{k}^{a}]
of null normals, where any two are equivalent if and only if one is obtained from the other by a rescaling by a positive constant, and denotes IHS geometries by the triplets
([\mathring{k}^{a}],\,\mathring{q}_{ab},\,\mathring{D})
. Since the permissible rescaling factor is a constant on
\mathfrak{ih}
, all null normals
\mathring{k}^{a}\,\in\,[\mathring{k}^{a}]
define the same the rotation 1-form
\mathring{\omega}_{a}
.

The fact that
\mathring{k}^{a}
is a symmetry of the IHS geometry leads to two interesting results. First following Ref.
13
, one can show that the zeroth law holds on all IHSs . One starts with the observation that, an immediate consequence of the property
[\mathcal{L}_{\mathring{k}},\,\mathring{D}_{a}]\,\mathring{k}^{b}\,\hat{=}\,0
(of
\mathring{k}^{a}
) is
\mathcal{L}_{\mathring{k}}\mathring{\omega}_{a}\,\hat{=}\,0
. By the Cartan identity, we also have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathring{k}}\,\mathring{\omega}_{b}\,\,\hat{=}\,\,

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\,\mathring{k}^{a}\mathring{D}_{[a}\mathring{\omega}_{b]}+\mathring{D}_{b}(\mathring{k}^{a}\mathring{\omega}_{a})

\displaystyle\,\hat{=}\,\,

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\,{\rm Im}\,\Psi_{2}\,\,\mathring{k}^{a}\mathring{\epsilon}_{ab}+\mathring{D}_{b}\mathring{\kappa}\,\,\hat{=}\,\,\mathring{D}_{b}\mathring{\kappa}\,,

(2)

since
\mathring{k}^{a}\mathring{\epsilon}_{ab}=0
. Hence,
\mathring{D}_{b}\mathring{\kappa}\,\,\hat{=}\,\,0
: surface gravity
\mathring{\kappa}
is constant on
\mathfrak{ih}
.
This constancy holds in spite of the fact that
\mathfrak{ih}
can be highly distorted due to an infinite dimensional freedom in the higher shape moments of IHSs
12
. Thus,
the zeroth law of black hole mechanics is generalized from KHs to IHSs.

The second result concerns charges associated with the
\mathfrak{ih}
-symmetry
\mathring{k}^{a}
. Let us begin by recalling a fact about asymptotically flat space-times. If such a space-time admits a Killing field, it automatically preserves the universal structure at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
and is therefore also a BMS symmetry. Therefore one can associate with the Killing field the charge associated with the BMS symmetry. Similarly, the exact symmetry

\mathfrak{nh}
-symmetry group
\mathcal{G}

16

\mathcal{G}
there is a conserved charge on the covariant phase space of all solutions of Einstein’s equations that admit an NEHS as its inner boundary
15

this phase space charge is given by :

Q_{{}_{\rm PS}}^{{}^{(\mathring{k})}}\,[S]\,\,\hat{=}\,\,\frac{1}{8\pi G}\,{\oint_{S}}\mathring{\omega}_{a}\,\mathring{k}^{a}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}V\,\,\hat{=}\,\,\frac{1}{8\pi G}\,\mathring{\kappa}\,A[S]\,,

(3)

3

\mathfrak{ih}

of the linear combination

for

on
\mathfrak{ih}
. NEHSs satisfying the weaker condition are called
weakly isolated horizons
and, in the literature on quasi-local horizons, the zeroth law was derived for WIHs, without reference to the stronger notion of IHSs (for details, see
13
).
However, our discussion of the first law in section
III.3
uses IHSs.

2. The prescription (
5
) to select a preferred
\mathring{k}^{a}
from the equivalence class
[\mathring{k}^{a}]
of null normals to an IHS would provide a non-negative
\mathring{\kappa}
only if

. Interestingly, this condition is always met by IHSs considered in this section, thanks to the available area-angular momentum inequalities on axisymmetric MTSs

;
46
;
47
;
48
. Now, as discussed in Appendix
A
, thanks to results in Refs.
16

15

68

Of course, this cannot occur for stationary BHs in vacuum which, by uniqueness theorems, would be in the Kerr-Newman family. But, as we have emphasized, IHSs can exist in wider contexts in which there are dynamical fields and gravitational waves. The discussion of the initial value problem based on two null surfaces

1.
Existence and uniqueness:
While QLHs are 3-manifolds defined in their own right, to explore the issues of existence and uniqueness one has to use the Cauchy problem. If a Cauchy slice admits a MTS
S

S
yield a unique QLH under evolution? Local existence has been shown if the initial MTS
S

5

is non-zero somewhere on
S

\mathcal{E}
vanishes identically on
S

\mathfrak{Dh}

\mathfrak{Dh}=\widetilde{\mathfrak{Dh}}

2.
Approach to equilibrium:
Of particular interest to thermodynamic considerations is the process by which a dynamical BH approaches equilibrium. In our framework, the non-equilibrium phase is described by a DHS, and the equilibrium state by an IHS. Both are parts of a QLH. As mentioned above, the approach can be asymptotic –as in a binary black hole merger– or may occur at a finite instant of time –as in the left panel of Fig. 2– when an open DHS approaches a MTS which is a boundary of an IHS. In these situations, the metric
\tilde{q}_{ab}
on the MTS and the curl of the rotational 1-form
\tilde{\omega}_{a}

\varphi^{a}
does so by its construction). Therefore, the area and the angular momentum on the DHS side approach those on the IHS. This matching holds for other observables as well –for example, once
(k^{a},\underline{k}^{a})
are rescaled appropriately so that they are smooth on the entire QLH, the shear
\tilde{\sigma}_{ab}

V
Thermodynamics of BHs far from Equilibrium: Strategy

Already for ordinary thermodynamical systems, one faces a key conceptual obstacle in extending the first law to non-equilibrium situations due to lack of intensive parameters. In section
V.1
we first state these difficulties and then present a strategy, motivated by constructions introduced in section
III.3
, to overcome them. In this respect, BHs are simpler than ordinary thermodynamic systems. However, they are also more complicated in that, unlike in systems normally considered in thermodynamics, for dynamical BHs, we do not have a natural notion of instantaneous energy. In section
V.2
we show that this difficulty can be overcome using a natural additional input.

Remarks:

1. The rich literature on DHSs contains a number of relations that carry the flavor of the first law (see, in particular,
19

However, in all but one of these relations, individual terms do not have physical dimensions of energy as in the BCH first law (and the first law of thermodynamics.) The one with the same physical dimensions as the BCH first law featured the Hawking mass
M_{\rm Haw}
in place

V.1
; and, (ii) the preferred vector field

introduced in section

ensuring the seamless matching between the notions of energy on DHSs with those on IHSs when equilibrium is reached. This is why the previously known relations did not contain the first law (

2. To gain intuition for the flux integrals, it is instructive to consider the case when the angular momentum

vanishes identically on a DHS. Of course this can happen in a spherical collapse where the area increase is entirely due to collapsing matter. But it can also occur in much more interesting dynamical situations in which there is gravitational radiation. Consider, in particular, a head-on collision of two BHs. In this case, the angular momentum vanishes both on the DHSs of the progenitors and the DHS of the remnant. But there is energy flux into the DHSs. In fact in this case,
\mathfrak{F}_{\rm matt}^{(\xi)}
vanishes and the growth in the horizon area is

due to

More generally, when

As we noted in section

Historically, the BCH first law

VII
Discussion

This section is divided into two parts. In the first we summarize the main results, emphasizing the new conceptual ingredients. In the second we make a number of remarks, putting our results in a broader context.

VII.1
Summary

Einstein’s equations imply that there is a multitude of interesting relations between geometrical quantities on the Killing horizons

In section

A.2
Angular Momentum

On an axisymmetric
\mathfrak{nh}
with
\mathring{\varphi}^{a}
as the rotational Killing field of
\mathring{q}_{ab}

\mathring{\omega}_{a}
or
{\rm Im}[\Psi_{2}]
as:

J^{(\mathring{\varphi})}\,\hat{=}\,\,-\frac{1}{8\pi G}{\oint_{S}}\mathring{\omega}_{a}\,\mathring{\varphi}^{a}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}V\,\,\hat{=}\,\,-\frac{1}{4\pi G}\oint_{S}\mathring{f}\,{\rm Im}[\Psi_{2}]\,\mathrm{d}^{2}V,

(65)

where, in the second step, we have used the fact that, any divergence-free vector field
\mathring{\varphi}^{a}
on
S
has the form
\mathring{\varphi}^{a}\,\hat{=}\,\mathring{\epsilon}^{ab}\,D_{b}\mathring{f}

23
. Since
2\,{\rm Im}\,\Psi_{2}\,=\mathring{\epsilon}^{ab}\,\mathring{D}_{[a}\mathring{\omega}_{b]}
, the second expression in terms of
{\rm Im}[\Psi_{2}]
makes it manifest that
J^{(\mathring{\varphi})}
depends only on the exterior derivative of the pull-back of
\mathring{\omega}_{a}
to the cross-section
S
, and is thus unaffected by rescalings of
\mathring{k}^{a}
. Finally, the value of
J^{(\mathring{\varphi})}
is independent of the choice of
S
. On the Kerr horizon one obtains
J^{(\mathring{\varphi})}=Ma
, as expected.

However, recall that one can define an angular momentum charge at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
using Lorentz subgroups of the BMS group
\mathcal{B}
even in the absence of a rotational Killing field. Similarly, one can define an angular momentum charge on
\mathfrak{nh}
even when the physical metric
\mathring{q}_{ab}
is
not
axisymmetric by using generators of Lorentz subgroups of
\mathcal{G}
. In fact the procedure is simpler because there is no gravitational radiation on
\mathfrak{nh}
. Korzynski has spelled it out in
68

Lorentz subgroups of
\mathcal{G}
are intimately intertwined with groups of conformal isometries of the round metrics
\mathring{r}_{ab}
introduced in section
A.1
. Fix any cross-section
S
of
\mathfrak{nh}
and consider the pull-backs of unit round metrics
\mathring{r}_{ab}
to
S
. Now, there is a unique Lorentz subgroup
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathcal{G}
that leaves
S
invariant. Its generators are just the six conformal Killing fields of any of the pulled-back
\mathring{r}_{ab}
on
S
. Fix a
\mathring{r}_{ab}
and denote its three Killing fields
\mathring{\varphi}^{a}_{(i)}
(
i=1,2,3
). They provide the three components
L_{(i)}
of angular momentum on
S

L_{(i)}\,\hat{=}\,\,-\frac{1}{8\pi G}{\oint_{S}}\mathring{\omega}_{a}\,\mathring{\varphi}^{a}_{(i)}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}V\,.

(66)

Similarly the three proper conformal Killing fields
\mathring{\eta}^{a}_{(i)}
(of the fixed
\mathring{r}_{ab}
) provide the three components
K_{(i)}
of the boost angular momentum:

K_{(i)}\,\hat{=}\,\,-\frac{1}{8\pi G}{\oint_{S}}\mathring{\omega}_{a}\,\mathring{\eta}^{a}_{(i)}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}V\,.

(67)

Let us first ensure that
L_{(i)}
and
K_{(i)}
are well-defined. The vector fields
\mathring{\varphi}^{a}_{(i)}
and
\mathring{\eta}^{a}_{(i)}
are
divergence-free with respect to the
\mathring{r}_{ab}
we fixed, but not with respect to the
physical
metric on
S
(which determines the area element
\mathrm{d}^{2}V

\mathring{\omega}_{a}
by
\mathring{\omega}_{a}+D_{a}f
, i.e. if we replace
\mathring{k}^{a}
by
e^{f}\,\mathring{k}^{a}

\mathring{\omega}_{a}
remains unchanged. Thus
L_{(i)}
and
K_{(i)}

Our task is to extract from
L_{(i)}
and
K_{(i)}
a single number
J

\mathfrak{nh}
. Now, the decomposition of the Lorentz angular momentum into
L_{(i)}
and
K_{(i)}
depends on the choice of a specific round metric
\mathring{r}_{ab}
in the 3-parameter family. However, Korzynski
68
has shown that it is possible to extract, in an invariant manner, a preferred
rotational vector field

\mathring{\varphi}^{a}
among the six Lorentz generators using just the Lorentz Casimirs
A:=\vec{L}^{2}-\vec{K}^{2}
and
B:=\vec{K}\cdot\vec{L}
.
13
13

13

The construction assumes a genericity condition that both Casimirs do not vanish simultaneously. Therefore it does not provide a preferred rotation in the case when
\mathring{q}_{ab}
is spherical symmetric. But in this case
J
vanishes identically.

It is a rotation in the sense that: (i) it has closed orbits, with exactly two zeros; and, (ii) the range of its affine parameter is
[0,\,2\pi)
. The
\mathring{\varphi}^{a}
picked out by this procedure depends on
\mathring{q}_{ab}
and
\mathring{\omega}_{a}

\mathfrak{nh}

\mathfrak{nh}

J_{\mathfrak{nh}}\,\hat{=}\,\,-\frac{1}{8\pi G}{\oint_{S}}\mathring{\omega}_{a}\,\mathring{\varphi}^{a}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}V\,,

(68)

where we have dropped the suffix
(\mathring{\varphi}^{a})
on

to emphasize the fact that
\mathring{\varphi}^{a}
is not a pre-specified Killing field, as in (
65

\mathfrak{nh}
itself. Thus,
J_{\mathfrak{nh}}
is the angular momentum of a generic
\mathfrak{nh}
which need not be axisymmetric. In the special case that the physical metric
\tilde{q}_{ab}
on

happens to be axisymmetric, the
\mathring{\varphi}^{a}
selected by this procedure coincides with the axial Killing field, and

equals
J^{(\mathring{\varphi})}
of (
25

\mathfrak{nh}
were an axisymmetric
\mathfrak{ih}
, it would yield the expression (
7

III.2

However, there is a potential pitfall: we fixed a cross-section
S
of
\mathfrak{nh}
at the very beginning of the construction. A priori

could depend on the choice of the cross-section
S
, in which case it would not represent an observable associated with the full
\mathfrak{nh}
but only with
S
. Now, any two cross-sections
S
and
S^{\prime}
are related by a supertranslation and all supermomentum charges
vanish identically
on an NEH
15
. Consequently, the value of
J
does not depend on the initial choice of
S
. This feature can be made manifest by working entirely on the 2-sphere
\hat{S}
of orbits of the vector fields
[\mathring{k}^{a}]
. Because
\mathring{\omega}_{a}\mathring{k}^{a}\,\,\hat{=}\,\,0
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathring{\ell}}\mathring{\omega}\,\,\hat{=}\,\,0

\mathring{\omega}_{a}
on
\mathfrak{nh}
is the lift of a 1-form
\hat{\omega}_{a}

\hat{S}
and one can carry out the entire discussion using

\hat{S}
. However, while this construction would then single out a rotational vector field

on

\mathfrak{nh}
would not be unique; there is a freedom to add a supertranslation to
\mathring{\varphi}^{a}

\mathfrak{nh}

picked by the Korzynski construction is tied to the choice of
S

because, as mentioned above, all supertranslation charges vanish. To summarize, this construction assigns any given
\mathfrak{nh}

Appendix B
An alternative approach based on field equations.

One can just use Einstein’s field equations. This discussion will be helpful to experts in geometric analysis and mathematical relativists with expertise on horizons, who are not as familiar with the intricacies associated with Hamiltonian formulations of GR.
