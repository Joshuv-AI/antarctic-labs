A patent perspective of RSK inhibitors to treat cancer (2020-present)
Authors: Aviv Preminger, Deborah A. Lannigan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The Ser/Thr protein kinase family, p90 ribosomal S6 kinase (RSK), can be activated by inputs from ERK1/2, PDK1 and in some cases MK1/MK2. Dysregulated RSK activity is implicated in diseases associated with inflammation such as cancer. There are four RSK family members of which RSK1/2 are tumor promoters whereas RSK3/4 can act as tumor suppressors.Areas covered:The review summarizes patents that have identified compounds that decrease RSK activity. These patents all focus on the use of RSK inhibitors as cancer therapeutics. The patents were identified using the World Intellectual Property Organization, United States Patent and Trademark Office, and Derwent World Patents Index databases.Expert opinion:The compounds disclosed in this patent review do not exhibit isoform specificity and there is limited or no information on selectivity and on target activity. The importance of RSK as a target is demonstrated by transition of the pan-RSK inhibitor, PMD-026, to phase 2 for metastatic breast cancer. RSK isoforms differ in their contributions to transformation. Therefore, specific inhibitors are needed, which is challenging as RSK kinase domains are similar. It is possible that PROTACs for RSK2 and RSK4 can be generated by exploiting their allosteric binding pockets with SL0101 or floxacin antibiotics, respectively.

Introduction

The extracellular signal-regulated kinase1/2 (ERK1/2) signaling pathway, consisting of RAS-RAF-MEK1/2-ERK1/2, is activated by various stimuli to regulate numerous homeostatic and disease processes [1]. This pathway has been the subject of intense drug development and FDA approved drugs are shown in Figure 1. However, crosstalk and negative feedback occurring in the cascade can result in drug-induced toxicities. The family of p90 ribosomal S6 kinases (RSK) are serine/threonine (Ser/Thr) protein kinases, which are downstream effectors of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway. Drug development efforts for RSK have lagged behind other members of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway. However, a pan-RSK inhibitor, PMD-026, is being evaluated in a clinical trial for invasive or metastatic breast cancer.

In vertebrates there are four members of the RSK family, RSK1–4, with RSK1 most distantly related according to phylogenetic analysis [2]. RSK and mitogen- and stress-activated kinases (MSK) are the only examples of kinases that have dual non-identical functional kinase domains. The N-terminal kinase domain (NTKD) is a member of the AGC kinase group that includes cAMP-dependent protein kinase 1 (PKA), cGMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG) and protein kinase C (PKC). Within the AGC family the NTKD of RSK is most closely related to p70 S6 kinase (S6K). The C-terminal kinase domain (CTKD) is a member of the calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase family and its only known function is autophosphorylation. RSK1–3 are activated by sequential inputs from ERK1/2, autophosphorylation by the CTKD and 3-phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase 1 (PDPK1). RSK4 does not appear to require PDK1 for its activation [3]. Additionally, RSK4 is phosphorylated by basal levels of ERK1/2 and is constitutively active in most cell types. Interestingly, in dendritic cells and in some tumor cells stress activated mitogen activated protein kinase-activated protein kinases (MK2/3) are able to phosphorylate and initiate RSK activation by replacing autophosphorylation by the RSK CTKD [4,5]. Whether ERK1/2 or MK2/3 phosphorylates RSK is dependent on the extent of their upstream pathway activation. The phosphorylation motifs required for activation of the RSK family members are identical and therefore, RSK phospho-specific antibodies do not distinguish between the different RSK family members. Unfortunately, studies ascribing activation to a particular RSK member using phospho-specific antibodies confounds the literature.

Individual RSK isoforms have overlapping functions as well as unique functions [6]. Although, inappropriate activity of RSK1–4 has been associated with a number of diseases the contributions of the RSK family to cancer are the most studied [7]. RSK involvement in tumor transformation and progression is independent of developmental origin and it has been suggested that RSK contributes to cancer due to its role in inflammation [7]. Therefore, despite the focus of patented RSK inhibitors for use in cancer it is likely that RSK inhibitors will be useful in the treatment of other disease modalities. In this review we will specifically highlight RSK inhibitor patents from 2020 onwards as a previous review presents earlier patents [2]. However, there are compounds prior to 2020 that we will reference (Table 1) as analogues of these compounds have been recently reported in the patent literature.

Historically important RSK inhibitors prior to 2020

The first evidence of a causative function of RSK in transformed cells was provided by the discovery of the RSK1/2 specific inhibitor, SL0101 [8–10] (Table 1). In 2005 Smith et al. demonstrated that SL0101 inhibited the proliferation of the estrogen receptor positive breast cancer line, MCF-7, without inhibiting the non-transformed breast line, MCF-10A. These studies were supported by observations using siRNA against RSK1 and RSK2 and this report also demonstrated that RSK is overexpressed in ~50% of human breast cancer tissue samples. This study was followed by Clark et al. [11] that showed inhibition of a prostate cancer line using a more potent SL0101 analogue and that RSK is also overexpressed in human prostate cancers. These studies were followed by the synthesis and characterization of the pan-RSK inhibitor, BI-D1870, which was described by Sapkota et al. in 2007 [12] (Table 1). BI-D1870 has also been used to show the importance of RSK in cancer but BI-D1870 also inhibits other targets in addition to RSK and has a rapid biological T1/2 in vivo [13,14]. BI-D1870 was not patented. Based on these early observations, numerous groups have now reported on the function of RSK in diverse cancer types [7,15–18] and importantly, these studies have led to the entry of the first-in-human pan-RSK inhibitor, PMD-026, into clinical trials (NCT04115306). PMD-026 is being developed by Phoenix Molecular Designs and although the company has not released the structure of PMD-026 they have obtained two different patents for RSK inhibitors [19] (Table 1). In phase 1/1b PMD-026 demonstrated an increase in median progression free survival of 4.8 months in patients with triple negative breast cancer (TNBC), which have been pre-treated [20]. Evidence in support of using a RSK inhibitor for TNBC has been reported by several groups [21,22]. However, there is also data supporting RSK2 involvement in estrogen receptor α (ERα) positive (ER+) breast cancer in which the physical association of RSK2 with ERα in the nucleus drives ER+ breast cancer [23,24]. Thus, in the ongoing phase 2 trial PMD-026 is now being evaluated in combination with the selective estrogen receptor degrader, fulvestrant, in patients with locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer that are estrogen receptor positive in combination with high RSK2 and are resistant to CDK4/6 inhibition. Preclinical studies have also shown potential for use of PMD-026 in treating additional types of cancer, including melanoma, prostate cancer and hematological-based cancers [15,25,26]. TAS0612 a multi-kinase inhibitor that targets RSK, AKT and p70 S7 kinase [27] was tested in a phase 1 clinical trial (NCT04586270). Unfortunately, this trial was halted early because of a lack of efficacy in patients with advanced or metastatic solid tumors and a poor safety profile.

Patented RSK inhibitors 2020-present

Six world patents and eleven country patents specifying new chemical matter have been published since 2020. Patents which have been awarded are indicated in bold in the tables. We have divided these chemical entities into four categories: (1) carboxamides derivatives structurally similar to earlier patents on RSK inhibitors by Phoenix Molecular Designs (Table 2); (2) pyrimidine derivatives based on BI-D1870 (Table 3); (3) repurposed drugs demonstrated to be RSK inhibitors (Table 4) and (4) miscellaneous compounds binding to RSK (Table 5). If appropriate the base structure and the placement of the substitutions within that structure and the compound demonstrating the best efficacy in inhibition of RSK in vivo, if provided, or in vitro are shown for each patent. Additionally, modifications creating unique chemical matter are circled for each compound in the tables, if applicable.

Carboxamide derivatives

This series of patents describes analogues that are structurally similar to the original patents described by Phoenix Molecular Designs and presented in Table 1. The patents presented in Table 2 focus on the use of the various analogues for the treatment of breast cancer. The patents did not present the specificity of the analogues for RSK2. The world patent WO2023236960-A1 and the associated country specific patents (Table 2) investigate analogues that were based on WO2017141116-A1 and WO2020165646-A1 (Tables 1 & 2) [19,28,29]. This patent describes unique chemical matter around Ring A (Table 2). An example of the best compound described in this group of patents, Kinoteck-15, as determined by its pharmacokinetic behavior, is provided. The IC50 for RSK2 inhibition for this compound was ~0.1 nM as determined using homogenous time-resolved fluorescence technology in which phosphorylation of substrate is detected by fluorescence resonance energy transfer. These patents included the compound, Pheonix-18, the best compound described in WO2017141116-A1 and WO2020165646-A1, which had in vitro IC50 of 1 nM using the same in vitro assay as for Kinoteck-15. But in a cell based assay the IC50 for Kinoteck-15 in the MDA-MB-453, a TNBC cell line, was ~2.5 μM, only slightly improved compared to Phoenix-18. However, importantly, pharmacokinetic studies of Kinoteck-15, with additional methyl and fluorine groups compared to Phoenix-18, demonstrated a > ~2.5-fold T1/2 in mice. These data are consistent with the literature in which addition of methyl and fluorine moieties are known to improve pharmacokinetic properties [30,31].

Country specific patents have also reported carboxamide derivatives with the best analogue, Sanhome-55, reported by patent CN115611897, which inhibited RSK2 with an IC50 similar to Pheonix-18, however, assay conditions were not provided [32]. It is not possible to determine whether the inhibition of in vitro cell proliferation was improved compared to other patents based on PMD-026 because a different TNBC cell line was tested. The cell line, MDA-MB-468, a HER2- line was used to evaluate Sanhome-55 whereas Kinoteck-15 was tested in MDA-MB-453, a HER2+ cell line [33]. In vivo analysis was not reported. The best analogue described in patent CN117186097-A, Keentai-19, had an in vitro IC50 ~25-fold less than Kinoteck-15 in a similar in vitro assay [34]. However, in vitro cell proliferation using the MDA-MB-453 line demonstrated only an ~2.5-fold improved efficacy compared to Kinoteck-15. Pharmacokinetic studies were not reported. CN115611897 and CN117186097-A do not appear to have chemical matter unique from WO2017141116.

Pyrimidine derivatives

This series of patents are based on the unpatented pan-RSK inhibitor, BI-D1870, which has a number of off target activities [35] (Table 3). This group of patents primarily focuses on identifying analogues, with one exception, for identifying improvements in RSK4 inhibition compared to BI-D1870. There is evidence that RSK4 may drive esophageal [16,36–38] and renal cell carcinoma [39] and promote resistance to some therapies [40–46]. However, there is evidence that RSK4 acts as a tumor suppressor in some cancers [47–53]. The patent WO2021249558 and associated country specific patent introduced modifications around the R3 group that were not previously covered in the patent US7423038 B2 [54,55] (Table 1). The compounds evaluated did not improve the IC50s for the various RSK isoforms compared to BI-D1870 and no further characterization of these compounds was performed. The patents WO2024056091-A1, WO2024056090-A1, and their associated country specific patents also describe various modifications to BI-D1870 [56,57]. WO2024056091-A1 describes unique chemical matter around R2 and R4 compared to WO2021249558. WO2024056090-A1 describes unique chemical matter at R2 and R3 compared to US7687506-B2 and US11851436-B2 [58]. Analysis of the top compounds from these patents showed improved in vitro affinity compared to BI-D1870 for RSK4 in the same assay. These newly reported compounds were also tested on various kidney and esophageal cell lines with most compounds demonstrating a substantial decrease in the IC50 compared to BI-D1870 in those same cell lines. The half-life for the best compounds were <a half hour by intravenous route and ~2 h by oral administration in rats but the results are not directly comparable to BI-D1870 as pharmacokinetics for BI-D1870 were only reported in mice [59]. The country specific patents CN117756805-A and CN113801139-A showed modest improvement in affinity for binding to RSK4 and where tested showed similar affinities for RSK1–4 [60,61]. No other analysis was reported. CN117756805-A has two different base structures with unique chemical matter around R2 position (top structure) and R1 and R2 (bottom structure) compared to BI-D1870 and WO2021249558. CN113801139-A does not appear to have unique chemical matter compared to US7687506-B2 and BI-D1870.

The country patent US11851436-B2 is based on the work described in Casalvieri et al. [62–64]. This patent has unique chemical matter around the amine linkage and R2 compared to the prior patents US7687506-B2 and US8592433B2 (Table 1). CU-17 (labeled as #11 in [63]) had a decreased in vitro efficacy for RSK2 compared to BI-D1870. However, CU-17 has an IC50 of ~0.07 μM in the human myeloid leukemia line, MOLM-13, compared to BI-D1870 which has an IC50 of ~2.6 μM. On target inhibition for RSK2 was provided using a bioluminescence energy transfer to measure the interaction of RSK2 with CU-17 in HEK293 cells. No selectivity screens were performed.

Repurposed compounds targeting RSK4

Several patents have been issued repurposing compounds as RSK4 inhibitors in the treatment for various cancers and as such are method for use patents (Table 4). These repurposed compounds are not specific for RSK and a known RSK inhibitor was not used for comparison in an in vitro kinase assay for any of the patents. Additionally, although not patented, moxifloxacin and trovafloxacin antibiotics, were repurposed to inhibit RSK4 activation by ERK1/2 through an undefined allosteric mechanism [40]. These floxacin antibiotics have an IC50 for RSK4 in the μM range but importantly, trovafloxacin treatment replicated the effects of silencing RSK4 using in vitro and in vivo lung and bladder cancer models.

A world patent WO2024061385-A1 and country specific patents describe the repurposing of selonsertib as a RSK4 inhibitor [65]. Selonsertib was originally developed as an inhibitor for the Ser/Thr kinase, apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 and has an IC50 of ~5 nM [66]. Selonsertib has been evaluated in a number of phase 3 clinical trials for various liver and kidney diseases but these trials were terminated due to lack of efficacy [67,68]. The in vitro IC50 of selonsertib for RSK4 was ~50 nM but information on other RSK isoforms was not provided. Selonsertib has also been found to stimulate the ATPase activity of ATP-binding cassette transporters in the low μM range [69]. The in vitro kinase assay was based on the measurement of ATP in a luminescence assay suggesting that the mechanism of inhibition for selonsertib differs from the floxacins as ERK1/2 was not present in the assay. Selonsertib inhibited proliferation and in vivo tumor growth of the esophageal carcinoma line, TE10, but whether that was due to RSK4 inhibition was not provided. In general, selonsertib at 15 mg/kg inhibited similar or with slightly improved tumor inhibition compared to BI-D1870 at 50 mg/kg in the tumor models evaluated but no evidence was presented for on target RSK inhibition. Addition of a PD-1 monoclonal antibody with selonsertib did not enhance tumor efficacy and a rationale for this combination was not provided.

A world patent WO2026020045-A1 outlines the use of the ruboxistaurin metabolite, N-desmethyl ruboxistaurin, as a pan-RSK inhibitor with an in vitro IC50 in the low nM range, although, the assay was not fully described [70]. However, this metabolite was originally developed for protein kinase C beta (PKCβ) with a reported in vitro IC50 of ~5 nM. N-desmethyl ruboxistaurin also inhibits PKCσ with an IC50 of ~50 nM and PKCα and PKCα with affinities in the high nanomolar range [71]. Ruboxistaurin was originally developed for the treatment of diabetes complications and was evaluated in phase 3 clinical trials but regulatory approval has not been obtained yet [72]. The patent proposes that N-desmethyl ruboxistaruin can be used for the treatment of various cancers via RSK inhibition, but in vitro cell line or in vivo efficacy was not provided.

A country specific patent CN115969855-B describes the repurposing of APY29 for RSK4 inhibition [73]. APY29 was initially described as an allosteric modulator of the active form of inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), which is involved in the unfolded protein response [74]. APY29 inhibited RSK4 with an IC50 of ~55 nM based on the measurement of ATP in a luminescence assay. Data using other RSK isoforms was not provided. Interestingly, the expression level of RSK4 decreased with increasing concentrations of APY29. APY29 also inhibited proliferation of the renal cell carcinoma line, ACNH, with an IC50 ~1 μM. In various xenograft models APY29 (15 mg/kg) partially reduced tumor volume. However, evidence supporting that inhibition of tumor growth was due to targeting of RSK4 was not provided.

The country specific patent CN116763792-B describes the compound HG-14-10-04 for RSK4 inhibition [75]. HG-14-10-04 was previously reported as an inhibitor of anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) and mutants of epidermal growth factor receptor [76] (US20120028924-A1) with IC50s in the range of ~15 – 125 nM. HG-14-10-04 inhibits RSK4 with an IC50of ~84 nM. Inhibition of other RSK family members was not reported. HG-14-10-04 inhibited migration and proliferation of the TE10 line in vitro with a similar IC50 as selonsertib but whether these effects were due to RSK4 inhibition was not presented.

RSK targeting compounds

This section describes compounds that are able to interact with RSK and are structurally distinct from the compounds in Tables 1–4. These patents describe chemical matter that would be unique for inhibiting RSK. The RSK family members have both kinase and scaffolding functions [77] and therefore, proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) technology that results in the degradation of the targeted protein eliminates both functions. PROTAC technology potentially has the ability to degrade a specific RSK isoform, however, the current PROTACs listed in Table 5 for RSK are not specific. The world patent WO2024072178-A1 was designed to target and degrade epidermal growth factor receptor mutant (EGFR) proteins [78]. However, degradation of focal adhesion kinase and RSK1 with Cyrus-G1 occurred within 6 hour at 3 nM concentration, whereas degradation of EGFR required 24 hour at a concentration of 3 nM in lung cancer cells.

World patents WO2021003339-A1 and WO2022006412-A3 focused on identifying PROTAC molecules for AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) inhibition and degradation [79,80]. The two best AMPK degraders were tested against RSK and these were found to be pan-RSK inhibitors. At 100 nM the kinase activity of the N- and C-terminus of RSK were partially inhibited but the kinase assay used was not defined. Furthermore, degradation of RSK was not shown. In a screen of UC-10 showed greater than 50% inhibition at 100 nM in 25% of the kinases tested. The IC50 in the myelogenous leukemia cell line K562 was achieved at high μM concentrations of UC-10 but whether this effect was due to RSK inhibition was not presented.

CSU-1 in the country patent CN119306714-A was identified by modeling to the NTKD crystal structure of RSK2 [81]. In vitro kinase activity was not reported and an IC50 of ~4 μM was obtained in the ovarian cell line, SK0V3 but whether the effect was due to RSK inhibition was not reported.

Conclusion

The patents described in this review either extend on scaffolds that were known to act as RSK inhibitors and were identified prior to 2020 or describe compounds whose off-target effects include RSK inhibition. Most of the patents do not determine whether the described compounds target a particular RSK family member and if presented the isoform specificity is <ten-fold in an in vitro kinase assay, which is unlikely to be physiologically relevant. Additionally, in most patents, the on-target effect of the compounds in the cell-based or in vivo models through assessing biomarkers or by genetic manipulation is not described, so it is unclear if the observed effects are due to RSK inhibition. Furthermore, the specificity of the compounds for RSK are not provided. Very few patents contain pharmacokinetic data. Thus, there appears to be a paucity of efforts toward the development of RSK inhibitors either as useful tool compounds or as drugs, which is surprising given the wide range of physiological outputs that RSK regulates [7].

The recent patents have focused on developing inhibitors to the NTKD, which is responsible for phosphorylation of exogenous substrates. An overlay of the crystal structures of the RSK1/2/4 NTKDs complexed to a non-hydrolysable ATP analogue [40,82,83] demonstrates the similarity of their kinase domains and illustrates the difficulty in developing isoform specific inhibitors that are ATP competitors (Figure 2). The individual functions of each of the RSK family members have not been fully elucidated so it is unknown whether toxic side effects from treatment with a pan-RSK inhibitor will be observed. A RSK isoform can be oncogenic in one tissue or act as a tumor suppressor in a different tissue and therefore, a specific isoform inhibitor would be ideal. For example, RSK1 acts to promote breast [22] and prostate cancer [17] but acts as a tumor suppressor in lung cancer [85]. Based on the ongoing clinical trial with PMD-026 we anticipate more interest in the development of RSK inhibitors.

Expert opinion

Improving treatments for inhibition of metastatic spread continues to be a major goal for increasing overall survival of cancer patients. The RAS-RAF-MEK1/2-ERK1/2 signaling pathway is a driver of metastasis [86]. However, drugs targeting this pathway and the resultant ERK1/2 inhibition can lead to cardiovascular, skin, gastrointestinal, renal and ocular side effects [1]. RSK is known to regulate actin cytoskeleton remodeling, adhesion and leading edge protrusion [87,88], which makes RSK an attractive drug target for metastasis. Targeting RSK, which controls fewer substrates, than its global upstream activator, ERK1/2, will likely result in reduced side effects for patients. Furthermore, in some tissues RSK acts as a negative regulator of ERK1/2, resulting in increased ERK1/2 activity in response to a RSK inhibitor [89]. Thus, loss of the RSK-ERK1/2 negative feedback loop through inhibition of RSK may be protective for some organs [1]. PMD-026 is currently proceeding to phase 2 clinical trials having shown efficacy and a good safety profile in contrast to MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 inhibitors. The structure of PMD-026 has not been publicly disclosed but the observation that Phoenix-18 (Table 1) was described with other compounds in various patents but was also described as a single-compound patent in WO2020165646 (Table 2) suggests that is a valuable asset.

The patents reviewed focus on RSK as a drug target for various cancers but the function of RSK in inflammation suggests that drugs targeting RSK could be beneficial for a variety of diseases [7]. Ideally, the goal for RSK drug development is the generation of isoform specific compounds as these would also elucidate the physiological functions of each isoform and reduce potential side effects for patients. However, information in the various patents is not provided on whether the various compounds are ATP competitors, which is likely as the compounds, when evaluated, act as pan-RSK inhibitors. As illustrated, the NTKDs are very similar to each other (Figure 2) and continued development of ATP competitive inhibitors is unlikely to yield isoform specific compounds. The only inhibitors that have been described, which show isoform specificity, are SL0101 and its analogues and the floxacin antibiotics. SL0101 specifically inhibits RSK1/2 through generation of a unique binding pocket in the NTKD [90]. Furthermore, a SL0101 analogue has been developed that binds to RSK2 but not RSK1 [13]. The floxacin antibiotics specifically inhibit RSK4 through an undefined allosteric mechanism [40]. These results suggest that it may be possible to develop a PROTAC that will result in isoform specific degradation by using the SL0101 or floxacin antibiotic binding site to recruit the ubiquitin-proteasome machinery for specific isoform degradation. Development of an isoform-specific RSK inhibitors would dramatically increase our understanding of the RSK family in disease.
