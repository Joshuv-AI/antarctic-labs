Comparing behavioral cues of self- versus other-assessed authenticity using automated video interviews
Authors: Gloria Liou, Louis Tay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Authenticity at work has become a popular topic among both researchers and practitioners. Yet, what authenticity looks like and how to assess it have remained elusive. Our research uses automated video interviews (AVIs) to empirically identify behavioral cues of authenticity and further examines whether these cues differ between self-evaluations of authenticity and others’ evaluations of one’s authenticity. Using AVI recordings of mock job interviews, we found that using 1st-person singular and present tense, as well as less planned, less filtered, and less formal speech, were related to self-assessed authenticity. Interestingly, the behavioral cues related to interviewer-assessed authenticity were different: more total words, more words per minute, fewer “big” words, and fewer adverbs. Fewer words per sentence was related to both self- and interviewer-assessed authenticity. Notably, we did not find any nonverbal or paraverbal behavioral cues of authenticity. Our research provides a novel, technology-based perspective of the behavioral cues of authenticity and the implications of these findings at work and beyond.

Introduction

In recent years, authenticity at work has attracted attention in both the popular press and academic research. This has ranged from expert advice on being authentic during job interviews to empirical studies on the positive impact of authenticity on job performance and well-being1–5. Although authenticity has been examined in various work and personal contexts, the core definition has remained consistent in the organizational sciences and psychology more generally: behaving in line with one’s “true self” or, in other words, behaving on the outside how one feels on the inside6,7.

Despite this straightforward and broadly accepted conceptualization, what authenticity looks like and how to assess it have remained elusive to researchers. In fact, there is an open debate as to whether authenticity can be accurately assessed by anyone other than oneself. On one hand, to judge whether a person is behaving authentically would require knowing how that person feels on the inside - and that knowledge is only available to the person themselves8,9. On the other hand, authenticity is fundamentally about how one behaves in relation to others and manifests in social contexts10, so people have experience making judgments of authenticity, even if it is being done subconsciously. Assessing whether someone is authentic or not - for example, by noticing behavioral cues - is important because authentic people are more engaged, less stressed, and perform better at work2,7, and in general, are more mindful and less defensive11.

Different situations offer different opportunities to display authenticity behavioral cues12,13. One consequential and relevant situation for assessing authenticity is the job interview. Job interviews are high-stakes situations for both the organization and the applicant, where applicants may be motivated to not be authentic14,15. Furthermore, job interviews are zero-acquaintance situations, as the interviewer and interviewee are typically strangers; examining whether it is possible to assess authenticity when meeting a person for the first time can inform whether time, resources, and mental energy should be invested into the given relationship - and whether there are salient authenticity behavioral cues to look for.

At the same time, automated video interviews (AVIs) have become a popular tool for assessing traits in job interviews. AVIs have been shown to be able to assess Big Five personality with fairly high accuracy and predict levels of different personality traits with behavioral cues that may be hard for the average human to detect (e.g., extraversion using smile intensity and speech rate)16. Moreover, unlike personality, where some behavioral cues are relatively self-evident (e.g., smiling for extraversion), the behavioral cues of authenticity are less straightforward (conceptually, “behaving on the outside how one feels on the inside” shouldn’t look the same for every person) and are not well-established17.

Our research uses AVIs to identify behavioral cues of authenticity and examine whether these cues differ when considering self-evaluations of authenticity versus others’ perceptions of one’s authenticity. In addition to showcasing the use of AVIs for uncovering behavioral cues in psychological research, we also investigate whether AVIs can be used to accurately assess authenticity during job interviews. In summary, our research seeks to uncover behavioral cues of self- vs. other-assessed authenticity with the assistance of modern AVI technology, contributing to authenticity theory and practice and advancing AVIs as a viable authenticity assessment tool.

Results

AVI assessments of authenticity

We asked participants to complete a 10-minute job interview scenario and a self-assessment of their authenticity as part of a larger study. The 10-minute job interview scenario consisted of seven questions displayed on a computer screen and was video recorded using a webcam and microphone. The authenticity self-assessment used the standard psychological measure of dispositional authenticity and assessed three dimensions7. The first dimension is self-alienation, the extent to which a person’s conscious awareness of their values, states, and emotions is mismatched with their actual values, states, and emotions (low self-alienation = high authenticity). The second dimension is authentic living, the extent to which a person behaves in consistency with their conscious awareness of their values, states, and emotions (high authentic living = high authenticity). The third dimension is accepting external influence, the extent to which a person conforms to the expectations and influence of other people (low accepting external influence = high authenticity).

Following standard AVI assessment procedures, we extracted verbal, nonverbal, and paraverbal features from the job interview video recordings and trained ML models to predict authenticity self-assessments using those features. Models were developed and tested using ridge regression and nested cross-validation with k = 10. Our models achieved cross-validated Pearson’s correlations of r = .16-0.26 (all ps < 0.001) with self-assessed authenticity for the model trained on verbal data (i.e., extracted RoBERTa embeddings). Specifically, our models had correlations of r = .26 for self-alienation, r = .16 for authentic living, and r = .23 for accepting external influence. These results provide evidence for AVI’s ability to assess whether an applicant is authentic or not based on self-assessed authenticity, as these values surpassed those found in previous research using AVIs to assess other traits during job interviews; notably, our AVI models were better able to predict self-assessments of authenticity compared to AVI models trained to predict self-assessments of personality (correlations of r = .01-0.21) when following the same procedure16,18.

Interestingly, the models trained on nonverbal and paraverbal features performed significantly worse (i.e., both yielded negligible Pearson’s correlations, r = − .02-0.04, with self-assessed authenticity), suggesting that authenticity does not manifest as paraverbal and nonverbal behaviors. In fact, the combined model (verbal + paraverbal + nonverbal features) yielded smaller correlations (r = .10-0.15) than the verbal-only model, which can happen when adding noisy features in AVI models - in this case, due to contamination from the paraverbal and nonverbal features18.

Additionally, we recruited six trained raters to assess the participants’ authenticity, and we conducted the same AVI procedure on these other-rated assessments. Raters were undergraduate psychology research assistants with experience conducting interviews for student organizations and underwent two additional hours of training, including practice ratings and reading seminal research on authenticity. The raters were randomly assigned to participants to watch their 10-minute job interview video recordings and assess authenticity, with three raters evaluating each participant. The correlation between the raters’ assessments (averaged across the three raters) and self-assessments of authenticity was r = .55 (p < .001) for the full authenticity measure (r = .43-0.57, all ps < 0.001, for dimensions). The AVI models developed and trained to predict other-assessed authenticity performed similarly to previous AVI models trained to predict other-assessed personality (r = .27-0.36, all ps < 0.001, for the verbal model predicting authenticity dimensions compared to r = .27-0.65 for personality dimensions16. As with self-assessed authenticity, the nonverbal and paraverbal models performed significantly worse (r = − .03-0.15).

In summary, our findings suggest that AVIs can be used for assessing authenticity, and that verbal features are predictive of authenticity while nonverbal and paraverbal features are not.

Behavioral cues of authenticity

We identified behavioral cues of authenticity (as assessed by both self and others) with the extracted verbal, nonverbal, and paraverbal features that were used to train the AVI models. We used Pearson’s correlations to identify features that were related to authenticity (specifically, those that were significantly correlated, p < .05 after applying Benjamini-Hochberg’s procedure for Type I correction, and met standard benchmarks for a meaningful effect size in psychological research, r ≥ .1019–21). There were no nonverbal or paraverbal features significantly related to either self- or other-assessed authenticity, which is aligned with our findings that nonverbal and paraverbal AVI models are unable to predict authenticity.

Behavioral cues of self-assessed authenticity

We found that individuals who evaluated themselves as having high authenticity were more likely to speak in 1st person singular (I, me, my, myself) and present focus (most frequently used exemplars: is, are, I’m, can)22; in other words, a person who talks about themselves in present tense tends to rate themselves as more authentic than someone who does not, at least in job interview situations. Additionally, individuals who rated themselves as having high authenticity used fewer words per sentence. They also scored lower on LIWC-22’s Analytic Thinking summary measure, defined as “the degree to which people use words that suggest formal, logical, and hierarchical thinking patterns” and scored higher on LIWC-22’s Authenticity summary measure, defined as language where people “tend to speak more spontaneously and do not self-regulate or filter what they are saying”22. In other words, individuals who used language that was less planned, less filtered, and less formal in job interviews rated themselves as being more authentic than those who used more planned, filtered, and formal language. Behavioral cues of other-assessed authenticity

Interestingly, we found completely different behavioral cues associated with authenticity based on the raters’ evaluations of authenticity (with the exception of fewer words per sentence, which was a behavioral cue of both self- and other-assessed authenticity), despite the significant positive relationship between self- and other-assessed authenticity. See Fig. 1 for the behavioral cues of interviewer-assessed authenticity. Individuals who were rated by others as having high authenticity had significantly higher total word count and words per minute, and used significantly fewer big words, defined as “percent of words 7 letters or longer”22. Based on these findings, those who talk a lot and quickly, but use short words and short sentences, are more likely to be seen by others as authentic. Additionally, individuals who were seen as more authentic in the job interview used fewer adverbs.

Discussion

Our findings provide a novel, technology-based perspective of the behavioral cues of authenticity, and the nuances that surround this conceptually simple yet empirically elusive construct. Foremost, we identified concrete behavioral cues related to authenticity by correlating extracted video data features and self vs. other assessments of authenticity - and notably, all of the behavioral cues we identified were verbal. Past research and expert advice has emphasized facial and body language to convey authenticity17,23–25, but these were based on speculation and theoretical inference rather than empirical data. Using rich behavioral data from video recordings and modern analysis tools (i.e., feature extraction), we found an absence of paraverbal and nonverbal cues related to authenticity. Previous research using AVIs to assess other individual difference traits also found few or no paraverbal and nonverbal behavioral cues and that models trained on paraverbal and nonverbal data were less predictive of traits than those trained on verbal data16,18. Furthermore, in the context of hiring, there are concerns about bias, adverse impact, and the legality of using software to analyze nonverbal behavior26, providing additional reason to focus on verbal behavioral cues when assessing authenticity during job interviews.

The specific verbal behavioral cues of self- and other- assessed authenticity that we identified yield implications for organizations. For example, a job interviewer may perceive an applicant who uses big words and long sentences as being less authentic. If the behavioral cues associated with other-assessed authenticity generalize to work contexts beyond interviews, workers may perceive teammates or supervisors who use big words and long sentences as less authentic as well. For workers, this finding of longer words and sentences being associated with lower authenticity offers practical advice that “sounding smart” (e.g., by using advanced vocabulary or jargon) may not always be beneficial.

Furthermore, while using more 1st-person singular (I, me, my, myself) or less planned and less formal speech was related to self-assessments of authenticity, these cues were not related to others’ assessments of one’s authenticity. This also provides guidance for job applicants: while talking about yourself and sounding “casual” and unrehearsed might feel more authentic (i.e., it is a cue correlated with self-assessed authenticity), it does not necessarily make others see you as authentic (i.e., it is not a cue correlated with other-assessed authenticity). Recent reviews of the authenticity literature have observed that authenticity has been approached in two distinct ways - as an internal state (i.e., feeling) versus as a social perception27,28. Our findings of different behavioral cues for self- and other-assessments of authenticity support this distinction, providing evidence that authenticity as an internal state may perhaps be a distinct construct from authenticity as a social perception, although this warrants further exploration.

Finally, our research encourages and supports the broader use of AVIs as a research tool in the psychological sciences. While AVIs have previously been used primarily in the hiring context for trait assessments, we show that this technology can also be used to identify behavioral cues for constructs where the associated behavioral cues are unknown and in situations requiring analysis of human (or animal) behavioral cues that the average person is unable to detect16,18. AVIs may be especially helpful for research and practice in therapy, rehabilitation, and other health contexts where identifying “small things” (e.g., signs of lying or relapse) is consequential and needs to be scalable.

Limitations and future research

Our research was limited in several ways that warrant further research. First, the behavioral cues we identified were correlates of self- and other-assessed authenticity in this particular interview context and should be interpreted as such. Authenticity may manifest in different ways in other contexts, and both self- and other-assessments of traits during job interviews are subject to known biases14,15. Our study also used a mock interview study design. Although we prompted participants to approach the study as a real job interview (e.g., the instructions immediately prior to starting the AVI video recording said: “You should treat this as a job interview”), participants may have behaved differently than they would in a live, high-stakes job interview with a real company. Partnering with an organization to collect real interview data would strengthen the ecological validity of our findings and would also allow future researchers to examine differences in authenticity cues for interviews conducted live versus asynchronously. Likewise, using trained students as interviewers was another limitation of our study. There is evidence from the organizational literature that trained students can be as accurate as professionals in job interview assessments29–31, and we further ensured that the students had previous experience conducting interviews for extracurricular organizations and underwent two additional hours of training for our study. Nevertheless, future research could investigate whether seasoned HR professionals or hiring managers may notice different authenticity cues based on their experience, and whether the number of years of experience conducting job interviews changes accuracy and/or cues identified.

Additionally, our sampling criteria included native English fluency. This criterion, combined with our finding of authenticity behavioral cues that may be influenced by language fluency, such as using more words per minute (e.g., speaking faster) or using less planned speech, raises questions around assessment of authenticity for non-native speakers. Future research could examine whether non-native English speakers, as well as interviews conducted in other (not English) languages, elicit the same authenticity behavioral cues uncovered in this study, and whether these behavioral cues remain related to authenticity when controlling for adjacent constructs such as verbal ability, communication skill, social fluency, confidence, and extraversion. Moreover, future research could explore whether displaying authenticity behavioral cues alone changes others’ assessments of authenticity (i.e., whether authenticity cues can be “faked”). Faking of behaviors associated with desirable traits is an issue in job interviews32,33, and with concrete behavioral cues that are not particularly difficult to copy or train (e.g., using fewer “big” words), applicants may try to appear authentic to interviewers without actually being authentic (i.e., without actually behaving on the outside how they feel on the inside). On the other hand, it is possible that exhibiting the behavioral cues associated with self-assessed authenticity (e.g., using more 1st-person singular) can increase applicants’ feelings of authenticity or support applicants in expressing on the outside how they are feeling on the inside.

Finally, future research could explore whether these authenticity behavioral cues generalize beyond job interview and organizational contexts. For example, researchers could examine whether politicians, public figures, and social media influencers exhibiting the identified behavioral cues of other-assessed authenticity (e.g., using short vs. long words and sentences) are seen as more or less authentic, respectively, or whether behavioral cues such as using less formal speech are associated with self-assessments of authenticity. Our findings lay the groundwork for future research in this increasingly relevant area.

Methods

Sample and procedures

We recruited 500 participants on CloudResearch Connect to participate in an asynchronous job interview. All participants were required to be full-time employed (i.e., paid to work at least 30 h per week), native English speakers, and have access to a computer with a camera and microphone. We removed 46 participants who did not provide usable responses (e.g., interview responses too short, no audio and/or video in recordings, failed attention checks), leaving an analytic sample of 454. The analytic sample consisted of adults ranging from ages 18 to 73 (M = 38.26, SD = 10.48); 241 women (53%), 205 men (45%), and 8 nonbinary individuals (2%); 341 White (75%), 69 Black (15%), 20 Asian (4%), 7 American Indian (2%), and 17 individuals (4%) who did not identify with any of the provided racial group options (4%). We obtained informed consent from all participants before beginning study procedures, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. This study was approved by Purdue University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB-2024-382).

For the asynchronous job interview, participants video recorded themselves answering three common interview questions (e.g., “Please tell us about yourself and your background”), two at the beginning and one at the end of the interview34. In the middle, they answered three questions designed to tap into the three dimensions of authenticity: self-alienation, authentic living, and accepting external influence7, detailed in the Results section above. For example, for self-alienation, the screen showed: “In general, do you feel as if you know yourself well? Consider whether you feel in touch with the ‘real you’ - your emotions, beliefs, thoughts, etc.” The inclusion of questions specifically assessing authenticity and its dimensions was based on previous research assessing other traits in job interviews, including those using AVI procedures16,35–37. All questions were unstructured to allow for more variation in response.

We instructed participants to spend 1–3 min responding to each question. On average, participants in the analytic sample spent 1 min 18 s on each question, totaling 9 min and 6 s for the seven interview questions. Participants whose total interview length was less than 6 min were not included in the analytic sample as their responses were considered too short. After the job interview, participants completed survey questions for the authenticity measure detailed below.

Measures

Self-assessed authenticity

Participants completed the established dispositional Authenticity Scale7. The scale consists of twelve items, and all items use a seven-point Likert scale from 1 (Does not describe me at all) to 7 (Describes me very well). The scale measures the three authenticity dimensions (self-alienation, authentic living, and accepting external influence) with four items for each dimension.

Other-assessed authenticity

Three of the six raters were randomly assigned to watch each video recording and rate participants on an adapted version of the Authenticity Scale, with each question reframed to an observer’s perspective. For example, “I think it is better to be yourself, than to be popular” became “They think it is better to be themselves, than to be popular.” Interrater reliability was Cronbach’s α = 0.94 for the other-assessed authenticity scale.

AVI assessments of authenticity

Feature extraction

To assess authenticity using AVIs, we first extracted verbal, paraverbal, and nonverbal features from our video data. For verbal features, we first used OpenAI Whisper to transcribe participant responses38, and then extracted RoBERTa embeddings using the HuggingFace sentence transformer39 and categories from the 2022 version of LIWC (LIWC-22)22. We removed LIWC categories that did not occur in at least 5% of the interviews. For paraverbal features, we used openSMILE to extract the standard Geneva Minimalistic Acoustic Parameter Set, which includes pitch, frequency, and jitter40. For nonverbal features, we used OpenFace to extract facial action units (AUs) and head poses41 and calculated the mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis of the intensity for each AU. Additionally, we extracted co-occurrence distributions for each pair of AUs42,43, which captures more complex facial expressions than individual AUs. For example, co-occurrences for smile and eye-related AUs may differentiate genuine smiles from acted smiles where the mouth moves independently of the muscles around the eyes16.

Model development and accuracy testing

Using the extracted features, we developed models to predict self- and interviewer- assessments of authenticity. We explored three model types (ridge regression, elastic nets regression, and lasso regression) that have been used for similar psychological research and were techniques noted in a recent review of AI-based deception detection using video data16,18,44; the difference between model accuracy was negligible, with ridge regression performing better than the other two models. Models were developed and tested using nested cross-validation with k = 10. In other words, the data was split into 10 equal parts, and nine of these parts were used for model selection (i.e., choosing the optimal hyperparameters). The selected model is then trained using the nine parts, and the model’s accuracy is tested on the 10th part. This process is repeated 10 times, using each of the 10 parts once for testing. This process combines the benefits of k-fold cross-validation, which balances model bias and variance to maximize out-of-sample accuracy45,46, and nested cross-validation, which ensures that information about model accuracy is not used for hyperparameter tuning, reducing the likelihood that accuracy will be overestimated16. We used 100-repeats for testing, meaning that the entire process is repeated 100 times, with randomly chosen 10-part divisions each time. We developed models for all features combined, as well as models with verbal-only (RoBERTa embeddings, LIWC categories), paraverbal-only (openSMILE), and nonverbal-only (OpenFace) features.

Behavioral cues of authenticity

To identify behaviors that differentiate high and low authenticity individuals, we examined the correlations between the extracted features of both self- and interviewer-assessed authenticity. In particular, we considered features with significant (p < .05 after applying Benjamini-Hochberg’s procedure for Type I correction) correlations and Pearson’s r ≥ .10. We focused on identifying the LIWC categories (verbal features) and head poses and facial action units (nonverbal features) as these are more observable to the average person than embeddings or paraverbal features, although we performed analyses for all feature types.
