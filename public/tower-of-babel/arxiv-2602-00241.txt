Does Algorithmic Uncertainty Sway Human Experts? Evidence from a Field Experiment in Selective College Admissions
Hansol Lee, AJ Alvero, René F. Kizilcec, Thorsten Joachims

Abstract
. Algorithmic predictions are inherently uncertain: even models with similar aggregate accuracy can produce different predictions for the same individual, raising concerns that high-stakes decisions may become sensitive to arbitrary modeling choices. In this paper, we define algorithmic reliance as the extent to which a decision outcome depends on whether a more favorable versus less favorable algorithmic prediction is presented to the decision-maker. We estimate this in a randomized field experiment ( n = 19,545 n=19{,}545 ) embedded in a selective U.S. college admissions cycle, in which admissions officers reviewed each application alongside an algorithmic score while we randomly varied whether the score came from one of two similarly accurate prediction models. Although the two models performed similarly in aggregate, they frequently assigned different scores to the same applicant, creating exogenous variation in the score shown. Surprisingly, we find little evidence of algorithmic reliance: presenting a more favorable score does not meaningfully increase an applicant’s probability of admission on average, even when the models disagree substantially. These findings suggest that, in this expert, high-stakes setting, human decision-making is largely invariant to arbitrary variation in algorithmic predictions, underscoring the role of professional discretion and institutional context in mediating the downstream effects of algorithmic uncertainty.

1.
Introduction

Algorithmic predictions are inherently uncertain. Even when trained on similar data and exhibiting comparable aggregate accuracy, different models can produce meaningfully different predictions for the same individual—a phenomenon known as
predictive multiplicity

(
2
)
. This instability raises a fundamental concern for high-stakes decision-making: whether outcomes become sensitive to arbitrary modeling choices rather than substantively meaningful differences between individuals. A growing literature has raised normative and practical concerns about such algorithmic uncertainty, emphasizing the risk that consequential decisions may depend on modeling choices that are difficult to justify on substantive grounds
(
21
;
6
)
.

Much of this concern implicitly assumes settings in which algorithmic predictions are treated as authoritative or determinative. In practice, however, many high-stakes decisions remain human-mediated, with algorithms deployed as inputs to human decision-making rather than automated decision-makers
(
16
;
30
)
. In these contexts, the central question is not whether algorithms are correct—since many such decisions lack a canonical notion of correctness
(
5
)
— but whether, and how, variation in algorithmic assessments influences high-stakes human decisions.

This issue is increasingly salient as algorithm-assisted decision-making becomes embedded in consequential decision-making processes. Colleges and universities, in particular, face rapidly growing application volumes and have begun integrating algorithmic tools into admissions review. For example, Virginia Tech recently announced a new undergraduate admissions process that pairs human essay readers with an AI-supported scoring system developed by university researchers
(
23
)
. The system is explicitly framed as a support tool rather than a decision-maker: discrepancies between human and AI assessments trigger additional human review, and final admissions decisions are made exclusively by trained admissions professionals. This design reflects a broader institutional emphasis on human–algorithm collaboration, while underscoring the importance of understanding how algorithmic assessments interact with human judgment in practice.

In human-mediated settings, the central risk posed by predictive multiplicity is not that algorithms make incorrect decisions, but that human decision-makers may place undue weight on the particular algorithmic score they observe. We refer to this phenomenon as
algorithmic reliance
: a decision-making pattern in which algorithmic predictions exert disproportionate influence on human judgments, even when those predictions reflect model-contingent variation rather than substantively meaningful differences between individuals.

This conception of algorithmic reliance differs from much of the prior literature, which evaluates reliance relative to a known ground truth and defines appropriate use in terms of improved decision accuracy. In domains such as college admissions, decisions are not objectively optimal in this sense
(
5
;
29
)
, making such definitions inapplicable. Instead, the relevant normative concern is whether human decisions become sensitive to algorithmic variation that lacks substantive justification. In contexts characterized by predictive multiplicity, algorithmic reliance is present if different algorithmic scores—produced by similarly accurate and equally defensible models for the same individual—lead to meaningfully different human decisions.

Empirically identifying algorithmic reliance in real-world settings is challenging. Directly randomizing whether decision-makers observe higher versus lower algorithmic predictions is typically infeasible or unethical in high-stakes contexts, as it would deliberately disadvantage some individuals. As a result, existing evidence on human–algorithm interaction largely comes from observational studies (e.g.,
(
3
;
16
)
) or controlled laboratory experiments that abstract away from organizational context and stakes (e.g.,
(
9
)
). These approaches provide limited insight into whether algorithmic uncertainty affects decisions in real institutional settings.

In this paper, we leverage predictive multiplicity to overcome this challenge. We study a randomized field experiment embedded in a selective U.S. college admissions process (
n=19{,}545
applications). During the 2022 admissions cycle, admissions officers reviewed applications alongside an algorithmic score intended as a coarse, directional signal to support attention allocation. We developed two predictive models using the same features and modeling pipeline but trained on slightly different historical data. Although the two models achieved similar aggregate predictive performance, they frequently assigned different scores to the same applicant. For each application, we randomized which of the two models’ score is presented to the admissions officers. This design generates ethically supportable exogenous variation in the algorithmic prediction presented to decision-makers, without explicitly altering the model prediction. By conditioning on the pair of model predictions and exploiting randomized score presentation, we identify the causal effect of presenting a more favorable versus less favorable algorithmic prediction for the same applicant.

We find substantial individual-level algorithmic disagreement: the two models assign different score deciles to over 70% of applicants, with disagreement concentrated among borderline cases. Despite this, we find little evidence of algorithmic reliance. Presenting a more favorable score does not meaningfully increase an applicant’s probability of admission on average, even when the models disagree substantially. Across specifications, admission outcomes are largely invariant to which of the two disagreeing algorithmic scores is shown. These findings suggest that, in this expert, high-stakes decision-making setting, algorithmic uncertainty does not mechanically propagate into final decision outcomes. Rather than responding directly to arbitrary variation in algorithmic assessments, admissions officers appear to integrate algorithmic scores as one input among many within a holistic evaluation process.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we conceptualize algorithmic reliance as sensitivity of human decisions to model-contingent variation in algorithmic predictions, a notion particularly suited to human-mediated settings that lack an objective ground truth. Second, we introduce an ethically supportable experimental approach for identifying algorithmic reliance in high-stakes contexts by leveraging predictive multiplicity to generate exogenous variation in algorithmic assessments without degrading advice quality or disadvantaging individuals. Third, we apply this framework in a real institutional setting, providing rare field-based causal evidence on how algorithmic uncertainty interacts with expert human judgment in practice.

2.
Related Work

Our study sits at the intersection of research on human reliance on algorithmic advice and research on instability and arbitrariness in predictive models.

2.1.
Human Reliance on Algorithmic Advice

A large literature examines how humans respond to algorithmic advice. Prior work shows that algorithmic decision aids can improve efficiency and accuracy in a range of domains
(
16
;
20
)
. At the same time, a growing body of research highlights risks of miscalibrated reliance, including excessive deference to algorithmic outputs (often termed automation bias)
(
12
)
and systematic discounting of algorithmic advice following observed errors (algorithmic aversion)
(
9
)
. Recent work has proposed formal frameworks for evaluating reliance and decision quality
(
14
)
and documented reliance failures in settings such as policing, hiring, and social services
(
3
;
32
;
27
;
31
)
.

Importantly, the degree of reliance often depends on the user’s expertise and the task at hand. In web search, for example, it is well documented that algorithmic advice in the form of rankings has profound impact on outcomes
(
15
)
. On other tasks, experts may act as a corrective force, overriding or contextualizing algorithmic recommendations using domain knowledge and professional judgment
(
8
;
26
)
. However, this literature typically studies reliance with respect to a single, fixed algorithm. It largely leaves open how human decision-makers respond when algorithmic assessments themselves are unstable, and when different, equally defensible models produce conflicting recommendations for the same case.

2.2.
Model Instability and Algorithmic Arbitrariness

A parallel line of research shows that instability is a structural feature of modern predictive modeling. Early work on the
Rashomon Effect
emphasized that many distinct models can achieve similar aggregate performance on the same task
(
4
)
. More recent work formalizes this phenomenon as
predictive multiplicity

(
21
;
2
)
or
underspecification

(
11
)
, highlighting that equally accurate models may yield different predictions for the same individual.

This instability has raised normative concerns about arbitrariness and procedural fairness. When outcomes depend on which of several equally defensible models is deployed, decisions may appear difficult to justify on substantive grounds
(
6
)
. Such arbitrariness threatens perceived legitimacy and trust, which are central to the acceptance of algorithmic systems
(
19
;
10
)
. However, this literature primarily documents instability at the level of model outputs. It often implicitly assumes that model-level disagreement translates directly into outcome-level arbitrariness, without empirically testing whether or how such instability propagates through human-mediated decision processes.

3.
Context: Algorithm-assisted college admissions at a selective U.S. university

Selective college admissions provides a natural and consequential setting for studying human–algorithm interaction. Admissions decisions are not reducible to ranking applicants by a single notion of merit; instead, they involve constructing a class that satisfies multiple, often competing institutional goals
(
29
)
. Admissions officers therefore engage in holistic review, synthesizing quantitative indicators with qualitative assessments of context, potential, and institutional fit
(
22
)
. As a result, admissions decisions are inherently judgment-based and normatively contested, rather than objectively correct
(
5
)
.

Our study is situated at a highly selective U.S. university during the 2021–2022 admissions cycle, following the widespread adoption of test-optional policies in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. These changes substantially reduced the availability of standardized test scores—historically a central quantitative input into admissions decisions—while application volumes increased, placing additional time and attention constraints on admissions officers
(
1
)
. To support holistic review under these conditions, the case institution introduced predictive algorithms trained on historical admissions data. The goal was not to automate admissions decisions, but to provide an additional, test-free signal that could help reviewers allocate limited attention and ensure that strong applicants were not inadvertently overlooked (for details on the design of these algorithms, see
(
17
;
18
)
).

The algorithms generated predicted probabilities of admission based on information available at the time of application, including academic records and other observed characteristics. For operational use, these predictions were discretized into deciles (1–10, with 10 indicating the highest predicted probabilities) and displayed to admissions officers to guide their workflow, for example by prioritizing higher-scoring applications for earlier review. Importantly, admissions officers retained full discretion to make holistic judgments based on essays, recommendation letters, contextual background, and institutional priorities not captured by the model.

A key feature of this setting (not uncommon in social prediction contexts) is that predictive models are inherently uncertain
(
25
)
. Even when trained on similar data and achieving comparable aggregate performance, different models can assign meaningfully different scores to the same applicant
(
2
)
. As a result, the specific algorithmic score shown to a reviewer may depend on modeling choices rather than substantively meaningful differences between applicants. If admissions officers place undue weight on the particular score they observe, such model-contingent variation could propagate into high-stakes downstream decisions.

Directly manipulating or degrading algorithmic advice to study reliance would be ethically impermissible in consequential domains such as college admissions. Our study addresses this common challenge by exploiting predictive multiplicity
(
2
)
as a source of naturally occurring, ecologically valid variation. By randomly assigning applications to be accompanied by a score from one of two similarly accurate models that sometimes disagree at the individual level, we test whether, and to what extent, variation in algorithmic assessments causally influences human decision-making. In this setting, systematic sensitivity to which of two equally plausible scores is presented constitutes evidence of algorithmic reliance.

4.
Methods

In the following, we define algorithmic reliance and introduce an experimental design that permits its estimation in a field setting.

4.1.
Defining Algorithmic Reliance

We study whether decision outcomes for the same individual depend on which algorithmic prediction is presented when multiple plausible predictions exist. We define
algorithmic reliance
as the population-level causal effect of presenting a more favorable algorithmic prediction rather than a less favorable one on downstream decisions, holding fixed the individual. Throughout, algorithmic reliance is the estimand of interest. In settings of predictive multiplicity, positive reliance reflects sensitivity to model-contingent variation in algorithmic scores (over-reliance), while negative reliance reflects systematic counteracting of the algorithmic signal (algorithmic aversion).

Let
A
denote the space of algorithmic predictions that may be presented to a decision-maker. For any
a\in A
, let

Y_{i}(a)\in\{0,1\}

denote the potential decision outcome for individual
i
if prediction
a
were presented, where
Y_{i}(a)=1
indicates a favorable decision outcome (e.g., admission).

Let
\succ
denote an ordering over
A
such that
a^{H}\succ a^{L}
means
a^{H}
is more favorable than
a^{L}
. For any ordered pair
(a^{H},a^{L})\in A\times A
with
a^{H}\succ a^{L}
, define
algorithmic reliance
as

(1)

\text{AR}(a^{H},a^{L})\;:=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_{i}(a^{H})-Y_{i}(a^{L})\right].

Under this definition, algorithmic reliance has the following behavioral interpretation:

(1)

\text{AR}(a^{H},a^{L})\approx 0
: showing
a^{H}
rather than
a^{L}
does not materially change decisions.

(2)

\text{AR}(a^{H},a^{L})>0
: showing
a^{H}
rather than
a^{L}

increases
the likelihood of a favorable decision; larger values indicate stronger reliance on the algorithmic signal.

(3)

\text{AR}(a^{H},a^{L})<0
: showing
a^{H}
rather than
a^{L}

decreases
the likelihood of a favorable decision; larger negative values indicate stronger counteracting or aversive responses to the algorithmic signal.

This definition does not assume the existence of a ground-truth or objectively correct decision, nor does it require that either the human decision or the algorithmic prediction be accurate. Algorithmic reliance is defined purely in terms of how observed decisions respond to changes in the algorithmic input, holding the individual fixed.

For example, if across individuals decisions are favorable 40% of the time when
a^{H}
is shown and 30% of the time when
a^{L}
is shown, then
\text{AR}(a^{H},a^{L})=0.10
. This means that presenting
a^{H}
rather than
a^{L}
increases the probability of a favorable decision by 10 percentage points on average, regardless of whether either prediction—or the resulting decisions—is correct. In settings characterized by predictive multiplicity, algorithmic reliance captures whether model-contingent differences in algorithmic scores translate into differences in real outcomes for the same individual.

4.2.
Experiment Design

Studying algorithmic reliance in high-stakes decision settings poses an ethical challenge. Directly randomizing whether a decision-maker is presented with a higher versus a lower algorithmic score would require intentionally disadvantaging some individuals by exposing reviewers to systematically less favorable information, which is infeasible in consequential domains such as college admissions. This constraint has contributed to the prevalence of reliance studies in low-stakes or synthetic settings.

We address this challenge by leveraging
predictive multiplicity

(
2
)
: the fact that multiple predictive models can achieve similar aggregate performance while producing different scores for the same individual. Rather than manipulating the favorability or quality of algorithmic advice, our design exploits naturally occurring, model-contingent variation among equally plausible predictions. This approach generates exogenous variation in the algorithmic score observed by decision-makers without degrading model accuracy or explicitly disadvantaging any applicant.

Our field experiment was conducted during the 2022 Regular Decision admissions cycle at a highly selective U.S. university with
n=19{,}545
applications. At the time of the experiment, the institution used algorithmic scores as a coarse, directional signal to help organize reviewer attention within a large and competitive applicant pool. The scores were explicitly framed as decision aids rather than determinative inputs, with final admissions decisions made exclusively by trained admissions professionals.

4.2.1.
Model development

We developed two predictive models, Model 1 and Model 2, using the same modeling pipeline but slightly different training data. Both models are gradient boosted decision tree classifiers trained to predict the probability of admission, following prior work in this setting
(
17
;
18
)
. The data used to train the two models comes from the university’s 2019–20 and 2020–21 admissions cycles. This includes all information submitted via the Common Application, such as:

•

Academic Information:
High school GPA, class rank, courses taken, AP/IB scores, and optional SAT subject scores.

•

Personal Information:
Personal essays, descriptions of extracurricular activities, honors and awards, intended major, legacy status, and demographic indicators (gender, race and ethnicity, first-generation status).

•

Not included in the feature set
: We did not have access to recommendation letters or school-specific counselor reports. To simulate the test-optional environment of our experiment, we explicitly excluded SAT/ACT and TOEFL/IELTS scores from the feature set.

The two models differ only in their training data, designed to induce realistic variation:

•

Model 1
was trained only on applicants from the 2020–21 Regular Decision cycle.

•

Model 2
was trained on a larger dataset including applicants from both the 2019–20 and 2020–21 Regular Decision cycles.

Each model outputs a predicted probability of admission, which is discretized into deciles (1–10) before being displayed to reviewers. This discretization aligns with institutional practice and reflects the goal of providing a coarse, directional signal rather than a precise probability.

4.2.2.
Randomized Algorithmic Score Presentation

For each applicant
i
, both model scores are computed offline prior to human review, denoted
(S_{i1},S_{i2})\in\{1,\ldots,10\}^{2}
. Exactly one of these scores is shown to admissions officers. Which model’s score is displayed is randomly assigned for each
i
:

W_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5),\quad W_{i}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{Model~1 score presented},\\
1,&\text{Model~2 score presented}.\end{cases}

All other aspects of the applications and the decision-making processes are held fixed. Admissions officers were blind to which model produced the scores they were presented with.

Randomization yielded 9,765 applications assigned to the Model 1 condition and 9,780 to the Model 2 condition. The outcome of interest,
Y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, indicates whether applicant i was ultimately admitted; the overall admission rate in the analytic sample is 5.44%. Applicant characteristics are well balanced across experimental arms (Table
2
), consistent with successful randomization.

4.3.
Estimating Algorithmic Reliance

Our experimental design randomizes which model’s score is displayed, not whether reviewers see a more favorable versus a less favorable prediction. When the two models disagree for a given applicant, however, the randomized model assignment induces exogenous variation in the favorability of the displayed score. We show how this permits identification and unbiased estimation of algorithmic reliance for the subset of prediction pairs that arise from model disagreement.

4.3.1.
Setup and estimand

For each applicant
i
, two model scores are computed prior to review,

(S_{i1},S_{i2})\in\{1,\ldots,10\}^{2},

corresponding to Model 1 and Model 2. Exactly one score is shown to admissions officers. Let
W_{i}\in\{0,1\}
indicate which model is displayed, where
W_{i}=0
denotes Model 1 and
W_{i}=1
denotes Model 2, with
\Pr(W_{i}=1)=\Pr(W_{i}=0)=1/2
by random assignment. Let
Y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
denote the realized admission outcome.

Define the disagreement set
\mathcal{D}:=\{i:S_{i1}\neq S_{i2}\}
. For each
i\in\mathcal{D}
, define the more and less favorable realized scores as

H_{i}:=\max(S_{i1},S_{i2}),\qquad L_{i}:=\min(S_{i1},S_{i2}).

Let
Y_{i}(H_{i})
and
Y_{i}(L_{i})
denote the potential admission outcomes if the more favorable versus less favorable score were shown. We define algorithmic reliance in our setting as

(2)

\text{AR}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y_{i}(H_{i})-Y_{i}(L_{i})\mid i\in\mathcal{D}\right].

4.3.2.
Decomposition

By the law of iterated expectations,

AR

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Pr(S_{i1}>S_{i2}\mid i\in\mathcal{D})}_{\omega_{1>2}}\cdot\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(H_{i})-Y_{i}(L_{i})\mid S_{i1}>S_{i2}]}_{\text{AR}_{1>2}}

(3)

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\Pr(S_{i1}<S_{i2}\mid i\in\mathcal{D})}_{\omega_{2>1}}\cdot\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(H_{i})-Y_{i}(L_{i})\mid S_{i1}<S_{i2}]}_{\text{AR}_{2>1}}.

Here
\omega_{1>2}
and
\omega_{2>1}
are the frequencies of the two disagreement directions, and

\text{AR}_{1>2}
and
\text{AR}_{2>1}
are the corresponding stratum-specific components of algorithmic reliance.

4.3.3.
Identification

Within
\mathcal{D}
, whether the higher score is shown is a deterministic function of
(S_{i1},S_{i2})
and
W_{i}
:

•

If
S_{i1}>S_{i2}
, the higher score is shown if and only if
W_{i}=0
.

•

If
S_{i1}<S_{i2}
, the higher score is shown if and only if
W_{i}=1
.

Since
W_{i}
is randomized, the stratum-specific components satisfy

\text{AR}_{1>2}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid W_{i}=0,S_{i1}>S_{i2}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid W_{i}=1,S_{i1}>S_{i2}],

and

\text{AR}_{2>1}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid W_{i}=1,S_{i1}<S_{i2}]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid W_{i}=0,S_{i1}<S_{i2}].

In our analysis, we therefore estimate
\text{AR}_{1>2}
and
\text{AR}_{2>1}
by sample differences in means and combine them using the empirical weights
\widehat{\omega}_{1>2}
and
\widehat{\omega}_{2>1}
to obtain

\widehat{\text{AR}}=\widehat{\omega}_{1>2}\widehat{\text{AR}}_{1>2}+\widehat{\omega}_{2>1}\widehat{\text{AR}}_{2>1}.

5.
Results

Our analysis proceeds in three steps. First, we establish that the algorithmic scores shown to admissions officers are decision-relevant inputs: higher scores are associated with higher admission probabilities, and this relationship is similar between the two models. Second, we characterize the nature of the algorithmic variation introduced by the experiment, documenting how often and in what ways the two models disagree in their predictions of the same applicant. Finally, leveraging this randomized variation in score presentation, we estimate algorithmic reliance: whether admission decisions are systematically influenced by which of two disagreeing but equally plausible algorithmic scores is shown. Together, these analyses allow us to assess whether individual-level algorithmic instability propagates into downstream admission decisions made by admissions officers.

5.1.
How do algorithmic scores relate to admission decisions?

Before presenting our estimates of algorithmic reliance, we first examine how the algorithmic scores used in the experiment relate to downstream admission outcomes. Understanding this relationship is important for two reasons. Substantively, it establishes whether the scores are meaningful inputs into the decision process. Methodologically, it clarifies whether the two models operate on comparable score scales, which matters for interpreting the experimental manipulation.

Figure 1.
Proportion of applicants admitted, by displayed algorithmic score decile, shown separately for applicants randomly assigned to display Model 1 scores (green) or Model 2 scores (orange) to admissions officers. Error bars indicate 95% confidence intervals.

Figure
1
plots observed admission rates by presented score, shown separately for applicants randomly assigned to present the score from Model 1 or Model 2. We observe two things: first, in both conditions, admission rates increase monotonically with the presented score. Second, we observe that across the full score distribution, the two curves closely overlap. This indicates that although the two models may differ in their assessments of individual applicants, a given score value corresponds to similar admission outcomes regardless of which model generates it. In other words, the score scales produced by the two models are similarly calibrated with respect to observed decisions.

Taken together, these results show that algorithmic scores are strongly predictive of admission outcomes and are comparably situated across models. At the same time, calibration alone does not describe the experimental manipulation itself. The experiment operates by randomly revealing one of two potentially different scores for the same applicant. To interpret the causal analyses that follow to estimate algorithmic reliance, we therefore next characterize the nature and extent of the algorithmic disagreement induced by this design. This establishes that the algorithmic scores are consequential inputs into the admissions process, so differences in which score is shown could plausibly affect decisions if reviewers are sensitive to them.

5.2.
What algorithmic disagreement does the experiment introduce?

Although the two models are trained on the same feature set and achieve comparable predictive performance, they need not assign identical scores to the same applicant. This implies that even equally accurate models can disagree at the individual level. The experiment exploits this disagreement by randomly revealing one model’s score rather than the other.

5.2.1.
How much do the models disagree at the individual level?

We begin by quantifying the extent of individual-level disagreement between the two models. Although the models are similar in aggregate, they frequently diverge in their assessments of the same applicant. In our analytic sample, the two models assign different decile scores to 73.2% of applicants. The mean absolute difference in assigned scores is 1.49 deciles (SD = 1.38), and the correlation between the two scores is moderate (
\rho=0.75
).

Together, these patterns indicate that the experimental manipulation introduces meaningful variation in the algorithmic scores shown to admissions officers—variation that is more concentrated among applicants for whom decision-making is most difficult. This individual-level disagreement, despite similar aggregate model performance, provides the key source of variation we exploit to study algorithmic reliance in subsequent analyses. Because applicants are randomly assigned to which model’s score is shown, this disagreement creates exogenous variation in the algorithmic information presented to reviewers.

5.2.2.
Is one model systematically more favorable?

Figure 3.

Despite substantial individual-level disagreement, the two models are intentionally constructed to be symmetric in the aggregate. The mean predicted score is 5.4822 under Model 1 and 5.4821 under Model 2, with no statistically significant difference, and the distribution of signed score differences (Model 2 minus Model 1) is approximately symmetric and centered near zero (Figure
3
) by design. This symmetry is also evident conditional on disagreement. In the notation of Section
4.3
, the weights in the decomposition of algorithmic reliance satisfy

\omega_{1>2}=\Pr(S_{i1}>S_{i2}\mid i\in\mathcal{D})=0.501,\qquad\omega_{2>1}=\Pr(S_{i1}<S_{i2}\mid i\in\mathcal{D})=0.499.

Thus, the two disagreement directions occur with nearly equal frequency. Neither model is systematically more lenient or more stringent in the aggregate, so the weighting in the algorithmic reliance estimand does not mechanically favor one model’s scores over the other. Consequently, any estimated algorithmic reliance reflects sensitivity to individual-level disagreement rather than global, systematic differences between the models.

6.
Discussion

This study asks whether exposure to a more favorable algorithmic assessment biases human decisions through
algorithmic reliance
. In a randomized field experiment embedded in a selective U.S. college admissions process, we find little evidence of such reliance. Although algorithmic scores are strongly associated with admission outcomes and the two models frequently disagree at the individual level, particularly for applicants near the margin, these disagreements do not translate into systematic differences in admission decisions based on which score is shown. Across multiple specifications, estimated reliance effects are small in magnitude, often statistically insignificant, and inconsistent in direction, indicating neither over-reliance nor algorithmic aversion on average.

These findings directly address concerns about algorithmic arbitrariness: the possibility that equally reasonable models yield conflicting evaluations for the same individual, rendering high-stakes decisions sensitive to arbitrary modeling choices
(
2
;
6
;
21
)
. While predictive multiplicity is substantial in our setting, we find that it does not propagate into final outcomes through algorithmic reliance. In this human-mediated context, instability at the level of model outputs does not imply instability at the level of realized decisions.

The absence of systematic reliance likely reflects key features of the decision-making context. Admissions decisions are holistic, integrating rich qualitative materials—such as essays, letters, and contextual background—alongside quantitative indicators
(
29
)
. Algorithmic scores were explicitly framed as coarse, directional signals intended to support attention allocation rather than as prescriptive recommendations. which likely discouraged over-reliance. In addition, decisions were made by expert admissions officers operating under strong institutional norms and accountability structures. Together, these features plausibly reduce sensitivity to modest, model-contingent variation in algorithmic scores by enabling decision-makers to contextualize, cross-check, and discount signals that conflict with broader assessments
(
26
)
.

From a policy perspective, our results suggest that the risks posed by predictive multiplicity depend critically on how algorithms are embedded within organizational decision processes. Much concern about arbitrariness implicitly assumes settings in which algorithmic outputs directly determine outcomes or are treated as authoritative judgments. Our evidence highlights the role of professional discretion as a buffering mechanism. At the same time, this buffering role should not be assumed to generalize. In settings with less expertise, weaker accountability, or tighter coupling between algorithmic predictions and downstream decisions, algorithmic arbitrariness may have more pronounced downstream effects. In this admissions context, human judgment appears to attenuate, rather than amplify, algorithmic reliance on unstable model outputs.

More broadly, this study contributes to the literature on human–algorithm collaboration by emphasizing that the consequences of algorithmic uncertainty are properties of socio-technical systems, not models alone. Prior work shows that reliance on algorithmic advice varies with task structure, incentives, expertise, and institutional context
(
9
;
13
;
28
)
. The admissions process studied here is highly structured and staffed by expert reviewers, conditions that may facilitate calibrated algorithmic reliance rather than mechanical adoption. Identifying when such calibration holds, and when it breaks down, remains a central challenge for the design of responsible algorithm-supported decision-support systems.

Methodologically, our study offers a field-based approach to evaluating algorithmic reliance under predictive multiplicity. Whereas prior work has documented disagreement across models in static datasets or examined advice-taking in laboratory settings
(
16
;
9
;
7
;
31
)
, our randomized design isolates model-contingent variation within an operational decision process. By constructing two counterfactual algorithmic environments that differ only in which similarly accurate model is shown, we provide direct causal evidence on whether realistic modeling variation induces algorithmic reliance in consequential human decisions. This design can be extended to other domains, such as hiring, lending, or peer review, where predictive multiplicity is common and final decisions remain human-mediated.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the two models were trained on temporally adjacent cohorts rather than randomized splits of identical data, so some of the observed disagreement may reflect cohort-level distributional shifts rather than pure sampling variability
(
24
)
. While this reflects realistic deployment conditions, future work could more cleanly isolate responses to sampling noise—for example, by training multiple models on random subsets of the same underlying data. Second, our findings are specific to a setting with substantial procedural structure and expert reviewers; different dynamics may emerge in less structured environments, where algorithmic outputs are more tightly coupled to decisions or where decision-makers have less discretion or expertise.

Finally, interpreting estimates of algorithmic reliance requires care. Because our estimand captures the causal effect of being shown one algorithmic score rather than another, a null effect does not by itself imply robustness to algorithmic information. Little or no reliance (
\text{AR}\approx 0
) may arise either because decision-makers largely ignore algorithmic scores, as in settings where professional judgment dominates
(
3
)
, or because expert decision-makers actively incorporate algorithmic information while discounting modest or unstable variation across models
(
26
)
. Positive reliance (
\text{AR}>0
) occurs when more favorable scores increase the likelihood of favorable decisions and, in contexts characterized by predictive multiplicity, is consistent with over-reliance on model-contingent variation. Negative reliance (
\text{AR}<0
) reflects counteracting responses to algorithmic recommendations, consistent with algorithmic aversion
(
12
;
9
)
. Our results suggest that the admissions context we study falls into the first of these regimes: across specifications, we find little evidence of reliance in either direction. However, because our estimates capture an intent-to-treat effect of score presentation, the design cannot distinguish between indifference to algorithmic information and calibrated discretionary use that corrects for algorithmic uncertainty—an important direction for future work.
