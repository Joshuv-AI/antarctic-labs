Antibiotic overuse in children with viral infections: lessons from the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic
Authors: Juliane Wurm, Michael Buettcher, Eric Giannoni, Lisa Kottanattu, Guido F Laube, Anita Niederer-Loher, Nina Schöbi, Jessica Wey, Petra Zimmermann, Nicole Ritz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough COVID-19 is usually mild in children, antibiotics are frequently prescribed during hospitalisation, often without evidence of bacterial infection.ObjectiveTo assess the frequency and predictors of antibiotic use in children hospitalised with SARS-CoV-2 infection in Switzerland.MethodsIn this nationwide prospective study, children aged 0–18 years hospitalised with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection or symptoms compatible with paediatric inflammatory multisystem syndrome temporally associated with SARS-CoV-2 (PIMS-TS) between January 2022 and March 2023 were included. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to identify risk and protective factors for antibiotic use. A sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding high-risk patients.ResultsAmong 1944 hospitalised children, 586 (30%) received antibiotics. Of these, 71 (12%) had a clinically suspected bacterial infection without microbiological confirmation and 41 (7%) had microbiologically confirmed infection. Predictors of antibiotic use were microbiologically confirmed bacterial infection (aOR 138.08), clinically suspected bacterial infection (aOR 114.48), haematologic/oncologic disease (aOR 6.77), PIMS-TS (aOR 5.36), fever (aOR 2.98), neonatal age (aOR 2.80) and treatment in tertiary centres (aOR 1.65). After excluding high-risk patients, however, treatment in tertiary centres and PIMS-TS were no longer significant predictors in the sensitivity analysis. Respiratory distress/tachypnoea (aOR 0.72) and admission with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 as the primary diagnosis (aOR 0.42) were associated with lower antibiotic use in the primary analysis. After excluding high-risk patients, this association was no longer observed and nasal symptoms (aOR 0.68) and seizures (aOR 0.34) emerged as protective factors.ConclusionAntibiotics were frequently prescribed to children hospitalised with SARS-CoV-2 despite limited evidence of bacterial infection. Our findings underscore the need to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship in paediatric COVID-19 care.Keywords: COVID-19, Child Health, Adolescent Health

Introduction

Children with SARS-CoV-2 infection are generally less severely affected than adults and have a low risk for serious complications.1,3 Nevertheless, many hospitalised children received antibiotics during the COVID-19 pandemic, often despite the absence of documented bacterial co-infection.4 5 This practice may reflect clinical uncertainty, institutional habits, cognitive biases or a lack of paediatric-specific antibiotic prescribing guidelines. Inappropriate antibiotic use can drive antimicrobial resistance, disrupt the microbiota and cause avoidable adverse effects as well as increased healthcare costs, underscoring the importance of antibiotic stewardship in paediatric care, generally and also in pandemic situations.6 7

In this study, we assessed the frequency and determinants of antibiotic use among hospitalised children with SARS-CoV-2 in Switzerland. Associations between antibiotic prescribing and demographic, clinical, and institutional characteristics were systematically analysed.

Methods

In this nationwide prospective observational study, data were collected on hospitalised children (0–18 years) with laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection (done by PCR from nasopharyngeal or throat swabs, PCR from saliva or antigen testing) through the Swiss Paediatric Surveillance Unit (SPSU). SPSU is a national surveillance system for rare paediatric diseases and rare complications of more common conditions in hospitalised children.8 All children admitted because of SARS-CoV-2 infection, as well as those admitted for other reasons with an incidental laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, and children presenting with symptoms compatible with paediatric inflammatory multisystem syndrome temporally associated with SARS-CoV-2 (PIMS-TS), were included. All 29 paediatric hospitals in Switzerland reported cases monthly and completed a standardised electronic case report form using REDCap.9 This analysis includes data collected between 1 January 2022 and 31 March 2023.

Collected variables include demographic data, symptoms (eg, fever, cough, etc.), comorbidities (respiratory, cardiovascular, haematologic/oncological disease, diabetes mellitus and/or other), reasons for hospitalisation (symptoms related to SARS-CoV-2, symptoms compatible with PIMS-TS, hospitalisation not related to SARS-CoV-2, other/unknown), and the hospital’s level of care. The following levels of care were used: tertiary care hospitals (university hospitals and large paediatric hospitals with a high number of specialist care), secondary care hospitals (lower number or only selected specialist care), primary care hospitals (primary care without specialists).10 Analyses were performed using the following age groups: neonates (birth–28 days), infants (29 days–12 months), toddlers (1–3 years), preschoolers (4–6 years), school-age children (7–12 years) and adolescents (13–18 years). Bacterial infections were classified into three categories: (1) no documented bacterial infection, (2) clinically suspected bacterial infection without microbiological confirmation and (3) microbiologically confirmed bacterial infection. Suspected bacterial infection was recorded based on the documented clinical diagnosis in the medical record (e.g., pneumonia, ear–nose–and–throat infections or sepsis). Microbiological testing was based on the treating clinicians’ judgement and was not applied systematically across all hospitalised children. Data on blood and urine cultures were recorded in the dataset; respiratory samples and non-culture-based diagnostic methods such as PCR for bacterial co-infection and antibiotic susceptibility testing were not captured in our surveillance dataset. Positive culture results were interpreted by the treating clinicians, and findings considered not clinically relevant were classified as contamination. Outcomes included length of hospital stay, complications (respiratory, cardiac/vascular, neurologic and other) and severity (not admitted to intensive care unit (ICU), admitted to ICU without ventilation/administration of inotropics, admitted to ICU with ventilation/administration of inotropics). Clinical and microbiological data reflected the entire hospitalisation episode; the protocol did not capture predefined or time-stamped assessment points (eg, at admission or discharge).

Differences between children who received antibiotics and those who did not were assessed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables. Continuous variables (eg, length of hospital stay) were summarised using the median and IQR. Institutional variability was examined by comparing antibiotic use across hospitals’ levels of care using pairwise comparisons between groups. Temporal trends in antibiotic prescribing were visualised by month to explore potential seasonal patterns. We did not analyse differences between SARS-CoV-2 variants, as individual-level information on viral variants was not available and Omicron was the predominant variant throughout the study period.

We performed univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses to identify factors associated with antibiotic use. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, given the exploratory nature of the univariable analyses. Variables with a p value <0.05 in univariable analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable models using a forward selection approach. A forward selection approach was chosen to obtain a parsimonious model and to avoid overfitting given the number of candidate predictors and outcome events. All variables included in the regression models were categorical; hospital level was modelled with primary care hospitals as the reference group.

To assess the robustness of our findings and minimise potential confounding from standard guidelines recommending empiric use of antibiotics due to vulnerability or clinical conditions, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis excluding the following patients: (1) neonates, who often receive antibiotics empirically due to the high risk of sepsis and non-specific early infection signs,11 (2) children admitted to ICU, who are frequently treated empirically with broad-spectrum antibiotics due to non-specific signs of inflammation and the potentially severe consequences of missed bacterial infection,12 13 (3) children with haematologic or oncologic comorbidities, who may be immunocompromised and at increased risk for bacterial infection, often prompting prophylactic or early empiric antibiotic use14 and (4) children hospitalised for other reasons with an incidental laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, as antibiotic use in this group likely reflects unrelated indications.

All analyses were done with R software V.4.2.2 (R Foundation).15

Results

A total of 1944 hospitalised children with SARS-CoV-2 infection were included, corresponding to approximately 86% of all eligible cases nationwide during the study period. SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis was based predominantly on PCR on nasopharyngeal swabs (97%), followed by antigen testing (2%), while other diagnostic methods (throat-swab PCR, saliva PCR and other methods) were each used only rarely (<1%). Overall, 586 (30%) received antibiotics (see table 1). Children who received antibiotics were significantly more likely to be neonates (12% (95% CI 10 to 16) vs 6% (95% CI 5 to 8), p<0.005) and preschoolers (12% (95% CI 10 to 15) vs 8% (95% CI 6 to 9), p<0.005), whereas infants were less likely to receive antibiotics (42% (95% CI 38 to 46) vs 52% (95% CI 50 to 55), p<0.005). Underlying comorbidities were more common in children treated with antibiotics (18% (95% CI 16 to 24) vs 10% (95% CI 9 to 13), p<0.005), with haematologic/oncologic diseases being most frequent (9% (95% CI 7 to 13) vs 1% (95% CI 1 to 2), p<0.005). Hospital admission reasons also differed: children receiving antibiotics were less frequently hospitalised for SARS-CoV-2-related symptoms (71% (95% CI 67 to 75) vs 78% (95% CI 76 to 81), p<0.005) but were more often admitted for conditions unrelated to SARS-CoV-2 (22% (95% CI 19 to 26) vs 18% (95% CI 9 to 13), p<0.005) or with symptoms suggestive of PIMS-TS (3% (95% CI 2 to 5) vs 1% (95% CI 0 to 1), p<0.005).

Among children treated with antibiotics, 474 (81%) had no documented bacterial infection, 71 (12%) had a clinically suspected bacterial infection without microbiological confirmation and 41 (7%) had a microbiologically confirmed infection (see figure 1). Among those with clinically suspected bacterial infection without microbiological confirmation, 30 (42%) had pneumonia as the most common diagnosis, followed by 22 (31%) with ear–nose–and–throat infections and 3 (4%) with sepsis. In total, blood cultures were performed in 391 (67%) children receiving antibiotics compared with 297 (22%) children not receiving antibiotics, while urine cultures were performed in 260 (44%) and 278 (21%) children, respectively. Microbiologically confirmed infections were based on 29 (71%) positive urine cultures and 12 (29%) positive blood cultures.

Symptoms differed between groups: fever was more common in the antibiotic group compared with the no-antibiotics group (76% (95% CI 72 to 79) vs 67% (95% CI 64 to 69), p<0.005), whereas rhinorrhoea/nasal congestion (35% (95% CI 31 to 39) vs 40% (95% CI 37 to 43), p=0.029), cough (32% (95% CI 29 to 36) vs 40% (95% CI 37 to 42), p<0.005), respiratory distress (19% (95% CI 16 to 22) vs 25% (95% CI 23 to 28), p<0.005) and pharyngitis (15% (95% CI 13 to 19) vs 23% (95% CI 21 to 25), p<0.005) were less frequent in the antibiotic group compared with the no-antibiotics group (see figure 2).

Antibiotic use varied widely between hospitals, ranging from 0% to 50%. When grouped by level of care, usage was highest in tertiary care hospitals (33% (95% CI 30 to 35)) and lower in secondary care hospitals (19% (95% CI 14 to 25)) and primary care hospitals (23% (95% CI 17 to 30)), with both comparisons (tertiary vs primary and tertiary vs secondary care hospitals) reaching statistical significance (p<0.05) (see online supplemental figure 1). Among children treated with antibiotics, clinically suspected bacterial infection was most frequent in secondary (20% (95% CI 10 to 35)) and primary care hospitals (19% (95% CI 9 to 35)) and lowest in tertiary care hospitals (10% (95% CI 8 to 13); p=0.08 and p=0.13, respectively). Microbiologically confirmed bacterial infection among children treated with antibiotics was also most frequent in primary care hospitals (12% (95% CI 4 to 26)), followed by secondary care hospitals (11% (95% CI 4 to 25)) and tertiary care hospitals (6% (95% CI 4 to 9); p=0.24 and p=0.31, respectively).

The results of the univariable analysis are presented in online supplemental table 1. In the multivariable logistic regression, the strongest predictors of antibiotic use were a microbiologically confirmed bacterial infection (aOR 138.08 (18.51 to 1029.77), p<0.001) and a clinically suspected bacterial infection (aOR 114.48 (95% CI 34.75 to 377.11), p<0.001, see figure 3 and online supplemental table 4). Other independent predictors included haematologic/oncologic comorbidities (aOR 6.77 (95% CI 3.84 to 11.94), p<0.001), PIMS-TS (aOR 5.36 (95% CI 1.94 to 14.79), p<0.005), fever (aOR 2.98 (95% CI 2.14 to 4.14), p<0.001), neonatal age (aOR 2.80 (95% CI 1.95 to 4.02), p<0.001) and being treated in a tertiary care hospital compared with primary care hospitals (aOR 1.65 (95% CI 1.08 to 2.52), p=0.02). In contrast, admission with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection as the primary diagnosis (aOR 0.42 (95% CI 0.23 to 0.79), p=0.006) and respiratory distress/tachypnoea (aOR 0.72 (0.53 to 0.99), p=0.05) were associated with reduced antibiotic use.

In a sensitivity analysis, neonates, patients admitted to the ICU, those with haematologic or oncologic comorbidities, and children hospitalised for reasons unrelated to SARS-CoV-2 infection were excluded. The results of the univariable analysis are presented in online supplemental table 2. In the multivariable logistic regression model, microbiologically confirmed bacterial infection (aOR 140.98 (95% CI 17.63 to 1127.61), p<0.001) and clinically suspected bacterial infection (aOR 95.54 (95% CI 27.56 to 331.18), p<0.001) remained the strongest predictors of antibiotic use (see figure 4 and online supplemental table 5). Fever was also associated with increased antibiotic use (aOR 2.79 (95% CI 1.73 to 4.48), p<0.001). In contrast, nasal symptoms (aOR 0.68 (95% CI 0.50 to 0.92), p =0.01) and seizures (aOR 0.34 (95% CI 0.17 to 0.69), p < 0.005) were associated with reduced antibiotic use. PIMS-TS, respiratory distress/tachypnoea and the hospital’s level of care did not remain significant predictors in this cohort.

Multivariable regression analysis of factors associated with antibiotic use in children without high-risk conditions. Excluded from the analysis were neonates, patients admitted to the intensive care unit, children with haematologic or oncologic comorbidities, and those whose hospitalisation was unrelated to SARS-CoV-2 infection. The model included variables that were significant in univariate analysis: see online supplemental table 2 (n=1137).

Systemic corticosteroids were administered in 31 (5%, 95% CI 4 to 7) children receiving antibiotics and in 144 (11%, 95% CI 9 to 12) children not receiving antibiotics, with a significantly lower proportion among antibiotic-treated children (p<0.001). Other (not further specified) anti-inflammatory treatments were used in 235 (40%, 95% CI 36 to 44) children receiving antibiotics and in 609 (45%, 95% CI 42 to 48) children not receiving antibiotics, without a statistically significant difference between groups (p=0.06).

Children who received antibiotics had a longer median hospital stay compared with those who did not (3 vs 2 days, p<0.005, see online supplemental table 3). The proportion of non-ICU hospitalisations was lower in the antibiotic group (86% (95% CI 83 to 89) vs 92% (95% CI 90 to 93), p<0.005), whereas ICU admissions for non-severe disease were more frequent (9% (95% CI 6 to 11) vs 4% (95% CI 3 to 5), p<0.005).

Antibiotic use varied across months, ranging from 22% to 38% (SD=5%). There were no significant differences between seasons (p=0.73, online supplemental figure 2).

Discussion

Diagnostic uncertainty and cognitive biases in paediatric antibiotic prescribing

In our paediatric COVID-19 cohort, a third received antibiotics, although among those only 19% had a clinically suspected bacterial infection without microbiological confirmation or a microbiologically confirmed infection. A major challenge in interpreting antibiotic prescribing patterns is the difficulty of confirming bacterial infections in children, particularly in the context of viral illness. Clinical features such as fever and organ-specific symptoms overlap between viral and bacterial infections.16 17 Laboratory biomarkers may provide supportive evidence but lack sufficient specificity,18 19 while cultures required for confirmation take several days. This diagnostic uncertainty often drives empiric use.

Cognitive factors further influence antibiotic prescribing decisions. According to the dual process theory of decision-making, clinicians alternate between fast, intuitive judgments (type 1) and slower, deliberate reasoning (type 2).20 In the stressful and fast-paced environment of clinical care, reliance on type 1 thinking supports efficiency but increases susceptibility to cognitive biases21: fear of missing a severe bacterial infection can lower the threshold for antibiotic use, corresponding to commission bias—a tendency toward action rather than inaction.21 Time preference may play a role, as clinicians may prioritise immediate benefits over potential long-term harms, also corresponding to hyperbolic discounting. Heuristics also shape decisions—for example, reliance on recent memorable cases (availability bias), typical disease patterns (representativeness bias) or anchoring on initial clinical impressions. While these mental shortcuts facilitate rapid judgments, they also introduce systematic errors.21 22 In addition, physicians may arrive at different conclusions when confronted with similar clinical scenarios at different times, a variability conceptualised as noise, reflecting random fluctuations in judgement.23 24

The lower use of corticosteroids among children receiving antibiotics likely reflects different clinical scenarios, with antibiotics initiated in the context of suspected bacterial infection and diagnostic uncertainty, and corticosteroids used more commonly in predominantly inflammatory disease; differences in treatment timing may also have contributed.

International comparisons

Antibiotic use of 30% in our cohort lies in the lower range compared with international reports. US studies reported rates of 49%–63% of antibiotic use despite bacterial co-infection being confirmed in only 7%–10% of cases.4 25 In Spain, 54% of children hospitalised with COVID-19 or PIMS-TS received antibiotics, with confirmed bacterial infection in 7%.26 In contrast, a study including children from Peru, Costa Rica, Argentina, Colombia and Mexico reported a lower rate of 25%.5 These variations likely reflect the influence of cognitive bias and local clinical practice.

Antibiotic prescribing is also common in children with other viral respiratory tract infections, with reported rates ranging from 8% to 40%, and up to 60% in influenza cases.25 27 Nevertheless, children with viral respiratory tract infections consistently receive fewer antibiotics than adults with similar infections.28,30 Furthermore, according to data from the electronic health records of the largest private healthcare company in Finland, antibiotic prescription rates for upper respiratory tract infections in children in one setting halved from 18% in 2014 to 9% in 2020.31

Risk factors for antibiotic prescription in children with SARS-CoV-2 infection

In both of our models, the main predictor of antibiotic use was a clinically suspected bacterial infection without microbiological confirmation or a microbiologically confirmed infection. This suggests that prescribing was driven by suspected or confirmed co-infection, although in cases of clinical suspicion, the infection may not actually have been present. Children with haematologic/oncologic comorbidities and neonates were more likely to receive antibiotics. This reflects established clinical practice, where thresholds for treatment are deliberately low to avoid missing life-threatening bacterial infections.1132,35 PIMS-TS was another predictor of antibiotic use, aligning with clinical guidelines recommending empiric treatment if bacterial sepsis cannot be excluded—within 1 hour of presentation in patients with shock, and within up to 3 hours for those without shock.36 Elevated inflammatory markers likely further influenced decision-making in this context.37 In line with this, Spanish and Polish studies reported antibiotic use in 89% and 90% of children with PIMS-TS, respectively.26 38 In our sensitivity analysis, however, the association between PIMS-TS and antibiotic use was no longer significant, likely because many of these patients required intensive care and were excluded from the model. Fever was the only individual symptom independently associated with higher antibiotic use, even after excluding high-risk populations. A finding, which is consistent with a Spanish study, where fever was also a predictor of antibiotic prescription in children with COVID-19.26 Although fever rarely triggers antibiotic use in isolation, it is often interpreted in combination with signs of systemic inflammation, the child’s age, maximum temperature and fever duration. High fever (≥40°C) in infants under 3 months is associated with an increased risk of serious bacterial infection; however, this risk decreases substantially in older children with similar temperatures.39 40 Furthermore, prolonged fever has been linked to a higher likelihood of bacterial infection. However, most children with prolonged fever do not have a confirmed bacterial infection.41 42 Unfortunately, our study lacked data on both maximum temperature and duration of fever, limiting further interpretation. Nevertheless, our findings highlight the need for clearer guidelines on the age-appropriate management of febrile presentations in children, as fever alone—particularly in older children without additional risk factors or clinical signs of bacterial infection—should not be considered a sufficient indication for antibiotic treatment.

Although tertiary centres appeared more likely to prescribe antibiotics despite lower rates of clinically suspected bacterial infection without microbiological confirmation or microbiologically confirmed infection, this association disappeared after excluding high-risk children. This suggests that differences in patient case-mix rather than institutional prescribing practices largely explain the observed variation. However, some differences between centres may be explained by differences in how clinicians manage diagnostic uncertainty, resulting in varying thresholds for initiating antibiotic treatment, particularly in febrile children or those with non-specific symptoms.

Protective factors for antibiotic prescription in children with SARS-CoV-2 infection

Interestingly, certain symptoms—such as respiratory distress/tachypnoea, rhinorrhoea and seizures—are associated with lower odds of antibiotic treatment. This likely reflects clinicians’ recognition that these symptoms are more commonly linked to viral or non-bacterial aetiologies, which in children are typically self-limiting.43 Respiratory symptoms such as respiratory distress/tachypnoea and nasal congestion likely reduced diagnostic uncertainty by aligning with a typical viral respiratory phenotype of paediatric COVID-19, allowing clinicians to withhold antibiotics.3 Similarly, seizures, particularly febrile seizures, are generally self-limiting and not indicative of bacterial central nervous system infection unless accompanied by other warning signs.44 Furthermore, seizures emerged as a well-described symptom of COVID-19 during the Omicron phase—affecting 5%–6% of hospitalised children—and may, therefore, not have been perceived as a warning sign for bacterial infection.3

Harms associated with antibiotic use and overuse in children

First, antibiotic use in children is associated with acute adverse effects, including gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhoea, as well as skin reactions like rash and, more rarely, anaphylaxis.45 Second, antibiotics disrupt the intestinal microbiota and may have long-term effects on allergic symptoms, wheezing, asthma, overweight or obesity, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, psoriasis, autism spectrum and neurodevelopment disorders.6 7 Third, inappropriate antibiotic prescribing accelerates the development of antimicrobial resistance, increasing the risk of treatment failure, hospitalisation and mortality.46 Reducing unnecessary antibiotic exposure in children is, therefore, a key component of both individual patient safety and global antimicrobial stewardship efforts.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s key strength is the use of a comprehensive national cohort of almost 2000 children hospitalised with SARS-CoV-2 infection, with standardised data collection from all paediatric hospitals in Switzerland, capturing a large majority of all eligible cases during the study period. Several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. Microbiological testing for bacterial infection was limited to blood and urine cultures and was not performed systematically, but rather according to clinical judgement. Respiratory microbiological testing and non-culture-based diagnostic methods (including rapid molecular assays) were not recorded. In addition, suspected bacterial infection was based on clinical judgement rather than predefined criteria, introducing potential misclassification. Limited microbiological testing and reliance on clinical judgement are likely to have resulted in underestimation and non-differential misclassification of bacterial coinfections and to have weakened rather than inflated the observed associations. Data on inflammatory biomarkers were missing for a substantial proportion of patients, although elevated biomarkers may have triggered antibiotic prescribing in many cases. Furthermore, we lacked information on the type, timing and duration of antibiotic treatment, including when antibiotics were discontinued, as well as on the timing of diagnostic testing and detailed clinical data such as symptom duration and maximum temperature, which limited the interpretation of some predictors and the differentiation between community-acquired and nosocomial infections. Given the number of univariable comparisons performed, some associations observed in univariable analyses may be due to chance as a result of multiple testing.

Conclusion

In this national cohort, antibiotics were frequently prescribed to children hospitalised with SARS-CoV-2 infection despite a low prevalence of microbiologically confirmed or clinically suspected bacterial infections. Fever emerged as a key trigger for antibiotic use. Our findings highlight the need for targeted antibiotic stewardship interventions to reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure in children with viral infections.
