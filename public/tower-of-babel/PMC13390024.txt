Virus Research Special Issue “Cross-kingdom infection by viruses”
Authors: Nobuhiro SUZUKI, Shin-Yi L MARZANO, Taiyun WEI, Massimo TURINA
Published: 2026

There is an increasing number of cases in which viruses naturally infecting wild vertebrates undergo host-jump or spill over into humans and cause epidemics and/or pandemics. Viruses generally infect organisms within a single kingdom and rarely do so across kingdoms. For example, SARS-CoV-2, still occurring in humans is assumed to have originated from a bat (mammalian animal). Although we eat a variety of vegetables and fruits infected by plant viruses on a daily basis, these viruses never cross kingdoms and infect us. It is a rule of modern virology that every virus can infect a limited number of taxa within single kingdoms, whether its host range is broad or narrow.

Plants, fungi and animals, belonging to the three major kingdoms, have their own histories of evolution with different cellular environments and immune systems. Thus, it is assumed that there are barriers which prevent cross-kingdom infection and spread of single viruses. However, diverse eukaryotic viruses are predicted based largely on phylogeny to have been horizontally transferred among organisms across the three kingdoms, Animalia, Plantae and Fungi (Dolja and Koonin, 2018; Kondo et al., 2022). Historically, rice dwarf virus (a phytoreovirus) is the first virus that has demonstrated the ability to infect both plants and insect vectors (Fukushi, 1969). Some other groups of viruses such as plant rhabdovirids and tospovirids, causing serious diseases in important crops, are now known to be replicated in and transmitted by vector insects, shuttling between plants and animals (Sun et al., 2021). Great advances have been made on the tripartite interactions between plants/insects/viruses (Chen et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2022; German et al., 2020; Wei and Li, 2016).

Recently, natural or experimental cross-kingdom infections by single viruses or viroids were reported between fungi and insects, and between plants and fungi independently (Andika et al., 2023; Andika et al., 2017; Bian et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2021; Nerva et al., 2017; Telengech et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2019) (Figure 1). These cross-kingdom infections have generated considerable research interest and raised a number of intriguing questions. For example, how do viruses adapt to host environments across different biological kingdoms? How do hosts from different kingdoms mount defenses against the same virus? Conversely, how do viruses deploy counterdefense mechanisms against distinct host antiviral responses? These and related questions are highlighted in this Special Issue (SI) of Virus Research.

Kondo et al. (Kondo et al., 2025) demonstrated the replication of a plant rhabdovirus with a negative-sense (-), bi-segmented RNA genome in its arthropod mite vector, Brevipalpus californicus s.l. Wu et al. (Wu et al., 2025) reported the infection of the phytopathogenic fungus Diaporthe helianthi by a (-)RNA virus showing similarity to insect qinviruses. Yoshioka et al. (Yoshioka et al., 2025) described a novel multisegmented positive-sense (+)RNA fungal virus with rod-shaped particles and structural similarities to plant potyviruses and closteroviruses. Adegbola et al. (Adegbola et al., 2025) illustrated the high prevalence and genetic divergence of palo verde broom virus, a multisegmented (-) RNA plant virus belonging to the genus Emaravirus, which is transmitted by and likely replicates in eriophyid mites. Lin et al. (Lin et al., 2026) demonstrated bidirectional promoter activities of the intergenic region (IR) of the monopartite single-stranded DNA geminivirus, ageratum yellow vein virus in Escherichia coli. Bono et al. (Bono et al., 2026) showed that a homolog of a plant movement protein, encoded by a fungal phenuivirid with a multisegmented (-)RNA genome, is unable to facilitate virus movement. Finally, Telengech et al. (Telengech et al., 2026) described a platform for studying persistent fungal partitiviruses in planta, which is also applicable to other viruses that are difficult to inoculate experimentally.

We believe that this topic will be of broad interest to the readership of Virus Research, including bacterial, vertebrate, invertebrate, plant, and fungal virologists.
