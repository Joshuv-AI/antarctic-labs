COVID-19 awareness and preparedness in secondary schools, Egypt
Authors: Nahed M. Abdel Ghani, Ekram W. Abd El-Wahab, Azza Mehanna, Sherif Omar Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic emphasized the need for health literacy and preparedness in schools. Evaluating students’ awareness and school readiness is crucial to identify gaps and guide effective interventions for safer educational environments. This study aimed to assess knowledge, attitude, practice (KAP), and school environmental preparedness regarding COVID-19 among secondary school students in Desouk City, Egypt. A cross-sectional study was conducted from January to May 2022 involving 800 students from five randomly selected schools using proportional and systematic random sampling. Data was collected through a self-administered questionnaire and an adapted school environment inspection checklist. Participants had a mean age of 15.95 ± 1.11 years, and most were female (54.5%). The internet (58.8%) and social media (40.4%) were their main sources of COVID-19 information. While 60.6% had good knowledge about the disease, the majority demonstrated poor attitudes (52.9%) and practices (56.1%). Recognition of key symptoms like anosmia and dyspnea was relatively high. Female students and those attending a specific private school were more likely to have good knowledge. Despite satisfactory knowledge levels, gaps remain in students’ attitudes and practices. Interventions tailored to their primary information sources are recommended, alongside school-level efforts to foster a health-supportive environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-36285-9.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the novel coronavirus SARS-CoV-2, emerged in late 2019 and rapidly evolved into one of the most significant global public health crises in modern history. Its widespread transmission resulted in millions of infections and fatalities worldwide, along with substantial disruptions to healthcare systems, education, economies, and social structures1. In response, governments implemented various mitigation strategies including international travel restrictions, nationwide lockdowns, closure of educational institutions, and public health campaigns aimed at curbing virus transmission2.

In Egypt, the reported case and mortality rates were relatively lower compared to many countries in the region and globally3. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain this phenomenon, including Egypt’s younger population structure, climatic and environmental conditions, and early adoption of public health measures. With over 60% of its population under the age of 304, school-aged children and adolescents represent a significant demographic. This segment plays a pivotal role not only in community-level disease transmission but also in the long-term success of public health education and behavior change interventions.

School environments are particularly relevant in the context of respiratory virus outbreaks, as overcrowded classrooms, limited access to hygiene resources, and varying levels of health literacy make them both vulnerable to disease spread and vital targets for preventive interventions5. The COVID-19 pandemic further underscored the critical role of schools in public health by significantly impacting education systems worldwide and highlighting the need for improved health literacy and preparedness. In this context, assessing the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of secondary school students toward COVID-19 alongside the environmental readiness of educational settings, is essential for understanding students’ awareness levels, behavioral patterns, and the overall capacity of schools to support disease prevention. Addressing these factors in secondary schools helps identify gaps and inform targeted interventions to promote safer learning environments.

Globally, numerous studies have explored COVID-19-related KAP among adolescents and youth populations in countries such as China, Italy, Ethiopia, and Saudi Arabia, revealing varying levels of understanding and adherence to preventive behaviors6–8. However, in Egypt, the literature on COVID-19 KAP among secondary school students remains limited. Most available research has focused on university students or the general adult population9,10, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of younger students’ preparedness, especially within public school settings.

The present study aims to assess the level of COVID-19 awareness and preparedness among secondary school students in Desouk city, Egypt. It evaluates their knowledge of the disease, perceptions of its severity and transmission, adherence to recommended preventive practices, and the influence of environmental and institutional factors on these behaviors. The findings will inform the development of tailored school-based health education programs and contribute to Egypt’s broader efforts in strengthening public health preparedness for current and future pandemics.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted between January to May 2022, in secondary schools located in Desouk, a major city (150768 inhabitants over 5,30 km) in Kafr El-Sheikh Governorate, in the Nile Delta region of northern Egypt. The target population included students enrolled in secondary education across Desouk City. The external and internal facilities of these schools were also evaluated as part of the environmental preparedness assessment.

Sampling strategy

The minimum sample size was calculated based on the assumption that 50% of students had good knowledge of COVID-19, with a 5% margin of error, α = 0.05, and a design effect of 2. The estimated sample size was 768, which was rounded to 800 for convenience. A stratified random sampling method was used to select participating schools. Desouk City comprises 10 administrative villages and includes 15 secondary schools: 11 public, 3 language schools, and one private school. Of these, three language schools were excluded due to their limited secondary-level enrollment. The final sampling frame included 12 schools. These were stratified based on student enrollment size into high-density schools (more than 750 students) and low-density schools (fewer than 750 students), with two schools randomly selected from each stratum.

In the high-density stratum, School A for Boys (924 students) and School B for Girls (1996 students) were selected. In the low-density stratum, School C for Boys (670 students) and School D for Girls (700 students) were selected. From each of these four schools, all available first- and second-grade students were invited to participate. Third-grade students were excluded from governmental schools due to unavailability during the data collection period.

The only private secondary school in the city, “School E” was also included. From this school, all available third-grade students were invited to participate, as first- and second-year students were not available.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data was collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire3,6,11–22. The school environment inspection checklist was adapted from the Educational Facilities Vulnerability/Hazard Assessment Checklist23 and aligned with the WHO/UNICEF guidance on safe school operations to ensure comprehensive assessment of COVID-19 preventive measures24. The questionnaire was developed based on an extensive review of relevant literature and comprised four main domains.

The first domain collected demographic and background data, including school name, academic year, age, sex, residence, housing type and ownership, family size, crowding index, transportation mode, healthcare provider, health information source, and family income.

The second domain assessed knowledge through four multiple-choice questions with a total of 20 statements, addressing COVID-19 symptoms, transmission, prevention, and vaccination. Each correct response was scored as one, and incorrect responses as zero, with a total possible score ranging from 0 to 20. Scores of 13 or higher indicated good knowledge.

The third domain measured attitudes through 12 statements concerning perceived importance, feasibility, and beliefs about the pandemic and its control measures. Responses were rated on a two-point Likert scale (agree/disagree), and the total score ranged from 0 to 12. Scores above 4 were considered indicative of a positive attitude.

The fourth domain assessed practices using 13 statements regarding behaviors such as hand hygiene, mask use, and avoidance of risky activities, rated on a three-point scale (always, sometimes, never). Positive responses were scored as one, while partially positive or negative responses were scored as zero. The practice score ranged from 0 to 13, with scores above 4 categorized as good practice.

The cutoffs for positive attitude (> 4/12) and good practice (> 4/13) were based on the observed mean scores, providing a data-driven way to categorize participants relative to the sample. For practice items, only consistent behaviors (“always”) were scored as positive (1), while “sometimes” or “never” were scored as 0, reflecting that partial adherence may not be sufficient for effective COVID-19 prevention. Sensitivity analyses using alternative cutoffs or graded scoring were not performed, though these could be explored in future studies.

The school environmental checklist was designed to assess readiness to prevent respiratory infections, particularly COVID-19. It included items assessing external factors such as location and proximity to highways or industrial areas; internal infrastructure including classroom density, ventilation, hygiene facilities, and health education materials; school facilities such as playgrounds, clinics, and sanitation; and administrative policies related to COVID-19 prevention, including hand hygiene enforcement, mask mandates, daily temperature checks, and communication with parents. Each checklist item was rated according to presence, adequacy, and appropriateness.

Quality control measures

The content and face validity of the questionnaire were established through an extensive literature review3,6,11–22and consultations with public health experts, including specialists in tropical health and infectious diseases.

Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was pilot-tested on a convenient sample of 20 secondary school students to assess clarity, comprehensibility, feasibility, and the time required for completion, as well as to identify any potential difficulties or inappropriate items. The pilot also allowed evaluation of logistical procedures to ensure participants’ privacy and anonymity. Based on the pilot findings, necessary modifications to the questionnaire and study procedures were made. The average time required to complete the questionnaire was approximately 10 min. Data from the pilot participants were excluded from the final analysis.

Data collection was conducted by a trained surveyor (the principal researcher) who met predefined eligibility criteria, including a background in public health or a related health discipline and prior experience in field-based survey administration. The surveyor received standardized training on the study objectives, sampling procedures, ethical considerations, and administration of the study tools to ensure consistency and reliability of data collection across all participating schools. The completed questionnaires were reviewed daily for completeness and internal consistency.

Development of a composite school preparedness score

A composite score was developed to quantify school readiness for COVID-19 prevention. It comprised four domains: physical environment (class density, desk spacing, cleanness, ventilation), hygiene infrastructure (trash bins, handwashing basins, soap, sanitizer), preventive enforcement (temperature screening, mask use, social distancing, COVID-19 education, indoor smoking ban), and administrative preparedness (school clinic, educational material, written plans, parent communication, awareness of local authority plans). Each item was scored 0–2 (0 = poor/absent/never, 1 = moderate/partially/sometimes, 2 = ideal/present/always), with subdomain maxima of 8, 8, 10, and 10 points, respectively, yielding a total maximum score of 36 points. This score facilitated comparison of preparedness across schools and domains.

Data management and analysis

Data was entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS version 22. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize data as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Student’s t-test was employed to compare means between two groups, and the chi-square test was used to examine associations between categorical variables. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Variables with significant associations in bivariate analyses were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model (backward stepwise) models where appropriate.

Results

Sociodemographics of the study participants

All enrolled students completed the questionnaire. Among the five participating secondary schools, the highest proportion of students was from School B, contributing 32.8% of the sample, while the lowest was from School C, with 11.9%. The most frequently reported age was 15 years (44.6%), with a mean age of 15.95 ± 1.11 years. The majority of participants were in Grade 10 (64.9%), reflecting greater willingness to participate compared to higher-grade students who were more engaged with academic commitments. Females represented 54.5% of the study sample, and most students (82.0%) were enrolled in public schools. A significant portion resided within Desouk city (78.2%) and lived in flats (51.6%). Family sizes typically included five or six members (38.6% and 31.8%, respectively), with a mean crowding index of 1.61 ± 0.65 persons per room. Walking was the predominant mode of school transport (55.0%), followed by the use of microbus (25.7%). Regarding family income, the majority of students (54.1%) reported that their income was “enough,” as noted in the final item of the sociodemographic section (Tables 1, S1).

Healthcare providers and sources of health information

Approximately two-thirds of the participants (64.9%) reported relying on the private healthcare sector for their medical needs, while only 6.0% utilized primary healthcare centers. The internet was the most frequently reported source of health information (58.8%), followed by social media platforms (40.4%) (Table 1).

Knowledge about COVID-19

Table 2 displays student responses related to knowledge of COVID-19. A large proportion of the students correctly identified anosmia (69.5%) and dyspnea (59.2%) as key symptoms. Diarrhea was less commonly recognized (20.0%), and the vast majority (94.6%) correctly rejected hemorrhage as a symptom. Regarding transmission, most students acknowledged respiratory droplets as the primary mode (68.4%), and correctly denied misconceptions such as mosquito bites (89.9%) or contaminated food and water (83.1%) as transmission routes. Students widely identified social distancing (65.0%), handwashing (64.5%), and mask-wearing (63.1%) as preventive measures. In contrast, 83.5% excluded blood transfusion as a preventive method. Knowledge about the vaccine was also favorable, with 82.8% aware that two doses are required, while most students correctly rejected false claims regarding the number of doses (77.9% and 93.1% denied the need for four and five doses, respectively) (Table 2).

Table 1 shows the relationship between KAP scores and sociodemographic variables. Students from School E had the highest rate of good knowledge (95.1%) and those from School D had the lowest (44.0%), with statistically significant differences across schools (p < 0.0001). Interestingly, School E students scored the lowest in both attitude and practice, and differences were significant (p < 0.0001). Older students (18 years or older) had better knowledge scores (93.3%), whereas younger students (15 or 16 years) had more favorable attitudes and practices. These differences were also statistically significant. Similar patterns were observed across academic grades, where senior students had better knowledge but poorer attitudes and practices compared to juniors (p < 0.0001).

Female students had higher knowledge scores (68.6%) compared to males, with the difference being significant (p < 0.0001). Although females also scored higher in attitudes (49.3% vs. 44.5%), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.175). For practice scores, males outperformed females significantly (54.1% vs. 35.3%, p < 0.0001). Students attending private schools had the highest knowledge but lowest attitude and practice scores compared to those in public schools, and the differences were statistically significant. Housing type and mode of transportation were not significantly associated with KAP scores, although students using bicycles (85.7%) or public buses (60.3%) had better attitude scores (p = 0.002). Regarding family income, students from families in debt had the lowest knowledge score (23.5%) but the highest good practice score (64.7%), with all differences across income categories being statistically significant (p = 0.000).

When we examined the influence of healthcare provider type on KAP scores, we found that students visiting private clinics had significantly higher knowledge (p < 0.0001) but lower practice scores (59.5% scored poorly; p = 0.008). Those served by pharmacists had higher attitude scores (56.7%, p = 0.002). No significant associations were found with other healthcare providers.

We also explored sources of health-related information. The use of internet and social media correlated with significantly higher knowledge scores (69.8% and 72.4%, respectively; p < 0.0001), but these students tended to have poorer attitudes and practices. School books had no effect on knowledge or practice, but were linked to negative attitudes (p = 0.001). Students who consulted family doctors showed significantly more positive attitudes (p = 0.005) and better practices, though the latter was not statistically significant (p = 0.053). Information from other family members improved attitudes (p = 0.002), while sources such as TV, radio, and neighbors had no significant effect on any of the KAP components (Table 1).

In our multivariate logistic regression model, significant predictors of good knowledge included school type (p = 0.021) and sex (p = 0.036). Students from School E were 1.4 times more likely to have good knowledge (AOR: 1.412, 95% CI 1.053–1.893), and female students were nearly twice as likely as males to have good knowledge (AOR: 1.962, 95% CI 1.044–3.686). For positive attitude, only the school administrative system was a significant predictor (p < 0.0001), with students from public schools more likely to exhibit positive attitudes (AOR: 0.022, 95% CI 0.003–0.172). Academic grade was the only significant predictor for good practice, with younger students outperforming seniors (AOR: 2.279, 95% CI 1.219–4.260, p = 0.010) (Table 6).

School preparedness to implement COVID-19 preventive measures

Most considered their classroom density and desk spacing to be adequate (60.5% and 59.8%, respectively), while nearly half rated classroom cleanliness and ventilation as suitable (49.6% and 47.8%). Fixed hygienic infrastructure such as trash bins and handwashing stations were generally available, while consumables like soap and hand sanitizers were reported as lacking by over 40% of students.

Compliance with preventive measures appeared inconsistent. Daily temperature checks were reportedly never conducted in 37.2% of cases. Mask usage, social distancing, and COVID-19 education were reported as “sometimes” enforced by approximately half of the respondents (48.1%, 50.8%, and 54.4%, respectively). Nonetheless, nearly half (48.6%) stated that smoking was banned indoors, and most schools had on-site clinics (79.4%) and playgrounds (88.6%) (Table S2,3).

Table S2 also summarizes the administrative preparedness of schools. All five schools confirmed awareness of local prevention plans and the existence of formal protocols for managing suspected COVID-19 cases. They also reported having written guidelines for COVID-19 preventive measures. However, variations were observed among schools regarding student capacity, classroom density, and availability of hygiene facilities.

Total composite preparedness score and its association with student practices

The overall composite school preparedness score was approximately 64%, reflecting moderate readiness for COVID-19 preventive measures (Table 7). Discussion

The global outbreak of COVID-19 in late 2019 marked an unprecedented public health emergency. SARS-CoV-2 spread rapidly across the world, overwhelming healthcare systems and resulting in significant morbidity, mortality, and socio-economic disruption. In response, governments and health authorities implemented stringent measures such as nationwide lockdowns, international travel bans, the repurposing of healthcare services, and the accelerated development of vaccines and public health strategies1.

Despite its high population density and limited resources, Egypt reported a relatively lower COVID-19 burden compared to several other countries. While underreporting and limited testing capacity may partially explain this, Egypt’s demographic profile, specifically its predominantly young population may also have played a role3. Given that secondary school students represent a large segment of this demographic, understanding their KAP regarding COVID-19 is essential to developing effective prevention strategies.

The present study surveyed 800 students from both public and private schools in Desouk city and surrounding areas. The mean age of participants was 15.95 ± 1.11 years. Similar age profiles were observed in studies from Iran (16.5 ± 1.7 years)22, while others reported older cohorts, such as in Italy (17.0 ± 3.64 years)20 and Ethiopia (18.0 ± 1.8 years)21. The age variation may reflect greater participation among junior students, likely due to reduced academic obligations compared to senior students.

Females constituted a higher proportion of respondents (54.5%), a trend consistent with numerous similar studies in Egypt9, Ethiopia7, Bahrain8, Saudi Arabia19 and Nigeria25. This may reflect higher voluntary engagement of females in health-related research. Conversely, a study in Bangladesh18 showed male predominance, possibly due to sociocultural or institutional factors influencing participation.

Multiple cross-sectional studies have explored the KAP related to COVID-19 among students in various countries during the early pandemic phase (Table 8). Overall, most students globally had moderate to high knowledge and attitudes about COVID-19, but actual practices varied. Misconceptions, demographic disparities, and psychological factors shaped KAP outcomes, highlighting the ongoing need for targeted, evidence-based public health education.

COVID-19 KAP among students globally.

Regarding sources of COVID-19-related information, students primarily relied on web-based and digital platforms, with 58.8% citing internet websites and 40.4% using social media. This preference aligns with findings from Egypt9, Lebanon17, and Vietnam26, where youth populations increasingly depend on web-based platforms. In contrast, studies in Ethiopia21 and Saudi Arabia19 found traditional media, especially television, to be the dominant source. These cultural preferences highlight the influence of media penetration and digital literacy across regions.

Knowledge of COVID-19 symptoms was generally sound where respondents correctly identified key symptoms such as anosmia/loss of taste, dyspnea, and fever. These findings are consistent with those from other studies, though variations in symptom prioritization exist. In a Saudi study, 98% recognized fever, fatigue, and cough as primary symptoms19, and in Iran, over 90% were aware of major symptoms22. Discrepancies may be due to differences in the timing of the studies, information exposure, or education campaigns targeting students.

Misconceptions regarding transmission routes were evident. While 68.4% of our students correctly mentioned droplet transmission through coughing/sneezing, a notable proportion believed in incorrect routes such as mosquito bites or contaminated food could spread the virus. Similar misconceptions were found in studies from Ethiopia7 and Bangladesh18, where misinformation influenced public perceptions. These findings reflect persistent gaps in basic epidemiological understanding and highlight the urgent need for accurate, targeted education to address persistent myths.

Preventive measures such as social distancing, handwashing, and mask use were widely recognized by participants, which reflect appropriate health behaviors. These rates are comparable to studies from Iran and Italy, where adherence to similar practices exceeded 60%20,22. However, in other settings such as Bangladesh and Indonesia, actual preventive behaviors lagged despite good knowledge and attitudes18,27. This inconsistency between knowledge and practice is a recurrent theme in KAP studies, suggesting that awareness alone does not guarantee behavior change and highlights the need for reinforcement and practice-based education.

Multivariate analysis identified school type and female sex as predictors of good knowledge. School type also played a role. Public school attendance was associated with a more positive attitude, potentially reflecting differences in health messaging or policy enforcement. Younger academic grade predicted better practices, which may indicate stronger adherence to school-imposed rules or greater responsiveness to guidance. The predictor identified in this model underscore the need for tailored health education strategies that consider gender, school environment, and academic level to improve overall KAP among students.

Assessment of school environmental preparedness revealed several strengths and weaknesses. While class density and desk spacing were deemed appropriate by most students, classroom ventilation and cleanliness received mixed ratings. Critical deficiencies were noted in the availability of hygiene supplies, such as hand sanitizers and soap, and in the consistent implementation of preventive measures like daily temperature screening, mask enforcement, and health education.

The present findings align with a growing body of regional and global evidence showing that adequate knowledge about COVID-19 among adolescents does not necessarily translate into positive attitudes or consistent preventive practices. Similar KAP gaps have been reported among secondary school students in other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) in the Middle East, Asia, and Africa, where knowledge levels were moderate to high, yet attitudes toward vaccination and adherence to preventive behaviors remained suboptimal13,14,28. The observed reliance on the internet and social media as primary information sources mirrors global trends among adolescents; however, consistent with studies from Europe and Southeast Asia, these platforms were associated with better knowledge but poorer attitudes and practices, likely reflecting exposure to misinformation and mixed risk perceptions29,30. The inverse relationship between age/academic seniority and preventive behaviors has also been documented internationally, suggesting that younger students may be more compliant with school-based rules, while older adolescents exhibit greater autonomy and risk-taking behaviors31. Importantly, the dominance of private healthcare use and the minimal role of primary healthcare centers echo broader health-system challenges in LMICs, where school–primary care linkages are underutilized despite their potential to reinforce preventive behaviors31. These findings highlight transferable policy lessons beyond Egypt: school health programs should move beyond information dissemination to behavior-focused interventions, integrate media literacy to counter online misinformation, and strengthen collaboration between schools and primary healthcare services. Ensuring consistent availability of hygiene consumables and enforcing school-level preventive measures are also critical, as global evidence indicates that supportive environments are essential for translating knowledge into sustained healthy practices among adolescents.

The data for this study were collected between January and May 2022, a period that coincided with ongoing efforts by Egyptian health authorities to improve COVID-19 vaccination coverage and maintain pandemic response measures. National surveillance around this time indicated suboptimal vaccination uptake in the broader population; a large population-based survey conducted from March to May 2022 found that only 48.3% of adults had received at least one COVID-19 vaccine dose and 44.3% were fully vaccinated, well below the 70% target recommended by the WHO for effective population immunity32. Although specific vaccination coverage figures for adolescents were not reported in national surveys during that period, policy initiatives had begun prior to 2022 to extend vaccine access to younger age groups, including plans to vaccinate teens aged 15–18 years through school-based campaigns. These efforts likely influenced adolescents’ awareness and attitudes toward vaccination during the study window. By early 2022, reported restrictions and COVID-19 mandates in Egypt had generally become less stringent compared to earlier waves, with mask-wearing and basic preventive behaviors encouraged but few compulsory measures widely enforced. Observational reports from this period suggest that community adherence to practices such as mask use was variable, and formal restrictions were relaxed relative to peak pandemic phases. Such shifts in policy and public perception may have contributed to the mixed attitudes and relatively poor adherence to preventive practices observed among students, despite a generally favorable knowledge profile. In particular, lower perceived risk and pandemic fatigue at the time could help explain discordance between knowledge and actual preventive behavior in the surveyed adolescent population.

In conclusion, the study demonstrates satisfactory knowledge and awareness among secondary school students in Egypt regarding COVID-19, though some misconceptions remain. These insights can support more tailored and culturally appropriate health education strategies, especially in school settings, to strengthen pandemic preparedness and response. Improved hygiene infrastructure, and consistent enforcement of preventive measures are essential to promote safer school environments.

Policy interventions that strengthen school-based health programs and support pandemic resilience in educational settings are warranted.
