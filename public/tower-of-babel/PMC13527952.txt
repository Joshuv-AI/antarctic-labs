The marsupial imprinted gene MLH1 has retrocopies in three marsupial families
Authors: Jenna Pride, Geoff Shaw, Marilyn B. Renfree, Trent A. C. Newman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe mut-L homologue 1 gene (MLH1) is a DNA repair gene. Loss of biallelic expression of MLH1 in humans leads to increased predisposition to several cancer types. In the koala (Phascolarctos cinereus) and brushtail possum (Trichosurus vulpecula), MLH1 is imprinted, resulting in monoallelic parent-of-origin specific gene expression.ResultsAllele-specific methylation and expression analysis of MLH1 in tammar wallaby (Notamacropus (Macropus) eugenii) pouch young indicated MLH1 was maternally methylated and paternally expressed. A second site of MLH1 was identified, lacking introns. This site was a retrocopy, formed through the MLH1 mRNA incorporating into the tammar genome. An orthologous MLH1 retrocopy was identified in two macropodid marsupials and an additional two non-orthologous MLH1 retrocopies were identified in the bilby (Macrotis lagotis) and marsupial mole (Notoryctes typhlops). The tammar, bilby and marsupial mole MLH1 retrocopies had intact open reading frames and the tammar and bilby retrocopies were expressed. Protein structure alignment indicated the conformation of the ‘parental’ and retrocopy MLH1 protein may be conserved and therefore may have a comparable function to the parental MLH1 protein.ConclusionsThis second site of MLH1 expression may have introduced redundancy to MLH1 expression and compensate for silencing of the maternal MLH1 allele in marsupials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13072-026-00684-z.

Introduction

The emergence of new genes significantly contributes to species evolution and adaptation. Novel genes may arise through gene duplication, followed by the copy gaining distinct functions. Gene duplication can be RNA-mediated, involving the reverse transcription of mRNA before being incorporated into the genome. Traditionally, these retrogenes (retrocopies) have been thought of as ‘dead on arrival’ as they lack regulatory elements required for transcription [1]. Retrogenes lacking transcriptional activity were previously termed ‘processed pseudogenes’ and considered junk DNA. However, some retrogenes may, by chance, incorporate near regulatory elements, or recruit regulatory machinery following incorporation and become transcriptionally active retrogenes [1]. Functional retrogenes represent a method of forming either a new gene, or a second functional copy of the parental locus [2, 3]. Many functional retrogenes have been identified in mammals, approximately 100 per species [4]. Functional retrogenes are frequently near other genes or within introns [4]. For example, there is a retrocopy of the mut-L homologue 1 gene (MLH1) in the common marmoset (Callithrix jacchus) genome [5].

The MLH1 protein is involved in DNA mismatch repair (MMR) and meiotic recombination. MLH1 forms a heterodimer with postmeiotic segregation increased 2 (PMS2) to form MutLα [6]. When bound by MutSβ and other repair enzymes, PMS2 introduces a single-stranded break near DNA mismatches, allowing for excision of the error (reviewed in [7]). During meiosis I, MLH1 heterodimerises with MLH3, forming MutLγ, which is required for resolution of double Holliday junctions into crossovers [8].

MLH1 is composed of two domains: the N-terminal ATPase domain and the C-terminal endonucleolytic domain. In addition to being the site of ATPase function, the N-terminus contains the binding site of mut-S homologue 2 (MSH2) and recruits MLH1 to double-stranded breaks [9]. The C-terminal domain houses the PMS2 binding site and is required for heterodimerisation [10]. The two domains of MLH1 are connected by a disordered linker sequence with a predicted high tolerance of missense protein variants [11]. The linker sequence is a flexible connector between the two domains and is vital for DNA-binding and MMR [12]. A five amino acid sequence in the linker sequence has been termed the core ‘conserved motif’ due to the high level of conservation across diverse taxa and one identified missense mutation in this sequence abolishes MMR activity [13].

In humans, mutations in MLH1 and other MMR genes can cause Lynch syndrome, characterised by an increased risk of several cancer types, predominantly colorectal cancer and endometrial cancer. Most Lynch syndrome cases are caused by heterozygous germline mutations in the coding sequences of one of the four MMR genes; MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2 [14]. Hypermethylation of the human MLH1 promoter has been identified as a rare cause of Lynch syndrome [15, 16].

In contrast to eutherians which require biallelic expression of MLH1, some marsupials have a silenced copy of the maternal allele of MLH1 resulting from monoallelic methylation of the MLH1 promoter [17]. Thus, in the koala and brushtail possum, MLH1 is an imprinted gene [17, 18]. In brushtail possums, MLH1 imprinting was identified in conjunction with imprinting of the upstream gene epilepsy progressive myoclonus type 2 A interacting protein 1 (EPM2AIP1) [17]. EPM2AIP1 is associated with glycogen synthase activity and promotes growth during early development [19]. As a DNA repair gene, the selective advantage of imprinting of MLH1 is unclear. Bond et al. proposed that MLH1 may be a ‘bystander’ imprinted gene due to the close proximity between MLH1 and EPM2AIP1 [17].

Imprinting has been well-studied in eutherian models, with over 200 imprinted genes identified each in mice and humans and at least 63 common to both species (reviewed in [20]). In comparison, imprinting in marsupials has been less well studied. Most imprinted genes in marsupials have been identified in marsupial orthologues to eutherian imprinted genes [21–24]. Marsupial-specific imprinted genes have been identified in only a few studies [17, 18, 25, 26]. Further understanding of marsupial-specific imprinting may identify unique selection pressures leading to new imprinted genes and inform our understanding of the evolution of imprinting in therian ancestors.

Although many imprinted genes have global monoallelic silencing, some genes have tissue-specific imprinting, which for the purposes of this study is defined as ‘genes with parent-of-origin specific expression in some tissues but in which at least one tissue has biallelic expression’. The brain and placenta are key sites of tissue-specific imprinting [27]. Knowledge of tissue-specificity of imprinted genes is limited by the tissues examined in each study, and some genes may be imprinted in all except one tissue type. Biallelic expression in only one tissue has been thought to result from a reduced need to maintain imprinting. Some tissue-specific imprinted genes appear to have a developmentally-required or controlled loss of imprinting in specific tissues [28].

Marsupial development has marked differences when compared with eutherians. The placenta is much less invasive or non-invasive in marsupials, and the gestational period is very short, resulting in the delivery of a highly altricial young [29]. During an extended lactation period, developing pouch young (PY) are supported by a complex and changing milk supply [30]. Nevertheless, placentation is essential for marsupial embryonic and fetal development. Most marsupials have a chorio-vitelline placenta comprised of a non-vascular bilaminar and vascular trilaminar omphalopleure (BOM and TOM respectively) regions. The avascular BOM is primarily involved in uterine secretion transport to the developing embryo and the vascular TOM functions in respiration [31]. The BOM and the TOM have different transcriptional profiles and different imprinted gene expression [23, 24, 32].

An expressed retrocopy may compensate for the reduced gene expression that results from genomic imprinting. This study confirmed that MLH1 is imprinted in the tammar wallaby and identified expressed MLH1 retrocopies in multiple marsupials. This raises questions about the evolutionary mechanisms facilitating imprinting of MLH1 and whether an MLH1 retrocopy in marsupials influences the functional consequences of imprinting.

Results

The tammar EPM2AIP1/MLH1 DMR was maternally methylated in PY tissue

The methylation of this region was assessed in PY tissue by allele-specific methylation analysis of bisulfite-converted DNA (bsDNA). Genotyping five animals identified four animals with a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) site and an ‘informative’ homozygous mother. In this putative differentially methylated region (DMR), 28 CpG sites were assessed in clones of DNA inserts from PY liver, lung and spleen (Fig. 1A, B; Fig. S1). The parental origin of 67% of clones could not be determined as there was a cytosine/thymine SNP where the bisulfite conversion of unmethylated cytosines to thymines prevented converted cytosines from being distinguished from other thymines. The maternally methylated clones were hypermethylated. In PY liver, 14 maternal clones had high levels of methylation (mean: 95%) [95% confidence interval: 92%, 98%, see Methods] and the four maternal clones from PY spleen were also highly methylated (94%) [88%, 99%]. In the lung, the seven maternal clones were all 96% methylated.

Clones of unknown parental origin in which the maternal allele was a ‘C’ had low levels of methylation in all 12 liver clones (16%) [10%, 22%] and in the 14 clones from the spleen (34%) [17%, 50%]. The clone of unknown parental origin from a single PY lung with a maternal C allele was 11% methylated (Fig. S1). In animals in which the maternal allele was a ‘T’, all except one clone were of unknown parental origin. In the PY liver of one animal (paternal C allele), the paternal clone was highly methylated (96%), while the clones of unknown parental origin had variable methylation levels (Fig. S1). The eight liver clones and ten spleen clones of unknown parental origin from PY with a maternal T allele had a mix of hyper- and hypomethylated signals, whereas the ten lung clones of unknown parental origin were hypermethylated (89%) [85%, 93%].

MLH1 was monoallelically expressed from the paternal allele in PY tissue.

The expression of MLH1 was assessed in PY tissue to determine whether parent-of-origin specific methylation of the EPM2AIP1/MLH1 DMR was associated with monoallelic expression of the adjacent genes. Genotyping 11 PY in exons 11 and 12 of MLH1 (Fig. 1C) identified SNPs in 10 animals. Eight animals had a homozygous mother and therefore were ‘informative’ regarding the origins of parental alleles. The expression of each allele was assessed using cDNA from liver, kidney, spleen and brain. In the PY tissues assessed, complete monoallelic paternal expression of MLH1 was observed (Fig. 1D, E). The expression of MLH1 was also assessed in gravid endometriu

m. Determining the parental origins of the alleles was not possible in these samples due to the absence of a maternal sample so the allelic expression was reported (Fig. 1F). Five out of six animals had monoallelic expression and one animal had biased expression, indicating more variation in the endometrium than PY tissues but a consistent pattern of skewed expression.

To determine whether differential methylation of the EPM2AIP1/MLH1 DMR was associated with monoallelic expression of EPM2AIP1, animals were genotyped in two regions of the 5’ UTR of EPM2AIP1 (Fig. 1G) due to indications of variation in transcriptomes. In region 1, 37 animals were genotyped and an additional four animals were genotyped in region 2. As no SNPs were identified, expression analysis in this locus was not possible.

The EPM2AIP1/MLH1 DMR had a variable methylation pattern in the placenta

The methylation status of the EPM2AIP1/MLH1 DMR was assessed in the tammar placenta across 28 CpG sites (Fig. 2A; Fig. S2). Genotyping 14 animals identified informative SNPs in five animals. In the three animals with a maternal C allele, the 14 maternal clones in the TOM were hypermethylated (92%) [87%, 97%]. Clones with unknown parental origin from the same animals were mostly hypomethylated; 10 clones out of 21 had less than 20% methylation and four clones had more than 80% methylation. The two animals with a maternal T had almost all clones of unknown parental origins due to the T/C SNP. Six clones out of 21 were less than 20% methylated and nine clones were more than 20% methylated. The paternally derived clone was hypomethylated (11% CpG sites methylated).

In clones from BOM bsDNA, methylation appeared to be dependent on the developmental stage of the fetus (Fig. 2A, Fig. S2). Before day 25, all clones were of unknown parental origins and 32 out of 33 were hypomethylated (9%) [6%, 12%]. A day 25 animal with 12 clones of unknown parental origin had two hypermethylated clones (96%) and seven hypomethylated clones (< 20% CpG sites methylated) (6%) [0%, 11%] and three clones with between 20% and 80% methylated CpG sites. A second day 25 animal had five maternal clones with hypermethylated signals (82%) and seven clones of unknown parental origin with moderate methylation signals (30%) [18%, 47%].

MLH1 was differentially expressed in tammar placenta

The expression of MLH1 was assessed in the tammar placenta. Genotyping 28 fetal samples identified SNPs in 21 animals and nine animals with an informative homozygous maternal sample. The parental origins of transcripts expressed from MLH1 were assessed. In the TOM, there was either full or partial repression of transcription from the maternal MLH1 allele (i.e., MLH1 expression was exclusively from or skewed toward the paternal allele). The fraction of the maternal MLH1 allele expressed (see Methods) in the TOM was on average 0.22 [95% confidence interval: 0.03. 0.42] (Fig. 2B, C). In the BOM, allele-specific expression was highly variable. Three animals (#2, #6, #7) showed monoallelic paternal expression, one animal (#1) had monoallelic maternal expression in the BOM and one animal (#4) had maternally skewed expression in the BOM (Fig. 2B, C).

Marsupials had a second MLH1 locus

Amplification of tammar gDNA using primers in exons of MLH1 showed an unexpected band (Fig. S3). The band was approximately the size of the amplicon expected but without introns. Sequencing of the amplicon indicated it was an intronless copy of MLH1; that is, a retrocopy. Alignment of the MLH1 mRNA sequence against the tammar genome indicated the retrocopy was on chromosome 6 in the first intron of the metabolism of cobalamin associated A gene (MMAA) (Fig. 3A). The retrocopy had 100% coverage of the ‘parental’ MLH1 locus and 96% sequence similarity.

Evidence of at least three retropositioning events of MLH1 in the marsupial lineage

Four marsupial genomes aligned with the tammar MLH1 retrocopy with 96–100% query cover; two macropodids, the rufous-hare wallaby (Lagorchestes hirsutus) and Eastern grey kangaroo (Macropus giganteus), and two other marsupials (bilby and marsupial mole). The locations of the retrocopies were identified, noting the closest annotated gene, indicating that the macropodid and bilby retrocopies were in different regions of the genome (Fig. 3B).

Sequence similarity between the retrocopy and parental MLH1 sequences were assessed using a Maximum-Likelihood tree (Fig. 3C). The sequence similarity between the retrocopy and parental sequence from each species indicated that the macropodid, bilby, marsupial mole and New World monkey retrocopies all shared more sequence homology to the respective parental MLH1 sequence than to the other retrocopies (Fig. 3C).

The MLH1 retrocopy in the common marmoset was identified from the retrocopy database RCPedia [5] assessing retrocopy loci in 44 animal species. No other MLH1 retrocopies were found on RCPedia or RetrogeneDB [33] (which assesses retrocopies in 62 animal species). The marmoset MLH1 retrocopy sequence was used in a BLAST and identified retrocopies in the tufted capuchin (Sapajus apella) and the Panamanian white-faced capuchin (Cebus imitator). Neither capuchin species are included in the two retrocopy databases. Assessment of the New World monkey MLH1 sequence similarity indicated that the three retrocopies likely originated from one retropositioning event (Fig. 3C).

Partial MLH1 retrocopies

In addition to the three full-length marsupial MLH1 retrocopies, an additional four partial retrocopies were identified (Fig. 3D). To identify additional partial retrocopies in other marsupials, tammar MLH1 intron-spanning primers were used to amplify DNA from seven other marsupials (Fig. S3). Gel electrophoresis indicated bands of the expected size in all samples, and sequences from gel extracts from ringtail possum (Pseudocheirus peregrinus), koala and grey short-tailed opossum (Monodelphis domestica) indicated the band was from the MLH1 region amplified. Using these sequences in a BLAST against the reference genomes returned alignments only in the parental MLH1 sequence.

The tammar and bilby retrocopies were expressed

The sequences of the three marsupial retrocopies were assessed to determine if there were intact open reading frames (ORFs). The NCBI ORF Finder identified continuous ORFs in the retrocopies (Fig. 4A). The retrocopy loci in tammar and bilby were assessed for evidence of transcript expression using cDNA from several tissues and primers designed to specifically amplify regions of the retrocopy (Fig. 4A, B). In the bilby, the transcript was present in liver cDNA from both a male and female sample. In the tammar, gel bands indicated retrocopy expression in cDNA from placenta, gonads, spleen and liver. Sequencing indicated expression of the tammar retrocopy was biallelic in the liver, which was expected as the retrocopy lacks the CpG island of the parental MLH1.

The relative abundance of the tammar and bilby retrocopies was assessed in publicly available transcriptomes to determine if retrocopy transcripts were expressed at similar levels to the parental MLH1 and adjacent genes (Fig. 4C, D). In the set of tammar transcriptomes, the number of relative reads from the MLH1 retrocopy were dependent on the library selection method (Fig. S4A, B). As the tammar and bilby retrocopies may lack polyA tails, transcriptomes generated with ‘random library selection’ were used. RNA-seq reads mapped correctly to the retrocopy and parental MLH1 in tammar and bilby. Base mismatches relative to the reference sequence for reads aligned to the retrocopy did not reflect parental MLH1 sequences (Fig. S5).

When examining only transcriptomes generated from a randomly-selected library, there was no correlation between expression from the parental and retrocopy MLH1 in nine tissues (p = 0.27, Fig. 4D). In general, retrocopy expression was elevated compared to the parental MLH1 and retrocopy expression was particularly high in the endometrium (TPM > 10) but moderate in the spleen and mammary gland (TPM > 5). In these transcriptomes, tammar MLH1 retrocopy expression was significantly correlated with expression of the adjacent MMAA gene (p = 0.0015, Fig. 4D).

Examining the parental MLH1 expression in the bilby identified abnormally elevated expression in the testis (Fig. S4C). After excluding the testis as an outlier there was no significant correlation between retrocopy and parental MLH1 expression in twelve remaining bilby transcriptomes (p = 0.36, Fig. 4D). In the bilby spleen, mammary gland and pouch skin, the retrocopy was highly expressed (TPM > 10) and in the spleen and mammary gland, retrocopy expression was elevated compared to parental MLH1 expression (Fig. 4D). In the testis, expression of EPM2AIP1 was highly upregulated (Fig. S4D). After excluding the testis, there was no significant correlation between of bilby MLH1 retrocopy expression with EPM2AIP1 (p = 0.20, Fig. S4E). Retrocopy expression was also not correlated with the adjacent genes, C1H21orf91 (p = 0.64, Fig. S4F) or CHODL (p = 0.068, Fig. S4G).

Predicted MLH1 retrocopy protein had comparable structure to parental MLH1

To predict whether the three marsupial MLH1 retrocopies could be functionally equivalent to the parental MLH1, the structure of the MLH1 proteins were analysed. Protein structures were visualised and the ‘fit’ of the retrocopy proteins against parental proteins was calculated (Fig. 4E; Fig. S6). Predicted retrocopy proteins were superimposed over the respective parental MLH1 (with the marsupial mole retrocopy superimposed over the bilby parental MLH1 as no protein sequences are available for the marsupial mole). The ‘fit’ of the N-terminal and C-terminal domains of the retrocopy and parental proteins were analysed separately as superpositioning of the proteins only aligned one domain due to differences in linker sequences. Root Median Square Deviation (RMSD) values (Fig. 4E) indicated that for all domains excluding the tammar C-terminal domain, the retrocopy proteins had nearly identical structure to the parental proteins (RMSD < 2 Å).

The linker sequence between the N-terminal domain and the C-terminal domain was also assessed for the conserved motif required for MLH1 function. Alignment of the protein sequences indicated that in all proteins analysed excluding the bilby retrocopy, the conserved motif was maintained (Fig. 4F). The bilby retrocopy had a p.Val349Ile substitution.

Discussion

There were distinct retrocopies of the marsupial imprinted gene MLH1 in at least three marsupial lineages. A transcriptionally active retrocopy has functional redundancy with the parental locus in MLH1 expression. This second MLH1 locus may increase overall transcript abundance and permit monoallelic expression of the parental MLH1 locus.

Close proximity to another gene can allow retrocopies to co-opt transcriptional machinery and become expressed [4]. Retrocopies which are incorporated into gene-rich regions are more likely to be located in open chromatin and can subsequently recruit transcriptional regulators. The close proximity of the tammar and bilby retrocopies to adjacent genes likely influences their expression levels. Expression levels of the tammar MLH1 retrocopy were related to expression levels of the adjacent gene MMAA (p < 0.005), therefore it is possible that the tammar retrocopy uses the same transcriptional machinery as MMAA. In the bilby, expression of the retrocopy was not correlated with expression of either neighbouring gene, CHODL or C1H21orf91. It is therefore unlikely the bilby retrocopy uses transcriptional machinery from either adjacent gene but instead has independent gene regulation.

Retrocopy expression was observed in several tissues in both the tammar and bilby. Elevated expression was noted in the tammar and bilby spleen and mammary gland, the bilby testis and the tammar endometrium. Mammalian retrocopies tend to have high expression in the testis in particular and the spleen, a pattern that may be due to transcriptional promiscuity in the testis [34, 35]. In both tammar and bilby transcriptomes, MLH1 retrocopy expression was high in the endometrium and the parental MLH1 had leaky allele-specific expression in the tammar.

Retrocopies can fulfill the same function as the parental gene [3]. Examining the structural and sequence homology of the parental and retrocopy MLH1 proteins indicated a high degree of conservation. The two domains of the retrocopy proteins showed nearly identical structure excluding the tammar C-terminal domain which had moderate conservation. The linker sequence between the two domains of MLH1 is disordered and unstructured. Deletion of a key sequence motif conserved in the linker sequence results in loss of MMR activity of human MLH1 in HEK293 cell lines [13]. In the tammar and marsupial mole, the five amino acids of the conserved motif were maintained within the retrocopies but in the bilby, there was a single amino acid variant, p.Val349Ile. The conserved motif is tolerant of some variants so the function of the parental protein may be maintained in the bilby retrocopy despite the mutation [13]. Valine and isoleucine are both non-polar and share a similar structure, supporting the possibility that this missense mutation does not disrupt function of the bilby MLH1 retrocopy.

Although infrequent, other genes have been identified with an imprinted parental locus and expressed retrocopy. The imprinted long non-coding RNA Gtl2 within the mouse Dlk1 imprinting cluster is a maternally-expressed RNA with a biallelically expressed retrocopy on the mouse X chromosome [36, 37]. The Gtl2 retrocopy Gm2700 is expressed in the frontal cortex which may alter the overall transcript abundance of Gtl2 [37, 38].

In addition to the three marsupial lineages with a retrocopy and a previously identified retrocopy in the New World monkey lineage, partial MLH1 retrocopies were found in four other marsupial lineages. These partial retrocopies may represent ancestral regions where the MLH1 transcript is incorporated into the genome in the germline with most of the sequence lost over time due to a lack of function or expression. The partial retrocopies may also indicate loci where only part of a transcript incorporated into the genome.

Although DNA amplification indicated at least partial MLH1 retrocopies were present in the koala, ringtail possum and grey short-tailed opossum, these sequences were unable to be identified in the genome by BLAST. Since the possibility of other full-length marsupial MLH1 retrocopies cannot be excluded, it is possible an MLH1 retrocopy is frequently located in the genomes of species with parental MLH1 imprinting. Identification of potential retrocopies in the brushtail possum and koala could clarify the frequency of MLH1 imprinting in conjunction with a retrocopy. As monoallelic MLH1 silencing is deleterious in eutherians, it is possible that marsupials with an imprinted parental MLH1 rely on a retrocopy to increase transcript abundance and compensate for reduced MLH1 expression.

The tammar, bilby and marsupial mole retrocopy sequences were each more similar to their respective parental MLH1 sequences and are therefore more closely related. Thus, these MLH1 retrocopies represent three distinct retropositioning events and likely evolved independently. Taken together with the evidence of at least four partial retrocopies in other marsupials, there was a high frequency of MLH1 retropositioning. This may have resulted from the timing of MLH1 expression - MLH1 expression is high in the germline where it has a key role in recombination and the resolution of crossovers in meiosis [39, 40]. This would provide an increased opportunity for the MLH1 transcript to incorporate back into the genome in a heritable manner, a characteristic common to genes with many retrocopies [2, 41].

The combined occurrence of an expressed retrocopy and monoallelic silencing of the parental MLH1 locus raises questions regarding the underlying selection pressure and succession by which these mediators of gene abundance were acquired. There may have first been imprinting of the EPM2AIP1/MLH1 locus with the selection pressure acting on EPM2AIP1, MLH1, or both, followed by the acquisition of the MLH1 retrocopy (Fig. 5A). Given imprinting of the EPM2AIP1/MLH1 locus appears to have evolved in an ancestral Australian marsupial and MLH1 retrocopies are likely lineage-specific, this hypothesised evolutionary order is likely. Alternatively, an MLH1 retrocopy may have been acquired by chance, followed by the establishment of the EPM2AIP1/MLH1 imprint (Fig. 5B). Overexpression of MLH1 increases the mutation rate in yeast [42], so an MLH1 retrocopy may not be tolerated in the genome in addition to biallelic parental MLH1 expression. If the MLH1 retrocopy was acquired first, this may have introduced a selection pressure for reduced MLH1 abundance that was achieved through monoallelic gene silencing of the EPM2AIP1/MLH1 locus. Characterising the imprinting status of EPM2AIP1/MLH1 in additional lineages of Australian marsupials or American marsupials could clarify the order in which the imprint and the retrocopy were established.

MLH1 may be an innocent bystander to imprinting of a shared CpG island with EPM2AIP1, as proposed by Bond et al. [17]. Constitutional epimutation (monoallelic methylation) of the MLH1 promoter has adverse effects in humans. EPM2AIP1 functions in glycogen synthesis and promoting fetal growth, which provides a basis for imprinting of this locus under the current hypotheses for the evolution of imprinting [19]. Although assessment of EPM2AIP1 expression in the tammar was not possible due to a lack of heterozygosity, it is likely that EPM2AIP1 is similarly imprinted in the tammar and MLH1 may have become imprinted by association. It is also possible that MLH1 imprinting has been actively selected, since imprinting has been identified in genes with functions aside from growth regulation. Several tumour-suppressor genes have been identified with monoallelic expression in normal tissue and genes imprinted in the brain typically have a role in neural development and modulating behaviour (reviewed in [43]).

There is a CpG island between the tammar EPM2AIP1 and the parental MLH1 which is conserved with brushtail possums [17]. In several postnatal tissues (liver, lung and spleen), the EPM2AIP1/MLH1 CpG island was maternally methylated in the tammar, as in the brushtail possum. Specific sequencing of the opposite strand could have overcome the loss of information at the C/T allele after bisulfite conversion, however no additional tissue was available.

EPM2AIP1/MLH1 imprinting had not previously been assessed in the tammar placenta, but there was maternal methylation at this locus in the TOM similar to that of postnatal tissues. In contrast, the BOM had stage-specific methylation patterns. BOM from animals before late pregnancy at day 25 of gestation was mostly unmethylated, but animals at day 25 had a monoallelic methylation pattern. In the tammar placenta, day 23 to 25 of gestation is a key period of growth and development accompanied by changes in gene expression [32]. The stage-specific methylation pattern observed in the BOM at MLH1 may reflect broader gene regulatory changes occurring in this tissue during the later stages of tammar pregnancy.

There was monoallelic paternal expression of MLH1 in the postnatal tissues in the tammar. Liver, kidney, spleen and brain all had a completely silenced maternal allele. There was paternal expression in the endometrium of most animals but one animal had leaky expression from the maternal allele. There was reduced maintenance of monoallelic expression of MLH1 in the placenta. In the BOM and TOM, there was monoallelic paternal expression in some animals but several animals had biallelic expression in one or both tissues. In two animals, there was maternally skewed expression of the MLH1 transcript. Taken together with previous findings of MLH1 imprinting in the koala and brushtail possum [17, 18], it is likely that imprinting of MLH1 in postnatal tissues is conserved in Diprotodontia and potentially in all Australian marsupials and arose at least 42 million years ago [44]. Examining the methylation and expression of MLH1 in other lineages such as North and South American marsupials could provide an indication of the evolutionary origins of the MLH1 imprint.

Some animals had monoallelic methylation of MLH1 in the placenta and yet had biallelic expression (Fig. 2A, C). Other animals had a hypomethylated CpG island and monoallelic expression (Fig. 2C; Fig. S2). Tissue specific biallelic expression of bovine SLC22A2 can be achieved despite an imprinted methylation pattern at the IGF2R/AIR locus [45]. Conversely, monoallelic expression of Cdkn1c in mice can be maintained despite loss of local differential methylation, possibly as a result of the KvDMR1 locus or other epigenetic regulators [46]. Paternal MLH1 expression may be regulated by additional factors, such as chromatin modification in addition to promoter methylation. Polymorphic expression of MLH1 in the placenta suggests that DNA methylation may not be required for silencing of one allele and may be insufficient to independently maintain monoallelic silencing. The role of the marsupial BOM in nutrient transfer between the mother and fetus make it a key site of imprinting in marsupials. Differences between imprinted gene expression in the tammar BOM compared to the TOM have been previously identified [23]. It is possible either EPM2AIP1 or MLH1 have a specific role in the BOM that requires biallelic or increased expression. In the tammar, bilby and marsupial mole, MLH1 is an imprinted gene with an expressed retrocopy which could encode a functional protein. This second site of MLH1 expression may have introduced redundancy to MLH1 expression and compensate for the effects of monoallelic expression of the parental gene. This is an infrequent occurrence among imprinted genes and influences our understanding of how imprinted loci can arise.

Materials and methods

Animals

Tammar wallabies of Kangaroo Island origin were held in open grassy yards in the University of Melbourne colony under permits from the Victorian Department of Energy, Environment and Climate Action. Adults and PY were killed humanely as previously described [31, 47, 48]. Placentas were collected postmortem from adults between 19 and 25 days of the 26.5 day pregnancy (see Fig. 2C) and tissues from PY and immediately snap frozen (Tables S1 and S2). Animal experiments were conducted with approval from the University of Melbourne Animal Experimentation Ethics Committees and followed the National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia 2013 guidelines [49]. Liver tissue from a male and female bilby were provided by Professor Carolyn Hogg (University of Sydney, Sydney, Australia) and were collected under appropriate permits and approvals as previously described [50].

Genomic DNA preparation

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from snap frozen tissue with the Wizard genomic DNA purification kit (cat. no. A1120, Promega, Wisconsin, USA) after homogenisation using either a T10 Handheld Homogeniser (IKA, Staufen, Germany) or Precellys 24 Homogeniser (Bertin, Lund, Sweden) with 1.4 mm ceramic beads (cat. no. 19–645, Omni, Georgia, USA) and 10 µL Antifoam C (cat. no. A8011, Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA). Genotyping was performed with GoTaq Green Master Mix (cat. no. M5123, Promega, Wisconsin, USA) and the primers designed using Primer3 (v 4.1.0) [51] (Table S3). PCR products were visualised by gel electrophoresis and bands were extracted using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (cat. no. 28706, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), omitting isopropanol. Purified gel extracts were sequenced by the Australian Genome Research Facility (AGRF), Melbourne, Victoria.

Primer specificity

To differentiate between retrocopy and parental MLH1 in spite of high sequence similarity, primers were designed at regions of highly dense sequence differences visualised on Benchling [52]. Sequence differences were prioritised at the 3’ end of primers. Genotyping primers were specific to parental MLH1 due to positioning at least one primer in an intron (which were absent from the retrocopy). Expression primers were confirmed to be targeted to the parental or retrocopy MLH1 by amplifying gDNA and visualising PCR products by gel electrophoresis, and confirmation of amplicon sequence by Sanger sequencing.

Clonal amplification of bisulfite-converted sequences

Genomic DNA from tammar PY tissue was bisulfite-converted using the EpiMark Bisulfite Conversion kit (cat. no. E3318S, New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA). Placenta gDNA was bisulfite-converted using the EpiTect Bisulfite kit (cat. no. 59104, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

Bisulfite-converted DNA was amplified with primers designed using MethPrimer [53] (Table S3). Amplified DNA was separated using gel electrophoresis and extracted using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit, omitting isopropanol. Extracted bsDNA was ligated into the pGEM-T Easy Vector (cat. no. A1360, Promega, Wisconsin, USA) in a 7:1 ratio of DNA to insert. JM109 E. coli competent cells (cat. no. L2005, Promega, Wisconsin, USA) were transfected with the insert and incubated using SOC outgrowth media (cat. no. ST0215, TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). Transformed competent cells were plated onto LB/Ampicillin/IPTG/X-Gal plates. Colonies with inserts were identified by blue-white screening, then cultured in LB media. Plasmids were prepared using the Wizard Plus SV Miniprep DNA Purification System (cat. no. A1330, Promega, Wisconsin, USA) then sequenced by AGRF using the M13 reverse primer (Table S3). The methylation of CpG sites was quantified using QUMA [54].

Total RNA extraction

RNA was prepared from 50 to 100 mg of snap frozen tissue after homogenisation with the T10 Handheld Homogeniser (IKA, Staufen, Germany) or Precellys 24 Homogeniser (Bertin, Lund, Sweden) with 1.4 mm ceramic beads (cat. no. 19–645, Omni, Georgia, USA). The TRIzol reagent was used to extract total RNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (cat. no. 15596018, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). RNA was DNase-treated using the DNA-free kit (cat. no. AM1906, Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA) and cDNA was synthesised from 100 to 200 ng of RNA using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (cat. no. 18091050, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) and oligo(dT)20.

Statistical analysis

All methylation statistics are reported as (mean) [95% confidence interval]. Confidence intervals were calculated using a Student’s t-distribution. Parent of origin specific expression analysis is reported as ‘maternal expression’ (maternal allele signal intensity/combined maternal and paternal signal intensity).

Retrocopy expression analysis

Publicly available RNA sequencing data (Table S4) were downloaded from NCBI SRA and trimmed using TrimGalore! (v0.6.10). Trimmed reads were aligned using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) [55] and mapped reads were separated by strand using SAMtools (v1.16.1) [56] and filtered for high mapping quality scores (MAPQ) of 60. The MAPQ = 60 filter discards multimappers: reads that map ambiguously to both the parental copy and the retrocopy. Raw read counts were extracted using featureCounts [57] with default settings. Graphs were assembled using R statistical software (v4.5.1).

BLAST

The tammar MLH1 retrocopy sequence was extracted from the genome and used as the query sequence to align to all other marsupial species with a reference genome listed on NCBI (Table S5). For each species, a BLAST search was conducted using megablast, blastn and discontiguous megablast search parameters. All alignments were reported regardless of the search parameter used. Continuous alignments (unbroken in the subject sequence) that included an exon-exon junction were noted. The positions of the retrocopies were identified by locating the nearest gene (for annotated genomes), or by aligning an approximately 10 kb region surrounding the retrocopy against the tammar genome.

Phylogenetic analysis

MLH1 sequences were retrieved from NCBI. Sequences were aligned using MUSCLE and a Maximum Likelihood phylogenetic tree was generated using MEGA12 [58] and the Tamura-Nei model [59]. The tammar MLH1 sequences are from MeugV7.0. The bilby MLH1 sequences were from NCBI; XM_074199101.1 (parental) and LOC141506845 (retrocopy). The marsupial mole parental MLH1 transcript sequence was obtained using BLAST alignment of the bilby transcript sequence to identify exon loci. The marsupial mole retrocopy sequence was obtained using BLAST alignment of the tammar retrocopy sequence. The marsupial mole was not included in the analysis of relative position of the retrocopy compared to other genes of interest due to a lack of genome annotation in mNotTyp1.pri and inconclusive BLAST alignments to determine positions of genes of interest. The marmoset MLH1 sequences were from NCBI; XM_054247245.1 (parental) and RCPedia [5] (retrocopy). The Panamanian white-faced capuchin MLH1 sequences were from NCBI; XM_017508452.2 (parental) and XR_001830725.2 (retrocopy). The tufted capuchin MLH1 sequences were from NCBI; XM_032269801.1 (parental) and XR_004275458.1 (retrocopy). The chicken (Gallus gallus) MLH1 sequence was from NCBI; XM_015281693.3 (parental).

Protein conformational change analysis

The predicted protein sequences of the retrocopies were identified using the ExPASy bioinformatics portal [60] and visualised using the NCBI ORF Finder with default parameters. AlphaFold3 was used to generate protein structure predictions [61]. The model with the highest confidence value was used and PDB files were generated from the AlphaFold3 CIF files using Gemmi [62].

The N-terminal domain, linker sequence and C-terminal domain of the marsupial MLH1 parental and retrocopy proteins were identified by aligning to the Homo sapiens MLH1 sequence using MEGA12. The location of the conserved motif was identified and the conservation was assessed. Using UCSF ChimeraX 1.10 [63], the N-terminal domain of the parental and retrocopy of each species were aligned separately to the C-terminal domain. The sequence alignment scores and Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) values were noted.
