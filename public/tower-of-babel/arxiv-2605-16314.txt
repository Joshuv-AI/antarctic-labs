Special Relativistic Kinematics from Wave Phase Coherence
Emiliano Puddu

Abstract
We present a phase-based formulation of special relativity in which the kinematical structure of the theory is reconstructed from the requirement of phase coherence of localized wave states. Starting from the assumption that physical propagation is associated with surfaces of constant phase and that matter admits an intrinsic rest-frame oscillation, we show that the relativistic relations for time dilation, energy, and momentum follow from the invariant accumulation of phase along particle trajectories. In this framework, proper time is identified operationally as the phase count of an internal clock, and the Minkowski interval arises as the quadratic form consistent with phase invariance across inertial observers. The relation between mass and rest-frame frequency emerges naturally, providing a unified interpretation of relativistic kinematics in wave-mechanical terms. This formulation does not introduce new dynamics but offers a coherent structural perspective that bridges relativistic kinematics and wave propagation.



I
Introduction

Special relativity is traditionally formulated as a geometric theory in which the structure of spacetime is encoded in the invariant Minkowski interval

;
6
. In this standard perspective, physical processes are described in terms of events and their coordinates, and Lorentz transformations arise as the symmetry transformations preserving the spacetime metric. While this formulation has proven extraordinarily successful, it is not the only possible viewpoint. In several areas of physics—most notably wave mechanics and quantum theory—phase plays a central role, as physical observables are determined through interference and coherence

;
2
;
4
.

This observation suggests that relativistic kinematics may be reformulated by taking phase as the primary organizing quantity. For a relativistic wave characterized by frequency
\omega
and wavevector
\mathbf{k}
, the phase

\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega t

(1)

can be written covariantly as
\Phi=p_{\mu}x^{\mu}/\hbar
, where
p_{\mu}
is the energy-momentum four-vector. In this form, the phase is a Lorentz scalar, and its invariance expresses the consistency of wave propagation across inertial frames. Although this property is well known, its role as a structural principle for relativistic kinematics is often not made explicit.

In this work, we explore the consequences of adopting phase coherence as the organizing principle of special relativity. We assume that physical propagation is associated with surfaces of constant phase and that localized states of matter admit an intrinsic oscillation characterized by a rest-frame frequency
\omega_{0}

1
;
2
. Within this framework, the accumulation of phase along a particle trajectory provides a natural definition of proper time. In particular, the invariant phase increment can be written as

d\Phi=-\omega_{0}\,d\tau,

(2)

which identifies proper time
\tau
as the parameter counting the cycles of an internal phase clock.

From this identification, the standard relativistic relations follow in a unified manner. Time dilation emerges from the reduced rate of phase accumulation for moving states, while energy and momentum arise from the relations
E=\hbar\omega
and
\mathbf{p}=\hbar\mathbf{k}
. The invariant Minkowski interval,

ds^{2}=c^{2}dt^{2}-d\mathbf{x}^{2},

(3)

is then recognized as the quadratic form compatible with phase invariance across inertial observers, rather than being introduced as an independent postulate. In this sense, the geometric structure of spacetime can be viewed as encoding the conditions required for relativistic phase coherence.

Within the same framework, the relation between mass and rest-frame frequency,

\omega_{0}=\frac{mc^{2}}{\hbar},

(4)

acquires a direct physical interpretation: mass is associated with the intrinsic oscillation of a localized state, while proper time measures the accumulation of its phase. Relativistic kinematics can thus be interpreted in unified wave-mechanical terms.

We emphasize that the present formulation does not modify the predictions of special relativity and does not introduce new dynamical equations. Rather, it provides a structural reinterpretation in which phase invariance plays a central role. This perspective clarifies the connection between relativistic kinematics and wave propagation and may serve as a useful bridge toward more fundamental formulations in which spacetime structure emerges from underlying degrees of freedom.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II we analyze the relativistic properties of phase and its invariance. In Sec. III we derive proper time as the invariant accumulation of phase. In Sec. IV we show how the Minkowski interval arises as the quadratic structure compatible with phase coherence. In Sec. V we discuss the relation between energy, momentum, and mass in this framework. In Sec. VI we discuss the scope and limitations of the present formulation, and in Sec. VII we summarize the main conclusions.

II
Phase invariance and relativistic coherence

We consider a localized wave state described, in a given inertial frame, by the phase

\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega t.

(5)

Surfaces of constant phase,
\Phi=\mathrm{const}
, represent wavefronts associated with physical propagation. We take these wavefronts to provide an operational identification of propagation events.

A basic consistency requirement is that different inertial observers agree on the identification of the same wavefronts. If
(\mathbf{x},t)
and
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
denote the coordinates of the same physical event in two inertial frames, then the phase labeling that event must satisfy

\Phi(\mathbf{x},t)=\Phi^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

(6)

This expresses the condition of relativistic phase coherence.

For inertial frames related by linear coordinate transformations, the phase must remain linear in the spacetime coordinates in order to preserve the identification of wavefronts. The invariance of the phase therefore requires its coefficients to transform contragrediently to the coordinates, so that the phase is naturally written as the scalar contraction of a four-vector with the spacetime position,

\Phi=k_{\mu}x^{\mu},

(7)

or equivalently as

\Phi=\frac{1}{\hbar}p_{\mu}x^{\mu},

(8)

where
k_{\mu}=p_{\mu}/\hbar
is the wave four-vector and
p_{\mu}=(E/c,-\mathbf{p})
is the energy-momentum four-vector.

In this form, the phase is a Lorentz scalar: its value is the same for all inertial observers describing the same physical event. The physically relevant content is not the absolute phase itself, but phase differences, which govern interference and coherence phenomena. In this sense, phase invariance provides the basic structural condition for a consistent wave description across inertial frames.

III
Proper time as phase accumulation

We now consider the phase evolution along the trajectory of a localized wave packet. The differential of the phase is

d\Phi=\mathbf{k}\cdot d\mathbf{x}-\omega dt.

(9)

This structure is consistent with the Hamilton–Jacobi formulation of relativistic dynamics, in which the phase plays the role of the action function
5
. In this correspondence, the phase can be identified with the classical action through
\Phi=S/\hbar
, so that
d\Phi=dS/\hbar=p_{\mu}dx^{\mu}/\hbar
.
Restricting attention to the packet trajectory, we write

d\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{u}\,dt,

(10)

where
\mathbf{u}
is the group velocity. The phase increment along the trajectory is therefore

d\Phi=(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{u}-\omega)\,dt.

(11)

We now introduce the central physical assumption of the present construction: a localized material state admits an intrinsic oscillation characterized, in its rest frame, by a frequency
\omega_{0}
. This rest-frame oscillation defines an internal clock. We then define a scalar parameter
\tau
by requiring that the phase accumulated along the worldline be measured in units of this intrinsic oscillation,

d\Phi=-\omega_{0}\,d\tau.

(12)

In this way,
\tau
represents the phase count of the internal clock.

Equating the two expressions for
d\Phi
, we obtain

(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{u}-\omega)\,dt=-\omega_{0}\,d\tau.

(13)

To evaluate the factor
(\omega-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{u})
, consider the state in its rest frame, where
\mathbf{k}_{0}=0
and the phase evolves as

\Phi_{0}(t_{0})=-\omega_{0}t_{0}.

(14)

In a frame in which the packet moves with velocity
\mathbf{u}
, phase invariance implies the Lorentz transformation of the wave four-vector, yielding

\omega=\gamma\omega_{0},\qquad\mathbf{k}=\gamma\frac{\omega_{0}}{c^{2}}\mathbf{u},

(15)

with

\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}/c^{2}}}.

(16)

Hence

\displaystyle\omega-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\gamma\omega_{0}-\gamma\frac{\omega_{0}}{c^{2}}\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\gamma\omega_{0}\left(1-\frac{u^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{0}}{\gamma}.

(17)

Substituting this result into the phase increment equation yields

d\tau=\frac{dt}{\gamma}=dt\sqrt{1-\frac{u^{2}}{c^{2}}}.

(18)

This identifies
\tau
with the relativistic proper time. In the present framework, proper time is not introduced as a geometric postulate, but as the invariant parameter governing the accumulation of phase of the internal oscillation. Time dilation then acquires a direct physical meaning: for a moving state, the internal phase accumulates more slowly than in the rest frame, and the corresponding clock runs more slowly.

IV
Invariant interval from phase coherence

We now examine the implications of phase invariance for the structure of spacetime. From the previous section, the phase increment along the trajectory of a localized state is given by

d\Phi=-\omega_{0}\,d\tau,

(19)

where
\tau
has been identified as the parameter governing the invariant accumulation of phase.

Using the relation obtained for
d\tau
, we write

d\tau=dt\sqrt{1-\frac{u^{2}}{c^{2}}},

(20)

where
u=|\mathbf{u}|
is the velocity of the state. Squaring both sides, we obtain

d\tau^{2}=dt^{2}\left(1-\frac{u^{2}}{c^{2}}\right).

(21)

Since
u^{2}=d\mathbf{x}^{2}/dt^{2}
, this expression can be rewritten as

d\tau^{2}=dt^{2}-\frac{d\mathbf{x}^{2}}{c^{2}}.

(22)

Multiplying by
c^{2}
, we obtain

c^{2}d\tau^{2}=c^{2}dt^{2}-d\mathbf{x}^{2}.

(23)

This is the standard Minkowski interval. In the present framework, it is not introduced as an independent geometric postulate, but arises as the quadratic structure compatible with the invariant accumulation of phase.

The spacetime interval can therefore be interpreted as encoding the conditions required for relativistic phase coherence across inertial observers. In this sense, the Minkowski structure provides the natural geometric representation of the invariance properties of phase, rather than serving as the primary starting point of the theory.

V
Energy, momentum, and mass from phase structure

We now examine how energy, momentum, and mass are represented within the phase-based framework. From Sec. II, the phase can be written in covariant form as

\Phi=\frac{1}{\hbar}p_{\mu}x^{\mu},

(24)

where
p_{\mu}
denotes the energy-momentum four-vector.

Comparing this expression with

\Phi=\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega t,

(25)

one recovers the standard de Broglie–Planck relations

E=\hbar\omega,\qquad\mathbf{p}=\hbar\mathbf{k}.

(26)

These relations connect the frequency and wavevector of the state to its dynamical quantities.

From Sec. III, the phase increment along the trajectory of a localized state is

d\Phi=-\omega_{0}\,d\tau,

(27)

where
\omega_{0}
is the intrinsic rest-frame frequency. This identifies an invariant frequency scale associated with the state. Defining the corresponding rest energy by

E_{0}=\hbar\omega_{0},

(28)

one obtains the usual mass-frequency relation

m=\frac{E_{0}}{c^{2}}=\frac{\hbar\omega_{0}}{c^{2}}.

(29)

Using the transformation properties of the wave four-vector, the energy and momentum of a state moving with velocity
\mathbf{u}
take the standard relativistic form

E=\gamma mc^{2},\qquad\mathbf{p}=\gamma m\mathbf{u},

(30)

with

\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^{2}/c^{2}}}.

(31)

These quantities satisfy the invariant relation

E^{2}=p^{2}c^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}.

(32)

In this framework, mass acquires a direct physical interpretation as the intrinsic oscillation scale of the state in its rest frame. Proper time measures the accumulation of phase of this internal oscillation, while energy and momentum describe its frequency and wavevector content in a generic inertial frame. Relativistic kinematics is thus recovered as a unified manifestation of the underlying phase structure.

VI
Discussion

The formulation presented in this work provides a phase-based reconstruction of relativistic kinematics, in which proper time, energy, momentum, and the invariant interval arise from the requirement of phase coherence of localized wave states. The results obtained are fully consistent with standard special relativity and do not introduce modifications to its predictions.

It is important to emphasize that the present approach does not constitute a new dynamical theory. Rather, it offers a structural reinterpretation in which phase invariance plays a central role. In this perspective, the Minkowski interval is not taken as a primary postulate, but is identified as the quadratic form compatible with the invariant accumulation of phase along particle trajectories.

The interpretation of proper time as the phase count of an intrinsic oscillation connects naturally with the wave-mechanical description of matter. In particular, the relation between mass and rest-frame frequency is consistent with the de Broglie and Compton frameworks, and highlights the role of internal periodicity in relativistic systems.

This viewpoint also establishes a direct conceptual link between relativistic kinematics and interference phenomena
4
, where phase differences determine observable effects. In this sense, the present formulation may be regarded as a bridge between the geometric structure of spacetime and the phase-based descriptions commonly used in quantum theory.

Several limitations should be noted. The present work is restricted to kinematical aspects and does not address the dynamical origin of the intrinsic frequency or the underlying field equations governing the wave. Furthermore, no extension to accelerated frames or gravitational fields is developed here. These aspects require additional assumptions and are left for future investigation.

Despite these limitations, the phase-based perspective developed here provides a coherent and unified interpretation of relativistic kinematics, and may offer a useful framework for exploring connections between wave propagation, spacetime structure, and more fundamental formulations of physics.

This perspective may also motivate future extensions to effective optical metrics and weak-field gravitational settings, but such developments lie beyond the scope of the present work.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented a phase-based formulation of special relativity in which the kinematical structure of the theory is reconstructed from the requirement of phase coherence of localized wave states. By treating phase as the primary invariant quantity, we have shown that proper time can be identified with the accumulation of an intrinsic oscillation, and that the Minkowski interval arises as the quadratic structure consistent with this invariance.

Within this framework, energy and momentum follow naturally from the wave properties of the system, while mass is interpreted as the rest-frame frequency associated with its internal oscillation. These results provide a unified perspective in which relativistic kinematics is understood in terms of phase evolution, rather than being introduced purely geometrically.

The formulation developed here does not alter the predictions of special relativity, but clarifies its structural foundations and highlights the role of phase coherence as an organizing principle. This perspective may prove useful in contexts where wave and geometric descriptions intersect, and suggests possible directions for future work aimed at extending these ideas to more general settings.
