The impact of family health resources on the economic risk of diseases in rural China: based on a path analysis
Authors: Yixuan Zhong, Sangsang Li, Jing Wang, Dan Han, Shiping Gu, Xinnan Du, Yizhou Ao, Xinwei Liu, Yanyu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesFamily health resources are critical elements for rural households to withstand health shocks. However, the mechanisms through which these resources are linked to the economic risk of diseases remain unclear. Grounded in the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, this study investigates the association of family health resources with the economic risk of diseases among rural households.MethodsUtilizing data from the 2020 wave of the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS), the economic risk of diseases was quantified using the Three-Stage Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) method. Path analysis was employed to examine the direct effects and the mediating roles.ResultsThe average score of family health resources among rural households was 3.34 ± 0.73. A total of 400 households (9.42%) were identified as high-risk. Family health resources significantly predicted a significantly lower economic risk of diseases (P < 0.001). Mechanism analysis revealed that resource quantity is associated with economic risk through three mediating functions: positive effects from accumulation and investment function, and a negative effect from compensation function.The mediation effects of these three functions accounted for 12.492%, 2.580%, 7.196% of the total effect, respectively. Compared to overall resource-disadvantaged households, the resource-balanced advantaged households demonstrated stronger mediation proportions through the accumulation function and investment function—29.774% and 9.548%, respectively.ConclusionFamily health resources are negatively associated with the economic risk of diseases by promoting the accumulation and investment functions while a lower reliance on the compensation function. Targeted optimization of family health resource allocation is needed to strengthen resilience against disease-related economic risk.

Introduction

Health shocks not only impair individual physical and mental health but also exert adverse effects on household economic status. This economic risk of diseases poses a universal challenge facing households globally [1]. The UHC Global Monitoring Report 2025 reveals that despite improvements in global healthcare service coverage, approximately 2.1 billion people still face financial hardship when accessing health services, with 1.6 billion falling into or deepening poverty due to health expenditures. Illness imposes an immediate strain on household finances through high out-of-pocket medical expenses and may also reduce or even eliminate the labor capacity of those affected. This leads to substantial productivity losses [2], and crowds out essential consumption and productive investments, thereby weakening the ability of patients and their families to generate future income and jeopardizing their long-term economic welfare [3, 4]. The economic consequences are amplified for rural households due to their reliance on manual labor, which makes income contingent upon physical health [5]. This challenge is notably pronounced in China, where a rapid epidemiological transition has fundamentally altered the nature of household economic risks. While acute infectious diseases were once the dominant concern, the disease burden is now overwhelmingly driven by chronic conditions. According to data released by the National Health Commission of China, chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs) now account for 88.5% of total deaths and over 70% of the disease burden, posing potential risks to families. Given the magnitude of this problem, identifying factors associated with a lower economic risk of diseases is a critical academic and policy imperative.

Existing literature has extensively explored the determinants of the economic risk of diseases from a multi-dimensional perspective. First, regarding the disease itself, characteristics such as severity, type, and duration directly dictate the magnitude of the financial burden. Severe or chronic conditions not only incur high direct medical costs but also lead to long-term labor loss and productivity decline, thereby acting as the primary stressor [6, 7]. Second, at the micro-level, individual and household endowments influence the internal capacity to resist these shocks. Individual factors like education, health literacy and lifestyle influence the proactive adoption of preventive behaviors [8, 9], while household-level factors (e.g., financial status, dependency ratio) may influence the immediate economic buffering capacity [9–11]. Third, at the macro-level, societal determinants such as social support networks, regional socioeconomic development, and health policy design constitute the external safety net that moderates the impact of shocks by providing risk-sharing mechanisms [12–15].

Furthermore, there is a growing consensus that relying solely on external protection is insufficient. Beyond external protection and support mechanisms, enhancing the endogenous coping capacity of individuals and households is critical for mitigating the economic risk of diseases, preventing cycles of economic hardship, and achieving sustainable economic security [13, 16]. This has shifted focus toward the resources and capabilities inherent within the household itself. However, family health resources—defined as all internal and external resources accessible to households that promote member health and well-being [17–19]—have received limited attention as a potential key determinant. Besides, previous studies have generally examined the role of family resources from a single dimension—such as financial or social capital [20, 21]—or have focused exclusively on observable coping behaviors, such as the utilization of medical services [22], to evaluate family strategies for managing risks. However, empirical evidence on how the overall stock and structural composition of these resources influence economic risk, as well as the specific pathways involved, remains limited. To sum up, a critical gap exists in understanding this process: what are the precise mechanisms through which diversified family health resources translate into the capability to predict a lower economic risk of disease.

According to the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, this study further proposes a mechanism hypothesis regarding the functional pathways through which family health resources influence the economic risk of diseases. The COR theory posits that individuals and families are motivated to obtain, retain, and protect valuable resources to mitigate stress from potential threats [23, 24]. Furthermore, the Resource Caravan principle posits that resources travel together in aggregated sets suggesting that the aggregate level and configuration of the resources are critical for their capacity to cope with threats [25]. This principle provides the theoretical foundation for our use of Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) to identify distinct structural configurations of family health resources. Secondly, the theory posits that individuals and families proactively invest current resources to acquire additional resources and reduce the probability of future resource loss. Third, the Loss Spiral principle states that resource-depleted individuals and families are disproportionately vulnerable to further resource loss, making them dependent on external resource supplementation to halt the downward cycle.

Guided by the COR theory, we propose that households engage in a series of behaviors to manage their resources to maintain a reasonable amount and structure, collectively termed “Resource Management Functions”: (1) The Accumulation Function: building a resource surplus (e.g., assets) to buffer against future uncertainty, reflecting the COR emphasis on resource preservation and surplus maintenance; (2) The Investment Function: allocating resources to enhance capital, thereby reducing the probability of shocks, derived from the Resource Investment principle; and (3) The Compensation Function: mobilizing reserve resources to offset losses after a shock occurs, preventing a “loss spiral”. We hypothesize that the effective operation of these integrated resource management functions serves as a key potential mediating pathway linking static family health resources to dynamic resilience against economic risk.

Therefore, this study aims to empirically investigate the association and mechanisms linking family health resources to the economic risk of diseases among rural households in China. Specifically, the objectives of this study are to: (1) evaluate the status of family health resources in rural China in terms of quantity and structure; (2) verify the direct associations of the quantity and structure of family health resources with a lower economic risk of diseases; (3) examine the mediating role of resource management functions (accumulation, investment, and compensation) in this relationship; and (4) identify the specific pathways through which different dimensions of resources operate. By elucidating these mechanisms, this study expects to provide evidence-based insights for enhancing household-level risk management capabilities in developing contexts.

Methods

Data source and sample selection

The study sample was drawn from the 2020 wave of the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS), which was conducted by the Institute of Social Science Survey (ISSS) at Peking University. The CFPS employed a multi-stage probability sampling design and collected information at three levels: individual, household, and community. The individual-level dataset contains a wide range of variables, including basic demographic information (age, gender, marital status, household size, etc.), education, employment and income, social relationships, financial assistance, and health-related indicators (such as self-rated physical and mental health, evaluated health status, health insurance coverage, health expenditure, and utilization of health services).

In this study, the sample was initially drawn from 11,620 households included in the CFPS family economic dataset. The financial respondent (resplpid) was used as the matching key to merge relevant variables from the individual-level dataset. After conducting preliminary data cleaning and variable alignment across the two datasets, a merged sample comprising 10,322 households was obtained. Subsequently, 5,732 non-rural households were excluded based on household registration information, resulting in a subsample of 4,590 rural households. Further exclusion was applied to 342 households with missing values in key variables such as age, gender, and marital status. The final analytic sample consisted of 4,248 rural households.The following figure (Fig. 1) presents the flowchart for sample screening.

Variables and definitions

Dependent variable

The economic risk of diseases in this study is defined as the probability that a household will fall into economic distress in the future due to health shocks. Unlike static ex-post measures, this concept represents a forward-looking perspective, serving as a comprehensive prediction of future risks based on multiple household indicators, which aligns with the concept of poverty vulnerability. Therefore, the economic risk of diseases in this study was measured using the three-stage Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) method proposed by Chaudhuri. Independent and mediating variables

The independent variable in this study was family health resources, which—guided by the Conservation of Resources (COR) Theory and existing literature—were categorized into seven dimensions: human health resources, financial health resources, physical health resources, informational health resources, healthcare resources, social network, and policy-related health resources (Table 1). Human health resources focused on the stock of health capital within the household, encompassing both quantity and quality. Following prior studies, the quality dimension was reflected by the number of household members with chronic diseases and those with poor self-rated health, while the quantity dimension was captured by household size as a proxy for potential caregiving capacity [26–28]. Financial health resources emphasized liquidity available for maintaining and improving health and were measured using total household income and per capita income [29]. Physical health resources referred to tangible assets related to health promotion and disease prevention, proxied by household expenditure on health-related products. Informational health resources captured access to health knowledge, measured by whether any household members used mobile devices to obtain online health information—an indicator increasingly relevant under the worldwide shift from traditional to digital health information channels [28, 30, 31]. Healthcare resources were assessed using perceived quality of medical services, which reflects the extent to which regional health systems meet residents’ needs [32]. Social network resources referred to external social ties and were measured by neighborhood support, indicating the availability of community-level social capital during times of need [33]. Lastly, policy-related health resources were measured by whether household members were covered under the basic health insurance scheme, given its essential role in China’s social protection system [34, 35].

Measurement Indicators of Family Health Resources and Resource Management Functions among Rural Households

The mediating variable in this study was the resource management functions. The functions were conceptualized to include three core components: the resource accumulation function, the investment function, and the compensation function (Table 1). The resource accumulation function primarily captured the household’s ability to accumulate wealth over time, directly reflecting the COR emphasis on resource preservation and surplus maintenance as a buffer against future threats. When coping with health shocks, families often prioritize the use of liquid assets and only resort to selling fixed assets or restructuring property when liquid resources are insufficient [36, 37]. Therefore, the total value of accumulated household assets was used to measure this function, as it reflects both the level of accumulated health resources and the household’s actual capacity to withstand health-related financial risks. The investment function focused on proactive health-promoting behaviors, derived from the COR resource investment principle—the idea that individuals and families invest current resources to enhance future capital stocks. Health-related behaviors constitute an investment in health capital, with returns realized as extended healthy life expectancy and sustained labor productivity. Prior studies have shown that the frequency of physical exercise can indicate the level of health investment [38, 39]. Accordingly, this study used physical activity frequency as the indicator for the investment function. The compensation function referred to external support obtained by the household to offset the economic burden of illness. This component was grounded in the COR theoretical corollary of resource loss and substitution: when primary resources are depleted, individuals will attempt to substitute alternative resources to fill the void. While some studies have emphasized the importance of informal income transfers and caregiving assistance, due to data availability, this study adopted formal transfer income as a proxy for compensation. Formal transfer payments (e.g., social insurance, government subsidies) were conceptualized as institutionalized compensation mechanisms designed to smooth consumption and prevent health shocks from triggering a poverty trap. This indicator reflects the extent of financial support households received from government and social security systems [40].

Covariates

In this study, demographic characteristics were included as covariates to control for potential confounding effects on the main analytical models. Specifically, age was grouped into three categories (1 = ≤ 60 years, 2 = 61–75 years, 3 = > 75 years), and gender was coded as 1 = male and 2 = female. Marital status was categorized as 1 = married and 2 = divorced/widowed/never married. Educational attainment was divided into three levels: 1 = primary school or below, 2 = junior high school, and 3 = senior high school or above. The household dependency ratio was classified into three levels based on the ratio of non-working to working household members: 1 = 0, 2 = 0–0.7, and 3 = ≥ 0.7. These covariates were included in the regression models to account for basic socio-demographic differences among households.

Statistical analysis

The entropy weight method was used to assign weights to indicators of family health resources and resource management functions, while latent profile analysis (LPA) was employed to classify family health resources into different structural types. The entropy weight method calculated indicator weights across dimensions, enabling the aggregation of standardized indicators into a composite quantity of family health resources, where higher scores indicated better overall resource levels. LPA was then conducted to identify group-level patterns in health resource distribution. Model fit was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and entropy. In general, lower AIC and BIC values indicate better model fit, while entropy values closer to 1 suggest greater classification accuracy. An entropy value above 0.8 is typically considered indicative of reliable classification.

Based on the above, this study employed descriptive statistics and regression analysis to examine the distribution of family health resources among rural households and their role in managing economic risk, and to preliminarily explore the association between health resources and the economic risk of diseases. For categorical variables, frequency and percentage were used to describe household characteristics, family health resources, resource management functions, and the economic risk of diseases. For continuous variables, mean ± standard deviation was reported. Group differences in continuous variables were assessed using independent samples t-tests, while chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. Further, multivariate linear regression models were constructed with the economic risk of diseases as the dependent variable, family health resources and resource management functions as the key independent variables, and household characteristics as control variables, to assess the associations of the explanatory variables with the economic risk of diseases.

This study further applied path analysis to systematically examine the mechanism through which family health resources are associated with a lower economic risk of diseases. Specifically, the analysis focused on the potential mediating effect of resource management functions in the relationship between family health resources and the economic risk of diseases, aiming to reveal how health resources are linked to risk outcomes through functional pathways. Results

Based on entropy-weighted scores for each dimension of family health resources, this study employed latent profile analysis (LPA) to identify the structural types of family health resources. Model fit results are presented in Table 2. The findings showed that when the number of latent classes was set to three, the entropy value was close to 1 and markedly higher than those of the four-class and five-class models. Additionally, the three-class model exhibited relatively lower AIC and BIC values and significantly higher minimum posterior classification probabilities, indicating better model fit, stronger class separation, and greater reliability. Therefore, the structure of family health resources among rural households was categorized into three types.

Table 3 presents the distribution of household demographic characteristics, health resources, and resource management functions between high-risk and low-risk households. Based on the international poverty line standard (USD 1.90 per person per day) and the high-risk threshold of 0.3 for the economic risk of diseases, the results showed that a total of 400 households (9.42% of the sample) were identified as high-risk households (risk ≥ 0.3). This indicates that although these households were not currently in poverty, they had a 30% probability of falling into economic distress due to health shocks within the next year, placing them in a category of potentially high economic risk of diseases requiring targeted policy attention and intervention. Significant differences in demographic characteristics were observed between high-risk and low-risk households (P < 0.001). Specifically, households with members aged 61 or older, female household heads, Divorced/Widowed/Never Married status, Not Employed, Primary School or Below education level, and a household dependency ratio of ≥ 0.7 were more likely to exhibit high economic risk of diseases.

Furthermore, the accumulation function, investment function, and compensation function also served as mediators in the relationship between health resource structural types and the economic risk of diseases. Compared with households in the Overall Resource-Disadvantaged Type, those in the Resource-Balanced Advantage Type showed statistically significant relative mediation effects through the accumulation function (β = − 0.047) and investment function (β = − 0.015), accounting for 29.774% and 9.548% of the total effect, respectively. Discussion

This study confirmed that both the aggregate quantity and structural configuration of family health resources were significantly associated with lower economic risk of diseases among rural households. Households with greater resource quantity and those with a more balanced structure exhibited significantly lower economic risk. Path analysis further indicated that these resources were linked to risk outcomes through optimizing resource management functions, specifically through higher levels of accumulation and investment and a reduced reliance on compensation. The possible reasons for the above results are as follows. The accumulation and investment functions primarily operate as ex-ante active defense mechanisms. Meanwhile, the compensation function typically serves as an ex-post passive remedy. Structural advantages (such as information resources) act as catalysts that amplify the efficiency of active defenses. Households with balanced resource structures can more effectively activate accumulation and investment strategies.

The accumulation function primarily operates before a health shock occurs, helping to build resilience. The path analysis indicated that family health resources were negatively associated with economic risk through higher levels of the accumulation function. Based on existing health resources, rural households can leverage the accumulation function to build financial assets, thereby creating a “cushioning effect” that is associated with lower economic risk of diseases. Economic resources accumulated over time enhance a household’s ability to access necessary medical services during sudden health shocks, serving as a buffer against financial impact and helping to avert a vicious cycle of economic distress and disease. Before a health shock occurs, rural households with low or temporarily reduced incomes—especially those lacking access to formal credit—tend to accumulate resources and increase savings as a proactive strategy to guard against potential economic risk of diseases [42, 43].Following a health shock, households often experience labor loss, leading to declines in wage and business income. This typically forces them to adjust their consumption patterns and increase precautionary savings to manage future expenditure uncertainties. Although such adjustments often come at the expense of spending on education, recreation, and preventive care, which may compromise long-term development, they remain an effective defensive strategy for preserving liquidity and reducing immediate economic risk of diseases. This suggests that the core of risk defense lies in robust asset accumulation. Policy interventions should therefore focus on enhancing the household’s capacity for wealth accumulation to ensure households have a sufficient “material buffer” to withstand shocks.

The investment function also comes into play before the occurrence of health shocks. Our findings suggest that this function contributes to health risk reduction potentially through sustained health-promoting behaviors, which are expected to maintain health capital, support a stable labor supply, and thereby alleviate the economic burden associated with illness. Existing studies have shown that health investment behaviors—such as maintaining a nutritious diet, avoiding tobacco and excessive alcohol consumption, engaging in regular physical activity, and ensuring adequate sleep—can significantly improve blood pressure and glucose levels, prevent the onset of chronic diseases, or improve health outcomes for individuals already living with such conditions [44]. By safeguarding the physical capacity to work, sustained health capital helps households avoid the income disruptions that often follow health shocks, thereby reinforcing the stability of household finances when crises occur [45]. These findings suggest that initiatives aimed at fostering health-promoting behaviors could serve as a valuable complement to asset-building strategies, potentially strengthening rural households’ proactive defense against health-related economic shocks. In the Chinese context, recent policy efforts—such as the Healthy China 2030 initiative and the expansion of community-based health education programs—have begun to prioritize health literacy and preventive behaviors, providing an existing institutional platform through which such health-promoting interventions could be delivered.

The compensation function is typically mobilized after a shock as a safety net. The path analysis indicated that greater family health resources were associated with a reduced reliance on the compensation function, which in turn was linked to a lower economic risk of diseases. Households with richer and more balanced health resources tended to rely less on compensation, because their internal buffers reduced the need for emergency external assistance. For resource-depleted households, compensation functions such as relying on health insurance reimbursements to offset hospitalization costs, seeking medical assistance to cover out-of-pocket expenses [46, 47], or depending on support from relatives and friends for caregiving needs [48] are typically activated when households experience substantial depletion of health capital and lack sufficient internal resources to manage the crisis. This interpretation aligns with extensive evidence from China’s rural health insurance, which plays an indispensable role in preventing households from falling into poverty traps following health shocks. Therefore, the policy objective should not be to uniformly reduce reliance on compensation, but rather to optimize the functional mix: by strengthening accumulation and investment as ex-ante defenses, compensation can be preserved as a robust safety net for those who genuinely need it.

Structural configurations of family health resources matter for economic risk resilience. Compared to “Overall Resource-Disadvantaged” households, both “Resource-Balanced Advantage” and “Information Resource-Advantaged” households tended to exhibit lower levels of economic risk of diseases. For this balanced group, the mechanism likely operates through the absence of critical shortages: a balanced and rational allocation of family health resources to avoid the “bucket effect” caused by critical resource shortages that may weaken the overall capacity for preventing risks. Existing literature has established that structural imbalances in external health resources, such as insufficient human resources and pharmaceutical shortages, directly impede continuity of care and exacerbate financial burdens [49]. Our study reveals a parallel mechanism at the micro-household level, indicating that internal structural imbalances are equally detrimental. Even if a household possesses certain assets, a “weakest link” in other dimensions may prevent the effective utilization of these assets.

Furthermore, informational health resources, in particular, function as a lever within the resource system. Adequate information enhances household members’ understanding of disease prevention, treatment options, and reimbursement policies, thereby improving the allocative efficiency of limited health resources [50]. Conversely, a deficit in information may hinder engagement with external social network resources and limit the acquisition of critical support. This informational asymmetry undermines rational health-related decision-making [51] and financial planning, ultimately constraining the household’s potential for maintaining long-term economic resilience [31]. In policy terms, this suggests a dual-track approach—strengthening foundational health assets across multiple dimensions for all households, while simultaneously targeting health information deficits, which may disproportionately constrain the resource-poor. China’s current emphasis on cultivating grassroots health literacy is precisely oriented toward strengthening informational resources for rural households.

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment.

First, regarding the research design, the reliance on cross-sectional data restricts our ability to establish strict causal sequencing. Although we controlled for key demographic covariates, the reported path coefficients may be subject to omitted variable bias (e.g., unobserved wealth endowments or health history) and must be interpreted as conditional associations rather than strict causal effects. Furthermore, the positive association between the compensation function and economic risk is susceptible to reverse causality, as China’s means-tested transfer programs typically target households already facing elevated risk. Future panel-data studies employing instrumental variable or fixed-effects approaches would be valuable to further address this concern and disentangle these temporal dynamics.

Second, utilizing a general-purpose national survey necessitated the use of proxy indicators with inherent constraints. Specifically, the investment function was proxied solely by physical activity, capturing only a fraction of multidimensional health behaviors. The compensation function, measured by formal transfer income, inevitably excludes non-monetary informal support (e.g., caregiving) and simultaneously captures both institutional compensation and underlying socioeconomic disadvantage. Furthermore, measuring the accumulation function via total assets captures a static stock rather than a dynamic process. Finally, the inability to fully control for unobserved confounders—such as regional fixed effects and specific occupational types—may introduce omitted-variable bias. Future research utilizing specialized surveys should incorporate comprehensive multi-indicator measures to further validate the pathways identified in this study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, when family health resources are sufficient and structurally balanced, they are directly and indirectly associated with a lower economic risk of diseases through resource management functions. Family health resources are linked to a lower economic risk of diseases through higher levels of the resource accumulation and investment functions, alongside a reduced reliance on the compensation function. Establishing long-term risk resilience requires shifting governance focus from resource infusion to capacity enhancement. Policy efforts should focus on enhancing households’ capacity to optimize resource configuration through informational empowerment, strengthening their ability to accumulate assets as a material buffer, and fostering their capability for proactive health investment. Ultimately, this facilitates a transition for rural households from passive risk coping to active risk management.
