Integrated pan-cancer multi-omics profiling and experimental validation identify LSM12 as a prognostic biomarker and candidate therapeutic target in human cancers

Background

The molecular landscape of tumorigenesis is increasingly recognized as a multi-layered phenomenon extending beyond primary genomic alterations to encompass complex regulatory networks operating at the post-transcriptional level [1, 2]. Emerging evidence has positioned RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) as pivotal orchestrators of epitranscriptomic and post-transcriptional programs that sustain the hallmarks of cancer [3, 4]. By governing mRNA processing, transport, stability, and translation, RBPs enable malignant cells to rapidly rewire their proteomes in response to oncogenic signaling and environmental stressors [5, 6]. Consequently, dysregulation of RNA-metabolism-related proteins is frequently exploited by tumors to drive uncontrolled proliferation and facilitate immune evasion [7].

Among the diverse families of RBPs, the Like-Sm (LSM) protein family has emerged as a critical, yet partially understood, component of cellular homeostasis [8, 9]. Characterized by the conserved Sm domain, LSM proteins typically assemble into hetero-heptameric ring structures that function as molecular chaperones for various RNA species [10]. The LSM1–7 complex is cytoplasmic and instrumental in mRNA decapping and decay [11], whereas the LSM2–8 complex resides in the nucleus, facilitating U6 small nuclear RNA (snRNA) maturation and spliceosomal assembly [12]. While family members such as LSM1 and LSM14 have been extensively characterized as oncogenic facilitators in solid tumors, often through stabilization of pro-survival transcripts, the specific biological and clinical significance of LSM12 remains largely understudied [13].

Unlike its canonical counterparts, LSM12 possesses a unique evolutionary profile and has been tentatively linked to specialized functions, including regulation of nucleocytoplasmic transport, cellular stress granule dynamics, and calcium mobilization from acidic organelles [14]. Despite these fundamental roles, a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of LSM12 has not been conducted to delineate its expression landscape, interaction networks, or impact on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Furthermore, the potential of LSM12 to serve as a bridge between RNA metabolism and immunosuppressive signaling, such as the recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [14], remains a profound area of interest with implications for the efficacy of contemporary immunotherapies [15, 16].

In this study, we address these gaps through an integrative multi-omics framework that combines large-scale bioinformatics from TIMER2, GEPIA2, CPTAC, TISIDB, and cBioPortal with in vitro functional validation. We hypothesized that LSM12 is a widespread oncogenic driver whose overexpression is driven by promoter hypomethylation and copy-number amplification rather than somatic mutations, and that it coordinates RNA splicing and cell-cycle pathways to promote tumor aggressiveness [17]. Additionally, molecular docking, absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profiling, and DFT calculations were performed to evaluate LSM12 druggability and identify candidate small-molecule inhibitors [18, 19]. By providing the first comprehensive pan-cancer characterization of LSM12, this study identifies a novel prognostic biomarker and expands the therapeutic horizon for targeting the RNA metabolism machinery in oncology.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed an integrated multi-omics and experimental approach to investigate the oncogenic and immunomodulatory roles of LSM12 across multiple cancer types. Publicly available transcriptomic, genomic, proteomic, and epigenetic datasets were leveraged to evaluate LSM12 expression, genetic alterations, DNA methylation, protein levels, immune infiltration patterns, and clinical relevance. Complementarily, computational molecular docking was performed to ascertain LSM12 druggability and identify candidate inhibitors. Finally, in vitro siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments were conducted in human cancer cell lines to validate key in silico findings.

Differential expression and clinical correlation analysis

To assess LSM12 differential expression between tumor and normal tissues, we utilized the TIMER2.0 platform [20]. For tumor types lacking sufficient adjacent normal samples in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), the GEPIA2 database was employed using GTEx normal tissue data as a reference [21]. The TNMplot web server was accessed to compare LSM12 expression in normal, primary tumor, and metastatic tissues and to evaluate its association with tumor progression [22]. For validation of protein-level expression, data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) were accessed via the UALCAN portal, with additional confirmation from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA).

Survival prognosis and genetic alteration analysis

The prognostic value of LSM12 was determined using two complementary platforms. The GEPIA2 “Survival Analysis” module generated heatmaps showing correlations between LSM12 expression and overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). The Kaplan-Meier (KM) Plotter was used to perform univariate survival analyses across five major carcinoma types (breast, ovarian, lung, gastric, and liver) using log-rank tests to determine statistical significance [23]. It should be noted that all survival analyses were univariate; multivariable adjustment for clinical covariates was not performed, which represents an important limitation. To characterize LSM12 genomic instability, we queried the cBioPortal database [24] for somatic mutations, copy-number variations (CNVs), and structural variants across TCGA cancer cohorts.

Epigenetic regulation analysis

Given the established role of epigenetic dysregulation in oncogenesis [25], we examined LSM12 promoter DNA methylation in tumor versus normal tissues using UALCAN [26]. Methylation levels are reported as aggregate beta-values per tumor type; probe-level analysis was not performed and represents a limitation of the present study.

Immune infiltration, microenvironment, and checkpoint correlation

The impact of LSM12 expression on the TIME was assessed using the TIMER2.0 algorithm [27]. We evaluated infiltration levels of key immune populations, including MDSCs, natural killer T (NKT) cells, and T helper subsets, due to their critical roles in tumor immunosurveillance and immune escape. Correlations between LSM12 expression and immune infiltration were quantified using partial Spearman correlation coefficients, adjusted for tumor purity. The relationship between LSM12 expression and established biomarkers of immunotherapy response, including tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and immune checkpoint (ICP) molecules, was analyzed using the SangerBox platform [28].

Single-cell functional profiling

To resolve LSM12 expression and function at single-cell resolution, we queried the Cancer Single-cell State Atlas (CancerSEA) [29]. This resource enabled mapping of LSM12 across 14 functional states (e.g., invasion, metastasis, DNA repair) in approximately 900 single cancer cells spanning 25 tumor types. These analyses represent bulk-level correlation data from a curated atlas and do not substitute for primary single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) deconvolution.

Protein-protein interaction and functional enrichment analysis

Proteins interacting with LSM12 were retrieved from the STRING database [30], while LSM12-correlated genes in the TIME were identified via GEPIA2. Overlapping proteins from both lists were visualized using a Venn diagram (R VennDiagram package). The combined protein set was subjected to functional enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package [31] to identify enriched pathways related to RNA processing, spliceosome regulation, and immune modulation.

Structural modeling and virtual screening

The full-length three-dimensional structure of human LSM12 (UniProt ID: Q3MHD2) was obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database [32–34]. Binding-site prediction was performed using Fpocket3, which employs Voronoi tessellation and physicochemical scoring to identify druggable cavities. The top-ranked pocket, selected based on volume, hydrophobicity, polarity, and druggability score, was designated for virtual screening. The LSM12 structure was prepared for docking using AutoDockTools [35]: Kollman charges were assigned [36], polar hydrogens added, and rotatable bonds defined where appropriate. A focused library of oncology-relevant small molecules was sourced from the ChemDiv Anti-cancer Library [37]. Ligands were preprocessed using Open Babel [38] and Avogadro [39]: geometry was optimized under the MMFF94 force field, protonation states were adjusted to physiological pH (7.4), salts and duplicates removed, and structures converted to PDBQT format with Gasteiger charges.

Molecular docking studies

Docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina v1.2.6 [40, 41] to evaluate ligand binding within the Fpocket-predicted site. A cubic search grid was centered on the selected pocket and sized to fully accommodate ligand conformational flexibility. Each ligand was docked using an exhaustiveness value of 16, and the best-scoring poses were retained for further inspection. Predicted binding modes were visualized and analyzed using Discovery Studio Visualizer [42], with a focus on hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic contacts, π- π interactions, and shape complementarity with the pocket geometry.

ADMET analysis

Pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles of the lead isoindolinone derivative (Compound 1) were predicted using ADMETlab 2.0 [43]. Absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) descriptors were computed using the platform’s integrated models for membrane permeability, plasma-protein binding, CYP450 interactions, clearance, and safety-related endpoints.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations

Quantum-chemical calculations were performed using ORCA [44]. Geometry optimization of the best-scoring compound employed the B3LYP-D4 functional [45–47] with the def2-SVP basis [48] set and RIJCOSX approximation [49, 50]. Frequency calculations confirmed the absence of imaginary vibrational modes. Frontier molecular orbital energies (HOMO/LUMO) and electrostatic potential (ESP) distributions were extracted from single-point energy calculations, providing electronic descriptors relevant to molecular reactivity and intermolecular interactions.

Cell lines, culture, and transfection

Human HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma) and TE-8 (esophageal carcinoma) cells (Nawah Scientific, Cairo, Egypt) were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. HepG2 and TE-8 cells were cultured in DMEM (High Glucose) and RPMI 1640 medium (both from Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (P/S). Cells were passaged at 70–80% confluence, and only early-passage cells were used for experiments. siRNA-mediated knockdown of LSM12 was performed using a Silencer Select pre-designed siRNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with a Silencer Select Non-Targeting siRNA as the negative control at an equimolar concentration. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) per the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using Takara reagents with β-actin as the internal normalization control. Each reaction was performed in technical triplicate. Primer sequences were as follows: LSM12 forward, 5’-CCTCTTCCAGTGGAAAGCCCAA-3’; LSM12 reverse, 5’-TCTCTGTCCGTGCTTTGCTGGC-3’; β-actin forward, 5’-CACCATTGGCAATGAGCGGTTC-3’; β-actin reverse, 5’-AGGTCTTTGCGGATGTCCACGT-3’.

Flow cytometry

At 48 h post-transfection, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stained with the viability dye Zombie NIR (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Cells were then fixed and permeabilized using the eBioscience™ Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, cells were stained with antibodies against Ki-67 (a proliferation marker) and Cyclin D1 (a cell-cycle marker).

Wound-healing assay

HepG2 and TE-8 cells were transfected with si-LSM12 or siNC and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. At 48 h post-transfection, a linear wound was created using a 200 µL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed by washing with PBS. Wound width was measured at 0 and 48 h post-wounding using brightfield microscopy.

Colony formation assay

At 24 h post-transfection, 3,000 cells were seeded into six-well plates and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 1–2 weeks. Colonies were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Colony numbers were counted manually.

CCK-8 proliferation assays

Cells transfected with LSM12 siRNA or negative control siRNA were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. Cell viability was assessed at 24, 48, and 72 h post-transfection using the CCK-8 kit (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Rockville, MD, USA). CCK-8 reagent (10 µL per well) was added, incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

All in vitro data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significance thresholds: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. Bioinformatics correlations (immune infiltration, co-expression) were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation; results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Widespread upregulation of LSM12 across human tumor types

Analysis using the TIMER2.0 platform revealed statistically significant upregulation of LSM12 mRNA expression in 18 tumor types: BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KICH, KIRC, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, READ, SKCM, STAD, THCA, and UCEC (Fig. 1A). For tumor types lacking sufficient matched normal tissue, the GEPIA2 platform (with GTEx as normal reference) was employed; significant LSM12 overexpression was confirmed in DLBC, OV, and THYM (p < 0.05; Fig. 1B). TNMplot analysis further demonstrated a stepwise increase in LSM12 expression from normal tissue to primary tumor to metastatic tissues in breast, liver, lung, and skin (Fig. 1C), suggesting a potential role in tumor progression.

TISIDB analysis demonstrated a statistically significant positive association between LSM12 expression and tumor grade in KIRC, LGG, and LIHC (Fig. 3A–B), as well as pathological stage in KIRC, KIRP, LUAD, and TGCT (Fig. 3C). GEPIA2 analysis further confirmed a positive correlation between LSM12 expression and pathological stage in ACC, BRCA, KICH, KIRP, and LIHC (Fig. 3D). Prognostic value of LSM12 expression in human tumors

The pronounced disparity in LSM12 expression between tumor and normal tissues prompted an investigation of its potential association with patient survival. GEPIA2 analysis revealed that elevated LSM12 expression was significantly negatively correlated with DFS in four cancer types: ACC, BLCA, LIHC, and UVM (p < 0.05; Fig. 4A). Six tumor types (ACC, LGG, LIHC, MESO, PAAD, and UVM) exhibited significant negative associations with OS (Fig. 4B). Complementary KM Plotter analysis demonstrated an inverse correlation between LSM12 expression and breast cancer recurrence-free survival (RFS; Fig. 5A). Lung and gastric cancers showed strong negative associations across all examined survival endpoints (Fig. 5C–D), while liver cancer showed a significant negative correlation with OS specifically (Fig. 5E). All survival analyses were univariate; the absence of multivariable adjustment for clinical covariates (stage, age, treatment exposure) represents a limitation of these prognostic estimates.

LSM12 genomic alterations

cBioPortal analysis revealed that LSM12 exhibited the highest alteration frequency in prostate cancer, followed by adrenocortical carcinoma, endometrial cancer, esophageal cancer, and pancreatic cancer, with copy-number amplifications and deep deletions being the most prevalent alteration types (Fig. 6A). Lollipop plot analysis showed mutations distributed across the protein, with notable clustering within the AD domain and a recurrent L79V variant observed in multiple patients (Fig. 6B). However, the functional consequences of L79V and other observed mutations have not been experimentally characterized; their biological significance remains to be established. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses revealed no statistically significant differences across any survival endpoint between patients with altered versus unaltered LSM12 status (DFS: p = 0.764; DSS: p = 0.611; OS: p = 0.450; PFS: p = 0.529; Fig. 6C), indicating that somatic mutations are unlikely to be the primary functional driver of LSM12 dysregulation in cancer.

Divergent methylation patterns of LSM12 in human cancers

LSM12 promoter methylation was assessed across multiple cancer types using TCGA-derived datasets via UALCAN. Distinct methylation patterns were observed between normal and tumor tissues in COAD, PAAD, PRAD, READ, TGCT, and UCEC. Importantly, LSM12 promoter methylation was consistently lower in primary tumor tissues compared with corresponding normal tissues in COAD, PAAD, READ, TGCT, and UCEC, suggesting that promoter hypomethylation may contribute to LSM12 overexpression in these cancers (Fig. 7). These results demonstrate divergent, tumor-specific LSM12 promoter methylation patterns, consistent with the hypothesis that epigenetic reprogramming is a key upstream mechanism of LSM12 dysregulation.

Effect of LSM12 expression on the tumor immune microenvironment

To evaluate the association between LSM12 expression and immune cell infiltration within the TIME, a pan-cancer correlation analysis was performed focusing on MDSCs and natural killer (NK) cells across TCGA cohorts (Fig. 8A–C). After adjustment for tumor purity, LSM12 expression showed a significant positive correlation with MDSC infiltration in most cancer types (Fig. 8A). Statistically significant positive associations (p < 0.05) were detected in ACC, BLCA, BRCA, COAD, LIHC, LUAD, and STAD, among others. Notably, no cancer type exhibited a significant negative correlation between LSM12 expression and MDSC abundance, suggesting a broadly immunosuppressive role for LSM12.

Correlation of LSM12 expression with immune checkpoints, MSI, and TMB

To further clarify the immunological relevance of LSM12, we examined its association with immune checkpoint (ICP) genes, microsatellite instability (MSI), and tumor mutational burden (TMB) across multiple cancers using the SangerBox platform (Fig. 9). LSM12 expression demonstrated widespread correlations with ICP molecules across cancers (Fig. 9A). UVM, KICH, and LIHC exhibited strong and significant positive correlations between LSM12 expression and most ICP genes, encompassing both co-inhibitory and co-stimulatory markers. In contrast, TGCT displayed a predominantly negative correlation with ICP genes, indicating a distinct immune regulatory context in this tumor type.

Single-cell functional profiling of LSM12

To assess LSM12’s association with cancer-relevant functional states, we queried CancerSEA (Fig. 10). Across blood cancers (AML, ALL, CML), LSM12 correlated with cell-cycle and differentiation programs. In brain tumors (HGG, ODG), it correlated with DNA damage repair and the cell cycle. In LUAD, LSM12 was associated with apoptosis, cell-cycle, DNA damage, and DNA repair programs. In ocular tumors (RB, UM), it is associated with angiogenesis, differentiation, inflammation, metastasis, quiescence, and stemness. Six functional states showed statistically significant pan-cancer associations: differentiation (r = 0.56), angiogenesis (r = 0.54), inflammation (r = 0.47), DNA repair (r = − 0.54), cell cycle (r = − 0.45), and DNA damage (r = − 0.38) (Fig. 10). These functional state correlations are derived from the CancerSEA atlas and represent aggregate single-cell data; primary scRNA-seq deconvolution to link LSM12-high cells to specific cellular lineages was not performed.

LSM12 protein interactome and functional enrichment analysis

The top 50 experimentally validated and predicted LSM12-interacting proteins were retrieved from the STRING database, and a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed. LSM12 occupies a central position within a densely connected network (Fig. 11A). Pan-cancer co-expression analysis using GEPIA2 identified 100 genes significantly associated with LSM12 expression (Fig. 11B). Intersection analysis between the STRING-derived interacting proteins and GEPIA2 co-expressed genes revealed two overlapping genes (Fig. 11C). The relatively small overlap reflects the complementary, non-redundant nature of protein interaction and transcriptional co-expression data and is a recognized limitation of the joint STRING/GEPIA2 approach.

The correlation between LSM12 and five representative genes—CCDC43, DENR, ELAVL1, LSM12P1, and PSME3 —was evaluated across pan-cancer TCGA data. Spearman correlation analysis demonstrated significant positive associations in the majority of tumor types (Fig. 11B), with limited exceptions in CHOL, PCPG, READ, TGCT, and UCS. Scatter plot analyses confirmed strong positive correlations between LSM12 and each of these genes across pan-cancer samples (Fig. 11D). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment of LSM12-associated genes revealed significant enrichment in RNA splicing, chromosome segregation, RNA localization, and non-membrane-bounded organelle assembly (Fig. 11E). Cellular component analysis indicated predominant localization to ribonucleoprotein granules, nuclear specks, spliceosomal complexes, and mitotic spindles (Fig. 11F). Molecular function analysis demonstrated enrichment in single-stranded RNA binding, basal transcription machinery binding, and microtubule motor activity (Fig. 11G). KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted nucleocytoplasmic transport, spliceosome, RNA degradation, and proteasome pathways as significantly associated with LSM12-related genes (Fig. 11I).

The DFT-optimized geometry of Compound 1 is illustrated in Fig. 14. The electrostatic potential (ESP) surface map (Fig. 16) confirmed a polarized electron density distribution, with electron-rich regions (red/orange) and electron-deficient regions (green/blue) consistent with the hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic interactions observed in the docking analysis. These quantum-chemical descriptors support the plausibility of Compound 1’s intermolecular interactions within the LSM12 binding pocket, though experimental biophysical confirmation of binding remains necessary.

LSM12 knockdown suppresses cancer cell proliferation, migration, and clonogenicity

siRNA-mediated knockdown of LSM12 was confirmed by qRT-PCR at 48 h post-transfection in both HepG2 and TE-8 cell lines (Fig. 17A). LSM12 silencing significantly reduced Ki-67-positive proliferating cells compared with negative control-transfected cells (Fig. 17B) and markedly decreased Cyclin D1 protein expression as assessed by intracellular flow cytometry (Fig. 17C), suggesting impaired G1/S cell-cycle progression. Cell viability was not significantly altered at 48 h post-transfection, as assessed by Zombie NIR exclusion (Fig. 17D), indicating that the observed anti-proliferative effects were not attributable to nonspecific cytotoxicity.

Discussion

The present study, to our knowledge, provides the first comprehensive pan-cancer multi-omics characterization of LSM12, an RNA-binding protein belonging to the Like-Sm family. By integrating large-scale transcriptomic, proteomic, epigenomic, and immune deconvolution data from publicly available cancer genomics repositories, we delineated the expression landscape, clinical significance, and immunomodulatory potential of LSM12 across diverse human malignancies. Importantly, the findings of this study are observational and hypothesis-generating; the multi-omics bioinformatics analyses presented herein do not constitute definitive mechanistic proof, and definitive causal conclusions will require prospective experimental validation and functional studies beyond the scope of the present work.

The consistent upregulation of LSM12 at both mRNA (TIMER2, GEPIA2) and protein (CPTAC, HPA) levels across 18 tumor types establishes a broad oncogenic expression signature for this previously understudied protein. These findings are concordant with prior reports of LSM family member dysregulation in cancer. Dong et al. [51] identified LSM12 as a tumor-associated gene and prognostic biomarker in oral squamous cell carcinoma, while Zhuang et al. [52] demonstrated upregulation of LSM12 in hepatocellular carcinoma. Most recently, Wu et al. [14] reported that LSM12 promotes lung squamous cell carcinoma progression by regulating the alternative splicing of ARRB1, and Tang et al. [17] characterized LSM12 as a functional driver of uveal melanoma oncogenesis. The present findings extend these tumor-specific reports to a pan-cancer framework. The positive associations between LSM12 expression and advanced tumor stage and grade across KIRC, LIHC, and LUAD, among others, further support a potential role in tumor progression. However, we acknowledge a key analytical limitation: the present pan-cancer analysis aggregates diverse tumor types without stratification by molecular subtypes (e.g., MSI-high versus microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer; luminal versus basal-like breast cancer). As Wang et al. [53] demonstrated in a tumor microenvironment-responsive profiling framework, subtype-specific profiling may reveal LSM12 expression patterns that are diluted in pan-cancer aggregates. Subtype-stratified analyses are warranted in future investigations.

LSM12 expression demonstrated inverse associations with OS and DFS in several tumor types, including ACC, LIHC, LGG, and UVM, in both GEPIA2 and KM Plotter analyses. We emphasize that these survival estimates are based on univariate Kaplan-Meier analyses using median expression as the dichotomization threshold. As such, they may be confounded by clinical variables including tumor stage, patient age, and treatment exposure. Multivariable adjustment, as exemplified by interaction-adjusted approaches for mutation-tumor suppressor interactions [54], would be required to determine whether LSM12 retains independent prognostic significance after controlling for established clinical parameters. Such analyses represent an important avenue for future clinical validation studies.

Promoter hypomethylation was identified as a potential epigenetic driver of LSM12 upregulation in COAD, PAAD, READ, TGCT, and UCEC, consistent with the broader paradigm of oncogene activation through methylation loss at promoter CpG islands. This mechanism may act in concert with the copy-number amplifications identified in cBioPortal analyses, jointly dysregulating LSM12 expression in specific tumor contexts. We acknowledge, however, that the present methylation analysis was based on aggregate beta-values from UALCAN and does not include probe-level resolution across individual CpG sites. As highlighted by Feng et al. [55] in the context of DNA methylation prediction, probe-specific effects can confound inter-tumor methylation comparisons, and probe-level re-analysis would strengthen the epigenetic conclusions of this study.

The correlation between elevated LSM12 expression and MDSC enrichment, alongside inverse associations with NK cell infiltration in the majority of tumor types, suggests a potential immunosuppressive role for LSM12 in the TIME. MDSC-mediated suppression of cytotoxic immunity is a well-established mechanism of tumor immune evasion, and the present data are consistent with the hypothesis that LSM12-high tumors may promote an immune-excluded or immune-suppressed microenvironment. However, several important caveats apply. First, tumor purity adjustment using partial Spearman correlation, while standard in TIMER2 analyses, varies in effectiveness across tumor types with heterogeneous cellularity. Second, the immune correlations have not been benchmarked against established TIME regulators. Application of orthogonal microenvironment deconvolution pipelines, such as those employed by Liu et al. [56] for cell-line-based immune profiling and Wang et al. [57] for macrophage modulation in ovarian cancer, would provide independent validation of LSM12’s immunosuppressive axis. Third, the CancerSEA functional state correlations were derived from a curated single-cell atlas and do not constitute primary scRNA-seq deconvolution; linking LSM12-high cells to specific immune lineages within tumors requires targeted single-cell analyses in future studies.

The observed pan-cancer correlations between LSM12 expression and TMB/MSI are intriguing and suggest that LSM12 may be relevant to immunotherapy response prediction. However, these associations are presented as raw Spearman correlations from aggregate pan-cancer data, without adjustment for neoantigen quality, mutational context, or immunotherapy treatment status. As Zhang et al. [58] and Wang et al. [59] have highlighted in the context of immunotherapy cohort benchmarking and immune regulation, TMB/MSI correlations require careful contextualization within prospectively defined immunotherapy-treated cohorts before predictive claims can be established. Whether LSM12 expression can serve as a true predictor of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) response remains an open question for future clinical investigation.

The PPI network analysis positioned LSM12 centrally within RNA splicing, spliceosome, and nucleocytoplasmic transport networks, consistent with its established role as an Sm-domain protein, and identified five pan-cancer co-expressed genes: CCDC43, DENR, ELAVL1, LSM12P1, and PSME3. The co-expression of LSM12 with ELAVL1 (HuR), a well-characterized post-transcriptional regulator of mRNA stability and cancer progression [60], suggests potential functional crosstalk in RNA stability regulation. Similarly, co-expression with PSME3 suggests proteasome pathway involvement in LSM12-associated gene networks. However, these co-expression associations do not constitute evidence of direct protein–protein interaction or shared RNA targets; RNA immunoprecipitation (RNA-IP) or ribosome profiling experiments would be necessary to identify direct LSM12 RNA targets in cancer cells. Additionally, the STRING/GEPIA2 overlap yielded only two shared genes, limiting the robustness of PPI-based pathway inference. Emerging computational approaches for coarse-to-fine feature extraction in drug-target networks [61] and synergistic pulse strategies in the TIME [62] may provide refined interactome models for LSM12 in future analyses.

Structure-based virtual screening identified Compound 1, an isoindolinone derivative, with a favorable docking score of − 11.779 kcal/mol and multiple interaction contacts within the Fpocket-predicted LSM12 binding site. DFT analysis provided quantum-chemical support for the observed binding interactions, and ADMET profiling indicated acceptable drug-like properties with no major safety alerts. We note several important limitations of this computational analysis. First, the ligand library was derived from a generic anticancer compound collection without LSM12-specific pharmacophore constraints; incorporating binding-site-focused library curation approaches, as advocated by Qi et al. [63] and Qian et al. [64], would increase the likelihood of identifying high-affinity and selective LSM12 binders. Second, pose reproducibility across replicate docking runs was not systematically evaluated, and the HOMO-LUMO gap of Compound 1 was not benchmarked against known LSM family binders. Third, experimental biophysical confirmation of Compound 1 binding to LSM12 and its on-target activity in cellular systems has not been performed. These in silico findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and as a starting point for structure-activity relationship (SAR) optimization and experimental validation.

siRNA-mediated silencing of LSM12 in HepG2 and TE-8 cells consistently reduced proliferation, migration, clonogenicity, and Cyclin D1 expression, supporting an oncogenic functional role for LSM12 in vitro. These findings are consistent with recent functional studies in OSCC [51] and lung squamous cell carcinoma [14]. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study employed only two cancer cell lines, and while both represent malignancies in which LSM12 is overexpressed, they do not fully represent the pan-cancer scope of the computational findings. Validation in additional LSM12-high cell models derived from prognostically relevant tumor types, such as LUAD, ACC, or UVM, is required to generalize these results. Furthermore, cell-cycle analysis relied on Cyclin D1 and Ki-67 as proxy markers; mechanistic dissection of G1/S arrest via phospho-Rb quantification or CDK inhibitor rescue experiments was not performed and represents a priority for future mechanistic studies.

Conclusion

This study presents the first systematic pan-cancer multi-omics characterization of LSM12, demonstrating its widespread transcriptional and proteomic overexpression across 18 + human tumor types. Elevated LSM12 expression correlated with advanced tumor stage and grade, and was associated with unfavorable OS and DFS in univariate survival analyses across multiple cancer types. Promoter hypomethylation and copy-number amplification were identified as candidate epigenetic and genomic drivers of LSM12 dysregulation. At the immune level, LSM12 expression correlated with an immunosuppressive TIME characterized by MDSC enrichment and NK cell exclusion, and was associated with ICP molecules, MSI, and TMB. Functional enrichment and PPI analyses implicated LSM12 in RNA splicing, nucleocytoplasmic transport, and proteasome pathways, with potential crosstalk with the RNA stability factor ELAVL1. In vitro silencing of LSM12 suppressed proliferation, migration, and clonogenicity in HepG2 and TE-8 cells, providing experimental support for a functional oncogenic role. Structure-based virtual screening identified Compound 1 as a candidate LSM12 binder with favorable in silico drug-like and electronic properties.

Collectively, these findings position LSM12 as a candidate prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target in pan-cancer precision oncology. We emphasize that this work is hypothesis-generating; the bioinformatics analyses are based on publicly available datasets and are subject to the limitations inherent to pan-cancer aggregation, univariate survival analysis, absence of molecular subtype stratification, and indirect immune deconvolution methods. Future studies should prioritize: (1) multivariable survival validation in independent cohorts with clinical covariate adjustment; (2) subtype-stratified pan-cancer analyses; (3) probe-level methylation re-analysis; (4) primary scRNA-seq deconvolution to define LSM12’s cellular context in the TIME; (5) RNA-IP experiments to identify direct LSM12 RNA targets; (6) experimental validation of Compound 1 binding and on-target activity; and (7) functional studies in additional cancer cell models, including G1/S arrest mechanistic dissection. Addressing these priorities will be essential to translate the present findings into clinically actionable insights.
