Kernel-based guarantees for nonlinear parametric models in Bayesian optimization
Rafael Oliveira

Abstract
Modern Bayesian optimization and adaptive sampling methods increasingly rely on nonlinear parametric models, yet theoretical guarantees for such models under adaptive data collection remain limited. Existing analyses largely focus on Gaussian processes, kernel machines, linear models, or linearized neural approximations, leaving a gap between theory and the nonlinear models used in practice. We develop a kernel-based framework for analyzing regularized nonlinear parametric models trained on adaptively collected data. Our approach uses kernels over the parameter space to induce reproducing-kernel Hilbert space structures over the corresponding model class, yielding confidence bounds for models trained with broad classes of regularized convex losses. We show how these bounds can support convergence guarantees for nonlinear acquisition and surrogate models, including randomized regularized policies that select points by maximizing a trained random model. These results provide a unified route to analyzing nonlinear parametric models in Bayesian optimization and related adaptive optimization settings.



1
Introduction

Bayesian optimization (BO) and adaptive experimental design provide principled frameworks for the optimization of expensive black-box functions by selecting data points sequentially in resource-constrained settings
(
24
)
, which makes them attractive for problems where observations require costly simulations, physical experiments, or human evaluation
(
28
)
.
The adaptive nature of the data collection process allows for choosing data points which are most informative for learning the model, typically improving data efficiency and reducing costs
(
12
;
24
)
. However, this adaptiveness also introduces dependencies that invalidate typical assumptions of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data in classic statistical learning theory.
As a result, standard i.i.d. learning-theoretic arguments do not directly apply, and performance guarantees typically require tools from bandits
(
31
;
5
)
and derivations based on martingales and self-normalized stochastic processes
(
7
;
1
)
.

Most existing regret analyses for BO rely on Gaussian processes
(
25
)
, kernelized models
(
5
)
, linear models
(
26
)
, or linearized neural approximations
(
6
)
. These assumptions enable tractable uncertainty estimates and information-theoretic arguments, but they do not fully capture many nonlinear parametric models used in modern machine learning, such as (finite-width) deep neural networks. This gap becomes especially relevant for likelihood-free Bayesian optimization (LFBO) methods
(
30
)
, which learn acquisition functions or utility-based decision rules directly from data, often using flexible parametric models such as neural networks
(
35
;
32
)
, rather than deriving acquisitions from an explicit Gaussian process (GP) surrogate. Despite their practical appeal, regret guarantees for such nonlinear finite-dimensional acquisition models remain limited.

This paper develops a kernel-based framework for analyzing regularized nonlinear parametric models trained on adaptively collected data. Our starting point is to view the parametric model class

{\mathcal{{G}}}=\{g(\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$},{\theta}):{\theta}\in\Theta\}

through a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS,
27
) induced by a positive-semidefinite kernel over the parameter space. This construction gives a Hilbert space geometry for nonlinear models, allowing regularization, functional gradients, and prediction errors to be controlled using RKHS tools. Moreover, under smoothness conditions, the induced model-dependent kernel can be dominated by standard kernels, such as Matérn kernels
(
25
, Ch. 4)
, leading to GP-style predictive-variance bounds, even though the learned model is not itself a Gaussian process or kernel machine.

We then apply this framework to randomized regularized policies. At each round, the model is trained with a regularization penalty around a fresh random initialization, and the next point is selected greedily by maximizing the trained model. This construction is closely related to randomize-then-optimize methods and provides an approximate Thompson-sampling mechanism for nonlinear parametric models. The random initialization is closely related to how models are deployed in practice and plays two key roles: it induces exploration, and, under sufficient regularization, it stabilizes the trained model around the initialized function.

Our main formal regret result focuses on the finite-domain approximate Thompson-sampling setting, where the randomized model is trained as a surrogate for the objective itself. We prove that, under realizability, a unique optimum, and prior regularity assumptions expressed in the RKHS-induced pseudometric, the algorithm obtains a sublinear cumulative regret. The proof combines three ingredients: a self-normalized generalization bound for the randomized regularized estimator, an effective small-ball condition ensuring that favourable initializations occur often enough, and a variance-decay argument showing that repeated visits to the optimum shrink a GP-style uncertainty term appearing in the regret decomposition.

Our results provide a first regret guarantee for randomized nonlinear parametric models in an LFBO-related setting, moving beyond analyses based on GP surrogates, kernel machines, or linearized neural models. Beyond this specific setting, the framework suggests a route for developing principled non-GP-based Bayesian optimization, active learning, and adaptive experimental design methods where flexible parametric models admit kernel-based uncertainty and regret analyses.

2
Problem setting and background

We consider an adaptive data collection setting, as it is the typical case in Bayesian optimization, bandits and general adaptive experimental design problems. In the following, we describe the basic problem formulation, state our assumptions on the training objectives for the models, and connect the formulation with existing frameworks.

2.1
Adaptive data collection

At each round
t\in\mathbb{{N}}
, an algorithm selects a point
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}
based on a model
g_{t-1}:{\mathcal{{X}}}\to{\mathcal{{Y}}}
and then observes
y_{t}\in{\mathcal{{Y}}}
, updating a dataset
{\mathcal{{D}}}_{t}:=\{({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t},y_{t})\}\cup{\mathcal{{D}}}_{t-1}
. We consider parametric models
g:{\mathcal{{X}}}\times\Theta\to{\mathcal{{Y}}}
, where the parameters at round
t\geq 1
are chosen by minimizing an empirical loss function with respect to
g_{\theta}:=g(\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$},{\theta})
:

L_{t}(g_{\theta})=R_{t}(g_{\theta})+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\ell(g_{\theta}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}),y_{i}),\qquad{\theta}\in\Theta,\quad t\in\mathbb{{N}},

(1)

where
\ell:{\mathcal{{Y}}}\times{\mathcal{{Y}}}\to\mathbb{{R}}
defines a pointwise approximation loss with respect to the data, and
R_{t}(g_{\theta})
is a regularization penalty, such as
\ell_{2}
regularization
R_{t}(g_{\theta})=\lambda_{t}\lVert{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{2}
, for a given regularization factor
\lambda_{t}>0
. Our analysis will focus on a compact domain
{\mathcal{{X}}}
and scalar-valued observations
{\mathcal{{Y}}}\subseteq\mathbb{{R}}
, though most of our results can be extended to the vector-valued setting after mild adjustments.

2.2
Loss functions

We consider strictly convex smooth loss functions, which are common in the machine learning literature. Formally, we make the following assumption on the individual loss functions:

A1
Loss.

The loss function
\ell_{t}:{\mathcal{{Y}}}\times{\mathcal{{Y}}}\to\mathbb{{R}}
is such that the map
\ell_{y}:=s\mapsto\ell(s,y)
is twice differentiable and strongly convex with
\ddot{\ell}_{y}(s)\geq\alpha_{\ell}>0
, for all
y,s\in{\mathcal{{Y}}}
.

Most loss functions commonly found in the ML literature are strongly convex, at least when restricted to model outputs lying in a compact interval. A classic example includes the cross-entropy loss:

\ell_{\mathrm{CE}}(g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}),y)=-y\log g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-(1-y)\log(1-g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})),

(2)

whose second derivative is
\ddot{\ell}_{y}(s)=\frac{y}{s^{2}}+\frac{1-y}{(1-s)^{2}}>0
, which is strongly convex whenever
s
remains within a compact subset of the open unit interval
(0,1)
(e.g., when we model log-probabilities). Another example is the squared error loss:

\ell_{\mathrm{SE}}(g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}),y)=\frac{1}{2}(g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-y)^{2},

(3)

which is strongly convex by construction, with
\alpha_{\ell}=1
.

2.3
Bayesian optimization

The driving example framework we consider is Bayesian optimization. However, parallels can be drawn to multi-armed bandits and active learning. In the BO setting, we seek to estimate the global optimum of an objective function
f:{\mathcal{{X}}}\to\mathbb{{R}}
,

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}}f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}).

(4)

We are given a maximum budget of function evaluations
T\geq 1
, selecting
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}
and observing
y_{t}=f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})+\epsilon_{t}
, where
\epsilon_{t}
represents zero-mean observation noise, for
t\in\{1,\dotsc,T\}
. To derive their point selection strategy for
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}
, BO frameworks usually place a prior distribution over the objective function, such as a Gaussian process
f\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k)
, given a positive-semidefinite kernel
k:{\mathcal{{X}}}\times{\mathcal{{X}}}\to\mathbb{{R}}
. Then decisions are made by maximizing an acquisition function
a_{t}:{\mathcal{{X}}}\to\mathbb{{R}}
,

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}}a_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}),\quad t\in\{1,\dotsc,T\}.

(5)

The acquisition function is generally formulated as an expected utility
a_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})=\mathbb{E}[u_{t}(y)\mid{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\mathcal{{D}}}_{t-1}]
, where
u_{t}:{\mathcal{{Y}}}\to\mathbb{{R}}
is a non-negative utility function, such as improvement
u_{t}(y):=\max\{y-\tau_{t},0\}
over a threshold
\tau_{t}\in\mathbb{{R}}
, e.g.,
\tau_{t}:=\max_{i\leq t}y_{i}
. The expectation is set to marginalize both epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty stemming, respectively, from the model and the observation noise. Alternatively, other popular acquisition functions are not directly expected utilities but work surprisingly well in practice, such as Thompson sampling,
a_{t}=\hat{f}_{t}
, where
\hat{f}_{t}
is a sample from the GP posterior given the available data
(
26
)
, and the upper confidence bound
a_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})=\mathbb{E}[f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\mid{\mathcal{{D}}}_{t}]+\beta_{t}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\mid{\mathcal{{D}}}_{t}]}
, where
\beta_{t}>0
is a confidence parameter, and
\mathbb{V}[\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$}]
represents the variance. We refer the reader to
11
for further background on BO.

Beyond the classic GP-based formulation, BO methods have been extended to use other types of models, such as Bayesian neural networks
(
29
;
16
)
, and likelihood-free Bayesian optimization (LFBO) methods
(
35
;
30
;
32
;
19
)
. The latter bypass the need for an explicit surrogate for the objective function
f
and instead model the acquisition function
a_{t}
directly by fitting a model
g_{t}\approx a_{t}
according to observation-based utilities
{\mathcal{{D}}}_{t}^{u}:=\{({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i},u_{t}(y_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{t}
. In this case, one uses utilities
u_{t}
which can be directly computed from the data, e.g.,
u_{t}(y)=\mathbb{I}[y\geq\tau_{t}]
, where
\tau_{t}
is typically an empirical quantile of the marginal observations distribution
(
35
)
. We mainly focus on the LFBO setting, though our results are also applicable to methods that model the objective function using a parametric surrogate, such as neural Thompson sampling
(
38
)
.

3
RKHS view of nonlinear models

To analyze nonlinear parametric models under adaptive data settings, we need a function space in which distances, gradients, and confidence widths can be controlled. Hilbert spaces are particularly useful for this purpose because they provide an inner product geometry, allowing gradient-based arguments and self-normalized concentration inequalities
(
2
;
17
)
. Since our observations involve model evaluations
g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\theta})
, we also require point evaluation to be continuous. Hilbert spaces of functions satisfying this property are precisely reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
(
33
, Def. 4.18)
,
which motivates embedding the model class into an RKHS.

Model-induced RKHS.

To analyze the learned model’s approximation error, we embed the parametric model class:

{\mathcal{{G}}}:=\{g(\cdot,{\theta})\mid{\theta}\in\Theta\}.

(6)

into an RKHS. Assume that
g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$})\in{\mathcal{{H}}}_{\Theta}
, where
{\mathcal{{H}}}_{\Theta}
is an RKHS of real-valued functions over
\Theta
. For instance, if the model is simply linear with respect to the parameters, i.e.,
g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\theta})={\theta}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})
, for some fixed feature map
\phi:{\mathcal{{X}}}\to\Theta
, then
g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},\cdot)
lies in the RKHS of the linear kernel
k_{\Theta}({\theta},{\theta}^{\prime})={\theta}\cdot{\theta}^{\prime}
, for
{\theta},{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta
. This is the case for linear regression and neural networks in the infinite-width limit
(
13
)
. More generally, if
g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\theta})
is at least
s
times differentiable with respect to
{\theta}\in\Theta
, for
s\in\mathbb{{N}}
such that
s\geq\nu+\frac{M}{2}
, with square-integrable derivatives over
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{{R}}^{M}
, then
g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$})
can be shown to lie in the RKHS of a Matérn kernel
k_{\Theta}
with smoothness parameter
\nu>0

(
25
)
under mild assumptions on the domain and the parameter space. Having a kernel
k_{\Theta}
and the associated RKHS
{\mathcal{{H}}}_{\Theta}
of functions over
\Theta
, we can use the latter as a feature space to construct an RKHS over
{\mathcal{{X}}}
which contains
{\mathcal{{G}}}
. An application of a classic RKHS result
33
then yields the following construction.

4
On the cumulative regret of likelihood-free Bayesian optimization

In this section, we aim to analyze the optimization performance of LFBO algorithms
(
30
)
. In their general setting, an algorithm learns an approximation to the acquisition function
g_{t}\approx a_{t}
by minimizing a loss function over utility data
{\mathcal{{D}}}_{t}^{u}:=\{({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i},z_{t,i})\}_{i=1}^{t}
, where
z_{t,i}=u_{t}(y_{i})
, for
i\leq t
and
t\in\mathbb{{N}}
. The next data point is selected by optimizing over the learned model:

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}}g_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}),\qquad t\geq 1,

(8)

where
g_{t}:=g(\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$},{\theta}_{t})
, and
{\theta}_{t}
represents the empirical loss minimizer (
1
). An observation is collected
y_{t}=f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})+\epsilon_{t}
, where
\epsilon_{t}
is observation noise, and the process repeats for up to
T\geq 1
iterations.

The goal of the algorithm is to estimate the global optimum of
f
(
3
). Optimization performance can be characterized by analyzing the algorithm’s regret. The instant regret of an algorithm quantifies how much is lost in performance for selecting
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}
when the global optimum is at
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}
:

r_{t}:=f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})-f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}),\qquad t\in\mathbb{{N}}.

(9)

The cumulative regret
{{R}}_{T}:=\sum_{t\leq T}r_{t}
is typically the target performance metric, as it captures how often the algorithm makes sub-optimal choices. We also have an upper bound on the simple regret with it as
\min_{t\leq T}r_{t}\leq\frac{{{R}}_{T}}{T}
. Therefore, if
{{R}}_{T}
grows at most sub-linearly,
r_{t}
eventually vanishes.

4.1
Randomized policies

A deterministic acquisition learner does not, by itself, provide exploration. Once the learned model is optimized greedily, any uncertainty not encoded in the trained acquisition target or in the training procedure is lost. For example, deterministic BORE-type classifiers can fail to explore beyond the region of currently positive training labels
(
20
)
. Hence, introducing a certain amount of randomness in the training of the model is beneficial. We, therefore, consider randomized models:

{\theta}_{t}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{{\theta}\in\Theta}\frac{\lambda_{t}}{2}\lVert g_{\theta}-g_{t,0}\rVert_{\mathcal{{G}}}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\ell(g_{\theta}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}),y_{i}),\qquad{\theta}_{t,0}\sim\Pi_{0},\quad t\geq 1,

(10)

for a non-decreasing, real-valued, positive sequence
(\lambda_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{{N}}}
. We then set
g_{t}:=g(\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$},{\theta}_{t})
, for
t\geq 1
, with
g_{0}:=g_{0,0}
. Thus, we are penalizing large deviations from the random initialization, according to the pseudometric defined by the RKHS norm in
\Theta
:

\rho({\theta},{\theta}^{\prime}):=\lVert g_{\theta}-g_{{\theta}^{\prime}}\rVert_{\mathcal{{G}}}=\lVert k_{\Theta}(\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$},{\theta})-k_{\Theta}(\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$},{\theta}^{\prime})\rVert_{{\mathcal{{H}}}_{\Theta}}=\sqrt{k_{\Theta}({\theta},{\theta})-2k_{\Theta}({\theta},{\theta}^{\prime})+k_{\Theta}({\theta}^{\prime},{\theta}^{\prime})},

(11)

The next data point
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t+1}
is located at the maximizer of the randomized model, for
t\in\{1,\dotsc,T\}
. When the amount of data is small, the strength of the regularization then prevents the model from overfitting, staying close to the random initialization
g_{t,0}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})
at points
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}
away from the data. As the algorithm progresses, this promotes exploration of the design space.

We highlight that most modern machine learning frameworks implement their main model building blocks with randomly initialized parameters, e.g., PyTorch
(
22
)
, besides providing options for
\ell_{2}
-based regularization with their optimizers, such as weight decay. In addition, as no model is trained for an infinite amount of time, early stopping provides an implicit level of regularization that prevents models from going too far from their initialization
(
10
;
13
)
. However, we make explicit the dependence on the initialization here for our analysis, which is essential to ensure sufficient exploration. This construction also agrees with recent results on explicit regularization for neural networks under noisy data
(
4
)
.

A2
Prior.

There exist constants

such that, for any

\mathbb{P}\left[\rho({\theta}_{\star},{\theta}_{0})\leq\alpha\right]\geq c_{\star}\alpha^{d_{\mathrm{eff}}},\quad\forall\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{0}),

In practice, the lower-tail condition is a local support requirement in the geometry induced by the model class, while the upper-tail condition controls unusually poor initializations. For example, for
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{{R}}^{M}

{\theta}\mapsto g(\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$},{\theta})

4.2
Generalization error bounds

For our theoretical analysis, we need generalization error bounds for the model’s predictions. The main advantage of the RKHS-based framework is that it allows us to quantify error bounds with respect to the RKHS minimizer of the regularized loss:

\hat{g}_{t}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{g\in{\mathcal{{H}}}_{\mathcal{{G}}}}L_{t}(g),\qquad t\geq 1,

6
Discussion and limitations

We have developed a kernel-based framework for analyzing nonlinear parametric models trained under adaptive data collection. The main technical idea is to use a kernel over the parameters space to induce an RKHS geometry over the model class, which allows for regularized nonlinear estimators to be controlled through concentration arguments in Hilbert spaces. As a result, we obtain GP-style predictive-variance bounds for models that are not themselves Gaussian processes or kernel machines, and that provides a way to reuse information-gain tools in settings involving finite-dimensional nonlinear models without requiring their linearization. In the randomized-policy setting, the framework also clarifies how random initialization and explicit regularization can jointly induce exploration: favorable initializations occur with a rate controlled by the prior small-ball probability, while regularization ensures that such initializations are not overwhelmed by noise.

Our regret analysis instantiates this framework for finite-domain approximate Thompson sampling. To the best of our knowledge, this provides one of the first cumulative regret guarantees for nonlinear finite-dimensional randomized models in an LFBO-related setting without replacing the learned model by a GP surrogate. The proof identifies a concrete stability-recurrence tradeoff: increasing
\lambda_{t}
stabilizes the trained model around its initialization, while also reducing the probability of drawing an initialization sufficiently close to the target model. The resulting bound remains sublinear, with an additional logarithmic factor depending on the effective small-ball dimension
{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}
. This dimension is expressed in the RKHS-induced pseudometric, rather than being fixed a priori as the ambient parameter dimension, leaving room for sharper problem-dependent characterizations.

Several extensions remain open. The formal regret theorem is currently stated for the finite-domain, realizable, unique-optimum approximate Thompson sampling setting. These assumptions keep the proof focused on the main new difficulty: controlling the approximation error of nonlinear randomized models under adaptive sampling. A full regret analysis for general LFBO acquisition functions would additionally require acquisition-specific alignment arguments, ensuring that the maximizers of learned utility-based targets eventually agree with the maximizers of the objective. This is particularly delicate for improvement-based or classification-based LFBO methods, where changing thresholds can relabel past data and affect predictability. Extending the analysis to continuous domains would also require replacing exact visits to
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}
with local variance-reduction arguments over neighborhoods of the optimum. We view these as natural next steps enabled by the general RKHS-based approximation bounds developed here. More broadly, the framework suggests a path toward principled non-GP-based BO, active learning, and adaptive experimental design algorithms: once a nonlinear model class can be embedded into a suitable RKHS and its induced kernel compared to a tractable reference kernel, adaptive-sampling guarantees can be derived without requiring the deployed model to be a Gaussian process or kernel machine.

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\lambda_{t}(g_{\star}-g_{t,0})\right\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}+\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{t}\dot{\ell}_{y_{i}}(g_{\star}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}))\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\right\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}\lVert g_{\star}-g_{t,0}\rVert_{\mathcal{{G}}}+\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{t}\dot{\ell}_{y_{i}}(g_{\star}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}))\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\right\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}},

(42)

where the last inequality follows from

{{H}}_{t}\succeq\lambda_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}
.

Define

\varepsilon_{i}:=\dot{\ell}_{y_{i}}(g_{\star}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})).

(43)

By assumption,
\varepsilon_{i}
is conditionally
\sigma_{\ell}^{2}
-sub-Gaussian
given
{\mathfrak{{F}}}_{i-1}
, and
\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})
is predictable. Since

\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{t}
, we have

{{H}}_{t}\succeq\lambda_{0}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\otimes\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}).

(44)

Therefore,

\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{i}\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\right\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}\leq\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{i}\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\right\rVert_{\left(\lambda_{0}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\otimes\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\right)^{-1}}.

(45)

Applying
A.1
with the fixed operator

V=\lambda_{0}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}
and

h_{i}=\sqrt{\alpha_{\ell}}\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})
yields, with probability at
least
1-\delta
, simultaneously for all
t\in\mathbb{{N}}
,

\left\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{t}\varepsilon_{i}\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\right\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}\leq\sigma_{\ell}\sqrt{2\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\det({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\lambda_{0}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}_{t}^{\mathcal{{G}}})^{1/2}}{\delta}\right)}.

(46)

Thus,

\lVert\widehat{g}_{t}-g_{\star}\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}}\leq\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta),

(47)

where

\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta)=\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}\lVert g_{\star}-g_{t,0}\rVert_{\mathcal{{G}}}+\sigma_{\ell}\sqrt{2\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\det({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\lambda_{0}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}_{t}^{\mathcal{{G}}})^{1/2}}{\delta}\right)}.

(48)

For any
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}
, by the reproducing property and
Cauchy-Schwarz,

\lvert\widehat{g}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-g_{\star}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rvert=\lvert\left\langle\widehat{g}_{t}-g_{\star},\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{{G}}}\rvert\leq\lVert\widehat{g}_{t}-g_{\star}\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}}\lVert\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}.

(49)

Therefore,

\lvert\widehat{g}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-g_{\star}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rvert\leq\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta)\lVert\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}.

(50)

It remains to pass from the RKHS minimizer
\widehat{g}_{t}
to the
deployed parametric model
g_{t}
. Since

{\theta}_{t}
minimizes
Equation 10
over the
parametric class and
g_{\star}=g(\makebox[4.73611pt]{$\cdot$},{\theta}_{\star})

belongs to this class, we have

L_{t}(g_{t})\leq L_{t}(g_{\star}).

(51)

Moreover, since
\widehat{g}_{t}
minimizes
L_{t}
over

{\mathcal{{H}}}_{\mathcal{{G}}}
, the strong convexity lower bound gives

\frac{1}{2}\lVert g_{t}-\widehat{g}_{t}\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}}^{2}\leq L_{t}(g_{t})-L_{t}(\widehat{g}_{t}).

(52)

Combining the last two displays,

\frac{1}{2}\lVert g_{t}-\widehat{g}_{t}\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}}^{2}\leq L_{t}(g_{\star})-L_{t}(\widehat{g}_{t}).

(53)

Applying the upper loss-geometry bound at
g_{\star}
, we also have

L_{t}(g_{\star})-L_{t}(\widehat{g}_{t})\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\nabla L_{t}(g_{\star})\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}^{2}.

(54)

Therefore,

\lVert g_{t}-\widehat{g}_{t}\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}}\leq\lVert\nabla L_{t}(g_{\star})\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}.

(55)

On the concentration event derived above,

\lVert\nabla L_{t}(g_{\star})\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}\leq\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta),

(56)

and hence

\lVert g_{t}-\widehat{g}_{t}\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}}\leq\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta).

(57)

For any
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}
, the reproducing property and
Cauchy–Schwarz give

\lvert g_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-\widehat{g}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rvert\leq\lVert g_{t}-\widehat{g}_{t}\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}}\lVert\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}\leq\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta)\lVert\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}.

(58)

The RKHS-minimizer part of the proof already gives

\lvert\widehat{g}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-g_{\star}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rvert\leq\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta)\lVert\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}.

(59)

Thus, by the triangle inequality,

\lvert g_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-g_{\star}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rvert\leq 2\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta)\lVert\phi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rVert_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}},\qquad\forall{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{{X}}},\ \forall t\geq 0.

(60)

This proves the claim.
∎

B.3
Proof of
4.2

Proof.

Let

{{H}}_{t}=\lambda_{t}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\otimes\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}),

(61)

where
\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}):=k_{\mathcal{{G}}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})
. Since
\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{t}
, we have

{{H}}_{t}\succeq\lambda_{0}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\otimes\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}).

(62)

Therefore,

{{H}}_{t}^{-1}\preceq\left(\lambda_{0}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\otimes\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\right)^{-1}.

(63)

It follows that

\|\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\|_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\left\langle\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}),\left(\lambda_{0}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\otimes\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})\right)^{-1}\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{{G}}}.

(64)

By Woodbury’s identity,

\|\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\|_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\lambda_{0}^{-1}\left[k_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}_{{\mathcal{{G}}},t}^{\top}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}^{\mathcal{{G}}}_{t}+\lambda_{0}\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}_{{\mathcal{{G}}},t}\right],

(65)

where

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}_{{\mathcal{{G}}},t}:=[k_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i})]_{i=1}^{t}
and

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}^{\mathcal{{G}}}_{t}:=[k_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{t}
.

We now compare the posterior variances induced by
k_{\mathcal{{G}}}
and
k
. Let

r:=\lambda_{0}\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}
. For any positive-semidefinite kernel

\widetilde{k}
, the GP posterior variance at
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}
with noisy observations at

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}
and ridge
r
is the Schur complement

\sigma_{\widetilde{k},t}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})=\widetilde{k}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\widetilde{k}}}}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})^{\top}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{\widetilde{K}}}}}_{t}+r{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\widetilde{k}}}}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}).

(66)

Since
k-k_{\mathcal{{G}}}
is positive semidefinite, the joint covariance matrix over

({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})
induced by
k
dominates the
corresponding joint covariance matrix induced by
k_{\mathcal{{G}}}
. Hence the conditional variance,
given by the Schur complement, is also larger:

\sigma_{k_{\mathcal{{G}}},t}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\leq\sigma_{k,t}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}).

(67)

It remains to compare the log-determinant term. Since
k\succeq k_{\mathcal{{G}}}
and
\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{t}
,

\alpha_{\ell}\lambda_{t}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}^{\mathcal{{G}}}_{t}\preceq\alpha_{\ell}\lambda_{0}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}_{t}.

(68)

By monotonicity of the determinant over positive-semidefinite matrices,

\log\det\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\lambda_{t}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}^{\mathcal{{G}}}_{t}\right)\leq\log\det\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\lambda_{0}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}_{t}\right).

(69)

Combining this with
Theorem 4.1
, on the same event of probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle|g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\theta}_{t})-g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\theta}^{\star})|

\displaystyle\leq 2\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta)\|\phi_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\|_{{{H}}_{t}^{-1}}

(70)

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda_{0}^{-1/2}\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta)\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})

(71)

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta_{t}(\delta)\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}),

(72)

where

\beta_{t}(\delta)=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\lambda_{0}}}\|g^{\star}-g_{t,0}\|_{\mathcal{{G}}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{\ell}^{2}}{\alpha_{\ell}\lambda_{0}}\log\left(\frac{\det({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\lambda_{0}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}_{t})^{1/2}}{\delta}\right)}.

(73)

This proves the claim.
∎

B.4
Proof of
4.3

Proof.

Let

{\mathcal{{E}}}_{t}:=\left\{\rho({\theta}^{\star},{\theta}_{t,0})\leq\lambda_{t}^{-1/2}\right\}.

(74)

By
A2
, for all sufficiently large
t
,

\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathcal{{E}}}_{t}\right]\geq c_{\star}\lambda_{t}^{-{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}/2}.

(75)

Since
{\theta}_{t,0}\sim\Pi_{0}
is sampled independently of the past at round
t
, the same lower bound holds conditionally on the past before drawing
{\theta}_{t,0}
.

We first show that, on the high-probability event of
Theorem 4.1
,
{\mathcal{{E}}}_{t}
eventually implies
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}
. On
{\mathcal{{E}}}_{t}
, the initialization term in
\widehat{\beta}_{t}(\delta)
satisfies:

\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}\|g^{\star}-g_{t,0}\|_{\mathcal{{G}}}\leq 1.

(76)

Moreover, since
{\mathcal{{X}}}
is finite, the log-determinant term in Theorem 4.1 is
O(\sqrt{\log t})
. Since:

H_{t}\succeq\lambda_{t}I,

(77)

we have:

\|\varphi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\|_{H_{t}^{-1}}\leq\frac{\|\varphi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\|_{\mathcal{{G}}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}}\leq\frac{\kappa_{\mathcal{{G}}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}},\qquad\kappa_{\mathcal{{G}}}^{2}:=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}}k_{\mathcal{{G}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})<\infty.

(78)

Therefore, on
{\mathcal{{E}}}_{t}
,
\widehat{\beta}_{t}
is
{\mathcal{{O}}}(\sqrt{\log t})
(recall
1
), and:

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}}|g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\theta}_{t})-g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\theta}^{\star})|=O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log t}{\lambda_{t}}}\right).

(79)

Because
\lambda_{t}\in\omega(\log t)
, the right-hand side converges to zero. Since
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}
is the unique maximizer of
f=g(\cdot,{\theta}^{\star})
over the finite set
{\mathcal{{X}}}
, the gap

\Delta_{\star}:=f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})-\max_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\neq{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}}f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})

(80)

is strictly positive. Hence, for all sufficiently large
t
,

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}}|g({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\theta}_{t})-f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})|<\Delta_{\star}/2,

(81)

which implies that the maximizer of
g(\cdot,{\theta}_{t})
is
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}
. Thus, eventually,

{\mathcal{{E}}}_{t}\subseteq\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}\}.

(82)

Consequently,

\mathbb{P}\left[{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}\mid{\mathcal{{D}}}_{t}\right]\geq c_{\star}\lambda_{t}^{-{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}/2}

(83)

for all sufficiently large
t
.

Let

N_{t}^{\star}:=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{I}[{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}={\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}].

(84)

Since

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{t}^{-{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}/2}=\infty,

(85)

an application of the second Borel-Cantelli lemma
(
9
, Thm. 4.5.5)
gives

N_{t}^{\star}=\Omega\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lambda_{i}^{-{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}/2}\right)\qquad\text{a.s.}

(86)

It remains to translate visits to
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}
into predictive-variance decay. Since
4.2
uses the fixed-ridge GP variance

\sigma_{t}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})=k({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})^{\top}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}_{t}+\lambda_{0}\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{k}}}_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}),

(87)

retaining only the
N_{t}^{\star}
observations collected at
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}
can only increase the posterior variance. Writing
\kappa_{\star}^{2}:=k({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})>0
, the variance at
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}
based only on these repeated observations is

\kappa_{\star}^{2}-\kappa_{\star}^{4}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}}^{\top}\left(\kappa_{\star}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}}^{\top}+\lambda_{0}\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}}\geq\sigma_{t}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star}),

(88)

where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}}\in\mathbb{{R}}^{N_{t}^{\star}}
. By the Sherman-Morrison identity, this equals

\frac{\kappa_{\star}^{2}\lambda_{0}\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}}{\lambda_{0}\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}+\kappa_{\star}^{2}N_{t}^{\star}}={\mathcal{{O}}}\left(\frac{1}{N_{t}^{\star}}\right).

(89)

Therefore,

\sigma_{t}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})={\mathcal{{O}}}\left(\left[\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lambda_{i}^{-{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}/2}\right]^{-1}\right).

(90)

If
\lambda_{t}\asymp\log^{q}t
, with
q>1
, then

\sum_{i=2}^{t}\lambda_{i}^{-{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}/2}\asymp\sum_{i=2}^{t}(\log i)^{-q{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}/2}\asymp\frac{t}{(\log t)^{q{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}/2}}.

(91)

Hence,

\sigma_{t}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})={\mathcal{{O}}}\left(\frac{(\log t)^{q{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}/2}}{t}\right),

(92)

as claimed.
∎

B.5
Proof of
Theorem 4.4

Proof.

Our analysis will condition on the events:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{{E}}}_{\mathrm{error}}(\delta)

\displaystyle:=\{\forall t\geq 1,\quad\lvert f({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-g_{t}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})\rvert\leq\beta_{t}(\delta)\sigma_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}),\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\in{\mathcal{{X}}}\}

(93)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{{E}}}_{\mathrm{init}}(\delta)

\displaystyle:=\{\forall t\geq 0,\quad\rho({\theta}_{\star},{\theta}_{t,0})\leq b_{t}(\delta)\},

(94)

where setting
b_{t}(\delta):=\psi_{0}\left(\log\frac{\pi^{2}t^{2}}{6\delta}\right)
(as in
A2
) yields
\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathcal{{E}}}_{\mathrm{init}}(\delta)\right]\geq 1-\delta
, and, according to
4.2
,
\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathcal{{E}}}_{\mathrm{error}}(\delta)\right]\geq 1-\delta
. Then, by an union bound,

\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathcal{{E}}}_{\mathrm{init}}(\delta)\cap{\mathcal{{E}}}_{\mathrm{error}}(\delta)\right]\geq 1-2\delta.

(95)

Conditioning on
{\mathcal{{E}}}_{\mathrm{error}}(\delta)
, as discussed in
Section 4.3
, it holds that:

r_{t}\leq\beta_{t-1}(\delta)(\sigma_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})+\sigma_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})),\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{{N}}.

(96)

For
\beta_{t}
, on
{\mathcal{{E}}}_{\mathrm{init}}(\delta)
, we have that:

\begin{split}\beta_{t}(\delta)&\leq\beta_{t}^{\star}(\delta):=b_{t}(\delta)\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\lambda_{0}}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{\ell}^{2}}{\alpha_{\ell}\lambda_{0}}\log(\det({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\alpha_{\ell}\lambda_{0}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}_{t})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}/\delta)},\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{{N}},\end{split}

(97)

which is non-decreasing, so that:

\beta_{t}^{\star}(\delta)\in{\mathcal{{O}}}(\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}(\log t)^{\zeta}+\sqrt{\lvert{\mathcal{{X}}}\rvert\log t})={\mathcal{{O}}}((\log t)^{\zeta+q/2}+\sqrt{\lvert{\mathcal{{X}}}\rvert\log t}),

as discussed in
1
. On
{\mathcal{{E}}}_{\mathrm{error}}(\delta)\cap{\mathcal{{E}}}_{\mathrm{init}}(\delta)
, the cumulative regret is then bounded by:

\begin{split}{{R}}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}&\leq\sum_{t=1}\beta_{t-1}(\delta)(\sigma_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})+\sigma_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}))\\
&\leq\beta_{T}^{\star}(\delta)\sum_{t=1}(\sigma_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})+\sigma_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}))\\
&\leq\beta_{T}^{\star}(\delta)\sqrt{T\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})\right)},\end{split}

(98)

where an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields the last line.
Considering the sum of
\sigma_{t-1}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})
, for large
T
, we have that
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{t}
is
{\mathcal{{O}}}(\log T)
, so that,
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\log^{s}t}{t}
is
{\mathcal{{O}}}(\log^{s+1}T)
, for any
s>0
. Now applying
4.3
with
\lambda_{t}\asymp\log^{q}t
, we obtain:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{\star})\in{\mathcal{{O}}}\left((\log T)^{1+q{d_{\mathrm{eff}}}/2}\right).

(99)

Regarding the sum of
\sigma_{t-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})
, using the information gain bound for the sum of predictive variances
(
31
;
5
;
8
)
yields:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t-1}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t})\leq c_{\sigma}\log\det({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{I}}}}+\lambda_{0}\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{{K}}}}_{t})\in{\mathcal{{O}}}(\lvert{\mathcal{{X}}}\rvert\log T),

following the argument in
1
, which is dominated by (
99
). The result in
Theorem 4.4
then follows after dropping the lower order terms, simplifying the exponent with the fact that
q>1
, and rescaling
\delta
with
\delta/2
in the definition of the conditioning events.
∎
