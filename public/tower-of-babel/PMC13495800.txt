Gut–Brain Axis Modulation by Short‐Chain Fatty Acids Exerts Disease‐Modifying Effects in a Murine Model of Drug‐Resistant Epilepsy
Authors: Akash A Bera, Rossella Di Sapia, Greta Volpedo, Sneha Anand, Eray Sahin, Pasquale Baldassarre, Francesca Buffelli, Andrea Petretto, Chiara Lavarello, James D Mills, Gabriele Cattaneo, Ilaria Craparotta, Eleonora Aronica, Antonella Riva, Pasquale Striano, Annamaria Vezzani, Teresa Ravizza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDrug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE) remains a clinical challenge, as therapies modifying disease trajectory are lacking. Increasing evidence implicates gut microbiota dysbiosis in epilepsy pathophysiology, with short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) emerging as key microbial metabolites with neuroprotective and anti‐inflammatory properties. Clinical studies show that people with DRE exhibit gut microbiota alterations that may impair fecal SCFAs production. Here, we investigated whether supplementation of SCFAs confers disease‐modifying effects in a preclinical model of DRE.MethodsAdult male mice were subjected to status epilepticus (SE) and subsequently treated with a balanced mixture of acetate, propionate, and butyrate, or vehicle. Seizure frequency and temporal progression were monitored for 70 days by electroencephalography (EEG). At the study end point, cognitive performance, brain and gut histopathology, and neuroinflammation were assessed, together with metabolomic profiling of feces and blood. Brain SCFA levels and receptor expression were also analyzed in mice and in brain tissue from individuals with DRE.ResultsSCFA supplementation reduced the proportion of mice exhibiting a progressive phenotype and decreased the overall progression index (PI) 3‐fold, without significantly altering overall daily seizure frequency. Treatment reduced seizure clustering, improved cognitive deficits, restored hippocampal and intestinal alterations, and partially normalized cerebral SCFAs levels. Metabolomic profiling in epileptic mice and analysis of human epilepsy brain tissue support a mechanistic contribution of gut‐brain axis dysfunction to disease progression.InterpretationThese findings identify SCFAs supplementation as a therapeutic strategy capable of modifying disease trajectory in experimental DRE, with clear translational relevance. ANN NEUROL 2026;100:613–627

Drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE) remains a major clinical challenge because approximately one‐third of individuals with epilepsy fail to achieve sustained seizure freedom with trials of 2 tolerated and appropriately chosen antiseizure medications (ASMs). 1 Current ASMs act predominantly as symptomatic agents, suppressing ictal events without addressing the underlying biological mechanisms that sustain epileptogenesis and chronic disease progression. To date, no therapies have demonstrated true disease‐modifying effects capable of altering the long‐term trajectory of epilepsy beyond transient seizure suppression. Such interventions would be expected not only to limit seizure progression but also to mitigate neuropathology, and associated cognitive comorbidities.

Growing preclinical and clinical evidence indicates that the gut microbiota is altered in DRE, and interventions such as antibiotic, ketogenic diets, or pre‐/probiotic supplementation can remodel gut microbiota composition and have been shown to reduce seizures in animal models and pilot clinical studies. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Although the mechanisms linking gut microbiota to brain function remain under investigation, potential mediators include vagal and immune signaling, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, and microbiota‐derived metabolites. 10

Among microbial metabolites, short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs)– acetate, propionate, and butyrate – produced by bacterial fermentation of dietary fibers, are particularly notable for their pleiotropic roles in modulating energy metabolism, neurotransmission, blood–brain barrier integrity, glial function, and neuroinflammation. 11 , 12 SCFAs act via G protein‐coupled free fatty acid receptors expressed in peripheral organs and the central nervous system (CNS), 11 or by inhibiting histone deacetylases, thereby modulating gene expression programs implicated in epileptogenesis. 13 Their ability to enter systemic circulation and reach the brain, together with their influence on brain processes disrupted in epilepsy, 14 positions them as attractive candidates for modulating epileptogenesis and managing drug‐resistant seizures.

Although SCFAs have been studied in neurodevelopmental, inflammatory, and neurodegenerative disease models, 15 , 16 their therapeutic potential in epilepsy — particularly in drug‐resistant forms — remains largely unexplored. 12 , 17 , 18 Previous preclinical studies have focused on individual SCFAs in acute seizure models, 19 , 20 or using preventive paradigm in the kindling model, 21 but no studies to date have evaluated chronic post‐insult supplementation with a physiologically relevant SCFA mixture in models that recapitulate human epilepsy progression. Addressing this knowledge gap is critical, given the multimodal neurometabolic effects of SCFAs.

To this end, we tested whether direct chronic supplementation with a balanced SCFA mixture modulates seizure progression, cognitive outcomes, and gut–brain integrity in a validated mouse model of acquired DRE, 22 , 23 mimicking temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE). In this model, epilepsy is characterized by drug‐resistant seizures, with a subset of mice showing progressive seizures over time, and associated cognitive deficits, along with structural and inflammatory changes in the brain and the gut. 5 , 22 , 24 These alterations are paralleled by gut dysbiosis and a reduced abundance of SCFA‐producing bacteria. 5

Our findings demonstrate that SCFA supplementation effectively attenuates seizure progression, mitigates cognitive deficits, and partially restores cerebral SCFA profiles, while normalizing histopathology in both the brain and gut. Metabolomic profiling of feces and blood, together with analysis of SCFAs in human epilepsy brain tissue, further supports a multi‐level modulatory effect of SCFAs on the gut–brain axis. These findings position SCFAs as promising disease‐modifying strategy for DRE, particularly in progressive forms, 25 with strong potential for rapid clinical translation.

Methods

Experimental Model of Epilepsy

The experimental procedures of this study received prior approval by the intramural ethical committee and the national authority (Italian Ministry of Health; Authorization number: 23/2023‐PR).

Animal housing and further details on ethical approval are reported in the Supplementary Information.

Epilepsy arises following status epilepticus (SE) induced by intra‐amygdala kainate (KA) in C57BL6N male young adult mice (see Supplementary Information), as described in detail. 22 , 24 Each mouse was electroencephalography (EEG) recorded (24/7) starting from SE induction for 70 days after epilepsy onset, in order to reach the chronic epilepsy stage. Epilepsy onset was defined by the occurrence of 2 spontaneous seizures after 48 hours from the end of SE, to exclude acute symptomatic seizures.

In each mouse, we measured the total number of EEG seizures during the recording period, their daily frequency and average duration (total time spent in seizures divided by total number of seizures). Based on the daily seizure counts, we calculated the progression index (PI), defined as the ratio of the total number of seizures occurring during days 36 to 70 to those recorded during days 1 to 35. We also determined the proportion of mice exhibiting disease progression (PI > 1) and the occurrence of seizure clusters (≥3 seizures/24 hours), 26 both considered indices of epilepsy severity. Seizure parameters were compared within the same EEG recording period between SCFA‐ and vehicle‐treated mice.

We recently described gut dysbiosis in this murine model of DRE. 5 Building on these findings, the extracted data showed a significantly reduced abundance of taxa associated with SCFA production in epileptic compared with sham mice (Supplementary Fig S1). Accordingly, we designed a treatment protocol to increase SCFAs availability during disease development.

Experimental Protocol

We treated mice at the end of SE (5 hours after KA injection) with a balanced mixture of SCFAs (40 mM Na butyrate, 26 mM propionate, and 67.5 mM acetate) dissolved in drinking water, or 133 mM NaCl as vehicle, until completion of the experiment (Supplementary Fig S2A). This SCFAs mixture was shown to improve neuropathology and neurological deficits in mouse models of stroke and neurotrauma. 27 , 28

Water was changed in the bottles every 3 days. During the first 4 days post‐SE, the SCFAs mixture or vehicle were additionally administered by gavage twice/daily to compensate for the decreased water intake during the recovery time after SE. Sham mice were similarly treated with SCFAs mixture or vehicle (n = 8/group). Water intake was not affected by the treatment in both mice developing epilepsy and sham mice (Supplementary Fig S2B).

SE developed similarly in mice later supplemented with SCFAs and vehicle (Supplementary Fig S2C–F). At termination of EEG monitoring (70 days after epilepsy onset in each mouse), mice were disconnected from EEG set up and tested in the open field and Barnes Maze under SCFAs or vehicle administration. At completion of behavioral tests, the mice were euthanized, and blood serum (from the retromandibular plexus), gut, brain, and feces were collected and stored for further histological or molecular analysis (see Supplementary Information).

Animal Randomization, Outcomes, and Statistical Analysis

Mice were randomly allocated to the vehicle or SCFAs group based on SE onset and duration, and the individual mouse represented the experimental unit. Blinding was applied to treatment administration, outcome assessment, and data analysis. Sample size was a priori determined based on previous experience with this TLE model. 5 , 22 , 24

Because anti‐inflammatory and anti‐oxidant interventions attenuate disease progression and cognitive impairment in TLE models, 22 , 29 , 30 our primary end points were defined a priori as: (i) attenuation of seizure progression during the 70‐day EEG monitoring, quantified by the PI, and the incidence of progression in each cohort; and (ii) improvement in cognitive performance. Secondary end points included mitigation of brain and gut pathology.

Statistical analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA) for Windows using raw data. The choice between parametric or nonparametric test depended on passing the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. Differences were considered statistically significant for p ≤ 0.05. Animal exclusion from statistical analysis and the statistical tests used are reported in the figure legends.

Results

Seizure Progression and Cognitive Assessment

Consistent with the natural history of this acquired epilepsy model 22 and other similar models, 29 , 30 approximately 40% of vehicle‐treated mice exhibited a progressive increase in seizures over the 70‐day monitoring period. In the SCFA‐treated group, only 1 of 10 mice exhibited seizure progression, compared with 5 of 12 vehicle‐treated mice; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig 1A; p = 0.16, Fisher's exact test; Supplementary Fig S3A–D). Nevertheless, the overall PI was reduced 3‐fold in SCFA‐treated mice relative to vehicle‐treated controls (p < 0.05, Mann–Whitney U test; Fig 1B).

Seizure clusters (≥3 seizures/24 hours) were also evaluated. The overall proportion of mice experiencing clusters during the 70‐day period was comparable between groups (vehicle = 9/12 and SCFA = 8/10). However, de novo clusters were observed during days 36 to 70 in vehicle‐treated mice but not in the SCFA‐treated mice (p < 0.05, Chi‐square test; Fig 1C).

SCFA treatment did not significantly modify the time to epilepsy onset (vehicle = 7.1 ± 1.39 and SCFA = 8.3 ± 1.29 days), the overall daily seizure frequency during the 70‐day period (vehicle = 1.7 ± 0.72 and SCFA = 2.1 ± 0.84; Supplementary Fig S3E) or mean seizure duration (vehicle = 39.8 ± 3.1 seconds and SCFA = 39.2 ± 2.7 seconds), with representative seizure traces shown in Figure 1D. Including mice with and without seizure progression, daily seizure frequency decreased by approximately 30% from the acute (days 1–35) to the chronic (days 36–70) phase of epilepsy in SCFA‐treated mice compared with 24% in vehicle‐treated mice (p < 0.05 by Mann–Whitney U one tail test; Supplementary Fig S3E).

At the end of EEG recording, mice were tested in the Barnes maze to assess hippocampal‐dependent spatial memory (Fig 1E, F and Supplementary Fig S4A). No motor seizures occurred during behavioral testing. Open‐field analysis revealed no difference in total distance traveled or mean velocity between epileptic and sham mice, irrespective of SCFA treatment (Supplementary Fig S4B, C).

During the learning phase, epileptic mice treated with vehicle exhibited longer primary latencies to locate the escape hole compared with sham controls (p < 0.05; p < 0.01; mixed‐effects model followed by Tukey's post hoc test). Epileptic mice treated with SCFAs displayed a similar learning deficit (see Supplementary Fig S4A).

Memory was assessed in a probe trial conducted 24 hours after the final training session (Fig 1E). Two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed significant main effects of disease (F = 9.456, p = 0.0041) and treatment (F = 4.656, p = 0.0381) on latency to reach the target hole (Fig 1E). Vehicle‐treated epileptic mice required significantly more time to locate the escape hole compared with sham controls (p = 0.0094, Fisher's post hoc test), confirming memory impairment. SCFA supplementation attenuated this deficit in epileptic mice (p = 0.0326 vs vehicle‐treated epileptic mice) with performance comparable to sham mice (Fig 1E).

Memory retention was assessed by comparing latency to locate the escape hole on the last training day (day 5) with the probe trial. In vehicle‐treated epileptic mice, latency did not change between day 5 and the probe trial (74.9 ± 9.4 vs 84.6 ± 12.3 seconds). In contrast, SCFA‐treated epileptic mice exhibited an average 45% reduction in latency from day 5 to probe trial (90.9 ± 8.1 vs 51.1 ± 12.1 seconds; p < 0.05, Mann–Whitney U test; Fig 1F).

Brain and Serum Lipid Metabolite Profiling

We assessed the effects of chronic SCFA supplementation on related lipid metabolites in the brain and serum of sham and epileptic mice in cortical tissue.

Two‐way ANOVA showed disease × treatment interaction on cortical brain levels of acetic acid (F = 41.10, p < 0.0001; Fig 2A) and 2‐methyl butyric acid (F = 6.793, p = 0.0136; Fig 2B). Epileptic mice treated with vehicle exhibited significant reductions in both acetic (p < 0.0001) and 2‐methylbutyric acid (p = 0.0099) compared with respective sham controls (Fisher's post hoc test). SCFA supplementation restored acetic acid levels (p < 0.0001 vs vehicle‐treated epileptic mice) but did not affect 2‐methylbutyric acid (p = 0.6538; Fig 2A, B).

Significant disease × treatment interactions were also observed for valeric acid (F = 9.842, p = 0.0036; Fig 2C). SCFA treatment did not modify this lipid level in epileptic mice relative to vehicle‐treated epileptic mice, but significantly increased it in sham mice (p = 0.0004; Fisher's post hoc test). Vehicle‐treated epileptic mice did not differ from sham controls for this metabolite.

For propionic acid, 2‐way ANOVA indicated significant main effects of disease (F = 14.67, p = 0.0005) and treatment (F = 19.31, p = 0.0001; Fig 2D). Isovaleric acid (F = 16.58, p = 0.0003; Fig 2E) was affected by treatment only. SCFA supplementation increased propionic and isovaleric acids level in epileptic mice relative to vehicle‐treated epileptic controls (propionic: p = 0.0173; isovaleric: p = 0.0018; Fisher's post hoc test), although vehicle‐treated epileptic mice did not differ from respective sham mice (see Fig 2D, E). Propionic acid was also increased by SCFAs in sham mice versus respective vehicle (p = 0.0204).

Analysis of isobutyric acid revealed a significant effect of disease (F = 16.95, p = 0.0003; Fig 2F). Vehicle‐treated epileptic mice exhibited a nonsignificant trend toward reduction (approximately 65%, p = 0.1249), whereas SCFA‐treated epileptic mice showed a significant decrease compared with respective sham controls (p = 0.0064; Fisher's post hoc test). No significant changes in butyric acid levels were observed across experimental groups (Fig 2G).

Serum SCFAs did not differ significantly across experimental groups (Supplementary Fig S5).

SCFA Receptor Expression in Mice

We assessed whether supplementation with SCFAs was associated with changes in the expression of cognate receptors HCAR2 (GPR109A), FFAR2 (GPR43), and FFAR3 (GPR41) in the hippocampus of epileptic mice. They represent the principal receptors mediating SCFA signaling in both the gut and the brain. 10 , 11 , 31 , 32

Two‐way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of disease on HCAR2 transcript level (F = 3.666, p = 0.0451; Fig 2H). Although HCAR2 mRNA expression did not differ between vehicle‐treated epileptic mice and sham controls, SCFA supplementation significantly increased HCAR2 transcript levels in epileptic mice (p = 0.049 vs sham; Fisher's post hoc test; see Fig 2H). In contrast, FFAR2 and FFAR3 transcript levels were not affected by the disease, or SCFA treatment in sham and epileptic mice (Fig 2I, J).

SCFAs Levels and Receptor Expression in Human Epilepsy Brain Specimens

We analyzed the same panel of metabolites as above in hippocampal tissue from surgically resected specimens of subjects with TLE with hippocampal sclerosis (HS), a neuropathological hallmark of drug‐resistant TLE that is closely recapitulated by our murine model of acquired epilepsy. 22 Hippocampal specimens from TLE subjects without hippocampal sclerosis (no‐HS) were included to evaluate the contribution of structural pathology to lipid metabolite alterations.

All lipid metabolites were detectable in human hippocampal specimens (Fig 3A–G). Acetic acid was the most abundant metabolite, whereas butyric and isobutyric acids were present at lower levels, mirroring the metabolic profile observed in mouse brain tissue (see Fig 2A–G). Comparative analysis revealed a significant reduction in propionic acid levels in TLE‐HS subjects compared to TLE no‐HS (p < 0.01, Mann–Whitney U test; Fig 3D). This difference was independent of seizure burden, as both groups exhibited comparable seizure frequencies (median 5 seizures/month; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

By comparing hippocampal tissue from TLE‐HS subjects versus non‐neurological controls, we found a selective and significant reduction of FFAR2 transcript levels (p < 0.01; Fig 3I), a receptor preferentially activated by propionate, whereas no significant changes were detected for other SCFA receptor subtypes (Fig 3H, J).

Hippocampal Pathology and Gut Integrity Assessment in Epileptic Mice

We analyzed hippocampal cellular alterations involved in epileptogenesis, 14 as well as structural and cellular changes in the gut.

Neuronal Cell Loss and Aberrant Neurogenesis

In the hippocampus ipsilateral to KA‐injected amygdala, 2‐way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of disease on the NeuN‐immunoreactive area in CA1 (F = 5.284, p = 0.0287) and CA3 (F = 7.601, p = 0.0098) pyramidal cell layers, as well as a significant disease × treatment interaction in the dentate gyrus hilus (F = 7.42, p = 0.0108; Fig 4A–C). Vehicle‐treated epileptic mice exhibited marked neuronal loss across all hippocampal subfields compared to respective sham controls (CA1: p = 0.0058; CA3: p = 0.0018; hilus: p = 0.0008; Fisher's post hoc test). SCFA supplementation conferred significant neuroprotection in the CA3 region (p = 0.0288) and hilus (p = 0.0007) relative to vehicle‐treated epileptic mice (Fig 4B, C and Supplementary Fig S6E–L). NeuN immunoreactivity in the CA1 layer did not differ between SCFA‐treated and vehicle‐treated epileptic mice (Fig 4A and Supplementary Fig S6A–D).

We next examined the impact of SCFA treatment on hilar interneuron populations implicated in network hyperexcitability and maladaptive plasticity, focusing on calretinin‐positive interneurons 33 and doublecortin (DCX)‐positive immature neurons. 34

Two‐way ANOVA revealed a significant disease × treatment interaction for calretinin‐positive interneuron number (F = 4.496, p = 0.0421; Fig 4H). Vehicle‐treated epileptic mice showed a significant loss of these interneurons compared with sham mice (p = 0.0031), an effect fully rescued by SCFA supplementation (p = 0.0039 vs vehicle‐treated epileptic mice; Fisher's post hoc test; Fig 4D–H).

Two‐way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of treatment on the number of hilar ectopic DCX‐positive cells (F = 4.959, p = 0.0331). Vehicle‐treated epileptic mice exhibited a significant increase in ectopic hilar DCX‐positive neurons compared with sham controls (p = 0.0121), whereas SCFA supplementation normalized their number (p = 0.0067 vs vehicle‐treated epileptic mice; Fisher's post‐hoc test; Fig 4I and Supplementary Fig S6M–P). SCFA treatment did not affect DCX‐positive cell number in the subgranular or intragranular zones in either epileptic or sham mice (Supplementary Fig S6Q).

Reactive Gliosis

Reactive gliosis was assessed in the same hippocampal tissue. Two‐way ANOVA revealed significant disease × treatment interactions for the microglial marker Iba‐1 (F = 7.65, p = 0.0107) and the astrocytic marker GFAP (F = 10.28, p = 0.038) (Fig 4J, K and Supplementary Fig S6R–Z). Both markers were markedly increased in vehicle‐treated epileptic mice relative to sham controls (Iba‐1: p = 0.0002; GFAP: p < 0.0001; Fisher's post hoc test), and this increase was reversed by SCFA supplementation (Iba‐1: p = 0.0022; GFAP: p = 0.0002 vs vehicle‐treated epileptic mice).

Neuroinflammation

Analysis of pro‐inflammatory cytokines implicated in seizures and epileptogenesis 14 , 24 , 35 in contralateral hippocampi revealed a significant main effect of disease on IL1b (F = 5.228, p = 0.0297) and TNF mRNA levels (F = 3.568, p = 0.048) (Fig 4L, M). Both transcripts were induced in vehicle‐treated epileptic mice compared with sham mice (IL1b: p = 0.0333; TNF: p = 0.0455; Fisher's post hoc test). SCFA supplementation reduced IL1b and TNF expression toward sham levels, although these effects did not reach statistical significance vs vehicle‐treated epileptic mice (IL1b: p = 0.07 and TNF: p = 0.09). In contrast, Cxcl1 expression was significantly affected by both disease (F = 11.95, p = 0.0018) and treatment (F = 5.999, p = 0.0211), being robustly increased in vehicle‐treated epileptic mice (p = 0.0058) and normalized by SCFA supplementation (p = 0.0368 vs vehicle‐treated epileptic mice; Fisher's post hoc test; Fig 4N).

An exploratory Pearson's correlation analysis of the relationships between cerebral SCFA levels and functional and neuropathological hallmarks in the brains of epileptic mice is described in Supplementary Figure S7.

Duodenal Morphology

We next assessed the duodenum, the intestinal segment most affected in acquired epilepsy models, 4 , 5 by quantifying the villus height, crypt depth, and number of goblet cells as functional markers.

Two‐way ANOVA revealed significant effects of disease (F = 4.13, p = 0.05) and treatment (F = 14.18, p = 0.0007) on villus height (Fig 5A). Vehicle‐treated epileptic mice exhibited reduced villus height (p = 0.011 vs shams), which was restored by SCFA supplementation (p = 0.0002 vs vehicle‐treated epileptic mice, Fisher's post hoc test).

Disease had a statistically significant effect on the crypt depth (F = 7.339, p = 0.01065). Crypt depth was significantly increased in vehicle‐treated epileptic mice (p = 0.0072 vs shams) and normalized by SCFAs (Fig 5B). Disease × treatment interactions were observed for villus height/crypt depth ratio (F = 8.419, p = 0.0066) and goblet cells/villus (F = 7.508, p = 0.0098). Both measures were reduced in vehicle‐treated epileptic mice (p < 0.0001 for both measures vs sham mice; Fisher's post hoc test) and were restored by SCFA supplementation (villus height/crypt depth: p < 0.0001; goblet cells/villus: p = 0.0004 vs vehicle‐treated epileptic mice; Fisher's post hoc test; Fig 5C, D).

Exploratory correlation analyses between gut morphological/cellular parameters and hippocampal readouts revealed negative associations among villus height, villus height/crypt depth ratio, and goblet cell number with hippocampal Iba‐1 immunoreactivity (Fig 5E–G). Additionally, the number of goblet cells negatively correlated with memory retention (Fig 5H).

Intestinal Barrier

We found a statistically significant effect of disease on transcripts of tight junction proteins Ocln (F = 43.14, p < 0.0001) and TJP1 (ZO‐1; F = 173.2, p < 0.0001, 2‐way ANOVA) (Fig 5I, J). Both transcripts were significantly reduced in vehicle‐treated epileptic mice (p < 0.0001 vs shams) but were not restored by SCFA supplementation (Ocln: p = 0.0004; TJP1: p < 0.0001 vs shams; Fisher's post hoc test).

Fecal and Serum Metabolic Profiling in Epileptic Mice Supplemented With SCFAs

Untargeted fecal and serum metabolomic analyses were performed to identify metabolic processes potentially underlying the therapeutic effects of SCFA administration in epileptic mice. SCFA‐treated epileptic mice exhibited distinct metabolic pathway alterations compared with vehicle‐treated epileptic controls. Overall, fecal samples displayed a substantially higher number of differentially regulated metabolites than serum samples, indicating a more pronounced metabolic response in the intestinal compartment (volcano plot in Fig 6A, D and Supplementary Table S3).

Pathway enrichment analysis of fecal metabolites in SCFA‐treated epileptic mice versus vehicle‐treated epileptic mice, revealed significant modulation of vitamin B6 metabolism and amino acid biosynthesis and degradation (Fig 6B, C), accompanied by upregulation of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and the associated glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (see Fig 6B). Pathway topology analysis indicated that most altered metabolites occupied central positions within these metabolic networks. In contrast, steroid hormone biosynthesis was downregulated in fecal samples and exhibited a high pathway impact (Fig 6C).

In serum, vitamin B6 metabolism was the only pathway showing consistent modulation with fecal samples (Fig 6B, C, E). Differently, the biosynthesis of the branched‐chain amino acids valine, leucine, and isoleucine was upregulated in serum but downregulated in feces (Fig 6C, E), indicating compartment‐specific metabolic reprogramming. Similarly, glycine, serine, threonine, and tryptophan metabolism were upregulated in serum only (Fig 6E), likely reflecting host‐driven responses rather than microbiota‐derived activity.

Metabolic alterations associated with the epileptic condition itself and in sham mice treated with SCFAs are described in Supplementary Figures S8 to S10.

Discussion

In this study, we provide novel evidence that chronic supplementation with a balanced mixture of acetate, propionate, and butyrate exerts disease‐modifying effects in a mouse model of DRE. Treatment markedly attenuated seizure progression, reduced the emergence of new seizure clusters, and improved memory performance. These functional outcomes were accompanied by neuroprotective effects, including normalization of hippocampal cellular alterations critical for epileptogenesis, such as aberrant neurogenesis, loss of inhibitory interneurons, reactive gliosis, and neuroinflammation. 14 , 35 Together, these findings suggest that SCFAs can influence the long‐term trajectory of epilepsy beyond acute seizure suppression, providing a potential strategy for disease modification in conditions where current therapies remain largely symptomatic.

Importantly, the central effects of SCFAs were paralleled by normalization of gut structure and cellular composition. The reduced villus height observed in epileptic mice, despite increased crypt depth, indicates a decoupling between villi and their regenerative niche, 36 which may contribute to impaired nutrient absorption. Although tight junction reductions were not restored by treatment, normalization of goblet cell numbers suggests improved mucosal barrier function, potentially enhancing both physicochemical protection and immune defense. This may limit epithelial exposure to harmful agents and prevent leakage of toxic metabolites into the circulation. 37

The explorative correlation analysis showed that goblet cell number is positively associated with memory performance suggesting that mucosal barrier integrity may influence peripheral signals relevant to hippocampal‐dependent cognitive function. Goblet cells and intestinal structural alterations were also positively correlated with hippocampal microgliosis, an index of neuroinflammation in epileptic mice. 14 , 35 In turn, microgliosis correlated with memory deficits, and neuroinflammation with seizure progression (Supplementary Fig S7A–C). Together, these findings suggest that intestinal pathology parallels central neuroinflammatory processes and may contribute to disease severity.

The therapeutic effects of SCFAs in epileptic mice were associated with normalization of cerebral acetate and increased levels of propionic and isovaleric acids. These SCFAs may synergistically act to reduce seizure susceptibility and cognitive deficits while conferring neuroprotection through converging metabolic and neuromodulatory mechanisms. In particular, these metabolites provide alternative energy substrates for neurons and astrocytes, supporting ATP production and ionic homeostasis, under conditions of heightened neuronal demand, thereby limiting excitotoxicity. Their metabolism also contributes to replenishment of TCA cycle intermediates and GABA synthesis, shifting the excitation–inhibition balance toward inhibition. 12 , 38 Acetate and propionate can further suppress astrocytic reactivity, restore their homeostatic phenotypes (AQP4, glutamine synthase, and glutamate clearance), and modulate neuroinflammatory/oxidative stress pathways, stabilizing synaptic transmission. 13 , 39 , 40 , 41 Additionally, acetate, propionate, and isovalerate modulate histone acetylation, through direct HDAC inhibition (propionate and isovalerate) or as acetyl donors (acetate), with potential long‐term effects on gene expression relevant to neuronal excitability.

SCFA supplementation elevated cerebral valeric and 2‐methyl‐isobutyric acids level in sham mice, but not in epileptic mice, suggesting a preferential utilization of these metabolites in the diseased brain to support energy metabolism and neurotransmitter homeostasis during hyperexcitability.

Overall, stable plasma SCFA levels despite fluctuating brain concentrations in epileptic mice, including after SCFA supplementation, suggest that central turnover is largely driven by local metabolic sequestration and consumption, decoupling brain metabolite dynamics from systemic levels. Seizure‐induced modulation of monocarboxylate transporters in brain microvessels and astrocytes, 42 along with SCFA competition and differential transporter affinities, may also impact cerebral SCFA concentrations.

An exploratory correlation analysis of cortical SCFA levels with functional outcomes across all epileptic mice, irrespective of SCFA supplementation (Supplementary Fig S7), supports a potential role in modulating disease severity. Specifically, higher SCFA levels were associated with reduced glial reactivity and preservation of hippocampal neurons, which in turn correlated with attenuated seizure progression, whereas microgliosis was linked to cognitive deficits. Consistently, lower SCFA levels were associated with impaired memory performance.

Overall, our results support that elevated cerebral levels of SCFAs contribute to therapeutic effects on seizures, neuropathology, and associated comorbidities.

We selected HCAR2, FFAR2, and FFAR3 for transcriptomic analysis because they are the principal G‐protein‐coupled receptors mediating SCFA signaling in both the gut and the brain. 10 , 11 , 31 , 32 Collectively, these receptors orchestrate the major SCFA‐dependent pathways involved in gut–brain axis communication. 31 , 32 Increased brain SCFA levels in treated epileptic mice were accompanied by upregulation of HCAR2 that mediates anti‐inflammatory responses and regulate lipid metabolism. HCAR2 is expressed in both neurons and microglia in the hippocampus, 43 and its activation by butyrate, β‐hydroxybutyrate, fumarate, or niacin reduces microglia reactivity and neuroinflammation in models of inflammatory diseases. 44 Furthermore, hippocampal HCAR2 activation has been reported to reduce excitability and firing in dentate granule neurons, as well as excitatory synaptic transmission and seizure severity in mice exposed to SE or kindling, 43 suggesting a role in modulating seizure thresholds. Induction of HCAR2 in SCFA‐treated epileptic mice may represent a homeostatic attempt to attenuate hyperexcitability and neuroinflammation. The physiological expression of FFAR2 and FFAR3 receptors in an epileptic mouse brain suggests that canonical receptors remain available to mediate SCFA signaling. Beyond their established metabolic and immune roles, FFAR2 and FFAR3 have also been implicated in neuroimmune signaling, autonomic regulation, and microbiota–gut–brain axis communication. 10 , 11 , 31 , 32

To enhance translational relevance, we analyzed SCFA levels and receptor expression in hippocampal tissue from TLE subjects. Although direct comparison with autoptic control tissue was not possible due to rapid postmortem metabolite degradation, 45 analysis of TLE subjects with versus without HS revealed pathology‐specific alteration in propionic acid levels independent of seizure burden. Consistent with evidence that metabolic dysfunctions in epilepsy are accentuated in brain tissue with structural abnormalities, 46 the reduction of propionic acid may reflect increased local utilization due to energetic demand and oxidative stress, and disrupted mitochondrial metabolism. 47 Propionate contributes to TCA cycle via conversion to succinyl‐CoA, 48 highlighting its role as a metabolically sensitive node linking gut‐derived SCFAs to cerebral energy homeostasis and neuronal excitability. In human sclerotic hippocampi, FFAR2 expression was selectively reduced, whereas FFAR3 and HCAR2 remained unchanged. Given that FFAR2 is expressed by astrocytes, and its activation promotes the glutamate‐glutamine shuttle and modulates neuroinflammation, 40 its downregulation may contribute to disrupted glutamate homeostasis and chronic brain inflammation characteristic of TLE‐HS49 that influence seizure thresholds and epileptogenesis in experimental TLE models. 35 , 49

Finally, comparison of untargeted metabolomic profiles in fecal and serum specimens of epileptic mice provided further insight into mechanisms which may underly the central effects of SCFA supplementation. The greater number of differentially regulated metabolites in fecal samples compared with serum indicates that the gut represents the primary site of metabolic reprogramming in response to SCFA supplementation. Enrichment of vitamin B6 metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis and degradation, and TCA cycle‐related pathways in feces suggests enhanced intestinal availability of key cofactors and energy‐related substrates. Convergent modulation of vitamin B6 metabolism in both fecal and serum compartments points to this pathway as a key metabolic interface linking intestinal SCFA exposure to central neurochemical homeostasis. Vitamin B6 is essential for the synthesis of key neurotransmitters, including GABA, catecholamines, and serotonin, implying that SCFA‐driven alterations in B6 metabolism could directly influence neuronal function and network excitability. 50 SCFA supplementation in epileptic mice selectively modulated tryptophan metabolism in the circulation, but not in the feces, indicating that peripheral rather than intestinal metabolism may mediate SCFA effects on neuroactive tryptophan‐derived metabolites.

Although SCFA supplementation reduced intestinal steroid biosynthesis, this is unlikely to be pro‐inflammatory or counteract their therapeutic effects, as the intestine is not the main source of circulating corticosteroids, and SCFAs exert direct anti‐inflammatory and neuroprotective actions through receptor signaling and epigenetic modulation. 10 , 11 , 31 , 32

Collectively, these data indicate that supplementation with SCFAs does not produce uniform changes in metabolites across body compartments. Instead, it appears to regulate a metabolic flux along the gut–blood–brain axis, with the epileptic brain selectively retaining and utilizing specific metabolites according to local energetic demand and neurochemical signaling.

These findings support a mechanistic framework in which SCFA supplementation induces metabolic reprogramming at the intestinal level, promotes cerebral utilization of related metabolites, and activates receptor‐mediated anti‐inflammatory, neuroprotective, and neuromodulatory pathways. This multi‐layered modulation positions SCFAs as active regulators of gut–brain axis homeostasis rather than passive microbial products.

Emerging clinical evidence suggests that people with DRE exhibit alterations in the gut microbiota that may affect SCFA production and broader metabolic output. 19 , 51 Whereas untargeted serum metabolomics studies in epilepsy sufferers have reported systemic alterations in energy metabolism, amino acid catabolism, and lipid homeostasis, 52 our data indicate that circulating SCFA levels do not differ across experimental groups. Importantly, despite the lack of detectable changes in serum, we observed marked alterations in fecal metabolites as well as SCFA levels in the brain, suggesting that gut‐derived metabolic changes may be compartmentalized and not fully reflected in the circulation. Together, these findings indicate that blood‐based SCFA measurements may have limited sensitivity as biomarkers, whereas direct assessment of fecal metabolomics may provide more relevant insights into gut‐derived metabolic alterations in epilepsy and their potential contribution to gut–brain axis mechanisms.

These observations, together with our experimental data, support the view that gut microbiota dysbiosis and altered SCFA metabolism are integral features of epilepsy, and may contribute to disease pathophysiology through gut–brain axis dysfunction.

SCFAs are compounds with a favorable safety profile in humans and have demonstrated preliminary beneficial effects in pilot clinical studies in neurological disorders. 52 Therefore, our data provide a strong rationale for clinical translation to determine whether adjunctive SCFA‐based interventions can restore metabolic and neurochemical homeostasis and durably modify disease trajectories in people with DRE, paving the way for microbiome‐targeted therapeutics in epilepsy.

Limitations of the Study

The use of a mixture of SCFAs does not allow the specific contribution of individual metabolites to be disentangled; however, this approach better reflects physiological conditions, in which acetate, propionate, and butyrate are co‐produced by the gut microbiota and may act synergistically on energy metabolism, neuroinflammation, and epigenetic regulation. Future studies should therefore address the relative contribution of individual SCFAs, define their downstream signaling pathways, and clarify their interactions with host microbiota composition.

SCFA administration via drinking water was selected as a practical and well‐established approach to ensure sustained systemic exposure during the longitudinal study. However, this strategy does not fully recapitulate physiological colonic production and does not allow precise tracking of distribution in the absence of labeling approaches. Alternative delivery strategies, such as colon‐targeted formulations or dietary carriers, 53 , 54 may provide physiologically relevant insights into gut‐derived SCFA signaling. These approaches, together with isotope‐labeling studies, will better define the spatial dynamics and site‐specific actions of SCFAs along the gut–brain axis.

Importantly, the present work was designed as an initial exploratory study to determine whether SCFAs exert measurable effects in a well‐characterized model of TLE. In this context, male mice were used because we have extensively characterized seizures, neuropathology, gut structural alterations, and microbiota dysbiosis in male animals in this model of TLE. 5 Furthermore, this approach helped to minimize animal use while maintaining adequate statistical power. Inclusion of female models will be essential to identify potential sex‐dependent responses, and testing SCFA supplementation in established chronic epilepsy will be critical to delineate therapeutic windows.
