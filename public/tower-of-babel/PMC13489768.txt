ABSTRACT
PURPOSEIn large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL), use of high-dose methotrexate (HD-MTX) to prevent CNS relapse (so-called CNS prophylaxis) remains controversial. International guidelines continue to recommend HD-MTX in patients deemed ultra high risk (UHR) because of a lack of robust data specific to these subgroups. This study was designed to examine the impact of HD-MTX specifically in UHR patients.METHODSData from two previous retrospective analyses were combined to produce a cohort of UHR patients (≥1 of the following criteria: CNS international prognostic index score 5-6; testicular, renal/adrenal, or breast involvement; ≥3 extranodal sites), treated with or without HD-MTX. The primary outcome was CNS relapse rate (isolated or concurrent with systemic relapse) measured from diagnosis with additional landmark analysis of all responding patients with no progression event at 6 months.RESULTSAnalyses were performed on 1,923 patients meeting UHR criteria. No significant difference in 3-year CNS relapse rate was observed between no HD-MTX (n = 1,051) versus HD-MTX patients (n = 872), including in multivariable analyses adjusting for baseline characteristics (3-year rate, 9.3% v 8.1%; adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 1.13 [95% CI, 0.82 to 1.57]). Analyses restricted to isolated CNS relapse also confirmed no difference (5.9% v 5.7%; adjusted HR, 1.03 [95% CI, 0.69 to 1.53]). In the landmark analysis (no HD-MTX n = 782, HD-MTX n = 773), no difference in 3-year CNS relapse was observed (6.7% v 6.6%, adjusted HR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.62 to 1.44]).CONCLUSIONTo our knowledge, this is the largest international comparative data set of patients with LBCL with UHR features showing no significant reduction in CNS relapse with HD-MTX in all UHR or in any individual subgroup. Despite inherent limitations of retrospective analyses, the data strongly support previous studies in suggesting HD-MTX prophylaxis has no meaningful benefit for most patients.

INTRODUCTION

Relapse of large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL) in the CNS, either in isolation or concurrent with systemic relapse, is uncommon (incidence approximately 2-6%).1-5 Often referred to as secondary CNS lymphoma (SCNSL), it is important to distinguish this from de novo SCNSL where both CNS and systemic disease are evident at first presentation. Outcomes from CNS relapse occurring after first-line treatment remain poor with a median survival of <6 months.6-10 Therefore, CNS relapse is a clinical challenge with devastating consequences, and significant efforts have been made to minimize its incidence.11-14

CNS relapse risk is associated with several clinical and biologic risk features.15 The CNS International Prognostic Index (CNS-IPI) is a validated risk stratification tool, adding kidney/adrenal involvement to conventional IPI factors where high-risk patients have a CNS-IPI of 4-6.16 CNS relapse risk in this group is 10%-12%, with CNS-IPI 5 and 6 associated with risks of 15% and 32%, respectively. The CNS-IPI has significant limitations—it omits some high-risk primary extranodal lymphomas with low CNS-IPI score (eg, testicular,17 breast18). Furthermore, sensitivity is low, as approximately 50% of CNS relapses occur in patients with low-intermediate score.

Owing to the ability to penetrate the CNS and use as the backbone of treatment for CNS lymphoma, intravenous (IV) high-dose methotrexate (HD-MTX) has been widely used as CNS prophylaxis for approximately 20 years. Supporting data for this practice are restricted to retrospective series.11-14 However, the relative rarity of CNS relapse limited power of historical studies to demonstrate differences in outcomes between prophylactic approaches. Two recent large analyses have questioned its benefit. The first assessed timing of HD-MTX prophylaxis in 1,384 patients with LBCL19 and demonstrated no reduction in CNS relapse with intercalated HD-MTX delivery between cycles of rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisolone (R-CHOP) compared with delivery after R-CHOP. The crude CNS relapse rate in patients with high CNS-IPI was 9.1% despite HD-MTX in all patients, similar to that in high-risk patients who did not receive CNS prophylaxis in the original CNS-IPI study. In 2023, Lewis et al20 analyzed the impact of HD-MTX in 2,418 patients (HD-MTX n = 425 v no HD-MTX n = 1,993). A small but statistically significant reduction in CNS relapse in the HD-MTX group was identified, but significance was not retained in a landmark analysis restricted to patients in complete remission (CR) after systemic therapy. The authors concluded that HD-MTX use was not associated with a clinically meaningful reduction in CNS relapse with 5-year adjusted risk difference of 1.4% [95% CI, –1.5 to 4.1]. Owing to relatively low numbers in the HD-MTX group, analyses of ultra high-risk (UHR) subgroups were underpowered.

As such, many clinicians have significantly reduced or stopped HD-MTX prophylaxis because of the potential futile exposure to well-established toxicity of HD-MTX.21 However, benefit in UHR subgroups remains uncertain, the most established (with corresponding historical estimates of CNS relapse risk) being CNS-IPI 5-616 (15-32%), involvement of ≥3 extranodal sites22 (15%), and testicular (10%-24%), renal/adrenal,16,23 (15-31%) and breast18,24 (15%) involvement. International guidelines have continued to recommend consideration of HD-MTX prophylaxis, particularly in UHR patients.25-28

Adequately powered prospective, randomized trials addressing HD-MTX efficacy in UHR patients are impractical because of the low frequency of each subgroup and the rarity of CNS relapse. Therefore, we amalgamated data sets from the two aforementioned studies,19,20 producing to our knowledge the largest ever series of patients treated with and without HD-MTX. We specifically focused analyses on patients with LBCL with ≥1 UHR feature.

METHODS

Detailed methods for each study are previously published. Wilson et al (study 1) retrospectively identified patients with LBCL from 47 international sites treated with R-CHOP/R-CHOP-like therapy and HD-MTX CNS prophylaxis. HD-MTX was defined as any IV MTX dose intended to cross the blood-brain barrier and exert a prophylactic effect, given for ≥1 cycle. Patients treated with more intensive regimens were excluded.19 Lewis et al (study 2) collected data on patients from 23 international sites with prespecified high-risk features: diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) with CNS-IPI 4-6, high-grade B-cell lymphoma with rearrangements of MYC plus BCL2 and/or BCL6 (double/triple hit HGBL, DHL/THL), primary breast/testicular LBCL (stage I and II), or renal/adrenal involvement irrespective of CNS-IPI. Patients had received R-CHOP/R-CHOP-like therapy, including DA-EPOCH-R. HD-MTX was defined as ≥1 cycle of IV MTX at any dose.20 Both studies excluded patients with known baseline CNS involvement, but baseline CNS screening was not mandated.

Data were collected in fully anonymized or deidentified format, recorded in standardized study-specific collection sheets, and returned to principal investigators for secure central database storage. Patients were selected for CNS prophylaxis according to local policies and delivery (timing, dose, cycle number) determined by center preference.

CNS relapse was defined as first CNS relapse/progression, including relapses isolated to the CNS or occurring concurrently with systemic relapse (synchronous relapse; defined as CNS and systemic relapse within 30 days of each other). Death, systemic relapse (without CNS relapse), and stable or progressive disease as best response at the end of chemotherapy were competing events. Time-to-CNS relapse was measured from the date of diagnosis/landmark until the first event, with patients not experiencing an event censored at date last seen. Overall (OS) and event-free survival (EFS) were measured from diagnosis/landmark until the first event (death for OS, any event as listed above for EFS). A landmark analysis was performed to mitigate for potential immortality bias, particularly in the HD-MTX treated group whereby patients may have been planned for HD-MTX but had an early progression event and therefore were not captured. The landmark was set as 6 months from diagnosis and included only patients alive, progression free, and in partial (PR) or complete (CR) response to first-line chemotherapy.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox regression were used to compare groups for OS and EFS. The primary analysis for time-to-CNS relapse was performed using competing risks survival analysis by the Fine and Gray method with sensitivity analyses performed using Cox regression and censoring for competing risks. Isolated CNS relapses were analyzed separately, whereby synchronous systemic relapse was also counted as a competing event. Adjusted analyses were performed for the total UHR population; from diagnosis, these were adjusted for age (as continuous), sex, all CNS-IPI factors, breast and testicular involvement, and the landmark cohort was also adjusted for IT prophylaxis. Differences at 3 years were calculated by applying the hazard ratio (HR) to the 3-year rate in the no HD-MTX group and taking the difference.

To reduce residual confounding, a further sensitivity analysis was performed using propensity score matching. This matched patients on all the factors used within the adjusted analysis as well as response (CR v PR) in the landmark cohort. The caliper used for matching was set as ¼ of the standard deviation of the propensity score.29

Baseline characteristics were compared using Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney (continuous variables), Chi-squared for trend (ordinal), and Fisher exact (discrete) tests. All analyses were performed using STATA version 19.5 (STATA Corp, College Station, TX)

Individual ethical approvals were previously in place for both studies. For this study, additional ethical approval was secured (West of Scotland Research Ethics Service, June 12, 2025).

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

Data amalgamation is summarized in the Data Supplement (Fig S1). All 1,384 patients from study one were included. Sites contributing to study two were included if data transfer agreements permitted use for subsequent analysis. Data from all HD-MTX/no HD-MTX treated patients were included from nine sites in study two and no HD-MTX patients only from a further six sites who had already contributed patients to study 1. This produced a total cohort of 3,491 patients (no HD-MTX n = 1,866, HD-MTX n = 1,625, baseline characteristics; Data Supplement, Table S1).

Analyses were restricted to 1,923 patients meeting UHR criteria (no HD-MTX n = 872, HD-MTX n = 1,051, baseline characteristics Table 1). Landmark analysis included 1,555 patients (no HD-MTX n = 782, HD-MTX n = 773). The median follow-up was 56.3 months (IQR, 33.6-87.9) for all patients but longer in no HD-MTX patients compared with HD-MTX treated (76.0 months [IQR, 46.7-112.9] v 40.6 months [IQR, 23.8-64.4]). This is explained by earlier median date of diagnosis in no HD-MTX patients (November 2012) versus HD-MTX treated (December 2016).

HD-MTX treated patients displayed more frequent high-risk features including advanced stage, ≥3 extranodal sites, and renal/adrenal or testicular involvement. Fewer HD-MTX treated patients had CNS-IPI 5-6, likely driven by the aforementioned differences in PS and age. In HD-MTX treated patients, 70.2% received a cumulative dose of ≥6 g/m2. Intrathecal prophylaxis was given in 32.4% no HD-MTX patients versus 50.3% HD-MTX (P < .0001; Table 2). Propensity score matching produced 618 matched pairs in the UHR cohort and 501 in the landmark cohort.

CNS Relapse Results

In all UHR patients, there was no statistically significant difference in the CNS relapse rate between no HD-MTX and HD-MTX patients, including on multivariable and propensity-matched analyses adjusting for key baseline characteristics (3-year rates, 9.3% v 8.1%; adjusted HR, 1.13 [95% CI, 0.82 to 1.57]; Fig 1; Data Supplement, Table S3). Moreover, analyses restricted to isolated CNS relapse also confirmed no difference (5.9% v 5.7%; adjusted HR, 1.03 [95% CI, 0.69 to 1.53]; Fig 1; Data Supplement, Table S4). In the landmark analysis, no difference in 3-year CNS relapse (any or isolated) was seen in the UHR group (any CNS relapse 6.7% v 6.6%; adjusted HR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.62 to 1.44]; Data Supplement, Tables S5 and S6). Conclusions remained the same when sensitivity analyses using Cox regression or propensity score–matched populations were applied.

Survival Outcomes

Overall, UHR patients not receiving HD-MTX had inferior 3-year EFS and OS (Table 2). Within the landmark population only, the differences were less marked but remained significant (Table 2; Figs 1E and 1F). Outcomes Post-CNS Relapse

CNS relapses in the amalgamated data set (non-UHR and UHR) were analyzed given the large data set and opportunity to analyze this uncommon clinical event. Of the 264 CNS relapses observed, there were 221 deaths with a median OS of 3.8 months (IQR, 1.5-10.8 months). There was no difference in OS according to HD-MTX receipt (P = .95, Fig 3A). There was no significant difference in survival according to isolated versus synchronous CNS relapse (P = .13, Fig 3C). DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the largest analysis of patients treated with and without HD-MTX CNS prophylaxis ever conducted, demonstrating no clinically or statistically significant reduction in CNS relapse with HD-MTX in all UHR patients or any individual subgroup. The key strengths are the very large sample size with focus on UHR patients and the robust statistical analysis, including multiple sensitivity analyses to adjust for inherent imbalance in patient characteristics and potential selection bias in choosing patients suitable for HD-MTX or not. The amalgamation of two previously published data sets addresses important individual study limitations, one lacking a comparator arm of patients receiving no HD-MTX19 and the other having insufficient patients treated with HD-MTX to robustly analyze UHR subgroups.20

Published literature on CNS prophylaxis in LBCL is conflicting and challenging to interpret. The isolated CNS relapse rates observed in our study appear lower than historical UHR estimates. However, most prior studies failed to separately describe isolated CNS relapse versus those occurring concurrent with or following systemic progression, the latter scenarios having different biologic basis and primarily reflecting a failure of systemic therapy. Our overall CNS relapse rates are closer to these historical estimates, with any remaining difference potentially explained by our focus on the landmark population, the most relevant analysis for assessing HD-MTX prophylaxis efficacy, and that our study included more PET-CT staging compared with historical data, potentially upstaging some patients into higher-risk categories.

Before Lewis et al in 2023, most consensus guidelines recommended consideration of HD-MTX in patients meeting established criteria for high CNS relapse risk. This included all patients with CNS-IPI 4-6, resulting in >20% of patients being considered for HD-MTX despite its inherent toxicity and a lack of robust evidence. Older and less fit patients are disproportionately selected by these criteria. Lewis et al strongly questioned this approach, demonstrating no clinically meaningful reduction in CNS relapse risk overall with HD-MTX. However, the number of HD-MTX–treated patients, particularly those with UHR features, was low and left open questions about the utility in the highest risk patients.

We believe this study provides practice-informing data in an area where no adequately powered prospective or randomized studies guide clinicians. HD-MTX CNS prophylaxis is theoretically aimed at preventing isolated CNS relapse, yet critically we could find no significant reduction in such events for UHR patients. Although there was a numerical reduction in isolated CNS relapse for CNS-IPI 5 patients receiving HD-MTX, this did not reach statistical significance, CIs are wide, and the lack of difference in CNS-IPI 6 patients suggests this may have been spurious. Furthermore, the well-established risk of treatment-related mortality,30 toxicity, cumbersome delivery,19 and associated resource utilization with HD-MTX must be carefully balanced against any theoretical, nonproven benefit.

Only two prospective trials have been attempted, recruiting 142 and 100 patients, respectively, with random assignment to IT or HD-MTX prophylaxis.31,32 Both stopped early because of low accrual or lack of efficacy signal for HD-MTX. Neither demonstrated a difference in CNS relapse between IT and HD-MTX prophylaxis. Given the large body of evidence suggesting lack of benefit of stand-alone IT prophylaxis in LBCL,33 these studies further question the efficacy of HD-MTX.

A recent single-center series made conclusions contrary to our findings. Of 336 patients with LBCL achieving CMR following R-CHOP, 69 received HD-MTX at 3 g/m2 over 3 hours preceded by a bolus.34 A reduction in CNS relapse was reported compared with a historical cohort with similar high-risk features but managed before HD-MTX adoption (0% v 14%, P = .0006). However, the inherent selection bias and low patient numbers treated with HD-MTX prevent meaningful conclusions on efficacy being drawn.

Testicular LBCL is the only entity where prospective trial evidence supports CNS prophylaxis. The IELSG30 trial investigated R-CHOP with concurrent IT prophylaxis as well as contralateral testicular radiotherapy and two cycles of intermediate dose (1.5 g/m2) IV methotrexate following R-CHOP. No CNS relapses were reported from 54 patients after 5 years.17 However, this was a nonrandomized trial recruiting early-stage/low-risk patients and it is unclear which therapeutic components have the most impact on CNS relapse risk. Our study demonstrated a nonsignificant trend toward benefit of HD-MTX in those with testicular involvement. The effect was larger in those with advanced stage disease and no difference was seen on propensity matched analysis. Many clinicians may continue to follow the protocol adopted in IELSG30, but our results question whether IV MTX at 1.5 g/m2 is a necessary component.

Our study has several limitations. Notably, the variation in HD-MTX timing and dose used, including number of cycles, dose per cycle, and lack of information on infusion schedules. It is established that rapid HD-MTX infusion over 4-6 hours optimizes CNS penetration.35 It is possible some patients were treated with longer infusion schedules. However, our analysis of cumulative dose and impact on CNS relapse rate supports published evidence which indicates increasing dose does not significantly reduce CNS relapse, but does increase the risk of toxicity.21 We are also aware that patient selection for HD-MTX may have varied according to individual center policy and clinician discretion. This selection bias is inherent and unavoidable in retrospective studies, but the very large sample size used here, as well as adjusted analyses for differences in baseline characteristics, may counteract this.

We did not include DHL/THL as a specific UHR subgroup because of a lack of evidence supporting independent risk prediction beyond other established clinical factors and inconsistent reporting within our data set. Recent evidence suggests a different pattern of CNS relapse in this group, with more frequent leptomeningeal involvement and the majority co-occurring with systemic relapse.36 Although our subanalyses did not suggest a benefit of HD-MTX in DHL/THL patients, the role of CNS prophylaxis in this group requires separate consideration given the very low event rates observed here.

A further limitation is the imbalance in patient characteristics. These differences likely reflect the understandable willingness from clinicians to consider HD-MTX in younger and fitter patients, particularly those with high-risk characteristics and the potential drop out during treatment in those planned for (but not given) HD-MTX. We observed more frequent high-risk extranodal site involvement in the HD-MTX group but overall lower CNS-IPI in this group, likely driven by differences in performance status and age. However, it was reassuring to observe CNS relapse similarities and overall conclusions remaining unchanged in our sensitivity analyses, with similar survival outcomes in the landmark analysis illustrating a more balanced comparison. Finally, restricting analyses to a landmark population further addresses imbalance in treatment groups, mirrors the timepoint for HD-MTX decision making in clinical practice, and mitigates for immortal time bias and lack of uniformly recorded data on intention-to-treat with HD-MTX.

Baseline CNS screening was relatively low in this study. However, the proportion screened was higher in HD-MTX–treated patients, suggesting reduced risk of including patients with occult CNS disease in this group which theoretically could skew toward a lower risk of CNS events. Despite this, we demonstrated no reduction in CNS relapse compared with the no HD-MTX group. In those with CNS relapse, there was also no significant difference in timing whether they had baseline screening or not (data not shown).

The dismal outcomes observed after CNS relapse are consistent with other contemporary series.10 The risk of such events is not reduced by HD-MTX prophylaxis, and there is an urgent need to explore alternative approaches. Accumulating evidence suggests that fit patients with proven de novo SCNSL can be treated with dose-intensive, CNS-penetrating chemotherapy.7,10 Therefore, a greater emphasis should be made on investigating highly sensitive baseline screening techniques moving forward, with identification of occult CNS disease justifying the aforementioned SCNSL-type approach rather than HD-MTX alone. Emerging data suggest that circulating tumor DNA CSF analysis and more sophisticated, biologic LBCL risk stratification may result in superior predictive value for future CNS progression compared with established techniques.37-39 This requires validation in larger data sets before considering implementation into clinical practice, but may inform future treatment decisions in first-line LBCL. Finally, incorporation of bispecific antibodies and chimeric antigen receptor T cells into first-line treatment may potentially improve systemic disease control and reduce CNS relapse risk given their efficacy in CNS lymphoma treatment.40,41

In conclusion, we report what is to our knowledge the largest international data set of patients with LBCL with UHR features, demonstrating no significant reduction in CNS relapse with HD-MTX prophylaxis. There is an urgent need to design prospective, randomized trials aimed exclusively at UHR patients, using novel therapies other than HD-MTX.
