Scaling Diradicaloid
Thermoelectricity

Lewis Hamilton, Jiung Jang, Amit Sil, Peng He, Abdalghani H S Daaoub, Yongjun Choi, Sara Sangtarash, Andrea Vezzoli, Hyo Jae Yoon, Hatef Sadeghi

Abstract

The identification of molecular materials with high thermoelectric
performance has attracted considerable attention due to their unique
quantum properties, elemental abundance, and environmental friendliness.
Achieving this requires overcoming the conventional tradeoff between
the Seebeck coefficient (S) and electrical conductance
(G). Here, we exploit the thermoelectric properties
of diradicaloid molecular junctions and demonstrate the coenhancement
of G and S. Calculations predict
that elongating the diradicaloid backbone systematically reduces the
energy gap, pushing transport resonances closer to the electrode’s
Fermi energy, leading to the simultaneous improvement of G and S, and a length-dependent increase in power
factor unlike that of conventional conjugated molecules. This is confirmed
in self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) formed from diradicaloids, where
we observe an increase in G with the diradicaloid
length and S reaching values exceeding 40 μV/K.
This shows exotic quantum transport properties of single-molecule
diradicaloids translate to technologically relevant SAMs for next-generation
high-performance thermoelectric energy conversion devices.

Thermoelectricity, the conversion
of thermal energy into electrical energy, offers highly attractive
solutions to modern energy challenges, ranging from waste heat harvesting
and wearable devices to Peltier refrigeration. Identifying materials with high thermoelectric performance remains
a challenge in molecular electronics.
−

 Organic materials formed primarily of π-conjugated molecular
systems are particularly promising candidates. These organic materials typically offer low phonon thermal conductance
(κ

p
) due to their
lower mass and weaker intermolecular interactions.
−

 This suppression of the phonon contribution to thermal conductance
in turn can yield higher values for the overall thermoelectric figure
of merit, ZT = P
T/κ where P is the power factor, T the temperature, and κ = κe + κp
 the thermal conductance due to electrons (κ
e
) and phonons (κ
p
). In order to increase ZT, and therefore
enhance efficiency of a thermoelectric device to convert heat to electricity,
the power factor P = GS
2 also needs to be improved. However, in typical thermoelectric materials,
attempting to increase S is often accompanied by
a reduction in G.
,

In the
pursuit of overcoming this trade-off, molecular materials
offer unique opportunities because they can exhibit sharp transport
features close to the energy of molecular frontier orbitals. These
sharp features are essential because G is proportional
to the amplitude of the transmission coefficient T(E) at E
F, while S is proportional to its slope (d ln­(T(E))/dE); thus, sharp
resonances near E
F can simultaneously
lead to high-G and high-S.
−

 In most conventional molecules, however, transport resonances are
not located close enough to E
F to realize
this coenhancement. Consequently, the field has increasingly turned
its attention toward organic molecular materials with open-shell properties,
such as Blatter radicals, which show immense potential.

Open-shell derivatives with partially
filled orbitals can lead
to new transport resonances, favorable transitions from destructive
to constructive quantum interference, and significantly smaller energy
gaps between frontier orbitals.
−

 These features collectively contribute to higher conductance and
closer orbital alignment to the Fermi level of the electrodes, fundamentally
altering the slope and value of T(E) to enhance both G and S. Despite
this potential, leveraging these materials is practically challenging
because radicals that remain stable at room temperature are currently
limited to exceptional cases such as Blatter, Nitroxide (TEMPO), Trityl,
and Verdazyl radicals.
−

Recently, we designed a series of quinoidal polycyclic hydrocarbons featuring a bis­(indeno)­acene core (a) and established
anti-Ohmic transport at the single-molecule level, with conductance
increasing as the diradicaloid backbone is extended. These highly
tailorable molecules exist with a sterically protected diradicaloid
electronic configuration through the 3,5-dimethylthioanisolyl anchor
groups to contact to the electrodes. A diradicaloid electronic configuration
describes a partial diradical character characterized by resonances
between a closed-shell configuration and a pure diradical form, achieved
through the delocalization of unpaired electrons over the organic
conjugated π-system. This configuration greatly enhances stability
at room temperature while successfully retaining the highly attractive
electronic properties of traditional radicals leading to a remarkable
negative beta factor, a proportional increase in conductance as the
molecular backbone length increased. These phenomena are associated
with a significant reduction in the HOMO–LUMO gap and enhanced
orbital alignment to the Fermi level. This is desirable for coenhancement
of G and S, and therefore, in this
paper, we investigate for the first time the thermoelectric properties
of diradicaloids both theoretically and experimentally. Here, we address
a distinct question: whether unusual electronic structure of diradicaloids
can simultaneously enhance G and S, and whether these single-molecule trends persist in large-area
self-assembled monolayers. We therefore combine quantum-transport
calculations with ensemble thermovoltage and current-density measurements
to establish the length dependence of S, G, and the thermoelectric power factor. We demonstrate that
the exceptional single-molecule transport properties successfully
translate into scalable SAM configurations (b), yielding both simultaneous enhancement
and high values of G and S.

of the molecular
junctions formed from diradicaloids, we first investigated the gas

the SIESTA implementation of DFT combined

For all energies within the HOMO–LUMO gap of R3, the magnitude of S increases with molecular length
(b). This enhancement
is driven by a rapid narrowing of the HOMO–LUMO gap in these
diradicaloids with increasing length, which stems from a progressive
increase in their diradicaloid character as the backbone length increases. This phenomenon can be rationalized through
Clar’s rule, and the resulting trend in increasing aromatic
stabilization as the number of π sextets also increases across
the series R1 → R3. This narrowing brings the
molecular frontier orbitals and associated resonances closer to the
electrode’s Fermi energy, enhancing the Seebeck coefficient,
as S is proportional to the slope of the transmission
at the Fermi energy. This leads to remarkably high S for an organic molecular junction at room temperature, where values
are typically much smaller and often below ±20 μV/K.
,

This enhancement of the S by the backbone
length
leads to a positive Seebeck attenuation factor (d). Furthermore, since the transport resonances
get closer to the Fermi energy as a result of the reduced HOMO–LUMO
gap by length, the amplitude of transmission probability at the Fermi
energy increases leading to the enhancement of G (c). This contrasts
with the conventional thermoelectric tradeoff, where increasing S is often accompanied by reduced G. The
significant coenhancement of S and G as shown in c and d is key to enhancing the power factor,
leading to values of 6.5 × 10–5 pW/K2 per molecule in R3, over an order of magnitude greater
than OPE3, a widely used molecular-electronics benchmark.

To confirm this and demonstrate that the
enhanced thermoelectric
properties can also translate to technologically relevant SAM junctions,
we synthesized the R1–R3 molecules
and fabricated large-area SAMs. The partial open–shell character
of R1–R3 has been confirmed in our
previous study by optical and magnetic
measurements: visible spectroscopy shows significant absorption well
into the near-infrared region, while variable-temperature NMR was
used to characterize the small paramagnetic population that causes
signal broadening. Furthermore, we performed thermally assisted occupation
DFT calculations to estimate the degree of diradicaloid character y
0 as defined by Yamaguchi. We found a clear trend of increasing y
0 across the series, from y
0 = 0.13 for R1 to y
0 = 0.49
for R3, indicating an almost perfect superposition of
quinoidal and diradical resonance as the best description for the
longest molecular wire considered here. The synthesis and single-molecule
characterization of R1–R3 are described
in detail in our previous study.

To fabricate SAMs, we incubated template-stripped Au (AuTS) electrodes in toluene solutions saturated with the respective R1–R3 molecules for 24 h, followed by
rinsing with anhydrous toluene and drying under a nitrogen stream.
The use of the ultraflat AuTS substrate effectively suppresses
leakage currents arising from defect sites associated with surface
roughness, thereby enabling reliable characterization of molecular
transport properties. Details of the AFM scratch measurements are
provided in the SI. The AFM scratch measurements

measured film thicknesses is consistent with the molecular dimensions
estimated from the reported X-ray diffraction (XRD) structures and
DFT calculations (Table S5), supporting
the successful formation of the desired SAMs. For the following analyses
of large-area junctions, we use the thickness determined using the
AFM scratch measurement as a tunneling distance (d), as it more directly reflects the effective thickness of the molecular
layers in the junctions.

the SAMs and examine their

distinct Si 2p1/2 and 2p3/2 peaks were observed

of the TIPS groups upon surface deposition. Together with the AFM
thicknesses, these results indicate the successful formation of R1–R3 molecular layers with relatively
extended molecular configurations on AuTS.

The R1–R3 molecules have been
reported to exhibit an anomalous tunneling behavior at the single-molecule
level, where the tunneling current increases
with molecular length due to the progressive stabilization of the
diradical character. To investigate whether this diradical character
is transferable to the large-area molecular junctions, we measured
the current density (J) using eutectic gallium-indium
(EGaIn)-based junctions (see the experimental methods for detailed
protocols). All log tunneling current density histograms displayed
a normal distribution (Figure S5 in the SI). a shows
the J–V curves of R1–R3 large-area junctions. The tunneling current
follows the simplified Simmons tunneling model, J = J
0 e–dβ, where d is the tunneling distance, J
0 the extrapolated zero-length current density,
and β the attenuation coefficient. The R1–R3 large-area junctions exhibited a negative β value
of −3.20 ± 1.29 nm–1 (b), meaning that the conductance
increases with the length. While the magnitude of β is less
than that of single-molecule junctions (−6.1 nm–1) and calculated β in c (−16.6 nm–1), likely due
to intermolecular interactions and electrostatic effects, the negative
attenuation behavior is remarkably preserved upon scaling to large-area
molecular junctions.

The length dependence of S is
often described
by the simplified semiempirical equation: S = d · βS + S
0. Here, d is the tunneling barrier width (defined
as the film thickness of the R1–R3 SAMs measured using the AFM-scratch method), S
0 is the hypothetical extrapolated S at d = 0, and βS is an empirical length dependence
factor. Conjugated molecular wires typically exhibit positive βS because the HOMO–LUMO gap decreases with increasing
conjugation length. For conventional conjugated molecules, such as
oligophenylene (di)­thiols,
,,
 heteroacenes, oligothiophenes and imine-condensed molecular wires, βS remains below 10 μV
K–1 nm–1. In contrast, the R1–R3 molecules exhibit a significantly
larger βS value of 56.7 ± 3.10 μV K–1 nm–1. This enhanced length dependence
implies a more pronounced reduction in the HOMO–LUMO gap with
molecular extension, in agreement with our calculations (a).

the current density. f compares the length dependence of P

In summary, we have demonstrated that diradicaloid molecular wires
offer a robust pathway to high-performance organic thermoelectrics.
By engineering a sterically protected open-shell electronic configuration,
the R1–R3 series exhibits a rapidly
reducing HOMO–LUMO gap with increasing molecular length. This
aligns sharp transport resonances closely with the Fermi level as
demonstrated by our calculations, overcoming the standard molecular
thermoelectric tradeoff to simultaneously enhance electrical conductance
and the Seebeck coefficient. Experimental realization via SAM devices
confirmed these predictions, demonstrating that the unique quantum
transport and orbital characteristics of single diradical molecules
translate effectively to scalable, technologically relevant large-area
monolayer junctions. This structural paradigm holds exceptional promise
for next-generation energy harvesting technologies.

Experimental Methods

SAM Formation and Characterization

Freshly prepared
substrates were immersed overnight in saturated solutions of R1–R3 in anhydrous toluene, which were
filtered through a 0.2 μm PTFE syringe filter before use. The
samples were then removed from the solution, thoroughly rinsed with
pure toluene, and dried under a N2 flow. All solvents were
degassed by N2 bubbling and three freeze–pump–thaw
cycles, and all transfer steps were performed in a N2-filled
glovebox (O2 < 0.1%). The AFM scratch method was used
to estimate the film thickness, following protocols reported in the
literature and in our previous work.
−

 AFM measurements were
performed using a Multimode 8 system (Bruker). Briefly, a 1 μm
× 1 μm area of the film was removed by scratching in contact
mode using SCANASYST-AIR probes with a resonant frequency of 70 kHz
and a spring constant of 0.4 N/m. The peak force set point was set
to 4 V during scratching. Subsequently, topographic images were acquired
in ScanAsyst Air mode over a larger 5 μm × 5 μm region.
During imaging, the peak force set point was automatically adjusted
by feedback control to prevent damage to the remaining film. The XPS
measurements were conducted using Thermo Scientific K-Alpha photoelectron
spectrometer equipped with a monochromated Al Kα source (1486.6
eV). The binding energies were calibrated by setting the Au 4f7/2 peak of the AuTS to 84.0 eV and the Ag 3d5/2 peak of the AgTS to 368.2 eV. Peak fitting was
performed using XPS Peak Fit software.

Thermoelectric Measurement

Thermoelectric measurements
were conducted following established protocols reported in our previous
studies.
−
,,
 All samples were positioned on the Peltier devices, which heat the
substrate to induce a temperature gradient (ΔT) across the molecular junctions. Significant heat-carrier scattering
at the metal/molecule and molecule/metal interfaces facilitated the
formation of ΔT.
,,
 To quantify the ΔT, the temperatures
of both the substrate and the ambient were monitored during the measurements.
Conical EGaIn electrodes were gently brought into contact with the
diradicaloid molecular films to form large-area molecular junctions.
The geometric contact areas of the junctions were confirmed via 480×
magnification optical microscopy, assuming a circular contact geometry,
and consistently maintained at approximately 1.9 × 109 nm2. To ensure reproducibility and eliminate potential
contamination, conical EGaIn electrodes were freshly fabricated prior
to each junction formation. Each junction was monitored for 30 s,
yielding 76 data points per junction. ΔV data
were collected to construct histograms, and each histogram shows single
Gaussian distributions. The mean (ΔV
mean) and standard deviation (σΔV
) values extracted from these distributions were used to construct
ΔV
mean–ΔT plots. Linear fitting of the ΔV
mean–ΔT data enabled the determination
of the Seebeck coefficient (S, μV/K) for the
large-area molecular junctions.

Tunneling Current Measurement

Electrical characterization
of the same junctions used for thermopower measurements was performed
in accordance with previously reported methods. All measurements were conducted under ambient conditions.
Each J–V trace consisted
of a voltage sweep sequence as follows: 0 V → +0.5 V →
0 V → −0.5 V → 0 V, corresponding to two complete
scans per trace. The contact area diameter was determined at high
magnification (480×), assuming a circular geometry, and this
value was used to calculate the current density (J, A/cm2). The yield (%) of operational junctions was calculated
as the ratio of functional junctions to the total number of junctions
tested, excluding those that short-circuited. Shorted junctions, which
were identified by currents reaching the compliance limit of the electrometer
(±0.105 A in this study), were excluded from further analysis
as they do not reflect the properties of the diradicaloid molecular
films and can skew current density distributions toward higher values.
Distributions of log-current density (log |J|) were analyzed by fitting to a Gaussian distribution, yielding
mean (μ
