Mechanisms of open and hidden placebo in stroke recovery: targeting neuroplasticity insights of the placebo effect – study protocol for a controlled, parallel, randomised clinical trial
Authors: Jorge Ortega-Márquez, Lucas Camargo, Anna Carolyna Gianlorenço, William Sosa, Randie Black-Schaffer, Qing Mei Wang, Felipe Fregni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStroke stands as one of the worldwide leading causes of disability, highlighting the need to develop new therapies. Although placebo effects in motor recovery have been reported to produce clinically meaningful improvements, the neural substrates underlying these effects, particularly across different modalities such as open-label and hidden placebo, remain unclear. This study aims to characterise the neurophysiological signatures of hidden and open placebo during motor-cortex stimulation in chronic stroke survivors and its correlation to motor recovery.Methods and analysisThis single-centre, parallel-group, controlled, randomised clinical trial will enrol 56 adults (≥6 months post-stroke) with upper-limb hemiparesis. Participants are block-randomised (2:2:2:1) to (i) open-label placebo tablets, (ii) hidden sham repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), (iii) no intervention or (iv) active low-frequency (1 Hz) rTMS over the contra lesional primary motor cortex. The primary endpoint is change in frontal alpha asymmetry (FAA) on high-density electroencephalogram from baseline to 2 weeks. Secondary endpoints include β-band premotor-to-M1 coherence and Fugl–Meyer Upper Extremity scores. Exploratory outcomes include additional motor performance measures (Jebsen–Taylor Hand Function Test and Nine-Hole Peg Test), corticospinal excitability (single-pulse and paired-pulse TMS) and patient-reported measures (mood, expectancy and behavioural activation–inhibition), all assessed from baseline to 2 weeks. Analyses will follow an intention-to-treat framework with multivariable regression adjusting for age, sex, stroke severity, lesion location and time since stroke.Ethics and disseminationThe study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (protocol #2023P000884). All participants will be required to provide informed consent before enrolling in the study. An open-label phase will be offered to participants allocated to any of the groups that received no active intervention (sham rTMS stimulation, open-label placebo or no-intervention groups). This phase will consist of a task-specific, upper-limb, home-based rehabilitation programme. The results of this study will be shared in peer-reviewed journals and presented at scientific conferences.Trial registration numberNCT05832567.

Introduction

Stroke stands as the second leading cause of disability worldwide with around 12.2 million incident cases and 143 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) lost.1 The increase of 32% of DALYs by stroke and the estimated related costs per year US$891 billion (equivalent to 1.12% of Gross Domestic Product) makes the burden of stroke an important global public health issue.2 3 During the last decades, the efforts on implementation of neurorehabilitation therapeutics have been intensified, especially approaching the recovery of motor disability which is the most affected neurological function after stroke.4 5 Despite these valuable efforts, different related factors and effects surrounding those interventions remain almost unexplored and limit the complete mechanisms and effects understanding.

Placebo groups in randomised clinical trials are typically used to minimise psychological effects of interventions, such as emotional investment, motivation and expectations.6 Interestingly, increasing evidence in rehabilitation challenges this conventional view by reporting unexpected results showing similar or even better outcomes in post-stroke motor recovery. For example, a meta-analysis studying the placebo effect of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) showed a similar motor improvement in both sham and active rTMS groups for at least 3 months.7 Additionally, a study combining fluoxetine and rTMS, the active fluoxetine group exhibited smaller motor recovery than placebo.8 The significance of this evidence raises questions and brings into discussion the billions of dollars spent, and unnecessary risks exposed during clinical trials. The truth is that, to date, the understanding of the mechanisms of placebo in post-stroke motor recovery remains almost unexplored. Placebo responses can be elicited through partially distinct pathways: ‘hidden’ (covert) placebo/sham procedures which are expected to minimise conscious expectancy and rely more strongly on implicit learning/conditioning from contextual cues, whereas open-label placebo (OLP) is administered transparently and may depend more on explicit cognitive processes such as expectancy, appraisal and agency even without deception.9–13 Consistent with this framework, OLP trials have reported clinically meaningful effects across several conditions.14 15

Neuroplasticity-based theories represent one of the most valuable frameworks for understanding the mechanisms of placebo effect in rehabilitation of neurological disorders such as its influence on the modification of pain, Parkinson’s disease symptomology and mood disorders. These theories incorporate the combination of behavioural and psychological factors interacting within the brain’s function and structure, leading to neuroplasticity adaptations, mainly in prefrontal cortical networks.16–19 A proposed mechanism behind the placebo effect in post-stroke motor recovery is based on the integration of reward and motivation activating on the dopaminergic corticostriatal circuitry to regulate the sensorimotor functions.20 21 Additionally, prior evidence has shown that expectation increases the activity in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and the nucleus accumbens (NA), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the connectivity between this area and motion-sensitive medial temporal sensory area (MT).22–25 Although these findings underscore the anatomical bases of placebo effect on post-stroke motor rehabilitation, there is still a critical need for the implementation of neurophysiological tools to quantify and confirm this structural evidence.

Brain oscillatory activity measured by electroencephalogram (EEG) stands as a promising tool to strengthen the rooted anatomical understanding of placebo effect as a result of it having been extensively used for the study of human behavioural mechanisms.26 27 Recent studies have shown frontal alpha asymmetry (FAA) may serve as a potential biomarker for quantifying placebo effect.28 Evidence has supported that FAA is linked to positive emotions and expectations and related behaviours, likely through its role in modulating amygdala reactivity and dopaminergic pathways.20 29–31 Given the extensive literature demonstrating the influence of placebo on prefrontal neural circuits activity, we hypothesise that placebo, through expectations effects, leads to activation of the VTA and the NA to enhance activity in the medial prefrontal cortex and cause an effect in subthalamic/thalamic nuclei that will project to the primary motor and premotor area, enhancing connectivity. We anticipate that this activity will be reflected in the regulation of FAA and increased beta-band premotor EEG connectivity.

This study aims to test whether the placebo effect can enhance focused treatments such as motor cortex stimulation. Moreover, our study will address the major challenge of having a comparable placebo when designing clinical trials involving behavioural and surgical interventions. By exploring the use of an unmatched placebo, our findings may redefine the framework of non-pharmacological trials—particularly in stroke recovery—by demonstrating that placebo responses can boost left FAA and beta-band premotor EEG connectivity. Methods and analysis

Study design

This is a controlled, parallel, randomised clinical trial. 56 subjects will be randomised using a web-based randomisation programme (www.randomization.com). We will use a 2:2:2:1 blocked allocation ratio to assign participants to one of the following groups, respectively: (1) open-label placebo (OLP) alone (16 subjects); (2) sham rTMS alone (16 subjects); (3) no intervention (16 subjects); or (4) active rTMS alone (8 subjects). The staff member conducting the randomisation will not be involved in any other aspect of the trial. The randomisation order will be kept in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Subjects will be allocated to their group according to the order of entrance into the stimulation phase (figure 1). During the study, participants allocated to the rTMS arms (active or sham stimulation) will remain blinded to stimulation condition, whereas participants allocated to the open-label placebo or no-intervention arms will remain unblinded. Outcome assessors will remain blinded to allocation, while personnel responsible for stimulation delivery and staff involved in the randomisation process will be unblinded.

This is a single-site trial, and all the related procedures will be conducted at Neuromodulation Center, Spaulding Rehabilitation Hospital, Cambridge, MA, USA.

Intervention

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS)

Low-frequency rTMS stimulation (active stimulation): during this stimulation session, the low-frequency rTMS will be delivered to the contralesional primary motor cortex. Low-frequency rTMS stimulation will be applied according to the following parameters: stimulation will be applied at intensity of 80% of the motor threshold (MT) (intensity could be adjusted if not comfortable to the subject), frequency of 1 Hz and 1200 pulses delivered as a single, continuous train lasting 20 min. This intervention is associated with significant motor gains in chronic32 and acute stroke33 populations and is considered a safer approach for individuals with chronic stroke.32

Open-label placebo

Open-label placebo will consist of a typical prescription medicine bottle of placebo pills made from microcrystalline cellulose with a label clearly marked ‘placebo pills - take 2 pills twice daily.’ We will use an open-label placebo strategy based on the few randomised trials that validated this approach.14 15 34 During the intervention, researchers will explain that while the placebo may have an effect, there is also uncertainty about its benefit. The interaction is designed to make participants feel comfortable taking an open-label placebo and to minimise negative feelings about placebo use. This interaction consists of a brief semi-scripted explanation of the OLP rationale that emphasises transparency and uncertainty of benefit, to standardise communication and manage expectations.

No intervention group (treatment as usual)

This group will continue with standard care and rehabilitation during the study; the protocol does not restrict or withhold any indicated therapies. We will explain the procedures in a semi-scripted manner, emphasising the following: (a) research is important to improve stroke rehabilitation; (b) the control group without open-label placebo and without rTMS is essential for comparison; (c) it is important to continue with the usual care during the study; (d) this is one of the most important groups for the study.

To standardise communication and manage expectations, participants in the open-label placebo, sham rTMS and no-intervention arms will receive a brief semi-scripted explanation of the study rationale and the importance of their group in the trial.

Open label phase

The open-label phase consists of a task-specific upper limb home-based rehabilitation at the end of participation to subjects allocated to any of the groups which received no active intervention (sham rTMS stimulation, open-label placebo and no intervention groups). This programme is based on the National Institutes of Health (NIH),35 National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS) guidelines and the American Stroke Association36 37 recommendations for improving disabilities related to motor and sensory impairments. The exercises are stratified by levels of intensity based on the subject’s level of impairment and include muscle stretching, flexibility and neuromuscular exercises. The programme will begin with three supervised sessions during the first week in the laboratory, led by a trained co-investigator, to review the instructions and proper technique. Participants will then complete 21 unsupervised home-based sessions (3×/week for 7 weeks − total of 25 PT sessions) to subjects allocated to the Sham rTMS stimulation, open-label placebo and no intervention groups. This opportunity is offered in order to reduce disappointment for not having received any intervention and perhaps minimise any possible nocebo effects.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

EEG - (FAA index)

EEG is a reliable tool for measuring electrical activity in the brain.38 Quantitative EEG analysis (QEEG) is a helpful marker in chronic stroke research.39–41 EEG will be recorded using a 64-channel actiCHamp amplifier EGI, Eugene, USA. Procedures for the EEG will include: (1) setting up (about 5 min); (2) providing instructions to the participant and waiting for signal stabilisation (about 5 min); (3) 5 min recording with eyes opened; (4) 5 min recording with eyes closed; (5) 8 min task-related conditioning. We will ensure optimal signal transduction with impedances below 50 kΩ. The task-related condition includes 60 randomised trials, divided equally among movement observation (MO), movement imagery (MI) and movement execution (ME).42 43 Each trial will involve initial fixation (on a cross on a screen), followed by a visual cue stating which task is to be performed (they will be given instructions that read ‘imagine’ and ‘clench’), and a video which will automatically play for observation. During the ME task, the subject will be asked to clench her/his unaffected hand first, and their affected hand after. There will be a 4 s rest period between each trial. The purpose of the task-related condition is to evaluate event-related desynchronisation (ERD) which reflects motor cortex activation.42 The electrophysiological data will be preprocessed and analysed in EEGLAB (Version 14.1.2b) Toolbox44 and in MATLAB r2019a (MathWorks, Inc.), following a standardised EEG preprocessing pipeline.45 Data will be down sampled to 250 Hz, re-referenced to the average of all electrodes and filtered (1–60 Hz) using Butterworth zero-phase high-pass and low-pass filters. Artefact removal and ocular correction will be performed with independent component analysis (ICA) using a slope algorithm to detect eye blinks and movements. Data will then be visually inspected, and any remaining artefacts will be rejected. Additionally, for power extraction, Fast Fourier Transformation with 2 s windows with 50% overlap will be applied.

The frontal alpha asymmetry score (FAA) will be calculated by subtracting the natural log-transformed alpha power in the left frontal region (F3, F7, Fp1) from the natural log-transformed power in the right frontal region (F4, F8, Fp2), consistent with previous evidence.29

Secondary outcomes

Motor function

Fugl-Meyer motor scale (FM) stands as a valid, responsive to change, and widely used for motor function and recovery assessment in stroke patients. Items are scored on a 3-point ordinal scale, being 0 the lowest and 2 the highest value. A higher score means a better outcome.

Premotor-M1 (PM-M1) connectivity β-band PM–M1 coherence - frontoparietal, inter-hemispheric, cortico-thalamic connectivity

Premotor-M1 (PM-M1) connectivity in EEG beta-band: EEG connectivity between premotor/prefrontal and motor areas will be used to assess motor gains from placebo and rTMS effects on motor learning (through functional measures such as Jebsen–Taylor Hand Function Test (JTHF) and pegboard) after 10 sessions. The Wu et al study46 found the best gains after 2 weeks of PT (R2=0.67), which is approximately the time point we selected for motor learning maximal gains. PM–M1 connectivity will be estimated using the spectral coherence method, which quantifies functional connectivity as a function of frequency.47 As done in prior studies,48–51 coherence will be computed with a multivariate autoregressive model via the Source Information Flow Toolbox (SIFT) in EEGLAB,44 focusing on the following electrode pairs: C3–F3, C4–F4, C3–Fp1 and C4–Fp2. We will measure two important EEG markers: (I) EEG alpha band (8–13 Hz) activity reflects underlying cortical activation29 52 and is central to the valence motivation model.53 FAA captures right–left frontal cortex activation differences, with increased activity contralateral to the affected side indicating greater approach motivation and positive affect.9 Multiple studies link left frontal activity to positive emotions9–11 and motivational traits,12 which will also be assessed using the Behavioral Activation and Inhibition System (BAS/BIS) scales.13 We will explore additional connectivity changes associated with placebo effects and motor recovery in stroke, such as the fronto-parietal interhemispheric connectivity (M1-M1 and PM-PM)47–49 and the corticothalamic connectivity (Fronto-thalamic and M1-thalamus).49–51

(II) We will perform a Low-Resolution Brain Electromagnetic Tomography Analysis (LORETA), using a realistic head model (6239 voxels, with a spatial resolution of 5 mm3) and EEG electrode coordinates (64 channels). We will perform functional connectivity using a voxel-wise approach to determine the regions of interest (ROIs) (M1, PM, thalamus, frontal and parietal areas). Functional connectivity will be analysed using voxel-wise lagged phase synchronisation, a non-linear method that measures the similarity of signals in the frequency domain based on normalised Fourier transforms.46 47

Secondary outcomes were pre-specified in the trial registry (NCT05832567). Outcomes not included in the registry are considered exploratory.

Single and paired-pulse TMS (corticospinal excitability and cortical inhibition)

Study sample

The target population of this study is adults with upper limb hemiparesis due to chronic stroke. We plan to enrol 56 subjects divided into 4 groups: (1) OLP alone (16 subjects); (2) sham rTMS alone (16 subjects); (3) no intervention (16 subjects); (4) active rTMS alone (8 subjects).

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation (table 2) of this study was based on (a) preliminary data on stroke8 and studies measuring placebo and motor intervention effects on the EEG markers.29 52 56 Thus, we considered studies in which placebo had an effect on EEG asymmetry and alpha power with a pooled effect size (ES) of 3.15; and also, a study showing the effect of training for motor deficit on EEG connectivity measures in patients with stroke that resulted in an ES of 2.7. We decided on a conservative approach and chose the smallest ES of 1.1 (changes in Fugl-Meyer scale upper extremity motor assessment score),8 assuming a type I error of 1% (alpha) and a power of 80%. The calculation was performed using a two-sample, two-tailed difference-in-means t-test framework, yielding a required total sample size of 44 participants for a key two-group comparison. This approach was selected to provide a conservative estimate for the planned multivariable regression analyses and to ensure adequate power for prespecified pairwise contrasts. To account for an anticipated 20% attrition rate (based on our previous study)8 and to preserve power for secondary/exploratory analyses, the target sample size was increased to 56 participants.

Data analysis

The collected data will be kept in a secured and protected database, accessible only to Institutional Review Board (IRB) trained and approved study staff. All analyses will be conducted on an intention-to-treat basis (using the method of multiple imputation for missing data).

The primary endpoint will be change in EEG FAA from baseline to visit 12 (2 weeks). To test the primary hypothesis, we will use multivariate regression models including all four study groups. Group differences from baseline to visit 12 will be evaluated with regression analyses, followed by pairwise comparisons. Models will adjust for important baseline variables, including sex and age (as biological factors), stroke severity, location and time since stroke, as well as cognition and infarct size (from medical records). Psychosocial factors such as motivation and mood will be included as potential moderators of open-label placebo effects, and thus, the interaction terms will also be tested in the models. After responding to the main question of this aim (whether open-label placebo induces a significant change in EEG FAA), effect modification (interaction) with emotional and motivational scales will be tested.

Secondary analyses will examine changes from baseline to visit 12 in β-band premotor-to-M1 (PM–M1) coherence and Fugl–Meyer Upper Extremity scores using the same modelling framework and covariate adjustments. For the secondary aim, the main contrasts will be open-label placebo vs no intervention and open-label placebo vs active rTMS. Exploratory analyses will evaluate additional motor performance measures (Jebsen–Taylor Hand Function Test and Nine-Hole Peg Test), as well as psychosocial factors including mood (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale), motivation/approach–avoidance traits (BAS/BIS), therapeutic relationship (CARE) and expectancy (placebo expectancy rating and Expectations of Treatment Questionnaire), as potential moderators of treatment effects alongside demographic and clinical characteristics. Exploratory analyses will also assess changes in corticospinal excitability (single-pulse and paired-pulse TMS measures) and patient-reported measures (mood, expectancy, behavioural activation–inhibition), assessed from baseline to visit 12. An independent monitoring committee will review data on recruitment, adherence and safety. Meetings will be held annually, on enrolment of 25% of the target sample, or in response to any reports of serious adverse events.

Discussion

This controlled, parallel, randomised clinical trial aims to investigate the mechanisms of the placebo effect of motor cortex stimulation for chronic stroke survivors. The primary aim of this study is to evaluate whether a hidden placebo produces a distinct EEG signature compared with no treatment and to investigate the relationship between this signature and the clinical outcomes induced by the placebo. In addition, we aim to evaluate the neural signature of an OLP compared with no treatment, assess whether active rTMS shares a similar signature with sham rTMS or OLP, and determine whether this signature is associated with motor outcomes.

The mechanisms of the placebo effect and its different modalities of induction remain incompletely understood. On one hand, hidden (covert) administration of placebo strips away conscious expectation, and any residual benefit is thought to reflect implicit learning and conditioning.9 Studies using a hidden placebo have demonstrated that the analgesic potency of morphine, diazepam and even deep-brain stimulation in Parkinson’s disease fell by 30%–70% when the same doses were delivered without the patient’s knowledge, indicating that overt treatment recruits additional opioid, dopaminergic and endocannabinoid cascades beyond the drug’s pharmacodynamics.10 11 OLP, by contrast, is administered transparently and is thought to derive its therapeutic benefit from explicit cognitive processes such as expectancy, positive appraisal and a sense of agency.12 Studies assessing the effects of OLPs on conditions such as back pain, cancer-related fatigue, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, major depression and irritable bowel syndrome have reported beneficial effects. Despite these recent findings, research on the underlying mechanisms of the placebo effect is still in its infancy.13 Our trial stands as a unique approach of not only comparing hidden (sham rTMS) and open-label placebo (inert pills) but also having a control group with no intervention as a reference. By comparing different placebo modalities and a no-treatment arm, our trial aims to offer a unique perspective on whether these induced placebo methods produce distinct or overlapping mechanisms and how each map onto motor recovery in chronic-stroke survivors, guiding towards smarter control-group design in future studies.

Neural biomarkers are emerging as key tools for uncovering the anatomical, psychological and neuroplastic mechanisms underlying the placebo effect.57 Among these, brain oscillatory activity possesses significant explanatory and mechanistic potential as it reflects feed-forward communication among synchronised neuronal assemblies and coordinates responses to internal and external stimuli.26 In fact, these oscillatory patterns have become valuable predictors of motor function and can help to inform clinical prognosis.58 59 For example, a study using an inert ‘ergogenic’ drink in athletes reported an increase in frontal alpha asymmetry and improved motor performance comparable to the effects of caffeine.29 Another trial investigating premotor cortico-muscular coherence in severely impaired stroke patients found that higher beta band premotor-to-M1 coherence predicted greater training gains and could be upregulated through reward-based feedback.60 Furthermore, a quantitative EEG study in patients with ischaemic stroke showed that the pairwise derived Brain Symmetry Index (pdBSI) and the (delta+theta)/(alpha+beta) ratio (DTABR) were significantly correlated with functional outcomes.61 Our study aims to deepen the exploration of brain oscillatory activity as a potential explanatory marker of the neuroplasticity mechanisms of the placebo effect by examining frontal alpha asymmetry and beta band premotor EEG connectivity, two biomarkers previously reported in placebo interventions related to both rehabilitation and motor performance.29 62

In this study, we anticipate that OLP, hidden sham rTMS and active rTMS may each differentially influence both subjective and objective outcomes, as well as neurophysiological markers. The most favourable scenario would be that OLP elicits improvements in both subjective outcomes (eg, perceived motor improvement, patient global impression of change) and objective measures (eg, Fugl-Meyer scores, Nine-Hole Peg Test), accompanied by measurable neurophysiological changes such as increased frontal alpha asymmetry FAA and enhanced premotor-to-M1 beta-band connectivity on EEG, as well as modulation of corticospinal excitability measured by TMS. Such a result would strengthen the hypothesis that placebo effects can recruit neuroplasticity mechanisms—particularly those mediated through dopaminergic corticostriatal and prefrontal–motor circuits—that translate into clinically meaningful motor gains.20–22 Indeed, prior work suggests that expectation can activate mesocorticolimbic pathways, including the ventral tegmental area and nucleus accumbens, with downstream effects on premotor and motor cortical connectivity.24 25

Alternatively, one possible outcome is that OLP enhances only subjective outcomes without corresponding changes in objective or neurophysiological measures. This would suggest a predominantly cognitive–emotional mechanism, aligning with studies showing that OLPs may act via explicit expectancy, appraisal and affective regulation, including modulation of prefrontal–amygdala networks and frontal asymmetry linked to positive emotion.28 30 31 Conversely, improvements in objective motor measures without corresponding subjective change would suggest implicit conditioning effects, which can operate independently of explicit awareness, as has been observed with hidden placebos in analgesia and Parkinson’s disease.9 11 Such a dissociation would highlight the role of non-conscious associative learning and sensorimotor adaptation in driving functional recovery.

Finally, if neurophysiological changes—such as altered FAA, increased PM–M1 coherence or shifts in corticospinal excitability—are observed without immediate clinical translation, this would underscore the sensitivity of EEG and TMS biomarkers in detecting early or subclinical plasticity not yet captured by functional scales. Similar findings have been reported in stroke cohorts where quantitative EEG markers predicted motor outcomes months before behavioural improvement became evident.46 61 By systematically exploring these possible scenarios, our study aims to disentangle the complex interplay between expectation, conditioning and neuroplasticity, thereby clarifying the conditions under which placebo responses contribute to rehabilitation. Such insights could inform the rational harnessing of placebo effects as therapeutic adjuncts in stroke recovery, reframing them from methodological nuisances into clinically valuable modulators of brain plasticity.

Limitations of this study. First, recruitment is limited to a single site, which reduces external validity. Second, despite using a validated sham coil, the sensory cues of low-frequency rTMS may still differ from real stimulation, risking partial unblinding; prior audits report correct guess rates of 40%–60% even in ‘double-blind’ designs.63 Third, expectation effects may spill over across arms, making it difficult to keep hidden and open placebo psychologically ‘pure’. Fourth, despite its potential, EEG signals are susceptible to ocular, muscular and movement artefacts, which are accentuated in hemiparetic patients; rigorous recording and cleaning protocols are therefore required to preserve a high-quality recording of brain oscillations.64

Trial status

The project has commenced. Participants are currently being recruited, and the trial is expected to be completed by 31 July 2026.
