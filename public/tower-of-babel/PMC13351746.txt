Diagnostic challenges of spontaneous heterotopic pregnancy with concurrent viable intrauterine gestation: a case report
Authors: Jennifer Owusu Adjei, Nana Abena Andowah Sackey, Emily Hewitt, Parveen Verasingam, Lee Dvorkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heterotopic pregnancy — the simultaneous coexistence of an intrauterine pregnancy and an ectopic pregnancy — is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition. A critical diagnostic pitfall is that the sonographic confirmation of a viable intrauterine pregnancy does not exclude a concurrent ectopic gestation. This report describes the case of a 26-year-old woman who presented at an estimated eight weeks of gestation with haemodynamic instability and free intra-abdominal fluid on focused abdominal sonography. Transvaginal ultrasound confirmed a viable intrauterine pregnancy with no sonographically identifiable adnexal mass, prompting initial conservative management on a working diagnosis of ruptured haemorrhagic corpus luteum. Over the following forty-eight hours, the patient's haemoglobin level fell from 105 g/L to 69 g/L and repeat imaging demonstrated markedly increased pelvic free fluid. Diagnostic and therapeutic laparoscopy confirmed a ruptured left tubal ectopic pregnancy; laparoscopic salpingectomy was performed and the intrauterine pregnancy was preserved. This case underscores that haemodynamic parameters and the trend in serial haemoglobin must guide clinical decision-making even when an intrauterine pregnancy has been confirmed sonographically, and that a low threshold for diagnostic laparoscopy is essential.

Introduction

Heterotopic pregnancy (HP) is the simultaneous occurrence of an intrauterine pregnancy (IUP) and an ectopic pregnancy at separate implantation sites [1], [2]. First reported in 1708 by Duverney as an incidental autopsy finding [1], HP was long considered exceedingly rare, with a calculated incidence of approximately 1 in 30,000 spontaneous pregnancies [1]. Contemporary estimates place the incidence between 1 in 100 and 1 in 3900 in pregnancies conceived following the use of assisted reproductive technology (ART) [1], [3], [4].

The fallopian tube is the most common ectopic site, accounting for approximately 90–95% of cases; less frequent locations include the interstitial tube, the ovary, the cervix, a caesarean scar, and the abdominal cavity [3], [5], [6]. The clinical presentation overlaps with that of isolated ectopic pregnancy — pelvic pain, haemodynamic compromise, and haemoperitoneum — but diagnosis is complicated by the false reassurance that may follow sonographic identification of a coexisting IUP [2], [3], [7]. The RCOG's Green-top Guideline No. 21 explicitly notes that serum β-human chorionic gonadotrophin (β-hCG) is of limited diagnostic value in HP because of the concurrent IUP contribution [8]. Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) is the diagnostic modality of choice [8], though its reported sensitivity ranges widely, from 26.3% to 92.4% [9], [10], and as few as one-third of HP cases are identified pre-operatively in older case series [3]. This report presents a case of spontaneous HP in a young woman with no recognised risk factors, in whom the initial focus on a confirmed viable IUP delayed diagnosis until clinical deterioration prompted operative intervention.

Case Presentation

Initial Presentation

A 26-year-old woman (gravida 2, para 1) presented to the emergency department with a one-day history of sudden-onset lower abdominal pain. By her last menstrual period, her estimated gestational age was eight weeks. She had delivered her first child by spontaneous vaginal delivery several months earlier and had recently confirmed an unplanned but desired pregnancy. Her past medical, surgical, and gynaecological history was otherwise unremarkable. She was on no regular medications and had no known drug allergies. There was no history of pelvic inflammatory disease, prior ectopic pregnancy, tubal surgery, use of an intrauterine contraceptive device, smoking, or fertility treatment.

On arrival in the resuscitation bay, her blood pressure was 70/30 mmHg and stabilised with crystalloid resuscitation. Focused abdominal sonography demonstrated free intra-abdominal fluid. Venous blood gas analysis revealed a haemoglobin level of 105 g/L and a lactate level of 3.5 mmol/L. She denied vaginal bleeding, syncope, shoulder-tip pain, or change in bowel or urinary function. Abdominal examination revealed generalised tenderness, most pronounced suprapubically and in the left iliac fossa, with voluntary guarding but no rebound tenderness. A ruptured ectopic pregnancy was the leading differential; the patient was consented for emergency laparoscopy and the on-call gynaecology consultant and anaesthetic team were informed.

Prior to theatre, bedside transabdominal ultrasonography identified a small volume of free fluid in the right upper quadrant and a visible intrauterine pregnancy, prompting reconsideration of the working diagnosis. Transvaginal ultrasound confirmed a single viable intrauterine pregnancy with fetal cardiac activity and a crown–rump length corresponding to approximately eight weeks of gestation. A right corpus luteum was identified, the left ovary appeared sonographically normal, and only a minimal volume of free fluid was visualised in the pouch of Douglas. No adnexal mass or extrauterine gestational sac was identified. A repeat venous blood gas demonstrated a stable haemoglobin level of 106 g/L and a lactate level of 3.9 mmol/L. On this basis — a confirmed viable intrauterine pregnancy, the absence of any adnexal ectopic mass, haemodynamic stabilisation, and minimal pouch-of-Douglas fluid — a working diagnosis of ruptured haemorrhagic corpus luteum was made. Emergency theatre was stood down, and ward admission with intravenous analgesia, hourly observations, and serial full blood count was instituted.

Clinical Course and Management

Two days after admission, the patient reported progressive abdominal pain with a new epigastric component. Examination demonstrated left iliac fossa and epigastric tenderness with voluntary guarding. Repeat transvaginal ultrasound confirmed a live intrauterine pregnancy but showed markedly increased pelvic free fluid; both ovaries appeared normal and no adnexal mass was identified. Transabdominal ultrasound, arranged to evaluate non-gynaecological causes, demonstrated a bulky, heterogeneous pancreatic tail with peripancreatic fat stranding and diffuse minimal ascites. The liver, gallbladder, kidneys, spleen, and appendix were unremarkable. Serum amylase, normal on admission, was again normal. In the absence of cholelithiasis, alcohol exposure, recent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, hypertriglyceridaemia, or any other recognised precipitant for pancreatitis, and given a clinical picture more consistent with progressive intra-abdominal haemorrhage, acute pancreatitis was felt to be inadequate as an explanation. A fall in haemoglobin level from 105 g/L to 69 g/L within forty-eight hours, combined with the sonographic increase in pelvic free fluid, confirmed clinically significant ongoing intra-abdominal haemorrhage.

Following multidisciplinary discussion, a decision was made to proceed to diagnostic and therapeutic laparoscopy. The patient was counselled regarding the small but real risk of miscarriage of the intrauterine pregnancy, and cross-matched blood was made available. Intraoperative findings confirmed a ruptured left tubal heterotopic pregnancy with significant haemoperitoneum. Laparoscopic salpingectomy was performed, the pelvis was lavaged, and haemostasis was secured. The patient received intra- and post-operative red cell transfusion. The intrauterine pregnancy demonstrated continued fetal cardiac activity on early post-operative transabdominal ultrasonography. Postoperative recovery was uneventful.

Discussion

This case illustrates a well-documented diagnostic pitfall: sonographic confirmation of a viable IUP must not be taken to exclude a concurrent ectopic gestation [3], [7], [11]. The risk is not confined to women undergoing fertility treatment. Spontaneous HP can occur in the absence of any recognised risk factor, as in this case, in which the patient had no history of pelvic inflammatory disease, prior ectopic pregnancy, tubal or pelvic surgery, intrauterine contraception, smoking, or ART [4], [11].

TVUS is the diagnostic modality of choice for ectopic pregnancy and is recommended by the RCOG [8]. Its sensitivity for the ectopic component of HP is, however, highly operator-dependent: reported figures range from 26.3% to 92.4% [9], [10], and approximately one-third of HP cases were diagnosed sonographically pre-operatively in older comparative series [3]. The visualised IUP appears to divert attention from a careful inspection of the adnexa, interstitial segments, cornua, cervix, and pouch of Douglas. In the present case, two sequential examinations failed to identify the tubal ectopic component. Serum β-hCG was not helpful and the RCOG guideline explicitly notes its limited diagnostic value in HP because of the contribution of the IUP to circulating hCG [8].

A particular feature of this case was the incidental sonographic finding of peripancreatic fat stranding, which briefly introduced acute pancreatitis as a competing differential. The Atlanta criteria require two of three features (characteristic pain, lipase or amylase greater than three times the upper limit of normal, and characteristic imaging); a normal serum amylase combined with the absence of any identifiable precipitant rendered acute pancreatitis inadequate as an explanation. In any pregnant patient with progressive free fluid and a falling haemoglobin level, intra-abdominal haemorrhage from a ruptured ectopic gestation must remain the working diagnosis. When clinical and radiological findings are discordant, clinical findings must take precedence.

When surgery is indicated, the laparoscopic approach is preferred in the haemodynamically stabilised patient, with established advantages of shorter operative time, reduced blood loss, faster recovery, and lower analgesic requirement [7], [8], [12]. For a tubal HP, salpingectomy is generally favoured over salpingotomy because the risk of persistent trophoblast cannot be safely monitored by serial β-hCG in the presence of an IUP [8], [12]. The prognosis for the concurrent IUP, although guarded, is in fact reasonable: Li et al. reported a live birth rate of 70.5% (93/132) following surgical management of HP after IVF–embryo transfer [10], and the systematic review by Oancea et al. found that the majority of spontaneous HP cases with an otherwise unaffected IUP progressed satisfactorily after surgical intervention, with no consistent excess of fetal anomaly attributable to first-trimester surgery [4]. The patient was counselled accordingly.

Conclusion

The identification of a viable intrauterine pregnancy on ultrasound does not exclude a concurrent ectopic gestation. In any pregnant patient with haemodynamic instability, falling haemoglobin levels, or progressive intra-abdominal free fluid, heterotopic pregnancy must remain in the differential diagnosis irrespective of mode of conception, and a low threshold for diagnostic laparoscopy is essential.
