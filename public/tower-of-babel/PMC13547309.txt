Blockchain-enabled data management system of internet of medical things
Authors: Tingyu Xu, Guanya Zhu, Qiang Wei, Yun Liu, Lindu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) faces critical security challenges, including vulnerability to cyber-attacks, data forgery, and unauthorized access, due to its historically centralized architecture and insufficient built-in device security. This paper presents an integrated consortium blockchain-enabled data management system for IoMT environments, focusing on secure and auditable management of data generated by heterogeneous medical instruments. The system implements a multi-layered architecture comprising data, network, consensus, contract, and application layers. It employs RAFT for ledger commitment among authorized consortium nodes, SHA256 hashing and RSA digital signatures for transaction integrity verification and source authentication, and an RBAC-centered hybrid authorization model with metadata-index-assisted retrieval. Under a standardized workload of 10,000 health-data transactions across 4–10 consortium nodes, the proposed system achieved a throughput of 260–480 transactions per second and an average index-assisted query latency of 45–65 ms. Throughput decreased as the number of nodes increased, whereas query latency remained below 65 ms across all evaluated configurations. The proposed consortium blockchain framework supports integrity-protected, access-controlled, and auditable IoMT data management. Within the evaluated simulation setting, the system achieved sub-65 ms index-assisted query latency. Further deployment-oriented validation is required to characterize consensus and propagation latency, computational and storage overhead, energy consumption, and crash-fault recovery performance.Keywords: Blockchain, Internet of Medical Things (IoMT), Data management, Access control, Consortium blockchainSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The Internet of Medical Things (IoMT), an ecosystem of interconnected medical instruments, wearable sensors, and healthcare information systems, is fundamentally transforming modern healthcare delivery by enabling real-time patient monitoring, remote diagnosis, and data-driven clinical decision-making1–3. This paradigm shift, often termed Healthcare 5.0, promises unprecedented levels of personalized treatment, operational efficiency, and expanded access to medical services, particularly for chronic disease management and aging populations4,5. Large-scale Remote Patient Monitoring (RPM) initiatives are being increasingly deployed, with healthcare providers managing thousands of patients through IoMT platforms in home environments6. However, this rapid digital transformation has introduced critical security challenges. Medical instruments, historically designed for closed environments, often lack robust built-in security mechanisms, exhibiting widespread vulnerabilities including inadequate encryption protocols, weak authentication methods, and irregular firmware update capabilities7,8. These deficiencies create an expansive attack surface, exposing healthcare systems to cyber threats such as data forgery, ransomware, Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks, and unauthorized remote control of life-critical devices9,10. Healthcare data has become the most targeted asset for cybercriminals, with ENISA reporting that 53% of all cyber incidents affect healthcare providers, and patient data accounts for 30% of all targeted assets11. The consequences extend beyond financial losses to include misdiagnosis, treatment delays, and direct threats to patient safety12. Therefore, establishing robust, secure, and trustworthy data management frameworks for IoMT environments represents a critical research imperative with profound implications for public health and national security13.

Significant research efforts have been directed toward addressing IoMT security challenges through various technological approaches. Early work focused on conventional security mechanisms, including encryption standards, intrusion detection systems, and access control protocols adapted from traditional IT environments14,15. However, these solutions often prove inadequate for resource-constrained IoMT devices and fail to address the fundamental issue of trust in centralized architectures16. The emergence of blockchain technology has catalyzed a new research direction, leveraging its inherent characteristics of decentralization, immutability, and cryptographic security. Numerous studies have explored blockchain applications in healthcare data management. Chen et al. proposed a blockchain-based preserving and sharing system for medical data privacy, demonstrating improved security through distributed ledger technology17. Soni et al. developed a blockchain-based user authentication and data-sharing framework for healthcare industries, addressing identity management challenges18. Chenthara et al. introduced Healthchain, a privacy-preserving framework built on Hyperledger Fabric that utilizes InterPlanetary File System (IPFS) for electronic health record storage19. Pandey and Litoriya examined blockchain implementation for large-scale healthcare services, emphasizing patient-centric security models20. Systematic reviews by Ehteshami and Sattari identified China and Italy as leading contributors to blockchain telemedicine research, with applications spanning electronic health records, telecare, and e-health services21. Li et al. conducted a comprehensive systematic review of privacy preservation in blockchain-based healthcare data sharing, analyzing blockchain details, applied privacy methods, and regulatory compliance22. Their Technology Readiness Level (TRL) assessment revealed that most solutions remain at proof-of-concept stage (TRL3), highlighting the gap between experimental designs and real-world deployment. Despite these advances, critical gaps persist. Most existing research focuses on general healthcare data or electronic health records, with limited attention to the unique challenges posed by large medical instruments23. Studies specifically addressing IoMT device data management remain scarce, particularly for scenarios involving magnetic resonance devices, radiological equipment, and clinical monitors that generate massive volumes of sensitive patient data24. Furthermore, scalability concerns, interoperability with legacy hospital systems, and the absence of standardized access control mechanisms for multi-stakeholder healthcare environments remain inadequately addressed25,26.

Consortium blockchain technology has recently emerged as a particularly promising approach for healthcare applications, offering a balanced solution between the openness of public blockchains and the restrictiveness of private chains27. Unlike public blockchains that must contend with malicious nodes and require extensive computational resources for consensus, consortium blockchains operate within a trusted network of pre-verified institutions, enabling higher transaction throughput and more efficient governance28. This architectural choice aligns naturally with healthcare ecosystems, where multiple hospitals, research institutions, and regulatory bodies require controlled data sharing while maintaining patient privacy29. The RAFT consensus algorithm, in particular, has demonstrated effectiveness in permissioned blockchain environments by providing crash fault tolerance with lower computational overhead compared to Byzantine fault-tolerant protocols30. Recent advances in hybrid architectures combining blockchain with edge computing have shown promise for IoMT applications. Khan et al. proposed a lightweight scalable hybrid authentication framework integrating blockchain Hyperledger consortium networks with edge computing, achieving 98.33% authentication efficiency and reduced latency31. Similarly, research integrating blockchain with edge-fog-cloud computing layers has demonstrated improved data access time and system reliability for smart health environments32. Quantum-resistant authentication schemes incorporating blockchain with Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) are also emerging to address future security threats from quantum computing advances33. These methodological innovations suggest that consortium blockchain-based systems, properly designed with efficient consensus mechanisms and layered architectures, can simultaneously address security, scalability, and interoperability challenges in IoMT data management34,35. Despite these advances, secure management of data generated by heterogeneous medical instruments remains challenging for several reasons. First, IoMT environments involve diverse device types and data categories, requiring standardized acquisition and transaction handling across heterogeneous sources. Second, medical-instrument-generated data must be shared among multiple stakeholders under differentiated and context-sensitive authorization requirements, while preserving data provenance and traceable access records. Third, blockchain-based integrity protection alone does not ensure efficient retrieval of committed clinical records, which is essential for practical healthcare data interaction. Existing studies have mainly emphasized electronic health record sharing, privacy preservation, authentication, or secure storage, but have not sufficiently integrated medical-instrument-oriented data acquisition, differentiated and context-constrained authorization, verifiable metadata-index-assisted retrieval, and auditable access management within a unified permissioned IoMT workflow. Accordingly, the research gap addressed in this study is the absence of an integrated framework that jointly supports secure acquisition, controlled interaction, efficient retrieval, and traceable access for health data generated by heterogeneous medical instruments. Building upon these foundations, this paper develops an integrated consortium blockchain-enabled data management system for IoMT environments, with particular emphasis on data generated by heterogeneous medical instruments. The proposed system implements a multi-layered architecture encompassing data, network, consensus, contract, and application layers. The system utilizes RAFT consensus for efficient ledger commitment among authorized consortium nodes and employs SHA256 hashing with RSA digital signatures for data-integrity verification and source authentication. A metadata index incorporating block hash values, transaction identifiers, transaction categories, classification indices, digital signatures, timestamps, public keys, and storage addresses enables rapid data localization and retrieval. The system implements an RBAC-centered hybrid authorization model for medical instrument collection clients, data interaction management clients, and data consumption clients, supporting role-differentiated data governance and context-constrained access control. Accordingly, the contribution of this study lies not in introducing a fundamentally new blockchain protocol, consensus algorithm, or cryptographic primitive, but in integrating established mechanisms into an IoMT-oriented framework that supports secure acquisition, controlled sharing, verifiable retrieval, and traceable access for medical-instrument-generated health data. This integrated design provides a permissioned and auditable approach to IoMT data management and establishes the basis for evaluating the feasibility and limitations of blockchain-supported management of medical-instrument-generated health data.

Methods

Design goals and blockchain selection

The primary objective of the proposed framework is to establish a secure, trustworthy, and efficient data management system for IoMT environments, with a particular focus on data generated by large medical instruments. To achieve this, the system must guarantee data integrity, provide fine-grained access control, ensure tamper-proof auditability, and maintain acceptable performance in terms of throughput and query speed10,13. Blockchain technology, with its inherent characteristics of decentralization, immutability, and cryptographic security, offers a promising foundation5,17. Based on the specific requirements of the healthcare domain—where participants are known entities such as hospitals and regulatory bodies—the consortium blockchain model was selected as the most suitable architectural choice27. Unlike public blockchains that must contend with malicious nodes and require extensive computational resources, consortium blockchains operate within a trusted network of pre-verified institutions, enabling higher transaction throughput and more efficient governance while maintaining a high degree of security and privacy28,29. This aligns naturally with the multi-institutional, collaborative nature of modern healthcare ecosystems26.

System architecture

The proposed consortium blockchain-based data management system is structured into a multi-layered architecture, comprising a data layer, a network layer, a consensus layer, a contract layer, and an application layer, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This layered design promotes modularity, scalability, and separation of concerns4,7. The data layer is responsible for the fundamental storage of blocks and the indexing mechanism that enables rapid data retrieval. The network layer governs the peer-to-peer communication network among all blockchain nodes, ensuring reliable data propagation. The consensus layer implements the RAFT consensus algorithm to achieve agreement on the state of the ledger across all participating nodes28,30. The contract layer manages access control permissions by defining and enforcing rules for different types of clients and transactions. More specifically, the proposed authorization framework follows an RBAC-centered hybrid model. Under routine operation, permissions are assigned according to three predefined roles. Medical instrument collection clients are authorized to submit clinical data transactions generated by registered devices; data interaction management clients are authorized to manage entity information, including patient–instrument relationship records, within their designated scope; and data consumption clients are authorized to retrieve permitted clinical records and generate interactive information transactions for audit purposes. In addition to role membership, each request is evaluated against contextual constraints, including transaction type, target-data scope, authorization validity period, requester identity, and emergency-access status. Therefore, regular operations are governed primarily by role-based permissions, whereas exceptional or time-constrained operations are further restricted through attribute-based policy conditions. Permission revocation and dynamic role updates are managed as policy-management transactions initiated by an authorized access-control administrator. After validation and commitment through the consortium blockchain, the updated policy state becomes effective for subsequent requests, thereby invalidating revoked permissions or activating approved role assignments while preserving the immutability of previously committed records. For emergency clinical scenarios, the framework defines a time-limited and scope-restricted break-glass access procedure. An authenticated clinical requester must submit the emergency justification, target-data scope, timestamp, and validity period; once authorized, the access event is recorded as an interactive information transaction on the blockchain for subsequent audit and accountability. Finally, the application layer provides the interface for interacting with the IoMT ecosystem, encompassing functionalities such as health data collection from instruments, permission configuration, and data querying, display, and modification.

Blockchain architecture.

Block and index design

To establish the blockchain, the structure of a block was first defined13. As shown in Fig. 1, each block consists of a block header and a block body. The block header contains critical metadata, including the previous block’s hash (creating an immutable chain), the public key and digital signature for verification, the Merkle tree root hash (summarizing all transactions in the block), a timestamp, and encrypted transaction order identifiers. The block body stores the actual health data transactions, encompassing diverse IoMT data types such as monitoring data, ventilator data, blood pressure readings, dialysis data, transfusion records, ECG data, and analgesic pump data. To address the challenge of managing large volumes of health data and to enable efficient data synchronization and zero-knowledge proof capabilities, the system stores only the data hashes on the blockchain, not the raw data itself3,19. Furthermore, a critical innovation in the data layer is the addition of an index module. This module maintains a comprehensive mapping table indexed by key attributes, including block hash value, block number, transaction ID, transaction type, classification index, storage address (pointing to off-chain storage if used), digital signature, timestamp, and public key. This index is generated based on transaction type and facilitates rapid location of specific blocks and efficient interaction with health data, significantly improving query performance7. Unlike healthcare blockchain frameworks that mainly store encrypted hashes or off-chain pointers for integrity verification and data sharing19,36, the proposed index module establishes a verifiable association among transaction type, classification index, block hash, digital signature, timestamp, public key, and storage address. This design enables authorized clients to retrieve specific medical-instrument-generated records without scanning the entire ledger and allows subsequent access events to be traced back to the corresponding committed transactions. Therefore, the index module serves not only as a query-acceleration mechanism but also as a linkage layer between cryptographic verification, role-based data interaction, and auditability.

Data integrity verification, authentication, and cryptographic selection

Data integrity, source authenticity, and non-repudiation are supported through a combination of cryptographic primitives. The SHA256 hashing algorithm is employed to generate a fixed-size digest of the health data, thereby supporting integrity verification1. Any alteration to the data would result in a different digest, making unauthorized modification detectable9. For source authentication and non-repudiation, the system utilizes the RSA digital signature algorithm12. During a data transaction, the authenticated collection client signs the hash of the health data using its private key and submits the signed transaction to the consortium blockchain network. Upon receipt, the validating node verifies the digital signature using the sender’s public key and independently recomputes the hash of the received data. Consistency between the verified signed digest and the recomputed digest confirms that the transaction originated from the authenticated sender and that the transmitted data have not been modified. This mechanism therefore supports source authentication, non-repudiation, and detection of data tampering during data interaction14.

The selection of RSA in the current prototype was based on its standardized digital-signature functionality, mature implementation support, and straightforward integration with the permissioned blockchain client and node environment. Importantly, the proposed architecture does not require low-power medical sensing devices to execute blockchain consensus or digital-signature operations directly; instead, transaction signing and verification are performed by authenticated collection clients and consortium blockchain nodes deployed in a server-supported environment. Therefore, RSA is employed in this study as an implementation-level mechanism for transaction integrity verification, source authentication, and non-repudiation, rather than as a claim of optimality for resource-constrained IoMT endpoints.

Nevertheless, ECDSA remains a relevant alternative for deployments in which digital-signature operations must be executed directly on wearable, implantable, or otherwise resource-constrained medical devices. According to NIST security-strength guidance, comparable security can be achieved with substantially shorter elliptic-curve keys than RSA keys; for example, RSA-2048 and ECC keys of 224–255 bits correspond to approximately 112-bit security strength, whereas RSA-3072 and ECC keys of 256–383 bits correspond to approximately 128-bit security strength37,38. Thus, ECDSA may reduce key-storage and communication overhead in device-constrained deployment scenarios. The present study focuses on RSA-based verification within a gateway/node-assisted consortium architecture; comparative evaluation of RSA- and ECDSA-based implementations under device-side IoMT constraints will be investigated in future work. Beyond lightweight elliptic-curve alternatives, quantum-secure authentication mechanisms represent another relevant direction for long-term IoMT security. Post-quantum digital-signature schemes, such as the NIST-standardized Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA), are designed to preserve signature security against adversaries equipped with large-scale quantum computers. In parallel, blockchain-enabled quantum authentication schemes incorporating Quantum Key Distribution have recently been investigated for secure communication in Internet of Nano Medical Things environments33. These approaches address a stronger future-oriented threat model than that considered in the present prototype and may introduce different implementation and communication requirements. Accordingly, the current study does not claim resistance to quantum-enabled attacks; comparative evaluation of RSA, ECDSA, and quantum-secure authentication alternatives under IoMT resource and latency constraints remains a direction for future work.

File storage and ledger management

Transaction orders are categorized into three primary types: clinical data (e.g., the health data itself with associated instrument ID), entity information (e.g., patient identity and instrument relationship data managed by the data interaction client), and interactive transaction types (e.g., records of data access by consumer clients). This classification is crucial for implementing differentiated access control. During the block creation and chaining process, the index module automatically records the relevant metadata—transaction ID, type, index, timestamp, digital signature, and public key—for each transaction, generating the aforementioned mapping table to accelerate data access. Through its consortium-based distributed ledger, the system supports distributed validation and tamper-evident maintenance of committed transaction records among authorized nodes2. However, because indexed retrieval in the current prototype is supported by an Oracle 11 g-based metadata service, the overall implementation should be regarded as a hybrid architecture rather than a fully decentralized data-management solution. In the storage procedure, each block’s data is serialized into bytecode and written to data files (e.g., .dat files). Concurrently, all associated metadata is captured and stored in a database by the index module, creating a comprehensive and searchable record of the blockchain’s state. Blockchain applications

Building upon the methodological framework described in Section “Methods”, this section presents the detailed implementation and operational workflow of the proposed consortium blockchain-enabled IoMT data management system. The system was implemented using JavaScript for blockchain node logic and client interfaces, with Oracle 11 g employed to realize the index module for high-performance query execution. The RAFT consensus algorithm was configured to establish a node-to-node communication network, enabling rapid block certification and synchronization28,30. Transaction integrity and source authenticity were protected using SHA256 hashing and RSA digital signatures, while authorized data exchange was conducted through controlled communication interfaces14. The overall architecture, illustrating the interactions among medical instrument collection clients, data interaction management clients, consumption clients, and blockchain nodes, is depicted in Fig. 2. The following subsections provide a comprehensive explanation of node authentication mechanisms and the complete data interaction workflow.

Node authentication and consensus mechanism

In a consortium blockchain environment, rigorous node authentication is fundamental to establishing a trusted network perimeter29. The proposed system categorizes blockchain nodes into two primary functional roles: validators, which verify the correctness and legitimacy of incoming transactions, and generators, which are responsible for bundling verified transactions into new blocks. Both roles are integral to the RAFT consensus algorithm’s operation, which was selected for its crash fault tolerance and suitability for permissioned networks where the number of participating nodes is relatively small and known a priori28,30.

The RAFT consensus algorithm organizes nodes into three distinct states based on an election mechanism: leader node, follower node, and candidate node28. All nodes begin as followers. If a follower does not receive communication from a leader within a specified timeout period, it transitions to a candidate state and initiates a new election term by requesting votes from other nodes. The node that receives a majority of votes becomes the new leader for that term. This leader election process ensures chronological rotation of leadership and maintains system liveness even in the event of node failures30.

Once a leader is elected, it assumes responsibility for log replication and client request processing. The operational workflow is as follows: when a data system client (e.g., a collection client) sends a transaction request, the request is routed to the current leader node. Upon receiving the request, the leader node appends the transaction to its local log and then sends parallel append-entries messages to all follower nodes. Each follower node independently verifies the correctness of the received transaction data, validating the digital signature, data format, and adherence to access control policies. After successful verification, followers send an acknowledgment back to the leader. Upon receiving confirmations from a majority of nodes (a quorum), the leader considers the transaction committed and notifies all followers in the next heartbeat message to update their local copies of the blockchain, thereby finalizing the new block28. This distributed consensus mechanism ensures that all non-faulty nodes maintain a consistent and tamper-evident ledger, thereby reducing single-point-of-failure risk at the ledger-commitment level. However, centralized dependency remains in the current metadata-index implementation and is discussed as a limitation in Section “System limitations and future research directions”.

Data interaction workflow

The data trading and interaction process involves four primary entities: the medical instrument collection client, the data interaction management client, the data consumption client, and the blockchain node network (comprising leader and follower nodes). The system defines three distinct transaction types corresponding to these client roles: clinical data transactions (originating from collection clients), entity information transactions (originating from management clients), and interactive information transactions (originating from consumption clients). Each transaction type is subject to differentiated access control permissions, ensuring that clients can only perform operations authorized for their role14,19. All data exchanges utilize a standardized, normalized data transmission interface to ensure interoperability across heterogeneous medical instruments and hospital information systems3. The complete data interaction sequence is illustrated in Fig. 3, in which the numbered procedures correspond to Steps 1–8 described below.

Numbered workflow of secure health-data interaction in the proposed consortium blockchain-enabled IoMT system.

Step 1: Data Acquisition by Collection Client. The medical instrument collection client interfaces directly with IoMT devices—including ventilators, patient monitors, infusion pumps, hemodialysis machines, and ECG devices—via device-specific collectors. These collectors retrieve raw health data along with corresponding instrument identifiers and timestamps.

Step 2: Transaction Generation and Signing. The collection client structures the acquired data into a clinical data transaction. It computes a SHA256 hash of the transaction payload and signs this hash using its RSA private key. The signed transaction, containing the data, the digital signature, and the client’s public key, is then transmitted to the blockchain node network.

Step 3: Transaction Propagation to Leader. The transaction is routed through the network layer to the current RAFT leader node, which assumes responsibility for initiating consensus.

Step 4: Parallel Verification by Follower Nodes. The leader node broadcasts the transaction to all follower nodes. Each follower independently performs verification: (a) decrypting the signature using the sender’s public key to retrieve the original hash, (b) recalculating the hash of the received data, and (c) comparing the two hash values. Successful verification confirms data integrity and authenticity14.

Step 5: Consensus and Block Generation. Verified transactions are aggregated into a candidate block by the leader node. The leader appends this block to its local blockchain and sends a commit notification to all followers.

Step 6: Ledger Update and Indexing. Upon receiving the commit notification, each follower node updates its local copy of the blockchain by appending the new block. Simultaneously, the index module extracts metadata from the new block—including transaction ID, transaction type, timestamp, block hash, and storage address—and updates the mapping table in the Oracle database. This indexing operation enables rapid, content-based retrieval of health data without requiring full blockchain scans7.

Step 7: Data Accessibility and Query. Following successful chaining, the data becomes accessible to authorized clients. The data interaction management client can now query, display, and, where permitted, update patient identity information and instrument relationship data (entity information transactions). The data consumption client, representing third-party systems or researchers, can request access to specific health data through the blockchain network.

Step 8: Audit Logging of Data Access. To maintain a comprehensive audit trail, all data access operations initiated by consumption clients are themselves recorded as interactive information transactions on the blockchain. Each access event—including the identity of the requester, the data accessed, and the timestamp—is immutably logged, providing a transparent and tamper-proof record for regulatory compliance and security auditing10,15. Under the RBAC-centered hybrid authorization model, routine access requests are validated according to registered client roles and permitted transaction categories, whereas policy changes and emergency access requests are additionally evaluated using contextual attributes. Permission-revocation and role-update transactions modify the active authorization state only after blockchain commitment. Emergency access is restricted by requester identity, target-data scope, justification, and validity period, and all such access events are immutably logged for post-event auditing.

This comprehensive workflow ensures that all health data, from initial acquisition through final consumption, is cryptographically protected, consensually validated, and immutably recorded. The combination of differentiated access control, standardized interfaces, and robust indexing mechanisms creates a secure yet efficient platform for IoMT data management, addressing the critical vulnerabilities of traditional centralized systems while maintaining practical usability for healthcare providers. The formal algorithmic representations of transaction generation and digital signing, RBAC-centered hybrid authorization, transaction verification and RAFT-based commitment, and metadata index updating, together with an operation-level cost and complexity analysis, are provided in Appendix A.

Results and discussion

To evaluate the performance and scalability of the proposed consortium blockchain-enabled IoMT data management system, a comprehensive simulation environment was established. The experimental testbed comprised 10 Windows Server 2016 (64-bit) virtual machines, each equipped with 16 GB RAM and an Intel Xeon 2.4 GHz processor. The SHA256 hashing algorithm and RSA digital signature operations were implemented using standard cryptographic libraries to ensure computational realism. The RAFT consensus algorithm was configured with heartbeat intervals of 50 ms and election timeouts randomly selected between 150 ms and 300 ms to prevent split votes28,30. The blockchain network was configured with varying numbers of nodes—specifically 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10 nodes—to systematically investigate the relationship between network size and system performance. For each configuration, the system processed a standardized workload of 10,000 health data transactions, with each transaction representing a typical clinical data record of approximately 2 KB. Throughput was measured as the number of transactions successfully committed to the blockchain per second (TPS), while query latency was measured as the average time required to retrieve a specific health data record using the index module. The present evaluation focuses on these two primary endpoints to assess ledger-processing capability and index-assisted retrieval efficiency within the controlled simulation setting; broader deployment-related overhead and robustness metrics are discussed as directions for subsequent validation.

Throughput analysis

The experimental results, illustrated in Fig. 4, reveal a clear inverse relationship between the number of blockchain nodes and system throughput. With 4 nodes, the system achieved a maximum throughput of approximately 480 TPS. However, as the node count increased to 10, throughput declined progressively to approximately 260 TPS, representing a reduction of approximately 46%. This finding aligns with the fundamental characteristics of consensus mechanisms in distributed systems: as the number of participating nodes increases, the communication overhead required to achieve consensus correspondingly increases, thereby extending the time required for transaction finalization5,7.

Throughput of the consortium blockchain system under different numbers of consensus nodes (4–10 nodes).

This throughput degradation can be attributed to several factors inherent in the RAFT consensus protocol. First, the leader node must broadcast each transaction to all follower nodes and await acknowledgment from a majority before committing the transaction to the ledger28. With each additional node, the leader incurs incremental network transmission delays and processing overhead for signature verification and log replication. Second, the increased node count extends the time required for log replication across the network, as the leader must ensure that all followers have successfully appended the new block before proceeding to the next consensus round30. Third, network propagation delays, while minimized in the controlled simulation environment, still contribute to cumulative latency as the node count scales9.

Query performance and indexing efficiency

In addition to throughput, the system’s query performance was evaluated to assess the effectiveness of the index module. Across all node configurations, the average query latency for retrieving specific health data records remained consistently low, ranging from 45 ms to 65 ms. Notably, query latency exhibited only marginal degradation as node count increased, increasing by approximately 20% from the 4-node to the 10-node configuration, compared to the 46% degradation observed for throughput.

This resilience in query performance can be directly attributed to the index module architecture described in Section “Block and index design”. By maintaining a comprehensive mapping table in Oracle 11 g that associates transaction identifiers, block hashes, and storage addresses with relevant metadata, the system enables rapid data localization without requiring full blockchain scans7. Queries are executed against the index database, which supports optimized search operations, and only the verified block reference is retrieved from the blockchain ledger for confirmation. This hybrid storage approach effectively decouples query performance from blockchain size and node count, addressing a common scalability bottleneck in blockchain-based storage systems4,13.

The importance of efficient query mechanisms in healthcare applications cannot be overstated. Clinical workflows often require immediate access to patient historical data during emergency situations, where delays of even seconds can impact clinical decision-making3. The observed sub-65 ms index-assisted query latency demonstrates efficient retrieval under the evaluated controlled workload. However, this result should not be generalized to peak concurrent clinical-access conditions without further stress testing under heterogeneous and burst-like request patterns.

Scalability considerations and trade-offs

The experimental results highlight a fundamental trade-off in blockchain system design: the tension between decentralization (achieved through increased node participation) and performance (measured as throughput)27. In the context of healthcare data management, this trade-off must be carefully navigated based on specific application requirements.

The current evaluation covers consortium configurations ranging from 4 to 10 nodes and should therefore be interpreted as an initial assessment of small-to-moderate permissioned healthcare networks. Within this evaluated range, increasing the number of participating nodes resulted in reduced transaction throughput, reflecting the additional communication and acknowledgment overhead required for RAFT-based ledger commitment. The reported performance results should not be extrapolated to substantially larger healthcare ecosystems without further validation.

Large-scale deployments involving numerous hospitals, clinics, and regulatory entities may introduce greater consensus overhead, heterogeneous network conditions, increased transaction concurrency, and more complex governance requirements. Consequently, the present study does not establish scalability for regional- or national-level healthcare consortium networks. Future work will evaluate the proposed framework under larger and more heterogeneous node configurations and examine scalable extensions, including hierarchical consortium organization and sharding-assisted transaction processing, to mitigate performance degradation in large-scale deployments.

Comparison with state-of-the-art permissioned and consortium blockchain healthcare frameworks

To clarify the positioning of the proposed system, Table 1 provides a feature-oriented comparison with representative permissioned or consortium blockchain healthcare/IoMT frameworks in terms of application focus, blockchain architecture and consensus mechanism, authorization and cryptographic design, storage/indexing strategy, retrieval and audit support, and reported evaluation endpoints. Because these studies address different workloads and operational objectives, their reported performance values are used for contextual comparison rather than direct ranking.

Recent IoMT-oriented studies have increasingly integrated blockchain with IPFS-assisted storage, fog or edge computing, role-based access control, and lightweight security mechanisms. Mallick et al. proposed a blockchain–fog–IoMT framework with IPFS-assisted storage to improve secure and scalable healthcare-data management39, and subsequently developed a fog-assisted architecture incorporating role-based access control for critically ill patient scenarios40. Their later studies further investigated blockchain–IoT–IPFS-based healthcare data sharing41, dual-blockchain/IPFS data management with ECDSA-supported device protection42, and edge-assisted verifiable electronic-record management with queueing mechanisms43. These studies provide important advances in distributed storage, role-based authorization, device-side security, and edge-assisted processing. Their survey work44 is cited to contextualize continuing research challenges in blockchain-enabled IoT healthcare systems and is not treated as a direct system-level performance comparator.

Other representative permissioned blockchain healthcare frameworks address complementary aspects of secure medical-data management. Chen et al.17 developed a Hyperledger Fabric-based medical-data sharing system integrating IoT-enabled perioperative data collection, proxy re-encryption, dual-channel deployment, and chaincode-based access control. Healthchain19 emphasized privacy-preserving electronic health-record sharing through Hyperledger Fabric, IPFS-based storage, encrypted record hashes, and stakeholder access permissions. Wang et al.36 proposed a healthcare-data management framework based on Hyperledger Fabric, RAFT consensus, cloud-assisted storage, and hierarchical smart-contract-based access control. Abdorrahimi et al.45 employed blockchain and RAFT consensus for secure physician-prescription recording and diagnosis-related agreement. Khan et al.31 addressed IoMT stakeholder and device authentication through a Hyperledger Indy consortium network integrated with edge computing, NuCypher threshold proxy re-encryption, and APBFT consensus. In addition to architecture-oriented blockchain healthcare frameworks, recent security-oriented studies have proposed complementary mechanisms for protecting sensitive clinical data. Selvaraj et al. developed an Adaptive Feature Centric Polynomial (AFCP) data security model that combines feature-sensitive encryption, blockchain-supported record protection, and access restriction for EHR data46. Dhinakaran et al. further proposed an Adaptive Feature-Centric Polynomial Data Security Model (AFPDSM) with blockchain integration to enhance EHR encryption and malicious-access restriction47. Their subsequent Contextual Polynomial-Based Data Protection Model (CPDPM) extended this line of research toward context-aware protection of clinical data48. For IoMT environments, a hybrid keyless encryption framework has recently been proposed to improve scalable and privacy-preserving protection of heterogeneous medical data49. In addition, the Fusion-Driven Medical Image Encryption Framework addresses secure medical-image transmission and storage through entropy-calibrated control and embedded integrity assurance50. These studies provide relevant advances in encryption-oriented healthcare-data security; however, they do not directly address the integrated consortium-blockchain management workflow examined in the present study, including RAFT-based ledger commitment, metadata-index-assisted retrieval, and immutable audit recording of medical-instrument-generated data.

As shown in Table 1, existing blockchain-enabled healthcare and IoMT frameworks have provided important advances in privacy-preserving record sharing, role-based authorization, fog/edge-assisted processing, prescription-related consensus, lightweight authentication, and off-chain storage scalability. However, their primary objectives differ from that of the present study. The proposed framework addresses the integrated management of data generated by heterogeneous medical instruments in IoMT environments by combining: (i) RAFT-based consortium ledger commitment; (ii) differentiated transaction categories and an RBAC-centered hybrid authorization model for collection, interaction-management, and consumption clients; (iii) SHA256/RSA-based integrity verification and source authentication; (iv) a metadata index linking block hashes, transaction identifiers, transaction categories, signatures, timestamps, public keys, and storage addresses; and (v) immutable recording of data-access interactions. Accordingly, the contribution of this work should be interpreted as application-oriented and integrative rather than as protocol-level or cryptographic novelty. The integrated design is evaluated in this study through transaction throughput across different consortium-node configurations and index-assisted query latency.

System limitations and future research directions

The experimental evaluation also revealed several limitations that warrant further investigation. First, the current implementation does not account for malicious node behavior, assuming only crash failures in accordance with RAFT’s fault model28. In real-world deployments, Byzantine faults—where nodes may behave arbitrarily or maliciously—pose a more significant threat9. Extending the system to incorporate Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus mechanisms, such as PBFT or HotStuff, would enhance security but potentially at the cost of additional performance overhead22,29.

Second, the throughput degradation observed with increasing node count suggests that alternative scalability solutions may be necessary for large-scale deployments. Sharding techniques, which partition the blockchain network into parallel processing segments, have shown promise in improving scalability while maintaining security guarantees7,10. Integrating such techniques into our consortium blockchain framework could potentially enable linear throughput scaling with node count, addressing the primary performance limitation identified in this study.

Third, the current evaluation was conducted in a controlled simulation environment with homogeneous node configurations and idealized network conditions. Real-world deployment would introduce additional complexities, including heterogeneous hardware, variable network latency, and fluctuating transaction loads5. Future work should include pilot deployments in actual hospital environments to validate system performance under operational conditions and to identify unforeseen integration challenges with legacy healthcare information systems3,8. In addition, the present experimental evaluation was limited to transaction throughput and index-assisted query latency under a standardized workload, which represent the ledger-processing capacity and retrieval efficiency of the proposed system, respectively. These metrics do not yet provide a complete characterization of deployment overhead, operational robustness, or query-performance stability under peak healthcare-access conditions. In particular, the current evaluation does not include heterogeneous read/write workloads, increasing concurrent-user levels, or burst-like clinical query scenarios. Further validation should therefore quantify block propagation delay, consensus latency, cryptographic and indexing-related computational overhead, blockchain and metadata-index storage growth, energy consumption on physical IoMT gateway or medical-device platforms, and latency/throughput stability under mixed and concurrent access workloads. Moreover, because RAFT operates under a crash-fault model, subsequent robustness experiments should evaluate follower-node failure, leader re-election time, service continuity, and post-recovery ledger consistency. Resistance to Byzantine or malicious node behavior would require the integration and evaluation of alternative Byzantine-fault-tolerant consensus mechanisms. Although the revised framework specifies permission revocation, dynamic role updating, and time-limited emergency access, the present evaluation does not independently quantify policy-update propagation latency, emergency-access authorization latency, or the associated audit-processing overhead. These access-control-specific performance and usability metrics should be examined in future clinical workflow-oriented validation.

Fourth, the current prototype relies on an Oracle 11 g-based metadata index to accelerate retrieval of committed health-data records. Although this design improves query efficiency, it introduces a centralized dependency and may represent a single point of failure for query availability. Importantly, the index module functions as an auxiliary retrieval layer, whereas the consortium blockchain ledger remains the authoritative tamper-evident record of committed transactions. Consequently, an index-service failure may impair data-location efficiency and retrieval availability, but does not modify previously committed blockchain records. The present study does not implement or evaluate index replication, failover recovery, or decentralized indexing. Future work will investigate replicated index services and decentralized alternatives, such as distributed hash tables or blockchain-supported directory mechanisms, to improve availability and reduce centralized dependency while preserving retrieval efficiency.

Finally, the security analysis presented in this study focused primarily on data integrity and authentication. Comprehensive security evaluation should also consider resistance to various attack vectors, including double-spending attacks, Sybil attacks, and eclipse attacks31,33. Simulation frameworks such as RBlockSim could be leveraged to systematically evaluate system resilience under adversarial conditions5,9. The current prototype employs RSA digital signatures at the collection-client and consortium-node levels and does not directly compare RSA with lightweight elliptic-curve-based alternatives. Although this choice is consistent with the present server-supported implementation, ECDSA may be more suitable for resource-constrained medical endpoints because of its smaller key size at comparable security strengths37,38. Future work will therefore specifically compare RSA- and ECDSA-based signing and verification performance under device-constrained IoMT conditions.

Conclusion

The proliferation of Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) devices in modern healthcare has introduced critical challenges in data security, integrity, and access control that traditional centralized architectures are ill-equipped to address. This paper has developed an integrated consortium blockchain-enabled data management system for IoMT environments, with particular emphasis on the secure and auditable management of data generated by heterogeneous medical instruments. The proposed system establishes a multi-layered architecture incorporating data, network, consensus, contract, and application layers, integrating RAFT consensus with differentiated access control permissions for diverse client roles. An index module leveraging SHA256 hashing and RSA digital signatures enables rapid data localization while maintaining robust cryptographic security guarantees. Experimental evaluation demonstrated that the system achieves throughput of 260–480 transactions per second across 4–10 consortium nodes, with query latency consistently maintained below 65 milliseconds through the optimized indexing mechanism. These results demonstrate the feasibility of the proposed architecture within the evaluated simulation setting and highlight the throughput trade-off associated with increasing consortium size. The contribution of this study is application-oriented and integrative: established consortium-blockchain, cryptographic-verification, authorization, metadata-indexing, and audit-recording mechanisms are organized into a unified framework for medical-instrument-generated IoMT data management. However, because the current evaluation focuses on throughput and index-assisted query latency, additional deployment-oriented experiments are required to characterize propagation and consensus latency, computational and storage overhead, energy consumption, and crash-fault recovery performance before large-scale clinical deployment.
