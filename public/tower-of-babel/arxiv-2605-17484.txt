On the Stability of Inverse Conductivity Problem for Polyhedral Inclusions under a Single Measurement Thanks: This research was partially supported by National Science and Technology Council through grant number NSTC 113-2115-M-008-003-MY2.
Chun-Hsiang Tsou

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the stability of the inverse conductivity problem of determining a convex polyhedral inclusion embedded in a homogeneous isotropic medium from a single boundary measurement. The main tools in our analysis are singularity decomposition for elliptic equations in non-smooth domains, propagation of smallness, and microlocal analysis. Combining these tools, we establish a logarithmic stability estimate for the Hausdorff distance between inclusions in terms of the measurement error. Keywords: Calderón’s problem, polyhedral inclusion, logarithmic stability, singularity decomposition, propagation of smallness, single measurement. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 35R30, secondary 35B35, 31B20

1.
Introduction

We first present the mathematical formulation of the problem and the principal result. We then briefly review the position of our work in the literature. Before entering the proofs, we describe the technical ingredients and the structure of the paper.

1.1.
Statement of the main result

In this paper, we consider the classical Calderón problem with the following conductivity equation.

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}{\rm div}\,(\sigma\nabla u)=0&{\rm in}&\Omega,\\
u=f&{\rm on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\Omega
denotes a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

We focus on the case where the conductivity distribution
\sigma
is piecewise constant with a jump across an interface. More precisely, we assume that

\displaystyle\sigma=1+(k-1)\chi_{D},

where
k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a positive constant representing the conductivity contrast between the inclusion and the surrounding medium, and
\chi_{D}
denotes the indicator function of the subdomain
D\Subset\Omega
, which takes the value
1
inside
D
and
0
outside. We are particularly interested in the case where
D
has a special geometric shape, namely a convex polyhedron. On the boundary
\partial\Omega
, we impose a non-constant Dirichlet condition
f\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Standard elliptic theory implies that the forward problem (
1.1
) is well posed. Equivalently, this equation can be rewritten with transmission conditions as follows.

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}\Delta u=0&{\rm in}&D\cup(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})\\
u|_{-}=u|_{+}&{\rm on}&\partial D\\
k\partial_{\nu}u|_{-}=\partial_{\nu}u|_{+}&{\rm on}&\partial D\\
u=f&{\rm on}&\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Here, the subscripts
-
and
+
represent the limits from the interior and exterior of
D
respectively. The normal derivative
\partial_{\nu}
is defined as
\partial_{\nu}u=\nabla u\cdot\nu
where
\nu
is the unit outward normal vector on
\partial D
.

The inverse problem considered here is to determine the unknown inclusion
D
from a single partial boundary measurement
\partial_{\nu}u|_{\Gamma_{0}}
. Here,
\Gamma_{0}
is an open subset of
\partial\Omega
representing the accessible part of the boundary where measurements are available. The main result of this paper is a quantitative estimate of the reconstruction error for
D
in terms of the measurement error.

Before stating the main result rigorously, we clarify the geometric framework of our analysis. Many parameters may influence the stability estimate, but we focus only on its dependence on the measurement error. We therefore introduce the following assumptions to fix the geometric setting and avoid unnecessary complications. All these parameters are treated as
a priori
data, meaning that they are known in advance and are not part of the stability estimate.

Assumption 1.1

(Geometric)
.

Throughout this paper, we impose the following geometric conditions, and the corresponding parameters are regarded as
a priori
data.

•

The Lipschitz domain
\Omega
is arcwise connected, with Lipschitz constant
L_{\Omega}>0
.

•

The conductivity contrast
k>1
, i.e., the inclusion is more conductive than the surrounding medium.

•

The inclusions are at distance at least
\delta_{0}>0
from the boundary
\partial\Omega
, i.e.,
D\subset\Omega_{\delta_{0}}
where
\Omega_{\delta_{0}}:=\left\{{x\in\Omega}\;\middle|\;{{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)>\delta_{0}}\right\}
.

•

The interior domain
\Omega_{\delta_{0}}
is also arcwise connected.

•

Each inclusion
D
is a convex polyhedron with edges of length at least
l_{0}>0
.

•

The openings of all vertices are assumed to lie in a fixed range
[\vartheta_{m},\vartheta_{M}]\subset(0,2\pi)
.

•

The openings of all edges are assumed to lie in a fixed range
[a_{m},a_{M}]\subset(0,\pi)
.

•

The Dirichlet boundary condition
f\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is nontrivial, with
\|f\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}>0
.

Notations.

•

We will use the symbols “
\lesssim
” and “
\gtrsim
” to denote the inequality up to a multiplicative constant depending only on
a priori
data.

•

We denote by
d_{H}(D,D^{\prime})
the Hausdorff distance between
D
and
D^{\prime}
, which is defined as follows,

\displaystyle d_{H}(D,D^{\prime})=\max\left\{\sup_{x\in D}{\rm dist}(x,D^{\prime}),\;\sup_{x^{\prime}\in D^{\prime}}{\rm dist}(x^{\prime},D)\right\}.

The following theorem is the principal result of this paper.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume the geometrical and analytical conditions

hold. Let
u
and

be solutions to (

) with respect to

and

. We suppose that

for

being sufficiently small. Then, there exists positive constants
\kappa

1.2.
Context positioning and technical ingredients

The inverse problem considered in this paper is a special case of the classical Calderón problem. Problem (
1.1
) was first proposed by Calderón in his 1980 seminar talk
[
13
]
and has been extensively studied over the past decades. Equation (
1.1
) models the steady state of the electric potential
u
in a conducting medium with conductivity distribution
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Calderón asked whether one can determine the unknown conductivity
\sigma
from boundary measurements of the voltage
u
and current flux
\partial_{\nu}u
on
\partial\Omega
. This question initiated major developments in PDE inverse problems. Its importance comes not only from its mathematical challenges but also from broad applications in areas such as medical imaging, geophysics, and non-destructive testing. A representative application is electrical impedance tomography (EIT), which aims to reconstruct the conductivity distribution inside a body from boundary data. We refer to
[
3
,
47
]
for comprehensive introductions to the theory and applications, and to
[
21
,
33
,
37
]
for recent developments.

Broadly speaking, research on inverse problems focuses on uniqueness, stability, and numerical reconstruction. The uniqueness question asks whether the unknown parameter is uniquely determined by the measurements. The stability question asks how reconstruction errors depend on measurement errors. By the pioneering works of Sylvester and Uhlmann
[
45
]
and Alessandrini
[
2
]
, smooth conductivities
\sigma
can be uniquely and stably recovered from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, that is, from infinitely many boundary conditions in (
1.1
). However, from an application viewpoint, only finitely many measurements are typically available. Moreover, piecewise constant conductivities with jumps across interfaces are often more realistic for inhomogeneous media. These two facts motivate the framework studied in this paper: piecewise constant conductivity with finitely many measurements.

For inverse problems that determine the inclusion
D
in (
1.1
) from a single measurement, general uniqueness and stability results remain open. Existing results rely strongly on geometric features of
D
. In two dimensions, uniqueness for polygonal inclusions was established in
[
24
,
44
]
, and stability was obtained recently in
[
35
]
. Also in two dimensions, stability for disks was studied in
[
23
]
; in
[
46
]
, the authors improved stability and proposed a numerical reconstruction scheme. Another two-dimensional uniqueness result
[
34
]
replaces polygonal inclusions with smooth boundaries having high-curvature points. In three dimensions, uniqueness for balls was addressed in
[
28
]
. For polyhedral inclusions, uniqueness was proved in
[
4
]
, while the corresponding stability is the main objective of the present paper. It is also worth mentioning that, using the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, one expects Lipschitz stability for polygonal inclusions
[
7
,
6
]
. Furthermore, for general inclusion shapes, local stability results in
[
5
,
15
,
14
]
show that if two inclusions are sufficiently close (in the sense that one boundary is a small
\mathcal{C}^{1}
perturbation of the other), then Lipschitz stability can be obtained. Combining Beretta’s global Lipschitz stability with this local result allows reducing the number of measurements to two
[
27
]
for polygonal inclusions, at the cost of assuming that the number of vertices is known a priori. In this paper, we do not make that assumption and we do not use the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map.

We now introduce the three main ingredients of our proof: singularity decomposition, propagation of smallness, and microlocal analysis. Singularity decomposition extracts explicit local behavior of solutions to elliptic equations near polyhedral interfaces. Propagation of smallness provides estimates of solution differences near the inclusion in terms of the measurement error. To derive the final stability estimate, we perform a microlocal analysis in a carefully selected region with a suitably chosen test function.

It is well known that the regularity of solutions to elliptic equations depends strongly on boundary smoothness. In the presence of corners or edges, solutions exhibit singular behavior near these non-smooth regions. The study of such singularities was initiated by Kondratiev in the 1960s
[
30
]
. Since then, a rich theory has been developed to describe the singular structure of solutions in non-smooth domains; see, for example,
[
26
,
19
,
32
,
38
]
. In general, a solution can be decomposed into a singular part and a regular part. The regular part has regularity unaffected by boundary non-smoothness, while the singular part is expressed as a linear combination of explicitly constructed singular functions determined by the local geometry and boundary conditions. We mention here several relevant results for transmission problems
[
29
,
42
,
39
,
40
,
18
,
43
,
20
,
16
]
.

The other two ingredients are unique continuation and microlocal analysis. Unique continuation addresses Cauchy problems for elliptic equations with partial boundary data; we refer to
[
1
]
and references therein. Our microlocal argument is mainly inspired by ideas from inverse scattering in
[
11
,
22
]
. The method consists of constructing a special test function with a tunable parameter, inserting it into the variational formulation, and carefully estimating each integral term. The final stability estimate is then obtained by an appropriate choice of this parameter. This technique has led to several single-measurement uniqueness and stability results in inverse scattering; see
[
8
,
10
,
9
,
36
,
12
]
.

2.
Singular decomposition near a polyhedral corner

The following theorem appears in Theorem 7.1 of
[
20
]
, Theorem 5 of
[
41
]
, and
[
16
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
u
be the solution to (
1.1
). Then,
u
admits the following decomposition into vertex-singular part, edge-singular part and a regular part.

(8)

\displaystyle u=\sum_{{\bf v}\;{\rm vertex}}u_{{\bf v}}+\sum_{E\;{\rm edge}}u_{E}+u_{\rm reg}.

The structure of singular functions is intricate. In the points below, we explain in detail the expected regularity of the regular part
u_{\rm reg}
, as well as the local behavior of singular functions
u_{{\bf v}}
and
u_{E}
near vertices and edges.

1.)

Let
s>0
. This parameter originally describes the regularity of the right-hand side in (
1.1
) with homogeneous boundary conditions. It characterizes the regularity that
u
may have due to possible source terms. As we will see below,
s
also determines the maximal order in the singular expansion. In our case, by a standard lifting argument, we can choose
s
to achieve the desired regularity of
u_{\rm reg}
. We discuss the choice of
s
later.

2.)

The regular part
u_{\rm reg}\in H^{s+1}(D)\cup H^{s+1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})
and satisfies the same transmission conditions as in (
1.1
). Moreover, the following estimate holds in general,

(9)

\displaystyle\|u_{\rm reg}\|_{H^{s+1}(D)}+\|u_{\rm reg}\|_{H^{s+1}(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim 1.

3.)

For each vertex
\bf v
of
D
, the singular function
u_{\bf v}
can be expressed as follows.

\displaystyle u_{\bf v}(\rho,\vartheta)=\eta(\rho)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\tilde{S}({\bf v})\\
-\frac{1}{2}<{\rm Re}(\lambda)<s-\frac{1}{2}\end{subarray}}\rho^{\lambda}\sum_{m=0}^{K_{\bf v,\lambda}}\gamma_{{\bf v},\lambda}^{m}\log^{m}(\rho)\Phi_{{\bf v},\lambda}^{m}(\vartheta).

•

(\rho,\vartheta)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
represent spherical coordinates centered at the vertex
{\bf v}
.

•

\eta
is a smooth cut-off function such that
\eta(0)=1
and its support is sufficiently small.

•

\lambda
is called the singular exponent associated with the vertex
{\bf v}
. The set
\tilde{S}({\bf v})
corresponds to the poles of an operator-valued meromorphic function, referred to as Mellin symbols or pencil operators.

•

The
\log
terms appear only when
\lambda
is an integer. We will later impose a generic condition that
K_{{\bf v},\lambda}=0
for the singular exponents
\lambda
under consideration.

•

The singularity coefficients
\gamma_{{\bf v},\lambda}^{m}\in\mathbb{C}
depend continuously on the input data and the geometry of the polyhedron
D
. In fact, the coefficients
\gamma_{{\bf v},\lambda}^{m}
can be expressed as duality pairings between the input data and associated singular functions of the adjoint problem. Such formulas can be found, for example, in
[
42
]
. Moreover, the following estimate holds
[
39
,
40
]
,

\displaystyle|\gamma_{{\bf v},\lambda}^{m}|\leq C\|f\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim 1.

•

The singular functions
\Phi_{{\bf v},\lambda}^{m}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
are harmonic in each subdomain of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and satisfy the same transmission conditions across interfaces. We discuss in more detail below the characterization of the singular exponents
\lambda
and the associated functions
\Phi_{{\bf v},\lambda}^{m}
.

4.)

For each edge
E
of
D
, the singular function
u_{E}
can be expressed as follows.

\displaystyle u_{E}(r,\theta,z)=\eta(\tilde{r})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\in\tilde{S}(E)\\
0<{\rm Re}(\mu)<s\end{subarray}}\tilde{r}^{\mu}\sum_{m=0}^{K_{E,\mu}}\mathcal{K}[\gamma_{E,\mu}^{m}](\tilde{r},\widetilde{z})\log^{m}(\tilde{r})\varphi_{E,\mu}^{m}(\theta).

•

(r,\theta,z)
are cylindrical coordinates with the
z
-axis coinciding with the edge
E
.

•

\displaystyle\tilde{r}=\frac{r}{\delta(z)}
, where
\delta
is a smooth function equivalent to the distance from the endpoints of the edge
E
.

•

\mu
is called the edge singular exponent and is obtained by arguments similar to those for vertex singular exponents.

•

The edge singularity coefficients
\gamma_{E,\mu}^{m}
are functions in (Kondratiev-type) weighted Sobolev spaces defined on
E
.

•

The behavior of
\gamma_{E,\mu}^{m}
near vertices is influenced by vertex singularities, as discussed later.

•

The operator
\mathcal{K}
is defined by convolution with a Schwartz kernel. It acts as a lifting operator from the edge
E
to its cylindrical neighborhood. For any fixed
\tilde{r}\neq 0
, the map
z\mapsto\mathcal{K}[\gamma](\tilde{r},z)
is smooth on
E
. For any fixed
z\in E
,
\mathcal{K}[\gamma](\tilde{r},z)
converges to
\gamma
as
\tilde{r}\rightarrow 0
.

•

Remarks analogous to those for the vertex coefficients
\gamma_{{\bf v},\lambda}^{m}
apply to the computation and estimates of
\gamma_{E,\mu}^{m}
. In particular, one has
[
17
]
,

(10)

\displaystyle\|\gamma_{E,\mu}^{m}\|_{V_{-s}^{s-{\rm Re}(\mu)}(E)}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\lesssim 1.

•

The same remarks on
\log
terms as in the vertex case apply here. We will also impose a generic condition
K_{E,\mu}=0
for the edge singular exponents
\mu
under consideration.

•

\varphi_{E,\mu}^{m}\in H^{1}(0,2\pi)
is harmonic in each subinterval and satisfies the same transmission condition across the interfaces.

•

For a more detailed description of edge singular functions, we refer to

5.)

The cut-off function
\eta
equals
1
on
[0,\ell]
and vanishes on
[2\ell,+\infty)
, where
\ell
is sufficiently small. For instance, we can choose

(11)

\displaystyle\ell=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}E,E^{\prime}\;{\rm edge}\\
{\bf v}\;{\rm vertex}\end{subarray}}\left\{\frac{1}{2},\;\frac{1}{5}l_{0}\;,\tan\left(\frac{\Theta_{{\bf v},E,\;E^{\prime}}}{2}\right)\right\}.

Here,
l_{0}
denotes the smallest edge length and
\Theta_{{\bf v},E,E^{\prime}}
represents the opening angle between two adjacent edges
E
and
E^{\prime}
meeting at the vertex
{\bf v}
. With this choice, the supports of vertex singular functions are pairwise disjoint, and the same holds for edge singular functions. We remark that
\ell
depends only on geometric parameters of the polyhedron
D
, and can therefore be treated as an
a priori
parameter.

6.)

To determine the vertex singular exponents
\lambda
, we use the fact that the singular functions
\rho^{\lambda}\Phi_{{\bf v},\lambda}(\vartheta)
satisfy the same transmission problem in a sufficiently small ball centered at
{\bf v}
. Writing the transmission problem in spherical coordinates centered at
{\bf v}
, we deduce that
\Phi_{{\bf v},\lambda}
satisfies

\displaystyle{\rm div}\,_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}((1+(k-1)\chi_{G_{\bf v}})\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Phi_{{\bf v},\lambda})+\lambda(\lambda+1)\Phi_{{\bf v},\lambda}=0\quad{\rm on}\;\;\mathbb{S}^{2}.

Here,
C_{\bf v}
denotes the polyhedral cone generated by the vertex
{\bf v}
and its adjacent edges, and
G_{\bf v}=\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap C_{\bf v}
is the corresponding spherical polygon. Then the eigenvalues
\lambda
can be expressed as follows.

\displaystyle\lambda=-\frac{1}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\upsilon},

where
\upsilon
describes the eigenvalues of the associated Laplace-Beltrami operator acting on
H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\bf v}:u\mapsto-{\rm div}\,_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}((1+(k-1)\chi_{G_{\bf v}})\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}u),

where the derivatives are understood in weak sense. This elliptic operator is unbounded, positive, self-adjoint with dense domain, and
(Id-\mathscr{L}_{\bf v})^{-1}
is compact. From spectral theory, its eigenvalues can be ordered as
\upsilon_{1}\leq\upsilon_{2}\leq\cdots
with
\upsilon_{j}\rightarrow+\infty
when
j\rightarrow+\infty
. The first eigenvalue
\upsilon_{1}
can be characterized by the following Rayleigh quotient:

\displaystyle\upsilon_{1}=\min_{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2}),\;u\perp{\rm const}}\frac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}u|^{2}+(k-1)\int_{G_{\bf v}}|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}u|^{2}}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|u|^{2}}.

We can obtain, by using the Poincaré inequality, that
\upsilon_{1}>\min\{1,k\}\Lambda_{1}
, where
\Lambda_{1}
is the first positive eigenvalue of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
. Direct calculation shows that
\Lambda_{1}=2
. Consequently, the vertex singular exponents follow the order,
1<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
.

7.)

The edge singular exponents
\mu
are exactly the same as the corner singularity of a plane sector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For an edge of an opening angle
a\in(0,\pi)
, the singularity exponents
\mu
satisfies, (see e.g. (2.31) in
[
5
]
)

(12)

\displaystyle\sin(\mu(\pi-a))=\left|\frac{k+1}{k-1}\right|\sin(\mu\pi).

And the associated functions
\varphi_{E,\mu}^{m}
are simply
\cos(\mu\theta+\phi_{\pm})
with two phases
\phi_{\pm}
such that the transmission conditions hold. Using the same argument as the vertex singularity, the edge singular exponents follow the order,
0<\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots
. Moreover, it is sufficient by studying the behaviors of the function
t\mapsto\frac{\sin(t(\pi-a))}{\sin(t\pi)}
to deduce that the first two exponents satisfy
\frac{1}{2}<\mu_{1}<1<\mu_{2}
.

8.)

Interaction between vertex and edge singularities. Another crucial point is how edge-singularity coefficients behave near an adjacent vertex. In the absence of
\log
terms, the edge-singularity coefficients
\gamma_{E,\mu}
vanish near endpoints with a specific order.
The following result can be found in
[
20
]
,

(13)

\displaystyle\gamma_{E,\mu}(\rho)=a_{E,\mu}^{{\bf v},\lambda}\rho^{\lambda}+o(\rho^{\lambda})\quad{\rm as}\;\;\rho\rightarrow 0,

where
a_{E,\mu}^{{\bf v},\lambda}
are constant coefficients.

We now impose some generic assumptions to simplify the stability analysis. The exceptional cases are very limited and can be avoided by a suitable choice of boundary condition. Introduce the smallest singularity exponent,

(14)

\displaystyle s_{0}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf v}\;{\rm vertex}\\
E\;{\rm edge}\end{subarray}}\left\{\lambda_{{\bf v}}+\frac{1}{2},\;\;\mu_{E}\right\}.

Assumption 2.2

(Singularity)
.

Throughout the paper, we will assume that the following conditions hold.

1.)

We adapt the case b) in Theorem 7.1 in
[
20
]
. That is, the parameter
s
is chosen such that
s_{0}\leq s<s_{0}+1
in order to have sufficient regularity for
u_{\rm reg}
and to avoid the “shadow terms” in condition c) for singular functions. Furthermore, from the estimates of the first singularity exponents
\frac{1}{2}<\mu_{1}<1<\lambda_{1}
, we have
s_{0}=\mu_{1}>\frac{1}{2}
and
s_{0}+1>\frac{3}{2}
. With this condition, we will choose
s
close enough to
\mu_{1}+1
such that,

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}<s<\mu_{1}+1.

As an immediate consequence, the regular part
u_{\rm reg}
has at least
H^{5/2+\epsilon}
regularity in each subdomain, where
\epsilon:=s-\frac{3}{2}>0
.

2.)

There is no
\log
terms in the vertex or edge singular expansions. That is,
K_{\bf v,\lambda}=K_{E,\mu}=0
for any vertex
\bf v
and any edge

. This condition corresponds to Remark 5.3 in
[
20
]
.

3.)

The first vertex or edge singularities are not degenerate, i.e.,

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

We now introduce complex geometrical optics (CGO) solutions to the Laplace equation. Generally speaking, CGO solutions are functions of the form

Appendix A
Edge singularity and smoothing operator

Edge singular functions are constructed with an exponent
\mu
and a coefficient
\gamma
in the following form (without
\log
terms, for simplicity):

(33)

\displaystyle u_{E,\mu}(r,\theta,z)=\mathcal{K}[\gamma](\widetilde{r},z)\widetilde{r}^{\mu}\varphi(\theta).

In full generality,
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
comes from the spectrum of the operator pencil associated with the edge
E
. In our case,
\mu
are roots of (
12
) and can be ordered increasingly as
0<\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\cdots
. We also have
\frac{1}{2}<\mu_{1}<1
.

Essentially, edge singularities behave as vertex singularities on cross-sections perpendicular to the edge, except that singular coefficients
\gamma(z)
are functions along the edge rather than constants. They are obtained by inverting the operator pencil and applying an inverse Mellin transform. Since this approach does not directly provide the required regularity, we introduce weighted Sobolev spaces
V_{\eta}^{m}(E)
and the smoothing operator
\mathcal{K}
.

First, we introduce a stretching change of variables. Let
\delta:\overline{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a positive smooth function equivalent to the distance to the endpoints of
E
. For instance, we can choose
\delta(z)=(1-z)^{2}
if
E=(-1,1)
. Let
z_{0}
be an interior point of
E
; we define the stretching variable
\widetilde{z}
by

\displaystyle\widetilde{z}:=\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\frac{1}{\delta(s)}ds.

For a function
f
defined on
E
, define the stretched function
\widetilde{f}
by
\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{z}):=f(z)
. As an immediate consequence of this change of variables, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\widetilde{z}}\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{z})=\delta(z)\partial_{z}f(z),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{z})d\widetilde{z}=\int_{E}\delta(z)^{-1}f(z)dz.

Then, for
p\in[1,+\infty)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we deduce that
\partial^{k}_{\widetilde{z}}\widetilde{f}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
if and only if
\delta^{k-1/p}\partial_{z}^{k}f\in L^{p}(E)
. This motivates the following weighted Sobolev spaces.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle V_{\eta}^{m}(E):=\left\{{f\in L^{2}(E)}\;\middle|\;{\delta^{\eta+k}\partial_{z}^{k}f\in L^{2}(E),\;\forall k\leq m}\right\}.

If
m
is non-integer,
V_{\eta}^{m}(E)
is defined by interpolation. From the previous result,
V^{m}_{-1/2}(E)
is isomorphic to
H^{m}(\mathbb{R})
under the stretching change of variables. Since
\delta
is positive and smooth on
E
, the spaces
V_{\eta}^{m}(E)
are increasing, i.e., there are continuous embeddings
V_{\eta_{1}}^{m}(E)\hookrightarrow V_{\eta_{2}}^{m}(E)
for
\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}
. In our case, the edge-singularity coefficient satisfies
\gamma\in V_{-s}^{s-\mu}(E)
, where
s_{0}\leq s<s_{0}+1
and
s_{0}
is given by (
14
). With our choice of
s_{0}
, we always have
\frac{3}{2}\leq s<\mu_{1}+1
. Therefore,

(34)

\displaystyle V_{-s}^{s-\mu}(E)\hookrightarrow V_{-1/2}^{s-\mu}(E)\cong H^{s-\mu}(\mathbb{R}).

It is worth noting the role of
\mu
. Since
s\geq 3/2
, we have
s-\mu>1/2
for all
\mu<1
, in particular for
\mu=\mu_{1}
. Using the one-dimensional Sobolev embedding

, we deduce that
\widetilde{\gamma}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\Rightarrow\gamma\in L^{\infty}(E)

[
38

Next, we introduce the smoothing operator
\mathcal{K}
by the following convolution product,

\displaystyle\forall\widetilde{r}=\frac{r}{\delta(z)}>0,\;z\in E,\quad\mathcal{K}[\gamma](\widetilde{r},z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi(\frac{t}{\widetilde{r}})\widetilde{\gamma}(\widetilde{z}-t)dt.

Here, the kernel
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
is smooth, rapidly decreasing and satisfies
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(t)dt=1
.

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{r}^{-1/q}\|\phi\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R})}\|\widetilde{\gamma}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})}=\widetilde{r}^{(s-\mu)-1/2}\|\phi\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R})}\|\widetilde{\gamma}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R})},

Case

. Using Young’s inequality, we have

Lemma A.2
.

Let
1/2<\mu
and let
\mu+1/2<s<\mu+1
. Then, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}_{\phi,\mu}>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall z\in E,\;\widetilde{r}>0,\quad|\mathcal{K}[\gamma](\widetilde{r},z)-\gamma(z)|\leq C^{\prime}_{\phi,\mu}\widetilde{r}^{s-\mu-1/2}\|\gamma\|_{V_{-s}^{s-\mu}(E)}.

Proof.

Using the fact
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(t)dt=1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[\gamma](\widetilde{r},z)-\widetilde{\gamma}(\widetilde{z})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi(\frac{t}{\widetilde{r}})(\widetilde{\gamma}(\widetilde{z}-t)-\widetilde{\gamma}(\widetilde{z}))dt.

We split this integral into two parts, one for
|t|\leq\widetilde{r}
and the other for
|t|>\widetilde{r}
.

Since
s-\mu>1/2
and
\widetilde{g}\in H^{s-\mu}(\mathbb{R})
, it follows from Sobolev embedding that
\widetilde{\gamma}\in\mathcal{C}^{s-\mu-1/2}(\mathbb{R})
, and for certain constant
C>0
that

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R},\quad|\widetilde{\gamma}(x)-\widetilde{\gamma}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\widetilde{\gamma}\|_{H^{s-\mu}(\mathbb{R})}|x-y|^{s-\mu-1/2}.

Then, we can estimate the first part of the integral as follows,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{|t|\leq\widetilde{r}}\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi\left(\frac{t}{\widetilde{r}}\right)(\widetilde{\gamma}(\widetilde{z}-t)-\widetilde{\gamma}(\widetilde{z}))dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\widetilde{\gamma}\|_{H^{s-\mu}(\mathbb{R})}\widetilde{r}^{s-\mu-1/2}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}.

For the second part, since
\phi
is rapidly decreasing, there exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that
\displaystyle|x^{2}\phi(x)|\leq C^{\prime}
. Then, we can estimate the second part of the integral as follows,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{|t|>\widetilde{r}}\frac{1}{\widetilde{r}}\phi\left(\frac{t}{\widetilde{r}}\right)(\widetilde{\gamma}(\widetilde{z}-t)-\widetilde{\gamma}(\widetilde{z}))dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2C^{\prime}\|\widetilde{\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\widetilde{r}}^{+\infty}\frac{\widetilde{r}^{2}}{t^{2}}dt\leq 2C^{\prime}\|\widetilde{\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\widetilde{r}.

Thus, the claim follows by combining those estimates and the embedding (
34
).
∎
