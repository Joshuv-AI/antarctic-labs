is somewhat non-trivial. But it is
easy to see that the exponent of factor
\delta
is
the dimension of the module
(which is the Bell number
B(n)
from (
2.1
));
and the total
polynomial degree is

\sum_{l=1}^{n}lS(n,l)

(coincidentally also dim
(\mathcal{S}_{n}((1)))
)
— this is the sum of the exponents down the main diagonal, by the composition rule;
and it is straightforward to check that this gives the highest order term in the Laplace expansion.

(
3.2
)
On the other hand we observe that the polynomial

\det(\Gamma_{n}(0))

must,
on representation theory grounds,
contain factors as follows.
(NB, we sometimes write
\mathcal{S}_{n}(0)
for
\mathcal{S}_{n}(\emptyset)
- purely for aesthetic reasons.)
The head of
\mathcal{S}_{n}(\lambda)

when working over a field
depends on the specialisation
\delta\leadsto\delta_{c}
. For given
\delta_{c}
we write
{\mathcal{D}}_{n}^{\delta_{c}}(\lambda)
for the head,
as in (

).
For the factors
(\delta-1)
in the determinant we observe that
dimension of head of
\mathcal{S}_{n}(0)

when
\delta_{c}=1

is
\dim({\mathcal{D}}_{n}^{1}(0))=1
.
We can observe this directly in our low rank
diagram in Fig.

or in general by inspecting the Potts representation.
Thus the rank of the
morphism (unique up to scalars, since necessarily head to socle) from
standard module
\mathcal{S}_{n}(0)
to
costandard module
\nabla_{n}(0)
is 1.
The gram matrix
\Gamma_{n}(0)
intertwines these two modules over any ground ring.
By (
9
) it
always maps the head to the socle, but its rank can
vary in specific specialisations (evalutations of
\delta
).
In order to have the right rank when
\delta=1
,
it follows that all but one entry in the Smith form
of
\Gamma_{n}(0)
over
\mathbb{Z}[\delta]
(strictly speaking for a Smith form it should be over, say,

\mathbb{C}[\delta]
, but here it makes no difference)
has a factor
(\delta-1)
.
So there are at least dim
(\mathcal{S}_{n}(0))
-1 such factors
in the determinant.
That is, writing

\det(\Gamma_{n}(0))=\prod_{\delta_{c}}(\delta-\delta_{c})^{\alpha_{\delta_{c}}}

(25)

we have
a bound on the exponent:

\alpha_{1}\geq\dim(\mathcal{S}_{n}(0))-1
.
A similar argument gives a bound

\alpha_{Q}\geq\dim(\mathcal{S}_{n}(0))-\dim({\mathcal{D}}_{n}^{Q}(0))

(26)

on the number of
factors of each form
(\delta-Q)
for

.
In general we have

\dim({\mathcal{D}}_{n}^{Q}(0))\;=\;\sum_{l=1}^{Q}S(n,l)

(27)

arguing as in (
2.4
-
2.4
).
Hence

\dim(\mathcal{S}_{n}(0))-\dim({\mathcal{D}}_{n}^{Q}(0))\;=\;\sum_{l=1}^{n}S(n,l)-\sum_{l=1}^{Q}S(n,l)\;=\;\sum_{l=Q+1}^{n}S(n,l)

Summing we obtain an overall bound for the polynomial degree

degree(|\Gamma_{n}(0)|)\;\geq\;\sum_{Q}\sum_{l=Q+1}^{n}S(n,l)\;=\;\sum_{l=1}^{n}lS(n,l)

By
our earlier observation in (
3.1
)
this is saturated.
Thus the formulae (
26
) are equalities; and
all factors in (
25
) come from
these
Q
values. Thus this gram determinant does not vanish for any other
\delta
value.
Thus
\mathcal{S}_{n}(0)
is simple for all
n
for all other
\delta
