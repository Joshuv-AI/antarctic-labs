Reliability, repeatability, and age associations of the dogSIT (dog Smell Interaction Test) battery in untrained companion dogs (Canis lupus familiaris)
Authors: Amelia C Corona, Helena Harrison, Ryan G Hopper, Andrew L Smith, Callie M Rogers, Cassandra L Cook, Michele M Salzman, Kyle D Peterson, Nathaniel J Hall, Freya M Mowat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent olfactory tests for untrained dogs have limitations due to use of a single odorant, requirement for ambulation, and potential for vision interference. We hypothesized that our new dogSIT odorant test battery measures would be reliable, repeatable, and age-associated in untrained companion dogs. Gently restrained dogs were presented with 18 odorants (in 9 pairs, one pair was subsequently excluded from analysis); odorants were contained in identical stainless-steel tea-balls. Odorants were presented for 30s in a habituation-dishabituation paradigm (4 consecutive presentations of the same odorant followed by a different odorant). Interaction times were measured post-hoc using masked video analysis; outcome measures represented novel interactions, habituation, and dishabituation. We assessed inter-rater reliability and test-retest repeatability (n = 25); and analyzed age, sex, and bodyweight covariates (n = 65). Outcome measures had significant inter-rater and test-retest repeatability. There was heterogeneity in odorant preference between dogs. Age, and to a lesser extent bodyweight and sex, were associated with outcome measures relating to novel odorant interaction and dishabituation. Our dogSIT battery is reliable, repeatable, and identified reduced odorant interaction behaviors in older dogs. Our findings have implications for research on food motivation and scent discrimination in aging dogs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-59321-0.Keywords: Canine, Behavior, Sense of smell, Food motivation, AgingSubject terms: Neuroscience, Zoology

Introduction

Olfactory evaluation incorporates four major aspects, mediated by different receptor characteristics and brain regions. Aging as a process influences all four of these aspects: odor identification, discrimination, sensitivity, and habituation1. The association between olfactory dysfunction and aging is supported in multiple human studies2–4; the majority of studies in humans focus primarily on odor identification5. Olfactory system function in aging dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) is comparatively understudied, yet dogs are an important model species for human aging research. Geriatric dogs develop olfactory epithelium atrophy, olfactory bulb accumulation of ubiquitin deposits and beta-amyloid, astrocytic gliosis, and by the age of 14 years, develop olfactory system neuron atrophy6. Using functional MRI, it has been determined that older dogs have reduced strength of olfactory functional connectivity in the brain7. Based on owner reports, prevalence of companion dog olfactory dysfunction (decline in sniffing behavior) begins to increase at approximately 12 years of age8.

An established behavioral method of assessing olfactory performance in untrained dogs is the natural detection task (NDT). The NDT is a masked selection task using dog food, treats, or raw meat as the odorant and thus relies on single odor identification and/or discrimination from environmental or extraneous odorants. In the NDT, a fasted dog is presented with several containers, one of which has a hidden food reward underneath it – the dog ambulates and investigates all containers and “selects” the baited container if their behavior indicates substantial interest in the baited container. In advanced levels, odor sensitivity is assessed; odor availability becomes progressively more limited in higher levels. Outcome variables for the NDT include level-specific “success score” (an ordinal metric composed of 0, 25, 50, 75 or 100% correct choice in four trials comprising four container options)9,10, and achievement of the “top level” (greater than chance in the lowest odor availability test; a Boolean variable)10. While the NDT is highly useful for population-level evaluation of olfactory dysfunction in dogs, a perceived limitation to this test includes the necessity for the dog to ambulate a relatively long distance over the multiple trials. In addition, in the NDT, the dog receives the food as a reward for a correct selection11, introducing a learning component with repeated successful trials. Indeed, prior training history influences NDT outcome measurements11. Although not specifically reported, satiation may also influence motivation to participate in higher level trials when testing smaller dogs in this manner. Prior NDT studies have identified that between 15% and 32% of dogs do not complete testing9,11, and older dogs are significantly less likely to complete testing11. Finally, the non-continuous nature of the data means the test is susceptible to floor/ceiling effects, and individual dog performance is likely difficult to monitor longitudinally. The NDT and other studies of companion dog olfaction have largely used a single odorant10,12,13, and individual dog variance in odor or food preference14–16 may impact the generalizability of the findings.

Olfactory function testing in humans typically involves testing of multiple odorants in an odor identification format17,18, and the number of correctly identified odorants is used as a semicontinuous outcome measure. These methods require verbal or written feedback, demonstrating perception. A multiple odor span test of working memory has been validated in trained detection dogs. However, this test requires prior training through reinforcement19. Multiple odorant tests for dogs that do not require training have not been described to-date. We sought to develop a multi-odorant behavior test for untrained companion dogs that minimized the potential for vision to influence results. In dogs, vision dominates in food selection tasks20, and during object discrimination21. Some researchers have assessed olfaction by determining the ability of a dog to select a food baited bowl from three options, performed in near darkness to minimize vision involvement12. Despite these accommodations, the contribution of vision in this task remains uncertain. Many companion dogs use vision as the preferential sense for searching for food20,22, and familiar humans22. When dogs are searching for toys, they switch to other senses (including olfaction) only in complete darkness21. In addition, a bowl selection task requires a relatively large space, and for dogs to ambulate repeatedly, which may be influenced by age-related changes in frailty23. Olfactory function can be reliably tested with likely minimal visual influence using multiple baited bowls, all baited with similarly appearing but differently smelling food or treats and assessing food preference16. A main limitation of using baited bowls with ingestible food in testing is that caregivers of companion dogs often wish their dogs to avoid novel diets because of perceived food intolerances. Additionally, dog motivation to participate in food-based testing may decline during repeated trials due to increasing satiation, which may vary depending on dog size, food motivation, and other factors unrelated to olfactory sensitivity.

The goal of the study was to develop a reliable (adequate inter-observer reliability and test-retest repeatability) odorant interaction test battery for dogs that encompassed a variety of odorants, minimized visual contribution, did not require ambulation or food ingestion, and could be applied universally to untrained companion dogs. We aimed to generate continuous or semi-continuous outcome measures that could be applied in cross-sectional and future longitudinal aging research studies in dogs. A test that incorporated multiple aspects of olfactory evaluation1 was desired. A habituation-dishabituation test using multiple odors was selected because it fulfilled many of the above criteria and would allow us to investigate various aspects of dog odorant interaction behavior. Habituation involves repeated presentation of the same stimulus, resulting in reduced behavioral response with repeated presentation. Dishabituation is the introduction of a novel stimulus following habituation, typically resulting in a modified behavioral response. In a habituation-dishabituation paradigm involving multiple odorants, we would anticipate assessing the olfaction aspects1 of identification (dog perceives initial presentation of odorant as novel and demonstrates observable behaviors), habituation (dog demonstrates progressively diminishing behaviors in response to repeated presentation of the same odorant), and discrimination (following habituation phase, presentation of a novel odorant requires memory of prior presented odorant and understanding of novelty, resulting in demonstration of new or accentuated observable behaviors). A study of working dogs found weak age association with reduced habituation, and a stronger association between age and reduced discrimination between the habituant and a novel odorant (i.e. a dishabituant)24. In this study, and another study of human odor25, dishabituants were presented concurrently with the final habituant. However, the psychological and cognitive interpretations of habituation-dishabituation as a “memory trace” in consecutive presentation (i.e., not requiring concurrent presentation of habituant and dishabituant) are well-established in human infants26, and this method is used extensively in sound exposure studies, where competing stimuli cannot be presented simultaneously without modifying the perception of both stimuli27.

A main hypothesis for this study was that older dogs would have lower measures of dishabituation. There are several lines of evidence supporting this hypothesis. A study using a habituation-dishabituation paradigm with a single odorant (familiar/unfamiliar urine) in working dogs found age effects on discrimination/dishabituation24. Similarly, a study of companion dogs’ interactions with novel toys showed that age significantly impacts curiosity behaviors28, which are associated with dishabituation in human infants29. We included covariates of sex and bodyweight, which we hypothesized would have smaller, variably significant effects on measurable outcomes, similar to our findings of retinal function in a similar heterogeneous group of companion dogs30. We included American Kennel Club breed group as a covariate in model analyses, as previously described31, and hypothesized that it would not substantially improve model fit because there is minimal evidence of genetic, anatomic32, or behavioral9 breed-related variance in olfaction in dogs.

Results

Subject demographics and lifestyle

Demographics of dogs used for each component of the study (inter-observer and test-retest reliability testing, and cross-sectional age/bodyweight/sex analyses) are described in Table 1. Questionnaire data were recorded from 43/65 aging study subjects’ cohabiting human caregivers. The majority of dogs in which data were available on diet protein source were fed a chicken-based diet (61%, 22/36). The remainder of respondents fed diets based on salmon (9/36), lamb (3/36), beef (2/36), or trout (1/36); one dog received a diet that contained both salmon and beef. Caregivers most commonly graded their dog’s hunger as moderate (56%, 24/43). The remainder of respondents graded their dog’s hunger as “very” (9/43), “slightly” (8/43) or “extremely” (2/43); no caregivers graded their dog’s hunger as the lowest level (“not at all hungry”). A minority (23%, 9/39) of caregivers acknowledged that they had participated in some kind of scent sport (tracking, or scent/nose work) with their dog at least once in the preceding year. The most common response was a few times a year (4/9), followed by > 1 x monthly (2/9), with 1 caregiver each selecting > 1 x weekly, 1 x daily and > 1 x daily. A greater proportion acknowledged using odor (food, clothing, etc.) in interactions with their dog at least once per month (26/40, 65%). The greatest proportion of caregivers responded that they did this several times a day (19/40, 48%), followed by several times a week (3/40), once daily (2/40), once weekly or every 2 weeks (1/40 each response). One third of caregivers reported they never interacted with their dog using odor (13/40, 33%).

Because mean dishabituation score did not have significant test-retest repeatability and also had the lowest inter-observer correlation (Spearman r 0.58, p = 0.01), it was not included as an outcome variable in subsequent analysis.

Odorant preferences and trial order analysis

The goals of this study component were to determine (a) whether certain odorants had consistently longer interaction times, (b) whether younger and older dogs interacted differently with individual odorants, and (c) whether trial order influenced interaction times. Overall, dogs interacted with a wide range of presented odorants. The median number of the 16 odorants with an initial interaction duration (IID) greater than 0 s was 10 (interquartile range 6–14). Because dogs aged over 8 years are at higher risk for cognitive dysfunction34, we compared outcome measures between younger and older dogs. Younger dogs (aged < 8 years, n = 36) interacted with a greater number of odorants (median 11.5, IQR 8–14), whereas older dogs (aged ≥ 8 years, n = 29) interacted with fewer (median 8, IQR 5.5–10). No single odorant consistently had the longest IID on novel presentation for all dogs, indicating heterogeneity in preference. The IID on novel presentation is shown for all odorants in younger dogs (aged < 8 years), and older dogs (aged ≥ 8 years) in Fig. 1A. There were significant differences in IID between odorants (p < 0.0001), and age group (p < 0.0001), and there was a small, significant interaction between odorant type and age group (p = 0.002, aligned rank transform; significant comparisons are shown as annotations in Fig. 1A; full analysis is provided in supplementary data S2 Table). The odorant with the longest average IID was beef, and the largest proportion of dogs “ranked” this odorant as their “preferred” odorant (odorant with the longest interaction time of all novel presentation IIDs: all dogs 26/64; 41%, younger dogs 16/36; 44%, older dogs 10/29; 34%). Overall, the meat and vegetable odorants were preferred by the most dogs (72.3%, Fig. 1B), whereas food flavor odorants and chemical odorants were preferred by fewer dogs (20% and 7.7% respectively). The proportion of meat and vegetable odorants preferred by older dogs was smaller than younger dogs (younger dogs 80.6%, older dogs 62.1%) whereas the opposite was true for food flavors (younger dogs 13.9%, older dogs 27.6%) and chemical odorants (younger dogs 5.6%, older dogs 10.3%). For novel interactions, both age (β = 0.013, p < 0.001) and bodyweight (β = 0.031, p = 0.019) were positively associated with proportion of novel odorant initial interactions that were less than 1 s. Including breed group modestly improved model fit (p = 0.053), with age contributing the most to explained variance.

For habituation characteristics, older age was associated with shorter mean habituant odor interaction time (β = − 0.007, p = 0.005), and bodyweight showed a marginal negative association (β = − 0.020, p = 0.062). The addition of breed group trended toward significance (p = 0.061) and modestly increased the explained variance in habituation time. For the proportion of trials meeting habituation criterion, sex was a significant covariate (β = − 0.702, p = 0.003), with males exhibiting a lower proportion, while age and bodyweight were not significant. Adding breed group did not improve model fit (p = 0.241). All dogs habituated to at least 2/8 habituant odorants. In contrast, for the proportion of trials failing to meet the habituation criterion, bodyweight was significantly negatively associated (β = − 0.036, p = 0.008), suggesting that heavier dogs performed better. Age and sex were not significant, and breed group did not significantly enhance model fit (p = 0.127).

For dishabituation characteristics, age was significantly associated with smaller recovery of attention (β = − 0.004, p = 0.007), while bodyweight and sex were not significant covariates. Including breed group did not improve model fit (p = 0.202), and age accounted for the largest proportion of explained variance in recovery of attention (Table 3). Finally, for the proportion of trials meeting dishabituation criterion, both age (β = − 0.010, p = 0.003) and bodyweight (β = − 0.066, p < 0.001) were negatively associated with the outcome, indicating that older and heavier dogs showed lower proportions of dishabituation. Neither sex nor breed group were significant covariates in this model (p = 0.449).

Discussion

In this work, we have described the development of a reliable, repeatable test battery assessing untrained companion dog odorant interaction behaviors. Dogs have heterogeneous odorant preferences. In cross-sectional analysis, we have identified that age, bodyweight, and to a lesser extent sex covariates influenced battery outcome measures. The most consistent covariate associated with battery outcome measures in cross-sectional analyses was age.

We used a habituation-dishabituation paradigm to assess olfactory behavior in dogs. The paradigm is based on the concept that animals will habituate to familiar stimuli and show neophilia with novel stimuli which has been demonstrated in younger kennel housed dogs (aged less than 8 years) in relation to object play35. In that study, 50–69% of dogs habituated to a novel toy on the basis of color and/or olfactory cues within an average of 4–5 presentations. Dishabituation proportion in this study was not reported, but presented results indicate a substantial increase in average toy interaction duration when a novel toy was introduced after habituation trials, indicating a high frequency of dishabituation. Odorant habituation-dishabituation has been reported in only one study to-date to determine olfactory detection threshold for amyl acetate in young (< 8 years) companion dogs36. The authors describe that only some dogs dishabituated to the odorant. There were notable differences in study design between our study, and this published work. Only 75% of study dogs could be tested using the published protocol, compared with 100% in our study. The published study confined dogs to an unfamiliar enclosure with minimal (1 min) acclimation and potential residual dog odor (multiple dogs were tested in succession), which may have increased the chance of environmental focus, limiting engagement with the olfactory task. To minimize environmental distraction during testing, our protocol incorporated defined acclimation protocols to handlers and environment (10–30 min; a duration shown in other studies to result in suitable environmental habituation in most dogs37, and limited testing to a single dog on each day. In the published study, the odorant (amyl acetate) was not anticipated to incite strong innate motivation for interaction, unlike many of the odorants we implemented in our test battery. The published study also demonstrated that retest within 1 week indicated persistent habituation, whereas in our study, we demonstrated significant test-retest repeatability when the test-retest interval was greater than 1 month, indicating that significant habituation did not persist beyond that longer timeframe in our study. We did not assess repeatability within 1 week, so it is not clear if we would have encountered similar persistence in habituation to the published study.

Our test outcomes were overall reliable. We found significant inter-observer reliability and test-retest repeatability outcomes for all measures except the dishabituation score, which was not further studied in the cross-sectional age cohort. We found significant but relatively low test-retest correlation values (with r values ranging from 0.44 to 0.70). Test-retest repeatability correlation values for behavioral outcomes in human infants undergoing visual habituation and dishabituation tests are in a similar range, despite being statistically significant26,38. Other studies of companion dog olfaction behavior have either not reported test-retest repeatability statistics12,39,40, performed retesting in the same session as testing which likely minimizes environmental distraction13, or were testing Boolean variables that limited the fidelity of assessment of reliability statistics9. The exact factors that contribute to relatively low (albeit significant) test-retest correlation values in behavioral testing are likely complex. A meta-analysis study of manuscripts relating to working dog behavioral assessments cited similarly low test-retest reliability correlation values41; reasons cited included behavioral drift over time, and persistent habituation. This persistent habituation might reflect task habituation, or habituation to the context of the task (e.g., handlers, environment).

We identified substantial heterogeneity in odorant preference between dogs. In pilot testing, pooled dog urine (or artificial coyote “urine”) did not result in substantial engagement compared with food-related odorants. These results are similar to a study of off leash dogs tested at a dog park, which determined average engagement times with coyote “urine” using 2 different presentation methods of 1.2 and 2.5 s respectively42. Similarly, working dogs had only a slight interest in interacting with novel intact male urine24, and in kennel-housed dogs, interaction times with food odorants were greater than with urine43. The most interesting odorants to our cohort of dogs were meat and vegetable odorants, which are likely to be highly motivating to dogs that regularly receive meat-based food and treats. Our dogs were fasted for 12 h prior to testing, and therefore motivation for food was likely high. Interestingly, despite most dogs receiving a chicken-based diet, beef was the preferred odorant for most dogs. This finding supports prior research regarding food preference using operant conditioning and two bowl preference testing that showed that laboratory dogs prefer beef over chicken44,45. Despite an overall meat or vegetable preference, dogs had a broad interaction with the presented odorants (the majority of dogs interacted with over half of presented novel odorants). The substantial heterogeneity in “preference” (the odorant in the battery with the longest interaction time) in this group of dogs is interesting. In humans, food preference after odor exposure is susceptible to individual characteristics, food/odor type, and exposure duration46, and there are likely to be many unexamined covariates in our study (presentation order, training/food history, level of hunger, arousal state) that influenced preferences during testing. We did determine a slight effect of the presentation order on interaction time with the most “popular” odorant (beef), indicating that the odorant test as a whole might induce some level of habituation or disinterest with repetition. A future direction might be to assess the performance of a truncated battery of odorants to reduce across-odor habituation to the test. Given that we observed a decline in interest only in the last quartile of the 16 odorants presented, a battery of 12 or fewer odors may be ideal. A similar approach has been attempted for olfactory screening for Parkinson Disease, with high sensitivity and specificity identified for a subset of odorants from a larger screening set47.

We identified variance in odorant interaction and preference between younger and older dogs. This was reflected in multivariable analyses showing that age was associated with an increase in the proportion of odorants with an initial interaction duration less than one second, and with a decrease in the mean summed habituant interaction time. This may have implications for food or treat motivation in older dogs, and further studies should expand on this work determining whether odor detection, motivational, or cognitive factors contribute most to this decline in interest. We identified subtle differences in odorant preferences between younger and older dogs, whereby a greater proportion of older dogs had their longest interactions with food flavors or chemicals compared with younger dogs. This finding, however, is complicated by the overall shorter interaction times confounding the interpretation of preference. Longitudinal studies will be needed to determine if individual dog odorant or food preferences change over the lifespan. In humans, food preferences change with age, with a somewhat counterintuitive increased acceptance of novel foods in the elderly48, despite a decline in olfactory sensitivity49. This increased acceptance of novel foods is proposed to be related to a lack of detection of the aversive nature of certain odors/flavors. It is possible that this is also the case with dogs, whereby older dogs are more inclined to interact with an odorant that might be perceived as aversive to a younger dog. Because our study ethogram did not specifically evaluate signs of aversion, and odorant selections and presented concentrations were made on the assumption of interest, future research will be needed to determine the effect of age on odor aversiveness in dogs.

It is unclear from our study if dog age is associated with odorant habituation, while we determined that dishabituation is age-associated. Although we found significant age relationships to mean summed habituation time (the sum of H1-4), the extent to which this is impacted by novel odorant interaction (H1) is uncertain, as the summed habituation time is largely driven by the first interaction, which we found had significant age-association. In addition, we did not find a significant age association with the proportions of odorants that met habituation criterion (greater than a 50% decline in interaction time over the habituation period). In our study, all dogs habituated to at least 2/8 habituant odorants, meaning that all dogs underwent some degree of habituation. It is possible that we did not give dogs sufficient trials to fully habituate, although in a similarly designed study, dogs typically habituated to a novel odorant within 3 trials36. Similarly, dogs that failed to interact with the first 2 presentations of an odorant (a phenomenon that occurred frequently) would also not habituate given additional trials. Similar to our findings, a study with working dogs also described a weak trend toward diminished habituation in older dogs, albeit using dog urine as the odorant, which generated short interaction times overall24. They found no significant influence of age on odor discrimination but acknowledged that because of the limited numbers of animals that participated in the discrimination aspect of the task, their conclusions were limited in terms of statistical power. In contrast, we found significant age-associated variance in dishabituation in dogs. Both mean recovery of attention to the dishabituant odorant and the proportion of odorants that met dishabituation criterion were age-associated. The exact nature of this effect requires further study; however, MRI studies support at least some contribution of central processing pathways to age-related olfactory decline in dogs7. There is increasing evidence for a relationship between olfactory dysfunction and cognitive dysfunction in both humans3,4,50 and dogs8,12,51,52, implicating shared processing pathways between olfactory and cognitive domains.

In addition to age effects, we identified significant effects on battery outcome measures of bodyweight, and to a lesser extent, sex. Breed group did not appear to influence outcomes substantially. Heavier dogs had a higher proportion of initial interactions less than 1 s, and a lower proportion of odorants that met dishabituation criterion, suggesting poorer olfactory function. Overall, our cohort of dogs was relatively large in stature, reflecting a suburban/rural demographic in the Midwest United States. We did not analyze the body condition (level of obesity) or activity levels of dogs that participated, but this would be an interesting future direction, based on the bodyweight associations found, and evidence in humans that obesity diminishes olfactory function53, and physical activity improves odor sensitivity and discrimination54.

This study had several limitations. Because we used commercially available odorants, many of which are naturally derived, the exact odorants available to the dog were not defined. Creation of a standardized odorant interaction test similar to human tests would help to limit effects of preparation, storage and concentration variance. The pairing of odorants into a habituant/dishabituant pair was relatively arbitrary, and the increased interaction time with a dishabituant may have been diminished by persistent interest in the habituant. However, this did not happen universally, as dogs commonly habituated, and all dogs dishabituated to at least some odorants. Because we found that individual dog preference was so heterogeneous, the generalizability of the findings should be confirmed with longitudinal studies to determine how individual dog odorant interactions change over the dog lifespan. Confounding factors of aging may have influenced outcomes irrespective of olfactory function aspects, which include aspects of motivation, and interest in novelty or exploration. These could be explicitly examined in future studies comparing these outcomes with other behavior tests that specifically assess these aspects of behavioral aging.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Companion dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) were recruited as part of a longitudinal study of companion dog aging (test-retest, n = 25 and aging study, n = 65). This study recruited from University of Wisconsin-Madison staff, students, prior research participants, and respondents to a call for participants in a university newsletter. Dogs were eligible if they were aged over 1 year, and amenable to behavioral testing without excessive anxiety or need for anti-anxiety medication. For the purposes of test-retest repeatability, a subset of dogs returned for a second visit, a minimum of 4 weeks following the first visit. Work was approved by the UW-Madison Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, in accordance with institutional protocols and the ARRIVE guidelines55. Because human subject questionnaires were completed, the work was reviewed and approved by the UW-Madison Institutional Review Board, and work complies with the declaration of Helsinki. All dog caregivers signed an informed consent for participation. Dogs were fasted overnight prior to testing for a minimum of 12 h, and testing was performed in the morning. Dog caregivers provided dog demographics, a brief dog medical history, and information about dog food/treat preference at intake. The majority of dog caregivers for the aging study completed a questionnaire about their dog’s home, lifestyle, training and behavior.

Odorant test battery

Eighteen test-battery odorants (S1 Table) were chosen based on outcomes of a pilot study (S1 Methods/Results), predicted dog interest due to association with food or treats (chicken, beef, turkey, ham, lobster, vegetable, peanut butter), prior published use in dog olfactory testing (r/s-limonene56, benzaldehyde14,57, lemon58, mint14,39,58, and/or prior use in human olfactory tests (cinnamon59,60, chocolate59,60, coffee60, peanut butter60, lemon59, banana59, cotton candy59, coconut59. Aqueous or oil odorants were paired for the habituation-dishabituation paradigm with odorants requiring a similar diluent and in a similar category (meat/vegetable, food flavors, chemicals), thus 9 pairs of 18 odorants were presented to each dog.

Odorant preparation

Information on odorants used in the dogSIT battery is provided in S1 Table. Odorants were prepared in a chemical fume hood. Aqueous solution odorants were stored at 4 °C and used undiluted (broths) or diluted per manufacturer recommendations (bouillons; diluted in ultrapure deionized water). Prepared aqueous odorants were filtered (Whatman grade 202, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA) prior to use. Flavored oils and chemicals were stored at room temperature and diluted in light mineral oil (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO). Odorants were prepared no more than 24 h in advance. Odorants (3 ml for aqueous-based odorants, 1 ml for oil-based odorants) were placed on a 100% cotton round (Cliganic Premium, Covina CA) inside individual identical spring handled stainless-steel tea balls, stored individually in a heat-sealed Mylar or polyethylene bag in the dark at 4 °C prior to presentation. Odorants were brought to room temperature before presentation to the dog and stored outside of the testing environment until the time of testing. Cotton rounds were discarded after a single use. Between dogs, tea balls were cleaned with dish soap, rinsed with tap water, followed by 1% bleach soaking and tap water rinsing/air drying. Each tea ball was used for a dedicated odorant throughout its use. The testing environment was sanitized with Rescue (Diversey Holdings, Fort Mill, SC) between different dogs. A single dog was tested per day.

Testing procedure

Temperature and humidity on the day of testing were recorded in the testing environment (TP50, ThermoPro, Duluth GA). Upon arrival to the testing environment, dogs were assigned a Fear, Anxiety, and Stress (FAS) score (dog FAS ladder, FearFree LLC, Broomfield CO) and allowed to acclimate without interaction from the two study personnel, with FAS scoring completed every 10 min. Acclimation time was stopped once the dog achieved an FAS score of 0–1 or after a maximum of 30 min, dogs that did not achieve an FAS score of ≤ 1 did not proceed to testing. Acclimation and testing were performed in a dimly lit room with background white noise (MagicTeam, Savannah GA).

A habituation-dishabituation paradigm of odorant pairs was performed, whereby a habituant odorant was presented in 4 consecutive presentations (using different tea-balls and cotton rounds; designated H1, H2, H3, H4 in subsequent analysis), followed by a single presentation of a novel odorant (dishabituant, designated D1). A 4-trial habituation presentation was used based on evidence that dogs rapidly habituate to novel odorants within 4 trials36. Odorant pairs presented (habituant/dishabituant) were beef/chicken, turkey/vegetable, lobster/ham, lemon/mint, banana/coconut, chocolate/coffee, cotton candy/peanut butter, benzaldehyde/cinnamaldehyde, (S)-limonene/(R)-limonene. Odorant pairs were presented in random order. Each individual tea-ball was contained in an identically appearing bag, and bags were lined up along a countertop for presentation sequentially.

A seated handler gently restrained the dog (in a standing, seated or lying position) using a leash and the seated odorant presenter faced the dog without making eye contact and presented the dog with odorants. The dog could interact with each odorant without needing to make substantial physical movement, although the dog was allowed to move toward or away from the presented odorant. Each odorant was presented approximately 15 cm from the dog’s nose for 30 s, the duration justified by pilot testing and a previous study36. A minimum of 15 s elapsed between different odorant presentations, and typically a longer break of 1–2 min occurred between different odorant pairs while new odorants were arranged. No more than 10 presentations (two odorant pairs) were made consecutively, after which the dog had a minimum of a 10-minute break from odorant testing. Presentation of the 18-odorant battery lasted approximately 30 min, total time including breaks was approximately 60 min.

To minimize the effect of visual distraction of the dog on behavior during testing, testing was performed under very low ambient lighting. Tests were video recorded for post-hoc analysis under diffuse infrared illumination (A14 IR Illuminator, Tendelux Technology Co., China) using an infrared modified video camera to maximize visibility of the dog on video review (GoPro Hero 7, San Mateo, CA, modified for infrared by Kolari Vision, Raritan, NJ) mounted on a tripod. An example video of a dog interaction is shown in S2 File.

Post-hoc video analysis

Videos were edited to remove the odorant identifier and into individual 30-second presentation clips (Adobe Premiere Pro versions 23.0-25.3, Adobe, San Jose, CA) and masked/randomized within each dog for post-hoc evaluation to avoid observer bias regarding trial order or odorant type. Observers reviewed videos and recorded interactions, using a predefined ethogram (Table 4). The initial interaction duration (IID; the duration of the first interaction during the 30-second presentation), and the summed interaction duration (SID; sum of all interactions within the 30-second presentation time) were used in downstream analysis.

Statistical analysis

Subject demographics summary statistics were generated for both groups (test-retest/inter-observer and cross-sectional age groups) using Prism (10.3.1 for Mac). Test-retest repeatability and inter-observer reliability statistics were calculated using Spearman rank correlation (Prism), due to non-normality of some outcomes. Baseline data for some dogs evaluated for reliability testing were also included in the cross-sectional age-association analysis.

To assess whether age (greater than or less than 8 years) and odorant type influenced initial interaction duration, we performed a nonparametric factorial analysis using the aligned rank transform (ART) framework with age group, odorant type, and their interaction included in the model. Post hoc comparisons between age groups within each odorant were conducted using estimated marginal means with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. Eight years was selected as the cutoff because neophilia in novel object interaction is high in dogs < 8 years35, and olfactory habituation-dishabituation has been reported in at least a proportion of dogs aged < 8 years36. Similarly, cognitive function, which is a component of sensory information processing, declines significantly in dogs aged > 8 years34. For the purpose of assessing whether trial order (1st, 2nd, 3rd, or 4th quartile of presentation order) was related to beef H1 summed interaction duration, we used a Kruskal-Wallis test – a nonparametric test was chosen due to non-normality of some outcomes (Prism). Beef was selected because it was the odorant that had most consistently the longest interaction duration in tested dogs.

For cross-sectional regression analysis, multiple linear and beta regression models were used (R version 4.4.0) to examine the associations between age, bodyweight, sex, and AKC breed group with behavioral outcomes. Linear regression was used to analyze continuous outcomes, including mean recovery of attention and mean habituation time, both of which were log-transformed prior to analysis to improve normality and stabilize variance. Beta regression was used to analyze continuous outcomes bounded between 0 and 1, such as proportion of initial interactions <1s, proportion met (or did not meet) habituation and dishabituation criterion. For each outcome, a base model including age, bodyweight, and sex was compared to an extended model that also included AKC breed group. Model comparisons were conducted using likelihood ratio tests or nested F-tests. Because AKC breed group and body weight are biologically related, we assessed potential multicollinearity using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIFs), adjusted for degrees of freedom. Adjusted GVIF values were all close to 1, indicating no evidence of problematic collinearity. The relative importance of each covariate was assessed by partitioning the explained variance (R2) across covariates based on their marginal contributions to model fit.

All illustrations were generated in Prism (10.3.1 for Mac).
