Educational Pathways, Parenting, and the Development of Self-Esteem from Adolescence to Young Adulthood: Evidence from South Korea
Authors: Sue-Yeon Song, Wei-Lin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite strong research traditions in educational stratification and parenting, few studies have simultaneously examined how educational tracks and parenting styles jointly predict self-esteem from adolescence into young adulthood, particularly in the East Asian context where status and pressure are markedly salient. This study examines how educational pathways and parenting dimensions shape self-esteem trajectories from early adolescence to post-high school in South Korea. Using three waves of data (2010–2016, ages 13 to 19) from the nationally representative Korean Children and Youth Panel Survey (KCYPS) (N = 1,484, 52% females; Mage = 15.89 years, SD = 0.34), results indicated pathway stratification in self-esteem. Compared with the academic-to-university pathway (56%), adolescents in the academic-to-junior (21%) and technical-to-postsecondary (10%) pathways reported significantly lower self-esteem across waves, though no significant differences emerged for the technical-to-labor market (9%) and academic-to-labor market (4%) pathways. Parenting dimensions showed robust associations: affection was positively associated with self-esteem, whereas excessive expectations and excessive interference were negatively associated. Moderation analyses revealed parenting operates as a context amplifier, primarily among the technical-to-postsecondary pathway. The results demonstrate that self-esteem development in adolescence is shaped by an integrated framework that combines macro-level educational structures with micro-level family processes, suggesting a practical need for school–family partnerships and parent education programs that emphasize warm and autonomy-supporting parenting, especially for youth experiencing major educational transitions.Keywords: educational pathways, educational stratification, self-evaluations, transition to adulthood, East Asia

Introduction

Educational pathways and family processes are central to how youth interpret their standing in school and their prospects for adulthood, yet research has rarely examined how these institutional and relational contexts jointly relate to self-esteem during the transition out of high school. This gap is especially important in South Korea, where educational pathways are highly visible, credential hierarchies remain salient, and parental educational involvement is intensive (Park, 2015). Although other dimensions of adjustment, such as well-being, flourishing, and resilience, are broader and more reflective of overall psychological functioning, this study focuses on self-esteem because it aligns with the theoretical focus on the status and opportunity that educational pathways signal, and how parental behavior may relate to the self-evaluative implications of those pathways. Self-esteem refers to a global evaluation of one’s worth, which developmental research indicates is relatively stable yet responsive to major role transitions such as leaving high school and entering higher education or the workforce (Orth et al., 2018). This study examines how educational pathways and parenting dimensions are associated with self-esteem from early adolescence to young adulthood in a nationally representative Korean panel.

The South Korean Context

Educational systems shape self-evaluations by sorting adolescents into socially legible pathways. In South Korea, upper-secondary differentiation is organized through school types rather than through a single, uniform merit screen. Students enter general and vocational high schools through a combination of prior academic achievement, application to schools with different curricular missions, and, in many urban areas that are slated as “equalization zones”, a lottery system. Vocational high schools include specialized vocational and Meister schools (i.e., elite schools modeled after the German master craftsman tradition), but, importantly, vocational pathways are not synonymous with immediate labor-market entry. The Korean case is therefore better understood as a mixed tracking environment than as a purely exam-based hierarchy (Byun et al., 2012). Psychological research on social comparison has clarified that self-evaluations depend partly on the composition of achievements within a given setting, not only on individual performance (Fang et al., 2018). In high-stakes systems, the evaluative meaning of educational placement may therefore extend beyond academic self-concept to broader self-evaluations, including global self-esteem.

South Korea is a useful case for examining these questions because credential hierarchies are salient, the social meaning of pathways is highly visible, and Korean adolescents often experience intensive parental educational involvement, e.g., close monitoring of academic progress and private tutoring, which can intensify evaluative pressure (Park, 2015). The present study distinguishes five educational pathways in the South Korean context, all of which start at middle school and then differentiate into: technical high school to labor market (i.e., technical-to-labor market), technical high school to postsecondary education (i.e., technical-to-postsecondary), academic high school to the labor market (i.e., academic-to-labor market), academic high school to junior college (i.e., academic-to-junior college), and academic high school to university (i.e., academic-to-university).

A few clarifications capture important nuances of the Korean system and its richness for studying self-esteem outcomes. First, technical refers to vocational or specialized vocational high schools; postsecondary refers broadly to any tertiary transition after high school; junior college refers to short-cycle tertiary institutions below the four-year university sector; and university refers to the four-year sector. This typology captures not only secondary-track placement but also the social meaning of post-high-school destinations. Destination uncertainty refers not to any change in educational orientation per se, but to cases in which a student’s observed destination is less clearly aligned with the destination ordinarily implied by the secondary track. Intermediate credentials are qualifications such as junior college qualifications that occupy a middle position in Korea’s credential hierarchy, offering more schooling than direct labor-market entry but less status than a four-year university degree (Park, 2015).

Within this relatively high-stakes system, pathway placement and pathway misalignment may shape global self-esteem by organizing social comparison and signaling competence and future opportunity (Fang et al., 2018). Four-year university attendance remains a major status marker, but vocational-to-tertiary transitions are emerging as an uneven, longer-term trajectory. Recent increases in tertiary transitions among vocational high-school graduates therefore reflect changing pathway logics within an already credential-conscious system (OECD, 2022).

Family processes provide a proximal context in which these institutional placements are interpreted. Research consistently links parental warmth and support with higher self-esteem and psychologically controlling or intrusive parenting with lower self-esteem, because such practices undermine autonomy and internalized self-worth (Pinquart & Gerke, 2019). These institutional and family contexts are unlikely to operate independently; family climate may buffer or amplify the self-evaluative implications of educational pathways. This is especially likely in Korea, where educational competition is intense, and family involvement in schooling is often closely tied to future status attainment.

Self-Esteem as a Contextual Developmental Resource

A rigorous educational account of self-esteem must reconcile two well-established empirical realities. First, global self-esteem exhibits substantial rank-order stability over time, indicating that individual differences are partly trait-like. Second, self-esteem also shows systematic mean-level change and meaningful within-person variability across developmental periods and major life transitions, indicating sensitivity to context and experience (Orth et al., 2018; Reitz, 2022). Meta-analytic evidence suggests that self-esteem is relatively stable in early adolescence, rises from mid-adolescence into young adulthood, and varies considerably across individuals as they negotiate salient transitions and changing social roles (Orth et al., 2018; Reitz, 2022). Transition-focused studies further show that self-esteem trajectories may shift around the move from education to work, with differences emerging as a function of achievement-related experiences, institutional destinations, and the quality of adjustment to new roles (Filosa et al., 2022).

In educational research, this means that self-esteem should be understood neither as a purely fixed trait nor as a diffuse byproduct of schooling. Although global self-esteem is conceptually distinct from domain-specific self-evaluations, longitudinal and cross-cultural evidence indicates that it is closely linked to evaluations in salient domains such as academic competence and peer relations (Orth et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2023). In school settings, repeated experiences of competence, belonging, and social recognition are therefore likely to feed into broader self-evaluations. This interpretation is consistent with longitudinal meta-analytic evidence showing that self-esteem and social relationships reciprocally predict one another over time, as well as with recent syntheses indicating that self-esteem during adolescence is intertwined with social relationships and academic achievement across development (Harris & Orth, 2020). Korean longitudinal evidence similarly suggests that diminished teacher support is associated with lower adolescent self-esteem amid academic burnout, underscoring that relational climates within schools matter for self-evaluative development (Choi et al., 2024). Accordingly, self-esteem is best conceptualized here as a contextual developmental resource: it can support engagement and adaptation, but it also registers the cumulative psychosocial meaning of adolescents’ location within educational pathways and credential hierarchies (Orth & Robins, 2022).

Educational Pathways, Institutional Stratification, and Self-Evaluations

Educational pathways are not merely sequences of courses and credentials; they are institutionalized positions. Comparative research on stratified educational systems has emphasized that characteristics of the system, such as stratification, standardization, and tracking (e.g., vocational or academic specificity), shape how students are sorted through school and into the labor force, thereby structuring young adults’ life chances and social comparisons (Van de Werfhorst, 2019). Within educational institutions, social comparison is embedded in institutional labeling and peer composition, acting as a central mechanism linking pathway stratification to self-evaluations. For example, the big-fish–little-pond effect demonstrates that self-perceptions depend in part on contextual achievement norms, showing how higher-performing educational contexts can produce less favorable self-beliefs among equally capable students (Fang et al., 2018). In strongly exam- and credential-oriented systems, such as South Korea and East Asian countries, these contextual processes plausibly extend beyond academic self-concepts to broader self-evaluations, particularly when educational tracks are widely interpreted as signals of competence and worth (Fang et al., 2018; Park, 2015). Related Korean studies have found that academic achievement and self-esteem are positively associated and mutually reinforcing over time, which reflects the tight coupling of performance and self-beliefs in the educational context (Park, 2024).

Indeed, empirical evidence from tracked systems suggests that educational placement can carry meaningful broader self-evaluative consequences, although the strongest evidence concerns academic self-concept rather than global self-esteem. Research on the big-fish-little-pond effect has shown that students’ self-perceptions are shaped by the achievement level of their reference group, and comparative work indicates that these contextual effects tend to be stronger in more selective school systems (Salchegger, 2016). A smaller body of research on global self-esteem further suggests that track differences may emerge, but that they vary by gender and by the institutional organization of tracks. For example, students in technical or vocational tracks have, in some contexts, reported lower self-esteem than peers in general tracks (Van Houtte et al., 2012). For the Korean case, these findings imply that a pathway-sensitive approach is preferable to a simple academic-vocational binary. Vocational high schools in Korea have long functioned not only as routes to employment but also as routes into tertiary education, while the tertiary sector itself distinguishes short-cycle colleges from four-year universities (Park, 2015). The social meaning of an educational route therefore depends on the combination of secondary-track placement and postsecondary destination. Under these conditions, pathway misalignment, for example, academic-track students entering the labor market or vocational-track students moving into tertiary education, may alter self-evaluations because students must reinterpret their relative standing, future prospects, and the value of their credentials within a highly visible status hierarchy (Fang et al., 2018).

In South Korea, the structure of competition and the long-term restructuring of vocational education have made these distinctions increasingly salient. The share of vocational high school graduates enrolling in tertiary education has followed a markedly non-linear trajectory. Enrollment rates rose from 44.9% in 2001 to a historical peak of 73.5% in 2009, remaining high at 71.1% in 2010. They then declined steadily to 32.5% in 2017, before recovering to 45.0% in 2018. After standing at 42.5% in 2020, the rate increased again from 45.0% in 2021 to 49.2% in 2025 (Korean Educational Development Institute [KEDI], 2020; Ministry of Education & Korean Educational Development Institute [KEDI], 2025). This markedly non-linear pattern, rather than a simple upward or downward trend, underscores the importance of examining distinct educational pathways among vocational high school graduates, particularly the technical-to-tertiary and technical-to-work tracks, rather than assuming a uniform vocational trajectory.

Parenting Dimensions and Adolescent Self-Esteem

Research on parenting and adolescent self-evaluation has consistently highlighted the benefits of warmth and the risks associated with psychologically controlling parenting. Although classic typologies remain useful as descriptive frameworks, more recent work suggests that parental support, behavioral control, and psychological control should be differentiated rather than treated as a single continuum (Kuppens & Ceulemans, 2019).

Further, recent work on overinvolved parenting during the transition to adulthood suggests that autonomy-thwarting involvement is associated with poorer adjustment, including lower self-efficacy and greater internalizing difficulties. This pattern reinforces the importance of distinguishing supportive involvement from intrusive involvement when interpreting parental educational engagement (Pinquart & Gerke, 2019).

Korean and other East Asian contexts complicate the interpretation of parental control and expectation because high educational involvement may be embedded in local values of guidance, care, and parental responsibility. In the Korean case, intensive parental educational involvement is more appropriately understood in relation to the institutional salience of competitive university admissions, extensive private tutoring, and credential competition, all of which can normalize close parental monitoring while also heightening evaluative pressure (Chen & Chen, 2026; Park, 2015).

Distinguishing culturally normative involvement from psychologically harmful control therefore requires an explicit threshold. In the present study, that threshold is not defined by measuring parental expectations alone, but by adolescents’ reports on whether it exceeds their capabilities or is experienced as pressure. Under this definition, the analysis does not assume that strong parental involvement is harmful in itself; rather, it identifies the point at which culturally intelligible parental involvement is experienced by youth as burdensome and autonomy-thwarting (Soenens & Vansteenkiste, 2010; Kuppens & Ceulemans, 2019). This definition is consistent with theory on psychological control, which treats parental involvement as problematic when it undermines autonomy rather than simply when it is intensive, as well as with how the KCYPS itself measures parental involvement, which assesses parental demands in terms of one’s ability and pressure and excessive interference in terms of restricting autonomy.

Integrating Educational Pathways and Parenting: An Ecological and Life-Course Perspective

Ecological and life-course perspectives suggest that educational pathways and parenting should be understood as linked contexts rather than as separate influences on self-evaluation. The bioecological model emphasizes that development unfolds through proximal processes within nested institutional settings, whereas life-course theory highlights the importance of timing, transitions, and linked lives (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006; Elder et al., 2003). Together, these perspectives imply that the self-evaluative meaning of pathway placement is interpreted within ongoing family relationships and within the opportunities and constraints attached to particular educational routes (Conger et al., 2010).

From this perspective, parental affection may soften the psychological costs associated with ambiguous pathways or paths that have less credential value by affirming a child’s worth beyond academic placement. Conversely, excessive parent expectation and interference may intensify evaluative pressure when youth encounter pathway misalignment or intermediate credential positions. This account is consistent with longitudinal evidence showing that self-esteem remains responsive to major educational and work transitions (Filosa et al., 2022; Orth et al., 2018). It is also consistent with developmental research indicating that warm and psychologically controlling parenting shape how adolescents internalize stressful experiences and evaluate themselves (Pinquart & Gerke, 2019).

Current Study

Research on self-esteem during the transition from adolescence to young adulthood has rarely examined educational pathways and parenting within a single longitudinal framework. South Korea, where educational routes are highly visible and parental educational involvement is often intensive, provides a useful context for addressing this gap. Aiming to connect educational pathway-based stratification with distinct parenting dimensions, the study uses longitudinal data to examine whether self-esteem differs across five educational pathways: technical-to-labor market, technical-to-postsecondary, academic-to-labor market, academic-to-junior college, and academic-to-university (Research Question 1). It also examines whether parental affection, excessive expectation, and excessive interference are associated with self-esteem from adolescence to the period immediately after high school (Research Question 2). Finally, it examines whether these parenting dimensions moderate educational pathway differences in self-esteem (Research Question 3). In doing so, the study provides a focused test of how institutional pathways and family processes jointly relate to global self-evaluation during a major educational transition.

Methods

Data and Participants

The data used in this study were drawn from the Korean Children and Youth Panel Survey (KCYPS) 2010, a nationally representative longitudinal survey that performed seven waves of data collection from 2010 to 2016 (N = 1,881), interviewing adolescents and their guardians. Employing a multistage stratified cluster sampling design, the KCYPS 2010 selected students in the first year of middle school and followed them annually until the first year of college. In the KCYPS 2010, participants were followed annually from 2010 to 2016, allowing analyses to examine trajectories over time. The KCYPS 2010 collected detailed information on respondents’ experiences in family and at school, deviant and health behaviors, and developmental outcomes, spanning adolescence through young adulthood (ages 13 to 19). The dataset’s documentation indicates that the parenting attitude instrument includes subfactors such as affection, excessive expectations, and excessive interference (National Youth Policy Institute, 2017). As such, the KCYPS 2010 provides rich information that enables an in-depth examination of individuals’ self-esteem from adolescence to young adulthood and its association with educational pathways and parenting styles. A few studies have utilized the KCYPS 2010 to examine the development of adolescents and young adults (Choi, 2022; Cho et al., 2018).

This study analyzed data from only three waves, i.e., waves 1, 6, and 7, because not all waves included measures of self-esteem and parenting styles. Cases were eliminated due to missing information on self-esteem, educational pathways, and parenting styles. The final analytic sample contained 1,484 respondents.

Measures

Self-esteem

Self-esteem was measured at waves 1, 6, and 7 using a 10-item scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.86) rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The scale captures respondents’ global evaluations of self-worth and self-acceptance. Negatively worded items were reverse-coded prior to analysis so that higher scores consistently reflected higher levels of self-esteem. A composite score was computed by averaging responses across all 10 items.

The items assessed both positive self-regard (e.g., “I am satisfied with myself,” “I feel that I have many strengths,” “I take a positive attitude toward myself”) and negative self-evaluations (e.g., “At times, I think I am no good at all,” “I feel that I do not have much to be proud of,” “I tend to feel that I am a failure”). Additional items included perceptions of competence and personal value (e.g., “I am able to do things as well as most other people,” “I feel that I am at least as valuable as other people,” “At times, I feel that I am a useless person,” and “I wish I could have more respect for myself”). Higher composite scores indicate greater global self-esteem. Factor analysis was conducted to validate the 10-item combination for self-esteem.

Educational pathways

This study identified educational pathways spanning three stages: middle school, high school, and post-graduation routes. All respondents attended middle school between ages 13 and 15, followed by either a technical or academic high school between ages 16 and 18. After graduation, they either entered the labor market or pursued higher education. Based on high school type and post-graduation route, five pathways were identified, all beginning from middle school: technical high school to labor market (labeled technical-to-labor market), technical high school to postsecondary education (labeled technical-to-postsecondary), academic high school to the labor market (labeled academic-to-labor market), academic high school to junior college (labeled academic-to-junior college), and academic high school to university (labeled academic-to-university).

Parenting styles

Information on parenting styles was assessed at waves 1, 6, and 7 and included three types: affection, excessive expectation, and excessive interference. Affection was assessed by four items (α = 0.82, 4-point Likert scale): (1) “They respect my opinion,” (2) “They express their love often,” (3) “They cheer me up when I am having a hard time,” and (4) “They compliment me a lot.” Factor analysis was conducted to validate the four-item combination for affection. Excessive expectation was assessed by using four items (α = 0.89, 4-point Likert scale), and they were (1) “Their expectations are higher than my ability so I feel pressure,” (2) “I wish they wouldn’t worry so much about me,” (3) “They push me harder on studying than anything else,” (4) “They always emphasize that I have to do better than others.” Factor analysis was conducted to validate the combination of the four items for excessive expectation. Excessive interference was assessed by using four items (α = 0.64, 4-point Likert scale): (1) “They are nervous about things I do and tell me not to do even those things that other kids normally do,” (2) “They always emphasize that I have to be strong and overcome any kind of difficulty I face,” (3) “They interfere too much about everything,” and (4) “They often tell me not to do what I really want to do.” Factor analysis was conducted to validate the four-item combination for excessive interference.

Covariates

The analysis included time-invariant covariates: gender (male and female), parental educational attainment (less than college, some college, college or more), residence (Seoul area and non-Seoul area), family income (continuous variable), and living with parents (biological parents and others). All covariates were measured at wave 1.

Analytic Approach

To begin, descriptive statistics were calculated to provide an overview of respondents’ self-esteem, the distribution of educational pathways, and the three parenting styles. Next, two-level multilevel models were used to estimate self-esteem trajectories from adolescence to young adulthood (ages 13 to 19), controlling for all covariates. The inclusion of covariates allowed the researchers to assess how self-esteem trajectories were influenced by respondents’ educational pathways, parenting styles, and both individual and familial factors. Level 1 consists of repeated observations nested within individuals, capturing within-person variation in self-esteem over time. The time-varying parenting style measures (i.e., affection, excessive expectation, and excessive interference) and the time-invariant predictors measured at Wave 1 (i.e., educational pathways and covariates: gender, parental educational attainment, residence, family income, and living arrangement) were specified at this level. Level 2 consists of individuals, capturing stable between-person differences. The model estimates fixed effects, representing the average associations between self-esteem and all Level 1 predictors across individuals, as well as random effects, including the variance of the Level 2 random intercepts (quantifying between-person differences in average self-esteem) and the residual variance (capturing remaining unexplained within-person variation at Level 1). Finally, the interaction between educational pathway and parenting style was included in the two-level multilevel models to estimate changes in self-esteem associated with different educational pathways and parenting styles. This study also presents predicted self-esteem values by educational pathway and parenting style and illustrates the differential trajectories of self-esteem from adolescence to young adulthood. All analyses were conducted using Stata 16.1.

The most common type of educational pathway was academic-to-university, with 56% of respondents reporting it, followed by academic-to-junior (21%), technical-to-postsecondary (around 10%), technical-to-labor market (around 9%), and finally academic-to-labor market, with only 4% of respondents reporting. The mean of self-esteem by educational pathway was 2.47 (SD = 0.49) for academic-to-university, 2.34 (SD = 0.52) for academic-to-junior, 2.29 (SD = 0.53) for technical-to-postsecondary, 2.30 (SD = 0.51) for technical-to-labor market, and 2.43 (SD = 0.51) for academic-to-labor market. A one-way ANOVA was conducted to examine differences in baseline self-esteem across educational pathways. Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons indicated that the academic-to-university group reported significantly higher self-esteem than the technical-to-postsecondary (p = .001), academic-to-junior (p = .001), and technical-to-labor market groups (p = .003). Regarding parenting styles, the mean for affection was 3.01, with a range from 3 (somewhat agree) to 4 (strongly agree). The mean of excessive expectation was 2.75, with a range from 2 (disagree) to 3 (somewhat agree). The mean of excessive interference was 2.41, with a range from 2 (disagree) to 3 (somewhat agree).

Finally, to better illustrate the patterns of self-esteem as they differ by educational pathway and parenting style, Fig. 1 presents the predicted values of self-esteem by (a) educational pathway and affection, (b) educational pathway and excessive expectation, and (c) educational pathway and excessive interference. Predictive margins were estimated for each educational pathway across four levels of parenting styles (i.e., affection, excessive expectation, and excessive interference). All margins were statistically significant (p < .001). Two distinct patterns emerge in Fig. 1.

Adjusted Self-Esteem: Affection, Excessive Expectation, and Excessive Interference

First, looking at the gray lines, the technical-to-labor-market and technical-to-postsecondary groups showed similar patterns in parenting style, particularly in affection. Both groups had lower self-esteem when their parents showed less affection, but higher self-esteem when their parents showed more affection. The parenting styles of excessive expectation and excessive interference had a similar influence on the technical-to-labor-market and technical-to-postsecondary groups; both groups had higher self-esteem when their parents showed lower levels of excessive expectation and excessive interference, but lower self-esteem when their parents’ excessive expectation and excessive interference increased.

Second, looking at the black lines, among academic high school attendees (i.e., the academic-to-labor market, academic-to-junior, and academic-to-university groups), the academic-to-junior group generally had lower self-esteem when any parenting style was higher. This group, which completed an academic track but enrolled in junior college rather than a four-year university, shows a pattern in which higher parenting involvement of any type is associated with lower self-esteem, rather than the protective pattern observed in the technical-to-postsecondary group. The data do not permit identification of the process underlying this pattern, and future research with more detailed measures of students’ subjective experiences would be needed to explain it.

A series of sensitivity analyses was conducted to assess the robustness of findings. First, results from the main analysis (listwise deletion) were compared with those from multiple imputation. Multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) with 20 imputations was applied to handle missing values in parental educational attainment (0.93% of missing), family income (5.36% of missing), and living arrangement (0.14% of missing), assuming data were missing at random (MAR). Pooled estimates across imputed datasets yielded standard errors and confidence intervals that appropriately reflect imputation uncertainty. Results from the imputed data were consistent with those from the main analysis, supporting the robustness of findings and the appropriateness of listwise deletion as the primary analytic approach. Second, given evidence of a potential association between psychological functioning and self-esteem (Zell & Johansson, 2025), an additional model was estimated with respondents’ well-being at Wave 1 included as a covariate. Results remained similar to the main analysis, further supporting the robustness of the findings.

Discussion

Research on self-esteem during adolescence and young adulthood has rarely integrated educational stratification and family processes within a single longitudinal framework, even though educational routes and parental involvement are both likely to shape how young people interpret their own competence, status, and future prospects. This gap is especially important in South Korea, where credential hierarchies are salient, educational pathways are highly visible, and family involvement in education is often intensive. This study addresses that gap by examining five educational pathways and three parenting dimensions in relation to repeated measures of self-esteem, showing that pathway differences and parenting associations coexist and that moderation is concentrated in the technical-to-postsecondary pathway.

Educational pathways were systematically related to self-esteem across the observed waves. The academic-to-university pathway consistently showed the highest self-esteem, whereas the academic-to-junior-college pathway showed persistently lower self-esteem. In addition, once parenting dimensions are included, the technical-to-postsecondary pathway also showed significantly lower self-esteem than the academic-to-university pathway, whereas the academic-to-labor-market pathway did not. These findings suggest that self-esteem is shaped not simply by the academic or technical track the student is in, but by the social meaning of the full pathway that follows from that track. In stratified systems, pathways operate as status signals and structure adolescents’ comparison environments. From this perspective, the comparatively lower self-esteem observed in the academic-to-junior-college and technical-to-postsecondary routes may reflect the comparatively ambiguous standing of pathways that remain institutionally legitimate but less strongly aligned with the highest-status route in Korea’s credential hierarchy (Dekeyser et al., 2024; Van Houtte et al., 2012).

Parenting dimensions showed strong and coherent associations with self-esteem. Affection was positively associated with self-esteem, whereas excessive expectation and excessive interference were negatively associated. These findings are consistent with developmental theory that distinguishes warmth and autonomy-supportive involvement from psychologically controlling forms of parenting, which intrude on adolescents’ internal world and render self-worth more contingent on parental approval (Ryan & Deci, 2018; Soenens & Vansteenkiste, 2010). These findings also align with more recent evidence showing that parental warmth and autonomy support are broadly beneficial for adolescent well-being across families, whereas conditional and pressuring forms of parental regard are associated with contingent self-esteem, greater distress, and poorer relational functioning (Haines & Schutte, 2023). In the Korean context, this distinction is especially important because strong parental educational involvement is culturally common and institutionally reinforced. The present results therefore suggest that what matters for adolescents’ self-esteem is not parental involvement per se, but whether that involvement is experienced as affectionate and supportive or as pressuring and autonomy-thwarting.

A moderating effect of parenting style was present but not generalized across all pathways. The interaction results indicated that parenting matters especially strongly for adolescents in the technical-to-postsecondary pathway: higher affection was associated with higher predicted self-esteem, whereas higher excessive expectation and excessive interference were associated with lower predicted self-esteem. A comparable moderation was not observed consistently across the other pathways. This selective pattern suggests that parenting may operate as a context amplifier for youth in educational positions that are institutionally mixed or socially less straightforward, rather than exerting uniform moderation across the entire pathway structure. Consistent with the expectation that context would matter, the influence of family resources and pressures may become particularly salient when adolescents navigate educational transitions characterized by greater uncertainty or ambiguity (Kretschmer et al., 2014). One plausible interpretation is that the technical-to-postsecondary group occupies a particularly sensitive position in Korea’s education system: these adolescents originate from a track historically associated with labor-market entry but are now continuing into tertiary education, thereby navigating both upward aspiration and intensified academic comparison. In such contexts, parental affection may serve as a stabilizing source of recognition, whereas parental pressure or interference may further heighten self-evaluative strain. At the same time, the present data do not allow a definitive test of whether this pattern reflects differential susceptibility, vantage sensitivity, or more asymmetric vulnerability. The more cautious conclusion is therefore that parenting carries heightened relevance for self-esteem among youth in this specific pathway, not that all youth in nontraditional or uncertain pathways are uniformly more sensitive to family influence (Boele et al., 2024; Reuter et al., 2022).

Several methodological considerations should temper interpretation. Although the KCYPS is an annual panel, the variables used here are not available in every wave. The present results are best interpreted as associations across repeated measurement occasions rather than as continuous annual growth trajectories (National Youth Policy Institute, 2017). Attrition, as in all longitudinal panel studies, remains a concern. Parenting is measured through adolescents’ own reports, which is substantively meaningful because perceived parenting is central to the experience of support, pressure, and autonomy threat, but it also raises the possibility of shared method variance. The internal consistency of excessive interference is relatively modest, which likely attenuates estimates and warrants cautious interpretation. Finally, even with a longitudinal design, educational pathway selection and parenting processes are likely endogenous to prior achievement, family resources, and earlier self-esteem. Recent longitudinal and idiographic work further suggests that associations between psychological control and self-esteem can be reciprocal and heterogeneous across families. For this reason, the present study interprets the findings as longitudinal associations rather than causal effects (Boele et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2024).

The findings also carry broader implications for Korean education policy. In South Korea, parental educational involvement is embedded in a wider institutional environment marked by intense university entrance competition, the continuing salience of a shadow education at home through private tutoring and other means, and strong social returns to prestigious credentials. The psychosocial consequences identified here are therefore unlikely to be reducible to family dynamics alone. Policy responses should move beyond individual-level counseling and address the institutional uncertainty surrounding postsecondary routes themselves, including clearer public guidance on the meaning and prospects of different tertiary options, stronger school-based career counseling, and more credible bridges linking technical high schools to both tertiary education and sustainable employment (Park, 2015).

Conclusion

How self-esteem is patterned across the educational transitions of late adolescence remains insufficiently understood, particularly how institutional pathways and family processes operate together in a high-stakes, credential-centered context such as South Korea. Addressing this gap, the present study linked five educational pathways and three parenting dimensions to repeated measures of self-esteem from adolescence into the period immediately after high school. Self-esteem was not distributed randomly across pathways, and parenting style was influential, most notably for adolescents in the technical-to-postsecondary pathway, a pathway notable for its greater uncertainty. The findings indicate that late-adolescent self-esteem is closely associated with both institutional pathways and proximal family climates, supporting an understanding of self-esteem as a contextual developmental resource during the transition to adulthood. In practical terms, the results underscore the value of strengthening credible school-to-tertiary and school-to-work bridges for youth in less clearly advantaged pathways while helping families replace intrusive pressure with warmth and autonomy-supportive involvement.
