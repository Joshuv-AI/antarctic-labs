3. Executive Summary:
The goals of this SETO project (DE-EE0009516, Open-Air Manufacturing of Efficient
Large-Area Perovskite Solar Cells to Meet Stability and Cost Targets) are to build on
current open-air spray-based capabilities to develop fully open-air manufactured
perovskite cells with scalable processing that eliminates the remaining vacuum- based
processes and concomitantly improves cell stability. The focus is on robust materials
integration with a fundamental understanding of degradation mechanisms. Stable spray
coated NiO hole transport layers (HTL), patented rapid spray-plasma processed (RSPP)
robust perovskite cells, and leverage from previous success with perovskite module
encapsulation and moisture barrier technologies will complement the production of
commercially competitive and stable perovskite solar modules with T 80 above 20 years.
The project is intended to mitigate the foremost processing barriers to wide-scale
commercial deployment of perovskite technology and provide significant momentum
towards the long-term technology objective of 0.02 $/kWhr.
Through this project, we successfully demonstrated a fully open-air compatible perovskite
device stack alongside manufacturing pathways for perovskite solar cells and modules
using scalable, high-throughput processes. Open-air ultrasonic spray deposition,
combustion oxidation, rapid thermal processing, and plasma curing were deployed and
optimized for all functional device layers, including charge transport layers and
electrodes, without reliance on vacuum-based methods. Significant advances were
achieved in electron transport layer (ETL) and electrode materials designed for open-air
processability and improved device stability. Sprayed organic ETLs preserved perovskite
crystal structure and optoelectronic properties, enabling >20% efficiencies. Open-air
processed transparent conducting oxides and rear electrodes were developed to mitigate
metal diffusion and degradation pathways while drastically reducing manufacturing costs
over vacuum-based alternative techniques.
Device stability was a central focus of this program. Accelerated aging protocols informed
by IEC and ISOS standards were implemented alongside high-throughput optical
diagnostics to identify degradation mechanisms and defect formation. These tools
enabled rapid screening of materials systems, validation of encapsulation strategies, and
correlation of optoelectronic degradation with device performance under combined
stressors including heat, humidity, and illumination. Results demonstrate that open-air
processed architectures can achieve stability metrics compatible with commercial targets.
Lastly, a comprehensive technoeconomic analysis was developed to quantify the cost
and throughput advantages of open-air manufacturing relative to conventional vacuum-
based processes. The model identifies vacuum deposition of electrodes and transport
layers as dominant cost drivers in perovskite module fabrication and shows that the fully
open-air approach demonstrated in this project enables substantial reductions in
manufacturing cost, significantly lowering the barrier for commercial deployment and
establishing a clear pathway toward challenging incumbent photovoltaic technologies.
Five peer-reviewed journal publications in top journals, eleven conference presentations,
and five Ph.D. dissertations were produced under this project.

5. Background:
Demonstrating a high-performance and stable organic-inorganic metal halide perovskite
PV technology that maintain T80 above 20 years at low-cost represents the remaining
grand challenge for cost-effective perovskite field operations.

efficient perovskite devices with PCE ~19% [1] containing highly stable NiO hole transport
layers (HTL),[2] perovskite films with 10x improvement in carrier lifetimes, [3] integrated
high-throughput laser scribing for large-area module development, [4] and a current focus
on module encapsulation and moisture barriers. [5] Our advanced technoeconomic
manufacturing analysis clearly indicates that the remaining vacuum-based processes
dominate the cost of production, with sputtered ITO ($6.20/m 2), thermally evaporated

Perovskites are prone to degradation from many stressors including moisture, heat,
mechanical stress, light and electrical bias. Moisture ingress can cause rapid degradation
of perovskite films due to hygroscopic organic cations that form hydrogen bonds with
water leading to a hydrated perovskite phase which compromises the crystal’s structural
stability. Exposure to elevated temperatures causes degradation from the rapid formation
of PbI2, volatilization of the organic component, diffusion of halide species and corrosion
of metal contacts. Residual tensile stress resulting from thermal expansion mismatch
between the perovskite film and substrate[6] generates a driving force for cracking and
delamination, which negatively impacts environmental stability. [7] Finally, the migration of
vacancies and perovskite phase segregation has been attributed to light exposure. In all
cases, a combination of stressors accelerates the perovskite degradation process.
We will build on our current open-air spray-based capabilities to develop fully open-air
manufactured perovskite cells with scalable processing that eliminates the remaining
vacuum-based processes and concomitantly improves cell stability. In addressing this
remaining grand challenge, we focus on robust materials integration with a fundamental
understanding of degradation mechanisms. We will complement our stable spray coated
NiO HTL, patented rapid spray-plasma processed (RSPP) robust perovskite cells, and
leverage from our current program’s demonstration of perovskite module encapsulation

and moisture barrier technologies, to produce commercially competitive and stable
perovskite solar modules with T80 above 20 years.
We have demonstrated rapid open-air processing of manufacturable device layers by
developing a robust and stable spray coated NiO HTL ($0.70/m 2) and perovskite active
layer ($2.49/m2). Inorganic ETLs have excellent stability and barrier properties but are not
easily processed on perovskite films in inverted P-I-N architectures due to high energy
deposition or annealing temperatures required. Also, they rely on sol-gel spin-coating or
vacuum-based deposition not amenable to large-scale inline production. Additionally, we
have reported on a low-temperature TiO x ETL produced directly on top of perovskite using
open-air chemical vapor deposition to form a fully inorganic, conformal, and compact TiO x
film with >30X fracture resistance than commonly used fullerene-based ETLs and
improved ambient stability, [8] although the lower conductivity of the amorphous layer has
motivated efforts to explore different inorganic materials with improved electrical
properties. This work established a path for open-air deposition of inorganic transport
layers on top of perovskite without degrading the underlying film.
We have also previously developed open-air processes for TCO films without damage on
plastic substrates using rapid thermal processing (RTP) or plasma curing. Implementing
a transparent conducting oxide (TCO) top electrode eliminates the concern of metal
diffusion through the ETL and reacting with the halide in the perovskite. Current state-of-
the-art architectures with the best reported stability under operational conditions thus
involve a TCO or carbon-based top electrode. The requirement that the ETL serve as an
effective buffer layer to prevent perovskite damage from RTP or plasma exposure is
fulfilled by the fact that inorganic-based ETLs are effective buffer layers against plasma
sputtering damage. In contrast, there are no requirements for minimizing thermal and
plasma exposure for the bottom electrode, which enables us to leverage more
conventional spray pyrolysis for TCO formation.
Scaling is another critical aspect of our proposed program. We have already resolved
some of the most formidable barriers to module-level scaling that the perovskite
community has been facing for years in our recent breakthrough in the rapid open-air
fabrication of perovskite solar modules (Fig. 2). Key advances include scalable large-
area spray deposition, new monolithic integration scribing techniques, advanced
photoluminescence characterization, and reproducible high-throughput
manufacturability. This work was the first industrially relevant attempt to address both
scalable and fast open-air PV module manufacturing for the perovskite layer in a single-
step conversion. The methods detailed in Fig. 2 are all directly translatable to continuous,
in-line production with throughputs that significantly outpace any other scalable deposition
process. The capabilities described would enable scaling to a pilot line producing efficient
and stable monolithically integrated perovskite modules—both electrodes and charge
transport layers, the perovskite layer, and all three scribes (P1, P2, and P3) using a single-
source rapid fiber laser technique—entirely produced in open-air at unparalleled
manufacturing speeds and module costs.

An important innovation with broader application to the perovskite community is our
comprehensive cell degradation investigation strategy that focuses on high-throughput
degradation monitoring of devices with a focus on targeting interfaces for improving
device stability. We have developed a full suite of optoelectronic, structural, and
mechanical characterization including color-normalized images, large-area optical
microscopy, X-ray diffraction, time resolved photoluminescence, and detailed
photoluminescence mapping at length scales from nm to cm to understand the
fundamental degradation pathways due to each individual stressor and combinations of
stressors (Fig. 4). This holistic approach including multiple synergistic environmental
stressors and in-situ characterization capabilities represents a significant advance over
existing stability studies. Detecting early-onset of degradation streamlines the design
iteration process towards achieving stable perovskite cells.

We will include a comprehensive study to understand and mitigate degradation
mechanisms in perovskite solar cells and modules. These will be validated by adherence
to IEC standards and ISOS protocols for accelerated aging and characterized by tracking
device performance under specific combinations of heat, light, humidity, and electrical
bias. We will conduct a full suite of optoelectronic, structural, and mechanical
characterization including color-normalized images (~1 mm resolution), maximum power
point tracking/open circuit operation, external quantum efficiency (~1 mm resolution),

laser beam induced current (LBIC) mapping (~10s at µm resolution), hyperspectral
photoluminescence mapping (~1 µm resolution), and electron microscopy (~1 nm
resolution). Including multiple synergistic stressors and in-situ characterization
capabilities over a wide range of length scales represents a significant advance over
existing stability studies and will allow for determination of the spatial distribution of
surface defects, grain boundaries, phase segregation, and interfacial reactions. Each
individual stressor and combinations of stressors for both unencapsulated (intrinsic
material stability) and fully encapsulated (extrinsic device stability) devices informs our
iterative design process. Additionally, we will establish relationships between
optoelectronic properties and device performance together with film morphology/structure
and stability. The ability to predict degradation and evaluate stability with rapid
characterization such as in-situ imaging and will streamline the design iteration process
towards the critical requirement of stable perovskite solar cells and modules.

6. Project Objectives:
This program will build on current open-air spray-based capabilities to develop fully open-
air manufactured perovskite cells with scalable processing that eliminates the remaining
vacuum- based processes and concomitantly improves cell stability. In addressing this
remaining grand challenge, the focus is on robust materials integration with a fundamental
understanding of degradation mechanisms. Stable spray coated NiO hole transport layers
(HTL), patented rapid spray-plasma processed (RSPP) robust perovskite cells, and
leverage from the current program’s success with perovskite module encapsulation and
moisture barrier technologies will complement the production of commercially competitive
and stable perovskite solar modules with T80 above 20 years.
The end of project goals are to develop a fully open-air spray route to manufacture
encapsulated perovskite solar cells and modules with an end objective of 100 cm 2 in size
with NREL certified power conversion efficiencies (PCE) > 20%. The project is intended
to mitigate the foremost processing barriers to wide-scale commercial deployment of
perovskite technology and provide significant momentum towards the long-term
technology objective of 0.02 $/kWhr.
The program is executed in 9 Tasks. Tasks 1-5 will commence in the initial Budget Period
(BP1) with a focus on open-air spray fabrication of the electrode and electron transport
layer (ETL) materials (Tasks 1-2), in addition to the initialization of stability assessments
(Task 3) and cost modeling (Task 4) towards levelized cost of energy (LCOE)
calculations. Engagement with industry partners throughout BP1 (Task 5) will establish
baseline understanding of key areas of interest to ensure relevant and impactful goals
throughout performance, stability, and cost optimization. The objectives of BP2 involve
scaling of the electrode and electron transport layers towards large-area films (Task 6)
utilizing the spray-deposition mechanisms developed in BP1. LCOE calculations and
refinement (Task 8) will complement studies on cell and module stability according to
published standards (Task 7), with a focus on perovskite module commercialization.
Formal evaluations of program accomplishments and end-of-project goals will be
completed by industry stakeholders (Task 9) to evaluate commercial potential.

The Workplan will be executed in nine Tasks and are summarized in two Budget Periods
(BP) that have been identified as most important.
BP1: Open-Air Spray Processing of ETL and Front and Rear Electrodes – The stack
architecture (Appendix A) and geometry of the cells and modules will be designed to
achieve optimal performance, mechanical reliability, and environmental stability. Task 1
will focus on developing open-air scalable spray fabrication of the top electron transport
layer using an optimal combination of organic, functionalized-organic, or inorganic layers.
This process will ensure that the sensitive underlying perovskite crystal structure is
undamaged and preserved to achieve high efficiency cells and modules. The intrinsic
material stability of the ETL and its barrier properties to perovskite volatile cation egress
and rear electrode metal ingress will be validated by subjecting unencapsulated cells and
modules to maximum power point tracking and thermal aging according to ISOS
standards.
The Task 2 goals are to address the need to reduce cost and increase throughput for the
fabrication of front electrode materials. Indium tin oxide along with aluminum zinc oxide
and indium zinc oxide will be investigated as transparent electrodes deposited using
delayed ignition combustion oxidation for lower temperature synthesis from liquid
precursors. The films will be sprayed in open air and cured in reductive plasma
environments to enhance film conductivity. Various fuel sources will be tested to optimize
for cost and film quality with a goal of producing sputter-quality films via open-air
fabrication processes. Low-temperature spray-deposition of metal films, nanowire
networks, nanoparticle systems, and inks will complement explorations in TCO materials
for enhanced conductivities for composite electrode systems. Various electrode
compositions will be explored for optimal device performance and mitigation of metal-
halide degradation observed in thermally evaporated rear electrodes.
Preliminary progress towards the program objective of stability will be addressed in Task
3. Progress towards stability objectives will involve demonstration of baseline stability of
fully open-air deposited unencapsulated perovskite cells in addition to the development
of an optimized encapsulation scheme for stability standard evaluations.
Cost modeling within Task 4 will show progress towards a commercially viable
architecture with the high-throughput spray-deposition developed in Tasks 1 and 2. Cost
models will consider and evaluate the proposed materials systems and spray-deposition
techniques against traditional low-throughput, vacuum-based deposition methods.
Task 5 will establish baseline contact with industry partners and stakeholders. These
relationships will leverage industry insights into relevant and impactful areas for
optimization with the unique processing capabilities explored throughout Tasks 1 and 2.
Consistent engagement with industry partners will ensure industrially relevant research
and development towards end-of-project goals.
BP2: Large-Area Spray Deposition and Stability Validation – Large-area open-air
processing of perovskite modules will be explored through scalable, high-throughput
deposition of the ETL, front and rear electrode materials while evaluating the impacts of
open-air deposition on stability, throughput, and levelized cost of energy (LCOE). The
goals of Task 6 are to challenge the low-throughput, high-cost vacuum-based deposition
with alternative solution-deposited methods towards entirely open-air processed

                                        Page 9 of 102

                                                                              Award # DE-EE0009516

perovskite solar modules. Efforts from Tasks 1 and 2 will be extended to larger deposition
areas while evaluating the impact of entirely open-air processed architectures on
degradation pathways demonstrated in vacuum-deposited layers.
Task 7 will focus on employing the innovations established in Tasks 1-3—ETL, front
electrode, rear electrode, and encapsulation scheme—for applications evaluating stability
standards. The materials and structure of the module encapsulation scheme will be
optimized for modules by a thorough screening process involving various encapsulation
architectures aged in a variety of environmental conditions (heat, humidity, UV, thermal
cycling) while tracking performance using IV measurements, laser beam induced current
mapping, X-ray diffraction, optical methods, and photoluminescence. Final optimization
of the encapsulation scheme will be assessed by Module Accelerated Sequential Testing
(MAST), simulating real-world UV, moisture, and thermal cycling.
Initial cost modeling in Task 4, along with the stability assessments throughout Task 6,
will inform development of LCOE calculations in Task 8. The materials systems and
throughputs developed in Task 5 will update the initial cost model for calculating LCOE
and show pathways towards commercialized perovskite solar modules.
Task 9 will incorporate formal evaluations of program accomplishments and objectives
through active engagement with stakeholders. The active program developments, in
conjunction with target end-of-project goals, will be evaluated for their industrial relevance
and potential impact, and industry feedback will inform key areas of interest towards
bridging the gap between program completion and commercialization.

7. Project Results and Discussion:
7.1. Open-Air Spray Processed Electron Transport Layers
Preliminary explorations into open-air spray compatible electron transport layer (ETL)
materials involved the down selection of candidate materials and architectures. Initial
selection of ETL material and architecture candidates was based on meeting the following
criteria: 1) Precursors must be dissolved into solution for spray processing 2) Precursor
solvent must be orthogonal to perovskite absorber layer 3) ETL deposition must not
involve high-temperature treatment or high-energy particle bombardment 4) ETL film
must be compatible with open-air processing. Both organic and inorganic materials, as
well as hybrid bilayers, were originally considered. Based on previous experience with
all-inorganic ETL layers[9] and very few successful reports in literature, [10] selection of ETL
architectures was narrowed down to either all-organic or bilayer organic-inorganic ETL
materials. Figure 5 shows the selected structures and the methods for depositing each
film layer.

  Figure 5. Selection of three ETL material architectures and methods for film deposition.

The first ETL selection consists of an organic fullerene derivative, phenyl-C61-butyric acid
methyl ester (PCBM), in combination with a hole-blocking layer of bathocuproine (BCP).
This ETL architecture is by far the most commonly used in inverted P-I-N devices
including current world record efficiencies. [11] Whereas these materials are typically spin-
coated or thermally evaporated, this program looks at fabricating these layers instead
through in-line open-air ultrasonic spray processes. Rapid thermal processing (RTP) may
also be utilized to promote rapid solvent removal for improved film morphology. The
second ETL selection consists of an organic PCBM layer in combination with an inorganic

                                         Page 16 of 102

                                                                       Award # DE-EE0009516

tin oxide (SnO 2) nanoparticle (NP) crystalline layer on top. The SnO 2 NP layer will utilize
a separate ultrasonic nozzle for raster spray deposition forming a dense layer that may
help improve device stability by limiting rear electrode metal diffusion. The third and final
ETL selection consists of an organic phenyl-C61-butyric acid (PCBA) layer in combination
with a dense and amorphous tin oxide (SnO x) layer. PCBA is the acid functionalized
version of PCBM and can form strong acid-base interactions with the basic SnOx layer.
The SnOx layer will be deposited using our previously developed open-air chemical vapor
deposition process. [9]
The SnO2 nanoparticle precursor is a commercial ink (Sigma) containing 2.5 wt% SnO 2
dispersed in butanol solvent. The tin oxide nanoparticles contain proprietary ligands to
aid in dispersion and prevent agglomeration to maintain the stated 7 nm particle size. An
X-Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) survey of a spin-coated film with 5 minute post-
anneal at 100°C reveals that the film retains up to 26% atomic concentration of carbon
(Figure 6a). X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) diffractograms indicate that only weak crystalline
phases are observed (PDF #00-041-1445) (Figure 6b).

Figure 6. a) XPS survey and b) GI-XRD of spin-coated SnO2 nanoparticle film with standard
(PDF# 00-041-1445).

The SnO2 NP film resistivity was also measured at 7.5×10 5 Ω∙cm. This value falls within
the range of literature values of 103 - 106 Ω∙cm, and may fall on the higher end of resistivity
values due to the presence of voids in the film. However, the measured value is
comparable to the resistivity of typical organic ETL films of C60/BCP measured at 2.0×105
Ω∙cm. Additional test structures containing front electrode/SnO 2/rear electrode were
fabricated to determine if metal-semiconductor Schottky barriers existed that would limit
charge extraction. Current-Voltage (I-V) scans with SnO 2 films and both Ag or Al rear

with C60/BCP/Ag (Figure 7).

Several challenges arise when transitioning from a spin-coated process to an ultrasonic
spray process such as the presence of coffee ring features from droplet drying
phenomena and dilution to a lower precursor concentration. Coffee ring features can be
mitigated by lowering the concentration of solute particles while increasing the rate of
evaporation of the solvent, either by changing to a lower boiling point solvent or increasing
the hot plate or substrate temperature. This allows less time for the solute particles to
redisperse and agglomerate at the edge of the drying droplet by means of capillary flow.
Dilution of the precursor with iso-propanol allowed ultrasonic spraying of smoother SnO 2
films. Despite the visual disappearance of coffee ring features, scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) images of the sprayed film nevertheless revealed agglomeration of
SnO2 nanoparticles into web-like channels (Figure 8). The channels appeared alongside
the existence of ~500nm size voids in the film. To reduce the formation of agglomerates,
strict precursor preparation methods including horn sonication, addition of a non-ionic
fatty ether surfactant, and sub-micron filtration were utilized. Despite these procedures,
Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) measurements revealed that particle sizes had an
effective diameter of ~90nm, which is an order of magnitude larger than the commercially
advertised 7nm. The commercial and proprietary ligands attached to the SnO2

                                                                          Award # DE-EE0009516

nanoparticles present extra challenges in attempts to reduce particle size. As a result, a
homemade synthesis with more precise particle size control may be necessary.

(left) and film voids (right, red circles).

Rapid Thermal Processing of Organic ETLs
In parallel development, an open-air spray fabrication process for deposition of organic
ETLs of PCBM and BCP was explored. PCBM, a C60 functionalized derivative, was
chosen because of its higher solubility in chlorobenzene, an orthogonal solvent to the
underlying perovskite. Sprayed films using C60 found needles that formed from particles
crashing out of solution during dry film formation. A one-pass spray process (Figure 9)
was developed as it was found that a multiple pass, raster-spray pattern would redissolve
the formed film with each subsequent pass and lead to an uncontrolled surface
morphology. Initial spray attempts consisted of spraying the precursor followed by thermal
drying from a hot plate underneath the substrate. However, lower hot plate temperatures
(< 50°C) resulted in pooling of the wet film and repeated pinning and unpinning of the
contact line, leading to thickness variations and a “ripple” like morphology. On the other
hand, higher hot plate temperatures resulted in droplets drying upon impact, preventing
the precursor from spreading and forming a blanket film.

Figure 9. (Left) Schematic of inline open-air ultrasonic spray processing of PCBM film. (Right)
Peak substrate temperature profile versus Adphos module distance-to-substrate.

                                        Page 19 of 102

                                                                     Award # DE-EE0009516

The solution to a controlled spray morphology came from the introduction of the Adphos
rapid thermal processing (RTP) module. First, the PCBM precursor is first sprayed onto
a room temperature substrate and a wet film is allowed to form. The Adphos module is
then immediately placed over the substrate. Using near-infrared (NIR) heating and
convective drying, the Adphos module can effectively flash off the chlorobenzene solvent,
allowing a PCBM film to form with a smooth morphology. The final PCBM morphology
and roughness is crucial because the subsequent bathocuproine (BCP) layer is typically
very thin (< 10nm).
The Adphos outputs an energy density of 330 kW/m 2 , and the substrate underneath can
experience a temperature change depending on the height-to-substrate distance of the
Adphos module and the length of the dwell time. Figure 9 shows the peak substrate
temperature from a 15 second Adphos as a function of distance to the substrate, as
measured by a thermocouple affixed to a glass substrate. Depending on process
conditions, substrate temperatures can range from 60°C - 375°C, which is more than
enough thermal energy to flash off chlorobenzene (boiling point = 132°C). An engineering
challenge with the Adphos module is to be able to provide the PCBM wet film with
sufficient thermal energy to ensure complete evaporation of chlorobenzene while avoiding
any thermal degradation damage to the perovskite layer. This becomes a main focus of
Milestone 1.1.2.
Spraying the next layer of BCP on top of PCBM requires finding another solvent that is
orthogonal to both PCBM and the perovskite layer. The BCP precursor is dissolved in
isopropanol at very low concentrations (0.5mg/mL), both because isopropanol is a poor
solvent for BCP and because the final film thickness is typically < 10 nm. BCP is sprayed
in a similar fashion to Figure 9 above, utilizing a one-pass spray followed by an Adphos
rapid thermal processing treatment. Efforts to reliably quantify the quality of the PCBM
and BCP layers is crucial to ensure uniform film deposition towards eventual application
on top of the perovskite absorber layer.
Through our stakeholder engagements with Adphos, we have secured a new NIR module
(NIR 96-125-E) that has a significantly larger heated area than our original unit with the
NIR 50-50. A comparison of the different heating modules is shown below in Figure 10.

 Figure 10. Comparison of different Adphos heating modules and peak substrate temperature
 measurements.

The NIR 96-125-E has three 1kW tungsten emitters but has an overall slightly lower
energy density than the NIR 50-50 due to the larger heated area. Figure 10 shows the
peak substrate temperature from a 15 second Adphos dwell versus distance-to-substrate,
as measured by a thermocouple affixed to a glass substrate. Compared to the NIR 50-

A significant upgrade in the new NIR 96-125-E module is the redesign of the cooling air
flow to the emitters. The smaller NIR 50-50 module is “front-cooled” meaning that a
cooling fan blows air across the emitters. Unfortunately, this cooling air can be
unintentionally redirected to impinge on the substrate surface and affect the wet PCBM
film morphology during the curing process (Figure 11). On the other hand, the NIR 96-
125-E is “back-cooled” and cooling air is redirected away from the substrate with the help
of a quartz shield between the emitters and the substrate. The absence of unwanted
cooling air impinging on the substrate surface results in much smoother, large area PCBM
films.

Post-Metrology Techniques for Sprayed BCP Films
While characterization of the relatively thick PCBM ETL layer could employ conventional
thin-film measurement techniques and visual analysis, the much thinner BCP layer
required alternative approaches to ensure target thickness, uniformity, and coverage.
Additionally, unlike evaporated films that have an in-situ Quartz Crystal Monitor to
measure film thickness, spray-processed BCP films lack a convenient film thickness
metrology. The sprayed films are both very thin (<10nm) and transparent, limiting the
ability of both contact and optical profilometry techniques. Ellipsometry was previously
used as a quick, vacuum-free method to measure the thickness of the sprayed films.
However, interpretation of ellipsometry measurements relied on implementing a single
model from literature found for BCP. [12] In their paper, Liu et al. formulated a model based
on evaporated BCP films that are >100 nm, which is two orders of magnitude larger than
the thicknesses of sprayed BCP films. Due to the large thickness of their BCP films, they
do not model the native silicon oxide layer on top of the silicon substrate as it is a small
fraction of the overall thickness of their film. Thus, while their model worked well for
approximating the thickness of sprayed BCP films, the model was fundamentally lacking
in accounting for the SiO 2 interlayer.
Angle-resolved X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (AR-XPS) is another technique that
can be used to measure the thickness of films <10 nm. The technique is based on
measuring the intensity ratio of the incident radiation to the emergent radiation. Using

                                       Page 21 of 102

                                                                        Award # DE-EE0009516

some materials properties to account for the attenuation coefficient, and thus the
attenuation length, the thickness of the analyzed material can then be calculated. Test
samples were fabricated consisting of a silicon substrate, native silicon oxide layer, and
a BCP film. The results of AR-XPS are shown below in Figure 12, clearly indicating where
one layer ends and the next begins. The results indicate that the sprayed films are within
the target 5-7nm range for high performing devices.

 Figure 12. Atomic concentration profiles collected from Angle-Resolved X-ray Photoelectron
 Spectroscopy and thickness fitting of individual layers.

The success value for UV-Vis spectroscopy to indicate preservation of optoelectronic
properties is retaining 99% absorbance in the 400 - 700 nm wavelength range after spray
ETL deposition when compared to the pre-ETL perovskite film. The %absorbance
retained is plotted as a function of wavelength in Figure 17. Upon initial examination, both
sprayed ETLs and solution spin-coated ETL test structures consisting of PCBM and BCP

retained to ~96% at 700 nm. Further investigation suggested that the PCBM layer rather
than the BCP layer was responsible for the drop in %absorbance retained. Given that the
PCBM precursor consists of PCBM dissolved in chlorobenzene, a separate UV-Vis
measurement consisting of a perovskite film washed only with chlorobenzene solvent (CB

that the PCBM material itself and not the processing method (solution spray or spin-coat)
causes the %absorbance decrease. Furthermore, if the ETL (spray or spin-coat) was then

measurements show >99% absorbance retained. The drop in %absorbance retained is
thus likely due to optical effects from the perovskite/ETL and ETL/ambient interfaces
during the UV-Vis spectrometer measurements, and the underlying perovskite itself
retains its original optoelectronic properties after spray ETL deposition indicating no
apparent chemical decomposition of the underlying perovskite film due to ETL
processing. The sampling protocol was satisfied for all UV-Vis measurements.

Optoelectronic Properties and Optimization of Sprayed Electron Transport Layers
In Task 1, characterizations of the optoelectronic properties of spray-deposited ETLs are
performed using steady-state and time-resolved photoluminescence. These techniques
measure the ability of the ETLs to act as carrier selective contacts, extracting photo-
induced carriers and “quenching” any photo-emission from the bare perovskite film.
Figure 19 shows the steady-state photoluminescence comparing test structures
consisting of glass/perovskite and either evaporated C60 and BCP, or spray-deposited
PCBM and BCP. In both cases where the ETL is present, there is significant quenching
of the perovskite emission peak, indicating good carrier extraction from the ETL layers.
While the shape of the emission peak differs slightly in between test architectures, the
peak emission wavelength remains at 793 nm. Figure 19 also shows the time-resolved
photoluminescence of the different test architectures following a time-correlated single
photon counting method (TCSPC). The decay curves are fitted to a bi-exponential form
where the fast time constant τ1 corresponds to monomolecular surface recombination and
the slow time constant τ2 corresponds to bimolecular bulk recombination. These results
indicate that the charge transfer dynamics of the spray-ETL films are comparable to those
of evaporated ETL films.

In conjunction with the Go-No-Go performance targets for the sprayed ETL, Milestone
2.6.2 focuses on ETL scalability and uniformity with a low cross-web variation on larger
area films. The success value is determined by <10% relative PCE difference over 5
subcells on 25 cm2 mini-module. An important consideration when scaling up ETL films
in a mini-module are the contributing effects of the underlying layers and the geometry of
the mini-module, in addition to potential scribing and design-related losses. With
increasing area, there is a possibility of including more defects and pinholes from the
underlying HTL and perovskite layers would negatively affect device performance. The
inclusion of these artifacts make it challenging to accurately measure the performance of
the ETL film.
To control for the potential defectivity of the accompanying device layers and to
exclusively evaluate the scalability and uniformity of the target ETL bilayer film, we
deposited ETL films in one-pass for each of PCBM and BCP on 6 separately prepared
substrates (2 x 2cm 2) lined up in a row for a total area of 24 cm 2. While edge effects
between substrates might be more pronounced, this method allowed for greater defect
control with respect to the non-target device layers, minimizing the influence of extrinsic
non-ETL factors.
The normalized PCE performance of the 5 sub-cells for two different batches is shown
below in Figure 30, with the top performing subcell at ~16% in both independent batches.

7.2. Combustion Oxidation with Rapid Plasma Curing of Transparent Electrodes
In a parallel thrust to the ETL optimization, extensive work focuses on ideation and
development of a spray-deposited transparent conducting oxide (TCO) front electrode.
Multiple synthetic approaches will be used to optimize scalable TCO fabrication including
spray pyrolysis and delayed ignition combustion oxidation (DICO). Spray pyrolysis forms
TCOs at high temperatures (>450 ºC) with metal chlorides as precursors. For example,
InCl3 is mixed with hydrated SnCl4, the film is sprayed or spin-coated onto a substrate and
then heated rapidly to 500 ºC. In comparison, DICO utilizes a combination of metal nitrate
salts, metal acetylacetonate salts, and organic fuel to create an in-film energy source
upon combustion of the organic fuel in the presence of the oxidant nitrate species. [15] The
TCO formation can occur at lower applied temperatures such as 300 ºC which enables
lower-cost, scalable manufacturing. TCO films will first be fabricated via spin coating to
optimize the chemical properties and film morphology and then translated to scalable
spray coating. Figure 31 demonstrates the synthetic pathway for forming ITO and can
easily be modulated to aluminum zinc oxide (AZO) or indium zinc oxide (IZO) by changing
the metal nitrate/metal acetylacetonate species.
Figure 31 also discusses the use of a variety of proposed curing mechanisms for the
liquid precursor inks. Of particular interest is method 3 which combines plasma with
tailorable gas chemistry and hotplate heating. The plasma gas would be modified with
forming gas (96% N2 and 4% H2) to introduce oxygen vacancies in the ITO crystalline
lattice. The introduction of oxygen vacancies produces two electron charge carriers for
each oxygen site that reacts and is a powerful tool to adjust the charge carrier
concentration in our films.

Rapid Plasma Curing Optimization
The TCO formation and annealing step can occur at lower applied temperatures such as
<350 ºC and at lower necessary annealing times which enables lower-cost, scalable
manufacturing. The CORP method holds additional promise because of the ability to use
the reactive ions in plasma to introduce oxygen vacancies while removing residual solvent
and ensuring complete combustion of the complexing agent. To perform rapid plasma
curing, a shroud is constructed out of stainless steel that allows for gas injection into the
plasma shroud (Figure 34). A nitrogen plasma is formed via blown arc discharge and the
introduction of hydrogen species then allows for the open-air plasma to take on reducing
properties.

TCO Chemistry Optimization
Our development of the CORP process has centered on ITO as the most promising front
electrode material; however, similar work on scalable production of FTO and AZO, two
other notable TCOs, is possible within the CORP framework. Simply exchanging the
metal ion and optimizing the deposition chemistry is required to generate a new chemical
make-up of the metal oxide film. We have grown considerably in our understanding of the
nature of combustion oxidation to yield highly pure and dense oxide films. One area of
importance is the fuel to oxidant ratio (φ) which determines if the thin film requires oxygen
uptake from the surroundings or if there is sufficient oxygen liberated from the nitrate for
successful conversion of the oxide. Likewise, if there is an excess of fuel, elemental
carbon can form and yield porosity in the film when elemental carbon oxidizes during
annealing into CO 2. φ must be calculated for the wet, solvent-swollen film which can pose
a challenge for identifying the true fuel content especially after kinetic drying processes
like spin-coating. Two main chemical synthetic approaches were taken in the ITO
synthesis to explore the bounds of φ as outlined in Figure 36. The excess fuel chemistry
uses acetylacetone (AcAc) as a chelating fuel due to its ability to inhibit hydrolysis and
condensation of indium hydroxide and tin hydroxide sol particles. The fuel/oxidant
balanced approach uses far less AcAc and occurs in the highly polar 2-methoxyethanol
(2ME) solvent. This approach forms some acetylacetonate complex similar to the fuel-
rich approach, however, it is hypothesized that some hydroxide can form especially upon
adding NH4OH used to promote complexing.
The resulting crystal structures expected are also detailed in Figure 36. XRD reveals
varying crystallite size with an amorphous background signal which has been shown in
the literature to arise from crystallites connected by amorphous metal oxide lattice (Figure
37). Due to the unique challenges posed by tin ions and their oxophilicity, care must be
taken to produce tin with strong chelating fuel protecting groups to avoid forming tin oxide
nanocrystallites rather than integrating into the lattice.

7.3. Rear Electrode Materials for Open-Air Module Assembly
To complete the fully open-air spray-deposited architecture outlined in this program, work
is required towards identifying candidate rear electrode materials and deposition
strategies compatible with our selected ETL materials and multi-modal curing capabilities
(RSPP, CORP, NIR-RTP). Catalytic metal deposition and a variety of conductive inks are
considered for both metal and carbon-based electrodes compatible with spray deposition.
Meanwhile, both NIR-RTP and plasma systems are considered as potential curing
sources–the unique oxidative/reductive environments enabled by the plasma and curing
shroud explored in the TCO work could enable the formation of a more conductive and
interconnected metal network. The localized energy density of the plasma enables
sintering of metal nanowire connections while the controlled plasma shroud environment
enables reduction of oxidized metal films deposited in open-air (Figure 44). Laminated
rear electrode strategies are also considered as a route for manufacturing vacuum-free
perovskite devices through the employment of conductive adhesives and inks deposited
on metal foils. The goal of this program task is to successfully employ existing electrode
materials for successful open-air perovskite module assembly.

7.5. Optical Monitoring for Accelerated Aging of Perovskite Devices
Optimization of multiple individual layers of the perovskite device leads to a significant
number of device stack permutations. Performance assessments, especially with the
multi-channel device testing system of Figure 24, provide immediate feedback on the
viability of tested materials and processing conditions; however, stability optimization
must complement that of performance to achieve a robust and manufacturable perovskite
device. Monitoring the stability of device architectures can employ a variety of
characterization techniques probing the morphology, crystalline structure, and
optoelectronic properties of the various device layers. Many employed techniques,
however, may only evaluate partial device structures, require device destruction, and are
otherwise incapable of in situ monitoring over the entire device lifetime.
In conjunction with morphological and optoelectronic degradation of the perovskite is
often an evolution of the apparent color of the device. Common degradation products
such as PbI2 and metal halides result in noticeable lightening and yellowing of the device,
respectively, meanwhile interfacial degradation and roughening can result in device
haziness. Thus, color monitoring of the perovskite devices can inform the degradation
behavior and pathways resulting from variations in device architecture and processing
conditions (Figure 56).

 Figure 56. Relationship between perovskite device morphology, crystal structure,
 optoelectronic properties, and color. These film and device characteristics inform observed
 differences and changes in device performance and stability in response to changes in
 materials and processing conditions.

Development of Color Monitoring Analysis
Initial studies employing the optical monitoring setup are aimed at correlating the
observed evolutions in device color with changes in morphology, crystal structure, and
optoelectronic properties. Devices have been aged in dry heat conditions (85° C heated
stage, inert atmosphere) with periodic JV measurements to probe the evolution in device
performance. An observed decrease in output current of the devices is correlated with an
upwards drift in the B-value in LAB color space, indicating an increased yellowing of the
sampled devices (Figure 60). Further studies include additional characterization methods
such as X-Ray Diffraction to further inform the nature of the observed changes in color.
Additionally, hardware improvements are being employed to reduce the observed
discontinuities in the tracked color changes.

To account for possible changes in lighting conditions during the experiments, we monitor
the images of a color standard (passport color checker) with each image from our
samples. The deviation between tabulated and measured color checker values are stored
in a conversion matrix whose inverse is applied to each raw image to generate its
respective calibrated image at every time step. Initially, this calibration was performed in
the RGB color space. We found that performing this correction in the LAB color space
reduced the error in the color conversion and accelerated the image treatment.
Color monitoring of photovoltaic devices has been demonstrated earlier in the literature,
where mostly RGB values were extracted and the evolution of the R values trends with
the decay of PV performances over time. [18] We decided to explore additional metrics and
color spaces that might be more relevant to perovskite device degradation. In particular,
we chose to extend the color processing to extract the LAB color space values. LAB is
another color space where L (lightness), a (red/green balance) and b(yellow/blue
balance) replace the R (red), G (green) and B (blue) metrics.
During the monitoring of devices under thermal stress, we observed an initial lightening
of perovskite devices, which resulted in an increase in R, G, and B values. These
observations are in line with the literature. Interestingly, the global lightening of devices
can be equally captured in the L values of the LAB color space. Besides the lightening of
the devices, we observe a yellowing which result in both R and G values in the initial RGB
color space. This effect can also be capture in the LAB space where b (yellow/blue
balance) is more sensitive than either the R or G values in the RGB scale (Figure 61).
Isolating the lightening and yellowing affects with LAB space provides further resolution
over degradation mode over RGB space.

demonstrates greater sensitivity to the lightest and yellowest portions of the active areas,
in addition to recognition of bimodal distributions that indicate localized defects and edge
effects, allowing the user to differentiate between homogeneous degradation and defect-

Optical Analysis of Thermally Aged Perovskite Devices
Evolution of L,a,b color values have been correlated with changes in device
optoelectronic properties and film morphology. Device lightening corresponds with
chemical changes within the perovskite film towards non-photoactive phases, resulting in
less absorption within the perovskite film and thus an increase in the L value over time.
Such behavior reduces the effective active area of the device, resulting in an overall drop
in current production as the availability of photoactive perovskite has decreased.
Furthermore, device yellowing corresponds with the production of specific perovskite
degradation byproducts, most notably lead iodide (PbI 2). Device yellowing is easily
tracked with the b-value within L,a,b color space, and increases in the b-value over time
are correlated with observed increases in the lead iodide (001) X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)
peak intensity relative to the perovskite (110) peak (Figure 63).

Analyses of the mode of degradation are extended to include degradation originating from
the edge of the electrode design in addition to the homogenous and defect-induced
degradation explored previously, indicated in Figure 64. While previous degradation
characterization was observed qualitatively through both image and distribution analysis,
further studies have enabled automatic identification of defect type according to relative
changes in the values of L_mean, L_std_dev, and skewness. Variations in the relative
intensities of these quantities can successfully identify the type of defect responsible for
degradation in device performance (Figure 64). Further analysis is exploring convolution
of the identified defects and the ability to correctly identify a collection of various
degradation behavior on the ultimate device performance degradation.
Optical monitoring has served as a valuable high-throughput tool for the optimization of
several open-air deposited thin films and a wide variety of perovskite module
architectures. These capabilities, with both uniformity and degradation analysis, provide
preliminary insights towards achievement of high-performing large-area films, which are
critical towards completing standardized stability assessments for the validation of high-
performing and durable open-air manufactured perovskite solar modules.

Device Encapsulation Optimization and Stability Implications
Encapsulation strategies have been extensively explored throughout our stability
investigations. A concurrent collaboration with the Perovskite PV Accelerator for
Commercializing Technologies (PACT) through the National Renewable Energy
Laboratory (NREL) has explored a 3-glass lamination architecture for evaluating stability
of the demonstrated open-air deposited perovskite devices against environmental
stressors while mitigating oxygen and moisture exposure (Figure 69). Encapsulation
often requires applying pressure against the 3-glass structure at elevated temperatures
for an extended duration, allowing the polyisobutylene (PIB) edge seal material to form a
compact, cohesive barrier against oxygen and moisture diffusing into the package. Many
encapsulation structures also incorporate additional encapsulant material within the
package surrounding the devices, though no such material is used throughout this
program to avoid alternative degradation pathways due to chemical evolutions and off-
gassing associated with these materials.

                                                                          Award # DE-EE0009516

 substrates, and busbars within a 15 x 15 cm package.

The role of temperature and pressure in the lamination procedure is investigated in
Figure 70 and Figure 71. Previous studies utilizing the optical imaging chamber
developed in this program have investigated the role of applied pressure on device
stability, following the L mean value trend as a proxy for perovskite film quality and
integrity (Figure 63a). For assessment of encapsulation effects on device performance,
open-air spray deposited devices are aged at 85° C in an inert atmosphere within the
optical imaging chamber. Each device is placed within a 3-glass structure to provide
identical lighting conditions across each sample, though no encapsulation step is
performed. The control devices contain spacers within the 3-glass structure to ensure no
pressure is applied to the film side of the devices. A second condition removes the
spacers, allowing the films to experience the pressure from the substrate and top
encapsulation glass. A third condition amplifies the pressure of the second condition by
using external clamping devices to apply additional pressure on the films.
Throughout aging in the optical imaging chamber, the first condition with no applied
pressure, denoted “no pressure,” demonstrates the greatest degree of device lightening
(Figure 70a). The second condition with light pressure applied by the weight of the
encapsulation glass shows a slight decrease in lightening more pronounced at longer
aging times. The third condition with additional externally applied pressure yields the
greatest reduction in overall lightening, especially at the beginning of device thermal
aging. Both pressure-inducing conditions showed similar rates of lightening at later times,
the role of increased pressure appearing most significantly in mitigating early device
degradation.
Considering the demonstrated role of pressure for device stability, of interest is an
increase in the pressure of the final encapsulated package. The external connections of
the package must contact the film-side of the device and have a finite thickness, thus
providing a consistent gap between the films and encapsulation glass, preventing any
pressure application. To investigate the role of pressure in the package, an additional
flexible layer is placed on top of the films over the active area of the device while still
allowing for the external connections (Figure 70b). Devices are packaged and aged at

                                        Page 70 of 102

                                                                           Award # DE-EE0009516

85° C in open air. Upon initial encapsulation, all devices demonstrate a drop in
performance within the first 24 hrs of aging attributed to a transient effect from the heating
required for successful encapsulation. This behavior is exacerbated with the additional
pressure-applying film. It is hypothesized that the additional film, while applying an
external pressure to the device layers, also allows for direct thermal contact between the
preheated encapsulation glass and the device films, reducing the device performance
due to an increased initial thermal dose. This demonstrates the careful balance between
package pressure and lamination temperature further complicated by the pressure and
temperature needs of the PIB edge seal to ensure successful encapsulation.

MAST Testing
Module Accelerated Sequential Testing (MAST) is a protocol designed for rigorous
module stability assessment for commercializable products. This testing protocol
commonly consists of 3 independently rigorous stability assessments: damp heat, thermal
cycling, and UV. The specific sequence for this testing protocol is outlined in Figure 74a.
While these individual stability assessments are targeted at demonstrating 25+ year T 80
lifetimes, primarily for Si-based photovoltaics, these assessments must be adapted to
account for the different targets of perovskite photovoltaics in terms of realistic and
commercializable short-term stability targets. The MAST protocol is thus adapted to
account for target 8 – 10-year T80 lifetimes based on Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE)
analysis targeting <$0.03/kWhr. The resulting adapted MAST protocol and testing
sequence is shown in Figures 74b and 74c. After careful development of a targeted

For complete UV + TC aging, transitioning to thermal cycling after UV slows the rate of

difference between the predicted and actual device behavior indicates that the thermal
cycling degradation mechanism interferes with the UV aging initial performance
enhancement attributed to alterations of the HTL/Perovksite interface, thus UV
performance enhancement is only possible in pristine devices.
Aging evolution of device resistances, including series resistance and shunt resistance,
is shown in Figure 76. While series resistance shows minimal evolution between both
stressors and sequences (Figure 76a), the shunt resistance behavior appears dependent
on the specific aging source (Figure 76b). Thermal cycling provides a thermal stimulus
sufficient to heal device defects, reorganize grain boundaries, and improve (increase)
shunt resistance over time. Meanwhile, UV aging at open-circuit conditions exposes and
exacerbates device shunts by introducing “hot” carriers with energies well beyond the
perovskite bandgap, capable of tunneling through device barriers and introducing shunt
pathways. Additionally, UV aged devices are held at open-circuit conditions, which has
been shown to increase shunting behavior compared to maximum power point tracked
devices (Figure 76b, inset). The reduction in shunt resistance for UV aged devices is the
primary contributor to the drop in fill factor observed in Figure 75c.

Post-Mortem Analysis of MAST Devices
Encapsulated devices without external leads are subjected to identical UV and TC aging
for dedicated post-mortem evaluation. Steady state photoluminescence is performed on
pristine open-air manufactured RSPP perovskite devices along with both UV→TC and
TC→UV sequences and double doses of individual UV and TC stressors (Figure 77a).
Pure thermal cycling up to 128 cycles shows minimal alteration of the photoluminescence
of the perovskite device with a slight blue shift in emission wavelength. Meanwhile the
introduction of UV aging dramatically reduces the photoluminescence behavior of the
perovskite. For pure UV aging, the photoluminescence signal is virtually eliminated.
Additionally, for UV aging of a non-pristine sample (TC→UV), photoluminescence signal
is similarly eliminated. However, for pristine UV aging followed by thermal cycling, the
photoluminescence is mostly maintained. The introduction of charge recombination
centers indicated by the reduction in shunt resistance (Figure 76b) after UV aging is
primarily responsible for the loss in photoluminescence signal. Meanwhile, TC aging both
preserves large shunt resistances and somewhat heals shunts developed under previous
UV aging, thus all aging sequences ending in TC aging maintain appreciable
photoluminescence signal. Additionally, devices undergoing UV aging are held at open-
circuit voltage (OCV) conditions, and previously performed bias aging indicates
considerable accelerated degradation under open-circuit conditions (Figure 77b). Due to
the reduction in shunt resistance, however, a non-negligible amount of short-circuit
current (SCC) behavior might also be present during UV aging, thus both OCV and SCC
bias aging conditions provide additional degradation phenomena to consider alongside
the role of UV in introducing over-excited hot carriers.

7.7. Technoeconomic Analysis of Open-Air Processed Perovskite Modules
Previous Cost Modeling of Open-Air Spray Deposition
Previous efforts in the development of a cost modeling framework for all open-air
deposited perovskite solar modules revolved around production of the rapid spray plasma
processed (RSPP) perovskite layer for a 100 MW pilot line. The RSPP cost model was
initially developed to compare open-air spray and plasma processing to other solar
technology active layer costs. Technoeconomic analysis was performed for the pilot line
with processing speeds of 12 m/min using a ganged array of spray and plasma nozzles,
resulting in a cost of ownership model dominated almost entirely by the perovskite
materials costs. The active layers of the module were compared for RSPP and the active
layer production of silicon, organic, and perovskite devices (Figure 79). The top 5 factors
that comprise the cost model for these solar technologies include materials (including
consumables), labor, utilities, maintenance, and equipment/facilities.

The cost model framework was extended to include previously demonstrated scalable
efforts including the spray-deposited HTL layer and high-throughput laser scribing steps
alongside vacuum-based deposition of the accompanying layers and encapsulation
components (Figure 80). The costs of these additional steps were each calculated based

fractions of the total module cost since multiple systems are required to match the module
throughput set by the RSPP perovskite step. Thus, replacing each of these vacuum-
based steps with high-throughput, open-air processes demonstrate great potential for
further cost reduction in our RSPP perovskite modules.

One of the demonstrated strengths of the open-air spray processing of the RSPP

without considering the tools for open-air spray deposition, such as belt-driven gantries
and ultrasonic spray nozzles, which are much cheaper than sputtering and physical vapor
deposition (PVD) systems. Thus, further reductions in cost would be observed in the
equipment and utilities categories for the ITO, ETL, and rear electrode.

Our focus for the modeling framework throughout this program is to develop an accurate

Development of All Open-Air Spray Cost Model
Extensions of the previous cost model incorporates the existing thin-film deposition
strategies alongside the thin film focuses of this program in the ETL and electrode
materials towards a complete perovskite solar module cost model based on the device
architecture: CORP ITO, sprayed NiOx, RSPP perovskite, sprayed PCBM and BCP, and
blade coated silver pastes. The thin-film device architecture was combined with balance-
of-module costs associated with glass-glass CIGS module development. [19]
The vacuum-based processes of the original cost model (sputter, thermal evaporation)
are replaced with the optimized open-air spray deposition and plasma/RTP processes
explored throughout this program for a fully open-air assembled perovskite module cost
model (Figure 82a). This model is compared to a “conventional” perovskite solar module
wherein popular film deposition methods based on high-performing perovskite modules
in literature are similarly evaluated (Figure 82b). These models directly compare
transitions between vacuum- and spray-based processes for the TCO front electrode,
ETL bilayer, and rear electrode. Specifically, the TCO front electrode compares sputter
deposition to open-air Combustion Oxidation with Rapid Plasma (CORP) processing; the
ETL bilayer compares thermally evaporated C60 and bathocuproine (BCP) with spray-
deposited PCBM with a near infrared rapid thermal processing (NIR-RTP) cure followed
by spray-deposited BCP; and the rear electrode compares thermally evaporated silver to
spray-deposited silver nanowires with an NIR-RTP cure. The spray deposition and
plasma curing modeled in the open-air cost model assume linear spray deposition and
plasma curing arrays shown in Figure 82c. For the remaining device processing methods
not addressed within the tasks of this program (HTL, perovskite, and laser scribing), the
open-air model assumes spray pyrolysis NiOx (Figure 53b) and RSPP perovskite
(Figure 53c) alongside indirect liftoff laser scribing. Meanwhile, the conventional model
assumes a blade-coated poly(triaryl amine) (PTAA) HTL, blade-coated perovskite, and
direct ablation laser scribing. For the other module components (glass, encapsulation,
junction box, and testing), all component costs are equal—calculated $/m2 values only
vary due to differences in manufacturing throughputs for the entire module pipeline.
Module performances are assumed to be 19% for the open-air modules and world record
27% for the conventional modules.

To ensure model accuracy and reliability, a sensitivity analysis was performed on the

assemblies across 1) baseline manufacturing inputs, 2) module design parameters, 3)

materials of the perovskite device. Individual model inputs were varied from -50% up to
+200% of their baseline values apart from the perovskite active area device efficiency
(PCEaa), which was varied by +/- 5% absolute PCEaa. Sensitivity analysis for both open-
air and conventional perovskite module assemblies is shown in Figure 85.
The module manufacturing cost of energy for the open-air assembly is centered at
$0.26/WDC. For all analyzed inputs, increases in the input value resulted in increased
module manufacturing costs with the exception of the depreciation factor and perovskite
efficiency. Across the entire open-air manufacturing model, the most sensitive inputs were
found to be the front glass materials, ETL materials, equipment costs, and ITO materials,
with 200% increases in parameter values resulting in manufacturing cost of energy
increases of 34%, 24%, 22%, and 20%, respectively. These parameters also provide the
greatest potential cost reductions at the modeled 50% decrease in parameter values.
Despite modeling parameter input variations as large as 200%, only the front glass and
ETL materials individually provide sufficient changes in the manufacturing costs to yield
identical open-air and conventional module costs.
The conventional perovskite manufacturing model is centered at $0.33/W DC. The most
sensitive parameters at 200% increases in input values were equipment costs, labor, and
front glass materials costs, resulting in manufacturing cost of energy increases of 50%,
40%, and 19%, respectively. These three parameters also provided the most potential for
cost reductions based on 50% decreases in parameter values.

Levelized Cost of Energy
The projected cost savings for electricity generation is calculated through the Levelized
Cost of Energy (LCOE), which utilizes the System Advisor Model (SAM, version
2022.11.21) developed by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). Initial
LCOE calculations based on the previous cost model only incorporating the open-air
manufacturing steps for the NiOx HTL, RSPP perovskite, and indirect liftoff laser scribes,
are shown in Figure 87 as a function of the open-air manufactured perovskite module
device performance and lifetime. LCOE calculations LCOE values demonstrates
negligible change against changes in module power conversion efficiency (PCE). This is
partly a result of the low-cost high-throughput manufacturability enabled by the open-air
framework where much of the installation cost and thus LCOE is tied to auxiliary
components such as the inverter and other balance of systems (BOS) components.
LCOE values of $0.06/kWh are predicted for open-air manufactured perovskite module
lifetimes of roughly 5 years, achieving the 2030 target lifetimes of $0.03/kWh at roughly
15 year lifetimes. Meanwhile, incumbent Si technologies require 25-30 year lifetimes to
reach comparable LCOE values. Thus our low-cost manufacturing is demonstrating
considerable cost reductions towards enabling a low cost of energy.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
This program has demonstrated significant accomplishments across a wide array of
perovskite module optimization strategies towards all open-air manufacturing of
perovskite solar modules. The goals of the program to develop and optimize open-air
spray-processed perovskite solar modules have been thoroughly achieved, with
extensive focus paid to each of the individual task and subtasks throughout.
 • Demonstrate an open-air spray deposited ETL. We successfully developed and
 deployed a rapid, open-air spray deposition method for an organic electron
 transport bilayer consisting of PCBM and BCP capable of providing elevated
 device performances >20% and considerable operational stability. The films
 demonstrate significant improvements in process throughput over alternative ETL
 deposition methods for inverted perovskite devices.
 • Develop a technique for the open-air fabrication of TCOs. Achieving high-
 performing TCOs typically requires high temperature vacuum-based processes for
 precise control of optoelectronic properties. The combination of solution
 combustion synthesis, lowering the energetic barrier for oxide formation, and rapid
 plasma treatment with afterglow environmental control using forming gas for a
 reducing plasma environment enable low-temperature TCO formation with
 comparable optoelectronic properties to high-performing, low-throughput sputter
 alternatives.
 • Evaluate candidate rear electrode materials. Multiple candidate rear electrode
 materials exist towards manufacturing cost reductions over vacuum-based
 alternatives. A wide variety of promising candidates were evaluated within the
 same device framework, demonstrating the most promising solutions for
 successful manufacturing of low-cost perovskite modules.
 • Demonstrate the scalability of the open-air deposition methods. All thin-film
 deposition methods maintained target optoelectronic performance metrics from
 small-area spin-coating baselines towards >300 cm 2 large-area demonstrations.
 • Achieve high-performing open-air perovskite solar devices. Open-air
 manufacturing modules achieved high efficiency across a range of architectures
 and deposition areas. Owing to the successful scalability of the constituent films,
 open-air perovskite devices achieved >20% PCE on small area devices and >17%
 PCE on larger area single-cell devices. Additionally, >17% PCE was maintained
 for open-air perovskite modules with 9 sub-cells across 10 cm deposition lengths.
 • Enable high-throughput optimization with optical monitoring. Optimization
 across multiple thin-film developments and device architectures was aided by high-
 throughput optical monitoring and accelerated aging. Color calibration and areal
 analysis in LAB color space, alongside parallel performance and materials
 characterization, classified film defects and degradation modes, enabling
 enhancements in film uniformity, device performance, encapsulation procedure,
 and device stability.
 • Elucidate antagonistic interactions between perovskite aging stressors.
 Accelerated sequential testing for open-air perovskite devices exposed previously
                                      Page 97 of 102

                                                                       Award # DE-EE0009516

 obscured interactions between varying aging stressors involving humidity, heat,
 and illumination. These interactions highlighted degradation pathways that
 exacerbate the effects of other stimuli, informing future strategies on the mitigation
 of perovskite degradation.
 • Extensive cost model deployment and LCOE calculation. The cost model
 expanded in this program provided an extensive look at the strengths and
 weaknesses of current perovskite module fabrication techniques. High-throughput
 open-air methods were thoroughly compared to conventional solution-processing
 and vacuum-based deposition techniques across various perovskite architectures.
 Open-air methods were shown to reduce the barrier for commercial deployment
 by offering dramatic reductions in manufacturing costs. LCOE analysis predicts
 commercially viable single-junction perovskite modules with open-air processing,
 potentially offering the lowest cost photovoltaic technology.

10. Path Forward:
The four principal areas for further investigation based on the results and success of this
program are as follows:
 1. Demonstration of wider open-air processing widths. The open-air spray-
 plasma processing has proven remarkably scalable as an inline processing
 technique capable of depositing and curing strips up to 30 cm long, even further
 with appropriately scaled gantry and hotplate equipment. The ultrasonic spray,
 plasma curing, and NIR-RTP curing support expanded process widths including
 array configurations wherein multiples of each nozzle are mounted next to each
 other to form a uniform line for deposition and curing.[20] Optimization of such a

 industry collaborations in the atmospheric pressure plasma curing space.
 2. Strategies for the mitigation of perovskite degradation. Although considerable

 strategies targeted towards the observed degradation mechanisms and
 antagonistic interactions. We plan to directly explore proposed solutions for
 enhanced perovskite stability employed across champion perovskite stability
 results in literature and evaluate stability challenges and solutions unique to our
 open-air manufacturing methods and capabilities.

 3. Encapsulation methods for optimal performance retention. In addition to the

11.5. Collaborations:
Collaboration with industry partners has occurred throughout the program to ensure the
development of useful goals and targets compatible with commercialization of perovskite
solar technology.
Fruitful discussions with the Adphos Group on their NIR-RTP (near-infrared rapid thermal
processing) technology has resulted in extensive collaboration in the design of a unique
curing tool. Initial collaboration enabled the implementation of a preliminary tool design in
the exploration of NIR-RTP. Further engagement has resulted in the design and
deployment of a unique customized NIR-RTP system with tailored heating and ventilation
design made specifically for both the ETL and electrode in-line processing.
We have continued active engagement with PlasmaTreat, who have provided extensive
industry expertise throughout our various EERE efforts, through the design of a slot-type
nozzle where the gas flow is directed entirely in one direction to maximize width better
suited to continuous in-line processing. We have also engaged with them on optimizing
plasma conditions including duty cycling to manage plasma head nozzle heating.
Additionally, PlasmaTreat has offered critical feedback in the completed design of a
plasma curing shroud compatible with the provided slot-type nozzle, enabling curing in a
variety of oxidative and reductive environments. Rather than introduce different curing
gases into the initial arc discharge of the plasma, the gases are introduced into the

plasma curing with precision over the gas environment. Further equipment design
considerations include the development of the parallel array of plasma nozzles employed
within the cost model and increased curing width of the individual plasma nozzles.
