Toward a decolonial One Health: Findings from a participatory action research initiative with indigenous, Campesino, and Afro-descendant communities in the Amazon☆
Authors: Fabian Méndez-Paz, Alain Santandreu, Óscar Betancourt, Andrés Burgos, Frédéric Mertens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe One Health approach is central to addressing pandemic and planetary crises. However, its framework, predominantly shaped by perspectives from the Global North, has significant limitations with respect to equity and effectiveness in the Global South, particularly in Latin America and the Caribbean. This article develops a critical and decolonial One Health perspective structured around arguments aimed at overcoming four central limitations of the current framework: 1) the predominance of perspectives from the Global North that prioritize health security over local endemic problems; 2) instrumental or superficial forms of community participation that reinforce power asymmetries; 3) the lack of interculturality and the marginalization of ancestral knowledge; and 4) the limitations in addressing the structural and interconnected causes of health problems. Results are based on the One Amazon project, a One Health participatory action research initiative conducted with Indigenous, Campesino, and Afro-descendant communities in Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru between 2021 and 2024. Drawing on mixed qualitative and quantitative data, including participatory mapping, semi-structured interviews, and population surveys, the article demonstrates how these methods yielded empirically grounded insights into health, territory, and governance in Amazonian communities. Results show that collaborative research, focused on local needs and involving genuine participation from Amazonian organizations and their communities, including community co-investigators, intercultural dialogue that values ancestral knowledge, and a focus on territorial sovereignty, generated more relevant and sustainable solutions. Ultimately, the findings indicate that a decolonization process grounded in socio-environmental and epistemic justice would enhance the transformative potential of the One Health approach by enabling knowledge from the Global South to inform and enrich the global health agenda.Keywords: One Health, Decolonial, Planetary Health, Knowledge dialogue, Epistemic justice, Amazon, Participatory-action research, Indigenous people, Afro-descendant communities, Campesinos, COVID-19, Equity, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Global north, Global south

Introduction

The One Health (OH) framework is a widely accepted approach to confronting pandemic threats and the planetary crisis [1]. This holistic perspective emphasizes the interdependence of human, animal, and environmental health and advocates for interdisciplinary and cross-sectoral collaboration worldwide to address complex challenges. The Quadripartite Alliance is a strategic collaboration formally established among the World Health Organization (WHO), the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH), and the United Nations Environmental Programme (UNEP) to advance the implementation of the OH approach globally. The Quadripartite developed the 2022–2026 One Health Joint Plan of Action (OH JPA) to address threats to human, animal, and plant health, as well as to the environment, with the aim of developing sustainable solutions and preventing future pandemics [2], [3].

Despite the indisputable achievements of global OH initiatives, implementing the OH JPA faces multiple challenges, including historically entrenched bureaucratic silos, competition for resources, limited interdisciplinary training, and potential conflicts between the global plan and national, subnational, and community interests [4], [5]. Moreover, there is a pressing need to develop more equitable and effective approaches that genuinely benefit populations and ecosystems in the Global South, particularly in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC).

To address these challenges, we integrated a decolonial perspective into research practices within the One Amazon project, a Participatory Action Research (PAR) initiative conducted between 2021 and 2024 across the Amazon regions of Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru. As part of the “Collaborative One Health Research Initiative on Epidemics”, the One Amazon project aimed to reduce risk and vulnerability and to enhance the resilience of Amazonian peoples in the face of COVID-19 and emerging zoonotic epidemics. In this article, we develop a critical perspective from LAC to advance OH approaches that are more attuned to realities rooted in the region's history, socio-ecological conditions, and priorities. By taking this critical position, we aim to contribute to a holistic vision of the health of socio-ecological systems in the LAC region. This vision aligns with the decolonization agenda in global health, which seeks to address the entrenched power structures that perpetuate inequalities in the practice and application of global health [6]. It challenges power imbalances by calling for the dismantling of hierarchical systems that privilege the voices of the Global North and by advocating for the recognition and integration of Indigenous and non-biomedical knowledge, which have historically been neglected, into global health [7].

Following this introduction, the next section presents four arguments that support our critical approach to OH. The methods section outlines the foundations of our data collection and analysis, followed by the presentation of the results, including the strategy implemented in four case studies, each corresponding to one of the proposed arguments. The discussion summarizes our contributions to an OH approach sensitive to local contexts in LAC, and the conclusion reflects on the implications of these findings for a decolonial OH perspective and for the politics of more equitable North-South collaboration.

Challenges faced by current OH approaches

Below, we present four arguments in support of developing a critical and decolonial OH perspective. These arguments address key challenges that, we propose, limit the capacity of current OH approaches to generate equitable and effective actions and benefit populations and ecosystems in the Global South.

1.The predominance of perspectives from the Global North. The current OH framework is predominantly shaped by perspectives from the Global North, prioritizing the surveillance of emerging diseases that pose a potential threat to everyone, particularly to high-income countries. The 2023 report from the One Health High-Level Expert Panel (OHHLEP) prioritizes the development and review of approaches to guide global efforts to reduce the risk of zoonotic pandemics, such as COVID-19 [3]. While this is an important objective, it can divert attention and resources from endemic and everyday health and socio-environmental problems that are of greater concern to populations in the Global South. As a result, research and funding tend to prioritize the surveillance of pathogens with pandemic potential, which frequently originate in the Global South but have a greater economic impact in the Global North [8]. By contrast, food insecurity, hunger, limited access to safe drinking water, chronic exposure to pollutants — all of which are associated with extractive industries — and high disease burdens in the Global South are comparatively overlooked within this global framework Consequently, the concept of “pandemic prevention” has been employed to justify interventions that do not always align with local priorities, socioeconomic conditions, and cultural contexts [9].Additionally, current OH approaches tend to prioritize high-tech solutions (e.g., advanced genomic surveillance, targeted vaccines, satellite monitoring) and standardized intervention frameworks [10] that are assumed to be universally applicable, without considering the diversity of socio-ecological, economic, and cultural contexts. However, these solutions may be inaccessible for many communities and unsustainable for many countries in the Global South. Decision-makers may underestimate the significance of community-based approaches and overlook more effective and equitable interventions, such as strengthening local health systems, implementing communal land tenure policies, and adopting agroecological models. The emphasis on “rapid intervention” in the current approach may take precedence over the need to fortify health and environmental systems in the long term in a sustainable manner.

2.Instrumental or superficial forms of community participation. Lack of participation, as well as instrumental or superficial forms, has historically characterized externally imposed or top-down approaches [11]. Such approaches have the potential to undermine genuine community agency and reinforce power asymmetries, thereby impeding the equitable and sustainable implementation of OH initiatives [12]. Communities are frequently invited to attend research workshops and consulted on their priorities and demands; however, their contributions are rarely meaningfully incorporated into the planning and design of research and intervention processes. In such situations, participation is included to satisfy project requirements or legal requirements for prior consultation, without engaging communities and researchers in a genuine co-development process. In many cases, the community is expected to validate previously made decisions or execute externally defined tasks [13].Researchers and institutions often employ data collection methods, including surveys and focus groups, that confine community members to the roles of informants, data sources, or inexpensive labor. We argue that these field methods are frequently shaped by a paternalistic attitude, stemming from an underlying lack of trust in the community's inherent capacity to generate and interpret evidence for its own decision-making. Additionally, these approaches are often utilitarian, relying on community resources, including time and knowledge, without adequate compensation, which can generate frustration, mistrust, and a sense of lack of ownership. Such top-down approaches overlook opportunities to create meaningful, applicable knowledge through horizontal forms of participation, in which community members actively serve as co-investigators and receive appropriate compensation, including monetary remuneration for their work. The outcomes of such top-down participatory processes can, in turn, jeopardize the long-term viability of OH initiatives.

3.The lack of interculturality and ancestral knowledge. The formulation and implementation of OH privilege Western scientific and technical knowledge (biomedicine, molecular epidemiology, quantitative ecology) as the primary means of understanding and addressing socio-ecological problems. Ancestral knowledge, traditional land and animal management practices, and the relational ontologies of Indigenous peoples and local communities in the Global South are frequently underestimated or instrumentalized [14]. They are usually only valued if they “validate” Western scientific findings [15]. This limitation hinders OH's ability to address the heterogeneity of socio-ecological contexts and overlooks contextually suitable solutions already present in the Global South. The “one” in “One Health” risks becoming an obligation to adhere to a single form of knowledge and practice within a rich and diverse ecosystem of knowledge.

The social-ecological systems paradigm provides the essential foundation for OH, framing society and nature as a single complex system [16]. However, OH often oversimplifies this perspective by neglecting indigenous cosmologies and reciprocal human–nature relationships, thereby emphasizing a unidirectional, extractive ecosystem-services framework [17]. By privileging Western worldviews, OH tends to marginalize spiritual and “more-than-human” components—such as ontological relations of worship and care—that are fundamental to indigenous worldviews.

4.The limitations in addressing the structural and interlinked causes of health problems. Structural and interlinked causes of health problems are often under-addressed within the OH framework. The conceptual framework of OH is fundamentally rooted in risk discourses and is supported by biomedical and individual-behavioral theories of disease. Consequently, concepts such as “planetary health” or “biodiversity hotspots” [18] are susceptible to interpretations and interventions under these frameworks that align with the priorities of developed countries. This phenomenon can depoliticize socio-ecological problems, presenting them as technical challenges rather than as conflicts rooted in historical inequalities, colonial injustices, and economic power relations [19]. A neglected aspect concerns the prevailing interests of the world's predominant economic and political powers regarding natural resources (minerals, oil, forests, etc.), which exert substantial influence on the environment, climate, and the health and well-being of the majority of the population.Such depoliticization can be interpreted as an expression of the ‘coloniality of power’ [20], whereby health governance functions primarily as a biosecurity apparatus designed to shield Global North interests from ‘pathogen spillovers’ emanating from the South. By framing the Global South merely as a source of risk or a ‘hotspot’ to be managed, OH obscures the fact that these risks are often the byproducts of centuries of extractive colonial history and the ongoing commodification of nature [21].

Methods

Study design and methodological framework

This study employed a multicenter, community-based mixed-methods design, implemented across three countries in the Amazon basin: Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru. To ensure consistency across diverse territories while respecting local autonomy, the theoretical and methodological approach shared an explicit commitment to advancing social change for vulnerable groups. It was anchored in a common methodological framework defined by three core principles: i) Participatory Action Research (PAR): the study moved beyond the usual data collection by incorporating community members as active co-investigators. This principle dictated that research questions and validation of results were co-constructed with local stakeholders [22]; ii) ecosystem approaches to health and the socio-environmental determinants of health: the inquiry was designed to capture the structural and intermediate determinants of health and to analyze the interdependence among human health, animal health, and environmental dynamics, thereby avoiding siloed epidemiological analyses [23]; and iii) a new Amazonian ontology for the care of life: the methodological approach adopted local ontologies where “health” is understood not merely as the absence of disease, but as the “Care of Life,” integrating spiritual, territorial, and biological dimensions [24], [25].

Study setting and population

The study areas were defined by organizations representing Amazonian peoples and nationalities, while the communities identified the co-investigators and wise elders (Sabios y Sabias). The study was conducted in 31 Amazonian communities, including Indigenous, Campesinos, and Afro-descendants in the Amazon regions of the Caquetá and Amazonas departments (Colombia), Achuar, Shuar, Kichwa, and Waorani peoples in Orellana, Napo, Pastaza, and Morona Santiago provinces (Ecuador), and in Asháninka communities in the Pichis River basin (Peru). These territories share characteristics of high biodiversity, limited access to state health services, different levels of urban influence, and a strong presence of traditional knowledge systems. Local co-investigators included community leaders and indigenous knowledge holders (sabedores), and family units were selected through convenience sampling, depending on the specific community structure in each country.

Due to the participatory and multi-country nature of the One Amazon project, sampling strategies and sample sizes were not rigidly standardized across all sites; instead, they were purposefully adapted to the specific demographic, cultural, and logistical characteristics of each participating territory. Local adaptation of methods

While the three core principles provided a unified conceptual umbrella, data collection instruments and fieldwork procedures were adapted to the socio-cultural and logistical realities of each country. Transdisciplinary working groups in each site oversaw this adaptation process. The methods were structured around common objectives and questions, which served as a guiding framework for fieldwork across all participating countries. Prioritized research topics included: health responses to COVID-19, the risk of zoonotic diseases, food insecurity, and other problems affecting the territories, such as access to water and land, as well as political and social violence. To this end, transdisciplinary and multi-country working groups review the data collection instruments and fieldwork procedures, although each country adapted them to its local needs before implementation [26].

Qualitative component

To understand the “Care of Life” ontology, including the use of traditional medicine and the community response to the COVID-19 pandemic, we employed different qualitative techniques: i. A timeline technique was developed in collaboration with traditional knowledge holders and other community co-investigators to build a comprehensive understanding of historical contexts and the interconnections between the pandemic and other community processes. This approach facilitated the chronological representation of key events in the territory, enhancing knowledge and interpretive capacity regarding health problems before, during, and after the pandemic. ii. Focus groups and community assemblies helped to map community resources, identify perceived health threats, and validate the relevance of the research activities; iii. In-depth semi-structured Interviews were conducted with traditional healers and community leaders to document ancestral knowledge regarding ecosystem balance and healing practices. iv. Research teams used social and environmental mapping exercises to identify threats to ecosystems, territories, and local food systems, and to assess access to water and food resources; these findings were then compared with cartographic information compiled by the participating institutions.

Quantitative component

To characterize health status, disease burden, and access to health services during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, we collected epidemiological data, including: i. Morbidity and Mortality; Information was collected from official surveillance reports using instruments designed to characterize the demographic, temporal, and spatial distribution of disease. ii. 24-h dietary recalls to characterize diet. Iii. Population surveys; questionnaires were designed to describe access to health services and to assess the frequency of food consumption, including local products and crops for personal consumption. Sample sizes were determined based on the territory's specific characteristics and employed cluster sampling of communities, followed by inclusion of all selected family units to achieve adequate precision.

We collected qualitative and quantitative data in parallel or sequential strategies and analyzed them to achieve integration as part of an iterative research process that combined collective systematization and interpretive analysis. Some data were analyzed using traditional statistical methods for descriptive and association analyses, including regression analysis to estimate prevalence ratios, as well as through a socioecological food network framework complemented by thematic analysis, categorization matrices, and cross-case comparison.

Data analysis and validation

Data analysis followed a highly participatory, hybrid deductive-inductive approach. Initial deductive categories were guided by the project's three core methodological principles (PAR, ecosystem approaches, and Amazonian ontologies). Within this framework, specific sub-themes emerged inductively from the qualitative data in each country, capturing local nuances. Crucially, analytical procedures were not confined to academic researchers; instead, they relied on collective systematization. Community co-investigators actively participated in interpreting the data, filtering the findings through local cultural lenses. The validation of findings was iterative and continuous; preliminary interpretations were presented back to the communities through focus groups and community assemblies, ensuring that the synthesized results accurately reflected their lived realities and perspectives. Finally, to illustrate this participatory data generation and analysis, we have introduced selected visual materials, including photographs of community assemblies and examples of the social cartography (‘talking maps’) co-created in the field.

The study was not intended to be an exhaustive epidemiological investigation of morbidity and mortality, and its results are not generalizable to other territories within the Amazon region, given the localized nature of the analysis and the specific characteristics of the selected communities. However, while the specific epidemiological results are intrinsic to the studied communities, the principles for One Health implementation identified here respond to structural challenges common to the Latin American and Caribbean context.

Before data collection, the study protocol and instruments received formal approval from the Institutional Review Boards in each participating country and authorization from the governing bodies of the communities involved.

Reflexivity and positionality statement

The author team consists of researchers from Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Brazil, most of whom are rooted in the academic and social contexts of the Latin American territories where this study took place. We acknowledge our position as ‘insider-outsiders’; while we share regional identities and a long-term commitment to the Amazon, we also hold academic roles that carry inherent power dynamics. To mitigate paternalistic or extractive practices, our team approach was built on a decade-long history of collaborative work, prioritizing research as a tool for local transformation. Before the project's inception, we engaged in formal recognition processes with local leaders and organizations to co-define objectives and adjust activities to community needs. Throughout the project, power dynamics were managed through horizontal co-production, such as the inclusion of community members as co-investigators and the use of collective systematization workshops that balanced academic and ancestral knowledge. Our reflexivity remained a continuous process of self-questioning to ensure that the ‘Care of Life’ remained the project's ethical and ontological compass.

Project findings

We describe below how our approach addressed the four challenges to OH by presenting case studies from the One Amazon project. The results synthesize empirical findings emerging from cross-case comparative analysis of community health practices, territorial dynamics, and governance arrangements documented across the project.

Identifying local priorities and community-based responses

The COVID-19 pandemic initially affected the most urbanized centers of the Amazon, which had direct contact with travelers passing through international airports. In Leticia, the capital of Colombia's Amazonas department, the initial mortality rate, according to Ministry of Health reports, was nearly 8 times the national rate. However, after the first peak, the mortality rate remained comparatively low. During the pandemic, 82% of individuals surveyed in Caquetá, Colombia (from 189 households), reported not receiving formal assistance, despite 59% reporting that at least one family member had exhibited symptoms consistent with the virus. Notably, only 12% of these cases were diagnosed by healthcare services, whereas 75% managed their symptoms with medicinal plants. Vaccination coverage was low due to limited access to health services and vaccine refusal driven by mistrust of the state. Only 35% of families vaccinated all their members, and in many cases, with incomplete schedules. In all countries, the utilization of Western medicine was concomitant with the implementation of epidemiological cordons, quarantine for probable cases, and the employment of traditional Amazonian medicine.

Two case studies from the One Amazon project, shown below, describe that responses grounded in local needs and community-defined health priorities were more effective at addressing the interlinked health and food security impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic and at guiding an OH approach that responds to socioeconomic, historical, and cultural contexts.

Orienting research priorities toward community needs

In Colombia, communities identified food insecurity as the primary health issue during the pandemic. The persistence of fumigation and the war on drugs, together with the expansion of livestock farming as the predominant activity, transformed the rural landscape into grassland, causing deforestation, loss of food sovereignty, and soil degradation. This process, consolidated by land grabbing and speculation, increased inequality in land tenure. By 2015, the Gini coefficient had risen to 0.70 in the department, thereby reducing agricultural diversity and favoring large livestock farms.

Accordingly, the focus of research shifted from analyzing the direct effects of COVID-19 infection on health to examining how pandemic-associated social, economic, and sanitary changes affected individuals' access to food. The survey conducted in Caquetá, Colombia, found that 33% of households experienced moderate or severe food insecurity, approximately 13 percentage points higher than the national average. [27]

Furthermore, in discussions with local leaders, the lack of access to potable water was also identified as a central community health issue. In response, the One Amazon project developed a community training program in water quality monitoring that helped communities strengthen emerging biopolitical self-defense strategies. By ‘biopolitical self-defense’, we refer to the process where communities transform technical public health tools—such as water quality monitoring—into mechanisms for political advocacy and territorial sovereignty. This aligns with what Grove and Pugh [28] describe as an ‘affirmative biopolitics’, wherein participatory activities challenge existing power structures and create new possibilities for collective life, as opposed to a ‘negative biopolitics’ that merely regulates and objectifies populations. By independently generating evidence of contamination, the communities emerged not as passive objects of intervention, but as active political subjects capable of confronting extractive industries and demanding state accountability. In this emancipatory process, the research team was positioned precisely as Grove and Pugh suggest: not as directors of the process, but as an additional resource for communities in their struggles against environmental insecurity.

Additionally, analysis of causes of disease and death demonstrated the heterogeneity of health problems within the Amazon region and, consequently, the need for a contextual understanding of local priorities to orient research: from areas with predominant infectious diseases to others with chronic conditions, like cancer and cardiovascular diseases, and injuries associated with motorcycle use and violence.

Community-based responses versus global development interventions during the COVID-19 pandemic

In Peru, we showed that the pandemic's health and food-security impacts were substantially lower in Asháninka indigenous communities that implemented community-based responses than in those that received standardized interventions aligned with global development models. Asháninka communities located far from urban centers developed effective responses to the pandemic, primarily by maintaining traditional livelihoods centered on subsistence agriculture, hunting, fishing, and the gathering of medicinal plants. Strong indigenous knowledge systems, grounded in worldviews that regard humans as inseparable from other species and ecosystems, enabled these communities to sustain integrated health and food systems through biodiverse territories that provided nutritious foods and access to medicinal plants. These communities, therefore, responded effectively to the intertwined health and food security impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic through a holistic health approach.

In contrast, Asháninka communities more heavily influenced by urban development and deforestation exhibited weaker territorial and food sovereignty, relying on health and food systems over which they had little agency. Urban families were increasingly dependent on services provided by government-controlled biomedical health systems, while their diets shifted toward commercial foods associated with globalized, processed-food systems. During the pandemic, government responses to health threats and food insecurity were implemented mainly in isolation, with little integration or coordination between the two sectors. Health measures prioritized reducing infection risk through mobility restrictions, confinement, and vaccination campaigns [29], whereas food insecurity was addressed through standardized interventions to increase the availability of processed foods in supermarkets and to provide food aid. As a consequence, families in urban communities faced a complex trade-off between protecting their health and ensuring their food security. Those who reduced their mobility due to mandatory confinement, fear of infection, or limited financial resources were less exposed to the virus but experienced greater food insecurity. Meanwhile, families who left confinement to purchase commercial food or to collect government cash aid were more likely to report symptoms such as high fever, muscle aches, fatigue, headaches, and loss of appetite, which are indicative of probable COVID-19 infection.

Developing authentic forms of community participation

The key strategy to foster collaborative activities in which communities and academic researchers share knowledge, perspectives, priorities, and decision-making power was to involve community members as co-investigators. Similar to academic researchers, postdoctoral fellows, and post-graduate students, community co-investigators received monetary compensation for their contributions to the research. They were also supported with field expenses and participation in regional meetings. We implemented this fair compensation model that provided financial remuneration to community members, recognizing their contributions not as passive participants but as experts and laborers whose time and knowledge have economic value comparable to that of academic staff. This approach was key to incorporating local and ancestral priorities and knowledge in the research process.

In Ecuador, for example, participating communities were selected in collaboration with leaders of the Indigenous organizations of the Interprovincial Federation of Shuar Centers and the Pueblo Shuar Arutam. In partnership with academic researchers, through knowledge sharing and integration, a body of scientific and social evidence was generated that served as the foundation for policy advocacy at the local, subregional, and national levels. Ten individuals selected by Indigenous organizations led the training of co-investigators in health and the environment.

Community members were trained and received a diploma in collective health. The training course was co-designed and implemented by community co-investigators and academic researchers, incorporating local and ancestral knowledge, including that of wise elders.

Addressing interculturality and ancestral knowledge

To improve our understanding of indigenous perspectives on human–nature relationships, we conducted a participatory workshop in Puerto Bermúdez, Perú, with 43 representatives from Asháninka communities (see Fig. 1). Attendees included Indigenous co-investigators of the One Amazon project, Indigenous authorities/leaders, practitioners of traditional medicine, and community health agents. The workshop methodology combined intercultural dialogue with social cartography, focusing on the collective production of talking maps. This approach enabled participants to represent their understandings of human–nature relations, and to visualize territorial dynamics from the perspective of Indigenous peoples (see Fig. 2).

The workshop showed that, whereas non-indigenous relationships with the environment are often characterized by domination and control over land and life, indigenous relationships are grounded in respect, reciprocity, and coexistence with both human and non-human beings. The indigenous worldview also incorporates “more-than-human” components (e.g., ‘dueños’, forest beings, cosmic or spiritual entities, non-human agencies, deities). It is expressed through ontological, spiritual, and ancestral relations with the land and the universe. These include harmony and balance, kinship and conviviality ties, mystical or magical relations, and, in some instances, avoidance or confrontation with potential enemies.

Similarly, a series of threats associated with the non-indigenous world (e.g., environmental impacts, manifestations of modernity, and institutional violence) were identified as weakening and even disrupting these relationships, particularly by directly or indirectly affecting non-human entities. These perceptions highlighted how contexts of interaction with non-indigenous society shape indigenous experiences and understandings of social-ecological systems. This exercise demonstrates that incorporating indigenous perspectives and ontologies not only enriches our understanding of social-ecological systems but also provides key insights for developing more intercultural, inclusive, and context-sensitive OH approaches.

Structural causes of health inequalities

In Colombia and Ecuador, our project revealed that health outcomes are deeply rooted in complex structural antecedents rather than in isolated biological events. In the Caquetá region of Colombia, vulnerabilities such as food insecurity were inextricably linked to the legacy of armed conflict and overlapping inequities related to ethnicity and gender. Furthermore, systemic barriers—including tensions over land titles—prevent state and NGO support from reaching productive local projects. These structural issues are exacerbated by the criminalization of campesinos, which functions as a strategy of “violence of exclusion,” further marginalizing rural communities from health and social support systems. [30]

In Ecuador, the findings highlight the profound social-ecological impacts of extractive development models, particularly those associated with mining. These activities have led to a significant loss of traditional ecological knowledge and increased dependence on external agri-food systems, thereby undermining local resilience. The data also point to unexpected health impacts resulting from these economic shifts; for instance, motorcycle-related injuries have become a leading cause of morbidity, directly linked to the precarious labor demands of extractive industries.

To address these complex structural causes, our findings suggest that interventions must prioritize the strengthening of social organizations and community-led needs. An illustrative example from the Caquetá project involved addressing the limited participation of women in governance. To facilitate their participation, a “mini-diploma” was created for children, enabling maternal involvement in training sessions. This adaptive approach not only ensured women's inclusion in decision-making but also engaged future generations, demonstrating a community-led approach to building long-term health sovereignty.

Discussion

In this article, we presented the limitations of the prevailing OH framework with respect to equity and effectiveness in the Global South, with particular focus on Latin America and the Caribbean. Using the One Amazon project as a case study, we developed and applied a critical and decolonial OH perspective, structured around arguments to overcome four central limitations of the current framework.

Defining local needs: from global standardization to community sovereignty

Drawing on two case studies from the One Amazon project in Colombia and Ecuador, we showed that an adaptive OH approach that addressed local health priorities and prioritized community-based responses over standardized interventions from the Global North was more effective at understanding and developing solutions to the interrelated health problems affecting Indigenous Amazonian communities.

These results highlight the limitations of the prevailing OH framework, which is shaped by Global North perspectives that prioritize the surveillance of pandemic-potential zoonoses and antimicrobial resistance. While relevant to global security, this focus diverts attention and resources from the urgent endemic challenges facing communities in the Global South—such as diseases exacerbated by climate change, extractivism, and ecosystem degradation [31]. Consequently, during the pandemic, reliance on standardized frameworks that focused narrowly on infection risk often neglected pressing, interrelated issues such as food security and sovereignty.

These results also suggest that OH should pay more attention to the scale at which health responses are understood, designed, and implemented [32]. The cultural and ecosystemic diversity of territories, peoples, and nationalities underscores that solutions effective for some populations or contexts may not be adequate or relevant for others. Therefore, by focusing on the community scale, researchers, community members, and decision-makers can identify and implement solutions that are not readily identified or implemented at the global scale.

To address this, we propose reinterpreting community surveillance within OH not as a conventional top-down instrument, but as an articulation of the affirmative biopolitics of resistance and self-defense [33]. As demonstrated by Amazonian communities during the pandemic, they do not passively receive viruses; instead, they collectively adopted active, strategic measures. Therefore, community surveillance can emerge as a de facto exercise of health sovereignty.

Additionally, given the region's cultural and ecosystemic diversity, OH should prioritize the community scale at which responses are designed [32]. Focusing on the community level enables identification of context-specific solutions that are invisible at the global scale. At the same time, multilevel approaches ensure that these local diversities contribute to broader global health knowledge [14].

Toward genuine community participation and sovereignty

Results provide evidence for developing transparent, situated OH models that respect human and nature rights while supporting community autonomy. Such models can strengthen the capacity of civil society organizations and local communities to actively design and implement OH initiatives. In this context, community participation transcends externally imposed monitoring; it becomes a systematic, community-led involvement in surveillance and decision-making processes [34]. Genuine participation enables communities to exercise governance over their lives and safeguard their territories against threats, without being constrained by external forces [35], [36]. OH models should provide adequate technical support to co-develop monitoring systems that generate community-owned data on epidemiological, environmental, and social risks.

Furthermore, we showed that participation should evolve beyond mere validation or data extraction toward meaningful engagement. Our project's PAR model—where community leaders served as co-investigators and training programs were co-designed using local knowledge—demonstrated that genuine participation fosters ownership and sustainability while combating paternalism and knowledge extractivism. Results showed that informing communities is insufficient; social organizations must influence decision-making from the outset, spanning research design, budgeting, data collection, and dissemination [12] [34], [35].

A key component of this approach was the implementation of a fair compensation model. By providing financial remuneration to community co-investigators and indigenous knowledge holders, the project recognized them not as passive participants but as experts whose time and knowledge have economic value comparable to that of academic staff. This strategy, alongside active involvement in decision-making, serves as a powerful counter to paternalism. Solutions emerging from such inclusive processes, which shift power dynamics, are more likely to be accepted and sustained over the long term because they are grounded in local realities and priorities [37]. Consequently, decolonial OH strategies must prioritize empowering grassroots organizations—including Indigenous peoples, campesinos, Afro-descendant communities, and women—to lead the construction of a decolonized OH.

Ancestral knowledge and relational ontologies in One Health

The One Amazon project's findings demonstrated that relational ontologies—in which humans, animals, plants, territories, and spirits are constituted through mutual relationships [38]—are fundamental to understanding health in the Amazon. We showed that the core principles of OH are not new to Indigenous peoples; their knowledge systems have long conceived of territory not merely as a resource, but as a living system intimately interconnected with community life [39].

Consequently, confronting complex modern challenges, from climate emergencies to pandemics, requires transcending the dichotomy between Western science and ancestral knowledge. We argue that integrating ancestral knowledge is an act of epistemic justice that enriches Western science rather than substituting it. This integration is a pragmatic necessity that requires acknowledging historical power imbalances and colonial vestiges [40]. Ancestral knowledge, characterized by long-term empirical observation of ecosystems and ethical depth, offers a relational vision that Western science often fragments. Conversely, Western science can complement these local insights with its own analytical tools and methodological rigor.

Bridging the gap between ancestral and academic perspectives through a “dialogue of knowledges” is essential for inspiring intercultural learning [41] [42]. Integrating these relational values can transform global health foundations, strengthening OH's ability to address complex health challenges within social-ecological systems.

Results call for an OH approach that actively fosters intercultural dialogue and validates traditional practices, such as the use of medicinal plants and communal solidarity. By achieving a balance between Western and ancestral approaches, OH can promote local autonomy, strengthen community resilience, and foster respectful, responsible behaviors toward all living systems, opening pathways toward more sustainable ways of inhabiting the planet.

Addressing structural causes through decolonial frameworks

The One Amazon project yielded significant evidence that structural violence (e.g., armed conflict, land tenure insecurity) and the expansion of extractive activities directly affect community health. Therefore, a transformative OH approach should prioritize the social determination of health. This would involve promoting policies that prioritize equity rather than merely protecting against health risks [43]. It is imperative for OH to explicitly address structural causes—such as social inequality, poverty, and extractivist development models—by incorporating socio-environmental justice into its core agenda. This entails shifting away from the tendency to depoliticize socio-ecological problems, which often prioritize technical challenges while neglecting historical inequalities and colonial injustices.

To achieve this transformation, our results advocate for enhancing OH by incorporating the Rights of Nature and Biocultural Rights [[37], [44]]. The former recognizes nature as a subject of rights regardless of its utility to humans [45], while the latter protects the indissociable interdependence between biological and cultural diversity, validating traditional ecological knowledge alongside Western science [46]. Integrating these frameworks allows for a transition toward alternative economic models that respect the intrinsic value of ecosystems and the ontological values of local communities.

Drawing on our empirical observations, we propose as an interpretive framework that a ‘politics of care’ aligned with the Indigenous concept of ‘Buen Vivir’ (Sumak Kawsay) should inform a decolonized OH. While not all participating groups explicitly self-identified with the specific terminology of ‘Buen Vivir’, our findings demonstrate that Indigenous, Afro-descendant, and Campesino communities alike possess lived ontologies where well-being is intrinsically tied to the territory. As we have previously documented, for instance, among Campesino communities in the Colombian Amazon [30], these local practices of resistance and territorial care are deeply analogous to the relational principles of Buen Vivir. Therefore, we utilize this concept to analytically posit that individual well-being is inextricably linked to the harmony of the community, territory, and the natural world [38]. Adopting this care framework strengthens democratic practices and facilitates navigating complex interactions among gender roles, vulnerabilities, and knowledge within socioecosystems [[47], [48]].

Furthermore, giving voice to Indigenous knowledge beyond the local level is crucial. Our results confirm that collective well-being requires multilevel participation—engaging community leaders to influence regional and global political spaces—to challenge the root causes of the global health and environmental crisis.

It is important to acknowledge that the global One Health discourse has evolved significantly in recent years. The Quadripartite's 2022–2026 One Health Joint Plan of Action (OH JPA) [3] explicitly grounds its theory of change in principles of equity, inclusivity, multicultural parity, and community engagement. Furthermore, a growing body of literature, including recent case studies published in platforms such as CABI One Health [49] [[50], [51]], highlights increasing efforts to embed equity and transdisciplinarity into OH research and practice. However, our findings underscore that a profound implementation gap remains between these global normative frameworks and the realities in the Global South. While current global plans recognize the need for community engagement, our critical review of the four structural limitations demonstrates that, in practice, interventions often default to instrumental participation or prioritize Global North health security over local systemic inequalities. Our participatory action research helps bridge this gap. By demonstrating how to transition from mere consultative approaches to genuine epistemic co-production—where Amazonian communities act as co-investigators and ancestral knowledge is placed on equal footing with Western science—we provide an empirically grounded pathway to operationalize the equity and decolonial goals that global OH frameworks aspire to achieve.

Conclusion

In summary, our critique of One Health approaches from the perspective of LAC in the Global South advocates an integrated approach. It emphasizes that, to be genuinely effective and equitable, One Health must undergo a profound decolonization process. The process would entail authentic collaborative creation with the Global South, acknowledging and respecting its knowledges and priorities, and reconfiguring power and financial dynamics to ensure that solutions are genuinely contextual and responsive to the varied realities and aspirations of all regions. The objective of our decolonial OH approach is to facilitate dialogue between perspectives, enabling a two-way exchange of recommendations and models between the Global South and the Global North. Establishing authentic cooperation between Western science and ancestral and community knowledge is an act of justice. Acknowledging the inherent value and autonomy of such knowledge is imperative as a foundational step toward decolonizing thought and constructing a more equitable world. This suggests that all forms of knowledge can articulate their perspectives and contribute to the discourse from their respective vantage points, guided by their internal logic and dignity.
