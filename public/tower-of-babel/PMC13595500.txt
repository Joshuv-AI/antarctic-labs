Total Neoadjuvant Treatment for Esophageal Adenocarcinoma With Oligometastases: TNT‐OES‐1 Trial
Authors: Charlène J. van der Zijden, Tamara J. Huizer, Ben M. Eyck, Ate van der Gaast, Leni van Doorn, Marjolein Y. V. Homs, J. Jan B. van Lanschot, Joost J. M. E. Nuyttens, Lindsey Oudijk, Manon C. W. Spaander, Bas P. L. Wijnhoven, Bianca Mostert, Sjoerd M. Lagarde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChemotherapy (FLOT) and chemoradiotherapy (CROSS) are both effective as neoadjuvant regimens for esophageal/junctional cancer. Total Neoadjuvant Therapy (TNT) aims to increase efficacy by combining chemotherapy with chemoradiotherapy. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and safety of TNT FLOT‐CROSS. Patients with histologically proven esophageal/junctional adenocarcinoma with oligometastases (maximum four lesions in maximum two organs) were included. Treatment consisted of four cycles of FLOT followed by response evaluation (CT‐scan). Patients without disease progression proceeded to CROSS, followed by response evaluation (CT‐scan, endoscopy with biopsies and endoscopic ultrasonography with fine‐needle aspiration on indication). Primary endpoint was the safety and tolerability of TNT FLOT‐CROSS. Secondary endpoints included progression‐free survival (PFS), disease control rate (DCR), quality of life (QoL) using QLQ‐C30 and QLQ‐OG25, toxicity and the proportion of patients proceeding to local therapy. Twenty patients were included of whom 16 (80%) had a single metastatic lesion. Median follow‐up was 47.9 months (95% CI 32.8–63.1). Fifteen patients (75%) completed TNT FLOT‐CROSS. FLOT came with manageable, expected toxicity, and all patients without progression were able to start and complete CROSS. No grade 3–5 toxicities occurred during CROSS. After TNT FLOT‐CROSS, two patients underwent surgery. The 1‐ and 2‐year PFS were 40% and 13%, respectively. Three months after TNT FLOT‐CROSS completion, DCR was 55%. Dysphagia scores significantly decreased over time, while fatigue significantly increased. Sequencing TNT FLOT‐CROSS in patients with oligometastatic esophageal adenocarcinoma is feasible and comes with manageable toxicity and promising efficacy, with a 1‐year progression free survival of 40%. Introduction

Neoadjuvant treatment followed by esophagectomy is the standard of care for patients with locally advanced esophageal or esophagogastric junction (EGJ) adenocarcinoma in Western countries. There are two different approaches: neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT; CROSS) which primarily aims for better locoregional control, and perioperative chemotherapy (FLOT) which primarily aims for better systemic control [1, 2]. Both strategies come with survival benefit as compared to surgery alone. The Neo‐AEGIS study showed no difference in survival between the groups undergoing FLOT versus CROSS as neoadjuvant treatment regimen [3]. Total Neoadjuvant Therapy (TNT) combines the potentially strong systemic effect of FLOT chemotherapy with good locoregional control of CROSS chemoradiotherapy. While TNT is increasingly used in rectal cancer and shows promising results, there are no data regarding the addition of CROSS to FLOT or vice versa in gastroesophageal cancer [4, 5, 6].

This more intensive treatment might be valuable especially in patients with oligometastatic esophageal cancer. These patients are situated in a grey area where the disease condition hovers between incurable and curable. Palliative treatment may seem insufficient for these patients given that some of them have a good response to therapy and may be able to proceed to surgery, as shown by previous studies [7, 8]. Intensification of multimodality treatment might facilitate the selection of patients with a good response and an improved prognosis. As of yet, the role of surgery in patients with oligometastatic disease remains uncertain and is under investigation in the AIO‐FLOT5 trial [9].

Possibly, a more intense treatment combining the systemic effects of FLOT chemotherapy with the locoregional effect of CROSS chemoradiotherapy aims for improved disease‐free and overall survival as it adds radiotherapy and docetaxel to the standard of care regimens for (oligo)metastatic esophageal adenocarcinoma. Besides that, the addition of chemoradiotherapy even makes an organ‐sparing approach (i.e., surveillance strategy) a real possibility. In the oligometastatic setting, the feasibility and survival outcomes of TNT are unknown. However, this is a patient group where a TNT treatment—such an escalation of treatment with more side‐effects, decreased HRQOL and impact on health care resources—is warranted. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the safety and feasibility of TNT (the sequential combination of the FLOT and CROSS regimens; TNT FLOT‐CROSS) in patients with oligometastatic esophageal or EGJ adenocarcinoma.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

The TNT‐OES‐1 trial was a phase‐II, prospective, single‐center, single‐arm intervention study performed in the Erasmus MC Cancer Institute.

Eligible patients were between 18 and 75 years old and had baseline clinical stage T1N1‐3 or T2‐4a N0‐3 esophageal adenocarcinoma (Siewert I‐II) and oligometastatic disease (defined in this study as a maximum of four lesions in up to two organs, excluding lymph nodes). Major exclusion criteria were peritoneal or pleural dissemination, gastric carcinoma (Siewert III) and peripheral neuropathy grade > 1. A detailed description of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as the study procedures and sample size calculation have been published previously [10].

Treatment and Study Procedures

All patients started treatment with four cycles of FLOT according to standard care [1]. This regimen consists of two weekly cycles of 5‐fluorouracil intravenous (iv) 2600 mg/m2 in a 24 h continuous infusion, leucovorin 200 mg/m2, oxaliplatin iv 85 mg/m2, and docetaxel iv 50 mg/m2 on the first day of treatment. Granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) was only administered in case of neutropenic complications or delay due to neutropenia (secondary prophylaxis).

After completion of four cycles of FLOT, patients underwent a response evaluation consisting of a CT‐scan of the neck, thorax and abdomen (CRE‐1). Patients with progressive disease according to RECIST criteria (v1.1) went off‐study and were treated following the recommendation of the multidisciplinary tumor board (MTB). If the primary tumor was still resectable and no distant metastases were detected, patients proceeded to CROSS chemoradiotherapy [2]. This regimen consists of five weekly cycles of carboplatin intravenously at an area under the curve (AUC) of 2 mg/mL/min and paclitaxel intravenously at a dose of 50 mg/m2 on the first day of each week, with concurrent radiotherapy 41.4 Gy given in 23 fractions of 1.8 Gy for 5 days per week, starting on the first day of each cycle of chemotherapy.

After completion of CROSS, patients underwent a second response evaluation consisting of a combination of upper endoscopy with bite‐on‐bite biopsies, endoscopic ultrasound with fine‐needle aspiration of suspicious lymph nodes and a CT‐scan of the neck, thorax, and abdomen (CRE‐2), which marked the end of the study treatment. The MTB advised whether patients could proceed to surgery (i.e., partial or complete response to therapy) and/or local therapy of metastases or qualified for four additional cycles of FLOT chemotherapy. Patients were asked to complete quality of life (QoL) questionnaires prior to start of FLOT, after FLOT and after CROSS (QLQ‐C30 and QLQ‐OG25) [11, 12].

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the safety and tolerability of TNT FLOT‐CROSS, defined as the proportion of patients that completed four cycles of FLOT chemotherapy and all five cycles of chemotherapy and the full radiation dose of 41.4 Gy during CROSS chemoradiotherapy. Patients who completed both regimens but required dose reduction (i.e., up to 50% of the initial dose) or experienced delay (i.e., not more than 3 weeks), were also counted as patients completing the regimen.

Secondary outcomes included the proportion of patients with progressive disease after four cycles of FLOT and who therefore were not able to proceed to CROSS. The safety and tolerability of a second course of four cycles of FLOT after CROSS was calculated as the number of patients who completed the TNT FLOT‐CROSS‐FLOT regimen out of the patients starting the second part of FLOT chemotherapy. Serious adverse events were reported according to the CTCAE version 5.0 [13]. Response to therapy was reported as disease control rate (DCR), defined as the percentage of patients with stable disease, partial or complete response 3 months after completion of TNT FLOT‐CROSS [14]. After completion of FLOT‐CROSS, the proportion of patients that proceeded to esophagectomy and/or local therapy of metastases was calculated, including surgical morbidity (according to Clavien‐Dindo) and mortality (30‐day, 90‐day, and/or in‐hospital) [15]. Also the percentage of patients without residual locoregional disease or distant metastases (i.e., clinically complete response; cCR) was determined. The progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were calculated at 1 and 2 years. Quality of life (QoL) was measured at baseline (i.e., before start of FLOT), during treatment (i.e., at the first response evaluation) and after completion of TNT FLOT‐CROSS, using the EORTC QLQ‐OG25 and QLQ‐C30 [11, 12]. The selected QoL outcomes were dysphagia (QLQ‐OG25), fatigue (QLQ‐C30), emotional functioning (QLQ‐C30), and physical functioning (QLQ‐C30).

Statistical Analysis

The completion rate for neoadjuvant treatment with FLOT is 90%, which is 95% for CROSS chemoradiotherapy. The null hypothesis that the true success rate (success defined as completion of all four FLOT cycles and the full chemoradiotherapy regimen) is 40% is tested against a one sided alternative. Further investigation of TNT FLOT‐CROSS is warranted if more than 70% of patients will complete both regimens (alternative hypothesis). Using Simon's two‐stage design with a type I error rate of 0.05 and power of 80%, the sample size for the current study was set to 20 patients. All analyses were performed on the intention‐to‐treat population, with the exception of the safety endpoints, which were performed in the per‐protocol population (defined as the patients who received at least one cycle of FLOT). Patient and tumor characteristics were analyzed using descriptive statistics and were presented as mean, median with interquartile range (IQR) or frequencies (%). The proportion of patients that completed the TNT FLOT‐CROSS regimen was calculated relative to all patients of the intention‐to‐treat population. The median follow‐up was calculated from the first cycle of FLOT until date of death or last day of follow‐up. DCR was calculated for the intention‐to‐treat population. The number of patients who underwent surgical resection and/or local therapy of metastases, surgical morbidity and mortality, as well as the number of patients with a cCR were reported as frequencies. Median time to disease progression was calculated from date of start of FLOT until disease progression using the Kaplan–Meier curve, defined as the time at which 50% of patients had experienced progression. The PFS was calculated from date of first cycle of FLOT until date of disease progression or death, whereas patients alive were censored using the Kaplan Meier method. The OS was calculated from date of informed consent until date of death or last day of follow‐up, whichever occurred first. The difference in QoL at baseline and after completion of TNT FLOT‐CROSS was analyzed using generalized estimated equations, followed by a repeated measure analysis. Questionnaire scores were transformed into a 0–100 scale, according to the EORTC manual [16]. A p‐value of < 0.05 (two‐sided) was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with the use of R version 4.0.0 (R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Treatment Completion and Response

Nineteen patients started FLOT, and eighteen patients (90%) successfully completed all four initial cycles of FLOT and underwent a response evaluation (Figure 1). One patient died following the first cycle of FLOT due to esophageal tumor necrosis and perforation with secondary sepsis. Disease progression was observed in three patients (17%). Subsequently, fifteen patients (75%) proceeded to CROSS chemoradiotherapy, and all patients completed the initiated treatment. After CROSS, disease progression was seen in two of the 15 patients (13%). The remaining 13 patients (87%) had partial response or stable disease. Of the intention‐to‐treat population, disease control was reported in 11 patients (11/20), resulting in a DCR of 55% three months after completion of TNT FLOT‐CROSS.

Flowchart and results of the TNT‐OES‐1 trial. CROSS chemoradiotherapy, carboplatin/paclitaxel with concurrent 41.4 Gy radiotherapy; FLOT, fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, docetaxel.

Following TNT FLOT‐CROSS, two patients underwent esophagectomy and in one patient metastatic disease was detected at surgical exploration and therefore no resection could be performed. All other patients (n = 10) proceeded to another four cycles of FLOT, again followed by a response evaluation. Two patients showed disease progression and eight patients had partial response or stable disease. Four patients underwent esophagectomy including two patients that also underwent local treatment of metastases. Four of eight patients (50%) had a clinically complete response and opted for active surveillance after shared decision making, which was performed according to the SANO‐2 protocol [17].

Treatment of Metastatic Sites

The majority of patients (n = 18) did not undergo metastatic‐directed therapy or resection of metastatic lesion(s). One patient underwent resection of a liver metastasis concurrently with the esophagectomy, and another had ablation of a liver metastasis performed during the same procedure as primary tumor resection.

Toxicity of TNT FLOT‐CROSS

During FLOT chemotherapy, treatment delay due to toxicity was reported in 12 patients (63%). Ten cases of grade 3–4 toxicity were reported, including febrile neutropenia (n = 1), uncomplicated neutropenia (n = 8) and syncope (n = 1). Most grade 1–2 toxicities were fatigue (n = 6), peripheral sensory neuropathy (n = 6) and neutropenia (n = 4) (Table 3). Serious adverse events (SAEs) occurred in two patients, both requiring hospitalization for stomach pain, deemed unrelated to the treatment. No grade 3–5 toxicities or treatment delays/discontinuations occurred during CROSS. One patient faced a serious adverse event shortly after completion of CROSS, requiring hospitalization for acute renal failure, classified as related to treatment.

Surgical Outcomes

Six patients underwent esophagectomy for recurrent or suspected locoregional disease, two after TNT FLOT‐CROSS and four after TNT FLOT‐CROSS‐FLOT. A pathologically complete response of the primary tumor and lymph nodes (i.e., ypT0N0) was seen in two of the six patients (33%), one after TNT FLOT‐CROSS and one after TNT FLOT‐CROSS‐FLOT. Both patients did not undergo surgery of metastatic lesions as they were not visible on restaging images (i.e., clinically complete response of metastatic lesions). Median length of hospital stay was 9.5 days (interquartile range [IQR] 8.00–12.25). Four patients had postoperative complications (Clavien‐Dindo ≥ 3) within 90 days from surgery. One patient had intrathoracic anastomotic leakage treated with intravenous antibiotics and placement of an endoscopic vacuum sponge. One patient had chyle leakage for which fat‐restricted enteral feeding was started. The patient was readmitted to the hospital within 35 days and subsequently died due to respiratory failure and septic shock resulting from pulmonary abscesses. Another patient had chyle leakage for which fat‐restricted enteral feeding was started and the patient also underwent an ileocecal resection for a volvulus. The last patient had bile leakage of a bile duct defect requiring laparotomy and repair of the defect. No deaths were observed < 30 days from surgery. Two patients died within 90 days postoperatively. One patient died as a result of postoperative complications and the other patient suffered from rapid postoperative tumor progression.

Progression Free Survival and Overall Survival

Median time to progression from study inclusion was 5.8 months (IQR 3.3–8.1) (Table S2). The 1‐ and 2‐year progression‐free survival rates were 40% (95% CI 18.7%–85.5%) and 13% (95% CI 2.3%–78.4%), respectively (Figure 2). Median overall survival was 21.3 months (95% CI 9.6–NA) (Figure S1).

Quality of Life

Quality of life questionnaires were completed at baseline, and after completion of four cycles of FLOT and the full CROSS chemoradiotherapy regimen. Dysphagia significantly decreased over time, with a mean score of 27.3 at baseline, 5.9 at CRE‐1 and 15.4 at CRE‐2 (p < 0.001) (Figure 3A). Fatigue significantly increased following TNT FLOT‐CROSS, with a mean score of 16.2 at baseline, 20.7 at CRE‐1 and 37.6 at CRE‐2 (p = 0.01) (Figure 3B). Emotional functioning did not change significantly during treatment, with a mean score of 69.2 at baseline, 80.0 at CRE‐1 and 78.2 at CRE‐2 (p = 0.23) (Figure 3C). Physical functioning tended to decrease slightly, with a mean score of 90.8 at baseline, 87.6 at CRE‐1 and 80.5 at CRE‐2 (p = 0.18) (Figure 3D).

Discussion

This study showed that TNT FLOT‐CROSS in patients with oligometastatic esophageal adenocarcinoma is safe and feasible with 75% of patients being able to complete the full TNT FLOT‐CROSS regimen. We observed promising efficacy, with 55% of patients achieving disease control at 3 months (i.e., median 31.8 weeks after start of study treatment).

This is the first study investigating the feasibility of TNT by using the sequential combination of FLOT chemotherapy and CROSS chemoradiotherapy in esophageal cancer patients. TNT represents an escalation in treatment intensity, both in terms of duration and of potential side effects. Previous trials have reported that serious adverse events (SAEs) related to neoadjuvant therapy occurred in 27% (FLOT4‐AIO trial) and 23% (CROSS trial) of patients [1, 2]. In the present study, only two patients who underwent TNT FLOT‐CROSS experienced an SAE. Regardless of the low rate of SAEs, it is also important to consider non‐serious adverse events. While these toxicities may not outweigh the potential survival benefit, they may affect patients' QoL and treatment adherence. In this trial, sequencing FLOT and CROSS did not lead to increased toxicity compared to the known toxicity of FLOT or CROSS by itself. However, it is important to note that this was a small study with limited patient numbers, and toxicity and treatment adherence warrant close monitoring in larger follow‐up studies.

This study indicates that TNT FLOT‐CROSS yields promising outcomes. Other studies on patients with oligometastatic esophageal cancer reported varying PFS rates at 1 year ranging from 25% to almost 50%, which is in line with the findings in our study (2‐year PFS of 13%) [18, 19]. It should be noted that studies used different definitions of oligometastatic disease and encompassed a heterogeneous population which limits their comparison and the generalizability of outcomes. Another study (RENAISSANCE trial) investigated the role of surgical intervention in limited‐metastatic gastric or junctional cancer after systemic chemotherapy ± trastuzumab or nivolumab [9, 20]. The primary endpoint overall survival was not met, seemingly due to increased early mortality in the surgery arm. It appeared that patients with retroperitoneal lymph node metastases were the only group to possibly benefit from surgery, albeit in a post hoc analysis of a small subgroup, making them a key focus for future studies.

The potential benefits of TNT FLOT‐CROSS are two‐fold; combining locoregional and systemic treatment might lead to better disease control, reduction of symptoms, and increased survival, while its extended treatment schedule and repeated evaluations can also serve as a test of time, a valuable tool for observing tumor biology over time and to see whether systemic disease expands. Previous research on induction chemotherapy has demonstrated that this approach enables selection of responders who may qualify for attempted cure‐intended therapy [7, 8]. Patients in whom disease progresses during FLOT or CROSS can be spared further intensive treatment or futile surgery, which is associated with significant morbidity and long‐term impact on health‐related quality of life [21, 22, 23].

This study also explored the potential for additional treatment in patients who completed TNT FLOT‐CROSS. All patients without disease progression following TNT FLOT‐CROSS were considered eligible for additional treatment. A subgroup of patients underwent four additional cycles of FLOT or esophagectomy, and a pathologically complete response was seen in two patients. This suggests that some patients with oligometastases who respond well to therapy might proceed to additional local therapy (i.e., surgical resection) but the benefit in terms of prolonging survival remains to be determined. In addition, three patients had a clinically complete response following TNT FLOT‐CROSS‐FLOT, which allowed these patients to opt for an organ‐sparing active surveillance strategy instead of surgery. As the current study shows promising results of TNT FLOT‐CROSS, it could be interesting to validate these findings in a larger cohort of patients with oligometastatic esophageal cancer. The OligoMetastatic Esophagogastric Cancer (OMEC) project has multiple research projects on patients with oligometastatic disease to improve outcomes for these patients, including a clinical trial on different treatment regimens [24, 25, 26].

Based on outcomes of the current study, TNT FLOT‐CROSS also seems an attractive treatment option for patients with locally advanced disease with a poor prognosis. Adenocarcinoma patients with lymph node positive disease have, despite neoadjuvant treatment (FLOT or CROSS), a dismal prognosis with a survival rate of 36% after 5 years. Patients with nodal‐positive adenocarcinoma are at a higher risk of developing distant metastases (63%) or a combination of locoregional and distant recurrence after 10‐year follow‐up in comparison to patients with nodal‐negative esophageal cancer (37%) [27].

The optimal sequence of TNT in such a neoadjuvant setting is yet unknown. In the current oligometastatic setting, we opted for FLOT‐CROSS to attain potential systemic disease control first. However, reversing the TNT sequence to locoregional treatment first may have the added benefit of improving dysphagia and odynophagia before introducing intensive chemotherapy. This issue will be addressed in the TNT‐OES‐2 protocol (see below). Moreover, previous research in rectal cancer showed that reversing the sequence to chemoradiotherapy followed by chemotherapy resulted in higher pathologically complete response rates (25% compared to 17% for chemotherapy followed by chemoradiotherapy) and a higher organ preserving rate (54% vs. 39%) [4, 5, 28]. While this higher and sustained response rate may be an effect of a longer time interval between chemoradiotherapy (with its late efficacy) and the response evaluation upon which the decision to offer active surveillance is made, it could also be a direct effect mediated by tumor biology. A higher pathologically complete response rate allows more patients to opt for active surveillance strategies, an attractive alternative for esophagectomy in patients with a clinically complete response as shown in the SANO trial [29]. Therefore, it is worthwhile investigating both sequences in patients with esophageal cancer at high risk for disease recurrence. We are preparing a prospective, randomized, multicenter phase‐II study to investigate the safety, feasibility and efficacy of both TNT FLOT‐CROSS and TNT CROSS‐FLOT in the neoadjuvant setting [30].

This study has strengths and limitations. The data for this phase‐II study were collected prospectively, resulting in accurate and reliable data with minimal missing information. Moreover, toxicity and SAEs which occurred during treatment were reported in accordance with the CTCAE (v5.0), providing a transparent overview of the potential risks of the TNT FLOT‐CROSS regimen. It is important to note that this study had a small sample size, and larger studies are needed to confirm these results. Additionally, the decision to proceed to four additional cycles of FLOT chemotherapy or local therapy of metastases and/or esophagectomy was made by the multidisciplinary tumor board, which may introduce bias and limit reproducibility. Lastly, the analysis of progression‐free survival did not account for surgical resection, which may have influenced the observed outcomes given the potential impact of surgery on disease progression.

In conclusion, our study showed that TNT FLOT‐CROSS is feasible and safe, and comes with promising efficacy. Given the increasing demand for an organ‐sparing approach and the poor prognosis of nodal‐positive adenocarcinoma patients, the next step is to determine the efficacy of TNT in the neoadjuvant setting in patients with locally advanced disease without distant metastases.
