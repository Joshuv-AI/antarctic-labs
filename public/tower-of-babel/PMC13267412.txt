Luspatercept and imetelstat for myelodysplastic syndrome: updates from ASH 2025 annual meeting
Authors: Yagya Ahlawat, Heng Jiang, Delong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLuspatercept, an erythroid maturation agent, and imetelstat, a first-in-class telomerase inhibitor, have expanded the therapeutic landscape for patients with myelodysplastic neoplasms (MDS). Recent data presented at the 2025 American Society of Hematology (ASH) Annual Meeting further clarify their evolving clinical roles. New analyses evaluated the real-world use of luspatercept in patients with lower-risk MDS (LR-MDS), while additional studies characterized the efficacy and outcomes associated with imetelstat in transfusion-dependent (TD) LR-MDS and in advanced MDS or acute myeloid leukemia following hypomethylating agent failure. We summarized the latest updates on luspatercept and imetelstat for MDS from the 2025 ASH Annual Meeting.Keywords: Luspatercept, Imetelstat, Myelodysplastic neoplasms, Telomerase inhibitor

To the editor

Recent presentations at the 2025 American Society of Hematology (ASH) Annual Meeting provided new insights into therapies for anemia in myelodysplastic neoplasms (MDS), particularly luspatercept in lower-risk MDS (LR-MDS) and imetelstat in transfusion-dependent (TD) LR-MDS and advanced disease following hypomethylating agent (HMA) failure.

Luspatercept in LR-MDS

Luspatercept is an erythroid-maturation agent that promotes late-stage red cell development by binding to the TGF- superfamily members [1]. The ASH 2025 luspatercept data can be grouped on three clinically relevant themes: activity compared with erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs), earlier use, and dosing strategy. A post hoc analysis of the COMMANDS trial evaluated the efficacy of luspatercept in ESA-naïve, TD, LR-MDS. Although patients with earlier-stage features, including hemoglobin (Hgb) ≥ 8 g/dL, transfusion burden (TB) of 2 units, and serum erythropoietin levels (sEPO) ≤ 100 U/L, had higher response rates in both treatment arms, luspatercept achieved higher rates and longer duration of RBC transfusion independence (RBC-TI) than epoetin alfa across these subgroups. Among luspatercept-treated patients, those with Hgb ≥ 8 g/dL achieved higher rates of ≥ 12 weeks RBC-TI than those with Hgb < 8 g/dL (87.5% vs. 69.1%), with longer median durations of RBC-TI with luspatercept (150.0 vs. 108.3 weeks); similar trends were observed when stratifying by TB and sEPO levels [2]. These findings support consideration of luspatercept as a preferred first-line option in selected ESA-naïve TD LR-MDS patients, particularly those with lower TB and other favorable baseline features.

Real-world studies further support earlier use of luspatercept in LR-MDS (Table 1). In a U.S. retrospective cohort, 182 patients receiving luspatercept and 181 patients receiving ESAs were evaluated; 69.6% patients receiving luspatercept achieved Hgb ≥ 10 g/dL within 6 months compared with 58% with ESA, with a shorter median time to response (2.3 vs. 5.3 months) [3]. Additional data reported RBC-TI ≥ 12 weeks in 64.2% patients within 6 months [4]. Another real-world analysis reported dose escalation in 27.5% and discontinuation in 28.1%, mainly due to lack of response [5]. These findings are limited by retrospective design, heterogeneous baseline characteristics, and variable prior ESA exposure.

The open-label phase 3b MAXILUS trial evaluated luspatercept initiated at the maximum-approved dose of 1.75 mg/kg in TD LR-MDS regardless of prior ESA exposure [6]. Interim analysis (n = 105) showed good tolerability, with no new safety signals or thromboembolic events. Among ESA-naïve patients, 73.3% achieved ≥ 8-week RBC-TI and 70.0% achieved ≥ 12-week RBC-TI during a 24-week course; rates were lower in ESA-relapsed/refractory or intolerant (R/R/I) patients (65.3% and 49.0%). Dose reductions occurred in 13.2% of ESA-naïve patients and delays in 36.5% [6]. QOL-ONE Phoenix, a phase 2 single-arm multicenter study in TD LR-MDS patients with deletion 5q, reported Hgb improvement in some evaluable patients and improvement in fatigue-related quality-of-life measures, although longer follow-up and larger patient numbers are needed to clarify the durability and clinical impact of these findings [7].

Imetelstat in MDS

Imetelstat is a competitive telomerase inhibitor that induces apoptosis in malignant haematopoietic progenitor cells and was approved for TD LR-MDS based on results from the IMerge trial, particularly in ESA-R/R/I patients [8, 9]. Secondary analyses from the IMerge trial further identified features associated with improved response and suggested a potential survival benefit [10, 11]. With median follow-up time of 45 months, median overall survival (OS) in the intent-to-treat population was 47.8 months with imetelstat group versus 44.8 months with placebo [10]. Favorable trends were also observed in progression-free survival (PFS) and time to AML progression (Table 2). Another post hoc analysis demonstrated that early treatment-emergent cytopenias correlated with greater hemoglobin increases and higher erythroid response and transfusion independence rates [11]. Although this may represent an on-target pharmacodynamic effect, imetelstat-associated myelosuppression remains clinically important and may require treatment delays, dose modifications, close laboratory monitoring, and proactive management of cytopenia-related adverse events. In the phase 2 IMpress study, imetelstat showed no marrow responses in patients with relapsed or refractory AML/high-risk MDS after HMA-based therapy, suggesting minimal single-agent activity in this higher-risk setting,, while increased exposure was not associated with increased toxicity [12].

In summary, the updates from ASH 2025 may help refine treatment selections for LR-MDS anemia. Although many analyses were post hoc, retrospective, or interim, the consistency of findings across data sources supports earlier luspatercept use, particularly in patients with lower TB, higher baseline Hgb, and lower sEPO. Imetelstat remains an important option for ESA-R/R/I TD LR-MDS, but cytopenia-related toxicity requires careful patient selection and monitoring.
