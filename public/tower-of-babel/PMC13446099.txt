Microwave and solvent extraction of Opuntia cladode mucilage: Functional characterization and application as natural hydrocolloid substitute for egg yolk in mayonnaise
Authors: Raha Jamaati, Mohammad Ali Sahari, Roghayeh Amini Sarteshnizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Growing health concerns associated with cholesterol and allergenic potential of egg yolk have increased the demand for plant-based hydrocolloid substitutes for emulsion based food systems. This study investigated the potential application of mucilage derived from Opuntia ficus-indica and Opuntia stricta cladodes as a natural hydrocolloid substitute for egg yolk in mayonnaise formulations. Mucilage was extracted using two methodologies- solvent and microwave extraction- from peeled and unpeeled cladodes. The mucilage powders were characterized in terms of extraction yield, physicochemical, structural, and functional properties. Mayonnaise formulations were prepared by partially replacing egg content with mucilage at 25% (M25) and 50% (M50) levels, as well as egg-free formulations incorporating 25% and 50% mucilage (EFM25 and EFM50). Results revealed that the highest extraction yield (15.95%) was obtained from the unpeeled cladodes of O. ficus-indica using the microwave method, whereas the lowest yield (5.01%) was observed in solvent extracted, peeled O. stricta. Mucilage obtained by microwave extraction showed higher carbohydrate content and molecular weight (up to 4234.20 kDa), with glucose as the predominant monosaccharide, particularly in O. ficus-indica. While peeling improved mucilage purity of O. ficus-indica, it was associated with a reduction in total phenolic content (from 13.75 to 10.60 mg GAE/g) and antioxidant activity. Partial substitution (M25 and M50) significantly improved emulsion stability and textural properties compared to the control. Specifically, M50 demonstrated the highest hardness (86.5 g), heat stability (80.15%), and microstructural uniformity compared to the control (25 g and 54.15%, respectively). Partial replacement improved sensory acceptance compared to control (with M25 scoring 4.3 in overall acceptability versus 3.6 for the control), while egg free samples showed the lowest sensory scores (<3.0). Therefore, Opuntia mucilage can be recommended as a natural hydrocolloid for egg substitution in mayonnaise formulations.

Introduction

Mayonnaise is a widely consumed oil-in-water emulsion characterized by its creamy texture, distinctive flavor, and high physical stability. Conventional mayonnaise is mainly composed of vegetable oil, usually 70–80%, egg yolk, vinegar, salt, and selected seasonings (Baek et al., 2025). Egg yolk plays a central role in mayonnaise formulation due to its strong emulsifying capacity, which is mainly attributed to amphiphilic constituents such as low- and high-density lipoproteins, livetin, and phosvitin. These components adsorb at the oil–water interface, orienting their hydrophobic regions toward the oil phase and their hydrophilic regions toward the aqueous phase, thereby reducing interfacial tension and promoting emulsion stability (Bie et al., 2025). Additionally, egg yolk plays a critical role in maintaining the mayonnaise's desirable texture and preventing coagulation, thereby, contributing significantly to product stability (Mirzanajafi-Zanjani et al., 2019). However, various challenges have increased the demand for egg alternatives. Overconsumption of eggs may contribute to cardiovascular complications and higher mortality rates, primarily associated with increased blood cholesterol (Drouin-Chartier et al., 2020; Xia et al., 2020). Furthermore, egg allergy is among the most common food allergies in children. In many cases, it continues into adulthood and can lead to serious symptoms such as skin rashes, nasal blockage, and both digestive and respiratory issues (Dang et al., 2019; Sabouraud et al., 2021; Uneoka et al., 2021). Early research efforts focused on animal-based substitutes such as milk powder, casein, whey protein, and plasma derived from bovine blood (Boukid & Gagaoua, 2022). However, increasing awareness of health, environmental sustainability, and ethical considerations have shifted focus toward plant-derived alternatives (Fehér et al., 2020; Sabouraud et al., 2021). Therefore, identifying natural plant-based ingredients with both emulsifying and stabilizing functions is an important scientific and industrial challenge.

Hydrocolloids, as biopolymeric macromolecules, are extensively employed in food systems for their thickening, stabilizing, emulsifying, fat replacing, encapsulating, gelling, and coating properties (Jayakody et al., 2023). The worldwide hydrocolloid market was reached USD 5.70 billion in 2015, and it is expected to expand substantially in coming years (Yousefi & Jafari, 2019). For instance, Arabic gum has been successfully applied to replace egg in mayonnaise formulations due to its emulsifying capacity coupled with antioxidant and antimicrobial properties (Ali & el Said, 2020). However, the functional properties of hydrocolloids may vary depending on their botanical source, chemical composition, molecular structure, and extraction method. Hence, the investigation of new hydrocolloid sources is necessary for their effective use in complex food systems.

Mucilage, a complex polymeric compound with a carbohydrate-based nature, has been considered as a functional natural hydrocolloid applicable in food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic sectors. Its functional properties include high water absorption and retention, emulsification, stabilization, and gel-formation. Mucilage mainly consists of branched polysaccharides, proteins, phenolics and minerals (Shiam et al., 2025). Among mucilage sources, Opuntia spp. represent a sustainable source for mucilage extraction due to their adaptability to dry and semi-dry environments, rapid growth rates, and wide availability (Garfias Silva et al., 2022; Goksen et al., 2023). Among all species of genus Opuntia, O. ficus-indica and O. stricta (syn. O. dillenii) are extensively cultivated for fruit production, pigment extraction, animal feed and ornamental purposes facilitated by their high ecological adaptability, ability to grow in poor soils, high temperatures and limited water resources (Humphries et al., 2022; Kampamba et al., 2022; Méndez et al., 2015). The mucilage derived from the Opuntia cactus has exceptional stabilizing properties owing to its large molecular weight, viscoelasticity, and gelation capacity (Barba et al., 2022). In addition, Opuntia mucilage has health benefits such as reducing the glycemic index and cholesterol levels in human blood (Guedes et al., 2023; Tilahun & Welegerima, 2018). Chia seed mucilage (Cornelia et al., 2015), clove basil and sweet basil seed mucilage (Neeharika & Vijayalaxmi, 2024) have been assessed in mayonnaise formulations. However, limited research has focused specifically on Opuntia mucilage as an egg substitute, especially in mayonnaise formulation.

Hot water extraction (HWE) is widely regarded as the common technique for isolating polysaccharide which has advantages such as being cheap, environmental compatibility and food-grade safety, but low extraction yield, prolonged high temperature exposure, and poor selectivity are the main disadvantages of this method (Yang et al., 2020). Other conventional solvent-based methods, such as maceration, Soxhlet extraction, and solid–liquid extraction, may also require long processing times, large solvent volumes, and high energy input, which restrict their efficiency and sustainability (Gonzaga et al., 2025). Therefore, microwave-assisted extraction, ultrasound-assisted extraction, supercritical fluid extraction, and Pressurized fluid extraction methods have been investigated as alternative green extraction techniques (Hedayati et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018). Although these methods can improve extraction performance, their application may be limited by factors such as high equipment cost, pressure requirements, matrix-dependent efficiency, or limited suitability for certain polar compounds (Gonzaga et al., 2025). Among them, microwave-assisted extraction has received particular attention because it can provide shorter extraction time, lower solvent and energy consumption, higher extraction yield, and better reproducibility compared with many conventional solvent-based and emerging extraction methods (Su et al., 2019). These advantages are mainly attributed to microwave-induced dipole rotation and ionic migration, which generate rapid volumetric heating, enhance solvent penetration, promote cell-wall disruption, and accelerate mass transfer. Accordingly, under controlled conditions, microwave-assisted extraction may provide an effective balance between extraction efficiency and preservation of polysaccharide techno-functional properties (Mirzadeh et al., 2021).

To the best of our knowledge, limited or no comprehensive studies have systematically explored the combined effects of Opuntia species and extraction method on the techno-functional performance of Opuntia mucilage and its applicability as an egg-yolk substitute in mayonnaise. Although Opuntia mucilage has been recognized as a promising natural hydrocolloid, comprehensive studies evaluating its performance in mayonnaise systems, particularly in relation to extraction method and species-dependent functionality, remain limited. Therefore, the main scientific question of this study was whether microwave extraction could improve the functional properties of Opuntia mucilage compared with conventional solvent extraction and whether such improvements could enhance its performance in egg-free mayonnaise formulations. The objective of this study was to extract mucilage from two Opuntia species, namely O. ficus-indica and O. stricta, using solvent and microwave extraction methods, compare their functional characteristics, and apply the mucilage with superior properties in mayonnaise as an egg-yolk replacer. This work provides both theoretical insight into the relationship between extraction method, botanical source, and mucilage functionality, and applied evidence for the potential use of Opuntia mucilage as a plant-based stabilizing and emulsifying ingredient in egg-free mayonnaise formulations.

Materials and methods

Materials

The O.ficus-indica and O.stricta vegetable cladodes were harvested locally in Varamin, Tehran, Iran from April to May 2023. Chemical reagents including 96% (v/v) food-grade ethanol, selenium reagent, crystalline phenol, sulfuric acid, sodium dihydrogen phosphate, disodium dihydrogen phosphate, sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid, trifluoroacetic acid, methanol, gallic acid, folin-Ciocalteu reagent, sodium carbonate and Trolox (6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchromane-2-carboxylic acid) were procured from Merck (Germany). Also, ABTS (2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid diammonium salt) and standard monosaccharide including Glucose, Galactose, Arabinose, Rhamnose, Mannose, and Xylose were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (Denmark). Ingredients required for the formulation of the mayonnaise samples were purchased locally markets in Tehran, Iran.

Mucilage extraction

Solvent extraction

Pulped cladodes were oven-dried (50 °C, 24 h), milled, and sieved (40-mesh). The powder (1:15 w/v) was extracted in water (40 °C, 500 rpm, 1 h), centrifuged (1792 ×g, 20 min), and the supernatant was precipitated with ethanol (1:3). The resulting mucilage was freeze-dried (Labconco, USA) (Bayar et al., 2018).

Microwave extraction

Microwave extraction of mucilage from two types of cladodes was carried out using the method described by Messina et al. (Messina et al., 2021), with slight modifications. Extraction was used for peeled and unpeeled cladodes. Cladodes were cooked for 4 min in a domestic microwave oven (Butane, model 300CE S-TDU) operating at 2450 MHz, with a rated microwave output power of 900 W and an input power of 1400 W and then mixed using a blender. The pulp was centrifuged at 716 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was mixed with 96% ethanol at the ratio of 1:3 to precipitate mucilage and then freeze-dried (Labconco Corp., Kansas City, MO, USA). The dried mucilage was powdered and sieved with a mesh size of 40 (Felkai-Haddache et al., 2016).

Extraction yield, physicochemical and structural characteristics of mucilage powder

Yield, moisture, ash, and protein content

Moisture content was determined using the oven drying method at 105 °C (AOAC 934.01), crude protein was measured by the micro-Kjeldahl method (N × 6.25) according to AOAC 981.10, and ash content was evaluated by incineration at 550 °C overnight (AOAC 930.05). According to the AOAC (AOAC, 2000). The extraction efficiency for the solvent and microwave extraction techniques was determined using the following Eq. 1 (Kalegowda et al., 2017).(1)Extraction yield%=W0W×100where W0 (g) was the weight of the dried mucilage after extraction, and W (g) was the dry weight of the cladodes.

Total carbohydrate and monosaccharide composition

Total carbohydrate content was determined using the phenol–sulfuric acid method with glucose as the standard (Bradford, 1976). Monosaccharide composition was analyzed according to Ahmadi et al. (Ahmadi et al., 2013) Mucilage powder (20 mg) was hydrolyzed with 2.5 M TFA at 121 °C for 2 h. After acid removal under nitrogen and freeze-drying, 1 mg of sample was dissolved in distilled water, and 10 μL was injected into a Dionex ICS3000 system equipped with a CarboPac™ PA20 column. Separation was performed using 500 mM NaOH and water at 0.5 mL/min.

pH and color

The pH was measured with a Metrohm-780 device, as described by (Monrroy et al., 2017). The color characteristics of the mucilage powders, including L* for lightness, a* for redness, and b* for yellowness were evaluated with a Hunter Colorflex Colorimeter (Hunter Lab, Reston, VA, USA) (Messina et al., 2021). The pH and color were measured in triplicate for each sample.

Molecular weight

The average molecular weight of mucilage was determined by high-performance size exclusion chromatography (HPSEC). Samples (2 mg/mL) were heated for 30 min and filtered through a 3.0 μm cellulose acetate membrane (ADVANTEC). Separation was carried out on a TSK G5000 PW column (7.5 × 600 mm; Toso Biosep, USA) using 0.15 M NaNO₃ and 0.02% NaN₃ as the mobile phase (0.4 mL/min). Detection was performed using a UV detector, MALLS (HELEOS), and RI detector, collectively referred to as HPSEC-UV-MALLS-RI (Gavlighi et al., 2018).

2.3.6. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectroscopy (TENSOR II, Bruker, Germany) was used to identify functional groups and bonding patterns of the mucilage. Freeze-dried samples (2 mg) were mixed with KBr, pressed into pellets, and scanned at room temperature over the 400–4000 cm−1 range with 4 cm−1 resolution (de Andrade Vieira et al., 2021).

Total phenolic content (TPC) and antioxidant activity assay

TPC was measured using the Folin–Ciocalteu method as described by Sukhtezari et al. (Sukhtezari et al., 2024), with gallic acid as the standard. Results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry sample (mg GAE/g). Antioxidant activity was assessed via the ABTS assay, following the protocol of Lalegani et al. (Lalegani et al., 2018). A 20 μL aliquot of each extract was mixed with 980 μL of ABTS•+ solution, incubated for 10 min in the dark at room temperature, and absorbance was recorded at 734 nm.

The percentage of inhibition was calculated using Eq.2(2)Free radical scavenging activity%=Ac−AsAc×100

Where Ac and As were the absorbance of control and sample at 734 nm, respectively.

WHC was determined following the method of Bayar et al. (Bayar et al., 2016), with slight modifications. Briefly, each sample (0.2 g) was added to 20 mL of buffer solution with pH = 7 and stirred at 50 °C (100 rpm) for 1 h. After complete dissolution, the dispersion was centrifuged at 4500 ×g for 20 min, and the supernatant was removed. The sediment was drained for 30 min by inversion. Emulsifying properties

Emulsifying Properties including Emulsion capacity (EC) and stability (ES) were assessed using Cui and Chang (Cui & Chang, 2014) methodology with some modifications. Mucilage solutions at concentrations of 0.2%, 0.6%, and 1% (w/v) were prepared, then, 10 mL of each was mixed with 5 mL of sunflower oil. Emulsification was carried out using a homogenizer (IKA T25, Germany) at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. After that, the emulsions were centrifuged at 1100 ×g for 5 min. EC was estimated according to Eq. 4(4)EC%=VfVi×100

Where Vf represents the emulsion volume and Vi represents the total volume.

To estimate ES, the prepared emulsions were incubated at 80 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 1100 ×g for 5 min. The percentage of emulsion stability was calculated as Eq. 5:(5)ES%=VtVf×100

Where Vt is the emulsion volume after incubation and centrifugation.

Analysis of mayonnaise samples

pH and color measurement

The pH of the mayonnaise samples was recorded at 25 °C using a digital pH meter (Metrohm-780, Switzerland) in triplicate (Kumar et al., 2021). The color attributes (L*, a*, b*) were measured with a Hunter Colorflex Colorimeter. Color changes and whiteness index were calculated using the following Eq. 6 & 7 (Kumar et al., 2021).(6)∆E∗=∆L∗2+∆a∗2+∆b∗2(7)WI=100−100−L∗2+a∗2+b∗2

Where ΔL* = L* Sample – L* Standard, Δa* = a* Sample - a* Standard and Δb* = b* Sample - b* Standard.

Microstructure analysis

The microstructure of the mayonnaise samples was observed using an Olympus BX51 microscope equipped with differential interference contrast (DIC) Nomarsky view and an Olympus DP72 camera. A single drop of each sample was mounted on a glass slide and examined at 40× magnification at room temperature (Flamminii et al., 2020).

Rheological properties

Rheological measurements were performed using an MCR 502 rheometer (Anton Paar, Austria). All analyses were carried out at 25 °C using a parallel-plate geometry with a diameter of 25 mm and a gap of 1 mm. To determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVR), a strain sweep test was first conducted at a constant frequency of 1 Hz over a strain range of 0.01–100%. Subsequently, frequency sweep measurements were performed over the frequency range of 0.01–100 Hz at a constant strain of 1%, which was selected within the LVR. During the oscillatory measurements, the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded as a function of frequency. Moreover, loss tangent (tan δ) was calculated as G′′/G′ (Mohammadi et al., 2024).

Texture analysis

The texture profile analysis (TPA) was performed on the mayonnaise samples using a texture analyzer (Brookfield CT3 USA) equipped with a cylindrical probe (TA11/100) of 2.5 cm diameter. About 50 g of each sample with a height of 3 cm was used. The depth of penetration was set at 1.5 cm at a speed of 1 mm/s using a load cell with a capacity of 1 g (Raikos et al., 2020).

Heat stability

The heat stability was evaluated by heating 5 g of each sample at 80 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 3000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C (Primacella et al., 2019). The separated oil was removed, and stability was calculated according to the following Eq. 8:(8)Mayonnaise stability%=Weight of mayonnaise after oil separationgInitial weight of mayonnaiseg×100

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation of the mayonnaise samples was carried out by 30 semi-trained panelists from the Department of Food Science, Tarbiat Modares University, following the procedure of Mohammadi et al. (Mohammadi et al., 2024). A 5-point hedonic scale was employed (1 = extremely disliked, 5 = extremely liked). The evaluation factors included color, flavor, aroma, texture, and overall acceptability.

Statistical evaluation

A one-way ANOVA was carried out using JMP software (version 18.0.1) to analyze the results. Tukey's test was used to investigate significant differences among samples (p < 0.05). All measurements were conducted in triplicate and, the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Results and discussion

Extraction yield, physico-chemical and structural characteristics of mucilage powder

Extraction yields and composition of mucilage powders

Extraction yields of mucilage obtained from cladodes of two Opuntia species using solvent and microwave methods are presented in Table 2. A statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) was observed between the two extraction methods. The highest extraction efficiency (15.95%) corresponded to the unpeeled cladodes of O.ficus-indica via microwave extraction method, whereas the lowest yield (5.01%) was related to the peeled cladodes of the O.stricta extracted by the solvent method. The increased extraction efficiency observed with microwave treatment in both species might be related to the accelerated disruption of cell wall structure, facilitating mucilage release (Shiehnezhad et al., 2023). Felkai-Haddache, Remini (Felkai-Haddache et al., 2016), in a comparable investigation, found that microwave-assisted extraction from O.ficus-indica cladodes reduced extraction time and improved extraction yield compared to the conventional solvent method. Furthermore, O.ficus-indica consistently produced more mucilage yields than O.stricta, irrespective of the extraction method (Cruz-Rubio et al., 2020). It should be noted, however, that microwave-assisted extraction may induce physicochemical and structural changes in the treated matrix due to rapid heating and possible local temperature peaks, which may be irreversible depending on the applied operating conditions (Melikoglu, 2025). Therefore, careful selection of the parameters involved in extraction such as extraction time, temperature, and irradiation power plays an important role in efficient extraction. In the present study, microwave treatment was applied under fixed operating conditions (microwave power and treatment time) in order to maintain consistency between experimental runs. Nevertheless, because all samples were subjected to identical microwave conditions, the procedure remained internally consistent for comparative analysis. However, a limitation of the present work is that the temperature reached during microwave treatment was not directly measured. Consequently, process control relied on standardized microwave operating conditions rather than direct temperature monitoring. Future studies should include real-time temperature measurements to improve process reproducibility and facilitate comparison among extraction studies. Also, the yield of mucilage extracted from unpeeled cladodes was higher than peeled ones for the two species demonstrating that the parenchyma tissue of the cladode contains mucilaginous cells. This observation is supported by the research of Gheribi and Khwaldia (Gheribi & Khwaldia, 2019) which highlighted that mucilage is localized within the outer chlorenchyma in addition to the inner parenchymatous tissue of the cladodes. According to the chemical composition of mucilage samples the predominant component was carbohydrate. Among the samples, the highest carbohydrate content was observed in OFI-M-P (74.48%), OS-M-P (74.36%) and OS-M-UP (73.82%), while OFI-S showed the lowest content of carbohydrate (63.07%) (p < 0.05).

Peeling significantly improved mucilage purity in O.ficus-indica, though this effect was not statistically significant on O.stricta. The microwave-assisted extraction method for O.ficus-indica yielded higher carbohydrate content, likely due to the electromagnetic radiation facilitating cell wall disruption and subsequent release of cellular polysaccharides. Felkai-Haddache et al. (Felkai-Haddache et al., 2016) also reported increased carbohydrate yield using microwave extraction compared to conventional solvent method.

The monosaccharide profile of the mucilage powders (Table 3) indicated different composition for two species. O.ficus-indica mucilage comprised glucose, galactose, arabinose, rhamnose, and xylose, whereas O.stricta mucilage contained glucose, galactose, arabinose, rhamnose, and mannose. Polysaccharides of mucilage for both species had an arabinogalactan backbone, with galactose and arabinose as the dominant monosaccharide in solvent-extracted sample. According to previous reports, arabinogalactans are typically found in various cactus family genera (da Silveira Agostini-Costa, 2020). Microwave extracted mucilage demonstrated a markedly different monosaccharide profile, with glucose containing 91.32% and 58.97% of the total monosaccharide content in O.ficus-indica and O.stricta mucilage, respectively. The elevated glucose content of mucilage extracted using microwave method may be attributed to the co-extraction of α-glucans. According to Cruz-Rubio et al. The moisture content of the extracted mucilage samples ranged from 3.28% to 4.5%, with no significant differences observed among treatments. The solvent method yielded more protein content than microwave-assisted method in O.ficus-indica. This difference may be attributed to the longer duration and higher temperature associated with solvent extraction approach (1 h vs 4 min), as increased extraction time and temperature are known to enhance protein solubilization (Jouki et al., 2014). Given that the low protein content observed in both species may be attributed to the fact that mucilage-producing cells contain little or no protein (Luna-Zapién et al., 2023). Additionally, the low protein level of mucilage demonstrated the effectiveness of the extraction procedure, as the high protein content is a sign of polysaccharide contamination with no polysaccharide materials (Mannai et al., 2024). Ash content of mucilage did not differ among samples (p > 0.05). Although ash represents the total inorganic residue within the polysaccharide matrix and can influence functional properties, reduced ash content is typically associated with higher mucilage content (Kalegowda et al., 2017).

pH and color

The pH values of mucilage extracts were not significantly influenced by the extraction methods for either species (p > 0.05; Table 2), However microwave extracted mucilage from O.stricta showed significantly lower pH values than mucilage obtained from O.ficus-indica extracted by the same method. The acidic nature of cactus mucilage is primarily related to the presence of organic acids, particularly malic acid in the plant tissue and uronic acid components within the polysaccharide matrix (Monrroy et al., 2017).

Table 2 summarizes the colorimetric parameters of the mucilage samples. Based on the lightness value (L*), which ranges from 0 to 100, mucilage solutions were comparatively transparent. The mucilage powders were yellowish-green in hue which were shown by the negative redness (a*) and positive yellowness (b*) across all samples. Under microwave-assisted extraction, species type significantly affected the color parameters and O.ficus-indica mucilage had higher L* than O.stricta samples and b* values of mucilage from O.stricta was significantly higher than that of O.ficus-indica. However, the species type did not significantly affect the final color of mucilage when the solvent extraction method was applied. Color difference could be attributed to the greater co-extraction of water-soluble compounds and polysaccharides during mucilage extraction. However, in the present study, microwave-assisted extraction produced mucilage with lower ash content, indicating a lower level of impurities. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Biltekin et al. (2025), who observed that microwave-assisted extraction resulted in lower ash content and higher pectin purity compared with conventional extraction methods (Biltekin et al., 2025). Furthermore, the evaluation of total phenolic content (TPC) revealed no significant differences between mucilage samples obtained using the two extraction methods. This result may be related to the polysaccharide composition of the species studied. During microwave-assisted extraction, water acts as the extraction solvent and efficiently absorbs microwave energy. This enhances solvent penetration into the plant material, promoting rapid swelling and loosening of the plant matrix. As a result, solvent–tissue interactions are intensified, leading to improved mass transfer. Similar observations have been reported by Spinei and Oroian (2022), who found that pectin obtained through microwave-assisted extraction exhibited lower brightness than pectin extracted by conventional methods because of high microwave power and elevated temperatures (Spinei & Oroian, 2022).

Molecular weight (MW)

As presented in Table 3, mucilage extracted via microwave-assisted extraction exhibited a significantly higher molecular weight compared to that extracted through conventional solvent extraction. Notably O.ficus-indica mucilage extracted by microwave showed the highest molecular weight which also contained the highest level of glucose. The co-extraction of α-glucans, such as soluble starch, likely contributed to the increased molecular weight profile (Shiehnezhad et al., 2023). Also, according to Ridleyet al. (Ridley et al., 2001), microwave treatment may inactivate pectolytic enzymes responsible for polysaccharide depolymerization, thereby preserving polymer integrity and molecular size. The molecular weight values obtained in the present study (1223.85–4234.20 kDa) were within the range reported for Opuntia mucilage. Previous studies reported molecular weights of approximately 15,300–17,500 kDa for O.ficus-indica mucilage (Felkai-Haddache et al., 2016) and 1900 kDa for the O.stricta (Kalegowda et al., 2017). The observed differences may be attributed to variations in species, extraction conditions, purification procedures, and molecular weight determination methods.

All mucilage solutions showed heat stability above 80%, with stability increasing alongside mucilage concentration. This high ES can be attributed to elevated viscosity imparted by the mucilage, which restricts oil droplet movement and prevents coalescence (Dick et al., 2019). At higher concentrations of polysaccharides, enhanced polymer-polymer interactions result in more entangled macromolecular networks and reduced molecular mobility (Quinzio et al., 2018). Additionally, presence of uronic acids (galacturonic and glucuronic acids) in the mucilage may increase the surface charge of oil droplets, increasing electrostatic repulsion and further improving emulsion stability (Wang et al., 2022). Kalegowdaand Chauhan (Kalegowda et al., 2017) reported that O.stricta mucilage at the concentration of 1%, showed EC and ES values exceeding 60% and 50%, respectively. Gheribi et al. Given the functional properties, the mucilage obtained in present study considered as a potential functional ingredient in mayonnaise formulations. Mucilage extracted from O. ficus-indica using unpeeled cladodes (OFI-M-UP) was selected for incorporation into the mayonnaise formulation because of shorter extraction time and highest extraction yield, remarkable emulsifying properties and higher molecular weight. It was used for partial and complete egg replacement in mayonnaise formulations.

Microstructure

The microstructures of mayonnaise samples are presented in Fig. 5. In the control, M25, and M50 emulsions, small oil drops were uniformly dispersed within a continuous aqueous phase, forming stable emulsions. Among samples, M25 and M50 exhibited the most cohesive droplet morphology and the highest degree of droplet size uniformity, indicative of enhanced emulsion stability. In contrast, EFM25 which contained 1.5% mucilage and lacked egg, presented with the largest oil droplets, suggesting inadequate emulsification. Increasing the concentration of mucilage to 3% in EFM50 led to a reduction in droplet size and a more compact structure than EFM25. These findings align with the results reported by Akcicek et al. (Akcicek et al., 2022) who observed a similar effect when xanthan gum was employed as a partial egg substitute. The poor microstructural integrity observed in EFM25 can be attributed to the absence of egg proteins, which are key emulsifying agents, and the insufficient mucilage concentration, leading to larger inter-droplet voids and increased susceptibility to droplet coalescence (Fernandes & Mellado, 2018). Droplet size is a critical determinant of mayonnaise characteristics, affecting rheological behavior, stability, shelf life, texture, and flavor release. Smaller droplets enhance product lightness due to increased light scattering and contribute to a creamer texture (Yildirim et al., 2016). The control, M25, and M50 samples exhibited more uniform oil droplets, which can limit droplet coalescence and thereby reduce phase separation; additionally, increased continuous-phase viscosity contributes to improved emulsion stability and structural integrity (Xu et al., 2025).Fig. 5Microstructure of different mayonnaise formulations. Conversely, polydisperse emulsions (such as EFM50), contain a heterogeneous mix of droplet sizes, which weakens the emulsion structure and reduces cohesiveness and hardness.

Storage stability of these formulations may be influenced by droplet size and the microstructural stability of the samples. Increasing the mucilage concentration reduced droplet size and resulted in a more compact microstructure in EFM50. Larger droplet sizes promote a sequence of destabilization processes, including flocculation, coalescence, and Ostwald ripening (Gohtani & Yoshii, 2018). These phenomena are more likely to occur in EFM25 because its larger droplets are more susceptible to flocculation than smaller droplets. In contrast, EFM50 may exhibit greater storage stability due to its smaller oil droplets and the higher concentration of mucilage, which increases the viscosity of the continuous phase. This viscosity enhancement is associated with the increased solid content of the formulation and the inherently viscous nature of mucilage (Gohtani & Yoshii, 2018). Therefore, emulsion instability may occur in both formulations; however, it is expected to be more pronounced and to occur more rapidly in EFM25.

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of mayonnaise formulations were evaluated by oscillatory amplitude and frequency sweep tests. In the amplitude sweep test (Fig. 6), all samples exhibited nearly constant G′ and G″ values at low strain level which indicates a linear viscoelastic region (Kumar et al., 2025). By increasing strain more than 10%, G′ gradually decreased and the internal structure progressively weakened. All formulations showed higher G′ than G″ over the studied strain range. This confirms the predominance of elastic behavior and the weak gel-like character of the emulsions. Among the samples, M50 showed the highest G′ value, followed by M25 and EFM50. These samples therefore had more compact and stronger internal structures (Ozcan et al., 2023). In contrast, EFM25 exhibited lower G′ values and weaker viscoelastic networks than other mucilage containing samples. The G′ values declined noticeably for all samples at higher strain levels. This shows that the structure of mayonnaise formulations was less resistant to large deformation. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that increasing the concentration of hydrocolloids strengthens the viscoelastic network of mayonnaise systems. Akcicek et al. Texture profile analysis of mayonnaise

The textural attributes of the mayonnaise formulations are summarized in Table 4. Samples containing both egg and cactus mucilage (M25 and M50) were found to have higher hardness and adhesiveness than the control and the formulations containing only mucilage. The sample M50 formulated with 3% egg and 3% mucilage was characterized by the highest hardness (102 ± 3.77 g), indicative of a strong emulsion microstructure (Fig. 5). The control and EFM25 samples showed comparable textural characteristics. In egg-free formulations, hardness increased significantly with higher mucilage levels (1.5–3%). Moreover, Egg-free mayonnaise samples (EFM25 and EFM50) presented higher cohesiveness than the control and samples containing a combination of egg and mucilage. In contrast, the combination of egg and mucilage markedly enhanced mayonnaise adhesiveness compared to the control. While the EFM25 sample showed similar adhesiveness to the control, increasing the mucilage concentration to 3% in EFM50 led to a significant rise in this parameter. These results suggest that cactus mucilage functions as an effective structural agent in egg-free mayonnaise by enhancing the internal resistance of the matrix. The incorporation of cactus mucilage into the aqueous phase elevates viscosity, thereby improving textural properties. This effect is consistent with previous reports indicating that the incorporation of mucilages or gums promotes the formation of a rigid gel-like network within the continuous phase, leading to a denser and more cohesive structure (Xu et al., 2025).

Heat stability

The heat stability of mayonnaise formulations is summarized in Table 4. Among the samples, M50 exhibited the highest heat stability, whereas EFM25 demonstrated the lowest. Formulations containing both mucilage and egg presented a more uniform and compact microstructure. The superior heat stability of M50 is likely attributed to the increased viscosity of the aqueous phase and the emulsifying properties of the egg. In contrast, EFM25 with the largest oil droplet size showed diminished resistance to heat treatment. The reduced stability can be attributed to the absence of egg and low concentration (1.5%) of mucilage in EFM25, resulting in decreased viscosity of the continuous phase and insufficient interfacial particle coverage. Capitani et al. (Capitani et al., 2016) reported that replacing egg with chia seed mucilage decreased the stability of the mayonnaise samples, while in the present study, it was shown that the addition of cactus mucilage enhanced the heat stability of mayonnaise formulations except in the EFM25 sample. The observed results of heat stability are correlated with the microstructure and texture characteristics of samples. Smaller particle size and increased viscosity restrict the mobility of oil molecules and prevent emulsion breakdown under elevated temperatures. Consequently, samples with smaller droplets and elevated viscosity demonstrate enhanced resistance under temperature changes and were more stable (Yildirim et al., 2016).

Sensory properties of mayonnaise

The sensory results are provided in Fig. 9. Partial replacement of egg with cactus mucilage at 25% and 50% levels (M25 and M50) exerted negligible effects on the aroma and taste profiles of mayonnaise samples. M25 and M50 samples had superior scores in overall acceptability and visual appearance compared to other formulations. Textural acceptability was highest in M25, followed by M50 and M50 was close to the control sample. In contrast, mayonnaise formulations with complete egg replacement (EFM25 and EFM50) received lower scores for appearance, color, texture, and overall acceptability relative to the control. Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the extraction method used to extract mucilage (microwave and solvent) significantly affects its yield, physicochemical and functional properties. Mucilage extracted by microwave showed higher extraction yield, purity, molecular weight, and water holding capacity compared to that extracted with water. Additionally, using peeled cladodes, particularly from O. ficus-indica, resulted in mucilage with higher purity. In the investigation of mucilage application in mayonnaise, formulations with 25% and 50% egg replacement (M25, M50) produced a more compact, uniform structure and retained sensory acceptability, whereas complete egg replacement led to lower scores for appearance, color, texture, and overall acceptability. Among these, M50 had the highest hardness and thermal stability and elastic strength, indicating the formation of a more compact viscoelastic network. These findings indicate that both cactus species and extraction method play key roles in determining mucilage functionality. This study provides new evidence that cactus mucilage can serve as a partial egg replacer in mayonnaise, allowing up to 50% substitution while maintaining acceptable textural and sensory properties. Although microwave extraction has been scientifically proven to be effective and promising, its economic feasibility needs further investigation. The need for microwave equipment, ethanol precipitation, and freeze-drying may increase processing costs, especially on an industrial scale. Therefore, future studies should address the techno-economic feasibility and scale-up of the process to assess the commercial applicability of this extraction method.
