Reported Cryptococcus neoformans heat-killing protocols show incomplete killing
Authors: Binyi An, Yingjun Gao, Yingying Wu, Yubing Wu, Xiaoyu Wang, Qingtao Kong, Hong Sang, Haibo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat killing is commonly employed to kill microorganisms, including Cryptococcus neoformans (C. neoformans), particularly in immunological studies and vaccine development. To assess the reliability of existing methods, we systematically reviewed 50 publications and identified 23 distinct protocols differing in temperature, duration, suspension concentration, and sample volume. Using two virulent strains, H99 and B3501, we experimentally tested these conditions and found that only 9 protocols achieved complete (100%) killing, whereas 14 resulted in partial killing. Consistent with this, only 17 publications (34%) reported complete killing, and just 20 studies described plating suspensions for validation, raising concerns that incomplete killing may have gone undetected. Further analysis revealed that suspension volume and fungal concentration critically influenced outcomes. These findings highlight substantial variability and insufficient validation across reported protocols, which may undermine reproducibility and biosafety. Our study provides evidence-based guidance for selecting effective heat-killing conditions, thereby supporting safer and more reliable use of heat-killed C. neoformans in experimental research.

Introduction

Cryptococcus neoformans is an encapsulated opportunistic fungal pathogen that is acquired mainly through inhalation and causes cryptococcal pneumonia and meningitis particularly in immunocompromised hosts (Maziarz and Perfect 2016, Chang et al. 2024). Cryptococcal disease remains a major global health burden, with recent estimates indicate that HIV-associated cryptococcal meningitis still causes approximately 152 000 cases and 112 000 deaths annually worldwide (Rajasingham et al. 2017, 2022). Because host–pathogen interactions strongly influence disease outcome, C. neoformans is widely used as a model for studying antifungal immunity (Campuzano and Wormley 2018, Nelson et al. 2020).

Killed C. neoformans is commonly used in immunological experiments to provide standardized stimulation without the confounding effects of fungal replication (Kelly et al. 2005). Accordingly, heat-killed C. neoformans has been used for cytokine profiling in human PBMCs, studies of dendritic-cell and phagocyte responses, and whole-cell vaccine development in animal models (Schoffelen et al. 2013, Upadhya et al. 2016, Wang et al. 2019).

Because different inactivation methods are not directly interchangeable, the choice and validation of killing conditions are important. UV inactivation may not ensure uniform killing of C. neoformans because of its measurable UV tolerance, especially in melanized cells (Wang and Casadevall 1994, Schiave et al. 2009). Chemical fixation raise greater concerns regarding alterations in fungal antigenic features (Ikeda-Dantsuji et al. 2015). Heat killing may also affect fungal cell properties, but it remains a commonly used approach. These considerations support the need to carefully validate heat-killing conditions.

In the present study, we hypothesized that published heat-killing protocols differ in their ability to achieve complete killing of C. neoformans. To test this, we reviewed published protocols and systematically evaluated their efficacy using two virulent strains, H99 and B3501, with the aim of defining more reliable conditions for complete heat killing of C. neoformans.

Materials and methods

Literature review

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, MEDLINE, EBSCO, and Web of Science through July 2025 using the terms (“Cryptococcus neoformans” OR “C. neoformans”) AND (“heat-killed” OR “heat-killing”). Additional references were identified by screening the citations of relevant studies. Articles were included if they reported specific heat-killing conditions for C. neoformans, including at least temperature and duration; information on suspension volume and concentration was also recorded when available.

Strains and culture conditions

Two virulent C. neoformans strains, H99 and B3501, were used. Strains were freshly recovered from −80°C stocks and plated on YPD agar (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) at 30°C for 3–4 days. A single colony was inoculated into 10 ml YPD broth and cultured with shaking at 30°C for 16–18 h. Cells were washed and resuspended in sterile PBS to 1 × 10⁶ or 1 × 104 CFU/ml based on colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration.

Heat-killing experiments

Aliquots (2 ml or 10 ml) were subjected to heat treatment at defined temperatures (55°C–120°C) and durations (2–120 min). Following treatment, suspensions were serially diluted and plated onto YPD agar using a plated volume of 100 µl per plate. Plates were incubated at 30°C, and colony-forming units (CFUs) were enumerated after 48 h. Plates were then monitored for up to 7 days to confirm complete killing, defined as no detectable colony growth on all replicate plates. Under these plating conditions, the approximate detection limit was 3.3 CFU/mL. Killing efficiency was calculated as [1 − (CFU_treated/CFU_untreated)] × 100%. Three independent experiments were performed, with each condition plated in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons were performed only for the prespecified volume- and inoculum-comparison experiments shown in Fig. 2, using unpaired two-tailed t-tests.

Results

Literature review of heat-killing protocols

A total of 50 publications reporting heat killing of C. neoformans were analyzed. The most frequently studied strains were H99, B3501, and 184A. Reported conditions varied widely, with temperatures from 55°C to 120°C and durations from 20 min to 17 h, yielding 23 distinct temperature–time combinations (Table 1). Verification of killing was inconsistently reported. Only 20 studies reported culture-based confirmation by plating. Among these 20 studies, the incubation period for culture-based confirmation was explicitly stated in only a minority of reports and ranged from 3 to 7 days. The remaining 30 studies did not mention confirmation, raising concerns that incomplete killing may have gone undetected.

Experimental evaluation of heat-killing efficiency

We tested the 23 conditions identified from the literature using H99 and B3501 (Table 2). For clarity, Fig. 1A–D presents only the conditions under which residual survival was detected. Replicate-level untreated CFU, treated CFU, and calculated killing rates are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Under these conditions, incubation at 55°C for up to 90 min resulted in kill rates of ∼85%–95%, with colonies persisting. At 60°C, viability decreased with longer exposure, but complete killing was not achieved even after 120 min. At 65°C and above, kill rates approached 100%, but residual survival was still detected under some conditions, including 70°C for 15 min and 75°C for 30 min. Across all protocols, 9 of 23 (39.1%) achieved complete killing, whereas 14 of 23 (60.9%) left residual viable colonies. Consistent with these findings, complete (100%) killing of C. neoformans (H99 or B3501) was documented in only 17 of the 50 included publications (34%). H99 and B3501 showed comparable heat susceptibility under the tested conditions.

We next examined whether suspension parameters influenced killing efficiency. Three representative conditions (55°C for 20 min, 55°C for 60 min, and 60°C for 60 min) were applied to suspensions of 10 ml at 1 × 10⁶ CFU/ml, 10 ml at 1 × 104 CFU/ml, and 2 ml at 1 × 104 CFU/ml. As shown in Fig. 2A–D, higher inoculum density and larger suspension volumes consistently reduced killing efficiency. Statistical comparisons further supported these effects, showing significant differences between selected inoculum-density and suspension-volume groups. For example, at 55°C for 60 min, 10 ml suspensions at 1 × 10⁶ CFU/ml retained visible colonies, whereas smaller-volume or lower-density suspensions showed little or no detectable residual growth.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that published heat-killing protocols for C. neoformans vary in their ability to achieve complete killing. Heat-killing efficacy depended not only on the reported temperature and duration, but also on experimental factors such as suspension volume and inoculum density. These findings help identify more reliable heat-killing conditions for C. neoformans.

This interpretation is consistent with the broader literature on microbial thermal inactivation. In bacteria and fungi, heat killing is generally understood to result from cumulative injury to multiple cellular targets, including membranes, proteins, enzymes, and ribosome-associated functions (Cebrián et al. 2017, Xiao et al. 2022). Therefore, microbial survival depends not only on nominal temperature and holding time, but also on the actual thermal exposure experienced by the cells (Huertas et al. 2016, Jimenez et al. 2023). The effects of larger suspension volumes and higher inoculum densities may be mechanistically related, as both increase the total fungal burden within the sample. In addition, a prominent polysaccharide capsule and thermotolerance-related stress responses may contribute to survival under suboptimal heat-killing conditions in C. neoformans, although these biological possibilities were not directly tested in the present study (Zaragoza et al. 2008, Bloom et al. 2019).

These findings have important practical implications. Incomplete killing may compromise both experimental reproducibility and biosafety, as residual viable cells could alter host responses and remain a potential laboratory hazard. Under the conditions tested here, no detectable colony growth was observed after treatment at 75°C for 90 min and under selected higher-temperature protocols with shorter exposure times. We further suggest explicitly reporting suspension volume and inoculum density and confirming nonviability by culture-based methods with sufficient incubation time.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Only two strains and a single growth condition were tested, and capsule-deficient mutants were not included. All assays were conducted in PBS rather than in biologically complex matrices. Accordingly, complete killing should be interpreted as no detectable viable growth under the assay conditions used. In addition, not all temperatures were tested across equivalent time ranges, which limited comprehensive statistical comparisons across temperature–time conditions.

In conclusion, heat killing of C. neoformans is highly condition-dependent. Explicit validation and transparent reporting of heat-killing protocols for C. neoformans are therefore important to improve reproducibility and biosafety.
