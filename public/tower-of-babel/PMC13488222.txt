Septic shock in the operating room immediately after surgery: a case requiring differentiation from drug-induced anaphylaxis
Authors: Masaki Orihara, Tomonori Takazawa, Chigusa Nakasone, Tadanao Hiroki, Tatsuo Horiuchi, Kazuhiro Nagumo, Shigeru Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere are few reported cases of sepsis becoming apparent in the operating room, and even fewer where septic shock was misdiagnosed as anaphylaxis. Background

Diagnosing the cause of hypotension during anesthesia is sometimes challenging. In the face of unexpected hypotension, anesthesiologists must consider a wide range of potential conditions in the differential diagnosis [1]. Both sepsis and anaphylaxis are classified as forms of distributive shock. While sepsis is one of the most common causes of distributive shock, anaphylaxis is relatively rare. Consequently, emergency physicians may be inclined to initiate treatment for sepsis, and several cases of anaphylactic shock misdiagnosed as septic shock have been reported [2–4]. On the other hand, cases of septic shock misdiagnosed as anaphylaxis are rarely reported. Furthermore, although intraoperative sepsis has even been used as a simulation case for anesthesiology residents, there have been a few urological cases in which sepsis became apparent in the operating room, and reports involving other surgical disciplines are limited [5–7].

Here, we describe a case of septic shock during extubation immediately after elective abdominal surgery, which required differentiation from drug-induced anaphylaxis.

Case presentation

Written informed consent for publication of this report was obtained from the patient. A 72-year-old, 54-kg, 156-cm-tall woman with hepatocellular carcinoma underwent extended left lobectomy under combined general and epidural anesthesia. Her medical history included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hepatitis C. She had no previous history of surgery. Preoperative cardiac evaluation included a 12-lead electrocardiogram, which showed sinus rhythm at 80 beats per minute, and transthoracic echocardiography, which revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60–65% without regional wall motion abnormalities and grade I diastolic dysfunction.

Fifteen days before surgery, she underwent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) for biliary evaluation. During the procedure, sulbactam/ampicillin was initiated as prophylactic antibiotics. She developed a fever and a skin rash two days later. Therefore, twelve days before surgery, she was suspected of having cholangitis and was treated with endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD) and meropenem. A skin rash appeared during meropenem administration. Two sets of blood culture samples collected at that time were negative, and her white blood cell (WBC) count was 4.5 × 10³/µL, C-reactive protein (CRP) was 1.65 mg/dL, and procalcitonin was 0.19 ng/mL. Ten days before surgery, daptomycin was initiated due to an increase in CRP, which was 2.83 mg/dL. However, it was discontinued because a skin rash worsened. Meropenem was discontinued eight days before surgery after the patient’s fever had resolved and inflammatory markers had improved. On the day before surgery, inflammatory markers were nearly normalized, with a WBC count of 3.1 × 10³/µL, CRP of 0.17 mg/dL, and procalcitonin of 0.02 ng/mL.

At the time of surgery, following standard epidural catheter placement, general anesthesia was induced with propofol and rocuronium. Remifentanil infusion was started simultaneously. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane, remifentanil, and intermittent administration of rocuronium. Clindamycin was also administered as antibiotic prophylaxis. Intraoperative cholangiography was performed during the hepatic resection. The surgery was completed uneventfully in six hours. Sevoflurane and remifentanil were discontinued approximately five minutes after the end of surgery. Prior to endotracheal extubation, approximately one minute after intravenous administration of 200 mg sugammadex for reversal of neuromuscular blockade, her systolic blood pressure fell to approximately 70 mmHg. Continuous electrocardiographic monitoring showed sinus rhythm without arrhythmia or ST-segment changes. Since she showed no respiratory symptoms, she was extubated. However, her blood pressure did not recover despite receiving 12 mg of ephedrine, 0.3 mg of phenylephrine, continuous noradrenaline infusion at 0.1 µg/kg/min, and 850 mL of hydroxyethyl starch. Initially, postoperative bleeding or the potential contribution of epidural anesthesia was suspected as the cause of her hypotension. However, the appearance of generalized erythema during her initial management raised the suspicion of anaphylaxis. Consequently, 0.01 mg adrenaline was administered, and a continuous infusion of adrenaline 0.03 µg/kg/min was started (Fig. 2). The patient received 4,225 mL of fluids, including 2,375 mL of crystalloid solution and 1,850 mL of hydroxyethyl starch. Taking into account a blood loss of 193 mL and urine output of 500 mL, she had a positive fluid balance of 3,532 mL. She was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU), where the adrenaline infusion was continued.

Upon admission to the ICU, her sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score was 7, and her blood lactate level was elevated to 3.9 mmol/L (Table 1, Supplementary Table 1). Her procalcitonin level also increased the following day. Based on a preoperative bile culture that identified Klebsiella pneumoniae, treatment with levofloxacin (LVFX) was initiated. Suspecting septic shock, two sets of blood culture samples were collected after her admission to the ICU, all of which grew Klebsiella pneumoniae. The patient had a diagnostic score of 6 for acute disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) by the Japanese Association for Acute Medicine (Supplementary Table 1) [8]. She was treated for sepsis and discharged from the ICU on postoperative day 9, and was subsequently discharged from the hospital on postoperative day 22 (Fig. 3). During the acute event and ICU stay, echocardiography was not performed.

A retrospective analysis of her laboratory data was performed. Serum tryptase levels measured at 30 min, 2 h and 24 h after the initial reaction were within normal limits, at 2.5, 3.1 and 1.9 µg/L, respectively. Serum histamine levels at the same time points were also within normal limits, at 0.35, 0.40 and 0.23 ng/mL, respectively. According to the consensus clinical scoring system [9], this resulted in a total score of 8 (12 points from symptoms minus 4 points based on serum tryptase level), which indicates a “possible immediate hypersensitivity reaction”.

Fifty days after the event, skin tests performed with all agents suspected of causing anaphylaxis were negative (Table 2). Approximately seven months later, she uneventfully underwent rectal resection under general anesthesia with no restrictions on medication, including the use of sugammadex.

Saline and histamine were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. Skin prick tests (SPTs) were followed by intradermal tests (IDTs). Both SPTs and IDTs were initiated with a 100-fold dilution of the stock solution; a 10-fold dilution and undiluted stock solution were subsequently tested. SPT reactions were considered positive when the diameter of the wheal was at least 3 mm greater than that of the negative control, or at least half the size of the positive control after 20 min. IDT reactions were considered positive when the wheal diameter was at least twice that of the initial post-injection wheal after 20 min [10].

Discussion and conclusions

We report a case of postoperative hypotension initially suspected to be drug-induced anaphylaxis but ultimately diagnosed as septic shock. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of distinguishing between anaphylaxis and sepsis in the perioperative period, particularly when repeated skin rashes during preoperative antibiotic administration may bias the diagnostic impression toward an allergic reaction.

Diagnosing perioperative anaphylaxis can be difficult for several reasons: the patient is unconscious, many of the drugs administered perioperatively affect circulation and respiration, and surgery itself has physiological effects [1, 11]. Although clinical scoring systems for diagnosing anaphylaxis are helpful, a definitive diagnosis of perioperative anaphylaxis relies on a triad including clinical signs, biochemical assessment, and allergology testing [9, 12]. In this case, despite the presence of hypotension and generalized erythema, there was no biochemical or allergology evidence to support a diagnosis of anaphylaxis; serum tryptase and histamine levels were not elevated, and skin tests were negative [13].

On the other hand, the patient had several risk factors for sepsis, including a recent history of cholangitis and positive preoperative bile cultures. Intraoperative cholangiography involves biliary manipulation and transient elevation of intrabiliary pressure and may therefore act as a precipitating factor for bacteremia or recurrent cholangitis in susceptible patients [14, 15]. Metabolic abnormalities suggestive of early sepsis, characterized by a negative shift in base excess and rising lactate levels, were already present before the clinical onset of hypotension. Although lactate elevation after ICU admission may partly reflect type B lactic acidosis associated with epinephrine administration, the lactate level had already increased prior to epinephrine treatment, suggesting that additional factors such as evolving sepsis or intraoperative metabolic stress may also have contributed [16]. Thus, although hypotension became clinically apparent after extubation, the patient was likely already in a pre-septic state during surgery, with overt manifestation possibly influenced by the stress of surgery and extubation. However, the possibility that the intraoperative metabolic abnormalities were influenced by low central venous pressure management, which is commonly applied during hepatic surgery should be considered [17, 18]. The subsequent elevation of the SOFA score and positive blood cultures for Klebsiella pneumoniae strongly supported a diagnosis of septic shock [19]. The observed erythema might be attributable to sepsis-induced vasodilation rather than a hypersensitivity reaction.

In addition, the potential contribution of mast cell priming associated with sepsis may also be considered. Gram-negative bacteremia, including Klebsiella pneumoniae, produces lipopolysaccharide, which can induce mast cell activation and enhance responsiveness to additional stimuli [20, 21]. Furthermore, inflammatory cytokines and complement activation during sepsis may augment mast cell responses to receptor-coupled mediators or non-immunologic triggers, including drugs [22, 23]. It is possible that sepsis-related mast cell priming increased susceptibility to additional triggers, and that sugammadex administration coincided with or contributed to mediator release in this primed state. Therefore, although septic shock was considered the primary diagnosis, the possibility that sugammadex-induced mast cell mediator release occurred in the context of sepsis-related mast cell priming cannot be completely excluded.

The detailed perioperative course, serial laboratory data, hemodynamic changes, and follow-up allergologic evaluation provide practical insights into diagnostic challenges encountered in clinical practice. This case underscores the need for careful evaluation of the potential contributions of both septic shock and drug-related mast cell activation. These observations may contribute to improved recognition and management of perioperative hypotension through a multifactorial diagnostic approach.

Notably, the patient later underwent another surgery under general anesthesia, during which the same agents were used without any adverse events. This finding supports that septic shock was the most plausible explanation for the initial episode, although a drug-related reaction cannot be completely excluded. Takotsubo syndrome was also considered as a possible differential diagnosis; however, the overall clinical course was less consistent with stress cardiomyopathy.

This case highlights the importance of including sepsis in the differential diagnosis of perioperative hypotension, particularly in patients with risk factors such as recent infections or biliary tract procedures. A definitive diagnosis requires a comprehensive assessment that integrates clinical signs, laboratory markers, microbiological data, and follow-up allergology testing.

In conclusion, we presented a case that initially mimicked drug-induced anaphylaxis, but was subsequently determined to be septic shock. This serves as a crucial reminder for clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion for septic shock in the perioperative setting, even when the initial presentation strongly suggests an allergic reaction.
