Development of a cost-effective end-to-end commissioning test for simulation-omitted direct-to-unit adaptive radiotherapy
Authors: Da Wang, Justin Visak, Sean Domal, Zhisheng Tong, Viktor Iakovenko, Tsuicheng Chiu, Andrew Godley, David Parsons, Mu-Han Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeDirect-to-Unit (simulation-omitted) adaptive radiotherapy (ART) enables same-day treatment by generating treatment plans from diagnostic images, eliminating the need for computed tomography (CT) simulation. These workflows rely on online plan re-optimization to account for anatomical, CT-number, and setup variations. However, no standardized end-to-end (E2E) credentialing protocol currently exists to validate the accuracy of Direct-to-Unit ART. Although customized ART phantoms are available, they are often costly, and impractical for one-time commissioning. To address this gap, we developed a low-cost E2E commissioning framework using a commercially available CIRS ZEUS phantom to verify workflow accuracy while minimizing financial burden.Materials and MethodsE2E commissioning tests were performed on Varian Ethos and Elekta Unity systems using a CIRS ZEUS phantom (Model 008Z) equipped with inserts for ionization chamber point-dose and planar film measurements. The ionization chamber and Gafchromic EBT4 films were independently cross-calibrated on an Elekta Versa linear accelerator. Pseudo–diagnostic CT datasets were generated and deformed to simulate anatomical variation. Pre-plans (2 Gy × 30 fractions) were created and delivered across three ART fractionation schemes. Point-dose measurements were compared with system-reported values, and planar dose distributions were evaluated using global gamma analysis.ResultsAll point-dose differences were within 3% (for high-dose regions) or 3 cGy (for low-dose regions) of the system-reported values. All planar film measurements achieved gamma pass rates exceeding 90% using 3%/3 mm criterion.ConclusionThis cost-effective E2E approach provides a practical commissioning solution for Direct-to-Unit ART, supporting safe clinical implementation and facilitating cross-institutional standardization.

Introduction

Direct-to-Unit (simulation-omitted) radiotherapy (DTU) represents a transformative advancement in clinical workflow, enabling treatment planning to begin directly from diagnostic imaging and eliminating the need for a separate computed tomography (CT) simulation session [1], [2]. By initiating pre-planning immediately after diagnostic image acquisition, DTU supports same-day consultation and treatment delivery, offering substantial advantages for urgent or time-sensitive cases [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. Adaptive radiotherapy (ART) platforms used for DTU [9], [10], [11] provide online plan re-optimization to reconcile discrepancies between diagnostic and on-board treatment imaging. These systems dynamically accommodate CT-number differences, anatomical changes, and setup variations [12], [13], [14], [15], enabling preservation of planning objectives despite the additional uncertainty inherent to DTU workflows. Unlike conventional online ART (oART), where adaptation is performed from a simulation CT representing the treatment anatomy, DTU planning is generated from diagnostic imaging and therefore lacks a fully simulated baseline treatment geometry. As a result, DTU workflows introduce additional uncertainty in anatomy, CT calibration, and patient setup prior to online adaptation, making robust online adaptation becomes crucial [2].

Given these unique uncertainties, End-to-End (E2E) validation of DTU workflows must assess more than dose delivery accuracy alone. It must also evaluate the ability of the adaptive system to reconcile discrepancies between the diagnostic pre-plan and the treatment-day anatomy through the online adaptation process. Specifically, the commissioning test should challenge the adaptive workflow with CT-number differences, anatomical variations, and setup discrepancies representative of those encountered in DTU implementation. Currently, no dedicated guidelines or commercially available tools exist for commissioning or credentialing DTU workflows. Consequently, there is a pressing need for an accessible, shareable, and reproducible commissioning process that can be implemented with commonly available phantoms.

In alignment with AAPM MPPG reports [16], [17], institutions are strongly encouraged to conduct independent E2E credentialing and commissioning tests when implementing any new clinical workflow. While patient-specific QA [18], [19] remains indispensable for daily safety checks, it is inherently limited in detecting subtle or systematic deviations between planned and delivered dose [20]. E2E credentialing and commissioning fill this gap by providing comprehensive verification across the entire planning-to-delivery chain, identifying workflow inconsistencies, software limitations, or latent failure modes that routine QA may overlook [16], [21]. Despite its importance, E2E commissioning has not yet been widely adopted for DTU ART workflows, leaving a significant unmet need.

Although the Imaging and Radiation Oncology Core (IROC) offers credentialing programs for IMRT, VMAT, and other advanced techniques [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], no analogous program currently exists for adaptive or DTU radiotherapy. Custom phantoms designed for ART credentialing are available, but these are often expensive and difficult to justify when used primarily for single-time commissioning.

To bridge this gap, we developed a cost-effective and practical commissioning method for DTU ART using the widely available CIRS ZEUS phantom (model 008Z) [29], [30]. This approach enables institutions to perform rigorous verification of DTU workflows without specialized equipment, providing a reproducible framework that enhances confidence in treatment accuracy, workflow robustness, and patient safety. In this work, we further demonstrate the applicability of this framework across different adaptive platforms, including cone beam CT (CBCT)-guided workflows with HU-appropriate CBCT direct dose calculation and MR-guided workflows utilizing a density override–based dose calculation approach, thereby supporting the generalizability of the proposed method.

Materials and methods

System and materials

The ZEUS phantom includes a slotted film insert for planar dosimetry and a chamber insert compatible with both an A16 micro-ionization chamber and a PinPoint 3D MR ionization chamber (PTW 31025) for point-dose measurements. Gafchromic EBT4 films (Ashland Inc.) were used to evaluate planar dose distributions. A brief cross-calibration was conducted on an Elekta C-arm Versa machine for both the A16 chamber and the film batch against an accredited dosimetry calibration laboratory (ADCL)-traceable PTW Semiflex chamber (0.3 cc TN31013). A TG-51 6 MV calibration setup was used to ensure consistency across all measurements [31], [32]. The PinPoint 3D MR ionization chamber was independently calibrated by an ADCL. For film processing, an optical density (OD)–to–dose calibration method was employed [32]. Even within the same batch of EBT4 films, we found that the mean background among films varies by up to approximately 1000 pixel-values, corresponding to approximately a 10% (20 cGy) systematic dose difference in our DTU commissioning workflow. To reduce uncertainties associated with film-to-film background variation and polymerization response, a subset of films with minimal background deviation was selected for the E2E testing. Each film was scanned both before and at least 24 hours after irradiation to ensure full development, and OD was calculated to normalize residual background differences and improve the accuracy of planar dosimetry [33], [34], [35].

Digital phantom creation and pre-planning

Digital phantoms were constructed by contouring all structures present in the physical ZEUS phantom and assigning each region its corresponding mean CT number (Fig. 1a-b). To simulate geometric variability between diagnostic CT and on-board CBCT or MRI, a controlled, clinically realistic deformation was applied to the digital phantom before planning, as shown in Fig. 1a. a) Deformed digital phantom mimicking a diagnostic CT and cross-platform variation. b) An extensively deformed digital phantom. ITV encompasses targets of c) sagittal film insert, d) coronal film insert and e) Ionization chamber insert.

The internal target volume (ITV) was created as the Boolean union of the two tumor inserts in different orientations (vertical and horizontal, shown in Fig. 1c-d). This ITV is sufficient to encompass the point-dose measurement location within the chamber insert (Fig. 1e). The ITV was assigned a uniform density override of 270 Hounsfield units (HU). During each oART session, the ITV—along with the three individual tumor structures—was propagated [15], [36], [37], [38] and rigidly aligned to the corresponding insert-specific target to ensure reproducibility of target shape and position throughout the DTU commissioning workflow, as illustrated in Fig. 1c–e. A planning target volume (PTV) was generated with a 5 mm isotropic and 1 cm superior–inferior expansion for CBCT-based ART systems [39]. For the Magnetic Resonance Imaging-guided ART (MRIgART) system, a 3 mm isotropic expansion was used in accordance with our institutional protocol.

Pre-plans (Fig. 2) were generated on this digital phantom using a 14-field IMRT technique in the Ethos 2.0 treatment planning system (TPS), and a 13-field IMRT technique in the Monaco TPS. Both pre-plans were normalized such that 95% of the PTV received the prescription dose (2 Gy × 30 fractions), consistent with institutional lung planning guidelines and IROC lung credentialing objectives. The planning goals and their priority rankings within the Ethos planning system are summarized in supplementary Table S1. In accordance with IROC credentialing requirements, the PTV objectives included V60 Gy ≥ 95%, D99% ≥ 57 Gy, and all hotspots confined entirely within the PTV (evaluated by no dose exceeding 105% of the prescription dose spilling outside PTV, i.e. Dmax(Ring0mm) ≤ 6300 cGy), throughout the DTU commissioning process [39]. In the Monaco TPS, the density overrides were assigned according to the electron density (ED) as detailed in supplementary Table S2. The CIRS phantom housing consists of a 1.2 cm-thick plastic wall with a density of 1.1 g/cm3. Online adaptive treatment sessions

During each oART session, standard clinical workflows were followed on both the Ethos system equipped with HyperSight CBCT and the Elekta Unity systems, including phantom setup, structure re-contouring, and plan re-optimization [40]. On the Ethos system, failure to meet certain planning objectives—such as Dmax(Ring0mm) ≤ 6300 cGy—was observed when the treatment isocenter deviated substantially from the PTV isocenter defined in the pre-plan. Therefore, appropriate couch shifts following the initial CBCT are critical to ensure that the adaptive plan meets all dose guidance. The Ethos system generates two deliverable plans: a scheduled plan, which is based on the original digital pre-plan, and an adaptive plan, which is re-optimized using the anatomy from the session-specific CBCT [41], [42], [43], [44], [45]. The CBCT datasets were reconstructed with a matrix size of 512 × 512, an in-plane resolution of 1.05 × 1.05 mm2, and a slice thickness of 2 mm. All treatment plans were generated using 2 mm3 dose calculation grid. From a practical standpoint in simulation-omitted workflows, scheduled plans are unlikely to be selected for clinical delivery. Accordingly, only adaptive plans were delivered and evaluated during DTU commissioning tests. We defer to individual institutions to determine whether inclusion of scheduled plan evaluation is necessary within their commissioning process. A total of six fractions were delivered on each system: three using pre-plans based on a large-deformation digital phantom and three using a minor-deformation digital phantom (Fig. 3). To avoid imaging dose contributions on the Ethos system, a dummy film was used during CBCT acquisition, and measurement films were inserted immediately prior to irradiation. Dosimetric evaluation

Point doses were measured using the A16 and PinPoint chambers positioned in the chamber insert. Measurements included PTV point dose and organs of interest point doses in the spinal cord and liver. Measured doses were compared directly with the TPS-reported plan dose for each session. Planar films were placed in both coronal and sagittal orientations for each ART session, resulting in eight film measurements. Films were analyzed using global gamma criterion of 3%/3 mm as the primary endpoint, with a 10% low-dose exclusion threshold. Additional results using 5%/3 mm are also reported [19]. Machine output was properly accounted in the film processing. Because the Ethos system does not currently export planar dose maps, all dose planes were transferred to Eclipse and exported as 15.24 cm × 15.24 cm, 75 dpi dose grids for gamma comparison. Film and TPS dose maps were rigidly aligned using translation-only registration. A complete summary of all data acquisition and sequencing is presented in Table 1. Across all DTU oART sessions, a comprehensive set of dosimetric measurements was acquired. For our three-fraction delivery strategy, the first fraction was used for point-dose verification within the PTV. Results

Point dose measurements were performed at three anatomical locations: the target (PTV), liver, and spinal cord, as summarized in Table 1. Although the CIRS phantom includes a point measurement site within the kidney structure, this region lies outside the axial planes of the PTV and was therefore excluded from our analysis. All measured point doses were within 3% (for high-dose points) or 3 cGy (for low-dose points) of the TPS calculated values, demonstrating excellent agreement.

An example from a coronal-plane film exposure during an ART session is shown in Fig. 4a-b, illustrating the TPS dose distribution (Fig. 4c), exposed film (Fig. 4d), and gamma analysis map (Fig. 4e). Line profiles across the dose plane are presented in Fig. 4f. According to IROC standards, a gamma pass rate exceeding 85% using a 7%/4 mm criterion is required. In our study, all eight planar dose measurements exceeded a 90% pass rate across various gamma criteria. Notably, our results meet IROC benchmarks by achieving a 98% + pass rate using the more stringent 5%/3 mm gamma criterion. Detailed gamma pass rates for all measurements are provided in Table 1. No additional post-processing, such as mathematical smoothing or dose normalization, was applied to the film measurements beyond our proposed quality control (QC) of film background consistency, film processing, and OD-to-dose conversion. Discussion

This study presents a novel and scalable commissioning framework for Direct-to-Unit adaptive radiotherapy, addressing an important gap in validating systems for simulation-omitted workflows. The central innovation lies in the digital–physical phantom transformation: a deformed digital phantom is used for pre-planning to emulate diagnostic-image variability, while the physical ZEUS phantom undergoes online adaptation from a deliberately perturbed setup and pre-planning. This design creates a controlled yet realistic surrogate and an extensive stress test for the geometric and CT-number mismatches inherent to DTU treatments, enabling an E2E evaluation of whether an adaptive radiotherapy system can generate clinically reliable plans without conventional simulation CT. We acknowledge that multiple DTU workflow implementations exist, including approaches using solid water with contour placeholders [1] and inter-patient ART [2]. For such workflows, we recommend substituting the proposed deformed digital phantom with the institution-specific dataset used for pre-planning, thereby enabling tailored validation of DTU processes.

The primary purpose of the proposed E2E testing for simulation-omitted workflows on adaptive platforms is to verify that the system can consistently generate clinically acceptable plans and support safe implementation of DTU ART. Through this evaluation, we identified several practical workflow considerations. For example, in an early E2E phantom test, we encountered CBCT truncation due to suboptimal isocenter placement. This truncation impacted dose calculation and resulted in significant underdosing, leading to failure in film dosimetry. In addition, suboptimal isocenter positioning contributed to the inability to meet key planning objectives. In another instance, suboptimal beam arrangement in the pre-plan led to discrepancies in ionization chamber measurements. These findings informed refinements to our general planning approach for simulation-omitted cases to improve robustness. These observations emphasize the importance of optimizing gantry angles, beam isocenter placement, planning template robustness [2], [46], [47], [48], and CBCT field of view to ensure accurate dose delivery in DTU ART. Overall, these results demonstrate the value of E2E testing in identifying practical workflow considerations and strengthening confidence in clinical implementation.

We demonstrated this approach using the ZEUS phantom in a thoracic-like configuration. The default phantom geometry represents a typical planning scenario without critical organs of interest adjacent to the target and is suitable for 3D-CRT, IMRT, or VMAT. For more complex cases (e.g., high modulation or sharp dose gradients), users can introduce dummy organs of interest to mimic the desired geometry and modulation. This study provides a foundational method, which can be further customized based on clinical needs. Importantly, the proposed framework should be viewed as a commissioning methodology rather than a workflow-specific solution. Institutions may adapt the digital phantom component to reflect their own DTU implementation, including diagnostic-image-based planning datasets, library-based planning approaches, synthetic placeholder datasets, or planning anatomies derived from a different phantom than the treatment phantom, while preserving the same E2E commissioning principles. The main limitation is that the phantom heterogeneity cannot be easily modified without changing the phantom plug. This can be addressed by using 3D-printed plugs to introduce additional heterogeneity and further stress the system. Nevertheless, the proposed framework is inherently extensible across anatomical sites. The digital phantom can be re-segmented and deformed to reflect the characteristic uncertainties of spinal, abdominal, pelvic, or head-and-neck regions, while the physical phantom remains unchanged. A key strength of this approach is the ability to incorporate 3D-printed, site-specific inserts [49]. With modern additive manufacturing, institutions can fabricate custom structures with defined geometry and density to reproduce clinically relevant challenges—such as lung–soft tissue transitions, bowel gas variability, prostate–bladder deformation, or pediatric-sized targets. By combining multiple insert configurations, institutions can systematically stress test the robustness of DTU workflows through multi-fraction oART delivery schemes incorporating variations in target shape, target location, proximity to organs of interest, and setup conditions. Across all fractions, the DTU workflow should consistently meet planning objectives and be verified using ionization chamber and film measurements. These printed inserts can be easily exchanged among centers or archived digitally, enabling multicenter benchmarking and fostering a reproducible, standardized approach to DTU commissioning. This modularity means that a single base phantom, such as ZEUS, can support commissioning across multiple disease sites without the need for purchasing dedicated commercial phantoms.

The practicality of the workflow is another advantage. The ZEUS phantom is already common in many clinics and provides compatibility with both film and ionization chamber measurements. Because the digital phantom manipulation does not require physical modification, the same physical phantom may be shared across institutions, with only digital datasets or 3D-printed inserts varying by site or use case. This design supports cross-institutional reproducibility—an essential step toward developing community-wide expectations for DTU readiness.

Together, the digital deformation strategy, film- and chamber-based dosimetry, and compatibility with customizable 3D-printed inserts form a comprehensive and accessible commissioning framework for DTU workflows. By reproducing the full diagnostic-to-treatment pathway while introducing realistic uncertainty, this method provides a rigorous yet practical mechanism for assessing system readiness prior to clinical deployment. As DTU workflows continue to expand—particularly for urgent, same-day, or palliative treatments—this commissioning approach offers a scalable foundation for safe, reproducible, and widely adoptable implementation across diverse treatment sites.
