Assessing land use and carbon stock dynamics coupled with the PLUS-InVEST-OMGD model: a multi-scenario simulation in Sichuan Province, China
Authors: Lin Zhong, Yuxuan Ma, Fanlei Meng, Dexin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting future carbon stock dynamics under different development scenarios is crucial for balancing regional economic growth with ecological protection and achieving sustainable development. Taking Sichuan Province as a case study, this study established a framework combining PLUS-InVEST-OMGD (Optimal Multivariate-Stratification Geographical Detector) model to assess changes in carbon stock and their driving factors from 2000 to 2020, and to predict carbon stock for 2040 and 2060 under three scenarios: the Natural Trend Scenario (NTS), Economic Priority Scenario (EPS), and Ecological Conservation Scenario (ECS). The results show the following: (1) Between 2000 and 2020, the carbon stock in Sichuan Province decreased by 32.61 × 106 t, with the areas of decline primarily concentrated in the central and southern parts of the Sichuan Basin. (2) NDVI (q = 0.276) emerged as a critical factor influencing spatial variation in carbon stocks and showed the most significant interactive effect with elevation (q = 0.399). (3) Compared with 2020 levels, carbon stocks under the NTS and EPS were projected to continue declining between 2040 and 2060. In contrast, carbon stocks under the ECS were expected to exhibit an increasing trend, with an additional increase of 44.31 × 106 t by 2060. This study offers critical insights into optimizing China’s spatial land-use patterns to achieve its “dual carbon” strategy objectives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-63470-7.Keywords: Land use change, Carbon stock, Multi-scenario prediction, Optimal multivariate-stratification geographical detector modelSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Excessive emissions of greenhouse gases, primarily carbon dioxide from human activities, have contributed significantly to global warming1. If urban temperature increases continue at the current rate, environmental issues such as the heat island effect and extreme weather events will occur more frequently, posing serious threats to the sustainable development of human society and the natural environment2,3. In this context, China, as the world’s largest emitter of carbon dioxide, has committed to peaking carbon emissions by 2030 and achieving carbon neutrality by 20604. Quantifying and modeling ecosystem carbon stock under multiple scenarios holds great practical significance for addressing climate change and advancing the objectives of the “dual carbon” strategy5.

Carbon stocks in terrestrial ecosystems constitute a vital component of the global carbon pool and play a critical role in regulating the global carbon cycle and mitigating climate change6,7. Their primary function is to reduce atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations through carbon sequestration, thereby alleviating global warming8. However, land-use change has substantially altered the spatial distribution patterns of terrestrial carbon sources and sinks. Numerous studies have demonstrated that land use and land cover (LULC) change significantly impacts regional carbon stock and has become the second-largest global source of carbon emissions after fossil fuel combustion9. Rapid urbanization further constrains the recovery of carbon stock to its original levels, and this process is often regulated by geomorphological conditions10. Moreover, landscape change influences carbon stock by modifying material circulation and energy flow within ecosystems. Therefore, investigating changes in land-use patterns is of considerable practical significance for enhancing ecosystem carbon sequestration capacity and mitigating global climate change11,12.

In recent years, methods for increasing carbon stocks through the optimization of land use patterns have received considerable attention from researchers. To reasonably estimate carbon stock, three main approaches have been employed: field surveys, remote sensing inversion, and model simulations13. However, field surveys require long sampling periods and limit applicability over large spatial scales; the remote sensing inversion method often suffers from high estimation errors and low inversion accuracy14,15. With advances in science and technology, model simulation has become increasingly popular due to its efficiency and ease of implementation. For example, Luo et al.16 applied the FLUS-InVEST model to explore changes in carbon stocks under multiple objectives, using Lintao County as a case study, and provided a robust scientific basis for its future land use planning. Similarly, Jiang et al.17 integrated the CLUE-S and InVEST models to simulate various scenarios, including Natural Increase, Cultivated Protection, and Ecological Protection, to predict potential changes in carbon stocks within the Changsha–Zhuzhou–Xiangtan urban agglomeration. Nevertheless, the aforementioned land use models lack flexible mechanisms to address multiple land use patch transformations in large-scale simulations. They are unable to capture land use changes at an acceptable spatial resolution18. The PLUS model, however, can dynamically track the evolution of multiple land use patches through an advanced land use analysis strategy19. Compared to other models, the InVEST and PLUS models have been widely applied to predict and assess the spatiotemporal dynamics of land cover and carbon stocks under various development scenarios due to their accessibility to data and strong spatial visualization capabilities20,21.

A comprehensive investigation of carbon stock drivers using robust modeling approaches is essential for the rational regulation of carbon stock patterns. Zhi et al.22 employed the geodetector model in the context of rapid urban expansion and industrial transformation, concluding that natural factors were the primary determinants of carbon stock in the Harbin–Changchun urban cluster. Satdichanh et al.23 applied Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to examine factors influencing soil organic carbon sequestration during tropical forest succession in Southeast Asia. Their findings revealed that increases in soil organic carbon stocks were indirectly and positively correlated with species diversity, mediated by functional trait diversity and succession age. Lai et al.24 utilized the CatBoost model to quantitatively assess potential drivers of spatial carbon stock patterns on Hainan Island, demonstrating that natural factors predominantly shape spatial heterogeneity. Although substantial progress has been made in identifying these drivers, these models still face limitations in quantifying their explanatory power for the spatial heterogeneity of geographical phenomena and in reducing subjectivity. Although the Optimal Parameter-based Geographical Detector (OPGD) model addresses the above limitations, further improvements are needed in the flexibility of spatial scale optimization and hierarchical classification selection. As an enhanced version of the OPGD framework, the Optimal Multivariate-Stratification Geographical Detector (OMGD) model integrates five novel clustering-based hierarchical analysis methods with five univariate analysis approaches25, thereby providing greater flexibility for automated parameter optimization. This integration enhances the explanatory power of the variables and enables a more comprehensive evaluation of both the main and interaction effects of influencing factors.

As the core area of the Southwest Forest Region, which is the second largest natural forest region in China, Sichuan Province serves as a vital ecological barrier in the upper reaches of the Yangtze and Yellow Rivers. Its advantageous geographic position and favorable climatic conditions endow its forests with a strong capacity to act as a carbon sink. However, as urban expansion and population growth increasingly disrupt natural ecosystems, the ecological security challenges associated with future land-use demands in Sichuan are becoming more severe. Against this backdrop, investigating the spatiotemporal patterns of historical and future carbon stocks, while promoting ecological conservation and restoration initiatives to enhance carbon sequestration capacity, is essential for achieving Sichuan’s “dual-carbon” goals. This study integrated the PLUS and InVEST models to develop a comprehensive simulation framework for predicting future changes in carbon stocks under three scenarios: the Natural Trend Scenario (NTS), Economic Priority Scenario (EPS), and Ecological Conservation Scenario (ECS). In addition, the OMGD model was employed to identify the key factors influencing the spatiotemporal distribution of carbon stocks. The results provide valuable insights for optimizing regional land resource allocation, strengthening ecological and environmental management, and informing the formulation of sustainable socio-economic development policies.

Material and methods

Overview of the study area

Sichuan Province, located in southwestern China at the upper reaches of the Yangtze River (97°22′–102°29′E, 27°58′–34°20′N) (Fig. 1), covers an area of approximately 486,000 km2, ranking fifth in the country in terms of size. The terrain was characterized by high elevations in the west and lowlands in the east. The western region is dominated by highlands and mountains, exhibiting pronounced climatic gradients and vertical zonation. The southwest experiences a subtropical, semi-humid climate, whereas the northwest has a high-altitude, alpine climate. The eastern region consists primarily of hills and basins, characterized by a subtropical monsoon humid climate, which is favorable for agriculture and human settlement. The province is also rich in energy resources, including hydropower, coal, and natural gas.

Location and topography of the study area. The map was generated using ArcGIS Pro 3.4.3 (https://www.esri.com). The map has been reviewed and approved under the national map review number GS (2024) 0650, ensuring compliance with China’s cartographic regulations.

By 2024, Sichuan’s resident population reached 83.64 million, while its gross domestic product (GDP) accounted for 4.8% of the national total, ranking fifth nationwide. Over the past 20 years, driven by rapid economic development, Sichuan’s land use structure has undergone a substantial transformation: construction land and water area have continued to expand, whereas arable land, forest land, grassland, and unused land have remained stagnant or declined. Consequently, ecological land faces dual pressures from metropolitan expansion and plateau ecological protection. The complex interactions among population mobility, industrial upgrading, and spatial planning make Sichuan an exemplary case for investigating land use change under the coupled mechanisms of urbanization and ecological security in central and western China.

Data sources

The land use data employed in this study were obtained from the Resource and Environmental Science Data Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (RESDC, https://www.resdc.cn/). The land use raster maps had a spatial resolution of 30 m, and datasets from three time periods (2000, 2010, and 2020) were selected. The land cover types were reclassified into six categories: arable land, forest land, grassland, water area, construction land, and unused land.

The rational selection of driving factors was crucial for enhancing the accuracy of future land use predictions. Drawing on previous studies, 13 indicators were selected and grouped into three categories: natural, economic, and social factors. The natural factors included elevation (ELE), slope (SL), annual mean precipitation (AMP), annual mean temperature (AMT), annual mean evapotranspiration (AME), and normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI). The economic factors comprised nighttime light (NL), population (POP), and GDP. The social factors included distance to highway (DTH), distance to Class I road (DTC), distance to government (DTG), and distance to water (DTW).

PLUS model

The PLUS model simulates future LULC changes based on existing land use types. It integrates a rule-mining framework derived from the Land Expansion Analysis Strategy (LEAS) and a Cellular Automata model based on multiple Random Seeds (CARS)29–31.

This study incorporated 13 driving factors encompassing both natural and social elements (Fig. 3). Using baseline data from 2000 and 2020, parameters such as land use demand and neighborhood weights were configured to simulate the spatial distribution of land use types in Sichuan Province for the years 2040 and 2060. To achieve optimal simulation performance, the neighborhood weight coefficients were calibrated based on the proportion of each land use type’s expansion area to the total expansion area from 2010 to 2020, as follows: 0.245 for arable land, 0.351 for forest land, 0.275 for grassland, 0.029 for water area, 0.063 for construction land, and 0.036 for unused land.

Driving factors affecting land use change (taking the driving factors of 2020 as an example). The map was generated using ArcGIS Desktop 10.8 (https://www.esri.com).

The PLUS model was applied to simulate land use in Sichuan Province for 2020, and the results were compared with actual land use data for the same year. The model’s applicability and reliability were evaluated using the Kappa coefficient and overall accuracy. The simulation achieved a Kappa coefficient of approximately 0.876 and an overall accuracy of about 0.914, demonstrating that the model meets the reliability requirements for spatial simulation and can be confidently used to predict land use in Sichuan Province for 2040 and 2060.

Optimal multivariate-stratification geographical detector model

This study investigated the drivers of carbon stock distribution using the four components of the OMGD model, namely the scale detector, factor detector, optimization of factor discretization, and interaction detector.

Scale detector

Scale detector

The scale detector allows flexible adjustment of the q-value quantile according to predefined criteria. It calculates its magnitude under different spatial unit sizes to identify the optimal spatial scale.

(2)Optimization of factor discretization

Optimization of factor discretization

Optimization of factor discretization ensures the statistical significance of the results (p < 0.05) by calculating the p-values for each factor while simultaneously considering the q-values of factors. In addition to the five standard univariate stratification methods in the OPGD model, namely natural breaks (jnb), quantiles (qt), equal intervals (uni), geometric intervals (gmt), and standard deviation (std), five clustering-based stratification methods were introduced: K-means (kms), bisecting K-means (bkm), agglomerative clustering (agg), spectral clustering (spc), and Gaussian mixture model (gsm). These methods were employed to determine the optimal discretization scheme, with the discretization intervals set to a range of 3 to 7.

(3)Factor detector

Factor detector

The factor detector overlays the carbon stock layer with various factor layers to extract their spatial information, thereby quantifying the degree of influence of each factor on carbon stock. It also performs significance testing to assess the statistical significance of each factor’s effect on changes in carbon stock. The q-value for each factor is calculated as follows:

Here, q represents the explanatory power of each factor on carbon stock, with values ranging from 0 to 1. A larger q-value indicates a more substantial explanatory effect of that factor on carbon stock; L denotes the number of strata of the explanatory variable; is the number of observations in stratum h of the explanatory variable; represents the variance of the dependent variable in stratum h; N and denote the total number of observations and the overall variance of the dependent variable, respectively.

(4)Interaction detector

Interaction detector

The interaction detector is designed to examine the interactive effects between two variables under the optimal stratification scheme. Based on the q-value, the interaction type can be categorized as nonlinear weakening, univariate weakening, bivariate enhancement, independence, or nonlinear enhancement.

Results

Land use change and spatial and temporal distribution patterns in Sichuan Province

As shown in Fig. 4a, the spatial distribution of land use types in Sichuan Province exhibited clear regional differentiation. From west to east, grassland, forest land, and arable land constituted the dominant land use types, respectively. Water areas were primarily distributed east of the Jinsha River and in the southern part of the Daxue Mountains. Construction land was mainly concentrated in the Chengdu Plain (encompassing the cities of Mianyang, Deyang, and Chengdu), whereas unused land was primarily located in the Shaluli Mountains, the Daxue Mountains, and the northern region of the western Sichuan Plateau. The spatial distribution of land use in Sichuan Province was strongly influenced by socioeconomic development—regions with higher levels of development exhibited more pronounced urban expansion. Between 2000 and 2020, the conversion of arable land to construction land in central Chengdu was particularly significant, reflecting the gradual transition from an agricultural to an industrial economy.

(a) Land Use distribution in Sichuan Province over the past 20 years. (b) Proportional chord chart of the land use changes from 2000 to 2020. The map was generated using ArcGIS Desktop 10.8 (https://www.esri.com).

Figure 4b illustrates that, over the past 20 years, grassland has consistently occupied the largest proportion of land, accounting for 36.02% in 2000, 35.19% in 2010, and 35.10% in 2020, followed by forest land, which ranged from 34.09% to 34.78%. The areas of arable land and grassland exhibited a declining trend, decreasing by 3,411.89 km2 and 4,480.37 km2, respectively. Forest land, water areas, construction land, and unused land showed increasing trends, expanding by 3,561.31 km2, 907.04 km2, 3,272.38 km2, and 151.53 km2, respectively. Among all land use types, construction land experienced the most substantial change, with a growth rate reaching 110.06% relative to 2000.

From 2000 to 2020, Sichuan Province experienced significant changes in land use, with a total conversion area of 55,113.23 km2 (Table S1). During this period, forest land had the largest area of inflow, reaching 20,268.90 km2, primarily from arable land and grassland. Notably, the conversion from grassland to forest land dominated, accounting for 12,170.76 km2. Meanwhile, under the influence of ecological construction, forest land also showed a clear trend of conversion to grassland (8,520.36 km2), accounting for 51.00% of the area transferred out of this category. In the process of land development, the transfer from unused land to grassland dominated, with the transfer area far exceeding that to other land types, accounting for 90.06%. In addition, 3,312.74 km2 of arable land were converted to construction land, which is the fundamental reason for the decline in carbon stock. This trend was especially pronounced in the central urban area of Chengdu, reflecting that the region is gradually shifting from an agricultural economy to an urbanized economy.

Carbon stock changes and spatial and temporal distribution patterns in Sichuan Province

As shown in Fig. 5, carbon stocks in Sichuan Province exhibited pronounced spatial heterogeneity from 2000 to 2020. Areas with high carbon stocks were mainly distributed in the mountainous and canyon regions along the eastern margin of the western Sichuan Plateau, as well as in the southern part of Sichuan Province. In contrast, areas with low carbon stocks were primarily concentrated in the eastern region, where arable land and urban land are extensively distributed.

Spatial differentiation and change areas of carbon stock. The map was generated using ArcGIS Desktop 10.8 (https://www.esri.com).

Spatiotemporal analysis of carbon stock trends indicates that the total carbon stock in Sichuan Province continuously decreased by 32.61 × 106 t between 2000 and 2020. Areas with increasing carbon stocks were mainly located in the Aba Tibetan and Qiang Autonomous Prefecture, the Garze Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, and the northern Sichuan Basin. In contrast, areas with declining carbon stocks were concentrated in the central and southern parts of the Sichuan Basin. Furthermore, changes in carbon stock between 2000 and 2010 were more spatially concentrated than those between 2010 and 2020, with the decline in the latter decade reaching 0.33%, far exceeding that of the previous decade.

Drivers of carbon stock differentiation based on land use

Scale detection

To examine the spatial scale effects of driving factors on carbon stock, it was essential to identify the optimal spatial scale for analysis. Three grid sizes (1 km, 2 km, and 4 km) were established based on carbon stock and driving factors from 2000 to 2020. The magnitude of the spatial scale effect was evaluated using the 80% quantile of the q-value. As shown in Fig. 6, at any given time, the 80% quantile of the q-values increased progressively with increasing spatial unit size. Therefore, this research selected a grid size of 4 km for subsequent analyses of the driving effects.

Results of scale detection in Sichuan Province.

The results show that the p-values for all variables were less than 0.05, indicating that each variable has a statistically significant effect on the spatial distribution of carbon stocks. With the incorporation of the discretization method, the OMGD model produced higher q-values than the OPGD model for most variables (Table 3). Cases without improvement occurred because both models used identical stratification methods and category counts, resulting in identical q-values. The single-factor detection results revealed substantial differences in the explanatory power of the driving factors (Fig. 7). Over the 20-year study period, natural factors exhibited stronger explanatory power for the spatial differentiation of carbon stocks, with an average q-value of 0.177. Among the 13 factors, NDVI, ELE, and SL were identified as the three dominant factors, with q-values of 0.276, 0.216, and 0.211, respectively. In contrast, socioeconomic factors showed considerably weaker explanatory power, with average q-values of only 0.082 and 0.045, respectively. In 2000 and 2010, NDVI exhibited the strongest association with the spatial distribution of carbon stocks, with the highest q-values of 0.267 and 0.235, respectively. In 2020, SL demonstrated the strongest explanatory power, with a q-value of 0.216, likely because steeper slopes effectively constrained human disturbance to ecosystems. Overall, these findings highlight the critical roles of vegetation conditions and topographic characteristics in shaping the spatial distribution of carbon stocks.

Interaction detection

The interaction effects identified by the interaction detector showed either dual-factor enhancement or nonlinear enhancement (Fig. 8). Over the past three years (2000, 2010, and 2020), the interaction between ELE and NDVI consistently exhibited the strongest explanatory power, with q-values ranging from 0.375 to 0.415. In contrast, the interaction between SL and NDVI ranked second, with q-values varying between 0.366 and 0.381. Overall, the four highest q-values identified in the interaction-effect analysis were associated with ELE ∩ NDVI (q = 0.399), SL ∩ NDVI (q = 0.387), NDVI ∩ POP (q = 0.355), and AMT ∩ NDVI (q = 0.352). These results indicate that interactions between NDVI and multiple factors, particularly natural factors, exert substantial influences on carbon stock levels. Therefore, further investigation of the interactions between NDVI and other natural factors is necessary to improve the accuracy of carbon stock distribution assessments.

Results of the interaction detection.

Simulation of land use dynamics under multiple scenarios

Using the PLUS model and incorporating multiple policy constraints, dynamic land use simulations were conducted under three scenarios (NTS, EPS, and ECS), yielding the projected future areas and spatial distributions of each land use category (Fig. 9 and Table S2). Under the influence of rapid industrialization and urbanization in Sichuan Province, construction land increased across all three scenarios, with the most substantial expansion observed in the EPS. Compared to 2020, construction land increased by 65.54% in 2040 and 114.15% in 2060, primarily due to the conversion of arable land in the Chengdu Plain. Meanwhile, large portions of grassland and forest land were converted into arable land, resulting in a continuous rise in their area by 3.36% in 2040 and 6.77% in 2060. Due to strengthened protection of the water area and the restoration of rivers and lakes, both NTS and ECS exhibited an apparent increase in water area. Furthermore, effective ecological protection measures in ECS led to significantly greater forest land expansion than in the other scenarios, increasing by 4,653.95 km2 in 2040 and 7,953.12 km2 in 2060 compared with 2020. At the same time, construction land expansion was notably constrained, with growth rates reduced by 24.75% and 43.07% relative to NTS.

Land use areas under different future development scenarios in Sichuan Province. The map was generated using ArcGIS Desktop 10.8 (https://www.esri.com).

From a spatial distribution perspective, the differences in land use among the three scenarios were primarily reflected in two aspects. First, compared with the other scenarios, forest land in the central and southern regions under the ECS exhibited a clear trend of northward expansion. Second, under the EPS, Sichuan Province followed an intensive provincial capital–driven development model, concentrating resources and investment in urban construction centered on Chengdu. The substantial expansion of construction land in the Chengdu Plain, which was most evident by 2060, illustrates the significant reduction of agricultural space caused by rapid urbanization.

Prediction of carbon stock

Based on the land-use simulation results for 2040 and 2060, the spatiotemporal dynamics of carbon stocks were further evaluated using the InVEST model. Compared with 2020, all three scenarios exhibited varying degrees of change (Fig. 10). By 2060, carbon stocks under the ECS increased by 44.31 × 106 t (Table S3). In contrast, those under the other two scenarios continued to decline. Under the NTS, carbon stocks decreased by 87.24 × 106 t in 2060 relative to 2020, while the EPS exhibited the most pronounced decline, reaching 203.60 × 106 t. Specifically, under the EPS, the uncontrolled expansion of construction land led to extensive encroachment of this low-carbon-density land type into high-carbon-density land categories. Although carbon stocks in built-up areas increased by 114.21% from 2020 to 2060, they accounted for only 0.26% of the total carbon stock in 2060. Meanwhile, carbon stocks in forests and grasslands decreased by 5.27% and 3.66%, respectively, leading to a substantial decline in total carbon stocks. In contrast, the ECS strictly constrained the expansion of construction land and implemented effective forest conservation measures. Consequently, the increase in forest carbon stocks became the dominant contributor to total carbon stock growth, accounting for 475.20% of the total increase by 2060.

Distribution of carbon stocks in Sichuan Province under different scenarios and regions with significant changes in carbon stocks. The map was generated using ArcGIS Desktop 10.8 (https://www.esri.com).

A comparison of the spatial distribution of carbon stocks under future scenarios with that of 2020 reveals a significant spatial correlation between land use and carbon stocks. Driven by rapid population growth and policy dynamics, areas with low carbon stocks expanded radially from Chengdu. This pattern was most pronounced under the NTS and EPS, whereas the ECS imposed stricter constraints on construction land expansion, making this pattern relatively less apparent. In addition, under the NTS and EPS, areas experiencing declines in carbon stocks were primarily concentrated on construction land in the northern Aba Tibetan and Qiang Autonomous Prefecture and on arable land in the Liangshan Yi Autonomous Prefecture. In contrast, areas exhibiting increases in carbon stocks under all three scenarios were more spatially dispersed.

Discussion

Spatiotemporal evolution of carbon stock

Natural conditions and socioeconomic factors jointly shape land-use patterns, with their combined effects primarily manifested through land fragmentation, increased ecological fragility, and policy-driven land-use transitions, all of which substantially influence the spatial distribution of carbon stocks34,35. Over the past 20-year period, carbon stocks in Sichuan Province exhibited an overall declining trend, with distinct stage-specific characteristics observed throughout the study period.

From 2000 to 2010, carbon stock declined slightly, primarily because ecological restoration projects implemented during this period, such as natural forest protection and the conversion of cropland to forests and grasslands, were still in their early stages. Although these measures promoted vegetation recovery and enhanced carbon sequestration in some regions, their carbon-sink effects were insufficient to offset the increased carbon emissions and carbon-sink losses associated with the expansion of construction land36. Consequently, regional carbon stocks continued to exhibit an overall net decline. This pattern reflects an imbalance between the positive effects of ecological restoration and the negative impacts of development and construction. Between 2010 and 2020, the decline in carbon stocks became even more pronounced, driven by intensifying conflicts among ecological conservation, food production, and urban expansion, consistent with the findings of Yan et al.37. To sustain economic growth, forest land, grasslands, and croplands in economically advantageous areas were continuously converted into construction land, resulting in a persistent transition from high-carbon-sequestration land types to low-carbon-sequestration land types. This conversion not only directly reduced regional carbon stocks but also intensified land fragmentation, thereby weakening the stability of carbon sink functions in the remaining ecological lands38,39. Spatially, the expansion of construction land was concentrated in the Chengdu Plain and adjacent transportation corridors, areas originally characterized by highly productive croplands and secondary forests. As a result, the associated carbon stock loss per unit area was particularly pronounced. At the same time, the effectiveness of some earlier ecological restoration projects gradually weakened because of diminishing marginal returns40. Collectively, these processes contributed to a persistent decline in carbon stocks, punctuated by periodic intensification throughout the study period. This trend reflects the continuous weakening of the carbon sink function of Sichuan’s terrestrial ecosystems under the dual pressures of economic development and ecological conservation.

Insights from multi-scenario simulation

The multi-scenario assessment framework provides a systematic perspective for understanding potential changes in carbon sink functions under different policy constraints41. In this study, all scenarios except the ECS exhibited a continuous decline in carbon stocks. Among these scenarios, the EPS resulted in the greatest reduction in carbon sequestration capacity, as short-term development objectives substantially encroached on high-carbon-sink ecological spaces42. In the NTS, the gradual expansion of construction land at the expense of arable land and ecological land led to persistent carbon stock losses. By contrast, the ECS primarily functions by preventing the irreversible conversion of high-carbon-density land into low-carbon-efficiency land-use types through strict spatial access controls, thereby maintaining and enhancing carbon sink functions. These findings are consistent with previous studies. For example, multi-scenario simulations in Dalian showed that only conservation-oriented scenarios resulted in carbon stocks above baseline levels43. At the same time, studies in the central-southern hilly region similarly reported that only ecological protection scenarios achieved net carbon stock growth through forest expansion and the regulation of human activity intensity44.

These results suggest that the persistence of carbon sink functions is not an inevitable consequence of natural succession, but rather a spatial manifestation of institutional choices. The divergent carbon stock trajectories observed across scenarios indicate that ecosystem carbon sequestration capacity is highly sensitive to the institutional thresholds governing land-use change. Low regulatory thresholds create excessive flexibility for modifying ecological redline plans, thereby exposing high-carbon-sink lands to sustained degradation pressures. In contrast, strict restrictions on land-use conversion, higher approval requirements, and stronger penalties for violations establish sufficiently robust regulatory thresholds capable of preventing degradation pathways at their sources. Therefore, the trade-off between economic growth and carbon sink protection is not inherently irreconcilable; instead, the balance largely depends on the enforcement intensity of spatial regulatory frameworks. Furthermore, from a policy-design perspective, an early-warning and intervention mechanism for carbon sink degradation should be established by incorporating carbon stock dynamics into the environmental performance assessment system of territorial spatial planning. Such measures would facilitate a transition from passive ecological protection to proactive spatial regulation. Ultimately, enhancing carbon sink functions requires transforming ecological space control from a planning-oriented principle into an institutionally binding mechanism, thereby converting the precision of spatial governance into the long-term sustainability of carbon sink functions.

Analysis of the influence of driving factors on the distribution of carbon stock from the perspective of different study areas

Scientifically identifying the drivers of spatial heterogeneity in carbon stock requires not only recognizing dominant individual factors but also clarifying the optimal combinations of driving factors and their scale-dependent sensitivities, providing a theoretical basis for differentiated, precise management strategies. The results indicate that NDVI was the dominant factor influencing the spatial variation in carbon stock across the province, and its interaction with elevation was significantly stronger than other two-factor interactions, demonstrating a pronounced synergistic enhancement effect. This finding suggests that elevation, as a large-scale geographic gradient, determines the fundamental patterns of human accessibility, thereby constraining the physical limits of vegetation growth45. In contrast, NDVI, as a direct remote-sensing indicator of surface vegetation biomass, reflects the actual vegetation conditions shaped by both natural environmental endowments and anthropogenic activities within these constraints. Consequently, elevation and NDVI do not operate independently; rather, they form a coupled “potential–realization” relationship that jointly governs the spatial distribution of carbon stock. In high-elevation regions, ecosystems are strongly constrained by harsh natural conditions, including low temperatures and poor soil quality, resulting in slow vegetation growth and relatively simple community structures46. Under such conditions, carbon stock capacity depends heavily on the native vegetation cover, as reflected in NDVI. Therefore, declines in NDVI caused by climate change or disturbances such as overgrazing can lead to substantial carbon losses and extremely slow ecosystem recovery, highlighting the high ecological vulnerability and sensitivity of these areas. By contrast, although human activities such as urban expansion and infrastructure development directly reduce NDVI in low-elevation areas47, these regions may offer more favorable hydrothermal conditions, providing greater potential for vegetation restoration48. If NDVI can be maintained at comparatively high levels through ecological restoration and urban greening initiatives—particularly in mid- and low-elevation areas surrounding cities—these regions can still sustain considerable carbon sequestration capacity, demonstrating strong responsiveness to positive human interventions.

The driving factors of carbon stocks exhibit distinct transition patterns across different spatial scales and regional contexts. At the provincial macro-scale, broad and continuous environmental gradients, particularly NDVI and elevation, constitute the dominant factors influencing carbon stock distribution. However, as the analytical scale shifts to smaller geographic units, both the nature and relative importance of these driving factors undergo systematic changes. Xiang et al.49 reported that the human activity index was the primary driving factor in the western Sichuan Plateau, whereas Tang and Peng9 found that anthropogenic factors, particularly the non-agricultural population, exerted substantially greater influence than natural factors on the Chengdu Plain. In contrast, Zhou et al.50 identified NDVI as the dominant factor in the Panxi region. These findings collectively indicate that, in regions where natural environmental background conditions exhibit limited variation, vegetation-related indicators such as NDVI provide stronger explanatory power for spatial variability in carbon stocks. By contrast, in plateau pastoral regions or urban core areas characterized by pronounced gradients in human activity intensity, anthropogenic disturbance and land-use conversion emerge as the dominant driving forces. Overall, regional variations in carbon stocks across different spatial scales in Sichuan Province are primarily shaped by the synergistic interactions among natural environmental gradients, vegetation responses, and human activities.

Limitations and future perspectives

This study was the first to employ the OMGD model to evaluate the driving factors of carbon stock. By integrating the optimization of factor discretization, factor detection, and interaction detection, the OMGD model can be broadly applied to studies on spatial differentiation and driving mechanisms involving geographic elements. However, the model relies solely on the quantile of the q-value to determine the optimal unit size. When the unit size corresponding to the maximum q-value is considerable, it may overlook numerous geographic features, thereby diminishing the significance of geographic analysis. Therefore, future studies should establish a constraint framework that balances q-value quantile optimization with the preservation of spatial detail by defining an upper threshold for the loss of geographical feature information, thereby identifying the optimal spatial scale that maximizes both statistical significance and geographical relevance.

Although the InVEST model was used to estimate carbon stock, its accuracy remains a significant challenge. The primary sources of error include data quality, parameter uncertainty, and mismatches between spatial and temporal scales. These limitations stem primarily from the model’s reliance on pre-existing carbon density data, which are often derived from assumptions or expert judgment rather than real-time, comprehensive field surveys, thereby constraining the accuracy of carbon stock estimations51. Additionally, the InVEST model does not account for differences in vegetation types or growth conditions within the same land use category, which reduces its applicability as a reference for fine-scale ecological management and policymaking52. In future research, long-term fixed observation plots could be established across representative land-use types to generate time-series data on carbon density dynamics, thereby replacing the currently used static parameters. Meanwhile, vegetation functional types and growth-condition subcategories should be incorporated into land-use classifications, and the standard deviation of carbon density within each land-use category should be included in model outputs as an indicator of uncertainty.

In the PLUS model, the rules governing land use type conversion and parameter settings involved a certain degree of subjectivity5,53,54. These conversion rules were typically derived from historical data and underlying assumptions, resulting in limited precision and controllability. Future research should focus on enhancing the PLUS model to enable adaptive parameter optimization and improve its overall modeling accuracy. This study considered only natural, social, and economic driving factors. Future sudden natural disasters, extreme climatic events, and policy changes that substantially affect land use dynamics remain difficult to predict and simulate55,56. Future research should integrate the PLUS model with a reinforcement learning framework, treating land-use conversion rules as learnable policy functions and using multi-temporal observed land-use patterns as reward signals for adaptive parameter optimization, thereby further enhancing the model’s predictive accuracy. In addition, policy instruments, such as land-use plans and ecological red-line delineations, should be quantified as spatial constraint layers or conversion-probability adjustment coefficients and incorporated into the land-use transition rules of the PLUS model. Furthermore, probabilistic perturbations could be introduced into land-use conversion probabilities for specific years or seasons based on historical disaster records to improve the model’s responsiveness to external disturbances.

Conclusion

This study investigated the primary drivers of historical carbon stock dynamics and explored evolutionary trends in carbon stocks under the NTS, EPS, and ECS. The main conclusions are as follows. Between 2000 and 2020, carbon stock in Sichuan Province decreased by 32.61 × 106 t, primarily due to the conversion of cropland into construction land, with the most pronounced declines concentrated in the central and southern regions of the Sichuan Basin. Compared with the OPGD model, the OMGD model demonstrated greater explanatory power for variations in carbon stocks across multiple variables and time periods. Univariate and interaction analyses based on the OMGD model identified NDVI as the dominant driving factor (q = 0.276). In contrast, its interaction with ELE had the strongest explanatory power (q = 0.399). By 2060, carbon stocks under the NTS and EPS were projected to decrease by 87.24 × 106 t and 203.60 × 106 t, respectively. In contrast, carbon stocks under the ECS were expected to increase by 44.31 × 106 t. Future territorial spatial planning should prioritize transforming ecological space management from a planning-oriented approach into an institutionally constrained governance mechanism, thereby translating the precision of spatial governance into the long-term sustainability of carbon sink functions.
