Outcome evaluation of regenerative endodontic therapy using injectable-platelet-rich fibrin and platelet-rich fibrin in nonvital immature permanent teeth: A prospective observational study
Authors: Tolivi T. Chishi, V.G. Sam Joseph, K.G Vidya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Pulp necrosis in an immature permanent tooth disrupts root development, often treated with calcium hydroxide or Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (MTA) apexification, however, these approaches do not improve structural integrity of teeth. Regenerative endodontic therapy (RET) promotes root maturation, with blood clot and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) commonly used as a scaffold. Injectable PRF (i-PRF) may offer enhanced biological properties.Aim:This study aimed to compare clinical and radiographic outcomes of RET using i-PRF and PRF in nonvital immature permanent teeth.Settings and Design:Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics, Department of Paediatric Dentistry, Government Dental College, Thiruvananthapuram. Prospective Observational Study.Materials and Methods:Thirty-eight patients undergoing RET were allocated into two groups: Group I (i-PRF, n = 19) and Group II (PRF, n = 19). Clinical outcomes were assessed at 3, 6, and 12 months. Radiographic evaluation was performed at 6 and 12 months, including assessment of periapical healing using periapical index (PAI) scores, root development (length and dentinal wall thickness) measured using ImageJ, and apical responses evaluated according to the criteria described by Chen et al. Statistical analysis was performed with the level of significance set at P < 0.05.Results:Group I demonstrated a higher success rate (100%) and significantly greater reduction in PAI scores (P < 0.05) than Group II (89.47%). Both groups exhibited increases in root length and dentinal wall thickness without significant intergroup differences. Type 3 apical response was the predominant outcome, indicating ongoing root maturation with incomplete apical closure. No changes in coronal discoloration or vitality responses were observed at the 12-month follow-up.Conclusion:i-PRF demonstrated superior periapical healing and overall treatment outcomes compared to PRF, whereas both groups showed comparable effects on root development. These findings suggest that i-PRF may represent a promising biological scaffold for regenerative endodontic treatment of immature permanent teeth.

INTRODUCTION

“Regenerative endodontic therapy” (RET) was introduced as a novel treatment method for nonvital immature permanent teeth. The success of RET depends on scaffolds that provide a conducive microenvironment that allows for stem cell migration, proliferation and differentiation. Earlier technique was to induce periapical bleeding to form a scaffold from the blood clot when an endodontic file is extended beyond the apex.[1] However, this method provided limited growth factor release which led to the use of first and second-generation autologous platelet concentrates, i.e., platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), respectively, as scaffolds because of their higher concentration of growth factors.[23] Subsequent refinements in PRF aimed to optimize the concentration of growth factors and cells while retaining the fluidity of PRP led to the development of injectable PRF (i-PRF).

The i-PRF forms a flexible fibrin matrix that better adapts to the complex root canal anatomy with simpler procedural steps compared to PRF, where the scaffold must first be prepared in a PRF box, cut, and then placed into the canal. In addition, i-PRF has been associated with the highest concentration of leukocytes reported so far and demonstrated long-term release of platelet-derived growth factor-AA (PDGF-AA), PDGF-AB, epidermal growth factor (EGF), and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) which may contribute to enhanced healing and regenerative outcomes.[4]

Although i-PRF is a promising scaffold in RET, no long-term clinical studies could be traced. Hence, this prospective observational study was conducted to compare and evaluate the treatment outcomes of i-PRF and PRF as scaffolds in RET of nonvital immature permanent teeth with a 1-year follow-up period.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary care dental institute in Thiruvananthapuram, Kerala, India during the period of May 2023–April 2025, after getting approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee on 25/05/23 (IEC/T/23/25). This study strictly adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration and is reported following the STROBE guidelines.

The sample size was calculated to be nineteen participants per group who had received RET with either i-PRF or PRF. The case selection was done according to the American Association of Endodontists (AAE) guidelines.[5] Participants aged from 9 to 30 years with permanent maxillary central incisors that became nonvital secondary to trauma and/or caries and radiographic evidence of incomplete root development with a wide-open apical foramen were a prerequisite for inclusion.

Patients were thoroughly informed about the procedure, potential risks, discomforts, benefits and expected outcome of the study. A total of 38 teeth were randomly selected for the two treatment groups: Group I: i-PRF (n = 19) and Group II: PRF (n = 19) as a scaffold for RET.

Clinical signs and symptoms, including pain, swelling, and tenderness to percussion (TTP) at baseline were recorded. Pulp vitality test was assessed using cold test (Roeko Endo-Frost, Coltene/Whaledent GmbH, Germany) and electric pulp test (Digitest, Parkell, Inc., NY, USA); Preoperative and postoperative radiographic assessments were performed using radiovisiography (RVG) (Carestream Kodak 2100 Dental X-Ray Machine) with a standardized paralleling technique, utilizing a sensor positioning device (HUALNYK Digital RVG Sensor 2.0 Positioner Holder) [Figure 1], operated by a single calibrated examiner. Baseline parameters including periapical index (PAI) score were recorded. Dentinal wall thickness and root length were measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

The treatment protocol was in accordance with the AAE Clinical Considerations for RET.[5] Following access cavity preparation under rubber dam isolation and local anaesthesia (lignocaine 2% and adrenaline injection IP, Nandani Medical Laboratories PVT. LTD.), irrigation was performed using 20 ml of 1.5% NaOCl for 5 min, followed by 20 ml saline irrigation for 5 min. Modified triple antibiotic paste (MTAP) was prepared using equal proportions (1:1:1) of metronidazole (Metrogyl 400 mg, J. B. Chemicals and Pharmaceuticals LTD.), clindamycin (Dalacin C – 300 mg, Pfizer), and ciprofloxacin (Ciplox-500, Cipla) with 1 ml of propylene glycol as vehicle (BRM Chemicals) and diluted to a final concentration of 1 mg/ml, i.e., considered safe for apical stem cells.[67] The access cavity was sealed with a temporary restoration (Dentsply Intermediate Restorative Material, IRM). The patients were recalled after 4 weeks and were assessed for any signs or symptoms. If symptomatic, an additional antimicrobial therapy was given, and if asymptomatic, the access cavity was reassessed under local anesthesia using 3% mepivacaine (Scandonest 3% plain) without vasoconstrictor under rubber dam isolation. MTAP was removed with normal saline, and final irrigation was done using 20 ml 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (Prevest DentPro), delivered through side-vented irrigation needles (Neoendo Sidevent Irrigation Needles, 30 g).[8]

For Group I, i-PRF was freshly prepared using a centrifuge (Dr. Choukroun DUO Quattro PRF Centrifuge) using 10 ml of intravenous blood obtained through a standard venepuncture in the median cubital vein and drawn into a 13 ml sterile plastic i-PRF tube (Dr. Choukroun Plastic i-PRF+Tubes) without an anticoagulant and centrifuged at 700 rpm for 3 min.[9] The upper yellow fluid was collected and then injected into the root canal up to a level 3 mm below the cementoenamel junction (CEJ). Following a coagulation period of 10–15 min, it was covered with a resorbable collagen matrix (CollaPlug/Cologenesis sterile collagen sponge).[10]

For Group II, the PRF scaffold was prepared according to Choukroun’s method using the same centrifuge and blood collection method as that of i-PRF. The blood was drawn into a 10 ml sterile glass tube (Dr. Choukroun Glass PRF Test tubes) without an anticoagulant and centrifuged at 2700 rpm for 12 min.[11] The PRF fibrin clot was retrieved using sterile tweezers (GDC, DP18LS), separated by cutting (GDC Micro Iris surgical scissors) approximately 1 mm below the buffy coat layer and compressed into a membrane in the sterile PRF box (Waldent PRF Container Box). It was then cut into small pieces and condensed into the root canal up to a level of 3 mm below the CEJ with an endodontic plugger (GDC Root Canal Plugger), followed by the placement of a collagen matrix above the scaffold. Biodentin (Septodont Biodentin) was applied as the capping material above the collagen matrix for both groups, and the access cavity was sealed using composite restoration (Ivoclar Vivadent Te-Econom Plus).[12] All the treatments were carried out under a Dental Operating Microscope (Labomed MAGNA Dental Microscope). Patients were instructed to return for follow-up at time intervals of 3, 6, and 12 months.

Outcome assessment was performed based on clinical and radiographic examinations in accordance with the recommendations of the AAE for RET, wherein outcomes are categorized as primary goal, which is the elimination of symptoms and the evidence of bony healing. Secondary goal: increased root wall thickness and/or increased root length and tertiary goal is positive response to vitality testing (revitalization).[5] Evaluations were carried out by two independent, precalibrated and blinded observers.

The primary goal of RET was assessed clinically and radiographically. The clinical criteria included the absence of pain/discomfort, TTP, swelling, and sinus tract. Radiographic criteria measured evidence of bony healing based on PAI score.[13] Based on combined clinical and radiographic findings, treatment outcomes were categorized as follows:

Apical development at 12 months was assessed according to the criteria described by Chen et al.[17] Treatment success was defined as the absence of clinical signs and symptoms (pain, swelling, inflammation) in conjunction with radiographic evidence of healing. Both healed and healing categories were considered indicative of successful treatment outcomes.[18] The tertiary outcome measure (revitalization) was assessed at the 12-month follow-up using both the cold test and electric pulp test.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Fisher’s exact test was employed to analyze categorical variables across the time points. Radiographic outcomes were described using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, whereas continuous variables such as the PAI scores and percentage changes in root length and dentinal wall thickness were described as means with standard deviations. The association between treatment group and apical response was assessed using the Pearson Chi-square test. Comparisons of PAI scores and changes in root dimensions between groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test (P < 0.05).

RESULTS

All patients completed the follow-up period, resulting in a 100% recall rate. The sample consisted of 25 males (65.79%) and 13 females (34.21%). Among the 38 teeth studied, 21 were right, and 17 were left maxillary central incisors.

At baseline, none of the patients in either group presented with pain, swelling, or sinus tract, and no new symptoms developed during the follow-up period. TTP was initially was observed in 3 out of 19 patients in Group I and 4 out of 19 patients in Group II. For Group 1, TTP got resolved in the 3rd month follow-up for all the patients. For Group II, 2 cases got resolved by 3 months and 2 remained tender up to the 12-month follow-up. Coronal discoloration was observed at baseline in 6/19 patients in Group I and 5/19 in Group II, with no change in extent or occurrence of new cases during the study period. Vitality testing using EPT and cold test showed no response in any case at baseline or at 12 months. Fisher’s exact test revealed no statistically significant association between the treatment groups and any of the clinical parameters assessed (pain, swelling, sinus tract, TTP, coronal discoloration, and vitality responses) (P > 0.05).

PAI scores were used to assess periapical healing. Group I showed a decrease over time, with mean ranks of 19.11 at baseline, 16.66 at 6 months and 16.11 at 12 months. In contrast, Group II demonstrated an increasing trend, with mean ranks of 19.89 (baseline), 22.34 (6 months) and 22.89 (12 months). These findings indicate superior periapical healing in Group I (i-PRF group), which was statistically significant when analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test (P < 0.05). Treatment outcome evaluation using the Pearson’s Chi-square test at 12 months revealed that the i-PRF group demonstrated significantly better outcomes, with complete healing observed in 89.5% (17/19) of cases compared to 52.63% (8/19) in the PRF group (P < 0.05). Moreover, the PRF group exhibited a higher incidence of diseased outcomes (10.53%), whereas no diseased cases were observed in the Group I. The i-PRF group achieved a 100% clinical success rate with no cases classified as diseased, whereas the PRF group showed a lower success rate of 89.47% and a corresponding failure rate of 10.53%. The success and failure outcomes were analyzed using the Pearson Chi-square test, which revealed no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). The overall success rate of RET (utilizing i-PRF and PRF) was 94.74% [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to compare and evaluate the outcome of RET using i-PRF and PRF as scaffolds in immature permanent teeth. Throughout the follow-up period, both groups maintained symptom-free clinical status, indicating the efficacy of both scaffolds to promote healing in a nonvital immature permanent tooth. However, the i-PRF group demonstrated statistically significantly superior treatment outcomes at 12 months (P < 0.05), with a notably higher complete healing rate (89.47%) compared to the PRF group (52.63%). PAI scores decreased significantly more in the i-PRF group (P < 0.05), reflecting superior periapical healing compared with the PRF group. The observed differences in healing outcomes may be explained by the biological and structural differences between the two platelet-rich scaffolds.

Comparative analysis of PRF and i-PRF reveals critical differences in preparation, composition, physical properties, and growth factor release profiles. PRF is prepared at higher centrifugation speeds (2700 rpm x 12 mins (~700 gmax)), producing a solid fibrin matrix rich in fibronectin and vitronectin, with leukocytes concentrated at the buffy coat (between the upper plasma layer and red blood cell layer) and collected as a gel-like clot. In contrast, i-PRF is prepared at lower speeds (700 rpm x 3 min(~60gmax), yielding a liquid form that remains uncoagulated for 15–20 min post-centrifugation; volume obtained is about 1–1.5 ml. The injectable liquid form of i-PRF enables three-dimensional adaptation within the root canal system before coagulating into a fibrin scaffold. This fibrin matrix acts as a reservoir for growth factors, allowing their sustained release over time and thereby supporting tissue repair and regeneration.[19]

Cellularly, PRF contains platelets and leukocytes but with reduced cellular presence in the usable upper layers due to centrifugation forces and longer centrifugation time, which result in greater sedimentation of cells, whereas i-PRF produced using the low-speed centrifugation process exhibits higher and more evenly distributed concentrations of platelets and leukocytes in the liquid phase, enhancing cellular content.[9] Growth factor release profiles also differ between the two concentrates. PRF releases bioactive molecules such as PDGF, vascular endothelial growth factor, IGF, EGF, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), bone morphogenetic protein 2, sustaining release up to 7 days,[2021] whereas i-PRF demonstrates higher total long-term release of PDGF-AA, PDGF-AB, EGF, and IGF-1 after 10 days, indicating prolonged bioactivity. i-PRF also has been shown to promote higher fibroblast migration and elevated mRNA expression of TGF-β, PDGF, and COL1a2, all critical for tissue regeneration.[4]

TTP, a clinical indicator of periapical inflammation, resolved in all but two patients in the PRF group, both older than 20 years. Although age was not statistically significant in this study, existing literature documents diminished RET efficacy in older patients, due to decreased stem cell activity limiting cellular migration and vascularization necessary for pulp-dentin regeneration.[22] Further studies are required to determine whether the biological advantages of i-PRF can improve regenerative outcomes in older patients.

Throughout the study, negative pulp vitality responses persisted, consistent with prior findings which showed restricted or delayed pulp sensibility recovery during the 1st year of follow-up. Research shows that pulp vitality signs emerge between 18 and 24 months after treatment.[232425] Hence, long-term studies are warranted to better evaluate the potential for sensory re-innervation. As demonstrated in the present study, significant clinical and radiographic healing was achieved despite the absence of vitality response, aligning with the broader understanding that functional revitalization may lag behind structural healing. Moreover, the reparative tissue formed may resemble cementum or periodontal ligament-like tissue rather than true pulp-dentin complex.[2627]

Regarding root development, the i-PRF group exhibited slightly higher mean percentage increases in root length (12.57%) and dentinal wall thickness (13.94%) compared to the PRF group (11.89% and 12.34%, respectively). These differences were not statistically significant, likely due to the limited sample size, short follow-up duration, and confounding variables such as age, apical morphology, and trauma duration. Notably, the increase in dentinal wall thickness exceeded the increase in root length, a finding consistent with several other studies that have similarly reported greater gains in dentin thickness than in root elongation during regenerative endodontic procedures.[28]

Both groups demonstrated comparable apical responses, predominantly Type 3 (57.89% Group I and 68.42% of Group II), indicative of ongoing stem cell-mediated root maturation with incomplete apical closure. 36.84% of patients from Group I and 21.05% of patients from Group II exhibited Type 1 response (Thickened canal walls and continued root development). The present study did not identify Type 2 (no significant continuation of root development; apex becomes blunt and close), Type 4 (calcific obliteration) or Type 5 (hard tissue barrier formed between the coronal plug) responses. These results matched with those reported by Shivashankar et al.[29] The 12-month follow-up was sufficient for initial healing assessment but insufficient to observe complete apical closure, highlighting the need for extended longitudinal monitoring beyond 24 months to fully characterize regenerative outcomes.

The overall success rate of RET was 94.74%, validating that both autologous biological scaffolds used in this study met the primary treatment objectives outlined by the AAE and ESE guidelines.[518] The i-PRF group exhibited a 100% success rate, with no cases categorized as diseased compared to 78.9% for the PRF group, however, with no statistical significance (P > 0.05), likely due to relatively small sample size.

Within the limitations of this study, both PRF and i-PRF proved to be effective scaffolds for regenerative endodontic treatment of immature permanent teeth. However, i-PRF demonstrated superior periapical healing and overall treatment outcomes at 12 months, suggesting that it may serve as a promising biological scaffold for enhancing regenerative endodontic procedures. Further long-term studies are required to confirm its clinical advantages.

Limitations

Differences in patient age across groups, variations in apical anatomy, and the extent of preoperative periapical lesions are important biological variables that can significantly affect healing responses following RET. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size and short-term follow-up period limit the generalizability and the long-term predictive value of the study’s findings.

Given these limitations, future studies should prioritize strict case selection with appropriate matching of patient-related variables such as age, apical anatomy and initial lesion size to minimize confounding factors. Moreover, prospective randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up durations, and standardized protocols are necessary to better assess the comparative efficacy of different biological scaffolds. Investigating additional biomarkers of healing and employing advanced imaging modalities could also enhance the understanding of true pulp-dentin complex regeneration, ultimately leading to optimized clinical outcomes in RET.

CONCLUSION

This prospective observational study provides evidence on the potential use of i-PRF as a biologically active adjunct in RET. Its autologous nature, ease of preparation and rich concentration of growth factors make it an attractive option for enhancing tissue healing and promoting cellular proliferation. Clinically, the i-PRF group exhibited significantly enhanced healing outcomes, more favourable apical responses and improved PAI scores compared to PRF. In addition, regenerative parameters such as root length and dentinal wall thickness showed comparable improvements with the i-PRF application. These results highlight the potential of i-PRF as an effective scaffold in RET.
