Comparison of Y20 with GEN11CN (Harmonic HD 1000i) ultrasonic energy device for sealing 5-7 mm pulmonary vessels in thoracoscopic surgery: a multicenter, randomized, assessor- and statistician-blinded, noninferiority trial
Authors: Weijia Wu, Zhangqiang Chen, Yumei Shen, Qifeng Ding, Xuejun Xu, Xing Jin, Yongsheng Gong, Ning Zhong, Shanzhou Duan, Yongbing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReliable sealing of 5–7 mm pulmonary vessels remains a persistent challenge in thoracoscopic surgery. Endostaplers are difficult to maneuver within the narrow interlobar fissure, and vascular clips are prone to slippage that can obscure the operative field. Ultrasonic energy devices offer an alternative for vessels of this caliber, but direct clinical comparisons between different device platforms are lacking. This study aimed to compare the success rate of sealing 5–7 mm blood vessels between a novel device (Y20) and the widely used GEN11CN (Harmonic HD 1000i).MethodsThis was a multicenter, randomized, assessor- and statistician-blinded non-inferiority study. We enrolled patients aged 18–80 years scheduled for thoracoscopic anatomic segmentectomy or lobectomy who had at least one 5–7 mm vessel requiring sealing on computed tomography (CT) images, and randomly assigned them 1:1 to Y20 group or GEN11CN group. Patients with coagulopathy, severe thrombocytopenia, or recent active bleeding were excluded. Randomisation was stratified by centre using a central, computer-generated sequence held by an independent statistician. Device branding was masked with opaque covers so that the outcome evaluator remained blinded. The primary endpoint was the intraoperative vessel sealing success rate, defined as the absence of stump bleeding within a 10-minute observation window, with a prespecified non-inferiority margin of −10%. Secondary endpoints included intraoperative blood loss, operative time, postoperative drainage volume, and complications. Safety outcomes included laboratory parameters, vital signs, device-related sealing failures, and device-attributable complications.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable except for higher systolic blood pressure (BP; 126.16 vs. 122.16 mmHg; P=0.02) and platelet count (221.07 vs. 200.73 ×109/L; P=0.03) in the Y20 group. Intraoperative vessel sealing success was achieved in 100.00% (90/90) of the experimental group and 98.88% (88/89) of the control group (P=0.30). The between-group difference was 1.12% [95% confidence interval (CI): −3.15% to 6.21%]. The lower limit of the 95% CI (−3.15%) was greater than the non-inferiority margin of −10% (P<0.001). There were no statistically significant differences in intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage, or operative time (all P>0.05). Device-related adverse events (AEs) occurred in 0/90 (0%) Y20 versus 1/89 (1.11%) GEN11CN patients. No serious device-related events, deaths, or postoperative hemorrhage occurred through 30 days.ConclusionsY20 was noninferior to GEN11CN for intraoperative sealing of 5–7 mm pulmonary vessels and showed a comparable safety profile, supporting Y20 as a clinically acceptable alternative for this specific indication. These findings should not be generalized to vessels >7 mm or to settings without immediate rescue capability.Trial RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400088694; https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=223068).Keywords: Pulmonary vessels; hemostasis; randomized controlled trial; thoracic surgery, video-assisted; ultrasonic surgical procedures

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this multicenter, assessor- and statistician-blinded randomized non-inferiority trial [N=179, full analysis set (FAS)], Y20 achieved a 100.00% intraoperative vessel sealing success rate for 5–7 mm pulmonary vessels in thoracoscopic surgery, meeting predefined non-inferiority versus GEN11CN [difference: 1.12%; 95% confidence interval (CI): −3.15% to 6.21%; lower CI bound >−10% margin; P<0.001].

What is known and what is new?

• Ultrasonic high-frequency integrated devices such as GEN11CN are safe and effective for sealing pulmonary vessels ≤7 mm, but no randomized trial had directly compared different device platforms for this vessel-size range.

• This is among the first randomized controlled trials to provide a direct, head-to-head comparison of two ultrasonic high-frequency integrated devices—Y20 and GEN11CN—in 5–7 mm vessel sealing during thoracoscopic surgery, employing assessor and statistician blinding and three pre-specified analysis sets (FAS, per protocol set, and safety set) in accordance with regulatory guidance for non-inferiority device trials.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Y20 was noninferior to GEN11CN for intraoperative sealing of 5–7 mm pulmonary vessels and showed a comparable safety profile, supporting Y20 as a clinically acceptable alternative for this specific indication. These findings should not be generalized to vessels >7 mm or to settings without immediate rescue capability.

Introduction

Background

Ultrasonic energy has been employed for hemostasis since the 1970s. It functions by producing high-frequency harmonic motion of a metallic rod that denatures proteins (1). Unlike traditional electrocautery, which operates at temperatures between 150 and 400 ℃ and can cause thermal injury to adjacent tissues (up to 4 mm) while generating significant surgical smoke, ultrasonic devices produce considerably less heat, resulting in minimal collateral thermal damage and reduced smoke production (2-6). Multiple studies have shown that ultrasonic scalpels promote a blood-free surgical field and facilitate precise tissue dissection (7-9).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Secure sealing of pulmonary vessels is critical during thoracoscopic surgery, as intraoperative vascular injury may lead to conversion to thoracotomy or, in rare cases, death. Hemostasis of these vessels is commonly achieved with vascular endostaplers, vascular clips, or energy devices (10,11). Endostaplers provide reliable closure but are large and bulky, making them difficult to maneuver in the tight spaces around the pulmonary vasculature. Vascular clips are quick to apply but may be dislodged during subsequent dissection and can crowd the operative field. Energy-based sealing avoids these drawbacks but historically performed inconsistently for vessels larger than 5 mm (12-18). Ultrasonic high-frequency integrated devices that combine high-frequency electrocautery with ultrasonic vibration were developed to extend reliable energy sealing into the 5–7 mm range (19,20).

The GEN11CN (Harmonic HD 1000i) device (Ethicon Endo-Surgery, LLC; Cincinnati, OH, USA) is one of the most frequently studied ultrasonic energy devices in prospective trials and has shown good safety and efficacy in sealing pulmonary vessels ≤7 mm. Nevertheless, energy platforms in general carry recognized practical limitations, including acquisition and per-procedure cost, blade tissue-sticking, and lateral thermal spread (17,18). The Y20 device (Innolcon Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) is a newly designed ultrasonic energy device in China that combines high-frequency and ultrasonic energy delivery with a jaw geometry intended for precise application to small vessels, features designed to achieve reliable sealing within the 5–7 mm range. However, randomized data directly comparing Y20 with GEN11CN for vessels 5–7 mm in diameter remain lacking.

Objective

We therefore conducted a multicenter randomized controlled trial comparing the safety and efficacy of Y20 versus GEN11CN in sealing 5–7 mm blood vessels in thoracoscopic surgery. We present this article in accordance with the CONSORT reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1286/rc).

Methods

Trial design and oversight

This was a multicenter, randomized, assessor- and statistician-blinded, parallel-controlled, non-inferiority trial. The trial was retrospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400088694) on August 26, 2024, following the enrollment period (July–October 2023). The full protocol was finalized before first enrollment [2023], and no protocol element was altered after data inspection. Trial data were collected prospectively. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (No. JD-LX2023007-IR01), Suzhou Municipal Hospital (No. Q-2023-013-H01), and The First People’s Hospital of Kunshan (No. 2023-02-029-K01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding

Enrolled subjects were randomized 1:1 to experimental or control groups using stratified randomization by study center, implemented with a pre-specified seed and block design generated by an independent statistician. Because the operating surgeon could not be blinded to device identity, the primary endpoint was assessed by a designated outcome evaluator distinct from the operating surgeon. This evaluator entered the field only after device activation, recorded whether there was bleeding at the vascular stump within 10 minutes, and remained unaware of group assignment. Device labels were masked with opaque covers and statisticians received only anonymized codes. Accordingly, the trial is characterized as assessor- and statistician-blinded rather than surgeon-blinded.

Non-inferiority design

A non-inferiority design was chosen because GEN11CN already achieves a near-ceiling sealing success rate. Therefore, the clinically relevant question was whether Y20 could preserve this level of efficacy and broaden the range of available devices, rather than whether it could outperform GEN11CN. The −10% margin was based on a presumed 95% reference success rate, resulting in a clinically acceptable lower limit of 85%, which is feasible due to direct intraoperative observation of each seal and the availability of prompt corrective measures for any failures. This margin and one-sided alpha (0.025) match the convention adopted by contemporaneous non-inferiority trials of energy-based sealing for 5–7 mm pulmonary vessels (19,20), ensuring cross-trial comparability.

Patient population

Patients were consecutively enrolled from three centers: The Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, Suzhou Municipal Hospital, and The First People’s Hospital of Kunshan. Sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome assuming 95% control device success and a −10% non-inferiority margin. A minimum of 75 subjects per group was required. Because final eligibility could only be confirmed after randomization intraoperatively, the sample size was inflated by 15% to allow for post-randomization non-evaluability rather than conventional loss to follow-up. With block randomization, 180 participants were enrolled in a 1:1 ratio. The realized non-evaluability rate was low (1/180), well within this conservative allowance.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age 18–80 years; (II) scheduled for thoracoscopic anatomic segmentectomy or lobectomy requiring hemostasis of 5–7 mm vessels; and (III) written informed consent provided.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) platelet count <50×109/L, severe anemia (hemoglobin ≤60 g/L), or active bleeding within the past month; (II) clinically significant abnormalities in coagulation function; (III) cardiac disease [New York Heart Association (NYHA) class ≥III], impaired liver or kidney function [serum creatinine, alanine aminotransferase, or aspartate aminotransferase >2 times the upper limit of normal], autoimmune diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus), or immune dysfunction at screening; (IV) presence of other systemic diseases or local/systemic infections that rendered the patient unsuitable for surgery; (V) pregnant or lactating women; (VI) participation in another interventional clinical trial within 1 month prior to the start of the present trial; and (VII) any other condition deemed by the investigator to make the patient unsuitable for participation.

Withdrawal criteria were as follows: (I) voluntary withdrawal requested by the subject; (II) occurrence of an adverse event (AE) or serious adverse event (SAE) that prevented continued participation; (III) poor compliance or loss to follow-up, resulting in failure to adhere to the trial protocol; (IV) violation of inclusion criteria or meeting any exclusion criteria after enrollment; (V) failure to use the investigational device following randomization; and (VI) discretion of the investigator to terminate participation based on medical or safety considerations.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with double-lumen endotracheal intubation in the lateral decubitus position. Vessels were measured intracorporeally using a sterile calibrated ruler. Vessels exceeding 7 mm were sealed with thoracoscopic linear staplers. In the experimental group, vessels 5–7 mm were sealed using Y20’s “SEAL” mode; in the control group, GEN11CN’s advanced hemostasis mode was used. Surgeons ensured proper vessel positioning before activation and avoided excessive tension. A prophylactic No. 0 suture was loosely looped around the proximal vessel as a rescue safeguard. The vessel was sealed and divided using the energy device alone, with no mechanical contribution from the loop. The loop was to be cinched only as an immediate rescue in the event of hemorrhage from the device-sealed stump. The sealed site was observed for 10 min following transection. All operators received standardized device training, and all procedures were video-recorded. Patients received standard postoperative care per institutional protocols.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was intraoperative vessel sealing success, defined as the absence of hemorrhage from the transected stump during a 10-min observation period—consistent with prior prospective device trials (14-16) and providing an objective, reproducible measure of immediate hemostatic efficacy. This endpoint does not capture delayed bleeding, transfusion need, or reintervention, which constrains external validity. To characterize durability beyond the intraoperative period, postoperative hemorrhagic complications (reintervention, hemothorax requiring drainage, or transfusion) were recorded through discharge and at 30-day follow-up as a supplementary safety outcome.

Secondary outcomes included intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage volume (day 1 and cumulative), operative time (skin incision to chest closure), device performance, and surgeon satisfaction. The operating surgeon evaluated device performance once per case immediately post-procedure using a predetermined instrument encompassing three domains: stability of the intraoperative working status, operability of the functional interface, and intraoperative smoke generation. Each domain was assessed on a 3-level ordinal scale (satisfactory/average/unsatisfactory) with clear behavioral anchors (Table S1). Surgeon satisfaction with cutting and hemostasis performance was likewise rated once per case using two further 3-level ordinal instruments with explicit criteria (Table S2). All instruments were applied uniformly across centers after standardized operator training.

Safety outcomes encompassed laboratory parameters, vital signs, device-related sealing failures, and device-attributable complications.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test; categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. The primary endpoint yielded only one failure event across both arms, so a conventional logistic regression was not estimable. We therefore used a Firth penalized-likelihood logistic regression, adjusting for systolic blood pressure (BP) and platelet count. The Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test was used to adjust for center effects. The Newcombe-Wilson method was applied for the 95% CI of the between-group difference in proportions. All tests were two-sided unless specified otherwise; the non-inferiority hypothesis was tested with a one-sided alpha of 0.025. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Three analysis sets were predefined: the full analysis set (FAS; intention-to-treat), per protocol set (PPS; protocol-compliant subjects), and safety set (SS; all subjects receiving the intervention). Missing primary-endpoint values were to be imputed as failures. No primary-endpoint data were missing (179/179), so this rule was not triggered. Protocol deviations were managed through the analysis-set definitions: the FAS analyzed subjects as randomized, the PPS excluded major deviations, and the non-inferiority conclusion required concordance in both FAS and PPS. Results

Patient flow

Of 196 approached subjects, 180 were enrolled and randomized (90 per group) between July and October 2023 (Figure 1). One control subject was withdrawn due to intraoperative confirmation of a vessel diameter outside the inclusion criteria; one sealing failure occurred, which is described further in the Discussion. The FAS therefore comprised 90 experimental and 89 control subjects.

Patient characteristics

Most baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups (all P>0.05), with the exception of systolic BP and platelet count. Systolic BP was higher in the Y20 group (126.16 vs. 122.16 mmHg; P=0.02), as was platelet count (221.07 vs. 200.73 ×109/L; P=0.03) (Table 1). A post hoc analysis adjusting for both variables did not alter the primary findings (“Primary outcome” section).

Surgical outcomes

Mean vascular diameter was 5.92 mm (range, 5.10–7.00 mm) in the experimental group and 5.97 mm (range, 5.10–7.00 mm) in the control group (P>0.05). Both the experimental and control groups underwent thoracoscopic surgery under general anesthesia. Representative intraoperative photographs illustrating vessel sealing with the Y20 device (Figure 2A-2C) and the GEN11CN device (Figure 2D-2F) are shown.

Intraoperative photographs of vessel sealing with ultrasonic devices. (A-C) Y20 device sealing a pulmonary vessel (~6 mm). (D-F) GEN11CN device performing the same procedure. A prophylactic suture loop is visible per protocol.

The rescue loop was never tightened and no additional hemostatic measures were required within 10 min in the experimental group. One seal failure was recorded in the control group, which was managed by tightening the rescue loop and applying a vascular endostapler at the vessel root (Table S3).

Primary outcome

Both FAS and PPS analyses showed vessel sealing success of 100.00% [95% confidence interval (CI): 95.98–100.00%] in the experimental group and 98.88% (95% CI: 93.90–99.97%) in the control group (Figure 3A). The between-group difference was 1.12% (95% CI: −3.15% to 6.21%); the lower CI bound of −3.15% exceeded the −10% non-inferiority margin (P<0.001) (Figure 3B). After Firth-model adjustment for systolic BP and platelet count, the odds ratio for sealing failure (Y20 vs. control) was 0.67 (95% CI: 0.004–99.7; P=0.83). No significant between-group difference was observed in artery or vein subgroup analyses (Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

Device performance satisfaction was 100% in both groups across all categories. Hemostasis satisfaction was 100% (experimental) versus 98.9% (control; 1 dissatisfied subject; P=0.50) (Figure S1). No significant differences were observed in intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage, or operative time (all P>0.05; Figure 4A-4D).

Safety outcomes

Detailed laboratory and vital sign data are presented in Tables S4,S5. Screening platelet counts were significantly higher in the experimental group [221.07±57.15 (×109/L) vs. 200.73±63.91 (×109/L); P=0.03]. However, both values fell within the normal range. Greater systolic BP reduction was observed in the experimental group on postoperative day 1 (−8.86±13.95 vs. −3.94±14.23 mmHg; P=0.02). All other parameters were comparable.

The safety profile was consistent with the expected outcomes of thoracoscopic resection. Although at least one AE was recorded in all patients in both groups, the overwhelming majority were Grade 1–2 (mild pain, transient fever, or minor wound discomfort) and were attributable to the procedure rather than to device use. Grade ≥3 AEs were uncommon, occurring in 1 (experimental) and 2 (control) patients, with no significant between-group difference. Device-related AEs occurred in 0 (0%) experimental subjects vs. 1 (1.11%) control subject (pleural effusion; P>0.05). One SAE was reported in the experimental group (prolonged hospitalization due to air leak), while two SAEs occurred in the control group (prolonged hospitalization due to abnormal liver function and bleeding from the incision). The between-group difference in SAEs was not statistically significant (P=0.62). Detailed results are provided in Table S6.

Postoperative and 30-day follow-up outcomes

No episodes of postoperative hemorrhage, hemothorax, transfusion, or reintervention for bleeding were observed in either group through discharge or at 30-day follow-up (0/90 vs. 0/89; P>0.99), consistent with the absence of delayed seal failure, although the study was not powered to formally validate the intraoperative endpoint as a long-term surrogate.

Discussion

Key findings

The present multicenter randomized non-inferiority trial demonstrated that Y20 was non-inferior to GEN11CN for intraoperative vessel sealing success during thoracoscopic surgery for 5–7 mm pulmonary vessels. Y20 achieved a 100.00% sealing success rate, with a between-group difference of 1.12% (95% CI: −3.15% to 6.21%), exceeding the prespecified non-inferiority margin of −10%. No postoperative hemorrhagic events were observed through 30-day follow-up. Secondary outcomes—including blood loss, drainage volume, operative time, and surgeon satisfaction—were comparable between groups.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this trial include its multicenter design across three Grade A tertiary centers; pre-specified analyses in three predefined sets (FAS, PPS, SS) following regulatory guidance for non-inferiority device trials; assessor and statistician blinding to mitigate detection and analytical bias; standardized operator training; and complete video documentation of all procedures.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the 10-min intraoperative observation period, although consistent with prior device trials (14-16) and supported by the absence of postoperative hemorrhagic events through 30-day follow-up, does not formally validate long-term seal durability; future studies with longer follow-up are warranted. Second, although the prophylactic loop was non-occlusive during seal formation and made no mechanical contribution to hemostasis, its routine presence as a safety net is not universal in practice. This modestly constrains external validity in settings where such precautions are not standard. Third, surgeon discretion in determining vessel suitability introduced selection that cannot be fully corrected statistically. Fourth, with only one failure across both groups, subgroup analyses and logistic regression are exploratory and hypothesis-generating only; the study was not powered to detect differences in rare AEs. Fifth, the success of blinding was not formally quantified with a blinding index; given the objective, binary nature of the primary endpoint, residual detection bias is expected to be minimal, but this remains a limitation. Sixth, the absence of cost-effectiveness analysis precludes economic comparisons relevant to institutional adoption. Seventh, although the protocol was approved before enrollment and was not modified after data inspection, retrospective registration is a limitation with respect to public transparency.

Comparison with similar research

Before the advent of high-frequency integrated devices, surgeons relied on vascular clips and frequent instrument switching for pulmonary vessel hemostasis. Liberman et al. (16) demonstrated in a prospective multicenter phase II trial that 95.3% of patients achieved hemostasis using ultrasonic energy alone for pulmonary artery branches ≤7 mm, with no postoperative transfusion or reoperation, establishing this approach as clinically valid. Goudie et al. (14,15) reported similar results in earlier phase 1 trials using ultrasonic energy in open and video-assisted thoracoscopic lobectomy. The 100.00% sealing success rate observed for Y20 in the present trial compares favorably with these historical benchmarks. Moreover, GEN11CN, a commercially available ultrasonic high-frequency integrated device widely used in thoracic surgical practice in China, was selected as the reference comparator based on its established clinical adoption and available safety data. To our knowledge, the present multicenter randomized trial is among the first to provide a direct head-to-head comparison of two ultrasonic high-frequency integrated devices for sealing 5–7 mm pulmonary vessels.

Explanations of findings

The Y20 device met the predefined non-inferiority criterion with 100.00% vessel sealing success versus 98.88% in the control group. The single control-group failure was attributed on post-hoc video review to inadvertent device submersion in pleural fluid—an operator-related technical event rather than a device failure. A single failure event across both arms precludes any informative covariate adjustment: the Firth-penalized estimate (odds ratio 0.67; 95% CI: 0.004–99.7) spans several orders of magnitude and should be read only as a formal robustness check, not as evidence of an effect size. Interpretive weight therefore rests on the rarity of failure itself rather than on this estimate. Moreover, both imbalanced variables were higher in the Y20 group, and higher systolic pressure would, if anything, bias against Y20 by increasing stump-wall stress. We present this as a supporting consideration, not as proof that confounding is absent. No significant differences were observed in operative time, blood loss, drainage volume, or SAE distribution. Surgeon satisfaction was high in both groups, although ceiling effects and the subjective nature of satisfaction scales limit interpretation of these findings. The comparable performance of Y20 and GEN11CN likely reflects similar underlying engineering principles: both devices integrate high-frequency electrocautery with ultrasonic vibration to achieve coaptive vessel sealing through simultaneous coagulative and mechanical denaturation of vessel-wall proteins.

Implications and actions needed

The present findings support Y20 as a clinically acceptable alternative to GEN11CN for sealing 5–7 mm pulmonary vessels during thoracoscopic anatomic resection, expanding the available instrument options for thoracic surgeons. Clinical adoption decisions should also consider local cost, availability, and operator familiarity. Future studies should evaluate longer follow-up durations to formally establish long-term seal durability, perform formal cost-effectiveness analyses, and explore performance in larger and more heterogeneous vessel subsets, including vessels >7 mm or in patients with prior thoracic radiation, pleural adhesions, or hilar lymphadenopathy. Equivalence between the two devices should not be inferred from the present results; the non-inferiority conclusion is specific to the primary endpoint and the evaluated patient population.

Conclusions

In this multicenter randomized non-inferiority trial, Y20 was noninferior to GEN11CN for intraoperative sealing of 5–7 mm pulmonary vessels and showed a comparable safety profile, supporting Y20 as a clinically acceptable alternative for this specific indication. These findings should not be generalized to vessels >7 mm or to settings without immediate rescue capability.
