Abstract
. In this paper we study the range of possible almost sure dimensions of random measures arising from a natural model of random Moran measures. Specifically, we consider the Assouad-like “large” Φ \Phi -dimensions of these measures. These dimensions can be tuned to consider a specific range of depths in scale and so provide refined local geometric information. The quasi-Assouad dimension is a well-known and important example of a “large” Φ \Phi -dimension. We determine the range of possible almost sure Φ \Phi -dimensions for random measures generated by the model and are supported on any given random Moran set. We do this for both the case when the probability weights depend on the scaling factors and the case when they do not. In the later situation, we show that usually there is a “gap” between the dimension of the set and that of the smallest attainable upper dimension and largest attainable lower dimension. As a consequence of our results, we also determine the a.s. dimensions of the underlying random Moran set.

Introduction

Random constructions have been investigated in the fractal geometry literature for many years
(see
[
6
,
10
,
11
,
16
,
17
,
20
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
), with a common focus being the dimensional properties of the resulting random objects.
The random context often helps to illuminate the robustness of the dimension in illustrating how fluctuations might change or not
change the dimension from that of a related deterministic model.
In this paper we continue our examination of the possible dimensions of random Moran sets and measures which arise from random iterated function
systems (or 1-variable random models).
The random set and measure are constructed by an iterative Moran construction where the contractions and probability weighting factors are randomly chosen at each step.
As with most such constructions, there are almost sure values for the dimensions of the resulting random set and measure.
The almost sure Hausdorff dimension of the random set was determined in
[
17
]
(see also
[
3
]
) and in
[
30
]
it was shown that the Hausdorff and upper box dimensions coincide almost surely for these random sets.

The Assouad and Assouad-like dimensions, in contrast with the Hausdorff and box dimensions, are defined to assess the most extreme local scaling behaviour of a set or measure and are thus sensitive to small local changes.
These dimensions all appear in dual “upper” and “lower” versions, similar to the pairing of the upper and lower box dimensions.
The Assouad dimensions have seen a surge of applications in fractal geometry in recent years (see
[
2
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
14
,
18
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
24
,
28
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
), in part because of their ability to quantify the inhomogeneity of a set.
The most general of these Assouad-like dimensions are the
\Phi
-dimensions, first introduced in
[
12
]
and then extensively studied in
[
14
]
.
These use a function,
\Phi
, to control the ratio of two scales at which to asymptotically compare the local “size” of the set or measure.
By varying
\Phi
one can get a precise quantification of the extreme local scaling behaviour of the set or measure under consideration.
The Assouad, quasi-Assouad and
\theta
-Assouad spectrum are important examples of
\Phi
-dimensions.
There are “large” and “small”
\Phi
dimensions, roughly depending on how separated are the two comparison scales (see the formal definition in the next section); the Assouad dimension is in this sense small, while the quasi-Assouad and
\theta
-Assouad spectrum are large.

Following on the work started in
[
10
,
30
,
31
]
, the authors of
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
investigated the
\Phi
-dimensions of these random Moran sets and measures.
In
[
20
,
21
]
they determined formulas for the almost sure dimensions of the random measures under various different scenarios on the model.
The analysis is different for the small and large
\Phi
functions, as well as for the upper and lower versions of the dimensions.
The same threshold phenomena of small versus large was also found in previous work
[
14
,
32
]
.

In this paper we examine the range of attainable large
\Phi
dimensions of random measures on a given random set, following up on a similar examination for the attainable small
\Phi
in
[
22
]
.
More specifically, imagine that we are given a random iterated function system which will produce a random set
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.
Based on this, we now add a model for randomly choosing probability weights at each step of the construction.
This will produce a random probability measure
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
supported on
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.
It is known (see
[
18
]
) that the
\Phi
dimension of
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
bounds that of
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, but which of the allowable

\dim_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
are attainable?
The answer turns out to depend on the details of the underlying random process.
If the choice of probability weights can be dependent on
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
then the entire allowable range is achievable.
On the other hand, if the probability weights are independent of
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
then there is usually a “gap” between
the almost sure values of
\dim_{\Phi}F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
\dim_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.
However, the set of attainable values for
\dim_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is always an interval, contains the minimal (or maximal) value, and
contains values arbitrarily close to
\infty
(respectively,
0
).

Section
2
presents the necessary background on
\Phi
dimensions and
Section
3
presents the details of the random model.
Section
4
proves technical results about the machinery necessary to obtain the
\Phi
dimensions where the main tools

(for the lower dimensions).
We prove that the
\Phi
dimension is an extremal value of

(or

) in Theorem
6
and
in Theorem

show that it is the only place where
G
(or
G^{\prime}
) crosses the diagonal.
We also give some conditions under which the dimension is a continuous
function of the parameters of the model in Propositions
10
and
18
.

The main results of the paper are given in Sections
5
and
6
which study the set of attainable a.s. values for
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.
Section
5
concentrates on models where the probability weights are allowed to depend on the random scaling factors (the “dependent” case) and here
Theorems

indicate that the entire possible range is attainable.
Specifically, with

the almost sure Hausdorff dimension of

, we have that
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

(0,D]

\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

As a consequence, we get that
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\dim_{H}F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

By contrast, in Section

Theorem

Additionally, Propositions

\inf\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

We mention that all of our results are given in the context of subsets of
[0,1]

This restriction is not necessary and it is simple to extend the results to compact subsets of

.

2.
Assouad-like Dimensions: Definitions and Basic Properties

Let
X
be a bounded metric space. We will write
\ B(x,R)
for the open ball
centred at
x
belonging to the bounded metric space
X
and radius
R
, and
denote by
{\mathcal{N}}_{r}(E)
the least number of open balls of radius
r

required to cover
E\subseteq X
. By a measure on
X
we mean a Borel
probability measure supported on
X
.

Definition 1
.

A
dimension function
is a map
\Phi:(0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the property that

t^{1+\Phi(t)}
decreases to
0
as
t
decreases to
0
and is doubling near zero, that is, there are
T,C>0

so that
(2t)^{1+\Phi(2t)}\leq C\cdot t^{1+\Phi(t)}
for all
0<t<T
.
We will say that a dimension function
\Phi
is
large
if

\Phi(t)=H(t)\frac{\log\left|\log t\right|}{\left|\log t\right|}

where
H(t)\rightarrow\infty
as
t\rightarrow 0
and
small
if
(with the same notation)
H(t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow 0
.

Examples of large dimension functions include the constant functions
\Phi(t)=c>0
, as well as the functions

1/|\log t|^{\alpha}
and
(\log\left|\log t\right|)^{1/\alpha}/\left|\log t\right|

for
\alpha<1
. The function
\Phi=0
and the functions
1/|\log t|^{\alpha}
and
(\log\left|\log t\right|)^{1/\alpha}/\left|\log t\right|
for
\alpha>1
are some examples of small
dimension functions. We are primarily interested in large dimension
functions in this paper.
The doubling assumption is met, for instance, if
\Phi
is non-decreasing and bounded near
t=0
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\Phi
be a dimension function.

(i) The
upper
and
lower

\Phi
-dimensions
of
the set
E\subseteq X
are given, respectively, by

\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}E=\inf\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}d:(\exists C_{1},C_{2}>0)(\forall 0<r\leq R^{1+\Phi(R)}<R<C_{1})\text{}\\
{\mathcal{N}}_{r}(B(z,R)\bigcap E)\leq C_{2}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{d}\text{ }\forall z\in E\end{array}\right\}

and

\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}E=\sup\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}d:(\exists C_{1},C_{2}>0)(\forall 0<r\leq R^{1+\Phi(R)}<R<C_{1})\text{}\\
{\mathcal{N}}_{r}(B(z,R)\bigcap E)\geq C_{2}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{d}\text{ }\forall z\in E\end{array}\right\}.

(ii) The
upper and lower

\Phi
-dimensions
of the measure
\mu
on
X
are given, respectively, by

\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu=\inf\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}d:(\exists C_{1},C_{2}>0)(\forall 0<r<R^{1+\Phi(R)}\leq R\leq C_{1})\\
\frac{\mu(B(x,R))}{\mu(B(x,r))}\leq C_{2}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{d}\text{ }\forall x\in\supp\mu\end{array}\right\}

and

\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu=\sup\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}d:(\exists C_{1},C_{2}>0)(\forall 0<r<R^{1+\Phi(R)}\leq R\leq C_{1})\\
\frac{\mu(B(x,R))}{\mu(B(x,r))}\geq C_{2}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{d}\text{ }\forall x\in\supp\mu\end{array}\right\}.

In the special case of the small dimension function
\Phi=0
, the upper and lower
\Phi
-dimensions are known as the
upper
and

lower Assouad dimensions
(or the Assouad dimension and lower dimension) and
denoted
\dim_{A}\Theta
and
\dim_{L}\Theta
respectively, for sets or measures
\Theta
.
The Assouad dimensions of sets were first introduced in
[
1
]
and have been extensively studied since.
We refer the reader to Fraser’s book,
[
8
]
, and the many references cited there-in.
The Assouad dimensions of measures are also known as the upper and lower regularity dimensions and were studied in
[
9
,
24
,
25
]
.
The Assouad dimensions are important both because of their extremal properties (see the Proposition below), as well as the fact that a measure
\mu
is doubling if and only if
\dim_{A}\mu<\infty
(
[
9
]
) and uniformly perfect if and only if
\dim_{L}\mu>0
(
[
24
]
).
We warn the reader that some sources (like
[
2
]
) use a slightly different definition and instead require that
r=R^{1+\Phi(R)}
, which
leads to subtle differences.

The
\Phi
-dimensions of sets were introduced by Fraser and Yu in
[
12
]
who focussed on the
upper
and

lower

\theta
-Assouad spectrum
which are essentially the special case of
\Phi

dimensions arising from the large dimension functions
\Phi_{\theta}=1/\theta-1
for
0<\theta<1
.
From their limiting behaviour one obtains the
upper
and
lower quasi-Assouad dimensions
of sets,
introduced in
[
27
]
and defined as

\dim_{qA}E=\lim_{\theta\rightarrow 1}\overline{\dim}_{\Phi_{\theta}}E\text{ and }\dim_{qL}E=\lim_{\theta\rightarrow 1}\underline{\dim}_{\Phi_{\theta}}E.

General
\Phi
-dimensions for sets were first extensively investigated in
[
14
]
.

Motivated by these dimensions for sets, the upper and lower quasi-Assouad dimensions of measures (defined analogously) were studied in
[
19
,
23
]
and the most general
\Phi
-dimensions of measures were introduced in
[
18
]
.

The ‘large’ versus ‘small’ dimension dichotomy corresponds to the threshold phenomena which is observed in the almost sure values of the
\Phi
dimensions of certain random Moran sets and measures in
[
21
]
, and as well as in other random iid constructions found in
[
15
]
and
[
32
]
.

One obvious property of the
\Phi
dimensions is their ordering.

Proposition 1
.

If
\Phi(t)\leq\Psi(t)
for all
t>0
, then whether
\Theta
is a set or measure

\overline{\dim}_{\Psi}\Theta\leq\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\Theta\ \text{ and }\ \underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\Theta\leq\underline{\dim}_{\Psi}\Theta.

Other well known relationships between these dimensions are stated below.
Proofs can be found in
[
12
,
14
,
18
]
and the references cited therein.

Proposition 2
.

Let
E
be a set,
\mu
be a measure and
\Phi
be any dimension function.
We denote by
\underline{\dim}_{B}
(
\overline{\dim}_{B}
) the lower (upper) box dimension, by
\dim_{H}
the Hausdorff dimension
and by
\overline{\dim}_{M}
the upper Minkowski dimension.

(i) Then

\dim_{L}E\leq\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}E\leq\underline{\dim}_{B}E\leq\overline{\dim}_{B}E\leq\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}E\leq\dim_{A}E,

and

\displaystyle\dim_{L}\mu

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu\leq\dim_{H}\mu\leq\dim_{H}\supp\mu

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\ \supp\mu\leq\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu\leq\dim_{A}\mu.

(ii) If
\mu
is a doubling measure, then

\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu\leq\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\supp\mu.

(iii) If
\Theta
is either a set or measure and
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}

\Phi(t)=0
, then

\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\Theta\leq\dim_{qL}\Theta\ \text{ and }\ \dim_{qA}\Theta\leq\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\Theta.

(iv) If
\Phi(t)\rightarrow\infty
as
t\rightarrow 0
, then
\overline{\dim}_{B}E=\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}E
and
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu=\overline{\dim}_{M}\mu
.

In general, the inequalities above can be strict.
Furthermore, part (iv) of the proposition is not true for the lower dimension as can be seen by the following example. Take
E=C\cup[2,3]
with
C\subset[0,1]
the standard
1/3
Cantor set.
Then
\underline{\dim}_{B}E=1
but
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}E=\ln(2)/\ln(3)
.

Another interesting fact (see
[
18
]
) is that given any
0<\alpha<\beta<1
, there is a central Cantor set
E\subset[0,1]
such that if
\mu
is the uniform Cantor measure supported on
E
, then

\left\{\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}E:\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\Phi=0\right\}=\left\{\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu:\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\Phi=0\right\}=[\alpha,\beta]=[\dim_{qA}E,\dim_{A}E].

The ‘large’ versus ‘small’ dimension threshold is of interest because in
several random iid constructions (see
[
15
,
21
,
32
]
) it has been
observed that the almost sure values of the large
\Phi
dimensions
coincide with the quasi-Assouad dimensions while the almost sure small
\Phi
dimensions coincide with the Assouad dimensions.

3.
The random Moran sets and measures

3.1.
Description of the Model

The model for the random Moran sets and measures that we study in this paper
is the same as that studied in
[
20
,
21
]
.
We outline the description here for the convenience of the reader.

Fix the integer
K\geq 2
and constants
A,B,\tau>0
with
0<K\cdot A\leq B<1
and
\tau\leq(1-B)/K
.

We start with a collection
{\mathcal{I}}_{u}=\{S_{1}(u),S_{2}(u),\ldots,S_{K}(u)\}
, indexed by
u\in{\mathcal{A}}
, of IFSs consisting of
K
similarities
acting on
[0,1]
.
We denote the scaling ratio of
S_{i}(u)
by
a(i,u)
and assume that for all
u\in{\mathcal{A}}

(3.1)

A\leq\min_{1\leq i\leq K}a(i,u)<\sum_{i=1}^{K}a(i,u)\leq B.

We further assume that

dist(S_{i}(u)[0,1],\,S_{j}(u)[0,1])\geq\tau\text{ for all }i\neq j\text{ and all }u,

so each
{\mathcal{I}}_{u}
is a strongly separated IFS.

Next, for each
v\in{\mathcal{W}}
, we have a probability weight vector with components
p(i,v)
for
i=1,2,\ldots,K
.
For each
v\in{\mathcal{W}}
these satisfy

(3.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{K}p(i,v)=1\quad\text{ and }\quad p(i,v)\geq 0\text{ for all }i=1,2,\ldots,K.

The set
{\mathcal{A}}
indexes all the IFSs that could potentially be used in our model and
{\mathcal{W}}
all the probability weights that could potentially be used.

We set
\Lambda={\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{W}}
and place a probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}
on
\Lambda
.
For simplicity, for
\lambda=(u,v)\in\Lambda
we will often write
p(i,\lambda)
for
p(i,v)
and
a(i,\lambda)
instead of
a(i,u)
.

For technical reasons we require that there be some
t>0
so that
{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(p(i,\lambda)^{-t})<\infty
for each
i=1,2,\ldots,K
.
In particular, this guarantees that the probability that any
p(i,\lambda)=0
or
1
is zero.
In the special case when
{\mathcal{W}}
is a single point (we call this the
single probability
case) the
requirement simply says
p(j)\neq 0
for any
j=1,...,K
, and we refer to these probabilities as the
valid probabilities
in this case.

Our random Moran sets and measures are constructed through an iid sequence
of choices of scaling ratios and probability weights,
meaning that
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n})\in\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
is chosen according to the infinite product measure
{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
This ensures that all pairs
\{a(i,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n}),p(j,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n})\}
are independent of
\{a(k,{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}_{m}),p(l,{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}_{m})\}
for
n\neq m
.

3.1.1.
Dependent/Independent probabilities

A natural and particularly interesting example of our setup is when
\Lambda

is a product of probability spaces, that is
{\mathbb{P}}
is a product of probability measures on
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{W}}
respectively.
Then the contraction factors, which depend only on
u
, and the probabilities, which depend only on

v
, are independent of each other.
We refer to this special situation as the
independent probabilities
case to distinguish it from the

dependent probabilities
case.
As we will see later in this paper, the dimensional properties of the random measures (defined below) are quite
different in the two cases.

3.1.2.
Random Moran set

Given
{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\in\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
we put

I_{i_{1},...,i_{n}}({{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})=S_{i_{1}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1})\circ\cdots\circ S_{i_{n}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n})[0,1],

a
step (or level)

n
Moran interval
.
The step
n+1
Moran intervals,
I_{i_{1},..i_{n},j}({{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})
for
j=1,...,K
, are called the
children
of the step
n
Moran interval
I_{i_{1},...,i_{n}}({{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})
.
We note that the length of
I_{i_{1},...,i_{n}}({{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})
, denoted

\left|I_{i_{1},...,i_{n}}({{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})\right|
, is equal to the product
a(i_{1},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}_{1})\cdots a(i_{n},{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}_{n})

and the distance between any two children of
I_{i_{1},...,i_{n}}({{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})
is at least
\tau\left|I_{i_{1},...,i_{n}}({{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}})\right|
.
Corresponding to each
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
is the
random Moran set (or attractor)

F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\bigcap_{n}\bigcup_{i_{1},...,i_{n}\in\{1,...,K\}}I_{i_{1},...,i_{n}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}).

When
{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\in\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
is clear we may write
I_{n}(x)

for the unique step
n
Moran interval that contains
x\in F_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
.
We remark that the assumptions on the contraction factors ensure that

A^{n}\leq\frac{\left|I_{N}(x)\right|}{\left|I_{N+n}(x)\right|}\leq B^{n}.

3.1.3.
Random Measure

The
random probability measure

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is almost surely supported on the random set
F_{\mathbf{\lambda}}
and is
defined by the rules
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}([0,1])=1
and if
\nu=(i_{1},...,i_{n})
then for each
j\in\{1,...,K\}

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(I_{\nu,j}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}))=\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(I_{\nu}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}))p(j,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n+1})=p(i_{1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1})\cdot\cdot\cdot p(i_{n},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n})p(j,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n+1}).

Notice that the random Moran set
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
depends only on the IFSs
\{{\mathcal{I}}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\}
, while the random measure depends on both the IFSs
and the probabilities
\{{\mathcal{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\}
.
By varying the probabilities we obtain a family of random measures all of which are supported on
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
almost surely.

For those familiar with V-variable fractals (c.f. [2]),
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is a 1-variable fractal and
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
a 1-variable fractal measure.

4.
Determining the large
\Phi
-dimensions

4.1.
The basic dimension theorem

As shown in Lemma 2 of
[
21
]
(see also arXiv:2207.14654 for more details), the uniform separation satisfied by the Moran sets makes it easy to establish that the
\Phi
dimensions
(for both large and small
\Phi
) of both the random set
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and the random measure
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
can be computed
using only the lengths and measures of the Moran intervals.
We remark that the doubling assumption from Def.
1
is not needed to establish this for the upper dimensions.
Using this Lemma, a simple formula was found for the
\Phi
dimensions of the random measures for small dimension functions
\Phi
in
[
21
, Theorem 21]
.

The problem of determining the
\Phi
dimensions for large
\Phi
is much more intricate, however, because of the depth of scales which must be considered.
In
[
21
]
it was shown that we can understand these dimensions in terms of properties of functions, denoted
G
and
G^{\prime}
, which are
ratios of expected values of logarithms of probabilities to logarithms of scaling ratios.
As we make extensive use of the functions
G
and
G^{\prime}
in this paper, we state their definitions here.

Notation:
Given our model as described above, we define the following functions with common
domain
(\theta,\lambda)\in[0,\infty)\times\Lambda

\displaystyle Y(\theta,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\log p(m,\lambda),

\displaystyle\qquad Y^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\log p(m^{\prime},\lambda)

\displaystyle Z(\theta,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\log a(m,\lambda),

\displaystyle\qquad Z^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\log a(m^{\prime},\lambda)

where
m=m(\theta,\lambda)\in\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
is the minimal index which satisfies the condition

(4.1)

\frac{a(m,\lambda)^{\theta}}{p(m,\lambda)}=\max\left(\frac{a(j,\lambda)^{\theta}}{p(j,\lambda)}:j=1,...,K\right)

and
m^{\prime}=m^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda)
is the minimal index which satisfies

(4.2)

\frac{a(m^{\prime},\lambda)^{\theta}}{p(m^{\prime},\lambda)}=\min\left(\frac{a(j,\lambda)^{\theta}}{p(j,\lambda)}:j=1,...,K\right).

Then define

(4.3)

G(\theta)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(Y(\theta,\lambda))}{{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(Z(\theta,\lambda))}\quad\text{ and }\quad G^{\prime}(\theta)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(Y^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda))}{{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(Z^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda))}

where the notation
{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}
means that the expectation is taken with respect to
\lambda\in\Lambda
using the measure
{\mathbb{P}}
.
However, as we will only be taking expectations with respect to
\lambda
we will usually omit the subscript.
Note that with
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
, each family,
\{Y(\theta,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n})\}
,

\{Z(\theta,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n})\}
,
\{Y^{\prime}(\theta,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n})\}
and
\{Z^{\prime}(\theta,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{n})\}
, is an independent and identically distributed sequence of random variables.

Here is simple fact about these functions.

Proposition 3
.

Assume the random model as given above.

(i) All four quantities
\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(Y(\theta,\lambda))\right|
,
\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(Y^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda))\right|
,
\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(Z(\theta,\lambda))\right|
and

\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(Z^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda))\right|
are bounded above and bounded away from
0
.

(ii) Both
G(\theta)
and
G^{\prime}(\theta)
are bounded above and bounded away from
0
.

Proof.

(i) Since
A\leq a(i,\lambda)\leq B
for all
i
and
\lambda
, we have
|\log(B)|\leq|\log(a(i,\lambda))|\leq|\log(A)|
.
That proves the claim about
\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(Z(\theta,\lambda))\right|
and

\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(Z^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda))\right|
.

As the probabilities
p(i,\lambda)
can only take on the value
1
with probability zero, we know that

{\mathbb{P}}(\{\lambda:\max_{i}p(i,\lambda)=1\})=0.

Thus there is a
\delta>0
so that if
\Lambda_{\delta}=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\max_{i}p(i,\lambda)\leq 1-\delta\}

then
{\mathbb{P}}(\Lambda_{\delta})\geq\eta
for some
\eta>0
.
Notice that
Y(\theta,\lambda)\leq\log(1-\delta)
for any
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\delta}
and all
\theta
.
Using this we get that

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}(Y(\theta,\lambda))\right|

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\{Y(\theta,\lambda)\leq\log(1-\delta)\}}\hskip-56.9055pt-Y(\theta,\lambda)\ d{\mathbb{P}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Lambda_{\delta}}\left|\log(1-\delta)\right|\ d{\mathbb{P}}(\lambda)\geq\eta\left|\log(1-\delta)\right|\geq\delta\eta.

The argument is the same for
\left|{\mathbb{E}}(Y^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda))\right|
.

By our assumptions on the probabilities, there exists some
t<0
such that
{\mathbb{E}}(p(i)^{-t})<\infty
for
i=1,2,\ldots,K
. Since

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{-\log(x)}{x^{-t}}=0

there is an
0<\eta<1
so that whenever
0<p\leq\eta
we have
|\log(p)|\leq p^{-t}
.
But then, for all
\theta
,

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}(Y(\theta,\lambda))|

\displaystyle\leq\int\sum_{i=1}^{K}|\log(p(i,\lambda))|\ d{\mathbb{P}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(\int_{p(i,\lambda)\leq\eta}p(i,\lambda)^{-t}\ d{\mathbb{P}}(\lambda)+\int_{p(i,\lambda)>\eta}-\log(p(i,\lambda))\ d{\mathbb{P}}(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\mathbb{E}}(p(i,\lambda)^{-t})+\sum_{i=1}^{K}-\log(\eta)<\infty.

The argument is the same for
|{\mathbb{E}}(Y^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda))|
.

(ii) The boundedness of
G
and
G^{\prime}
(above and below) follow immediately from (i).
∎

Theorem 6

\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\inf_{\theta\geq 0}G^{\prime}(\theta)

[

We provide a sketch for the benefit of the reader.

For each

and thus

which implies that

for all
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\Omega_{\delta}^{\epsilon}
,

and

sufficiently large.

\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\geq d
almost surely.

The proof for the lower dimension is similar.

The next technical lemma will be useful to us.
It roughly indicates that the choices of indices in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) are the extremes.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\chi_{n}:\Lambda\to\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
be iid rv’s and let

H(\chi)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log p(\chi,\lambda))}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(\chi,\lambda))}.

Then

\inf_{\theta}G^{\prime}(\theta)\leq H(\chi)\leq\sup_{\theta}G(\theta).

Remark 1
.

Of course, in the special case that
\chi(\lambda)=m(\theta,\lambda)

(or,
m^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda)
) then
H(\chi)=G(\theta)
(resp.,

G^{\prime}(\theta)
).

Proof.

Given
\lambda,
consider the Moran intervals which arise by choosing the
\chi_{n}(\lambda)
’th - child at step
n
.
Notice these Moran intervals are nested and we will denote the interval arising at step
n
as

I_{n}^{(\lambda)}
.
For
n>N
we have

\frac{\mu_{\lambda}(I_{N}^{(\lambda)})}{\mu_{\lambda}(I_{n}^{(\lambda)})}=\left(\prod_{i=N+1}^{n}p(\chi_{i}(\lambda),\lambda)\right)^{-1}

and

\frac{\left|I_{N}^{(\lambda)}\right|}{\left|I_{n}^{(\lambda)}\right|}=\left(\prod_{i=N+1}^{n}a(\chi_{i}(\lambda),\lambda)\right)^{-1}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. For
N
,
n>N
sufficiently large we have

\frac{\mu_{\lambda}(I_{N}^{(\lambda)})}{\mu_{\lambda}(I_{n}^{(\lambda)})}\leq\left(\frac{\left|I_{N}^{(\lambda)}\right|}{\left|I_{n}^{(\lambda)}\right|}\right)^{\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu+\varepsilon},

thus for all
\lambda

\displaystyle-\log\prod_{i=N+1}^{n}p(\chi_{i}(\lambda),\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-(\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\lambda}+\varepsilon)\log\prod_{i=N+1}^{n}a(\chi_{i}(\lambda),\lambda).

Equivalently,

\frac{\sum_{i=N+1}^{n}\log p(\chi_{i}(\lambda),\lambda)}{\sum_{i=N+1}^{n}\log a(\chi_{i}(\lambda),\lambda)}\leq\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}+\epsilon.

But, as
n\rightarrow\infty
the left hand side of the inequality above converges almost surely to
H(\chi)
while the right hand side is equal to
\sup_{\theta}G(\theta)+\epsilon
almost surely.
Since the inequality holds for all
\varepsilon>0
we conclude that
H(\chi)\leq\sup_{\theta}G(\theta)
.

A symmetric argument gives the proof of the lower bound.
∎

Our next result uses this lemma to show that
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu
is also a solution to
G(t)=t
.

Theorem 9
.

(i)
\sup_{\chi}H(\chi)=\sup_{\theta}G(\theta)=\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
a.s.

(ii)
t=\sup_{\chi}H(\chi)
if and only if
G(t)=t

(iii)
\inf_{\chi}H(\chi)=\inf_{\theta}G^{\prime}(\theta)=\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
a.s.

(iv)
t=\inf_{\chi}H(\chi)
if and only if
G^{\prime}(t)=t

Proof.

Statements (i) and (iii) are immediate from Lemma
8
.

(ii) First, suppose
t=G(t)
.
Then
t\leq\sup_{\theta}G(\theta)=\sup_{\chi}H(\chi)
so we only need prove the other inequality.

To shorten notation, we will write
m_{n}=m(t,\lambda_{n})
and
\chi_{n}=\chi(\lambda_{n})
.

We first observe that for any
\chi

\lim_{N}\log\prod_{n=1}^{N}a(\chi_{n})^{t/N}=t\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log a(\chi_{n})=t{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(\chi))\text{ for a.a. }\lambda

and since the exponential function is continuous it follows that
\lim_{N}\prod_{n=1}^{N}a(\chi_{n})^{t/N}
exists a.s.
Furthermore, this limit is bounded away from
0
as the contraction factors are bounded away from
0
.
Similarly,
\lim_{N}\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(\chi_{n})^{1/N}
exists a.s.

Since
t=G(t)
we have
t{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(m))={\mathbb{E}}(\log p(m))
.
Consequently, almost surely

\lim_{N}\log\prod_{n=1}^{N}a(m_{n})^{t/N}=\lim_{N}\frac{t}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log a(m_{n})=\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p(m_{n})=\lim_{N}\log\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(m_{n})^{1/N}

and therefore

\lim_{N}\prod_{n=1}^{N}a(m_{n})^{t/N}=\lim_{N}\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(m_{n})^{1/N},

equivalently,

1=\lim_{N}\left(\prod_{n=1}^{N}\frac{p(m_{n})}{a(m_{n})^{t}}\right)^{1/N}.

But by the definition of
m
,

\frac{p(m_{n})}{a(m_{n})^{t}}\leq\frac{p(\chi_{n})}{a(\chi_{n})^{t}}\text{for all }\chi,

thus

1\leq\lim_{N}\left(\prod_{n=1}^{N}\frac{p(\chi_{n})}{a(\chi_{n})^{t}}\right)^{1/N}

and that implies

\lim_{N}\prod_{n=1}^{N}a(\chi_{n})^{t/N}\leq\lim_{N}\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(\chi_{n})^{1/N}.

Taking logarithms we deduce that

t{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(\chi))=\lim_{N}\frac{t}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log a(\chi_{n})\leq\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p(\chi_{n})={\mathbb{E}}(\log p(\chi))\text{ a.s.}

hence

t\geq{\mathbb{E}}(\log p(\chi))/{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(\chi))=H(\chi).

Since this holds for all
\chi
it follows that
t\geq\sup_{\chi}H(\chi)

as we desired to show.

Now assume that
t=\sup_{\chi}H(\chi)=\sup_{\theta}G(\theta)
, but that
t\neq G(t)
.
In that case,
G(t)=t-\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
.
But then for a.a.
\lambda
and for
N
sufficiently large

t-\varepsilon\geq\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p(m_{n})}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log a(m_{n})}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Hence
(t-\varepsilon/2)\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log a(m_{n})\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p(m_{n})

for large enough
N,
whence
\prod_{n=1}^{N}a(m_{n})^{t-\varepsilon/2}\leq\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(m_{n})
,
or, equivalently,

\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{N}a(m_{n})^{t}}{\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(m_{n})}\leq\prod_{n=1}^{N}a(m_{n})^{\varepsilon/2}.

It follows from the definition of
m
that for any
\chi
we have

\prod_{n=1}^{N}a(\chi_{n})^{t}\leq\prod_{n=1}^{N}a(m_{n})^{\varepsilon/2}\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(\chi_{n})

and thus

\frac{t}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log a(\chi_{n})\leq\frac{\varepsilon/2}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log a(m_{n})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p(\chi_{n}).

Taking limits we deduce that

t{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(\chi))\leq(\varepsilon/2){\mathbb{E}}(\log a(m))+{\mathbb{E}}(\log p(\chi)).

Since the contraction factors are bounded away from
0,1
there is some constant
C>0

such that
{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(m))/{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(\chi))\geq C>0
for all
\chi
.
Thus

t\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(m))}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(\chi))}+\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log p(\chi))}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(\chi))}\geq\frac{C\varepsilon}{2}+H(\chi)\text{ for all }\chi

and therefore
t\geq C\varepsilon/2+\sup_{\chi}H(\chi)
which is a
contradiction.

The proof of (iv) is similar using the obvious modifications.

Proposition 10
.

Suppose that there are only finitely many sets of scaling factors and finitely many probability weight vectors.
Then
\sup_{\theta}G(\theta)
and
\inf_{\theta}G^{\prime}(\theta)
are continuous as a function of the scaling factors and probability weights and thus
so are
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.

Proof.

Using the notation from the proof of Theorem
9
we know
\sup_{\theta}G(\theta)=\sup_{\chi}H(\chi)
and
\inf_{\theta}G^{\prime}(\theta)=\inf_{\chi}H(\chi)
.
Since there are only finitely many possible
\chi
and each
H(\chi)
is continuous in its parameters, so are
\sup_{\chi}H(\chi)
and
\inf_{\chi}H(\chi)
.

The almost sure Hausdorff dimension of the random set
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
arising from the contraction factors
\{a(i,\lambda)\}
is the unique solution
x\geq 0
to the equation (see
[
17
]
)

We first note that for any function

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log\left(\dfrac{a(\chi(\lambda),\lambda)^{D}}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}a(i,\lambda)^{D}}\right))}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(\chi(\lambda),\lambda))}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(D\log a(\chi(\lambda),\lambda))-{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i=1}^{K}a(i,\lambda)^{D})}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(\chi(\lambda),\lambda))}

\displaystyle=D-0,

Noting that

\displaystyle G(D)-D

5.
The range of dimensions for dependent probabilities

In this section and the next we study the dimensional properties of all the random
measures which arise on a given random set.
That is, having fixed a set of random scaling factors which produce a random set
E
, we wish to investigate all the
possible dimensions of the measures supported on
E
that we could get by using random probability weights.
Since we want to allow any possible set of probability weights we will take

{\mathcal{W}}=\{p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}:p_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i}p_{i}=1\}.

The weights which are used in a given situation are those in the support of the measure
{\mathbb{P}}
.
In addition, this means that we fix
{\mathcal{A}}
, the similarities
\{I_{u}\}
, and the distribution
{\mathcal{Q}}
on
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Thus we consider all probability measures
{\mathbb{P}}
on
\Lambda={\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{W}}
which have
{\mathcal{Q}}
as its marginal distribution on
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Recall that we impose on
{\mathbb{P}}
the condition
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}(p(i,\lambda)^{-t})<\infty
for some
t>0
.

Recall that we use
D
to denote the almost sure Hausdorff dimension of the random set
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
(see Eq.
4.5
).
Notice that if
a(i,u)=a(j,u)
almost surely for all
i,j
, then
D=-\log K/{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(1,\lambda))
.

From Proposition
11
we know that for any random measure
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
supported on
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
we have

\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\geq D
and
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\leq D
.
In this section and the next we address the question as to whether the full interval
[D,\infty)
(or
(0,D]
) can be attained as the
upper (resp., lower)
\Phi
-dimension of one of these random measures.
The answer turns out to be yes when the probabilities are allowed to depend on the contraction factors, but not in general in the case of independent probabilities.
In both situations the minimal (resp. maximal) possible measure dimension is attained by some random measure and this measure can arise from a single choice of probability weights in the independent case.

We begin with the upper dimension.

Theorem 13
.

For any
d\in[D,\infty)
, there is a choice of probabilities
p(i,\lambda)
so
that almost surely
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=d
.

Proof.

With no loss of generality we assume that the indexing is such that
a(1,\lambda)\geq a(2,\lambda)\geq\cdots\geq a(K,\lambda)

for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

First, suppose that
{\mathbb{P}}(\{\lambda:a(1,\lambda)>a(K,\lambda)\})>0
.
Under this assumption, there is some
\delta>0
so that

{\mathbb{P}}(\lambda:\{a(1,\lambda)\geq(1+\delta)a(K,\lambda)\})>0
.
Choose
t\geq D
and for
j=1,2,\ldots,K
set

(5.1)

p(j,\lambda)=\frac{a(j,\lambda)^{t}}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}a(i,\lambda)^{t}}.

Then we note that

\frac{a(i,\lambda)^{\theta}}{p(i,\lambda)}\geq\frac{a(j,\lambda)^{\theta}}{p(j,\lambda)}\Leftrightarrow\left(\frac{a(i,\lambda}{a(j,\lambda)}\right)^{\theta-t}\geq 1

which happens when either
a(i,\lambda)\geq a(j,\lambda)
and
\theta\geq t
or
a(i,\lambda)\leq a(j,\lambda)
and
\theta\leq t
.
This means that for
\theta\leq t
, the maximum of
a^{\theta}(i,\lambda)/p(i,\lambda)
occurs for
i=K
(the smallest scaling factor)
whereas for
t\leq\theta
the maximum occurs for
i=1
(the largest scaling factor)
and so

Y(\theta,\lambda)=\begin{cases}\log p(K,\lambda)&\text{ if }\theta\leq t\\
\log p(1,\lambda)&\text{ if }t<\theta\end{cases}\quad=\quad\begin{cases}t\log a(K,\lambda)-\log(\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})&\text{ if }\theta\leq t\\
t\log a(1,\lambda)-\log(\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})&\text{ if }t<\theta\end{cases}

and

Z(\theta,\lambda)=\begin{cases}\log a(K,\lambda)&\text{ if }\theta\leq t\\
\log a(1,\lambda)&\text{ if }t<\theta.\end{cases}

Thus

G(\theta)=\begin{cases}t-\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})}{{\mathbb{E}}{\log a(K,\lambda)}}&\text{ if }\theta\leq t\\[10.0pt]
t-\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})}{{\mathbb{E}}{\log a(1,\lambda)}}&\text{ if }t<\theta,\end{cases}

and so
G
takes at most two different values.
Notice that if
t=D
then
G(\theta)=D
for all
\theta
and thus
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=D
almost surely in this case.
On the other hand, if
t>D
, then
{\mathbb{E}}(\log(\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t}))<0
.
Further since
{\mathbb{E}}(\log(a(K,\lambda)))\leq{\mathbb{E}}(\log(a(i,\lambda)))<0
we know that for any given
t
the value of

G(\theta)
for
\theta\leq t
is the larger of the two values.
So

\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=t-\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=t-\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}=t-\frac{t\,{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}-\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log(\frac{\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t}}{a(K,\lambda)^{t}}))}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i}\left(\frac{a(i,\lambda)}{a(K,\lambda)}\right)^{t})}{-{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{-{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}\int_{a(1,\lambda)\geq(1+\delta)a(K,\lambda)}\log(\sum_{i}\left(\frac{a(i,\lambda)}{a(K,\lambda)}\right)^{t})\ d{\mathbb{P}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{-{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}\int_{a(1,\lambda)\geq(1+\delta)a(K,\lambda)}\ \log(\left(\frac{a(1,\lambda)}{a(K,\lambda)}\right)^{t})d{\mathbb{P}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{-{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}\int_{a(1,\lambda)\geq(1+\delta)a(K,\lambda)}t\log((1+\delta))\ d{\mathbb{P}}(\lambda)\rightarrow\infty

as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
Thus by an appropriate choice of
t>D
we can get arbitrarily large values for the almost sure
\Phi
-dimension of

\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.
Since

t\mapsto t-\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}

is a continuous function of
t
, we get our desired result by the
Intermediate value theorem in the case where
{\mathbb{P}}(\{a(1,\lambda)>a(K,\lambda)\})>0
.

To finish the proof of the theorem we now assume that
a(1,\lambda)=a(K,\lambda)
almost surely
(and thus all the scaling factors are the same almost surely).
In this case, we set

p(1)=q<1/K\ \text{ and }\ p(i)=\frac{1-q}{K-1}\text{ for }i=2,3,\ldots,K.

Since
a(i,\lambda)=a(j,\lambda)
almost surely for all
i,j
, we have

\frac{a(1,\lambda)^{\theta}}{p(1,\lambda)}\geq\frac{a(i,\lambda)^{\theta}}{p(i,\lambda)}\Leftrightarrow\frac{1}{q}\geq\frac{1}{\frac{1-q}{K-1}}=\frac{K-1}{1-q}\Leftrightarrow q\leq 1/K

and thus since we choose
q<1/K
this means that
G(\theta)=\frac{\log q}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(1,\lambda))}
for all values of
\theta
.
Hence
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\log(q)/{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(1,\lambda))
almost surely.
Clearly as
q\rightarrow 0
this goes to
\infty
and so again in this case we obtain any dimension in the range
[D,\infty)
.
∎

Next, we see that the analogous answer holds in the lower dimension case,
although the proof is necessarily different.

Theorem 14
.

Let
d\in(0,D]
.
There is a choice of probabilities
p(i,\lambda)
so that almost surely
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=d
with
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
doubling.

Proof.

We start by setting
p(K,\lambda)=q
and
p(i,\lambda)=\frac{1-q}{K-1}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,K-1
where
q\in[1/K,1)
.
Then
\min_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{\theta}/p(i,\lambda)=a(K,\lambda)^{\theta}/q
for any
\theta
since
a(K,\lambda)\leq a(i,\lambda)
and
q\geq 1/K
implies
q\geq(1-q)/(K-1)
.
Thus
Y^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda)=\log(q)
and
Z^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda)=\log a(K,\lambda)
for any
\theta\geq 0
and so

G^{\prime}(\theta)=\frac{\log q}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}.

This means that almost surely
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\dfrac{\log q}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}
which spans the entire
interval
(0,-\log K/{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))]
as
q
varies over
[1/K,1)
.

Notice that if
a(1,\lambda)=a(K,\lambda)
almost surely, then the almost sure Hausdorff dimension of
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is

D=\displaystyle\frac{-\log K}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}
.
That shows that in this case we see that we can obtain all possible lower dimensions.

So suppose that
{\mathbb{P}}(a(1,\lambda)>a(K,\lambda))>0
.
We have seen that for any
d\in(0,\displaystyle\frac{-\log K}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}]
there is a choice
of probabilities which gives
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=d
almost surely.
Thus we have only to show that this is also true for
d\in\displaystyle[\frac{-\log K}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))},D]
.

Let
0\leq t\leq D
and set
p(j,\lambda)=\dfrac{a(j,\lambda)^{t}}{\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t}}
for
j=1,2,\ldots,K
.
Notice that since
t\leq D
we have
{\mathbb{E}}(\log(\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t}))\geq 0
.

With this choice of
p(j,\lambda)
, we have

Y^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda)=\begin{cases}\log p(1,\lambda)&\text{ if }\theta\leq t,\\
\log p(K,\lambda)&\text{ if }t<\theta\end{cases}=\begin{cases}t\log a(1,\lambda)-\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t}&\text{ if }\theta\leq t,\\
t\log a(K,\lambda)-\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t}&\text{ if }t<\theta;\end{cases}

and

Z^{\prime}(\theta,\lambda)=\begin{cases}\log a(1,\lambda)&\text{ if }\theta\leq t,\\
\log a(K,\lambda)&\text{ if }t<\theta;\end{cases}

and so

G^{\prime}(\theta)=\begin{cases}t-\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log(a(1,\lambda)))},&\text{ if }\theta\leq t,\\[10.0pt]
t-\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))},&\text{ if }t<\theta.\end{cases}

Now
{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(1,\lambda))<0
and
{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))<0
, while
{{\mathbb{E}}}(\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})\geq 0
(since
t\leq D
) and
thus
G^{\prime}(\theta)\geq t
for any
\theta
.
Thus the smaller value of
G^{\prime}
is for
\theta>t
and so

\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=t-\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{t})}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}=:f(t).

Note that
f(D)=D
since
{\mathbb{E}}(\log(\sum_{i}a(i,\lambda)^{D}))=0
and
f(0)=-\frac{\log K}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))}
.
Since
f
is continuous as a function of
t
, the Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees that for any

d\in\displaystyle[\frac{-\log K}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(K,\lambda))},D]
there is some
t
for which
f(t)=d
.

We note that
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
will be doubling in all the above cases since the probabilities are chosen to be strictly bounded away from zero.
∎

One immediate consequence of our results is a value for the almost sure
\Phi
-dimension of the random Moran set
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.

Corollary 15
.

For any large dimension function
\Phi
we have that

\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=D=\dim_{H}F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\ \text{ a.s.}

where
D
is the unique solution to

{\mathbb{E}}(\log\sum_{i=1}^{K}a(i,\lambda)^{x})=0.

In addition,
D=\max\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\min\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

where the maximum (or minimum) is taken over the associated random measures.

Proof.

We always have
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\leq\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and since the random measure constructed in Theorem
14
is doubling, we also have
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\leq\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
for those measures.
Since we can arrange for
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=D
or
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=D
, the conclusion follows.
∎

6.
The range of dimensions with independent probabilities

We continue to use the notation described at the beginning of Section
5
, but now we consider the situation where the probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}
on
\Lambda={\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{W}}
is a
product measure,
{\mathbb{P}}={\mathcal{Q}}\times{\mathcal{R}}
. So we will fix
{\mathcal{Q}}
and allow any possible
{\mathcal{R}}
.

In the case when the random Moran set,
F_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, is generated from the IFSs each of which has
K
children, is equicontractive with common contraction factor
r(\lambda)
and is strongly separated, then the situation turns out to be very simple, as we see first.
Almost surely
\dim_{H}F_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}=D
where
D
satisfies

{{\mathbb{E}}}(\log Kr^{D}(\lambda))=0
.
Solving this gives

D=\frac{\log 1/K}{{{\mathbb{E}}}(\log r(\lambda))}.

Take the associated random measures with the fixed set of probabilities
\{p,p,...p,1-(K-1)p\}
,
0<p\leq 1/K
.
As
p\leq 1-(K-1)p
, Theorem
4
gives

\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\frac{\log p}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log r(\lambda))}\ \text{ and }\ \underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\frac{\log(1-(K-1)p)}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log r(\lambda))}\text{ a.s.}

By making suitable choices of
p\leq 1/K
we obtain the full intervals
[D,\infty)

and
(0,D]
as in the dependent probability case.

Thus our focus will be on the general, non-equicontractive case.

6.1.
Do we obtain the set dimension as a measure dimension?

Our study of the dimensions of random measures arising from independent
probabilities in the non-equicontractive case will begin by first seeing
that the answer to this question is, in general, no.

Theorem 16
.

Suppose there are
\epsilon>0
and
t\in(0,1)
, sets
\Upsilon_{1},\Upsilon_{2}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}

both of positive probability, and an index
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
so that

\eta_{1}:=\sup_{\lambda_{1}\in\Upsilon_{1}\times{\mathcal{W}}}\frac{a(i,\lambda_{1})^{D}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}a(\ell,\lambda_{1})^{D}}<\inf_{\lambda_{2}\in\Upsilon_{2}\times{\mathcal{W}}}\frac{a(i,\lambda_{2})^{D}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}a(\ell,\lambda_{2})^{D}}:=\eta_{2}.

Then there are
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
so that for any choice of independent probabilities we have that

G(D)-D>\delta_{1}
and
D-G^{\prime}(D)>\delta_{2}
,
thus
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}>D+\delta_{1}
and
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}<D-\delta_{2}
almost surely for any large dimension function
\Phi
.

Proof.

To simplify notation, let
\mathfrak{S}(u)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}a(\ell,u)^{D}
for a given
u\in{\mathcal{A}}
.
Choose
t
and
\epsilon>0
so that
\eta_{1}+\epsilon<t<\eta_{2}-\epsilon
.
Next, choose
\lambda=(u,v)\in\Lambda={\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{W}}
where
u_{1}\in\Upsilon_{1}
and
u_{2}\in\Upsilon_{2}
are fixed and consider
p(i,v)
.
Either
p(i,v)\leq t
or
p(i,v)>t
.

If
p(i,v)\leq t
, then

p(i,v)\leq t\leq\frac{a(i,u_{2})^{D}}{\mathfrak{S}(u_{2})}-\epsilon

and so with
m_{2}=m(D,(u_{2},v))
we have

\mathfrak{S}(u_{2})\leq\frac{a(i,u_{2})^{D}}{p(i,v)}-\epsilon\frac{\mathfrak{S}(u_{2})}{p(i,v)}\leq\frac{a(m_{2},u_{2})^{D}}{p(m_{2},v)}-\epsilon\frac{\mathfrak{S}(u_{2})}{p(i,v)}\leq\frac{a(m_{2},u_{2})^{D}}{p(m_{2},v)}-\epsilon\mathfrak{S}(u_{2}).

Thus

(1+\epsilon)\mathfrak{S}(u_{2})\leq\frac{a(m_{2},u_{2})^{D}}{p(m_{2},v)}\implies p(m_{2},v)\leq\frac{a(m_{2},u_{2})^{D}}{(1+\epsilon)\mathfrak{S}(u_{2})}

and therefore

(6.1)

\log(p(m_{2},v))\leq\log\left(\dfrac{a(m_{2},u_{2})^{D}}{\mathfrak{S}(u_{2})}\right)-\log(1+\epsilon).

On the other hand, if
p(i,v)>t
, then

p(i,v)>\frac{a(i,u_{1})^{D}}{\mathfrak{S}(u_{1})}+\epsilon\implies\sum_{\ell\neq i}p(\ell,v)<\sum_{\ell\neq i}\left(\dfrac{a(\ell,u_{1})^{D}}{\mathfrak{S}(u_{1})}-\frac{\epsilon}{K-1}\right)

and thus there is some
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
with

p(j,v)<\frac{a(j,u_{1})^{D}}{\mathfrak{S}(u_{1})}-\frac{\epsilon}{K-1}.

Similar to the first case, with
m_{1}=m(D,(u_{1},v))
this results in

p(m_{1},v)\leq\frac{a(m_{1},u_{1})^{D}}{(1+\frac{\epsilon}{K-1})\mathfrak{S}(u_{1})}

and so

(6.2)

\log(p(m_{1},v))\leq\log\left(\dfrac{a(m_{1},u_{1})^{D}}{\mathfrak{S}(u_{1})}\right)-\log(1+\frac{\epsilon}{K-1}).

By the definition of
D
,
{\mathbb{E}}_{\lambda}(\log(\mathfrak{S}(\lambda)))=0
(for any
\lambda=(u,v)\in{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{W}}
), so we have

\displaystyle G(D)-D

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\left(Y(D,\lambda)-\log\left(\dfrac{a(m(D,\lambda),\lambda)^{D}}{\mathfrak{S}(\lambda)}\right)\right)}{{\mathbb{E}}(Z(D,\lambda))}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\mathbb{E}}(Z(D,\lambda))}\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}\int_{{\mathcal{W}}}\log(p(m(D,\lambda),\lambda))-\log\left(\dfrac{a(m(D,\lambda),\lambda)^{D}}{\mathfrak{S}(\lambda)}\right)\ dv\ du

\displaystyle\geq\frac{-1}{{\mathbb{E}}(Z(D,\lambda))}{\mathbb{P}}(\Upsilon_{2}){\mathbb{P}}(\{p(i,v)\leq t\})\log(1+\epsilon)

\displaystyle+\frac{-1}{{\mathbb{E}}(Z(D,\lambda))}{\mathbb{P}}(\Upsilon_{1}){\mathbb{P}}(\{p(i,v)>t\})\log(1+\frac{\epsilon}{K-1})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\left|{\mathbb{E}}(Z(D,\lambda))\right|}\min\left\{{\mathbb{P}}(\Upsilon_{2})\log(1+\epsilon),{\mathbb{P}}(\Upsilon_{1})\log(1+\frac{\epsilon}{K-1})\right\}.

Notice that (
4.6
) implies that

\int_{A\setminus(\Upsilon_{1}\cup\Upsilon_{2})}\int_{\mathcal{W}}\log(p(m(D,\lambda),\lambda))-\log\left(\dfrac{a(m(D,\lambda),\lambda)^{D}}{\mathfrak{S}(\lambda)}\right)\ dv\ du\leq 0,

which, along with (
6.1
) and (
6.2
), give the first inequality in the development above.
Since the last line is always strictly positive, the existence of
\delta>0
is established.
From this we see that
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\sup_{\theta}G(\theta)\geq G(D)>D+\delta
.

The conclusions regarding the lower dimension and
G^{\prime}
are argued similarly.
∎

Theorem
16
is simpler in the case where there only finitely many different choices of sets of scaling factors.

Corollary 17
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{A}}
is finite.

1. If there is an index
i\in\{1,...,K\}
and indices
j\neq j^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that

\frac{a(i,j)^{D}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}a(\ell,j)^{D}}\neq\frac{a(i,j^{\prime})^{D}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}a(\ell,j^{\prime})^{D}},

then there are
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
so that for any choice of independent
probabilities we have that
G(D)-D>\delta_{1}
and
D-G^{\prime}(D)>\delta_{2}
.
Thus
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}>D+\delta_{1}
and
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}<D-\delta_{2}

almost surely for any large dimension function
\Phi
.

2. If there is no such triple
\{i,j,j^{\prime}\}
, then there is a choice
of independent probabilities for which
G(D)=D
,
G^{\prime}(D)=D
and
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=D
almost surely for any large dimension
function
\Phi
.

Proof.

For (2), the probabilities

p_{i}=\frac{a(i,j)^{D}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}a(\ell,j)^{D}}

do not vary with
j
and so
m(D,\lambda)=m^{\prime}(D,\lambda)
and thus
G(D)=D=G^{\prime}(D)
.
Therefore by Theorem
9
we have
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=D=\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.

For (1), let
i
,
j
, and
j^{\prime}
be such that

\eta=\left|\frac{a(i,j)^{D}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}a(\ell,j)^{D}}-\frac{a(i,j^{\prime})^{D}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}a(\ell,j^{\prime})^{D}}\right|>0.

Without loss of generality we assume that

\alpha:=\frac{a(i,j)^{D}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}a(\ell,j)^{D}}<\alpha^{\prime}:=\frac{a(i,j^{\prime})^{D}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}a(\ell,j^{\prime})^{D}}.

Then we set
\epsilon=\eta/2
,
\Upsilon_{1}=\{j\}
, and
\Upsilon_{2}=\{j^{\prime}\}
and use Theorem
16
.
∎

Notice that in the situation of part (2) of the Corollary, since

D=\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\lambda}=\inf_{\chi}H(\chi)\leq\sup_{\chi}H(\chi)=\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=D,

we have
G(\theta)=G^{\prime}(\theta)=D
for all
\theta
(because any value of either
G
or
G^{\prime}
is
H(\chi)
for some
\chi
).

6.2.
Attaining the full interval of possible measure dimensions

In this subsection we will prove that it is possible to attain the full
range of possible measure dimensions, as in the dependent probabilities
case. Moreover, we are able to do this with the random measures arising from
single valid probability vectors.

It will be important to emphasize the dependence of the random measure and
G
function on the probability weights.
Thus if we use the probability
{\mathcal{R}}
on the weight parameter space
{\mathcal{W}}
, we denote the resulting random measure as
\mu_{\mathcal{R}}

and function as
G_{\mathcal{R}}
.
In the special case that
{\mathcal{R}}
is supported on a single probability vector
p\in{\mathcal{W}}
we instead write
\mu_{p}
and
G_{p}
.
In this case the choice of probability is deterministic and not random and thus the only
randomness influencing the measure arises from the random choice of similarities.
We can naturally identify the set of valid single probabilities with a subset of

\mathbb{R}^{K}
and give this set the inherited metric.

We first show that the almost sure
\Phi
-dimensions are continuous maps on
this metric space.

Proposition 18
.

The maps
p\rightarrow\sup_{\theta}G_{p}(\theta)=:M(p)
and

p\rightarrow\inf_{\theta}G_{p}^{\prime}(\theta)=:M^{\prime}(p)
on the space of valid
single probabilities are continuous.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and valid single probability
q.
Using the continuity
of the logarithm function, choose
\delta>0
such that if
|p(j)-q(j)|<\delta
for all
j\in\{1,...,K\}
, then
|\log p(j)-\log q(j)|<\varepsilon
for all
j
.

First, take
\theta_{q}
such that
M(q)\leq G_{q}(\theta_{q})+\varepsilon
. As usual, let
m_{q}(\theta_{q},\lambda)
be the minimal index
m_{q}

such that

\frac{a(m_{q},\lambda)^{\theta}}{q(m_{q},\lambda)}=\max_{j}\frac{a(j,\lambda)^{\theta}}{q(j,\lambda)}.

Then

G_{q}(\theta_{q})=\frac{\mathbb{E(}\log q(m_{q},\lambda))}{\mathbb{E(}\log a(m_{q},\lambda))}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E(}\log p(m_{q},\lambda))}{\mathbb{E(}\log a(m_{q},\lambda))}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\left|\mathbb{E(}\log a(m_{q},\lambda))\right|},

for any
p
satisfying
\max_{j}|p(j)-q(j)|<\delta
.

Applying Lemma
8
with
\chi=m_{q}
and recalling that

a(j,\lambda)\leq 1-A
for all
j,\lambda
, we obtain

G_{q}(\theta_{q})\leq\sup_{\theta}G_{p}(\theta)+\frac{\varepsilon}{|\log 1-A|}.

Thus

M(q)\leq G_{q}(\theta_{q})+\varepsilon\leq M(p)+C\varepsilon.

But we can also reverse this argument. Again take any probability
p
satisfying

|p(j)-q(j)|<\delta
for all
j
.
Choose
\theta_{p}
so that
M(p)\leq G_{p}(\theta_{p})+\varepsilon
and use

m_{p}
and Lemma
8
to deduce, as above, that

M(p)\leq G_{p}(\theta_{p})+\varepsilon\leq\sup_{\theta}G_{q}(\theta)+\frac{\varepsilon}{|\log 1-A|}=M(q)+C\varepsilon.

Thus

|M(p)-M(q)|\text{ }<C\varepsilon,

consequently,
M
is continuous.

The argument for
M^{\prime}
is symmetric.
∎

Remark 2
.

We remark that the same conclusion holds if we take
\mathcal{W}
to be a
finite set, rather than a single element.

Corollary 19
.

The maps
p\rightarrow\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p}
and

p\rightarrow\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p}
are continuous
(where here by the
\Phi
-dimensions we mean the almost sure dimensions).

Proof.

According to Theorem
6
,
M(p)=\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p}

a.s. and similarly for the lower dimension.
∎

Using this result, we will next prove that the minimal possible upper
\Phi

measure dimension is attained by a measure arising from a single probability
vector.

Lemma 20
.

Let
d=\inf_{p}\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p}
, where the infimum is over all valid single probabilities.
Then for any distribution
{\mathcal{R}}
on
{\mathcal{W}}
, we have
d\leq\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\mathcal{R}}
.

Proof.

Let
\psi<d
. By part (i) of Theorem
4
, we must have that
\psi\leq G_{p}(\psi)
for any
p\in{\mathcal{W}}
.
Thus for any fixed
v\in{\mathcal{W}}
we have that

\psi\int_{\mathcal{A}}\left|\log a(m(\psi,u,v),u)\right|d{\mathcal{Q}}(u)\leq\int_{\mathcal{A}}\left|\log p(m(\psi,u,v),v)\right|d{\mathcal{Q}}(u)

and thus for any distribution
{\mathcal{R}}
on
{\mathcal{W}}

\psi\int_{\mathcal{W}}\int_{\mathcal{A}}|\log a(m(\psi,u,v),u)|d{\mathcal{Q}}(u)d{\mathcal{R}}(v)\leq\int_{\mathcal{W}}\int_{\mathcal{A}}\left|\log p(\psi,m(\psi,u,v),v)\right|d{\mathcal{Q}}(u)d{\mathcal{R}}(v).

Hence
\psi\leq G_{\mathcal{R}}(\psi)
which again by Theorem
4
means that
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\mathcal{R}}\geq\psi
for any
{\mathcal{R}}
.
Since this is true for all
\psi<d
, we have
d\leq\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\mathcal{R}}
for any
{\mathcal{R}}
as claimed.
∎

Corollary 21
.

We have
\inf_{p}\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p}=\inf_{{\mathcal{R}}}\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\mathcal{R}}
and also
\sup_{p}\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p}=\sup_{{\mathcal{R}}}\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\mathcal{R}}
almost surely.

Proof.

Of course
\inf_{{\mathcal{R}}}\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{\mathcal{R}}\leq\inf_{p}\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p}
since point masses are special cases of general
distributions, hence the corollary is an immediate consequence of the previous proposition.
∎

It is also not hard to see that we can arrange to have arbitrarily large upper dimensions and arbitrarily small lower dimensions.

Proposition 22
.

Given
N
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that if

is any valid
single probability satisfying
\min p(j)<\varepsilon
(or
\max p(j)>1-\varepsilon
),
then
\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p}>N
(resp.,
\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p}<1/N
).

Proof.

Take
C>0

for all

j,\lambda
and choose
\varepsilon>0
so
that
C|\log(\varepsilon)|>N
.

Let
\min p(j)=p(j_{0})
and suppose

.
Put
\chi=j_{0}
.
In the notation of Lemma
8
,

H(\chi)=\frac{|{\mathbb{E}}\log p(j_{0})|}{|{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(j_{0},u)|}\geq C|\log(\varepsilon)|>N

and as that lemma tells us

a.s. we obtain the desired conclusion for the upper
\Phi
-dimension.

The lower dimension result is similar.
∎

Proposition 23
.

There is a valid single probability
p_{0}
such that
d=\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p_{0}}
a.s.

Proof.

For each valid single probability
p
, let
M(p)=\sup_{\theta}G_{p}(\theta)=\overline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p}
.
Choose probabilities
p_{n}\in{\mathcal{W}}
such that
M(p_{n})\rightarrow\inf_{p}M(p)=d
.
It follows from Proposition
22
that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

p_{n}\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]^{K}
for large enough

, consequently
(p_{n})
has a
convergent subsequence (not renamed) with limit
p_{0}
which is a valid probability.

However, as
M
is continuous by Proposition

,

converges to

whence

.
∎

The situation for the lower dimension is the same but the proof is more delicate.
For the upper dimension, Proposition

shows that a small

leads to a large dimension, which allows for control of both uniform upper and lower bounds on
p(i)

However, just because

\min_{i}p(i)>0
and so the argument for the lower dimension is more involved.

Proposition 24
.

There exists a valid single probability
q
such that

M^{\prime}(q)=\sup_{p}M^{\prime}(p)
where the supremum is taken over all valid
single probabilities
p
.

Proof.

Let

and choose valid single probabilities

p_{n}
such that
\Delta_{n}:=M^{\prime}(p_{n})\rightarrow\Delta
.
By passing to a subsequence (not renamed) we can assume
p_{n}\rightarrow p
.
Certainly,
p
is a probability and if it is a valid probability then since
M^{\prime}

is continuous we have
M^{\prime}(p)=\lim M^{\prime}(p_{n})=\Delta

and we are done.

So assume otherwise.
We know from the Proposition

that there exists
N_{1}
and

such that for all

and for all

and hence also

.
Consequently, if
p
is not valid it must be that the set

Of course, as

is a probability

is also not empty.

Let

so small

Pick

for all

For

Clearly, if

Thus if

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underline{\dim}_{\Phi}\mu_{p_{n}}\leq H_{p_{n}}(m_{n}^{\prime})=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\log p_{n}(m_{n}^{\prime}))}{{\mathbb{E}}(\log a(m_{n}^{\prime},\lambda))}

∎

Theorem 25
.

Let

7.
The minimal measure dimension when each IFS has two similarities

In this section we will continue our study of the minimal attainable
dimension of the random Moran measures arising from independent
probabilities, specializing to the case of finitely many IFSs
each of which consists of two similarities.
We will describe a simple algorithm for determining the minimum possible dimension which we have already seen is
equal to
\min_{p}M(p)
(see Proposition
23
) where

M(p)=\sup_{\theta}G_{p}(\theta)

and the minimum is taken over all valid single probability vectors
p
(in this case meaning,
the probability weights
(p,1-p)
).
Thus we assume that
{\mathcal{A}}=\{1,2,\ldots,L\}
,
K=2
, and
{\mathcal{W}}
is a single point.
We set
\pi_{i}={\mathcal{Q}}(\{i\})
to be the likelihood of choosing the
i
’th IFS.

Using these assumptions, all of the possible values of
Z_{p}(\theta)
and
Y_{p}(\theta)
, and thus
G_{p}(\theta)
, come from a finite
set of
log-ratios
each of the form

\frac{\sum_{i}\pi_{i}\log(q_{i})}{\sum_{i}\pi_{i}\log(r_{i})}

where each
q_{i}
is choice of either
p
or
1-p
and
r_{i}
a choice of left or right scaling; there are
2^{L}
such possible expressions.
Our analysis involves a careful consideration of which of these gives the dimension as we change the value of
p
.
In particular, it turns out that we only have to consider at most
L+1
such ratios, which we will denote by
f_{j}
for
j=0,1,\ldots,L
(see below).
Figure
1
gives some indication of how these functions typically look.

7.1.
Further notation and some elementary facts

In this situation, we can assume that the left and right contraction factors of the
i
’th IFS are of the form

a^{\alpha_{i}}
,
a^{\beta_{i}}
for
i=1,...,L
and some
a>0
where, without loss of
generality, we assume
(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})
is decreasing.
We will suppose that

\displaystyle\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0\text{ for }i\in\{1,...,N\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 0\text{ for }i\in\{N+1,...,L\}

and assume that there is some index
i
with
\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}\geq 0
.
There is no loss of generality in making this assumption since if
\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}<0
for all
i
we
instead consider the random set generated by the IFSs with left contractions
a^{\beta_{i}}
and right contractions

a^{\alpha_{i}}
.
The random measure supported on this new random set and associated with the single probability

(1-p,p)
mirrors the measure supported on the original random set arising from the probability
(p,1-p)
.
Hence the second random set has the same minimum measure dimension as the
original.

Our ordering assumption ensures that for all
\theta\geq 0
,

\frac{1}{1+a^{(\beta_{i+1}-\alpha_{i+1})\theta}}=\frac{a^{\alpha_{i+1}\theta}}{a^{\alpha_{i+1}\theta}+a^{\beta_{i+1}\theta}}\leq\frac{a^{\alpha_{i}\theta}}{a^{\alpha_{i}\theta}+a^{\beta_{i}\theta}}=\frac{1}{1+a^{(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})\theta}}

and that for
\theta\geq 0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+a^{(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})\theta}}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle(0,1/2]\text{ for }i\geq N+1

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+a^{(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})\theta}}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle[1/2,1)\text{ for }i\leq N.

For
j=0,1,...,L
let

S_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\pi_{i}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad T_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\alpha_{i}\pi_{i}+\sum_{i=j+1}^{L}\beta_{i}\pi_{i},

with suitable interpretations when

or
L
.
Given all of this, one can easily see that for each
\theta\geq 0
,

G_{p}(\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}f_{0}(p)&\text{if }p>\dfrac{1}{1+a^{(\beta_{1}-\alpha_{1})\theta}}\\[10.0pt]

f_{L}(p)&\text{if }p\leq\dfrac{1}{1+a^{(\beta_{L}-\alpha_{L})\theta}}\end{array}\right.

where

f_{j}(p)=\frac{S_{j}\log p+(1-S_{j})\log(1-p)}{T_{j}\log a}\quad\text{ for }j=0,...,L.

If we suppose, in addition, that all
\pi_{i}=1/L
(that is, the
IFS are chosen with equal likelihood), then the functions
f_{j}
simplify
to

f_{j}(p)=\frac{j\log p+(L-j)\log(1-p)}{(\sum_{i=1}^{j}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{i=j+1}^{L}\beta_{i})\log a}.

(a) The function

7.2.
Transitioning

We will say that “

.

(a) If

(

(

f_{i}
to
f_{j}
at

at

(a) We will prove the left transitioning case as the right case is symmetric.

Since

i=j+1

or
j=i+1
as claimed.

The argument is similar if
i
or
j

=N
.

(b) This follows immediately from the fact that in the left transition case
we assume
b<1/2
, so
j\geq N
and hence
j\neq N-1
. Similarly
j\neq N+1

in the right transition case.

(c) Consider the left transition case. As
b<1/2
,
i

\geq N
, and since
we have already done the case
i=N
in (b) we can assume that
i\geq N+1
.
As
f_{i}
is increasing on
[b,1)
, so is
f_{i-1}
and thus Lemma
26
(b) shows that
f_{i-1}-f_{i}
is increasing on
[b,1)
. If
f_{i}

left transitioned to
f_{i-1}
, then
f_{i}(b)=f_{i-1}(b)
and thus the
increasing property would yield
f_{i-1}(b+\varepsilon)>f_{i}(b+\varepsilon)
contradicting the definition of left transitioning.

The right transition case is similar.
∎

7.3.
The Algorithm

Case 1. Suppose
\alpha_{N}\neq\beta_{N}
. We have already shown that
M(1/2)

=f_{N}(1/2)>f_{j}(1/2)
for all
j\neq N
, hence
\min_{p}M(p)\leq f_{N}(1/2)
.

Sub-case 1a:
f_{N}
is increasing at
1/2
. Then for
p>1/2
,
M(p)\geq f_{N}(p)>f_{N}(1/2)
, so to minimize
M
we must consider
p\leq 1/2
. By
continuity
f_{N}(p)
will continue to be the unique maximum as we decrease
p
until the largest
b<1/2
where
f_{N}
left transitions to
f_{j}
,
i.e.,
M(p)=f_{N}(p)
for all
p\in[b,1/2]
.

If
b\leq S_{N}
, then
M(S_{N})=f_{N}(S_{N})\leq f_{N}(p)\leq M(p)
for all

p\geq S_{N}
. Further,
f_{N}
is decreasing on
(0,b]
, which ensures that

M(p)\geq f_{N}(p)\geq f_{N}(b)\geq f_{N}(S_{N})
for all
p\leq b
.
Therefore
\min_{p}M(p)=f_{N}(S_{N})
.

If, instead,
b>S_{N}
, then
f_{N}
is increasing at
b
and therefore
M(b)=f_{N}(b)\leq f_{N}(p)\leq M(p)
for all
p>b
.
Lemma
28
(c) tells us that
j=N+1
. If
f_{N+1}
is decreasing at
b
, then

M(p)\geq f_{N+1}(p)\geq f_{N+1}(b)=f_{N}(b)=M(b)
for all
p\leq b
and thus
\min_{p}M(p)=f_{N}(b)
.

Otherwise,
f_{N+1}
is increasing at
b
. Then
M(p)\geq f_{N+1}(p)>f_{N+1}(b)=M(b)
for all
p>b
, so to minimize
M
we must
consider
p\leq b
and we basically repeat the argument. We have that
f_{N+1}(p)
is the unique maximum for
p\in(b_{1},b)
where
b_{1}<

b
is
maximal with the property that
f_{N+1}
left transitions to some
f_{j}
at

b_{1}
. If
b_{1}\leq S_{N+1}
, then
f_{N+1}
is decreasing at
b_{1}
and
hence
\min_{p}M(p)=f_{N+1}(S_{N+1})
. If
f_{N+1}
is increasing at
b_{1}

then Lemma
28
tells us that
j=N+2
. If
f_{N+2}
is
decreasing at
b_{1}
, then similar reasoning to that given above shows that

\min_{p}M(p)=f_{N+1}(b_{1})
.

Otherwise,
f_{N+2}
is increasing at
b_{1}
and we repeat the argument.

This argument must terminate in a finite number of steps producing a choice
of
p
which minimizes
M
. Furthermore, it is clear from the argument that
this is the unique choice of a (single) probability minimizing
M
.

Sub-case 1b:
f_{N}
is decreasing at
1/2
. The argument is symmetric. We
have
M(p)\geq f_{N}(p)>f_{N}(1/2)
for all
p<1/2
, so to minimize
M
we
must consider
p\geq 1/2
. We find the smallest
b>1/2
where
f_{N}
right
transitions to
f_{j}
at
b
. If
b\geq S_{N}
, then similar reasoning to
above shows that
M(S_{N})=f_{N}(S_{N})=\min_{p}M(p)
.

If
b<S_{N}
, then
f_{N}
is decreasing at
b
and
j=N-1
. If
f_{N-1}
is
increasing at
b
, then
\min_{p}M(p)=f_{N}(b)
, while if
f_{N-1}
is
decreasing at
b
we repeat the argument. Again, it must terminate in a
finite number of steps with the choice of
p
which minimizes
M
.

This completes the algorithm when
\alpha_{N}\neq\beta_{N}
.

Case 2. Now suppose
\alpha_{N}=\beta_{N}
, so that
M(1/2)=f_{N}(1/2)=f_{N+1}(1/2)>f_{j}(1/2)
for all
j\neq N,N+1
.

If
f_{N}
is decreasing at
p=1/2
, then
M(p)>f_{N}(1/2)
for
p<1/2
, so
to minimize
M
we must consider
p>1/2
. Since condition
N+1
does not
hold for
p>1/2
,
f_{N}
will be the unique maximum on some interval
(1/2,1/2+\varepsilon)

and thus we simply repeat the argument of case 1b.

If
f_{N}
is increasing at
p=1/2
, then we must consider
p<1/2
since
M(p)>f_{N}(1/2)
for
p>1/2
. If
f_{N+1}
is decreasing at
1/2
, then
M(p)\geq f_{N+1}(p)>f_{N+1}(1/2)
for
p<1/2
so
M(1/2)
is minimal.

Finally, suppose
f_{N+1}
is increasing at
1/2
.
For
p<1/2
,
\log p<\log(1-p)
and as
T_{N}=T_{N+1}
, we have
f_{N+1}(p)>f_{N}(p)
.
Thus
f_{N+1}
is the unique maximum on some interval
(1/2-\varepsilon,1/2)

and we basically repeat the case 1a argument, beginning by finding the
largest
b_{1}<1/2
where
f_{N+1}
transitions to
f_{j}
.

Remark 4
.

The two examples shown in Figure
1
illustrate two different ways that
M(p)
can occur,
with
p
being at an intersection (or transition) point in the top example and
p
being at a local minimum
of some
f_{j}
in the bottom example.

7.4.
The special case of two IFS

Suppose there are two IFSs, each of which has two children,
(a^{\alpha_{i}},a^{\beta_{i}})
for
i=1,2
, with
\beta_{1}-\alpha_{1}>\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}
, and chosen with equal likelihood. Let
\theta_{0}
be
the unique solution to

\theta=\frac{2\log(1+a^{(\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2})\theta})}{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\left|\log a\right|}.

Proposition 29
.

(i) If
\beta_{1}-\alpha_{1}\geq 0>\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}
, then the a.s. minimum
measure dimension is

\min_{p}M(p)=f_{1}(1/2)=\frac{2\log 2}{(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{2})|\log a|)}.

(ii) If
\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}\geq 0
, then
\min_{p}M(p)=\theta_{0}
and
occurs with the choice

p=p_{0}=1/(1+a^{(\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2})\theta_{0}})>1/2.

(iii) If
\beta_{2}=\alpha_{2}
, then the almost sure minimal measure
dimension is

\min_{p}M(p)=\frac{2\log 2}{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})|\log a|}.

Proof.

(i) Here we have
N=1
so
M(1/2)=f_{1}(1/2)
. Furthermore, as
S_{1}=1/2
,
f_{1}
changes from decreasing to increasing at
1/2
. Thus
f_{1}(1/2)\leq f_{1}(p)
for all
p
so that
\min_{p}M(p)=f_{1}(1/2)
.

(ii) In this case,
N=2
and
S_{2}=1
. Thus
f_{2}
is decreasing on
(0,1)

and
f_{1}
increases on
[1/2,1)
. Hence Case 1b of the algorithm shows
\min_{p}M(p)=f_{2}(p^{\prime})
where
p^{\prime}>1/2
is the minimal
p

where
f_{2}
right transitions to
f_{1}
at
p
. Of course we must have
f_{1}(p)=f_{2}(p)
at the transition point
p
. But there can only be one
such choice of
p>1/2
since
(f_{1}-f_{2})^{\prime}>0
on
(1/2,1)
and it
is a routine calculation to verify that
f_{1}(p_{0})=f_{2}(p_{0})
. Thus

\displaystyle\min_{p}M(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M(p_{0})=f_{2}(p_{0})=\frac{2\log p_{0}}{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\log a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\log(1+a^{(\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2})\theta_{0}})}{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\left|\log a\right|}=\theta_{0}.

(iii) is immediate from (ii).
∎

Remark 5
.

We made the special assumption that
(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})
was strictly
decreasing. This was essentially a notational convenience for the algorithm.
There is clearly no loss of generality in assuming the sequence is decreasing.
But if, for example, we have
\beta_{j+1}-\alpha_{j+1}=\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j}
,
then the interval

\left(\frac{1}{1+a^{(\beta_{j+1}-\alpha_{j+1})\theta}},\frac{1}{1+a^{(\beta_{j}-\alpha_{j})\theta}}\right]

is empty and consequently the function
f_{j}(p)
is not present in the
definition of
G
. In Lemma
28
we saw that
M
will transition
from
f_{i}
to one of the functions
f_{j}
with an adjacent domain. In the
strictly decreasing case these are the functions
f_{j}
with
j=i\pm 1
.
But if, say,
f_{i+1}
is not present, then the functions with domain
adjacent to that of
f_{i}
will be
f_{i-1}
and
f_{i+2}
(assuming these
are both present). This notational modification is the key change one needs
to make to the algorithm when we do not have the strictly increasing
property.
