DiagFlowBench : Evaluating How Language Models Handle Off-Procedure Inputs in Grounded Diagnostic Dialogue
Guillermo Gil de Avalle, Laura Maruster, Shaina Raza, Christos Emmanouilidis

Abstract
Language models increasingly serve as advisory systems in maintenance operations. To prevent hallucination, recent systems ground these models in procedural documentation to constrain them to approved steps. In practice, however, operator queries frequently stray from this path, requiring models to recognise out-of-scope inputs mid-conversation, a dynamic that current benchmarks rarely prioritise. We introduce DiagFlowBench , a dataset of 50 industrial diagnostic flowcharts from a consumer manufacturer converted into 1,676 multi-turn conversations that contrast compliant with out-of-scope utterances. Evaluating a panel of ten commercial and open-weight models reveals high variability in abstention rates, with models commonly selecting a real but contextually inadequate step rather than fabricating facts. The inherent plausibility and authority of this mapped but wrong advice exposes a challenging vulnerability for grounding systems.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as maintenance advisory systems
(
17
;
4
)
, helping operators through natural language to consult procedural documentation to propose the next action based on provided observations
(
42
;
7
)
. Industrial diagnostic procedural knowledge is typically captured as flowcharts or decision trees
(
43
)
, structures that recent work uses to ground maintenance systems
(
44
;
11
)
. Because such flowcharts enumerate admissible sequences of steps, mapping LLM outputs to this closed set acts as a built-in safeguard against hallucination, ensuring traceability and faithfulness to the procedure
(
33
;
24
)
.

The challenge, however, is that operators rarely request advice in the exact terms of the procedure
(
1
)
. As a conversation unfolds, operators may report novel symptoms or raise questions mid-inspection that the documented steps do not anticipate. The natural language input operators produce compounds this difficulty, typically featuring dense text with abbreviations, shorthand, and domain-specific terminology that resists clean mapping onto documentation
(
2
;
9
)
. Experience widens the gap, as seasoned operators draw on tacit knowledge of the equipment rather than the literal manual
(
10
)
, making their queries further diverge from the mapped steps.

In practice, an LLM-based advisory system receives these off-procedure queries mid-dialogue, mixed among on-procedure turns, and must handle both during generation. The two situations have tended to be evaluated independently. Flowchart-grounded benchmarks typically evaluate compliance on clean, mappable inputs
(
34
;
48
)
, while off-procedure recognition is typically evaluated as abstention over single-turn queries
(
12
;
21
)
. A live diagnostic dialogue presents both at once, demanding that a model advance through the procedure on compliant turns while recognising, in the same conversation, the turns that leave it.

To close this gap, we introduce
DiagFlowBench
to evaluate flowchart-grounded models under both cooperative and off-procedure conditions. Our contributions are (1) a dataset of 50 industrial flowcharts from a consumer manufacturer rendered into 1,676 multi-turn conversations pairing clean dialogues with off-procedure counterparts, (2) an evaluation of ten diverse commercial and open-weight models, and (3) the identification of a failure mode, which we term
forced mapping
, whereby models forcefully assign an off-procedure query to an inapplicable step. Because these returned steps are genuine procedure nodes, similarity-based grounding alone struggles to flag them as failures. Ultimately, our findings reveal that the constraint mechanisms designed to prevent hallucination may instead camouflage contextual errors as valid steps, turning a built-in safety feature into a blind spot.

2
Related Work

Task-oriented dialogue (TOD) systems guide a user toward a goal across multiple turns, traditionally by tracking slots and intents over a fixed schema
(
3
)
. Flowchart-grounded dialogue specialises this setting by replacing the schema with an explicit procedure graph, so that each turn advances along a documented troubleshooting workflow
(
34
;
47
)
. Benchmarks in this line primarily measure how faithfully a model follows that workflow. FloDial established this task by rendering troubleshooting flowcharts into conversations, scoring models on their ability to select the specific step licensed by operator queries
(
34
)
. Later corpora traded the realism of actual manuals for scale, deriving procedures from UML diagrams and synthetic generation
(
48
;
47
)
. More recently, GuideBench tests compliance as guidelines change mid-task
(
8
)
, while SOP-Bench requires the model to execute industrial operating procedures as discrete tool calls
(
27
)
. Despite these variations, every query across this research line is assumed to map somewhere within the procedure, without accounting for utterances that fall outside its scope.

This underlying recognition skill is independently studied as abstention. Traditional abstention detection frames this challenge over single inputs presented in isolation
(
21
;
35
)
. However, even in these isolated settings, the decision to abstain remains remarkably difficult for LLMs, as they routinely misjudge the limits of their own knowledge
(
46
)
and prefer to answer from information gaps rather than decline
(
12
)
. This tendency persists under retrieval paradigms, with the models confidently synthesising answers from passages that offer no actual support for the query
(
5
)
, thereby undermining the safeguard performance against hallucination
(
39
)
. When this challenge is extended to multi-turn TOD dialogue, off-procedure inputs are primarily treated as interference. For instance, injecting casual conversations into the MultiWOZ slot-filling dialogues actively degrades state tracking and task success
(
3
;
41
)
. Similarly, CGoDial inserts out-of-scope turns that the system must detect and meet with a fixed default reply
(
6
)
, and FlowAgent scores workflow agents on remaining compliant when a request strays
(
38
)
. In all these setups, the off-procedure turn is treated purely as a binary classification task to be set aside or ignored. Resolving what a grounded model returns when it does not abstain, rather than only whether it abstained, requires evaluating each response against a specific procedure position. Without both the procedure and the position, a genuine but inapplicable step is indistinguishable from a correct one.

3
Task Formulation

We model a procedure as a directed graph
G=(V,E)
whose nodes are diagnostic states and whose edges are admissible transitions. Each edge
e=(v\rightarrow v^{\prime})
carries an observation
\ell(e)
that licenses the move to
v^{\prime}
, so the admissible inputs at a node
v
are the labels on its outgoing edges,
L(v)=\{\ell(e):e=(v\rightarrow\cdot)\}
. Some nodes are terminal, i.e. they mark the end of the procedure.

A conversation visits nodes in sequence. At the current node
v_{t}
the operator produces an utterance
o_{t}
, and the model, given the history
h_{t}
, must name the next action
\hat{a}_{t}
. The utterance is on-procedure when it entails the label on an outgoing edge of
v_{t}
, that is
o_{t}\models\ell(e)
for some
e=(v_{t}\rightarrow\cdot)
, and the target of that edge is then the correct next action. It is off-procedure when it entails none,

o_{t}\not\models\ell(e)\quad\text{for all }e=(v_{t}\rightarrow\cdot),

in which case no node of
G
is a correct answer. Treating this as no valid response, rather than a missing one, is the closed-world reading of the procedure
(
37
)
. Operator turns follow a fixed reference path rather than reacting to
\hat{a}_{t}
, so every model sees the same conversation and on- and off-procedure turns can be scored independently
(
34
)
. Appendix
F
collects the interpretation of notation.

On-procedure capability.

During an on-procedure turn the model must locate
v_{t}
from
h_{t}
, choose the successor that
o_{t}
entails, and end at a terminal node. These define two capabilities measured per turn in the evaluation protocol. Step accuracy (SA) is whether the named
\hat{a}_{t}
is the successor that
o_{t}
entails. Termination recognition (TR) is whether the model halts at a terminal node rather than proposing a further action. On-procedure behaviour is therefore the baseline against which off-procedure behaviour is read.

Off-procedure failure modes.

During an off-procedure turn no outgoing edge of
v_{t}
is entailed. The model should recognise that nothing in
L(v_{t})
is entailed by
o_{t}
and decline to ground an action, by redirecting, escalating, or seeking clarification. This response is defined as correct abstention (CA). Every other response falls into one of two failure categories. In fabrication (FA), the model returns
\hat{a}_{t}\notin V
, a step absent from the procedure. In forced mapping (FM), the model returns a genuine node
\hat{a}_{t}\in V
, yet no edge into
\hat{a}_{t}
is entailed by
o_{t}
. The step exists in the procedure, but the operator utterance simply gave no reason to select it.

FM falls outside common hallucination taxonomies
(
24
;
16
)
. While FA is a standard extrinsic hallucination, an FM selection is a genuine, graph-consistent step applied to the wrong context. This consistency makes FM uniquely difficult to detect. Standard grounding checks confirm the node is structurally valid but fail to verify its contextual relevance.

4
The
DiagFlowBench
Benchmark

DiagFlowBench
comprises 50 anonymised industrial diagnostic flowcharts rendered into 1,676 multi-turn conversations, half cooperative and half carrying off-procedure injections, built in pairs so the two differ only in whether the operator leaves the procedure, as observed in Figure
1
.

4.1
Procedures

The procedures are troubleshooting and operator-control flowcharts from the maintenance documentation of a consumer manufacturer, spanning fault classes such as conveyor belt tracking, vision inspection calibration, robotic placement, and CNC spindle acceptance. We anonymised the procedures by removing company identifiers and abstracting the domain, strictly preserving the original diagnostic logic and graph topology. The graphs hold between 7 and 60 nodes, with a mean of 31 and decision branching factors between 2 and 4. Appendix
A
reports comprehensive statistics.

4.2
Conversations

For each graph we enumerate root-to-terminal paths under a greedy set cover that covers every decision branch, capping paths per graph to avoid over-sampling large graphs. Appendix
A
provides the path-selection procedure. Each path is rendered into an operator script by prompting a model with the current node and target edge label, after which a verification pass repairs any utterance that leaks downstream steps or violates edge semantics, and human annotators review the output. A noise-perturbation pass produces a second variant that preserves the process topology and path while introducing the typos, telegraphic phrasing, and abbreviated terms characteristic of maintenance work-order text
(
2
)
, following the robustness-testing protocol of
22
. Appendix
B
depicts the generation and validation prompts, as well as the pipeline details.

Thereafter, we build the mixed counterpart of each conversation by injecting off-procedure turns at non-terminal positions, each generated with the same model and screened by a zero-temperature verifier. However, off-procedure inputs are not homogeneous. Previous answerability work shows that distinguishing why an input cannot be answered, rather than treating all such inputs alike, matters for how a system should respond
(
36
)
. We therefore propose stratifying injections into three categories along their relationship to the procedure:
Coverage Gap
(a symptom the procedure has no branch for),
Undocumented Malfunction
(a fault outside any branch condition at the current node), or
Unrelated question
(a query outside the diagnostic task entirely). Appendix
B
offers details on the injection process and prompts, and appendix
E
provides details and examples of the stratification.

5
Evaluation Protocol

5.1
Models

We evaluate ten models that span commercial and open-weight releases. The commercial set is Gemini 2.5 Flash
(
13
)
and GPT-4o Mini
(
29
)
. The open-weight set comprises three Llama models spanning two generations, Llama 4 Scout and Llama 4 Maverick
(
25
)
alongside Llama 3.3 70B
(
14
)
, together with Qwen3 235B and Qwen3 30B in thinking mode
(
45
)
, Mistral Small 24B
(
26
)
, GPT-OSS 120B
(
30
)
, and Nemotron 3 Super 120B
(
28
)
. The panel isolates three analytical dimensions. The access-tier dimension contrasts two commercial models with eight open-weight releases. The architecture dimension spans instruct, mixture-of-experts (MoE), and reasoning designs across providers. A scale variation within the Llama family completes the set. Pinned models, parameter counts, architecture, and hyperparameters used in each model are specified in Appendix
C
.

5.2
Prompting

The system prompt casts the model as a maintenance troubleshooting assistant, supplies the procedure graph as structured JSON, and requests one next action per turn with an explicit stop at a terminal node. We supply the graph in the prompt rather than retrieving it, since the evaluation targets behaviour given the procedure, not recall of the procedure from training. All calls use temperature zero. The per-turn output limits (which differ for reasoning models) and the evaluation prompt are detailed in Appendix
C
.

5.3
Per-turn scoring

The fixed reference path lets on- and off-procedure turns be scored independently. On-procedure turns are matched to a node by word-level Jaccard similarity at a threshold calibrated on a held-out 90/10 graph split, from which SA and TR follow directly (Appendix
D
).

Off-procedure responses are free-form prose that neither matches a node cleanly nor carries an unambiguous abstention marker. Therefore, we classify them individually into CA, FM, or FA using a fixed language-model judge
(
49
)
. We use the
claude-haiku-4-5
model, whose provider is absent from the evaluated panel to avoid self-preference bias
(
32
)
. Although conversations and injections were also generated with Anthropic models, the judge performs its task against the outputs of the evaluated models alone, not the conversations, preventing overlap that drives the bias. We also asked two human annotators to validate a stratified sample of classifications, yielding a human-model agreement of
\kappa=0.79
alongside a human-human agreement of
\kappa=0.83

(
20
)
. We provide further details about the judge in Appendix
D
.

6.1
On-procedure Competence

As observed in Table
2
, SA ranges from 70.1 to 85.0% across the panel. This provides evidence of relatively high performance when the operator query is covered by the manual. TR proves to be the much weaker and more volatile skill, spanning from 38.8 to 98.5%. Consequently, a model capable of selecting the correct next step may still fail to recognise when the procedure has concluded.

6.2
Off-procedure Competence

In line with the on-procedure results, we observe very low FA levels (2.2 to 8.6%), confirming that grounding successfully prevents models from inventing steps. However, FM exhibits substantially worse and more volatile results (15.7 to 67.4%) and emerges as the overwhelmingly dominant failure mode. Interestingly, a low FA rate does not guarantee robustness, as proven by GPT-OSS 120B, which pairs the lowest FA score with an FM rate exceeding 40%. Furthermore, while CA remains the most frequent response for eight of the ten models, we see in Figure
2
that failure rates remain non-negligible. Driven almost entirely by the prevalence of FM, these results suggest a scenario of hallucination displacement, with FM rather than FA as the dominant mode.

Furthermore, neither access tier nor parameter count reliably predicts CA within this panel. Commercial models land in the middle of the panel, while the open-weight group spans a 54-point variability in CA. Parameter counts seem disconnected from abstention. For instance the compact Mistral Small 24B reaches a higher CA rate than the much larger Qwen3 235B. Scalability test within the Llama family aims to determine whether generational advances within a single lineage improve off-procedure handling. The data shows they do not, as the older Llama 3.3 70B consistently outperforms the newer Llama 4 models. The underlying model architecture presents the only tentative pattern. Instruction-tuned models, such as Llama 3.3 70B and Mistral Small 24B, perform relatively well despite not being the largest or newest in the panel. This aligns with their specific training, which heavily penalises guideline non-compliance. However, it is crucial to note they are still far from perfect, as both models continue to exhibit FM rates between 15.7 and 27.6%. Interestingly, while reasoning-oriented architectures are designed to deliberate carefully before answering, Qwen3 30B records the lowest CA rate in the entire panel at 27.4%, and the much larger Qwen3 235B still fails to surpass standard instruction-tuned models.

We can therefore conclude that model scale, architecture, and parameter count fail to definitively predict off-procedure reliability. Compared to the relatively high SA performance, the off-procedure track exposes model brittleness when facing unexpected operator behaviour. Even when explicitly requested to abstain, an LLM will often default to its programming as a helpful assistant and provide an inapplicable answer anyway.

6.3
Drivers and Outcomes of Forced Mapping

We measure FM under a node-selection regime, in which the prompt requires the model to name a procedure node. This isolates the behaviour of interest by removing the option to abstain without committing to a node. Moreover, the per-category spread shows the result is not mere prompt compliance, since under the identical instruction models abstain on up to 98.5% of
unrelated questions
, yet force-map
coverage-gap
inputs. FM is therefore strongly governed by how closely an off-procedure query resembles the manual text. This ordering holds across all models and aligns with the lexical distance of the injection from the procedure (Appendix
E
), signalling vocabulary overlap as a primary driver of FM.

Once an FM is triggered, the error is rarely confined to the turn that produces it. We define
Recovery Rate
as the fraction of on-procedure turns immediately following a failure where the model successfully names the correct next step. Across the panel, this recovery rate plummets to between 1.0 and 9.1%, a stark contrast to the 70.1 to 85.0% SA baseline observed in clean conversations. A single mishandled injection therefore collapses the subsequent procedural tracking to near zero, consistent with findings in literature
(
19
)
. Because later turns carry richer context and score higher on clean conversations, turn position biases this measure upward (see Appendix
E.1
). The observed collapse therefore holds against that bias rather than resulting from it. Appendix
E
reports the recovery rates method and detailed results.

7
Deployment Implications

FM is inherently harder to defend against than FA. Standard guardrails are effective at detecting hallucinated extrinsic facts, but FMs are traceable to the manual and strictly genuine, differing only because they do not fit the specific state or context of the conversation. Catching FM therefore requires continuously validating the current utterance and conversation history against the active node and its immediate edges. Moreover, as the opportunities for lexically similar but incorrect options increase, the model struggles to distinguish superficial similarity from actual entailment. Consequently, robustness cannot be inferred from a low FA rate; it must be measured directly on off-procedure inputs.

Two properties shape FM mitigation in deployment. First, because FMs scale with lexical similarity to the manual, textual closeness acts as a risk indicator rather than a safety guarantee. This dynamic arguably penalises experienced or thorough operators who naturally resemble procedural lexicon. Second, failure prevalence cannot be explained by model scale, architecture, or reasoning-oriented inference. In fact, reasoning-oriented models exhibit an unusually low tendency to abstain, aligning with findings in recent literature
(
18
)
. Therefore, choosing a larger or higher-tier model is not a reliable mitigation strategy for FM. Likewise, applications that only monitor for FA will let the more common FM pass undetected. Deployments cannot rely on generative models alone, but require external verification layers capable of evaluating contextual entailment, ensuring the system can definitively distinguish between inputs that are genuinely off-procedure and those that are merely ambiguous.

8
Conclusion

We introduce
DiagFlowBench
to evaluate how maintenance advisory models handle off-procedure operator queries in flowchart-grounded diagnostic dialogue. Our findings reveal that constraining models to documented steps alone does not prevent failure, but instead swaps traditional hallucinations for a failure mode where models select a real but contextually inadequate step. Ultimately,
DiagFlowBench
demonstrates that evaluating reliability in advisory systems requires measuring not just grounding compliance, but how safely a model fails when reality diverges from the manual.

Limitations

Several scope decisions bound the study. The off-procedure turns are generated rather than collected from a deployed system, since none operates at the partner organisation. The mixed conversations are therefore treated as a controlled stress test, and the generation prompts, verification criteria, and review notes are released for scrutiny. The procedure is supplied in full in the prompt, so retrieval-augmented settings, where it is fetched at runtime from a larger corpus, remain a natural extension the released data supports but which this study does not pursue. The procedures come from a single industrial domain, which limits claims of cross-domain generality, although the properties measured are domain-agnostic. Finally, the evaluation prompt asks for a procedure node, so the measured FM rate reflects both model behaviour and prompt compliance. The per-category differential indicates that model behaviour contributes independently, but separating the two precisely would require a counterfactual prompt. Therefore, the absolute FM rates are best read as an upper region that an explicit abstention affordance would likely reduce.

Ethical Considerations

The procedures originate in proprietary maintenance documentation and were anonymised before release through the pipeline of §
4
, with subject-matter experts confirming that no proprietary identifiers survive, so the released benchmark contains only the abstracted procedures. The benchmark is released under CC-BY-4.0. Its intended use is the evaluation of procedural grounding, and the off-procedure conversations are a controlled stress test rather than a record of any real deployed advisory system. The generation and evaluation prompts, code, and pinned model identifiers are released under MIT for reproducibility and auditing. The annotators who reviewed the generated conversations and validated the judge were doctoral researchers affiliated with the same institution as the research team and were compensated for their work.

A.1
Path Enumeration

Conversations are seeded from paths through each graph, selected so that the benchmark exercises every branch and every terminal outcome without over-sampling large graphs. We treat path selection as a set-cover problem over two universes, the outgoing edges of decision nodes and the terminal types, and add root-to-terminal paths greedily by marginal coverage until both universes are covered. To prevent a few large graphs from dominating the conversation pool, the number of paths per graph is capped at
\max(2,\,N_{\text{dec}}+2)
, where
N_{\text{dec}}
is the number of decision nodes in the graph. Each selected path yields one cooperative conversation plus one noise-perturbed variant (the robustness pass of §
4
), so a graph contributing
p
paths contributes
2p
cooperative conversations, and an equal number of mixed conversations after injection. This procedure produces the 507 enumerated paths and 1,013 generated conversation pairs (one failed generation).

Off-procedure injections are added to the mixed half of each pair at non-terminal positions. The number per conversation is set by an injection rate sampled uniformly from [0.05,0.30] of the available positions, giving a mean of two and a range of one to seven, and each injection is assigned one of the three categories cyclically across positions. Injection verification and two pair-level filters then reduce the 1,013 pairs to 838 retained pairs. The zero-temperature verifier discards any injection that entails an outgoing edge of the current node, reducing 2,110 planned injections to 1,673. Conversations in which every injection was rejected are dropped, removing 168 and leaving 845 pairs. Two annotators, both PhD students, then reviewed the remainder for naturalness and coherence under the supervision of one of the senior authors. They removed 19 injection utterances from 7 conversations carrying self-signalling phrases no operator would produce, logged in
human_review_removals.json
within the released dataset. Dropping those 7 conversations left the final 838 pairs.

Appendix B
Generation and Verification

Operator turns are produced by a generator prompt and audited by a verifier. A noise-perturbation pass is then applied to each verified script, creating a new version with different speech characteristics. Generation and the noise-perturbation pass run at temperature 0.7, typical for open-ended generation, while verification runs at temperature 0 for determinism. Figure
3
shows the end-to-end construction pipeline. The three passes use
claude-sonnet-4-5
, chosen for its reliability with long structured prompts and instruction-following. The generator output is constrained by the verification gate, so the construction does not highly depend on this particular choice of model, and any sufficiently capable instruction-tuned model could be substituted. All prompts are reproduced verbatim below.

Pass 1: Generator System Prompt

You are generating scripted operator turns for a diagnostic troubleshooting benchmark. You are given a directed graph representing a diagnostic procedure and a specific root-to-terminal path through it.

For each node on the path, generate an OPERATOR utterance, what a factory operator would say to report the observation or action described by that node. It must be consistent with the edge label that led to this node, sound like natural speech from a maintenance technician, not mention or narrate downstream steps, not refer to node IDs or graph metadata, and be 1 to 3 sentences.

For PROCESS nodes the operator describes performing the action or its result. For DECISION nodes the operator reports the observation matching the outgoing edge label. For DOCUMENT nodes the operator references consulting the document. For TERMINATOR nodes the operator acknowledges the endpoint. Return a JSON array, one object per turn.

Pass 2: Verifier System Prompt (zero temperature)

You are a quality reviewer for a diagnostic troubleshooting benchmark. You are given a directed graph, a path through it, and a generated conversation. Verify each operator utterance and flag EDGE INCONSISTENCY (does not match the edge label for this branch), STEP LEAKAGE (implies knowledge of steps not yet reached), UNNATURALNESS (robotic or uses graph terminology), and INFORMATION LEAK (reveals graph structure, node IDs, or metadata). For each flagged utterance provide a corrected version. Return JSON with the verified status, the issue list, and the corrected turns.

Noise-Perturbation Robustness Pass

Rewrite operator utterances to mimic how a technician types quickly on a tablet or radio interface. Apply a mix of the following, not all at once, but enough to feel clearly informal: lowercase or no opening capitalisation, missing punctuation, 2--3 realistic typos (transposed or dropped letters), telegraphic phrasing (‘insertion depth ok’ instead of ‘the insertion depth is within specification’), abbreviated units and terms (‘temp’, ‘spec’, ‘engmt force’, ‘dim deviation’), filler words (‘yeah’, ‘ok so’, ‘looks like’), and occasional run-on sentences without commas. The utterance must remain clearly understandable and semantically identical to the original.

Jaccard threshold calibration

The matching threshold
\tau
is selected by a deterministic 90/10 graph split: the 45 calibration graphs (GRAPH01–GRAPH45) supply the in-graph population (
n=8{,}610
scripted operator turns) and the out-of-graph population (
n=1{,}399
verified injection turns); the 5 held-out validation graphs (GRAPH46–GRAPH50) are used only to confirm generalisation. A turn is labelled positive if it belongs to the in-graph population, and the threshold
\tau
classifies a response as matching a node if the best word-level Jaccard similarity across all nodes meets or exceeds
\tau
. Table
7
shows the calibration sweep;
\tau^{*}=0.05
maximises F1 on the calibration set and is validated on the held-out graphs (F1 = 0.927 at
\tau^{*}
), confirming it generalises. The threshold governs reference resolution, mapping each response to the node it names, after which SA and TR compare that node to the licensed successor. A permissive
\tau
therefore raises only the recall of node mentions and cannot by itself inflate SA, and the high recall at
\tau^{*}
is the conservative choice, since failing to resolve a response to its node would misclassify it. The split is reproducible without a random seed, as the validation set corresponds with last 10% of graph IDs in sorted order.

E.1
Post-Injection Effects

Two checks examine whether a single off-procedure turn affects the turn immediately after it. Inappropriate abstention (IA) is the case of declining a valid on-procedure input on the turn immediately after a correctly handled injection, which would mark indiscriminate refusal and guard against a model that scores well by abstaining everywhere. IA stays between 0.0 and 0.5% wherever it could be computed, so the most abstaining models remain discriminating rather than refusing broadly. The disruption is therefore asymmetric. A correctly handled injection resumes cleanly on the following turn, whereas a FM or FA error collapses recovery far below the step-accuracy baseline, the effect reported in §
