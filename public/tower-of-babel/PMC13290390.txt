Guilu Erxian Glue Restores Immune Homeostasis in Aplastic Anemia Mice by Regulating Treg Lineage Stability via the miRNA-17/10a and FasL/Fas Signaling Pathways
Authors: Song Sun, Boyang Meng, Jinghao Sang, Wei Liu, Yingkai Zhang, Deze Li, Dongyang Li, Pingxin Zhang, Jingmin Niu, Jie Wei, Limin Chai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigates the functional role and mechanisms of Guilu Erxian Glue (GEG) in regulating immune homeostasis to treat bone marrow (BM) failure in aplastic anemia (AA).MethodsDifferentially expressed miRNAs of AA patients were identified through bioinformatics analysis of the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, followed by enrichment analysis of the identified miRNAs and their predicted target genes. The chemical profile of GEG was characterized by UPLC-MS/MS. An AA mouse model was established via 60Co γ-ray irradiation combined with the infusion of an allogeneic lymphocyte suspension for immune induction. GEG + Immunosuppressive therapy (IST) was administered as the therapeutic intervention, with IST + Eltrombopag (EPAG) serving as the positive control. Bone marrow hematopoietic function was evaluated via hematological parameters, BM histopathology, and flow cytometry. CyTOF-2 mass cytometry was utilized to analyze the differentiation profile of CD4+ T-cell subsets, and flow cytometry was further used to detect the proliferation and differentiation of naïve T cells, Tregs, and follicular helper T cells (Tfh). The expression of genes and proteins involved in the miRNA-17/IKZF4, miRNA-10a/BCL6, and Fas pathways was quantified by RT-qPCR and Western blot, with serum cytokines measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).ResultsBioinformatic analysis of the GSE82095 dataset identified significantly dysregulated miRNAs in T cells from AA patients, specifically miRNA-17-5p and miRNA-10a-5p. Preliminary enrichment analysis indicated that these miRNAs are primarily involved in hypoxic stress, T-cell differentiation, and apoptosis. Subsequently, the downstream target genes of these miRNAs were predicted, and further functional enrichment analysis corroborated that these target genes are critically associated with T-cell differentiation. HPLC-MS/MS identified 22 compounds from the aqueous extract of GEG. In vivo, GEG treatment significantly improved BM histopathology and restored peripheral blood counts. It also reduced the percentages of Tfh, Th1, Th17, and effector memory T cells (Tem) (P < 0.01), while increasing the percentages of Tregs and central memory T cells (Tcm) (P < 0.01). Molecular analysis revealed that GEG upregulated miRNA-10a and downregulated miRNA-17 in Tregs (P < 0.01), leading to increased expression of the Foxp3 cofactor IKZF4 and decreased BCL6 expression (P < 0.01). GEG elevated Foxp3 protein levels and inhibited cleaved caspase-3 expression in Tregs, and reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6) (P < 0.01),while enhancing the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10 and IL-35) (P < 0.01).ConclusionOur findings suggest that GEG contributes to Treg lineage stability and survival through the potential modulation of the miRNA-17/IKZF4, miRNA-10a/BCL6, and Fas/FasL signaling axes. These synergistic effects appear to support the remodeling of T-cell homeostasis and restoration of cytokine balance, offering a promising, miRNA-targeted strategy for enhancing AA therapy that warrants further clinical investigation.

Introduction

Aplastic anemia (AA) is a bone marrow (BM) failure syndrome characterized by BM hypoplasia and pancytopenia, primarily driven by the aberrant activation of T cells.1 Clinical manifestations typically include refractory and symptomatic hemorrhage, anemia, and recurrent infections, which are driven by a profound reduction in all three peripheral blood lineages—including red blood cells (RBCs), white blood cells (WBCs), and platelets (PLTs) —as well as decreased hemoglobin (HGB) levels.2,3 Globally, the incidence of AA is approximately 2 to 7 cases per million inhabitants per year, with a notably higher prevalence in Asia compared to Western countries.4 While Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains a definitive curative option,5,6 its application is often constrained by patient age, donor availability, and the risk of graft-versus-host disease.7

Consequently, immunosuppressive therapy (IST), specifically the combination of anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) and cyclosporine A (CsA), serves as the standard alternative.8 However, the response rate to IST fluctuates between 60% and 70%, with significant risks of relapse or clonal evolution into myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML).9,10 Furthermore, slow hematopoietic recovery and the potential for iron overload-induced organ damage remain significant clinical challenges.11–13Recent efforts to enhance IST efficacy, such as the addition of Eltrombopag (EPAG)—an oral thrombopoietin receptor (TPO-R) agonist—have shown promise in stimulating hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell proliferation and expanding regulatory T cell (Treg) populations.14,15 Nevertheless, EPAG-related hepatotoxicity and potential biliary system damage, mediated through the cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme system, necessitate the exploration of safer complementary strategies.16

The central mechanism of AA involves a profound disruption of systemic immunity and the failure of immune tolerance. This process is primarily attributed to both quantitative and functional deficiencies Tregs, which, in a healthy state, maintain homeostasis by suppressing excessive autoimmune responses.17 However, in the inflammatory milieu of AA, Tregs exhibit significant lineage instability and tend to undergo a phenotypic plastic shift toward a follicular helper T cells (Tfh)-like profile. This aberrant conversion not only contributes directly to the contraction of the functional Treg pool but also triggers the uncontrolled expansion and activation of effector T cells (Teff).These activated CD4+ T cells orchestrate a coordinated immune onslaught against the hematopoietic system by secreting high concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines, specifically interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α).18,19 These cytokines trigger a cascade that activates the Fas/FasL (CD95/CD178)-mediated apoptotic signaling pathway.20,21 This pathological axis not only results in the direct depletion of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) but also compromises the viability of the Treg cell population itself, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of immune-mediated BM failure.

The biological functions and lineage stability of Tregs are strictly governed by the master transcription factor Foxp3. Beyond this central orchestrator, these processes are further refined by a sophisticated regulatory network involving epigenetic modifications and non-coding RNAs.22–24 Our group, through Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) data mining, found that the expression levels of miRNA-10a, and miRNA-17 differ significantly in T cells in BM of AA patients compared to healthy controls. Modulating these miRNAs to restore immunosuppressive function of Tregs may thus represent a promising therapeutic target and a complementary immunomodulatory strategy for AA.

Guilu Erxian Glue (GEG), a classical traditional Chinese medicine formula composed of Deer-horn glue (Cervi Cornus Colla), Tortoise-shell glue (Testudinis Carapacis ET Plastri Colla), Ginseng (Ginseng Radix et Rhizoma), and Lycium Barbarum L. (Lycii Fructus), documented in “Yi Bian” is renowned for its “Yin-nourishing and Qi-tonifying” properties and is widely used for hematological disorders.25 Our previous studies have demonstrated that a modified version of the GEG formula can reduce immune-mediated HSC damage.26 Building upon this, the present study provides a detailed investigation into whether GEG preserves Treg lineage stability and prevents apoptotic depletion by modulating the miRNA-17 and miRNA-10a signaling axes, offering a novel miRNA-targeted strategy for AA therapy.

Materials and Methods

Identification of Differentially Expressed miRNAs in T Cells from AA Patients

To identify differentially expressed miRNAs in T cells of AA patients, we searched the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) using the keyword “aplastic anemia”. We retrieved the miRNA expression profiling dataset GSE82095, which includes T cell samples from both AA patients and healthy controls. Data analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.5.1). Differential expression analysis was performed by setting thresholds of fold change (logFC)| > 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Significantly differentially expressed miRNAs were visualized using heatmaps and volcano plots to highlight patterns of upregulation and downregulation.

Functional Enrichment Analysis of miRNA

The predicted miRNA were subjected to functional enrichment analysis using the DIANA-miRPath platform (http://diana.imis.athena-innovation.gr/). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed across three domains: Molecular Function (MF), Biological Process (BP), and Cellular Component (CC). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was also conducted. These analyses aimed to elucidate the potential biological functions and signaling pathways regulated by the differentially expressed miRNAs in the context of AA.

Prediction and Enrichment Analysis of miRNA Target Genes

Three mature miRNAs, identified as key regulators of Tregs, were selected for downstream target gene prediction. To enhance reliability, we utilized four miRNA-target prediction databases: miRDB (www.mirdb.org), miRTarBase (www.mirtarbase.mbc.nctu.edu)., miRWalk (www.mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de), and TargetScan (www.targetscan.org). High-confidence target genes were defined by the intersection of predictions from these databases. A miRNA–mRNA regulatory network was constructed using Cytoscape software (version 3.7.2) to visually represent the regulatory relationships.To determine the biological functions and potential signaling pathways of overlapping target mRNAs, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the Metascape database (www.metascape.org).

Preparation of the GEG Herbal Combination

The GEG formula consists of the following four Chinese medicinal herbs: Cervi Cornus Colla (21010203) 10 g, Testudinis Carapacis ET Plastri Colla (20231130) 10 g, Ginseng Radix et Rhizoma (2307080) 10 g, and Lycii Fructus (20231130) 20 g. The plant names and taxonomic nomenclature have been validated in the World Flora Online (www.worldfloraonline.org) and Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China (www.ydz.chp.org.cn). Except for the two gelatins, the herbal components were macerated in 8 volumes (v/w) of deionized water for 0.5 h. The mixture was initially brought to a boil under high heat and then simmered for 0.5 h. After collecting the first filtrate, the residues were re-decocted with 6 volumes of deionized water for another 0.5 h. During this second decoction, Cervi Cornus Colla and Testudinis Carapacis ET Plastri Colla were added to the boiling liquid for melting and integration. Both extracts were subsequently pooled, filtered, and evaporated to reach a final concentration of 1 g/mL (crude drug equivalent).27,28

Detection by Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS)

GEG extract was filtered through a 150 μm standard test sieve and freeze-dried using a vacuum freeze dryer (Beijing Boyikang, FD-1A-80, BeiJing) to obtain a powder (Storage at −80 °C). The powder extraction yield of GEG was approximately 57 ± 1.2% (w/w). The freeze-dried powder was dissolved in methanol/water (1:1, v/v), then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 minutes, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane and transferred to an injection vial. The supernatant was analyzed using an SCIEX ExionLC AD UPLC system coupled with an SCIEX X500R Q-TOF mass spectrometer (equipped with an ESI electrospray ionization source). Separation used a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) with a mobile phase of 0.05% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B), at 0.3 mL/min. MS acquisition employed information-dependent acquisition (IDA) in positive and negative modes. Key settings were: ion spray voltage: ±5500 V; source temperature: 500 °C; gas pressures (nebulizer: 50, auxiliary: 60, curtain: 30 psi); scan ranges: m/z 100–2000 Da (TOF-MS) and m/z 50–2000 Da (TOF-MS/MS).All chemical profiling experiments were performed in triplicate.

Mouse Rearing and Induction of AA Model

In accordance with the 3R (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) principles for animal research, a pilot experiment was conducted prior to the formal study. The animal model of AA and the therapeutic effects of each drug regimen were preliminarily assessed using complete blood count (CBC) analysis and hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining.For the pilot experiment, female BALB/c mice (6 mice per group, 7–8 weeks old) and one female DBA/2 mouse (10 weeks old) were used. In the formal study, female BALB/c mice (14 mice per group, 7–8 weeks old) and two female DBA/2 mice (10 weeks old) were employed. All animals were purchased from Beijing Weitong Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Animal Production License No.: SCXK (Beijing) 2021–0011). This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Dongzhimen Hospital, Beijing University of Chinese Medicine, and all procedures adhered to the guidelines and regulations set by the Animal Ethics Committee of Dongzhimen Hospital, Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (Animal ethics batch number: No. 22–24), (Laboratory Animal Usage License: No. SYXK (Beijing) 2020–0013).

According to the guidelines of the American Veterinary Association (https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/avma-policies/avma-guidelines-euthanasia-animals), mice were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg). The depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal and tail-pinch reflexes. Blood was then collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus. Immediately thereafter, while still under deep anesthesia, the mice were euthanized by an intraperitoneal overdose of 5% pentobarbital sodium (200 mg/kg). Death was confirmed by cessation of breathing followed by bilateral thoracotomy. Post-mortem tissue collection was then performed. After euthanasia of DBA/2 mice, thymus and lymph nodes were collected to prepare single-cell suspensions. Cell viability was assessed using Trypan Blue staining (> 95%). Mice were irradiated with a sublethal dose of 3.5 Gy using a 60Co γ-ray source, followed by the injection of thymus and lymph node cell suspensions (1 × 106 cells, 0.2 mL per mouse) in the tail vein within 4 h. All mice, except those in the normal control group, were randomly assigned to four groups and administered treatment 1 day after irradiation.

Drug Treatment and Grouping

All mice, except those in the model group, received 28 days of treatment. In the pilot study, mice were randomly divided into six groups. The normal group was given standard feeding. The model group received the same diet as the normal group but was administered sterile saline via oral gavage. The dose for mice was estimated using the Body Surface Area (BSA) normalization method based on the clinical dosage for AA patients, according to the dose exchange algorithm.29 According to the body surface area measurement algorithm, the daily use of GEG by an adult weighing 60kg is converted to 10g/kg/d for Chinese herbal decoction to mice. The IST group received the same diet as the control group and was treated with tail vein injections of ATG (Genzyme Polyclonals S.A.S, J20150136, USA)(0.12 mg/day) for 5 consecutive days, followed by continuous oral gavage of CsA (Hangzhou Sino American Huadong Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, H10960122, China) (0.03 mg/day) for 28 days. The IST combined with EPAG (Novartis Pharma Schweiz AG, H20170387, Switzerland) group (IST + EPAG) received the normal diet and, in addition to IST treatment, was administered EPAG (0.45 mg/day) for 28 consecutive days. The IST combined with the clinically equivalent dose of GEG group (IST + CEDGEG) received the normal diet and, in addition to IST treatment, was given GEG decoction (0.2g/day) for 28 consecutive days. The IST combined with high-dose GEG group (IST + HDGEG) received GEG decoction (0.6 g/day) for 28 days in addition to IST treatment. The usage of GEG is based on previous research.30 This experiment focuses on clinical equivalent doses and provides effect anchoring through positive controls (IST + EPAG), which can meet the needs of mechanism exploration. Before the formal experiment, we treated mice with different doses of traditional Chinese medicine compound drugs and observed the number of hematopoietic stem cells and BM apoptosis in mice by HE staining and flow cytometry. We found that the equivalent dose drug had the most significant therapeutic effect.In accordance with the 3R principles, the equivalent dose of GEG was selected for treatment in the formal experiment.

Complete Blood Cell Analysis

Complete blood cell analysis was performed on mice at baseline Day 0, Day 7, Day 14, and Day 28 following drug intervention. A 10 μL blood sample was collected from the tail tip of each mouse and analyzed using a hematology analyzer (Nihon Kohden, DXH 900, Tokyo, Japan). The levels of RBCs, WBCs, HGB, and PLTs were quantified.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining

After 28 days of treatment, the femur from one side of 3 mice from each group was collected, fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde (Mindray, D022, Shenzhen, China), and then decalcified in 10% Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution (Solarbio, P1110, Beijing, China) for 14 days. After decalcification, the bone tissue was embedded in paraffin according to standard protocols. Tissue sections were deparaffinized using xylene and subsequently rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Finally, the sections were stained using the HE staining method. Pathological changes in the tissues were observed and photographed under an optical microscope (Leica, DM RAS2, Germany).

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Single-cell suspensions were prepared by filtering Bone Marrow Cells (BMCs) flushed from the femoral cavity and mechanically dissociated splenocytes through a 200-mesh strainer. For HSCs quantification, BMCs were stained with anti-mouse CD117-PE antibody and CD45-APC antibody (BD Bioscience, 561075, 561018, USA), while BMC apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BOSTER, MK1028, China). Splenic T cell subsets were analyzed using specific antibody panels: Tregs were identified by staining with anti-mouse CD4-FITC, CD25-PE (BioLegend, 100406, 102008, USA), and Foxp3-APC (Invitrogen, 17–5773-82, USA); effector T cells were detected using anti-mouse CD4-FITC, CD44-PE, and CD62L-APC (BioLegend, 100406, 103,024, 104411, USA); and Tfh cells were labeled with anti-mouse CD4-FITC, CD185-PE, and CD278-APC (BioLegend, 107712, 145,504, 145504, USA). Flow cytometry was performed on a Cytomics FC 500 system (Beckman Coulter, Cytomics FC 500, USA), and data were analyzed using CytExpert software (Version 2.5).

CyTOF-2 Mass Cytometry Analysis

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from mouse peripheral blood using a mouse peripheral blood mononuclear cell isolation kit (solarbio, P6340, Beijing, China). The isolated cells were incubated with a 2 μM cisplatin working solution (Invitrogen, 00–4975-93, USA) for 4 hours, followed by fixation with 1.6% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, cells were labeled using the Maxpar® X8 Antibody Labeling Kit to conjugate surface antibodies with metal tags. Cells were first labeled with a pre-mixed cocktail of metal-conjugated surface antibodies and incubated at 4°C for 30 minutes. Following fixation and permeabilization, an intracellular antibody cocktail was introduced for cell labeling, with incubation at room temperature for 30 minutes. Finally, cells were resuspended in 10% EQ™ Four Element Calibration Beads and thoroughly mixed. All data were analyzed using the X-shift clustering algorithm and non-linear dimensionality reduction via t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE). Data visualization was performed using the Visual Stochastic Network Embedding (viSNE) method. CD4+ T cells subsets were defined as follows: Th1 cells (CD4+IFN-γ+T-bet+), Th2 cells (CD4+IL-4+GATA3+), Th17 cells (CD4+ IL-17+ RORγT+), Tregs (CD4+CD25+Foxp3+).

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

After the final dose administration, 0.8 mL of blood was collected from the retroorbital venous plexus of each mouse. The blood was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C to separate the serum. The serum levels of FasL, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, IL-35, IL-4, IL-17 (Elabscience, E-EL-M0028c, E-EL-M0048c, E-EL-M3063, E-EL-M0044, E-EL-M0046, E-EL-M0733, E-EL-M0043, E-EL-M0006, USA) and TGF-β1 (Invitrogen, BMS608-4, USA) were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western Blot Analysis

Spleens were harvested and mechanically dissociated, followed by flushing with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)(Solarbio, P1020, Beijing, China) to generate a single-cell suspension. CD4+ T lymphocytes were then purified from the splenocytes using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS), strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Miltenyi Biotec, 130–117-043, Germany).CD4+ T lymphocytes were collected in RIPA buffer (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). Following protein quantification using the BCA method, samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Uppsala, Sweden). The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies Fas (R&D, AF435, USA), caspase-7, cleaved caspase-7 (Cell Signaling Technology, CST9492, CST9491, USA), Caspase-8 (R&D, AF705, USA), Cleaved Caspase-8 (Cell Signaling Technology, CST8592, USA), and GAPDH (Proteintech, hp-60004, USA). Tregs underwent purification using MACS (Miltenyi, 130–091-041, Germany). Protein extraction and quantification are the same as above.The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies Caspase-3, Cleaved Caspase-3 (Novus, NB100-56708, USA), with GAPDH (Proteintech, hp-60004, USA).Subsequently, the membranes were incubated for 1h with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and subjected to chemiluminescent detection using ECL reagent (NCM Biotech, P10100, Suzhou, China). GAPDH was used as the internal control, and all optical density measurements were normalized to it. Each experiment was repeated three times, and protein expression densities were quantified using ImageJ software (Bethesda, USA).

WES Automated Protein Blotting Analysis

Tregs underwent purification using MACS (Miltenyi, 130–091-041, Germany). Due to the small number of Tregs in the sorted sample and the low abundance of related proteins, we chose the highly sensitive WES fully automatic protein quantification detector (Protein Simple, America) for protein quantification detection, which is consistent with the traditional Western blot detection principle. The cells were lysed with RIPA buffer. After protein quantification using the BCA method, the appropriate volume of detection antibodies, including HIF-1α (Cell Signaling Technology, CST36169, USA), IKZF4 (Abcam, ab220329, USA), Foxp3 (BOSTER, MA00011, China), BCL6 (R&D, AF5046, USA) and GAPDH (Proteintech, hp-60004, USA), were added to the wells and incubated as per the kit’s instructions. The fluorescence reference, Ladder, and samples were examined using Compass software (Version 6.2.0) to ensure no abnormalities before proceeding with data analysis and image export. Each experiment was repeated three times, with GAPDH used as the reference gene.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (S.D). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Differences between treatment groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey-Kramer post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of Differentially Expressed miRNAs in AA

Analysis of differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs) obtained from the GEO database revealed significant alterations in the expression of miRNA-17, miRNA-10a, and miRNA-146a (Figure 1A). The specific expression levels of DEMs in AA and adjacent normal samples is shown in Figure 1B. To further investigate the mechanisms associated with these three miRNAs, we predicted their target genes using the online tools TargetScan and miRDB. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were subsequently performed using the R package clusterProfiler. The results suggested that miRNA-17, miRNA-10a, and miRNA-146a may be involved in 12 functional pathways (Figure 1C) and 20 signaling pathways (Figure 1D). Enriched biological processes included the cell cycle, HIF-1, TNF, and TGF-β signaling pathways (Figure 1C), while signaling pathways such as T cell differentiation and apoptosis were also significantly identified (Figure 1D). Construction of miRNA–mRNA Network and Functional Enrichment Analysis of Downregulated Target mRNAs

It is known that upregulated miRNAs can promote or suppress AA initiation and progression by downregulating target mRNAs. Therefore, we conducted further analyses using the target genes of upregulated miRNA-17 and downregulated miRNA-10a and miRNA-146a. Downstream target mRNAs of these three mature miRNAs were identified using miRDB, miRTarBase, miRWalk, and TargetScan (Figure 2A). To improve prediction accuracy, we selected only potential mRNAs that were associated with AA-related DEGs and were predicted by at least two databases. This approach revealed associations between the miRNAs and 15 corresponding target mRNAs (Figure 2B). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of these 15 target mRNAs indicated significant enrichment in processes related to hematopoietic regulation and T cell differentiation (Figure 2C and D). Discussion

This study elucidates the potential roles of miRNA-17, miRNA-10a, and miRNA-146a in the pathogenesis of AA through comprehensive bioinformatic analyses. Differential expression analysis based on the GEO database revealed that these three miRNAs were significantly dysregulated between AA patients and healthy controls, with miR-17 being upregulated and miR-10a and miR-146a being down-regulated. Subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analyses indicated that these miRNAs may influence the pathological process of AA by modulating 12 functional pathways—including HIF-1, TNF and TGF-β signaling pathway—as well as 20 signaling pathways related to T cell differentiation in thymus, Cellular response to hypoxia, and Apoptotic signaling pathway. By cross-referencing multiple databases (miRDB, miRTarBase, miRWalk, and TargetScan), we identified 15 key target genes associated with AA that are potentially regulated by these miRNAs. Functional enrichment analysis of these target genes demonstrated significant associations with processes related to hematopoietic regulation and T cell differentiation, further supporting the crucial roles of these miRNAs in the immune dysfunction and hematopoietic failure characteristic of AA. Collectively, this study proposes a plausible molecular mechanism: Hypoxic stress within the bone marrow microenvironment triggers the dysregulation of miRNA regulatory networks. These miRNAs likely participate in the initiation and progression of AA by collectively regulating downstream target genes, thereby modulating T-cell function and differentiation, disrupting the stability of the hematopoietic microenvironment, and promoting the expression of apoptotic signals. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for future research into miRNA-based diagnostic biomarkers or therapeutic targets for AA.

AA is primarily characterized by peripheral blood cell reduction, a decrease in BM hematopoietic cells, and fatty infiltration.31 In our model, BM cavities exhibited extensive adipocyte replacement and a profound reduction in nucleated cells. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed increased apoptosis of BMCs and a depletion of the hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) pool.Following intervention, both IST + GEG and IST + EPAG groups showed a significant reversal of these pathological features, characterized by reduced fatty infiltration and a restoration of HSC counts.Notably, IST + GEG and IST + EPAG treatments effectively improved the peripheral blood parameters of AA mice, with both treatments outperforming IST alone or IST + HDGEG in improving WBCs, RBCs, PLTs and HGB levels.The observed plateau or decline in efficacy with HDGEG suggests that excessive dosing may lead to metabolic saturation or signal interference. Experimental results indicate that GEG significantly potentiates the therapeutic efficacy of IST in alleviating immune-mediated BM injury, achieving a potency comparable to that of EPAG.

As the master regulator of Tregs, impairment of Foxp3 function significantly compromises the immunosuppressive potency and lineage stability of the Tregs.32–34 Emerging evidence indicates that Treg stability is stringently regulated by the miR-17/IKZF4 signaling axis.35–37 IKZF4 serves as a critical co-factor for Foxp3-mediated transcriptional repression; its deficiency leads to the structural dysfunction of the Foxp3 transcriptional complex, resulting in a loss of suppressive activity. Within the pro-inflammatory microenvironment, pathologically elevated levels of HIF-1α and IL-6 induce the overexpression of miR-17, which subsequently inhibits IKZF4 mRNA transcription and protein translation.38,39 This study confirms that the IST + GEG combination effectively downregulates IL-6 levels, suppresses the accumulation of HIF-1α in CD4+ T cells, and reduces miR-17-5p transcriptional abundance. This intervention relieves the repression of IKZF4, thereby restoring IKZF4 protein levels within Tregs.These findings further corroborate our previous research demonstrating that GEG and Danggui Buxue Tang (DBT) modulate miR-17 transcription in AA murine models, which influences Eos expression within Tregs and enhances their stability.40

Furthermore, Treg stability and lineage commitment are regulated by the miR-10a-5p/Bcl-6 pathway. Under conditions where Bcl-6 (the lineage-determining transcription factor for Tfh) is highly expressed, Tregs are susceptible to phenotypic plastic shifts toward a Tfh-like profile.41 MiR-10a-5p targets the Bcl-6 transcript after transcription, thereby maintaining Treg phenotype stability and blocking Tfh conversion.41–43 Our experimental results demonstrate that IST + GEG significantly elevates miR-10a-5p transcriptional levels in CD4+ T cells, leading to the subsequent downregulation of Bcl-6 mRNA and protein expression. This mechanism effectively preserves Treg phenotypic stability and prevents aberrant differentiation into Tfh cells amidst AA-associated inflammation, ensuring the reconstruction of peripheral immune tolerance.

In the inflammatory environment, activated effector T cells (primarily the Th1 subset) highly express Fas ligand (FasL).44 Correspondingly, Tregs express high levels of the Fas receptor (CD95), rendering them hypersensitive to FasL-mediated apoptosis.45 The engagement of FasL with Fas recruits the adapter protein Fas-associated protein with death domain (FADD), triggering a caspase cascade that ultimately culminates in Treg apoptosis.46,47 Western blot analysis revealed that IST + GEG significantly inhibits the apoptotic pathway initiated by the Fas/FasL signaling cascade, reducing systemic FasL levels and downregulating the expression of Fas, cleaved caspase-7/8 in CD4+ T cells, and cleaved caspase-3 specifically in Tregs. The reduction in FasL expression implies diminished activation of Fas receptors, both on Tregs and on effector T cells, thereby elucidating the mechanism underlying the generalized downregulation of apoptosis-related proteins in CD4⁺ T cells following treatment. In conclusion, the post-treatment recovery of Treg counts, the upregulation of Foxp3 expression, the reduction in Tfh proportions, and the increased levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10 and IL-35) are the collective results of these synergistic signaling modulations. These findings indicate that IST + GEG effectively promotes Treg lineage stability by integrating differentiation control and apoptosis inhibition, ultimately reconstructing immune homeostasis in AA patients.

During the pathological progression of AA, the secretion levels of core anti-inflammatory cytokines derived from Tregs, including IL-10, IL-35, and TGF-β1, are significantly diminished, thereby weakening their inhibitory potency against autoimmune responses.48 Specifically, the deficiency of TGF-β1 directly hinders the differentiation of naïve CD4⁺ T cells into induced Tregs (iTregs).49 Concurrently, under persistent antigenic stimulation, the naïve T cell pool undergoes attrition due to excessive differentiation toward effector and memory phenotypes. This results in the rapid activation of CD4⁺ Tem into effector T cells (Teff), which release a surge of pro-inflammatory cytokines (eg., IFN-γ and TNF-α), ultimately leading to immune-mediated destruction of HSCs.35,50–53 In this study, deep profiling via CyTOF-2 (mass cytometry) confirmed a profound imbalance in T-cell subsets within AA mice, while flow cytometric analysis corroborated the aberrant expansion of Teff cells alongside the contraction of both naïve T cell and Treg populations. Our experimental results demonstrate that the IST + GEG combination therapy significantly upregulates the secretion of inhibitory cytokines (IL-10, IL-35) and restores TGF-β1 levels. This therapeutic effect not only effectively suppresses the activation and proliferation of CD4⁺ Teff cells but also markedly curtails the release of pro-inflammatory IFN-γ and TNF-α. Consequently, this intervention restores T-cell homeostasis and alleviates immune-mediated BM destruction.

According to Traditional Chinese Medicine theory, multi-component herbal formulations exert therapeutic effects through complex synergistic interactions.54,55 Our UPLC-MS/MS analysis identified 22 primary bioactive constituents in GEG.While several constituents of GEG, such as Ginsenoside Rb1, Baicalin, Vitamin C, and Rutin, have been individually identified to possess anti-apoptotic or immunomodulatory properties in various disease models,56–59 their collective synergistic impact within the unique hyper-inflammatory and hypoxic BM microenvironment of AA remains largely unexplored,which is a limitation of our current research. In the present study, GEO database mining identified differentially expressed microRNAs in AA patients that corroborate previous findings. Compared with previous studies by Liu et al60 and Takahashi et al23 our findings extend the miR-17/IKZF4 and miR-10a/Bcl-6 paradigms into the unique hypoxic and hyper-inflammatory BM niche of AA. We demonstrated that GEG effectively reverses the HIF-1α/IL-6/miR-17-mediated epigenetic repression of IKZF4 and restores miR-10a-5p to block the aberrant Treg-to-Tfh phenotypic shift. Specifically, GEG restores Treg lineage stability by modulating miRNA networks and inhibits the Fas/FasL signaling cascade to prevent the apoptotic depletion of Tregs. These combined effects collectively drive the remodeling of T-cell subset homeostasis and the restoration of cytokine balance.Unlike traditional single target therapies that focus on cell depletion or hematopoietic stimulation, GEG utilizes its 22 bioactive ingredients to help rebuild immune homeostasis through multi-target synergistic effects, and may ultimately help reduce the risk of recurrence during IST reduction.

Conclusion

GEG precisely modulates the miR-17-5p/IKZF4 and miR-10a-5p/Bcl-6 signaling axes within Tregs, thereby maintaining their lineage stability and preventing a phenotypic plastic shift toward Tfh-like cells.Furthermore, GEG effectively inhibits the Fas/FasL-mediated apoptotic cascade, preventing the excessive depletion of the Tregs. These synergistic effects collectively drive a comprehensive remodeling of T-cell subset homeostasis and restore cytokine balance.These findings provide an innovative, miRNA-targeted combinatorial strategy to enhance the efficacy of immunosuppressive therapy for AA.
