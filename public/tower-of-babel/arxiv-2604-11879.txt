A Deep ALMA Survey of the Redshift Distribution of Dusty Star-forming Galaxies Facilities: ALMA, HST, JCMT, JWST astropy ( 12 ; 10 ; 11 ) , CASA ( 87 ) , emcee
S. J. McKay, A. J. Barger, L. L. Cowie, F. E. Bauer

Abstract
We present an Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) spectroscopic follow-up survey of an 870 μ \mu m-selected sample of dusty star-forming galaxies (DSFGs) in the GOODS-S field. We use these linescans to identify or confirm spectroscopic redshifts (spec-zs) for 20 sources. Including spec-zs from the literature, there are now secure or tentative spec-zs for 52 out of 75 DSFGs (69%). At f 870 ​ μ ​ m > 2.5 f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}>2.5 mJy, the sample is 97% spectroscopically complete, allowing us to model the full DSFG redshift distribution down to nearly the confusion limit for a 15-m telescope at 850 μ \mu m. This is the highest completeness for an unbiased sample at this flux limit to date. We find that nearly all of the DSFGs in our sample that were targeted with JWST/NIRSpec were spectroscopically identified, without much dependence on near-infrared or submillimeter flux or redshift. However, only 29% of our sample have JWST spectroscopic coverage. We use the spec-zs to evaluate various photometric redshift (photo-z) estimates, finding that all methods exhibit an outlier fraction of at least > 20 >20 %. Nearly all of the photo-z methods tend to overshoot the redshifts, leading to overestimates of the number of DSFGs at high redshift ( z > 4 z>4 ). Our results suggest that ≲ 10 \lesssim 10 % of f 870 ​ μ ​ m ≳ 2 f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}\gtrsim 2 mJy DSFGs lie at z > 4 z>4 and ≲ 2 \lesssim 2 % lie at z > 5 z>5 , reflecting a steep decline in the abundance of massive dusty galaxies in the first 1.5 Gyr.



I
Introduction

Submillimeter-bright (
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}\gtrsim 1
–2 mJy) dusty star-forming galaxies (DSFGs) represent an extreme phase of dust-obscured galaxy evolution that was most prevalent roughly 10 billion years ago (
z\sim 2.5
; see reviews by
32
;
67
).
While their extreme far-infrared (FIR) luminosities are comparable to those of local ultraluminous infrared galaxies (
L_{\rm IR}\gtrsim 10^{12}\,{\rm L}_{\odot}
), their space densities are
\sim
1000 times higher and they contribute
\gtrsim
30% of the total cosmic star formation rate density (SFRD) above
z>1

(
43
e.g.,)
, making them a crucial component of the overall framework of galaxy evolution.

Our current understanding of DSFGs suggests that they represent the progenitors of the most massive elliptical galaxies observed in the local Universe, after rapidly building up their stellar mass and passing through a compact quiescent phase, possibly triggered by major mergers
(
77
;
107
;
109
;
102
;
70
e.g.,)
, although there is evidence that a fraction of the population may evolve through more secular processes
(
110
;
20
;
7
e.g.,)
.
Even as recent state-of-the-art simulations have successfully matched some of the broad characteristics of the population
(
76
;
81
;
65
;
79
;
74
)
, reproducing the flux and redshift distributions and correlations for DSFGs continue to be a challenge for models
(
40
e.g.,)
.
Testing theories of DSFG evolution further requires properly accounting for their volume density, clustering, and contribution to the cosmic SFRD; accurately measuring properties such as their stellar and gas masses; and understanding their environments and descendants.

Crucially, all of these endeavors rely on accurate redshifts.
Although (sub)millimeter observations are effective at detecting DSFGs out to
z\gtrsim 8
, thanks to the very negative
K
-correction at these wavelengths, measuring spectroscopic redshifts (spec-zs) has proved difficult due to the challenge of successfully detecting rest-frame optical emission lines and/or FIR molecular transitions for faint and dusty sources
(
16
;
31
;
48
;
20
;
40
e.g.,)
.

For most galaxies, we are forced to rely on photometric redshifts (photo-zs) from either optical/near-infrared (NIR) or full panchromatic spectral energy distribution (SED) modeling
(
24
;
45
;
19
;
22
e.g.,)
, or from various FIR color- or template-based methods
(
68
;
14
;
8
;
30
;
72
;
34
;
13
e.g.,)
.
While state-of-the-art photo-z codes achieve excellent redshift accuracy for the broader galaxy population, for very dusty sources the results are more likely to be uncertain, or to fail catastrophically
(
122
;
73
e.g.,)
. Highly complete spectroscopic coverage is essential in order to test and refine our current photo-z methods for DSFGs.

Early DSFG studies were somewhat biased by the necessity of using probabilistic optical counterparts or by requiring radio counterpart identifications
(
15
;
38
;
9
;
36
;
120
e.g.,)
and therefore reported varying results for the overall DSFG redshift distribution.
As unbiased studies have been made possible by interferometers such as the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA), they have converged on a median redshift of around
z\sim 2.6
, increasing slightly with increasing flux and selection wavelength. Most show an asymmetric redshift distribution spanning roughly
1\lesssim z\lesssim 4
with a small subset at higher redshifts
(
45
;
44
;
101
;
51
)
.

However, to date, the only fully spectroscopically and flux-complete ALMA samples sit at the very bright end (
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}>25
mJy; the South Pole Telescope sample of
96
, which are mostly strongly lensed) and the very faint end (ASPECS;
6
, with only two sources that have
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}>2.25
mJy).
By contrast, for ALMA follow-up studies of James Clerk Maxwell Telescope (JCMT)/SCUBA-2 850
\mu
m surveys, with single-dish flux limits of
f_{{\rm 850\,\mu m}}\sim
3–10 mJy, the total spec-z fraction is typically
\sim
5–40%
(
48
;
51
;
101
;
85
)
, even in very well-studied fields, with full spectroscopic completeness only at the very brightest ends of the samples
(
20
;
40

\gtrsim
10–12 mJy; e.g.,)
.

With longer wavelength coverage, better sensitivity, and no atmospheric transmission concerns, JWST appears to be a powerful new tool for obtaining spec-zs for DSFGs.
Four years into JWST’s operation, there are now thousands of published JWST/NIRSpec spectra across key legacy fields, including for very dusty and high-redshift objects
(
41
;
49
;
18
e.g.,)
.
However, it is unclear to date how successful JWST has been at confirming redshifts for the overall DSFG population, and what the prospects are for completing the spectroscopic census, particularly towards the highest redshifts and at the faint end of the luminosity function.

In this paper, we present an ALMA spectroscopic survey of the large and deep sample of DSFGs in the GOODS-S field
(
59
)
presented by
44
. These sources benefit from many archival UV/optical/NIR spectroscopic campaigns, including medium-to-deep JWST/NIRSpec surveys, offering an opportunity to test how well JWST and ALMA can jointly map out the DSFG population.
The excellent multiwavelength archival data also provide extremely well-sampled SEDs from the UV to the radio, including wide and medium JWST/NIRCam bands and multiple ALMA bands for most sources. These enable us to compare the performance of various photo-z methods against the spec-zs for a sample with “ideal” photometric coverage.

This paper is organized as follows: In Section
II
, we describe our sample, the ancillary data, and our analysis of the ALMA data.
In Section
III
, we report the spec-zs obtained from the ALMA linescans and discuss our spectroscopic completeness.
In Section
IV
, we describe the various photo-z methods and evaluate their performance.
In Section
V
, we discuss the redshift distribution of the sample, the high-redshift population, and the sources that are not yet spectroscopically confirmed.
Finally, in Section
VI
, we summarize our results.

\Lambda
CDM cosmology with
\Omega_{m}=0.3

km s

(

II.1

\mu

\mu

II.3
ALMA linescans

We carried out two ALMA programs to perform millimeter spectral scans of sources drawn from the parent 870
\mu
m sample. The primary goal of the programs was to obtain spec-zs from CO and/or [C
i
] emission lines for a flux-complete sample of DSFGs.

In the initial program (project code #2021.1.00024.S), we targeted 51 sources with
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}>2.25
mJy in ALMA Bands 3, 4, and/or 6. The sources were observed in four tunings in band 3 spanning
\sim
84–111 GHz, three tunings in band 4 spanning
\sim
140–163 GHz, and two tunings in band 6 spanning
\sim
228–252 GHz. These tunings were chosen to cover 2+ CO and/or [C
i
] emission for the majority of redshifts at
z\gtrsim 1
. We included objects with known spec-zs only in the appropriate tunings to detect CO emission (i.e., not every source were targeted in every tuning); furthermore, our ALMA pointings covered six additional fainter sources from the
44
sample, for a total of 57 galaxies. The expected line fluxes (and required sensitivities) were estimated assuming empirical
L_{\rm IR}
–
L^{\prime}_{\rm CO}
conversions
47
;
78
, a FWHM of 300 km s
-1
, and the CO line brightness temperature ratios from
23
. For efficiency, we divided the sample into three subsamples (bright, medium, and faint) based on the observed
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}
and
K_{s}
-band fluxes, with the rms sensitivity requirements adjusted for each subsample. The data were calibrated with
CASA
version 6.2.1.7 using the default ALMA pipeline parameters. The continuum detections obtained from the ALMA data were initially discussed in
82
and
42
; in this paper we provide a more comprehensive analysis of the line detections in the dataset.

Later, in a follow-up program (project code #2024.1.01213.S), we obtained
\sim 2.5\times
deeper scans in ALMA Bands 3 and 4 for 24 sources with
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}>2.5
mJy that did not have multiple lines detected in the initial survey. The tunings were set up in a similar manner to the previous program, with four tunings in each band, spanning
\sim
85–111 GHz in band 3 and
\sim
137–163 GHz in band 4 (thus covering 2+ lines for nearly all redshifts from
2<z<5
, where most of the unconfirmed sources were likely to fall). The spacing between the tunings was reduced slightly compared to the previous program in order to remove any gaps between spectral windows. In this program, we divided the sample into two subsets based on the expected line flux to maximize efficiency. We also included several sources with known spec-zs in the appropriate tunings.
The data from this program were calibrated with
CASA
version 6.6.1.17 and the default parameters.

For both datasets, we produced cleaned spectral cubes and continuum images for each spectral window (without combining overlapping tunings) using the
tclean
routine, adopting multithreshold automasking at the standard thresholds, Briggs weighting with
robust
= 0.5, and the standard clipping at the edges of spectral windows. The cleaned spectral cubes have a frequency resolution of 3.9 MHz. We measure continuum fluxes in the cleaned continuum images at the known source positions using 1
\farcs
6 apertures, with errors measured by placing random apertures in other regions of the image with the source masked out.

II.4
Line detection procedure

To identify emission lines in the cleaned ALMA spectral cubes, we use a matched-filtering algorithm. Our method is similar to that of the
LineSeeker
code
(
60
)
, but designed for the format of our dataset, i.e., multiple spectral cubes for targets with known positions.
In brief, we first convolve the data cubes along just the spectral dimension with a series of 24 1-D Gaussian kernels ranging in FWHM from 200 km s
-1
to 1500 km s
-1
. We then search for peaks in the smoothed cube at the coordinate of the targets from the 870
\mu
m continuum imaging
(
44
)
.
The rms noise in the peak channel is determined by sampling random positions within 15
′′
of the 870
\mu
m position, after masking out the central 2
\farcs
0 region. We compare the peak signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) values obtained with each kernel and select the kernel producing the highest S/N for our final line measurement.
If the maximum S/N exceeds our initial detection threshold of 3.5, we then identify the brightest pixel in the 2D peak channel map within 1
\farcs
0 of the original coordinate and extract the final spectrum at this position, binning to a spectral resolution of 80 km s
-1
. The central frequency and FWHM of the line are measured from a Gaussian fit to the spectrum.

Assuming Gaussian statistics and the minimum smoothing kernel of 200 km s
-1
, the number of expected false positives at a threshold of
4.5\sigma
is
\lesssim
1 in all three ALMA bands (larger smoothing kernels/wider binning would reduce the number of expected false positives).
However, as a more complete check, we perform our identical extraction procedure at 2000 random positions in each data cube, smoothing the data with the same kernel size as the detected line and masking out the known source(s). We then do the same for the inverted cubes (multiplied by
-1.0
).
We find that at 4.5
\sigma
, the probability of a false detection is
\lesssim
1
\%
in both the original and the inverted cubes, and this is not highly dependent on the smoothing kernel, the observed frequency, or the radius used for the rms noise measurement. Based on this evidence, for simplicity, we adopt 4.5
\sigma
as our significance threshold for emission lines across all bands.

III
Spectroscopic Redshifts

III.1
Redshift identification

In all, we find
\geq 4.5\sigma
peaks in 27 DSFGs. We use these detections to identify redshifts for the sample in several quality tiers, taking into account the preexisting photometric and spectroscopic data.
For nine sources, we determine a secure redshift using two or more
>4.5\sigma
lines (four of these redshifts were reported in
42
from the same dataset, and three have other literature spec-zs). For five additional sources, we confirm a previous spec-z from the literature with at least one
>4.5\sigma
millimeter line (several have additional fainter lines as well). For SG-ALMA-9, we revise the redshift to
z_{\rm spec}=2.575
rather than
z_{\rm spec}=2.322
, as originally reported by
42
. Our updated redshift is in agreement with a previous VLT/FORS2 redshift from
111
.

Next, for 11 sources with a single
>4.5\sigma
ALMA line but no existing spec-z, we use the photo-zs from
105
and
53
(Section
II.2
) and the grism-zs from
88
to assign the most probable solution out of the possible line identifications. We consider these redshifts tentative, but we weight them higher if multiple redshift estimates converge towards one solution.
Several of these redshifts fall between
1.8<z<2.0
, where we expect to detect only one line given our spectral window setups.
For one source (SG-ALMA-55) that is blended in the
105
catalog and has no other photo-z estimate, we use a combination of our own photo-z estimates (Section
IV.2
) and the
K_{s}
-band magnitude to assign a tentative
z_{\rm spec}=2.163
based on a
5.4\sigma
line at
\nu_{\rm obs}=145.754
GHz.

In total, we have identified redshifts based on or in agreement with
>4.5\sigma
line(s) for 20 sources. We note that in two of these sources, we find additional spurious lines at
\gtrsim 4.5\sigma
, i.e., they do not correspond to any known FIR emission line, given the known spec-z that was verified by another method or from brighter lines. This number is higher than expected based on our false positive esimates; however, we note that the spurious lines typically appear as narrow spikes near the edges of spectral windows. They may be due to poor bandwidth calibration at the edges, or to incomplete continuum subtraction.

For two sources (SG-ALMA-13 and SG-ALMA-17) where we detect no
>4.5\sigma
lines, we were able to detect a lower significance line using a previously confirmed spec-z. Additionally, for one galaxy (SG-ALMA-4) that had a VLT/ISAAC redshift of
z_{\rm spec}=2.252
based off a
>4\sigma
2.1
\mu
m line identified as H
\alpha

(
29
)
, our follow-up scans detected neither of the expected lines at 106.33 GHz or 141.77 GHz, which are the locations of CO(3–2) and CO(4–3), respectively, at that redshift.
However, there is a single
4.4\sigma
peak in the ALMA data at 155.55 GHz, just below our detection threshold, which could correspond to CO(4–3) at
z=1.964
, in better agreement with the photo-zs. In this work, we keep the spec-z reported in
29
, but we caution that future follow-up is necessary to confirm this redshift.

IV
Photometric Redshifts

We next estimate the best redshifts for the remainder of our sample using several photo-z fitting codes, using the high fraction of spec-zs in our sample to test the performance of each method. We first describe the various methods we use to estimate photo-zs and then discuss their performance against the spec-zs and their relative agreement.

IV.1
Photo-z methods

The photo-z estimates that we compare are as follows:

EAZY
: As described in Section
II.2
, most of our sample have
EAZY
redshifts published in the
105
and/or
53
catalogs.
EAZY
constructs a redshift grid using a set of pre-defined templates and then performs a simple
\chi^{2}
-minimization routine to determine the best-fitting redshift.

It is important to note that the different sets of
EAZY
photo-zs considered here used different template sets and photometric bands (see Section
II.2
). The complementary datasets used by these studies make it possible to see how well deep optical/NIR legacy surveys constrain redshifts for the full submillimeter-selected DSFG population.

FIR redshifts
: FIR color-based or dust emission template-based photo-z methods are particularly attractive for submillimeter samples, because they do not require ancillary optical/NIR counterpart matching and can therefore be easily applied to wide single-dish surveys.

Here we test two FIR photo-z methods: The first is the
MMPZ
code (
34
), which takes into account the shape of the dust SED and the total luminosity, using an empirical relation between the rest-frame peak wavelength,
\lambda_{\rm peak}
, and integrated FIR luminosity,
L_{\rm IR}
, to attempt to break the redshift-temperature degeneracy.
The second is the
mbb
package
(
84
)
, a simple Bayesian modified-blackbody (MBB) fitting code that can fit the redshift along with the other dust parameters.
mbb
supports a variety of models and parameters governing the dust emission; however, here we mainly use it as a single-template fitter.

Fitting is performed across the excellent photometric coverage from 24
\mu
m to 3 mm in 11 bands, adding a 10% relative flux calibration error in quadrature to the error for each band.

MMPZ
does not require any user-specified parameters aside from the input photometry. Since the uncertainties on the Herschel/SPIRE flux deblending has been known to bias the fits, we increase the errors (which already include confusion noise) by a factor of 50%, finding slightly better accuracy compared to the spec-zs with this factor included.
For
mbb
, we use an optically thin MBB with fixed
T=35
K and
\beta=1.8
, and we include a MIR power law joined where the blackbody slope is equal to the power-law slope,
\alpha=2.0

(
50
e.g.,)
.
We find that the results with uniform priors on
z
are comparable to those with more informative priors (e.g., Gaussian); therefore, for simplicity, we adopt the uniform
z
priors, and we adopt log-uniform priors on
L_{\rm IR}
.

MAGPHYS
: Lastly, we perform fits to the full optical to millimeter photometry using the
MAGPHYS
code with the
photo-z
extension
(
46
;
45
;
19
)
. This code is unique among energy-balance SED codes in that it is specifically designed to include the FIR to radio data along with the optical/NIR data in estimating
z_{\rm phot}
, and it was designed with a particular focus on estimating DSFG properties.
MAGPHYS
has been used to derive photo-zs for most of the other large, unbiased ALMA 870
\mu
m surveys of DSFGs
(
45
;
51
;
101
e.g.,)
.

For the
MAGPHYS
fits, we use the same 11 bands as before (including the increased error on the
Herschel/SPIRE bands),
plus the HST and JWST fluxes from JADES DR5, where available.
For the one source not covered by the JWST mosaics, we use the CANDELS photometry
(
63
)
. We also take the Spitzer/IRAC 3.6–8.0
\mu
m photometry from
63
.

MAGPHYS
does not require user-specified parameters, using a Bayesian framework to marginalize over a large library of stellar and dust emission models and enforcing energy balance between the dust attenuation and emission.

In Table
3
, we give the best-fit photo-zs and errors obtained with each method for the full sample, along with the spec-zs from our ALMA data and the literature surveys (Section
II.2
). For convenience, we also list the ALMA 870
\mu
m fluxes of our sample and the FIR luminosity of each source, integrated from an additional
mbb
fit performed with the redshift fixed to the best available redshift.

IV.2
Comparison with spec-zs

We compare the results from each of the above photo-z codes to the available spec-zs for our ALMA sample. The latter do not include tentative spec-zs that we inferred using photo-zs, to avoid biasing the results. For completeness, we include the
88
HST grism-zs as one of the “photo-z” comparison samples.

In Figure
4
, we plot
\Delta z\equiv z_{\rm phot}-z_{\rm spec}
vs.
z_{\rm spec}
for each of the photo-z methods, labeling the code and the wavelength range used. We mark sources previously identified in
44
as either blended with foreground objects or
K_{s}
-dark (black open squares), since these are objects where we would expect optical/NIR-based photo-zs to perform poorly. The median reported error on
z_{\rm phot}
is plotted at the far right in each panel (gray error bars).

To compare performance consistently across methods, we measure the precision (scatter) by computing the normalized median absolute deviation (NMAD), defined as:

\sigma_{\rm NMAD}\equiv 1.483\times\textnormal{median}\left(\left|\frac{\Delta z-{\rm median}(\Delta z)}{1+z_{\rm spec}}\right|\right).

(1)

We also compute the outlier fraction,
\eta
, which we define as the fraction of sources with
|\Delta z|/(1+z_{\rm spec})>0.15
, and the bias, defined as the median
\Delta z/(1+z_{\rm spec})
for sources that are not classified as outliers. We report
\sigma_{\rm NMAD}
,
\eta
, and the bias in each panel of Figure
4
for reference.

We can draw several broad conclusions from Figure
4
. First, all of the methods have outlier fractions of at least
\eta>20\%
. Both sets of
EAZY
photo-zs perform relatively well, although they still have significant scatter and outlier fractions.
The
105
photo-zs have a slightly lower bias and scatter than the
53
photo-zs, although the difference is small.
Here we only consider the objects assigned quality flag
Q<3
in the
105
catalog based on the
EAZY
output. Objects with
Q>3
photzs perform much worse; indeed, all six of the
Q>3
sources with spec-zs are classified as outliers (including two optically dark sources, which have
z_{\rm phot}>7
).

As expected, the
EAZY
photo-zs do not perform well for the blends or
K_{s}
-dark sources well, generally underpredicting their redshifts (for blends) or overpredicting them (for
K_{s}
-dark sources). With the JWST data included, the
53
photo-zs perform better than the
105
photo-zs for the
K_{s}
-dark sources (thanks to the sensitivity improvement of JWST over HST and Spitzer); however, blends remain a problem for any optical/NIR photo-zs.

The
88
grism-zs, which we include for completeness, perform fairly comparably to the best photo-zs. They also achieve the lowest bias (
-0.002
) of any method. Nevertheless, they are not free from outliers and fail to access high redshifts (
z>3
) due to selection and/or sensitivity issues.

Next, we see that the FIR photo-zs with
MMPZ
and
mbb
have higher scatter and outlier fractions than the optical/NIR photo-zs, with
\sigma_{z}\sim 0.12
–0.14 and similar outlier fractions (37.5%). This is not surprising given the intrinsic variation in the dust SED and the smaller number of bands to fit compared to the optical/NIR. The overall scatter in the
MMPZ
results is slightly lower, but they suffer from a strong bias (
-0.087
) that is clearly correlated with
z
. The code tends to favor solutions close to
z\sim 2.0
–2.5. By contrast, the
mbb
fits are less biased but have the highest scatter of any of the methods tested. We observe that the error bars from
MMPZ
are extremely wide, likely reflecting the large range in dust models employed by the code, whereas the errors on the
mbb
fits are significantly underestimated since we fit a single isothermal template.

To summarize our findings, although FIR data alone do not provide accurate redshifts, we observe that combining them with the optical/NIR data tends to reduce the rate of outliers. However, no photo-z method tested here is able to reduce the outlier fraction for DSFGs below 20%, even with high-quality JWST data and FIR data included. This emphasizes the necessity of deep spectroscopy for future large DSFG surveys.

Dependence on NIR flux and redshift

We next illustrate that the redshifts of the DSFGs and the relative agreement of their photo-zs depends on their NIR flux and redshift. In Figure

This figure illustrates that as we move to higher
K_{s}

) for

; below

V
Discussion

V.1
Redshift distribution

As we have discussed, our sample’s redshift distribution is largely spectroscopically complete, especially at higher fluxes. Since all of the DSFGs have robust counterparts and our survey is flux-complete down to
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}=2.25
mJy, we are able to map the redshift distribution of DSFGs down to nearly the confusion limit for a single-dish 15-m telescope at 850
\mu
m
(
43
e.g.,)
.

We construct the redshift distribution of our sample using photo-zs for the 23 DSFGs that do not have spec-zs. For these, we adopt as the best available redshift the 3D-HST grism-z from
88
, if available (8 sources), followed by the ZFOURGE photo-z from
105
, if it has quality flag
Q<3
(high quality; 11 sources), and, finally, the JADES photo-z from
53
(4 sources). This ordering is based on the relative bias and scatter of each catalog (see Section
IV.2
).

In Figure
6
, we plot the
K_{s}
(top) and F444W (bottom) magnitudes versus the best redshift estimates.
This figure confirms that there is a correlation between the NIR brightness and redshift, as shown previously for DSFGs (e.g.,
48
;
44
;
51
;
83
). The relation is reasonably tight, with a
\pm
1 mag spread at a given redshift for F444W and a slightly larger dispersion for
K_{s}
.
For comparison, we plot the expected track for our sample’s mean
MAGPHYS
SED with fixed luminosity normalized to our median F444W magnitude and redshift. This track agrees well with the observed fluxes from
1\lesssim z\lesssim 4
, indicating that the evolution of the NIR flux with redshift can largely be accounted for by
K
-correction and distance effects, with the scatter at a given redshift reflecting the intrinsic spread in stellar mass and dust content for DSFGs.

As in other studies
(
120
;
102
e.g.,)
, we model the redshifts as a log-normal distribution,

P(z)=\frac{1}{(1+z)\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-[\ln(1+z)-\ln(1+z_{\mu})]^{2}/2\sigma^{2}},

(2)

where
\ln(1+z_{\mu})
and
\sigma
represent the mean and standard deviation of the normally distributed variable
\ln(1+z)
. This distribution captures the asymmetric shape of the DSFG redshift distribution, with a steep fall-off below
z\lesssim 1
and a tail out to
z\gtrsim 5
.

We fit the model to the redshift distribution of just the flux-complete (
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}\geq 2.25
mJy) subset, using a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm to sample from the log-normal distribution 15000 times, assuming log-uniform priors on
z_{\mu}
and uniform priors on
\sigma
. By limiting to the flux-complete subset, we remove any bias in the distribution introduced by incompleteness, at the cost of a smaller sample size (44 galaxies instead of 75). We comment that using a normal distribution instead of the log-normal also gives a good fit to our data. Using leave-one-out cross-validation, we confirm that the log-normal is slightly preferred, but the difference is not statistically significant.

In Figure
8
, we show the median and 5%–95% range of the fit posterior. The median (and 68% credible interval) of the posteriors for the parameters are
z_{\mu}=2.65^{+0.12}_{-0.12}
and
\sigma=0.21^{+0.03}_{-0.02}
. This is in good agreement with previous results for AzTEC 1.1 mm galaxies (
z_{\mu}=2.6
;
120
) and for ALESS (
z_{\mu}\sim 2.5
;
102
).

We also show the redshift distribution for the 707 AS2UDS DSFGs
(
51
)
, demonstrating that our best-fit is in good agreement with the redshifts of their larger sample, which were mainly
MAGPHYS
photo-zs. Since we cover a smaller sky area than AS2UDS, our sample is somewhat more prone to cosmic variance; as mentioned in Section
III.2
, many of our DSFGs appear to be associated with overdensities in the GOODS-S field. We plot the redshifts of several of these overdensities on Figure
8
(purple triangles) to illustrate the alignment with the peaks in our redshift distribution.

Nevertheless, our agreement with the AS2UDS redshift distribution is encouraging, since it implies that large photo-z-based studies may not suffer from substantial biases due to incorrect photo-zs—despite the fact that we find individual photo-z estimates result in catastrophic outliers in
>
20% of DSFGs regardless of method.
This agreement is important confirmation for simulations that attempt to match the DSFG redshift distribution, since these typically benchmark their models against the photo-z distributions for ALMA samples (e.g.,
55
and
74
compare to AS2UDS, which had a published spec-z fraction of
\sim
6%;
51
).

However, for smaller samples, the bias due to photo-zs may be more significant. For example, considering just the
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}>3
mJy subset of our sample (27 sources), which is 96% spectroscopically confirmed, the median
\langle z\rangle=2.70
is slightly lower than previous estimates from
44
: Using spec-zs and the
105
photo-zs, they reported a median
\langle z\rangle=2.86
, while with FIR photo-zs (based on an Arp 220 template) in place of photo-zs, they found
\langle z\rangle=3.26
, significantly higher than our median.

V.3
Constraints on the high-redshift population

Next, we attempt to use our sample to constrain the fraction of 870
\mu
m-selected DSFGs at high redshift (
z>4
–5). While it is known that a small subset of DSFGs populate the asymmetric high-redshift tail of the redshift distribution, the current constraints on the true prevalence and typical properties of dusty sources at
z>4
are still uncertain (
61
;
121
;
4
;
106
e.g.,
; A. Barger et al. 2026, in preparation).
Furthermore, the observed fraction is dependent on the survey depth, selection wavelength, flux completeness, and quality of available photometric redshifts.

We have only two spectroscopically confirmed
z\gtrsim 4
sources (one is tentative) and no confirmed
z>5
sources in our sample. While several of the photo-z posterior distributions include
z>5
solutions, no source has a best-fit photo-z at
z>5
. Based on the best available redshifts alone, we find a
z>4

%; errors are 1

58
) and a
z>5
fraction of 0 out of 75 (

%).

As a further test, we can use the log-normal fit to the redshift distribution of our flux-complete subset to make an estimate of the high-redshift fraction, simply by sampling the posterior and integrating the probability above each redshift threshold. This implies that 6.6% of DSFGs lie at
z>4
and 0.8% lie at
z>5
, with 1
\sigma
upper limits of 9.8% at
z>4
and 1.8% at
z>5
. These upper limits fully account for the sampling of the log-normal and the full uncertainties on the fit parameters.

Comparing to literature samples, our upper limits are mostly consistent with the fractions from AS2UDS (2.0
{}^{+0.6}_{-0.5}
% at
z>4
and 0.56
{}^{+0.45}_{-0.27}
% at
z>5
;

), ALESS (16

z>4
and 2.0
{}^{+2.7}_{-1.3}

These samples have somewhat different flux ranges and completeness limits, and these fractions are largely based off of photo-zs, which may contribute to the variance between them. There is also uncertainty due to cosmic variance that is not captured in the quoted error bars.

For a fairer comparison, we can ask whether the various photo-z estimates for our sample would have placed more objects at high redshifts if we did not have the spec-zs.
Using the
EAZY

Various explanations for the steep fall-off in the number of DSFGs of
z>4
have been offered in the literature. From an observational standpoint, the difficulty of selecting and confirming these galaxies amidst the more common DSFGs around cosmic noon has been a limiting factor for a long time. Nevertheless, our results indicate that at least above
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}>2.25
mJy, there is likely not a large population of
z>4
–5 DSFGs hidden in existing submillimeter surveys with underestimated photo-z estimates. There could still be a relatively numerous population of DSFGs that simply lie below the current single-dish and/or ALMA completeness limits, requiring alternative selections and/or stacking to fully survey
(

;
57
;

V.4
What sources are we still missing?

We now turn to the issue of spectroscopic completeness and which sources have eluded confirmation, despite benefiting from decades of spectroscopic campaigns, including significant investments of JWST and ALMA time.

We first explore the relative completeness of our sample as a function of submillimeter and NIR flux.
In Figure
10
, we plot 870
\mu
m flux versus
K_{s}
magnitude, using different colored symbols to show which method(s) were used to obtain the redshift.
Stacked symbols indicate redshifts obtained via multiple surveys (e.g., a ground-based spec-z later confirmed by ALMA). For clarity, we show the
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}=2.5
mJy threshold (green dotted line and shaded region), above which the spectroscopic completeness for secure (tentative) spec-zs is 72% (97%), while below it is 40% (49%).

This figure demonstrates some trends that we might expect. For example, ALMA (dark and light blue circles) is very effective at the brightest 870
\mu
m fluxes. Meanwhile, the distribution of ground-based spec-zs (green triangles) is fairly agnostic to
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}
but is limited to
K_{s}<24
mag, while the JWST spec-zs (purple hexagons) are fairly evenly distributed across both
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}
and
K_{s}
magnitude.

The success rate for sources targeted with JWST/NIRSpec appears to be much better than other methods. For example, for the 20 of our sources in the DJA NIRSpec catalog (version 4), only one has a NIRSpec PRISM observation that did not result in a secure spec-z (SG-ALMA-70, exposure time of
\sim 2400
s).
For the other 19 sources, all but one had PRISM exposure times ranging from
\sim
2400 to
\sim
8500s, while the last one was observed for
\sim
66500s (
\sim
18.5 hrs). This success rate suggests that, provided DSFGs can be selected and targeted on the NIRSpec MSA masks, the likelihood of obtaining a spec-z with the NIRSpec PRISM is remarkably high even with relatively shallow programs
(
80
e.g.,)
. Some of our sources were also observed in the higher-resolution NIRSpec gratings, although for these the success rate drops due to the relatively lower sensitivity and more limited redshift coverage.

However, only 29% of our DSFG sample have JWST spec-zs. For
K_{s}<23
mag (
K_{s}>23
mag), this fraction is 23% (32%), and for
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}>2.5
mJy (
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}<2.5
mJy), it is 38% (24%). In other words, JWST has not yet provided particularly complete spectroscopic coverage for submillimeter-selected samples such as this one, likely due in part to their low space density (
\sim
1 arcmin
-2
) (and hence difficulty in targeting large samples efficiently with MSA pointings).

Of the
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}>2.5
mJy sources, only one object (SG-ALMA-12) still lacks a spec-z from any method. This source was only targeted in a single ALMA tuning due to a mistaken Keck/DEIMOS spec-z that turned out to correspond to a foreground galaxy. It is likely that a full ALMA spectral scan would identify a redshift, considering the photo-z (
z_{\rm phot}=3.76
) and ALMA measured redshifts for sources with similar values of
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}
. A further eight
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}>2.5
mJy sources have tentative spec-zs based off a single millimeter line. There is also SG-ALMA-4, which has an optical spec-z that seems incongruous with the ALMA data, as discussed in Section
III.1
.

At the sensitivity of our ALMA scans, only five sources below
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}=2.5
mJy have a millimeter line detection, and only one has 2 or more lines. Moreover, since our ALMA 870
\mu
m survey is only 100% complete down to
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}\geq 2.25
mJy, we may be missing additional faint sources elsewhere in the field. Especially at
K_{s}>23
mag, these faint DSFGs are very hard to confirm. Only two have JWST redshifts, despite JWST programs targeting optically faint or dark galaxies
(
18
e.g.,)
. To complete the spectroscopic survey of this population, which may lie at the highest redshifts
(
18
;
123
e.g.,)
, will require significant additional investment of ALMA and/or JWST time.

The ALMA-based redshifts are distributed between
1.8\lesssim z\lesssim 4
, although we note that just 12 sources have secure redshifts (two or more millimeter lines) from ALMA alone. Our observing program was designed to detect CO lines at
1\lesssim z\lesssim 6
. Since we know the redshifts of most of the ALMA sources lie within this range, we can conclude that the non-detections are simply an issue of sensitivity.

Ground-based spec-zs account for a relatively small number of spec-zs in total. This reflects the difficulty of obtaining emission line detections in DSFGs in the optical/NIR from the ground, hampered by their optical faintness and atmospheric absorption.
Also, the HST grism-zs, while highly complete at
z<3
, fall off at high redshifts due to wavelength coverage and the difficulty of detecting optical emission lines for DSFGs (
88
).
As noted in Section
IV.2
, these are generally not as precise as spec-zs, and they can suffer from catastrophic outliers in some cases.

Thanks to its better wavelength coverage and sensitivity, JWST/NIRSpec is more effective at obtaining spec-zs at
z>3
than ground-based instruments. Since nearly all the sources targeted with NIRSpec are confirmed, as discussed above, the observed distribution of the JWST spec-zs reflects the selection biases associated with the various NIRSpec programs.

As single-dish millimeter observations with instruments such as TolTEC and NIKA2 begin to be available for wider fields, efficient targeted spectroscopic campaigns along with more accurate photo-z estimation methods will become crucial for understanding the volume distribution and for determining the eventual descendants of DSFGs. The advent of the ALMA Wideband Sensitivity Upgrade
(
28
)
will help with following up large, uniform samples with continuum imaging and spectroscopy down to fainter flux limits than are currently feasible, but it will likely be necessary to develop more efficient joint survey strategies with ALMA and JWST, or to utilize proxy selections based on other wavelengths or physical properties (
83
;
17
;
91
;
123
e.g.,
; A. Barger et al. 2026, in preparation)
in order to build spectroscopically complete samples of faint DSFGs.

VI
Summary

We presented new ALMA linescans of a large sample of DSFGs in the GOODS-S field. This sample is flux-complete and unbiased above
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}=2.25
mJy, and it benefits from a wealth of deep multiwavelength observations and other spectroscopy.

•

We used the ALMA data to obtain or confirm 20 spec-zs between
2\lesssim z\lesssim 4
, primarily at the bright end (
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}=3
–9 mJy) of our sample.

•

Combining these redshifts with others from the literature, we obtained the highest spec-z completeness for an unbiased,
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}\gtrsim 2
mJy flux-limited ALMA survey to date: In total, our sample is now 69% spectroscopically complete, while above
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}=2.5
mJy, the secure (tentative) spectroscopic completeness is 72% (97%).

•

We found that nearly all DSFGs targeted with JWST/NIRSpec had successful spec-z identifications. However, the total JWST spectroscopic coverage is just
\sim
30%, with no apparent variation with redshift, NIR magnitude, or submillimeter flux.

•

We used the spec-zs to test several photo-z codes and photometric ranges for our sample. All the photo-z methods that we tested had outlier fractions (
|\Delta z|/(1+z_{\rm spec})>0.15
) greater than 20%, including those using multi-band JWST data. While FIR-based photo-zs were not as accurate as those based on optical/NIR data, including the FIR data helped to limit high-redshift misidentifications and to reduce the overall outlier fraction.

•

Our results are consistent with
\lesssim 10
% of
f_{{\rm 870\,\mu m}}\geq 2.25
mJy DSFGs lying at
z>4
and
\lesssim 2
% lying at
z>5
. These fractions do not account for cosmic variance but they are not biased by incompleteness. Most of the photo-z methods tended to overestimate the number of DSFGs at
z>4
–5.
