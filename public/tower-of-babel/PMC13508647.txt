Outcomes of catheter-directed mechanical thrombectomy in patients with cancer-associated pulmonary embolism: a multicenter, prospective registry
Authors: Marta Banaszkiewicz, Sylwia Sławek-Szmyt, Jakub Stępniewski, Paweł Kurzyna, Aneta Klotzka, Wiktor Kuliczkowski, Jacek Klaudel, Maciej Lewandowski, Marek Grabka, Marek Roik, Marcin Waligóra, Ewa Mroczek, Michał Łomiak, Szymon Darocha, Zbigniew Krasiński, Adam Torbicki, Grzegorz Kopeć, Aleksander Araszkiewicz, Marcin Kurzyna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer-associated pulmonary embolism (PE) is a commonplace complication that contributes to morbidity and mortality. Data of catheter-directed mechanical thrombectomy (CDMT) in patients with cancer are limited.ObjectivesTo assess the efficacy and safety of CDMT in patients with intermediate-high or high-risk PE and cancer comorbidity.MethodsMulticenter prospective, observational study of 290 PE patients treated with CDMT using continuous vacuum aspiration thrombectomy systems. Patients were classified into 2 groups: oncologic patients (OP) and nononcologic patients (NOP). The primary safety endpoints included in-hospital all-cause mortality and procedure-related major bleeding. The primary efficacy endpoint was the change in right-to-left-ventricle (RV/LV) ratio before and 48 hours after CDMT. The efficacy and safety of CDMT were compared between OP and NOP.ResultsActive cancer was observed in 18.3% (53/290) of patients with PE treated with CDMT. OP did not differ significantly from NOP in terms of baseline characteristics. After treatment, both OP and NOP demonstrated RV/LV ratio reduction by 27% (the mean change of −0.37 ± 0.22 (P < .001) in OP vs −0.31± 0.21 (P < .001) in NOP, P = .166). Major bleeding occurred in 1.9% (1/53) of OP and in 0.8% (2/237) of NOP (P = .455). The rate of all-cause in-hospital mortality reached 9.4% (5/53) in OP and 6.4% within NOP (P = .383).ConclusionsCDMT in OP characterized an acceptable safety profile and substantial improvement in RV function, similar to those observed in NOP. Patients with cancer-related PE had similar in-hospital survival rate compared with patients without cancer, when treated with CDMT.

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE), as one of the venous thromboembolism (VTE) manifestations, constitutes a common complication in cancer patients. Epidemiologic data suggest that one in every 4 VTE events is associated with cancer [1,2] and one in 10 patients with cancer will develop VTE [[3], [4], [5]]. In line with current guidelines [6], anticoagulant therapy is the mainstay of treatment in patients with PE, whereas thrombolytic therapy is reserved only for patients with hemodynamic instability. However, systemic thrombolysis (ST) might be associated with an increased risk of bleeding in patients with active cancer [7,8]. Catheter-based therapies (CBTs), using a variety of newly developed tools, have emerged as an option for reperfusion reducing the risk of bleeding of ST. Data concerning efficacy and safety of CBT in patients with high-risk (HR) or intermediate-high-risk (IHR) PE are encouraging, presenting acute improvement of right ventricle (RV) function and pulmonary hemodynamics [[9], [10], [11]]. However, patients with active cancer have been underrepresented or excluded from registries and trials of CBT, likely due to the higher risks conferred by their disease and a tendency toward undertreatment [12,13]. Thus, the outcomes of endovascular therapies in patients with cancer comorbidity remain unclear. The aim of the study is to assess efficacy and safety of catheter-directed mechanical thrombectomy (CDMT) in patients with IHR or HR PE and cancer.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and data recording

This is a prospective, multicenter observational study conducted at 8 centers (Supplementary Table S1) in Poland from January 2019 to June 2025. Data for this research were received from the prospective, multicenter registry of patients treated by Polish PERTs: the Polish Multicentre PE Response Teams Outcomes Registry registered in ClinicalTrials.gov.NCT04879069.

The protocol of the study was in line with the Declaration of Helsinki and was accepted by the institutional bioethics committee of the coordinating center (Poznan University of Medical Sciences Bioethics Committee; the number of approval 271/2021). Informed consent was obtained from all patients (if a patient was unable to provide written consent, it was obtained from a relative). Eligibility criteria included adult patients diagnosed with IHR or HR PE (symptoms onset within past 14 days) according to the European Society of Cardiology Guidelines and qualified to CDMT by the institutional PE response team (PERT). Patients with IHR PE were eligible for CDMT if they presented at least one of the following characteristics, lasting longer than 12 to 24 hours despite adequate initial anticoagulation (AC): systolic blood pressure 90 to 100 mmHg, heart rate ≥ 100 bpm, arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2) ≤ 90% on room air, and were considered to be at risk of bleeding complications when treated with full dose of ST. In addition, patients with IHR PE and hemodynamic deterioration (defined as sudden occurrence of at least one of the abovementioned features of severe PE) were also included. Patients with HR PE were enrolled if they had absolute contraindications to ST or previously administered ST failed [6,9,12]. The key exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patient’s refusal to participate in the study, (2) history of chronic pulmonary hypertension, (3) presence of intracardiac thrombi, (4) active major bleeding as defined by the Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) criteria, (5) severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count below 20 000/μl), and (6) life expectancy <1 month due to comorbidities (as assessed by attending physician). Data collection comprised demographic characteristics, vital signs, laboratory results, imaging findings, as well as hemodynamic parameters and procedural characteristics.

Treatment and procedural details

All patients received AC with either low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) or unfractionated heparin (UFH) according to physician recommendations and current guidelines. CDMT was performed using one of the following devices, depending on the availability at the time and on investigational sites: 8F Indigo system, Lightning 12 system or Lightning Flash system (Penumbra, Inc). These devices operate using continuous vacuum aspiration and are computer-assisted to minimize blood loss. Procedural details regarding CDMT using either 8F Indigo or Lightning 12 System were described by our team previously [14,15]. A thorough review of thrombectomy details with Lightning Flash thrombectomy has been also published [16]. In brief, CDMT was performed primarily via the common femoral vein access. Immediately before thrombectomy, right heart catheterization was performed to assess pulmonary artery pressures (PAPs). Cardiac output (CO), cardiac index (CI), and total pulmonary resistance were calculated using the Fick method. Subsequently, pulmonary angiography was performed with a 6F pigtail catheter to assess thrombi load. A thrombectomy catheter was then advanced over a 0.035ʺ stiff guidewire into the right or left main pulmonary artery proximal to the clot, and aspiration was activated. The end of the procedure was decided by the operator and depended on the patient’s hemodynamic status, the completeness of thrombus aspiration, as well as the amount of aspirated blood (should not exceed 350 mL). The intraprocedural AC was managed with UFH under control of activated clotting time (therapeutic range: 200-300 seconds), unless contraindicated. The postprocedural AC was provided with UFH or LMWH in weight-adjusted dose for 24 to 48 hours. Switching to oral anticoagulant was at the treating physician’s discretion and depended on the patient’s comorbidities and clinical condition.

Outcomes and study endpoints

The primary efficacy endpoint was the change in RV to LV ratio (RV/LV ratio) assessed in the transthoracic echocardiography before and 48 hours (±8 hours) after the CDMT. The RV/LV ratio was assessed in the RV-focused four-chamber view by blinded, independent echocardiography specialists at each site. The same specialist assessed both measurements (before intervention and after intervention) in all cases. Secondary efficacy outcomes were the reduction in PAPs, including systolic PAP (sPAP) and mean PAP, assessed immediately after the procedure. The primary safety endpoint was defined as a composite of in-hospital all-cause mortality or procedure-related major bleeding categorized by the BARC scale [17]. Major bleeding was classified as meeting BARC types 3b, 3c, or 5. Additionally, procedure-related adverse events were monitored and included cardiac tamponade, cardiac injury, PA injury, or access-site complications. The evaluation of clinical safety outcomes was performed by physicians not involved in the procedure. All-cause mortality rate during the 30-day follow-up period was also evaluated.

Comparison between oncologic and nononcologic patients

The oncologic group (OP, oncologic patients) included patients with diagnosed active malignancy before or during anticancer treatment (defined as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, surgery, etc.), or patients who were ineligible for antineoplastic treatment due to lack of further therapeutic options. Oncologic status was determined as of the date of PE diagnosis. Cancer stage was defined as follows: (1) localized neoplastic disease (stage I): the tumor does not infiltrate deeply into nearby tissues or spread to the lymph nodes, (2) locally advanced neoplastic disease (stage II or III): the tumor has spread deeper into the neighboring tissues or/and to local lymph nodes, but there are no distant metastases; (3) metastatic neoplastic disease (stage IV): the presence of distant metastases. Nononcologic group (NOP, nononcologic patients) was defined as patients without active neoplastic disease. Baseline clinical characteristics, efficacy and safety of CDMT, and in-hospital and 30-day mortality rates were compared.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean with standard deviation or median with IQR according to distribution. Student’s t-test was used to compare continuous variables between groups with normally distributed data. Nonparametric U-Mann–Whitney or Wilcoxon tests were used to compare data that were not normally distributed. Qualitative variables were described with n (%) and analyzed using the chi-squared test. A univariable and multivariable analysis of postintervention RV/LV ratio was conducted using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). To account for baseline RV/LV ratio differences and within-subject variability while evaluating treatment effect, we modeled postintervention RV/LV with preintervention RV/LV as a covariate, rather than analyzing the change (ΔRV/LV) directly. The primary model included active cancer status, age, sex, body mass indexs, preintervention RV/LV, and important clinical covariates. Variables were selected based on clinical relevance, prior literature, and observed differences between groups in univariable analysis (oncologic vs nononcologic groups). Linearity was confirmed for continuous variables, and model assumptions—including normality of residuals, homoscedasticity (Levene’s test), and absence of multicollinearity—were verified. To evaluate the robustness of the findings to the outcome definition, a sensitivity analysis was performed in which change in RV/LV ratio (post–pre) was used as the dependent variable, with the same covariate adjustment (reported in Supplementary Materials). Consistency of results across both models was interpreted as evidence of robustness of the primary findings. All P-values result from two-sided tests, with significance inferred at P ≤ .05. IBM SPSS Statistics package, version 29.0 (IBM) was applied for statistical analysis. All graphs were created using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.0 for macOS (GraphPad Software).

Results

Baseline and clinical characteristics

A total of 290 patients with PE treated with CDMT (42.4% female, median age 63.0 [47.0-71.0] years) were enrolled in the study. Of these, 53 patients were qualified into OP group (49.1% female, median age 67.0 [53.5-74.0] years). Baseline characteristics of OP and NOP groups are presented in Table 1. In the OP group, CDMT was most commonly performed in patients with ovarian cancer (18.9%), colorectal cancer (17.0%), and lung cancer (15.1%). In terms of cancer stage, the majority of OP had locally advanced (35.8%) or localized (32.1%) neoplastic disease. Importantly, 69.8% patients with cancer were undergoing anticancer treatment (Table 2). There were no significant differences in PE clinical presentations with regard to oncologic status. Regarding hemodynamic status, 13.2% in OP group and 34.2% in NOP were in normotensive shock, defined as cardiac index below 2.2 L/min/m2 with preserved systemic arterial pressure (P = .003). Significantly more patients in the OP group had absolute contraindications to ST compared with the NOP group (41.5% vs 25.5%, P = .020). CDMT procedure

Majority of the procedures were performed using the Penumbra Lightning 12 system in both the OP and NOP groups (55.3% vs 52.8%, P = .947, respectively). The median thrombectomy time was similar (29 minutes [22.75-45.25] for OP vs 36 minutes [26-45] for NOP, P = .267), with comparable estimated blood loss in both subgroups (for OP 334 ± 114 mL vs 333 ± 113 mL for NOP, P = .854). Seven (13.2%) OP and 39 (16.5%) NOP (P = 0.558) received adjunctive catheter-directed fibrinolysis (CDF) because of insufficient thrombus clearance coexisting with the need to terminate CDMT due to significant blood loss. A trend toward more frequent implantation of retrievable inferior vena cava filters in OP group compared with NOP group (8.8% vs 2.0%, P = .079) was observed due to contraindications to AC therapy. Within the OP group 26 (49.1%) patients were discharged on LMWH and 17 (32.1%) on direct oral anticoagulants, whereas in the NOP group most patients (75.1%) were discharged on direct oral anticoagulants (P < .001). Safety and mortality outcomes

In-hospital all-cause mortality within overall study population reached 6.9%, including 9.4% for the OP and 6.4% for the NOP, with the difference not being significant (P = .383). Of those, there were 4 procedure-related deaths, 3 (F, 49 y.o., nononcologic; F, 87 y.o., with locally advanced breast cancer; and F, 71 y.o., nononcologic) caused by RV decompensation and one (F, 68 y.o., nononcologic) caused by pulmonary infarction and reperfusion injury followed by massive pulmonary bleeding (confirmed in pathologic examinations). There were 3 (1.0%) cases of major bleeding observed among all patients, comprising one (1.9%) for the OP and 2 (0.8%) for the NOP, with no significant difference (P = .455). In detail, the patient with localized breast cancer, undergoing anticancer treatment, and with a history of pacemaker implantation within several days before HR PE incident and unsuccessful ST as a first-line treatment developed pocket hematoma. The patient required a transfusion of 4 units of packed red blood cells within 48 hours after the procedure. Within the NOP group, one patient had a hemorrhagic transformation of ischemic stroke, and one patient experienced fatal pulmonary bleeding mentioned above. Of note, the overall 30-day mortality rates reached 8.6% among all patients, comprising 17.0% in OP and 6.8% in NOP, with the significant difference between the subgroups (P = .027). Factors associated with RV/LV reduction after CDMT

To investigate the possible association between cancer status and post-CDMT RV/LV ratio, a multivariable ANCOVA model was developed. The results showed that active cancer was not significantly associated with the post-intervention RV/LV ratio (B = −0.012, P = .684). Higher preintervention RV/LV and older age were significantly associated with the post-CDMT RV/LV ratio (both P < .05). There was a trend toward worse RV/LV outcomes in patients requiring catecholamine support (B = 0.065, P = .053), suggesting a possible association with hemodynamic instability (Figure 2). Neither sex, body mass index, diabetes mellitus, nor respiratory failure was significantly associated with post-CDMT RV/LV ratio (all P > .05) in the study cohort. In a sensitivity analysis using the change in RV/LV ratio (ΔRV/LV) as the dependent variable, active malignancy was also not associated with RV/LV improvement after CDMT (B = −0.010, P = .734; Supplementary Table S2). Discussion

This is the first prospective observational study assessing the use of CDMT in patients with cancer-associated PE. The study revealed that (1) over one in 6 patients with PE treated with CDMT had an active cancer comorbidity, (2) CDMT performed with various tools resulted in substantial improvement in biochemical and hemodynamic parameters in OP, and (3) percutaneous thrombectomy in OP is associated with relatively low periprocedural risk, similar to that of NOP.

Considering demographic and baseline characteristics, there were no significant differences between OP and NOP groups. While malignancy is predominantly the disease of older age and the geriatric population has expanded in the recent decades [18], we observed only a trend toward older age within the OP group. Correspondingly, apart from malignancy, the VTE risk profile of patients with PE associated with cancer did not differ significantly from that of noncancer patients. However, there was a tendency to lower rates of traditional risk factors (eg, immobilization) within the OP group, suggesting that cancer might be the main VTE risk factor within the studied population. This might also be true regarding the types of cancer observed in the study population. The most common types were ovarian cancer, colorectal cancer, and lung cancer; among these, ovarian and lung cancers are considered to be among the most thrombogenic malignancies, along with pancreatic, stomach, and brain cancers [19].

As the characteristics of patients consulted by local PERTs as well as general CDMT outcomes were previously presented [14,15,[20], [21], [22], [23]], here we focused specifically on patients with cancer-associated PE. The percentage of OP treated with CDMT in the study is lower than those consulted by PERT in the studies describing the overall activity of PERT. In the present study, 18.3% (53/290) of patients with PE suffered from malignancy, while in previously published reports and meta-analysis this number ranged from 23.5% to 34.5% [22,24]. However, in previous reports concerning only interventional treatment of PE rather than general PERT activity, the percentage of patients with cancer was lower and similar to those observed in the current study [14,15]. This observation may reflect an underlying trend that patients with cancer comorbidities are less likely to undergo interventional cardiovascular procedures for PE.

The cooccurrence of neoplastic disease raised risk of unfavorable course of PE [6]. Thus, in the current study, the risk scores of patients with PE with concurrent cancer diagnosis were higher compared with those without cancer. On the other hand, the cancer comorbidity is also a risk factor for bleeding, included in the VTE-BLEED bleeding risk assessment scale [25,26]. Thus, OP seem to have an a priori overestimation of the bleeding risk [27,28]. Of note, among patients with PE, those with malignancy are less likely to receive ST as compared with noncancer patients, as revealed by Shalaby et al. and Weeda et al. (OR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.64-0.72 and OR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.48-0.64, respectively) [8,29]. Indeed, in the present study, absolute contraindications to thrombolysis were significantly more frequent in the OP group than in the NOP group. Although CBT may offer the advantage of lower bleeding risk avoiding the need for systemic administration of thrombolytics, clinical trials in patients with concomitant malignancy are rather limited.

In the observational retrospective cohort study of patients with cancer diagnosed with intermediate- or high-risk PE, treatment with catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) was associated with a decreased risk of major bleeding, lower risk of in-hospital death, and a lower risk of 90-day readmission in comparison with ST (20.8% vs 24.8%; OR 0.8, 95% 0.68-0.94) [30]. These observations were confirmed in the study conducted by Colombo et al., in which only 3.4% of oncologic patients with IHR PE treated with ultrasound-assisted CDT experienced major bleeding defined as BARC ≥ 3 [31]. When comparing major bleeding or the need for transfusion, no significant differences were found between patients with or without active cancer (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.01-6.62, P = 1, and OR 2.43, 95% CI 0.61-9.48, P = 0.18, respectively) [31]. Correspondingly, in the present study, we noticed low rate of major bleeding in the studied subgroups, with no difference according to oncologic status (1.9% in OP vs 0.8% in NOP). Our results are consistent with previous studies assessing the safety of different thrombectomy tools. In detail, CDMT with 8F catheter was associated with serious bleeding rates of 1.7% to 1.8% [15,32], whereas in the cohort treated with 12F or 16F catheter, serious bleeds occurred in 1.3% and 2.1% of patients, respectively [14,33]. Noteworthy, CDMT is associated with a predictable decrease in hemoglobin concentration, and therefore low hemoglobin concentration may be a potential contraindication to the procedure [34]. In the studied population, there was a trend toward lower hemoglobin concentration in the OP group, but the postprocedural decrease in hemoglobin level was similar between OP and NOP groups, and we did not observe a more frequent need for transfusion in any of the subgroups. Moreover, hospitalization length between both groups was similar and relatively short, confirming the safety of this procedure in patients with cancer comorbidity.

RV failure due to acute pressure overload is a strong predictor of poor prognosis in advanced PE [35]. Therefore, a reduction in RV/LV ratio, as an early indicator of RV function improvement, remains a valuable marker of CDMT effectiveness [32,[36], [37], [38], [39]]. As we have proven, the mean reduction of RV/LV ratio in OP was 0.37 and did not differ significantly from that achieved in NOP. Although our study evaluates the treatment outcomes of 3 different devices, our results are consistent with those of previous studies in which these devices were evaluated separately. The abovementioned studies revealed a reduction in RV/LV ratio from 0.33 to 0.48 as assessed 48 hours after procedure [14,15,32,33]. Similarly, the present study revealed on-table improvement in hemodynamic parameters, such as substantial reduction in PAPs in both OP and NOP groups, with the difference between subgroups not being significant. Again, the median reduction of sPAP (−9 mmHg) in OP was comparable with our previously published real-world data using 8F (−10.4 mmHg) [15] and 12F (−11.3 mmHg) catheters [14], as well as with the results reported in the randomized (A Prospective, Multicenter, Randomized Controlled Trial Evaluating Anticoagulation Alone Versus Anticoagulation Plus Mechanical Aspiration With The Indigo Aspiration System for the Treatment of Intermediate High Risk Acute Pulmonary Embolism) STORM-PE trial using a 16F catheter (−10.8 mmHg) [33]. Altogether, CDMT yielded the same results in both OP and NOP groups immediately after the procedure and after 48 hours, which should be considered a success given the initial more severe condition of OP and greater prothrombotic state resulting from the cancer itself.

In both OP and NOP groups, we observed a similar all-cause in-hospital mortality rate (9.4% vs 6.4%, P = .383). However, the percentage of procedure-related deaths was lower and reached 3.8% in the OP group and 0.8% in the NOP group (P = .154). The noted all-cause mortality in previous studies on different CDMT devices ranged from 0.8 to 5.5% [14,15,32,39]. The results we present are real-world data, which may explain the slightly higher all-cause mortality rate than that recorded in single-arm trials published to date. This is especially true since active cancer was an exclusion criterion in many of them. Furthermore, in the current study, one in 4 patients was classified as HR PE, and mortality in this population remains high despite the use of advanced percutaneous thrombectomy techniques [40]. Of note, studies conducted by Pietrasik et al. [22] and Au et al. [41] also confirmed that correctly managed cancer-related PE may not affect in-hospital mortality, but has an impact on long-term survival.

Limitations

The study has several limitations that need to be emphasized. First, the study had observational character and included a rather heterogeneous population. Second, the analysis included a relatively large group of PE patients, but only 53 belonged to the group with cancer comorbidity. Third, we do not have detailed data on OP who received conservative treatment of PE. Fourth, patients with cancer who had a short life expectancy (<1 month) were intentionally excluded from the study. However, the precise assessment of cancer-related prognosis may pose a challenge in the acute phase of PE and may explain high short-term mortality in the OP group. Altogether, it may suggest a potential risk of selection bias, and generalization of results to all patients with PE and cancer must be done with caution. Fifth, this is a multicenter study and therefore PE management was relatively diverse, as qualification for CDMT was made by the local PERT. Sixth, regardless of cancer comorbidity as a modifier of mortality risk, most patients enrolled in the current study appear to be classified in categories C or D according to the latest American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines, and in both of these categories, the role of mechanical thrombectomy remains quite uncertain due to limited and nonrandomized studies (Class 2b recommendation) [42]. Seventh, a specified treatment protocol was not determined, and there were 3 thrombectomy devices evaluated; however, all of them were based on the concept of continuous vacuum aspiration thrombectomy. For the abovementioned reasons, prospective studies are needed to provide further evidence of the safety and efficacy of CDMT procedures in patients with PE with cancer comorbidity.

Conclusions

Active cancer was observed in 18.3% of PE patients treated with CDMT. Our novel findings indicate that CDMT for cancer-related PE is effective and safe. Patients with PE and concomitant malignancy may have similar in-hospital survival rate compared with patients without malignancy when treated with CDMT.
