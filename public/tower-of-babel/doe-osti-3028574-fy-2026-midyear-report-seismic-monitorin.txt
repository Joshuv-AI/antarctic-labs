 ABSTRACT

Safeguards-relevant temporal changes in underground facilities can be observed using geophysical
monitoring techniques. Seismic waves, in particular, provide valuable insights into subsurface activities
and can serve as an important tool for detecting anomalous events that may indicate containment breaches
at geological repositories. This midyear report summarizes ongoing efforts to automatically and rapidly
detect and locate anomalous vibration signals that could be indicative of potential containment breaches.

Previous work during FY 2025 focused on compiling continuous seismic datasets from two underground
sites and developing a database of continuous waveforms and ground-truth event data derived from
multiple sensing modalities. Building on this foundation, we are adapting anomaly detection and
geolocation algorithms to explore methods for monitoring underground activities using two relatively
low-maintenance sensing technologies: a dense surface geophone array deployed at the Pleasant Gap
mine in Pennsylvania, and a 3D fiber-optic cable array for distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) installed in
the subsurface at the Sanford Underground Research Facility (SURF) in South Dakota.

This report summarizes work conducted during the first two quarters of FY 2026, during which we
refined a dynamic power spectral density (PSD)-based detector, applied it independently to each
geophone station, and then combined the per‑station detections with density-based spatial clustering of
applications with noise (DBSCAN) to cluster events and produce spatial maps over a 9 day interval. In
addition, we outline plans for a field trial at the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP) in New Mexico to
compare traditional seismic monitoring approaches with DAS techniques and to evaluate the benefits of
combined data analysis. Activities during the past two quarters have included the preparation and
submission of a field test plan to WIPP for approval, as well as submission to Headquarters for review
and feedback.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Seismic monitoring is a well-established technique for detecting subsurface activity, with widespread
applications in geophysics, infrastructure monitoring, and nuclear nonproliferation. However, its use for
long-term monitoring of deep geological repositories (DGRs) for nuclear waste containment remains in
an exploratory phase because of several technical and operational limitations.

First, traditional seismometers—typically deployed underground or in boreholes to ensure proximity to
sources—require significant infrastructure for power supply, data transmission, and maintenance.
Although they provide high-fidelity data, these systems are difficult to scale for large, remote, or
logistically constrained sites. Moreover, conventional detection methods are primarily optimized for
impulsive signals such as earthquakes or explosions and are less effective for identifying routine
operational activities, including machinery use or personnel movement.

Second, DAS is an emerging technology that converts standard fiber-optic cables into dense arrays of
vibration sensors. DAS systems offer important advantages, including low maintenance requirements and
the absence of in situ power or networking needs. As a result, they are gaining traction in applications
such as geotechnical monitoring, oil and gas operations, and infrastructure health assessment. However,
their application to nuclear safeguards and DGR monitoring remains largely untested. In addition, DAS
measurements are limited to one-component axial strain, which complicates full 3D event localization
(Maceira et al., 2025)

Third, existing anomaly detection techniques are not well suited for the types of signals expected in DGR
environments. Classical approaches rely on the identification of clear signal onsets, such as P- and S-

 1

wave arrivals, and therefore perform poorly in the presence of low-amplitude, emergent, or overlapping
signals typical of routine facility operations. Although more advanced methods—such as PSD-based
detection and polarization analysis—have been developed, their application in this context has been
limited.

Finally, standard geolocation methods depend on precise travel-time picking, accurate subsurface velocity
models, or triangulation/trilateration. These requirements are difficult to satisfy in complex underground
environments, where wave propagation is heterogeneous and signals may lack clear arrival phases.

In this project, we are addressing these challenges by adapting and extending seismic monitoring
methodologies for DGR applications. First, we intend to demonstrate that surface-deployed geophones
and DAS systems can provide effective monitoring without the need for underground infrastructure,
thereby reducing logistical and operational constraints. Second, we are implementing and validating
advanced signal processing approaches, including PSD-based anomaly detection (e.g., Marcillo et al.,
2020; Guenaga et al., 2021), which are particularly well suited for identifying subtle or persistent signals
associated with facility activity. We are also applying amplitude-based localization techniques (e.g., Chai
et al., 2023) that do not rely on arrival-time picking or detailed velocity models, making them more
practical for real-world deployments.

In addition, we will enhance monitoring capabilities through the integration of complementary sensing
modalities. By combining one-component DAS data with three-component surface geophone
measurements, we enable improved 3D localization of underground events. This includes the novel
application of polarization analysis and cross-bearing methods using surface arrays alone—an approach
not previously explored in the context of nuclear safeguards. Our workflows are being designed to
support the development of autonomous, real-time, and long-duration monitoring systems.

The remainder of this report summarizes progress achieved over the past 3 months, following delays
associated with the government shutdown at the beginning of FY 2026. We begin by revisiting two real-
world datasets compiled during the previous fiscal year—the Pleasant Gap mine and SURF—which are
being used to develop and validate our methods. These datasets capture activities relevant to repository
operations, including excavation and equipment movement. We then present recent improvements to our
PSD-misfit detection approach, extending it from a single-station implementation to a multistation
framework. This enhancement mitigates the influence of site effects and station-specific noise, resulting
in more robust and reliable detections. Finally, we outline a planned field demonstration at the WIPP,
where DAS and traditional seismic monitoring systems will be deployed and evaluated both
independently and in combination.

 2. TWO DATABASES OF CONTINUOUS SEISMIC DATA

We compiled two waveform datasets: one from temporary deployments at Pleasant Gap mine
(Pennsylvania) and another from SURF in South Dakota. For both sites, we developed a ground-truth
database of recorded operational events.

These datasets incorporate two complementary sensing technologies: (1) surface-deployed geophones and
(2) underground DAS using fiber-optic cables. The types of activities captured across both sites provide a
useful proxy for operations relevant to safeguards in DGRs, including excavation, creation of
underground void space, operation of transport machinery, personnel movement, ventilation systems, and
air door activity. As such, these datasets serve as a foundation for developing, testing, and validating our
seismic monitoring approaches.

2.1 SURFACE GEOPHONES

The first dataset consists of a dense seismic array comprising 80–100 three-component geophones
(depending on the deployment campaign) installed above an active underground mine in Pleasant Gap,
Pennsylvania. Surface-deployed geophones offer several advantages over their underground counterparts,
including lower maintenance requirements, easier accessibility, more reliable timing synchronization, and
the ability to integrate with solar panels for sustained power and extended operational duration.

Station spacing ranged from approximately 100 to 400 m, with sensors distributed across a substantial
portion of the mine’s surface footprint. The underlying mine structure spans a wide range of tunnel
depths, from near-surface workings to more than 600 m below ground (Error! Reference source not
found.).

Two types of instruments were used across the seismic campaigns. The majority of stations were
equipped with FairfieldNodal ZLand 3C All-in-One seismometers, and a subset used Geospace GS-ONE
4.5 Hz three-component geophones paired with either Geospace GSX-4 (four-channel) or GSX-3 (three-
channel) recorders that were powered by lithium batteries.

2.2 SUBSURFACE DAS

The second dataset consists of DAS measurements acquired along more than 5 km of fiber-optic cable at
the active SURF site. DAS measures dynamic strain along the length of a fiber-optic cable, enabling the
detection of subtle ground vibrations with high spatial and temporal resolution. This technology has

 3

become increasingly valuable for seismic monitoring, particularly in environments where traditional
sensor deployment is challenging or where dense spatial sampling is required.

At SURF, the fiber-optic cable was deployed as a 3D array throughout the underground facility (Error!
Reference source not found.), and DAS data were recorded continuously. The system utilized an
OptaSense ODH-4 interrogator unit, with the sensing fiber comprising a combination of existing
telecommunications fiber, newly deployed telecom fiber, and tight-buffered four-fiber cable, of which a
single fiber was interrogated.

The fiber was installed using a variety of deployment configurations, including trenched and grouted
sections, segments suspended from tunnel ceilings, portions secured with surface materials (e.g., sandbags
or rocks), and sections laid directly on the ground. These varied installation conditions provide a realistic
representation of deployment scenarios in operational underground environments.

A comprehensive description of both datasets, including acquisition details and associated ground-truth
catalogs, is provided in Cunningham et al. (2025).

 3. EVENT DETECTION

Using continuous vertical‑component recordings from 44 geophone stations, we demonstrate a
network‑wide method that detects elastic disturbances by monitoring changes in the background signal’s
PSD. The PSD detector reveals subtle facility‑operation signatures that are difficult to capture with
conventional detectors (e.g., time‑domain short-term-average-to-long-term-average amplitude ratio
[STA/LTA] detectors) and that resemble the anomalous events expected from breaches in geological
repositories. In practice, the PSD‑misfit detector has shown strong potential for identifying operational
activities that conventional anomaly detectors often miss (Marcillo et al., 2020; Guenaga et al., 2021).

For a focused demonstration we limited the analysis to the 13–15 Hz band, a narrow frequency range
known to carry weak operational signals. The PSD‑misfit detector workflow utilized in this analysis uses
the dynamic‑mean‑σ method (i.e., a rolling background mean, and triggered by signals that are a set
multiple of the standard deviation). For the 9 day analysis period (May 6–14, 2023) the algorithm was
configured as follows:

 Spectrogram: 60 s windows with 50% overlap.

 4

 Background statistics: 30 min rolling window.
 Threshold: a log‑PSD pixel (a time‑frequency bin) exceeding the rolling mean by ± 2σ.
 Event persistence: only detections that remained above the threshold for ≥10 min were retained.

This detection is performed independently for each station on 24 h (00:00–24:00 UTC) data blocks.

3.1 STACKING ACROSS THE NETWORK

All flagged pixels from every station are combined and expressed as the percentage of stations that
recorded a given time‑frequency bin (i.e., proxy for multiplicity). Random, local glitches or disturbances
appear only at a few stations and therefore disappear in the stack, whereas a coherent signal is reinforced,
producing a bright, amplified region. Figure 3 and Figure 4 illustrate this effect; the darker regions
represent the network‑wide disturbance that would be invisible on any single station, and the light‑colored
patches correspond to localized, incoherent detections. Figure 3(b) shows a typical weekday pattern, with
strong energy in the 14 Hz band between 09:00 and 18:30 UTC. These signals line up with the daily
operation of the rock crusher at the facility and are an example of an event that the PSD‑misfit detector
captures readily.

3.2 CLUSTERING AND SPATIAL CONSTRAINT

Figure 3(a) illustrates the detection results. The dynamic‑PSD detector operates with a moving window,
which enables it to capture both the onset and offset of anomalous activity (Marcillo et al., 2020;
Guenaga et al., 2021). For the present development, we restrict the analysis to onset detections only (i.e.,
pixels that indicate an increase in power). A DBSCAN clustering step is then applied to the detections in
the 13–15 Hz band (the target is the 14 Hz signal). The algorithm uses ε = 0.05 and minimum samples =
5; each resulting cluster represents a distinct onset event; Figure 3(c) shows an example of these clusters.
Pixels within the 13–15 Hz band are clustered in the time–frequency domain using DBSCAN (ε = 0.05,
minimum samples = 5). The clustering was performed day-by-day for the 9 day data subset. Figure 4
presents a contrasting weekend day. For every event, we gather the stations that contributed detections

 6

and compute a convex hull—the smallest “rubber‑band” polygon that encloses those points. The hull
provides a quick visual estimate of the event’s geographic footprint without requiring any waveform
inversion, detailed modeling, or other computationally taxing process. Figure 5 shows four examples of
convex‑hull footprints generated by this workflow, providing an initial spatial constraint that can be used
to optimize more detailed source‑location techniques.

3.3 EMPIRICAL INTERPRETATION OF THE ANALYSIS

Furthermore, these results are preliminary and will require a more extensive analysis, but an initial
detection threshold can be estimated from an empirical analysis of the station‑multiplicity distribution.
Specifically, we can begin to answer at what multiplicity (i.e., the number of stations contributing to a
given detection) the confidence that a detection is real increases substantially relative to the probability of
coincidental noise triggers. By framing this as a binary hypothesis test, we apply an empirical

 7

survival‑function approach to identify a multiplicity cutoff for which the probability of observing ≥K
stations under noise falls below a chosen false‑alarm tolerance.

Figure 6 illustrates this approach using the 44‑station DBSCAN results, separated into presumed quiet
periods (weekends) and active periods (weekdays) for the targeted 14 Hz detections. This formulation
parallels classical multisensor detection logic (e.g., Chair et al., 1986), which selects thresholds to satisfy
constraints on false‑alarm probability. Based on the limited subset analyzed here, a multiplicity of 23
corresponds to a tail probability of approximately 0.05, indicating that only 5% of noise‑like detections
reach this level of network agreement. Thus, adopting 23 station detection agreement as a provisional
threshold yields a ≤5% false‑alarm rate under the tested conditions for the target signals. This is likely a
conservative threshold; analysis of additional periods may reveal that a lower multiplicity is sufficient for
high‑confidence detections, as suggested by the divergence in quiet and active counts and exceedance
probability near multiplicity 13 in Figure 6.

 Figure 6. Empirical multiplicity analysis for detection threshold calibration. (Left) Distribution of station
 multiplicity K during presumably inactive weekend (orange) and active weekday (green). (Right) Empirical
  survival function for inactive and active periods. The detection threshold K* = 23 (dashed red line) is defined as
  the smallest multiplicity (number of stations in agreement for a detection) at which the background exceedance
 probability falls below alpha = 0.05 (5% false alarm rate; gray dashed line).

3.4 FUTURE CONSIDERATIONS

Although the stacked PSD‑based detector has already shown that it can highlight subtle, low‑frequency
disturbances across a dense geophone network, several avenues remain to be explored before the
approach can be considered a fully operational monitoring tool. We plan to explore
(1) amplitude‑weighted mapping, where the ratio of detection amplitude to the local background is used
to construct pseudo‑topographic energy maps; this should sharpen source‑location estimates by exploiting
the spatial variation of signal strength. (2) Investigate adaptive filtering and edge‑enhancement techniques
that can further suppress incoherent noise and emphasize subtle spectral features before the PSD
calculation. (3) Continue to explore clustering methods and optimizations, leveraging more sophisticated
clustering algorithms and machine‑learning approaches. (4) Extend the pipeline to DAS, leveraging the
1–4 m spatial sampling of fiber‑optic cables.

Together, these enhancements will transform the presently robust, computationally inexpensive PSD
detector into a more focused high‑resolution monitoring system that will aim to deliver higher‑confidence

 8

detections, preliminary source localization, and an automatically generated event catalog, in line with
critical capabilities for underground activity surveillance and safeguards verification.

 4. WIPP FIELD TEST

We are planning a short-duration field trial at WIPP near Carlsbad, New Mexico, to evaluate DAS as an
alternative or complement to conventional seismic monitoring technologies for safeguards applications.
The primary goal of this effort is to assess the capability of DAS to meet safeguards-relevant objectives,
including (1) verifying that no undeclared excavation activities occur within the perimeter of the area
under safeguards and (2) confirming that loaded emplacement vehicles do not deviate from designated
emplacement locations.

Because of site constraints, the field trial will be conducted in the N-780 and N-940 drifts within the
northern underground experimental area at WIPP (Error! Reference source not found. and Error!
Reference source not found.). These locations are sufficiently removed from planned excavation
activities over the coming months, and no waste emplacement operations are permitted in either drift. A
heavily loaded forklift will be used as a surrogate for a standard emplacement vehicle. This short-duration
trial will not capture active excavation, but a longer-term deployment could potentially monitor such
activities in the future.

The FY 2026 field trial is designed as short-duration (approximately one week) data collection campaign
using equipment currently available at ORNL. A subsequent, longer-duration deployment using project-
procured equipment will be discussed and planned for later fiscal years.

The specific objective of the short-duration trial is to evaluate the ability of DAS to detect, localize, and
classify underground material-handling activities. For example, the study will examine whether DAS can
distinguish between a heavily loaded and an unloaded forklift operating within the N-780 and N-940
drifts, serving as a proxy for waste emplacement vehicles.

For the DAS deployment, the team will install an interrogator unit at the surface and use a single strand of
existing fiber-optic cable (shown in green) that extends down the waste shaft at WIPP. Additional fiber
(shown in red) will be procured and installed to extend coverage from the current cable endpoint to the N-
940 drift.

Conventional seismic monitoring equipment will be deployed alongside the DAS system to enable
comparative analysis. ORNL will coordinate with the WIPP management and operations contractor to
drill up to three boreholes—each approximately 10 ft (3 m) deep and 3 in. (76 mm) in diameter—for the
installation and grouting of geophones. These sensors will be installed at three underground locations.

An initial draft of the test plan has been submitted to the WIPP point of contact. The document is
currently being revised, with a second draft expected in FY 2026 Q2. The field trial is anticipated to take
place in late FY 2026 or early FY 2027.

 5. SUMMARY

Seismic monitoring emerges as a promising safeguards strategy for monitoring activities in
georepositories. Anthropogenic activities such as drilling, conveyor systems, and moving carts and
personnel can generate mechanical energy that propagates through solid rock as seismic waves and is
detectable with seismic instrumentation. We are exploiting continuous recordings from a surface

 10

geophone array and a DAS cable deployed in active mines to develop and test algorithms that detect and
roughly locate these anthropogenic sources.

In the first half of FY 2026, we have completed refined a PSD-misfit detector, applied it independently to
each of the 44 geophone stations, and then merged the per‑station detections with DBSCAN clustering to
produce network‑wide spatial footprints for a 9 day test period (May 6–14, 2023). The workflow was
demonstrated on the 13–15 Hz band, revealing coherent, facility‑related signals (e.g., rock‑crusher
activity) that were clearly captured by the stacked PSD analysis. In parallel, we prepared an initial draft of
the field‑test plan for a short‑duration trial at WIPP and submitted the plan to the site and to Headquarters
for review. The efforts in the third and fourth quarters of FY 2026 will focus on further exploration and
development of the PSD-based detector. This will include amplitude‑weighted mapping to generate
pseudo‑topographic energy maps that improve source localization, adaptive filtering, and
edge‑enhancement to improve spectrogram inputs into the PSD detector; evaluate more advanced
clustering and lightweight machine‑learning approaches to capture richer time‑frequency fingerprints; and
begin real‑time integration of the pipeline with DAS data, testing streaming operation on appropriate
hardware. These steps will build on the 9 day proof‑of‑concept and prepare the methodology for the
upcoming WIPP field trial and future long‑duration deployments.
