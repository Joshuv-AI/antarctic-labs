Hypoxia-inducible factor-1α promotes the malignant progression of cervical cancer cells by regulating lactate dehydrogenase A-mediated glycolysis
Authors: Yafang Kang, Yuxuan Huang, Yanlin Chen, Kelvin Stefan Osafo, Leyi Huang, Yuanyuan Chen, Xiaodan Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnhanced glycolysis is a hallmark of metabolic reprogramming in cervical cancer and plays a key role in tumor progression. Hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), a core regulator of glycolytic metabolism, remains incompletely characterized in cervical cancer. This study aimed to investigate the expression pattern and clinical significance of HIF-1α in cervical cancer, and to explore its association with malignant biological behavior and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA)-related glycolytic metabolism in cervical cancer cells.MethodsThe expression level, clinicopathological features, immune infiltration correlation, and prognostic value of HIF-1α in cervical cancer were analyzed based on the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. HIF-1α overexpression and knockdown models were established in HeLa and Caski cells. Cell viability and invasive ability were assessed by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and Transwell assays, respectively. Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot were used to detect changes in LDHA expression. Lactate production was measured using a lactate assay kit, and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were determined by flow cytometry.ResultsBioinformatics analysis showed that HIF-1α was highly expressed in cervical cancer and was closely associated with patient age, menopausal status, immune cell infiltration, and poor prognosis. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with high HIF-1α expression had significantly worse overall survival (OS) than those with low expression. In vitro functional experiments further confirmed that HIF-1α overexpression significantly enhanced the viability and invasive ability of HeLa and Caski cells, whereas HIF-1α knockdown produced the opposite effects. HIF-1α overexpression was associated with increased messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein expression levels of LDHA, a key glycolytic molecule, along with increased lactate production and elevated intracellular ROS levels.ConclusionsHIF-1α is aberrantly highly expressed in cervical cancer and may enhance glycolytic activity by upregulating LDHA expression, thereby promoting the proliferation and invasion of cervical cancer cells. These findings suggest that HIF-1α could serve as a potential diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic target biomarker for cervical cancer.Keywords: Hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), cervical cancer, glycolysis

Highlight box

Key findings

• Hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) is highly expressed in cervical cancer and promotes tumor cell proliferation and invasion by upregulating lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA)-driven glycolysis.

What is known and what is new?

• HIF-1α is a key regulator of tumor hypoxic adaptation and metabolic reprogramming, commonly associated with cancer progression and poor prognosis. However, its functional role in cervical cancer metabolism remains incompletely defined.

• In this study, we show that HIF-1α is significantly upregulated in cervical cancer and correlates with aggressive clinical features and poor outcomes. Functional analyses demonstrate that HIF-1α promotes proliferation and invasion in both HPV16- and HPV18-positive cervical cancer models. Mechanistically, HIF-1α enhances glycolytic reprogramming by upregulating LDHA, resulting in increased lactate production and elevated reactive oxygen species levels. Integrated The Cancer Genome Atlas and experimental data reveal a strong positive correlation between HIF-1α and LDHA expression, indicating a coordinated metabolic axis driving tumor progression. Collectively, these findings identify the HIF-1α-LDHA pathway as a central regulator of metabolic reprogramming in cervical cancer and a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings identify the HIF-1α-LDHA metabolic axis as a central driver of cervical cancer progression, suggesting that targeting hypoxia-driven glycolysis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy. Clinically, HIF-1α may serve as both a prognostic biomarker and a potential intervention target. Future studies should focus on validating this pathway in vivo and exploring combination strategies targeting metabolic reprogramming and immune modulation in cervical cancer.

Introduction

In recent years, with the widespread implementation of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination and the expanded coverage of cervical cancer screening, the World Health Organization (WHO) proposed a global initiative to eliminate cervical cancer in 2020 (1). Although many countries have responded to this global strategy, cervical cancer continues to affect the reproductive health and quality of life of a large proportion of women (2). Studies have estimated that the global burden of cervical cancer will still increase to 760,000 cases by 2030 (3). Therefore, further investigation of key molecular targets involved in cervical cancer progression remains an important issue. Metabolic reprogramming fundamentally alters cancer cells, among which enhanced glycolysis is one of the most typical metabolic changes. Normal somatic cells primarily meet their energy demands through mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, whereas tumor cells, in order to sustain rapid proliferation, exhibit the Warburg effect (4), namely, enhanced glucose uptake and glycolysis even under aerobic conditions. This metabolic shift not only supports adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production but also provides key intermediates required for the biosynthesis of macromolecules. At the same time, increased glycolysis leads to substantial lactate production. Multiple metabolic flux studies have shown that labeled lactate is closely associated with tumor progression and metastatic behavior (5), and accumulated lactate can even substitute for glucose as a metabolic fuel for the tricarboxylic acid cycle in tumor cells (6,7). In addition, high concentrations of lactate in the tumor microenvironment can strongly suppress antitumor immune responses through multiple mechanisms, including impairing immune cell differentiation, weakening immune activity, and facilitating immune evasion, thereby promoting tumor invasion and metastasis (8,9).

Metabolic reprogramming induced by oncogenic mutations plays an important role in the pathogenesis of cervical cancer. HPV oncoproteins E6/E7, as key pathogenic markers, can significantly enhance the aerobic glycolytic ability of cervical cancer cells (10,11). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), especially lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), is a key enzyme in the glycolytic pathway, which can catalyze the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, thereby maintaining the continuous progression of glycolysis and promoting lactate accumulation. Therefore, LDH/LDHA-related metabolic changes may be an important component of glycolytic reprogramming in cervical cancer. Abnormal metabolic changes in tumors are usually driven by multiple key signaling molecules. Hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1), especially its oxygen-sensitive subunit hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), is an important molecule connecting the hypoxic microenvironment and tumor metabolic reprogramming. HIF-1α can promote glycolysis in tumor cells by inducing the overexpression of glucose transporters such as Glucose transporter (GLUT1) (12,13). Increased HIF-1α expression has been reported in various malignant tumors, such as breast cancer (14), thyroid cancer (15), and liver cancer (16), and is associated with malignant biological behaviors such as tumor invasion, metastasis (17), and resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy (18). In addition to glycolytic metabolism, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are also an important manifestation of metabolic abnormalities and redox imbalance in tumor cells (19). ROS are highly reactive oxygen-containing molecules produced during cellular aerobic metabolism, and the mitochondrial respiratory chain is one of their important sources, which can affect tumor cell proliferation, survival, migration, and invasion (20). As a core factor of hypoxic adaptation and metabolic reprogramming, HIF-1α may participate in the regulation of ROS levels by affecting glycolysis, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, and redox-related pathways (21). Therefore, there may be complex relationships among HIF-1α, LDH/LDHA-mediated glycolytic metabolism, and ROS changes.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the expression level of HIF-1α in cervical cancer tissues and to investigate the relationship between HIF-1α and cervical cancer cell proliferation, invasion, LDHA expression, lactate production, and changes in ROS levels, with the aim of providing a novel diagnostic biomarker for patients with cervical cancer. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1019/rc).

Methods

Dataset and bioinformatics analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data processed using the STAR workflow and clinical data for the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project, including cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma, were downloaded from the TCGA database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The raw expression data were initially quantified as transcripts per million (TPM). For downstream differential expression analysis and visualization, we applied a log2 transformation [log2(TPM + 1)] to normalize the data distribution, and samples without clinical information were excluded before analysis. All analyses and visualizations were performed in R 4.2.1 using the ggplot2, stats, and car packages.

Cell culture

Since HPV16 and HPV18 are the most common high-risk HPV subtypes in cervical cancer, these two cell lines were selected in this study to investigate the role of HIF-1α in cervical cancer cells under different high-risk HPV backgrounds. The human cervical cancer cell lines HeLa (ATCC, CCL-2, RRID:CVCL 0030) and Caski (ATCC, CRL-1550, RRID:CVCL 1100) were cultured in DMEM (ABK0003C, ABKbio, Wuhan, China) and RPMI 1640 (ABK0002C, ABKbio) medium, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10099141, Gibco, Waltham, USA) and 100 mg/mL penicillin-streptomycin (C0222, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cell transfection

HeLa and Caski cells were seeded in 6-well plates (TCP010006, JET, Guangzhou, China) at a density of 1×105 cells per well. After cell attachment and reaching an appropriate confluence, the cells were subjected to lentiviral transduction (Figure S1). The HIF-1α overexpression lentiviral vector (ovHIF-1α), HIF-1α knockdown lentiviral vector (shHIF-1α), and corresponding negative control vectors were constructed and provided by Genechem (Shanghai, China). The HIF-1α overexpression vector contained the human HIF-1α coding sequence, whereas the HIF-1α knockdown vector carried a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequence targeting HIF-1α. The negative control for the overexpression experiment was the corresponding empty vector, while the negative control for the knockdown experiment was a non-targeting shRNA control vector with no known homology to HIF-1α or other human genes. Lentiviral transduction was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. After transduction, stable cells were selected using 2 µg/mL puromycin (Beyotime). HIF-1α overexpression and knockdown efficiency were subsequently verified by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR).

Cell viability assay

HeLa and Caski cells with HIF-1α overexpression or knockdown were seeded into 96-well plates (TCP010096, JET) at a density of 2×103 cells per well. After 24 h of attachment, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution (K2268, APExBIO, Houston, USA) was added to each well. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm using a Microplate spectrophotometer (SN20140725163117384, BioTek, Winooski, USA) to evaluate cell viability.

Transwell assay

HeLa and Caski cells with HIF-1α overexpression or knockdown were seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts (FTW001-6Ins, Beyotime) in serum-free medium, while 500 µL of complete medium was added to the lower chambers. After incubation at 37 °C for 48 h, the medium was discarded and the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime) for 30 min. The cells were then stained with 0.1% crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime) for 15 min. Migrated cells were observed under an optical microscope (CKX41, OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan) and counted using ImageJ software (1.52v, National Institutes of Health, USA).

Lactate assay

HeLa and Caski cells with HIF-1α overexpression or knockdown were seeded into 6-well plates (TCP010006, JET) at a density of 1×106 cells per well. After 24 h of attachment, cell pellets were collected and lysed. Samples were centrifuged at approximately 12,000 g for 3–5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were used for subsequent analysis. Lactate levels were measured using a lactate assay kit (S0208S, Beyotime). Lactate standards were prepared in advance according to the manufacturer’s instructions to generate a standard curve. Sample supernatants and working solution were added to a 96-well plate (TCP010096, JET) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Absorbance values were then measured with Microplate spectrophotometer (SN20140725163117384, BioTek), and lactate concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (DP424, TIANGEN, Beijing, China). RNA concentration was measured at 260 nm using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ND-ONEC-W, Thermo, Waltham, USA). Messenger RNA (mRNA) was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (RR047A, Takara, Kusatsu, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (PRA6101, Promega, Madison, USA). The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Table S1. GAPDH was selected as the internal reference gene because its expression showed no significant variation among different treatment groups under the present experimental conditions. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Specifically, ΔCt was calculated as Ct (target gene) − Ct (GAPDH), and ΔΔCt was calculated as ΔCt (treatment group) − ΔCt (control group). The final relative expression level was expressed as 2−ΔΔCt.

Western blot

HeLa and Caski, were lysed on ice for 20 min using RIPA lysis buffer (78501, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (JZ102, Epizyme, Shanghai, China). Proteins (20 µg per lane) were separated on 4–20% precast gradient PAGE gels (PG610, Epizyme) and transferred to PVDF membranes (WJ001, Epizyme). After blocking with rapid blocking buffer (PS108P, Epizyme) for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted in primary antibody dilution buffer: anti-HIF-1α (20960-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-LDHA (21799-1-AP, Proteintech), and anti-GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech). After washing three times with TBST (PS103, Epizyme) for 10 min each, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (TA005/TA006, ABKbio) at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies and secondary antibodies were diluted in TBST buffer. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced ECL detection kit (SQ201, Epizyme).

Measurement of intracellular ROS

After the indicated treatments, cells from each group were collected and washed twice with pre-cooled PBS (ABK0001C, ABKbio). ROS fluorescent probe working solution (S0033S, Beyotime) was then added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min, with gentle mixing every 3–5 min to ensure sufficient contact between the probe and the cells. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove excess probe that had not entered the cells and then resuspended in PBS. Fluorescence signals were detected by flow cytometry. Single-cell populations were selected for analysis, and fluorescence intensity was recorded in the fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) channel. Intracellular ROS levels were expressed as FITC fluorescence intensity and quantified as mean fluorescence intensity.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v26.0 (IBM, SPSS, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, USA). Each experimental condition was biologically replicated at least thrice to ensure reliability and reproducibility. The association between HIF-1α expression and clinicopathological variables was assessed by the χ2 test. Overall survival (OS) curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Multivariate analysis was conducted using the Cox proportional hazards regression model. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons among three groups within a single cell line, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test to compare each experimental group with the control group. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Expression pattern of HIF-1α in cervical cancer and its clinical significance

To clarify the potential role of HIF-1α in cervical cancer, we systematically analyzed its clinical features, expression pattern, immune relevance, and prognostic value based on the TCGA database. Analysis of baseline clinicopathological characteristics showed that 153 patients were included in both the low- and high-HIF-1α expression groups (Table 1). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in pathological tumor (T) stage, pathological node (N) stage, pathological metastasis (M) stage, clinical stage, initial treatment outcome, body mass index (BMI), histological type, or histological grade (all P>0.05). In contrast, the proportion of patients aged ≤50 years was significantly higher in the high-HIF-1α expression group, whereas the proportion of patients aged >50 years was relatively lower (P=0.02). In addition, menopausal status differed significantly between the two groups (P=0.02), suggesting that HIF-1α expression is associated with certain clinical characteristics of patients.

Further analysis revealed that HIF-1α was differentially expressed in multiple tumor types, including cholangiocarcinoma and esophageal cancer, and remained highly expressed in cervical cancer (Figure 1A). Further prognostic analysis indicated that the effect of HIF-1α on OS showed obvious cancer-type heterogeneity, and its high expression was associated with poorer survival in cervical cancer (Figure 1B). In addition, we assessed HIF-1α expression levels in cervical cancer tissues and found that they were generally higher than those in the control group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance due to the limited sample size (Figure 1C). We therefore further queried the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database and observed that HIF‑1α levels were higher in both cervical squamous cell carcinoma and cervical adenocarcinoma compared with normal tissues (Figure 1D). Immune infiltration correlation analysis showed that, in cervical cancer, HIF-1α expression was positively correlated with the infiltration of neutrophils, Th2 cells, and central memory T cells (Tcm), while it was negatively correlated with the infiltration of dendritic cells (DCs), cytotoxic cells, B cells, and total T cells (Figure 1E,1F). Notably, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high HIF-1α expression had worse OS than those with low expression [hazard ratio (HR) =1.39, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.86–2.22, P=0.17; Figure 1G]. Notably, although HIF-1α expression significantly stratified patient prognosis in univariate analysis, its effect was attenuated in multivariable Cox regression after including T stage as a covariate (Table 2).

HIF-1α promotes the viability and invasive capacity of cervical cancer cells

To further verify the role of HIF-1α in the malignant phenotype of cervical cancer cells, HIF-1α overexpression and knockdown models were established in HeLa and Caski cells, and their invasive ability and cell viability were subsequently evaluated. Transwell assays showed that, compared with the negative control group, knockdown of HIF-1α markedly reduced the number of invading HeLa and Caski cells, whereas overexpression of HIF-1α significantly increased the number of invading cells in both cell lines (all P<0.05, Figure 2A). Meanwhile, CCK-8 assays demonstrated that HIF-1α overexpression significantly enhanced the viability of HeLa and Caski cells, whereas HIF-1α knockdown markedly decreased cell viability (all P<0.05, Figure 2B). These results indicate that HIF-1α promotes the proliferative and invasive capacities of cervical cancer cells.

HIF-1α promotes glycolytic reprogramming in cervical cancer by upregulating LDHA

The Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed that the HIF-1α-related genomic regions in cervical cancer were significantly enriched in the hypoxia-related pathways and glycolysis pathways (Figure 3A). To further explore the potential mechanism by which HIF-1α mediates metabolic reprogramming in cervical cancer, we analyzed LDHA, a key downstream glycolytic molecule. TCGA data showed that LDHA expression was significantly higher in cervical cancer tissues than in normal cervical tissues (Figure 3B), and patients with high LDHA expression had significantly worse OS (HR =1.68, 95% CI: 1.05–2.69, P=0.03; Figure 3C). HIF-1α expression is significantly positively correlated with LDHA expression in cervical cancer (ρ=0.337, P=2.16×10⁻9, Figure 3D). ROC curve analysis further demonstrated that LDHA had better discriminatory performance for cervical cancer than HIF-1α, with a higher area under the curve (AUC) value (0.889 vs. 0.677; Figure 3E). At the cellular level, lactate concentrations in the culture supernatants of HeLa and Caski cells increased progressively over time (Figure 3F). After establishing HIF-1α overexpression and knockdown models, we found that in both HeLa and Caski cells, HIF-1α overexpression was associated with significantly increased mRNA and protein expression levels of LDHA, whereas HIF-1α knockdown was associated with markedly reduced LDHA expression (all P<0.05, Figure 3G,3H). Consistently, lactate quantification showed that HIF-1α overexpression significantly promoted lactate production in both cervical cancer cell lines, whereas HIF-1α knockdown markedly reduced lactate levels (all P<0.05, Figure 3I). In addition, ROS detection results showed that HIF-1α overexpression markedly increased intracellular ROS levels in HeLa and Caski cells (P=0.002 and P<0.0001, respectively; Figure 3J). However, after HIF-1α knockdown, ROS expression showed no significant change in HeLa cells, while ROS levels in Caski cells still showed a significant increase (P<0.0001, Figure 3J). Taken together, these results indicate that HIF-1α promotes glycolytic activity and lactate accumulation in cervical cancer cells by upregulating LDHA expression, accompanied by alterations in oxidative stress.

Discussion

We investigated the role of HIF-1α in cervical cancer. Our results showed that HIF-1α was highly expressed in cervical cancer and was associated with increased proliferation and migration of cervical cancer cells, as well as elevated expression of the glycolysis-related molecule LDHA and increased ROS levels. HIF-1α may affect cervical cancer progression by participating in tumor glucose metabolic reprogramming. Therefore, our study suggests that HIF-1α may serve as an important regulatory molecule in cervical cancer progression.

HIF-1 is a heterodimeric transcriptional regulator belonging to the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) protein family, consisting of an oxygen-sensitive and unstable α subunit and a constitutively expressed, oxygen-insensitive β subunit (22). Under normoxic conditions, HIF-1α is hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylase domain enzymes (PHDs), subsequently ubiquitinated, and then degraded through the von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) protein-mediated proteasomal pathway (23). Under hypoxic conditions, however, HIF-1α becomes stabilized and accumulates, thereby activating the transcription of downstream target genes (24). Because rapidly proliferating solid tumors are often accompanied by insufficient blood supply and increased oxygen demand, the tumor microenvironment is usually characterized by intermittent or persistent hypoxia. Proteins encoded by HIF-1α target genes help tumor cells survive under hypoxic stress by promoting angiogenesis and driving glycolysis (25). Accordingly, substantial evidence has shown that HIF-1α is significantly overexpressed in common malignancies of the respiratory and digestive systems (26-28). Previous studies have also demonstrated that, in female lower reproductive tract-related lesions, HIF-1α expression is low in normal cervical epithelium, normal endometrium, and inactive endometrial hyperplasia, but is markedly elevated in endometrial carcinoma and cervical cancer tissues (29). In addition, a study by Konstantopoulos et al. showed that the HPV16 E6 oncoprotein can regulate intracellular HIF-1α expression (30). Consistent with these findings, we found that HIF-1α was highly expressed in patients with cervical cancer.

Tumor progression is a complex process jointly regulated by multiple factors, in which biological behaviors such as cell migration, invasion, cell-cycle regulation, and proliferation all play important roles. In hepatocellular carcinoma, enhanced HIF-1α activity promotes the secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), thereby increasing vascular permeability, inducing tumor angiogenesis, and further facilitating tumor growth and metastasis (31). In ovarian cancer, downregulation of HIF-1α significantly suppresses tumor cell invasion and angiogenesis (32). In addition, HIF-1α overexpression has been shown to be significantly associated with the invasive phenotype and poor prognosis of breast cancer (33). A transcriptomic study comparing non-neoplastic and neoplastic vaginal lesions further found that high HIF-1α expression was closely associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes (34). Consistent with these observations, the present study also found that HIF-1α overexpression promoted the proliferation and migration of both HPV16-positive and HPV18-positive cervical cancer cells, further supporting the pro-tumorigenic role of HIF-1α in cervical cancer progression and suggesting that it may represent an important molecular basis driving the malignant phenotype of cervical cancer cells.

In addition, our pan-cancer survival results suggested that HIF-1α was associated with poorer survival in cervical cancer, which is consistent with previous findings showing that HIF-1α serves as an independent risk factor in Cox regression analysis (35). A HIF-1α-related risk score model may also provide reference value for predicting the prognosis and cisplatin response of patients with cervical cancer (36). However, the effect of HIF-1α on OS shows obvious cancer-type heterogeneity, and it is not consistently an unfavorable prognostic factor in all tumors. Hypoxia is essentially a spatial phenomenon (37). The study by Liu (38) further indicated that HIF-1α expression is regulated by intracellular oxygen partial pressure. In different cancer types, such as lung cancer, pancreatic cancer and colon cancer, differences in tissue oxygen partial pressure background and tumor-evolution microenvironment may lead to activation of the HIF-1α pathway at different stages of metastasis. Therefore, the stability of HIF-1α as an independent prognostic marker still needs to be further verified in combination with protein-level detection, clinical stage and other factors.

HIF-1α, as a key transcriptional regulator in tumor metabolic reprogramming, plays a central role in the network of glucose metabolism. By upregulating the expression of glucose transporters and multiple key glycolytic enzymes, HIF-1α promotes glucose uptake, lactate production, and metabolic flux remodeling, thereby enhancing cellular adaptation to the hypoxic microenvironment (39). In the present study, we found that HIF-1α overexpression promoted the expression of the glycolysis-related gene LDHA. As a key enzyme in the glycolytic pathway, LDHA catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to lactate and plays an important role in sustaining glycolysis, promoting lactate accumulation, and supporting the metabolic adaptation of tumor cells (40). In addition, our results showed that high HIF-1α expression was accompanied by increased intracellular ROS levels. ROS are oxygen-containing, highly reactive molecules generated during cellular aerobic metabolism, and the mitochondrial respiratory chain is one of their major sources. Studies have shown that HIF-1α can regulate mitochondrial activity by promoting glycolysis and activating PDK1, thereby inhibiting pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle and remodeling mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, which reduces broad and destructive ROS production (41). On the other hand, HIF-1α has been suggested to activate NADPH oxidases (NOXs) to generate ROS that function as signaling molecules (42). Moderately increased ROS, as key intracellular second messengers, can activate downstream signaling pathways such as MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and NF-κB, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation, survival, and metastasis (43-45). Based on these findings, we speculate that HIF-1α may promote the malignant progression of cervical cancer by enhancing LDHA expression, increasing glycolytic activity, and inducing ROS accumulation, thereby conferring sustained energy support and metabolic advantages to cervical cancer cells.

Notably, persistent HR-HPV infection is a key etiological factor in the development and progression of cervical cancer, among which HPV16 and HPV18 are the most common high-risk subtypes. Therefore, in this study, HPV16-positive Caski cells and HPV18-positive HeLa cells were selected as cervical cancer cell models to investigate the biological role of HIF-1α under different HPV backgrounds. HPV E6 and E7 are key viral oncoproteins involved in cervical carcinogenesis, and previous studies have shown that HPV16/18 E6/E7 can enhance intracellular HIF-1α expression (46). Our results showed that HIF-1α overexpression enhanced cell viability and invasive ability in both HeLa and Caski cells, suggesting that HIF-1α may contribute to the enhancement of malignant phenotypes in HR-HPV-positive cervical cancer. However, after HIF-1α knockdown, the ROS responses differed between the two cell lines: ROS levels showed no obvious change in HeLa cells, whereas ROS levels remained significantly increased in Caski cells. Previous studies have shown that HPV16/18 E6 can increase intracellular ROS levels (47), and the HPV18 E5 oncoprotein may also participate in oxidative stress-related processes by increasing ROS levels in cells expressing the E6 and E7 oncogenes (48). This phenomenon suggests that ROS levels may not be regulated solely by HIF-1α in a unidirectional manner, but may instead be influenced by multiple factors, including HPV subtype background and viral oncoprotein expression.

However, this study still has several limitations. First, the functional evaluation of HIF-1α in this study was mainly based on in vitro cell experiments, and further validation in animal models is still lacking. Second, the specific downstream molecular mechanisms of HIF-1α require further in-depth investigation. Future studies will use rescue experiments, chromatin immunoprecipitation assays, or other chromatin-binding analyses to further verify whether HIF-1α directly participates in the transcriptional regulation of LDHA. Third, the cell models included in this study were relatively limited, and future studies should incorporate more cell lines and clinical samples to further improve the reliability and translational value of the findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that HIF-1α is aberrantly overexpressed in cervical cancer and may contribute to the malignant progression of tumor cells by promoting glycolytic metabolism. HIF-1α therefore has the potential to serve as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for cervical cancer.
