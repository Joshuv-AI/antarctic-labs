Ameliorative effect of Annona muricata seed extract on testosterone-induced benign prostatic hyperplasia in rats: biochemical, histopathological, and in silico evidence of 5-alpha reductase inhibition
Authors: Juliet Nwamaka Ozioko-Agbo, Daniel Emmanuel Ekpo, Christian Chijioke Amah, Michael Chinedu Chukwu, Parker Elijah Joshua, Lawrence Uchenna Sunday Ezeanyika
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnnona muricata L., known for its medicinal properties, has been explored for its potential in treating benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). While previous studies have noted its numerous health benefits, this research evaluated the therapeutic potential of seed extract of Annona muricata (SEAM) against BPH in rats by exploring in vivo and in silico approaches.MethodsSEAM extraction, phytochemical analysis, in vitro antioxidant activities, and acute toxicity were achieved using standard methods. Thirty male Wistar rats (weight: 158–192 g) were randomly divided into six groups of five rats each. The groups were assigned as follows: (1) normal control, (2) negative control, (3) positive control, and (4–6) experimental groups. BPH was induced in rats of groups (2–6) by subcutaneous injection of 3 mg/kg body weight (b.w) testosterone for 28 days, followed by daily oral treatment with 5 mg/kg b.w. Finasteride, 200, 400 and 600 mg/kg b.w SEAM, respectively for 21 days. The effect of SEAM treatment on changes in rats’ biochemical, hematological, and histological indices was assessed.ResultsExtraction of 3000 g of A. muricata seeds yielded 255.6 g SEAM. GC-MS analysis of SEAM revealed flavonoids, alkaloids, polyphenols, and saponins. SEAM exhibited strong radical scavenging activity in vitro. BPH induction in rats triggered significant (p < 0.05) alterations in some biochemical, hematological and histological profiles of test rats. Notably, SEAM significantly (p <0.05) normalized altered levels of prostate-specific antigen, testosterone, antioxidant enzymes, and hematological parameters of treated rats, and is comparable to the standard drug, Finasteride. Molecular docking studies revealed strong binding interactions between SEAM phytochemicals and 5-alpha reductase, suggesting inhibition of this enzyme as a key mechanism of SEAM action.ConclusionsOur study not only confirms the therapeutic potential of SEAM in BPH management but also provides insights into novel biochemical pathways and molecular interactions. These findings indicate that SEAM possess significant anti-BPH property through antioxidant activity and 5-alpha reductase inhibitory activity, providing scientific validation for its potential as a natural remedy for BPH, and setting the stage for further mechanistic studies and clinical trials.

Introduction

Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) is a common condition among older men, characterized by non-cancerous prostate gland enlargement leading to lower urinary tract symptoms such as frequent urination, weak urinary stream, and difficulties with urination initiation or cessation [1]. Despite not being life-threatening, BPH significantly affects quality of life [2]. The exact mechanisms driving BPH’s onset and progression are not fully understood [3], necessitating further exploration of treatment options. The global prevalence of BPH has increased from 51.1 million cases in 2000 to 94.0 million in 2019 [4], highlighting its growing impact on public health. Key factors contributing to BPH include aging, genetics, and geographic location, which are non-modifiable. However, modifiable factors like hormonal changes, metabolic syndrome, and inflammation are increasingly recognized as significant in BPH’s progression [5]. While androgens do not directly cause BPH, they play a role through dihydrotestosterone (DHT) in the prostate’s epithelial stroma [6]. The conversion of testosterone to DHT by 5-alpha reductase in the prostate’s stromal cells is crucial [7]. Treatment options, such as Finasteride, are effective but costly, driving research into more affordable and safer herbal alternatives for BPH management.

Animal models are pivotal in understanding the mechanisms of diseases and evaluating potential treatments [8, 9]. Their utility lies in their ability to mimic human BPH closely, enabling insights into its onset, prevention, and management. Various models exist, including spontaneous, hormone-induced, and xenograft models, each serving different aspects of BPH research [10]. Androgens like testosterone and its metabolite, dihydrotestosterone (DHT), are crucial in BPH development. DHT, formed by the action of 5α-reductase, has a high affinity for androgen receptors, significantly influencing prostate growth and function [11, 12].

Across the globe, extensive research has been conducted on medicinal plants to understand their positive impacts on health. Annona muricata L., commonly known as Soursop or Graviola, is a tropical species native to the regions of tropical Africa and South America, traditionally used for treating various ailments, including prostate issues [13]. This plant, revered in many cultures for its medicinal properties, encompasses the treatment of conditions like BPH, prostate cancer, infections, inflammation, pain, and fever. As an evergreen tree from the Annonaceae family, A. muricata is known for its large, smooth, dark green leaves and edible fruits, reaching heights of 5 to 7 m. It has attracted scientific interest due to its rich array of bioactive compounds, including alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, amongst others [14].

A. muricata has shown considerable promise as a therapeutic agent for BPH through various research methods, including in vitro, in silico, and in vivo studies. These investigations have highlighted the plant’s capability to reduce inflammation and oxidative stress, thus ameliorating BPH symptoms in rat models. Leje et al. [15] reported that fruit extract of Annona muricata ameliorated BPH in rats. Notably, Ogbu et al. [16] reported that an acetogenin-rich fraction from A. muricata leaves significantly enhanced antioxidant capacity and reduced BPH symptoms in rats with testosterone-induced BPH. Similarly, Ebenyi et al. [17] confirmed the antioxidative and anti-androgenic effects of A. muricata leaf extracts using both in vivo and in silico approaches. Ezirim et al. [18] reported the potential of leaves of A. muricata as a candidate for treatment of BPH. In silico studies by Apeh et al. [19] have pinpointed specific phytochemicals like Annopentocin A, muricatetrocin A, and annohexocin as potential anti-prostate cancer agents. In addition, both in vitro and in vivo studies have indicated that A. muricata extracts can promote apoptosis and decrease prostate size in BPH-1 cells [20], with Almutairi et al. [21] showing antiproliferative effects of seed and fruit extracts on prostate cancer cells. While these studies provide a broad understanding of A. muricata’s benefits, there remains a notable gap in the research exhausting the efficacy of its seed extracts. Previous research has largely concentrated on leaf extracts, with limited exploration of seed extracts despite their potentially higher concentration of bioactive compounds, which are known for their potent biological activities. Seeds might offer a different or more concentrated profile of these compounds compared to other parts of the plant, potentially leading to more effective therapeutic outcomes for BPH. For instance, a study by Adaramoye et al. [22] found that A. muricata extract significantly reduced prostate weight and epithelial thickness in rats with testosterone-induced BPH. These findings suggest that A. muricata may possess bioactive compounds with therapeutic potential for BPH treatment.

In the present study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of seed extract of A. muricata L. (SEAM) in a testosterone-induced BPH model in rats. Our rationale for focusing on the seed extract stems from the relatively underexplored potential of seeds compared to leaves in the context of BPH, despite their unique or more concentrated phytochemical profiles that may yield enhanced therapeutic outcomes. Based on the evidence from previous studies showcasing the anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiproliferative properties of A. muricata leaves [16, 17, 21], we hypothesize that the seeds could provide significant therapeutic benefits in BPH, with the aim of offering new insights that could lead to the development of evidence-based natural remedies or complementary therapies for BPH.

All chemicals utilized in this investigation were of analytical grade and were procured from various reputable sources. Experimental animals

A total of thirty (30) male albino Wistar rats, each weighing between 158 g and 192 g, were employed for the study. They were obtained from the Animal Facility Center at the University of Nigeria, Nsukka, where they underwent a one-week acclimatization phase, maintained under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and were provided with standard rat chow and water ad libitum. The well-being of the animals utilized in the acute toxicity and BPH experimental studies was given precedence, adhering to the guidelines and ethical approval provided by the Ethics and Biosafety Committee of the Faculty of Biological Sciences, University of Nigeria, with approval Reference No: UNN/FBS/24/PG/PhD/17/00884.

Methods

Identification of plant material

The plant material was identified by Mr. Alfred Ozioko, a plant taxonomist at the Herbarium of the International Centre for Ethnomedicine and Drug Development (InterCEDD), University of Nigeria, Nsukka. A voucher specimen, labeled as InterCEDD/16,089, was deposited and is accessible to the public at the InterCEDD Herbarium, Nsukka, Nigeria.

Plant extraction and determination of percentage yield

The method previously described by Adaramoye et al. [22] was adopted with slight modification. The process involved washing, hulling, and pressing the fruit to obtain the seeds, which were then cleaned and air-dried for 4 weeks (Fig. 1A). The dried seeds were ground into a fine powder and used for crude plant extraction. Specifically, 3000 g of powdered seeds were soaked in 7.5 L of n-hexane for 72 h with intermittent stirring. The mixture was filtered through muslin cloth and Whatman No. 1 Filter Paper to remove residues. The filtrate was then concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 45 °C to yield the SEAM. Extraction of SEAM phytochemicals for gas chromatography analysis

Phytochemicals form SEAM were extracted following the procedure outlined by Buss and Butler [23]. Initially, a 0.2 g sample of SEAM was weighed and placed into a test tube. To this, 15 ml of ethanol and 10 ml of a 50% m/v potassium hydroxide solution were added. The mixture was then heated in a water bath at 60 °C for three h to facilitate the reaction. Thereafter, the content of the tubes was transferred to a separating funnel. The test tube was rinsed with 20 ml of ethanol, followed by 10 ml of cold water, 10 ml of hot water, and 3 ml of hexane, with all rinsate added to the funnel. The combined extracts underwent three washes with 10 ml of a 10% v/v ethanol aqueous solution. Subsequently, the ethanol was evaporated, and the residue was dissolved in 1000 µl of pyridine. Finally, 200 µl of this solution was taken and placed in a vial for subsequent analysis.

Quantification of SEAM phytochemicals using gas chromatography

The analysis of the phytochemical constituents of SEAM was performed following the method described by Kelly and Nelson [24], utilizing Gas Chromatography in tandem with Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS). The phytochemical evaluation was carried out on a BUCK M910 Gas Chromatography system, which featured a flame ionization detector (FID). A RESTEK 15-meter MXT-1 column (15 m × 250 μm × 0.15 μm) was employed for the separation process. The injector temperature was maintained at 280 °C, with a splitless injection of 2 µl of the sample and a linear velocity set at 30 cm/s. Helium 5.0 was used as the carrier gas, flowing at a rate of 40 ml/min. The oven temperature was initially set to 200 °C and was gradually increased to 330 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min, holding this temperature for 5 min. The detector operated at a temperature of 320 °C. The quantification of the phytochemicals was achieved by calculating the ratio of the area and mass of the internal standard to the area of the identified phytochemicals, with concentrations reported in µg/ml or ppm as the case maybe.

Determination of in vitro antioxidant activity of SEAM

Determination of DPPH scavenging activity

The assessment of SEAM’s capacity to neutralize 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical was conducted following the methodology established by Brand-Williams et al. [25]. Briefly, a 0.1 mM DPPH solution in ethanol was prepared, to which varying concentrations of SEAM or ascorbic acid as standard (200, 400, and 600 mg/ml) were added (3 ml). After thorough mixing, the solution was allowed to sit in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was then measured at 517 nm with a spectrophotometer (UV-VIS Shimadzu UV 2450). A decrease in absorbance indicated a greater effectiveness against free radicals. Ascorbic acid served as the reference compound due to its potent reducing properties and minimal metal-chelating effects. The experiment was replicated three times using different concentrations of ascorbic acid. The IC50 value, representing the concentration required to inhibit 50% of the DPPH free radical, was determined by plotting an inhibition curve. A linear graph was created to correlate concentration with percentage inhibition using various ascorbic acid concentrations, from which IC50 values were calculated. Acute toxicity study

The median lethal dose (LD50) was determined as a measure of acute toxicity through a two-phase approach, utilizing a modified method of Lorke [28], as described by Ekpo et al. [29]. A total of twenty-four Swiss albino mice, each weighing between 25 and 30 g, were allocated into two phases, with 12 mice in each. Each phase consisted of three groups of four mice. In Phase 1, the mice were administered doses of 10, 100, and 1000 mg/kg b.w. of SEAM and monitored for 24 h. Subsequently, in Phase 2, the mice received higher doses of 1600, 2900, and 5000 mg/kg b.W. of SEAM, respectively, and were observed for an additional 24 h for any signs of mortality, behavioral or morphological alterations, or toxicity indicators. The body weights of the mice were recorded both 24 h before and after the administration of SEAM for comparison. Experimental design and protocol

We induced BPH in rats via subcutaneous administration of 3 mg/kg b.w. of testosterone propionate dissolved in 1 ml of olive oil, administered on alternate days for 8 days [10]. Physical changes were noted throughout the induction period, and after 28 days, the concentration of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) was measured to confirm the presence of the disease in the rats. After confirming the successful induction of BPH in rats, thirty male albino Wistar rats were divided into six groups, each consisting of five rats, and assigned to various daily treatment protocol as follows: Group 1: Non-induced and untreated (normal control)Group 2: BPH-induced without treatment (negative control)Group 3: BPH-induced and administered 5 mg/kg b.w standard drug, FinasterideGroup 4: BPH-induced and treated with 200 mg/kg b.w of SEAMGroup 5: BPH-induced and treated with 400 mg/kg b.w of SEAMGroup 6: BPH-induced and treated with 600 mg/kg b.w of SEAM

At the end of the experimental period on day 21, all experimental rats underwent an overnight fasting phase. Subsequently, they were euthanized using an intra-peritoneal administration of sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 60 mg/kg body weight, followed by cervical dislocation as per established procedures [30]. Post-euthanasia, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture into both plain and EDTA-treated sterile tubes. These samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min to separate the serum or plasma, which was then carefully transferred into sterile, labeled tubes for subsequent hematological and biochemical analyses [31]. Additionally, sections of the prostate gland were excised and immediately fixed in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin for histopathological examination.

Determination of hematological parameters

The concentrations of red blood cells (RBCs) and white blood cells (WBCs), along with packed cell volume (PCV) and hemoglobin (Hb) concentrations, were determined following the methods outlined by Leibach [32]. Platelet counts were determined from whole blood collected in EDTA test tubes, and analyzed using the ABX Micros 60 Hematology Analyzer (Horiba-ABX, Montpellier, France). A thin blood film was prepared and stained with Leishman stain for the morphological evaluation of red blood cells. Stained slides were observed under a light microscope with × 40 objectives to identify an appropriate area for analysis, after which a drop of oil was applied to the slide for examination at the × 100 objective.

Determination of antioxidant status

Assay for superoxide dismutase activity

The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) was assessed according to the method of Xin et al. [33]. This enzyme catalyzes the conversion of superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide. The methodology relies on the competition between the activity of SOD and iodonitrazolium violet in their reaction with superoxide, produced during the xanthine oxidase reaction. The measured SOD activity corresponds to a 50% inhibition of the reduction observed in the detector.

Assay for catalase activity

The assay for catalase activity was carried out following the method of Aebi [34]. During the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the presence of catalase, a decrease in absorption over time is observed, which allows for the measurement of catalase activity. A mixture was prepared by adding 2.5 ml of phosphate buffer, 2 ml of H2O2, and 0.5 ml of the sample into a test tube. Subsequently, 2 ml of a 5% dichromate acetic acid reagent was introduced to 1 ml of the reaction mixture. Assay for glutathione peroxidase activity

The glutathione peroxidase activity was assayed following the method established by Paglia and Valentine [35], with minor modifications. A 100 µl aliquot of the standard solution was introduced into the designated wells for standards. Subsequently, 10 µl of the positive control, 10 µl of the blank solution, and 10 µl of the test samples were added to their respective wells in the microplate. Subsequently, 50 µl of the reaction mixture was pipetted into all wells, excluding the standard wells. To ensure that the total volume matched that of the standard wells, each well was brought to a final volume of 100 µl using a diluent buffer. The absorbance at 340 nm was recorded (A1) using a microplate reader (Sun Rise, TECAN, Austria). The microplate was then incubated for 10 min at 37 °C, after which the absorbance at 340 nm was measured again (A2). The GPX activity for the test samples was determined by the ELISA program, utilizing the standard curve and the difference in absorbance of the test samples at the two time points (A1-A2).

Determination of testosterone levels

Testosterone levels were assessed using the method of Boots et al. [36]. This technique relies on the competitive binding of testosterone present in the test sample and a testosterone-horseradish peroxidase (tHRP) conjugate to a fixed quantity of rabbit anti-testosterone during the incubation phase. Wells coated with goat anti-rabbit IgG were treated with testosterone standards or samples, along with the testosterone-HRP conjugate and rabbit anti-testosterone reagent, for a duration of 90 min. Each serum reference, control, and sample was analyzed in triplicate. The absorbance for each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader within 30 min after the addition of the stop solution.

Assay for prostate-specific antigen concentration

The estimation of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) in the sample was carried out according to the method of Uotila et al. [37]. Briefly, the micro plate wells was formatted for each serum reference, control and sample to be assayed in triplicate. Thereafter, 0.25 ml of the appropriate serum reference, control or specimen was pipetted into the assigned well, and 0.100 ml of PSA-enzyme reagent was added to each well. The micro plate was gently swirled for 20–30 s to mix properly and incubate for 30 min at room temperature. After this, the contents of the micro plate was discarded by aspiration. A known volume of 350 µl of wash buffer was added, tap and blot or aspirated. The step was repeat twice for a total of 3 washes. Thereafter, 100 ml of working substrate solution was added to all wells and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Then 0.05 ml of stop solution was added to each well and gently mixed for 20 s. The absorbance of the mixture in each well was read at 450 nm in a micro plate reader, and thereafter the PSA level was estimated.

Examination of tissue samples at the microscopic level

The histopathological evaluation of prostate tissues was conducted following the protocol described by Louis et al. [38]. In summary, prostate gland sections were obtained from rats and preserved in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin for at least 48 h. The tissues were then trimmed, subjected to dehydration through four concentrations of alcohol (70%, 80%, 90%, and absolute alcohol), cleared using three grades of xylene, and embedded in molten paraffin wax. Once solidified, the blocks were sliced into 5 μm sections using a rotary microtome, floated in a water bath, and incubated at 60˚C for 30 min. The 5 μm sections were subsequently cleared in three grades of xylene and rehydrated through three concentrations of alcohol (90%, 80%, and 70%). The sections were stained with Hematoxylin for 15 min, followed by bluing with ammonium chloride and differentiation using 1% acid alcohol before counterstaining with Eosin. Permanent mounts were created on degreased glass slides using DPX, a mixture of distyrene, a plasticizer, and xylene. The prepared slides were examined under a Motic™ compound light microscope with ×4, ×10, and ×40 objective lenses, and photomicrographs were captured using a Motic™ 5.0-megapixel microscope camera at ×160 magnification.

Interaction of phytochemicals in seam with 5α-reductase

Proteins retrieval and preparation

The crystal structure of 5-alpha reductase (PDB ID: 7BW1) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). The refinement of the protein structures was conducted using Discovery Studio software. Preparation of the proteins involved the incorporation of Gasteiger charges and polar hydrogen, facilitated by AutoDock tools.

Ligand retrieval and preparation

The crystal structures of specific compounds derived from SEAM were obtained from the PubChem database in SDF format. These SDF files were subsequently converted to PDB format utilizing Pymol software [39].

Active site prediction

Following the preparation of protein-ligand complexes, a receptor grid was created centered around the co-ligand’s centroid. The grid box was established by identifying the amino acid residues located at the active site. The analysis of the active site was conducted utilizing Biovia Discovery Studio software.

Molecular docking

Prior to docking, both the proteins and ligands were exported in PDBQt format. The molecular docking process was conducted utilizing AutoDock Vina software [40]. The 2D representations and 3D surface visualizations of the protein-ligand interactions were created using Discovery Studio and Pymol software, respectively [37]. The physicochemical properties, lipophilicity, solubility, pharmacokinetics, and Lipinski drug-likeness of the compounds under investigation were assessed using the SwissADME server [41].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of raw data obtained from the different experiment was conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS version 21, with post hoc multiple comparison used to determine significant differences in the means of the different groups. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation of the triplicate measurements. Statistical significance was established at p ≤ 0.05, with means having p-values of 0.05 or lower were deemed statistically significant when compared.

Results and discussion

Percentage yield of SEAM following solvent extraction

The extraction of 3000 g of the powdered seeds of A. muricata gave rise to an extract yield of 255.6 g which constitutes 8.52% of the total quantity of the powdered plant material extracted. The percentage yield achieved is comparable to the 8.21% reported by Prasad et al. [42] when using methanol as a solvent. Similarly, Heinrich et al. [43] recorded a yield of 9.463% with methanol, while Shaito et al. [44] noted yields of 25.56% and 23.6% for methanol and chloroform, respectively. These discrepancies may be attributed to the different solvents utilized. In addition to the solvent, various factors can significantly influence the extraction efficiency of plant extracts, including the type and concentration of the solvent, the solvent-to-solid material ratio, and size of the plant particles, leaf characteristics, harvest timing, acidification levels, drying temperature, storage conditions of the extracts, and the temperature and duration of the extraction process [45]. This was further illustrated by Indah et al. [46], who found that A. muricata from different districts (Jayasari Village, Pangandaran District, West Java) yielded 12.23%, 11.72%, and 10.72%, respectively, despite being extracted under identical conditions with the same solvent.

SEAM showed safe acute toxicity profile in experimental animals

The results of the toxicity test on SEAM are presented in Table 1. The findings indicate that SEAM was non-toxic to experimental animals, even at doses between 10 and 5000 mg/kg b.w. Additionally, no significant (p > 0.05) difference in the body weight of the mice 24 h post-administration of SEAM across both phases of the study, was recorded. Moreover, no cases of mortality or significant behavioral changes were observed among the test subjects throughout the duration of the study. The animals maintained a stable condition and showed no physical signs of toxicity during the assessment. These results affirm the safety of ASEDS for experimental use, aligning with the report of Lorke [28] that a median lethal dose (LD50) exceeding 5000 mg/kg b.w. is considered safe. Following this, we chose arbitrary doses of 200, 400 and 600 mg/kg also verifying from previous studies involving different parts of A. Murcata [15, 18, 22], for subsequent studies.

Phytochemicals identified in SEAM

Flavonoids are the key bioactive compounds that contribute to the mitigation of symptoms associated with BPH [49]. According to Iannuzzo et al. [50], oligomeric flavonoids such as proanthocyanidins, which are derived from grape seed extract and also present in SEAM, have been shown to lower prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels and diminish the expression of significant pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in homogenates of prostate and seminal vesicles. Naringin, another compound found in SEAM, has been documented to decrease both the absolute weight of the prostate and the ratio of prostate weight to body weight [51]. Juang et al. [52] posited that the inhibition of PSA gene expression by cardiac glycosides may result from the down-regulation of the prostate-derived Ets factor gene. The potential of dietary polyphenols in the management of BPH has been highlighted [53]. Kaempferol, also present in SEAM, has been indicated to counteract BPH by inhibiting androgen activity [54]. Ferrante et al. [55] suggested that catechins and epicatechins, which are also found in SEAM, may play a significant role in the prostatitis-related effects of E. angustifolium due to their capacity to inhibit cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2). Our findings indicate that SEAM contains notable levels of oxalate, which has been associated with the alleviation of prostatitis and BPH. A study by Sun et al. [56] demonstrated that calcium oxalate enhances clusterin expression and alleviates lower urinary tract symptoms in patients with prostatitis and BPH who have calculi. Resveratrol, a polyphenol present in SEAM, has been reported to induce apoptosis in the BPH epithelial cell line (BPH-1) via the p38 MAPK-FOXO3a signaling pathway [57]. Additionally, Jang et al. [58] found that resveratrol reduced the proliferation of prostatic stromal cells in BPH by modulating cell cycle progression, apoptosis, signaling pathways, BPH markers, and NF-κB activity. SEAM also contains sapogenin, a naturally occurring saponin. A study conducted by Chen et al. [59] demonstrated that diosgenin, a type of natural sapogenin, exhibited inhibitory effects on BPH in rat models. These findings underscore the potential of SEAM-rich phytochemicals in the treatment and management of BPH.

SEAM displayed strong radical scavenging activities in vitro

Figure 2A illustrates the DPPH radical scavenging activity of SEAM. The findings indicate a concentration-dependent reduction in the percentage inhibition of the DPPH radical, with an inhibitory concentration capable of scavenging 50% of the DPPH radical (IC50) measured at ₋5.99 ± 0.02 mg/ml and a strong positive correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.9948). A direct relationship was noted between the concentrations of SEAM and the percentage inhibition of the DPPH radical, where a concentration of 200 mg/ml exhibited the highest percentage inhibition, while the highest concentration of 600 mg/ml showed the lowest percentage inhibition. This suggests that stronger radical scavenging activity occurs at concentrations below 200 mg/ml. Ascorbic acid showed a superior DPPH radical scavenging activity with an IC50 of 921.74 ± 0.85 mg/ml and R² = 0.9944 when compared to SEAM, indicating its electron-donating or hydrogen atom-donating ability so as to stabilize the highly reactive DPPH radical [45]. The result aligns with the findings of Balderrama-Carmona et al. [60], who reported an extract from the leaves of A. muricata yielding 23.61 mmol of Trolox equivalent/g of extract. Additionally, Bektic et al. [61] noted a 44.7% DPPH radical scavenging effect at elevated concentrations of A. muricata leaf extract. The DPPH radical scavenging activity observed in SEAM could be attributed to the reduction of DPPH through the transfer of hydrogen atoms from antioxidants to the nitrogen atom of the DPPH.

SEAM exhibited a rapid change in the color of the purple DPPH radical, suggesting a heightened ability to donate electrons or hydrogen atoms, which stabilizes the highly reactive DPPH radical. The degree of this color change correlates with the concentration and effectiveness of the antioxidants present in SEAM in scavenging the radical. Research indicates that polyphenolic phytochemicals serve as effective radical scavengers and reducing agents due to their capacity to donate hydrogen atoms or electrons/protons. This scavenging activity is linked to the stable structure of polyphenols, which enables them to mitigate the reactivity of free radicals, transforming them into less reactive aroxyl radicals. The antioxidants in question provide a moderate level of protection against damage induced by radicals in biological systems by neutralizing oxidants through the donation of electrons or hydrogen atoms from their free hydroxyl groups, thus reducing oxidative stress and minimizing potential harm [62].

The FRAP of SEAM reported in Fig. 2B shows a concentration-dependent enhancement in ferric reducing power, with SEAM at 600 mg/ml exhibiting the highest reducing capacity at 28.9%, compared to 15.5% for SEAM at 200 mg/ml. The IC50 of SEAM was 1242.79 ± 9.80 mg/ml and an R2 of 0.9876, relative to Gallic acid standard IC50 66.67 ± 3.41 mg/ml, R2 of 0.9796, indicating a better ferric reducing antioxidant power. Our findings slightly surpasses the previously reported value of 24.50% by Padmini et al. [63]. The variations observed may stem from differences in the source or conditions of the plant sample, as well as the extraction methods employed. Our results imply that the reducing agents found in SEAM, including flavonoids and phenolic compounds, are integral to its ability to convert Fe3+ into Fe2+, which is a more biologically active form of iron. By donating electrons to reactive Fe3+, these compounds help stabilize the iron and interrupt free radical chain reactions. The free radical reduction potential of SEAM highlights its ability to reduce transition state iron and diminish free radical formation through the Fenton reaction, thereby underscoring its antioxidant capabilities. Research indicates that compounds with significant reducing power possess excellent electron-donating properties, allowing them to effectively neutralize oxidized intermediates associated with lipid peroxidation. Acting as both primary and secondary antioxidants, these compounds can efficiently reduce and stabilize harmful oxidized species, thus preventing further oxidative damage [64].

Figure 2C also presents the inhibition of in vitro inhibition of lipid peroxidation by SEAM. The assay was applied to measure the concentration of free radicals present at the end of peroxide oxidation [65]. Our results showed a concentration-dependent increase in the inhibition of lipid peroxidation by SEAM with 200 mg/ml SEAM displaying inhibition of 37.2% while 600 mg/ml SEAM showed the highest inhibition of (73.7%). SEAM indicated a decrease in inhibition of lipid peroxidation with an IC50 of 380.99 ± 3.32 mg/ml and R2 0.9858 when compared to the standard, Gallic acid, which recorded IC50 of ₋483.25 ± 6.32 mg/ml and R2 0.9796. The result showed the ability of SEAM to interfere with the lipid peroxidation reaction reducing and/or preventing lipid oxidation and scavenging reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) [66].

Antioxidants are known to prevent lipid peroxidation by inhibiting the propagation step of the reaction. Our result reveals the ability of SEAM to protect biological macromolecules from damage caused by free radicals. Polyphenols have the outstanding characteristic of inhibiting or delaying the autoxidation of unsaturated lipid molecules through mechanisms that may involve hydrogen atom transfer, electron transfer–proton transfer, and single-proton-loss electron-transfer [67]. Phenolic compounds, like those found in SEAM, are a class of chemicals characterized by one or more hydroxyl groups attached to an aromatic ring [67]. These compounds exhibit a wide range of bioactivities, influenced by the number, position, and type of hydroxyl groups and other substituents on the aromatic ring. Their structural diversity enables them to perform various biological functions, making them a significant component of plant extracts [68]. In general, antioxidant defense encompasses a multifaceted approach, involving several key mechanisms that work together to provide protection against oxidative stress and cell damage [69].

The progression of BPH has been linked to a compromised oxidative status, suggesting a shift in the activity levels of all antioxidant enzymes. Superoxide dismutase and catalase are crucial enzymes that directly contribute to the antioxidant defense system by neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS). SOD begins this protective process by catalyzing the transformation of superoxide (O2−) into hydrogen peroxide, effectively converting superoxide radicals into a less reactive form. This action helps to prevent the generation of more damaging radicals, such as hydroxyl radicals (OH−), thereby alleviating oxidative stress and cellular injury. By mitigating the detrimental effects of ROS, SOD plays a vital role, while CAT further decomposes hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, thereby completing the antioxidant cycle and supporting cellular homeostasis [70]. This initial enzymatic action paves the way for CAT to effectively reduce hydrogen peroxide, ensuring the maintenance of cellular balance.

Our findings are consistent with earlier studies by Minciullo et al. [71], and Ogbu et al. [16], which reported significant increases in the activities of SOD and CAT following the induction of BPH in rats. In contrast, Ejele et al. [72] observed a reduction in SOD activity among BPH patients receiving treatment. Additionally, Bm et al. [73] and Adaramoye et al. [22] noted declines in SOD and CAT activities after treatment with aqueous and n-hexane extracts of A. muricata, respectively. The reductions in SOD and CAT activities post-treatment may be linked to the enhancement of these enzymes through the administration of SEAM phytochemicals, which help sequester radical species in vivo. This process allows the enzymes to counteract the harmful effects of oxidizing radicals produced by the disease, ultimately leading to a restoration of antioxidant function and overall health, as suggested by Ogbu et al. [16]. Furthermore, incorporating exogenous natural antioxidants into the diet has been demonstrated to bolster the body’s endogenous antioxidant defense system, serving as an effective strategy to mitigate the adverse effects of RONS [66].

Glutathione peroxidase (GPX) serves as an essential antioxidant enzyme that facilitates the conversion of hydrogen peroxide into water, thus safeguarding cells from its detrimental effects of RONS. Our findings indicate that GPX activity was markedly (p < 0.05) elevated in all treatment groups when compared to the untreated group (Table 3). This shows the potential of SEAM to restore antioxidant equilibrium and may be effective in the management of BPH. This observation is consistent with earlier research conducted by Sajjaboontawee et al. [74], which noted reduced GPX levels in patients with BPH. Notably, Bizoń et al. [75] identified an inverse correlation between the activities of SOD and GPX, as well as between GPX and CAT, in individuals with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis. This phenomenon may be linked to elevated hydrogen peroxide levels, which could enhance CAT activity due to its role in catalyzing the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide. The functions of key antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, CAT, and GPX are crucial for the body’s defense systems. Their involvement in eliminating superoxide anions and sustaining oxidative balance is vital, underscoring their significance in health maintenance and disease management [76].

Histopathology of the prostate gland

BPH is a hyperplastic process characterized by an increase in the size of the prostates’ glandular-epithelial, stromal, and tissue components, leading to detectable growth or enlargement of the prostate resulting in pathological conditions [82]. We assessed histopathological changes in prostate tissues following BPH induction and the subsequent treatment with SEAM. Our results (Fig. 3), revealed a normal histology of prostate sections of normal control rats, with typical prostatic histology featuring regular acini lined by cuboidal-to-low columnar epithelium with eosinophilic secretory material in the lumen and minimal interstitium (Fig. 3A). In contrast, subcutaneous administration of Testosterone (3 mg/kg) for 28 days, induced characteristic histopathological changes consistent with BPH in rats of the untreated group (Fig. 3B). The prostate of the disease control group exhibited mild-to-moderate multifocal epithelial hyperplasia, with luminal infoldings, cribriform patterns, and occasional vacuolar degeneration of acinar epithelial cells. These alterations were accompanied by stromal expansion and disruption of normal glandular architecture. Treatment with finasteride (5 mg/kg b.w) largely restored normal histoarchitecture, although moderate acinar dilation suggestive of enhanced secretory activity was observed (Fig. 3C). Similarly, oral administration of SEAM produced dose-dependent protective effects. The lowest dose (200 mg/kg) partially ameliorated hyperplasia but still showed moderate epithelial infoldings and vacuolar changes (Fig. 3D). At 400 mg/kg and 600 mg/kg, SEAM markedly improved prostate morphology, resulting in near-normal glandular structure with only mild acinar dilation and attenuated epithelium indicative of restored secretory function without hyperplasia (Fig. 3E–F). These findings demonstrate that SEAM at higher doses provided histopathological protection comparable to the standard 5α-reductase inhibitor, finasteride.

BPH development is characterized by an increased number and enlargement of transition nodules, representing glandular tissue proliferation [83]. Prostate tissue growth requires new blood vessel formation to maintain adequate nutrient supply [84]. Oxidative stress, resulting from increased oxidative stress and depleted antioxidant systems during prostate enlargement has been linked to BPH development [71]. While there is suggestive evidence that reduced antioxidant activity contributes to oxidative stress-related biochemical and histological changes in BPH (e.g., inflammation, free radical damage, and cellular proliferation), the precise mechanisms and extent of influence are not yet fully understood. Hao et al. [85] noted that elevated antioxidant levels is associated with a higher risk of BPH. Minciullo et al. [86], reported that prostatic inflammation which can be triggered by a number of factors generates free radicals, worsened by weak antioxidant defenses, contributing to oxidative stress and prostate disease like BPH. Studies link oxidant products and low antioxidants to BPH, a premalignant condition. Prostate cancer patients show higher oxidative stress and lower antioxidant activity than BPH patients and controls [86]. In our study, we observed that induction of BPH with testosterone propionate altered antioxidant enzyme activities, indicating a compromised cellular architecture and an antioxidant response, leading to depleted levels. The ability of SEAM to reverse this trend suggests its potential as a therapeutic agent for managing BPH, and this could be attributed to the presence of antioxidant phytochemicals in SEAM which may act singly or in synergy to potentiate the observed effects, and possibly potentiate a normalization in altered biochemical and histological indices. Supporting evidence for this theory emerged from initial rat studies, which demonstrated a significant reduction in prostate size in BPH-induced mammals treated with isoflavones. These findings suggest that isoflavones and other flavonoid classes may have a beneficial effect in reducing prostate enlargement, providing a potential therapeutic approach for managing BPH [87].

SEAM phytochemicals showed strong inhibitory activities against 5-α reductase

Figure 4 illustrates the 3D-active site interaction of phytochemicals from SEAM with 5α-reductase, a key therapeutic target for BPH treatment [88, 89]. Inhibitors of 5α-reductase represents a category of medications used in the management of BPH. At present, Finasteride and Dutasteride are the sole medications within this class that have received approval from the Food and Drug Administration, FDA. These agents function by inhibiting 5-alpha reductase, thereby decreasing the transformation of testosterone into dihydrotestosterone. In this study, we carried out molecular docking studies to assess the role of SEAM phytochemicals as potential inhibitors of 5α-reductase activity thereby mitigating the onset and/or progression of BPH. We analysed the strength of the interaction of these phytochemicals to the active site (Fig. 5) of 5α-reductase and determined their binding energies as an index of their strength of interaction in comparison to the FDA approved inhibitor, Finasteride.

From the data obtained, the binding energies of SEAM phytochemicals revealed that sapogenin (-11.8 Kcal/mol) exhibited a slightly stronger affinity for the active site of 5α-reductas relative to the FDA-approved inhibitor, Finasteride (-11.7 Kcal/mol) (Table 6). Other SEAM phytochemicals, such as Naringin (-10.5 Kcal/mol), Rutin (-9.5 Kcal/mol), cardiac glycoside (-9.4 Kcal/mol), catechin (-9.2 Kcal/mol), Lunamarine (-9.0 kcal/mol), and Ribalinidine (-9.0 Kcal/mol), also displayed remarkably strong binding energies, although these interactions were not as strong as Finasteride, suggesting a synergistic effect that could enhance SEAM’s role in the treatment of BPH. These compounds, including Finasteride, formed conventional hydrogen bonds with key catalytic residues of 5α-reductase, including Tyr 91, Leu 224, Tyr 98, Ser 31, and Tyr 107, indicating a potential mechanism of SEAM’s action. Collectively, the observed docking properties of SEAM phytochemicals against 5α-reductase, comparable to Finasteride, is indicative of its potential in the treatment and management of BPH in rats. However, further investigation through molecular dynamics simulation, and 5-alpha reductase activity in vivo, is recommended to validate these findings.

Conclusions

The present study provides robust evidence that SEAM possesses significant anti-BPH activity in the testosterone-induced rat model. The observed restoration of prostate histoarchitecture, together with the previously noted normalization of PSA, testosterone levels, antioxidant enzyme activities, and hematological parameters, confirms the extract’s therapeutic efficacy. These effects were clearly dose-dependent, with 400 and 600 mg/kg doses approaching the protective outcome of finasteride, and without observable toxicity. The mechanisms underlying SEAM’s efficacy appear multifactorial. Strong in vitro radical scavenging activity, attributable to its rich content of flavonoids, polyphenols, alkaloids, and saponins (GC-MS), likely counters oxidative stress, a key contributor to BPH progression. More importantly, molecular docking studies revealed high-affinity interactions of several SEAM phytochemicals with 5α-reductase, suggesting direct inhibition of dihydrotestosterone biosynthesis, the primary driver of prostatic hyperplasia. This dual antioxidant and enzymatic inhibitory action explain SEAM’s superior tissue-level protection observed at higher doses. However, the study is limited by its use of an animal model and the absence of clinical data. Future research should focus on clinical trials to validate these findings, as well as detailed mechanistic and toxicological studies. Overall, the findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting plant-based alternatives for BPH management and offer a new perspective on natural therapeutic strategies for this prevalent condition.
