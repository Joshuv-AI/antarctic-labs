Assessing implementation of the Maternal Death Surveillance and Response (MDSR) framework in North Aceh District, Indonesia: a qualitative study
Authors: Sofyan Sufri, Misbahul Jannah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective implementation of the Maternal Death Surveillance and Response (MDSR) framework is critical for reducing maternal mortality. In Aceh Province, Indonesia, maternal mortality remains high, with rates exceeding the national average. Despite national policy adoption, limited evidence exists on how MDSR is implemented at the subnational level. This study assessed the implementation of the MDSR framework in North Aceh District by examining its five stages using eight implementation outcome variables.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using in-depth interviews with 60 participants, including policymakers, health practitioners, and community representatives across multiple administrative levels. In addition, five policy and program documents were reviewed. Data were analysed thematically using NVivo 12, guided by predefined implementation outcome variables (acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, compliance, implementation cost, coverage, and sustainability). Findings were further interpreted using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to identify multi-level factors influencing policy implementation.ResultsMDSR implementation was largely concentrated in the initial stage of maternal death identification, with limited progression to reporting, review, response, and monitoring and evaluation. While the framework was highly accepted and considered appropriate among policymakers, its implementation was constrained by inadequate public communication, low community engagement, limited capacity among frontline health workers, weak accountability and governance mechanisms, and frequent staff rotation. These challenges negatively affected adoption, coverage, compliance, and sustainability of the framework.ConclusionsMDSR implementation in North Aceh remains incomplete and uneven across stages, highlighting critical gaps in full-cycle implementation. Strengthening public communication, workforce capacity, accountability systems, and leadership commitment is essential to improve implementation performance. This study contributes to implementation science by demonstrating how implementation outcomes vary across MDSR stages and are shaped by multi-level determinants. The findings provide important insights for strengthening maternal death surveillance systems in Aceh and other similar decentralised health system contexts and may support efforts to accelerate progress towards maternal mortality reduction targets under the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28165-8.

Background

Maternal mortality remains a significant global public health concern. In 2020, approximately 287,000 women died during pregnancy, childbirth, or within the postpartum period [1]. Nearly 95% of these deaths occur in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), especially in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia [1, 2]. Despite global efforts, progress remains insufficient to meet the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) target of reducing the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) to lower than 70/100,000 live births (LBs) by 2030 [3].

Most maternal deaths are preventable through timely and appropriate health system interventions, particularly at the primary care level. Hence, the World Health Organisation (WHO) recommends the global implementation of the Maternal Death Surveillance and Response (MDSR) framework, which comprises five interlinked stages: identification and notification, reporting, review, response, and monitoring and evaluation [4]. Strengthening MDSR implementation is therefore critical to accelerating progress towards the SDGs.

Evidence from both high- and low-resource settings demonstrates that effective MDSR implementation can improve quality of care and reduce maternal mortality. For example, confidential inquiry systems in the United Kingdom have contributed to substantial reductions in maternal deaths, while MDSR-related initiatives such as the Campaign on Accelerated Reduction of Maternal Mortality in Africa (CARMMA) have declined nearly 50% of maternal mortality across the region [5, 6].

In Indonesia, although the MMR has declined from 226/100,000 LBs in 2021 to 118.5/100,000 LBs in 2024, it remains above the SDG benchmark [7, 8]. In fact, the Ministry of Health (MoH) has introduced various policies and programs to address maternal deaths since 1994. For example, the maternal death audit (MDA) programs in 1994 and the Confidential Enquiry into Maternal Death (CEMD) guidelines in 2010 (revised in 2015) aimed to improve reporting, review, and response mechanisms [9]. However, these approaches faced many limitations, including underreporting, incomplete data, and limited capacity to identify broader contributing factors to maternal deaths [9, 10]. Finally, in 2016, the MoH introduced the MDSR framework as a more comprehensive strategy to strengthen maternal death surveillance and response systems, mandating its implementation across all provinces and districts nationwide.

At the sub-national level, Aceh Province continues to experience a high burden of maternal mortality. In 2020, Aceh reported an MMR of 201/100,000 LBs, ranking 14th highest among 34 provinces and exceeding the national average of 189/100,000 LBs [11]. Within the province, North Aceh District reported the highest number of maternal deaths (28 cases in 2021), highlighting it a critical setting for examining MDSR implementation to reduce maternal deaths in the district [12, 13].

In response to these challenges, the Aceh government has developed various policy instruments to support maternal death reduction efforts, including local regulations and strategic plans that strengthen MDSR implementation at provincial and district levels. These include the Maternal and Infant Mortality Assessment 2016 and subsequent development plans and work plans issued by the Aceh Government [14–16].

However, despite these policy commitments, multiple factors continue to inhibit effective MDSR implementation in Aceh province, including in North Aceh District. Previous studies have identified three interrelated issues: (1) poor surveillance quality, leading to incomplete identification and delayed notification of maternal deaths [9, 10]; (2) limited analytical capacity among health officers, resulting in suboptimal reviews and insufficient identification of medical, social, and other systemic determinants [17, 18]; and (3) inadequate translation of review findings into actionable and accountable responses, contributing to the recurrence of preventable maternal deaths [12]. These issues are further exacerbated in North Aceh District by geographic barriers, limited health system capacity, and delays in accessing care, which contribute to adverse maternal outcomes [19].

To address these issues, it is essential to assess how MDSR is implemented in practice in Aceh province, with a particular focus on the North Aceh District. This study adopts an implementation research approach to examine the five stages of the MDSR framework by considering eight implementation outcome variables, including acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, compliance, implementation cost, coverage, and sustainability [20].

Despite policy emphasis on MDSR, limited evidence is available on how the framework has been implemented in Aceh Province since its adoption in 2016, particularly when analysed using implementation outcome variables. As such, important barriers, issues, and opportunities for strengthening MDSR implementation remain insufficiently understood.

Hence, this study aims to assess the implementation of the MDSR framework in North Aceh District by systematically investigating its five key stages – identification and notification, reporting, review, response, and monitoring and evaluation- and the proposed recommendations to reduce maternal deaths through the lens of eight implementation outcome variables. Specifically, this study addresses the following questions: (1) What is the current status of MDSR implementation across each stage of the framework in North Aceh District? (2) What are the key barriers and enabling factors influencing the implementation of each stage? and (3) What strategies and recommendations can be proposed to improve the implementation of the framework and reduce maternal mortality in the district?

Linking maternal death surveillance and response (mdsr) framework and implementation outcome variables: study conceptual framework

The Maternal Death Surveillance and Response (MDSR) policy framework, developed by the World Health Organisation (WHO), is a systematic approach that links surveillance of maternal deaths with actions to improve the quality of care and prevent future maternal deaths [4]. It builds on earlier WHO guidelines and is widely recommended for strengthening maternal health systems, particularly in the LMICs. The framework consists of a continuous five-stage cycle: identification and notification of maternal deaths, reporting, review of causes and contributing factors, implementation of response actions, and monitoring and evaluation [4]. These stages aim to ensure that maternal deaths are not only documented but also analysed and translated into actionable interventions.

In Indonesia, the MDSR framework has been adopted through national guidelines, which outline roles and responsibilities across the community, primary care, and health facility levels, including the use of verbal autopsy and routine reporting systems [10]. Overall, MDSR serves as a key strategy for strengthening health system accountability and improving maternal health outcomes.

Further, implementation outcomes are defined as “the effects of deliberate and purposive actions to implement new treatments, practices, and policies” [21]. They are conceptually distinct from traditional public health outcomes (e.g., health status or impact), as they focus specifically on evaluating the success of implementation processes.

As outlined in previous research [21], these outcomes serve as (1) indicators of implementation success, (2) proximal indicators of implementation processes, and (3) intermediate outcomes linking implementation efforts to service and clinical outcomes. In this study, the eight implementation outcome variables are used as analytical constructs (i.e., indicators within the assessment framework) to assess how well the MDSR policy framework is implemented across its five stages in practice (as illustrated in Fig. 1).

Methods

Study setting

The study was carried out in Aceh Province, which comprises 23 districts, 290 subdistricts, and 6,517 villages, with a population of 5.482 million people [22]. This study focused on Lhoksukon Subdistrict in North Aceh District of Aceh Province (as depicted in Fig. 2). Several considerations informed the selection of North Aceh District as the study area. First, although maternal deaths declined from 28 cases in 2021 to 12 cases in 2023, North Aceh continues to report the second-highest number of maternal deaths in Aceh Province and the highest number of live births (11,819), indicating a substantial maternal health burden [23]. Second, Lhoksukon Subdistrict, the district’s administrative centre, recorded the highest number of maternal deaths (nine cases between 2021 and 2024) among the 26 subdistricts, making it a critical area for in-depth investigation of MDSR implementation [24]. Third, despite relatively adequate availability of health facilities and human resources, including two PKMs, multiple health clinics, and Posyandu services, maternal deaths persist in this area. This contrast provides an important opportunity to examine potential gaps and relevant issues in the implementation of maternal health interventions [25]. Fourth, the Lhoksukon Subdistrict has implemented the MDSR program since 2022, enabling a detailed assessment of its implementation processes, challenges, and barriers within a real-world policy context [26]. Finally, access to institutional data and key stakeholders was facilitated through the lead researcher’s professional networks and prior collaborations with relevant institutions, including PKMs, the District Planning Agency, the Secretariat, and the North Aceh Regent’s Office [25].

Study population and recruitment

The research adopted a qualitative methodology, employing various data collection methods, such as in-depth interviews and document analysis, to explore the implementation of MDSR framework by the North Aceh government and its associated agencies and stakeholders. The analysis focused on each stage of the framework cycle.

Epistemologically, this study was informed by an interpretivist stance, which perceives knowledge as a social construct shaped by the experiences and contextual realities of participants. This approach is especially appropriate for investigating how different stakeholders interpret and engage with MDSR implementation within complex local health system settings [28, 29].

A total of sixty (60) participants, including policymakers, practitioners, and community representatives, were purposively selected and interviewed in this study. Specific inclusion criteria were established to recruit information-rich participants with relevant knowledge and experience of MDSR implementation. Participant recruitment and data collection continued until saturation was reached, with no substantial new themes or insights emerged from subsequent interviews, resulting in a final sample of 60 participants.

Participants were required to be at least 18 years old or married and were deliberately selected from multiple agencies operating at provincial, district, subdistrict, and village levels. They were chosen based on their roles and level of understanding in providing relevant information on each stage of the framework’s implementation. Additionally, these participants were expected to provide insights into the challenges and other contextual issues encountered in the target areas (North Aceh District, Lhoksukon Subdistrict, and selected villages) during the implementation of the framework. Furthermore, they could propose potential strategies and recommendations for improving MDSR implementation in the North Aceh District and across Aceh Province.

The participants were further categorised based on their institutional roles and responsibilities in MDSR implementation. These included: (1) policymakers and steering committee members at provincial and North Aceh District levels responsible for developing and enforcing MDSR-related regulations; (2) stakeholders involved in monitoring and evaluating maternal death reduction interventions; (3) individuals and organisations involved in capacity-building activities for healthcare providers and community actors at PKMs and village levels; (4) facility-level actors and agencies responsible for implementing recommended actions to prevent maternal deaths; (5) members of review committees and relevant institutions tasked with auditing maternal deaths and formulating recommendations for health facilities; (6) health officers at PKMs and district health facilities responsible for notifying and reporting maternal deaths to the DHO of North Aceh and the PHO; (7) frontline health personnel engaged in identifying maternal deaths in communities and health facilities, conducting maternal verbal autopsies (MVAs), and maintaining death records; (8) community representatives from two selected villages, who provided insights into local challenges, issues, and opportunities for improving MDSR implementation at the community level.

Snowball sampling was used selectively to identify additional participants based on recommendations from initial interviewees, particularly to capture more perspectives from relevant stakeholders not initially identified through purposive sampling. Detailed information on participant categories and their distribution across administrative levels, is provided in Table S1.

Semi-structured interview guides were developed based on the study aim and research questions, translated into Indonesian, and pilot-tested through an in-depth interview with a postgraduate public health student (the lead researcher’s student) in Banda Aceh. The pilot test assessed the clarity, relevance, and flow of the questions, resulting in minor revisions. The guides covered eight implementation outcome variables - acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, compliance, implementation cost, coverage, and sustainability - and were aligned with the five stages of the MDSR framework. The interview guide is provided in Table S2.

Data collection

Data collection took place from September to December 2024. All interviews were conducted face-to-face by the lead researcher. The process began with an in-depth interview with a key representative from PHO, which facilitated access to other stakeholders. A formal letter of permission from the Provincial Secretary (SEKDA) was obtained to enable interviews with all identified organisations and participants. Each interview lasted approximately 20–25 min and was recorded digitally.

After conducting thorough interviews, various documents related to policies and programs aimed at reducing maternal deaths were reviewed. These included North Aceh Qanun No. 1/2019 Concerning Maternal, Newborn and Child Health, the ໿North Aceh Regent Regulation No. 45/2021 Concerning Stunting Reduction, North Aceh Qanun No. 2/2021 Concerning the Medium-Term Development Plan, North Aceh Qanun No. 20/2023 Concerning the District Poverty Alleviation Plan 2023–2026, and the Joint Strategic Plans of Aceh Province and the Provincial Health Office (2023–2026) [14, 15, 26, 30–33]. The data collection was carried out in alignment with activities supported at both the provincial and North Aceh District levels. The specific aim was to gather information pertinent to the variables influencing the implementation outcomes of each of the five stages of the MDSR framework from the province, district, subdistrict, and community health centers (2 PKMs) located in two different villages (as shown in Fig. 2).

Data analysis process

Following each interview, audio recordings were transcribed in Indonesian by a member of the research team (MJ) and saved as Word documents. The transcripts were subsequently translated into English by a professional translator. To ensure accuracy and reliability, the translated transcripts were reviewed and cross-checked by the lead researcher and an additional qualified English translator for consistency and appropriateness.

All transcripts, field notes, and key documents were then organised and imported into NVivo 12 software (QSR International Pty Ltd) for analysis. A thematic analysis approach was applied to identify patterns and relationships across the data [34]. Although the analysis was guided by predefined outcome variables, the coding process remained iterative, allowing for the identification of patterns and insights emerging from the data [35].

Nodes were created in NVivo and organised into preliminary/initial codes that encompassed text excerpts from respondents [35]. These preliminary codes were linked to sub-themes representing the five stages of the MDSR framework before being classified into challenges and opportunities for implementing the framework (under the specified codes). The specified codes were examined and re-examined repeatedly, compared, and connected to the determined/predefined themes (the eight implementation outcome variables) to comprehend their similarities and differences [36]. The comprehensive process of data analysis for this research is outlined in Table S3. Each outcome variable has been clearly defined and is presented below in Table S3. Additionally, this research followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) to write these research reports [36].

To ensure trustworthiness, several approaches were utilised. Credibility was improved through triangulation of in-depth interviews and document reviews. Dependability was strengthened through maintaining a clear audit trail of coding and analytical decisions. Confirmability was ensured through systematic coding and the use of NVivo software. Regular discussions with the research team enhanced data validations and interpretations [35].

The analysis was further guided by implementation science frameworks, including the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), to support interpretation of multi-level implementation factors that influence the implementation outcome variables [37

Ethical Approval, Consent and Data Safeguarding

This research received ethical clearance from the Aceh Health Polytechnics Human Research Ethics Committee (No. 3367/GZKEP/2024). It was conducted in compliance with the ethical principles established in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The lead researcher explained the study procedures, including data collection processes, to all participants in the Indonesian language, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

Field notes were taken during and immediately after each interview to capture contextual observations and preliminary reflections, and were used to complement the interpretation of interview data. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent and transcribed verbatim for analysis.

To ensure data security, all recordings, transcripts, and field notes were anonymised and stored in password-protected electronic files accessible only to the research team. No identifiable personal information was retained in the stored data.

As a gesture of appreciation for participants’ involvement in this study, they received a small gift valued at approximately US $3–4.

Results

This section presents the study findings based on the assessment of implementation outcome variables related to the current MDSR implementation in Aceh Province. The findings are presented through determined themes (outcome variables) to describe the current implementation of MDSR framework, including barriers, opportunities, and other pertinent issues to reduce maternal mortality in the North Aceh District. Specifically, this section addresses the first research question: ‘What is the current status of MDSR implementation across each stage of the framework in North Aceh District?’ and the second research question: ‘What are the key barriers and enabling factors influencing the implementation of each stage?’.

Theme 1: acceptability

Analysis of 42 of the 60 participants indicated widespread acceptance of the 2016 MDSR framework among local authorities and maternal and child health actors at provincial, North Aceh District, and subdistrict levels. Participants recognised the framework’s role in reducing preventable maternal and neonatal deaths and emphasised that both institutional commitments and national policy support its implementation. The district has also issued several local regulations related to maternal death reduction efforts, such as North Aceh Qanun No. 1/2019 concerning maternal, newborn, and child Health [32]. Furthermore, reducing maternal deaths in the North Aceh District has become one of DHO’s primary targets from 2023 to 2026 [38].

However, not all actors in North Aceh District, especially communities at the grassroots level, accept the MDSR to reduce maternal deaths. Information gathered from community representatives indicated that limited acceptance of the MDSR framework at the community level is due to inadequate public communication about aims, urgency, benefits, and the essential role of communities within the first stage of the framework. Consequently, awareness of maternal mortality levels and their underlying causes remained limited in North Aceh District, including selected villages in Lhoksukon Subdistrict.

A respected villager acknowledged:“We do not understand what the MDSR framework is…we never heard it before. Most villagers, including pregnant women, husbands, and their families, are not aware of the framework. Thus, these villagers do not understand how they are involved in the MDSR implementation” (Interviewees #52 and 54, Villages).

“We do not understand what the MDSR framework is…we never heard it before. Most villagers, including pregnant women, husbands, and their families, are not aware of the framework. Thus, these villagers do not understand how they are involved in the MDSR implementation” (Interviewees #52 and 54, Villages).

As a result, a village midwife noted that many people in villages are reluctant to disclose information about maternal deaths of their family members, making it difficult to obtain accurate information during maternal verbal autopsies (MVAs) in the first stage of the MDSR framework.

Theme 2: appropriateness

Many interviewees from the Provincial Health Office (PHO), District Health Office (DHO), and District Planning Agency (locally known as Badan Perencanaan Pembangunan Daerah (BAPPEDA)) in the North Aceh District confessed that MDSR implementation is highly appropriate for accelerating maternal death reduction efforts. This is because the MDSR adopts a comprehensive approach through its five-stage cycle, which addresses key limitations of the previous Maternal Death Audit (MDA) policy (as mentioned earlier). As one provincial-level respondent noted, “Previously, the underlying causes of maternal deaths – both in health facilities and in the community – were not clearly identified” (Interviewee #12, Province). Likewise, a district-level respondent stated comparable limitations in earlier practice (Interviewee #31, District).

Key officers from the PHO also considered MDSR implementation appropriate because reliable data on identification and notification of maternal deaths remain limited in Aceh Province, including North Aceh District. For example, as explained by a senior employee from the provincial level, “around 70% of maternal deaths are recorded in general and private hospitals in North Aceh, but the patients often come from different districts, making it difficult to trace their original addresses” (Interviewee #11, Province). As a result, “their antenatal and postnatal care histories cannot be confirmed, as these are not recorded in the Community Health Centers/PKMs in the North Aceh District” (Interviewees #17, Province).

Therefore, village midwives and health cadres are required to record all pregnant and postpartum women, including their antenatal and postnatal examination histories, in the Village Maternal Cohort Registration form before submission to PKMs. In addition, electronically based village maternal cohort registration is more effective than paper-based registration currently used in villages, helping avoid missing or overlapping data and allowing pregnant women to receive regular pregnancy examinations anywhere in the province.

Likewise, interviews with 32 respondents in Lhoksukon Subdistrict revealed that indirect causes associated with some delays contribute to maternal deaths in many villages, especially in the desolate areas of Lhoksukon Subdistrict. Senior officers from Puskesmas reported that relatives or husbands only look for medical assistance only after their wives have developed abnormal conditions, such as bleeding, high blood pressure, or foot edemas.

Additionally, subdistrict-level respondents elaborated, “family members or husbands do not recognise labour signs, particularly in first pregnancies, and only seek help when complications such as bleeding occur” (Interviewees #22 and #29, Buket Hagu and Kp Baru villages). Such delays increase the risk of obstetric complications and maternal deaths. Additional delays arise from inadequate transportation in remote locations. As health workers explained, “pregnant women in far-flung areas often experience long delays in reaching referral hospitals due to limited transport facilities” (Interviewees # 48 and #51, PKMs).

Theme 3: feasibility

Participants generally viewed MDSR implementation in North Aceh District as feasible. The district head (the regent) has demonstrated high assurance, supported by existing provincial and district policies. Implementing the MDSR framework could strengthen current provincial and district maternal health policies and programs. Specifically, the goal is to reduce maternal deaths to 175 per 100,000 LBs by 2026, as stated in the Joint Strategic Plans of Aceh Province and the Provincial Health Office (2023–2026) [33].

The active engagement of key actors in maternal death surveillance activities further supported the feasibility of MDSR policy implementation in North Aceh District. For example, a key officer from the district level testified, “many health officers, including midwife coordinators, village midwives, and nurses at Puskesmas, actively participated in workshops and coordination meetings to introduce and operationalise the MDSR framework at the subdistrict level, particularly in Lhoksukon” (Interviewee #19, District). This engagement demonstrated both institutional support and frontline health workers’ readiness to adopt and implement the framework.

Theme 4. adoption, coverage and compliance

Currently, the MDSR framework for reducing maternal deaths in the North Aceh District has not been comprehensively adopted throughout all five stages of the framework, although the Aceh government has committed to implementing the MDSR to reduce maternal deaths by establishing various local regulations in the province, North Aceh District (regent regulations), PHO, and DHO in North Aceh (Interviewee #12, Province). According to a senior lecturer at the University in North Aceh, the actors involved (especially health facilities, such as provincial and district hospitals) in the MDSR framework currently focus only on activities in the first stage of the framework (i.e., identifying maternal deaths and their causes in health facilities).

For example, based on the results of the 2020 joint survey between the Provincial Planning Agency (BAPPEDA) and PHO, the three major delays contributed to indirect causes of 45 maternal deaths within the 9 surveyed districts in Aceh. A total of 97.8% of the deaths were delayed seeking health care, 95.6% delayed reaching referral health care facilities, and 91.1% delayed receiving adequate care (as shown in Table 1).

A key officer from the district-level argued, “although the three major delays contributing to maternal deaths have been clearly identified, there have been no follow-up actions in the subsequent stages, particularly in translating these findings into recommendations for health facilities (Interviewee #23, District).

Therefore, individuals involved in the second, third, fourth, and fifth stages of MDSR (e.g., midwife coordinators in Puskesmas, medical teams in district hospitals, and review and MONEV teams in the DHO) must perform their tasks accountably. To support this effort, the North Aceh regent (as the top district leader) and DHO chief should ensure that all actors involved in MDSR promptly fulfil their duties. Rewards and punishments should also be established to encourage compliance with MDSR implementation.

Another example of focusing solely on the first stage of the MDSR framework was observed in healthcare facilities based on document reviews at PHO. For instance, a combination of direct and indirect causes of maternal deaths (related to medical conditions) was identified in some referral hospitals in Aceh Province from 2020 to 2023. Of the 148 maternal deaths reported in hospitals, 49 cases (33%) involved bleeding, 18 cases (12%) involved eclampsia, and 4 cases (3%) and 3 cases (2%) involved cardiovascular diseases and infections, respectively [23].

Regarding MDSR coverage, key officials from North Aceh District, subdistrict, and two Community Health Centers (PKMs) mentioned:“preventive health services are provided to reproductive-age girls, pregnant women, and children through integrated health programs/Posyandu at PKMs, including nutrition support, supplements, and immunisation services” (Interviewees #23 and #25, District).

“preventive health services are provided to reproductive-age girls, pregnant women, and children through integrated health programs/Posyandu at PKMs, including nutrition support, supplements, and immunisation services” (Interviewees #23 and #25, District).

Nevertheless, there is limited knowledge and skills among Posyandu and Family Planning cadres in villages when it comes to identifying obstetrically associated issues with pregnancy, notifying village midwives, and conducting family planning counselling for postpartum women (Interviewee #47, Subdistrict; Interviewee #55, Buket Hagu village). Therefore, the chief of North Aceh DHO and the chiefs of Buket Hagu and Lhoksukon Community Health Centers need to improve the capacity of village cadres through regular maternal health training.

Regarding compliance with MDSR implementation, information derived from in-depth interviews with various stakeholders (43 respondents) at the provincial and district levels concluded that many barriers influencing compliance with MDSR implementation were identified within both communities and the health system. Senior employees from the subdistrict and Puskesmas admitted, “many villagers and pregnant women consider pregnancy a normal condition and therefore do not see regular check-ups as necessary or beneficial” (Interviewee #42, Subdistrict). In addition, another key respondent added, “family members are usually reluctant to provide clear information about maternal deaths during verbal autopsies because they are unfamiliar with the MDSR process.” (Interviewee #48, PKM Buket Hagu).

The other key respondents from the subdistrict also confessed, “many pregnant and postpartum women, especially those with obstetric complications, are not recorded in village maternal cohort registers due to limited skills among village health cadres and midwives” (Interviewees #52 and #54, Subdistrict). In addition, senior health officers from Puskesmas asserted, “limited capacity among village midwives to identify pregnancy-related health complications frequently delayed referrals of mothers to district hospitals” (Interviewees #55 and #57, PKM Lhoksukon).

Hence, a senior maternal and child health observer suggested, “policymakers and leaders at both provincial and district levels, including the regent and the DHO chief, need to strengthen public communication and socialisation of the MDSR framework to all stakeholders, particularly communities in village areas” (Interviewee #13, Province). Likewise, enhancing the capacity of health cadres and village midwives is crucial for strengthening MDSR implementation, especially in the first stage, which influences the effectiveness of subsequent stages of the framework (Interviewee #17, District).

Theme 6. sustainability

Although MDSR implementation is mandated as a national policy, its sustainability in Aceh depends largely on strong leadership of leaders and policymakers at the provincial, North Aceh District, Lhoksukon Subdistrict, Puskesmas, and village levels to ensure that all relevant actors implement the MDSR framework comprehensively throughout its five stages.

A senior media officer criticised, “the sustainability of MDSR implementation in North Aceh District, including Lhoksukon Subdistrict, remains questionable because so far, the framework has not been implemented comprehensively across all five stages. Activities have largely focused on the first stage, while reporting and review processes (second and third stages) are still incomplete. Recommendations for corrective actions are rarely adopted, despite the MDSR framework having been introduced in Aceh ten years ago” (Interviewee #9, Province).

Another challenge to MDSR sustainability in North Aceh is frequent staff rotation. A key district-level respondent described, “staff rotation occurs regularly across government institutions in Aceh, reducing the availability of skilled personnel and delaying the implementation of MDSR due to the loss of experienced staff” (Interviewee #24, District). Similarly, a senior officer from Lhoksukon Subdistrict explained that several skilled village midwives had been transferred to the District Health Office, requiring newly assigned midwives to undergo additional training.

Compiling and analysing information from Tables 3 and 4, and 5, this study revealed that the implementation outcomes vary across MDSR stages and are shaped by multi-level determinants within CFIR domains. It is important to note that implementation outcomes are conceptually distinct from traditional program outcomes, as they assess the effectiveness of implementation processes rather than health impacts (as elaborated previously).

This section discusses the study’s findings on key barriers and offers policy-relevant recommendations for PHO, DHO, Puskesmas, and other key stakeholders to improve MDSR implementation in Aceh Province, including the North Aceh District.

The key barriers were: (1) MDSR implementation remains concentrated in Stage 1 (identification); (2) inadequate public communication reduces acceptability and community awareness; (3) incomplete implementation across all MDSR stages; (4) limited political commitment among local leaders; and (5) frequent health staff turnover. Specifically, discussing these barriers addresses the third question of this study: ‘What strategies and recommendations can be proposed to improve the implementation of the framework and reduce maternal mortality in North Aceh District?’

The study found that MDSR implementation in Aceh, including North Aceh District, was largely confined to the first stage of the framework, limiting full-cycle MDSR implementation (as described in Table 3). Hence, provincial and district authorities, including officers from PHO and DHO in North Aceh District, should support implementation across all five MDSR stages to improve maternal mortality reduction efforts.

Analysis of the eight implementation outcome variables indicated that while the MDSR framework is highly accepted by policymakers, its level of acceptability drops significantly at the lower level, primarily due to insufficient public communication and engagement with relevant stakeholders at the grassroots level, among other factors.

In this sense, the Aceh government should learn from the success of conducting effective public communication to improve acceptability, adoption, coverage, and compliance with various health topics among local communities and achieve positive health outcomes in some developed and developing countries. For example, in African nations, community health workers, in collaboration with a district health agency, are pivotal in disseminating public health information, communication, and services directly to families. This approach has proven to be especially effective in enhancing maternal and child health outcomes by offering education on prenatal care, vaccinations, and nutrition [41]. The confidence and familiarity that community health workers build within their communities bolster the credibility, acceptance, and adherence to the health messages [42].

Furthermore, public communication related to smoking cessation programs has effectively increased the acceptability, adoption, and adherence to these programs in various cities in Brazil. Thus, the National Cancer Institute of Brazil reported that the prevalence of smoking dropped from 34.8% in 1989 to 10.1% in 2019 [43]. Also, intensive public communication about the importance of child immunisation in preventing infectious diseases has increased community awareness and compliance with child immunisation participation among local people in Nigeria. As a result, polio cases in the country fell to zero in 2020 [41]. In the UK, public health communication on reducing alcohol consumption has increased public awareness (acceptability, adoption and compliance) to reduce alcohol consumption in the country. Thus, binge drinking decreased from 20% in 2005 to 15% in 2017 [44].

An essential element of effective public health communication dissemination is selecting suitable channels or tailoring media to specific audiences based on demographic factors (e.g., age, gender, beliefs, and income level) [41, 45]. These media choices can greatly improve the success of the dissemination effort. For example, a public campaign designed to boost vaccination rates among young adults might utilise social media platforms, as this age group is particularly more engaged with these platforms. Conversely, a public communication initiative targeting older adults or those residing in remote areas with strong religious beliefs and local traditions may depend more on traditional media or on trusted local figures. Studies have indicated that utilising the proper media to convey public information is more likely to resonate with the target audience, resulting in increased engagement and changes in behaviour [46].

In summary, an effective public health communication approach should include the following elements. First, appropriate channels that effectively reach the targeted audience should be developed. Second, local customs, religious views, and languages across various cultures and environments should be acknowledged. Third, health literacy, access to the internet, and exposure to the media of audiences should be considered. Fourth, resources such as television segments, brochures, radio ads, newspapers, and other public service announcements should be created [47].

Therefore, PHO and DHO of North Aceh District should lead intensive public communication and dissemination of MDSR objectives, processes, and benefits to all relevant stakeholders, such as communities, frontline health workers, cadres, and village midwives. Given that Aceh Province has the most populous Muslims in Indonesia and is known for its adherence to Islamic sharia regulations, communication strategies should integrate public health messages with Islamic values through collaboration with Islamic figures (e.g., imams), respected figures (e.g., village heads and traditional customary leaders), and local governments. Such an approach could improve community understanding, acceptance, and reporting of maternal deaths, thereby strengthening identification, notification, and verbal autopsy processes. Enhanced communication among frontline health officers may also improve adoption, coverage, and compliance with MDSR implementation [48].

The other finding was that a comprehensive implementation of MDSR enables the identification of both direct and indirect causes of maternal deaths. In North Aceh, indirect causes included delays in recognising labour complications, delays in seeking referral care, and prolonged travel to referral hospitals due to distance and limited transportation.

However, the comprehensive MDSR implementation needs to state the roles and duties of all involved actors and integrate them into local short-term and long-term health program plans. This integration should improve confidential enquiries into maternal death (CEMD) by maintaining confidential (name-free) and nonpunitive (blame-free) audit principles in health facilities and communities. Importantly, strong political will and leadership of local leaders are essential to ensure implementation across all five stages of the framework [5, 49, 50]. Similarly, the importance of strong political commitment, supported by effective coordination between relevant actors, including government and nongovernment agencies, and good community involvement, has been demonstrated successfully in reducing the prevalence of child stunting in many developing countries such as Asia, Latin America, and Africa [51]. Therefore, the North Aceh regent, local secretariat (as the district’s senior administrative officer), and leaders of PHO and DHO should demonstrate strong leadership and support the full implementation of the MDSR framework. This recommendation is achievable in Aceh Province because the central government has already established the national CEMD and MDSR framework, clearly defining the roles of involved actors within the framework.

Nevertheless, evidence suggests that comprehensive MDSR implementation is typically phased (requiring time and effort) rather than achieved simultaneously [52]. For example, a regional meeting on MDSR implementation progress in Johannesburg, South Africa, in 2014 revealed that five countries in the region currently have national CEMD (South Africa, Lesotho, Botswana, Malawi, and Namibia). Other countries, like Zimbabwe and Swaziland, have formulated maternal death committees that make reports every year or every three years. Furthermore, Tanzania, Namibia, Zimbabwe, Malawi, and Eritrea have all incorporated MDSR into their integrated disease surveillance and response plans, reporting all maternal deaths within 48 h after the incident [5].

Hence, successful comprehensive MDSR implementation requires avoiding punitive approaches, particularly during identification, reporting, and review stages. Instead, a “think big, start small, and scale progressively” approach supported by strong leadership is more likely to achieve sustainable implementation [49, 52, 53].

This approach is applicable and achievable in Aceh Province, including the North Aceh District, where the local government has already started implementing the MDSR framework, albeit partially in certain stages. Continuous collaboration, coordination, and firm leadership actions from provincial to village levels are needed to achieve comprehensive and sustainable MDSR implementation in Aceh.

Therefore, the Aceh government should learn from the success of the Tanzanian government in reducing maternal deaths by 80% in seven years across the country. This success was attributed to strong leadership, political commitment, and effective coordination from central to village levels [54]. Tanzanian government officers ensured synergy between health priorities, budget allocation efficiency, and the method of financial disbursement to assist service provision at all levels.

The other findings indicated that staff rotation can threaten the sustainability of MDSR implementation due to the lack of skilled employees involved in the MDSR in the North Aceh District. This issue is also prevalent in many local government areas across Indonesia. Previous research indicated that regular personnel turnover across local governments, especially in provincial and district health offices in Indonesia, results in poor staff performance [9]. This suggests that the new employees who replace rotational employees often lack the knowledge and abilities necessary for their new assigned jobs, especially for maternal death surveillance and response (MDSR) programs.

Similar challenges have been reported in Ethiopia and Malawi, where high staff turnover and inadequate knowledge transfer left newly appointed personnel unfamiliar with MDSR procedures and documentation requirements [55–57].

In summary, frequent staff rotations at government agencies, including PHO and DHO in the North Aceh District and community health centers, result in a lack of expertise in public health and MDSR fields, poor leadership, and hinder MDSR program sustainability [45]. Therefore, Aceh government officers and relevant actors need to provide resources for regular education, training, and proper handover of responsibilities for newly assigned staff to maintain the sustainability of MDSR implementation programs in Aceh Province.

Although this study is context-specific to North Aceh District, the findings have relevance for other low- and middle-income countries with decentralised health systems facing similar challenges [28, 35]. The identified barriers – such as partial MDSR implementation, weak community engagement, limited data integration, and workforce instability – are not unique to Aceh but are widely reported in comparable areas. The study therefore contributes evidence on factors influencing the implementation of MDSR framework and highlights the importance of strong leadership, political will, community involvement, and system-wide coordination for enhancing maternal death surveillance and response systems in resource-constrained areas. In conclusion, the detailed contextual description provided in this study supports readers in assessing the transferability of findings to comparable settings.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides one of the first in-depth qualitative assessments of MDSR implementation in Aceh by examining implementation outcome domains across all five stages of the framework. A key strength lies in the use of multiple data sources, including in-depth interviews and document reviews, which enabled data triangulation and enhanced the credibility of the findings. Furthermore, the inclusion of participants from diverse roles and administrative levels (provincial, district, subdistrict, and village) allowed for a comprehensive understanding of multi-level implementation processes and challenges.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the findings reflect participants’ perspectives and experiences regarding the implementation of the MDSR framework in North Aceh and should therefore be interpreted within their specific organisational and contextual settings. To enhance the trustworthiness of these findings, several strategies were used, including triangulation of in-depth interviews and document reviews, maintenance of a clear audit trail of coding and analytical decisions, systematic coding using NVivo software, and regular discussions with the research team to strengthen data validation and interpretation. Second, as the data were translated during the analysis process, some linguistic and cultural nuances may not have been fully captured. Finally, certain information obtained through in-depth interviews could not be corroborated using documentary evidence because it was not consistently recorded or adequately documented in formal government records. However, as with all qualitative research, alternative perspectives and interpretations may exist beyond those captured in this study.

Conclusions

The implementation of MDSR framework in North Aceh District remains incomplete, with activities largely concentrated in the initial stage of maternal death identification and limited progression to subsequent stages, including review, response, and monitoring and evaluation. Although the framework is widely accepted among policymakers and considered appropriate for the local context, its implementation is constrained by inadequate public communication, limited community engagement, insufficient frontline workforce capacity, weak accountability mechanisms, and frequent staff rotation.

These findings demonstrate that MDSR implementation is a complex, multi-level process requiring strong leadership, effective coordination, and sustained commitment across all administrative levels. Strengthening public communication and community engagement is critical for improving data quality and participation in the early stages of MDSR, while enhancing workforce capacity, accountability, and trained staff continuity is essential for full-cycle implementation.

Although multiple factors interact to shape MDSR implementation, our findings suggest prioritising strong leadership and governance together with investment in workforce capacity is likely to yield the greatest systemic gains. Effective leadership can strengthen coordination, accountability, and resource mobilisation across administrative levels, while a trained and stable workforce can operationalise improvements in surveillance, review processes, data quality, and continuity of implementation.

This study provides important implications for policy and practice by informing government stakeholders and health system actors in Aceh on priority areas for strengthening maternal death surveillance and response systems. The findings can inform targeted strategies to improve implementation of the 2021 WHO MDSR Framework and the 2016 Indonesian Ministry of Health (MoH) MDSR Guidelines, thereby enhancing surveillance and response systems for reducing maternal mortality. These insights may also be relevant to other districts in Aceh Province and similar decentralised health system contexts.

Importantly, this study contributes to implementation science by demonstrating how implementation outcomes vary across different stages of MDSR and are shaped by multi-level determinants, as conceptualised within the CFIR framework. While the findings are context-specific, they offer transferable lessons for other low- and middle-income settings facing similar implementation challenges.

Overall, comprehensive MDSR implementation has the potential to support health system responsiveness and ultimately contribute to the reduction and prevention of maternal mortality in Aceh and other similar locations.
