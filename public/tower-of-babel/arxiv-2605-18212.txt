Short-Range Tests of the Gravitational Inverse-Square Law
Jiro Murata, Takuhiro Fujiie, Sae Suzuki

Abstract
Experimental constraints on the gravitational inverse-square law at short range are presented, employing a consistent formalism across a wide range of length scales. We provide comprehensive updates from the past decade, building upon our previous review. This work facilitates the direct comparison of experimental results with theoretical models that extend general relativity. Furthermore, a comparison between various model parametrizations, including extra-dimensional models, is introduced. Finally, results from tabletop experiments are compared with those from high-energy collider experiments for both Yukawa and power-law potentials.



keywords
short-range gravity, extra-dimensions, inverse-square law

1
Introduction

Testing gravity is testing spacetime.
Newton’s gravitational inverse-square law stands as the founding monument of modern science; it remains valid for most phenomena in our universe, except in extreme conditions where modifications from general relativity are necessary.
The inverse-square law has been tested with great precision, to be sure, but such high-precision tests have been performed only on planetary scales.
On the other hand, precision in short-range tests at sub-laboratory scales remains poor.
This paper provides an updated review of testing the gravitational inverse-square law at short range, following our previous review from a decade ago
Murata_2015
.

Since the end of the last century, searching for deviations from the inverse-square law at microscales has attracted significant interest, especially in light of the possible hypothesis of extra spatial dimensions beyond our four-dimensional spacetime.
Before then, several claims, such as the fifth-force hypothesis, also prompted numerous experimental searches for violations of the inverse-square law from the 1970s to today.
A wide variety of experimental reports can be found in such attempts, from the geographic scale down to the quark scale.
It is not always straightforward to compare their results with theoretical models, as each experiment reports its results using different parametrizations.
In this paper, we aim to summarize the wide variety of experimental results within the same parameter spaces and using the same criteria.

Observables and Parametrization

If an experiment measured a gravitational potential
V(r)
or a force
F(r)
between two point masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
at a separation distance
r
, then it is compared with the Newtonian gravitational law
V_{\rm N}
or
F_{\rm N}
,

\displaystyle V_{\rm N}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-G_{\rm N}\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{r},

\displaystyle F_{\rm N}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{\rm N}\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}

(1)

using Newton’s gravitational constant
G_{\rm N}
, with the following parameters introducing in this work as

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{V(r)}{V_{\rm N}(r)},\;\Delta\equiv\Gamma-1,

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{F(r)}{F_{\rm N}(r)},\;\delta\equiv\gamma-1.

(2)

If
\Delta
or
\delta\neq 0
is found, it means that the Newtonian gravitational law is violated at the experimental distance
r
.
In the following, basically we use
\Delta
for the theoretical part and
\delta
for the experimental part.

\Delta
and
\delta
are similar, but slightly different, as

\delta=\Delta-r\cdot d\Delta/dr
.
So this second term needs to be included for the analysis of experimental data for force measurements.

Experiments will test whether

Actual experiments cannot use ideal point masses; therefore, the net force must be estimated as volume integrals.
Because a single value of
\delta(r)
can represent only point mass measurements, most experiments report their results using the theoretical model parameters instead of using
\delta(r)
.
However, this is why interpretations become confusing when different models are used.

Note that there is no theoretically known value of
G_{\rm N}
.
Its determination was first made possible by Cavendish in 1798 for the first time by directly measuring

with known

, and
r=r_{\rm N}\sim 1{\rm m}
values, using a torsion balance bar on a meter-scale

To this day,
G_{\rm N}=6.708\,83(15)\times 10^{-39}\,\hbar c\,({\rm GeV}/c^{2})^{-2}
is measured in the same Cavendish-type experiments at the meter scale, improving its relative precision to
\sim 10^{-5}

\displaystyle V_{\rm power}(r)

\displaystyle:

.
Here,

The inverse-square law is based on the assumption that our world is an isotropic three-dimensional space, as implied by Gauss’s law.
On the other hand, if our space contains isotropic
n
-extra dimensions, the potential shape must be modified as
1/r\rightarrow 1/r^{1+n}
.
Equation (
3
) represents such extra-dimension models.

The Yukawa potential (
4

\lambda=\frac{h}{m_{\rm b}c}
, where
m_{\rm b}
is the mass of the exchanging boson.
This new interaction needs to couple to the masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
in the same way as gravity does.
Or, other quantities

, such as baryon number

If we do not distinguish between this charge

As we see here, testing the inverse-square law is closely related to the search for extra dimensions, new bosons, and violations of the equivalence principle.
High-energy collider experiments align with the power-law parametrization, whereas other short-range laboratory experiments usually prefer using the Yukawa

However, it should be quite simple to compare their results with each other if all of them can be expressed in

as model-independent parameters.
In this work, we relate the power-law parameters

\lambda

.
We provide updated constraints on these parameter spaces by incorporating recent short-range experimental data.
It should be noted that astrophysical constraints are beyond the scope of this study.

3
Interpretation on Extra Dimension

Most experimental attempts over this quarter century have been motivated by theoretical predictions of violations of the gravitational inverse-square law in the brane-world scenario.
So it should be helpful to discuss the parametrizations in the context of extra-dimension models.
Among them, the large extra-dimension model (the Arkani-Hamed–Dimopoulos–Dvali, or ADD model)
Arkani.Hamed1998263
and the warped extra-dimension model (the Randall–Sundrum, or RS model)
PhysRevLett.83.3370
;
PhysRevLett.83.4690
are the two notable predictions.
Indeed, both the ADD and RS-II models predict power-law-type modifications at short distances.
Historically, the idea of a compactified extra dimension was proposed in the Kaluza–Klein (KK) theory a century ago
Klein2916
.
Since then, the compactification scale has been assumed to be the Planck length,

L_{\rm Pl}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar G_{\rm N}}{c^{3}}},

(5)

which must be too small to be explored experimentally.

However, we should note that the gravitational constant used to estimate the Planck length is
G_{\rm N}
, which is measured at a laboratory scale
r_{\rm N}
and is assumed to have the same value at
L_{\rm Pl}
.
The revolutionary idea of the ADD model is that the gravitational constant should vary with the length scale.
As
G
increases, the Planck energy decreases and can reach the TeV scale, around which gravity and other Standard Model interactions are expected to have comparable energy scales.

It is useful to interpret the power-law parametrization (
3
) and the Yukawa parametrization (
4
) based on the extra-dimension theory.
Here, we will discuss based on the ADD model, which posits the existence of an additional compactified space of size
\lambda
, which is large compared to
L_{\rm Pl}
.
The scale parameter
\lambda
is interpreted as the circumference of a torus-compactified extra dimension in this work.
If all the extra-dimensions are isotropic and have the same size of
\lambda
, the gravitational potential obeys the power law
1/r^{1+n}
at
r\ll\lambda
, where the Newtonian inverse-square law still holds at
r\gg\lambda
, as expressed in Eq. (
3
).
Then modified gravitational potential
V_{\rm ADD}
at short scale is expressed using higher-dimensional gravitational constant
G_{4+n}
as

\displaystyle V_{\rm ADD}(r)

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-G_{4+n}\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{r^{1+n}}=-G_{\rm N}\frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{r}\left(\frac{\lambda}{r}\right)^{n},

which leads

\displaystyle G_{4+n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{\rm N}\lambda^{n}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;\;\;M_{\rm Pl}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M_{\rm D}^{2+n}\lambda^{n}(c/\hbar)^{n},

(7)

here

M_{\rm Pl}
and
M_{\rm D}
are the Planck masses corresponding to
G_{\rm N}
and
G_{4+n}
.
The strong power
1/r^{1+n}
leads to much stronger gravity at
r\ll\lambda
than
V_{\rm N}
.
The original motivation of the ADD model is that the higher-dimensional Planck mass
M_{\rm D}
becomes significantly smaller than
M_{\rm Pl}
, which can be at the TeV scale, resolving the hierarchy problem.
The power-law parametrization using
n
–
\lambda
can be expressed in
n
–
M_{\rm D}
parametrization by using Eq. (
7
).
Then, reports from high-energy collider experiments expressed in
n
–
M_{\rm D}
and results from experiments expressed in
n
–
\lambda
can be directly compared in the context of the ADD model.

The next point we need to consider is the relationship between the power-law and the Yukawa parametrizations, which represent different physical models.
Nevertheless, experiments continue to report their results in the
\alpha
–
\lambda
parameter space, even though they are motivated to search for extra dimensions.
To resolve this tangle, the idea of the extra-dimension model can be used again as follows.
We will introduce a simple discussion as an example in the followings.

The energy-momentum relation for a massless particle in a spacetime with extra dimensions is given by

\displaystyle E^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[(p_{1}c)^{2}+(p_{2}c)^{2}+(p_{3}c)^{2}\right]

(8)

\displaystyle\;\;\;+\left[(p_{4}c)^{2}+(p_{5}c)^{2}+\cdots\right].

If the particle has momentum components toward extra dimensions, such as “missing momentum” invisible from our four-dimensional spacetime, it will be observed as its non-zero mass

(Mc^{2})^{2}=(p_{4}c)^{2}+(p_{5}c)^{2}+...
.
Here
p_{i}
denotes the momentum component along the
i
-th spatial dimension.
The corresponding de Broglie wavelength in the 4th dimension is
l_{4}=h/p_{4}
, for example.
Considering a compactified extra dimension, a periodic boundary condition can be applied along with this dimension.
If we apply this condition like Bohr’s atomic model, this wavelength must be quantized as
\l_{4}=\lambda/m
, using a quantum number
m=0,1,2,3,\dots
, which means
p_{4}=mh/\lambda
if
\lambda
indicates the circumference.
Suppose this particle is a graviton; a quantum of graviton mass is
M_{0}=\frac{h}{\lambda c}
.
Then, in compactified extra-dimension models, the graviton mass
M
can be expressed as an integer multiplication of this unit mass
M_{0}
.
These modes,
M_{m}=0,M_{0},2M_{0},3M_{0},\dots
, are referred to as the Kaluza–Klein tower.
Each graviton with its mass
M
behaves as a massive boson that mediates the gravitational force.
Their strength are expressed as Yukawa potentials with their Compton wavelength
\frac{h}{Mc}=\lambda/m
.
This idea provides a particular interest in the dynamical origin of mass

HOSOTANI1983309
.

The total gravitational potential, summed over the
m
-th KK modes, can be expressed in terms of the coupling strengths
\alpha_{m}
as

\displaystyle V_{\rm KK}(r)/V_{\rm N}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm KK}(r)

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0,1,2...}\alpha_{m}e^{-mr/\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\alpha_{1}e^{-r/\lambda}+\alpha_{2}e^{-2r/\lambda}+\cdots\;.

If the distance
r
is smaller than the Compton wavelength
\lambda_{m}\equiv\lambda/m
of each
m
-th KK mode, the contribution from that mode must be strongly suppressed.
Therefore, higher mass modes can be neglected at
r\gg\lambda_{m}
.
Now it is clear that Eq. (
4
) is the first-order approximation of Eq. (
9
), omitting these higher mass modes.
Therefore, this approximation is effective only at
r>\lambda
.

By supposing appropriate factors
\alpha_{m}
considering the degree of freedom, it can be shown that Eq. (
9
) reproduces the power-law potential (
3
).
Let us see this relation in a model case by starting with a case of one extra dimension,
n=1
.
If we assume a case of
\alpha_{m}=1
for all
m
, the summation of the KK modes is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm KK}^{n=1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e^{-mr/\lambda}

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-e^{-r/\lambda}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{r}\;\;\;\;\;\;(r\ll\lambda),\;\;\;1\;\;\;\;\;\;(r\gg\lambda)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\lambda}{r}\;.

(11)

This summation of the KK tower’s potential shows the same characteristic as Eq. (
3
): a power-law potential with a higher power at
r\ll\lambda
.
In contrast, the Newtonian potential still dominates at
r\gg\lambda
.

For cases with multiple isotropic extra dimensions (
n\geqslant 1
), the modification effects resulting from momentum leakage into each dimension are multiplicative. Consequently, the total modification factor can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm KK}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\Gamma_{\rm KK}^{1}\right)^{n}=\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{-r/\lambda}}\right)^{n}

(12)

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\lambda}{r}\right)^{n}\;\;\;\;\;\;(r\ll\lambda),\;\;\;1\;\;\;\;\;\;(r\gg\lambda)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 1+\left(\frac{\lambda}{r}\right)^{n}.

(13)

Then, the power-law parametrization (
3
) can be understood as representing the two dominant contributions at
r\ll\lambda
and
r\gg\lambda
of Eq. (
12
).
Therefore, although explicit expression representing the KK model is given in Eq. (
12
), the conventional power-law potential (
3
) or (
13
) approximately works to represent it.

Next, we discuss the relationship between the conventional Yukawa potential (
4
) and the KK potential (
12
). While it is evident that the Yukawa potential represents a first-order approximation of the exact KK potential, it is instructive to consider the polynomial expansion of
(\Gamma_{\rm KK}^{1})^{n}
. Indeed, for the general case of
n
extra dimensions, the expansion can be expressed using the multiset coefficient (or the number of combinations with repetition),
H_{n}^{m}=\binom{n+m-1}{m}
, which represents the number of
m
-combinations from a set of
n
elements with repetition, as

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e^{-mr/\lambda}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}{\rm H}^{m}_{n}e^{-mr/\lambda}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+{\rm H}^{1}_{n}e^{-r/\lambda}+{\rm H}^{2}_{n}e^{-2r/\lambda}+\cdots\;,

which gives similar series expression in Eq. (
9
), but now the coefficient
\alpha_{m}={\rm H}^{m}_{n}
is given by using
n
.
For
n=2
, this coefficient reduces to a simple binomial form:
H_{2}^{m}=\binom{m+1}{m}=m+1
. These coefficients admit an interesting physical interpretation as the degeneracy of modes that share the same damping factor,
e^{-mr/\lambda}
.
Specifically, they represent the number of ways to distribute a total quantum number
m
across
n
extra dimensions such that
m=n_{4}+n_{5}+\dots+n_{3+n}
.
For instance, in the
n=2
case with
m=1
(the leading Yukawa term), the possible sets of quantum numbers for the two extra dimensions are
(n_{4},n_{5})=(1,0)
and
(0,1)
. This yields
H_{2}^{1}=2
, which corresponds to the number of possible distribution patterns. Similarly, for
m=2
and
n=2
, the possible combinations are
(2,0),(1,1)
, and
(0,2)
, giving
H_{2}^{2}=3
.

Considering the kinematic origin of
M_{m}
, there are two degrees of freedom associated with the direction of momentum along each compactified extra dimension. Specifically, gravitons propagating in both clockwise and counter-clockwise directions contribute to the same damping factor,
e^{-mr/\lambda}
. These modes correspond to the positive and negative integers of the KK quantum number,
m=\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots
.
Then, the corrected modification factor should be given as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm KK}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{m=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,...}e^{-|m|r/\lambda}\right)^{n}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+2{\rm H}^{1}_{n}e^{-r/\lambda}+\cdots.

Here, the factor 2 in the leading Yukawa term corresponds to the fact that the degeneracy number in Eq. (
14
) should be doubled due to the two-directional degree of freedom for the case of
m\neq 0
.
This is analogous to the expansion of a Fourier series, where the non-zero modes (
m\neq 0
) carry a factor of 2 relative to the constant term (
m=0
).
Degeneracy for higher modes should require more careful counting.
These coefficients depend on the choice of geometrical configurations of the theoretical models.

It should be interesting to expand them to concrete cases as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm KK}^{n=1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+2e^{-r/\lambda}+\cdots

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm KK}^{n=2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+4e^{-r/\lambda}+\cdots

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm KK}^{n=3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+6e^{-r/\lambda}+\cdots.

(16)

Now we see that the
n
-dependence on the first-order Yukawa parametrization (
4
) can be found in the coefficient
\alpha=2{\rm H}_{n}^{m=1}=2n
in the present simple discussion, which reproduces the results shown in a detailed discussion in Ref.
Kehagias200039
.
Here we see that the parameter
\alpha
used in Eq. (
4
) is not a free parameter but can be predicted by extra-dimension models, as a function of
n
.
As shown here, we should expect
\alpha\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
on these KK mode settings.

The following summarizes the properties of the model parametrizations,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm KK}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{-r/\lambda}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\lambda}{r}\right)^{n}\quad(r\ll\lambda),\qquad 1\quad(r\gg\lambda)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm power}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\left(\frac{\lambda}{r}\right)^{n}

(17)

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\lambda}{r}\right)^{n}\quad(r\ll\lambda),\qquad 1\quad(r\gg\lambda)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm Yukawa}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\alpha(n)e^{-r/\lambda}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle 1+\alpha(n)\quad(r\ll\lambda),\qquad 1\quad(r\gg\lambda).

Here, we write
\alpha=\alpha(n)
, explicitly indicating that
\alpha
is a function of
n
, which theoretical models can predict.
The features of each parametrization are shown in Fig.

.
As we see here, the Yukawa parametrization (
4
) significantly deviates from the KK parametrization (
12
) at
r\ll\lambda
.
It is because Eq. (
4
) is a first-order approximation, which approximately reproduces the KK potential (
12
) only at
r\gtrsim\lambda
, ignoring the contribution from higher modes.
In the case of
r\ll\lambda
, the contribution from these higher modes cannot be neglected.

So we must be careful when interpreting an experimental result on
\alpha
value reported for a much larger
\lambda
than the experimental distance
r
, if we are interested in extra-dimension models.
On the other hand, for
r\gg\lambda
, because all models in Eq. (

) show
\Gamma\rightarrow 1
, experiments rapidly lose their sensitivity to test models with small
\lambda
.
Therefore, one should trust
\alpha
–
\lambda
only around
\lambda\sim r
if one uses a single Yukawa parametrization, aiming to compare the experimental result with the prediction
\alpha=2n
of the extra-dimension model.
One way to avoid this confusion is to use the

–
\lambda
parameter space, which directly corresponds to the power-law models.

4
Properties of Yukawa Parametrization

Before discussing the experimental results, the general properties of the Yukawa parametrization should be outlined.
If we use the Yukawa parametrization (
4
) to analyze the experimental data
\Delta(r)
,
\alpha
can be experimentally obtained for a given setting parameter
\lambda
using

\Delta=\frac{V(r)}{V_{\rm N}(r)}-1=\alpha\;e^{-r/\lambda},

(18)

which leads

\alpha=\Delta\;e^{r/\lambda}.

(19)

If the result on
\Delta
is consistent with zero, the upper limit of
\Delta
gives most of part of the upper limit on
\alpha
.
Experimental uncertainty of
r
also contributes to that of
\alpha
, but it is usually relatively small.
In cases of force measurements,

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{F(r)}{F_{\rm N}(r)}-1=\alpha\left(1+\frac{r}{\lambda}\right)e^{-r/\lambda}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\alpha

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{1+r/\lambda}e^{r/\lambda}

(20)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\delta\,e^{r/\lambda}\quad(r\ll\lambda),

which gives approximately the same functional shape of
\alpha(\lambda)
as Eq. (
19
).
The functional shape of the constraint curve on the
\alpha
–
\lambda
plot should be dominated by the simple exponential shape of
e^{r/\lambda}
with its bottom at
\alpha=\delta
.
Fig.
3
(a) shows the examples of the functional shapes.

The above discussion assumes the simplest case of point-mass calculation using the known value of
G_{\rm N}
, which we call “absolute measurements”
Murata_2015
.
In this case, the uncertainty of
G_{\rm N}
or
F_{\rm N}
also contributes to the total uncertainty of
\delta
as

\left(\frac{\sigma_{\delta}}{\delta}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{F}}{F}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{F_{\rm N}}}{F_{\rm N}}\right)^{2},

(21)

here and in the following,
\sigma_{X}
denotes uncertainty of a quantity
X
in general.
Then, if the experimental relative precision of
F
,
\frac{\sigma_{F}}{F}
, is expected to be smaller than that of
F_{\rm N}
,
\frac{\sigma_{F_{\rm N}}}{F_{\rm N}}
, one should avoid using this absolute measurement using the literature value of
G_{\rm N}
.
Otherwise, the total precision will be dominated by the uncertainty of
G_{\rm N}
.

Instead, “relative measurements” do not suppose the absolute value of
G_{\rm N}
to avoid its relatively poor precision.
They perform measurement at a minimum of two different distances
r=r_{\rm near}
and
r_{\rm far}
to cancel
G_{\rm N}
.
As described in detail in Ref.
Murata_2015
, the relative measurements combine the results
\delta(r_{\rm near})
and
\delta(r_{\rm far})
to cancel
G_{\rm N}
, the resulting functional shapes are shown in Figs.
3
(b) and (c).

A feature of these relative measurements is
\alpha

\lambda

(>r_{\rm far})
.
The constraint curve has a minimum point, unlike the simple exponential curve (
20
) for the absolute measurements.
Most short-range laboratory experiments employ this technique and typically report asymmetric, convex constraint curves.
Now, the simple exponential curve for the absolute measurements (
20
) can be understood as the case of
r_{\rm far}=r_{\rm N}
relative measurements using
G_{\rm N}
as the far position data.

7.3
Atomic and Sub-Atomic Experiments

Atomic systems serve as ‘micro-laboratories’ for testing interactions between nuclei and orbital electrons.
Due to the extraordinary precision with which atomic energy levels can be measured, coupled with the ability to perform rigorous QED calculations of the electromagnetic interactions, these systems provide a unique platform for gravity tests.
The sensitivity to gravitational contributions is determined by the interplay between experimental precision and theoretical accuracy.
However, despite this remarkable precision, the immense ratio of the Coulomb force to the gravitational force limits the sensitivity to approximately
\alpha<10^{30}

As demonstrated in torsion-balance experiments, suppressing electromagnetic forces significantly enhances sensitivity to
\alpha

While the Casimir force cannot be shielded in experiments like Decca’s, the gravitational signal can still be extracted by designing sophisticated apparatuses that effectively cancel the Casimir contributions.
Without such mechanical means to suppress electromagnetic couplings, the sensitivity for gravity tests inevitably degrades exponentially, as previously discussed.

Conclusion

We have performed a comprehensive analysis comparing experimental results across unified parameter spaces, specifically
\alpha
–
\lambda
,
n
–
\lambda
, and
n
–
M_{\rm D}
. This framework demonstrates how diverse experimental data can be interpreted within the context of large extra-dimension models. Our analysis shows that the latest LHC results provide the most stringent upper limit on the extra-dimension scale for
n=2
, reaching
\lambda<4\,\upmu
m, while the micrometer-scale precision experiment by the HUST group establishes a competitive limit of
\lambda<11\,\upmu
m.

Notably, laboratory tests of the gravitational inverse-square law remain competitive with LHC constraints only for the
n=2
case at approximately the micrometer scale.
Looking forward, the development of novel experimental techniques or significant advancements in the theoretical estimation of electromagnetic couplings will pave the way for high-precision gravity tests at sub-micrometer scales.
