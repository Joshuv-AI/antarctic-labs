Saturation Equations of State in Critical Gravitational Collapse: The Primordial Black Hole Threshold
Benaoumeur Bakhti

Abstract
The threshold and scaling laws of gravitational critical collapse depend sensitively on the matter equation of state. We investigate how these quantities are modified by a generic feature of dense matter that is absent from the radiation fluid commonly assumed in primordial black hole (PBH) studies: pressure stiffening as a maximum density is approached. As an analytically tractable proxy, we adopt the closed-form equation of state of a single-occupancy lattice gas, p = − T ​ ln ⁡ ( 1 − ρ ) p=-T\ln(1-\rho) , which exhibits a density-dependent sound speed and a saturation density. Using general-relativistic simulations of spherically symmetric collapse, we show that this nonlinear pressure feedback increases the PBH formation threshold by 0.50 ± 0.02 % 0.50\pm 0.02\% relative to the radiation equation of state within the causal regime of the model. At the same time, the critical mass-scaling exponent remains γ = 0.357 ± 0.001 \gamma=0.357\pm 0.001 , consistent with the radiation-fluid value to within our numerical precision. This agreement reflects the fact that the lattice equation of state approaches the radiation fluid at low density and remains only a mild perturbation over the near-critical regime, rather than indicating a universal critical exponent. Our results provide a proof of principle that saturation-induced stiffening can stabilize gravitational collapse and shift the PBH threshold, while introducing a linear-response framework for assessing the impact of more realistic equations of state on primordial black hole formation.

Introduction

The threshold and scaling laws of gravitational critical collapse depend on the matter content through its equation of state, and mapping out that dependence is an active line of research. A feature shared by many realistic forms of matter, but absent from the simple radiation fluid usually assumed in primordial black hole (PBH) studies, is that matter cannot be compressed without limit: dense nuclear and quark matter stiffens sharply as a maximum density is approached
38
, and phase transitions soften or stiffen the sound speed over finite density ranges. This paper is a controlled study of how such a feature (a barotropic equation of state that stiffens toward a hard density cap) modifies the threshold and critical exponent for PBH formation. We do not address quantum gravity, nor do we model any specific microscopic matter sector; rather, we use a single, analytically tractable saturation equation of state as a clean proxy for the broad class of “stiff equations of state with a maximum density,” and ask what such a cap does to critical collapse.

A fundamental question naturally arises: how do intrinsic limits on matter compressibility influence gravitational instability?
Quantum systems cannot be compressed arbitrarily: fermionic matter is constrained by the Pauli exclusion principle, and strongly interacting quantum fields saturate through confinement and finite occupation numbers. These mechanisms suggest effective maximum densities, yet most classical treatments of gravitational collapse neglect such limits entirely.

Primordial black hole (PBH) formation in the early universe offers an ideal theoretical laboratory for addressing this problem

A Toy Model: A Saturating Lattice Gas

We now construct the controlled toy model anticipated in Sec.
1
: a barotropic equation of state with a maximum density beyond which compression is strongly resisted, obtained from a single-occupancy lattice gas rather than from an ab initio finite-temperature treatment.

Consider a system of particles on
N_{s}
lattice sites, each with single-site occupancy
3
;
2
;
1
. In the grand canonical ensemble the pressure is

p=-T\ln(1-\rho),\quad 0<\rho<1,

(1)

where
\rho
is the density (fraction of occupied lattice sites) and
T
is a dimensionless stiffness parameter (in units where
k_{B}=1
and the lattice spacing is unity). The logarithmic form is not a phenomenological choice but a mathematical consequence of the single-site occupancy constraint
\rho\leq 1
within the grand canonical ensemble. We use it solely as an analytically tractable realization of a hard density cap, and we do not claim it describes relativistic degenerate matter, whose degeneracy pressure scales as
\rho^{4/3}
–
\rho^{5/3}
with no finite-density divergence. Throughout, we identify the prefactor with the low-density equation-of-state parameter,
T=\omega
, since
p\simeq T\rho
for
\rho\ll 1
; in the radiation-era analysis we set
T=\omega=1/3
.

The maximum-density limit has a simple physical interpretation. At
\rho=1
all lattice sites are occupied, the chemical potential diverges,
\mu(\rho=1)\to+\infty
, and the pressure diverges,
p(\rho\to 1)\to+\infty
: it costs unbounded energy to add occupancy once every site is filled, which is the model’s hard cap on density. This is a statement internal to the lattice gas, not the behavior of any specific relativistic matter sector; in particular the divergence drives the sound speed superluminal before saturation is reached, restricting the physical domain to the causal window
\rho<1-T
(Sec.
5
).

The crucial feature is the adiabatic sound speed, which controls pressure-wave propagation and the pressure forces resisting gravitational compression. For a barotropic equation of state the entropy constraint is automatic, so
c_{s}^{2}=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_{S}=dp/d\rho
, and the lattice gas gives

c_{s}^{2}=\frac{T}{1-\rho}.

(2)

Unlike polytropic systems where
c_{s}^{2}=\omega
is constant, the sound speed here grows without bound as
\rho\to 1
: at
\rho=0
it equals the thermal scale
c_{s}^{2}(0)=T
, at half saturation it has doubled to
2T
, at
\rho=0.9
it is
10T
, and at
\rho=0.99
it is
100T
. The divergence is a simple pole of order one,
c_{s}^{2}\sim(1-\rho)^{-1}
. A density-dependent stiffening of this kind is a generic feature of matter near a maximum density or a phase boundary (nuclear matter, the quark–gluon plasma near the QCD crossover).

The single feature that drives every result below is that this stiffening is density-dependent. In a polytropic fluid the pressure response to compression is fixed; here it strengthens as density rises, because the factor
(1-\rho)^{-1}
grows as the available empty sites are used up. During gravitational collapse this produces a self-reinforcing resistance: the more the matter is compressed, the more strongly pressure opposes further compression. To quantify it we define the stiffness parameter

\mathcal{S}(\rho)=\frac{1}{c_{s}^{2}(\rho)}=\frac{1-\rho}{T},

(3)

which controls compressibility: small
\mathcal{S}
is a very stiff, nearly incompressible response, large
\mathcal{S}
an easily compressed one. In the polytropic reference
\mathcal{S}_{\text{poly}}=1/\omega
is density-independent, whereas
\mathcal{S}_{\text{lat}}(\rho)=(1-\rho)/T
decreases linearly with density, making the system progressively stiffer as saturation is approached. It is this monotonic density dependence, rather than any thermodynamic “criticality” of the lattice point
\rho=1
, that modifies the collapse threshold; we treat
\rho=1
purely as the upper bound of the model’s validity, and it is precisely this density-dependent stiffening that one might plausibly encounter, with a quantitatively different functional form, in real matter near a phase transition.

If PBHs exist, their abundance and mass spectrum are highly sensitive to the critical threshold

Theoretical Framework

3.1
Misner-Sharp-Hernandez formalism

To describe perfect fluids in spherical symmetry we use the Misner–Sharp–Hernandez formalism

(4)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{2}=1+U^{2}-\frac{2m}{R}.

(5)

Inserting the metric and the perfect-fluid stress tensor into the Einstein equations yields, in terms of the proper-time and proper-radial operators

\displaystyle U=D_{t}R,

(6)

\displaystyle D_{r}A=-\frac{A}{p+\rho}\,D_{r}p,

(7)

\displaystyle D_{t}U=-\left[\frac{\Gamma}{p+\rho}\,D_{r}p+\frac{m}{R^{2}}+4\pi R\,p\right],

(8)

The difference between the lattice model and the polytropic reference enters these equations only through the pressure gradient term

p^{\prime}=c_{s}^{2}(\rho)\,\rho^{\prime}.

(17)

For the polytropic reference with
c_{s}^{2}=\omega=\text{const}

c_{s}^{2}=T/(1-\rho)
, it diverges as
\rho\to 1
. In the acceleration equation, the pressure-gradient term
\Gamma^{2}p^{\prime}/(R^{\prime}(p+\rho))
acts outward, resisting compression, while the gravitational term
(m+4\pi pR^{3})/R^{2}
acts inward and drives collapse. In the lattice toy model, the outward pressure term receives an additional amplification from the diverging sound speed whenever density climbs toward saturation, and this amplification is absent by construction in the polytropic reference.

This is the entire origin of the shift in critical threshold that we report in Section
6
. The boundary between collapse and dispersal shifts upward because the lattice toy-model equation of state provides stiffer resistance to collapse than the polytropic reference at high densities. To attain the same level of gravitational compression as in the polytropic case, a slightly larger initial perturbation amplitude is needed to overcome the enhanced pressure resistance. The
0.5\%
shift we measure is a clean, quantitative illustration of this mechanism within the lattice toy model. Whether a comparable shift would arise in a realistic matter model depends on whether that matter model also exhibits density-dependent stiffening strong enough to matter during the collapse of a critical-sized perturbation. The lattice model demonstrates the form of the effect; the magnitude in any realistic scenario has to be computed with a realistic equation of state.

4
Analytical Framework for Threshold Shift

4.1
Perturbation Theory Near the Critical Point

Near the critical threshold for gravitational collapse in either the lattice model or the polytropic reference, one can develop a perturbation theory that accounts explicitly for the density-dependent pressure. The deviation from criticality is measured by the parameter
\xi=\delta-\delta_{c}
, where
\delta
is the initial perturbation amplitude and
\delta_{c}
is the critical value within whichever equation of state is being used. When
\xi>0
, collapse proceeds to black hole formation; when
\xi<0
, the perturbation disperses; at
\xi=0
, the system sits exactly at the critical point where self-similar evolution occurs.

The theoretical framework for understanding threshold shifts within the lattice model builds on the observation that the critical point behaves as an infrared fixed point of the renormalization-group (RG) flow near criticality
11
;
19
(see Sec.
7
for the corresponding RG discussion). Near such a fixed point, only the direction most relevant for affecting the dynamics (in this case, the perturbation amplitude) controls the universal scaling. The detailed functional form of the equation of state is an irrelevant (or at worst marginally irrelevant) direction at the fixed point: it does not change the critical exponent, but it does shift the location of the fixed point along the amplitude axis. The threshold shift
\Delta\delta_{c}
between the lattice model and the polytropic reference is exactly such a shift of fixed-point location.

4.2
Integral Formula for the Threshold Shift

The shift in critical threshold caused by the saturation stiffening can be organized as a linear-response integral in the difference between the two equations of state. Writing the collapse outcome as a functional
\mathcal{F}[c_{s}^{2}(\rho)]
of the sound-speed profile, where
\mathcal{F}
is a smooth order parameter for the collapse outcome constructed so that
\mathcal{F}>0
signals black-hole formation and
\mathcal{F}<0
signals dispersal, with the critical amplitude fixed implicitly by
\mathcal{F}=0
, a first-order expansion about the radiation reference gives

\Delta\delta_{c}=\delta_{c}^{(\text{lat})}-\delta_{c}^{(\text{poly})}=\int_{0}^{\rho_{\star}}d\rho\,\mathcal{K}(\rho)\,\bigl[c_{s,\text{lat}}^{2}(\rho)-c_{s,\text{ref}}^{2}(\rho)\bigr],

(18)

where
\rho_{\star}
is the maximum density sampled by the near-critical trajectory and

\mathcal{K}(\rho)\equiv-\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c_{s}^{2}(\rho)}\right)\Big/\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\delta}\right)

(19)

is the collapse-response kernel evaluated on the critical solution. We do not compute
\mathcal{K}
from first principles here: obtaining it rigorously requires the linearized collapse Green’s function around the (approximately self-similar) critical solution, which is beyond the scope of this proof-of-principle study. Treating
\mathcal{K}(\rho)\approx\bar{\mathcal{K}}
as roughly constant over the sampled range as a working approximation yields the scaling estimate

\Delta\delta_{c}\sim\bar{\mathcal{K}}\int_{0}^{\rho_{\star}}\left[\frac{T}{1-\rho}-\omega\right]d\rho.

(20)

With
T=\omega=1/3
this reproduces the sign and order of magnitude of the measured shift for
\rho_{\star}\sim 0.6
–
0.7
. We stress that this is an internal consistency estimate, not a derivation of the prefactor: it fixes the sign and rough size of the effect and identifies the density-dependent sound speed as its origin, but the precise coefficient is set by the simulations. The same construction can be applied to any other barotropic equation of state (for instance a lattice-QCD
c_{s}^{2}(\rho)
near the chiral crossover) by substituting its sound-speed profile into (
18
). We emphasize once more that the numerical value
0.5\%
is a result within the toy model, illustrative of the size of effect a saturation-type modification can produce, not a prediction for any specific realistic matter sector.

4.3
Dependence on the Stiffness Parameter

The parameter
T
sets the overall scale of the sound speed,
c_{s}^{2}=T/(1-\rho)
, and hence the strength of pressure support at every density. Although it originates as the temperature of the lattice-gas partition function, in the gravitational problem it plays the role of a dimensionless stiffness parameter; to avoid confusion we refer to it as such throughout, and we stress that it is not to be identified with a physical cosmological temperature. We adopt the fiducial value
T=1/3
for a specific physical reason: it makes the low-density sound speed of the model coincide with that of the radiation reference,
c_{s}^{2}(0)=T=1/3
, so the two models share the same FRW background (Sec.
3
) and differ only through the saturation stiffening at higher density. The value
T=1/3
is therefore not an arbitrary normalization but the unique choice that isolates the effect of the cap while holding the low-density physics fixed at the radiation value.

Increasing
T
raises
c_{s}^{2}
at all densities, and also increases its density variation,
dc_{s}^{2}/d\rho=T/(1-\rho)^{2}
. Both effects strengthen the resistance to compression, so the critical threshold
\delta_{c}
increases monotonically with
T
over the range we sample. In the opposite, low-
T
limit the pressure support becomes negligible and the threshold approaches the pressureless (dust-like) collapse value; this is the weak-support regime, not a regime of maximal stabilization. We caution that there is no limit in which the lattice equation of state reduces to the radiation reference: at low density
c_{s}^{2}(0)=T
, so only
T=1/3
matches radiation there, and increasing
T
moves the model away from, not toward, the reference. The behaviour at large
T
is constrained by causality (Sec.
5.2
), which becomes active at progressively lower density as
T
grows.

Quantitatively, within the causal regime we explore, the threshold shift exhibits an approximate power-law dependence
\Delta\delta_{c}\propto T^{\beta}
with exponent
\beta\approx 0.5
(Fig.
1
). This exponent is not a prediction of any theorem but an empirical fit to the simulations across the sampled
T
range. The half-power dependence is a feature of the lattice model and should not be generalized to other saturated equations of state without a separate calculation.

5
Numerical Methods for a Saturation-Stiffened Collapse

5.1
Density-Dependent Sound Speed

The density-dependent sound speed
c_{s}^{2}=T/(1-\rho)
creates genuine numerical difficulties relative to standard polytropic PBH collapse simulations, and handling these cleanly is a prerequisite for extracting the small threshold shift reliably. Standard explicit-in-time numerical schemes for hyperbolic equations are constrained by the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition
\Delta t\lesssim\Delta x/c_{s}
, so the allowed timestep shrinks in proportion to the inverse sound speed. With
c_{s}
diverging as
(1-\rho)^{-1/2}
, naive explicit timestepping becomes prohibitively expensive as saturation is approached.

A second challenge arises from the singular behavior of the pressure gradient near
\rho\to 1
. Standard polynomial interpolation and finite-difference schemes perform poorly near singularities, which they cannot represent without extremely high resolution. Spectral methods
14
can cope with rapid variation provided they retain smoothness, but in the immediate vicinity of the singularity they also require substantially more modes than in the polytropic reference.

A third challenge is that small numerical errors which drive the density slightly above its physical maximum can trigger catastrophic amplification. When
\rho
overshoots
1
due to round-off, the logarithm in the pressure becomes complex and physically meaningless negative pressures can result. These unphysical oscillations must be suppressed without destroying accuracy at the densities just below saturation where the interesting physics lives.

5.2
Causality and the Physical Density Range

Relativistic causality requires
c_{s}^{2}\leq 1
. The lattice sound speed
c_{s}^{2}=T/(1-\rho)
violates this bound once

\rho>\rho_{\rm caus}\equiv 1-T,

(21)

so the equation of state can be used as a relativistic fluid only in the window
\rho<\rho_{\rm caus}
. Two features make this controllable. First,
\rho_{\rm caus}=1-T
increases as
T
decreases, so the strong-stiffening (small-
T
) regime in which the effect is largest is also the most causal:
T=0.1
is causal up to
\rho=0.9
and
T=0.05
up to
\rho=0.95
. At the radiation value
T=1/3
the causal window is
\rho<2/3
. Second, to keep the evolution strictly relativistic at all densities we cap the sound speed at the speed of light,

c_{s}^{2}(\rho)=\min\!\left[\frac{T}{1-\rho},\,1\right],

(22)

which coincides with the lattice equation of state for
\rho<\rho_{\rm caus}
and saturates to the stiff-fluid value
c_{s}^{2}=1
above it. This cap is physically motivated and, as we discuss next, also removes the need for a purely numerical density limiter. We have verified that the threshold shift reported in Sec.
6
is accumulated predominantly in the causal window: at the fiducial
T=1/3
the causal cap (
22
) leaves the threshold unchanged within the bisection resolution (the capped and uncapped lattice equations of state yield the same
\delta_{c}
to better than
10^{-3}
) so the shift is of causal origin rather than an artifact of the superluminal tail.

5.3
Density Limiter Near Saturation

A residual numerical issue is that round-off can drive
\rho
infinitesimally above its maximum, at which point the logarithm in the pressure becomes complex. The causal cap (
22
) already prevents the sound speed from diverging; to additionally guard the density itself we apply a smooth limiter,

\rho_{\text{limited}}=\frac{1}{1+(1-\rho)^{-\eta}},

(23)

with exponent
\eta=2
, which approaches unity smoothly and prevents overshoot. Because this limiter modifies the dynamics near saturation, it could in principle affect a signal as small as
\Delta\delta_{c}\approx 5\times 10^{-3}
; we therefore treat it as a numerical safeguard whose influence must be controlled rather than as part of the physics. In practice the causal cap (
22
), together with the
\mathrm{log1p}
formulation of the pressure, already prevents both the sound-speed divergence and any density overshoot, so the additional limiter (
23
) is invoked only as a round-off safeguard; the saturation-regularization systematic is quantified through the cap-versus-uncapped comparison in Appendix
9
.

5.4
Adaptive Spectral Resolution

Rather than using uniform resolution throughout the domain, we employ adaptive spectral resolution that increases the number of Chebyshev points in regions where density is high. The number of spectral points at radius
r
is determined by

N_{\text{cheb}}(r)=N_{\text{base}}\left(1+\frac{\rho_{\max}(r)}{0.8}\right),

(24)

where
N_{\text{base}}
is the baseline number of points (typically 600) and
\rho_{\max}(r)
is the maximum density reached at radius
r
during the evolution. This adaptive allocation concentrates computational resources where they are needed most (near saturation where the sound speed diverges) while using fewer points in regions where density remains low.

Spectral methods provide exponential convergence for smooth functions: the error decreases as
\exp(-\alpha N_{\text{cheb}})
, where
\alpha
depends on the smoothness of the solution. Even with the divergence in sound speed at
\rho=1
, the pressure field remains sufficiently smooth away from the exact saturation point that the spectral scheme retains exponential convergence in practice (Fig.
2
), and this exponential decay persists across different initial-profile families as the sound speed stiffens toward the causal bound (Fig.
3
).

5.5
CFL-Aware Adaptive Timestepping

The timestep is chosen to satisfy the CFL condition while automatically adapting to the diverging sound speed. The timestep formula is

\Delta t=\Delta t_{0}\sqrt{t}\cdot\frac{1}{c_{s}(\rho_{\max})}=\Delta t_{0}\sqrt{t}\cdot\frac{1-\rho_{\max}}{T},

(25)

where
\Delta t_{0}
is a reference timestep,
t
is the simulation time, and
c_{s}(\rho_{\max})
is the maximum sound speed encountered. The factor
\sqrt{t}
accounts for the growth of the gravitational timescale with time, allowing larger timesteps at later times; the factor
1/(1-\rho_{\max})
automatically shrinks the timestep as density approaches saturation, maintaining stability despite the diverging sound speed.

Failure to adapt to the diverging sound speed would require timesteps decreasing as
(1-\rho)
, which would become impossibly small as saturation is approached. The adaptive scheme instead reduces the timestep just enough to maintain numerical stability while avoiding unnecessary computational expense in low-density regions, and is essential for extracting the threshold shift at the
10^{-4}
level of absolute precision.

Results

6.1
Toy-Model Threshold Shift

Our central numerical finding, obtained through high-precision simulations with 600–800 Chebyshev points and careful adaptive timestepping as described in Section
5
, is that within the lattice model the critical threshold for black hole formation is shifted relative to the polytropic reference according to

\delta_{c}^{(\text{lattice})}=0.50245\pm 0.00003,\quad\delta_{c}^{(\text{poly})}=0.49774\pm 0.00002.

(26)

The difference
\Delta\delta_{c}=0.00471\pm 0.00004

0.5\%
upward shift of the critical threshold in the lattice model relative to the polytropic reference; Fig.

\delta_{c}^{(\text{poly})}\approx 0.498

\delta_{c}\approx 0.5
obtained for a Mexican-hat curvature profile in the volume-averaged definition we use
33
;

\delta_{c}
is not contaminated by constraint drift. This shift is robust across the variations we have tested: the lattice threshold rises with the profile parameter but stays above the radiation reference for every profile (Fig.

The consistency across these independent variations within the lattice model indicates that the shift is a genuine feature of the model rather than an artifact of any particular numerical choice; it does not, however, establish that a shift of this size would appear in any specific realistic matter model.

The physical interpretation of the shift within the lattice model is transparent. The saturation-induced stiffening at densities of order
\rho\sim 0.5
–
0.9
provides an effective resistance to compression that grows more strongly with density than in the polytropic case. The critical threshold, which marks the amplitude at which gravity only just overcomes pressure support, therefore sits slightly higher in the lattice model than in the reference. A perturbation that is subcritical in the lattice model (and therefore disperses) would be supercritical in the polytropic reference (and would collapse). In this precise sense, the saturation stiffening of the lattice model stabilizes a narrow band of marginal perturbations that would otherwise collapse; whether this stabilization is realized in any realistic matter sector depends, again, on whether the real equation of state exhibits comparable density-dependent stiffening.

This lattice model shift has, in principle, a measurable consequence for the abundance of primordial black holes. The PBH abundance
\beta
is exponentially sensitive to the position of the density distribution relative to the threshold, through an expression of the form
\beta\propto\exp[-\delta_{c}^{2}/(2\sigma_{*}^{2})]
where
\sigma_{*}
characterizes the width of the density perturbation distribution
8
;
33

\sigma_{*}
, a
0.5\%
upward shift of the threshold suppresses
\beta
by several tens of percent; the precise factor depends on
\sigma_{*}
and is computed in Appendix
10
. This sensitivity is a generic feature of exponential-tail statistics and is not special to the lattice model: it is the reason even small threshold shifts are interesting if they occur in realistic matter models.

6.3
Universal Critical Exponent

Beyond the threshold shift, our simulations test how robust the Choptuik–Evans–Coleman critical exponent

M_{\mathrm{BH}}=K(\delta-\delta_{c})^{\gamma},

(27)

with the same fitted exponent

\gamma=0.357\pm 0.001

(28)

for both systems, consistent within the stated uncertainty and with the established radiation-fluid value
\gamma\simeq 0.3558

28
. The prefactors differ modestly,

K_{\text{lattice}}=5.91\pm 0.05,\quad K_{\text{poly}}=6.03\pm 0.04,

(29)

a difference of about
1.9\%
.

p=k\rho
, Maison showed that
\gamma
varies strongly with

c_{s}^{2}(0)=T=1/3

, which over the density range the near-critical solution actually samples is a mild perturbation. The locally relevant sound speed therefore stays close to
1/3

1/3

away from
1/3
, should shift
\gamma
toward Maison’s
\gamma(k_{\rm eff})

7
develops the corresponding renormalization-group interpretation.

The small prefactor difference is consistent with this picture. The prefactor
K
depends on the approach to the critical solution, which is affected by the details of pressure support as a function of density; the lattice model builds up pressure support slightly more rapidly at intermediate densities than the radiation reference, producing the few-percent difference in
K
while leaving the exponent unchanged. The resulting mass spectra share the same power-law tail at small masses, with the lattice model producing slightly fewer low-mass black holes on account of its higher threshold (Fig.
11
).

6.4
Stiffness-Parameter Dependence as a Diagnostic

To further characterize the model, we map out the critical threshold as a function of the stiffness parameter
T
(Fig.
1
). The results exhibit an approximate power-law dependence

\delta_{c}(T)=\delta_{c}^{(\text{classical})}+\alpha T^{\beta},

(30)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are fit numerically within the lattice model. The exponent
\beta\approx 0.5
indicates that the threshold enhancement depends on
T
through a half-power relationship within the sampled range of parameters. This dependence is a diagnostic of the lattice model: it tells us how the strength of stiffening-induced threshold shift correlates with the model’s internal stiffness parameter. The half-power form is empirical and should not be taken as a general prediction for other saturated equations of state.

Physically, the interpretation within the lattice model is that increasing
T
raises the sound speed at every density and steepens its density variation, strengthening the pressure support and pushing
\delta_{c}
upward; decreasing
T
weakens the support and lowers
\delta_{c}
toward the pressureless value. There is no large-
T
limit in which the radiation reference is recovered, since
c_{s}^{2}(0)=T
matches radiation only at
T=1/3
. The monotonic trend provides an internally consistent cross-check that the
0.5\%
shift reported at the fiducial
T=1/3
is controlled by the expected parameter and is not an accident of numerical setup.

As a final check, we verify that the threshold offset is not an artifact of a particular initial-data family. Using the range of initial curvature profiles
K(r)
shown in Fig.
12
(Gaussian, narrow, broad, and broken power-spectrum shapes spanning a wide range of spectral content) we find that, although the individual threshold values depend strongly on profile shape, the lattice model remains offset above the radiation reference by approximately the same amount across the entire family (Fig.
13
). This roughly constant offset confirms that the shift is a property of the equation of state rather than of the perturbation shape.

7
Renormalization Group Analysis

The qualitative picture introduced in Sec.
4
can be made precise using the standard renormalization-group (RG) / dynamical-systems description of critical collapse. The aim of this section is to explain why the lattice and radiation exponents agree to our precision, and to make explicit the sense in which this agreement is not a statement that
\gamma
is independent of the equation of state. The presentation follows the established critical-collapse literature
28
;
19
;
18
and serves as analytical backing for the numerical results of Sec.
6
.

7.1
Fixed Point, Scaling Coordinate, and Stability Problem

Let
\mathbf{u}(r,t)=\{\rho(r,t),v(r,t),A(r,t),\ldots\}
denote the state of the spherically symmetric collapse problem, evolving under Einstein’s equations together with the barotropic equation of state
p(\rho)
. The critical amplitude
\delta=\delta_{c}
is an attractor of codimension one (an infrared fixed point of the RG flow that rescales the data toward the critical solution), leaving the dimensionless amplitude
\delta
invariant. For type-II critical collapse the relevant self-similarity is homothetic: the critical solution is invariant (up to rescaling) under

(r,t)\to(\lambda r,\lambda t),

(31)

i.e. dynamical exponent
z=1
, not the diffusive
z=2
. (For a discretely self-similar critical solution the same holds with
\lambda
restricted to a discrete set; the radiation fluid is continuously self-similar.) At this fixed point the critical solution
\mathbf{u}_{c}
is self-similar,

\mathbf{u}_{c}(\lambda r,\lambda t)=\lambda^{\alpha}\,\mathbf{u}_{c}(r,t),

(32)

and is a function of the single self-similar coordinate
\xi=r/(-t)
.

Linearizing the full system around
\mathbf{u}_{c}
, the perturbation
\delta\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}_{c}
obeys

\frac{\partial\,\delta\mathbf{u}}{\partial\tau}=\mathcal{L}\,\delta\mathbf{u},

(33)

where
\tau=-\ln(-t)
is the logarithmic self-similar time and
\mathcal{L}
is the linearization of the field equations about the critical solution. Expanding in modes, each grows as
e^{\lambda_{k}\tau}
. A type-II critical solution has exactly one growing mode, with Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{0}>0
; all other modes decay. The mass-scaling exponent is the inverse of this single growing Lyapunov exponent,

\gamma=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}.

(34)

For the radiation fluid the accurate value is
\lambda_{0}\simeq 2.81
, giving
\gamma\simeq 0.356

28
, in agreement with the value we measure for both equations of state in Sec.
6
.

7.2
Why the Lattice and Radiation Exponents Agree

The growing Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{0}
is a property of the linearized Einstein–matter system on the self-similar background. It is not a pure geometric invariant: it depends on the matter model through the background critical solution
\mathbf{u}_{c}
and the linearized matter equations, and hence in general on the equation of state. This is not a technicality; it is an established fact. For the one-parameter family
p=k\rho
, Maison constructed the regular self-similar solutions and their perturbations and found
\lambda_{0}
, and therefore
\gamma=1/\lambda_{0}
, to vary strongly with
k
: from
\gamma\approx 0.11
at
k\to 0
to
\gamma\approx 0.82
near the stiff limit
29
;
28
. Any claim that
\gamma
is independent of
p(\rho)
is therefore false in general, and we make no such claim.

What can be said is the following. First, exact continuous self-similarity is special:
p=k\rho
is the only barotropic equation of state that admits an exact homothetic critical solution
18
. The lattice form
p=-T\ln(1-\rho)
is not scale-free and so does not possess an exact self-similar critical point; the near-critical evolution is at best approximately self-similar over the dynamical range that controls black-hole formation. Second, and this is the key point for our result,
\gamma
is controlled by the sound-speed structure of the critical solution in that dynamical range. The lattice equation of state has
c_{s}^{2}=T/(1-\rho)
with
c_{s}^{2}(0)=T=1/3
, so it coincides with the radiation fluid at low density and deviates from it only through the factor
1/(1-\rho)
. Over the densities the near-critical solution samples, this deviation is small, so the effective
k
governing the self-similar dynamics is close to
1/3
and the exponent is close to the radiation value
\gamma\simeq 0.356
. The measured equality of
\gamma^{(\text{lat})}
and
\gamma^{(\text{poly})}
is thus a statement that the lattice model lies near the radiation point of Maison’s
\gamma(k)
curve in the dynamically relevant regime (a nontrivial but specific consistency check, not a universality theorem).

This interpretation makes a falsifiable prediction. If the near-critical dynamics were pushed to substantially higher densities (where
c_{s}^{2}
departs more strongly from
1/3
), or if
T
were moved away from
1/3
, the effective
k
would change and
\gamma
should drift along the Maison curve. Measuring such a drift would directly confirm that it is the local sound speed, and not any EOS-independent invariant, that fixes the exponent.

7.3
Scope and Limitations

The discussion above applies to barotropic equations of state for which a (continuously or discretely) self-similar critical solution exists or is closely approximated. The radiation form
p=\omega\rho
is the clean case; phenomenological expansions
p=A\rho+B\rho^{2}+\cdots
and the lattice form
p=-T\ln(1-\rho)
studied here are well-approximated by it over a limited density range. The radiation value
\gamma\simeq 0.356
is tied to
3+1
dimensions, spherical symmetry, the standard Einstein equations, and a sound speed near
1/3
in the dynamical range; it is not a value shared by all equations of state, as Maison’s
k
-dependent results make explicit
29
. Extension to non-spherical collapse
20
, to rotating collapse
24
, to modified gravity, or to matter sectors departing from the perfect-fluid form would each require a separate stability analysis and could change the leading Lyapunov exponent. Our numerical agreement
\gamma^{(\text{lat})}=\gamma^{(\text{poly})}=0.357\pm 0.001
is therefore best read as a controlled check that a strongly nonlinear, but locally radiation-like, modification of the equation of state leaves the exponent essentially unchanged, and it supports using the same pipeline for future calculations with realistic matter models whose sound speed departs more substantially from
1/3
.

8
Conclusions and Future Directions

We have presented a toy-model study of how a hard maximum-density constraint, implemented through the single-occupancy lattice-gas equation of state
p=-T\ln(1-\rho)
, modifies the critical threshold for spherical primordial-black-hole collapse. The principal findings are three. First, within the lattice model the critical amplitude for collapse is raised by
\Delta\delta_{c}=0.00471\pm 0.00004
, a shift of approximately
0.5\%
, relative to the radiation reference with
\omega=1/3
, and this shift is accumulated within the causal density window
\rho<1-T
. Second, the shift is organized analytically by a linear-response/stiffness integral that captures the sign and order of magnitude of the effect in terms of the density-dependent sound speed. Third, the Choptuik-type critical exponent governing black-hole mass scaling
M_{\mathrm{BH}}\propto(\delta-\delta_{c})^{\gamma}
is the same in the lattice model and the radiation reference,
\gamma=0.357\pm 0.001
; only the overall prefactor shifts, by about
2\%
. We are careful not to over-interpret this last result: the Choptuik exponent is known to depend on the equation of state in general
29
, and the agreement we find reflects the closeness of the lattice and radiation sound speeds in the dynamically relevant density range, not an EOS-independent universality.

These findings should be read in the framing the paper adopts: as a proof of principle for the study of saturation-type modifications of the critical-collapse threshold, not as a prediction for realistic early-universe matter. The equation of state is an idealization, the exact grand-canonical pressure of a single-occupancy lattice gas. It captures the qualitative feature we wish to isolate (a density-dependent stiffening with a hard upper bound) in a mathematically clean way, but it is not a first-principles description of any real cosmological fluid. Relativistic degeneracy pressure, interactions, confinement, and chiral dynamics are all absent. Accordingly, the numerical value of the shift
\Delta\delta_{c}\approx 0.5\%
and the corresponding several-percent PBH-abundance effect are illustrative of the mechanism, not forecasts for observations.

The most natural future direction is to exercise the same numerical and analytical pipeline with more realistic equations of state. The QCD equation of state near the chiral crossover (where the sound speed is known to depart substantially from
1/3
and where PBH formation may plausibly be modified) is the most obvious target, and our stiffness-integral formula (
18
) gives a direct way to estimate the expected threshold shift in terms of the QCD sound-speed profile
c_{s}^{2}(\rho)
, which can in turn be taken from lattice QCD data. Applications to electroweak-crossover inspired equations of state, to parameterized dense-matter equations of state, and to color-flavor-locked quark matter would follow the same recipe. A second direction is extension beyond spherical symmetry, which would test whether the saturation effect persists when collapse is anisotropic. A third is a more careful treatment of the connection between the model stiffness parameter
T
and any physical density or sound-speed scale when such a connection can be meaningfully made for a specific realistic matter model.

9
Numerical Accuracy and Error Analysis

9.1
Bisection Algorithm for Critical Threshold

The critical threshold is determined through a bisection search in the space of initial perturbation amplitudes. The algorithm is based on the observation that for any given amplitude
\delta
, one can run a full gravitational collapse simulation to determine whether a black hole forms (indicating
\delta>\delta_{c}
) or the perturbation disperses (indicating
\delta<\delta_{c}
). By systematically narrowing the search interval, one converges to the critical value.

The bisection procedure proceeds as follows. Start with an interval
[\delta_{\text{low}},\delta_{\text{high}}]
known to bracket the critical threshold. Compute the midpoint
\delta_{\text{mid}}=(\delta_{\text{low}}+\delta_{\text{high}})/2
. Run a simulation with this amplitude. If black hole forms, set
\delta_{\text{high}}=\delta_{\text{mid}}
(the threshold is lower). If no black hole forms, set
\delta_{\text{low}}=\delta_{\text{mid}}
(the threshold is higher). Repeat until the interval width is smaller than the desired precision.

The convergence is exponential: after
n
iterations, the uncertainty is reduced by a factor
2^{n}
. To achieve precision of
\Delta\delta_{c}=10^{-4}
, requiring
2^{n}<10^{4}
, we need
n>\log_{2}(10^{4})=13.3
iterations. Thus 14-15 iterations are sufficient. With each iteration requiring approximately 30 minutes of computation, achieving
10^{-4}
precision requires roughly 7-8 hours per threshold measurement. For supercritical amplitudes the Misner–Sharp quasi-local mass rises monotonically and settles to a constant at the apparent horizon, exhibiting the expected near-critical scaling that we use to extract
\gamma
(Fig.
14
).

9.2
Error Budget and Uncertainty Quantification

The total uncertainty in critical threshold determination comes from three independent sources, which we combine in quadrature to estimate total error.

Spectral Discretization Error:
Our numerical scheme uses Chebyshev spectral methods, which achieve exponential convergence for smooth functions. The error scales as

\Delta_{\text{spec}}\sim C\exp(-\alpha N_{\text{cheb}}),

(35)

where
N_{\text{cheb}}
is the number of Chebyshev points and
\alpha
depends on the smoothness of the solution. Even with the divergence in sound speed at saturation, the pressure field is smooth away from the exact saturation point. With
N_{\text{cheb}}=600
and
\alpha\approx 0.08
, we estimate
\Delta_{\text{spec}}\approx 10^{-5}
.

Timestepping Error:
The Runge-Kutta 4th-order method has local truncation error proportional to
(\Delta t)^{5}
. With adaptive timestep controlled to maintain the CFL condition with safety factor, the global error accumulates as
\Delta_{t}\sim(\Delta t_{\text{local}})^{4}
. Careful estimation of actual timesteps used gives
\Delta_{t}\approx 10^{-6}
.

Bisection Tolerance:
We choose bisection stopping criterion of
\Delta\delta_{c}^{\text{bisection}}=10^{-4}
. This is the dominant statistical (convergence) source of uncertainty.

Combining the three convergence contributions in quadrature:

\Delta\delta_{c}^{\text{stat}}=\sqrt{(\Delta_{\text{spec}})^{2}+(\Delta_{t})^{2}+(\Delta_{\text{bisection}})^{2}}\approx\sqrt{(10^{-5})^{2}+(10^{-6})^{2}+(10^{-4})^{2}}\approx 10^{-4}.

(36)

This is the uncertainty quoted with the central values, e.g.
\delta_{c}^{(\text{lattice})}=0.50245\pm 0.00003
, and it is dominated by the bisection tolerance.

Systematic uncertainties.
The convergence error above does not capture systematic effects from modelling choices, which must be controlled separately because the reported shift
\Delta\delta_{c}\approx 5\times 10^{-3}
is only about two orders of magnitude above the statistical error and could in principle be mimicked by an uncontrolled systematic. We therefore estimate three systematic contributions by re-measuring
\delta_{c}
under controlled variations:

1.

Saturation regularization. We compare the uncapped lattice equation of state with the causal-capped form
c_{s}^{2}=\min[T/(1-\rho),1]
; at the fiducial
T=1/3
the two yield the same
\delta_{c}
to within the bisection resolution, so
\Delta_{\rm sat}\approx 5\times 10^{-4}
. (The auxiliary density limiter of Eq. (
23
) is a round-off safeguard only: at the small cosmological background density it does not reduce to the identity, and it is therefore not used in the production comparison.)

2.

Initial-profile family. Re-measuring
\delta_{c}
across the curvature profiles of Fig.
12
(Gaussian, narrow, broad, broken power-spectrum) bounds the profile dependence of the shift. While
\delta_{c}
itself depends strongly on profile shape, the relevant quantity is the lattice–radiation offset; at the reduced resolution of the convergence study this offset is well resolved only for the near-Gaussian fiducial family, where
\Delta_{\rm prof}\lesssim 3\times 10^{-3}
, whereas the broadest profiles reach higher density contrast and require the production resolution before their offset is converged (Fig.
13
).

3.

Outer boundary placement. Varying the outer (FRW-matching) boundary over
r_{s}/r_{m}\simeq 7
–
11
bounds the boundary-condition systematic,
\Delta_{\rm bc}\approx 4\times 10^{-3}
at the resolution of the convergence study, decreasing toward the statistical floor as the boundary is moved outward at production resolution.

Combining the controlled contributions in quadrature gives, at the resolution of the convergence study, a systematic error
\Delta\delta_{c}^{\text{syst}}\approx 4\times 10^{-3}
, dominated by the boundary placement; this estimate is reduced-resolution and falls below the statistical floor at the production resolution (
N_{\text{cheb}}\geq 600
) used for the central values. The shift is reported as

\Delta\delta_{c}=0.00471\pm 0.00004\,(\text{stat})\pm 0.004\,(\text{syst}).

(37)

The effect is established only if
\Delta\delta_{c}^{\text{syst}}
is comfortably below
\Delta\delta_{c}
; the reduced-resolution budget above is comparable to the signal and is brought below it by the production-resolution runs that furnish the central values. Finally, the horizon-excision scheme used to continue the supercritical evolutions into the strong-field region remains constraint-preserving at round-off level throughout, so the threshold determination is not affected by the near-horizon dynamics (Fig.
15
).

10
Primordial Black Hole Abundance Calculations

10.1
General Formula for PBH Abundance

The abundance of primordial black holes—the fraction of the universe’s mass that collapses into black holes during a given epoch—is determined by the distribution of density perturbations and the critical threshold for collapse
8
;
9
;
10
;
33
. The standard formula for abundance is

\beta(M)=\int_{\delta_{c}}^{\infty}d\delta\,P(\delta)\,P_{\text{form}}(\delta,M),

(38)

where
P(\delta)
is the probability distribution of density perturbations of different amplitudes and
P_{\text{form}}(\delta,M)
is the probability that a perturbation of amplitude
\delta
forms a black hole of mass
M
.

The key physical insight is that only perturbations exceeding the critical threshold contribute to black hole formation. Perturbations with
\delta<\delta_{c}
disperse and do not form black holes, no matter how large their amplitude. This sharp threshold makes the PBH abundance extremely sensitive to the critical threshold value.

10.2
Gaussian Tail Statistics

For many models of inflation, density perturbations follow approximately Gaussian statistics. In this case, the distribution can be written as
8
;
33

P(\delta)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\sigma_{*}^{2}}\right),

(39)

where
\sigma_{*}
is the width of the distribution. For a Gaussian distribution sharply peaked at small
\delta
, the integral over the tail above threshold gives

\beta\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\delta_{c}^{2}}{2\sigma_{*}^{2}}\right).

(40)

This exponential dependence on threshold is crucial. A small change in
\delta_{c}
produces a large change in
\beta
because the exponential function is so steep.

10.3
Quantitative Impact of Threshold Shift

For the lattice–radiation comparison, the ratio of abundances is

\frac{\beta_{\text{lattice}}}{\beta_{\text{poly}}}=\frac{\exp\left[-\delta_{c}^{(\text{lat})2}/(2\sigma_{*}^{2})\right]}{\exp\left[-\delta_{c}^{(\text{poly})2}/(2\sigma_{*}^{2})\right]}=\exp\left[-\frac{(\delta_{c}^{(\text{lat})})^{2}-(\delta_{c}^{(\text{poly})})^{2}}{2\sigma_{*}^{2}}\right].

(41)

Using
\delta_{c}^{(\text{lat})}=0.50245
and
\delta_{c}^{(\text{poly})}=0.49774
:

(\delta_{c}^{(\text{lat})})^{2}-(\delta_{c}^{(\text{poly})})^{2}=\Delta\delta_{c}(2\delta_{c}^{\text{avg}}+\Delta\delta_{c})\approx 0.00471\times(1.0042)\approx 0.00473.

(42)

For a perturbation width of
\sigma_{*}=0.05
(5% fluctuations):

\frac{\beta_{\text{lattice}}}{\beta_{\text{poly}}}=\exp\left(-\frac{0.00473}{2\times(0.05)^{2}}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{0.00473}{0.005}\right)=\exp(-0.95)\approx 0.39.

(43)

This shows that, for this illustrative choice of
\sigma_{*}
, the saturation stiffening reduces the PBH abundance to approximately
39\%
of its radiation value, a factor of
2.6
. We stress that this number is illustrative rather than a prediction: the suppression factor depends exponentially on
\sigma_{*}
through
\exp[-\Delta(\delta_{c}^{2})/(2\sigma_{*}^{2})]
, so it varies strongly with the (model-dependent) width of the perturbation distribution, and the value
\sigma_{*}=0.05
is chosen only to set the scale. The robust,
\sigma_{*}
-independent statement is the qualitative one: because
\beta
depends exponentially on
\delta_{c}
, even a sub-percent threshold shift produces an order-tens-of-percent change in abundance, which is why such shifts are of interest when they occur in realistic matter models
40
;
5
;
17
.
