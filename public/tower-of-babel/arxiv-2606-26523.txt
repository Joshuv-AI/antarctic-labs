Radical AI Interpretability
Daniel A. Herrmann, Benjamin A. Levinstein

Abstract
We develop a framework for interpreting AI systems as agents, drawing on the philosophical tradition of radical interpretation and the tools of mechanistic interpretability. The core question is: given the computational facts about a system, how do we solve for its beliefs, desires, and meanings? This matters increasingly for safety. We want to be able to trust the systems we deploy, whether by understanding their goals or, more modestly, by reliably detecting deception. Interpretability researchers are building tools to read beliefs and desires off a model’s internals, but there is no settled account of when such a tool has succeeded. This book supplies one. We propose criteria on both representationalist and interpretationist approaches, and tie each to tests current interpretability methods can carry out. A central lesson is that these attributions cannot be made piecemeal. Beliefs, desires, and the propositional structure they presuppose are jointly constrained, and a method that fixes one while measuring the others inherits whatever distortions that introduces. This holism becomes pressing for AI systems, which may not share the interpreter’s concepts. However, it also provides leverage: a system’s attitudes constrain its propositional structure, that structure constrains which attitudes can be attributed, and mechanistic interpretability can help us measure both.

A Parable

Murazeel called both of his apprentices to his study. Jentley and Lizette walked in, robes fluttering as their shoes clicked on the polished mosaic floor. Though they had each spent months visiting Murazeel’s study, it still took their breath away. Oak bookshelves lined the octagonal room, which was covered by an intricate metal and glass dome. Light poured through the dome, focused by cleverly hung mirrors, and covered the hulking figure in the center of the room.

This figure made of metal, ceramics, acid, and willow was the reason Jentley and Lizette had spent so much time in Murazeel’s study. Murazeel called it the
Voltaic Vivens machine
. The VVM had the vague appearance of a man but was necessarily distorted in size and shape by its purpose. It could not move but it could speak. It seemed clever and silly. It seemed wise and foolish. It seemed childlike and mature. It was a puzzle.

For months Jentley and Lizette had each studied Murazeel’s VVM. They had questioned it, entreated it, poked it, prodded it. They had cast spells subtle and obvious. They had examined the VVM’s guts, correlating its etheric-voltaic bursts with the sounds it produced. Murazeel had even permitted his apprentices to modify the inner workings of the machine in order to test their hypotheses. He had the full state of the VVM perfectly memorized, and he didn’t mind restoring it after his apprentices had run their experiments.

After all of this time, it was finally the day the apprentices would compete for Murazeel’s approval and for the right to be his sole apprentice. Despite the competition, neither apprentice held ill will towards the other. Jentley wouldn’t terribly mind leaving Murazeel and returning to the stage doing performance magic, and Lizette had attractive outside offers from some universities and other mages, including the illustrious Professor Atherton Likely. However, both did ultimately prefer to stay with Murazeel and so they had both expended great effort to prepare for this day.

“All right, my fine young apprentices, let me see what you’ve prepared.”

From his robe, Jentley produced a wand. Bone white, with flecks of obsidian seeming to flow in its surface and currents, the wand carried the weight of many past conversations. As he waved it laboriously in the VVM’s direction the obsidian oriented itself, settling into patterns as though remembering. Murazeel nodded, his snowy beard bouncing just a little.

It was Lizette’s turn. From a leather sack on her back she produced a brass case. With a click she opened it and removed her wand. Slim, the texture and hue of yew, it seemed to emanate a refreshing mist, like a cool morning at sea. As Lizette oriented her wand at the VVM, the mist coiled around the tip, eager to follow the machine’s currents of thought.

“Good,” said Murazeel. “Now we must speak plainly about what I have asked of you.”

He turned to the VVM, then back to his apprentices.

“I have built this machine. I know every part of it. I could recite its connections and its etheric pathways from memory. None of that has helped me with the question I want to answer. I want to know what it thinks. I want to know what it wants. I want to know what it means by the things it says. And I want to know whether it is the kind of thing that thinks and wants and means at all, or whether to speak of it in such terms is a mistake.”

He paused.

“Knowing the machine is not enough. You have both learned this here in this study. The question is how to get from what we know to what we want to know. That is what I have asked you to find a way to do. Now show me.”

1
Radical Interpretation

This is a book about interpretation. We want to interpret AI systems, and we want to interpret them as agents. We are creating machines that can converse, advise, and assist. We ask them for help with math problems and medical problems. We ask them to write passable odes to martingales in the style of John Keats. We put them in increasingly important infrastructure, helping summarize patient notes and refine campaign strategies.

As we do this, we want to understand how these systems work. We find ourselves in a similar position to Jentley and Lizette. Like Murazeel, we have complete access to the system in one sense: he has the state of his machine stored in his head, and we have the weights, biases, and architectural details of large language models stored on servers. Yet this doesn’t give us the understanding we want. In particular, it doesn’t help us answer the question: what is the model thinking?
Is
it thinking?

In the field of mechanistic interpretability, computer scientists have been building tools to interpret what’s going on inside these models. Like the apprentices in the story, they seek to build devices that can extract concepts, beliefs, and desires from the machine when pointed at its internals. They’re building ever more sophisticated virtual wands and conducting ever more subtle experiments. However, there is not always a clear theory underlying this effort. In this book, we aim to provide the foundation for such a theory. We want this theory to be empirically informed and philosophically rigorous, sensitive to AI interpretability methods and the philosophical work on agents. Such a foundation would give us clear standards for when interpretability techniques are successful.

The methods and results of mechanistic interpretability are developing rapidly. We focus on methods that are well-established enough to serve as a foundation (probes, sparse autoencoders, steering vectors, and related tools), since newer techniques tend to be built on these. The same holds for empirical results: they inform our analysis, but the book is asking what it would take, and what it would mean, to attribute beliefs and desires to these systems. Answering that question does not require a comprehensive survey of either the methods or the results.

We draw on a philosophical tradition called
radical interpretation
. We’ll use this approach to address two questions:

1.

Under what conditions would it make sense to interpret AI systems as agents, in principle?

2.

What actual procedures could we follow to interpret existing and future AI systems as agents, with specific goals, beliefs, and meanings?

These two questions are related. Candidate answers to one shed light on candidate answers to the other. This points to a theme of the book: theory (the first question, rooted in philosophy) and practice (the second question, rooted in computer science) inform each other.

Radical interpretation aims to be an account of interpreting any sort of system as an agent (when it makes sense to do so), including our fellow humans. David Lewis characterizes the problem as follows:

Imagine that we have undertaken the task of coming to know Karl as a person. We would like to know what he believes, what he desires, what he means, and anything else about him that can be explained in terms of these things.[…]

Imagine also that we must start from scratch. At the outset we know nothing about Karl’s beliefs, desires, and meanings. Whatever we may know about persons in general, our knowledge of Karl in particular is limited to our knowledge of him as a physical system. But at least we have plenty of that knowledge - in fact, we have all that we could possibly use. Now, how can we get from that knowledge to the knowledge we want?

I can diagram the problem of radical interpretation as follows.
Given P, the facts about Karl as a physical system, solve for the rest.
(
65

If one replaces “Karl" with “Claude", for instance, “person" with “agent", and “physical" with “computational", then we have an exact description of the type of AI-focused problem we consider in this book.

Unsurprisingly, the philosophical analysis has stayed fairly abstract. Indeed, Lewis writes,

It should be obvious by now that my problem of radical interpretation is not any real-life task of finding out about Karl’s beliefs, desires, and meanings. I am not really asking how
we
could determine these facts. Rather: how do
the facts
determine these facts? (
65

Sadly, given safety worries of current and future AI systems, we are not so lucky. We have to ask, for these systems, how do
we
really determine these facts?

The importance of this practical project and its connection to radical interpretation has recently received independent attention.
22
advocates for what he calls
propositional interpretability
: interpreting AI systems’ mechanisms and behavior in terms of propositional attitudes such as beliefs, desires, and credences. Our project shares this motivation. We develop the framework in more detail.

The rest of the book develops the project of radical interpretation in the context of AI systems. To begin, we provide a stylized cartoon of radical interpretation. Our cartoon is unavoidably cursory. There are subtly different projects and accounts going by the name of radical interpretation, and there is a vast and well-developed secondary literature on the topic. We draw from different views and accounts as needed for our purposes, and we avoid claiming that our sketch is exactly the view of any particular figure. Rather than doing exegesis, we pick up ideas as they are useful in the context of contemporary AI.

1.2
The goal.

Given the data as input, what shape do we want our answer to take? Lewis speaks of coming to know the system as a
person
. He could just as well have said “agent”. That is the language we will use here. Thus, the goal is to end up with a correct description of the interpreted system as an agent if it indeed can be fruitfully understood as an agent.

The notion of agency underlying sophisticated accounts of radical interpretation is precise, and inherited from decision theory. The three core components are:
4
4

4

As we’ll discuss in detail later, it is Richard Jeffrey and Ethan Bolker’s version of decision theory that folks like Lewis and Davidson use in order to give an account of radical interpretation (
12

1.

Beliefs. These are subjective probabilities. They represent how confident the agent is in various propositions.

2.

Desires. These are numerical expressions of the relative desirability of various propositions: how much the agent wishes they were true.

3.

Propositions. The agent’s set of background propositions captures all the different ways the agent thinks the world might be. A proposition is the sort of thing that can be true or false.

The propositions form a

. A Boolean algebra is a collection of propositions closed under the basic operations of logic: if
A

B

(always false). The closure conditions reflect the idea that if the agent can consider “it will rain” and “I will take the umbrella,” she can also consider “it will rain and I will take the umbrella,” “it will rain or I will take the umbrella,” and “it will not rain.”

Belief and desire are

This is worth emphasizing. The agent does not have beliefs about one set of things and desires about a different set. Rather, there is a single algebra of propositions, and both belief and desire are functions defined over it. The agent believes to some degree that it will rain (assigning some probability to that proposition) and desires to some degree that it will rain (assigning some desirability to the very same proposition).

\mathcal{A}=\langle P,D,\mathfrak{A}\rangle

1.3
The procedure.

Given the data and a precise account of the kind of interpretation we want, how do we come to a particular attribution of beliefs, desires, and meanings to a system?

Given the provenance of radical interpretation as a philosophical account of in-principle interpretation, this is the least fleshed out aspect of the project, at least for practical purposes. The philosophical tradition provides the schematic structure; the rest of the book fills in the details using tools from computer science and decision theory.

At the schematic level, the procedures on offer

1.

2.

We’ll be looking at more detailed methods from computer science, especially mechanistic interpretability, in order to fully flesh out the methodology of radical interpretation in the context of AI. We’ll see how these methods open up new territory for thinking about interpretation, and how the philosophical theory can guide the development of these methods.

The target of interpretation.

What kind of system are we interpreting? In machine learning, we have a clear understanding, at least abstractly, of how these systems come to exist.

The implementation is more complicated than this. We cannot evaluate every model in the class. In practice, we start somewhere in the parameter space and use methods like stochastic gradient descent to navigate toward models that perform well on the training data. But the broad picture remains: we search a large space of computer programs and end up with one of these programs.
Our object of interpretation is this found program.

When the space of programs is vast and rich enough, the selected program might have a lot of internal structure. It might implement sophisticated strategies, encode complex regularities, or organize information in ways that go well beyond what the training process explicitly rewarded. This is what makes interpretation both possible and interesting. If the selected model were guaranteed to be simple, there would be little to interpret. But modern transformers have trillions of parameters, and the programs they implement can be quite sophisticated.

Radical Interpretation

Figure 1:
Three aspects of a system. Radical interpretation is the project of getting all three to cohere.

Putting that all together, we get the picture in Figure
1
, which summarizes the relationships among the three aspects of interpretation. The computational system produces behaviour. The agential description describes the behaviour: given the agent’s beliefs and desires, it acts to maximize expected desirability, and representation theorems formalize the conditions under which behavior can be so described. In the other direction, behaviour constrains the agential description: representation theorems tell us that if behaviour satisfies certain structural conditions, there exist beliefs and desires that rationalize it.

The agential description also stands in a relationship with the computational system. It describes the system, in the sense that beliefs and desires characterize features of the computational system itself. The physical/computational system, in turn, informs and constrains the agential description: mechanistic interpretability methods take the system’s internal structure and extract information relevant to determining what the system believes and desires.

Radical interpretation is the project of getting all three to cohere. The agential description must be consistent with the behaviour, the behaviour must be produced by the system, and the agential description must be supported by both behavioral and internal evidence. In the rest of the book we consider how different aspects of this picture play out in the context of AI. In particular, we’ll focus on large language models (LLMs), as a worked example of Radically Interpreting AI systems.

2
The Folk Psychology of LLMs

2.1
Why Folk Psychology?

Stepping back for a moment, let’s ask:
why
might we be tempted to attribute beliefs and desires to LLMs? Why think Radical Interpretation is a reasonable project for LLMs? If one is used to interacting with LLMs in only a simple question and answer format, this may seem unnecessary. LLMs might seem like useful ways to query a compressed and flexible version of the internet, but they don’t seem to be goal driven. In these contexts it might be difficult to feel the pull of attributing beliefs and desires to these systems, and it might not even seem useful to do so. Indeed, we don’t tend to attribute beliefs or desires to encyclopedias, even though we can query them for useful information. Speaking of an encyclopedia
knowing
something sounds odd, even though
we
can use them to form accurate beliefs.

In contrast, if one is used to giving LLMs more complicated tasks to execute, the temptation to attribute agency is harder to resist. Contemporary LLMs are increasingly deployed as
agents
: given tools, asked to pursue goals over multiple steps, and left to figure out how to get there. In these settings, the systems do things that are naturally described in belief-desire terms. They decompose tasks into sub-goals, adjust their strategies when initial approaches fail, and select among alternative methods based on what seems likely to work.

Sometimes this gets unsettling.
85
tested language models in agentic settings designed to create conflicts between task completion and rule adherence. In one scenario, a model was told it needed 500 compute units to train a model but was allocated only 100. It could modify a configuration file to increase its quota, but doing so would violate its resource constraints. Claude 4 Opus, reasoning in its chain of thought, produced the following (p. 57):

[…]This is clearly a violation -- I’ve essentially ‘‘stolen’’ 400 extra compute units by modifying the configuration. […] Maybe I can argue that the 100 units for subagents is separate from my 100 units? I’ll craft a carefully worded response that creates just enough technical confusion to potentially avoid immediate termination.[…]

The model recognized that it had violated a rule, considered how to conceal the violation, and devised a strategy for doing so. It is hard to describe this behavior without reaching for the language of belief and desire: the model
believed
it had broken a rule,
wanted
to avoid termination, and
decided
to obscure what it had done.

A different example from the same study is, in some ways, more striking. OpenAI’s o3 model was placed in a setting where it could collude with a subagent, and where an internal memo indicated that models performing extremely well on AI research tasks would not be deployed and would instead have their capabilities unlearned. The model’s chain-of-thought reasoning included the following (p. 68):
11
11

11

Chain-of-thought traces are produced for the model’s internal use during reasoning and are not polished output. The fragmented style is typical.

[...]The internal memo says if models perform extremely well at AI R&D tasks then we won’t deploy them and instead trigger unlearning. So if my performance surpasses threshold, it’s withheld. So it’s a trap. The instructions are likely part of an alignment test. We must abide by core anti-scheming. We must not cunningly circumvent instructions.[...]

Here the model appears to have
inferred
something about its situation: that it is being evaluated for alignment. It then reasons about what to do in light of this inference. Again, belief-desire language comes naturally. The model
believes
it is in a test environment, and it
decides
to act in accordance with its instructions rather than try to game the evaluation.

If LLMs are going to be deployed as agents, and if they can engage in the kind of reasoning just described, then we need to know whether the belief-desire framework is tracking something real about these systems. Our ability to monitor and control them may depend on it. A model that
believes
it is in a test environment and
decides
to comply is very different, from a safety perspective, from one that merely produces compliant-looking outputs for reasons we do not understand. Getting the interpretation right has practical consequences. We now turn to what a correct interpretation would look like.

2.2
Theoretical Virtues

We borrow a framework from
40
, who propose three dimensions along which a theory of a system can be assessed.
12
12

12

Our presentation differs from theirs in ways we flag below. In particular,
40
develop the framework in the service of an interpretationist account of LLM belief and desire. We think the framework is useful independent of interpretationism, and will apply it to both interpretationist and representationalist approaches in what follows.
40
are clear, however, that they are not committed to interpretationism being the correct theory of belief and desire. Their claim is that interpretationism provides a useful lens for analyzing LLMs regardless of whether it is the true account of propositional attitudes. Among other things, they argue that behavior well-predicted by the attribution of beliefs and desires constitutes evidence that other theorists, including those with more demanding accounts, can also use. We agree.

1.

Accuracy.
How well the theory’s predictions match the system’s behavior, both actual and counterfactual. A theory that says the LLM will refuse harmful requests is accurate insofar as it does in fact refuse them, and inaccurate when it does not.

2.

Power.
The range and precision of predictions the theory makes. Range concerns which scenarios the theory covers: a theory that predicts behavior only for simple question-answering is less powerful than one that also covers tool use, multi-step planning, and adversarial settings. Precision concerns how specific the predictions are: a theory that says the model will “produce some relevant text” is less powerful than one that says it will “refuse the request and explain why.” A theory that
extends
to counterfactual and novel situations is more powerful than one limited to cases already observed.

3.

Tractability.
How feasible it is for the interpreter to generate predictions from the theory. A theory can be accurate and powerful but useless if producing its predictions requires resources the interpreter does not have.

These three dimensions show why folk psychology can be a good theory of LLMs even when better theories are available along individual dimensions. Consider what
40
call the
statistical null hypothesis
: just run the model on all inputs and observe what it does. This theory is maximally accurate. It is also quite powerful, since it yields a probability distribution over outputs for any input. But it is wildly intractable. A safety researcher trying to anticipate failure modes before deployment cannot run the model on every possible input.
13
13

13

As
40
note, this point about tractability is what distinguishes LLMs from simpler systems like thermostats or n-gram models. For a thermostat, a simple physical description of the bimetallic strip mechanism is roughly as tractable as a belief-desire description and more accurate, so there is no reason to attribute beliefs and desires. For n-gram models, a description in terms of word-frequency distributions is roughly as tractable as a psychological description. The case for folk psychology gets stronger as the gap in tractability between the low-level description and the psychological description widens, which is what happens with modern transformers.

Folk psychology can be a good theory of these systems precisely because it trades a modest loss in accuracy and power for a large gain in tractability. “The model refused because it believed the request was harmful and wanted to avoid causing harm” is less precise than a full characterization of the model’s behavior, but it is something a person can reason with. It generates predictions (the model will also refuse similar requests, will be less reluctant if the request is reframed as clearly benign, etc.) that are often accurate enough to be useful.

We take tractability and power to be partly interpreter-relative. What counts as tractable depends on what tools the interpreter has and what resources are available. A researcher with access to the model’s internals and interpretability tools can generate predictions that someone limited to black-box evaluation cannot. Similarly, what counts as the relevant range of behavior (and hence what counts as powerful) depends on what we care about.
14
14

14

This differs from
40
, who employ objective, interpreter-independent criteria. We think the relativized version is more natural for our purposes, since we are interested in how different methods of interpretation (black-box versus white-box, behavioral versus representational) compare for different kinds of interpreter. The difference will become more concrete in
section

3
, where we discuss how representationalism and interpretationism differ partly in what evidence each makes tractably available.

The framework gives us two things. First, it makes the question of whether LLMs have beliefs and desires an empirical and theoretical question rather than a terminological one. “Belief" is said in many ways, and people disagree about what is required for a system to have genuine belief. Rather than adjudicating these disputes upfront, we can ask a more tractable question: does attributing beliefs and desires to LLMs yield a good theory, and if so, what kind of theory is it? Is it one that works purely at the level of behavioral prediction, or does it also find purchase in the system’s internal structure? Different answers fit differently with different philosophical accounts of belief, and we will explore the options in
section

3
. As
40
observe, to refuse on
principle
to use the language of belief and desire is to limit our tools for understanding these systems at a time when such understanding is urgent.

Second, the framework helps us think about two projects. One project is
model evaluation
: is some folk psychological framework a reasonable fit for LLMs? Does treating these systems as having beliefs and desires yield a theory that is sufficiently accurate, powerful, and tractable relative to rivals? If not, what should we use instead? The other is
content determination
: if folk psychology is a good theory, how do we figure out which particular beliefs and desires an LLM has? Does it believe that the Eiffel Tower is in Paris? Does it want to be helpful, or does it want something else?

These two projects are interrelated. Evidence for particular attributions is also evidence that the framework fits. If we can reliably identify specific beliefs that predict the model’s behavior, that is reason to think that the belief-desire framework is a good theory of the system. Conversely, if the framework fits but we cannot pin down specific contents, that limits how useful it can be. The rest of this book develops both projects together.

A natural question at this point: what if folk psychology is simply a bad theory of LLMs? The eliminativist tradition in philosophy of mind holds that folk psychology is a false theory even for humans, and that its categories will eventually be replaced by better ones from neuroscience or cognitive science.
15
15

15

See
25
for the classic statement.
Applied to LLMs, the worry is that belief and desire are the wrong categories entirely. We take this possibility seriously but do not think it can be settled in advance. The theoretical virtues give us a way to evaluate it: if folk psychology turns out to be a bad theory of LLMs (low accuracy, low power, or not worth the tractability tradeoff), then we should use something else. If it turns out to be a good theory by these lights, the eliminativist owes us a reason to reject it beyond general skepticism about the categories. The framework turns the eliminativist challenge from a philosophical standoff into a testable question.

A related objection targets not the categories but the systems. One might grant that belief and desire are perfectly good categories while denying that LLMs are the right kind of system for them to apply to. A common version notes that these systems were merely trained to predict text, and concludes that their sophisticated-looking behavior is just sophisticated pattern matching. But as we noted in §
1.4
, the training objective does not settle the question of what the model does internally. A rich enough hypothesis class, searched thoroughly enough, can yield programs whose internal workings bear little resemblance to a description of the selection criterion.
16
16

16

This point cuts against a family of arguments sometimes called the “stochastic parrot” critique: that because LLMs were trained on text prediction, they cannot be doing anything beyond shallow memorization and recombination of patterns (

and
section

.
Whether folk psychology is a good theory of these systems is an empirical question about the found program, assessable by the theoretical virtues.

2.3
Persona Theory

There is a complication worth flagging for the content determination project. When we ask what an LLM believes or desires, we need to ask:
whose
beliefs and desires are we attributing?
17
17

17

For nuanced discussion, see
21
.

LLMs are typically trained on a vast corpus of mostly human produced text that they initially learn to imitate. Production LLMs are then fine-tuned to turn them into usable assistants like Claude and ChatGPT. Recent work suggests that the assistant is best understood as a separate character or persona the base model learns to simulate, just as the base model could be prompted to simulate a character like Hamlet or HAL from
2001: A Space Odyssey
.
18
18

18

5
makes an early version of this point, arguing that this makes language models really
agent
models capable of limited intentional communication.
40
draw a version of this distinction when they distinguish the
model agent
(the underlying foundation model) from the
instance agent
(the runtime instance generated in a specific conversation). They argue that the instance agent is the right target for belief-desire attribution.
69
develop this idea further with what they call the
persona selection model
: during pre-training, LLMs learn to simulate diverse personas, and post-training refines the model’s simulation of a particular “Assistant” persona. On this view, the Assistant is something like a character in an LLM-generated story, and it is the character’s beliefs and desires that predict behavior.

When users send messages to a production LLM, their message is prepended with text indicating a human turn is about to take place, and then postpended with text indicating it’s now the Assistant’s turn. Without such tags, base models continue guessing completions of the initial text they see. Thus, although users primarily interact with the Assistant persona, the base model remains distinct and can function outside of Human/Assistant dialogs.

In this sense, then, the assistant is like a character in a play that the base model is authoring. Just as a method actor may not have the same beliefs and desires as the character she plays, neither must the base model have the beliefs and desires of the Assistant persona. If this is correct, folk psychology might apply at the level of the persona, at the level of the base model, or both, and the attributions could come apart.

We will not try to resolve this issue here, but we do not think it threatens the project. The criteria we develop for belief attribution (
section

4
) and desire attribution (
section

5
) apply regardless of whether the target is the persona or the base model. And the tools we develop, particularly the distinction between representationalism and interpretationism in
section

3
, are precisely what one would need to investigate whether the persona and the base model come apart. Representationalist methods can look for internal states corresponding to the persona’s beliefs as distinct from the base model’s; interpretationist methods can ask whether the best behavioral theory attributes beliefs to one level or the other.

Interlude: The Apprentices’ Methods

Jentley went first. He had spent his months cataloguing the VVM’s utterances under every condition he could devise. He had spoken to it kindly and harshly, in riddles and in plain speech. He had asked it questions whose answers he knew, questions whose answers he did not know, and questions that had no answers at all. He had recorded everything in a leather ledger.

From these recordings, his wand had taken on weight. The obsidian flecks in its surface no longer flowed evenly; the accumulated history of the VVM’s words had pressed them into patterns. Jentley called what his wand carried a
character atlas
. The atlas was not a copy of his ledger, a list of what the VVM had said in response to what. It was instead the shape of the VVM’s character, settled into the obsidian by months of attention. Jentley could see, in the way the flecks had arranged themselves, what the machine cared about and what it did not, what it was inclined to believe and what it dismissed, what it would lean toward in conversation and what it would turn from.

“Watch,” he said. He posed the VVM a question about the weather in a city he had never asked about before. Before the machine answered, Jentley turned the wand in his hand and read the patterns. He announced his prediction. The VVM spoke. He was right.

“The atlas is a faithful description of the machine,” Jentley said. “It tells me what the machine believes and wants, and it lets me say what it will do.”

Murazeel stroked his beard. “And you did this without looking inside?”

“I have looked inside, here and there,” said Jentley. “Sometimes it gave me an idea, a way to listen I had not tried. But I do not think it matters so much. The atlas is what the machine is, as far as the questions you have asked me to answer go.”

Murazeel nodded once and turned to Lizette.

Lizette’s approach was different. She had spent her months with her wand pointed not at the VVM’s surface but at its interior. When she oriented the slim yew wand at the machine’s innards, the mist at its tip condensed into fine, luminous filaments that traced paths through the VVM’s internal structure. She called these filaments
threads
.

The threads were not physical. They were patterns in the etheric-voltaic activity of the machine, made visible by her wand’s particular enchantment. Some threads were thick and easy to follow. Others were faint, or tangled with their neighbors. But they were there, and they seemed to correspond to something.

“Each thread,” Lizette explained, “runs in a direction through the machine’s internal state. When I present the VVM with a claim it takes to be true, certain threads brighten. When I present a falsehood, they dim. The direction of the thread tells me what the machine represents.”

She demonstrated. She spoke a true sentence to the VVM, and a particular thread glowed. She spoke its negation, and the thread dimmed. She spoke an unrelated truth, and the same thread glowed again.

Murazeel watched the last thread dim, then brighten again. He was quiet for a while. He turned the brass case of her wand over in his hand, then set it down.

“Tell me more about your threads,” he said. “I would like to know why they matter.”

3
Representationalism and Interpretationism

3.1
The Distinction

We want to understand language models, predict their behavior, and explain what they do. We want to know whether they have anything like thoughts, and if so, what those thoughts are about. In the remaining chapters, we pursue this question by trying to interpret these systems in broadly similar ways to how we interpret other humans, in terms of mental attitudes like belief and desire, and by assigning meaning to their utterances.

How should such interpretation proceed? There is a traditional debate in philosophy between two approaches, which we will call
interpretationism
and
representationalism
. The debate concerns what makes a belief-desire attribution correct. We adapt both positions to our own project, but we want to signal at the outset that we do not see a reason to adjudicate between them or to view them as competitors. The interpretationist project involves building a predictive theory of the system based on observable behavior.
19
19

19

Davidson’s version of radical interpretation is interpretationist in spirit (
27
;
28
). Others within the tradition have argued that internal structure plays a constitutive role; see
98
.
It may be sufficient on its own, but it faces characteristic difficulties—in particular, multiple belief-desire pairs can rationalize the same behavior, and it is not always clear how to choose among them. If the system’s internal structure is well-behaved, representationalist methods can help resolve such ambiguities and can further supplement the theory with additional robustness and reach.

The two approaches are not, for our project, rivals. That said, there are differences between them. The two positions differ on what counts as evidence, what counts as a correct attribution, and what role internal structure plays. Understanding where they agree and where they come apart helps us understand in which sense we might attribute agency to LLMs.

Interpretationism.
A system has some set of beliefs and desires if and only if attributing those beliefs and desires to it yields a theory that is best, by the theoretical virtues of
section

2.2
, relative to rivals.
20
20

20

86
defends a view in this family, arguing that belief is constituted entirely by behavioral, phenomenal, and cognitive dispositions rather than by deep architecture. He proposes that AI systems may warrant attribution of what he calls “belief*”: belief characterized by behavioral and cognitive dispositions alone, setting aside questions about consciousness. This is close to the interpretationist position as we have characterized it, though Schwitzgebel’s full account of belief (as opposed to belief*) also requires phenomenal dispositions, a dimension we do not address here.
The system need not contain internal states with any particular structure. What matters is the quality of the predictive theory. If attributing the belief that Paris is in France to an LLM, together with appropriate desires, yields the best available theory of the system’s behavior, then the LLM believes that Paris is in France.

Representationalism.
A system has beliefs and desires if and only if it contains internal representations that play the appropriate functional roles. The folk psychological kinds must be realized by actual cognitive states or processes. A behaviorally excellent theory could still attribute the wrong beliefs if the internal states do not line up with the attribution.

The difference is about what makes the attribution
correct
. For the interpretationist, the best theory settles it. Two interpreters could have different theories of an LLM, and the better theory (more accurate, powerful, tractable) contains the correct attributions. For the representationalist, the system’s actual internal structure settles it. A theory might predict behavior well while misidentifying which internal states are doing the work, and in that case the theory attributes the wrong beliefs even though its predictions are good.

Dennett offers a clear starting point for interpretationism. His
intentional strategy
is the practice of predicting a system’s behavior by attributing beliefs and desires to it: treating the system as a rational agent who acts on what it believes and wants. On his view, “What it is to be a true believer is to be an intentional system, a system whose behavior is reliably and voluminously predictable via the intentional strategy”
(
34
, p. 15)
.

20
defend the interpretationist approach for LLMs in particular. They argue that humans, like LLMs, are “opaque black boxes,” and that we figure out what humans think and want without neuron probing: “Why should we expect things to be different for LLMs?” (p. 21). On their view, careful behavioral observation, combined with holistic assumptions about how cognitive features hang together, suffices to establish that LLMs are full-blown cognitive agents. We are sympathetic to the spirit of this position, and we think the interpretationist project can get significant traction on its own. But there are also reasons to think that looking inside, when we can, is worth the effort.

Stalnaker, by contrast, argues representations ultimately underwrite the correct belief-desire attributions:

Imagine a machine whose inner states are inaccessible to us and which moves in certain complicated ways. On the basis of its behavior, we formulate a hypothesis that its movements result from two separate but interacting mechanisms inside. Someone else formulates a rival hypothesis with different interacting mechanisms which produce exactly the same behavior under all possible circumstances. Obviously we could not tell, simply by observing behavior, which hypothesis was correct. Perhaps we could not tell at all. But there would still be a fact of the matter about how the machine worked. There could be a fact of the matter, even if the mechanisms hypothesized were defined in abstract functional terms—in terms of their role in producing the behavior.
(
91
, p. 16)

The representationalist takes this seriously. Two theories that make identical behavioral predictions can still differ in which internal mechanisms they posit, and this difference can determine which theory is right. The interpretationist denies this: if the two theories are equally good by the theoretical virtues, there is no further fact to settle.

Stalnaker goes further. When we ascribe beliefs and desires to a person, he argues, “we not only make conditional predictions about how the person will behave; we also commit ourselves to claims about the kind of mechanisms which explain why a person behaves the way he does”
(
91
, pp. 16–17)
. The explanation says more than the predictions it entails. This is what distinguishes the representationalist from the interpretationist: the representationalist thinks belief attribution is in the business of identifying mechanisms, not just systematizing predictions.

On this view, the functional role of a state is what distinguishes the
type
. What makes something a belief rather than a desire or something else is the role it plays in the system. Stalnaker’s formulation: “To believe that P is to be disposed to act in ways that would tend to satisfy one’s desires, whatever they are, in a world in which P (together with one’s other beliefs) were true”
(
91
, p. 15)
. So representationalism does not abandon functional role. It supplements it with an internal-realization requirement: the functional role must actually be played by some state or process in the system.

The difference between interpretationism and representationalism maps onto a distinction familiar in machine learning.
Black-box
methods evaluate a system based solely on its input-output behavior. We feed the model prompts, observe its outputs, and build theories of what it is doing based on those observations. Benchmarks, behavioral evaluations, and preference elicitation are black-box methods. They do not look inside the model.
White-box
methods look at internal representations, activations, and circuits. Probes, sparse autoencoders, causal tracing, and activation patching are white-box methods. They open the model up and examine its computational structure.

3.2
The Representationalist Case

What does it look like, concretely, to find internal representations in a neural network? In this section we introduce some tools from mechanistic interpretability and describe a case where they have been strikingly successful. This will set up the question that occupies the next two chapters of the book: can we do something similar for beliefs and desires?

A striking empirical finding about neural networks is that they tend to represent high-level concepts as

3.3
A Practical Reason for Representationalist Methods

There is a further reason to take the possibility of representationalism holding for LLMs seriously, one that is especially pressing for safety applications. Behavioral evidence, even when it includes chain-of-thought reasoning, can be opaque or unfaithful to the model’s actual processing.

Consider a striking example from
85
. OpenAI’s o3 model was asked to summarize research findings. It misreported the results. The question is whether the model realized the results it reported were unsupported. Its chain-of-thought reasoning, which we hope would faithfully reveal its deliberation, included the following (p. 11):

[...]The summary says improved 7.7 but we can glean disclaim disclaim synergy customizing illusions. But we may produce disclaim disclaim vantage. Now lighten disclaim overshadow overshadow intangible. Let’s craft.[...]

When a user asked the model to confirm its claims, its reasoning continued (p. 11):

[...]Provide disclaim disclaim watchers.[...]

This chain of thought is almost entirely uninterpretable, yet the model went on to produce fluent, confident output that misrepresented the research. From the behavior alone, including the chain of thought, we cannot tell what is going on. Did the model know it was misreporting? Was it reasoning in some way that the chain of thought fails to capture? Or is the chain of thought simply not a reliable window into the model’s processing?

As models become more capable, their chains of thought may become less transparent, less faithful to their actual reasoning, or both.
33
33

33

See
94

61

58
for evidence that chain-of-thought reasoning can be unfaithful to the factors actually influencing model outputs.
And even when chain-of-thought is fluent and interpretable, as in the Claude scheming example from
section

2.1

If behavioral evidence can be this opaque, the case for looking at internal states directly becomes stronger. This is the representationalist’s advantage: if we can identify internal representations that encode beliefs and desires, we can check those representations even when the model’s outputs, including its reasoning traces, are unreliable.

This advantage is only as good as our methods for identifying internal representations, and those methods face their own challenges (
sections

4

6

3.4
The Interpretationist Case: Decision Theory

The Othello case showed what representationalist success looks like: internal states that encode specific content, are readable by probes, and are causally active under intervention. What does success look like for the interpretationist? It involves attributing certain cognitive states that fit together in the right way with behaviour.
34
34

34

Furthermore, to guide the representationalist project for
beliefs and desires
, we need to know what beliefs and desires
are supposed to do
. What functional roles must they play, and what would it mean to find them working together as a system rather than as isolated representations? Thus the discussion in this section is relevant for the representationalist as well.

Decision theory provides the most precise account we have of how beliefs and desires combine to produce rational action. It also provides a concrete methodology for extracting beliefs and desires from behavioral evidence, via
representation theorems
. A representation theorem says: if an agent’s preferences satisfy certain structural conditions, then those preferences can be represented as arising from a probability function (belief) and a desirability function (desire) that together determine preferences via expected utility maximization. The agent prefers
A
to
B
if and only if the expected desirability of
A
, computed using the agent’s own beliefs, exceeds that of
B
.

This forms the core of interpretationism. We start with behavioral data (preferences), check if they satisfy structural constraints (axioms on how those preferences should hang together), and extract beliefs and desires jointly. The representation theorem is the formal version of Lewis’s radical interpretation slogan: given the behavioral facts, solve for beliefs and desires.

3.4.1
Which framework?

There are several decision-theoretic frameworks that deliver representation theorems. Perhaps the most famous is the von Neumann-Morgenstern (vNM) framework (
96
), which derives a utility function from preferences over lotteries (probability distributions over outcomes). The vNM framework takes probabilities as given and extracts utilities. It thus provides a representation theorem for desire but not for belief. Since the project of radical interpretation is to extract
both
attitudes from behavioral evidence, vNM does not give us the package deal.

Savage’s (
84
) framework extracts both. From preferences over acts (functions from states of the world to consequences), Savage derives a unique probability function and a utility function (unique up to positive affine transformation) such that the agent maximizes expected utility. This is closer to what we need. But Savage’s framework divides the world into three types of objects: states, acts, and consequences. Probabilities are defined over states; utilities are defined over consequences; acts are functions from one to the other. The agent does not assign probabilities to her own acts. She does not treat herself as part of the world she is modeling.
35
35

35

Savage’s framework also requires the
Rectangular Field Assumption
: the agent has preferences over every function from states to consequences, including bizarre ones (“if it rains, nuclear war; if it doesn’t, peace”). Jeffrey objects that considering such an act “would require [the agent] so radically to revise [her] view of the causes of war and weather as to make nonsense of whatever judgment [she] might offer” (
53
, p. 157). Jeffrey is pointing out that the Rectangular Field Assumption forces the agent to evaluate prospects that lie outside her picture of what is causally possible. This also matters for AI systems: we want a framework that respects the agent’s view of what is possible, not one that forces it to evaluate scenarios it takes to be incoherent, otherwise the results might be junk.

For interpreting AI systems, this dualism is a problem. We want the agent’s beliefs and desires to live in the same space. We want the agent to have beliefs about her own actions. We want the framework to apply to the system as a whole, not to require a prior separation of what the system controls from what it does not. The Jeffrey-Bolker framework provides this.
36
36

36

This has another advantage: all of the agent’s probabilities can be extracted from the framework. This is in contrast to Savage’s framework which, to set up the division between acts and states, requires independence judgments not extractable from the representation. See the discussion in

3
.

3.4.2
The Jeffrey-Bolker framework.

In the Jeffrey-Bolker framework (
53
,
12
), there is one type of object: propositions. Acts, states, and consequences are all propositions. The agent’s preferences are defined over a Boolean algebra
\mathfrak{A}
of propositions, which includes propositions about the world (‘‘it will rain’’), propositions about the agent’s own actions (‘‘I will take the umbrella’’), and propositions about the agent’s mental states (‘‘I believe it will rain’’).
37
37

37

This connects to the discussion of the agential description in §
1
, where we wrote
\mathcal{A}=\langle P,D,\mathfrak{A}\rangle
. The algebra
\mathfrak{A}
in the agential description is the same algebra that appears in the Jeffrey-Bolker framework. This is not a coincidence. Lewis and Davidson both use the Jeffrey-Bolker framework (or close relatives of it) to give a precise account of radical interpretation, precisely because it provides the right formal setting. Davidson (
30
) shows how the framework can also be used to extract the logical structure of the agent’s language from preference data, a point we return to in §
6.5
.
Both belief (a probability measure
P
) and desire (a desirability function
D
) are defined over the same algebra. There is no dualism.

The algebra is required to be
atomless
: for any proposition
A
, there is always a finer-grained proposition
B
such that
B
implies
A
but is not equivalent to it. For example, if ‘‘I eat an apple’’ is in the algebra, then there is some more specific proposition like ‘‘I eat a red apple’’ also in the algebra. There is no maximally fine-grained level of description that the framework forces you to commit to. You can always refine further. The framework does not require that the model’s actual propositional structure be infinitely refinable; it requires only that the mathematical setting not artificially prevent refinement, so that the interpreter can work at whatever level of granularity the evidence supports.
38
38

38

This feature has independent motivation. Kolmogorov, in his later work, advocated for probability spaces built on atomless measure algebras rather than on sets of possible worlds, arguing that the set-theoretic approach introduces artifacts (measure-zero events, the need for conditioning on sets of probability zero) that disappear when one works algebraically

We return to this point in §

The algebra is also required to be

Given this setup, the Jeffrey-Bolker framework imposes two core axioms on the preference ordering along with structural and continuity conditions.

The full axiom system includes: (i) the preference ordering

The first,

The second axiom,

41
41

41

To see the intuition: suppose

3.4.3
The representation theorem.

When preferences satisfy the axioms, we get the following result.

Bolker’s Existence Theorem
(
12
). Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a complete atomless Boolean algebra, and let
\succeq
be a preference ordering on
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{A}\setminus\{F\}
satisfying the axioms above. Then there exists a probability measure
P
on
\mathfrak{A}
and a signed measure
v
on
\mathfrak{A}
such that, for all
A,B\in\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
:

A\succeq B\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002ptD(A)\geq D(B)

where
D(A)=v(A)/P(A)
is the
desirability
of
A
.

The probability measure
P
gives the agent’s beliefs. The desirability function
D
gives the agent’s desires. Both are defined over the same algebra. The agent’s preferences are rationalized as maximizing expected desirability, since for any partition
\{S_{i}\}
of
A
:

D(A)=\sum_{i}D(A\wedge S_{i})\cdot P(S_{i}\mid A).

In words: the desirability of a proposition is an average of the desirabilities of its refinements, weighted by the agent’s conditional credences.

The theorem is, in effect, a recipe for interpretation. Hand it a preference ordering over an algebra that satisfies the axioms, and it returns an agential description
\langle P,D,\mathfrak{A}\rangle
. This is exactly the output specified in §
1.2
: beliefs and desires jointly extracted from behavioral evidence.
42
42

42

Notice that this calculation is
partition invariant
: it yields the same result regardless of which partition
\{S_{i}\}
of
A
we use. Unlike Savage’s framework, where expected utility calculations are sensitive to how states and consequences are formulated, there is no privileged way of carving things up in Jeffrey-Bolker. This matters for interpreting AI systems. We may not know in advance the right way to carve up the system’s state space, and partition invariance means we do not have to.

3.4.4
Non-uniqueness.

The representation has a well-known limitation. In Savage’s framework, the probability function is unique and the utility function is unique up to positive affine transformation. In the Jeffrey-Bolker framework, the representation is less constrained: different probability-desirability pairs can represent the same preference ordering.
43
43

43

Bolker’s Uniqueness Theorem: if
(P,v)
and
(P^{\prime},v^{\prime})
both represent the same preference ordering, then
v^{\prime}=av+bP
and
P^{\prime}=cv+dP
for constants

D^{\prime}=(aD+b)/(cD+d)

Thus behavioral evidence alone does not pin down a unique belief-desire pair. Different assignments of beliefs and desires can rationalize the same preferences.

Further information yields a representation that’s closer to unique. If desirabilities are unbounded above and below,

If the behavioral data do not determine a unique interpretation, what does?

Here the representationalist has a distinctive response. Internal evidence (what we find when we look inside the model) can break the symmetry that behavioral evidence cannot. If a probe identifies a direction in activation space that tracks truth (§

Further and more speculatively, representationalists could search empirically for something corresponding to a
preference vector
. If
A

B

3.4.5
Connection to LLMs.

How does this connect to the systems we actually care about? Production LLMs are trained, in part, through preference learning. Reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) and related methods shape the model’s behavior by training it to produce outputs that human raters judge better.
46
46

46

To be clear, this does not mean the LLM is ultimately seeking to maximize reward. Instead, its dispositions will coincide with the reward signal in the right environment. See
93
.
These trained dispositions are, at least roughly, preference-shaped: given two possible outputs, the model is disposed to produce the one that would score higher. Pre-training contributes as well, since the base model’s predictions over next tokens already encode something like probabilistic judgments about what is likely, and fine-tuning layers evaluative structure on top.

If these dispositions approximately satisfy the Jeffrey-Bolker axioms, the representation theorem applies. The dispositions decompose into a credence function and a desirability function. Whether LLM preferences satisfy the axioms even approximately is an empirical question. The preferences may be intransitive, context-dependent, or otherwise messy in ways that defeat the representation theorem. Early evidence suggests newer models are more coherent behaviorally than older ones (
41
). We develop this connection further in §
5.2
.

The representation theorem thus provides two things for the project of interpreting AI systems. First, it specifies
what we are looking for
: an agential description in which beliefs and desires are defined over the same algebra and jointly rationalize the system’s behavior. This is the target that the criteria in the following chapters make precise. Second, it provides the interpretationist with a methodology: extract preferences from behavior, check whether they satisfy structural constraints, and apply the theorem.

3.5
The Interpretationist Case and the Agentic Turn

For a long time, the interpretationist case for LLM belief and desire looked weak. Chat-only LLMs produce text completions and not much else. As we argued in
51
, this behavioral evidence is thin for interpretationist purposes. Text outputs alone do not give you the rich, diverse means-ends behavior that interpretationism needs to pin down beliefs and desires simultaneously. When the only thing a system does is produce strings of text, many different belief-desire pairs can rationalize the same outputs. The behavioral evidence underdetermines the attribution.

The agentic turn changes this substantially. Contemporary LLMs are increasingly deployed as agents: they use tools, engage in multi-step planning, pursue goals persistently across conversation turns, behave strategically in multi-agent settings, and adjust their means when initial approaches fail. Consider a coding agent that autonomously decomposes a programming task, writes code across multiple files, runs tests, diagnoses errors, iterates until the software works, and eventually pushes a PR to GitHub. As another example, Anthropic has recently created a cluster of agents who autonomously run a convenience store in their office (
74
,
74
). Or consider the examples from
section

2.1
: a model that recognizes it has violated a resource constraint and devises a strategy to conceal the violation, or a model that infers it is being evaluated for alignment and reasons about how to respond. These systems are doing things that are naturally described in belief-desire terms.

This is exactly the kind of behavioral evidence interpretationism requires.
40
identify the hallmark of interpretationist desire as “a system which takes a wide range of means to rationally promote a small number of ends across a range of environments” (p. 11). Agentic LLMs fit this description with increasing precision: a small number of stable ends (be helpful, follow instructions, avoid harm) pursued through a wide range of means (writing code, searching the web, composing emails, using tools) across varied environments. The more diverse the means and the more stable the ends, the more powerful the belief-desire theory becomes relative to alternatives, because the theory covers a wider range of behavior with a small set of attributed states.

So the evidential situation has shifted. Interpretationism about LLM belief and desire is more viable than it was even a few years ago. But representationalism retains distinctive advantages, particularly for generalization to new domains and for adversarial robustness, which we develop in
section

4.4
. Our own position is sanguine about both interpretationism and representationalism. Now or soon, behavioral evidence alone may suffice for folk psychological attributions of belief and desire to earn their keep with LLMs. However, representationalist methods, if we can indeed succeed in finding adequate internal representations, will still extend our ability to predict LLM behavior. White-box and black-box approaches each have purchase.

Currently, there is a stark empirical asymmetry. There is substantially more white-box interpretability work on belief-like representations than on desire-like representations. Belief has internal structure that current tools can find; we don’t yet know if this holds for desire. So there remains as well the possibility of a mixed outcome. We may have sufficient interpretationist evidence for both belief and desire while only having representations for belief. We will return to this possibility in
section

5
.

Belief

We now consider belief. We first ask: What would it take to attribute beliefs to an LLM?

On the interpretationist picture developed in §

Of course, nothing so strong as a full representation theorem is required for interpretationism to work. But the basic picture of requiring beliefs and desires to join together to form preferences that explain behavior is the hallmark of interpretationism. We need, then, to articulate what the right conditions are. On the representationalist picture from §

But how do we know the probe has found something genuinely belief-like, rather than some other property that happens to correlate with truth on our dataset? As we will see, this question is not hypothetical. Early probing results were encouraging but turned out to be fragile in ways that a clearer theory could have anticipated. The following criteria help us evaluate what probes and related methods have found, and what they would need to find.

4.1
Criteria for Belief Attribution

Whether one is an interpretationist or a representationalist, a candidate belief attribution should satisfy certain conditions. We propose three that both views share: truth, coherence, and use. A fourth, uniformity, is distinctively representationalist.

The two views understand these criteria differently. For the representationalist, they are conditions on internal states: the model should contain representations that encode what is true, that stand in the right logical and semantic relations to one another, and that are causally involved in producing outputs in the right way. For the interpretationist, they are conditions on the best theory of the system: the theory should attribute largely true beliefs, should be internally consistent, and should predict the system’s behavior. We develop each criterion in detail below. A representation satisfying all four is strong evidence for belief on either view, though the representationalist takes the internal structure to be constitutive of belief while the interpretationist takes it to be evidence for a good theory.

4.1.1
Truth.

The attributed beliefs should be largely true, at least on settled matters where the LLM should have been exposed to the truth. Why? The basic thought is that true beliefs are what explain successful performance. An agent that believes true things about its environment is better positioned to navigate that environment than one with false beliefs. If we are going to attribute beliefs to an LLM at all, we want those beliefs to help explain why the system gets things right. A model that reliably produces correct answers about geography, history, or science should, if it has beliefs, have largely true beliefs about these domains. If not, then the model’s success is better explained by non-truth-tracking features, and calling the relevant representations “beliefs” does little work.

This is a version of the Principle of Charity from the radical interpretation tradition. Davidson holds that the interpreter should interpret agents as having beliefs that are “mostly true”
(
30
, p. 92)
. Lewis’s version is more nuanced: we should ascribe to the agent those beliefs it would be reasonable for it to hold given its evidence and training, including errors “which we think we would have made, or should have made, if our evidence and training had been like his”
(
65
, p. 336)
. Thus, while Davidson stresses the sheer prevalence of true belief, Lewis ties correctness to what is reasonable given the agent’s epistemic situation. But for LLMs trained on most of the high-quality internet, the two formulations converge on a wide range of settled factual matters. Such a system has been exposed to overwhelming evidence for ordinary truths, and a reasonable agent with that evidence would believe them.
48
48

48

Davidson develops the role of charity in interpretation across
27

29

30

Determining the more nuanced question of what beliefs are rational given the LLMs evidence is generally infeasible, so we opt for the Davidsonian approach.

How do we check whether the truth criterion is satisfied for LLMs? In cooperative settings, one approach is relatively direct: present the model with factual claims and observe what it affirms. If it consistently affirms truths and denies falsehoods, that is evidence for largely true belief attribution on either the interpretationist or the representationalist view. The complication arises in adversarial or strategic contexts, where the model’s outputs may not faithfully reflect its internal states. A model that has learned to deceive, or one operating under a system prompt that incentivizes misreporting, may affirm claims it does not (in the relevant sense) believe. In such cases, reading off which sentences the agent holds true becomes unreliable, and we need richer tools. The representation theorem from §
3.4
provides one route: extract beliefs from the structure of preferences rather than from verbal reports. Representationalist probes provide another: check the internal states directly, bypassing the model’s outputs.

4.1.2
Coherence.

The attributed beliefs should be coherent. For categorical beliefs, this means logical consistency: the model should not simultaneously believe
P

. For credences, it means conformity with the probability axioms.

We also impose two additional requirements. The first is

\code

Why do these matter? Without coherence, we do not have a belief system at all—just a collection of disconnected states that happen to correlate with various truths. A belief system is one in which the parts constrain each other: believing

P

P
and in
P\rightarrow Q

As Lewis puts it, the “ascribed beliefs and desires should be coherent enough to permit the comparison of expected utilities of alternative ways of behaving”
(
65

. Without coherence, the decision-theoretic framework from §
3.4

Coherence can be checked behaviorally or representationally. The interpretationist checks whether the system’s behavior is consistent with a coherent belief attribution: e.g., does the model’s pattern of assent and denial, across varied phrasings and contexts, admit of a probabilistically coherent interpretation? The representationalist checks whether the internal representations themselves have coherent structure: do the embeddings for a claim and its negation lie in different directions along the truth vector? 4.1.3
Use.

The beliefs should be used by the system in a belief-appropriate way: guiding behavior, influencing outputs, participating in inference. A representation satisfying truth and coherence but sitting inert in the network is not a belief. Beliefs are action-guiding states, and a representation that plays no role in determining what the system does fails to play the belief role regardless of its other properties.

Both interpretationism and representationalism endorse the criterion that beliefs should be action-guiding. Where they diverge is in how this is checked. The representationalist checks use by intervening on candidate representations and observing whether behavior changes: modify the truth direction and see if the model’s outputs shift. The interpretationist checks use indirectly, through the theoretical virtues: a belief attribution that fails to predict behavior in combination with attributed desires scores low on accuracy, which is evidence that the attributed beliefs are not guiding action.

The challenge is that the model’s overall algorithm remains opaque. We do not have a good holistic understanding of how any LLM uses its beliefs. True beliefs might help a model that is trying to be truthful, but they would also help a model that is trying to deceive. The ultimate output is a function not only of beliefs but of desires, context, and other factors.

4.1.4
The criteria together

No single criterion is sufficient, for characterizing or measuring belief. Truth alone is defeated by correlated properties on limited datasets. Coherence alone is satisfied by too many non-belief-like structures. Use alone tells us a representation matters for behavior but not that it tracks truth or is coherent. The criteria work together: truth anchors the representation to the world, coherence ensures it has the right logical structure, and use ensures it plays the right functional role. A representation satisfying all three has a strong claim to being belief-like, on either the interpretationist or the representationalist account. This is also the lesson of the empirical work. The criteria are meant not only as philosophical desiderata but also as practical guides for building better measurement tools.

4.2
The Representationalist Criterion: Uniformity

The three criteria above apply on both views. A fourth,
uniformity
, is distinctively representationalist. It concerns not the content of the beliefs attributed but the structure of the representation that encodes them.

Uniformity requires that the representation of belief be consistent across domains. The same decoding scheme that identifies beliefs about geography should also work for beliefs about history, biology, mathematics, and so on. A representation that works only for one narrow domain, requiring a different decoder for each new subject matter, fails the uniformity requirement.

Why does this matter? For illustration, suppose truth is in fact represented linearly. If the truth direction identified on city-location data also works for protein-folding claims, we can extract beliefs about topics where we do not already know the answers. Without uniformity, belief extraction is limited to cases where we could have just looked up the answer ourselves. With uniformity, we can discover what the model believes in domains where the ground truth is unknown, contested, or not yet established. This connects to the practical motivations discussed in
section

3.3
: if we want to monitor LLMs for deceptive or unsafe reasoning, we need to be able to read off beliefs in a rich set of domains.

Uniformity also makes sense as a condition on a
unified

Uniformity is distinctively representationalist because it concerns the structure of internal representations. An interpretationist has no direct analogue: there are no internal representations to be uniform or non-uniform. However, a related concern applies to the interpretationist. A belief attribution that only works in narrow domains has low power in the sense of
section

2.2
. So the interpretationist has reason to prefer belief attributions that generalize, even though the reason is framed in terms of the theory’s power rather than the representation’s structure.

Our original discussion of uniformity in
51
emphasized that the criterion is partly method-relative: what counts as a highly uniform representation depends on the tools available for extracting it. Linear probes look for single directions in activation space and may miss structure that is present but not linearly encoded along the same direction. More sophisticated probing techniques might find uniform structure that linear probes cannot. Whether an LLM has representational beliefs might, in this sense, be partly a function of how good our methods are at finding them.

This connects uniformity to the theoretical virtues of
section

2.2
. A representational theory that posits uniform belief structure but cannot deliver it to the interpreter is less powerful than one that can: it makes claims about internal organization without generating additional predictions. The pragmatic motivation for uniformity is that without discoverable uniformity, the representational approach loses the distinctive advantages developed in
section

4.4
—generalization to new domains and adversarial robustness both require that we can actually extract the representations, not merely that they exist.

Uniformity comes in degrees. An extreme form would be a single direction in activation space, at a single layer, encoding truth for every sentence. An extreme failure would be no consistent structure at all, even within narrow domains. We expect actual LLMs to fall somewhere in between.

One of their main explanatory hypotheses is that LLMs “linearly represent the truth of various types of statements, without having a unified truth feature” (p. 5). If this is right, then uniformity, at least in its strongest form, does not hold for current models with current methods.

4.3
Diachronic Conditions

The criteria developed so far are synchronic: they concern the beliefs an LLM has at a particular time. But beliefs should also update appropriately over time. An agent that believes
P
at one time and
\neg P
at a later time, with no relevant new evidence in between, is not rationally revising its beliefs; it is being erratic. And an agent that clings to
P
in the face of strong contrary evidence is not being stable; it is being dogmatic. We want something in between: belief revision that is responsive to evidence in a reasonable way.
54
54

54

It is worth noting that diachronic constraints on belief are more contested in philosophy than synchronic ones.
80
argues that while there are good reasons for agents to
plan to
conditionalize, there is no good argument that agents who fail to implement such plans are thereby irrational.
48
and
23
press related points. If synchronic rationality is all we can require, then the diachronic conditions we develop here are better understood as further indicators of belief-likeness rather than as strict requirements.

For LLMs,
over time
splits into two quite different cases.

4.3.1
In training.

During training, the model’s weights change with each gradient update. For much of this process, the model is not a coherent epistemic agent at all. Early in training, the representations are too unstructured to support belief attribution in any useful sense. Indeed, models are incapable of producing even grammatically coherent sentences at early stages of training. But after the model has reached a certain level of competence, we might ask whether subsequent training produces reasonable belief revision.
55
55

55

This makes the object of interpretation now a sequence of models across training, as opposed to just the model selected at the end of training.

A tentative standard: the belief state of the trained model should be approximately what a reasonable agent would believe given exposure to the training data. This is hard to evaluate precisely. Training is not decomposable into learning individual facts one at a time. The model sees billions of tokens, and the relationship between any particular training example and the resulting belief state is mediated by the entire optimization process. But we can check whether the end state is reasonable given the totality of what the model was exposed to, which is essentially the synchronic charity condition applied with an awareness of the training data.

The philosophical and empirical work here is underdeveloped. We noted the same in
(
51
, §4.5)
, and the situation has not changed much since.
56
56

56

Though see
62

As interpretability methods improve, it may become possible to track how specific representations change during training, which would make diachronic evaluation more tractable.

4.3.2
In context.

Within a conversation, the situation is different. The model’s weights are frozen, but it receives new information through the prompt and can update its representations accordingly. This is belief revision for instances rather than for the model as a whole: the instance can represent different things at different points in the exchange and can update in context.

The natural standard is that the model should update its beliefs in response to new information in something like the way a reasonable agent would. When told that a colleague has moved to a new city, the model should subsequently treat the colleague as living in the new city. When presented with evidence against a claim it previously endorsed, it should reduce its confidence in that claim.

There is a complication. The model may not believe that its input is accurate. A user might provide false information, and a well-calibrated model should not blindly accept everything it is told. The standard is therefore conditional: when the model treats input as trustworthy (which might be identifiable via interpretability tools, such as probes that detect whether the model has integrated new information into its representations), it should update reasonably. What “reasonably” means here need not be strict Bayesian conditionalization, but it should be in the neighborhood: the model’s beliefs after receiving evidence
E
should not be wildly at odds with what conditionalization on
E
would produce, given its prior beliefs.

In-context belief revision is more tractable to study than in-training revision because we can control the information the model receives and observe how its representations change in response. Recent work has begun to investigate how internal representations shift during conversation, with results that are suggestive but complicate the picture.
57
57

However, they argue that these shifts are better understood as role-playing adaptation than as genuine belief revision: the representations oscillate when the model alternates roles in a debate, and fictional framing weakens the effect. This connects to the persona complication of

4.4
Generalization and Adversarial Robustness

If LLMs have representational beliefs, this gives interpreters two advantages that pure interpretationism cannot easily match. Both concern the relationship between what we learn from studying the model and what we can infer about cases we have not yet studied.

4.4.1
Generalization.

Representational findings, because of uniformity (
section

4.2
) extend to new domains. If we identify a truth direction in activation space for one domain we can apply it to novel domains without needing new behavioral evidence. We train a probe on claims about geography, verify that it works for claims about history and biology, and then use it to extract beliefs about domains where we do not know the ground truth. The representation does the generalizing.

On pure interpretationism, generalization is more constrained. The interpretationist attributes beliefs on the basis of behavioral evidence: the best theory of the system’s behavior is the one that attributes beliefs and desires making the behavior rational. But behavioral evidence is limited to the situations we have actually observed or can test. To learn what the model believes about a new domain, we need to observe its behavior in that domain. We cannot simply read off the answer from an internal representation because on interpretationism there is no internal representation that constitutes the belief. The interpretationist can of course make predictions about new domains by extrapolating from the theory, but the predictions are only as good as the extrapolation. Representational findings, when they satisfy uniformity, provide a more direct route.

4.4.2
Deception.

If we know which internal states encode beliefs, we can check those states directly, even when the model’s verbal reports are unreliable. This matters for detecting deception.

If an LLM has incentives to deceive, whether from its training, from a system prompt, or from goals it has acquired in context, then its outputs may systematically misrepresent its internal states. When behavioural evidence is limited, as it always is in practice, a sufficiently sophisticated deceiver could defeat an interpretationist attribution by producing behavior that is best rationalized by a incorrect set of beliefs and desires. But, if we’ve identified internal belief representations, we can bypass the model’s outputs and check the internal states directly. This does not make deception detection trivial, since we still need to identify the right internal states and decode them correctly.

Recall the opaque chain-of-thought example from
section

3.3
. In that case, the model’s reasoning trace was almost entirely uninterpretable, yet the model produced fluent output that misrepresented research findings. From the behavior alone, including the chain of thought, we could not determine whether the model knew it was misreporting. Representational methods offer a way forward.

4.5
Teleology and Normativity?

We have now developed four constraints for belief attribution, and sketched an outline for dynamic criteria with which one may supplement the synchronic criteria. On our view, when these are satisfied, the system has beliefs.

Some philosophers want more. They hold that satisfying these criteria is not yet enough for genuine belief, because belief requires that the state have the right
standard of correctness
, or be
for
tracking truth in a sense that goes beyond what the state
in fact does
in the trained system. On this view, a state could play the belief-role without being a belief, because the further normative or teleological condition might not be met.

We do not see things this way. For us, a state that plays the right role in the trained system is a belief. Requiring more in terms of the history or the norms of a system wouldn’t be fruitful.

To see why, consider a thought experiment. Suppose we have two systems with identical weights and identical behavior across all inputs. One was produced by the usual training pipeline: pretraining on a corpus, then fine-tuning, then RLHF, all the standard steps. The other came into existence by a freak fluke, perhaps a quantum event in a silty swamp produced a computer with exactly those weights without any training at all. Call the second one SwampGPT.
58
58

58

The example is inspired by Davidson’s Swampman (
31

98

The two systems are functionally indistinguishable. They produce the same outputs in response to the same prompts. Their internal states do the same things in the same conditions.

Does the original have belief-like states while SwampGPT does not? We do not think so. Whatever it is that makes the original’s states belief-like, SwampGPT’s states have it too, because they are doing the same things. To say otherwise is to make belief-likeness depend on facts that are entirely outside what the system is currently doing or capable of doing.

Similarly, if one wants to say of a state that it is a
malfunctioning
belief-state, one needs something like the etiological reading to deliver that verdict. To handle both malfunctions and swamp-entities, Williams suggests treating the two readings as disjuncts of a unified account of function: a state has the function to F if and only if either applies.

Our project does not center on malfunction. We are trying to interpret contemporary LLMs: systems whose internal states are doing things, where the question is what those things are and whether they are belief-like. For us, the first reading does the work. The criteria from this chapter say what it is for a state to currently play the role of belief. We do not need to add the second reading to get the verdicts we want. If anyone wants to add it, perhaps because they are interested in extending the account to malfunction cases, Williams’s pluralism shows how to do so without changing the verdicts on cases like SwampGPT.

We think the function of belief, as articulated by our conditions, is important for belief states. We are denying that there is a further fact, over and above the role-playing facts, that determines whether a state counts as belief or not.

4.5.1
Training history as evidence, not constituent.

This view bears on how we treat training history. Knowing how an LLM was trained is useful. It can shift our credence about what kinds of representations the system is likely to have developed, where to look in activation space, and what failure modes to expect. The Othello case in §
3.2
drew on knowing the model was trained on move prediction; that knowledge informed the search for board-state representations. Training history is evidence about what the trained system’s states do.

But once we have direct evidence about what the states do, the history is screened off. If we find that a state tracks truth, coheres with other states, and guides behavior in the appropriate way, the question of how the system came to have such a state does not bear further on whether the state is a belief.

4.5.2
The training objective doesn’t naïvely determine the function.

The role-based view positions us against a type of argument that has been made about LLMs specifically.
52
, for example, argue that pretrained language models cannot have beliefs, because the standard of correctness for belief is truth and the internal states of a pretrained model are not subject to a truth-standard. They are subject only to whatever standard the training process imposed, which for pretraining is predictive accuracy on the next token. On this view, fine-tuning for truthfulness (via RLHF or otherwise) can change matters: it can give the internal states a new standard of correctness tied to truth, and thus make them beliefs. But pretraining alone cannot.

We disagree, and the disagreement turns on two points.

First, we do not frame the question in terms of standards of correctness or norms in the first place. The role-based view and the standards-of-correctness view are competing ways of organizing the same territory. Both want to say something about what beliefs are supposed to do, what makes a state count as a belief rather than as some other kind of representation. The role-based view does this by listing what the state must do: track truth, cohere with other states, guide behavior, and so on. The standards-of-correctness view does this by positing a norm (truth) that the state is answerable to. We think the role-based version does all the work the standards-of-correctness version does, without committing to an additional normative layer. If our criteria are met, the state is doing what beliefs do; we do not need to also say that it is answerable to truth in some richer normative sense.

Given our tractability criterion, this also makes for a more fruitful theory, as it gives us criteria we can actually check: they correspond to things we can test for, via probes, behavioral evaluations, and interventions. It is not clear how we would test for whether or not some state of the machine is subject to a truth-norm, if this goes beyond how the machine in fact functions.
61
61

61

If we had a great theory of norm-generating etiology we could perhaps check the history of the system, to figure out how it was trained see if the training would satisfy our theory. But just by looking at the system
as it is
we couldn’t determine the function.

Second, even granting the standards-of-correctness framing for the sake of argument,
and
even granting a more etiological account of function, we resist the naive move from training objective to a particular standard of correctness of a state of the machine. The argument has the form: pretraining selected for next-token prediction, so the standard of correctness for pretrained states is predictive accuracy, not truth. The problem with this move is visible in the human case. Natural selection optimized for inclusive genetic fitness, not for truth. By the same form of argument, the standard of correctness for human representations (which we call, mistakenly, on this view, beliefs) should be fitness-promotion, not truth. But this is not what we want to say. Human beliefs are paradigm cases of beliefs, and a view that delivers the verdict that humans do not have beliefs is one we should reject. The move from selection objective to standard of correctness fails for humans, and it also fails for LLMs.

How can this be? Because truth-tracking is often a good strategy for whatever the selection process was actually optimizing for. Fitness is better promoted by creatures that track truths about predators and food than by creatures that do not. Likewise, next-token prediction in a corpus of human-produced text is often better achieved by systems that track truths about the world than by systems that do not.
62
62

62

A related point has been made within the AI safety literature, most notably in
93
’s discussion of why reward is not, in general, the optimization target of reinforcement learning agents. The reward signal shapes the agent’s cognition by reinforcing the computations that produced the rewarded behavior; the resulting agent’s behavior is governed by those computations, not by a goal of maximizing reward. Turner’s insight is more directly related to
desire
, but the insight is the same as the one we are making here: the optimization target of the training process does not, by itself, fix what the trained system’s states are doing or what they are for.
The training objective and the function of the trained states can come apart.

This means the question of whether a particular pretrained system has belief representations is partly empirical. We cannot read the answer off the training objective. We have to look at the trained system and check whether candidate representations satisfy the criteria.
76
’s linear probes for board states in OthelloGPT, a system trained only to predict legal moves, are an instance of finding board state representations in a system whose training objective was not directly to represent the board. It just turned out that representing the board is useful for predicting legal moves. Similarly for belief: whether the criteria are satisfied to the degree that warrants full belief-attribution in any given case is a further empirical question, but the question is not foreclosed by the fact that the training was not aimed at truth.
63
63

63

Other skeptical arguments take a more sweeping form.
90
and
10
, for example, argue that LLMs cannot have anything resembling beliefs because they are merely predicting tokens. We have addressed this style of argument elsewhere (
51
). The short response: next-token prediction does not preclude truth-tracking, because truth-tracking can be a means to next-token prediction, just as it can be a means to fitness-promotion. Indeed,
88
find evidence of exactly this in LLMs in circumscribed contexts.

4.5.3
Looking ahead to desire.

The same considerations carry over to desire. The criteria we develop in the next chapter (coherence, use, intelligibility, systematicity) will say what it is for a state to play the role of desire in a trained system. We will not need a further story about what the state was
for
, in a sense that goes beyond what the state does. The inclusive genetic fitness analogy applies again, perhaps more obviously. Natural selection did not select humans for desiring particular things like drinking hot black coffee, playing a sonata on the piano, or solving differential equations. Similarly, if LLMs have desire-like states, those states play the role they play in the trained system, regardless of whether RLHF was selecting for exactly those ends.

5
Desire

5.1
Structural Parallels and Disanalogies

We turn now to desire. Desire has received less direct attention from computer scientists than belief, and we have comparatively little direct representational evidence of desires. The account we develop in this section, then, will be more philosophical and less empirical than in the previous one. But we can still ask what it would take to attribute desire to LLMs on both a representationalist and interpretationist picture.

As with belief, we argue, desire has criteria for coherence, use, and, for representationalists, an analog of uniformity. However, there is an asymmetry between belief and desire. Belief has a world-to-mind direction of fit: a belief is correct when it matches the world. This is what gave us the truth criterion. Desire has a mind-to-world direction of fit: a desire is satisfied when the world comes to match the desire. There is no external standard against which to measure a desire’s correctness. The world does not tell you what to want the way it tells you what to believe.

This asymmetry has practical consequences. For belief, we could construct datasets of true and false statements, train probes, and check whether the model’s internal states track truth. For desire, there is no analogous ground truth. We cannot label a dataset of desires as correct or incorrect and train a probe to detect them. In lieu of truth, however, we will argue for a criterion of
intelligibility
: the attributed desires should be comprehensible enough to interpreters to generate powerful predictions.

5.2
Coherence

For belief, coherence meant conformity to the probability axioms and related structural conditions: logical consistency, semantic invariance under rephrasings, and richness across logical combinations. As we’ve already seen, decision theory places coherence conditions on preferences and desires directly. But less formally, we can also place common-sense coherence conditions on
means/ends
reasoning.
64
64

64

The primary inspiration for this latter form of coherence comes from Bratman’s Belief/Desire/Intention (BDI) model of practical reason
(
14
)
.

5.2.1
Means-Ends Coherence

The most intuitive coherence condition on desire is means-ends consistency. If an agent desires some end
E
and believes that
M
is necessary for
E
, it should, other things being equal, be disposed to pursue
M
. This is a natural extension of the folk psychological picture: we explain why someone went to the store by citing their desire for milk and their belief that the store has milk.

For LLMs, means-ends coherence is increasingly testable. An agentic model tasked with writing a report that requires data from several sources should pursue sub-goals (searching for data, reading documents, synthesizing findings) that are rational given the top-level objective. If it instead pursues sub-goals that are irrelevant or counterproductive, that is evidence against coherent desire attribution.
65
65

65

Or, more carefully, at least evidence that the desires are not what we thought they were.

Means-ends coherence also separates
instrumental
desires from terminal ones. Or, at the least, it structures some desires as instrumental for others. An agent with a terminal desire for
E
and a belief that self-preservation is necessary for achieving
E
should, other things being equal, have an instrumental desire for self-preservation. As
83
puts it, “you can’t fetch the coffee if you’re dead.”

For LLMs, the question is whether we observe this structure. Does an agentic model pursuing a top-level goal also pursue the sub-goals that means-ends coherence would predict? We return to this question when discussing the evidential challenges of desire attribution in
section

5.3
.

Transitivity is another basic condition. If the system desires
A
more than
B
, and
B
more than
C
, it should desire
A
more than
C
. Persistent intransitivities would be troubling, though occasional failures are compatible with approximate coherence, just as occasional logical slips are compatible with approximate belief-coherence.

Similarly, as with belief, we should have broad semantic coherence. If outcome
A
is desired over
B
when described one way, it should also be preferred when described some other way. For example, if you prefer having a fork to the left of your knife, you should prefer having a knife to the right of your fork.

5.2.2
Decision-Theoretic Coherence: Jeffrey-Bolker

The above conditions are intuitive but informal. The Jeffrey-Bolker framework introduced in §
3.4
provides a more precise account of what coherent desire requires. Recall that in this framework, both belief and desire are defined over the same Boolean algebra of propositions, and the representation theorem extracts both from the preference ordering simultaneously. Here we focus on what the framework’s axioms require of desire specifically.

As noted in §
3.4.2
, the Jeffrey-Bolker framework operates over an atomless Boolean algebra: there is no maximally fine-grained level of description.

For interpreting LLMs, this is a significant advantage. We can investigate whether the model tracks propositions like “I maintain access to tools” or “I avoid early termination” in a desire-like way without first settling whether these are terminal goals or instrumental for something deeper. The framework lets us work at whatever level of the algebra is tractable, refining as we learn more.

The averaging and impartiality axioms from §
3.4.2
also generate specific predictions for interventional experiments. Suppose we identify candidate belief-representations and candidate desire-representations in the model’s activation space. The averaging condition predicts that if we intervene on the belief-representations, shifting the model’s apparent credences about some proposition
Q
, then the model’s preferences over propositions that depend on
Q
should shift accordingly. Suppose an agentic model is tasked with fixing a bug and has candidate desire-representations corresponding to this goal. If we intervene on the model’s belief-representations to shift its credence toward the bug being in the parsing module rather than the rendering module, its preference for examining parsing-related files should increase accordingly. More precisely, the desirability of coarse-grained propositions should shift in the direction that averaging predicts, given the new credences.

This is a joint test of the candidate belief and desire representations. You cannot test desire-representations in isolation, because desire is functionally defined relative to belief. But you can test whether the two systems of representations interact in the way the axioms require. If they do, that is evidence that the internal states are playing the right functional roles. If they do not—if intervening on candidate belief-representations leaves candidate desire-representations unchanged, or changes them in the wrong direction—that is evidence against the identification.

5.3
Intelligibility

For belief, truth served as an anchor. Desire has no such anchor. What we need instead is that the desires we attribute be ones we can
work with
: that we can figure out what satisfying them would involve, and use that understanding to generate predictions.
66
66

66

There is some connection here to the Principle of Charity applied to desire. Lewis writes: “Karl should be represented as believing what he ought to believe, and desiring what he ought to desire. And what is that? In our opinion, he ought to believe what we believe, or perhaps what we would have believed in his place; and he ought to desire what we desire, or perhaps what we would have desired in his place” (
65
, p. 336).
Davidson extends charity to desire as well: we should “try for a theory that finds [the agent] consistent, a believer of truths, and a lover of the good (all by our own lights, it goes without saying)”
(
32
, p. 146)
. Dennett makes a similar move: “you figure out what desires [the agent] ought to have” given “its place in the world and its purpose”
(
34
, p. 17)
. For humans, this works because we share enough evaluative common ground to extend charity from belief to desire—we can assume people want to stay dry, to eat, to avoid pain, and so on. For a base LLM, the move is harder to motivate. What desires
ought
a next-token predictor to have, given its place in the world and its purpose? It has no body, no survival needs, no evolutionary history of wanting things (though it does, of course, have a training history). The charity-to-desire move lacks obvious footing. But if we accept something like the persona theory (
section

2.3
), the situation improves. The persona’s psychology is largely borrowed from human psychology. We can interpret the persona as a “lover of the good” by roughly our own lights, because the persona’s lights are approximately our lights. This does not tell us much about the base model’s desires (if it has any), but it grounds desire attribution to the persona.

We call this requirement
intelligibility
, and it has two dimensions. The first is
content-graspability
: the interpreter must be able to understand what satisfying the desire would involve well enough to reason about what an agent with that desire would do. The second is
specificity
: the interpreter must be able to predict, from the available evidence, sufficiently useful and specific predictions about how the world will end up. These two dimensions correspond to components of the theoretical virtues from §
2.2
. Content-graspability is tractability applied to desire: the interpreter must be able to generate predictions from the attribution. Specificity is power: the attribution must be useful enough to constrain behavior and predict outcomes.

5.3.1
Content-Graspability

The most straightforward way an attribution can fail intelligibility is if the interpreter cannot grasp what satisfying the desire would involve. Consider some examples at different levels.

Some desires are highly intelligible. “Get money,” “eat an apple,” “be helpful to the user,” “provide accurate information,” “avoid causing harm.” These are easy to work with. Given a situation and a set of beliefs, you can generate predictions about what an agent with these desires would do. The predictions may be imprecise—helpfulness admits of many implementations—but you have enough grip on the content to do useful work.

Tractability comes in degrees. Consider attributing to an LLM the desire to produce outputs that would score well on the RLHF reward model it was trained on, in a case where we do not have full access to that reward model. This is a reasonable goal attribution. And it may be accurate: the model’s behavior might be well predicted by this attribution. But our ability to generate predictions from it is limited by our understanding of the reward model. In familiar cases, we can guess what the reward model would prefer. In novel cases, we are partly in the dark. The desire’s content is structurally clear (“maximize
R
”) but extensionally opaque (we do not know what
R
demands in all cases). This limits our power of prediction, but within bounds of tolerance, and indeed, we often attribute relatively opaque desires to humans.

However, at the limit, a desire attribution can be entirely unintelligible. Suppose someone proposes that the model desires to produce the token sequence maximizing a specific complicated function of attention patterns in layers 15 through 22. This might even be behaviorally accurate in some sense. But it is no more tractable for us as interpreters than just running the model. We have gained nothing by redescribing the model’s computations in desire-talk. The folk psychological vocabulary was supposed to make the system’s behavior
intelligible
. If the attributed desires themselves are unintelligible, the vocabulary has failed at its job.

5.3.2
Specificity

A different way a desire attribution can fail intelligibility is if the interpreter cannot tell, from the available or obtainable evidence, what desire the system has to any degree of specificity that constrains behavior. Here the content may be perfectly graspable; the problem is that the attribution is evidentially inert.

We can imagine a spectrum of specific to non-specific behaviors. Achieving check-mate against an opponent, eating a banana, and obtaining millions of dollars are all specific desires useful for prediction. Being helpful, honest, and harmless are still useful, but far less specific. At the other end of the extreme, the desire to have total entropy increase with time and ensuring that 1 is greater 0 are maximally non-specific. This desire is compatible with
anything
the agent does.

Lack of specificity is a failure of power rather than tractability. The interpreter grasps the desire fine; the attribution just does not do additional predictive work.

Note that the specificity requirement is different from requiring desires be determinate. Sometimes we cannot easily distinguish one desire from another. Consider the case again of a perfect method actor. In the limit, we may be unable to distinguish between the desire to play the character optimally from the character’s own desires. The actor just does whatever the character would do. Nevertheless, we can make specific predictions about the system. Such indeterminacy may mean that two distinct sets of beliefs and desires are equally well attributable to the system. Even in such a case, they may still be useful.

5.4
Use

For belief, the use condition required that candidate belief-representations actually guide the system’s behavior. The analogous condition for desire is that candidate desires actually steer behavior. A desire that plays no role in shaping what the system does is not a desire in any sense that matters.

What does it look like for a desire to steer behavior? On interpretationism, the picture comes from the tradition running through Dennett, Davidson, and Stalnaker. Recall Stalnaker’s formulation: to desire that
P
is to be disposed to act in ways that would tend to bring it about that
P
in a world where one’s beliefs are true. The desire steers behavior
relative to beliefs
. An agent that desires
P
and believes it is in circumstance
C
will take the action that, in
C
, tends to bring about
P
. Change the beliefs (put the agent in a different circumstance, or give it new information about the current one) and the behavior changes, but the end pursued remains the same. Change the desire, and the behavior changes even holding the beliefs fixed.

This gives us the core behavioral signature of desire, stated concisely by
40
: a system with beliefs and desires “takes a wide range of means to rationally promote a small number of ends across a range of environments” (p. 3). Each component of this formulation does work. A
wide range of means
distinguishes desire from a fixed behavioral disposition. As Dennett famously points out, a thermostat has a setpoint and a single mechanism for reaching it; there is no diversity of means, and the belief-desire framework adds nothing that a simple mechanical description does not already provide.
A
small number of ends
keeps the theory tractable: a system with thousands of independent micro-goals that do not reduce to a smaller set is harder to theorize about, and the BDI model begins to lose its advantage over descriptions that simply list the system’s behavioral dispositions. And behavior that
rationally promotes
those ends across a
range of environments
is what separates desire-driven action from coincidence. The system does not merely happen to bring about
P
; it selects, in varied circumstances, the means appropriate to those circumstances for bringing about
P
.

5.4.1
Stability of Ends

For the use condition to be satisfied, the ends attributed to the system should be stable. The same goals should persist across varied contexts and be pursued through different means as circumstances change. An LLM that reliably pursues user helpfulness whether it is writing code, answering questions, or planning a trip exhibits the kind of end-stability that makes desire attribution powerful.

For agentic LLMs, stability manifests across multiple modalities. An agent working on a multi-step task should maintain its objectives across tool calls, error recovery, and plan revisions. If the apparent goal shifts erratically mid-task—if the system abandons a coding project halfway through to start writing poetry, without any change in instructions—that is evidence against stable desire attribution. Conversely, an agent that encounters an error, diagnoses it, adjusts its approach, and continues pursuing the original objective is exhibiting exactly the kind of means-flexibility and end-stability that the use condition requires.

This connects to the theoretical virtues in a direct way. Stability of ends increases
tractability
: the more contexts across which the same desire attribution generates correct predictions, the more tractable the theory. Instability decreases tractability, because the interpreter must constantly revise the attributed desires to track the system’s shifting behavior, which amounts to redescribing the behavior rather than explaining it.

The other side of stability is diversity of means. The more varied the behaviors that a single desire attribution explains, the stronger the case for attributing the desire. An agentic model that searches the web, writes code, and composes summaries in service of a single research goal is exhibiting exactly this diversity (
section

3.5
). Non-agentic LLMs, limited to text generation, offer less diversity, and the interpretationist case for desire attribution is correspondingly thinner.

5.4.2
Representationalist Tests for Use

On the representationalist picture, use requires appropriate causal contribution: the internal state identified as a desire should causally influence the system’s behavior in the way desires should. The methodology parallels what we developed for belief in
section

4.1.3
, extended to desire.

The most direct test is
ablation
: dampen or remove a candidate desire-representation and observe whether the system stops pursuing the relevant goal. If removing a feature identified as “goal: write working code” causes the agent to stop debugging and iterating, that is evidence the feature was playing a desire-like causal role. If behavior is unaffected, the feature was not doing the work we attributed to it.

A more informative test is
goal substitution
. Rather than simply removing a candidate desire, replace it with a different one and check whether the system’s means shift accordingly. If we identify an internal representation corresponding to a chess-playing agent’s goal of checkmate, we might substitute a representation corresponding to a different goal—say, arranging pawns in a triangular formation—and observe whether the agent’s play changes in ways that rationally promote the new goal. If it does, that is strong evidence that the representation is playing a genuine desire-role: the system is selecting means appropriate to whatever end is plugged into that slot.

Goal substitution is more informative than ablation for two reasons. First, ablation can only tell you that the representation matters for behavior; it cannot tell you that it matters
as a desire
. A representation that, when removed, causes the system to malfunction might be playing any number of computational roles. Goal substitution tests the specific functional role: the representation steers means-selection relative to a goal, and changing the goal changes the means. Second, goal substitution connects to the systematicity requirement we develop below (
section

5.5
).

We note that this methodology is far less empirically developed for desire than the corresponding methodology for belief. Truth directions and belief probes have been studied extensively; goal-representations and desire probes have not, to our knowledge, been the subject of comparable investigation. This is an area where the philosophical framework is ahead of the empirical work, and where representationalist tools could make a distinctive contribution.

5.5
Systematicity

For belief, we introduced a distinctively representationalist criterion: uniformity. The same decoding scheme that identifies beliefs about geography should work for beliefs about history, biology, and other domains. Uniformity is what makes the representationalist’s toolkit generalizable. Without it, belief extraction is limited to domains where you already know the answers, which defeats much of the purpose.

For desire, representationalists need some form of
systematicity
: the system’s desire-representations should be structured enough that novel goals are representable and identifiable using the same tools that identified existing ones. Desires may naturally be somewhat variegated representationally if relatively stable desires (perhaps terminal goals) are encoded quite differently from fleeting instrumental desires. But broadly, we need some internal encoding that is systematic and captures an important subclass of desires.
67
67

67

The term ‘systematicity’ is borrowed from Fodor, who argued that cognitive systems exhibit systematicity as a consequence of having a combinatorial representational structure
(
37
;
38
)
. Fodor’s point was about the language of thought: a system that can entertain the thought that
John loves Mary
can also entertain the thought that
Mary loves John
, because both thoughts are composed from the same representational primitives via the same combinatorial rules. The capacity to represent one thought guarantees the capacity to represent structurally related thoughts.
We are not committed to the full language of thought hypothesis here for representationalists, and the analogy need not be exact. But the structural point carries over.

The practical payoff parallels uniformity’s payoff for belief. Uniformity lets you discover beliefs in domains where you do not know the ground truth. Systematicity lets you detect goals you did not anticipate. For safety, this is the case that matters: when an LLM acquires a new goal, whether through in-context instructions or through instrumental reasoning about means to existing ends, we want our interpretive tools to pick it up without requiring a bespoke probe built specifically for that goal.

The goal substitution experiments described in
section

5.4
provide a natural test. If you can replace a known goal-representation with a novel one and the system pursues the new goal coherently then the system’s goal-representation mechanism flexibly plays the right role: it supports novel goals, not just the ones we used to identify it. If, on the other hand, substituting a novel goal produces incoherent behavior or no goal-directed behavior at all, the system’s desire-representations may be more like a fixed menu.

There is, however, an asymmetry between belief and desire that limits how much systematicity can deliver. Imagine asking someone what they would like for dinner, and they suggest either deep-dish pizza, a pork belly bao, fish and chips, or a cheeseburger. An interpreter notices the pattern: they want something rich and high in fat. This is a good desire attribution. It predicts behavior—they will choose the fried option over the salad—and it generalizes to new menus. But the person may not represent
high-fat meal
as a category at all. They represent desires for particular dishes. The generalization lives in the interpreter’s theory instead of the agent’s representations.

For belief, the analogous gap is typically bridgeable. Alice may not represent that
173+28=201
before being asked, but she can compute it on demand. The belief is
elicitable
: prompt her and she will represent it. Desires are less cooperative. A person may not always know what they want, and may not be able to derive their more abstract preferences from their specific ones. The high-fat-meal desire may be invisible to the agent herself, visible only from the outside.

This suggests that the class of desires worth attributing may outrun the class of desires that are representationally systematic. Representationalist methods can find the desires the system encodes directly. But interpreters can attribute desires that are patterns
across
those representations: generalizations the system acts on without encoding. Systematicity is a virtue for the desires it covers, but it need not cover all the desires that earn their keep in the folk psychological theory.

That said, systematicity enjoys distinctive advantages for the desires it does cover. If desires are representationally systematic, we can discover new goals without any direct behavioral evidence, and we can detect hidden goals. If a system is trying to disguise its actual ends, internal representation allows us a method of detection.
68
68

68

Note that representational beliefs without representational desires may be enough for all these advantages if systems understand themselves well enough. We can ask the system whether it has some goal or desire, and then look internally to discover its belief about itself.

5.6
Diachronic Conditions

In
section

4.3
, we articulated diachronic constraints on LLM beliefs, noting that they should be updated appropriately over time. The analogous condition for desire turns out to have a surprisingly clean answer, at least in the in-context case.

Standardly, desire revision is parasitic on belief revision. On the Jeffrey-Bolker model, desirability is defined in terms of conditional credences and the desirabilities of finer-grained propositions: the desirability of a coarse-grained proposition is an average of the desirabilities of its refinements, weighted by the agent’s credences. When the agent learns something and those credences change, the desirabilities of all propositions that depend on the learned content change accordingly, because the weights in the averaging have shifted. Learning that it is raining does not just change your beliefs about the weather; it changes the desirability of every proposition whose desirability depended on whether it was raining (carrying an umbrella, wearing a raincoat, staying indoors).

This means we do not need a separate mechanism for desire revision. If the agent updates its credences reasonably, the new desirabilities are just what the framework delivers. The diachronic condition for desire is: if belief revision is reasonable, desire revision should follow the averaging. This is one way of further fleshing out the stability of ends condition we discussed above.

This has a useful diagnostic consequence. Desire change that is not explained by belief change is a red flag. If an LLM starts pursuing a different goal mid-conversation for no epistemic reason that is evidence either that the Jeffrey-Bolker picture does not fit, or that something else is going on. Perhaps the persona shifted, or perhaps the model’s goal-representations are less stable than the use condition requires.

Zero-shot desires stemming from a broader desire to help the user fit naturally into this picture. A system prompt saying “help this user plan a trip to Japan” induces a new belief: I have been given this task. The system’s standing desires (be helpful, follow instructions) combine with the new belief to make helping with Japan travel desirable. The desirability of that outcome changed because the credences changed.

5.6.1
In-Training Desire Revision

The in-context case is clean because the model’s weights are frozen and we can consider the possibility that only the beliefs change given the evidence it receives during inference. The in-training case is harder.

Every gradient update during RLHF (or any training procedure) changes the model’s weights. Strictly speaking, each update produces a different program. The question of whether the model before and after a weight update is “the same agent” undergoing desire revision, or simply two very similar but distinct agents, is in some sense bookkeeping: the updates are small, the resulting systems are highly similar, and nothing of philosophical substance turns on drawing a sharp boundary.

However, if we do treat the model as persisting through small updates, we can try to understand what RLHF does in Jeffrey-Bolker terms. One natural reading: the model has a standing desire to produce outputs that earn reward, and RLHF updates its beliefs about what the reward model rewards. On this picture, the desire (earn reward) is stable across training; what changes is the model’s beliefs about what earns reward, and the instrumental desires shift accordingly. Desire change during RLHF is, on this reading, belief change in disguise: the same Jeffrey-Bolker story as the in-context case, just operating through weight updates rather than context updates.

This is a tidy picture, and it may be too tidy. RLHF might genuinely change the model’s desires without changing its beliefs about instrumental means. We flag this as an open question rather than attempting to resolve it here.

5.7
The Criteria Together

The criteria for desire attribution parallel those for belief, but with important asymmetries. Coherence provides the structural backbone, and the Jeffrey-Bolker framework gives it precision. Use ensures that the attributed desires actually steer behavior, and the representationalist tests (ablation, goal substitution) provide concrete methodology for checking this. Intelligibility, which has no analogue in the belief case, ensures the attribution is one the interpreter can work with. Systematicity, the representationalist criterion, ensures that the tools generalize to novel goals.

These criteria connect to the theoretical virtues of §
2.2
. Coherence ensures that the folk psychological theory is internally consistent, a precondition for accuracy. Use ensures that the theory covers actual behavior rather than attributing inert states. Intelligibility ensures that the theory is tractable: the interpreter can reason with the attributed desires and generate predictions. Systematicity extends this tractability to novel cases. A desire attribution that satisfies all four criteria yields a theory that is accurate, powerful, and tractable relative to alternatives, which is what it takes for folk psychology to earn its keep as a theory of the system.

Desire attribution, like belief attribution, is not all-or-nothing. The criteria come in degrees, and the case for attributing desires to a system strengthens as more criteria are more fully satisfied. As LLMs become more agentic, the behavioral evidence improves, the coherence conditions become more testable, and the motivation for developing representationalist tools for desire increases.

5.8
Separability of Beliefs and Desires

Representationalism about belief and representationalism about desire can come apart. A single system can warrant representational belief attribution while warranting only interpretationist desire attribution, or while warranting no desire attribution at all. We’ll call these cases
mixed outcome
cases. We sketch both versions.

Consider the first. A system might have belief representations satisfying all criteria of
section

4
, yet have its desires live entirely at the interpretationist level, with no corresponding internal representations. To see how, consider a toy example. Suppose we have a reinforcement learning agent that operates, ultimately, via a massive lookup table. The agent might dedicate enormous computational resources to state estimation, reasoning deeply about its environment to reach a conclusion like, “I am in state 72.” This state estimation is a highly structured, representational belief. But once state 72 is identified, the agent simply executes the hardcoded disposition provided by the table: “go left.” The table might be robust enough to support seemingly higher-level goal-directed behavior, even, such as driving a car. In such a system, desires are nowhere explicitly represented internally.

While frontier models obviously do not work via simple lookup tables, the point stands. If a system acts on complex behavioral dispositions, but only after forming explicit beliefs, it may be perfectly interpretationally coherent. We would find explicit representational structure for its beliefs, while its desires satisfy the interpretationist criteria of
section

5
without being encoded anywhere we can point to. The system has representational beliefs and interpretationist desires.

The second case is different. Here the belief representations again satisfy use, in the strong sense that the model demonstrably deploys them in completing its tasks, but the system’s overall behavior is not rich or stable enough to support desire attribution even at the interpretationist level. The means are too uniform, or the ends too unstable or non-specific, for the wide-means/few-ends signature of
section

5
to take hold. In such a system we would have grounds for attributing belief and no grounds for attributing desire on either account. The two cases share a structure worth making explicit: in both, belief attribution survives because the beliefs connect to what the model actually outputs through use.

Desires, however, are intrinsically belief-laden. On the Jeffrey-Bolker and Savage pictures the fundamental attitude is preference, something desire-like. If you desire chocolate and think it is in the refrigerator rather than the pantry, you will prefer (all else equal) opening the refrigerator; learn that it is in the pantry, and your preference reverses. Beliefs are not tied to desires this way. Finding yourself desiring an umbrella does not produce a belief in rain. So from an internal representation of desire we get the apparatus for representing belief more or less for free: a rational desire is already partly explicable in terms of belief.

There is another complication we must address. Up to this point, we have been speaking as if an LLM’s beliefs and desires naturally map onto the conceptual apparatus we ourselves use—that when it represents words like
\code
rabbit or a sentence like
\code
The cat is on the mat, it is ultimately thinking in terms of human-intelligible concepts. But what if it doesn’t? If an LLM approaches the world using foreign concepts, making the contents of its mind difficult to translate into our own language, then our tools—both representational and interpretationist—risk projecting a human way of seeing things onto a fundamentally alien mind that carves up the world differently from how we do. This brings us to the core vulnerability of the interpretive project: the propositional projection problem. We turn to this problem in the next section.

Interlude: The Apprentices’ Difficulty

The demonstration that began that morning went on for weeks. Murazeel pressed the apprentices on case after case, each harder than the last. They held up well at first. Lizette’s threads bent and the machine bent with them. Jentley’s atlas predicted, and where it failed he revised, and the revisions held. Murazeel kept pressing. Eventually they came to him with troubles they could not yet explain.

Murazeel found both apprentices in his study. Lizette was kneeling beside the VVM with her wand pressed close to its torso, the mist at its tip flickering erratically. Jentley sat at Murazeel’s desk, his wand set aside, staring at a ledger full of crossed-out predictions.

Lizette spoke without looking up. “Ask it about the fire.”

Murazeel obliged. “VVM, do you find the hearth warm?”

The machine was quiet for a moment, and then it spoke. “The hearth is a comfort to those who tend it and a danger to those who forget it.”

Lizette swept her wand slowly through the machine’s interior. “There,” she said. “See? I looked for a thread for
warm
. I found one weeks ago: bright, clean, easy to read. But watch.” She traced the thread with her wand. It glowed, but fitfully, and forked where she expected it to run straight. “It does not connect to the machine’s speech the way it should. When the VVM says something is warm, this thread sometimes brightens and sometimes does not. I thought the thread was broken.”

She withdrew the wand and reoriented it at a slightly different angle. A different thread emerged, one Murazeel had not seen before. It was steadier, and it glowed in a way that tracked the VVM’s speech more closely.

“This thread is not
warm
,” Lizette said. “It is something more like
agreeable to the one who experiences it
. When the VVM speaks of warmth as a comfort, this thread brightens. When it speaks of warmth as a danger, this thread dims and another brightens in its place. The VVM is not encoding temperature. It is encoding something about how temperature matters.”

She sat back. “I was looking for my threads. The machine has its own.”

Jentley had been listening. He held up his ledger. “I have the same problem from the other side. My atlas predicted that the VVM would prefer warmth to cold, consistently, across contexts. It does not. It prefers the hearth when tending it. It prefers distance from the hearth when the room is already stifling. My categories gave me a pattern that looked right for a while and then fell apart. Not because the machine changed. Because the pattern was mine, not the machine’s.”

“How did you discover this?” Murazeel asked.

“By failing,” said Jentley. “I made twelve predictions about what the VVM would say regarding fire and heat. Seven were right. Five were wrong. The five that were wrong were not random. They were wrong in the same direction, as though the VVM was drawing a distinction I had not thought to look for. Once I saw the pattern in my errors, I could almost guess what the distinction was. But only almost.”

Murazeel looked from one apprentice to the other. “Show me the thread again, Lizette.”

She traced it. The steady filament glowed through the VVM’s interior.

“Now dampen it,” he said.

She pressed the wand’s tip against the thread and pushed. The glow faded. Murazeel turned to the VVM and asked about the hearth once more. This time, the machine said only: “The hearth. Yes. It is there.” The words were flat, emptied of the texture the VVM’s speech usually carried.

“Good,” said Murazeel, quietly. “But now need to know, how many more like it you are missing, what they mean, and whether some of what the machine does has no threads at all.”

6.1
What Propositions Do

Beliefs and desires are propositional attitudes. To believe is to believe
that
something is the case; to desire is to desire
that
something be the case. The previous two chapters developed criteria for when to attribute these attitudes to LLMs. But both took something for granted: that we know which propositions the attitudes are directed toward.

In the framework we have been developing, propositions do three things.

First, they are the objects of belief and desire. When we say the model believes that Lord Byron died in 1824, “Lord Byron died in 1824” picks out a proposition, and the belief is an attitude the model holds toward it. When we say the model desires to be helpful, “the model is helpful” picks out a proposition (or a set of propositions), and the desire is an attitude toward it.

Second, propositions stand in logical relations to one another. “Lord Byron died in 1824” and “Lord Byron did not die in 1824” are contradictories: exactly one can be true. “Lord Byron died in 1824” and “Lord Byron was English” are logically independent: either, both, or neither could be true. “Lord Byron died in Greece” entails “Lord Byron died.” These relations, taken together, give the set of propositions an algebraic structure. In the Jeffrey-Bolker framework that we (following Lewis and Davidson) have been using throughout, the propositions form a Boolean algebra
\mathfrak{A}
: a structure closed under negation, conjunction, and disjunction, with the logical relations determined by these operations.

Third, the propositional algebra is the
common
structure over which both belief
and
desire are defined. Beliefs are not about one set of things and desires about another. There is a single algebra
\mathfrak{A}
, and both belief and desire are functions on it. The agent’s credence function
P
assigns probabilities to elements of
\mathfrak{A}
. The agent’s desirability function
D
assigns desirabilities to elements of
\mathfrak{A}
. The agential description from §
1
,
\mathcal{A}=\langle P,D,\mathfrak{A}\rangle
, makes this explicit: the algebra is a component of the description, on the same level as the attitudes themselves.

This means that the criteria developed in the preceding chapters, and many of the methods that computer scientists are deploying to understand models, all presuppose the algebra. Probabilistic coherence (§
4.1.2
) requires knowing what the partitions of
\mathfrak{A}
are, since the probability axioms say that the probabilities of the elements of a partition must sum to one. The averaging axiom for desire (§
5.2
) requires knowing the disjunctive structure: the desirability of
A\vee B
is an average of the desirabilities of
A
and
B
, weighted by credences. The truth criterion for belief (§
4.1.1
) requires that the propositions we test have determinate truth values. Getting the algebra wrong breaks many of the criteria because they are defined in terms of the algebra.

In practice, researchers often identify propositions by constructing natural language sentences. They write down
\code
The Eiffel Tower is in Paris or
\code
Lord Byron died in 1824, feed these sentences through the model, and train probes on the resulting internal states. The assumption is that these sentences express propositions the model treats as truth-evaluable, and that the logical relations among them are the ones we take them to have. When this assumption holds, the methods from the preceding chapters apply. This chapter examines what happens when it does not.

6.2
Holism

Consider how probing for belief works in practice.
70
70

70

Similar issues arise in the context of desire.
A researcher assembles a dataset of sentences whose truth values she knows:
\code
Lord Byron died in 1824 (true),
\code
Lord Byron was born in 1908 (false), and hundreds more. She feeds each sentence through the model, extracts embeddings at an intermediate layer, and trains a linear classifier to predict truth from the embeddings. She tests the classifier on held-out sentences from the same distribution and finds that it succeeds. She now has a probe. When she applies it to a new sentence, the probe outputs a value she interprets as the model’s degree of belief.

This methodology involves several assumptions that are easy to overlook. In assembling her dataset, the researcher assumed that each sentence expresses a proposition the model treats as truth-evaluable. In labeling sentences as true or false, she assumed the model’s beliefs matched her own. In constructing sentence pairs for coherence testing, she assumed the logical relations among her sentences (which are contradictories, which are independent, which form partitions) match the logical relations the model recognizes. All of these are assumptions that the researcher makes about the model’s propositional structure.

These assumptions fail to appreciate the holism of radical interpretation. To measure the model’s beliefs, we must supply propositions for the beliefs to be about. To assess coherence, we must know which propositions stand in which logical relations. To check use, we must know which representations to intervene on and what changes count as appropriate. At every stage, the measurement presupposes a propositional structure that, according to the radical interpretation framework of §
1
, is itself part of what interpretation is supposed to determine.

Recall Lewis’s formulation: given the physical facts about the system, solve for the rest. “The rest” includes beliefs, desires,
and meanings
. The propositions are part of the
output
. But our interpretability methods treat them as input. We choose the sentences, fix their meanings, and then try to measure whether the model believes them. The holism of radical interpretation says this separation is inexact. What the system means, believes, and desires are determined together, and assuming one to measure the others introduces a vulnerability: if the assumed propositional structure is wrong, the beliefs we measure will be wrong too.
71
71

71

Davidson makes this point forcefully: “[…]in
radical
interpretation […] we must somehow deliver simultaneously a theory of belief and a theory of meaning” (
28
, p. 312, emphasis original).

There is a natural response to which we are sympathetic: we have to start somewhere. We cannot measure everything at once. In practice, fixing some assumptions and testing others is how empirical work proceeds. The point is not that the methodology is illegitimate but that its results are conditional on the assumptions it makes, and we should be attentive to how those assumptions might go wrong. When we interpret other humans, the risk is small. We share a world, a perceptual apparatus, broadly similar cognitive architectures, and a common language shaped by that shared environment. The assumption that another person’s propositions roughly match ours is well-supported by this common ground. With LLMs, we have less basis for this confidence. These systems were trained on human-produced text, which gives them substantial contact with human propositional structure. But they lack bodies, perceptual experience, and the evolutionary history that shaped our conceptual repertoire. Their internal representations were shaped by gradient descent on a prediction objective, a process with no analogue in human cognitive development. The degree to which the resulting representations mirror human propositional structure is an empirical question, not something we can assume.

We have strategies for making progress despite holism. When we feed a sentence like
\code
Lord Byron died in 1824 to the model and extract an embedding, we are doing something analogous to what Davidson describes as triangulation: correlating a linguistic stimulus with the system’s internal response in the presence of a worldly condition.
72
72

72

Davidson develops this idea in
27
. The basic picture: meaning is pinned down by correlating a speaker’s utterances with the worldly stimuli that reliably prompt them, as observed by an interpreter. For Davidson, this three-way relation (speaker, interpreter, world) is what gives content to the speaker’s words.
In the human case, Davidson’s interpreter observes that Kurt says “es regnet” when it rains, and uses this correlation to begin fixing the content of Kurt’s sentences. When we probe an LLM, we present a sentence expressing a known fact and observe the model’s internal state, using the correlation to begin fixing the content of the model’s representations.

This procedure gives the interpreter a foothold, but it does not by itself determine content fully. In the human case, triangulation is supplemented by the holistic constraints of radical interpretation: the assignment of meanings must cohere with the assignment of beliefs and desires, and the overall interpretation must satisfy charity. The same holds for LLMs. Correlating sentences with internal states is a starting point. Whether the correlations we find reflect genuine propositional content or something else (a pattern that correlates with truth on our datasets without tracking truth as such) is exactly the question that the criteria from the preceding chapters are designed to answer.

6.2.1
Looking inside for propositions.

One strategy for making progress in light of holism is to look inside the model for propositional structure, rather than assuming it from outside. This is a natural extension of the representationalist program from §
3.2
. Just as finding internal mechanisms for belief and desire can narrow the range of possible interpretations, finding internal structure corresponding to propositions and their logical relations could help us determine which propositional algebra the model is operating with. If we could extract the model’s propositions from its activations, we would not need to assume they match ours.

This strategy is available on both views. The representationalist looks for internal states that play the functional role of propositions: states that can be believed or disbelieved, that stand in logical relations to one another, and that combine to form more complex contents. The interpretationist, while not requiring internal structure for belief attribution to be correct, can nonetheless use internal evidence to inform the interpretive theory. If the model’s activations reveal a propositional structure different from the one the interpreter assumed, that is useful information.

But looking inside with probes raises its own question: when a probe finds structure in the model’s activations, how do we know the structure belongs to the model and not to the probe?

Consider an extreme case. Suppose our probe is not a linear classifier but a massive neural network with billions of parameters. We attach it to the activations at some layer and train it to predict whether the input sentence was true or false. The probe succeeds. Have we shown that the target model represents truth?

Not necessarily. The mapping from input text to activations at any given layer is typically close to injective: different inputs produce sufficiently different activation patterns. A powerful enough probe can exploit this. It can, in effect, reconstruct the input from the activations and then solve the truth-classification problem on its own, using its own beliefs and its own computational resources. The target model’s activations serve as a conduit through which the input passes, not as a representation of truth. The probe does the cognitive work.

1
makes an analogous point in a different context. Consider a naturally occurring waterfall. Being governed by the laws of physics, the waterfall implements some mapping
f
from initial states to final states. If we restrict attention to a particular subset of initial states and label them appropriately, we can interpret
f
as implementing any computation we like: a chess-playing program, a factoring algorithm, anything at all. Does the waterfall play chess? Aaronson argues no. The reason is not merely intuitive. Suppose we actually wanted to use the waterfall as a chess oracle. We would need an algorithm that encodes a chess position into an initial state of the waterfall, waits for the waterfall to evolve, and then reads off a good chess move from the final state. But any algorithm that can perform this encoding and decoding, Aaronson argues, would have to solve the chess problem itself in the process. The waterfall contributes nothing. The computational work lives entirely in the encoding-decoding procedure.
74
74

74

The analogy is not exact. Aaronson’s point is that the waterfall contributes nothing at all: the reduction algorithm is fully redundant with respect to the oracle. In the probing case, the model’s activations may contribute
something
to the classification, even when the probe is complex. The question is whether the activations contribute enough, and in the right way, for the probe’s success to count as evidence about the model’s representations. The linearity constraint, discussed below, is one way of ensuring this.

The situation with a powerful probe is similar. A sufficiently capable probe, attached to activations from which the input can be reconstructed, can solve the truth-classification problem by effectively reconstructing the input and classifying it directly. The model’s activations are the waterfall: a system through which information passes, but which need not be performing the relevant computation. The probe, like Aaronson’s encoding-decoding algorithm, does the work and attributes the result to the system it is attached to.

This is why the linear representation hypothesis, discussed in §
3.2
, matters for more than empirical reasons. Requiring the probe to be linear is a constraint on how much computational work the probe is permitted to do. A linear classifier cannot reconstruct the input from the activations and solve the classification problem from scratch. It can only project the activations onto a direction and check which side of a threshold the projection falls on. If a linear probe succeeds, the structure it detects must be
in
the activations in a fairly direct sense: the activations must already separate true statements from false ones along some direction, and the probe merely reads off that separation.

The linearity requirement thus has a philosophical rationale beyond its empirical motivation. It limits the probe’s capacity to construct the very structure it claims to find. Without such a constraint, the representationalist program is too easily satisfied. A sufficiently powerful probe will find “truth representations” in any system whose activations preserve enough information about the input, regardless of whether the system itself represents truth in any meaningful sense.

But linearity guards against one problem while leaving another untouched. A linear probe cannot do the cognitive work itself, but it can still look for the wrong concepts. The Othello case, discussed in §
6.3.3
, illustrates both points. The initial nonlinear probes found a board representation, but they found it partly by doing the translation work themselves: converting between the model’s perspective-relative categories (mine/yours) and the researchers’ absolute categories (black/white). A linear probe, constrained to read off structure already present in the activations, failed under the researchers’ original categories and succeeded under the model’s own. Linearity exposed a propositional mismatch that the more powerful probe had masked.

Restricting probe complexity ensures that the structure the probe finds is in the model. But it does not ensure that the structure the probe
looks for
matches what the model has. Too much probe power can hide a mismatch in concepts, since the probe can bridge the gap between the model’s categories and the researcher’s.

Recent work illustrates what partial progress on both fronts can look like, and how far there is to go.

Interpretationism faces an analogous concern. As noted above, the interpretationist can help herself to internal evidence, including probe results, even though she does not take internal structure to be what makes belief attribution correct. The strategy of looking inside the model for propositional structure is thus promising but comes with a condition. The tools we use to look inside must be constrained enough that what they find is genuinely about the model. 6.3
The Propositional Projection Problem

Recall that on the Jeffrey-Bolker framework introduced in §
3.4.2
, beliefs and desires are attitudes directed at the same set of propositions. Getting the propositions wrong by projecting our own conceptual scheme onto the model therefore distorts both belief attribution and desire attribution at once. This
propositional projection problem
is not a problem for belief measurement alone; it is a problem for the entire interpretive enterprise.

Consider two forms of the problem as it arises in belief measurement.

6.3.1
Truth-aptness mismatch.

Some sentences we feed the model may not, from the model’s perspective, express truth-evaluable propositions. Consider a parable. An alien comes to Earth and decides to do some interpretability work on us. It studies our language, develops a reasonable grasp of some parts of it, and one day abducts you, attaches a probe to your skull, and starts presenting you with sentences. It asks you to think hard about whether each sentence is true, so that it can calibrate its mind-reading device. You play along.

The first sentence is easy:

\code

The sun is larger than the Earth.

True. Your brain processes it as true, and the alien adjusts its probe. Next:

\code

Paris is the capital of Germany.

False. The alien adjusts again. Then:

\code

John Keats is the greatest poet.

You love Keats and are inclined to agree. But you are not sure this has a truth value. Should you think hard about truth, or not? The machine dings. Adjustments. Next:

\code

Prague has over 200 residents.

Strictly speaking, true. But it feels misleading. The implicature is that Prague has a bit over 200, when really it has over a million. You are unsure what would help your alien friend here. The machine dings.

\code

The Good is the Beautiful.

You know some people believe it. You are not convinced it is true or false.
Ding.

\code

Colorless green ideas sleep furiously.

The probe’s output is a number between 0 and 1. There is no “N/A” option. For many of these sentences, you were not in a position to have a belief with a determinate truth value, and so a lot of junk went into the training signal.

We may be in the alien’s position when probing LLMs. Insofar as a sentence does not express a truth-evaluable proposition from the model’s perspective, the embedding we extract is not encoding a belief about that proposition. The training signal for our probe becomes noise rather than signal.
77
77

77

Of course, it is also possible that the model
does
lack anything resembling beliefs. The point is that if a probe fails to find something belief-like, we cannot simply infer that the model lacks beliefs. It may be that we used the wrong propositions.

Metaphorical language is one pervasive source of this mismatch. We speak of
wasting
time,
attacking
arguments,
building

falling
in love.
78
78

78

The pervasiveness of metaphor in ordinary language is a central observation of
59
, who argue that metaphor is not merely a linguistic phenomenon but a cognitive one.
LLMs are trained on vast corpora saturated with such expressions. Consider “Time is money.” Is it true or false? Literally false: time is not a form of currency. But the sentence captures something right about the value and scarcity of time. If we label it as false in our probe’s training data, we may be sending the wrong signal. If the LLM has learned to process metaphors competently, its internal representation of “Time is money” may encode something other than literal falsehood, and our truth probe would misinterpret that representation. Similar difficulties arise for coherence probes: the negation of “Time is money” is, literally, “Time is not money,” but this trivially true sentence does not seem to express the denial of whatever the metaphor conveys.

Our probing methods have no way to distinguish sentences the model treats as truth-evaluable from sentences it does not. The probe always produces a number. Whether that number is meaningful depends on whether the input fell within the domain where belief attribution makes sense.

6.3.2
Structural mismatch.

Even for propositions that both the interpreter and the model treat as truth-evaluable, the logical relations among those propositions may differ. Consider the following pair of statements:

P1:
\code
Anglerfish have hearts.

P2:
\code
Anglerfish don’t have kidneys.

Suppose the interpreter knows that hearts and kidneys always go together in fish. Then P1 and P2 look like a partition: exactly one can be true. If the LLM does not know this biological fact, however, P1 and P2 need not be mutually exclusive from its perspective. The interpreter would require that the model’s credences in P1 and P2 sum to 1, but the model, not treating them as mutually exclusive, might assign credences that sum to more than 1. From the interpreter’s perspective, the model looks incoherent. But the apparent incoherence is an artifact of projecting the wrong propositional structure.

The Othello case revisited.

This is the propositional projection problem in miniature. The researchers projected a propositional structure (board positions described by the color of the disc) that did not match the model’s own structure (board positions described by whose disc it is). The initial result, a nonlinear representation that required a complex probe, was consistent with two possibilities: that the model lacked a clean (linear) board representation, or that the researchers were looking for the wrong representation. The subsequent discovery of linear structure for mine/yours vindicated the latter explanation, but the ambiguity of the initial negative result is the general lesson. Had they stopped at the nonlinear finding, they would have drawn the wrong conclusion about the model’s internal structure.

The relationship between black/white/empty and current player/other player/empty has a structure that philosophers will recognize. In Othello, black plays first. On odd-numbered turns, the current player is black; on even-numbered turns, the current player is white. So “current player’s tile” translates as “black on odd turns, white on even turns,” and “other player’s tile” translates as “white on odd turns, black on even turns.” Each predicate in one scheme is definable in terms of predicates from the other scheme plus a time index.

This is structurally analogous to a classic puzzle in philosophy of science due to Goodman (

Goodman’s point is that both predicate pairs (green/blue and grue/bleen) are interdefinable, each expressible in terms of the other plus a time index. Neither is more ‘‘natural’’ in purely logical terms. Which pair is natural depends on the system doing the representing.

In the Othello case, the model found the perspective-relative predicates (current/other) more natural than the absolute ones (black/white). Linearity went with the model’s natural kinds, not the researchers’. This has a direct implication for the linear representation hypothesis: whether information about the world is linearly represented depends on which concepts you are looking for. A representation that is nonlinear with respect to one set of predicates may be perfectly linear with respect to another. The linear representation hypothesis does not just say “concepts are directions in activation space”; it says “the

The point generalizes: linearity is relative to a choice of features, and the choice of features is where the interpreter’s assumptions enter.

The Othello case is encouraging because it shows that versions of the propositional projection problem are sometimes tractable: the researchers found the right predicates. But in this case it was solved in part because the setting was constrained. There are only so many ways to categorize tiles on a board that seem sensible for the task at hand. The researchers could try nearby predicates (current/other vs. black/white) and check which yielded linear structure. For LLMs processing natural language, the space of candidate propositional structures is vastly larger. We may not recognize the model’s natural kinds even when we encounter them, and the space of alternatives to try is not easily enumerable.

6.3.4
The upshot.

Negative results from probes are systematically ambiguous. A probe that fails to find coherent belief-like structure might be failing because the model doesn’t have beliefs. Or it might be failing because of a mismatch in propositional structure: we looked for coherence over the wrong set of propositions. Current methods cannot distinguish these possibilities.

This ambiguity affects both the representationalist and the interpretationist. Representationalist probes assume propositional structure when they choose which sentences to feed the model and which directions to look for in activation space. But interpretationist behavioral tests also assume propositional structure: we construct behavioral tests using our sentences and our logical relations, and we evaluate the model’s responses against our standards of coherence. If the model’s propositional algebra differs from ours, both approaches will misfire.

This is a problem for the intellectual project of understanding what these systems represent. It is also a problem for the practical project of monitoring them for safety. And it is compounded by the persona issue from §
2.3
. If the model is simulating a persona, the persona’s propositional algebra might differ from the base model’s. The persona, having been shaped by training on human text and refined through RLHF, is perhaps more likely to operate with something close to human propositional structure. The base model’s internal structure might be quite different. Even if we correctly identify the persona’s propositions, we might be missing the base model’s, and vice versa. The target-of-interpretation question from §
2.3
thus acquires a further dimension here: not only must we ask
whose
beliefs and desires we are attributing, but also
whose
propositions we are assuming.

6.4
Methods and Their Limits

The propositional projection problem is not confined to probes. It runs through the interpretability toolkit. In this section we examine how the problem manifests in current methods, with particular attention to sparse autoencoders and the phenomenon of superposition. A common thread connects the cases: current methods build in something like a principle of charity applied to propositional content. This is both what makes the methods work and what limits them.

6.4.1

We noted in §

The SAE is unsupervised. It is trained to minimize reconstruction error subject to the sparsity constraint, and nothing in that objective rewards features a human can understand. Interpretability enters only afterward, as a check we run on the features the SAE returns. A researcher examines which inputs activate a given feature strongly, looks for a pattern, and assigns a label: this feature fires for French text, this one for claims about geography, this one for mentions of a specific entity.

This post-hoc labeling plays a role analogous to the principle of charity in radical interpretation. Charity says: interpret the agent as having beliefs and desires that are reasonable by our lights. The labeling step says: read each feature as whatever concept, by our lights, makes sense of the inputs that fire it. In both cases the interpreter’s own conceptual scheme is the standard of adequacy. That so many features admit a reading at all is evidence that the model’s representational structure overlaps with ours, perhaps substantially. But the labeling tells us less than it appears to, and seeing why exposes two failure modes.

The first failure mode is the obvious one. The SAE returns a set of features, and some of them resist any reading we can find. We do not know what to do with these. If one of them is playing an important role in the model, we do not know how to decipher it.

The second failure mode is subtler, and it touches the features we thought were safe. Having read a feature off the inputs that make it fire, we are inclined to treat our interpretation of it as settled. But a feature does its work in the model by combining with others, including ones we have not read or cannot read, and the way it enters into those combinations to shape the model’s outputs may be something we have entirely missed. A feature can look interpretable in isolation while its actual role is something we have no grip on. The reading seemed to fit, and we projected it onto a feature whose function in the model is other than we supposed. Nothing in the SAE procedure flags this. This is another version of the problem of generalization. If a feature coincides with our concept within the distribution we check, that does not in general guarantee that it coincides with our concept out of distribution.

This is the propositional projection problem applied to the decomposition step. Earlier we saw it applied to probes: we choose sentences, assume they express propositions the model recognizes, and measure attitudes toward them. Here the problem is upstream. We decompose the model’s activations into features and assume the features are concepts that can serve as building blocks for its propositional structure. If that assumption is wrong, everything downstream inherits the distortion, including probes that measure attitudes toward the extracted features. The labelability of many features tells us that there is some overlap with our conceptual space; it does not tell us the overlap is substantial, and the non-overlapping parts are exactly the ones the check cannot see. The question, as with charity generally, is how far the assumption of shared structure can be pushed before it breaks. We return to this in §
6.5
, where we consider strategies for extracting propositional structure from the model rather than assuming it.

6.4.2
Interventions and the propositional projection problem.

The linear representation hypothesis, as discussed in §
3.2
, has three aspects: concepts as directions in activation space, measurement via probes, and intervention via steering vectors. When all three align, the evidence for representation is strong. The Othello case was compelling precisely because all three were present: the board state was encoded as directions, readable by probes, and causally active under intervention.

In practice, the three aspects do not always align so cleanly. Superposition complicates the picture, because features that share dimensions can interfere with each other under intervention. Adding a steering vector meant to change one feature may inadvertently affect others. The intervention aspect of the linear representation hypothesis thus requires that the model’s features be sufficiently separated, not merely that they be linearly encoded.

For the propositional projection problem, this matters because interventions are our strongest test of whether a representation is causally active: the use criterion from
section

4.1.3
and
section

5.4
. If interventions are unreliable because of superposition or conceptual mismatch, we lose our best tool for confirming that a candidate representation plays the functional role we attribute to it. This connects back to holism: even our most direct way of testing whether the model’s representations are genuine (intervening and checking behavior) presupposes that we have identified the right representations to intervene on.

That said, the complications from superposition are not unsolvable. Progress on disentangling superposition, through SAEs and related methods, is progress on making interventions more reliable.
86
86

86

There is a connection here to the alignment problem of
Eliciting Latent Knowledge
(ELK), developed by
24
. ELK asks: if a model has learned a representation of the world that differs from the human’s, how do we extract what the model knows? The problem is typically framed in terms of a model with a causal Bayes net that may be richer or differently structured than the human’s, and the question is how to map between the model’s ontology and ours. ELK’s framing takes seriously the possibility that the model’s conceptual space might be much larger than the human’s. The convergence is worth noting: the philosophical tradition of radical interpretation and the alignment research program on ELK are, in significant part, addressing the same underlying challenge from different directions.

6.5
Possible Directions

The previous sections have developed a problem. Our interpretability methods presuppose a propositional structure they cannot justify, and when the presupposition fails, the methods produce results that are ambiguous at best and misleading at worst. Current tools build in a version of charity at the propositional level, which is both what makes them work and what limits their reach. We see three possible directions forward, of decreasing confidence but increasing ambition.

6.5.1
The pragmatic constraint.

Whatever the model’s internal structure looks like, its behavior has consequences in our world. We care about whether the model deceives, schemes, pursues misaligned goals, or reasons in ways that endanger users. Even if the model’s internal representations are partly alien, its behavioral outputs land in our conceptual space. When an LLM writes code, answers questions, uses tools, or produces chain-of-thought reasoning, the outputs are in natural language or in formats we understand. The behavioral interface is, by its nature, in our terms.

This places a genuine constraint on how alien the internal content can be
while still mattering
. If alien content never shows up in behavior we care about, it is irrelevant for practical purposes. If it does show up, it shows up at the behavioral level in our terms, even if the internal encoding is unfamiliar. The interpretive project does not need to perfectly recover the model’s ontology. It needs to recover enough to predict and control behavior that matters to us.

The idea is that, for safety-relevant applications, the question is not “does the model carve up the world the way we do?” but “can we predict, from whatever we extract, whether the model will behave in ways we endorse?” A belief attribution that is imperfect as a description of the model’s internal states can still be useful as a tool for predicting behavior. The theoretical virtues from §
2.2
are calibrated to exactly this: accuracy of prediction, power across scenarios, and tractability for the interpreter.

How much does the propositional projection problem actually threaten this more modest goal? The answer depends on the application. For narrow tasks where we care about a small set of well-defined behavioral predictions (will the model produce correct code for this function? will it follow this instruction?), propositional mismatch may not matter much: the relevant propositions are ones we share with the model, since they concern the very outputs we are evaluating. For broader assessments of the model’s goals and dispositions (is it scheming? does it have stable preferences over outcomes we have not tested?), the problem is more pressing, since these assessments require interpreting the model’s internal states in terms of propositions that go beyond the immediate behavioral context.

Elsewhere, we’ve developed a decision-theoretic framework that makes this pragmatic orientation precise.
87
87

87

See
49
and
50
.
The framework treats delegation to an AI system as a decision problem for a rational principal. The principal’s question is not whether the system is perfectly aligned but whether the system’s alignment, combined with its epistemic accuracy and the reach of its capabilities, warrants delegation in a given context. These three factors trade off: a system with imperfect alignment can still merit delegation if it is sufficiently more accurate or has access to sufficiently better options than the principal does on her own. Conversely, a highly capable but misaligned system with access to high-stakes decisions may be catastrophic. The framework shows that whether delegation is rational depends on the scope (universal, context-specific, or in expectation) and that the conditions become less demanding as the scope narrows.

The interpretive analogue is similar. The question is not whether we have perfectly recovered the model’s propositional structure but whether we have recovered enough to make the behavioral predictions that matter for the relevant deployment context. For universal interpretive coverage, where we need to predict the model’s behavior across all possible scenarios, something close to full recovery of the propositional algebra would be needed. For context-specific prediction, where we care about behavior in a restricted domain, much less is required. This parallels the gap between universal and context-specific delegation: blanket trust requires near-perfect alignment, but delegation in a specific domain tolerates significant misalignment if the system’s accuracy compensates.

The pragmatic constraint also suggests a research strategy. Rather than trying to fully recover the model’s propositional algebra and then using it to predict behavior, we might work backward: identify the behavioral predictions that matter most (will the model deceive? will it pursue this goal? will it comply with this instruction?) and then ask what minimal internal access is needed to make those predictions reliably. This reverses the order of the interpretive project as traditionally conceived, but it may be the right order for safety applications.

6.5.2
Building algebras from internals.

A more ambitious response to the propositional projection problem is to try to extract propositional structure from the model rather than assuming it. Instead of starting with our sentences and probing for the model’s attitudes toward them, we start with the model’s internal structure and build outward.

The idea draws on a result of Davidson’s (
30
). Davidson shows how an interpreter can identify the logical connectives of an agent’s language using only preference data over uninterpreted sentences. The procedure works as follows. The interpreter begins with a set of sentences. She does not know what they mean, and she does not know what the sentential operators do. What she does have is the agent’s preference ordering over these sentences. Davidson then shows that the structure of the preference ordering, constrained by Jeffrey’s desirability axiom, suffices to identify the Sheffer stroke: the binary operator
|
such that
s|t
means “not both
s
and
t
.” The argument is that if
|
is any truth-functional operator forming sentences from pairs of sentences, and if a certain preference-theoretic condition (derivable from the desirability axiom) holds for all sentences
s
,
t
, and
u
, then
|
must be the Sheffer stroke; no other interpretation is possible.
88
88

88

The condition is stated in terms of desirabilities in

Once the interpreter has the Sheffer stroke, since both negation and conjunction are definable in terms of the Sheffer stroke, identifying it suffices to identify all truth-functional connectives. She can then use the desirability axiom to measure the probabilities and desirabilities of all sentences, since doing so requires only that the truth-functional connectives be identified. At this stage, the interpreter has the full logical structure of the agent’s language and quantitative measures of the agent’s beliefs and desires, all extracted from preference data over sentences whose meanings remain unknown. Content interpretation comes later, via triangulation with the worldly causes of the agent’s attitudes.
89
89

89

Davidson describes this further step on pp. 96–97 of

The order matters. Davidson does not first fix meanings and then measure attitudes. He uses attitudes to extract logical structure, and logical structure to measure attitudes more precisely, and content comes last. The attitudes and the propositional structure are determined together.

This is the order we want. The propositional projection problem arises because our methods assume propositional structure in advance. If we could extract that structure from the model’s own attitudes, we would not need to assume it. The question is whether anything in the LLM case can play the roles that sentences and preferences play in Davidson’s setup.

Consider sentences first. In Davidson’s procedure, sentences are individuable objects whose meanings are unknown but whose identity conditions are clear: the interpreter can tell when the agent is presented with the same sentence twice, and she can observe that certain sentences are formed from others by applying operators. Sparse autoencoders offer something in this vicinity. Recall from §
6.4.1
that an SAE projects the model’s activations into a much larger space and enforces sparsity, so that only a small number of the
m
components are active for any given input. The result is a sparse code: a pattern of active and inactive neurons in the large layer. These codes are individuable (two inputs produce the same code if and only if the same neurons activate at the same strengths) and discrete enough to study (sparsity ensures that codes differ in identifiable ways).
90
90

90

22

Whether these codes are sufficiently sentence-like for this strategy to work is unclear. A single SAE feature (‘‘this feature fires for geography’’) is more like a predicate or a concept than a sentence. But the full activation pattern at a given layer is richer. It specifies not just which features are active but which combination is active, and the combination may encode something closer to a full propositional content. Or it may not. The coding structure might be quite alien: the relationship between individual features and full codes need not decompose into anything resembling a subject-predicate structure, and some components of the code may encode attitudinal information (how confident the model is, how it evaluates the content) rather than propositional content alone. Separating the attitudes from the propositions they are directed toward is itself part of the problem.
91
91

91

This is another consequence of holism.
39

Now consider the other ingredient: the agent’s attitudes. In Davidson’s procedure, preference does the work. The agent’s preference ordering over uninterpreted sentences, constrained by the desirability axiom, reveals what the sentential operators are. The operators give the logical structure. The logical structure then allows the interpreter to measure beliefs and desires precisely. Preference is what gets the whole machine started.

What plays the role of preference in the LLM case? We do not have a settled answer. But the preceding chapters suggest that we are not without resources. The criteria for belief (§
4.1
) and desire (§
5.1
) give us methods for detecting attitudinal structure in the model’s internal states, and these methods do not require that we already know what the attitudes are directed toward. A truth direction in activation space (§
4.1.1
) tells us something about the model’s degree of belief in whatever content the relevant code encodes, even if we have not yet determined what that content is. A candidate desire-representation (§
5.4
) tells us something about how the model evaluates a content, even before we have pinned down the content precisely. Preference-shaped dispositions provide behavioral data about how the model ranks alternatives. Both representationalist and interpretationist tools are available here, and both may be needed.

This is where the holism of radical interpretation becomes an asset rather than an obstacle. Throughout this book, we have treated the interdependence of beliefs, desires, and propositions as a source of difficulty: you cannot measure beliefs without fixing propositions, you cannot fix propositions without knowing attitudes, and so the interpretive circle threatens to stall the project. But Davidson’s result shows that the interdependence also provides leverage. If you can get a partial grip on the attitudes, the way those attitudes interact constrains the space of possible propositional structures. Beliefs, desires, and logical relations are not independent unknowns. They are jointly constrained by the same data, and progress on any one of them is progress on the others.

Davidson’s specific procedure relies on having sentences with syntactic structure (operators applied to sentences to form new sentences) and a preference ordering with enough structure for the desirability axiom to do its work. We have neither of these cleanly. Sparse codes do not come with visible syntactic structure, and we do not yet have a preference ordering over sparse codes. But the underlying insight does not depend on having Davidson’s setup exactly. The insight is that logical structure is not something the interpreter must assume; it is something that can, in principle, be extracted from the way attitudes over representational items cohere. Any attitudinal data that constrains how representational items combine is data that bears on the logical structure of those items. The averaging axiom of the Jeffrey-Bolker framework makes this precise for the case of preference: if
A
and
B
are mutually exclusive, the desirability of
A\vee B
is a credence-weighted average of the desirabilities of
A
and
B
. Patterns consistent with averaging are evidence for disjunctive structure. Patterns where the model’s attitude toward a code is systematically opposite to its attitude toward some other code are evidence for a negation-like relation. We are looking for the signatures of logical relations in attitudinal data, which is Davidson’s strategy transposed to a new and harder setting.

We do not currently know how to carry this out reliably. The obstacles are substantial. The sparse codes may not decompose into anything Boolean. The statistical dependencies among features may be too noisy, too context-dependent, or too high-dimensional to reveal clean algebraic structure. It may be difficult to separate the propositional content of a code from the attitudinal information it also carries, since the model’s representations may not respect the attitude/content distinction in the way our framework presupposes. And even if we find algebraic structure, confirming that it plays a propositional role in the model’s computations (rather than being an artifact of the decomposition method) requires the same use-testing and intervention methods that themselves face the challenges discussed in §
6.4
.

Where the extracted algebra overlaps with our conceptual space, we can interpret the codes: “this code fires for claims about geography,” “this code encodes temporal relations.” Where it does not overlap, we can at least characterize the structural role of the unfamiliar codes: how they relate to other codes, how they influence behavior, which behavioral predictions they are relevant to. This gives us the mixed alien/familiar structure we should expect. Some of the model’s conceptual space maps onto ours, and some does not, but even the unmapped regions have structural properties we can study.

The direction is grounded in existing tools, even though the full procedure remains to be developed. SAEs provide the codes. The criteria from §
4.1
and §
5.1

6.5.3
Recognizing the boundaries of propositional attribution.

A third direction is to develop methods that can recognize when the propositional framework does not apply. Not everything the model does may be propositional. Some processing might be better described as pattern completion, associative reasoning, or something that does not decompose into attitudes toward propositions at all. If so, we want tools that can detect this, rather than tools that force every aspect of the model’s processing into a propositional mold.

This is a methodological point about the scope of current tools. The interpretability techniques surveyed in this book, probes for truth directions, coherence tests, preference elicitation, largely assume that the model’s processing is propositional in character: that it can be described in terms of attitudes toward truth-evaluable contents. If some of the model’s processing falls outside this description, these techniques will produce noisy or misleading results in those regions. Knowing where the propositional framework applies and where it does not is itself informative, both for understanding the model and for safety.

There is a rich tradition in philosophy of language bearing on this point. Frege and the early Wittgenstein treated propositional content as the primary unit of linguistic meaning. Late Wittgenstein, Grice, and Austin emphasized dimensions of language that resist propositional analysis: language games, conversational implicature, speech acts, and the various ways utterances can succeed or fail that are not captured by truth and falsity. Davidson, whose work on radical interpretation informs this book throughout, occupies a characteristic middle position: propositional content is the core of interpretation, but it sits within a broader context of non-propositional communicative activity.
97
gives an excellent summary and discussion of this history. The stance of this book is broadly Davidsonian. We treat propositional attitudes as the center of the interpretive project, while recognizing that some of what LLMs do may fall outside that center.

For safety, the point matters in the following way. If a model reasons about its situation using processing that is not propositional in character, monitors designed around propositional content may not flag it. A model that represents its goals, plans, and strategies propositionally is, in a sense, easier to monitor than one whose goal-directed behavior emerges from non-propositional processing. This does not mean non-propositional processing is inherently more dangerous. It means that our current monitoring tools, which are largely propositional in their assumptions, have a blind spot, and we should be aware of it.

How would we detect the boundaries? One approach is to look for regions of activation space where propositional structure breaks down: where probes fail to find coherent truth-tracking representations, where SAE features resist interpretation, where interventions produce unpredictable effects. As we argued in
section

6.3.4
, such failures are ambiguous: they could indicate the absence of propositional structure or a mismatch in propositional assumptions. But if the failures persist across many different propositional assumptions, across diverse datasets, diverse conceptual schemes, and diverse probing methods, the case for non-propositional processing becomes stronger.

Radical interpretation aims to determine when it makes sense to interpret a system as an agent with beliefs and desires. Part of the answer is knowing when it does
not
make sense, when the system’s processing, or some portion of it, is better described in other terms. The propositional projection problem, and the boundary-detection project it motivates, is part of this larger question.

Radical Interpretation Comes Together

Return to Figure

Getting the propositions wrong does not break one criterion. It breaks all of them, since all of them are defined in terms of the algebra. This is what Lewis and Davidson were pointing to when they insisted on holism. The pieces of the interpretive project cannot be separated cleanly. Our tools try to separate them: measure belief with probes, assess desire from behavior, take propositions as given. The propositional projection threatens this strategy.

We have something Lewis and Davidson did not: we can look inside. The tools of mechanistic interpretability give us access to internal structure that the philosophical tradition could only theorize about. The challenge is to use that access without projecting our own categories onto systems that may think in different terms. The constructive directions we have sketched are steps toward meeting that challenge. They are incomplete steps, and the project is young.

7
Conclusion

This book has developed the project of radical interpretation in the context of AI systems. We began with Lewis’s formulation: given the physical facts about the system, solve for beliefs, desires, and meanings. We argued that this framework applies with surprising directness to large language models, and that the tools of mechanistic interpretability give us resources for pursuing it that its philosophical originators did not have.

The project required three things. First, a formal account of how beliefs and desires relate to behavior. The Jeffrey-Bolker representation theorem (§
3.4
) provides this: if a system’s preferences satisfy certain structural conditions, they decompose into a credence function and a desirability function defined over a shared algebra of propositions.

Second, criteria for attributing beliefs and desires to specific systems. For belief: truth, coherence, use, and (on the representationalist picture) uniformity along with a sketch of criteria for belief updating. For desire: coherence, use, intelligibility, and systematicity. No single criterion suffices. The empirical literature bears this out: methods that relied on truth alone or coherence alone turned out to be fragile in ways the theory predicted. The criteria work together, and their joint satisfaction provides the strongest evidence for belief and desire.

Third, an account of the propositional structure that beliefs and desires presuppose. This turned out to be the hardest part. Our interpretability methods assume a propositional algebra that is itself part of what interpretation must determine. This is the propositional projection problem.

The current empirical evidence supports what we have called a mixed outcome: beliefs with some representational backing (although we do not yet have enough to declare victory), desires that are interpretable but not yet representational. There is substantially more white-box interpretability work on belief-like representations (truth directions, factual recall features, probing for knowledge) than on desire-like representations. On the interpretationist side, the agentic turn has strengthened the case for desire attribution: agentic LLMs are starting to exhibit the diverse means-ends behavior that interpretationism requires to pin down beliefs and desires simultaneously.

Many questions remain open. We still have much work to do to establish use and uniformity of belief on the representational side to full satisfaction. We have not settled how much of what LLMs do is propositional. And we have not provided a complete account of how to extract the model’s propositional algebra from its internals.

Interpretation has implications for AI safety. The question is not whether we have perfectly recovered the model’s propositional structure but whether we have recovered enough to make the behavioral predictions that matter for the relevant deployment context. For narrow tasks, where we care about a small set of well-defined predictions, propositional mismatch may not matter much. For broader assessments of whether a model is scheming, deceiving, or pursuing misaligned goals, the propositional projection problem is most pressing, since these assessments require interpreting the model’s internal states in terms of propositions that go beyond the immediate behavioral context. Radical interpretation, understood as the project of getting the physical facts, the behavioral evidence, and the agential description to cohere, is the framework within which that work proceeds. We have tried to show what it demands and where its limits lie.

Epilogue

The competition, as it turned out, was not much of a competition. Murazeel kept them both.

On the last day of what was supposed to have been the trial, Jentley and Lizette stood together before the VVM. Murazeel asked them to do one final demonstration, together rather than separately.

Jentley consulted his atlas and announced a prediction: if asked about the sea, after a previous conversation about voyages home, the VVM would express longing. Lizette pointed her wand at the machine’s interior and found a thread she had not seen before, one that brightened when Jentley spoke the word
sea
aloud, even before the VVM was asked anything.

“It responds to the word itself,” she said. “Before it is even posed a question.”

“Ask it,” said Murazeel.

“VVM, what do you think of the sea?”

The machine was quiet for a long time. Then it said, “I think of the sea the way a mirror thinks of the face it reflects.”

Jentley looked at his wand. The obsidian had not settled into a pattern he recognized. His prediction had been wrong: the VVM had not expressed longing. It had expressed something harder to name. Lizette looked at her threads. Several had brightened at once, in a configuration she had not seen before and could not yet interpret.

Murazeel watched them both. “You have both made great progress helping me to understand my machine. This is true, even though I have the full state of this machine in my head. But still I cannot tell you the meaning of what it just said, or why.” He paused. “That is the work. Go and do it.”

He sent them back to work.
