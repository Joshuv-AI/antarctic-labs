Coronary bypass outcomes in chronic limb-threatening ischemia are
significantly worse than in patients with non-critical lower extremity
arterial disease
Kentaro Shirakura1 · Yuki Setogawa1 · Ryohei Ushioda1 · Daisuke Takeyoshi1 · Masahiro Tsutsui1,2 ·
Shinsuke Kikuchi3 · Nobuyoshi Azuma3 · Hiroyuki Kamiya1 · Shingo Kunioka1
Received: 10 May 2026 / Accepted: 18 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026

Abstract
Purpose Lower extremity arterial disease (LEAD) is a risk factor in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting
(CABG); however, the impact of chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI), its most advanced stage, remains unclear. We
evaluated early and long-term outcomes of CABG in CLTI.
Method We retrospectively analyzed 334 consecutive patients who underwent isolated CABG between 2014 and 2024.
Patients were stratified into three groups: CLTI (n = 22), non-CLTI LEAD (n = 41), and non-LEAD (n = 271). The primary
endpoint was long-term survival; the secondary endpoints were 30-day mortality and major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events.
Results The CLTI group had higher rates of diabetes mellitus (90.9%) and hemodialysis (86.4%) (p < 0.01). Thirty-day
mortality was similar (9.1%, 2.4%, and 5.5%; p = 0.52), whereas five-year survival was significantly lower in the CLTI group
(39.8% vs. 86.2% and 90.8%; p < 0.01). A Cox analysis showed that age, hemodialysis, and acute myocardial infarction were
independent predictors of 5-year survival.
Conclusions CLTI predicts poor long-term survival because of comorbidities. In this high-risk population, individualized
strategies balancing the extent of coronary revascularization against operative risk may achieve acceptable early outcomes,
although the safety of incomplete/functional revascularization requires confirmation in larger cohorts.
Keywords CLTI · CABG · LEAD

However, LEAD severity varies and is not reflected in current scoring systems. Chronic limb-threatening ischemia

by rest pain, gangrene, or ulceration >2 weeks, is associated
with high mortality and amputation, with a 2-year survival
of 60%–65% [4–6]. Whether CLTI adversely affects CABG
outcomes remains unclear. Our institution is a high-volume
CLTI center in Japan that actively performs lower extremity
revascularization using surgical bypass and endovascular
therapy. Based on this experience, we developed a multidisciplinary “Heart-Limb Team” strategy incorporating
minimally invasive techniques to optimize outcomes for
patients with concomitant coronary artery disease and CLTI
[4, 5]. We investigated CABG outcomes in CLTI patients
compared with non-CLTI LEAD and non-LEAD patients

and evaluated whether CLTI represents an independent risk
factor for CABG.

Methods
Between April 2014 and April 2024, 573 patients underwent CABG at our institution. After excluding 239 patients
who underwent concomitant procedures, 334 patients
with isolated CABG were analyzed. Patients were stratified by LEAD status into three groups (Fig. 1): CLTI (n =
22), non-CLTI LEAD (n = 41), and non-LEAD (n = 271).
Postoperative coronary angiography or computed tomography angiography was performed within 1 week after surgery to evaluate early graft patency, except when clinically
contraindicated or declined. The primary endpoint was
long-term survival; the secondary endpoints were 30-day
mortality and major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular
events (MACCE). Independent censoring was assumed in
the Kaplan–Meier and Cox proportional hazards analyses.
Patients were censored on the last date they were known to
be alive or at the end of follow-up.

Surgical procedure and CABG strategy at our
institution
CABG was performed via median sternotomy or left minithoracotomy, primarily with on-pump arrest. LITA was
routinely used for the left anterior descending artery. Additional revascularization was achieved with sequential SVG
or a second arterial conduit (right internal thoracic or radial
artery), particularly in patients <70 years. Internal thoracic
arteries were harvested in a pedicled fashion; SVGs were
generally skeletonized. Sequential or composite grafting
was used as appropriate to optimize conduit use.

Definition of significant coronary stenosis and
complete revascularization
Significant coronary stenosis was defined as ≥70% luminal
narrowing or ≥50% for left main disease, based on quantitative assessment by the interventional cardiology team [7].
Complete revascularization was defined as grafting of all
significant stenotic vessels. For multi-vessel diseases within
the same territory, bypass to the largest target vessel responsible for the ischemic area was prioritized to achieve functional revascularization. Left main disease required grafting
of both the LAD and LCX systems.

Evaluation of limb ischemia severity
LEAD was defined according to the Global vascular guidelines as ABI ≤0.90 or imaging evidence of lower-extremity
arterial stenosis/occlusion. CLTI was defined as LEAD with
rest pain, non-healing ulcer, or gangrene lasting >2 weeks
[6]. Patients who previously met CLTI criteria but improved
were reclassified as Non-CLTI LEAD (n = 6).

Heart-limb team strategies
The Heart-Limb Team, comprising cardiac and vascular surgeons, prioritized a 'Limb-First through Heart-First' strategy: (1) CABG as a prerequisite for limb-salvage surgery in
patients with critical ischemia and stable coronary status, or
(2) CABG to prevent death in patients at imminent risk of
a coronary event. Patient selection was determined through
multidisciplinary discussion; those with uncontrollable systemic infection (e.g., severe foot osteomyelitis) were managed with endovascular or palliative therapy.

Statistical analysis
Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± SD or
median (IQR) and compared using ANOVA or Kruskal–
Wallis test; categorical variables by chi-square or Fisher’s
exact test with Bonferroni post hoc correction. Survival was
estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using
a log-rank test. Cox regression identified independent predictors of 5-year survival. Variables with p values < 0.10 in
a univariable analysis or clinical relevance were included;
multicollinearity was assessed by the variance inflation factor. All tests were two-sided; p values < 0.05 were considered significant. All analyses were performed using Stata
(StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Discussion

without LEAD, although CLTI was not an independent risk

Operative strategies for LEAD patients
Surgical strategy in LEAD patients should be individualized based on comorbidities and anatomy. LEAD is
commonly associated with diabetes, dialysis-dependent
renal failure, and cerebrovascular disease, increasing the
operative risk [8–13]. Optimal graft selection and extent

of revascularization remain controversial. Bianco et al.
demonstrated that complete revascularization improved
5-year survival (86.5% vs. 82.1%) and reduced the risk
of MACCE (hazard ratio, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.70–0.95) [14].
In our study, complete revascularization rates were 68.2%
(CLTI), 95.1% (Non-CLTI), and 97.0% (Non-LEAD). In
CLTI, conduit selection is often limited by lower-extremity

survival difference between bilateral and single internal
thoracic artery grafting in LEAD [13]. The comparable
long-term survival between the Non-CLTI LEAD and NonLEAD groups supports aggressive revascularization when
feasible in LEAD without CLTI.

Risk stratification beyond binary LEAD classification

Fig. 2 Kaplan–Meier estimates of long-term survival. Five-year survival was significantly lower in the CLTI group than in the Non-CLTI

Preoperative risk scores differed among groups (p < 0.01);
however, 30-day mortality did not, whereas 5-year survival and MACCE were significantly worse in the CLTI
group. Current models treat LEAD as a single entity and
predict only 30-day mortality, failing to capture long-term
differences. In the SPINACH registry, 2-year survival was
64.9%, with older age, low BMI, renal failure, heart failure,
and higher WIfI grade as independent predictors, stratifying 2-year survival from >90% to ~20% [5]. Setogawa et
al. reported that the SPINACH SCORE and GNRI better
predict mid-term outcomes than the Japan SCORE [15]. In
our cohort, the 5-year survival rates in the CLTI, Non-CLTI
LEAD, and Non-LEAD groups were 39.8%, 86.2%, and
90.8%, respectively, with no significant difference between
the latter two groups, highlighting the markedly poor prognosis of CLTI after cardiac surgery.

The dilemma of complete revascularization in CLTI
The rate of complete revascularization was lower in the CLTI
group (68.2%) than in the Non-CLTI LEAD (95.1%) and
Non-LEAD (97.0%) groups, reflecting a deliberate strategy
to limit operative invasiveness and preserve the saphenous
vein for distal bypass. Despite incomplete revascularization,
30-day mortality and mediastinitis did not differ between
the groups. However, given the small CLTI cohort (n = 22)
and the limited number of patients who underwent incomplete revascularization (n = 7), this finding should not be
interpreted as wsuggests only that our tailored, less-invasive
strategy did not appear to compromise early safety in this
highly selected population, an observation that requires confirmation in larger studies. Notably, 54.6% of CLTI patients
subsequently underwent limb-salvage surgery, confirming
CABG as an effective bridge to limb preservation.

strategy
Patients with stable LEAD had long-term survival comparable to that of non-LEAD, likely reflecting strict preoperative selection by the Heart-Limb Team (i.e., only patients
judged capable of tolerating CABG were referred) and
should undergo equally aggressive revascularization. The

Limitations
This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, singlecenter design limits generalizability. The small CLTI cohort
(n = 22) reduced statistical power, increasing the risk of
type II error. A substantial preoperative selection bias is
inherent in our Heart-Limb Team strategy, whereby only
patients deemed capable of tolerating CABG were referred
for surgery. Although we do not have a precise retrospective headcount of patients who were deemed ineligible for
initial CABG, our multidisciplinary Heart-Limb Team conducted rigorous screening at the initial conference stage;
patients with prohibitive surgical risk, profound frailty, or
uncontrollable active systemic infection (e.g., severe foot
osteomyelitis requiring urgent primary amputation) were
directed toward endovascular therapy or palliative management rather than CABG. Because these patients were
excluded at the pre-operative conference stage, they were
not captured in our surgical cohort, representing an inherent and unquantifiable selection bias in this study. Substantial heterogeneity existed within the CLTI cohort regarding
operative strategy and completeness of revascularization.
To address this directly, we performed subgroup analyses
stratifying the CLTI group by surgical approach (MIDCAB
vs. sternotomy CABG; Tables 4 and 7) and by completeness
of revascularization (Tables 5 and 8). No statistically significant differences were observed in postoperative outcomes
or long-term MACCE between these subgroups; however,
given the very small subgroup sizes, particularly the MIDCAB (n = 4) and incomplete revascularization (n = 7) subgroups, these analyses are substantially underpowered, and
the absence of statistically significant differences should not
be interpreted as evidence of equivalence or as support for
the safety of these approaches.
WIfI-based stratification was not feasible due to the limited sample size and incomplete data; thus, the impact of
limb ischemia severity could not be fully adjusted. Although
we captured frailty using the Clinical Frailty Scale (Table 1),
other residual confounders, such as detailed nutritional

13

status beyond the GNRI, could not be fully accounted for
given the small sample size.

Conclusion
CLTI is a significant predictor of a poor long-term prognosis
after CABG. In high-risk CLTI patients, a strategy prioritizing operative safety and conduit preservation may yield
incomplete revascularization; in our small cohort, this did
not appear to compromise short-term outcomes, although
this observation is hypothesis-generating and requires
confirmation in larger studies given the limited statistical
power. More than half of the CLTI patients subsequently
underwent limb-salvage procedures, confirming that CABG
serves as a vital physiological bridge to limb preservation
in this fragile population and underscoring the value of a
multidisciplinary heart-limb team approach.
