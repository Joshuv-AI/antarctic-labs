Abstract
. The inducibility constant λ F \lambda_{F} of a graph F F is the asymptotically maximum induced density of F F in a growing sequence of graphs. This paper systematically investigates the case when F F has 6 vertices (and there are 78 cases to consider up to isomorphism and complementation). We show that flag algebras can compute the sharp upper bound on λ F \lambda_{F} in 36 cases of which, as far as the authors know, 30 are new results. In each of the solved cases, we also prove results about the structure of large (almost) extremal graphs. In particular, we establish perfect stability in all 32 cases when the extremal construction has no quasirandom parts. We also present conjectures about the value of λ F \lambda_{F} for 12 further cases (where the upper and lower bounds are very close to each other).



1.
Introduction

For graphs
F
and
G
with
\kappa\leq n
vertices
respectively, let
P(F,G)
denote the number of
\kappa
-subsets of

V(G)
that induce a graph isomorphic to
F
and let

p(F,G):=P(F,G)/\binom{n}{\kappa}
be the
(induced) density
of
F
in
G
.
For a given graph
F
, the
F
-inducibility problem
(or the
F
-problem
for short) asks for
p(F,n)
,
the maximum of
p(F,G)
over all graphs
G
with
n

vertices. By an easy averaging argument, one can show that the limit

\lambda_{F}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}p(F,n)

exists; we call it the

inducibility constant
of
F
.

The inducibility problem has drawn a great amount of interest since it
was introduced by Pippenger and Golumbic
[
47
]
in
1975. For some sample of results, see
e.g.
[
52
,
9
,
13
,
8
,
34
,
31
,
22
,
3
,
33
,
38
,
54
,
39
,
53
]
.
Also, it was considered for many other types of structures: for example, oriented
graphs
[
36
,
14
,
10
,
35
]
, hypercubes
[
27
,
28
,
6
]
, various tree-like structures
[
1
,
18
,
17
,
12
]
, constrained graphs (for example, triangle-free graphs
[
30
,
29
,
32
]
), etc.

Since the inducibility constant

\lambda_{F}
does not change if we replace
F
by its complement
\overline{F}
, it is
enough to consider only one graph from each complementary pair. If
F
is complete partite then a result by Brown and
Sidorenko
[
13
, Proposition 1]
implies that, in order to determine
the value of
p(F,n)
, it is enough to consider only complete partite
graphs on
[n]
and the problem in the limit reduces to maximising
some polynomial on the space of part ratios.

Each
3
-vertex graph or its complement is complete partite, so they are covered e.g. by the above result of Brown and
Sidorenko
[
13
]
.

All
4
-vertex graphs
F
were resolved by the results
in
[
9
,
23
,
13
,
34
]
except when
F=P_{4}
is
the
4
-vertex path. The best known lower bound

\lambda_{P_{4}}\geq 1173/5824=0.2014\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
is due to Even-Zohar and
Linial
[
22
]
while the best known upper bound

\lambda_{P_{4}}\leq 0.204513\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
comes from flag algebras.

Even-Zohar and Linial
[
22
, Table 2]
produced the summary of
known and new results for
5
-vertex graphs
F
.
After the publication of
[
22
]
, the values of inducibility constant for some new
5
-vertex graphs
F
were determined in
[

,
42
,
40
,
7
]
but there are still
5
(non-equivalent) remaining open cases, described in
[
7
]
.

In this paper, we systematically investigate the inducibility problem
for graphs on
6
vertices, using flag algebras to prove upper bounds. There are
156
non-isomorphic
6
-vertex graphs, which we
group into
78
complementary pairs
\{F_{i},\overline{F}_{i}\}
for
0\leq i\leq 77
. (Note that, since
\binom{6}{2}
is odd, no
6
-vertex graph is self-complementary.)
Our results are as follows; they are also summarised in Table
1
below.

Using the package
FlagAlgebraToolbox
of Bodnár
[
5
]
, we could produce the
sharp upper bound on the inducibility constant
\lambda_{F}
in 36 cases (out of
78
) of which 30 cases are, as far as we can see, new results.
There are three further cases where the package did not give the required sharp result but the exact value was previously known by other methods, and we include these cases in the table:
F_{1}
, whose inducibility constant was determined by Liu, Mubayi and Reiher
[
42
, Theorem 1.13]
, the cycle case
F_{51}=C_{6}
, treated by Brandt, Lidický and Pfender as recorded in Brandt’s thesis
[
11
]
, and the net graph
F_{43}
, determined by Blumenthal and Phillips
[
4
]
.
In all cases solved here, we also describe almost extremal graphs of large order
n
within
o(n^{2})
adjacency edits. It happens in each case that, informally speaking, there is a unique limit construction. The latter statement can be formalised using the language of graphons (see Lovász’ book
[
43
]
), where a graphon
W
is called
F
-extremal
if the appropriately defined density
p(F,W)
is equal to its maximum possible value
\lambda_{F}
. Then, in all cases solved here, the extremal graphon
W
is unique and happens to be a step graphon (with finitely many steps).

If each value of the extremal graphon
W
is 0 or 1 (which happens in 32 cases) then it is natural to represent constructions as blowups of a
pattern

B
(a graph, with loops allowed). In all of these 32 cases, we prove furthermore that the so-called
perfect
B
-stability
holds using criteria from
[
46
]
(see Section
2.2
for details). This is a rather strong structural property that, in particular, implies that every large extremal graph is exactly a blowup of
B
. Thus in order to determine
p(F,n)
exactly for large
n
, it is enough to maximise the density of
F
in an
n
-vertex blowup of
B
, which amounts to maximising an explicit polynomial in integers summing up to
n
. We do not attempt to do this integer optimisation. However, we do determine the (unique in all solved cases) limiting vector of optimal part ratios.

There are four cases, namely,
i\in\{13,70,75,77\}
in Table
1
, when almost extremal graphs are quasirandom balanced bipartite graphs of appropriate edge density
p_{i}
, and we show that every large almost optimal graph is close in the edit distance to this form.
Thus, in each of these cases, the (unique) extremal graphon
W
consists of two parts
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
, each of measure
1/2
, with
W
being
0
on all pairs inside a part and
p_{i}
on all cross-pairs. It is unclear how to define perfect stability when there are quasirandom pairs. Nonetheless, we also establish some finer structure results about almost extremal graphs that, in particular, imply the uniqueness of the extremal graphon.

There are eleven
6
-vertex graphs that are complete partite.
The package could determine the inducibility constant (and prove results enough for establishing perfect stability) for all of them except for
\overline{F}_{1}=T_{2,1,1,1,1}
,
where
T_{\ell_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\ell_{m-1}}
denote the complete
m
-partite graph with parts of sizes
\ell_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\ell_{m-1}
.
In the case of
F_{1}
, the inducibility constant was determined by Liu, Mubayi and Reiher
[
42
, Theorem 1.13]
and perfect stability was proved by Liu, Ma and Zhu
[
41
]
, and we note this result in our table. Given the general result of Brown and Sidorenko
[
13
]
for complete partite
F
, the inducibility constant (or even the whole inducibility function
p(F,n)
) was computed without flag algebras for many other
6
-vertex complete partite graphs
F
. Of course, the problem for the empty and complete graphs,
F_{0}=T_{6}
and
\overline{F}_{0}=T_{1,1,1,1,1,1}
, is trivial.
Already Pippenger and Golumbic
[
47
, Theorem 10]
determined the inducibility function of
T_{\ell,\ell}
for every
\ell
. Brown and Sidorenko
[
13
, Proposition 2]
proved that, for every
n\geq\ell_{0}+\ell_{1}
, the value of
p(T_{\ell_{0},\ell_{1}},n)
is attained by a complete partite graph with exactly two parts while the corresponding perfect stability was proved by Liu, Pikhurko, Sharifzadeh and
Staden
[
40
, Theorem 1.3]
; in particular, the solution for
\overline{F}_{32}=T_{5,1}
,
\overline{F}_{56}=T_{4,2}
,
\overline{F}_{53}=T_{3,3}
easily follows. Bollobás, Egawa, Harris and Jin
[
8
]
determined the inducibility function for
\overline{F}_{8}=T_{2,2,2}
with perfect stability proved by Liu et al
[
40
, Theorem 1.4]
. The value of the inducibility constant of
\overline{F}_{3}=T_{2,2,1,1}
was determined by Yuster
[
55
]
with perfect stability proved by Liu, Ma and Zhu
[
41
]
. Solutions to the three remaining 6-vertex complete partite graphs
F
, namely
\overline{F}_{37}=T_{4,1,1}
,
\overline{F}_{17}=T_{3,2,1}
and
\overline{F}_{6}=T_{3,1,1,1}
, do not seem to appear anywhere in the literature.

Table
1
below summarises our findings (and also lists the answer in the special case
i=1
for completeness). Recall that all graphs are grouped into
78
complementary pairs indexed by
i
running from
0
to
77
. Row
i
of the table plots both
F_{i}
and its complement.
The fifth column presents either the exact value of
\lambda_{F_{i}}
or an upper
bound on it. The seventh column lists
N
, which is the number of vertices in
0
-flags that we needed in order to prove the upper bound (see Section
2.1
for details).

If we have a gap between the bounds, then we list the upper bound as the floating-point real that was returned by computer using
8
-vertex
0
-flags (with
N=8
being the maximum value which is feasible for computer calculations). While it is routine to convert computer’s output into a mathematical proof with only very small loss in the bound, we do not do this here as this would not provide any new insights.

If we are able to prove the sharp upper bound on
\lambda_{F_{i}}
via flag algebras, then we also list the pattern
B
(which we prove to be unique in all solved cases) whose blowups give the matching lower bound. For the known recursive cases
F_{43}
and
F_{51}
, the fourth column instead displays the recursive graphon
W
with equal parts and with
R
indicating recursion inside the corresponding part. That is,
W
is a self-similar graphon, where the domain is partitioned into a (possibly non-uniform) grid and some of the diagonal blocks, namely those indicated by
R
, map to linearly rescaled copies of
W
itself. Optimal limiting part ratios (which happen to be unique up to an automorphism of
B
in all solved cases) are not listed in the table due to space limitations but can be found in the corresponding theorems of Section
3
. When the value of
\lambda_{F_{i}}
is an irrational number, we list it as a polynomial of
\alpha
(where
\alpha
is usually an optimal ratio for some collection of parts of
B
) with the sixth column listing the minimal monic polynomial having
\alpha
as a root. The last column indicates whether perfect
stability was established (which, remarkably, is ‘Yes’ in all solved cases apart from the four special cases with quasirandom pairs).
For the rows where this entry is a pattern
B
, extremal constructions can be obtained from the graph
B
listed in the
fourth column as follows. We replace each hollow vertex with an
independent set and each solid vertex with a clique (and
R
means a recursive part, see Section
4
for details). For each edge, we
add either a complete bipartite graph or a binomial random
bipartite graph between the corresponding sets. The precise sizes of
the independent sets and cliques, as well as further details of the
construction, are provided in Section
3
.

As far as we know, apart from the complete partite graphs and their complements (that we discussed above) and the previously known values for
F_{43}
and
F_{51}

There are 12 further cases where the upper and lower bounds come very close to each other, discussed in Section

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{R}
denote the set of real numbers, and let
\mathbb{N}
denote the
set of non-negative integers. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

[n]:=\{0,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,n-1\}
. We may abbreviate an unordered pair

\{u,w\}
as
uw
, including the case when
u
and
w
are
single-digit numbers. For an integer
\kappa\geq 0
, let
n^{\underline{\kappa}}:=n(n-1)\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt(n-\kappa+1)
denote
the
falling
\kappa
-th factorial
of
n
.
For a set
X
and an integer
\kappa\geq 0
, the set
of all
\kappa
-subsets of
X
is denoted by
\binom{X}{\kappa}
.
For
a,b,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
a=b\pm\varepsilon
if

b-\varepsilon\leq a\leq b+\varepsilon
. We may omit ceiling/floor signs when they
are not essential.

A
pattern
is a pair
B=(V(B),E(B))
where
V(B)
is a set (of
vertices
) and
E(B)
is a symmetric subset of
V(B)^{2}
(of
edges
). Thus we allow loops on
vertices but no multiple edges. Its
order
is
v(B):=|V(B)|
.
A loop on vertex
u
is indicated by
\{u,u\}\in E(B)
or

uu\in E(B)
.
A
pattern automorphism
is a bijection
f:V(B)\to V(B)
such
that for all
u,w\in V(B)
,
uw\in E(B)
if and only if

f(u)f(w)\in E(B)
. (Thus it preserves loops and non-loops.)
The
neighbourhood
of a vertex
u
in
B
is

\Gamma_{B}(u):=\{w\in V(B)\mid\{u,w\}\in E(B)\}.

Note that

u\in\Gamma_{B}(u)
if and only if
u
has a loop. The
degree

of
u
is
\deg_{B}(u):=|\Gamma_{B}(u)|
.
These definitions also apply to graphs, which are patterns without loops.

For a graph
F
, its
complement
is

\overline{F}:=\left(V(F),\binom{V(F)}{2}\setminus E(F)\right)
. The

induced subgraph
on
X\subseteq V(F)
is

F[X]:=(X,\{uw\in E(F)\mid u,w\in X\}),

and for disjoint

X,Y\subseteq V(F)
,

F[X,Y]:=\{(u,w)\in X\times Y\mid uw\in E(F)\}
denotes the set of edges
between
X
and
Y
. When we say
subgraph
, we mean
induced subgraph
.

We will need the following special graphs:
K_{n}
is the complete graph on
n
vertices,
P_{n}
is the
path with
n
vertices, and
T_{n_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,n_{m-1}}
is the complete

m
-partite graph with parts of sizes
n_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,n_{m-1}
. The graph

T_{2,1}\cong P_{3}
is called the
cherry
. The vertex-disjoint union of
graphs
F
and
H
is denoted
F\sqcup H
. Small graphs or patterns
can be written as
(m,E)
, meaning the vertex set is
[m]
; for
example, the
4
-vertex path is
(4,\{01,12,23\})
and the pattern consisting of two isolated
loops is
(2,\{00,11\})
.

Let
B
be a pattern with vertex set
[m]
. For pairwise disjoint sets

V_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,V_{m-1}
(some possibly empty), the
blowup

B(V_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,V_{m-1})
is the (loopless) graph on

V=\bigcup_{i=0}^{m-1}V_{i}
where distinct
x\in V_{i}
and

y\in V_{j}
are adjacent if and only if
\{i,j\}\in E(B)
. In
particular,
V_{i}
induces a clique if
i
has a loop in
B
, and an
independent set otherwise. The family of all blowups of
B
is denoted
by
B{()}
.
The standard
(m-1)
-dimensional simplex
is

(2.1)

{\mathbbm{S}}_{m}:=\left\{(x_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{m-1})\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{m}\mid x_{0}+\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt+x_{m-1}=1\mbox{ and }\forall i\in[m]\ x_{i}\geq 0\right\}.

For
\bm{x}=(x_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{m-1})\in{\mathbbm{S}}_{m}
,
let
p(F,B(\bm{x}))
be the limit as
n\to\infty
of

p(F,B(V_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,V_{m-1}))
, where
|V_{i}|=(x_{i}+o(1))n
for

i\in[m]
. This is a continuous function on
{\mathbbm{S}}_{m}
(in fact, a
polynomial). Let

p(F,B{()}):=\sup\{p(F,B(\bm{x}))\mid\bm{x}\in{\mathbbm{S}}_{m}\}.

By the compactness of
{\mathbbm{S}}_{m}
the supremum is attained by at least
one
\bm{x}\in{\mathbbm{S}}_{m}
; such vectors will be called

(F,B)
-maximisers
. The pattern
B
is called
F
-minimal

if, for every pattern
B^{\prime}
(of order
m-1
) obtained from
B
by
removing one vertex, it holds that

p(F,B^{\prime}{()})<p(F,B{()})
. By compactness and continuity,
this holds if and only if the (closed) set of
(F,B)
-maximisers is
disjoint from the boundary of the simplex
{\mathbbm{S}}_{m}
. The pattern
B
is
called
F
-optimal
if
\lambda_{F}=p(F,B{()})
, that is,
we can attain the asymptotically optimal constant
\lambda_{F}
by some
blowups of
B
. If
F
and/or
B
is understood, we may omit them
from the above notation.

A
homomorphism
from a graph
F
to a pattern
B
is a function

f:V(F)\to V(B)
(not necessarily injective) such that for all
distinct
x,y\in V(F)
,
\{x,y\}\in E(F)
if and only if

\{f(x),f(y)\}\in E(B)
. This corresponds to assigning vertices of

F
to parts in a blowup of
B
to form an induced copy of
F
.
Note that a homomorphism in our definition has to preserve both edges and
non-edges. An

embedding

f
of a graph
F
into a graph
G
, denoted
f:F\hookrightarrow G
, is an injective homomorphism from
F
to
G
that preserves edges and non-edges. (Thus
f
is an isomorphism of
F
onto its
image.) For graphs
F
and

G
with
\kappa
and
n
vertices respectively, the
embedding density

t(F,G)
is the probability that a random injective map

V(F)\to V(G)
is an embedding. Note that

(2.2)

p(F,G)=\frac{\kappa!}{|\mathrm{aut}(F)|}\,t(F,G),

where
\mathrm{aut}(F)
is the automorphism group of
F
.

The
edit distance

\delta_{\mathrm{edit}}(G,H)
between graphs

G
and
H
of the same order is the minimum of

|E(G)\bigtriangleup f(E(H))|
over all bijections

f:V(H)\to V(G)
, that is, the smallest number of
edits
(i.e. adjacency changes) needed to make
one graph isomorphic to the other. The distance from
G
to a graph family

{\mathcal{G}}
is

\delta_{\mathrm{edit}}(G,{\mathcal{G}}):=\min\{\delta_{\mathrm{edit}}(G,H)\mid H\in{\mathcal{G}}\text{ and }v(H)=v(G)\}.

We often consider

{\mathcal{G}}=B{()}
, so
\delta_{\mathrm{edit}}(G,B{()})
is the
minimum number of edits required to transform
G
into a blowup of
B
.

We call a sequence of graphs
(G_{n})_{n\in{\mathbbm{N}}}

whose orders tend to infinity
almost
F
-extremal
(resp.
almost
c
-regular
) if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is
n_{0}
such that, for
every
n\geq n_{0}
, it holds that
p(F,G_{n})\geq\lambda_{F}-\varepsilon
(resp. at least
(1-\varepsilon)v(G_{n})
vertices
u
of
G_{n}
satisfy

\deg_{G_{n}}(u)=(c\pm\varepsilon)v(G_{n})
).

Asymptotic notation, such as
o(1)
, is taken with respect to

n\to\infty
(where
n
is usually the order of the unknown graph

G
).

We will also need the following routine facts.

Proposition 2.1
.

The maximum value of
p(x):=x^{5}(1-x)+x(1-x)^{5}
for
x\in[0,1]

is attained if and only if
x\in\{y_{0},1-y_{0}\}
, where

(2.3)

y_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{2\sqrt{10}-5}{12}}=0.16776\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\ .

Proof.

Define
Q(x):=p(x+1/2)
for
x\in{\mathbbm{R}}
. It can be verified that

Q(x)=-2x^{6}-\frac{5}{2}x^{4}+\frac{5}{8}x^{2}+\frac{1}{32}
. Also,
the derivative
Q^{\prime}(x)
is
x\left(-12x^{4}-10x^{2}+\frac{5}{4}\right)
and its five (simple) roots are 0 and when
x^{2}
is
(-5\pm 2\sqrt{10})/12
, including two complex roots. Thus the two square roots of
(2\sqrt{10}-5)/12>0
are the only two local maxima of
Q
, both are in
(-1/2,1/2)
and give the same value of the symmetric polynomial
Q
. This proves the proposition.
∎

Let us remark that the minimal monic polynomial of the real
y_{0}
from (
2.3
) is

y^{4}-2y^{3}+\frac{7}{3}\,y^{2}-\frac{4}{3}\,y+\frac{1}{6}=\frac{p^{\prime}(y)}{6(1-2y)},

and this polynomial will appear in a number of our theorems.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any fixed reals
a,b>0
, the maximum of
r(x):=x^{a}(1-x)^{b}
for
x\in[0,1]
is attained if and only if
x=a/(a+b)
.

Proof.

The derivative of
r
is
r^{\prime}(x)=x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}(a-(a+b)x)
so the proposition follows by checking the values of
r
on
0
,
1
and
a/(a+b)
.∎

2.1.
Flag algebras

As the flag algebra framework introduced by Razborov
[
48
]

is now standard (with
e.g.
[
49
,
2
,
19
,
26
,
37
]
giving detailed treatments),
we only provide the essential definitions required to describe a
flag algebra certificate.

Let
\tau
be a
type
, that is, a graph with vertex set
[q]
for some

q\in{\mathbbm{N}}
, where all
q
vertices are regarded as labelled. A
\tau
-flag

is a pair
(F,f)
consisting of a graph
F
and an embedding

f:[q]\to V(F)
of
\tau
into
F
.
This represents a partially labelled graph in
which the labelled vertices, called
roots
, induce a copy of

\tau
.
For
\tau
-flags
(F,f)
and
(H,h)
, define
P((F,f),(H,h))
as the
number of
(v(F)-q)
-subsets
X
of
V(H)\setminus h([q])
such that
the
\tau
-flag
(H[X\cup h([q])],h)
is isomorphic to
(F,f)
, where
isomorphisms must preserve each labelled root (in addition to edges and
non-edges). If
v(F)\leq v(H)
, the corresponding
flag density

is

p((F,f),(H,h)):=\frac{P((F,f),(H,h))}{\binom{v(H)-q}{v(F)-q}}.

For
s\geq q
, let
{\mathcal{F}}_{s}^{\tau}
denote the set of all
\tau
-flags
with
s
vertices, considered up to isomorphism.
In particular,
{\mathcal{F}}_{s}^{0}
is the set of isomorphism class
representatives of graphs of order
s
, since we let
0
denote the empty type that has no vertices. We fix an ordering
on
{\mathcal{F}}_{s}^{\tau}
for indexing vectors and matrices. For a
\tau
-flag

(H,h)
with
v(H)\geq s
, define the column vectors

(2.4)

\bm{V}_{(H,h)}^{\tau,s}:=\left(P((F,f),(H,h))\right)_{(F,f)\in{\mathcal{F}}_{s}^{\tau}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{v}_{(H,h)}^{\tau,s}:=\left(p((F,f),(H,h))\right)_{(F,f)\in{\mathcal{F}}_{s}^{\tau}},

which list the counts and densities, respectively, of all
s
-vertex

\tau
-flags in
(H,h)
, following the fixed ordering of

{\mathcal{F}}_{s}^{\tau}
.

We now describe the contents of a flag algebra certificate that
establishes an upper bound
\lambda_{F}\leq u
for a given graph
F
on

\kappa
vertices and
u\in{\mathbbm{R}}
. The certificate specifies an
integer
N\geq\kappa
and, for each type
\tau
on
[q]
with
1\leq q\leq N-2
and
q+N
even, a positive semi-definite matrix

X^{\tau}
indexed by
{\mathcal{F}}_{s}^{\tau}
, where
s:=(N+q)/2
. More precisely, from each equivalence class
{\mathcal{C}}
of types under
isomorphism as unlabelled graphs, only one representative
\tau\in{\mathcal{C}}
and its matrix
X^{\tau}
are listed, with all-zero matrices assigned
to other types in
{\mathcal{C}}
. Additionally, for every
H\in{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{0}
, a
non-negative real coefficient
c_{H}
(referred to as the
slack
at
H
)
is provided, so that for any graph
G
of order
n\to\infty
, the
following identity holds:

(2.5)

\begin{split}\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{0}}(u-p(F,H))P(H,G)=&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq q\leq N-2\\
q\equiv N\bmod{2}\end{subarray}}\sum_{f:[q]\hookrightarrow V(G)}\left(\bm{V}_{(G,f)}^{\tau,s}\right)^{T}X^{\tau}\bm{V}_{(G,f)}^{\tau,s}\\
&+\sum_{H\in{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{0}}c_{H}P(H,G)+O(n^{N-1}),\end{split}

where, in the inner sum,
s:=(N+q)/2
and

\tau:=([q],f^{-1}(E(G)))
is the graph on
[q]
such that the
injection
f:[q]\hookrightarrow V(G)
embeds
\tau
into
G
. Note
that the right-hand side of (
2.5
), excluding the error
term, is non-negative because all matrices
X^{\tau}
are positive
semi-definite and all slacks
c_{H}
are non-negative. Meanwhile, the
left-hand side equals
(u-p(F,G))\binom{n}{N}
, confirming that

\lambda_{F}\leq u
.

Note that for any choice of square matrices
X^{\tau}
of
appropriate dimensions and a given
u\in{\mathbbm{R}}
, there exists a unique
set of slack coefficients
c_{H}
(some of which may be negative) that
satisfies (
2.5
) for every
G
. In essence, for a fixed
type
\tau
with
q\leq N-2
vertices and two
\tau
-flags

H_{1},H_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{s}^{\tau}
where
s:=(N+q)/2
, the sum

\sum_{f}P(H_{1},(G,f))P(H_{2},(G,f))
over all embeddings
f
of

\tau
into an
n
-vertex graph
G
can be expressed, up to an

O(n^{N-1})
error, as a linear combination of counts of
N
-vertex
subgraphs in
G
, with coefficients independent of
n
. This arises
because the sum counts pairs of copies of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
in
G
with
shared roots, and each such pair involves at most
N
vertices, with
the contribution from each
N
-set depending solely on the induced
subgraph isomorphism class.
Consequently, for a fixed
N
, the optimal upper bound
u
provable
via (
2.5
) corresponds to the value of an explicit, though
typically large, semi-definite program.

2.2.
Perfect stability

Let
F
be a given graph on
\kappa\geq 2
vertices.
Following the (more general) definition from

[
46
]
, we call the
F
-problem

perfectly
B
-stable
for a pattern
B
if there is
C>0
such
that for every graph
G
of order
n\geq C
we have

(2.6)

\delta_{\mathrm{edit}}(G,B{()})\leq C\left(p(F,n)-p(F,G)\right)n^{2}.

In particular, it follows that, for all
n\geq C
, every order-
n
graph

G
with
p(F,G)=p(F,n)
is a blowup of
B
and then the determination of
p(F,n)
reduces to maximising an explicit polynomial of degree at
most
v(F)
over (integer) part sizes summing up to
n
.
This definition was motivated by the earlier results in
[
25
,
44
,
45
]
proving versions of this property.

Pikhurko, Sliacan
and Tyros
[
46
, Theorem 7.1]
presented a sufficient condition for a flag algebra proof to
give perfect stability and successfully applied it to a number of
problems (including some instances of the graph inducibility problem). Here we use the version of this condition that appears in
[
7
, Theorem 2.2]
(with the latter allowing a pattern
B
to contain loops).

Theorem 2.3

(
[
46
, Theorem 7.1]
and
[
7
, Theorem 2.2]
)
.

Let
F
be a graph with
\kappa\geq 2
vertices. Let
B
be a pattern
(possibly with loops) on
[m]
and let
\bm{a}\in{\mathbbm{S}}_{m}
be a vector
with all entries positive. Suppose that all of the following
statements hold.

(1)

We have a flag algebra certificate
{\mathcal{C}}

on
N
vertices proving that

\lambda_{F}\leq p(F,B(\bm{a}))
as in (
2.5
).

(2)

We have a graph
\tau
(without loops) with
at most
N-2
vertices such that

(a)

if we restrict the maximisation of

p(F,n)
to graphs without induced copy of
\tau
then the
limit strictly decreases, that is,

(2.7)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\max\{p(F,G)\mid v(G)=n,\ P(\tau,G)=0\}<\lambda_{F};

(b)

up to an automorphism of
B
(which by
definition has to preserve loops and non-loops), there is a unique
homomorphism from
\tau
to
B
;

(c)

every two distinct vertices
x,y\in V(B)

have distinct neighbourhoods in
f(V(\tau))
, that is,

\Gamma_{B}(x)\cap f(V(\tau))\not=\Gamma_{B}(y)\cap f(V(\tau))
, for some
(or, equivalently by Item
2b
, for every) homomorphism

f
of
\tau
to
B
;

(3)

Every
H\in{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{0}
with
c_{H}=0
admits a
homomorphism to
B
.

Suppose further that at least one of the following
statements holds:

(1)

the certificate
{\mathcal{C}}
contains the graph

\tau
as a type and the corresponding matrix
X^{\tau}
in
{\mathcal{C}}
is of
co-rank 1 (that is, its kernel has dimension 1);

(2)

suppose that the vertices of
B
are either all loops or all non-loops, and the limiting maximum density of
F
in growing graphs without any induced copy of the graph
B^{\circ}
(which is obtained from
B
by removing
loops) is strictly less than
\lambda_{F}
;

(3)

the pattern
B
is
F
-minimal.

Then the problem of maximising
p(F,G)
over
n
-vertex graphs
G

is perfectly
B
-stable. Moreover, if
Item

1
holds (in
addition to Items
1
–
3
) then
\bm{a}
is
the unique vector
\bm{x}\in{\mathbbm{S}}_{m}
that maximises

p(F,B(\bm{x}))
up to the automorphisms of
B
.

The candidate graphons are specified by pairs

5.
Concluding Remarks

Here we present the numerical upper and lower bounds that we have in the remaining 27 cases where the value of
\lambda_{F_{i}}
is still unknown. We just summarise them in Table
3
. The stated upper bounds are the values returned by the
csdp
solver for the ‘plain’ flag algebra SDP with
N=8
.
One should be able to turn the obtained floating-point matrices into mathematical proofs with small loss in the bound. However, we do not do this since we do not expect this to provide any insights.

For the lower bounds, we use the same search as described in Section
4
, without doing any heuristic or other partial search in a larger set of graphons. So it is possible that some of these bounds can be easily improved.

Fox, Huang and Lee
[
24
]
and, independently, Yuster
[
54
]
proved that almost every
F
of order
\kappa\to\infty
is a
fractalizer
, that is, the value of
\lambda_{F}
is attained by iterated uniform blowups of
F
, where we repeat recursion inside each part. The known values for
F_{43}
and
F_{51}
(and therefore also for their complements) are attained in this way. The results collected here show that, excluding the trivial case of
\overline{F}_{0}=K_{6}
, the only further complementary pair that could be a fractalizer is the pair indexed by
i=29
. Thus there are between
4
and
6
non-trivial fractalizers with
6
vertices. It is not surprising that the value
\kappa=6
is too small to indicate the general trend as
\kappa\to\infty
.

The flag algebra method can also be applied to the inducibility of graphs with at most
8
vertices but we have not done any calculations in that direction.
