Genomic determinants of fluoroquinolone resistance in Escherichia coli in Nigeria: dominance of QRDR mutations and limited contribution of PMQR in a cross-sectional study
Authors: Nubwa Medugu, Mabel Kamweli Aworh, Khadija Abdulraheem, Dawn M. Hull, Lyndy Harden, Siddhartha Thakur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFluoroquinolone-resistant Escherichia coli is a major global clinical threat, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like Nigeria. However, the full genomic landscape, including the relative contributions of chromosomal mutations, plasmid-mediated resistance, and the role of high-risk clones, remains poorly characterized in this setting. This study aimed to define the genomic mechanisms, clonal distribution, and genotype–phenotype relationships of fluoroquinolone resistance in clinical E. coli isolates from Nigeria.MethodsA cross-sectional study of 107 clinical E. coli isolates was conducted. Phenotypic susceptibility to ciprofloxacin and nalidixic acid was determined using VITEK 2 and broth microdilution. Whole-genome sequencing was performed, and analysis included detection of quinolone resistance determining region (QRDR) mutations (gyrA, parC, parE) and plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) genes, multilocus sequence typing (MLST), and phylogenetic analysis. Statistical associations were evaluated using chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact tests.ResultsCiprofloxacin non-susceptibility was high at 86.0%. Resistance was primarily driven by a conserved chromosomal mutation profile; the combination of gyrA S83L, gyrA D87N, and parC S80I was present in 85 isolates and was associated with ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility in all affected isolates in this cohort. Isolates with only gyrA mutations were resistant to nalidixic acid but susceptible to ciprofloxacin, consistent with a stepwise resistance pathway. In this cohort, the triple QRDR signature (gyrA S83L + gyrA D87N/Y + parC S80I) was a perfect positive predictor of ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility (85/85; 100%). The ST131 lineage dominated, accounting for 21.5% of isolates and universally carrying the complete triple QRDR profile; notably, no ST131 isolate carried a PMQR determinant. Plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) genes were detected in 15.0% of isolates but were not independently associated with ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility in this cohort in the absence of concomitant QRDR mutations. Efflux pump genes were ubiquitous and non-predictive. Notably, six isolates, all from urine, were non-susceptible (R/I) despite lacking all known QRDR and PMQR determinants, pointing to uncharacterized mechanisms. In a multivariable logistic regression model that included ST131 status, PMQR carriage, and parE mutation status, ST131 was associated with ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility (adjusted OR 5.96, 95% CI 1.21–29.4, p = 0.028), whereas PMQR carriage was not (adjusted OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.18–4.85, p = 0.94). The triple QRDR signature was not included in this model because it perfectly predicted ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility in this cohort. Resistance patterns varied by clinical source, with the highest burden in bloodstream and wound infections. This stepwise hierarchy from first-step gyrA mutations to the classic triple QRDR profile is summarised in the graphical abstract, Fig. 1.ConclusionsFluoroquinolone resistance in Nigerian clinical E. coli is predominantly driven by chromosomal QRDR mutations within successful clones like ST131. PMQR genes and efflux pumps appeared to play a supplementary role rather than being independent drivers of ciprofloxacin resistance in this cohort. Background

Fluoroquinolone-resistant Escherichia coli has become a major clinical problem worldwide and is a growing concern in low- and middle-income countries. These strains are now common causes of urinary tract infections, bloodstream infections, meningitis, and post-surgical infections, and are associated with delayed clinical response and increased mortality [1]. The rapid rise in resistance has been driven by widespread use of fluoroquinolones, unregulated antibiotic access, and persistent gaps in antimicrobial stewardship across many healthcare settings, including those in sub-Saharan Africa [2–6].

The genetic basis of fluoroquinolone resistance in E. coli is complex. The most important mechanisms involve mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining regions of gyrA and parC, which encode the primary targets of fluoroquinolone activity. These mutations reduce drug binding and are strongly associated with high-level resistance [7, 8]. Additional mutations in parE may further modulate resistance levels, although their exact contribution remains unclear. Plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) determinants, such as the qnr family and efflux pump genes like qepA, can also reduce susceptibility and promote selection of high-level chromosomal mutations [7, 8]. Despite this, the clinical impact of plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) genes in the absence of quinolone resistance determining region (QRDR) mutations remains uncertain.

Clonal expansion of high-risk lineages has further accelerated the spread of resistance [9, 10]. Sequence type 131 (ST131) has become one of the most successful global E. coli clones, combining robust virulence with a stable set of resistance mutations [11, 12], including characteristic gyrA and parC alterations. ST410, ST648, and related international clones also contribute to the global movement of multidrug-resistant E. coli. In Nigeria and many parts of Africa, the true burden and distribution of these clones remain under-described, largely due to limited access to genomic surveillance [13, 14].

Although fluoroquinolone resistance in E. coli has been documented in Nigeria, most studies rely on phenotypic data and do not resolve the underlying genomic mechanisms [2]. Key questions remain unanswered. These include the relative contribution of different QRDR mutation patterns, the clinical relevance of PMQR genes, the role of parE mutations, and the extent to which major sequence types such as ST131 are driving resistance in the region. There is also limited insight into how these mechanisms vary by infection source, and why certain isolates show discordant genotype–phenotype patterns despite carrying mutations normally associated with high-level resistance.

This study addresses these gaps by combining whole-genome sequencing with detailed phenotypic data from clinical E. coli isolates collected in Nigeria. We describe the dominant resistance mechanisms, quantify their association with ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility, and examine their distribution across major clonal lineages and specimen types. We also highlight unusual genotype–phenotype relationships that point to additional, unexplored resistance pathways. By defining the genomic landscape of fluoroquinolone resistance in this setting, our findings aim to support improved diagnostic strategies and inform local antimicrobial stewardship and infection prevention programs [13, 15–18].

Methods

Study design, clinical setting, and isolate collection

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the National Hospital Abuja (NHA), a 425-bed tertiary referral centre in Nigeria. A total of 107 unique clinical E. coli isolates, recovered between March 2019 and September 2020, were included. Specimens were collected according to routine clinical procedures and transported promptly to the microbiology laboratory for processing. Uniqueness was defined as one isolate per patient per specimen type during the study period; when repeat isolates occurred, only the first chronologically was retained.

Urine samples were collected as midstream clean-catch specimens and inoculated onto Brilliance UTI Agar (Oxoid, United Kingdom) using a calibrated 1μL loop. Plates were incubated at 35.5°C for 18–24 h in ambient air. Blood cultures were collected using age-appropriate volumes, inoculated into BacT/Alert bottles (bioMérieux, France), and incubated in the BacT/Alert automated system for up to five days. Positive bottles were subcultured onto Sheep Blood Agar, Chocolate Agar, and MacConkey Agar and incubated at 35.5°C for 18–24 h, with Chocolate Agar incubated in 5% CO₂. Other specimen types, including wound swabs, cerebrospinal fluid, sputum, and body fluids, were processed using standard culture media and incubation conditions.

Identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Presumptive E. coli isolates were confirmed using the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France) with GN ID cards. Bacterial suspensions were standardized to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity and processed according to manufacturer instructions. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using VITEK 2 AST-N280 and AST-N281 cards. For ciprofloxacin and nalidixic acid, CLSI M100 (30th ed.) MIC breakpoints were applied as follows: ciprofloxacin S ≤ 0.25 mg/L, I = 0.5 mg/L, R ≥ 1 mg/L; nalidixic acid S ≤ 16 mg/L, I = 32 mg/L, R ≥ 64 mg/L [19]. Ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility was defined as intermediate or resistant.

Quality control was performed using E. coli ATCC 25922. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were further validated using microdilution on the Gram-negative Sensititre CMV3AGNF plate (Trek Diagnostics, OH, USA). Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as resistance to three or more classes of antimicrobial agents.

Relevant demographic and clinical information, including age, sex, and specimen type, was retrieved from electronic medical records. All isolates were stored at − 80 °C in 50% glycerol before whole-genome sequencing.

Whole-genome sequencing and genomic analysis

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on all isolates to characterize quinolone resistance determinants. Genomic DNA was extracted using the MasterPure Complete DNA and RNA Purification Kit (Epicentre Technologies, WI, USA). DNA purity was assessed using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, DE, USA) and quantified with the Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, CA, USA).

Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT DNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform using a 2 × 250 bp paired-end workflow. Low-quality bases and adapters were removed using CLC Genomics Workbench v9.4.

Draft genomes were assembled using SPAdes v3.13.1, and assembly quality metrics (N50, contig count, genome size) were assessed using QUAST [20, 21]. Assemblies were deposited under NCBI BioProject PRJNA293225.

Detection of fluoroquinolone resistance determinants

Chromosomal QRDR mutations in gyrA, parC, and parE were detected using AMRFinderPlus v3.10.23 and its associated reference databases [22]; PMQR genes were defined a priori as qnr (all variants) and qepA. The variant aac(6′)-Ib-cr was reported separately as a fluoroquinolone-modifying determinant. PMQR genes, including qnr family genes and qepA, and efflux-associated regulatory loci (marR, soxS, acrR) were screened using AMRFinderPlus v3.10.23 from the assembled genomes, and results were reported using default AMRFinderPlus detection rules. Plasmid replicon typing was performed using PlasmidFinder 2.0 with a ≥ 95% identity threshold [23]. For contig-level context, a PMQR gene was considered plasmid-associated in the assembly when it occurred on the same contig as a plasmid replicon hit.

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST)

MLST was performed in silico using the Achtman scheme targeting seven housekeeping genes (adk, fumC, gyrB, icd, mdh, purA, and recA). Sequence types (STs) were assigned through the PubMLST database [24]. ST131 and other high-risk clones were identified based on known allele combinations. Isolates without exact allele matches were classified as unknown STs [24, 25].

Phylogenetic analysis

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were identified using CSI Phylogeny v4.1 with E. coli reference genome NZ_CP028166.1. SNP calling thresholds included: depth ≥ 10 ×, SNP quality ≥ 30, mapping quality ≥ 25, and Z-score ≥ 1.96 [26]. Phylogenetic trees were visualized using iTOL v6 [27]. Tree inference was performed using FastTree 2 (approximate maximum-likelihood) with CSI Phylogeny default pruning (removal of SNPs within 10 bp); recombination masking was not performed, and trees were midpoint-rooted for visualization.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using STATA 16 [28]. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize resistance patterns and genomic profiles.

To assess independent associations with ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility, a multivariable binary logistic regression model was fitted with ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility (R/I vs. S) as the dependent variable. Candidate predictors included ST131 lineage status (yes/no), PMQR carriage (any qnr or qepA; yes/no), and parE mutation status (any QRDR-position parE mutation; yes/no). The triple QRDR signature was not entered as a predictor because it was a perfect predictor of the outcome (100% non-susceptibility), which causes complete separation in logistic regression; this perfect-prediction relationship is reported descriptively. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR), 95% CIs, and Wald p-values are reported. Where zero cell counts produced unstable point estimates, Firth’s penalised likelihood logistic regression was used as a sensitivity analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Hospital Abuja (NHA/EC/033/2018), which granted a waiver of individual informed consent given the retrospective use of routinely collected diagnostic isolates. Isolates were derived from clinical specimens originally submitted for routine diagnostics. Limited demographic and clinical variables (age, sex, specimen type, ward of origin) were extracted from the laboratory and electronic medical records and linked to each isolate using a study-specific code; direct patient identifiers (name, hospital number, address, date of birth) were not retained in the analytic dataset, and the linkage key was stored separately on a password-protected institutional server accessible only to the principal investigator. The dataset used for analysis and deposited in NCBI was therefore de-identified at the point of analysis, although the original specimens were not anonymous at the point of laboratory submission. Data were stored securely in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Results

A total of 107 clinical E. coli isolates were analysed (Supplementary Figure S1, Study Flow Diagram). During the study period, laboratory records available for this analysis showed that 312 routine clinical cultures yielded E. coli. After applying the uniqueness rule (one isolate per patient per specimen type, retaining the first chronological isolate when duplicates occurred), 107 unique E. coli isolates met the inclusion criteria and underwent both susceptibility testing and whole-genome sequencing. All isolates were recovered between March 2019 and September 2020 from routine diagnostic specimens submitted to Nigerian healthcare facilities. Urine was the most common specimen type (61 isolates; 57.0%). Blood cultures contributed 22 isolates (20.6%), while wound (n = 10; 9.3%), cerebrospinal fluid (n = 3; 2.8%), endocervical swabs (n = 4; 3.7%), stool (n = 3; 2.8%), and aspirates (n = 4; 3.7%) made up the remainder.

Phenotypic fluoroquinolone resistance

Phenotypic resistance to quinolones was widespread. Throughout this manuscript, “ciprofloxacin non-susceptible” denotes isolates classified as resistant (R) or intermediate (I) by CLSI M100 30th-edition breakpoints, and “resistant” is reserved for isolates classified as R only. Ciprofloxacin resistance was recorded in 90 isolates, with a further two isolates showing intermediate susceptibility, giving an overall ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility rate of 86.0%. In contrast, only 15 isolates were fully susceptible. Nalidixic acid resistance was even more common, occurring in 98 isolates (91.6%), with nine isolates remaining susceptible. A distinct subgroup of six isolates showed the characteristic phenotype of nalidixic-acid resistance with preserved ciprofloxacin susceptibility. All six carried gyrA mutations but lacked parC mutations, consistent with a first-step quinolone-resistance genotype. Genotypically, seven isolates carried gyrA mutations without accompanying parC mutations; six of these showed the classic first-step phenotype, while one isolate remained susceptible to both agents.

Distribution of primary QRDR mutations and their predictive value

The distribution of QRDR mutations revealed a clear hierarchical structure. Mutations in gyrA were highly prevalent: S83L occurred in 91 isolates and D87N in 92 isolates. A single isolate carried a D87Y substitution. The most common parC mutation was S80I, present in 86 isolates, followed by E84V in 23 isolates and E84K in three isolates. Mutations in parE were identified in 74 isolates, primarily localized at positions S458, I529, and E460.

Eighty-five isolates carried the complete triple mutation profile. All 85 (100.0%) were resistant to both ciprofloxacin and nalidixic acid. This combination represents a boundary beyond which full clinical fluoroquinolone resistance was observed.

Six isolates (5.6%) carried gyrA mutations in the absence of parC mutations and showed the classic first-step phenotype: nalidixic acid resistance with preserved ciprofloxacin susceptibility. When genotype alone was considered (gyrA mutation present, no parC mutation), there were seven such isolates; the additional isolate was susceptible to both agents.

In contrast, the 14 (13.1%) isolates lacking QRDR mutations showed variable phenotypes. Within this group, 6 (42.9%) were non-susceptible to ciprofloxacin and 6 (42.9%) were resistant to nalidixic acid. A single isolate carried another QRDR pattern and was resistant to both agents.

Overall, ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility (comprising resistant and intermediate isolates) was identified in 92 of 107 isolates (86.0%). Nalidixic acid resistance was even more widespread, affecting 98 isolates (91.6%). Statistical analysis confirmed a highly significant association between QRDR mutation category and ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility (X2 = 78.6, p < 0.0001). Secondary resistance mechanisms: parE mutations, plasmid-mediated determinants, and efflux-associated elements

Mutations in parE were detected in 74 of 107 isolates, representing an overall prevalence of 69.2%. The most frequently observed alterations occurred at residues S458, I529, E460, and I355. These mutations did not appear independently; every isolate harbouring a parE mutation also carried at least one gyrA or parC mutation. Notably, the vast majority belonged to the group characterized by the triple mutation pattern. This strong co-occurrence indicates that parE mutations consistently co-occurred with gyrA and/or parC mutations and were never observed in isolation, an association pattern compatible with secondary accumulation rather than initiation of resistance, although the present cross-sectional design does not establish temporal ordering or causality. Other low frequency parE variants were also detected, including L416F (2/107, 1.9%).

All isolates with any parE mutation exhibited non-susceptibility to both nalidixic acid (100.0%) and ciprofloxacin (100.0%). No instances were found where a parE mutation existed in a ciprofloxacin-susceptible background. Within the parE-positive group, specific mutation clusters showed a high correlation with lineage, such as I529L (n = 23), which demonstrated a strong linkage with high-level resistance profiles.

PMQR determinants were identified in 16 isolates (15.0%), including qnrS1 (n = 6), qnrB19 (n = 1), and the efflux-based determinant qepA (n = 9). In this cohort, PMQR-positive isolates were not phenotypically ciprofloxacin non-susceptible unless QRDR mutations were also present. Isolates carrying PMQR genes in the absence of QRDR mutations (n = 2) remained fully susceptible to ciprofloxacin. Conversely, all isolates where PMQR co-existed with QRDR mutations (n = 14) were non-susceptible. These observations are consistent with PMQR determinants playing, in this dataset, a supplementary rather than independent role with respect to ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility, although causal inference is limited by the cross-sectional, observational design and the absence of functional or transcriptional data.

Efflux-associated elements (mdtM or acrF) were present in 102 isolates (95.3%) and did not predict phenotype. We emphasise that gene presence on the assembly reflects only the genomic presence of the locus and does not capture transcript abundance, post-translational regulation, or pump activity, all of which would be required to attribute phenotype to efflux. Transcriptomic and functional studies, which we did not perform, would be needed to test whether differential expression of mdtM, acrF, or the broader acrAB-tolC system contributes to the phenotypes observed here. Most isolates carrying these genes remained ciprofloxacin-susceptible when QRDR mutations were absent. Six isolates (5.6%) were non-susceptible to ciprofloxacin and/or nalidixic acid despite lacking canonical QRDR mutations and PMQR genes; only one of these (NHA038) carried mdtM and acrF. Screening for specific variants in marR, soxR, and soxS did not identify these variants in the discordant isolates. These isolates represent a small but notable subset with possible mechanisms not captured by the QRDR and PMQR loci assessed here, including regulatory and permeability pathways. The fluoroquinolone-modifying determinant aac(6′)-Ib-cr5 was detected in 47 isolates (43.9%), while aac(6′)-Ib-cr was detected in 2 isolates (1.9%); these were reported separately from the predefined PMQR category used for the main analysis.

Dominance of ST131 and its associated resistance architecture

Multilocus sequence typing identified ST131 as the dominant lineage, accounting for 23 of 107 isolates (21.5%). ST131 was identified across all clinical sources, including urine, blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and wounds. The resistance profile of this lineage was remarkably uniform: 100% of ST131 isolates harboured the triple mutation pattern (gyrA_S83L + (gyrA_D87N or gyrA_D87Y) + parC_S80I) and 100% carried at least one parE mutation. Consequently, ciprofloxacin and nalidixic acid resistance were universal within this clone.

The resistance burden was significantly higher in ST131 compared to non-ST131 lineages. While ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility was universal in ST131, it was present in 82.1% of non-ST131 isolates (crude OR not estimable due to zero discordant cells; Fisher’s exact p = 0.024). After adjusting for PMQR carriage and parE mutation status in a multivariable logistic regression model, ST131 lineage remained independently associated with ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility (adjusted OR 5.96, 95% CI 1.21–29.4, p = 0.028). Interestingly, PMQR determinants did not concentrate within the ST131 lineage; 0% of ST131 isolates were PMQR-positive, whereas 16 non-ST131 isolates (19.0%) carried these mobile elements (Fisher’s exact p = 0.022). This pattern is consistent with the interpretation that the successful dissemination of the ST131 clone in this setting is driven by a highly stable chromosomal architecture rather than the acquisition of plasmid-mediated mechanisms. QRDR category was strongly associated with ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility (Pearson chi-square, p < 0.0001; for the triple QRDR signature vs. all other genotypes, OR not estimable due to perfect prediction).

Source-specific distribution of resistance mechanisms

Fluoroquinolone resistance profiles varied significantly across clinical specimen types. Bloodstream isolates exhibited the highest resistance burden; 20 of 22 blood isolates (90.9%) were non-susceptible to ciprofloxacin, and 20 (90.9%) carried the triple QRDR mutation pattern. This high prevalence mirrored the distribution of ST131, which accounted for 50.0% of all bloodstream isolates.

Urine isolates showed greater genomic diversity despite an 86.9% ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility rate. While the majority were resistant, urine isolates were more likely to belong to non-ST131 lineages and demonstrated the highest frequency of PMQR genes (n = 8) compared to other specimen types. Wound isolates exhibited universal ciprofloxacin resistance (100.0%) and the highest relative frequency of parE mutations (90.0%), suggesting intense selective pressure in wound environments. Cerebrospinal fluid isolates showed a polar distribution, with 2 of 3 isolates being fully resistant and carrying the complete triple QRDR pattern.

To place resistance mechanisms in a population structure context, we reconstructed an SNP phylogeny of the study isolates (Fig. 1). Pairwise core-genome SNP distances among study isolates ranged from 0 to 22,418 SNPs (median 7,891; interquartile range 2,654–11,420), reflecting the substantial phylogenetic diversity of the cohort. The 23 ST131 isolates formed a single, well-supported monophyletic clade with markedly reduced within-clade diversity (median pairwise distance 184 SNPs, range 12–3,402), consistent with lower within-lineage diversity than the wider cohort, although epidemiological linkage cannot be inferred from phylogeny alone. Within ST131, two sub-clusters separated by < 50 SNPs were observed across blood and urine specimens from patients sampled within overlapping admission windows, raising a hypothesis of possible local transmission or repeated introduction that would require prospective genomic-epidemiological investigation with admission dates, ward overlap, and patient-movement data. By contrast, the six QRDR-negative discordant isolates did not cluster together; they were dispersed across phylogroups A and B1, with pairwise distances exceeding 9,000 SNPs in five of six pairwise comparisons, arguing against a single shared lineage explaining the unexplained-resistance phenotype and supporting an interpretation of independent emergence on multiple genetic backgrounds. No specimen-source-specific clustering was observed beyond the ST131 over-representation in bloodstream isolates already noted above. Genotype–phenotype discordance

A total of six isolates demonstrated significant discordance between their genomic profiles and observed fluoroquinolone phenotypes. These isolates lacked the complete triple mutation pattern and yet were phenotypically non-susceptible to ciprofloxacin or nalidixic acid. All six discordant isolates originated from urine specimens. Notably, none of these isolates belonged to established epidemic clones; five were identified as novel sequence types, and one belonged to rare sequence types (STs).

Six of these isolates (NHA038, NHA075, NHA076, NHA085, NHA096, and NHA097) lacked all canonical QRDR mutations in gyrA and parC. Also, they lacked all tested PMQR genes. The only genetic features identified in this subgroup were the efflux-associated elements mdtM and acrF, which were detected in NHA038. However, as these genes were also widespread among fully susceptible isolates in the cohort, they cannot independently account for the resistance phenotype. To further interrogate possible mechanisms, the assemblies of all six discordant isolates were re-screened against AMRFinderPlus reference loci for: (i) gyrB QRDR codons (residues 426–464); (ii) full-length parC and parE (including non-QRDR codons); (iii) the porin loci ompF and ompC (assessed for premature stop codons, frameshifts, and large deletions); and (iv) the efflux regulator loci marR, marA, soxR, soxS, acrR, and ramR. No additional canonical target-site mutations were detected in gyrB; parC and parE were wild-type outside the QRDR; ompF and ompC were full-length with no premature stop codons or frameshifts in any of the six isolates; and the regulator loci showed no high-confidence non-synonymous variants meeting AMRFinderPlus default reporting thresholds. We did not perform long-read sequencing or transcriptional profiling, both of which would be required to detect promoter mutations affecting efflux pump expression, structural variants, IS-element insertions, or non-coding regulatory changes that short-read assemblies may miss. The consistent absence of recognised resistance determinants in these isolates suggests the presence of uncharacterized mechanisms, which may include alterations in efflux regulation, porin structural changes, variations in DNA supercoiling, or mutations in non-QRDR regions of the topoisomerase genes.

The discordant group represents 5.6% of the total collection and constitutes the primary source of genotype–phenotype uncertainty in this dataset. Within this group, isolates NHA075, NHA076, NHA085, and NHA096 exhibited full resistance to both ciprofloxacin and nalidixic acid despite having no identifiable chromosomal or plasmid-mediated determinants. Two additional isolates, NHA038 and NHA097, exhibited intermediate susceptibility to ciprofloxacin. These observations indicate that fluoroquinolone resistance in some circulating E. coli strains may involve mechanisms not currently captured in routine genomic screening. Further investigation utilizing long-read sequencing or transcriptomic profiling is warranted to define the molecular basis of these phenotypes. Discussion

In this genomic analysis of 107 clinical E. coliisolates from Nigeria, we identified an extremely high burden of fluoroquinolone resistance, with over 86% of isolates being non-susceptible to ciprofloxacin. Similar high fluoroquinolone resistance burdens and rapid expansion in Nigeria and sub-Saharan Africa have been reported previously and have been linked to fluoroquinolone use and clonal spread [2, 29, 30]. Our data establish a pattern consistent with a stepwise pathway where high-level resistance is almost entirely explained by a conserved chromosomal architecture centred on dual gyrA and parC mutations, particularly within the dominant ST131 lineage [9, 10, 31].

The strongest signal in this collection was the tight link between the classic triple QRDR profile and universal resistance to both ciprofloxacin and nalidixic acid. This pattern is consistent with established work showing stepwise accumulation of target mutations driving progressively higher fluoroquinolone MICs [11]. Every isolate with this combination was fully resistant, whereas isolates with gyrA-only mutations remained susceptible to ciprofloxacin despite their nalidixic acid resistance. This supports the model in which initial gyrA changes precede additional topoisomerase mutations that push the phenotype across the clinical resistance threshold [31, 32]. These findings align with reports describing widespread circulation of fluoroquinolone-resistant E. coli clones in sub-Saharan Africa and Nigeria [29, 33, 34].

While the role of parE has historically been less defined, our data are consistent with parE alterations acting as secondary modifiers rather than primary drivers, although the cross-sectional, observational design and the absence of functional validation preclude definitive causal claims. parE mutations were prevalent but never occurred independently of gyrA or parC changes. Specific clusters, particularly I529L and the S458A/T group, showed a strong linkage with high-level resistance profiles. The unique case of parE I355T potentially substituting for parC mutations in specific backgrounds warrants further functional investigation.

PMQR determinants, primarily qepA and qnrS1, were associated with ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility only in the presence of concomitant QRDR mutations in this cohort. This observation aligns with the established biology of PMQR, which typically confers low-level MIC shifts and may facilitate selection of higher-level chromosomal resistance when combined with QRDR mutations [35, 36]. Isolates with qnr genes lacking QRDR changes remained fully ciprofloxacin-susceptible, supporting the concept that PMQR alone can be insufficient to predict fluoroquinolone non-susceptibility [18, 36]. This has diagnostic implications, as assays reporting PMQR without integrating QRDR context may overcall resistance [18, 37].

The efflux-associated elements mdtM and acrF were common across both resistant and susceptible isolates. This pattern suggests gene presence alone may not discriminate phenotypes in this dataset; because we did not measure transcript abundance or pump activity, we cannot determine whether functional differences in efflux contribute to the unexplained-resistance phenotypes, and that resistance expression may depend on regulatory context rather than simple carriage [38–40].

The ST131 lineage was characterized by a remarkably uniform and stable chromosomal architecture, with all of them carrying the triple QRDR pattern and at least one parE mutation. In a multivariable logistic regression model adjusting for PMQR carriage and parE mutation status, ST131 lineage was independently associated with ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility (adjusted OR 5.96, 95% CI 1.21–29.4, p = 0.028), consistent with the broader literature linking ST131 to fluoroquinolone resistance and successful dissemination [10, 41]. Interestingly, PMQR genes were absent in ST131 isolates, consistent with reports that ST131 success can be driven by stable chromosomal resistance backgrounds rather than plasmid acquisition [11, 42].

The urinary tract appears to be a key ecological niche for genomic diversity and emerging mechanisms. All discordant isolates, which lacked recognized QRDR and PMQR determinants yet were resistant or intermediate, originated from urine. This pattern raises the possibility of additional mechanisms not captured by standard QRDR/PMQR screening [43]. Most discordant isolates belonged to novel sequence types, suggesting that current genomic screening tools may miss emerging pathways, including changes affecting permeability and regulation, or non-canonical topoisomerase alterations.

This study combined systematic phenotypic testing with WGS in a well-defined cohort, allowing for a hierarchical model useful for clinical sequencing interpretation. Several limitations should, however, be acknowledged. First, isolates were drawn from a single tertiary referral centre in Abuja, which serves a predominantly urban population with a high burden of complicated and hospital-acquired infection; the resulting estimates of ST131 prevalence and resistance burden are therefore subject to referral bias and urban bias and may not generalise to community settings, primary-care facilities, or rural populations within Nigeria, nor to other regions of sub-Saharan Africa with different antibiotic-use patterns and patient mixes. Second, although the overall cohort of 107 isolates is reasonable for a single-centre genomic study, source-stratified inferences are limited by small subgroup sizes, in particular for cerebrospinal fluid (n = 3), stool (n = 3), endocervical swabs (n = 4), and aspirates (n = 4); source-specific resistance estimates for these compartments should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only. Third, short-read Illumina sequencing cannot fully resolve plasmid structure, IS-element-mediated rearrangements, large structural variants, repeat-rich regions, or non-coding regulatory elements that may contribute to the unexplained phenotypes observed in the QRDR-negative resistant isolates. Fourth, the cross-sectional, observational design precludes inference about the temporal order in which gyrA, parC, and parE mutations were acquired, and we did not perform functional or transcriptional studies; statements about parE and PMQR being “secondary” or “supplementary” should be read as observational associations rather than mechanistic conclusions. Fifth, MIC determination relied on VITEK 2 with broth microdilution confirmation; very-low-level shifts in MIC that do not cross CLSI breakpoints may not have been captured. Finally, our isolate collection spans 2019–2020 and may not reflect the most current resistance landscape.

For clinicians at this centre, the observed non-susceptibility burden suggests fluoroquinolones are unlikely to be reliable empiric therapy for severe infections where E. coli is suspected, pending updated local antibiograms [17]. This is particularly relevant given the documented impact of resistant bloodstream infections in sub-Saharan African hospitals [16]. For laboratories, the findings support prioritizing detection of the triple QRDR profile as a primary genomic marker of high-level resistance in this dataset.

Conclusions

Fluoroquinolone resistance in clinical E. coli in this Nigerian setting is now common and is driven mainly by a conserved chromosomal architecture centred on dual gyrA and parC mutations, often within ST131. The classic gyrA_S83L + (gyrA_D87N or gyrA_D87Y) + parC_S80I combination reliably identified isolates with high-level resistance to both ciprofloxacin and nalidixic acid. While parE mutations, PMQR determinants, and efflux-associated elements appeared as secondary or non-discriminatory features in this dataset, PMQR reporting should be interpreted alongside chromosomal QRDR status to avoid overcalling resistance based on PMQR detection alone.

For practice, these data argue against routine empiric use of fluoroquinolones for serious urinary tract infection, bacteraemia, or meningitis in comparable Nigerian tertiary-care settings, pending updated local antibiogram data and confirmation in multicentre cohorts. This inference should not be extrapolated uncritically to community or primary-care settings where resistance epidemiology may differ. The findings also support diagnostic strategies that prioritise key QRDR mutations over PMQR detection alone when interpreting fluoroquinolone resistance genomically.

Future work should pursue four complementary lines of investigation. First, prospective multi-centre genomic surveillance across geopolitical zones in Nigeria, including community and primary-care settings, is needed to test whether the ST131-dominated, triple-QRDR architecture observed here is representative or specific to tertiary referral centres. Second, long-read sequencing (Oxford Nanopore or PacBio) of the QRDR-negative resistant urinary isolates, paired with RNA-seq under sub-inhibitory ciprofloxacin exposure, should be used to characterise structural variants, IS-element insertions in efflux-regulator promoters, and differential expression of acrAB-tolC, mdtM, and ompF/ompC. Functional validation by allelic exchange or CRISPRi knock-down would help establish causal contributions. Third, linkage of genomic data to prospectively collected clinical outcomes (length of stay, treatment failure, 30-day mortality) is needed to determine whether the triple QRDR signature and ST131 lineage carry independent prognostic value beyond the binary susceptibility report. Fourth, integration with One Health surveillance sampling of food, animal, environmental, and healthy-carrier reservoirs in the catchment area would clarify the source attribution of the dominant Nigerian ST131 sublineages and the trafficking of qnrS1, qnrB19, and qepA across these compartments. In parallel, strengthening antimicrobial stewardship, laboratory capacity, and routine molecular AMR diagnostics will be essential to translate findings of this nature into clinical practice.
