Cardiometabolic multimorbidity and atrial fibrillation: insights from traditional statistical and artificial intelligence approaches
Authors: Ruikun Jia, Xinai Cui, Siyu Jia, Min Xu, Xinmeng Wang, Nongyu Huang, Xiaochi Sun, Luzhao Wang, Jiong Li, Kaijun Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) is increasingly prevalent among patients with atrial fibrillation (AF), yet its independent impact on post-ablation outcomes, the underlying mechanistic pathways, and the optimal approach to risk prediction in this population remain incompletely defined.MethodsWe analyzed three independent cohorts of patients undergoing AF catheter ablation: a derivation cohort (n = 3,308), an external validation cohort, and a prospective testing cohort. CMM was defined as the coexistence of two or more cardiometabolic conditions. Cox proportional hazards models and propensity score–matched analyses assessed the association between CMM and AF recurrence, all-cause death, and cardiovascular death. Mediation analysis quantified the contributions of structural, metabolic, and inflammatory pathways. Ten machine learning algorithms were developed and validated for predicting AF recurrence, and model performance was compared against eight established clinical risk scores. Time-dependent ROC analysis was used to evaluate discrimination across multiple follow-up horizons.ResultsAmong 3,308 patients in the derivation cohort, 686 (20.7%) had CMM. CMM was independently associated with an increased risk of AF recurrence (adjusted HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.03–1.33), all-cause death (HR 1.83, 95% CI 1.11–3.00), and cardiovascular death (HR 2.22, 95% CI 1.24–3.98), with a graded dose–response relationship across the number of CMM components (p for trend < 0.001). These associations persisted in propensity score–matched analyses (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.50) and were replicated in external and prospective cohorts. Mediation analysis identified left atrial diameter (46.5% of total effect), insulin resistance (METS-IR, 25.6%), left atrial appendage emptying velocity (24.9%), and the uric acid-to-HDL ratio (UHR, 24.1%) as key mediators of recurrence risk. For pre-ablation prediction, the LightGBM model achieved the best discriminative performance (ROC-AUC 0.766, 95% CI 0.687–0.844; PR-AUC 0.755), outperforming all conventional risk scores. Time-dependent AUC values at 1, 2, and 3 years were 0.776, 0.760, and 0.730, respectively. A post-blanking model incorporating early recurrence (TabPFN) achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.873. Both models were validated in external and prospective cohorts.ConclusionsCMM is an independent predictor of poor outcomes after AF ablation, driven by structural, metabolic, and inflammatory remodeling that is partially mediated through left atrial enlargement. A machine learning–based prediction provides more accurate risk stratification than traditional clinical scores, supporting personalized management in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06143-7.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) represents one of the most prevalent and clinically significant patterns of multimorbidity worldwide [1]. It is defined by the co-occurrence of two or more cardiovascular metabolic disorders, including diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, and stroke [2]. Epidemiological evidence indicates that approximately 30% of older adults are affected by CMM, which is strongly associated with elevated risks of disability [3], dementia [4, 5], cognitive decline [6], and all-cause mortality [1]. Notably, the reduction in life expectancy attributable to CMM is comparable to that associated with established public health threats such as human immunodeficiency virus infection and lifelong tobacco use [1].

The prevalence of atrial fibrillation (AF) rises markedly with advancing age, with a lifetime risk of approximately one in three among individuals aged 45 years and older [7]. AF contributes to more than 25% of ischemic stroke cases and is linked to a twofold increase in cardiovascular mortality. Catheter ablation has emerged as a cornerstone of rhythm control therapy for AF, demonstrating superior efficacy compared to antiarrhythmic drug therapy [7]. The pathophysiology of AF is multifactorial, and in the context of CMM, conditions such as insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and hemodynamic stress synergistically promote structural and electrical remodeling of the atria, thereby facilitating both the initiation and perpetuation of AF [8]. CMM is highly prevalent among AF patients, and specific components—particularly prior stroke, heart failure, and coronary artery disease—are independently associated with adverse clinical outcomes. As emphasized by the 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS guideline, the presence of multiple comorbidities fundamentally shapes both the anticipated procedural benefit and the long-term management strategy for patients being considered for catheter ablation [7]. The presence of multiple comorbidities substantially complicates both treatment decision-making and long-term management in patients undergoing catheter ablation. Prior studies have examined facets of this problem in isolation: multimorbidity has been associated with higher rates of atrial arrhythmia recurrence following cryoballoon ablation [9], obesity has been linked to adverse post-ablation outcomes mediated through left atrial remodeling [10], and CMM has been shown to predict incident AF in population-based cohorts [11]. Yet whether CMM independently influences the full spectrum of post-ablation outcomes, from AF recurrence to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, has not been systematically examined. This gap motivated the present study.

Investigating the relationship between CMM and outcomes after catheter ablation for AF is critical for refining risk stratification and enabling personalized therapeutic approaches. This study leverages data from three independent AF ablation cohorts to systematically assess: (1) the association between CMM and the risk of AF recurrence, all-cause and cardiovascular death following catheter ablation; (2) exploring various factors as potential mediators in the relationship between CMM and AF recurrence; and (3) the development of an artificial intelligence (AI) –based predictive model for AF recurrence in patients with CMM, thereby addressing a pivotal knowledge gap in contemporary cardiovascular research.

Methods

Research subjects and data collection

This study included three independent cohorts: Cohort 1 served as the derivation cohort and comprised patients with non-valvular AF who underwent catheter ablation at West China Hospital of Sichuan University between 2015 and 2021. Cohort 2 was an external validation cohort, including patients with non-valvular AF who received catheter ablation at Mianyang 404 Hospital from June 2020 to June 2024. Cohort 3 was a prospective validation cohort, in which patients with AF undergoing catheter ablation at West China Hospital of Sichuan University were prospectively and consecutively enrolled from January to July 2022. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for each cohort are provided in the Supplementary Figure S1. The study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committees of West China Hospital of Sichuan University and Mianyang 404 Hospital, with approval numbers 2022- Review (383) and 2024-030, respectively. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Study design adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (Supplementary Table S1) and the EHRA Checklist for Artificial Intelligence Applications (Supplementary Table S2).

Sample size

Derivation cohort: Between 2015 and 2021, 4,041 consecutive patients were enrolled at West China Hospital of Sichuan University. After excluding 315 patients with repeat ablations, 2 patients with incomplete procedures, and 416 lost to follow-up, a total of 3,308 patients were included in the final derivation cohort.

External testing cohort: To evaluate the model’s external generalizability, 731 consecutive patients were retrospectively enrolled at Mianyang 404 Hospital from June 2020 to June 2024. After excluding 32 patients with repeat ablations, 117 lost to follow-up, 1 patient with an incomplete procedure, and 53 patients with more than 10% missing personal data, 528 patients constituted the final external validation cohort.

Prospective testing cohort: To ensure temporal relevance and clinical representativeness, 453 consecutive patients undergoing catheter ablation were enrolled at West China Hospital of Sichuan University from January to July 2022. After excluding 24 patients with repeat ablations and 17 lost to follow-up, 412 patients formed the final prospective validation cohort.

Catheter ablation protocol

Prior to the ablation procedure, transesophageal or intracardiac echocardiography was performed to exclude the presence of left atrial appendage thrombus. Antiarrhythmic drugs (excluding amiodarone) were discontinued for at least five half-lives before the intervention. The procedure was conducted by experienced electrophysiologists using three-dimensional electroanatomic mapping systems—either CARTO 3 (Biosense Webster Inc., USA) or EnSite NavX (St. Jude Medical, USA)—to guide ablation. Following transseptal puncture, intravenous heparin was administered to maintain an activated clotting time between 300 and 350 s. Circumferential pulmonary vein isolation was performed in all patients. After ablation, pulmonary vein potentials were systematically assessed, and bidirectional conduction block was confirmed via pacing maneuvers. Ablation targeting identified ectopic triggers, most commonly originating from the pulmonary veins. When a discrete non-pulmonary-vein trigger was identified during the procedure, it was also targeted. Additional substrate ablatio was performed at the operator’s discretion based on intraoperative electrophysiological findings.

Post-ablation management and follow-up

All patients received oral anticoagulation for at least three months following ablation. To suppress early post-procedural arrhythmias related to inflammation and myocardial injury, all patients were initiated on a Class I or Class III antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) immediately after the procedure. The choice of agent—flecainide, propafenone (Class Ic), or amiodarone, sotalol (Class III)—was determined by the treating electrophysiologist based on the presence or absence of structural heart disease, renal function, and individual comorbidity profile, consistent with guideline-directed care [7]. At the end of the 90-day blanking period, AADs were systematically discontinued in all patients. Re-initiation of AAD therapy occurred only after documented AF recurrence and was regarded as part of post-recurrence management.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was AF recurrence, defined as any documented atrial arrhythmia—including AF, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia—lasting more than 30 s beyond the 3-month blanking period, confirmed by 12-lead ECG or Holter monitoring [7]. Arrhythmia events were adjudicated by two independent electrophysiologists who were blinded to CMM status; discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Cardiovascular death was defined as death directly attributable to cardiovascular causes, including acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, ischemic stroke, sudden cardiac death, and other fatal cardiovascular events.

Definitions of covariates

Clinical variables. Demographic characteristics and medical history—including AF type (paroxysmal vs. non-paroxysmal), time since AF diagnosis, and the presence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, heart failure, and prior stroke—were ascertained through medical record review and patient interview at the time of enrollment. Blood pressure was measured in the seated position after five minutes of rest using an automated oscillometric device; the average of two consecutive readings was recorded.

Echocardiographic measurements. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed within 7 days prior to the ablation procedure by experienced sonographers using commercially available ultrasound systems. All measurements were obtained in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines [12]. Left atrial diameter, right atrial diameter, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, and right ventricular diameter were measured from the parasternal long-axis view. Left ventricular ejection fraction was calculated using the biplane Simpson method from apical four- and two-chamber views. Left atrial appendage emptying velocity was obtained by placing a pulsed-wave Doppler sample volume at the LAA orifice during transesophageal echocardiography. Early diastolic mitral inflow velocity (E) and early diastolic mitral annular velocity (e′) were recorded from the apical four-chamber view. Mitral and tricuspid regurgitation were graded on a 5-point scale (0: none; 1: mild; 2: mild-to-moderate; 3: moderate; 4: moderate-to-severe or greater) by integrating color Doppler, continuous-wave Doppler, and structural parameters. Left atrial appendage spontaneous echo contrast was classified as grade 0 (absent), 1 (mild), 2 (mild-to-moderate), or 3 (moderate or greater).

Laboratory assessments. Fasting blood samples were collected on the morning of the ablation procedure. All assays were performed in the institutional central clinical laboratory using automated analyzers. Parameters measured included fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, uric acid, and serum creatinine. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI equation. Furthermore, eight validated clinical risk scores for AF recurrence—APPLE [13], CAAP-AF [14], MB-LATER [15], DR-FLASH [16], CHADS₂ [17], CHA₂DS₂-VASc [17], SUCCESS [18], and FLAME [19]—were integrated into the analysis; their individual components are detailed in Supplementary Tables S3–S10. CMM was defined as the coexistence of two or more of the following conditions: diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease, heart failure, and stroke [1, 11].

Missing data handling

In this study, certain variables contained missing values, with overall missing data rates below 10% for all affected variables (Supplementary Table S9). To address these gaps, multiple imputation was performed using the missforest package (version 1.5). This method implements the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations framework based on random forest algorithms, which can effectively capture nonlinear relationships and complex variable interactions.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables, which were confirmed to be non-normally distributed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, are summarized as medians with interquartile ranges and compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. The association between CMM and study endpoints was assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and group differences were evaluated using the log-rank test. Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the association between CMM and AF recurrence across predefined strata: age (< 65 vs. ≥65 years), sex, AF type (paroxysmal vs. non-paroxysmal), and BMI (< 24 vs. ≥24 kg/m²).

To investigate potential mediating pathways linking CMM to AF recurrence, a formal mediation analysis was performed using the mediation package (version 4.5.1). The indirect effect and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated based on 1,000 bootstrap samples.

Sensitivity analysis using propensity score matching. To complement the multivariable Cox regression, a propensity score–matched analysis was performed. The propensity score was estimated via logistic regression with CMM status as the outcome and age, sex, AF type (paroxysmal vs. non-paroxysmal), left atrial diameter, and eGFR as predictors. Patients were matched using 1:3 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement (caliper = 0.1 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score). Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. The association between CMM and AF recurrence in the matched cohort was estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression with robust standard errors accounting for the matched design.

Dose–response analysis of CMM burden. To evaluate whether the association between CMM and AF recurrence followed a graded pattern, the number of cardiometabolic conditions (0, 1, 2, or ≥ 3) was modeled as an ordinal variable in Cox regression. Linear trend was assessed by treating the number of CMM components as a continuous variable.

For the outcome of AF recurrence, two sensitivity analyses were conducted: first, excluding patients with recurrence within 3 months post-ablation and re-evaluating the association; second, associations between CMM and AF Recurrence in the external testing cohort and prospective testing cohort. Given the low number of cardiovascular death events, the competing risks regression model was employed to account for the competing risk of all-cause mortality.

All analyses and visualizations were performed using R (version 4.4.1) and Python (version 3.11.14). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Artificial intelligence model development

Ten machine learning algorithms were evaluated to predict recurrence risk in patients with CMM: TabPFN (v2.2.1), AutoGluon (v1.4.0), H2O (v3.46.0.8), CatBoost (v1.2.8), XGBoost (v2.0.3), LightGBM (v4.6.0), and four classifiers from Scikit-learn (v1.4.2)—random forest, logistic regression, naïve Bayes, K-nearest neighbors, and support vector machine.

Data partitioning. The derivation cohort was randomly divided into a training set (80%) and an internal test set (20%) using stratified sampling to preserve the outcome distribution. The external validation cohort and prospective test cohort were held out entirely from this process and used only for final moFeature selection, encoding, and scaling. After the training–test split, the Boruta algorithm was applied exclusively to the training partition to identify relevant predictors. The selected feature subset was then propagated to the internal test set, external cohort, and prospective cohort without modification. Continuous variables were standardized (z-score transformation) and categorical variables were one-hot encoded; both transformations were fitted on the training set and applied unchanged to all test sets. No test set data were used at any point during feature selection, encoding, or scaling.

Model training and hyperparameter tuning. Hyperparameter optimization was performed using grid search with 5-fold cross-validation within the training partition. For each algorithm, the combination of hyperparameters yielding the highest mean cross-validated ROC-AUC was selected, and the final model was retrained on the entire training set using these optimized parameters.

Model evaluation and interpretability. Model performance was assessed on the internal test set, the external validation cohort, and the prospective test cohort. Metrics reported include ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, F1-score, and G-mean.del evaluation.

Model performance was evaluated across three independent datasets: the internal test set, an external validation set, and a prospective test set. Performance metrics included the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC), area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC), accuracy, specificity, precision, recall, and F1-score. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed to evaluate model discrimination across multiple follow-up horizons. Time-dependent ROC analysis was performed using the cumulative sensitivity/dynamic specificity approach with inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW). Time-dependent AUC values were estimated at 1, 2, and 3 years post-ablation for both the post-blanking TabPFN model and the pre-ablation LightGBM model.

The best-performing model was selected based on a comprehensive evaluation of these metrics and further interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to assess global feature importance and individual prediction contributions. The final model was also benchmarked against eight established clinical risk scores for AF recurrence.

Deployment tool using the streamlit framework

To enhance clinical applicability, the final prediction model was deployed as an interactive web application using the Python-based Streamlit framework (Supplementary Fig. 6). The tool enables risk assessment; upon input of the required predictor variables, it returns the estimated probability of AF recurrence (https://github.com/Jiaruikun/af.git).

Results

Clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients

The CMM group (n = 686) exhibited significantly higher baseline incidences of all clinical endpoints compared with the Non-CMM group (n = 2622), including all-cause mortality (4.23% vs. 1.60%), AF recurrence (50.9% vs. 34.2%), and cardiovascular mortality (3.35% vs. 0.99%) (all P < 0.001). The CMM group also demonstrated larger LAD, lower eGFR, and elevated white blood cell counts. See Supplementary Table 12 for complete details. The higher rate of AF recurrence in the CMM group was also observed in the external and prospective testing cohorts (Supplementary Tables S13 and S14), consistent with findings from the primary analysis.

Association between cardiometabolic multimorbidity and clinical outcomes

Over a median follow-up of 44 months, we observed 1245 patients of AF recurrence, 49 cardiovascular deaths, and 71 all-cause deaths. The association of the CMM with these outcomes was quantified using Cox proportional hazards models (Fig. 1). Initially, the unadjusted analysis revealed substantial associations with AF recurrence (HR 1.71, 95% CI: 1.51–1.94, p < 0.001), all-cause death (HR 3.29, 95% CI: 2.04–5.28, p < 0.001), and cardiovascular death (HR 4.14, 95% CI: 2.36–7.27, p < 0.001). Critically, these associations persisted after multivariable adjustment. In the fully adjusted model accounting for age, sex, AF type, left atrial diameter, and additional clinical risk factors, the CMM retained its significant predictive value for AF recurrence (HR 1.17, 95% CI: 1.03–1.33, p = 0.019), all-cause death (HR 1.83, 95% CI: 1.11-3.00, p = 0.017), and cardiovascular death (HR 2.22, 95% CI: 1.24–3.98, p = 0.008).

Sensitivity analysis using propensity score matching. After 1:3 nearest-neighbor matching on age, sex, AF type, left atrial diameter, and eGFR, 683 patients with CMM were matched to 1,654 patients without CMM. All standardized mean differences after matching were below 0.1, indicating adequate covariate balance between groups (Supplementary Table 21). In the matched cohort, CMM remained significantly associated with AF recurrence (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.50, p < 0.001), consistent with the primary multivariable Cox regression analysis.

Dose–response relationship between CMM burden and AF recurrence. When CMM was analyzed according to the number of component conditions, a graded increase in recurrence risk was observed (Supplementary Table S22). In the crude model, the presence of one, two, and three or more conditions was associated with progressively higher hazard ratios of 1.72 (95% CI 1.51–1.96), 2.13 (95% CI 1.83–2.50), and 2.57 (95% CI 2.05–3.23), respectively, relative to patients with no cardiometabolic conditions (p for trend < 0.001). After full adjustment for age, sex, non-paroxysmal AF, left atrial diameter, and eGFR (Model 2), a stepwise association persisted: HR 1.31 (95% CI 1.15–1.50) for one condition, HR 1.44 (95% CI 1.22–1.69) for two conditions, and HR 1.29 (95% CI 1.02–1.64) for three or more conditions (p for trend < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis

The increased risk of AF recurrence associated with CMM was consistently observed across all predefined subgroups (all p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 2a). This association was significantly modified by age (P for interaction = 0.044), with a stronger effect in patients younger than 65 years (HR 1.79, 95% CI: 1.44–2.21) compared to those aged 65 years or older (HR 1.39, 95% CI: 1.19–1.63). No significant heterogeneity in the association was observed according to sex, AF type, or body mass index.

CMM was significantly associated with higher risks of both all-cause and cardiovascular death across all subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 3b and 3c). For all-cause death, point estimates indicated a potentially greater risk in females (HR 5.31) than in males (HR 2.29), although the interaction test did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.089). The risk of cardiovascular death was consistently elevated across subgroups, with HR ranging from 2.78 to 5.37, and no statistically significant interactions were detected for any examined variable.

Mediation analyses

Mediation analyses revealed several significant mediators in the association between CMM and AF recurrence. As shown in Fig. 3, LAD exhibited the strongest mediating effect, accounting for approximately 46.5% of the total effect (ACME = 0.044, p < 0.001). Substantial mediating effects were also observed for METS-IR (25.6%), LAAeV (24.9%), and UHR (24.1%).

Predicting AF recurrence in patients with CMM: a comparative evaluation of artificial intelligence models and traditional clinical risk scores

Given the high risk of AF recurrence in patients with CMM, this study systematically evaluated clinical risk stratification and developed predictive models. First, we compared the performance of existing clinical risk scores in the internal test set (Supplementary Table S17 and Supplementary Figure S5). The MB-LATER score, which incorporates early recurrence predictors, achieved the highest PR-AUC (0.739), along with excellent specificity (0.971) and positive predictive value (PPV, 0.917), yet exhibited limited sensitivity (0.314), indicating a substantial risk of false-negative results in screening applications. In contrast, the CAAP-AF score demonstrated more balanced discriminative performance, achieving the highest ROC-AUC (0.699) and accuracy (0.645), together with moderate sensitivity (0.686) and specificity (0.603).

Recognizing that variables such as early recurrence—though prognostically informative and integral to established scores like MB-LATER—are unavailable before the ablation procedure, we developed two categories of machine learning models: a post-blanking model that additionally incorporated early recurrence, and a pre-ablation model restricted to pre-procedural variables.

We developed ten AI–based models using clinical features with early recurrence (Supplementary Table S16 and Supplementary Figure S5). In the internal test set, the TabPFN model exhibited superior overall discriminative performance, yielding a ROC-AUC of 0.873 and a PR-AUC of 0.895 (Fig. 4a and b). Statistical comparison using the DeLong test revealed that the TabPFN model exhibited significantly superior discriminative performance compared to both the MB-LATER and CAAP-AF scores in the internal test set (all p values < 0.001). This performance advantage was consistently maintained in the external test set and a prospective test cohort (Supplementary Tables S18). The pre-ablation model was built using demographic characteristics, comorbidities, echocardiographic measurements, and laboratory values available at the time of clinical evaluation. Among ten algorithms evaluated, LightGBM achieved the best discriminative performance, with an ROC-AUC of 0.766 (95% CI, 0.687–0.844) and a PR-AUC of 0.755 in the internal test cohort (Supplementary Table S19). CatBoost and TabPFN showed comparable overall accuracy, but LightGBM offered a favorable sensitivity–specificity balance and more consistent calibration across prospective and external testing cohorts (Supplementary Table S20). Across all three test cohorts, the pre-ablation LightGBM model outperformed both the FLAME and CAAP-AF scores in terms of ROC-AUC, F1-score, and G-mean (Supplementary Table S20).

Time-dependent ROC analysis demonstrated stable discriminative performance of both models across follow-up horizons extending to 3 years (Supplementary Figure S7). For the pre-ablation LightGBM model, time-dependent AUC values at 1, 2, and 3 years were 0.776, 0.760, and 0.730, respectively. The post-blanking TabPFN model, which additionally incorporates early recurrence, yielded corresponding AUC values of 0.860, 0.879, and 0.857.

SHAP analysis of the post-blanking TabPFN model in the internal test set identified key predictors of AF recurrence. In addition to well-established factors such as early recurrence, LAD, age, blood pressure, and NPAF, BMI, the model revealed the incremental predictive value of several echocardiographic and laboratory parameters, including LAAeV, interventricular septal thickness, Emv, RAD, FBG, HDL-C, and lactate dehydrogenase. SHAP analysis of the pre-ablation LightGBM model identified key predictors available prior to the procedure (Supplementary Figure S8).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the clinical impact, underlying mechanisms, and AI–based prediction of CMM in patients with AF undergoing catheter ablation. The findings are threefold: (1) CMM is independently associated with significantly increased risks of AF recurrence, all-cause death, and cardiovascular death; (2) left atrial structural and functional remodeling, along with insulin resistance markers, substantially mediate the association between CMM and AF recurrence; and (3) Third, for patients with CMM, an artificial intelligence–based model—whether restricted to pre-ablation features (LightGBM) or incorporating post-procedural variables (TabPFN)—consistently outperformed traditional clinical risk scores in predicting AF recurrence, with robust validation across both external and prospective cohorts.

Current AF management guidelines primarily focus on single-disease, with limited attention to multimorbidity [20]. However, the net benefit of AF therapies may be modified by coexisting conditions. While prior studies have associated CMM with higher AF incidence, particularly in younger individuals [11, 21]. Our data extend these findings by identifying CMM as a distinct high-risk phenotype among patients with AF. After comprehensive multivariable adjustment, CMM remained independently associated with a 17% higher risk of AF recurrence and 80% and 120% greater risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, respectively. Moreover, the association between CMM and AF recurrence was stronger in patients under 65 years, suggesting that CMM exerts more severe pathophysiological effects in younger individuals. This highlights the critical importance of early and aggressive management of cardiometabolic risk factors.

The present findings are consistent with observations from larger multicenter experiences. The ARRC-AF study, which analyzed rhythm control trajectories in 23,323 patients following index AF ablation, reported that 16.6% of patients required repeat ablation and that comorbidities—including obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, coronary artery disease, and valvular heart disease—were independently associated with the need for repeat intervention [22]. the consistent association of comorbidity burden with ablation outcomes across study designs and populations strengthens the case for examining multimorbidity as an integrated exposure rather than as a collection of independent covariates. Where our study extends beyond existing work is in quantifying cumulative CMM as a graded prognostic factor, identifying the structural and metabolic pathways through which it operates, and demonstrating that an AI-based approach can outperform conventional risk scores in this specific high-risk population.

Our mediation analysis clarifies the mechanistic pathways linking CMM to AF recurrence. The finding that LAD mediates approximately 46.5% of the total effect is pivotal. As a well-established structural substrate for post-ablation AF recurrence, LAD enlargement likely transmits the hemodynamic stress from CMM components—such as hypertension and heart failure—to progressive atrial remodeling [20]. Complementing this, left atrial appendage emptying velocity (LAAeV), a marker of atrial mechanical dysfunction, further highlights the central role of CMM-driven structural changes. Notably, insulin resistance (METS-IR; 25.6% mediation) and a novel hematological index, the uric acid to HDL-C ratio (UHR; 24.1%), also serve as key mediators. METS-IR reflects a metabolic pathway in which insulin resistance promotes systemic inflammation, fibrosis, and electrical instability [23, 24]. UHR indicates heightened oxidative stress and inflammation, fostering a pro-thrombotic and pro-fibrotic environment that contributes to endothelial dysfunction and atrial substrate formation [25]. Together, these mediators delineate a multifactorial pathophysiology involving structural, metabolic, and inflammatory pathways, revealing actionable targets for future preventive strategies. Although these findings underscore the central importance of left atrial remodeling in CMM-driven recurrence, left atrial dilatation is not specific to cardiometabolic pathology. The left atrium integrates multiple hemodynamic, electrical, and myopathic exposures over a patient’s lifetime. Cardiomyopathy—particularly hypertrophic cardiomyopathy—can produce substantial atrial myopathy and fibrosis even in the absence of hypertension, diabetes, or obesity. In a recent study of patients undergoing AF ablation, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy was associated with significantly greater LA fibrosis burden on late gadolinium enhancement MRI and a threefold increase in recurrence risk compared to patients without structural heart disease, independent of metabolic comorbidities [26]. Mitral regurgitation, whether degenerative or functional, imposes chronic volume overload that mechanically dilates the left atrium through a pathway distinct from the metabolic and inflammatory cascades that characterize CMM [27]. Additionally, prolonged periods of untreated atrial fibrillation promote tachycardia-induced electrical and structural remodeling, further enlarging the left atrium and perpetuating a vicious cycle [28]. These non-metabolic drivers do not weaken the CMM–LAD pathway we identified; instead, they complement it by accounting for the remaining variance in left atrial size and, in turn, a portion of the recurrence risk not captured by CMM alone.

Beyond risk stratification, the identification of patients with high CMM burden may have therapeutic implications. Emerging real-world data suggest that metabolic pharmacotherapies—including SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists—are associated with a reduced incidence of AF recurrence following catheter ablation among patients with type 2 diabetes or obesity [10, 29, 30]. Whether these agents exert their antiarrhythmic effects through direct atrial mechanisms—such as reduced epicardial adipose tissue inflammation, improved endothelial function, or modulation of autonomic tone—or indirectly via blood pressure reduction, weight loss, and glycemic improvement remains an open question. Nevertheless, the implementation of upstream metabolic interventions during the post-ablation follow-up period may further reduce the likelihood of AF recurrence in appropriately selected patients. Patients with high cumulative cardiometabolic burden may represent the subgroup in whom adjunctive metabolic therapy yields the greatest absolute risk reduction, a hypothesis that warrants testing in prospective trials that stratify enrollment by CMM status.

The TabPFN model represents a state-of-the-art deep learning framework specifically designed for tabular data [31]. In our internal test set, TabPFN achieved superior predictive performance (ROC-AUC: 0.873) compared to established clinical risk scores and other machine learning models. Crucially, its generalizability was robustly confirmed through both external and prospective validation. This enhanced performance likely stems from the model’s capacity to capture the complex, non-linear interactions among a wide array of clinical features. The temporal dimension of recurrence risk deserves explicit consideration when interpreting model performance. The time-dependent ROC analysis indicated that both the pre-ablation LightGBM model and the post-blanking TabPFN model retained useful discrimination through 3 years of follow-up. The observed attenuation at longer horizons—more pronounced in the pre-ablation model, which cannot account for post-procedural events—is biologically expected: baseline measurements reflect the arrhythmogenic substrate at a single time point, whereas recurrence risk years later is increasingly modulated by substrate progression, blood pressure control, weight fluctuation, and other time-varying factors.

The present findings can be situated alongside recent work by Noujaim et al. [22], who applied unsupervised k-means clustering to a cohort of patients with persistent atrial fibrillation and identified three phenotypic clusters defined predominantly by left atrial remodeling features. Despite a shared interest in machine learning–based phenotyping of the AF ablation population, the two studies differ in several fundamental respects. Noujaim et al. focused exclusively on persistent AF and employed an unsupervised, hypothesis-generating framework, whereas our study enrolled both paroxysmal and persistent AF and was driven by a specific a priori hypothesis concerning cumulative CMM. Moreover, the primary exposures differ: their clusters were anchored in echocardiographic indices of atrial substrate, while our analysis centered on the systemic metabolic milieu indexed by the co-occurrence of hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and dyslipidemia. These two perspectives—anatomic substrate versus systemic cardiometabolic burden—are complementary rather than competing. Future work integrating both echocardiographic phenotyping and quantitative CMM assessment may yield risk stratification tools that capture a more complete picture of the atrial arrhythmogenic milieu.

Our findings have important clinical implications. First, patients with CMM should be recognized as a high-risk population for AF recurrence, warranting intensified post-ablation monitoring and comprehensive management of modifiable risk factors—such as weight management, glycemic control, and blood pressure regulation. Evidence indicates that adherence to a healthy lifestyle may attenuate the progression from isolated cardiometabolic abnormalities to full-blown multimorbidity, particularly in younger individuals [32]. Second, our AI model paves the way for developing integrated, data-driven decision aids. Future research must prospectively validate its clinical utility, particularly in optimizing patient selection for catheter ablation to enhance outcomes in this high-risk population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, residual or unmeasured confounding may persist despite comprehensive multivariable adjustment, which is an inherent limitation of observational studies. Second, although the AI model was rigorously validated in both internal and external cohorts, its generalizability should be further confirmed in more diverse populations, including those from different healthcare systems and ethnic backgrounds, to ensure broad applicability. Third, the observational design limits our ability to draw definitive causal inferences regarding the identified mediators, despite the use of rigorous mediation analysis. Fourth, although the median follow-up extended to 44 months, event rates for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality were low, reflecting the overall favorable prognosis of patients selected for catheter ablation. In certain CMM subgroups, the number of patients at risk diminished substantially at later follow-up time points, which may amplify between-group differences in the tails of the survival curves. Studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up are needed to confirm the long-term prognostic impact of cumulative CMM burden on hard clinical endpoints. Fifth, with respect to arrhythmia monitoring, the use of intermittent 12-lead ECG and 24-hour Holter monitoring underestimates the true burden of asymptomatic atrial tachyarrhythmias compared to continuous monitoring with implantable loop recorders. This monitoring protocol is, however, consistent with that employed in the majority of published AF ablation studies. Finally, while the model demonstrated strong predictive performance, further improvements in accuracy may be achievable through the integration of emerging biomarkers or high-resolution imaging data, such as cardiac MRI or strain echocardiography.

Conclusion

Patients with CMM have a higher risk of AF recurrence and death. This risk is linked to atrial structural remodeling, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation. Conventional risk scores provide limited predictive value. In contrast, our TabPFN model offers more accurate and generalizable risk prediction by capturing complex clinical interactions. These results support treating CMM as a distinct high-risk condition and highlight the potential of combining biological insights with data-driven tools to improve post-ablation care.
