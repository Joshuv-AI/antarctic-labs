Outer-Crust Equations of State for Neutron Stars
P.S. Koliogiannis, ,, N. Paar ,

Abstract
Context. The outer crust of neutron stars, composed of neutron-rich nuclei embedded in a degenerate electron gas, provides a low-density regime in which nuclear-mass predictions are directly reflected in the equation of state. While experimentally measured masses constrain the composition in the outer layers, the deepest regions approaching neutron drip depend on theoretical extrapolations toward increasingly neutron-rich nuclei, introducing uncertainties in the outer-crust equation of state and its astrophysical implications. Aims. We construct and systematically assess four outer-crust equations of state based on relativistic nuclear mass models and a machine-learning mass table. Our aim is to quantify the sensitivity of the equilibrium composition and thermodynamic properties of the outer crust to the underlying nuclear input, and to evaluate how these differences propagate to neutron-star configurations that are particularly sensitive to crustal properties. Methods. Equilibrium sequences of nuclei were determined by minimizing the Gibbs free energy per baryon for cold, catalyzed matter in β \beta -equilibrium. The resulting outer-crust equations of state were then employed in neutron-star structure calculations near the minimum-mass limit, where global observables are especially sensitive to the low-density equation of state. Results. The four nuclear models predict different equilibrium sequences, last bound nuclei, and neutron-drip properties. These differences are confined to the deepest layers of the outer crust, beyond current experimental mass coverage. Nevertheless, they propagate only weakly to crust-dominated neutron-star configurations: the gravitational mass, radius, crustal thickness, and fractional moment of inertia differ by less than one percent among the models considered. Conclusions. Modern nuclear-mass models provide consistent outer-crust equations of state for neutron-star applications. Although the detailed composition near neutron drip remains model dependent, the corresponding uncertainties have only a minor impact on the global properties of crust-dominated neutron stars. Therefore, these outer-crust equations of state provide a robust low-density description for astrophysical modelling and for future extensions toward unified neutron-star equations of state.



1
Introduction

The ground state of matter at subnuclear densities, where neutrons remain bound within nuclei, is fundamental to a variety of astrophysical processes, from the late stages of stellar evolution to the structure and dynamics of neutron stars. In this regime, the properties of nuclear matter directly impact observable phenomena such as pulsar glitches, quasi-periodic oscillations in magnetars, and the thermal relaxation of transiently accreting neutron stars
(
Lattimer & Prakash 2004
;
Lattimer 2021
;
Burgio et al. 2021
;
Oertel et al. 2017
)
. Moreover, subnuclear-density matter also has important implications for the cosmic nucleosynthesis of heavy nuclei. A detailed understanding of matter in this density range is therefore essential for linking microscopic nuclear physics to a broad class of astrophysical observations
(
Lattimer & Prakash 2004
;
Lattimer 2021
;
Burgio et al. 2021
;
Oertel et al. 2017
)
.

At this density region, matter forms the outer solid crust of a neutron star, whose structure is governed by the low-density nuclear equation of state (EOS). The crustal EOS, encoding the relation between pressure and density, as well as intrinsic composition details, determines the equilibrium sequence of neutron-rich nuclei and locates the neutron drip point, where neutrons become unbound from nuclei signalling the transition toward the inner crust
Baym et al. 1971
. Beyond its impact on microscopic composition, the crustal EOS also affects macroscopic stellar properties, including the minimum stable mass and the corresponding radius, as well as characteristic crustal quantities such as the crust thickness and its contribution to the stellar moment of inertia
(
Baym et al. 1971
;
Roca-Maza & Piekarewicz 2008
;
Sharma, B. K. et al. 2015
;
Jiang et al. 2025
)
.

The outer crust of a neutron star consists of a Coulomb lattice of neutron-rich nuclei immersed in a highly degenerate electron gas. At the densities characteristic of this region, nuclei arrange themselves in a body-centered cubic (bcc) lattice, which minimizes the Coulomb energy of the system
(
Baym et al. 1971
;
Haensel et al. 1989
;
Haensel & Pichon 1994
)
. Although the lattice contribution provides only a relatively small correction to the total energy density, it plays an important role in determining the equilibrium nuclear species by favouring nuclei with larger mass numbers and thereby lowering the total energy of the system
(
Baym et al. 1971
)
. The presence of the degenerate electron gas also stabilizes increasingly neutron-rich nuclei against
\beta
decay, since the large electron chemical potential makes electron capture energetically favourable. At very low densities (
\rho\lesssim 10^{4}\penalty\ {\rm g\,cm^{-3}}
), the energetically favoured nucleus is
56
Fe, which represents the ground state of cold catalyzed matter
(
Feynman et al. 1949
)
. As the density increases, the electron Fermi energy rises and successive electron-capture reactions drive the composition toward progressively more neutron-rich nuclei. This sequence continues until the neutron-drip density is reached, where neutrons begin to escape from nuclei and form a dilute gas surrounding the nuclear clusters. The onset of neutron drip marks the transition from the outer to the inner crust.

A key ingredient in determining the composition of the outer crust is the knowledge of nuclear ground-state masses
(
Baym et al. 1971
;
Haensel et al. 1989
)
. At low densities, the equilibrium nuclei are experimentally known, allowing for a largely model-independent determination of the crustal composition. However, as the density increases and the equilibrium nuclei approach the neutron-drip line, experimental data become limited and theoretical nuclear mass models are required to predict the properties of increasingly neutron-rich systems.

The EOS of the neutron-star outer crust has been investigated for several decades, beginning with the pioneering work of
Feynman et al. 1949
, who studied the envelope of dense matter and identified
56
Fe as dominating nucleus in the ground state at low densities. A systematic description of the outer crust was later developed in the seminal work of
Baym et al. 1971
[hereafter BPS], who formulated the EOS and determined the sequence of equilibrium nuclei using the nuclear mass table of
Myers & Swiatecki 1966
. Subsequent studies aimed at improving this description by incorporating more sophisticated nuclear models and updated nuclear-mass data. In particular,
Haensel et al. 1989
employed Hartree–Fock–Bogoliubov calculations with a Skyrme effective interaction to investigate the composition of matter below the neutron-drip point. Later,
Haensel & Pichon 1994
combined the 1992 Atomic Mass Evaluation
(
Audi & Wapstra 1993
)
with theoretical nuclear mass tables from
Möller & Nix 1988
and
Aboussir et al. 1995
, providing an improved determination of the ground state of cold, dense matter. The work of
Rüster et al. 2006
further updated the original BPS calculations by employing the nuclear mass data from
Audi et al. 2003
. In a related study,
Roca-Maza & Piekarewicz 2008
analyzed the impact of the symmetry energy on the outer crust, demonstrating that models with a stiffer symmetry energy, which yield larger neutron skins, predict more neutron-rich equilibrium sequences near neutron drip, thereby linking nuclear structure properties to crust composition and highlighting the sensitivity of the outer crust to isovector properties of the effective interactions employed. With the continued development of microscopic nuclear models, several studies have investigated the outer crust using Hartree–Fock–Bogoliubov mass models and modern experimental mass evaluations, such as AME2012 and AME2017
(
Chamel & Fantina 2016
;
Pearson et al. 2011
;
Pearson et al. 2018
;
Pearson et al. 2019
;
Antić et al. 2020
)
. In parallel, significant effort has been devoted to constructing unified or consistent EOSs in which the crust and core are described within the same theoretical framework, enabling thermodynamically consistent modeling of neutron-star structure and global observables
(
Lattimer & Douglas Swesty 1991
;
Shen et al. 1998a
;
Shen et al. 1998b
;
Shen et al. 2011a
;
Shen et al. 2011b
;
Chabanat et al. 1998
;
Douchin & Haensel 2001
;
Chamel et al. 2007
;
Chamel et al. 2011
;
Roca-Maza & Piekarewicz 2008
;
Hebeler & Schwenk 2010
;
Hempel & Schaffner-Bielich 2010
;
Sharma, B. K. et al. 2015
)
.

In this work, we construct and analyze outer-crust EOSs using three modern nuclear mass tables based on the relativistic energy density functionals (EDFs) with density-dependent meson-exchange interaction DD–ME2
(
Lalazissis et al. 2005
;
Agbemava et al. 2014
;
Afanasjev et al. 2015
;
Afanasjev & Agbemava 2016
)
, and density-dependent point coupling interactions DD–PC1
(
Nikšić et al. 2008
;
Agbemava et al. 2014
)
, and DD–PCX
(
Yüksel et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
. In addition, for comparison, recently established machine-learning-based mass model ELMA
(
Agrawal et al. 2025
)
is also employed.
In particular, ELMA is an ensemble learning and averaging model, where the root mean square error is
\sim
65 keV (below the critical threshold of 100 keV) considering the AME2020 mass table
(
Huang et al. 2021
)
. Experimental masses from AME2020 are used wherever available, while the theoretical models determine the composition only in the neutron-rich region beyond presently available measurements. Our first objective is therefore to quantify how differences among these theoretical extrapolations affect the predicted equilibrium sequence of nuclei, the last bound nucleus, and the location of the neutron-drip transition in outer crust. Our second objective is to assess how such differences in microscopic description of nuclear masses propagate to neutron-star structure, focusing on observables that are especially sensitive to the crust, such as the minimum stable mass, the corresponding stellar radius, the crustal thickness, and the crustal contribution to the moment of inertia.

To assess the impact of the outer-crust EOS on neutron-star observables, all outer-crust EOSs are matched to the same liquid-core EOS, while the inner crust is described using two complementary prescriptions: (i) a microscopic model based on the SLy interaction
(
Douchin & Haensel 2001
)
, and (ii) a polytropic parametrization designed to mimic a unified treatment of the intermediate-density region
(
Link et al. 1999
;
Carriere et al. 2003
)
. This setup allows us to separate the sensitivity to the outer-crust composition from the residual uncertainty associated with the inner-crust description. In this way, the present work also provides a low-density benchmark for future extensions toward unified neutron-star EOS calculations within the same theoretical framework.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sect.
2
, we present the theoretical framework used to determine the equilibrium composition and construct the corresponding outer-crust EOSs, as well as the formalism employed for neutron-star structure and slow rotation. In Sect.
3
, we discuss the resulting equilibrium sequences, neutron-drip properties, and EOS characteristics, and then examine their impact on crust-dominated neutron-star observables. Finally, Sect.
4
summarizes the main conclusions and discusses the implications of the present results for future unified descriptions of neutron-star matter.

2
Theoretical Framework

2.1
Equilibrium Structure of the Outer Crust

The low-density regime of neutron stars is described by the EOS of the outer crust, extending from the stellar surface down to the onset of neutron drip. In this region, spanning baryon densities from
\sim 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{fm^{-3}}
to
\sim 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{fm^{-3}}
, matter consists of neutron-rich nuclei arranged in a Coulomb lattice, minimizing their electrostatic interaction energy, and embedded in an ultra-relativistic, degenerate electron gas. As the density increases, electron captures drive the composition toward progressively heavier and more neutron-rich isotopes, giving rise to a discrete sequence of equilibrium nuclides that minimize the Gibbs free energy per baryon. Nuclear shell effects play a central role in determining the sequence of nuclei that appear. Consequently, the outer crust forms a well-defined thermodynamic domain in which microscopic nuclear masses—experimental or theoretical—directly determine the EOS and provide essential boundary conditions for constructing the inner regions of the star
(
Baym et al. 1971
;
Haensel et al. 1989
)
.

To determine the equilibrium composition, structure, and corresponding EOS of the outer crust, we adopt the formalism developed by
Baym et al. 1971
for cold catalyzed matter in its ground state. At a given baryon density, the stable nuclide is obtained by minimizing the Gibbs free energy per baryon, which includes contributions from the nuclear mass, the Coulomb lattice, and the electron gas. The pressure is dominated by the relativistic electrons, while lattice effects introduce a small but non-negligible correction at higher densities. In the following, we summarize the expressions for the total energy density, pressure, and Gibbs free energy employed in our calculations, and outline how they are combined to construct the equilibrium nuclide sequence and the macroscopic outer-crust EOS.

The total energy per baryon in the outer crust is written as the sum of nuclear, lattice, and electronic contributions,

E(A,Z,n_{b})=E_{N}(A,Z)+E_{l}(A,Z,n_{b})+E_{e}(n_{b}),

(1)

where
A
and
Z
denote the mass and proton numbers of the nucleus, respectively, and

n_{b}=A/V
is the average baryon density associated with a charge-neutral cell of volume
V
.
The nuclear contribution is given by the rest-mass energy of the nucleus,

E_{N}(A,Z)\equiv M(N,Z)=(A-Z)m_{n}c^{2}+Zm_{p}c^{2}-B(A,Z),

(2)

where
m_{n}
and
m_{p}
are the neutron and proton rest masses, and
B(A,Z)
denotes the nuclear binding energy.
The Coulomb interaction between fully ionized nuclei arranged in a bcc lattice gives rise to the lattice term,

E_{l}(A,Z,n_{b})=-(\hbar c)C_{1}\,\frac{Z^{2}}{A^{1/3}}\,k_{F_{b}},

(3)

with
k_{F_{b}}=(3\pi^{2}n_{b})^{1/3}
the baryonic Fermi momentum and

C_{1}=3.40665\times 10^{-3}
for a bcc crystal
(
Roca-Maza & Piekarewicz 2008
)
.
The electrons form a uniform, degenerate Fermi gas and contribute to the total energy density as
(
Glendenning 2000
)

\mathcal{E}_{e}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int_{k\leq k_{F}}k^{2}\sqrt{\hbar^{2}c^{2}k^{2}+m_{e}^{2}c^{4}}dk.

(4)

The macroscopic pressure in the outer crust originates from the electron gas and the Coulomb lattice, since the nuclei are localized and do not contribute directly to the pressure.
The pressure is therefore obtained from the volume derivative of the total energy
(
Baym et al. 1971
)
,

P=-\left.\frac{\partial E}{\partial V}\right|_{A,Z}=\mu_{e}n_{e}-\mathcal{E}_{e}-(\hbar c)\frac{n_{b}}{3}C_{1}\frac{Z^{2}}{A^{4/3}}k_{F_{b}},

(5)

where
\mu_{e}=(\hbar^{2}c^{2}k_{F_{e}}^{2}+m_{e}^{2}c^{4})^{1/2}
is the electron chemical potential including the rest-mass contribution, and
n_{e}=Zn_{b}/A
follows from charge neutrality.
At a given pressure, the equilibrium composition is determined by minimizing the Gibbs free energy per baryon
(
Baym et al. 1971
)
,

\mu(A,Z,P)\equiv\frac{G}{A}=\frac{E(A,Z,n_{b})}{A}+\frac{P}{n_{b}}.

(6)

Since the outer crust is well described as cold catalyzed matter, thermal contributions can be neglected (T=0), and the quantity to be minimized reduces to

\mu(A,Z,P)=\frac{M(A,Z)}{A}+\frac{Z}{A}\mu_{e}-(\hbar c)\frac{4}{3}C_{1}\frac{Z^{2}}{A^{4/3}}k_{F_{b}}.

(7)

For each pressure, the optimal nucleus is obtained by minimizing
\mu(A,Z,P)
with respect to the mass number
A
and the proton number
Z
, yielding the equilibrium nuclide sequence that defines the outer-crust equation of state. The chemical potential is continuous across a transition from one nuclide to another.

For the nuclear masses appearing in Eq. (
2
), or the binding energies, we consider four modern nuclear mass tables: the relativistic density functional models DD–ME2
(
Lalazissis et al. 2005
;
Agbemava et al. 2014
;
Afanasjev et al. 2015
;
Afanasjev & Agbemava 2016
)
, DD–PC1
(
Nikšić et al. 2008
;
Agbemava et al. 2014
)
, and DD–PCX
(
Yüksel et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
, and the machine-learning-based mass model ELMA
(
Agrawal et al. 2025
)
.

2.2
Neutron Star Equations of State

The nuclear EOS for the description of neutron stars is divided into three density regions: the outer crust, the inner crust, and the core. In the present work, where the main subject is the outer crust and its composition, the formalism and nuclear mass models developed in Sec.
2.1
are employed, providing the equilibrium sequence of nuclei up to the neutron-drip point. For the inner crust, two complementary treatments are considered: (i) a microscopic description based on the SLy interaction
(
Douchin & Haensel 2001
)
, and (ii) a polytropic parametrization designed to represent the behaviour of a unified EOS, defined as
(
Link et al. 1999
;
Carriere et al. 2003
)

P(\mathcal{E})=A+B\,\mathcal{E}^{4/3},

(8)

where
P
is the pressure and
\mathcal{E}
the energy density. The constants
A
and
B
are determined by requiring thermodynamic continuity at the boundaries of the inner crust, namely the neutron-drip point at the outer-crust side and the crust–core transition density obtained from the corresponding unified EOS. The choice of Eq. (
8
) is motivated by the fact that fully microscopic calculations of the inner-crust structure for these specific functionals are not currently available. A consistent microscopic treatment of the inner crust would require large-scale calculations that account for nuclear clusters embedded in a neutron gas, which is beyond the scope of the present work.

These approaches allow us to assess the sensitivity of macroscopic neutron-star observables to the inner-crust modelling, and to determine whether the relative differences induced by the outer-crust EOS are preserved when different inner-crust prescriptions are adopted. The uniform liquid core is described using the DD-PC-J31 EOS, which provides a relativistic EDF framework consistent with both nuclear and astrophysical constraints
(
Koliogiannis et al. 2025a
;
Koliogiannis et al. 2025b
)
. The same inner-crust and core EOSs are therefore adopted for all outer-crust models so that the impact of the outer-crust EOS can be examined separately from additional uncertainties associated with the remaining layers.

It has to be noted here, that although a unified treatment of the outer crust, inner crust, and core is of significance
(
Sharma, B. K. et al. 2015
)
, such a construction is beyond the scope of the present work. A fully consistent unified description will be considered in future work.

2.3
Neutron Star Structure in the Slow-Rotation Approximation

The macroscopic structure of a neutron star is determined by Einstein’s field equations, which relate the spacetime geometry to the matter content. Assuming the star is composed of a perfect fluid, and neglecting anisotropic stresses, viscosity, magnetic fields, and heat transport, the energy–momentum tensor is
(
Shapiro & Teukolsky 1983
;
Glendenning 2000
)

(9)

where
u^{\mu}
and
u^{\nu}
are the four-velocities of the fluid,
\mathcal{E}
is the energy density,
P
is the pressure, and
g^{\mu\nu}
is the spacetime metric.

In the non-rotating limit, the spacetime is spherically symmetric, and the stellar structure is described by the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) equations

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi r^{2}}{c^{2}}\mathcal{E}(r),

r

f(r)\equiv e^{-2\lambda(r)}=1-2GM(r)/(c^{2}r)

For slowly rotating stars, the Hartle–Thorne approximation is employed

Differences between the models appear only in the deepest part of the outer crust, once the equilibrium sequence reaches nuclei beyond present experimental coverage. In this region, the composition is determined by the extrapolated masses of increasingly neutron-rich nuclei. As shown in Fig.
1

Although the associated reduction in proton number is consistent with the expected sequence of electron captures, the decrease in neutron number departs from the overall trend toward increasingly neutron-rich nuclei with increasing density. This behaviour illustrates the sensitivity of the equilibrium sequence to the underlying theoretical mass predictions once the experimentally constrained region is exceeded.

At higher densities, all models predict an extended plateau at the magic neutron number
N=82

The final isotopic chains correspond to Ru–Mo–Zr–Sr for DD–ME2, Mo–Zr–Sr–Kr for DD–PC1, and Mo–Zr–Sr for DD–PCX and ELMA. In all cases except for DD–PC1, the last bound nucleus belongs to a neutron-rich Sr isotopic chain.

These differences are clearly visible in the equilibrium sequence of nuclei, but they lead to comparatively small changes in the thermodynamic properties of the EOS. 3.2
Minimum mass configuration

One way to determine the stability of neutron star configurations is the turning-point criterion
(
Shapiro & Teukolsky 1983
)
,

\frac{dM}{d\mathcal{E}_{c}}=0,

(20)

where
M
is the gravitational mass and
\mathcal{E}_{c}
is the central energy density, which defines the boundaries of stable equilibrium along a sequence of configurations. This criterion gives rise to both a maximum and a minimum stable gravitational mass; configurations lying beyond these extrema are dynamically unstable. In this study, we focus only on the minimum-mass configuration.

Although neutron stars close to the minimum mass are not expected to be realized in nature, because core-collapse supernova simulations and evolutionary arguments suggest a lower bound of
\sim 0.1
–
0.2\,M_{\odot}
, their existence and properties are nevertheless important for understanding the stability sequence of neutron stars. The significance of the minimum mass is twofold. First, for isolated neutron stars, it marks the extreme limit at which cold, catalyzed nuclear matter can remain gravitationally bound and stable. Second, in binary neutron star systems, particularly during mass transfer phases, a low-mass component may be driven toward this stability threshold, beyond which the star becomes unstable and may undergo rapid structural rearrangement
(
Colpi et al. 1989
;
Haensel, P. et al. 2002
)
.

This reflects the fact that small changes in the central energy density translate into large variations in the stellar radius. Such behaviour is directly linked to the internal structure of minimum-mass neutron stars, which are only weakly bound and contain a very small liquid core, amounting to merely

A direct comparison between the two inner-crust treatments elucidates the role of the inner-crust EOS in shaping low-mass neutron-star properties. The microscopic description leads to slightly higher minimum masses and more compact configurations, whereas the unified parametrization yields lower minimum masses and significantly larger radii. These systematic shifts originate from differences in the stiffness of the inner-crust EOS and in its matching to the liquid core. Nevertheless, in both approaches the liquid core contributes only a minor fraction of the total mass and radius, confirming that minimum-mass neutron stars are predominantly crust-supported objects. While the absolute values of
M_{\rm min}
and

This conclusion is particularly relevant for astrophysical applications involving low-mass neutron stars, since uncertainties in the outer-crust composition are therefore not expected to significantly affect global structural predictions such as the crustal thickness and the moment of inertia, which are primarily determined by the inner-crust properties.

3.3
Crustal thickness and crustal moment of inertia

The structural and rotational properties of neutron stars near the minimum-mass configuration are mainly dictated by the extended crust
(
Baym et al. 1971
;
Xu et al. 2009
;
Sharma, B. K. et al. 2015
;
Suleiman et al. 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2025
)
. The radial extent of the solid crust is quantified by the crustal thickness, defined as
\Delta R=R-R_{\rm core}
, where
R
is the total stellar radius and
R_{\rm core}
denotes the extent of the liquid core. In these configurations, the core comprises only
\sim
2
–
3\%
of the total radius, highlighting the dominant influence of the crust. Relevant results are summarized in Tables
2
and
3
, while the ratio of the crustal thickness to the stellar radius,
\Delta R/R
, is shown as a function of gravitational mass in Fig.
6
(a). This ratio remains very close to unity,
\Delta R/R\simeq 0.98
, with only marginal variations among the considered models, indicating that the global crustal geometry of minimum-mass configurations is largely insensitive to differences in the outer-crust composition and only weakly dependent on the adopted inner-crust description. This behaviour is consistent with previous studies showing that crustal uncertainties have only a limited effect on macroscopic neutron-star properties
(
Suleiman et al. 2021
)
, and that outer-crust observables such as the crustal thickness and moment of inertia exhibit only weak model dependence when different outer-crust EOSs are employed
(
Jiang et al. 2025
)
.

A complementary measure of the crust’s mechanical significance is the fractional crustal moment of inertia,
I_{\rm cr}
, which is directly linked to the rotational evolution of neutron stars. Its astrophysical relevance arises from pulsar glitches, sudden spin-up events commonly interpreted as the result of angular-momentum transfer from the inner-crust neutron superfluid to the rigid crustal lattice
(
Anderson & Itoh 1975
;
Link et al. 1999
)
. In slowly rotating stars, such as those considered here, the Hartle–Thorne slow-rotation approximation provides an accurate description of
I_{\rm cr}
, since rotational corrections remain small for configurations close to the minimum-mass limit. As shown in Fig.
6
(b), the fractional moment of inertia,
I_{\rm cr}/I
, is likewise nearly constant across all models, with values around
0.98
, mirroring the behaviour of the crustal thickness. This reflects the fact that, in minimum-mass neutron stars, the overwhelming fraction of both the stellar mass and angular momentum is contained in the crust, while the liquid core contributes only minimally to both
\Delta R
and
I_{\rm cr}/I
.

The near-unity values of
\Delta R/R
and
I_{\rm cr}/I
confirm the strongly crust-dominated character of minimum-mass neutron stars and indicate that their structural and rotational properties are governed primarily by the crust. They also show that variations in the outer-crust composition have only a limited impact on global rotational observables, whereas the dominant sensitivity remains associated with the inner-crust EOS. This behaviour is consistent with previous studies indicating that crustal uncertainties have only a limited effect on macroscopic neutron-star properties
(
Suleiman et al. 2021
)
, and that crustal quantities such as the moment of inertia depend only weakly on the adopted outer-crust EOS
(
Jiang et al. 2025
)
. The present results are also relevant for glitch modelling, where the fractional crustal moment of inertia constrains the angular-momentum reservoir available in the superfluid component. The values obtained here exceed by a wide margin the fractional moment-of-inertia constraints inferred from large pulsar glitches, indicating that minimum-mass neutron stars naturally provide sufficient crustal angular-momentum reservoirs. Overall, the close agreement of
\Delta R/R
and
I_{\rm cr}/I
across all considered models indicates that the predicted properties of minimum-mass configurations are only weakly affected by the choice of outer-crust EOS.

4
Conclusions

In this work, we constructed outer-crust EOSs for neutron stars using three relativistic nuclear mass models, DD–ME2, DD–PC1, and DD–PCX, together with the machine-learning-based mass table ELMA. Experimental masses from AME2020 were employed wherever available, while the theoretical models determined the composition in the neutron-rich region beyond present experimental coverage. This framework enabled us to quantify how differences in nuclear-mass extrapolations affect the equilibrium composition of the outer crust and to assess how these differences propagate to crust-dominated neutron-star configurations.

At the microscopic level, the four models predict distinct equilibrium sequences in the deepest layers of the outer crust, together with moderate differences in the neutron-drip transition and in the identity of the last bound nucleus. These deviations arise only beyond the experimentally constrained region and reflect the model dependence of extrapolated masses for nuclei approaching the neutron-drip line.

At the macroscopic level, however, the resulting outer-crust EOSs lead to very similar neutron-star properties near the minimum-mass limit. In particular, the minimum gravitational mass, the corresponding stellar radius, the crustal thickness, and the fractional crustal moment of inertia show only weak variation among the models considered. This indicates that the model dependence of the equilibrium composition remains largely confined to the deepest part of the outer crust and propagates only weakly to global observables of crust-dominated neutron stars. The same qualitative behaviour is obtained for both inner-crust prescriptions employed in the present work, showing that the relative differences associated with the outer-crust EOS are preserved under changes in the treatment of the inner crust. Additionally, the ELMA mass table reproduces the main microscopic and macroscopic features obtained with the relativistic EDF-based models, both in the equilibrium sequence close to neutron drip and in the corresponding neutron-star observables. This agreement supports the use of machine-learning-based mass predictions in astrophysical applications involving nuclei beyond current experimental reach.

The EOS tables constructed in this work are publicly available at
https://github.com/pkoliogi/Outer-Crust-Equations-of-State
and may be used in future neutron-star studies. The present EOSs establish a low-density benchmark for future extensions toward unified neutron-star descriptions, in which the outer crust, inner crust, and core will be treated consistently within the same theoretical framework.
