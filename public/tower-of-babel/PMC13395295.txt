Unusual Causes of Food-Induced Anaphylaxis – A Review
Authors: Parvathy Santhosh, Ale Abraham, Sujoy Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anaphylaxis is a potentially fatal, acute, multisystemic hypersensitivity reaction. Food-induced anaphylaxis (FIA) is typically an IgE-mediated reaction. Although any food has the potential to cause FIA, there are some less well-known causes that have unusual cofactors/triggers, which pose diagnostic challenges and are often classified as idiopathic anaphylaxis. We describe three unusual causes of FIA in this review and discuss testing strategies in resource-limited settings. These include wheat-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis (WDEIA), alpha-gal syndrome, and pork-cat syndrome. WDEIA develops when wheat consumption occurs, along with exercise and additional cofactors like seafood, alcohol, and/or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. The major allergen is omega-5 gliadin, a protein component of the gluten fraction of wheat. Symptoms vary from intermittent urticaria and/or angioedema to severe anaphylaxis. Alpha-gal syndrome is a condition in which delayed allergy symptoms (2–4 hours later) develop after consumption of red meat, where sensitization to the carbohydrate develops following a tick bite. Pork-cat syndrome is a rare condition where patients sensitized to cat epithelium develop allergic symptoms on eating pork. An accurate history, followed by skin prick testing and blood tests for specific IgE to culprit allergens, forms the cornerstone of diagnosis. Awareness of these entities, particularly with changing dietary habits and the ease of international travel, is essential for appropriate diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis is a serious systemic hypersensitivity reaction, usually rapid in onset and potentially fatal.[1] The prevalence varies globally, with reports of a recent increase in diagnosis.[234] In the United States of America (USA), the lifetime prevalence of anaphylaxis is estimated to be 0.05–2%, while it is ~3% in Europe. Around 0.3% of the population will have an episode of anaphylaxis in their life.[56]

Acute onset (within minutes to hours) is the characteristic feature. Clinical features include mucocutaneous features (generalized urticaria, angioedema, itching, flushing) and a minimum of one of the following criteria: pulmonary involvement (breathlessness, wheeze/bronchospasm, hypoxemia), persistent gastrointestinal symptoms (abdominal cramps, vomiting, diarrhea), hypotension, or features of end-organ dysfunction (syncope, incontinence). Anaphylaxis can be diagnosed if the patient has hypotension, bronchospasm, or laryngospasm in association with exposure to a confirmed or likely allergen, even in the absence of cutaneous features.[7]

The most common causes of anaphylaxis are foods (e.g., milk, soy, eggs, nuts, and shellfish), drugs (e.g., antibiotics [penicillin], nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs]), insect venom (Hymenoptera), intravenous radiocontrast agents, and latex. Even with the availability of extensive allergens for testing, up to 20% cases remain idiopathic.[5]

Based on the pathogenesis, food allergies may be categorized as IgE-mediated, non-IgE- mediated, and mixed (both IgE-dependent and IgE-independent pathways). IgE-mediated reactions are usually of acute onset, with features of pulmonary, gastrointestinal, cutaneous, and cardiovascular involvement. The other two types have a slower onset, with gastrointestinal and skin symptoms.[8]

Food-induced anaphylaxis (FIA) is usually IgE-mediated, with specific IgE to the triggering antigen being detectable in blood tests. In tissues, the IgE antibody specific to the culprit allergen binds to the high-affinity IgE receptor (FcεRI) on basophils and mast cells. Upon antigen cross-linking of receptors, a series of reactions leads to the release of both preformed mediators (histamine, tryptase, carboxypeptidase A, proteoglycans, cytokines) and newly synthesized mediators (bradykinins, cytokines, leucotrienes, and platelet-activating factor), which cause the typical features of anaphylaxis.[9]

The most frequently implicated causes for FIA are peanuts, tree nuts, dairy,eggs, sesame seeds, fish, and shellfish in all age groups.[51011] However, less widely known causes may pose diagnostic difficulty. Recurrent attacks may be regarded as ‘idiopathic anaphylaxis’ or ‘idiopathic urticaria,’ where targeted testing for allergens would be beneficial for management.

In this review, we describe three of the lesser-known causes of FIA to raise awareness, particularly with ever-changing dietary habits and ease of international travel, as they have the potential to cause severe and potentially fatal anaphylaxis.

Methods

A systematic search of the PubMed/MEDLINE database up to November 2024 was performed. The key search terms employed were “wheat-dependent urticaria, wheat-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis, pork-cat syndrome, alpha-gal syndrome’’ aimed at identifying relevant articles.

Two independent co-authors carried out the screening process. Studies using animal and/or in vitro models, articles in languages other than English, and articles that did not mention the clinical details of anaphylaxis were excluded. Data collection included the following parameters: epidemiology, clinical features, laboratory, and molecular allergology data.

Wheat-Dependent Exercise-Induced Anaphylaxis

Wheat-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis (WDEIA) is an IgE-mediated food allergy with symptoms ranging from intermittent urticaria to severe anaphylaxis, triggered by wheat intake in association with certain augmenting cofactors. Exercise is the most important cofactor, but intake of seafood, NSAIDs, or alcohol can also be significant.[12] Even without cofactors, wheat is a common food allergen, particularly in central Europe. Cofactor-independent wheat-induced anaphylaxis is more common in children, while cofactor-dependent reactions like WDEIA are more prevalent in teenagers and adults.[1314]

Pathogenesis

The major allergen causing WDEIA is omega-5 gliadin (or Tri a19), a protein component of gluten. It is a water-insoluble, monomeric gliadin fraction of wheat seed storage protein. It can also induce IgE-mediated severe wheat-induced anaphylaxis in children.[1415]

There are cases of omega-5-gliadin-negative WDEIA; in some cases, other allergens have been identified, including high-molecular-weight glutenin subunits (HMW-GS), low-molecular-weight glutenin subunits (LMW-GS), and α/β/γ-gliadins. Non-gluten allergens have also been implicated, including wheat lipid transfer protein tri a14, which often has a stronger association with atopic diathesis, as well as cross-reactivity with other cereals and nuts.[16] Another allergen is hydrolyzed wheat protein (HWP), a component of some cosmetics, soaps, and shampoos (reported in Japan and Europe). Patients are sensitized percutaneously, and the first symptoms are local, like eyelid edema on using the product, with WDEIA symptoms occurring later, on consuming natural wheat products in combination with cofactors.[1718]

Another subtype of WDEIA, where omega-5-gliadin is negative, occurs in the background of allergy to grass pollen. Around 65% of subjects with allergy to grass pollen can be sensitized to wheat without any symptoms. WDEIA in these patients can occur owing to peroxidase-1 of grass pollen cross-reacting with beta-glucosidase of wheat.[19]

WDEIA falls under the spectrum of food-dependent exercise- induced anaphylaxis (FDEIA). Anaphylaxis develops if the trigger food is eaten before exercise; however, reactions can also develop on eating wheat following vigorous exercise.[2021] Although the precise pathogenesis of FDEIA has not been elucidated, there are multiple theories. Enhanced gut permeability during exercise, with increased intestinal absorption of certain allergens after intense exercise, has been reported in human models.[2223] Aerobic exercise lowers the activation/degranulation thresholds of basophils/mast cells. Other theories include autonomic dysregulation, mobilization of immune cells from the gut, and alterations in blood flow during exercise [Figure 1].[21]

The broad set of cofactors implicated in FDEIA includes alcohol, NSAIDs, seasonal aeroallergens, seafood, menstruation, opioid use, stress, infection, sleep deprivation, and dehydration. NSAIDs may increase allergen absorption by contributing to the dysregulation of intestinal tight junctions.[24]

Other foods reported to cause FDEIA include shellfish, nuts (peanuts and tree nuts), tomatoes, fish, red meat (including pork and beef), mushrooms, eggs, fruits such as apples and peaches, and dairy.[25]

Epidemiology

The reported prevalence of wheat allergy, including WDEIA, is 0.10-0.80% in adults, regardless of place of origin or ethnicity.[2627282930] The exact prevalence of WDEIA is not known. Males and females are equally affected, as per recent statistics, with adults forming the majority.[13] Diagnostic delay can occur due to a lack of correlation with cofactors.[3132]

Clinical features

The symptoms can vary from intermittent urticaria to severe anaphylaxis. Anaphylaxis can be more severe than the ones caused by allergies to other foods.[121333] The symptoms develop when wheat intake occurs in association with exercise or other cofactors, with a time interval of less than 4 hours, but longer time intervals have also been reported.[1234] Exercise is the most reported cofactor, followed by NSAIDs and alcohol. Again, the coexistence of multiple cofactors may lower the reaction threshold. This may explain why allergic symptoms can be infrequent in many, despite regular wheat intake, even alongside cofactors.[33353637]

Diagnosis

In all patients presenting with anaphylaxis in the acute setting, serum tryptase should be determined immediately, after 4 hours, and again after 24 hours. In non-acute settings, baseline serum tryptase must be estimated to exclude clonal mastocytosis.[38] Like other food allergies, the first essential step is to obtain an accurate history. A suspicion of WDEIA should not be invalidated by a reported tolerance to wheat. It is imperative to actively enquire about exposure to cofactors. WDEIA should be considered in cases of presumed idiopathic anaphylaxis, allergy to NSAIDs, or intermittent food-induced urticaria.[33]

Whole-wheat extract, native wheat flour, and wheat gluten (if available) can be used for skin prick tests. Wheat gluten has the highest diagnostic value. In possible HWP-WDEIA, tests should be done with HWP-containing products (those used by the patient, if available) or with pure HWP.[1739]

Specific IgE to whole-wheat extract may not be positive. Specific IgE to omega-5-gliadins is the most important test. Specific IgE to Tri a 14 and gliadins (where available), and specific IgE to grass pollen when grass pollen-related WDEIA is suspected, should also be tested.[1516]

Alpha-Gal Syndrome

Alpha-gal syndrome (AGS) presents with hypersensitivity responses to substances in which galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose (alpha-gal) is a component. This includes food products derived from animals like cows, goats, sheep, and pigs, including meat, dairy products, and innards, and a range of pharmaceutical products. Symptoms range from urticaria to potentially lethal anaphylaxis.[4041]

Pathogenesis

AGS differs from most other food allergies in that the symptoms develop hours after consumption of the triggering foods. In conventional food allergies, IgE is targeted against various protein antigens, while in AGS, IgE is directed against the carbohydrate galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose.[4243] Sensitization is usually triggered by certain ectoparasites, the most common form of exposure being tick bites.[4445]

Cetuximab is an epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitor licensed for the treatment of advanced colorectal carcinoma. Shortly after approval in 2005, reactions to cetuximab were observed with a higher frequency in various parts of the United States.[46] Patients with such reactions were found to have pre-existing IgE antibodies to the glycosylated part of the Fab region of the cetuximab heavy chain; the relevant oligosaccharide was identified to be alpha-gal.[424748] IgE antibodies against alpha-gal were detected in some patients with late reactions 3 to 6 hours after consuming red meat (beef, lamb, pork); however, white meat like fish, chicken, and turkey was tolerated.[43]

Screening of serum samples revealed a regional distribution pattern of positive samples in high-prevalence areas of Rocky Mountain spotted fever. Prospective studies in a few patients after being bitten by ticks demonstrated newly developed positive alpha-gal-specific IgE. Some patients who had previously not reacted to red meat developed symptoms of allergic hypersensitivity after tick bites, primarily to the Amblyomma and Ixodes species.[424345] It was thus established that tick bites induce sensitization to alpha-gal. Glycolipids containing alpha-gal are present in tick saliva. Invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells recognize glycolipids and produce interleukin (IL)-4. Glycoproteins are processed by antigen-presenting cells (including B cells), and the peptides are presented to naïve T lymphocytes. When IL-4 is present, T cells induce the class switching of B cells, thus causing the production of alpha-gal-specific IgE. On ingesting red meat, the alpha-gal bound to glycolipids is converted to lipid micelles, which are degraded by pancreatic lipase, and finally reach circulation in around 4 hours. On encountering basophils or mast cells with a coating of alpha-gal-specific IgE, there occurs cross-linking of IgEs that leads to a series of events, resulting in an allergic reaction.[49]

Medical products other than cetuximab that contain alpha-gal can cause reactions. These include heparins, gelatin capsules, some vaccines (e.g., zostavax), bioprosthetic vascular grafts, valves, patch materials, and red algae.[505152]

Epidemiology

AGS has been reported from every continent, except Antarctica, most frequently from Australia and the southeastern and mid-west USA.[5354] The predominant risk factors include male gender, blood groups other than B, systemic mastocytosis, bioprosthetic heart valves, and allergy to gelatin.[53] Approximately two-thirds of reported cases are in males, and predominantly in adults.[54]

Clinical features

The onset of symptoms of allergic reactions is delayed by a minimum of 2–4 hours after consumption of the culprit meat, as alpha-gal takes time to reach circulation.[4149] Reactions after exposure to parenteral drugs and other medical products with alpha-gal are usually rapid in onset.[42]

A 2020 systematic review of 236 cases in 79 studies reported that the most common symptoms are cutaneous (74.6%), primarily urticaria (71.2%), erythema, pruritus, and angioedema. Anaphylaxis was reported in 51.7%, indicating a high risk. Gastrointestinal symptoms (abdominal pain, bloating, gastroesophageal reflux, vomiting, diarrhea) were reported in 30.1% of cases, 17.4% reported respiratory features (cough, dyspnea, tightness of chest, wheeze, respiratory distress, hypoxia), and 15.3% reported other features like oropharyngeal (mouth and throat itching, swelling, tightness) and cardiovascular (syncope and presyncope) symptoms.[415455]

Diagnosis

AGS is diagnosed if there is a plausible history and a positive specific IgE to alpha-gal.[56] Tick bites can often cause an elevation of total IgE level as well. Clinically relevant allergy is more likely when specific IgE to alpha-gal is ≥2 IU/mL or >2% of the total IgE. However, specific IgE is not always positive in cases with a likely history. Delayed allergic reactions following red meat consumption in persons with a history of tick bites are highly compatible with a diagnosis of AGS. There is alleviation of symptomatic episodes after stopping the consumption of mammalian meat, which contributes to the diagnosis.[4345]

Skin prick testing with commercially available extracts of mammalian meat has a low sensitivity for the diagnosis of AGS and may not be positive.[43] Although more reliable, the potential risk of anaphylaxis negates the utility of intradermal tests using commercial preparations of mammalian meat extracts.[45] Prick-to-prick tests can be done with cooked mammalian meats, and skin prick and/or intradermal test using gelatin or cetuximab (under extreme caution and after informed consent), and these tests usually yield positive results in AGS.[57]

Pork-Cat Syndrome

Pork-cat syndrome is a rare entity, with allergic symptoms upon eating pork in individuals sensitized to cat epithelium.[58]

Pathogenesis

Sequence homology is shared between mammalian serum albumins, with over 80% identity. The allergen responsible for causing pork-cat syndrome was initially reported to be Sus s1, a pork serum albumin (SA), that cross-reacts with Fel d2, a cat SA.[5960] The primary sensitization is to Fel d2 due to exposure to cats; allergic reactions occur later after eating pork meat containing Sus s1.[61] Other animals, like dogs and hamsters, have also been reported to cause initial sensitization due to shared sequence homology. Also, other meats, like beef and horse meat, can cause reactions.[62]

Epidemiology

Literature on epidemiology is scarce. Among patients with cat allergy, 3-10% are senzitized to porcine albumin, and among them, approximately one in three has developed allergy symptoms upon ingestion of pork. It is considered quite rare in children, as cat allergy is rare in that age group. Both cat allergy and pork-cat syndrome usually present in adolescence or early adulthood.[63]

Clinical features

The sensitivity to pork appears to develop over time after exposure to cats.[6465] Reactions do not develop with every instance of eating pork. Fresh meat, inadequately cooked meat, and dried/smoked/barbecued meat are more likely to induce reactions, compared to well-cooked meat, as the serum albumin is thermolabile.[6065]

In contrast to AGS, symptoms of pork-cat syndrome begin soon after consumption of the meat, within a maximum period of 30–45 minutes. The initial presentation might be oral pruritus during the meal. Gastrointestinal symptoms, like abdominal cramping, may be prominent. Other reported symptoms include urticaria, angioedema, and respiratory distress. Fatal anaphylaxis has also been reported.[6667]

The levels of specific IgE to cat SA may gradually decline over time. Sensitization will persist if exposure to cats continues. Some patients safely consume pork again later in life.[66]

Diagnosis

Specific IgE tests for pork and cat (or other animal) dander are positive. Skin prick tests to cat hair allergen extract, and prick-to-prick tests with various preparations of pork, also have utility. The cross-reactivity to bovine SA is variable, and so are tests for beef allergy. Testing for alpha-gal may also be performed.[656668]

Management

The key aspect in the management of any food allergy is accurate recognition and dietary elimination of the allergenic food. Patients must be provided with appropriate information regarding specific foods to avoid in each type of allergy.

Diagnostic tests are not 100% sensitive, and despite extensive investigations, some cases of FIA may be labeled idiopathic.[1443] In cases of presumed idiopathic anaphylaxis, the above conditions should always be differentials. A detailed history is crucial in identifying the trigger.

In WDEIA, the most reliable measure for prevention is following a gluten-free diet. When the threshold for reactions is low, stringent avoidance of wheat and other cross-reacting cereals must be advised. In less severe cases, a discussion of situational avoidance may be of value. Such patients are then eat smaller amounts of cereals with gluten, as long as there is a time gap of at least 4–6 hours maintained between wheat consumption and cofactors.[14]

Newly diagnosed AGS patients should avoid mammalian meat, including, but not limited to, beef, lamb, pork, venison, and their internal organs. Offal (pork or beef kidney) can cause more severe reactions owing to a higher alpha-gal content. Fatty cuts of meat have a greater chance of causing symptoms. Cooking does not denature the alpha-gal to a significant extent. However, the severity of reactions can be reduced, possibly by reducing the amount of fat.[697071] Further tick bites can sustain or increase alpha-gal-specific IgE. Avoiding tick bites would result in a fall in specific IgE over time.[1131] Sensitivity thus can wean over time, and some patients have successfully reintroduced meat.[32] It has not been established whether continuing to consume mammalian meat would affect the titers of specific IgE to alpha-gal.[33] In pork-cat syndrome, avoiding both pork and cats would help to reduce the specific IgE over time.[72]

Labeling of food and anaphylaxis prevention: Natasha and Sabrina’s laws

To avoid anaphylactic reactions, patients must be in a position to correctly identify foods containing allergenic ingredients. To facilitate this, 66 countries mandated by legislation that allergenic ingredients in prepacked food items should be declared.[74] Eight major food allergens (aside from sulfites) should mandatorily be declared in most places, which are eggs, dairy, tree nuts, peanuts, fish, soy, wheat, and shellfish. Major allergens recognized and labeled vary between countries. Cereals with gluten are used as substitutes for wheat in some countries, such as Thailand, India, the Philippines, and Hong Kong.[75]

Natasha’s Law is a UK law that requires all prepackaged foods to have a full list of ingredients and allergens on their labels. It was named after Natasha Ednan-Laperouse, who, in 2016, while flying from Heathrow airport, died from an allergic reaction to a prepackaged baguette bought from the airport. Natasha was known to be allergic to sesame seeds, but tragically, there was no allergen information on the label of the baguette. After her death, Natasha’s family campaigned for the law to be introduced, and it came into effect in October 2021.[7677]

In 2003, Sabrina Shannon, a high school student who had been advocating for adolescents with food allergies, suffered a fatal anaphylaxis. Sabrina’s law, passed in 2005, necessitates that an anaphylaxis policy be implemented by all public schools in Ontario, and a plan must be in place for each student at risk of anaphylaxis.[78]

True idiopathic anaphylaxis

When anaphylaxis happens without identifiable triggers, it is deemed idiopathic anaphylaxis. However, many cases labeled as idiopathic may be a result of insufficient workup. Unusual triggers and unrecognized cofactors can make it difficult to identify the trigger. True idiopathic anaphylaxis includes cases in which extensive and appropriate investigations have failed to reveal a cause. The exact pathogenesis of idiopathic anaphylaxis is poorly described. Certain conditions can predispose to idiopathic anaphylaxis, including mast cell activation syndrome, autoimmunity, hormonal fluctuations, and genetic conditions like hereditary alpha-tryptasemia. In the absence of identifiable triggers, evaluation should be directed toward these conditions as well.[7980]

Future directions

Although not widely available, the basophil activation test has utility in the diagnosis of WDEIA, with the ability to identify specific allergen components causing sensitization.[81] Problems with standardization, availability of fresh basophils, and accurate interpretation remain ongoing challenges.

Sublingual immunotherapy with gluten has been described in a small case series and has been shown to increase reaction thresholds in WDEIA. A fall in specific IgE over time may be used to assess the residual level of sensitization.[82] Omalizumab may be another option for management.[83] Oral immunotherapy for alpha-gal syndrome has been recently described and appears promising.[84]

Conclusion

Anaphylaxis is potentially fatal, and prevention is ideal. Food-induced anaphylaxis is not uncommon. However, unusual causes of food-induced anaphylaxis like the ones described above might pose diagnostic difficulty and risk of serious reactions. Avoidance of suspected allergens is the best course of management, but patient education and diligence when reading food labels are equally important in the prevention of food-induced anaphylaxis.
