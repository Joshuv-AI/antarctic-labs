Fix Initial Codes and Iteratively Refine Textual Directions Toward Safe Multi-Turn Code Correction
Yuto Tanaka & Issei Sato

Abstract
Recent work on large language models (LLMs) has emphasized the importance of scaling inference compute. From this perspective, the state-of-the-art method Scattered Forest Search ( SFS ) has been proposed, employing Monte Carlo Tree Search with carefully crafted initial seeds and textual optimization for multi-turn code correction. However, its complexity makes it unclear what factors contribute to improvements in inference performance. To address this problem, we analyze SFS and propose a simpler method, Iterative Refinement of Textual Directions (IRTD), which fixes initial codes and iteratively refines textual directions. Because of the simplicity of IRTD, we theoretically establish the safety of IRTD using Oracle-Guided Inductive Synthesis (OGIS). Experiments on several code generation benchmarks suggest that IRTD achieves inference performance comparable to state-of-the-art methods. These results indicate that, even without complex search structures, refining initial codes with high-quality textual directions alone can effectively improve inference performance.



1
Introduction

Recent work on large language models (LLMs) has increasingly focused on scaling inference-time compute to improve inference performance
(
Brown et al. 2024
;
Snell et al. 2024
)
. In such approaches, the goal is to obtain higher-quality outputs by expanding the computational process at inference time, without modifying the model’s parameters or the training data. Thus, this paradigm is collectively referred to as inference scaling. Several representative approaches have been proposed under this paradigm, including best-of-N (BoN) sampling
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
;
Lightman et al. 2023
)
, linear self-refinement methods
(
Madaan et al. 2023
;
Shinn et al. 2023
)
, and tree-search-based methods that integrate multiple reasoning trajectories
(
Tian et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
.

A representative task in the study of inference scaling is code generation. In this context, multi-turn code correction, in which an initial program is refined through multiple rounds of feedback, has been widely studied as a practical approach to improving output quality. Previous methods such as BoN and iterative refinement have been shown to improve performance
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. One reason for this effectiveness is that providing enough trials increases the probability that an LLM will sample a correct answer
(
Brown et al. 2024
;
Snell et al. 2024
)
. However,
Light et al. 2025
have shown that existing methods often generate highly similar outputs, resulting in insufficient exploration of the solution space.

Light et al. 2025
have proposed
Scattered Forest Search
(
SFS
) that builds on Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) and incorporates techniques such as careful seed initialization and textual optimization. They have empirically reported that
SFS
achieves state-of-the-art performance in multi-turn code correction
(
Light et al. 2025
)
. However, since
SFS
tightly integrates multiple components, it is unclear what factors drive improvements in inference performance. This ambiguity hinders the development of simpler and more efficient approaches that could retain the benefits of inference scaling without relying on costly or complex search mechanisms.

To address the problem, we analyze
SFS
to identify the essential factors that contribute to performance improvements. In these analyses, we reveal that breadth-wise exploration toward high-quality textual directions plays a crucial role in the self-refinement process. From this finding, we propose
Iterative Refinement of Textual Directions
that fixes initial codes and iteratively refines textual directions. IRTD is a simpler method than
SFS
for multi-turn code correction and is capable of achieving comparable inference performance. Moreover, because of its simplicity, we theoretically establish the safety of IRTD using the framework of Oracle-Guided Inductive Synthesis (OGIS)
(
Jha & Seshia 2017a
;
Jha & Seshia 2017b
)
. This theoretical analysis ensures that IRTD does not lose the true solution and can reach the correct answer.

In summary, our main contributions include:

•

We analyze
SFS
and reveal that breadth-wise exploration is more important than depth-wise exploration for multi-turn code correction.

•

We propose a simpler method, IRTD, which iteratively revises textual directions while fixing initial codes as the target codes.

•

We theoretically analyze IRTD based on the framework of OGIS and show the safety of IRTD as a multi-turn code correction method. This guarantees that IRTD does not lose the true solution in the refinement process.

•

We empirically evaluate IRTD on several code generation benchmarks and confirm that IRTD achieves performance comparable to
SFS
.

2
Related work

Recent studies have increasingly focused on improving model performance by allocating more computation at inference time
(
Brown et al. 2024
;
Snell et al. 2024
)
. One of the simplest and most common methods is best-of-N (BoN) sampling
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
;
Lightman et al. 2023
)
. In this approach, multiple samples are generated from the same prompt to ensure diversity among responses and improve output quality. Prior work has also proposed self-correction methods in which an initial response is generated and then iteratively verified and revised
(
Madaan et al. 2023
;
Shinn et al. 2023
)
. These methods improve the model’s responses by incorporating internal or external feedback as additional information.
Yang et al. 2025
has proposed a probabilistic theory of self-correction to explain the performance improvements observed in these methods.

Furthermore, incorporating tree search methods such as DFS, BFS, and MCTS has been shown to be effective for inference scaling. Prior work has applied tree search to multi-step tasks, including selecting the next reasoning step in reasoning processes
(
Yao et al. 2023
;
Besta et al. 2024
;
Tian et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
and decoding the next token during generation
(
Feng et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. Tree search has also been applied to optimization problems, where it functions as a black-box method for exploring a solution space
(
Light et al. 2025
)
.

Overall, we build upon previous research
(
Light et al. 2025
)
and aim to identify the factors that truly contribute to performance improvements in increasingly complex inference scaling methods. Motivated by these observations, our study proposes a simpler method and demonstrates that performance comparable to state-of-the-art methods can be achieved without relying on complex search strategies.

3
Understanding
Scattered Forest Search
(
SFS
)

This section provides an overview of
SFS
, which is a state-of-the-art method for multi-turn code correction, and presents analyses of
SFS
.

3.1
Problem formulation

Following
Light et al. 2025
, we formulate code generation as a black-box optimization problem
(
Golovin et al. 2017
)
. A code generation task is represented as
x=\langle p,H\rangle
, where
p
denotes a prompt written in natural language or code, and
H
represents a set of hidden test cases used for evaluation. The objective is to provide an LLM with a prompt
p
and generate a code
c\sim\mathrm{LLM}(p)
that passes all hidden tests in
H
. Although the model cannot access the hidden tests, it can generate a set of validation tests
V
. Using these validation tests
V
and the program execution environment, the model obtains feedback on its generated code
c
. This feedback is then used to determine whether the code should be refined. In the evaluation phase, generated codes are evaluated against a hidden test set
H
and are deemed correct only when all tests are passed. For each task, an LLM is allowed to generate codes up to
k
times, and a task is considered solved if at least one of them is correct. Following these rules, for a task set
X
, the evaluation metric Pass@k Rate
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
is calculated as the proportion of tasks
x\in X
that are solved with at most
k
generated codes. An overview of this problem formulation is illustrated in Appendix
A
.

3.2
Scattered Forest Search
(
SFS
)

Several inference-time methods have been explored in prior work to improve LLM performance on code generation tasks. However,
Light et al. 2025
have shown that these methods often generate highly similar candidate codes, resulting in insufficient exploration of the solution space. To address this challenge, they have also proposed
Scattered Forest Search
(
SFS
)
(
Light et al. 2025
)
.
SFS
is a MCTS-based method that combines careful seed initialization with textual optimization and consists of the following three components.

•

Scattering
: Dynamically varies input prompts with different textual directions when sampling revised codes.

•

Foresting
: Generates multiple initial codes that act as starting points for MCTS.

•

Scouting
: Provides feedback on textual directions employed during the refinement process and distills general insights that can be shared across search branches.

Their study has confirmed that
SFS
outperforms existing methods in terms of inference scaling performance
(
Light et al. 2025
)
. Details of
SFS
are provided in Appendix
B
.

Iterative Refinement of Textual Directions
(IRTD)

From the findings in Section
3.3
, we hypothesize that if textual directions are of high quality,
refining initial codes may become effective in multi-turn code correction. Accordingly, we propose
Iterative Refinement of Textual Directions
(IRTD), which refines initial codes through iterative feedback on textual directions.

•

Section
4.1
presents the framework of IRTD, which becomes simpler than
SFS
.

•

Section
4.2
theoretically analyzes IRTD based on the framework of Oracle-Guided Inductive Synthesis (OGIS)
(
Jha & Seshia 2017a
;
Jha & Seshia 2017b
)
.

4.1
Proposed framework

Given an input prompt, IRTD first generates one or multiple initial codes. It then generates one or more textual directions for the selected initial code and uses one of them to refine the code. After this refinement step, it evaluates the refined code on the validation tests and provides feedback on the used textual direction, which is stored as shared information. This shared information is then used as an additional context for an LLM when generating textual directions in subsequent refinement steps. IRTD repeats this refinement process iteratively until a desired condition is met. In contrast to existing self-refinement methods, IRTD
fixes the initial codes as the refinement targets
throughout the process. Moreover, by
giving feedback on textual directions
, IRTD enhances the probability of successful refinements. An overview of IRTD is illustrated in Appendix
D
. Next, we describe IRTD in detail under the problem formulation introduced in Section
3.1
.

Initial Generation
: Given an input prompt
p
, IRTD generates one or multiple initial codes
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
.

c_{1}\sim\mathrm{LLM}(p),\ldots,~c_{n}\sim\mathrm{LLM}(p).

(1)

Refinement Process
: First, based on the selected initial code
c_{s}
and the shared information
\mathcal{I}
, IRTD generates one or more textual directions
d_{1},\ldots,d_{m}
. Next, IRTD refines
c_{s}
using one of them
d_{s}
.

d_{1},\ldots,d_{m}\sim\mathrm{LLM}(p,c_{s},\mathcal{I}),\quad c_{r}\sim\mathrm{LLM}(p,c_{s},d_{s}).

(2)

Feedback for Textual Directions
: After the refinement process, IRTD evaluates the revised code
c_{r}
on the validation tests and updates the shared information based on the selected code
c_{s}
, the textual direction used
d_{s}
, and the revised code
c_{r}
.

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{LLM}(p,c_{s},\mathcal{I},d_{s},c_{r}).

(3)

4.2
Theoretical analysis using Oracle-Guided Inductive Synthesis (OGIS)

We show the safety of IRTD using the framework of OGIS
(
Jha & Seshia 2017a
;
Jha & Seshia 2017b
)
. In OGIS, program safety is defined by the absence of counterexamples found by an oracle (a formal verifier). Building on this, the following three properties regarding the
version space
are required to guarantee that an inductive algorithm interacting with the oracle reliably converges to a safe program.

•

The
version space
shrinks monotonically with each step by eliminating incorrect hypotheses that contradict the counterexamples provided by the oracle.

•

As a premise for the search, the target hypothesis is assumed to exist within the initial hypothesis space.

•

Since the target hypothesis does not contradict any counterexample, it is never eliminated from the
version space
.

By satisfying these properties, it is shown that the algorithm can reliably synthesize a safe program in a finite number of steps. Therefore, it is important to theoretically demonstrate the safety of the method in multi-turn code correction.

4.2.1
Definition

For a code generation task
x
, let
D
be a set of textual directions,
C
be a set of codes, and
E
be a set of counterexamples. Following Section
3.1
, each element
e\in E
represents a failed test case on the validation tests
V
. We define
\operatorname{Pass}(c,d)\in\{0,1\}
to indicate whether the result of applying a textual direction
d\in D
to a code
c\in C
with an LLM satisfies the specification of task
x
. We also define a set of textual directions that succeed for a code
c
as

\displaystyle D_{c}^{\star}:=\{d\in D\mid\operatorname{Pass}(c,d)=1\}.

(4)

A single refinement step:
One or more textual directions are generated based on the previous observations or its summary (i.e., shared information
\mathcal{I}
in IRTD). Then, one of these directions is applied to a code
c
, and some observation
e
is obtained. Accordingly, for a code
c
and an observation
e
, we define
\operatorname{Cons}(c,d,e)\in\{0,1\}
to indicate whether the result of applying a textual direction
d
to a code
c
with an LLM passes a failed text case
e
. We then define a set of textual directions that satisfy an observation
e
as

\displaystyle D_{c}(e):=\{d\in D\mid\operatorname{Cons}(c,d,e)=1\}\subseteq D.

(5)

Over a single refinement step, a constraint set
D_{c}(e)
is derived from an observation
e
, thereby narrowing the space of possible textual directions.

Multi-turn correction:
We consider a history of a code
c_{t}
and an observation
e_{t}
as
H_{t}:=\left(\left(c_{1},e_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(c_{t},e_{t}\right)\right)
(i.e., shared information
\mathcal{I}
in IRTD). We then define a set of textual directions that satisfy all observations so far as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}D_{c_{i}}\left(e_{i}\right)\subseteq D.

(6)

Here, we refer to this as
version space
. The concept of a
version space
originates from the foundational theory of machine learning (specifically, concept learning) proposed by
Mitchell 1977
. The term
version
does not mean a chronological update or revision, such as
Version 1.0
in program codes. Rather, it is based on the idea of treating hypotheses that do not contradict observations as various possible versions of the correct one.

4.2.2
Safety of IRTD

We define a set of textual directions that succeed for at least one code as

\displaystyle D^{\star}:=\bigcup_{c\in C}D_{c}^{\star}=\{d\in D\mid\exists c\in C,\ \operatorname{Pass}(c,d)=1\}\subseteq D,

(7)

and define a set of codes for which a textual direction
d
acts correctly as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(d):=\{c\in C\mid\operatorname{Pass}(c,d)=1\}=\{c\in C\mid d\in D_{c}^{\star}\}.

(8)

Furthermore, we denote the joint distribution under which an LLM generates
m(\geq 1)
initial codes
\widetilde{C}_{\mathrm{init}}:=\{c^{(1)},\ldots,c^{(m)}\}
as
(c^{(1)},\ldots,c^{(m)})\sim\mathrm{LLM}
. For each
c\in\widetilde{C}_{\mathrm{init}}
, we define a set of observations that can actually arise as
E_{c}^{\mathrm{obs}}\subseteq E
. Since LLM outputs often concentrate in local regions that share common algorithmic structures and error patterns, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

There exist
d^{\dagger}\in D^{\star}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
such that, for each
j=1,\ldots,m
,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathrm{LLM}}\!\left[c^{(j)}\in\mathcal{R}(d^{\dagger})\right]\geq 1-\delta.

(9)

Furthermore, assume that the constraint set constructed from observations is locally sound, i.e.,

\displaystyle c\in\mathcal{R}(d^{\dagger}),\quad e\in E_{c}^{\mathrm{obs}}\;\Longrightarrow\;d^{\dagger}\in D_{c}(e).

(10)

Then, for the event that
d^{\dagger}
survives across all initial codes, i.e.,

\displaystyle G:=\left\{\forall j=1,\ldots,m,\;\forall e\in E_{c^{(j)}}^{\mathrm{obs}}:\quad d^{\dagger}\in D_{c^{(j)}}(e)\right\},

(11)

the following theorem holds.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumption
1
,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathrm{LLM}}[G]\geq 1-m\delta

(12)

holds. Furthermore, if
c^{(1)},\ldots,c^{(m)}
are generated independently,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathrm{LLM}}[G]\geq(1-\delta)^{m}

(13)

holds.

The proof of Theorem
2
is provided in Appendix
E.2
. This theorem probabilistically formalizes the existence of textual directions that survive across a set of initial codes, which can be interpreted as a probabilistic assumption that initial outputs concentrate within a certain local refinement basin. In particular, when
m
is small and
\delta
is sufficiently small,
G
is a high-probability event.

In the following, we fix a realization
\omega\in G
of the probability space and write
C_{\mathrm{init}}:=\widetilde{C}_{\mathrm{init}}(\omega)
. Under this realization,
d^{\dagger}\in D^{\star}
appearing in Assumption
1
satisfies

\displaystyle\forall c\in C_{\mathrm{init}},\forall e\in E_{c}^{\mathrm{obs}}:\quad d^{\dagger}\in D_{c}(e).

(14)

This means that, when restricted to a set of initial codes and the range of observations that can actually arise from them, at least one textual direction is never eliminated. We define a set of truly valid textual directions that survive under a local observation channel as

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{stab}}^{\star}:=\{d\in D^{\star}\mid\forall c\in C_{\mathrm{init}},\forall e\in E_{c}^{\mathrm{obs}}:\quad d\in D_{c}(e)\}\subseteq D^{\star}.

(15)

Given this condition,
D_{\mathrm{stab}}^{\star}\neq\varnothing
follows from
d^{\dagger}\in D_{\mathrm{stab}}^{\star}
, and the following theorem holds.

Theorem 3
.

Assume that
D_{\mathrm{stab}}^{\star}\neq\varnothing
. For any realized history

\displaystyle(c_{1},e_{1}),\ldots,(c_{t+1},e_{t+1}),\qquad c_{i}\in C_{\mathrm{init}},e_{i}\in E_{c_{i}}^{\mathrm{obs}},

(16)

the version space satisfies the following properties.

1.

\mathcal{V}_{t+1}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{t}
(monotonic shrinking of version space).

2.

D_{\mathrm{stab}}^{\star}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{t}
(version space retains truly correct textual directions).

3.

\mathcal{V}_{t}\neq\varnothing
(version space is not empty).

The proof of Theorem
3
is provided in Appendix
E.3
. This theorem guarantees the safety of IRTD under the probabilistic assumption. Since a single initial code allows for a deterministic assumption, the corresponding proof is provided in Appendix
E.4
.

Overall, IRTD is provably safe for multi-turn code correction. In contrast, as show in
E.5
, existing linear self-refinement methods are not necessarily safe. These analyses indicate that IRTD has an advantage over existing methods from the perspective of safety. Here, we discuss the safety of IRTD and do not claim that IRTD achieves better performance than prior approaches.

4.2.3
Effect of the number of initial codes on discriminative power

We use
C_{\mathrm{init}}
and
D_{\mathrm{stab}}^{\star}
corresponding to the realization
\omega\in G
. From the previous section, we have
D_{\mathrm{stab}}^{\star}\neq\varnothing
and
D_{\mathrm{stab}}^{\star}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{t}
for any realized history. Next, for any nonempty subset of initial codes
\varnothing\neq C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime}\subseteq C_{\mathrm{init}}
,
we define

\displaystyle U(C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime}):=\bigcap_{c\in C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime}}\bigcap_{e\in E_{c}^{\mathrm{obs}}}D_{c}(e)\subseteq D.

(17)

This is a set of textual directions that cannot be eliminated by any realized observation from
C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime}
. Therefore, a smaller
U(C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime})
indicates that the subset
C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime}
has higher discriminative power. Since the final target code is selected not from
C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime}
but from
C_{\mathrm{init}}
, we define a set of candidate pairs that can remain after exhausting all observations from
C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle Z_{\infty}(C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime}):=C_{\mathrm{init}}\times U(C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime}).

(18)

Here,
(c,d)\in Z_{\infty}(C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime})
represents a pair of an initial code
c
and a textual direction
d
that is consistent with all feasible observations from
C_{\mathrm{init}}^{\prime}
. Under this setting, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 4
.

For any nonempty subset of initial codes,

\displaystyle\varnothing\neq B_{1}\subseteq B_{2}\subseteq C_{\mathrm{init}},

(19)

the following holds.

1.

U(B_{2})\subseteq U(B_{1})
(monotonic shrinking).

2.

D_{\mathrm{stab}}^{\star}\subseteq U(B_{2})
(truly correct textual directions are not eliminated).

3.

U(B_{2})\neq\varnothing
and
\varnothing\neq Z_{\infty}(B_{2})\subseteq Z_{\infty}(B_{1})
(not empty).

The proof of Theorem
4
is provided in Appendix
E.6
. This theorem indicates that as the subset of initial codes is expanded, the number of feasible observations increases, and
U(B)
shrinks monotonically while containing
D_{\mathrm{stab}}^{\star}
. Therefore, increasing initial codes can be understood as an operation that increases discriminative power without compromising safety. In Appendix
E.7
, we describe the strict condition under which an increase in discriminative power occurs. However, since the probability of the event in which
d^{\dagger}
survives across all initial codes, i.e.,

\displaystyle G=\left\{\forall j=1,\ldots,m,\;\forall e\in E_{c^{(j)}}^{\mathrm{obs}}:\quad d^{\dagger}\in D_{c^{(j)}}(e)\right\},

(20)

decreases with increasing
m
, the soundness assumption becomes weaker. In other words, increasing the number of initial codes improves discriminative power, but also raises the likelihood that the soundness assumption no longer holds, leading to an inherent trade-off.

Conclusion

We have analyzed the state-of-the-art method
SFS
and found that breadth-wise exploration toward high-quality textual directions plays an important role in the self-refinement process. From this analysis, we propose IRTD as a simpler method for multi-turn code correction. The simplicity of IRTD enables theoretical analysis, allowing us to provide guarantees on the safety of the method. Experimental results demonstrate that IRTD achieves competitive performance compared to
SFS
without requiring complex search structures. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying improved inference performance in multi-turn code correction and suggest promising directions for future research.

Appendix B
Details of
Scattered Forest Search
(
SFS
)

To enable diverse exploration of the solution space in code generation,
Light et al. 2025
have proposed
Scattered Forest Search
(
SFS
).
SFS
builds on MCTS and incorporates techniques such as careful seed initialization and textual optimization. This method encourages broader exploration of the solution space by combining LLM-driven textual directions, the introduction of multiple initial codes, and the utilization of feedback and past search experience. Specifically,
SFS
consists of three main components:
Scattering
,
Foresting
, and
Scouting
, as illustrated in Figure
4
.

Scattering
: This component dynamically varies input prompts with different textual directions when sampling revised codes. More concretely, given a code selected by MCTS along with its associated feedback, an LLM generates multiple textual directions. Then, one of these textual directions is selected and incorporated into the prompt for generating the revised code.

Foresting
: This component generates multiple initial codes that act as starting points for MCTS. In this process, initial codes are generated using either the same base prompt or diversified prompts obtained by randomly appending instruction statements.

Scouting
: This component provides feedback on the textual directions used during the refinement process and distills general insights that can be shared across search branches. These insights are incorporated into the prompt as auxiliary information when an LLM generates reflections and textual directions. This enables the promotion of effective textual directions and the suppression of unproductive ones.

C.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Let
R
be the root and
S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}
be the children of the root at depth 1. For the
t
-th node selection, define the following.

•

D_{t}
: the depth of the node returned by the
t
-th selection.

•

M_{t}(v)
: the number of unexpanded actions of node
v
at time
t
.

•

B_{t}(v)
: the event that node
v
has at least one better child at time
t
.

•

J_{t}
: the index of the child selected by UCT at the stage of choosing a depth-1 child from the root.

•

G_{t}:=\left\{j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\mid M_{t}\left(S_{j}\right)=0\land B_{t}\left(S_{j}\right)\right\}
.

Furthermore, let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
denote the information that has already been determined as the state of the search tree up to the moment just before the root selects a depth-1 child in the
t
-th selection.

For example,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
includes the current evaluation value of the node under consideration. In
SFS
, each node is associated with a quality score, and a better child is defined as a child whose score is higher than that of its parent. Therefore, the score represents how promising the node is currently considered to be. Moreover,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
includes the number of times the node under consideration has been selected so far. That is,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
includes the current search tree, the randomness realized up to that point, and the outputs of an LLM. Note that it does not include any randomness used in the final selection determining
J_{t}
. Under this condition, the following holds.

From
M_{t}(R)=0
and
B_{t}(R)
, a selection does not stop at the root and descends to the depth-1 node
S_{J_{t}}
chosen at the root. Furthermore, a selection proceeds to depth 2 or deeper only if the node
S_{J_{t}}
satisfies

\displaystyle M_{t}\left(S_{J_{t}}\right)=0\quad\land\quad B_{t}\left(S_{J_{t}}\right).

(21)

By the definition of
G_{t}
, this is equivalent to
J_{t}\in G_{t}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(D_{t}\geq 2\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)=\Pr\left(J_{t}\in G_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)=\sum_{j\in G_{t}}\Pr\left(J_{t}=j\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right).

(22)

Here, if there exists some
\varepsilon_{t}\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in G_{t}}Pr\left(J_{t}=j\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\leq\varepsilon_{t},

(23)

then we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(D_{t}\geq 2\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\leq\varepsilon_{t}.

(24)

Hence,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(D_{t}=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)=1-\Pr\left(D_{t}\geq 2\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon_{t}.

(25)

From the above, unless there are few depth-1 nodes in
G_{t}
or the root-level UCT strongly concentrates on them, a selection stops at depth 1 with high probability, and the probability of reaching depth 2 is low.
∎

Appendix E

E.1
Conceptual origin of
version space

The notion of
version space
originates as a fundamental concept in learning theory, where it denotes the set of hypotheses that are consistent with the observed data. In learning theory, let
\mathcal{H}
denotes the hypothesis space, and let
D=\left\{\left(x_{1},y_{1}\right),\ldots,\left(x_{n},y_{n}\right)\right\}
denotes the observed (training) data. Then, the
version space
is defined as

\displaystyle VS_{\mathcal{H}}(D):=\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}\mid h\left(x_{i}\right)=y_{i}\text{ for all }i\right\}.

(26)

From the perspective of
version space
, learning can be viewed as a process of progressively eliminating hypotheses based on the training data.

E.4
Safety of IRTD in the case of a single initial code under deterministic assumption

Let
c_{f}
be a fixed initial code and define a set of observations that can actually arise from
c_{f}
as

\displaystyle E_{c_{f}}^{\mathrm{obs}}:=\{e\in E\mid e\text{ is observable from }c_{f}\}\subseteq E.

(40)

Assumption 2
.

\displaystyle\forall e\in E_{c_{f}}^{\mathrm{obs}},\forall d^{\star}\in D_{c_{f}}^{\star}:\quad d^{\star}\in D_{c_{f}}(e).

(41)

This assumption means that any valid textual direction is consistent with every observation arising from
c_{f}
. Under this condition, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 5
.

Under Assumption
2
and
D_{c_{f}}^{\star}\neq\varnothing
, the version space satisfies the following properties for any realized sequence of observations
e_{1},\ldots,e_{t+1}
such that
e_{i}\in E_{c_{f}}^{\mathrm{obs}}
.

1.

\mathcal{V}_{t+1}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{t}
(monotonic shrinking of version space).

2.

D_{c_{f}}^{\star}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{t}
(version space retains truly correct textual directions).

3.

\mathcal{V}_{t}\neq\varnothing
(version space is not empty).

Proof.

1
. By definition,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t+1}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{t+1}D_{c_{f}}\left(e_{i}\right)=\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}D_{c_{f}}\left(e_{i}\right)\right)\cap D_{c_{f}}\left(e_{t+1}\right)=\mathcal{V}_{t}\cap D_{c_{f}}\left(e_{t+1}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{t}.

(42)

2
. Take an arbitrary
d^{\star}\in D_{c_{f}}^{\star}
. By Assumption
2
, for any
i=1,\ldots,t
, it holds that

\displaystyle d^{\star}\in D_{c_{f}}\left(e_{i}\right).

(43)

\displaystyle d^{\star}\in\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}D_{c_{f}}\left(e_{i}\right)=\mathcal{V}_{t}.

(44)

\displaystyle D_{c_{f}}^{\star}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{t}.

(45)

. From

E.5
Linear self-refinement method

Linear self-refinement methods start from an initial code
c_{0}\in C
and renew the target code at each turn as a result of applying a textual direction with an LLM. That is, at time
t
, they refine the current code
c_{t-1}
and obtain an observation
e_{t}\in E
. The constraints induced by this observation can be expressed as

\displaystyle D_{c_{t-1}}\left(e_{t}\right)\subseteq D.

(47)

Here, the key point is that
D_{c_{t}}(e)
can vary depending on the code
c_{t}
even for the same observation
e
. Therefore, the
version space

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{t}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}D_{c_{i-1}}\left(e_{i}\right)

(48)

may become empty, since all observations are derived from different codes. We show this below.

Observation 1
.

Let
D=\{d_{a},d_{b}\}
and
E=\{e\}
. Consider the codes
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
, and define the consistency sets as

\displaystyle D_{c_{0}}(e)=\left\{d_{a}\right\},\quad D_{c_{1}}(e)=\left\{d_{b}\right\}.

(49)

These mean that the interpretation of the same observation
e
varies across different codes. In this case, even if a valid textual direction exists at each time, i.e.,

\displaystyle D_{c_{0}}(e)\neq\varnothing,D_{c_{1}}(e)\neq\varnothing,

(50)

we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{2}=D_{c_{0}}\left(e_{1}\right)\cap D_{c_{1}}\left(e_{2}\right)=\left\{h_{a}\right\}\cap\left\{h_{b}\right\}=\varnothing

(51)

for the counterexample sequence
e_{1}=e,e_{2}=e
. Namely, the version space become empty at time step
2
.

Observation
1
suggests that, from a certain time step onward, linear self-refinement methods may not have any textual direction satisfying the conditions up to that point. In other words, this implies that linear self-refinement methods are not necessarily safe for multi-turn code correction.

To describe the above more generally, we define a
windowed version space
using the most recent
w
observations as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{(w)}:=\bigcap_{i=t-w+1}^{t}D_{c_{i-1}}\left(e_{i}\right)\quad(w\leq t).

(52)

This represents a set of textual directions that satisfy the most recent
w
observations. In linear self-refinement methods, since
c_{t}
changes over time, the constraint set may vary even for the same observation. To capture this, we introduce the following two notions using a measure
\mu
on
D
.

•

The size of the candidate set consistent with a single observation
e_{t}
:

\displaystyle\alpha:=\inf_{t}\mu\left(D_{c_{t-1}}\left(e_{t}\right)\right).

(53)

•

The degree to which the constraint imposed by the previous observation is invalidated:

\displaystyle\delta:=\sup_{t\geq 2}\mu\left(D_{c_{t-2}}\left(e_{t-1}\right)\backslash D_{c_{t-1}}\left(e_{t}\right)\right).

(54)

When
\delta=0
, the interpretation of the constraint remains unchanged. As
\delta
increases, it changes more drastically.

As a simple choice of
\mu
, one can consider a proportion based on cardinality, such as

\displaystyle\mu(D_{c_{t-1}}\left(e_{t}\right))=\frac{|D_{c_{t-1}}\left(e_{t}\right)|}{|D|}.

(55)

In IRTD with a single initial code, we have
\delta=0
ideally. In contrast, under linear self-refinement methods, we generally have
\delta>0
. Under this condition, Theorem
6
holds.

Theorem 6
.

The maximum window size such that

\displaystyle\mu\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{(w)}\right)\geq\varepsilon

(56)

holds for any
t
is given by

\displaystyle w_{\max}:=1+\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{\delta}.

(57)

That is, the safety of linear self-refinement methods requires the finiteness of the observation history window used at each refinement step.

Proof.

First, for any
t
and
w\leq t
, we show that

\displaystyle\mu\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{(w)}\right)\geq\alpha-(w-1)\delta

(58)

holds.
Let
A_{t}:=D_{c_{t-1}}\left(e_{t}\right)
and
I_{1}:=A_{t-w+1}
. By definition,

\displaystyle\mu\left(I_{1}\right)\geq\alpha.

(59)

Next, for
k=2,\ldots,w
, define

\displaystyle I_{k}:=I_{k-1}\cap A_{t-w+k}.

(60)

Then,

\displaystyle\mu\left(I_{k}\right)=\mu\left(I_{k-1}\right)-\mu\left(I_{k-1}\backslash A_{t-w+k}\right).

(61)

Since
I_{k-1}\subseteq A_{t-w+k-1}
,

\displaystyle I_{k-1}\backslash A_{t-w+k}\subseteq A_{t-w+k-1}\backslash A_{t-w+k}

(62)

holds.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mu\left(I_{k-1}\backslash A_{t-w+k}\right)\leq\delta.

(63)

Hence,

\displaystyle\mu\left(I_{k}\right)\geq\mu\left(I_{k-1}\right)-\delta.

(64)

By repeating this process
w-1
times,

\displaystyle\mu\left(I_{w}\right)\geq\alpha-(w-1)\delta.

(65)

Finally, since
I_{w}=\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{(w)}
, for any
t
and
w\leq t
,

\displaystyle\mu\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{(w)}\right)\geq\alpha-(w-1)\delta

(66)

holds.
From the above, in order to guarantee that

\displaystyle\mu\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{(w)}\right)\geq\varepsilon

(67)

holds for any
t
, it is necessary that

\displaystyle\alpha-(w-1)\delta\geq\varepsilon,

(68)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle w\leq 1+\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{\delta}.

(69)

By defining this quantity as

\displaystyle w_{\max}:=1+\frac{\alpha-\varepsilon}{\delta},

(70)

the desired result follows.
∎

Theorem

suggests that, in linear self-refinement methods, as the observation history becomes longer, the
version space
(i.e., the candidate set) tends to shrink rapidly.

Overall, linear self-refinement methods are not necessarily safe in multi-turn code correction.
