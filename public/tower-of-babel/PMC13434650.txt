Atomically precise Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ catalyst orchestrates the synthesis and utilization of hydrogen peroxide
Authors: Xiao Cai, Jun Yao, Shuangshuang Huang, Yan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtomically precise metal clusters that merge structural clarity, functional modularity, and tunable spatial programmability have gained momentum over the past decade. Here, we report the catalysis of an atomically precise Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ (DPPF = 1,1’-bis(diphenylphosphino)ferrocene) cluster in the synthesis and utilization of hydrogen peroxide. The cluster shows a concerto-like synergy of metal core and surface ligand in the entire catalytic process, in which the metal core catalyzes the reaction of formic acid and oxygen to produce H2O2, and subsequently DPPF ligands work with the core to drive the conversion of H2O2 into hydroxyl radicals. Notably, with the computational fluid dynamics simulation assistance, the heterogeneous catalyst from the assembly of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ clusters into mesoporous Al2O3 monolith enables continuous generation and utilization of H2O2 performed in the fixed-bed reactor. This work offers an alternative route for the efficient upgrading of H2O2 synthesis and application.Subject terms: Catalysis, Materials for energy and catalysis

Introduction

The best heterogeneous catalyst requires atomically uniform sites for predictive structure-performance relationship1–3, multi-functional components to streamline the complex catalytic process4–6, and optimized macrostructure to enhance mass transfer efficiency in the diffusion-limited step7–10. Conventionally, concurrent satisfaction of these requirements presents a paradox due to stochastic metal nucleation within traditional impregnation methods, forcing the trade-offs among above three goals11–13. Atomically precise metal clusters protected by organic ligands, integrating structural clarity, functional modularity, and tunable spatial programmability, offer a solution to this paradox14,15. Single crystal X-ray crystallography reveals their atomic-packing total structures (metal core plus ligand shell), in which their discrete metal cores with uniform active sites avoid the heterogeneity of traditional nanoparticles16–18, alongside a ligand shell can serve as secondary sites19–21. Furthermore, the self-assembly behavior of clusters can be precisely regulated through coordination chemistry, enabling programmable spatial organization from the isolated clusters to extended superstructures22–24. These features render them exceptionally distinctive catalysts, completely different from typical heterogeneous catalysts.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), as a versatile green oxidant in energy and environmental applications, faces challenges in its traditional anthraquinone oxidation production method, including high energy consumption, safety hazards, and difficulties in product transportation and storage25–27. Meanwhile, the direct synthesis method using hydrogen and oxygen is limited in the practical applications due to the explosion risk of the gas mixture28. Formic acid (HCOOH), a promising liquid hydrogen carrier, offers an alternative route to on-demand distributed H2O2 synthesis, thereby effectively avoiding the safety hazard and mitigating the decomposition of H2O2 during storage. Recent advances have demonstrated the feasibility of formic acid oxidation for H2O2 synthesis29,30. Li et al. reported that the pair of single-atom Pd achieved the efficient conversion of HCOOH/HCOOK oxidation to H2O2 (Fig. 1a)31. Chen et al. realized the efficient synthesis of H2O2 from HCOOH/HCOOK oxidation using AuPt alloy catalyst and then coupled a single-atom Fe catalyst to utilize the generated H2O2 for the oxidation of coumarin (Fig. 1b)32. Both above systems were confined to batch operations. Integrating generation with utilization of H2O2 in the continuous-flow operations has emerged as an important challenge, and the design of catalysts needs to be systematically addressed. Specifically, the precise regulation of the active structures of catalysts is required to ensure highly efficient synthesis of H2O233–35, and meanwhile the utilization of H2O2 on the catalysts needs to be executed to avoid the diffusion loss36,37. These catalysts might be ideally prepared into a monolith to resist continuous liquid flushing, and the optimized alignment of catalytic site location with the reactant distribution is also necessary to improve the overall reaction efficiency38–40.

Previous works: a single-atom Pd pair sites catalyzed H2O2 synthesis from HCOOH/HCOOK oxidation performed in batch reactors; b AuPt alloy nanoparticles generated H2O2 coupled with single-atom Fe catalysts for H2O2 utilization in batch reactors. This work: c working principle in this Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ cluster for H2O2 production and utilization.

In this work, we report a distinctive performance of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ cluster on the H2O2 production and utilization, which is mainly due to the definite congruity between metal core and ligands. Figure 1c highlights the advancement of the catalyst system. The Au and Pd sites in the core enable the reaction of formic acid and oxygen to obtain H2O2, followed by the Fe and P sites in the ligand work with Au and Pd sites together to improve the conversion of H2O2 to hydroxyl radicals, in which the four sites in this cluster compose a whole melody to combine the two independent processes into one pot reaction. Based on this discovery, by controlling spatial distribution of clusters into the mesoporous alumina monolith, the continuous synthesis and application processes of H2O2 are finally accomplished in the fixed-bed reactor using prepared Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+/Al2O3 heterogeneous catalysts. This Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ example demonstrates the power of atomically precise metal clusters for achieving remarkably catalytic performances on important chemical reactions.

Results

Structure and reactivity of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ cluster

The atom-packing structure of the Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ cluster was shown in Fig. 2a, which featured a typical core-shell structure of metal core-organic ligand41. The octahedral like metal core comprised eight gold atoms surrounding a central palladium atom, with an average Pd-Au bond length of 2.62 Å, significantly shorter than that of bulk Pd-Au alloy, indicating strong metal-metal interactions within the cluster. The peripheral ligand shell consisted of four bidentate DPPF ligands, which was anchored by eight P-Au bonds, leaving the central Pd atom coordinatively unsaturated while positioning four redox-active ferrocene sites at a uniform 6.1 Å distance from the Pd center. We next evaluated the catalytic activity of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ cluster for the reaction of formic acid and oxygen in the batch reactor. Au8(DPPF)42+ without Pd center42, Au8Pd1(PPh3)82+ (PPh3 = triphenylphosphine) without DPPF ligand (Supplementary Fig. 1)41, mononuclear complex Pd(DPPF)Cl2, DPPF ligand and pure ferrocene (Fc) were selected as reference catalysts. Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ exhibited much higher activity than Au8(DPPF)42+, Au8Pd1(PPh3)82+ and Pd(DPPF)Cl2. In addition, pure DPPF ligand, pure Fc and the physical mixture of Au8Pd1(PPh3)82+ and Fc had little activity for formic acid oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 2). Thus, the observed catalytic performance of the Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ cluster was likely due to the synergy of Au, Pd and DPPF in the cluster.

The Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ cluster exhibited structural stability under reaction conditions. Ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectra of fresh and spent clusters showed no significant difference in the characteristic absorption bands of the two cases (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) data of fresh and spent clusters further confirmed that the formula of spent cluster was identical to that of fresh one with a dominant peak at m/z = 1949 (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Fourier transform infrared spectra (FT-IR) of the clusters before and after reaction showed that no obvious change was observed (Supplementary Fig. 3c), including the aromatic C-H stretching vibration at 3054 cm−1, the bending vibration of the benzene ring at 1436 cm−1, and the P-C bond stretching vibration at 1098 cm−143. Meanwhile, no new band appeared in the region of phosphine ligand oxidation from 1100 to 1220 cm−144, excluding the possibility of significant oxidation of the phosphine ligand.

Heterogeneous catalysts of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ supported on Al2O3 and catalytic performances

The heterogeneous catalyst of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ clusters supported on Al2O3 was next prepared via the electrostatic immobilization strategy (Fig. 2b). Utilizing the cationic nature of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+, it generated electrostatic attraction with the hydroxyl groups on the γ-Al2O3 microsphere45. During the immobilization, the CH2Cl2 solution of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ faded from deep red to colorless within 90 min (Fig. 2c), and UV-Vis spectra showed that the intensities of the characteristic bands decreased by >99%, confirming the efficient immobilization (Fig. 2d). Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images, corresponding element mapping, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis verified that the clusters were highly dispersed on the alumina support (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, the loading of clusters did not alter the mesoporous framework of Al2O3 support (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were conducted on the formic acid-O2 co-feeding system to help optimize clusters loaded into Al2O3. Simulation results showed that the concentration of formic acid decreased in the bulk phase of the Al2O3 microsphere due to the convective diffusion, while the oxygen molecules enriched in the bulk phase, and finally the two curves formed a concentration intersection zone 0.26 mm away from the surface edge of Al2O3 (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 6). Total mass-transfer resistance increased along the surface-to-bulk pathway, exhibiting zero slope at 0.17 mm from the surface (Fig. 3c), which indicated that the diffusion resistance escalated with deeper penetration, thereby predicting an optimal cluster embedding depth of ~0.2 mm into Al2O3.

The distribution of clusters onto the Al2O3 was experimentally optimized by regulating solvent polarity. We introduced methanol into the CH2Cl2 solvent of clusters to change the adsorption preference of clusters onto the support46, which promoted the migration of clusters from the surface to the interior of the support. As the volume fraction of methanol increased, a series of the Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ supported on Al2O3 catalysts named as AA-0, AA-1, AA-2, and AA-3 were obtained with the addition of 0%, 1%, 2%, and 3% methanol, respectively. To quantify the regulatory effect on the cluster distribution, the semi-quantitative optical microscopy analysis was adopted, based on the linear relationship between the characteristic deep red absorbance of clusters and concentration. The Al2O3 microsphere loaded with clusters were sectioned along the center (Fig. 3d), and the relative density of clusters was characterized, and distribution parameters were determined by the image analysis (Fig. 3e-h). Among them, the 95% cluster localization depth (D95%, defined as the radial thickness of the region where 95% of clusters accumulated) was used as the main evaluation index. As the volume fraction of methanol increased from 0% to 3%, the radial range of the cluster-enriched region expanded and D95% increased from 0.14 mm (AA-0) to 0.18 mm (AA-1), 0.28 mm (AA-2), and 0.42 mm (AA-3), respectively. This indicated that enhanced solvent polarity could lead to an increase in cluster embedding depth with the increase of methanol ratio (Supplementary Fig. 7-10), verifying the intrinsic spatial programmability of cluster-based catalyst design.

We next explored catalytic performances of these heterogeneous catalysts of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ supported on Al2O3 for the formic acid oxidation performed in the fixed-bed reactor. As expected, AA-1 catalyst showed an activity toward the target product of 737 μmol gcat.-1 h-1, which was higher than another catalysts such AA-0, AA-2 and AA-3 (Fig. 3i). The AA-1 catalyst retained >95% of its initial activity after continuous operation for 36 h, indicating its long-term stability (Fig. 3j). Moreover, the heterogeneous catalysts were robust under continuous-flow conditions. FT-IR spectra of the Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+/Al2O3 catalysts before and after reaction showed no obvious change (Supplementary Fig. 11), implying that no obvious ligand degradation and oxidation occurred on the supported clusters under reaction conditions. Moreover, from the TEM images of the catalyst after reaction (Supplementary Fig. 12), the clusters exhibited no aggregation during the reaction. Additionally, this catalytic system realized the coupling of H2O2 synthesis and formaldehyde degradation (Fig. 3k).

Concerto-like catalysis of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+

To reveal the reaction mechanism of this catalytic system, we focused on the activation of formic acid on the cluster. In-situ attenuated total reflection infrared spectroscopy (ATR-IR) was employed to track surface species evolution during formic acid oxidation (Fig. 4a). Two sets of characteristic absorption bands were observed, where one was the characteristic bands at 1714 cm-1 and 1210 cm-1 assigned to the adsorbed HCOOH, the other characteristic bands at 1575 cm-1 and 1386 cm-1 were assigned to the *HCOO47. These signals showed that formic acid underwent rapid dehydrogenation on the cluster. Furthermore, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) technology combined with 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) trapping agent was carried out to track the radicals formed in different systems (Fig. 4b). In the system of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ and H2O2, a clear four-line signal of the DMPO-OH adduct was detected, while no obvious radical signal was observed for Au8Pd1(PPh3)82+ under identical case. A similar phenomenon was observed in the HCOOH + O2 system, as DMPO-OH adduct was detected for Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ but not for Au8Pd1(PPh3)82+. In addition, pure DPPF ligand alone also produced the DMPO-OH signal with H2O2, while no radical was generated in the HCOOH + O2 system with pure ligand, confirming that pure DPPF ligand could not catalyze the reaction of HCOOH and O2 to form H2O2. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests further revealed that pure DPPF exhibited a clear anodic oxidation peak at 1.35 V and a corresponding cathodic reduction peak at 1.21 V, with a half-wave potential E1/2 = 1.28 V (Fig. 4c), verifying its reversible redox activity. The Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ retained a pair of redox peaks at 1.26 V and 1.12 V (E1/2 = 1.19 V), indicating that the coordination interaction did not destroy the electron transfer ability of the DPPF ligand. Notably, the CV curve of Au8Pd1(PPh3)82+ did not exhibit any relevant redox peaks (Supplementary Fig. 13), further highlighting the significance of the DPPF ligand in the formation of hydroxyl radicals.

To further probe the cooperativity of active sites in Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+, the formic acid oxidation and the hydroxyl radical generation were computed by density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Supplementary Data). As shown in Fig. 4d, a concerto-like catalytic mechanism was proposed. First, HCOOH attached to the cluster (state I, -0.90 eV) and underwent the first dehydrogenation producing *HCOO + *H (state III, -1.08 eV). The removal of the first hydrogen atom proceeded via a transition state TS1 involving the transfer of the H atom from the O–H moiety to the Au site (state II, -0.49 eV), surmounting an energy barrier of 0.41 eV. In contrast, the ligand-free Au8Pd1 cluster required a substantially higher barrier of 0.63 eV for this step. Subsequently, the C-bound hydrogen atom migrated from the *HCOO + *H (state III, -1.08 eV) to generate the *COO + *2H intermediate (state V, -0.95 eV) through the H-transfer transition state TS2 (state IV, -0.70 eV), with a remarkably low energy barrier of 0.38 eV. By comparison, the corresponding step on the bare Au8Pd1 encountered a much higher barrier of 0.85 eV, resulting in a significantly slower reaction rate than that observed for Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 14). Following this, O2 competitively adsorbed onto the cluster to afford the *O2 + *2H intermediate (state VI, -1.14 eV), which was then successively hydrogenated to *OOH + *H (state VII, -1.43 eV) and ultimately to *H2O2 (state VIII, -1.74 eV).

The key difference lay in the activation of H2O2. On Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+, the Fe site bound to ferrocene took part in breaking the O-O bond evenly, generating two ·OH radicals via transition state TS3 (state IX, -1.32 eV) with an energy barrier of merely 0.42 eV. One ·OH attached to the Pd center, while the second was temporarily stabilized by a Fe-O-H···P interaction before preferring to bind to Fe. The calculated dissociation free energy of ·OH on Pd and Fe were 1.09 and 0.83 eV (Supplementary Fig. 15), respectively, enabling rapid desorption of ·OH into the solution phase, where it subsequently catalyzed formaldehyde oxidation. Although the OH moiety on Fe-OH may partially participate in the direct oxidation of formaldehyde, this route did not constitute the dominant pathway. The free energy barrier for Fe-OH catalyzed formaldehyde oxidation was 0.96 eV, which exceeded the corresponding barrier for ·OH desorption into the solution phase (Supplementary Fig. 16). In contrast, decomposing H2O2 on bare Au8Pd1 needed to overcome a very high energy barrier (1.52 eV) (state IX relative to state VIII). As an alternative reaction pathway, hydrogen peroxide generation may proceed through pre-adsorption of O2 on the catalyst surface, followed by hydrogen abstraction from liquid-phase HCOOH to afford H2O2. DFT calculations revealed that the free energy barriers for H transfer from the C-H and O-H bonds were 1.60 eV and 1.93 eV, respectively, and both are substantially higher than the barrier for the initial adsorption and activation of HCOOH (Supplementary Fig. 17). This computational evidence showed that the DPPF ligands might not only speed up the dehydrogenation of formic acid but also reduce the activation energy needed for H2O2 to turn into hydroxyl radicals.

Cluster-mediated O2 enrichment microenvironment proposed by MD simulations

The physical accessibility of reactants to catalytic centers was further investigated by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Fig. 5a). The results showed that the Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ cluster created a specific microinterfacial environment, in which the hydrophobic ligand shell drove O2 to the cluster surface and concentrated poorly soluble oxidant within the reaction zone (defined as a 10 Å diameter from the Pd center). Analysis of radial distribution functions (RDFs) demonstrated that O2 dominated the first solvation shell. Importantly, the Pd-OO2 distance was substantially shorter than the Pd-CHCOOH distance (Fig. 5b). Kinetically, O2 had much higher local diffusion coefficient (0.11 Å2 ps-1) than formic acid (0.05 Å2 ps-1), which allowed O2 to be rapidly supplied at the Pd center (Fig. 5c). As a result, the residence time and collision frequency of O2 at the cluster surface were significantly higher than those of formic acid (Fig. 5d).

Discussion

Atomically precise Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ cluster with well-defined modular structure provides mechanistic insights into concerto-like catalysis between the Au8Pd1 core and DPPF ligand, where the two components collaborate to couple the generation and utilization of H2O2. Through optimizing gradient cluster distribution on Al2O3 monolith, heterogeneous Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+/Al2O3 catalysts achieve the concurrent enhancement of catalytic activity and stability under continuous-flow conditions. This study demonstrates a feasible strategy for realizing controlled gradient spatial arrangements of clusters into supports at the macroscopic scale, while maintaining the cluster structure at the nanoscale. It offers the rational design of high-performance catalysts, advancing the development of precise and efficient catalytic systems for distributed chemical manufacturing in the continuous-flow production lines.

Methods

Materials

1,1’-bis(diphenylphosphino)ferrocene (DPPF, ≥99%), ferrocene (Fc, ≥99%), formic acid ( ≥ 99%), o-phenylenediamine (OPD, ≥99%), horseradish peroxidase (HRP, ≥150 U/mg enzyme powder), formaldehyde (37 wt% in H2O), acetylacetone ( ≥ 99%), ammonium acetate ( ≥ 99%), and acetic acid ( ≥ 99%) were all purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of clusters

Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+: Au8Pd1(PPh3)82+ (50 mg) and DPPF (150 mg) were dissolved in a small amount of dichloroethane (1 mL). Tetrahydrofuran (20 mL) was added, and the mixture was transferred to a 50 mL PTFE-lined stainless steel autoclave reactor. The vessel was sealed, heated at 100 °C for 24 hours, yielding Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ as crystalline form. The successful preparation of Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ was confirmed via UV-Vis spectra and ESI-MS. The clusters Au8Pd1(PPh3)82+ and Au8(DPPF)42+ were prepared according to reported procedures41,42.

Preparation of heterogeneous catalysts

Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ clusters (10 mg) were dissolved in CH2Cl2 (10 mL). Al2O3 microspheres (1 g) were added, and the suspension was stirred for 90 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the solid product was separated and dried at room temperature, and the obtained catalyst was labeled as AA-0.

To tune the spatial distribution of clusters within Al2O3 microspheres, methanol was added to the CH2Cl2 solution to modulate solvent polarity and electrostatic interaction strength. Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ clusters (10 mg) were dissolved in CH2Cl2 (10 mL). 100, 200, and 300 μL of methanol, corresponding to 1%, 2%, and 3% v/v relative to the 10 mL solvent volume, were added to the cluster solution, respectively. 1 g of Al2O3 microspheres was added, and the mixture was stirred for 90 min. After the solid was separated and dried, the obtained catalysts were labeled as AA-1, AA-2, and AA-3 in sequence.

Catalytic evaluation

Synthesis of H2O2 in the batch system: high purity air was bubbled through deionized water (900 μL) at 20 mL min-1 for 5 min to achieve O2 saturation. Catalysts (1 mg) were dispersed in DMF (1 mL), and 20 μL of this solution was added to the O2 saturated water. Formic acid (40 μL, 500 mM) was then injected. After the reaction for 2 min, 20 μL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP, 0.1 mg mL-1) and 20 μL of o-phenylenediamine (OPD, 25 mM, aqueous solution) were sequentially added to perform the color reaction. The absorbance was determined at a wavelength of 450 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer.

Synthesis of H2O2 in the fixed-bed reactor: 1 g of supported catalysts was packed into a quartz fixed-bed reactor (outer diameter: 8 mm), 500 mM formic acid aqueous solution was pumped at a flow rate of 200 μL min-1, and high purity air was simultaneously introduced at a flow rate of 20 mL min-1. After the reaction stabilized, 40 μL of liquid sample was collected at the outlet and diluted to 920 μL with deionized water, and the subsequent detection method was the same as above. The data were presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Formaldehyde degradation in the fixed-bed reactor: under the same reactor and catalyst packing conditions, a mixed aqueous solution containing formic acid (500 mM) and formaldehyde (333 mM) was introduced at a flow rate of 200 μL min-1, and high purity air was simultaneously introduced at flow rate of 20 mL min-1. After the reaction, 5 mL of the liquid product was taken, 500 μL of color developing agent (aqueous solution containing 500 g L-1 ammonium acetate, 6% (v/v) acetic acid, and 0.5% (v/v) acetylacetone) was added, and the mixture was heated in a 60 °C water bath for 10 min. The absorbance was determined at a wavelength of 414 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer, the residual formaldehyde concentration was calculated through the formaldehyde standard curve.

Characterizations

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded on a Bruker D8 ADVANCE diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation operating at 40 kV and 40 mA.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on a JEOL JEM-2100F instrument operated at 200 kV.

Attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared (ATR-IR) spectra were acquired on a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer equipped with an ATR attachment. In-situ ATR-IR measurements were conducted to probe formic acid activation pathways. High purity air was bubbled through deionized water (600 μL) at 20 mL min-1 for 5 min to achieve O2 saturation. Au8Pd1(DPPF)42+ clusters (1 mg) were dispersed in DMF (1 mL), and 200 μL of this solution was added to the O2 saturated water. Background spectra were collected after cluster addition. Formic acid (200 μL, 500 mM) was then injected, and time-resolved spectra were recorded.

Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectra were characterized using a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were conducted on a CHI660E electrochemical workstation. The measurements were performed using a three-electrode system in a dichloromethane (DCM) solution containing 0.1 M tetrabutylammonium hexafluorophosphate as the electrolyte. The clusters to be tested were prepared at a concentration of 10 μM. The working electrode was a glassy carbon electrode, and a platinum sheet electrode served as the counter electrode, an Ag/AgCl electrode saturated with 3 M KCl was employed as reference electrode, with a scan rate of 50 mV s-1.

Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) spectra were recorded at room temperature using a Bruker EMX PLUS spectrometer. High purity air was bubbled through deionized water (940 μL) at 20 mL min-1 for 5 min to achieve O2 saturation. Catalysts (1 mg) were dispersed in DMF (1 mL), and 20 μL of this solution was added to the O2 saturated water. Formic acid (40 μL, 500 mM) was then injected. The resulting solution was mixed with 100 μL of 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO), and 30 μL of the mixture was transferred into a quartz standard sample tube. The bottom of the tube was sealed with wax, and the sample tube was then inserted into a dedicated EPR test tube for measurement.

Electrospray ionization mass spectra (ESI-MS) were obtained on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Q Exactive combined Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer.
