Sharp adaptive nonparametric testing for constant volatility
Johannes Brutsche, Lukas Riepl

Abstract
Based on discrete observations, we develop a test to infer if the volatility function σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) within the nonparametric Gaussian white noise model d ​ Y t = σ ⁡ ( t ) ​ d ​ W t dY_{t}=\sigma(t)dW_{t} is constant. The testing procedure is shown to be minimax-optimal and adaptive for infill asymptotics and these results entail that a deviation from the null hypothesis of constancy is best measured in terms of the ratio of σ ⁡ ( t ) \sigma(t) and its L 2 L^{2} -average. The derivation of optimal constants requires the construction of hypotheses with height h ⁡ ( b ) h(b) , where the parameter b b solves F n ​ ( b ) = 0 F_{n}(b)=0 for given functions F n F_{n} . Proving this equation to be solvable for each n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} and establishing quantitative bounds of the solutions is built upon the implicit function theorem.



Keywords:

Infill asymptotics, multiscale test, nonparametric volatility testing, sharp adaptivity.

1
Introduction

In this paper, we develop a (multiple) test to infer about constancy of the volatility function
\sigma
in the nonparametric Gaussian white noise model

\displaystyle dZ_{t}=\sigma(t)dW_{t},\qquad Z_{0}=z_{0}\in\mathbb{R},\quad t\in[0,1],

(1.1)

with driving noise
W
being a Brownian motion and
\sigma:[0,1]\rightarrow(0,\infty)
a measurable function. More specifically, given high-frequency observations
Z_{0},Z_{1/n},\dots,Z_{(n-1)/n},Z_{1}
of
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
, we develop a multiple test for the composite
constancy testing problem

\displaystyle H_{0}:\sigma\textrm{ is constant }\qquad\textrm{versus}\qquad H_{1}:\sigma\textrm{ is not constant}.

(1.2)

Figure 1:
The sample path of a given Brownian motion (blue) together with the process
Z
(orange) for some
\sigma=1+f
, where the support of
f
is marked by the grey regions.

Generally speaking, the testing problem (
1.2
) is a special case of testing for the presence of a parametric function within a nonparametric model, which has been investigated in the context of regression models in
Härdle and Mammen 1993
. Motivated by financial econometrics, it was studied for volatility coefficients of stochastic differential equations in
Dette et al. 2003
,
Dette et al. 2006
and
Dette and Podolskij 2008
. In this article, we focus on a special parametric hypothesis for which we build a multiple test statistic that is able to detect regions of deviations instead of merely solving the testing problem (
1.2
): In case the volatility
\sigma
is not constant, there exists at least one value
t\in[0,1]
where
\sigma^{2}(t)
differs from its squared
L^{2}
-norm
\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
. Given some regularity of
\sigma
, this possibly occurs on several unknown intervals and we develop our test in such a way that these can be identified at prescribed significance. This is illustrated in Figure
1
.

To this aim, we follow the so-called multiscale approach as described in
Dümbgen and Spokoiny 2001
in the context of testing qualitative hypotheses about the drift function in the Gaussian white noise model. Based on local log-likelihood ratio statistics, this approach naturally leads to a multiscale statistic suitable for testing
H_{0}:\sigma=c
against
H_{1}:\sigma\neq c
for a fixed constant
c
. To end up with a test statistic for the testing problem (
1.2
), we propose to use the same statistic, but replace the unknown
c
with a consistent estimator
\hat{c}_{n}
. It will turn out that the resulting statistic is again tight and distribution free under
H_{0}
, where it is crucial that
\sigma=c
can be estimated at the classical parametric rate by an estimator solely built upon squared increments of the observations.

In the context of volatility estimation, there exist several contributions addressing change-point phenomena, for instance
Bibinger et al. 2017
,
Bibinger and Madensoy 2019
and
Kokoszka et al. 2024
and references therein. We complement on studies like these by analyzing the behavior of our testing procedure for smooth local (and possibly very small) deviations by providing a minimax analysis over Hölder classes. Here, we obtain that our precedure is (sharp) minimax optimal. The most involved part of the proof is the lower bound and the appropriate choice of local alternatives. These will be introduced with an additional parameter
b
that is required to solve an equation of the form
F_{n}(b)=0
, which is analyzed via the implicit function theorem, in particular revealing quantitative bounds on the solution.
Finally, note that the minimax theory requires a quantitative measurement of a deviation from
H_{0}
in (
1.2
). Our study suggests to work with the difference of the ratio of local volatility and its squared
L^{2}
norm with one, i.e. measuring a deviation from constancy by
\sup_{t}|\sigma(t)^{2}/\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-1|
.

The article is organized as follows: In Section
2
we develop our multiple test for constancy and provide details on the calibration of the multiscale test statistic (see Theorem
2.1
). In Section
3
we discuss the minimax results for the particular choice of Hölder classes
\mathcal{H}(\beta,L)
, where the test is shown to be minimax optimal up to the constant for all
0<\beta\leq 1
and rate-optimal for every regime
\beta>1
. Moreover, the test is shown to be (sharp) adaptive in the unknown smoothness parameters
\beta
and
L
(see Theorems
3.1
,
3.4
and
3.5
). A route of proof highlighting our main technical contributions is given behind the respective results, while detailed proofs are provided in Section
4
.

2
The multiscale test for constant volatility

In this section, we will develop a multiple test for the testing problem (
1.2
) based on the following vector of discrete observations

\mathbf{Z}_{n}=\left(Z_{0},Z_{1/n},\dots,Z_{(n-1)/n},Z_{1}\right).

Here and subsequently, the subscript
\sigma
in the probability
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}
indicates that
Z
is given as in (
1.1
) with the corresponding volatility
\sigma
.
For the construction of the test statistic, suppose for a moment that we would like to test the simple null
\sigma=c
against the simple alternative
\sigma=c+f
for a fixed constant
c>0
and a given function
f:[0,1]\rightarrow(0,\infty)
. According to the Neyman-Pearson lemma, an optimal test is given by the log-likelihood ratio statistic
\log((\textrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{c+f}/\textrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{c})(\mathbf{Z}_{n}))
. As
(Z_{0},Z_{1/n},\dots,Z_{1})
is a Markov chain under
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}
where the conditional distribution

Z_{k/n}|Z_{(k-1)/n}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(Z_{(k-1)/n},\int_{(k-1)/n}^{k/n}\sigma(s)^{2}ds\right)

is a Gaussian distribution, this log-likelihood ratio is given in our model as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{n}{c^{2}}\int_{(k-1)/n}^{k/n}(c+f(s))^{2}ds\right)+\frac{n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}}{2c^{2}}\left[1-\frac{c^{2}}{n\int_{(k-1)/n}^{k/n}(c+f(s))^{2}ds}\right]\right).

(2.1)

The logarithmic term does not depend on the data
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
and is therefore omitted. Assuming additionally that the support of
f
is part of an interval
[t-h,t+h]
, the factor in square brackets is nonzero if and only if
[(k-1)/n,k/n]\cap[t-h,t+h]
is nonempty, meaning that this term acts like a localizer. For
h>0
and a kernel
\psi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we define

\displaystyle\psi_{t,h}(s):=\psi\left(\frac{s-t}{h}\right),\quad\lambda_{k}(t,h):=\sqrt{n}\int_{(k-1)/n}^{k/n}\psi_{t,h}(s)ds\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\Lambda_{n}(t,h):=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t,h)^{2}}.

(2.2)

Note that the dependence of
\lambda_{k}(t,h)
and
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)
on the kernel
\psi
is suppressed in the notation. Moreover, in case
|\psi(x)|\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we always have
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\leq 1
. By the above mentioned heuristics we now substitute the local log-likelihood ratio statistic in (
2.1
) by the local statistic

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t,h)n\left(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n}\right)^{2}.

(2.3)

Under
\mathbb{P}_{c}
,
(n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}/c^{2})_{k=1,\dots,n}
is a sequence of independent, identically
\chi_{1}^{2}
-distributed random variables. Thus, standardizing the expression in (
2.3
) by mean and variance provides the local test statistic

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{c}(\mathbf{Z}_{n}):=\frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t,h)\left(n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}-c^{2}\right)}{c^{2}\Lambda_{n}(t,h)}.

(2.4)

Based on these local test statistics we could now build a test for the nonparametric testing problem
H_{0}:\sigma=c
versus
H_{1}:\sigma\neq c
. In order to move on to the composite null of constancy given in (
1.2
), we substitute the unknown
c^{2}
by the quadratic variation estimator

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}^{2}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n}\right)^{2}.

(2.5)

This estimator is consistent, i.e.
\hat{c}_{n}^{2}\rightarrow_{\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma(s)^{2}ds
,
by the Fisk approximation of quadratic variation (see Proposition
18.17
in
Kallenberg 2021
) and the limit equals
c^{2}
for
\sigma=c
being constant. Moreover, an application of Markov’s inequality reveals that it actually attains the classical parametric
1/\sqrt{n}
-convergence rate, see the proof of the subsequent Theorem
2.1
in which this rate plays a key role.
The next result shows that the local statistics
\Psi_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
can be suitably combined for all
(t,h)
in

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}(K):=\left\{(t,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:h\in[K/n,1/2)\textrm{ and }t\in[h,1-h]\right\}

to construct the desired multiple test. Note that for each
n
and a continuous kernel
\psi
, the process
(t,h)\mapsto\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
is continuous.

Theorem 2.1

(Calibration of the test statistic)
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a function with
1=\psi(0)\geq\psi(x)
for
x\neq 1
and support in
[-1,1]
that is of bounded variation and Hölder continuous with Hölder constant one. Then, there exists a constant
K_{\psi}
such that
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)^{2}\geq 1/n
for all
(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi})
. Let
\Psi_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
be given as in (
2.4
) for this kernel
\psi
and
\hat{c}_{n}^{2}
in (
2.5
). Then, for any constant
c
, the family

\displaystyle\left(\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi})}\left(\big|\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\big|-G_{n}\left(2\log(1/\Lambda_{n}(t,h)),\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\right)\right)\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is tight under
\mathbb{P}_{c}
, where

\displaystyle G_{n}(\eta,\delta):=\sqrt{2\eta}+\frac{2\eta}{\max\{1,\sqrt{n}\delta\}}.

The calibration of the test statistic relies crucially on distinguishing between the bias generated by taking the supremum over
(t,h)
that necessitates the additive correction
G_{n}
, and the contribution of the parametric estimator
\hat{c}_{n}
of the unknown
c
. The key inequality here is

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi})}\left(\big|\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\big|-G_{n}\left(2\log(1/\Lambda_{n}(t,h)),\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\right)\right)\leq\Psi_{n}^{1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})+\Psi_{n}^{2}(\mathbf{Z}_{n}),

(2.6)

where

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}^{1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})

\displaystyle=\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi})}\left(\big|\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{c}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})-\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\big|\right),

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}^{2}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})

\displaystyle=\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi})}\left(\big|\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{c}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\big|-G_{n}\left(2\log(1/\Lambda_{n}(t,h)),\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\right)\right).

The term
\Psi_{n}^{1}
forms a difference to existing work and treats the effect of including the parametric estimator
\hat{c}_{n}
into the multiscale statistic. The proof of tightness of this term relies on the parametric
1/\sqrt{n}
-convergence rate of
\hat{c}_{n}^{2}
towards
c^{2}
that allows for an explicit upper bound of
\Psi_{n}^{1}
not involving a supremum any longer. On the other hand,
\Psi_{n}^{2}
equals our test statistic with known value
c
of the volatility level and can be analyzed under
\mathbb{P}_{c}
using arguments already present in other multiscale testing procedures in the literature, i.e.
Dümbgen and Walther 2008
,
Dümbgen and Walther 2007
testing shape constraints of densities,
Rohde 2011
covering higher dimensions or
Brutsche and Rohde 2024
with a test on the drift of an ergodic diffusion model.
The function
G_{n}(\eta,\delta)
is identified for our model as an inverted Bernstein tail which is due to the fact that our test statistic is built upon the squared increments
n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}/c^{2}
that follow a
\chi_{1}^{2}
-distribution under
\mathbb{P}_{c}
. The details are provided in Subsection
4.1
.

We now return to defining a test for the testing problem (
1.2
) on the basis of the statistic in Theorem
2.1
. The vector
(n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}/c^{2})_{k=1,\dots,n}
consists of independently
\chi_{1}^{2}
-distributed random variables and both
\hat{c}_{n}^{2}
and
\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{c}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
are continuous functions of this vector, where
t,h
only appear in deterministic factors. Therefore, it is easy to see that the process

\left(\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\right)_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi})}

has the same distribution under
\mathbb{P}_{1}
and
\mathbb{P}_{c}
for every constant
c>0
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we define

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}^{\alpha}:=\inf\left\{r\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}_{1}\left(\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi})}\left(\big|\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\big|-G_{n}\left(2\log(1/\Lambda_{n}(t,h)),\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\right)\right)\leq r\right)\geq 1-\alpha\right\},

for which Theorem
2.1
entails
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\kappa_{n}^{\alpha}|<\infty
. Finally, we introduce the
test for constant volatility

\displaystyle T_{n}(\mathbf{Z}_{n}):=\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi})}\left(\big|\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\big|-G_{n}\left(2\log(1/\Lambda_{n}(t,h)),\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\right)\right)>\kappa_{n}^{\alpha}\right\}}

(2.7)

and as
(\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n}))_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}
is distribution free under the null hypothesis, we directly obtain that the test is uniformly (over
H_{0}
) of level
\alpha
, i.e. that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sup_{c\textrm{ constant}}\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(T_{n}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=1\right)\leq\alpha.

We highlight at this point that the above statement is non-asymptotic. The power properties of the test for constant volatility are studied in the subsequent Section
3
for the particular example of Hölder smoothness classes. It also contains the discussion on how to appropriately measure deviations from the null hypothesis of constancy in (
1.2
).

Remark 2.2

(Simultaneous detection of regions of deviation)
.

The test statistic
T_{n}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
exceeds the
(1-\alpha)
-significance level if, and only if, the random family

\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\alpha}:=\left\{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi}):\big|\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\big|>\kappa_{n}^{\alpha}+G_{n}\left(2\log(1/\Lambda_{n}(t,h)),\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\right)\right\}

is non-empty. Therefore, one may conclude with confidence
1-\alpha
that the volatility
\sigma
differs from the
L^{2}
-average
\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
(estimated via
\hat{c}_{n}^{2}
) on every interval
[t-h,t+h]
with
(t,h)\in\mathcal{D}_{n}^{\alpha}
.

3
Minimax optimality and sharp adaptivity

In this section we establish minimax results for the test for constant volatility given in (
2.7
). This is exemplarily done for Hölder smoothness classes which are introduced below. As the test is built as a multiple test taking into account all possible bandwiths, we also derive that it is automatically adaptive in the unknown smoothness parameters.

3.1
Measuring deviations from constancy

In order to be able to specify minimax rates, we need to specify a distance between a given volatility function
\sigma
and then constant functions, i.e. those lying in the null hypothesis of our testing problem. We define this distance as

\displaystyle d_{J}(\sigma,H_{0}):=\sup_{t\in J}\left|\frac{\sigma(t)^{2}}{\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}-1\right|

for compact
J\subset(0,1)
to avoid boundary effects. Note that
d_{J}(\sigma,H_{0})=0
in case
\sigma=c
is constant and that
d_{J}(\sigma,H_{0})>0
for non-constant
\sigma
. This definition also meets our intuition in the following way: Assume
\sigma(s)=c+f(s)
for some given local deviation
f
. Then this deviation should be harder to detect, the larger the constant
c
.

3.2
Optimal power properties

For
\beta,L>0
denote by
\mathcal{H}(\beta,L)
the Hölder class of functions, that is all functions
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for which the first
\lfloor\beta\rfloor
derivatives exist and

\left|f^{(\lfloor\beta\rfloor)}(x)-f^{(\lfloor\beta\rfloor)}(y)\right|\leq L|x-y|^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor},

where
f^{(n)}
denotes the
n
th derivative of
f
and
\lfloor\beta\rfloor
the maximal integer strictly smaller than
\beta
. Our subsequent power results will specify those volatility functions
\sigma
satisfying
\sigma^{2}/\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,L)
that are close enough to the null hypothesis of constancy in the distance
d_{J}
such that they can be detected by the test
T_{n}
given in (
2.7
). To this aim, define the rate and constant

\displaystyle\rho_{n}=\rho_{n}(\beta):=\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}}\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad c_{*}=c_{*}(\beta,L):=\left(\frac{4L^{1/\beta}}{(2\beta+1)\|\psi_{\beta}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}},

(3.1)

where
\psi_{\beta}
is the so-called
optimal recovery kernel
which by definition is the solution to the optimization problem

\textrm{ Minimize }\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}\textrm{ over all }\psi\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,1)\textrm{ with }\psi(0)\geq 1.

It is known that
\psi_{\beta}
is an even function with compact support and satisfies
\psi_{\beta}(0)=1>|\psi_{\beta}(x)|
for
x\neq 0
, see for example
Leonov 1999
or
Donoho 1994
. For
0<\beta\leq 1
, it has the explicit representation

\displaystyle\psi_{\beta}(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}\left(1-|x|^{\beta}\right).

(3.2)

We start the minimax theory by providing a lower bound. The following result establishes that for every test of level
\alpha
for the null hypothesis of constancy there exist volatility coefficients
\sigma
in the alternative that deviate
(1-\epsilon_{n})c\delta_{n}
from being constant in the distance
d_{J}
which cannot be detected with probability
1-\alpha-o(1)
or larger. Note that this result even holds true under knowledge of both smoothness parameters
\beta
and
L
.

Theorem 3.1

(Minimax lower bound)
.

Let
\beta,L>0
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of tests satisfying
\sup_{\sigma\in H_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\phi_{n}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})]\leq\alpha
. Then for arbitrary numbers
\epsilon_{n}>0
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}^{2}\log(n)=\infty
there exists a constant
c=c(\beta,L)
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{2}/\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,L):\\
d_{J}(\sigma,H_{0})\geq(1-\epsilon_{n})c\rho_{n}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}[\phi_{n}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})]\leq\alpha

for any fixed compact interval
J\subset(0,1)
.
In case
\beta\in(0,1]
the result holds true for
c=c_{*}
.

The proof relies crucially on the construction of hypotheses
\sigma_{n,j}
for
j=1,\dots,N_{n}
and
N_{n}
appropriate in such a way that

(1)

\sigma_{n,j}^{2}/\|\sigma_{n,j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,L)
and

(2)

d_{J}(\sigma_{n,j},H_{0})\geq(1-\epsilon_{n})c\rho_{n}
with
c=c_{*}
for
\beta\leq 1
.

Here, item (2) is particularly challenging, since the deviation measure
d_{J}
compares the local quantity
\sigma_{n,j}(t)
with the global quantity
\|\sigma_{n,j}\|_{L^{2}}
. Although we construct
\sigma_{n,j}
in the common way of being local deviations from some baseline
\sigma_{0}
, the scaling required for these to fulfill (2) is not a priori clear. In view of (1), a natural choice for
\sigma_{n,j}
requires the form

\displaystyle\sigma_{n,j}^{2}(t)=1+L(1-\epsilon_{n})h_{n}^{\beta}\psi\left(\frac{t-t_{n,j}}{h_{n}}\right)

(3.3)

for some kernel
\psi
, suitable bandwidth
h_{n}
and location parameters
t_{n,j}
. Indeed, with such a choice we can obtain the rate
\rho_{n}
and we employ it for
\beta>1
. Deriving the optimal constant
c_{*}
, however, requires another choice. Here, we introduce a parameter
b>0
and define a bandwith

\displaystyle h_{n}^{b}:=\left(\frac{c_{*}}{L}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\left(\frac{\log(n)}{bn}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\beta+1}}.

(3.4)

For location parameters
t_{n,j}
that will be specified later in the detailed proof, we introduce the hypotheses

\displaystyle\sigma_{n,j}^{b}(t)=1+\frac{L}{2}(1-\epsilon_{n})(h_{n}^{b})^{\beta}\psi_{\beta}\left(\frac{t-t_{n,j}^{b}}{h_{n}^{b}}\right).

(3.5)

As compared to (
3.3
), the important difference is that we now specify
\sigma_{n,j}
in the given way and not
\sigma_{n,j}^{2}
.
The following two lemmas establish that there exists a sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that items (1) and (2) are satisfied. Moreover, the second result also contains a statement about the speed of convergence of the solutions
b_{n}
towards one, which is essential to derive the exact constant in the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\beta\leq 1
,
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a bounded sequence and
\sigma_{n,j}^{b_{n}}
be given as in (
3.5
). Then there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(\sigma_{n,j}^{b_{n}})^{2}/\|\sigma_{n,j}^{b_{n}}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,L)
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\beta\leq 1
and
\sigma_{n,j}^{b}
be given as in (
3.5
). Then there exists
n_{0}
and
b=b_{n}>0
such that
\sigma_{n,j}^{b_{n}}
solves (2) for
n\geq n_{0}
. Moreover, we have
\log(n)|b_{n}-1|\rightarrow 0
.

The strategy to prove Lemma
3.3
is to define
b_{n}
as a solution of the equality in

\displaystyle d_{J}(\sigma_{n,j}^{b},H_{0})\geq\left|\frac{\sigma_{n,j}^{b}(t_{n,j})^{2}}{\|\sigma_{n,j}^{b}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}-1\right|=(1-\epsilon_{n})c_{*}\rho_{n}.

(3.6)

Both the existence of such
b_{n}
and the bound on
|b_{n}-1|
can be derived via the implicit function theorem. All details for this, the proof of Lemma
3.2
and the proof of Theorem
3.1
are provided in Subsection
4.2
.

The next result shows that our test
T_{n}
given in (
2.7
) archives the corresponding rate to Theorem
3.1
and in case
\beta\leq 1
also the optimal constant.

Theorem 3.4

(Minimax upper bound)
.

Let
\beta,L>0
and
\psi
a kernel satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
. Then for arbitrary numbers
\epsilon_{n}>0
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}^{2}\log(n)=\infty
there exists a constant
c=c(\beta,L,\psi)
such that for the test
T_{n}
given in (
2.7
),

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{2}/\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,L):\\
d_{J}(\sigma,H_{0})\geq(1+\epsilon_{n})c\rho_{n}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}\left(T_{n}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=1\right)=1

for any fixed compact interval
J\subset(0,1)
. In case
\beta\in(0,1]
, we can choose
\psi=\psi_{\beta}
and the result holds true for
c=c_{*}
.

From the last result we know that any
\sigma
generating the data
\mathbf{Z}_{n}
and deviating at least
(1+\epsilon_{n})c\rho_{n}
in the distance
d_{J}
from the null hypothesis of constancy can be detected (i.e. the null is rejected) by the test
T_{n}
given in (
2.7
) with probability close to one. In particular, this test
T_{n}
attains the minimax optimal rate over the whole range of
\beta,L>0
and additionally is even optimal in the constant for
\beta\leq 1
. The next result states that rate-adaptivity is attained even uniformly over
(\beta,L)\in[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]\times[L_{1},L_{2}]
and sharp adaptivity for fixed
\beta
over
L\in[L_{1},L_{2}]
.

Theorem 3.5

(Adaptivity)
.

Let
\beta,L>0
and
\psi,\epsilon_{n}
be specified as in Theorem
3.4
. Then for any compact subset
[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]\times[L_{1},L_{2}]\subset(0,\infty)^{2}
the test
T_{n}
in (
2.7
) is rate-adaptive in both parameters
\beta
and
L
in the sense that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{(\beta,L)\in[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]\times[L_{1},L_{2}]}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{2}/\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,L):\\
d_{J}(\sigma,H_{0})\geq(1+\epsilon_{n})c\rho_{n}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}\left(T_{n}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=1\right)=1.

For
\beta\leq 1
we have sharp adaptivity in the parameter
L\in[L_{1},L_{2}]\subset(0,\infty)
in the sense

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{L\in[L_{1},L_{2}]}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma^{2}/\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,L):\\
d_{J}(\sigma,H_{0})\geq(1+\epsilon_{n})c_{*}\rho_{n}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}\left(T_{n}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})=1\right)=1.

Remark 3.6

(Optimal constant)
.

For composite hypotheses testing about the drift function in Gaussian white noise models, the optimal constant was derived in
Dümbgen and Spokoiny 2001
. Here,
4
in the constant in (
3.1
) is replaced with
2
. Our additional factor
2
is due to the fact that
\mathrm{Var}(Y)=2
for
Y\sim\chi_{1}^{2}
and our statistic being built upon the quantities
n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}/c^{2}
, which follow a
\chi_{1}^{2}
-distribution under
\mathbb{P}_{c}
, whereas the drift is estimated based on the increments
\sqrt{n}(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})/c\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

The following two lemmas are slight modifications and reformulations of results

Lemma 4.1

(see
Rohde 2008
, p. 1371)
.

Let

for
\beta,L>0
and set

Then there exists a constant
c=c(\beta,L)>0
such that we have

|f(x)|\geq(1/2)|f(x^{*})|\text{ on a closed interval }I(f)\subset[-a,a]\text{ with }\lambda(I(f))\geq c\,|f(x^{*})|^{1/\beta}.

Lemma 4.2

(see
Rohde 2008
, p. 1372)
.

Let
\beta,L>0
and
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of functions defined on
[-a,a]
with

and

|f_{n}(x^{*})|=\sup_{t\in[-a,a]}|f_{n}(t)|=\rho_{n}\leq 1
. Then there exists a constant
c=c(\beta,L)>0
such that

|f_{n}(x)|\geq(1/2)\rho_{n}\qquad\text{for}\qquad x\in[-a,a]\cap\left[x^{*}-c\rho_{n}^{1/\beta},\,x^{*}+c\rho_{n}^{1/\beta}\right].

Moreover, considered as a function in
\beta
and
L
, the constant

c(\beta,L)
is continuous in its parameters.

The next result establishes that
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)
is bounded from below by a multiple of the bandwidth
h
in case
h
is not too small.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\psi
be given as in Theorem
2.1
. Then there exists a constant
K_{\psi}
such that
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\geq 1/n
for all
(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi})
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C=C(\psi)
such that

\Lambda_{n}(t,h)^{2}\geq Ch\quad\textrm{ for }(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K_{\psi}).

Proof.

Using that
\psi
is nonnegative allows us to bound

\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}(t,h)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\int_{(k-1)/n}^{k/n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\psi_{t,h}(s)\geq 1/2\}}ds\right)^{2}.

By the Hölder continuity assumption on
\psi
we know
\psi\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,1)
for some
0<\beta\leq 1
. As
\psi(0)=1
, Lemma
4.1
reveals that there exists a constant
c_{\beta}
such that
\psi(x)\geq 1/2
for all
x\in[-c_{\beta},c_{\beta}]
. In particular, the support of
\mathbbm{1}_{\{\psi_{t,h}(s)\geq 1/2\}}
contains
[t-c_{\beta}h,t+c_{\beta}h]
. The number of indices
k
for which
k/n\in[t-c_{\beta}h,t+c_{\beta}h]
is at least
\lfloor 2nc_{\beta}h\rfloor-1\geq 2nc_{\beta}h-2
such that

\Lambda_{n}(t,h)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2n}\left(c_{\beta}nh-1\right).

Setting
K_{\psi}:=3/c_{\beta}
then yields
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\geq 1/n
for
h\geq K_{\psi}/n
. Moreover,
for these
h
we have
1/n\leq h/K_{\psi}
and obtain the second assertion as

\Lambda_{n}(t,h)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(c_{\beta}h-\frac{1}{n}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(c_{\beta}-\frac{1}{K_{\psi}}\right)h=\frac{1}{3}c_{\beta}h.

∎

4.1
Proofs of Section
2
(Calibration)

The identification of the correction term
G_{n}
in Theorem
2.1
is based on the following result on the tail probabilities of weighted sums of independent centered
\chi_{1}^{2}
-distributed random variables. This tail bound was derived in
Laurent and Massart 2000
and is given below in the form that we will use in the proof of Theorem
2.1
for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 4.4

(Lemma
1
in
Laurent and Massart 2000
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent and identically standard Gaussian random variables and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}>0
. Define
V_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}|a_{k}|^{2}
and
M_{n}:=\max_{k=1,\dots,n}|a_{k}|
.
Then for any
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\big(X_{k}^{2}-1\big)\right|\geq 2\sqrt{V_{n}x}+2M_{n}x\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-x\right).

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

The existence of
K=K_{\psi}
with
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\geq 1/n
for
(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)
was already proven in Lemma
4.3
.
Following the route of proof after Theorem
2.1
, it remains to prove that both quantities
\Psi_{n}^{1}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
and
\Psi_{n}^{2}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})
in (
2.6
) are tight. This is done separately for both of them in the sequel.

\bullet\ \mathbf{\Psi_{n}^{1}}
.
The crucial idea to prove tightness of
\Psi_{n}^{1}
is to define an event with high probability on which we can bound
|\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})-\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{c}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})|
by a quantity independent of
t,h
. For
\delta,\kappa>0
,

\left|\frac{1}{c^{2}}-\frac{1}{\hat{c}_{n}^{2}}\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\hat{c}_{n}^{2}-c^{2}|\leq\kappa c^{2}/\sqrt{n}\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{c}_{n}^{2}\geq\delta c^{2}\}}\leq\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{n}c^{4}\delta}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\hat{c}_{n}^{2}-c^{2}|\leq\kappa c^{2}/\sqrt{n}\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{c}_{n}^{2}\geq\delta c^{2}\}},

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)}\big|\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{\hat{c}_{n}}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})-\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{c}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\big|>C\right)\leq P_{1}+P_{2}+P_{3},

where

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(|\hat{c}_{n}^{2}-c^{2}|>\kappa c^{2}/\sqrt{n}\right),

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(\hat{c}_{n}^{2}<\delta c^{2}\right),

\displaystyle P_{3}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)}\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2n}\delta c^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{k}(t,h)}{\Lambda_{n}(t,h)}n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}>C\right).

Let
\epsilon>0
. First, we will choose
\kappa,\delta>0
in such a way that
P_{1},P_{2}\leq\epsilon/3
. For the respective choices of these parameters, we then show that
C
may be chosen such that
P_{3}\leq\epsilon/3
and tightness of
\Psi_{n}^{1}
follows.

\bullet\ \mathbf{P_{1}}
.

By Markov’s inequality, independence of increments of
Z
and
\mathbb{E}_{c}[(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}]=c^{2}/n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(|\hat{c}_{n}^{2}-c^{2}|>\frac{\kappa c^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{\kappa^{2}c^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{c}\left[\left(\hat{c}_{n}^{2}-c^{2}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{c}\left[\left(\frac{n}{c^{2}}(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{2}{\kappa^{2}},

where the last equality used
\textrm{Var}(Y)=2
for
Y\sim\chi_{1}^{2}
. For
\kappa\to\infty
, this term clearly vanishes.

\bullet\ \mathbf{P_{2}}
.

Let
Y_{n}\sim\chi_{n}^{2}
. For
a>0
we have the Chernoff-type bound

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}\leq n\delta\right)\leq e^{an\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-aY_{n}}\right]=e^{an\delta}(1+2a)^{-n/2},

where the last step uses that the moment generating function
M_{Y_{n}}(t)
of
Y_{n}
is
M_{Y_{n}}(t)=(1-2t)^{-n/2}
for
t<1/2
(see p.64 in
Davison 2003
). For
0<\delta<1
and
a=(1-\delta)/(2\delta)
this yields

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}\leq n\delta\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n}{2}\left(\delta-1-\log(\delta)\right)\right).

As
\sum_{k=1}^{n}n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}\sim\chi_{n}^{2}
under
\mathbb{P}_{1}
we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(\hat{c}_{n}^{2}<\delta c^{2}\right)=\mathbb{P}_{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}\leq n\delta\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta-1-\log(\delta)\right)\right).

As
\delta-1-\log(\delta)\rightarrow\infty
as
\delta\searrow 0
, we can choose
\delta>0
small enough such that this probability bound is smaller then
\epsilon/3
.

\bullet\ \mathbf{P_{3}}
.

Here, we start with a deterministic bound using Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality for sums. Recalling
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t,h)
, we find

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)}\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2n}\delta c^{2}}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{n}(t,h)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t,h)n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\leq\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)}\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2n}\delta c^{2}}\frac{1}{\Lambda_{n}(t,h)}\sqrt{\Lambda_{n}(t,h)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{n}n^{2}(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{4}}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2n}\delta c^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{n}n^{2}(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{4}}.

Hence, Markov’s inequality and
\mathbb{E}_{c}[(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{4}]=3c^{4}/n^{2}
reveals

\displaystyle P_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{2n}\delta c^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{n}n^{2}(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{4}}>C\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2n\delta^{2}c^{4}C^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{c}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}n^{2}(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{4}\right]=\frac{3\kappa^{2}}{2\delta^{2}C^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle C\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

\bullet\ \mathbf{\Psi_{n}^{2}}
.
We introduce the process
(Y_{n}(t,h))_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}
with

Y_{n}(t,h)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t,h)\left(c^{-2}n\left(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n}\right)^{2}-1\right)

and a semimetric
d_{n}
on
\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)
via

d_{n}((t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime}))=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\lambda_{k}(t,h)-\lambda_{k}(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})\right)^{2}}.

It is easily checked by Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality that
|\Lambda_{n}(t,h)-\Lambda_{n}(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})|\leq d_{n}((t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime}))
for all
(t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)
. Moreover, we define

G_{n}^{\prime}(\eta,\delta):=\sqrt{2\eta}+\frac{\sqrt{2}\eta}{\max\{1,\sqrt{n}\delta\}}

for which we observe
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{\eta\geq 0,0<\delta\leq 1}G_{n}^{\prime}(\eta,\delta)/(1+\eta)<\infty
. Then, by Theorem
10
in
Rohde 2011
with the subsequent remark replaced by

G_{n}^{\prime}(2\log(1/\delta)+C\log\log(e/\delta),\delta)\leq G_{n}(2\log(1/\delta),\delta)+o(1)\quad\textrm{ as }\delta\searrow 0

and Theorem
7
in
Dümbgen and Walther 2007
with its subsequent remark, we obtain that for some constant
C>0
,

\left(\sup_{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}(K)\left(\big|\hat{\Psi}_{n,t,h}^{c}(\mathbf{Z}_{n})\big|-G_{n}\left(2\log(1/\Lambda_{n}(t,h)),\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\right)\right)\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is tight, once we can prove the following three assertions:

(i)

For any
(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)
with
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\geq\delta
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(|Y_{n}(t,h)|\geq\Lambda_{n}(t,h)G_{n}^{\prime}(\eta,\delta)\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\eta\right).

(ii)

There exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for all
(t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)

\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(|Y_{n}(t,h)-Y_{n}(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})|\geq c_{1}d_{n}((t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime}))\eta\right)\leq c_{2}\exp\left(-\eta\right).

(iii)

There exists a constant
c_{3}>0
such that

D\left((\delta u),\{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K):\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\leq\delta\},d_{n}\right)\leq c_{3}u^{-4}\delta^{-2},

for all
u,\delta\in(0,1]
, where
D(u,A,d_{n})
denotes the capacity number of the set
A
w.r.t. the semimetric
d_{n}
, i.e.
D(u,A,d_{n})=\max\{\#A_{0}:A_{0}\subset A,d_{n}(s,t)>u\textrm{ for different }s,t\in A_{0}\}
. Observe that this is sufficient for an application of Theorem
10
in
Rohde 2011
as
N(u,A,d_{n})\leq D(u,A,d_{n})\leq N(u/2,A,d_{n})
for the covering number

, see p.147 in
van der Vaart and Wellner 2023
.

In what follows, we are going to prove (i)-(iii), thereby establishing tightness of
\Psi_{n}^{2}
and thus finishing the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of (i).
We apply Lemma
4.4
with
a_{k}=\lambda_{k}(t,h)/\sqrt{2\Lambda_{n}(t,h)^{2}}
. For this choice, we obviously have

V_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{k}(t,h)^{2}}{\Lambda_{n}(t,h)^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}.

As
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\geq\delta
by assumption and
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\geq 1/n
by Lemma
4.3
for
(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)
, one obtains the bound

M_{n}=\max_{k=1,\dots,n}|a_{k}|=\max_{k=1,\dots,n}\frac{\sqrt{n}\int_{(k-1)/n}^{k/n}\psi_{t,h}(s)ds}{\sqrt{2}\Lambda_{n}(t,h)}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\max\{1,\sqrt{n}\delta\}}.

As
(\sqrt{n}(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})/c)_{k}
is an i.i.d. sequence of standard Gaussian random variables, we can apply Lemma
4.4
with
V_{n}=1/2
and the bound
M_{n}\leq\max\{\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2n}\delta\}^{-1}
which yields (i).

Proof of (ii).
Writing out the definition of
Y_{n}(t,h)
, we observe

Y_{n}(t,h)-Y_{n}(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\lambda_{k}(t,h)-\lambda_{k}(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})\right)\left(c^{-2}n\left(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n}\right)^{2}-1\right).

Put
b_{k}=(\lambda_{k}(t,h)-\lambda_{k}(t^{\prime},h^{\prime}))/\sqrt{2d_{n}((t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime}))^{2}}
. Then, we find

V_{n}^{\prime}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}^{2}=1/2\leq 1\quad\textrm{ and }\quad M_{n}^{\prime}=\max_{k=1,\dots,n}|b_{k}|\leq\sqrt{V_{n}^{\prime}}\leq 1,

such that Lemma
4.4
yields for
\eta\geq 1
the tail probability bound

\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(\frac{|Y_{n}(t,h)-Y_{n}(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})|}{d_{n}((t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime}))}>4\eta\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{c}\left(\frac{|Y_{n}(t,h)-Y_{n}(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})|}{d_{n}((t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime}))}>2\sqrt{\eta}+2\eta\right)\leq\exp\left(-\eta\right).

For
0<\eta<1
we trivially bound the probability by one. Hence (ii) holds true with
c_{1}=4
and
c_{2}=2e
.

Proof of (iii).
In this proof,
\lambda(A)
denotes the Lebesgue measure for a measurable subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}
and
TV(\psi)
the variation of
\psi
(which by assumption is finite). We partition
[0,1]
into intervals
(M_{k})_{1\leq k\leq N}
such that
\lambda(M_{k})\leq\frac{1}{2TV(\psi)^{2}}(u\delta)^{2}
with equality for the first
N-1
.
Then we have

\displaystyle 1=\lambda[0,1]=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda(M_{k})=(N-1)\frac{1}{2TV(\psi)^{2}}(u\delta)^{2}+\lambda(M_{N})\geq\frac{(N-1)(u\delta)^{2}}{2TV(\psi)^{2}},

which yields the bound
N\leq 1+2TV(\psi)^{2}(u\delta)^{-2}
. Next, take
(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)
such that
\Lambda_{n}(t,h)^{2}\leq\delta^{2}
. By Lemma
4.3
, this implies
Ch\leq\delta^{2}
for some constant
C>0
. Then, for
t-h\in M_{i}
and
t+h\in M_{k}
,

\displaystyle(i-k-1)\frac{1}{2TV(\psi)^{2}}(u\delta)^{2}\leq 2h\leq\frac{2\delta^{2}}{C}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad(i-k)\leq 1+\frac{4TV(\psi)^{2}}{Cu^{2}}.

This implies that there are at most

\left(1+\frac{2TV(\psi)^{2}}{(u\delta)^{2}}\right)\left(1+\frac{4TV(\psi)^{2}}{Cu^{2}}\right)\leq\left(1+2TV(\psi)^{2}+\frac{4TV(\psi)^{2}}{C}+\frac{8TV(\psi)^{4}}{C}\right)u^{-4}\delta^{-2}

such pairs
(i,j)
. Let
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{T}
be a maximal subset of
\{(t,h)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(K):\Lambda_{n}(t,h)\leq\delta\}
with
d_{n}((t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime}))>u\delta
for arbitrary
(t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. Then, the proof is finished, if we could show that for all
(i,j)
there is at most one such point
(t_{ij},h_{ij})
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\prime}
with
t_{ij}-h_{ij}\in M_{i}
and
t_{ij}+h_{ij}\in M_{j}
. To this aim, we pick
(t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
such that
t-h,t^{\prime}-h^{\prime}\in M_{i}
and
t+h,t^{\prime}+h^{\prime}\in M_{k}
, and are done if we show
d_{n}((t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime}))^{2}\leq(u\delta)^{2}
. By Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{n}\left((t,h);(t^{\prime},h^{\prime})\right)^{2}&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}n\left(\int_{(k-1)/n}^{k/n}\psi_{t,h}(s)-\psi_{t^{\prime},h^{\prime}}(s)ds\right)^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\psi_{t,h}(s)-\psi_{t^{\prime},h^{\prime}}(s)\right)^{2}ds.\end{split}

As
\psi
is of finite variation, we can write

for all but at most countably many numbers

with

and

a measurable function with

. Inserting this representation into the last bound and consecutively applying Jensen’s inequality and Fubini’s theorem reveals

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{-1}^{(s-t)/h}g(r)dP(r)-\int_{-1}^{(s-t^{\prime})/h^{\prime}}g(r)dP(r)\right)^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq TV(\psi)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathbbm{1}_{B_{s}((t,h),(t^{\prime},h^{\prime}))}dsdP(r),

where

Now, as

where

is maximized for

4.3

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\beta,L>0
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(\beta,L)>0
that depends continuously on
(\beta,L)
such that for every function

with

Proof.

Set

. Then
g\geq 0
with
\int_{0}^{1}g(x)dx=1
and
g\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,L)
, i.e.

g\in\left\{p\in\mathcal{H}(\beta,L):p\geq 0,\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)dx=1\right\}=:\mathcal{P}(\beta,L).

The assertion of the Lemma follows once we show that
g
can be bounded by some universal constant
C=C(\beta,L)
that is independent of
g
. This in turn is a consequence of the existence of such
C
with
\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}(\beta,L)}\|p\|_{\sup}\leq C
, see
Tsybakov 2009
, inequality (

) and its proof below. In particular, the proof constructs the constant explicitly and the continuous dependence on
(\beta,L)
can be read off.
∎

j=1,2,3,4
, where the four terms are given as

\displaystyle T_{n}^{1}

\displaystyle:=B_{n}^{\sigma_{n}}(t_{n},h_{n})\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{c}_{n,\sigma_{n}}^{2}<\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}/2\}},

\displaystyle T_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t_{n},h_{n})|I_{k}(\sigma_{n})-\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}|}{\Lambda_{n}(t_{n},h_{n})}\frac{|\hat{c}_{n,\sigma_{n}}^{2}-\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}|}{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{4}},

\displaystyle T_{n}^{3}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sqrt{2}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t_{n},h_{n})I_{k}(\sigma_{n})(n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}-1)\right|}{\Lambda_{n}(t_{n},h_{n})\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}},

\displaystyle T_{n}^{4}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t_{n},h_{n})\big|\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\hat{c}_{n,\sigma_{n}}^{2}\big|}{\Lambda_{n}(t_{n},h_{n})\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}.

Let
\epsilon>0
be given and
K>0
. Subsequently, we prove
\mathbb{P}_{1}(|T_{n}^{j}|>K)\leq\epsilon
for
j=1,2,3,4
separately. In all cases, we use the bound

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[\left(\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\hat{c}_{n,\sigma_{n}}^{2}\right)^{2}\right]&=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}I_{k}(\sigma_{n})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[\left(n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right]\\
&=2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\int_{(k-1)/n}^{k/n}\sigma_{n}(s)^{2}ds\right)^{2}\leq\frac{2}{n}\|\sigma_{n}\|_{\sup}^{2}\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\end{split}

(4.12)

where the first equality is a consequence of the independence of increments of the process
Z
and the second one uses
\mathrm{Var}(Y)=2
for
Y\sim\chi_{1}^{2}
.

\bullet\ \mathbf{T_{n}^{1}.}

By Markov’s inequality and equation (
4.12
),

\mathbb{P}_{1}\left(|T_{n}^{1}|>K\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{1}\left(\hat{c}_{n,\sigma_{n}}^{2}<\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}/2\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{1}\left(\left|\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\hat{c}_{n,\sigma_{n}}^{2}\right|>\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}/2\right)\leq\frac{8}{n}\frac{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{\sup}^{2}}{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}},

which by Lemma
4.5
converges to zero.

To prove tightness of
T_{n}^{j}
for
j=2,3,4
we show that the respective second moment under
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is bounded by a polynomial in
\|\sigma_{n}\|_{\sup}/\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}
which by Lemma
4.5
is bounded uniformly in
n
. The claim then follows by an application of Markov’s inequality.

\bullet\ \mathbf{T_{n}^{2}.}

By definition,

\frac{I_{k}(\sigma_{n})-\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}=n\int_{(k-1)/n}^{k/n}\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}(s)^{2}}{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}-1\right)ds\leq\frac{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{\sup}^{2}}{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}+1.

Additionally, we obtain using Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t_{n},h_{n})\right)^{2}=n^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t_{n},h_{n})\right)^{2}\leq n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t_{n},h_{n})^{2}=n\Lambda_{n}(t_{n},h_{n})^{2}.

(4.13)

Inserting all of these inequalities together with the moment bound (
4.12
) yields

\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[|T_{n}^{2}|^{2}\right]\leq 4\left(\frac{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{\sup}^{2}}{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}+1\right)^{2}\frac{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{\sup}^{2}}{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}.

\bullet\ \mathbf{T_{n}^{3}.}

Here, we use
I_{k}(\sigma_{n})\leq\|\sigma_{n}\|_{\sup}^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}_{1}[(n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}-1)^{2}]=2
such that by independence of the increments of
Z
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[|T_{n}^{3}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\Lambda_{n}(t_{n},h_{n})^{2}\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t_{n},h_{n})I_{k}(\sigma_{n})\left(n(Z_{k/n}-Z_{(k-1)/n})^{2}-1\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\|\sigma_{n}\|_{\sup}^{4}}{\Lambda_{n}(t_{h},h_{n})^{2}\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{4}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(t_{n},h_{n})^{2}=\frac{4\|\sigma_{n}\|_{\sup}^{4}}{\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L_{2}}^{4}}.

\bullet\ \mathbf{T_{n}^{4}.}

By the moment bound (
4.12
) and (
4.13
) and directly obtain
\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[|T_{n}^{4}|^{2}\right]\leq 4\|\sigma_{n}\|_{\sup}^{2}/\|\sigma_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

The proof follows the lines of that of Theorem
3.4
. Here, the reasoning for Item (2) remains completely the same by noting that the constant in Lemma
4.5
can be chosen uniformly over
(\beta,L)\in[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]\times[L_{1},L_{2}]
. To achieve rate adaptivity in both parameters
\beta
and
L
, we can no longer choose the optimal recovery kernel
\psi_{\beta}
for building the test statistic. This means that for
\beta\leq 1
, we have to argue as in the proof of Item (1) for
\beta>1
. Here, it is important to note that
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(\beta,L)
is uniformly bounded over
(\beta,L)\in[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]\times[L_{1},L_{2}]
by Lemma
4.2
. The same applies for the remainder in (
4.10
) that is uniformly
o(1)
over this set. With these two ingredients one can follow the proof of Item (1) for
\beta>1
step-by-step.

For sharp adaptivity in
L
for
\beta\leq 1
, one has to note that
\log(c_{*}/L)
is uniformly bounded in
L\in[L_{1},L_{2}]
. Then (
4.10
) remains valid and the proof of Item (1) for
\beta\leq 1
remains the same as in the proof of Theorem
3.4
.
∎
