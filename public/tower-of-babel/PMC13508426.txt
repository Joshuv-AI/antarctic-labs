Sputum lautropia is decreased in rheumatoid arthritis-associated pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Timothy M. Wilson, Brendan Allen, J. Kirk Harris, Kristine A. Kuhn, Kevin D. Deane, Stephen M. Humphries, Joyce S. Lee, Joshua J. Solomon, M. Kristen Demoruelle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate whether lung microbial composition differs between individuals with rheumatoid arthritis and pulmonary fibrosis (RA-PF) compared to those without fibrosis (RA-no-PF).MethodsWe enrolled 54 RA patients (22 RA-PF, 32 RA-no-PF) from rheumatology and pulmonary clinics. PF was defined by high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) findings. Induced sputum was analyzed using 16 S rRNA sequencing to identify the relative abundance of sputum bacteria. Taxonomic differences between groups were evaluated using three independent analytic approaches: Mann-Whitney U, DESeq2, and EdgeR, with false discovery rate correction. Logistic regression examined associations between Lautropia abundance and clinical variables.ResultsIndividuals with RA-PF were older, more often male, and had a higher history of smoking than those with RA-no-PF. Alpha diversity was lower in RA-PF, while beta diversity did not differ between groups. Across the three analytic methods, Lautropia consistently showed decreased abundance in RA-PF compared to RA-no-PF. In multivariable models adjusting for age, sex, and smoking, RA-PF remained independently associated with undetectable levels of Lautropia (OR = 0.14, p = 0.023). Within RA-PF, when Lautropia was undetectable, there was no difference in pulmonary physiology but did show a trend towards more lung fibrosis.ConclusionSputum Lautropia is significantly reduced in RA-PF, and its absence may correlate with more severe fibrosis. These findings support a potential role for the lung microbiome in the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis and raise the possibility that commensal taxa such as Lautropia modulate fibrotic pathways. Longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether Lautropia depletion precedes fibrosis and whether microbial-directed approaches represent new therapeutic avenues in RA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-026-03845-3.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized primarily by synovial inflammation and joint damage. Beyond the joints, it is well established that RA can affect a variety of other organs, with the lung representing one of the most clinically significant extra-articular sites of disease. Interstitial lung disease associated with RA (RA-ILD) is clinically diagnosed in 5–10% of RA patients and contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. In addition, another 20–40% of RA patients can have lung imaging evidence of ILD without clinical symptoms or abnormal physiology, with many progressing to clinically significant ILD over time. Despite the significant burden of ILD in RA, the mechanisms driving pathogenesis remain poorly understood.

Fibrotic RA-ILD, as defined by patterns on pathology or imaging, carries a higher risk of lung disease progression and mortality [3]. With data on the ability of anti-fibrotic medications to slow progression in fibrotic RA-ILD, risk stratification can help inform decisions on the initiation of treatment. These differences in outcomes between fibrotic and non-fibrotic RA-ILD likely reflect distinct underlying pathogenic mechanisms, and, given the worse prognosis for fibrotic RA-ILD, there is an urgency to understand the mechanisms that contribute to these different disease phenotypes.

In recent years, the lung microbiome has been increasingly recognized as a contributor to pulmonary homeostasis and disease. Once believed to be sterile, it is now understood that the lower airways harbor a low-biomass but diverse microbial community shaped by a variety of mechanisms, including micro-aspiration, mucociliary clearance, and host immune surveillance. Lung microbial dysbiosis has been linked to several chronic lung disorders, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, sarcoidosis, and ILD [4]. In particular, prior work in diopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) has identified distinct microbial dysbiosis that has been associated with both disease severity and progression [5–8]. In IPF, which has similarities to fibrotic RA-ILD, it is suggested that dysbiosis can influence local and systemic immune dysregulation, potentially contributing to aberrant inflammation and subsequent fibrotic remodeling [8, 9].

While studies have identified microbial dysbiosis of the gut and gingival mucosae in patients with RA, the lung microbiome remains insufficiently characterized [10–12]. Scher et al. previously demonstrated dysbiosis of the lung microbiome in RA subjects compared to healthy controls using bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), although this study did not specify whether these subjects with RA had underlying ILD [13]. A deeper understanding of the lung microbial environment in RA-ILD, particularly those with fibrotic forms of RA-ILD, may provide novel insights into disease mechanisms. In this study, we investigated the composition of the lung microbiome, using noninvasive sampling of the lungs through induced sputum, in patients with RA with or without fibrotic ILD.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Patients with an established diagnosis of RA, confirmed by a board-certified rheumatologist, were enrolled from clinics at the University of Colorado and National Jewish Health. Patients were further characterized as having RA with or without pulmonary fibrosis (RA-PF). Pulmonary fibrosis was determined based on findings on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) imaging of the lung showing reticulations, traction bronchiectasis, and/or honeycombing. Notably, the RA-PF group included two groups of patients: (1) those who had an established clinical diagnosis of ILD (defined by respiratory symptoms and/or abnormal lung function on pulmonary function tests) confirmed by a board-certified pulmonologist, and (2) patients with RA who were identified as having fibrosis on research-based screening HRCT, although they did not have a prior clinical diagnosis of ILD. Subjects were excluded from the study if there was isolated radiographic evidence of non-fibrotic ILD, including isolated ground glass opacities. Subjects were also excluded if they reported oral antibiotic use in the three months prior to enrollment.

Sputum collection

After consent was obtained, participants underwent collection of an induced sputum sample using our established protocols. Briefly, sputum was induced using nebulized hypertonic saline inhaled by participants for 15 min. A cell differential was obtained by cytocentrifugation, and samples were considered adequate sputum quality if squamous epithelial cells were < 80%. Sputum was then diluted with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) followed by mechanical homogenization using syringe-based protocols. An unspun sputum sample was stored at -80 degrees C for microbial testing, described below.

Lung disease severity

Pulmonary function tests (PFT) and HRCT were obtained within 3 months of sputum collection to assess lung disease severity in participants with RA-PF. Specific PFT parameters that were evaluated included percent predicted forced vital capacity (%FVC) and percent predicted diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (%DLCO). The extent of lung fibrosis was quantified using a data-driven textural analysis (DTA), as previously described, with DTA-fibrosis scores representing the total extent of lung fibrosis, including reticular abnormalities, honeycombing, and traction bronchiectasis, expressed as a percentage of total lung volume [14]. A fibrosis score of at least 1% was considered representative of measurable fibrosis.

Microbial profiling

Sputum samples underwent Multiplex Illumina sequencing to determine the microbial community present. DNA extraction was performed using the Qiagen EZ1 Advanced System that uses magnetic particles to bind DNA. Bacterial load was determined using a TaqMan quantitative PCR assay 24 [15]. Bacterial community analysis was performed using primers that target V1/2 region (27 F/338R) of the ribosomal 16s rRNA gene. DNA was amplified in triplicate with barcoded PCR primers that include adaptors for the Illumina MiSeq sequencing platform and include a negative PCR control for each barcode. Amplicons were pooled after normalization of DNA concentration and sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq platform. Illumina MiSeq paired-end reads were aligned to human reference genome Hg19 with bowtie2 and matching sequences discarded. Paired-end sequences were sorted by sample via barcodes in the paired reads with a python script. Sorted paired end sequence data were deposited in the NCBI Short Read Archive under accession number (NCBI submission in progress). The sorted paired reads were assembled using phrap. Pairs that did not assemble were discarded. Assembled sequence ends were trimmed over a moving window of 5 nucleotides until average quality met or exceeded 20. Trimmed sequences with more than 1 ambiguity or shorter than 250 nt were discarded. Potential chimeras identified with Uchime (usearch6.0.203_i86linux32) using the Schloss Silva reference sequences were removed from subsequent analyses. Assembled sequences were aligned and classified with SINA (1.3.0-r23838) using the 479,726 bacterial sequences in Silva 115NR as reference configured to yield the Silva taxonomy. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were produced by clustering sequences with identical taxonomic assignments.

Statistical analysis

Raw read counts corresponding to constant taxonomic features or those only appearing in one sample were removed from the OTU table. The remaining features were further filtered according to a minimum abundance threshold of 4 counts, a minimum prevalence threshold of 10%, and a minimum variance threshold of 10% based on inter-quantile range (IQR). The filtered read counts were then normalized using the total-sum scaling (TSS) method. Alpha diversity was assessed using Mann-Whitney U testing of varying indices (Observed richness – measure of the number of unique taxa present, Simpson – measure emphasizing community evenness, and Shannon – measure incorporating both richness and evenness) and beta diversity was assessed using PERMANOVA with 999 permutations. Taxonomic specific differences were assessed across every taxonomic level using filtered and normalized read counts using three separate statistical methodologies: DESeq2, EdgeR, and Mann-Whitney U test. P-values generated by each statistical method were adjusted using the Benjamin-Hochberg method with an FDR threshold of 0.1.

Results

Patient demographics

Fifty-four patients were included in the study, 22 RA-PF and 32 RA-no-PF. Individuals with RA-PF were significantly older (mean age, 67.7 vs. 48.9 years, p < 0.001), had a lower proportion of females (45% vs. 91%, p < 0.001), and a higher proportion of former/current smokers (64% vs. 31%, p = 0.027) compared to RA-no-PF (Table 1). The mean DTA in individuals with RA-PF was 13.3%.

Community differences between RA-PF and RA-no-PF

When assessing alpha diversity, RA-PF subjects exhibited a lower observed richness compared to RA-no-PF (Supplementary Fig. 1A), but there were no significant differences in Shannon or Simpson indices (Supplementary Fig. 1B and 1 C). There were no differences in beta diversity between groups (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Taxonomic specific differences between RA-PF and RA-no-PF

A total of 153 unique taxa at all levels were identified amongst all samples. Three different methodologies were used to assess taxa specific differences across all levels between RA-PF and RA-no-PF including DESeq2, EdgeR, and Mann-Whitney U test. Significant differences were found in 6 taxa using DESeq2, 54 taxa using EdgeR, and 7 taxa using Mann-Whitney U testing when using an FDR adjusted p-value < 0.1 (Figs. 1A-B, Supplementary Table 1). There were 3 taxa that were significantly different across all three methodologies (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table 1), and all 3 encoded for Lautropia, which was significantly decreased in RA-PF compared to RA-no-PF (Fig. 2).

Lautropia abundance in RA-PF compared to RA-no-PF

Given that several individuals had no detectable Lautropia, sputum samples were categorized as Lautropia-negative when the relative abundance was < 0.01% and Lautropia-positive when it was > 0.01%. The threshold of 0.01% was selected to allow for the identification of very low abundant taxa that are likely biologically important while also minimizing non-specific background detection. A cutoff of 0.01% has been used in a similar fashion in prior microbiome studies, including the sputum microbiome [16, 17]. In univariate logistic regression analysis, female sex (OR 4.91, p = 0.018) and former/current smoking status (OR = 3.33, p = 0.035) showed a significant positive association with Lautropia-positive individuals, while age (OR 0.95, p = 0.018) and having RA-PF (OR = 0.10, p < 0.001) showed a significant negative association with Lautropia (Supplemental Table 2). In sensitivity analyses using alternate relative abundance cutoffs of 0.002% and 0.05%, RA-PF remained the only variable that was independently associated with the presence of Lautropia (Supplemental Table 3). In multivariate models adjusted for age, sex, and smoking history, RA-PF remained independently associated with undetectable levels of Lautropia (OR: 0.14, p = 0.023, Supplemental Table 2).

In individuals with RA-PF, there was no difference in % FVC (66.3 vs. 75.8, p = 0.320) and % DLCO (81.9 vs. 88.6, p = 0.482) but did show a trend toward higher quantitative fibrosis (15.6 vs. 6.1, p = 0.231) in Lautropia-negative individuals compared to Lautropia-positive individuals.

Discussion

Alterations in the microbiome are frequently observed in patients with RA and can shape immune function. While cross-talk between mucosal sites can occur, in organ-specific diseases like ILD, microbial-host interactions within the affected lung tissue are likely to exert a substantive impact on local pathobiology. Our study assessed the microbiome of the lung in RA patients with and without lung fibrosis. We identified a significantly decreased observed richness in RA patients with lung fibrosis without significant changes in Shannon/Simpson indices or β-diversity which suggests possible loss of low abundance taxa. We subsequently identified that the absence of the genera Lautropia was independently associated with the presence of lung fibrosis and showed a trend toward worse pulmonary physiology and more lung fibrosis.

Lautropia is a commensal gram-negative bacteria found at low abundance in healthy lungs and oral cavities. While our findings raise the possibility that Lautropia may be linked to lung tissue homeostasis or anti-fibrotic pathways, these observations are associative and do not establish causality. The direct cellular effects of Lautropia have not been previously reported, although commensal-mediated lung homeostasis has been described; in particular, certain Streptococcus and Rothia species are associated with inhibition of lung epithelial IL-8 secretion, and their depletion is associated with lung pathology [18, 19]. There is also evidence of certain microbes being associated with protection specifically from fibrosis, where Faecalibacterium prausnitzii can reprogram macrophages toward an antifibrotic phenotype [20], and Lactobacillus genera can inhibit TGF-β-induced fibrotic pathways, including myofibroblast activation [21, 22]. While Lautropia has not been previously implicated in mechanistic studies, it is of interest that it has been found to be significantly lower in relative abundance in the oral mucosa of patients with Sjogren’s syndrome compared to controls [23, 24].

Identifying the mechanisms that drive lung fibrosis in RA-ILD is critical, as fibrotic forms of ILD are more often progressive and associated with higher mortality in RA. Although recent advances have led to the approval of antifibrotic therapies in progressive pulmonary fibrosis, these medications only modestly slow the decline in lung function, do not reverse established disease, and can be challenging for patients to tolerate [25]. New therapeutic avenues are needed and emerging research is expanding microbiome-modulating strategies to the lung, including direct approaches such as inhaled probiotics or targeted delivery systems, as well as indirect strategies that modify the gut microbiome to leverage the gut-lung axis [26–28]. Importantly, recent studies evaluating the use of antibiotic therapy in IPF failed to demonstrate clinical benefit raising the possibility that disruption of commensal microbial communities could potentially disrupt lung homeostasis and contribute to fibrotic progression [29–31]. Strengths of our study include its unique focus on the lung microbiome in RA patients with fibrotic ILD, where data remain limited, and the use of CT imaging to ensure that our comparator group had no evidence of ILD. Moreover, our main finding of decreased sputum Lautropia in RA-PF was replicated across three independent analytic approaches.However, there are also several limitations to our study. The cross-sectional and correlative approach to our work limits its role in defining the mechanistic role and causative effects of lung microbial dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of ILD in RA, and future large scale, longitudinal studies will be needed to assess whether Lautropia is associated with progression of ILD. Age, sex, and smoking are known to influence the lung microbiome and while we did adjust for these in logistic regression models, we cannot completely remove the potential for residual confounding of these variables given the differences between the study populations. Also, the impact of chronic immunosuppression on alterations in the lung microbiome is poorly understood, and we were not able to control for immunosuppressive use in our study given the sample size. Lastly, while our use of non-invasive sampling of the lung with induced sputum allowed for a larger sample size, sputum does sample a more proximal airway microenvironment compared to BALF and can capture microbes from the oral mucosa. However, it has been shown that microorganisms within the oral cavity and proximal airways can influence distal composition through translocation and there can be a modest overlap between these communities [32].

In conclusion, this is the first study to identify distinct lung microbial dysbiosis in a population of RA patients with and without pulmonary fibrosis and the association with lung disease severity. Future studies will be needed to more clearly define the mechanistic role of the lung microbiome in the pathogenesis of ILD in RA.
