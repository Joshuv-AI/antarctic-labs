SDS-LoRA: Overcoming Anisotropic Gradient Scaling in Low-Rank Adaptation
Junghun Oh, Sungyong Baik, Kyoung Mu Lee

Abstract
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) enables efficient adaptation of large pre-trained models to downstream tasks by parameterizing weight updates with low-rank matrices. In this paper, we investigate the limitations of the LoRA parameterization from a geometric perspective. Specifically, we show that when a full fine-tuning gradient is backpropagated to the low-rank matrices, it undergoes anisotropic scaling driven by their singular values. We argue that this phenomenon is undesirable because it distorts the full fine-tuning gradient by skewing it toward dominant singular directions while suppressing others. Our analyses demonstrate that anisotropic gradient scaling reduces the effective rank of the low-rank matrices’ gradients and results in suboptimal alignment between the full fine-tuning gradient and its low-rank approximation in LoRA, thereby exacerbating the gap to full fine-tuning. To address these limitations, we propose a new low-rank parameterization, SDS-LoRA, which S tructurally D ecouples S ingular values from the backward pass. Our method ensures that the full fine-tuning gradient backpropagates only through the orthonormal bases of the low-rank matrices’ subspaces, independent of their scales. Convergence analysis demonstrates that while LoRA’s convergence rate degrades with the condition number of the low-rank matrices, SDS-LoRA remains independent of it. Experimental results across natural language and vision benchmarks show that SDS-LoRA improves loss convergence and reduces the gap to full fine-tuning, significantly enhancing adaptation performance.

Although LoRA has attracted significant attention for its efficiency, a substantial performance gap remains relative to full fine-tuning, motivating further improvements to the LoRA framework.
In this paper, we investigate the limitations of LoRA parameterization from a geometric perspective.
Specifically, we note that the optimization dynamics in LoRA are adversely affected by the singular-value structure of the LoRA matrices (i.e.,

We summarize the contribution of our work as follows:

•

We provide a novel perspective on the limitations of LoRA, showing that LoRA suffers from anisotropic gradient scaling induced by the singular values of the LoRA matrices.

•

We demonstrate that anisotropic gradient scaling reduces the effective rank of the LoRA matrices’ gradients and results in a suboptimal approximation of the full fine-tuning gradient.

•

We propose a new parameterization, SDS-LoRA, that overcomes anisotropic gradient scaling in LoRA by structurally decoupling the singular values from the backward pass.

•

SDS-LoRA substantially improves loss convergence and narrows the gap to full fine-tuning, outperforming LoRA and existing methods.

Proposed Method

3.1
Preliminaries

Let
{\bm{W}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}
denote the weight matrix of a linear layer in a pretrained model, with input dimension
d_{\text{in}}
and output dimension
d_{\text{out}}
.
We consider fine-tuning
{\bm{W}}_{0}
to adapt the model to a downstream task. Instead of updating all the parameters of
{\bm{W}}_{0}
, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) assumes a low-rank structure for the desired weight update
11
and learns a low-rank decomposition of the update:

{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}={\bm{W}}_{0}+\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{LoRA}}={\bm{W}}_{0}+s{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}},

(1)

where
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{in}}}
and
{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times r}
are trainable LoRA matrices of rank
r
, and
s
is a constant scaling factor. For a single layer, the total number of trainable parameters in LoRA is
r\times(d_{\text{out}}+d_{\text{in}})
.
Assuming
r\ll\text{min}(d_{\text{out}},d_{\text{in}})
, this is significantly smaller than the
d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}
parameters required in a full fine-tuning scenario.

Let
{\bm{G}}=\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}}{\mathcal{L}}
denote the gradient of the loss
{\mathcal{L}}
with respect to the effective weights.
We refer to
{\bm{G}}
as the
full gradient
, as it is used in full fine-tuning.
By the chain rule, the gradients with respect to the LoRA matrices are given by
\nabla_{\bm{A}}{\mathcal{L}}=s{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}
,
\nabla_{\bm{B}}{\mathcal{L}}=s{\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}
.
Let
{\bm{A}}={\bm{U}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}
and
{\bm{B}}={\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}
be the rank-
r
truncated singular value decompositions (SVD) of the LoRA matrices, where
{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}\times r}
and
{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times r}
have orthonormal columns,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
are the diagonal matrices of singular values, and
{\bm{U}}_{\bm{A}},{\bm{V}}_{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
are orthogonal matrices.
Substituting the SVD representations into the gradient equations, we obtain

\nabla_{\bm{A}}{\mathcal{L}}=s{\bm{V}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}},\>\>\nabla_{\bm{B}}{\mathcal{L}}=s{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}\>\>\>\>\>\text{(in LoRA)}.

(2)

3.2
Adverse Effects of Anisotropic Gradient Scaling via Singular Values

We provide a geometric interpretation of how the full gradient
{\bm{G}}
is backpropagated to yield
\nabla_{\bm{A}}{\mathcal{L}}
and
\nabla_{\bm{B}}{\mathcal{L}}
.
Equation
2
shows that
{\bm{G}}
undergoes the following transformations:

•

{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}
: Acting as partial isometries, these matrices perform isometric projections of
{\bm{G}}
into their respective subspaces.

•

\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}
: If the singular values of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
are non-uniform, these matrices induce anisotropic scaling that skews the projected gradient toward the dominant singular directions.

•

{\bm{U}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}
and
{\bm{V}}_{\bm{B}}
: These matrices perform isometric transformations.

Note that the isometric projection by
{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}
is an inherent bottleneck of low-rank adaptation, and
{\bm{U}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}
and
{\bm{V}}_{\bm{B}}
act merely as isometries, preserving the geometric properties of the projected gradient.

However, the anisotropic scaling through
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}
may be undesirable because it distorts the full gradient by amplifying directions associated with large singular values while relatively suppressing other directions.

As discussed in Section
E
, we observe that the stable ranks of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
are substantially lower than the intrinsic rank
r
, suggesting that their singular values are highly skewed.
To demonstrate the adverse effects of anisotropic gradient scaling resulting from these skewed singular values, we compare the effective rank of the original gradients (i.e.,
s{\bm{V}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}
and
s{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}
) with that of modified gradients that are independent of singular values (i.e.,
s{\bm{V}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}
and
s{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}
).
Figure
1(a)
shows that the effective rank is significantly reduced when the gradients are scaled by the singular values.
These results suggest that anisotropic gradient scaling collapses the rich information of
{\bm{G}}
into a narrower subspace by restricting it to a few dominant directions, potentially limiting LoRA’s learning capacity.

Furthermore, anisotropic gradient scaling causes the optimization dynamics of LoRA to markedly deviate from those of full fine-tuning.
To examine this, we first derive the effective gradient with respect to
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
.
The change in
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
resulting from updates to
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
is given by
\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\approx s(\Delta{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}+{\bm{B}}\Delta{\bm{A}})=-\eta s^{2}({\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}+{\bm{B}}{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}})
where
\eta
is a learning rate.
Substituting the SVD of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
into this, the effective gradient with respect to
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
is expressed as

\tilde{{\bm{G}}}=s^{2}({\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}+{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}})\>\>\>\>\>\text{(in LoRA)}.

(3)

Consequently, LoRA can be interpreted as full fine-tuning with
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}
, a low-rank approximation of
{\bm{G}}

(
42
;
43
;
49
;
47
;
38
)
.
We analyze how the gap between
{\bm{G}}
and
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}
influences the optimization dynamics through the descent lemma
(
33
)
.
Assuming that the loss function
\mathcal{L}
is
\beta
-smooth with respect to
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
, we obtain the following loss decrease bound when
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
is updated by
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}

\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}-\eta\tilde{{\bm{G}}})-\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}})\leq-\eta\langle{\bm{G}},\tilde{{\bm{G}}}\rangle_{F}+\frac{\beta}{2}\eta^{2}||\tilde{{\bm{G}}}||^{2}_{F},

(4)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{F}
and
||\cdot||_{F}
denote the Frobenius inner product and norm, respectively.
Equation
4
suggests that, for a sufficiently small
\eta
, the loss decrease bound is degraded as
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}
diverges from
{\bm{G}}
in terms of the Frobenius inner product.
As such, we consider
\langle{\bm{G}},\tilde{{\bm{G}}}\rangle_{F}
as a measure of the approximation quality of
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}
.
We then derive the following theorem on the conditions required for
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
to improve the approximation quality:

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the gradient approximation quality in LoRA:

\langle{\bm{G}},\tilde{{\bm{G}}}\rangle_{F}=s^{2}\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}+{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}.

Assume
||{\bm{A}}||_{F}^{2}=E_{\bm{A}}
,
||{\bm{B}}||_{F}^{2}=E_{\bm{B}}
, and the row space of
{\bm{A}}
and the column space of
{\bm{B}}
are given.
Then,
\langle{\bm{G}},\tilde{{\bm{G}}}\rangle_{F}
is maximized if
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}=\sqrt{E_{\bm{A}}/r}{\bm{I}}_{r}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}=\sqrt{E_{\bm{B}}/r}{\bm{I}}_{r}
.

See Section
A
for the proof.
Theorem
3.1
suggests that under the same subspace and energy configurations, the gradient approximation quality is optimized when the LoRA matrices have uniform singular values.
Under the optimal condition,
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}
is simplified to
s^{2}(E_{\bm{A}}{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}+E_{\bm{B}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}})
where
{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}
and
{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}
denote the orthogonal projection matrices onto the row space of
{\bm{A}}
(
{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})
) and the column space of
{\bm{B}}
(
{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})
), respectively.
As illustrated in Figure
1(b)
, while the non-uniform singular values distort the projected gradients, the uniform singular values scale the projection equally in all directions, resulting in a maximal alignment between
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}
and
{\bm{G}}
.

3.3
SDS-LoRA: Structural Decoupling of Singular Values from Gradient

Our analysis in Section
3.2
demonstrates the negative effects of non-uniform singular values of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
during the backward pass
However, addressing this limitation is not straightforward, as the roles of singular values in the forward and backward passes conflict.
In the forward pass, the singular values play a crucial role in representing
\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{LoRA}}
.
Enforcing uniform singular values of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
significantly restricts the class of representable weight updates.
1
1

1

By a simple algebraic manipulation, we can see that if both
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
have uniform singular values,
\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{LoRA}}
also has uniform singular values.

Conversely, allowing fully expressive weight updates conflicts with the uniform singular value condition for
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
.

To address this, we propose a new low-rank parameterization of weight updates, named SDS-LoRA, which
S
tructurally
D
ecouples the
S
ingular values of LoRA matrices from the backward pass while preserving their role in the forward pass for representing weight updates.
To do so, we utilize the orthonormal bases of
{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}
and
{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}
.
Specifically, we perform QR decomposition of
{\bm{A}}^{\top}
and
{\bm{B}}
:
{\bm{A}}^{\top}={\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{R}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}={\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{R}}_{\bm{B}}
where the columns of
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
correspond to the orthonormal bases of
{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}
and
{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}
, respectively.
We then formulate the weight updates as follows:

\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{SDS-LoRA}}=s({\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}+{\bm{B}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top})=s[{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}\;{\bm{B}}]\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{A}}\\
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}.

(5)

Importantly, we treat
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
as constants during the backward pass.
This ensures that the LoRA matrices receive gradients only through
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
, while also making training more efficient by eliminating the costly backpropagation through the QR decomposition.
Consequently, the gradients with respect to
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
are expressed as

\nabla_{\bm{A}}{\mathcal{L}}=s{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}},\>\nabla_{\bm{B}}{\mathcal{L}}=s{\bm{G}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}\>\>\>\>\>\>\text{(in SDS-LoRA)}.

(6)

Equation
6
shows that
the full gradient
{\bm{G}}
is backpropagated to the LoRA matrices only through the partial isometries
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
, eliminating the negative effects of anisotropic scaling via singular values.

This can be achieved without compromising the role of singular values in the forward pass, allowing
\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{SDS-LoRA}}
to represent any rank-
r
matrix.

As shown in Figure
1(a)
, SDS-LoRA overcomes the adverse effects of anisotropic gradient scaling, resulting in a higher effective rank in the gradients of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
compared to LoRA.
Moreover, SDS-LoRA narrows the gap between the full gradient
{\bm{G}}
and its low-rank approximation
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}
.
To examine this, we derive the change in the effective weight under the SDS-LoRA formulation,
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}={\bm{W}}_{0}+\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{SDS-LoRA}}
.
Although
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
are treated as constants during the backward pass, we periodically update them to reflect updates in the LoRA matrices, as described in the section on the training procedure.
Taking into account the variations in
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
, the resulting change in
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
is given by
\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\approx s({\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}\Delta{\bm{A}}+\Delta{\bm{B}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}+\Delta{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}+{\bm{B}}\Delta{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top})=-\eta s^{2}({\bm{G}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}+{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}+\Delta{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}+{\bm{B}}\Delta{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top})
.
Consequently, the effective gradient with respect to
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
is expressed as

\tilde{{\bm{G}}}=s^{2}({\bm{G}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}+{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}})+{\bm{E}}\>\>\>\>\>\text{(in SDS-LoRA)},

(7)

where
{\bm{E}}
denotes the terms arising from the change in
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
.
Because
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}={\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}={\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}
, our method achieves the optimal gradient approximation stated in Theorem
3.1
, up to the error described by
{\bm{E}}
.
In Section
D
, we show that the contribution of
{\bm{E}}
is negligible because the
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
change very slowly: the cosine similarity between
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}+\Delta{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
(or
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}+\Delta{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
) remains close to 1 throughout training.
Thus, our method nearly attains the optimal
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}
, substantially reducing the gap to full fine-tuning.

Training Procedure.

Following the standard initialization of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
, we sample
{\bm{A}}
from a uniform distribution and set
{\bm{B}}=0
.
Consequently,
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
cannot be defined from
{\bm{B}}
at the beginning of training.
To address this, we perform a warm-up training of
T
iterations using the standard LoRA formulation.
After the warm-up, we compute the rank-
r
truncated SVD of the weight updates,
\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{LoRA}}={\bm{U}}_{r}\bm{\Sigma}_{r}{\bm{V}}_{r}^{\top}
, and reparameterize it using the SDS-LoRA formulation by setting the LoRA matrices and orthonormal bases as follows:

{\bm{A}}=\frac{1}{2s}\bm{\Sigma}_{r}{\bm{V}}_{r}^{\top},\;{\bm{B}}=\frac{1}{2s}{\bm{U}}_{r}\bm{\Sigma}_{r},\;{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}={\bm{V}}_{r},\;{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}={\bm{U}}_{r}.

(8)

We reset all optimizer states after this process to ensure that the warm-up stage does not affect the subsequent main training stage.
During the main training stage, we recompute
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
as
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
evolve.
Although QR decomposition introduces minor computational overhead, we mitigate this cost by updating
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
intermittently.
To account for the larger learning rates early in training, we update them more frequently in the initial phase.
Specifically, at the
t
-th iteration, we update them if
t\bmod\left\lceil\frac{kt}{T_{\text{total}}}\right\rceil=0
where
k
is a hyperparameter and
T_{\text{total}}
indicates the total iteration.
This means that training is divided into
k
phases, with the update interval in the
i

3.4
Convergence Analysis

In Theorem
3.1
, we utilize the descent lemma to formally characterize the adverse effects of anisotropic gradient scaling.
To demonstrate how overcoming this issue improves loss convergence, we extend this analysis to compare the convergence rates of LoRA and SDS-LoRA.

We first assume that the loss function with respect to
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
satisfies the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) condition, a standard assumption in non-convex optimization used to establish linear convergence rates:
\|\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}}{\mathcal{L}}\|_{F}^{2}\geq 2\mu({\mathcal{L}}({\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}})-{\mathcal{L}}^{*})
where
\mu>0
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
denotes the minimum loss.
While this condition can be restrictive in general deep learning contexts, it is plausible when fine-tuning from a pretrained model, where the loss landscape is typically better conditioned
(
45
)
.
Accordingly, we assume that the PL condition holds in a neighborhood of the pretrained weight
{\bm{W}}_{0}
.
We also assume that the change in
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
is negligible, resulting in
{\bm{E}}\approx 0
in Equation
7
.
See Section
D
for empirical validation of this assumption.
Additionally, let
\alpha\in[0,2]
represent the alignment between
{\bm{G}}
and the low-rank subspaces
{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}
and
{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}
such that:
\|{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}\|_{F}^{2}+\|{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}\|_{F}^{2}=\alpha\|{\bm{G}}\|_{F}^{2}
.
Under these assumptions, we derive the following theorem regarding the linear convergence rates of LoRA and SDS-LoRA:

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that the
\mu
-PL condition holds near the pretrained weight
{\bm{W}}_{0}
, that the loss
{\mathcal{L}}
is
\beta
-smooth and that the subspaces of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
capture the energy of
{\bm{G}}
by
\alpha
.
Then, the linear convergence rates of standard LoRA and SDS-LoRA are given by:

{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1}-{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\leq\begin{cases}(1-\frac{\mu\alpha}{2\beta\kappa^{4}})({\mathcal{L}}_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}^{*})&\mbox{(LoRA)}\\
(1-\frac{\mu\alpha}{2\beta})({\mathcal{L}}_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}^{*})&\mbox{(SDS-LoRA)}\end{cases}

(9)

where
{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
denotes the minimal loss and
\kappa=\frac{\max(\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}),\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{B}}))}{\min(\sigma_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}}),\sigma_{\text{min}}({\bm{B}}))}

See Section
B
for the proof and details.
Theorem
3.2
suggests that as the singular values of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
become more skewed, the condition number
\kappa
increases, thereby degrading the convergence rate of LoRA.
In contrast, since singular values do not influence the backward pass in SDS-LoRA, its convergence rate is independent of
\kappa
.
Our theoretical analysis further highlights the significance of addressing anisotropic gradient scaling in LoRA.

Ablation Studies

To justify the design choice of SDS-LoRA
\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{SDS-LoRA}}=s({\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}+{\bm{B}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top})
, we compare it against the LoRA formulation and a variant where weight updates are defined as
\Delta{\bm{W}}=s{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
, with both
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
being learnable.
Specifically, for this variant, we compute the QR decompositions of
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
after their optimization step and replace them with new ones to ensure that their columns remain orthonormal.
Although this approach prevents anisotropic gradient scaling, its representational capacity is severely limited because the non-zero singular values of the weight updates are forced to be uniform.
In contrast, SDS-LoRA eliminates anisotropic gradient scaling without compromising the representational capacity of weight updates.
As shown in Table

, SDS-LoRA significantly outperforms both standard LoRA and the variant, which only satisfy one of the two desirable properties.
These results suggest that addressing anisotropic gradient scaling in LoRA is not straightforward due to the dual role of singular values in the forward and backward passes.

Conclusion

In this paper, we provide a novel perspective on the limitations of Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA).
Our analysis reveals that LoRA suffers from anisotropic gradient scaling induced by singular values, which distorts the rich information contained in the full fine-tuning gradient, thereby exacerbating the gap to full fine-tuning.
To overcome these limitations, we propose a new parameterization for low-rank weight updates that structurally decouples the singular values from the backward pass. Our convergence analysis confirms that the proposed method eliminates the dependence of the convergence rate on the condition number of the LoRA matrices. Experimental results across various benchmarks and pretrained models show that our method significantly accelerates loss convergence and closes the gap to full fine-tuning, achieving superior adaptation performance.

Appendix A
Proof of
Theorem
3.1

Proof.

Let
{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}
and
{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}
be the orthogonal projection matrices onto the row space of
{\bm{A}}
(
{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})
) and the column space of
{\bm{B}}
(
{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})
), respectively.
We then rewrite
{\bm{G}}
as
{\bm{G}}={\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}+{\bm{G}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})})
or
{\bm{G}}={\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}+({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}){\bm{G}}
.
Substituting this into
\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}+{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}=\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{F}+\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}
, we can simplify each term as follows:

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{F}&=\langle{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})},{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{F}+\langle{\bm{G}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}),{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{F},\\
\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}&=\langle{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}},{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}+\langle({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}){\bm{G}},{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}.\end{split}

(10)

The second terms in Equation
10
become zero because

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{G}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}),{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{F}&=\Tr({\bm{G}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}){\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}^{\top})\\
&=\Tr({\bm{G}}({\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}-{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}){\bm{G}}^{\top}),\\
\langle({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}){\bm{G}},{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}&=\Tr({\bm{G}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}){\bm{G}})\\
&=\Tr({\bm{G}}^{\top}({\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}-{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}){\bm{G}}).\end{split}

Thus, we can rewrite
\langle{\bm{G}},\tilde{{\bm{G}}}\rangle_{F}
as follows:

\langle{\bm{G}},\tilde{{\bm{G}}}\rangle_{F}=s^{2}(\langle{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})},{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}\rangle_{F}+\langle{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}},{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}).

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{G}},\tilde{{\bm{G}}}\rangle_{F}&\leq s^{2}(||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}\cdot||{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}||_{F}+||{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}\cdot||{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}||_{F}),\end{split}

where the equality holds when
{\bm{G}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}=c{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}
and
{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}=d{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}
for some
c>0
and
d>0
.
The sufficient condition for arbitrary
{\bm{G}}
is given by:
{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}=c{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}
and
{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}=d{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}
.
Under this condition, we obtain

\langle{\bm{G}},\tilde{{\bm{G}}}\rangle_{F}=s^{2}(c||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2}+d||{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}).

(11)

From the energy assumptions, we derive
||{\bm{A}}||_{F}^{2}=\Tr({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}})=\Tr({\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top})=\Tr(c{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})})=cr=E_{\bm{A}}
and
||{\bm{B}}||_{F}^{2}=\Tr({\bm{B}}{\bm{B}}^{\top})=\Tr({\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top})=\Tr(d{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})})=dr=E_{\bm{B}}
, leading to
c=\frac{E_{\bm{A}}}{r}
and
d=\frac{E_{\bm{B}}}{r}
.
Therefore, we can rewrite Equation
11
as

\langle{\bm{G}},\tilde{{\bm{G}}}\rangle_{F}=\frac{s^{2}}{r}(E_{\bm{A}}||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2}+E_{\bm{B}}||{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}).

(12)

Since all the terms except for
{\bm{G}}
in Equation
12
are fixed, we can conclude that
\langle{\bm{G}},\tilde{{\bm{G}}}\rangle_{F}
is maximized for arbitrary
{\bm{G}}
if
{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}^{2}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}=\frac{E_{\bm{A}}}{r}{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}
and
{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}=\frac{E_{\bm{B}}}{r}{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}
.
Since
{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{R}({\bm{A}})}={\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{V}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}
, and
{\bm{P}}_{\mathcal{C}({\bm{B}})}={\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}
, this condition is equivalent to

\begin{split}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{A}}&=\sqrt{\frac{E_{\bm{A}}}{r}}{\bm{I}}_{r},\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{B}}&=\sqrt{\frac{E_{\bm{B}}}{r}}{\bm{I}}_{r}.\end{split}

Details and Proof of Theorem

In this section, we provide details and proof of Theorem
3.2
.
We first formalize the assumptions and definitions for the theorem.

Assumption B.1
.

(
\beta
-smoothness
)
The loss function
\mathcal{L}
is
\beta
-smooth with respect to
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
:

{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}+\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}})\leq{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}})+\underbrace{\langle{\bm{G}},\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\rangle_{F}}_{\text{Descent}}+\underbrace{\frac{\beta}{2}||\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}||_{F}^{2}}_{\text{Penalty}}.

(13)

Assumption B.2
.

(
Local PL condition
)
The loss function
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the
\mu
-Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) condition near the pretrained weight
{\bm{W}}_{0}
:
||\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}}\mathcal{L}||_{F}^{2}\geq 2\mu(\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}})-\mathcal{L}^{*})
for
\|{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}-{\bm{W}}_{0}\|_{F}\leq\epsilon
, where
{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
denotes the minimal loss.
We assume that
\|{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}-{\bm{W}}_{0}\|_{F}\leq\epsilon
remains satisfied throughout training.

Assumption B.3
.

(
Slow subspace change in SDS-LoRA
)
In SDS-LoRA, the change in
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
is negligible after update of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
(i.e,
\Delta{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}\approx 0
and
\Delta{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}\approx 0
).

See Section
D
for empirical validation of Assumption
B.3
.

Definition B.4
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,2]
represent the alignment between
{\bm{G}}
and the low-rank subspaces
{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}
and
{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}
such that

||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2}+||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}=\alpha||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}.

(14)

In Equation
13
, there are two terms to determine the loss decrease bound: the descent term and the penalty term.
We first derive lemmas on these terms.

Lemma B.5
.

(
Descent term
)
For LoRA, the descent term in Equation
13
is bounded as follows:

\langle{\bm{G}},\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\rangle_{F}\leq-\alpha\eta s^{2}\sigma_{\min}^{2}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}\>\>\>\>\>\>\text{(in LoRA)},

(15)

where
\sigma_{\min}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})=\min(\sigma_{\min}({\bm{A}}),\sigma_{\min}({\bm{B}}))
.
For SDS-LoRA, the descent term is given by

\langle{\bm{G}},\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\rangle_{F}=-\alpha\eta s^{2}||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}\>\>\>\>\>\>\text{(in SDS-LoRA)}.

(16)

Proof.

1. LoRA

For LoRA, the gradients with respect to
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
are given by:
\nabla_{\bm{A}}{\mathcal{L}}=s{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}
and
\nabla_{\bm{B}}{\mathcal{L}}=s{\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}
.
Consequently, the effective weight update is derived as:

\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\approx s({\bm{B}}\Delta{\bm{A}}+\Delta{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}})=s({\bm{B}}(-\eta s{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}})+(-\eta s{\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}){\bm{A}})=-\eta s^{2}({\bm{B}}{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}+{\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}})
.
Substituting this into the descent term, we obtain

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{G}},\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\rangle_{F}&=\langle{\bm{G}},-\eta s^{2}({\bm{B}}{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}+{\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}})\rangle_{F}\\
&=-\eta s^{2}(\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{B}}{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}+\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}\rangle_{F})\\
&=-\eta s^{2}(||{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}+||{\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}||_{F}^{2}).\end{split}

(17)

We relate these terms to the projections onto the subspaces
{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})
and
{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})

{\bm{X}}
and
{\bm{B}}

||{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}||_{F}^{2}=||{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{X}}||_{F}^{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}^{2}({\bm{B}})||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{X}}||_{F}^{2}

\begin{split}||{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}&\geq\sigma_{\min}^{2}({\bm{B}})||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2},\\

Thus, using
\sigma_{\min}^{2}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})=\min(\sigma_{\min}^{2}({\bm{A}}),\sigma_{\min}^{2}({\bm{B}}))
and Equation
14
, we obtain

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{G}},\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\rangle_{F}&\leq-\eta s^{2}\sigma_{\min}^{2}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})(||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}+||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2})\\

(19)

Under Assumption

\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\approx s({\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}\Delta{\bm{A}}+\Delta{\bm{B}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}+\Delta{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}+{\bm{B}}\Delta{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top})\approx-\eta s^{2}({\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}+{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})})
.
Substituting this into the inner product:

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{G}},\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\rangle_{F}&\approx\langle{\bm{G}},-\eta s^{2}({\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}+{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})})\rangle_{F}\\
&=-\eta s^{2}(\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}+\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}\rangle_{F}).\end{split}

(20)

For any matrix
{\bm{X}}
and projection
{\bm{P}}
,
\langle{\bm{X}},{\bm{P}}{\bm{X}}\rangle_{F}=\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}{\bm{X}})=\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}{\bm{X}})=||{\bm{P}}{\bm{X}}||_{F}^{2}

Applying this to our terms:

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}\rangle_{F}&=||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2},\\
\langle{\bm{G}},{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}\rangle_{F}&=\langle{\bm{G}}^{\top},{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}^{\top}{\bm{G}}^{\top}\rangle_{F}=||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

Finally, applying Equation

, we obtain

\begin{split}\langle{\bm{G}},\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}\rangle_{F}&=-\eta s^{2}(||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2}+||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2})\\
&=-\alpha\eta s^{2}||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

Lemma B.6
.

(

is given by

(23)

For SDS-LoRA, the bound for the penalty term is given by

\text{Penalty}_{\text{SDS-LoRA}}\leq\alpha\beta\eta^{2}s^{4}||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}\>\>\>\>\>\>\text{(in SDS-LoRA)}.

(24)

where
\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})=\max(\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}),\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{B}}))
,
\sigma_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})=\min(\sigma_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}}),\sigma_{\text{min}}({\bm{B}}))
, and
\kappa=\frac{\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})}{\sigma_{\text{min}}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})}
.

Proof.

We bound the penalty term
\frac{\beta}{2}||\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}||_{F}^{2}
by relating it to the parameter (
\bm{\theta}=({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})
) updates.
For any factorization
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}={\bm{W}}_{0}+s{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}
(or involving
{\bm{Q}}
), the triangle inequality and sub-multiplicative property of norms give us

\begin{split}||\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}||_{F}&=s||{\bm{B}}\Delta{\bm{A}}+\Delta{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}||_{F}\\
&\leq s(||{\bm{B}}||_{2}||\Delta{\bm{A}}||_{F}+||\Delta{\bm{B}}||_{F}||{\bm{A}}||_{2})\\
&\leq s\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})(||\Delta{\bm{A}}||_{F}+||\Delta{\bm{B}}||_{F}),\end{split}

(25)

where
\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})=\max(\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{A}}),\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{B}}))
.
Using
(x+y)^{2}\leq 2x^{2}+2y^{2}
, we obtain

||\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}||_{F}^{2}\leq 2s^{2}\sigma_{\text{max}}^{2}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})(||\Delta{\bm{A}}||_{F}^{2}+||\Delta{\bm{B}}||_{F}^{2}).

(26)

1. LoRA

We relate the norm of
\Delta{\bm{A}}=-\eta s{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}
and
\Delta{\bm{B}}=-\eta s{\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}
to the subspace projections:

\begin{split}||\Delta{\bm{A}}||_{F}^{2}&=\eta^{2}s^{2}||{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}=\eta^{2}s^{2}||{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}\leq\eta^{2}s^{2}\sigma_{\text{max}}^{2}({\bm{B}})||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2},\\
||\Delta{\bm{B}}||_{F}^{2}&=\eta^{2}s^{2}||{\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}||_{F}^{2}=\eta^{2}s^{2}||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}{\bm{A}}^{\top}||_{F}^{2}\leq\eta^{2}s^{2}\sigma_{\text{max}}^{2}({\bm{A}})||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

(27)

Substituting these into Equation
26
:

\begin{split}||\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}||_{F}^{2}&\leq 2s^{2}\sigma_{\text{max}}^{2}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})\left(\eta^{2}s^{2}\sigma_{\text{max}}^{2}({\bm{B}})||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}+\eta^{2}s^{2}\sigma_{\text{max}}^{2}({\bm{A}})||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2}\right)\\
&\leq 2\eta^{2}s^{4}\sigma_{\text{max}}^{4}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})\left(||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}+||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(28)

Applying Equation
14
:

||\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}||_{F}^{2}\leq 2\alpha\eta^{2}s^{4}\sigma_{\text{max}}^{4}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}.

(29)

The penalty is therefore
\frac{\beta}{2}||\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}||_{F}^{2}\leq\alpha\beta\eta^{2}s^{4}\sigma_{\text{max}}^{4}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}})||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}
.

2. SDS-LoRA

Since
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}
have orthonormal columns/rows, their spectral norms are 1. Thus,
\sigma_{\text{max}}({\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}},{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}})=1
.
Using the update rules
\Delta{\bm{A}}=-\eta s{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}
and
\Delta{\bm{B}}=-\eta s{\bm{G}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}
:

\begin{split}||\Delta{\bm{A}}||_{F}^{2}&=\eta^{2}s^{2}||{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}=\eta^{2}s^{2}\Tr({\bm{G}}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}})=\eta^{2}s^{2}||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}.\\
||\Delta{\bm{B}}||_{F}^{2}&=\eta^{2}s^{2}||{\bm{G}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}||_{F}^{2}=\eta^{2}s^{2}\Tr({\bm{G}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{Q}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}^{\top})=\eta^{2}s^{2}||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

(30)

Substituting these into Equation
26
with
\sigma_{\text{max}}=1
:

\begin{split}||\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}||_{F}^{2}&\leq 2s^{2}\cdot 1^{2}\cdot\eta^{2}s^{2}(||{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}+||{\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}||_{F}^{2}).\end{split}

(31)

Applying Equation
14
:

||\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}||_{F}^{2}\leq 2\alpha\eta^{2}s^{4}||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}.

(32)

The penalty is therefore
\frac{\beta}{2}||\Delta{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}||_{F}^{2}\leq\alpha\beta\eta^{2}s^{4}||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{t}={\mathcal{L}}({\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}})
and

1. LoRA

Substitute the Descent and Penalty lemmas for LoRA:

{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1}\leq{\mathcal{L}}_{t}-\alpha\eta s^{2}\sigma_{\min}^{2}||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}+\alpha\beta\eta^{2}s^{4}\sigma_{\max}^{4}||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}.

(33)

We set

\eta^{*}

(34)

Substituting
\eta^{*}
back into Equation
33
:

\begin{split}{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1}&\leq{\mathcal{L}}_{t}-\alpha s^{2}\sigma_{\min}^{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{2\beta s^{2}\sigma_{\max}^{4}}\right)||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}+\alpha\beta s^{4}\sigma_{\max}^{4}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\min}^{2}}{2\beta s^{2}\sigma_{\max}^{4}}\right)^{2}||{\bm{G}}||_{F}^{2}\\

Applying the PL condition:

\begin{split}{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1}&\leq{\mathcal{L}}_{t}-\frac{\alpha}{4\beta\kappa^{4}}2\mu({\mathcal{L}}_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}^{*})\\
{\mathcal{L}}_{t+1}-{\mathcal{L}}^{*}&\leq(1-\frac{\mu\alpha}{2\beta\kappa^{4}})({\mathcal{L}}_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}^{*}).\end{split}

(36)

Appendix G
Comparison to Preconditioning-Based Methods

Several previous works
[
49
,
43
,
47
,
38
]
attempt to minimize the gap between the full fine-tuning gradient
{\bm{G}}
and its low-rank approximation
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}
described in Equation
3
.
Specifically, they modify the original gradient of the LoRA matrices as follows:
\tilde{\nabla}_{\bm{A}}{\mathcal{L}}=s({\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{B}})^{-1}{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}
and
\tilde{\nabla}_{\bm{B}}{\mathcal{L}}=s{\bm{G}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}({\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{\top})^{-1}
where
({\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{B}})^{-1}
and
({\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{\top})^{-1}
act as preconditioners.
Consequently, the effective gradient of
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
becomes
\tilde{{\bm{G}}}=s^{2}({\bm{G}}{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{R}}({\bm{A}})}+{\bm{P}}_{{\mathcal{C}}({\bm{B}})}{\bm{G}})
, achieving the optimal gradient approximation stated in Theorem
3.1
.

Although these preconditioning-based methods also achieve optimal gradient approximation, our work differs from theirs in several key aspects.
First, our work focuses on addressing the adverse effects of anisotropic scaling induced by singular values, whereas prior works primarily aim to improve the quality of gradient approximation.
While both approaches yield the same update rule in the effective weight space
{\bm{W}}_{\text{eff}}
, preconditioning-based methods fail to eliminate the influence of singular values in the gradients of the LoRA matrices.
Substituting the SVD of the LoRA matrices into
\tilde{\nabla}_{\bm{A}}{\mathcal{L}}
, we obtain
\tilde{\nabla}_{\bm{A}}{\mathcal{L}}=s{\bm{V}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{G}}
, indicating that these methods do not address the adverse effects of anisotropic scaling induced by singular values.
Second, because preconditioning-based methods modify the original gradients, they do not guarantee the steepest descent direction in the parameter space. The descent lemma suggests that deviations from the true gradient may lead to a weaker bound on loss reduction.
Indeed, Wang et al.
[
43
]
show that performance degrades as the modified gradients diverge from the original gradients.
The results in Figure
3(a)
demonstrate that SDS-LoRA achieves superior loss convergence compared to preconditioning-based methods.
Third, modifying the original gradients requires explicitly tracking optimizer momentum (e.g., in Adam).
These methods introduce additional mechanisms to adjust the momentum based on the modified gradients, which may hinder practical adoption.
In contrast, SDS-LoRA structurally decouples the influence of singular values from the gradients without modifying the natural gradient, allowing straightforward adoption.

In the proposed method, the effective gradient with respect to the full weight space deviates slightly from the optimal gradient, as represented by

Although we demonstrate empirically in Section

Broader Impacts.

This research enhances the performance of LoRA, enabling low-rank adaptation to achieve results comparable to full fine-tuning.
By improving the efficacy of parameter-efficient fine-tuning, our work allows high-quality model customization even in resource-constrained settings.
This contributes to the development of more accurate and reliable AI systems across various domains.
While improved performance could be utilized for unintended purposes, its primary impact lies in fostering robust and high-performing open-source models that do not require massive computational overhead.
