Diagnosis of the weakened global land carbon sink enhancement in recent decades: When and where the shifts occurred
Authors: Fusheng Jiao, Xiaojuan Xu, Haibo Gong, Chuanzhuang Liang, Jie Qiu, Kun Zhang, Jing Liu, Naifeng Lin, Changxin Zou, Huiyu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe terrestrial carbon sink is a major regulator of atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) growth, yet its long-term trajectory and stability remain uncertain. Linear trend-based assessments may obscure nonlinear evolution and trend shifts. Here, we reassess global terrestrial carbon sink dynamics from 1981 to 2020 using ensemble outputs from dynamic global vegetation models combined with ensemble empirical mode decomposition. We find that although the global land carbon sink has approximately doubled, its rate of enhancement has declined sharply, falling to nearly half of its early-period value. This slowdown is spatially heterogeneous, with widespread stagnation or reversals across equatorial and northern temperate regions, slower growth in boreal and southern temperate zones, and sustained increases mainly limited to the northern equatorial region. The fraction of land exhibiting positive trends has declined since the late 1990s, a signal largely masked by linear analyses. Dominance of interannual variability indicates low temporal stability, challenging linear, magnitude-based assessments of land-based climate mitigation potential.

Introduction

As a natural-based solution, terrestrial ecosystems annually absorbed approximately one-quarter of anthropogenic carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions and effectively mitigated global warming.1 Given the great contribution of land carbon sink to emissions reduction, understanding the historical trends is essential for addressing climate change.2 The land sink is comparable in size to the ocean sink, but it exhibits greater variability, with fluctuations several times more than the ocean sink.3 Understanding the variations of the land carbon sink is crucial for predicting future atmospheric CO2 levels, particularly in achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement.4 Such insights are increasingly important as climate-driven fluctuations in the land carbon sink may become more frequent and intense, potentially impacting its capacity to offset CO2 emissions.

The terrestrial carbon sink is commonly quantified as net ecosystem productivity (NEP), defined as the balance between carbon uptake through plant photosynthesis and carbon release via ecosystem respiration.5 As such, the magnitude of the land carbon sink reflects the combined effects of vegetation growth and respiratory losses across terrestrial ecosystems. Since the 1970s, a substantial body of research has focused on changes in the size of the terrestrial carbon sink at regional to global scales.6,7,8,9,10 A widely reported finding is the pronounced warming-induced enhancement of carbon uptake in high-latitude regions of the Northern Hemisphere since the mid-20th century.11

Despite this apparent long-term increase, recent studies have reported conflicting evidence regarding whether the global terrestrial carbon sink has weakened during the recent warming hiatus.12 These inconsistencies suggest that current understanding of recent carbon sink dynamics remains incomplete. In particular, it remains unclear whether the observed discrepancies arise from genuine changes in ecosystem carbon uptake or from limitations in the analytical approaches used to characterize NEP trends. Consequently, accurately reassessing the recent dynamics of the global terrestrial carbon sink and identifying possible shifts in NEP trajectories have become critical research priorities. To address these challenges, two key issues must first be examined.

The first issue concerns the methodological treatment of long-term trends in the terrestrial carbon sink. Numerous studies have reported a significant enhancement of the land carbon sink, most of which rely on linear regression to characterize NEP trends.13,14,15 Linear trend analysis implicitly assumes a constant rate of change over time, an assumption that is often inconsistent with the intrinsic properties of NEP time series. Carbon flux dynamics are inherently nonlinear and nonstationary, with trend rates that may vary gradually in response to climate variability, disturbances, and long-term environmental change.16 As a consequence, gradual trend deceleration, saturation, or potential reversals in the carbon sink can be obscured when linear methods are applied.17

To address these limitations, segmented or piecewise regression approaches have been widely adopted to analyze nonlinear secular trends by dividing the study period into multiple linear segments.18,19,20,21 While these methods can partially reduce trend masking effects, they depend on predefined breakpoints or assumed temporal structures. Consequently, different breakpoint selections have led to divergent conclusions regarding recent carbon sink dynamics. For example, some studies have reported an acceleration in carbon sink enhancement after the late 1990s based on piecewise regression analyses,22 whereas others, using alternative segmentation strategies, have argued for a largely linear increase in NEP over the past three decades.23 Such inconsistencies highlight that trend assessments based on linear assumptions or predefined timescales may be insufficient to robustly diagnose time-varying carbon sink behavior.

Moreover, under conditions of recent climate variability and the warming hiatus, nonlinear and nonstationary features in NEP dynamics may become increasingly pronounced.22 These challenges underscore the need for adaptive, data-driven approaches that can extract intrinsic, time-varying trends without prescribing fixed functional forms or breakpoints, thereby providing a more robust basis for assessing nonlinear changes in the terrestrial carbon sink.

The second issue concerns the relative role of secular trends in shaping overall variations in the terrestrial carbon sink. A large body of evidence suggests that the global land carbon sink has strengthened since the mid-20th century, with an average increase of approximately 0.1 PgC yr−1.24,25,26 However, most existing assessments quantify this enhancement primarily through linear or piecewise-linear trend analyses, which implicitly assume constant or predefined rates of change. While such approaches are effective for estimating mean trends, they provide limited information on how much of the observed variability in NEP is actually governed by long-term trends, as opposed to interannual fluctuations.

Recent studies indicate that key processes sustaining carbon sink enhancement may be changing, including a weakening CO2 fertilization effect and increasing temperature-related constraints.27,28 At the same time, climate extremes such as droughts and heatwaves are becoming more frequent and intense,29 potentially amplifying short-term variability in carbon fluxes. Under these conditions, an apparent increase in NEP does not necessarily imply a stable or persistent carbon sink, and the stability of long-term carbon sink trends remains poorly constrained. NEP variability consists of both interannual variability (IAV) and secular trend components, each contributing differently to overall carbon sink dynamics. A higher contribution from the long-term trend component indicates that NEP changes are predominantly governed by persistent processes, whereas dominance of IAV suggests a more transient and potentially unstable carbon sink behavior.30 From this perspective, a sustained increase in NEP magnitude alone is insufficient to assess the effectiveness of terrestrial ecosystems in supporting long-term carbon neutrality, particularly if long-term trends contribute only marginally to total variability.

Clarifying the divergent conclusions regarding recent NEP trends therefore requires analytical approaches that can quantify both time-varying trends and their relative contribution to overall variability without imposing a priori assumptions on the trend form or temporal scale. The ensemble empirical mode decomposition (EEMD) method fulfills these requirements by adaptively separating intrinsic trend and variability components, providing a robust framework for assessing the stability of global terrestrial carbon sink dynamics.31

In this study, we integrate an ensemble of global NEP simulations from dynamic global vegetation models (DGVMs) with a nonlinear analytical framework to investigate secular changes in the terrestrial carbon sink from 1981 to 2020. Specifically, we address four key questions: (1) whether nonlinear trend shifts in the terrestrial carbon sink have occurred at the global scale, (2) if such shifts exist, why they have not been consistently identified in previous studies, (3) when and where these trend shifts have emerged, and (4) how changing climatic constraints may be associated with their occurrence.

To address these questions, we first apply both linear and nonlinear trend analyses, using EEMD to extract intrinsic, time-varying secular trends that are often obscured by conventional linear approaches. This allows us to explicitly compare linear and nonlinear trend representations and identify previously unrecognized trend shifts. We then characterize the spatiotemporal trajectories of land carbon sink trends to determine the timing and spatial distribution of these shifts.

Next, we examine the independent influences of key climatic factors on the terrestrial carbon sink and employ a moving-window framework to assess temporal changes in climate sensitivities. In addition, we evaluate the stability of the terrestrial carbon sink by quantifying the relative contribution of long-term trends versus IAV to overall NEP dynamics, providing a variance-based perspective on carbon sink stability.

The novelty of this study does not lie in the EEMD method itself, which has been widely applied in climate and ecological research, but in its use as a diagnostic tool to reassess the long-term trajectory and stability of the terrestrial carbon sink. By combining EEMD with multi-model carbon flux simulations and variance-based stability analysis, we move beyond identifying trend direction to evaluate whether carbon sink enhancement remains dynamically stable or is increasingly dominated by IAV. By reframing carbon sink change as a problem of nonlinear trajectory and stability rather than linear trend magnitude alone, this study provides a different perspective on why apparent carbon sink enhancement may become increasingly uncertain under ongoing climate change.

Results

Spatial patterns of NEP variations

Both the results of DGVMs and the atmospheric inversion models showed that the global NEP increased significantly at an average rate of 0.033 PgC a−2 (Figures 1 and S5). The EEMD-derived trends also showed a monotonic increase. However, since the EEMD trend is time varying, we observed a deceleration in global sink enhancement, with the rate dropping from 0.056 to 0.028 PgC a−2. In the mid-2010s, the EEMD rate fell below the linear slope. Six of the nine DGVM ensembles consistently showed this trend (Figure S4), with LPJ and LPX-Bern exhibiting the greatest deceleration, even reversing from an increase in the carbon sink to a decrease toward the end of the study period. The remaining two models showed a slight acceleration in carbon sink growth. To quantitatively assess whether nonlinear trends provide additional explanatory power beyond linear regression, we compared the variance in NEP explained by the linear trend and the EEMD-derived trend (Figure S12). Across most regions, the EEMD-derived trend explained a larger fraction of NEP variance than the linear trend, with the largest gains occurring in areas characterized by trend reversals or decelerations. This result demonstrates that linear regression tends to underestimate or mask time-varying changes in NEP dynamics, whereas EEMD more effectively captures non-monotonic evolution.

Time-varying trends and rates at different levels

Figure 4 illustrates the changes in NEP trends across different gradients. For latitudinal levels, two major patterns of change were observed. The first pattern was a decline in the proportion of increasing trends in the southern temperate (30–60°S) and northern boreal (60–90°N) zones. In the southern temperate, the ratio of increasing trends initially rose, only to sharply decline by the late 1990s, ultimately returning to the original level. In the northern boreal zone, the proportion of areas showing increasing trends grew by 15% until the late 2010s, after which it gradually decreased by 5%. The second pattern was the saturation of increasing trends in the southern tropical and northern temperate zones. In both areas, the proportion of increasing trends initially rose sharply, but this growth plateaued in the late 2010s. The expansion of increasing trends was more pronounced in the northern mid-latitudes than in the southern low latitudes (84% vs. 80%). Finally, only the northern tropical region showed a continuous increase in the proportion of increasing trends, rising from 66% to around 80%. In terms of AI, hyper-arid and arid areas followed a similar temporal pattern, with the proportion of increasing NEP trends growing slowly by 5% over 40 years. In contrast, the other three areas saw a more significant increase in the proportion of increasing trends, rising from 70% to 85%. However, all regions experienced stagnation in growth after the 2010s. Notably, the ratio of increasing trends in arid areas even began to decline during 2010–2015. By continent, the European Union (EU) consistently had the highest proportion of increasing NEP trends, exceeding 80% throughout the study period. With the exception of Oceania, all continents saw an increase in the proportion of increasing trends, with the largest increases occurring in Africa and Asia. In Oceania, the proportion remained largely unchanged before gradually decreasing after the 2010s. A similar, albeit less pronounced, decline was observed in Europe. Regarding biomes, tundra exhibited the largest increase in the proportion of increasing NEP trends, rising by 20% from an initial 60%, followed by tropical forests. However, tundra’s growth stagnated toward the end of the study period, while tropical forests continued to increase. Croplands consistently had the highest proportion of increasing trends, rising from 80% to nearly 95%. Declining proportions of increasing trends were mainly observed in shrublands and extra-tropical forests, with smaller, non-significant declines seen in savannas and grasslands. We then examined the proportions of instantaneous increasing rates at different levels (Figure 5). A clear pattern emerged: the proportions of instantaneous increasing rates reversed across all areas, although the timing and extent of these reversals varied. Initially, the proportions of increasing NEP rates rose rapidly in all regions. However, after the late 1990s, the proportions of increasing rates for NEP declined across all latitudes, with the southern temperate zone experiencing the first and most pronounced decrease. This decline then spread to the northern boreal, southern tropical, and northern temperate zones, with the weakest reversal occurring in the northern tropical zone. By 2015, the expansion of increasing rates was weaker than at the start of the study period in all latitudes, except for the northern tropical region. At the level of aridity, the reversal in the proportions of increasing rates occurred at the end of the 20th century, with generally lower rates at the end of the study period, particularly in arid areas. In contrast, the timing of the reversal in the hyper-arid zone was less distinct, likely occurring in the 2010s. At the continental scale, the onset of the reversal in the proportions of increasing rates varied. The earliest decline was observed in Europe and Oceania around 1995, followed by Africa, Asia, and North America around 2000. South America experienced the latest reversal, with a significant decrease in the share of increasing rates beginning after 2005. Notably, Oceania exhibited the largest decrease in increasing rates, with a 20% decline by the end of the study period compared to the beginning. At the biome scale, shrublands showed the earliest reversal in the proportion of increasing rates, around 1995, while tropical forests had the latest transition, around 2005. Most other biomes experienced reversals around 2000. By the end of the study period, most biomes had a higher proportion of increasing rates compared to the beginning, except for shrublands and savannas. Shrublands, in particular, saw the largest reduction, with a 20% decline. Sensitivities of NEP to climatic factors

We further examined the spatial distribution of the sensitivities to each factor (Figure 6). The results indicated that temperature generally promoted carbon sink enhancement, with positive effects predominantly observed in the mid-latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere, whereas negative effects were concentrated in equatorial regions and high latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere. Soil moisture exerted a positive influence in approximately 60% of global terrestrial areas, contributing even more to carbon sink growth than temperature. Strong positive effects of soil moisture were primarily concentrated in southern Africa, the southwestern United States, the Indus River basin, and Australia, whereas negative effects were notable in the Congo Basin, East Asia, and the eastern United States. Although the spatial coverage of positive and negative effects of vapor pressure deficit (VPD) was nearly equal, negative effects were considerably stronger, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. In contrast, VPD exhibited moderate positive effects in high-latitude areas of the Northern Hemisphere. Compared to other climatic factors, the influence of radiation was relatively weak overall, but it showed stronger negative effects in high-latitude regions of the Northern Hemisphere and mid-latitude regions of the Southern Hemisphere.Figure 6Spatial patterns and the proportion of area of SIs to temperature, soil moisture, VPD, and radiationThe lower left inset in each image shows the percentage of area with positive (blue, SI > 0) and negative (red, SI < 0) correlations.

We also examined how SIs of different climatic drivers varied along an aridity gradient (Figure S8). A clear inverted U-shaped relationship was found between SItmp and AI. Temperature had the strongest promotive effect in semi-arid regions, but this effect diminished and even reversed in humid regions. SIsm showed a significant negative correlation with AI: the drier the region, the stronger the promotive effect of soil moisture. However, in humid regions, this effect was minimal. The influence of VPD (SIVPD) increased with AI, showing a strong suppressive effect in arid areas that weakened toward humid zones. Radiation exhibited a similar pattern to VPD, although its relationship with AI was weaker.

Using a 15-year moving window, we calculated the SIs in each window and assessed the temporal trends (Figure 7). An increase in SI indicates either an amplification of positive effects or a reduction of negative effects, whereas a decrease in SI suggests either a weakening of positive effects or a strengthening of negative effects. Our results found a significant decline in SItmp across the mid- to high latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere, regions where temperature has historically exerted strong positive effects on carbon sinks. Although increases in temperature sensitivity were also observed across broad regions, their magnitudes were notably weaker than the declining trends. The decline was particularly pronounced in humid regions (Figure S10). For SIsm, decreasing trends dominated over increasing trends and were broadly distributed across latitudes. Nearly all arid regions showed an overall decline in sensitivity, with semi-arid zones exhibiting the strongest reductions. SIVPD also exhibited a dominant declining trend, particularly concentrated in regions with moderate to low temperatures, especially in semi-arid areas. In contrast, the negative effects of VPD were alleviated in humid regions. SIrad showed decreasing trends primarily in the Sahel and southern Africa, especially in arid and semi-arid zones. However, parts of the mid- to high latitudes in the Northern Hemisphere displayed increasing radiation sensitivity, mainly in semi-humid and humid regions. To assess the robustness of the moving-window analysis, we repeated the sensitivity analysis using alternative window lengths (Figure S9). The results show that the spatial patterns and the direction of sensitivity trends remain unchanged across different window sizes, with only minor variations in slope magnitude in some regions. Relative contribution of secular trends

Regions dominated by trends, where variance contribution (VC) > 50%, accounted for only ∼10% of global land area (Figure 8), highlighting the global predominance of IAV. These trend-dominated regions were primarily located in the Brazilian Plateau, Western Europe, Southern China, and Southern India, with VC reaching up to 90%. Many of these areas are terrestrial carbon sink “hotspots,” where large local sinks are critical to the overall global carbon sink and to achieving carbon neutrality. In contrast, IAV-dominated regions, such as the Amazon and Congo River Basin, are experiencing a rare loss of carbon storage due to their unique responses to climate change, which could pose challenges to achieving carbon neutrality. Other regions, such as the Arctic and drylands, may contribute less to the global carbon sink in terms of size, but their trends and IAV are strongly linked to global carbon sink changes.Figure 8Variance contributions of the trends of NEP to the overall variations

To further examine regional differences, we analyzed the magnitude of long-term trend contributions as a proxy for carbon sink stability (Figure 9). From a latitudinal perspective, the north equatorial region exhibits the highest overall stability, slightly surpassing the north temperate zone. At the continental scale, Europe displays the highest stability but also the greatest internal variability, as indicated by a large standard deviation in trend contributions. Other continents show comparable stability levels with less variability. Among aridity zones, the wet zone demonstrates both the highest average stability and the largest variability, consistent with earlier studies. The arid zone, by contrast, shows the lowest stability, likely due to its strong sensitivity to water availability—an important control on productivity and carbon sequestration. Although warming has a more muted effect in drylands, increased wetting may amplify short-term vegetation fluctuations, weakening stability.

Across vegetation types, agricultural lands and savannas exhibit the highest stability, while shrublands and tundra show the lowest. Agricultural ecosystems benefit from active management, enhancing their functional stability. Savannas, adapted to variable environments and drought, also maintain high stability. In contrast, tundra ecosystems, located in alpine and polar regions, are sensitive to climatic fluctuations and exhibit strong IAV, resulting in lower long-term trend contributions and reduced stability.

Discussion

Our results suggested that the linear trend analysis method may fail to capture time-varying changes in land carbon sink trajectories, thereby overlooking aspects of intrinsic trend evolution. Linear methods assume a constant rate of change and therefore may reduce the detectability of nonlinear reversals, particularly when the average rate of change is small. Furthermore, the estimated rate and direction of linear trends can be sensitive to short-term fluctuations and abrupt changes,32 which may lead to substantial variability in the magnitude and direction of the NEP trend with relatively minor changes in the analyzed time span.30 These characteristics limit the ability of linear methods to comprehensively characterize secular trends in terrestrial carbon sinks. To address these limitations, various nonlinear trend analysis techniques, such as segmented regression,33 rolling regression,34 and moving averages,35 have been applied to explore nonlinear characteristics of carbon sink dynamics. Nevertheless, these approaches share a common limitation in that they rely on predetermined timescales and breakpoints.21 If the selected timescale is too short, trends may appear statistically weak, whereas overly long timescales can obscure shorterterm variations and potential trend transitions.4,17 In contrast, adaptive and iterative methods, such as the EEMD approach adopted in this study, provide a flexible framework for diagnosing intrinsic, time-varying trend components without prescribing fixed functional forms. By avoiding predefined functional forms, EEMD enables the extraction of long-term nonlinear changes in NEP that may not be well captured by fixed-parameter models, thereby facilitating interpretation of time-varying dynamics. This comparison does not imply that linear trends are incorrect but rather that they are limited in their ability to represent time-varying dynamics and potential reversals in NEP trajectories.

There is increasing disagreement regarding both the magnitude and, in some regions, direction of recent trends in the global land carbon sink. Previous studies have reported a sustained enhancement of the global land carbon sink, with estimates suggesting that sink strength nearly doubled from the 1960s to the 2010s.2 This enhancement has been commonly attributed to CO2 fertilization effects in tropical regions and warming-related productivity gains in high-latitude areas.36,37,38 Our results suggest that, although the global land carbon sink has continued to increase, the rate of enhancement has slowed in recent decades. While spatially aggregated trends remain predominantly positive, nonlinear trend analysis revealed that NEP trajectories exhibit reversals across a substantial fraction of the terrestrial surface. Moreover, we detected signs indicative of saturation and potential weakening of previous upward trends, with reversals occurring across multiple latitudinal zones and biome types. These patterns may be consistent with a weakening in the capacity of ecosystems to absorb carbon. Vegetation biomass constitutes a major component of the terrestrial carbon sink, and previous studies have estimated that global greening may account for approximately 12% of the observed increase in sink strength. However, emerging browning trends have also been reported, which may contribute to the apparent slowdown in carbon accumulation.28 In parallel, multiple studies suggest that the marginal effects of CO2 fertilization and warming-induced efficiency gains may be diminishing. Together, these processes may help explain the observed decline in the growth rate of the global land carbon sink, although direct causal attribution cannot be established within the scope of this study.

The degree of weakened carbon sink enhancement exhibits pronounced regional heterogeneity. Here, weakened enhancement refers specifically to a deceleration in the rate of increase of the terrestrial carbon sink, rather than an absolute decline in carbon uptake or a definitive structural limit to ecosystem carbon sequestration. Regions in the northern subtropical zone that exhibited the fastest increase in the proportion of positive trends before 2000 also experienced the most rapid decline in increasing rates after 2000. Similar temporal patterns were also observed across dry-humid transition zones and humid regions. As a major contributor to the global terrestrial carbon sink, the mid- to high latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere exhibit carbon sink trends that are strongly associated with ongoing warming. Rising temperatures can enhance carbon uptake by stimulating photosynthesis, particularly under temperature-limited conditions.39 However, warming is also accompanied by increased ecosystem respiration, which can act as a negative feedback on net carbon uptake.40 In cold regions, warming may alleviate thermal limitations and extend the growing season, potentially enhancing carbon sequestration.41 Yet, as temperatures exceed the optimal thresholds for photosynthesis,42 increases in respiration can outpace photosynthetic gains, resulting in lower-than-expected net carbon uptake. Moreover, although warming-induced earlier spring phenology may lengthen the growing season, this benefit can be offset by enhanced autumn respiration, particularly during certain years associated with El Niño events.40 Shifts in phenology may also accelerate carbon turnover and, in some cases, contribute to net carbon release.43 The evolution of the sensitivity indices revealed a clear declining trend across northern mid- to high-latitude regions, indicating that the positive association between warming and carbon sink enhancement has weakened over time (Figures 7 and S9). In addition, warming-induced water stress appears to exert increasing growing constraints on carbon sink capacity. For instance, the positive influence of soil moisture on NEP has weakened at the global scale, while constraints associated with VPD and radiation have intensified in tropical and subtropical regions. These findings indicate a slowdown in terrestrial carbon sink growth and a shift in trend dynamics, especially in the Northern Hemisphere. This emerging pattern may pose additional challenges for nature-based climate solutions aimed at achieving carbon neutrality. Our results highlight the need to revisit long-standing assumptions regarding the long-term resilience and effectiveness of terrestrial carbon sinks under a changing climate.

The magnitude of the long-term trend contribution can be interpreted as an indicator of relative ecosystem stability, reflecting a balance between resistance to short-term disturbances and long-term resilience.30 A high trend contribution implies reduced short-term variability in NEP, indicating comparatively stronger. Despite numerous studies investigating terrestrial carbon sink trends at various spatial scales, the relative contribution of long-term trends to overall carbon flux variability remains poorly constrained. Our analysis indicated that regions dominated by IAV far outnumber those governed by long-term trends, suggesting that short-term fluctuations exerted a substantial influence in shaping global carbon sink dynamics. Areas dominated by long-term trends were mainly located in the Brazilian Plateau, Western Europe, Southern China, and Southern India, where trend contributions exceed approximately 80%. In contrast, IAV dominated in the Amazon and Congo River Basins, where long-term trend contributions typically fall below approximately 20%. This spatial differentiation may be associated with vegetation change patterns. For instance, China and India have been identified as major contributors to global greening, and similar trends have been observed in Western Europe.44,45 These regions tend to exhibit higher contributions from long-term trends, consistent with sustained vegetation growth. Conversely, ongoing deforestation in the Amazon and Congo Basins may contribute to weaker long-term trend contributions, consistent with prior findings.46 Insights from ecosystem stability theory, particularly concepts of resistance and resilience, provide a useful framework for contextualizing these patterns.14,47,48

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, our analysis was based on the ensemble mean of DGVMs, but uncertainties remain because of the divergences among models. In recent years, remote sensing-based estimates of photosynthesis and carbon fluxes have become increasingly mature.49,50 Future studies could benefit from the fusion of multi-source datasets to generate longer, more accurate, and higher resolution carbon flux products, which would help reduce model-related uncertainties and advance understanding of global carbon sink dynamics. Second, although this study focuses on the stability of secular trends in the terrestrial carbon sink, recent studies have reported a strengthening sensitivity of vegetation productivity to compound drought events.51,52 This highlights the need for future attribution analyses that explicitly integrate climate stressors with vegetation physiological traits, in order to better resolve the mechanisms underlying changes in terrestrial carbon sink stability. Third, this study identifies signals indicative of a global slowdown or saturation in the enhancement of the land carbon sink. The terrestrial carbon sink reflects the balance between photosynthetic carbon uptake and ecosystem respiration. The extent to which changes in ecosystem respiration, potentially offsetting or amplifying trends in photosynthesis, contribute to the observed evolution of the terrestrial carbon sink remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

Method details

Carbon fluxes data

We employed monthly simulations of gross primary productivity (GPP), autotrophic respiration (Ra), and heterotrophic respiration (Rh) from an ensemble of nine Dynamic Global Vegetation Models (DGVMs), as provided by the 2021 Global Carbon Budget (GCB) covering the past four decades.53 In line with previous studies, we used the S2 simulations, driven by time-varying atmospheric CO2, and climate change, as a representation of real-world land carbon sink dynamics. We used the simulations from CABLE, IBIS, ISAM, LPJ, LPJ-GUESS, LPX, ORCHIDEE, SDGVM and VISIT models. To address differences in spatial resolution among models, all outputs were resampled to a common 0.5 ° × 0.5 ° grid. Terrestrial ecosystem respiration (TER) was calculated as the sum of Ra and Rh, while net ecosystem productivity (NEP) was derived by subtracting TER from GPP.

In addition, we obtained gridded surface-atmosphere CO2 exchange data for the period 1981–2020 from an atmospheric inversion model: the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS; version 20r2). The native spatial resolution of net ecosystem exchange (NEE) is 1.26 ° × 2.5 °. Monthly outputs were regridded to 0.5 ° × 0.5 ° to ensure spatial consistency. We redefined net carbon exchange as NEP by reversing the sign of NEE values.

Ancillary data

The study selected multiple climatic variables as explanatory factors, including temperature, surface soil moisture, atmospheric vapor pressure deficit (VPD), and downward surface radiation. Temperature, actual vapor pressure, and downward radiation data were obtained from the Climatic Research Unit of the University of East Anglia (CRU) and the Japan Meteorological Agency’s reanalysis dataset (JRA). The combined CRU–JRA v2.2 dataset provides a spatial resolution of 0.5° and covers the period from 1901 to 2020. In addition, surface soil moisture data (0–10 cm depth) were derived from the Global Land Evaporation Amsterdam Model (GLEAM v3.7), which has a spatial resolution of 0.25° and spans the period from 1980 to 2022. The atmospheric VPD refers to the difference between the saturated vapor pressure (SVP) and the actual vapor pressure (AVP) under standard atmospheric conditions. Although Equation 1 recommends calculating vapor pressure deficit (VPD) using daily temperature data, previous studies have shown that VPD estimates derived from monthly mean temperature closely approximate those calculated from daily observations, particularly at large spatial scales.54 In this study, VPD was calculated using monthly mean temperature.

We utilized the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) MCD12C1 annual land cover product (2001–2020) and applied the International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme (IGBP) classification scheme to determine the dominant ecosystem type for each pixel. Analytical framework

To systematically diagnose recent changes in the terrestrial carbon sink, we adopted a stepwise analytical framework that integrates nonlinear trend detection, stability assessment, and climate sensitivity analysis.

First, we quantified long-term trends in global and regional NEP using both conventional linear regression and ensemble empirical mode decomposition (EEMD). This comparison allowed us to identify nonlinear, time-varying trend components that are potentially obscured by linear trend assumptions, thereby addressing whether and how trend shifts in the terrestrial carbon sink have occurred.

Second, based on the EEMD-derived secular trends, we characterized the spatiotemporal trajectories of NEP to determine when and where trend shifts emerged. This step enabled us to map the spatial distribution of trend reversals, deceleration, or saturation across different climate zones and biomes.

Third, we assessed the stability of the terrestrial carbon sink by decomposing NEP variability into long-term trend and interannual variability components. By quantifying the relative contribution of the secular trend to total NEP variance, we evaluated whether carbon sink changes are dominated by persistent trends or by short-term fluctuations.

Finally, we examined the climatic controls on NEP and their temporal evolution by analyzing the sensitivities of NEP to temperature, soil moisture, vapor pressure deficit, and radiation using a ridge regression framework with moving windows. This approach allowed us to investigate how climate sensitivities have changed over time and how these changes may be associated with the observed nonlinear trend shifts and stability patterns.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using annual pixel-level NEP and climate data for 1981–2020. Unless otherwise stated, n represents the number of valid land pixels included in each spatial analysis after masking non-vegetated pixels, unstable land-cover pixels, and missing values. Nonlinear trends

We applied EEMD to extract intrinsic, time-varying secular trends in NEP, enabling the identification of nonlinear trend shifts that cannot be captured by linear regression. EEMD method was used to extract the nonlinear secular trends of annual carbon fluxes.58 EEMD is employed here not as a generic signal-processing tool, but as a diagnostic framework specifically suited to carbon sink time series, which are characterized by strong inter-annual variability, non-stationarity, and potentially nonlinear secular trends. Unlike linear or segmented regression, EEMD does not require predefined breakpoints or functional forms, allowing the intrinsic evolution of carbon sink trajectories to emerge adaptively. Through a ‘sifting’ process (Text S1), the original time series is decomposed into a finite set of intrinsic modal functions (IMF). The decomposition results are presented as follows:(Equation 5)x(t)=∑i=1NIMFi(t)+R(t)where x(t) is the original NEP time series in year t, IMFi(t) is the ith IMF, N is the number of the IMFs (in this study, N is 5), R(t) is the residual trend.

The secular trends of NEP are divided into cumulative trends and instantaneous rates. Given the time-varying characteristics, the cumulative trend is defined as the difference between a specific time t and the starting time t0. Based on the definition of EEMD methods, cumulative trends are either monotonic or have only one extremum. As a result, the trajectory of cumulative trends can be classified into four types (Table S4). The instantaneous rate represents the rate of change at time t and is calculated as the difference between t and the previous time step t-1. This measure reflects real-time changes in the secular trend, a feature difficult to capture with trend analysis methods based on linear assumptions.21,59 These two metrics were used to reveal potential nonlinearities in the NEP trends that may be masked by linear methods.(Equation 6)Tre(t)=R(t)−R(t0)(Equation 7)Rate(t)=R(t)−R(t−1)

To assess the role of the secular trend in the overall variations, we then calculated the variance contribution based on the variance decomposition methods. Although the IMFs derived from EEMD are approximately orthogonal, they are not strictly so, and variance-based metrics should therefore be interpreted as relative rather than exact partitions of variability. To evaluate the potential influence of this non-orthogonality, we assessed the temporal correlation between the EEMD-derived secular trend and the IAV component. The generally low correlations indicate approximate independence between these components, supporting the robustness of the variance-based analysis (Figure S11).

To identify the latitudinal difference, the global land was classified into five latitudinal bands, consisting of Northern Boreal (≥60°N), Northern Temperate (30°–60°N), Northern Tropical (0°–30°N), Southern Tropical (0°S–30°N), and Southern Temperate (30°–60°S).

Ridge regression model

Ridge regression was employed to quantify the independent sensitivities （SIs） of net ecosystem production (NEP) to multiple climatic drivers while accounting for multicollinearity among predictors. Ridge regression extends ordinary least squares by adding an L2 penalty term to the loss function, which is proportional to the sum of squared regression coefficients. This penalty shrinks coefficient magnitudes and stabilizes parameter estimation in the presence of correlated climatic variables.

Prior to ridge regression, both the climatic predictor variables and the response variable (NEP) were standardized to zero mean and unit variance. This standardization was applied to focus on relative statistical relationships between NEP variability and climatic drivers, rather than absolute changes in NEP associated with unit climate variations. Under this framework, the regression coefficients represent standardized response strengths, describing the change in the standard deviation of NEP associated with a one standard deviation change in a climatic factor. These coefficients are used for comparative purposes across factors, regions, and time windows, and are not intended to represent physical sensitivities or causal responses.

Temporal changes in climate sensitivities were assessed using a 15-year moving-window ridge regression. Alternative window lengths were tested to evaluate robustness, and the corresponding results are provided in Figure S9. Ridge regression was implemented using the MATLAB package autoridge from MATLAB Central File Exchange. Other data preprocessing, spatial analysis, visualization, and statistical calculations were conducted using MATLAB. Where applicable, exact test statistics, p values, sample sizes, and definitions of summary statistics are reported in the corresponding Results text, figure legends, tables, and supplemental materials.
