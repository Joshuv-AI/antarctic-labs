Treatment Patterns and Healthcare-Related Costs in Patients with Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease: Insights from a Large US Healthcare Insurer
Authors: Joseph L Smith, Laetitia N’Dri, Zhengzheng Jiang, Mukul Singhal, Joaquim Cristino, Batul Dahodwala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveAtherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is a leading cause of death in the USA. We evaluated lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) and the impact of adherence on healthcare costs among patients with ASCVD.MethodsThis observational cohort study used payor claims and outpatient laboratory data. Adults with ASCVD diagnosed from 1 October 2015 to 31 December 2019 were observed 12 months before, and up to 24 months after, first LLT use (index; categorized as any LLT, statin monotherapy, ezetimibe monotherapy, or anti-proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 monoclonal antibody [PCSK9 mAb] monotherapy). Treatment patterns, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) measurements, LLT adherence (proportion of days covered ≥ 0.80), and healthcare resource utilization and costs were reported.ResultsOf patients with 12 months’ follow-up post-ASCVD diagnosis, 35% did not receive LLT within 12 months. Among those receiving any LLT, 95.4% received statin monotherapy at index, 1.6% ezetimibe monotherapy, and 0.3% anti-PCSK9 mAb monotherapy. In the cohort with 12 months’ follow-up post-index (N = 417,625), the proportion of adherent patients was 66.0% for statin monotherapy, 58.0% for ezetimibe, and 54.9% for anti-PCSK9 mAb users. Among adherent patients, 25.0% of statin, 13.5% of ezetimibe, and 37.7% of anti-PCSK9 mAb users achieved LDL-C < 70 mg/dL within 12 months. ASCVD-related 12-month per patient per month medical costs were higher for nonadherent patients across LLT categories except ezetimibe.ConclusionsThe high proportion of patients not reaching their LDL-C goal, including those adherent to LLT, demonstrates persistent gaps in care. Adherent patients had lower LDL-C and lower ASCVD-related costs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40801-026-00559-5.

Key Points

This study used claims data from a large US healthcare insurer to examine treatment and adherence patterns for statins, ezetimibe, and anti-PCSK9 mAbs among adult patients with ASCVD; changes in LDL-C over 12 and 24 months of follow-up were also assessed.A third of patients with ASCVD remained untreated with LLT within 12 months of diagnosis, and among those who were treated, a substantial proportion did not reach an LDL-C goal of < 70 mg/dL, suggesting suboptimal use of LLT.Patients who were adherent to their prescribed LLT had lower LDL-C levels and lower ASCVD-related medical costs.

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), a leading cause of death in the USA, comprises disorders of atherosclerotic origin, including coronary artery disease (CAD), cerebrovascular disease such as transient ischemic attack, and peripheral artery disease (PAD) [1–5]. The 2026 American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA)/multisociety Guideline on the Management of Dyslipidemia emphasizes the importance of lowering low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), with numeric goals to guide lipid-lowering therapy (LLT) [6].

In secondary prevention, it is recognized that the majority of patients have a very high risk of ASCVD events; for these patients, the LDL-C goal is < 55 mg/dL. For the small subset of patients with clinical ASCVD who are not at very high risk, the LDL-C goal is < 70 mg/dL [6]. Statin medications are considered first-line therapy and are effective for reducing LDL-C for most patients; however, some patients require nonstatin therapies because they are intolerant of statins or do not achieve LDL-C goals in response to statin therapy [6]. Currently recommended nonstatin therapies include ezetimibe, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9)-targeting therapy (including monoclonal antibodies [mAbs] and inclisiran), and bempedoic acid [6].

Despite evidence supporting the effectiveness of statin therapy in reducing LDL-C and subsequent cardiovascular events, many patients are not adherent to or do not persist with statin therapy [7]. Furthermore, poor adherence to statins is associated with increased mortality risk among patients with ASCVD, and higher healthcare utilization and costs [8, 9]. Overall, despite the availability of LLT, approximately three-quarters of patients with ASCVD within large observational cohorts continue to have LDL-C > 70 mg/dL [10, 11].

Previous cohort studies have focused primarily on statin use only, using earlier AHA guidelines [8, 9]. Additionally, there has been little real-world research examining nonstatin treatment patterns, and the associated impact on LDL-C levels or ASCVD-related costs beyond 12 months of follow-up [12]. Here we present a study that aimed to measure the impact of adherence on overall and ASCVD-related healthcare costs among those treated with LLTs. This study adds to the evidence base by examining treatment and adherence patterns of statin, ezetimibe, and anti-PCSK9 mAbs and the associated changes in LDL-C values over 12 and 24 months of follow-up.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

The primary objective was addressed using an observational cohort study design using claims and eligibility data from the HealthCore Integrated Research Database (HIRD®) linked with outpatient laboratory results (provided by two national third-party laboratories). The HIRD contains a broad, clinically rich, and geographically diverse spectrum of fully adjudicated longitudinal claims data from Anthem insurance plans in the Northeastern, Mid-Atlantic, South-eastern, Midwest, Central, and Western regions of the USA, and represents members in each of the 50 states. The database collates claims from the largest commercially insured population in the USA. It includes plan designs such as health maintenance organizations (HMOs), point of service plans, preferred provider organizations (PPOs), consumer-directed health plans (CDHPs), and indemnity plans. This study utilized de-identified administrative claims data and was designed, implemented, and reported in accordance with the Guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices of the International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology, the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, and with the ethical principles promulgated in the Declaration of Helsinki [13, 14]. All data were accessed in a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act-compliant manner.

The study period began on 1 October 2014 and continued until 31 December 2020. Study intake was conducted from 1 October 2015 to 31 December 2019, to allow for 12 months of baseline data and 12 months minimum of follow-up. ASCVD diagnoses were identified during the study intake period (1 October 2015 to 31 December 2019). ASCVD diagnosis date was defined as the date of the first of either one or more inpatient visits or at least two outpatient visits with an International Classification of Diseases, Ninth/Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9/10-CM) diagnosis code (in any position on the claim) for ASCVD (myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack, PAD, ischemic heart disease or coronary heart disease, or a procedure code for coronary revascularization or inpatient coronary artery bypass graft). The index date was the earliest LLT fill date for patients during their 12- or 24-month post-ASCVD diagnosis observation period. All analyses were anchored to the index date (first LLT fill date), and cohort assignment was based on the LLT received at index. The baseline period was defined as the 12 months preceding the index date. The follow-up period was defined as 12 or 24 months following the index date (Fig. 1).

Study Population

Patients were included in the study if they had a diagnosis of ASCVD and one or more claim(s) for LLT. Patients were also required to be 18 years of age or older as of their diagnosis date and to have continuous health plan enrollment for the 12 months before and 12 months (or 24 months for the 24-month cohort) after the diagnosis date. Additionally, all patients included were required to have at least one LDL-C value recorded during the baseline period and one during the follow-up period.

Study cohorts were defined as statin monotherapy (≥ 1 claim for a statin), ezetimibe monotherapy (≥ 1 claim for ezetimibe), anti-PCSK9 mAb monotherapy (≥ 1 claim for evolocumab or alirocumab), and a combination therapy cohort of study members on multiple LLT with at least 90 days of concurrent use as follows: statin + ezetimibe, statin + anti-PCSK9 mAb, ezetimibe + anti-PCSK9 mAb, or statin + ezetimibe + anti-PCSK9 mAb. A complete list of LLT agents included in each cohort is available in Supplementary Table S1. For this analysis, results are reported from the overall cohort with any LLT and from the monotherapy groups only. Inclisiran and bempedoic acid were not included in the study because they were approved after the study intake period.

Baseline Variables

Baseline characteristics were identified using claims data and assessed during the 12-month baseline before the index LLT date (clinical), or on the index date itself (demographic). Baseline characteristics included age in years on the index date, sex, US geographic region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West, and missing), health insurance type (HMO, PPO, CDHP, other), Medicare Indicator (variable for Medicare Advantage, Medical supplemental plan/Part D, or commercial insurance), and index year (2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019). Clinical characteristics (measured during the 12 months prior to the index date) included the Quan–Charlson Comorbidity Index (QCCI), calculated on the basis of the presence of relevant diagnosis codes in the baseline period in any care setting and reported as a continuous variable. The individual comorbidities reflected in the QCCI score calculation are as follows: congestive heart disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, mild liver disease, moderate or severe liver disease, diabetes with complications, hemiplegia or paraplegia, metastatic solid tumor, renal disease, rheumatologic disease, Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome, and any primary malignancy [15].

Outcome Variables

Adherence to LLTs was calculated using the proportion of days covered (PDC). PDC was calculated as the proportion of days during the follow-up period that were “covered” by the number of days of medication supply received during the same time. Stockpiling rules were applied to PDC such that early fills were added to the end of the previous fill. The use of stockpiling rules can minimally increase PDC compared with methods that assume that the user discards leftover medication from previous fills [16]. Patients with PDC of < 0.80 were considered nonadherent, which has been shown to reduce mortality, and is used elsewhere [17]. Discontinuation was defined as a 60-day or greater gap after the end of the last fill + days of medication on hand. Adherence and discontinuation were reported by drug class.

All LDL-C outcomes were evaluated among a subset of patients with ASCVD who had at least two LDL-C values in linked outpatient laboratory data. Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Code (LOINC) coding was used to define specific laboratory test result data. For all patients included in the LDL-C outcomes, the baseline LDL-C measurement occurred within 90 days before or after the index LLT date (Fig. 1). For the 12-month cohort, one or more subsequent LDL-C values were obtained between index LLT date + 183 days and index LLT date + 365 days. For the 24-month cohort, one or more subsequent LDL-C measurements were obtained between index LLT date + 365 days and index LLT date + 730 days. This outcome was measured as the percentage of patients at-goal (LDL-C < 70 mg/dL).

All-cause and ASCVD-related healthcare costs, based on the total amount paid by the health plan and by the patient, were tabulated over the 12-month and 24-month LLT follow-up periods and reported as per patient per month (PPPM). All-cause and ASCVD-related total healthcare costs (medical plus pharmacy), as well as total medical costs (combined inpatient, emergency department, skilled nursing facility, and outpatient), were assessed. Laboratory services (for healthcare resource utilization analyses) were identified using Berenson–Eggers Type of Service (BETOS) codes.

Statistics

All outcomes were reported using descriptive statistics. No formal comparisons or multivariable models were utilized. Summary statistics such as mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range, and percentiles (e.g., 5, 25, 50, 75, and 95) were reported for continuous variables. By contrast, binary and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and relative frequencies (percentages). To protect patient privacy, counts were not reported when there were fewer than 10 patients.

Results

Patient Population/Demographics

From a population of 1,683,464 patients with ≥ 1 inpatient or ≥ 2 outpatient medical claims for ASCVD diagnosis (in any position on the claim) or a procedure code for coronary revascularization during the enrollment period, 417,625 (24.8%) patients were continuously enrolled for at least 12 months after the ASCVD diagnosis and 12 months after LLT initiation and met all other study criteria (12-month cohort). A cohort of 313,859 (18.6%) patients were continuously enrolled for at least 24 months after the ASCVD diagnosis and 24 months after LLT initiation and met all other study criteria (24-month cohort) (Fig. 2). Notably, 35% of patients with ASCVD with 12 months of continuous health plan enrollment were not treated with LLT within 12 months of ASCVD diagnosis (Fig. 2). Treatment Patterns

At index, among those treated with any LLT, 95.4% received statin monotherapy, 1.6% received ezetimibe monotherapy, and 0.3% received anti-PCSK9 mAb monotherapy (Table 1). Combination therapies included statin + ezetimibe (2.6%), statin + anti-PCSK9 mAb (0.04%), ezetimibe + anti-PCSK9 mAb (0.02%), and statin + ezetimibe + anti-PCSK9 mAb (0.02%). Among those receiving statin monotherapy (n = 398,295), 41.9% received high-intensity statins, 51.3% received moderate-intensity statins, and 6.8% received low-intensity statins. Adherence and discontinuation rates for monotherapy cohorts are presented in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S2. Adherence, defined as PDC ≥ 0.80, was suboptimal for all LLTs. In the 12-month cohort, adherence was attained by 66.0% of statin users, 58.0% of ezetimibe users, and 54.9% of anti-PCSK9 mAb users; however, the mean PDC for each drug category was not far below 0.80 (0.79 for statin users, 0.73 for ezetimibe users, 0.72 for anti-PCSK9 mAb users) (Supplementary Table S2). A similar pattern was observed over 24 months but with lower adherence in each group (Supplementary Table S2). Discontinuation (> 60-day gap without restart) rates were the highest among ezetimibe users (24.9% at 12 months; 30.5% at 24 months); for anti-PCSK9 mAb users, rates were 22.1% at 12 months and 25.5% at 24 months, with the lowest rates observed among statin users (17.5% at 12 months and 20.5% at 24 months).

LDL-C Goal Attainment

LDL-C values and goal attainment were measured in the subset of patients with available lab data (Supplementary Table S2). This subset included 38,561 patients in the 12-month cohort (9.2% of the overall 12-month cohort with ≥ 1 LLT claim) and 36,484 patients in the 24-month cohort (11.6% of the overall 24-month cohort with ≥ 1 LLT claim). Baseline mean LDL-C measurements ranged from 89.1 mg/dL (statin monotherapy) to 112.3 mg/dL (anti-PCSK9 mAb monotherapy), and the proportion of patients at LDL-C goal (LDL-C < 70 mg/dL) ranged from 17.2% (ezetimibe monotherapy) to 32.4% (statin monotherapy) in the 12-month cohort. Of those who were at-goal at baseline, approximately two-thirds remained at-goal for 12 months. Each cohort achieved reductions in mean LDL-C by the end of the follow-up period (Supplementary Table S2). The proportion of patients who achieved their goal at the last measured LDL-C during the 12-month follow-up ranged from 14.2% (ezetimibe) to 34.3% (anti-PCSK9 mAb), with a similar trend for 24 months.

A large proportion of adherent patients did not achieve LDL-C goal. Among adherent patients, 25.0% of statin monotherapy, 13.5% of ezetimibe, and 37.7% of anti-PCSK9 mAb users achieved an LDL-C goal of < 70 mg/dL for the last measured LDL-C during the 12-month follow-up, with similar results seen over the 24-month follow-up (Supplementary Table S2). Except for ezetimibe users, adherent patients achieved LDL-C goal more often than nonadherent patients. Additionally, in all LLT cohorts, adherent patients had lower mean LDL-C measurements at the end of both follow-up periods (Supplementary Table S2).

Cost Burden

All-cause medical costs (PPPM) were higher in nonadherent patients relative to adherent patients across all LLTs during the first 12 months of follow-up (Table 2). ASCVD-related 12-month PPPM medical costs were 30.6% higher in nonadherent patients in all LLT cohorts, and this trend was present across all LLT categories except ezetimibe (which had similar costs between adherent and nonadherent patients) (Fig. 4). The difference in total medical costs based on adherence persisted over time: nonadherent patients in the 24-month all LLT cohort had 37.5% (24-month) higher PPPM total medical costs than their adherent counterparts (Supplementary Table S2). When total costs were considered (medical and prescription), adherent statin users had lower all-cause and ASCVD-related costs than nonadherent statin users, but ezetimibe and anti-PCSK9 mAb users who were adherent had higher total costs than nonadherent users due to increased drug utilization and costs.

Within the LDL-C patient subset, 12-month PPPM ASCVD-related medical costs were generally 25.8% lower for patients who were adherent and at-goal compared with those nonadherent and at-goal. Total medical costs were also lower for patients who were adherent and not-at-goal compared with those nonadherent and not-at-goal for statin (22.3% lower) and anti-PCSK9 mAb (39.6% lower) users (Supplementary Table S3). For patients with 24-month follow-up, regardless of goal attainment, adherent patients experienced lower PPPM ASCVD-related medical costs in every LLT category (Supplementary Table S2). A closer look at components of ASCVD-related medical costs revealed no clear driver (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

Despite guidelines that recommend that all patients with a clinical diagnosis of ASCVD be treated with statin medications, in the current study population, a substantial proportion (35%) of patients with ASCVD were not treated with LLT within 12 months of ASCVD diagnosis [5]. Patients and providers may be concerned about possible side effects associated with statin therapy, particularly statin-associated muscle symptoms [18]. However, statin intolerance only offers a partial explanation for this high proportion of untreated patients within this population, with an estimated prevalence of 9.1%. Of those who were treated, 95.4% were treated with statins. The percentage of treated patients confirms the findings seen in other large observational studies. Peterson et al. found that only 69% of high-risk Medicare patients received statins at baseline, and even after a pay-for-performance provider intervention was initiated, the percentage of treated patients only increased modestly to 73% in the intervention group (compared with 70.6% in the control group) during a 1-year follow-up [19]. Conversely, Okerson et al. found that among 90,287 patients with ASCVD, statin use ranged from 31% to 32%, which was about half the rate we observed [20]. Literature also reports that statin therapy is particularly underutilized among women relative to men, which is consistent with our results (36.3% versus 63.7% were treated with statins, respectively) [21]. Overall, our observations were consistent with real-world studies demonstrating persistent gaps in the implementation of LLT in secondary prevention populations [22, 23]. These findings have important clinical and economic implications, as evidenced by analyses demonstrating the variability of LDL-C goal attainment in practice and the potential impacts of improved lipid management [24, 25].

Adherence was suboptimal among those who received LLT, and discontinuation was high. Differences were seen among the LLT classes, with the lowest adherence among anti-PCSK9 mAb users and higher discontinuation rates among ezetimibe users. The reasons for discontinuation are unknown owing to the nature of the observational study design. However, it is known that intolerance of LLT and adverse effects were the main reason for discontinuation among the 22% of statin users who discontinued in the Statin Adverse Treatment Experience Survey by Jacobson et al. [26]. Adherence among anti-PCSK9 mAb users was the lowest for the three drug classes in our study. This lower adherence was surprising given the less frequent dosing schedule of injectable therapies. Previous clinical trials of anti-PCSK9 mAbs have reported adverse events leading to discontinuation as rare and similar in frequency to placebo, suggesting that adverse events are not likely to be the leading cause of suboptimal adherence and discontinuation [27]. In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of adherence to PCSK9-targeting therapies in clinical practice, adverse effects and patient decision were the most common reported reasons for discontinuation; however, not all studies reported reasons for discontinuation and the authors noted a wide variation between studies [28]. In real-world settings, access to anti-PCSK9 mAb therapies may be influenced by insurance coverage restrictions, prior authorization requirements, and higher out-of-pocket costs, which can delay initiation or interrupt ongoing treatment [23, 29, 30]. Additionally, discontinuation or switching patterns may differ for anti-PCSK9 mAb therapies compared with oral therapies. Finally, adherence estimates derived from claims data reflect prescription fills and may not fully capture medication-taking behavior, particularly for injectable therapies administered every 2–4 weeks. These factors may contribute to the observed adherence patterns and should be considered when interpreting the results. The current body of published literature contains very few large studies with real-world evidence of adherence to anti-PCSK9 mAbs or ezetimibe. However, in one large observational study, Hines et al. found that 48.9% of anti-PCSK9 mAb users had a PDC ≥ 0.8, and 42.6% discontinued by 180 days post-index [17]. In the same study, 30.2% of ezetimibe users discontinued by 180 days post-index [17]. Hines et al. speculated that since the majority of discontinuations of anti-PCSK9 mAb medications coincided with reauthorization schedules, insurance-related barriers to treatment may have contributed to the pattern [17]. Additional research has also shown that anti-PCSK9 mAbs are associated with high costs and prescription rejection by insurers [31]. The results of the present study substantiate the findings of Hines et al. and add to the available body of evidence. Research suggests that adherence can be improved by good communication between patients and healthcare providers, and by involving specialist physicians in prescribing medication. Interviews have found that patients with ASCVD were more adherent when they had good communication and respect from their healthcare provider [32]. Furthermore, a study in the USA found that patients were more adherent to anti-PCSK9 mAbs or ezetimibe if they had a cardiologist visit prior to initiating therapy [33].

PPPM medical costs were lowest among patients who were adherent to LLTs over 12 and 24 months of follow-up. The difference between costs for those who were adherent and nonadherent was not limited to those who reached LDL-C goal. In every treatment cohort, adherent patients had lower mean LDL-C than nonadherent patients. The recent work of Han et al. demonstrates that secondary prevention of ASCVD utilizing early strategies to lower LDL-C has been shown to be associated with a reduction in future ASCVD events and hospitalizations [34]. Therefore, nonadherent patients may have greater long-term medical costs. Although Han’s population did not attain lower overall costs during 1 year of follow-up, the decreased risk of subsequent ASCVD-related hospitalizations would hopefully continue and result in lower long-term costs over a longer follow-up. In the second year of follow-up in the present study, within the subpopulation of patients who attained LDL-C goal, total medical costs for adherent patients were lower in each drug cohort than total medical costs for their nonadherent peers. This was also true among members of the All LLT cohort who met LDL-C goal: total costs (including the cost of prescriptions) were lower for adherent patients compared with nonadherent patients. As the PPPM medical cost of care for adherent patients trended lower than the cost of nonadherent patients aside from LDL-C goal attainment, this finding suggests that there are other mechanisms at play. According to Lee et al., medication-adherent patients are likely to make other lifestyle adjustments that improve overall cardiovascular health [35]. Additionally, nonadherent patients are more likely to report social determinants of health needs [32, 36].

The proportion of LLT-treated patients who reached LDL-C goal was suboptimal, despite patients being adherent to therapy. The goal of treating patients with ASCVD to reduce LDL-C by 50% with a target level of < 70 mg/dL for those at elevated risk of future ASCVD events was not accomplished within the current study population, confirming the challenges seen among other study populations. At baseline, a similar proportion of high-risk patients in the Million Hearts study by Peterson et al. were above the 70 mg/dL LDL-C goal (73%), with a mean LDL-C of 91.9 mg/dL [19]. In the intervention group, which received statin intensification, high-risk patients in the study experienced minimal gains in LDL-C reduction (− 2 mg/dL) at 1 year [19]. Minimal improvements in LDL-C levels may be due to statin monotherapy being insufficient in these patients; expert consensus recommends the use of combination therapy (statin and nonstatin treatments) in patients at very high risk whose LDL-C target goals are lower (< 55 mg/dL) [37]. Data regarding adherence rates to ezetimibe are lacking within the USA but have been reported at PDC of 0.56 among patients with ASCVD in Taiwan [38]. In the present study, about half of the ezetimibe users were considered adherent during 12 and 24 months of follow-up, with no discernible difference in the attainment of LDL-C goal between the adherent and nonadherent. However, across all drug categories, adherent patients had lower mean LDL-C measurements at the end of both follow-up periods. The mean overall adherence of the present study population was > 0.70 in all drug categories; the difference in mean PDC between the adherent and nonadherent was not large.

Recent advances in LLTs, including agents such as inclisiran and bempedoic acid, have expanded the nonstatin treatment options for patients who do not achieve LDL-C targets. In particular, the current US guideline notes that inclisiran (administered every 6 months, after the initial dose and 3-month dose) can be considered as an alternative to anti-PCSK9 mAbs for patients with a strong preference for less frequent dosing [6]. Although these newer therapies were not included in the present analysis owing to the study period occurring before their widespread use, their increasing use may help to address some of the treatment gaps identified in this study.

Limitations

Limitations are inherent in claims data-based observational research. LDL-C data were only available for a small subset of the study population (consistent with observations in a similar study by Navar et al. [39]); therefore, results should be interpreted carefully. Differences between patients with and without LDL-C values were not assessed. Owing to pharmacy carve-out health plans, cost data were not fully available for all patients in the population. All cost analyses were performed using only the subset of patients with all cost data. Additionally, some prescriptions may have been processed outside of the health plan (i.e., discount coupon cards and store-specific discounted pricing), which would lead to a lack of pharmacy claims. The study included patients with continuous enrollment in commercial health insurance plans only, which could limit the generalizability of the results. Other classes of lipid-lowering medications were not assessed. In particular, newer agents such as inclisiran and bempedoic acid were not included, as they were not available or widely used during the study period. All results were descriptive, with no formal comparisons or adjustments for confounding variables. Additionally, this study was not designed to demonstrate causality.

Conclusions

Despite guidelines that recommend that all patients with a clinical diagnosis of ASCVD be treated with statin medications, in the current study population, a notable proportion of patients with ASCVD did not have a record of claims for LLT within 12 months of ASCVD diagnosis. Additionally, LLTs may not be used optimally given the proportion of people not reaching their LDL-C goal, demonstrating that gaps in care persist. Among those who were treated, adherent patients had lower LDL-C levels and lower ASCVD-related costs. Strategies and/or medications that facilitate patient adherence and attainment of LDL-C goals are needed. Improvements in adherence and optimal LDL-C lowering can potentially reduce payor medical costs.
