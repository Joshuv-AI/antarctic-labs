Heat shock protein 72 attenuates atherosclerosis in apolipoprotein E‐deficient mice
Authors: Rintaro Yoshizumi, Tatsuya Kondo, Takuro Watanabe, Nobukazu Miyakawa, Sayaka Kitano, Mary Ann Suico, Miki Sato, Masaji Sakaguchi, Motoyuki Igata, Takeshi Matsumura, Hirofumi Kai, Eiichi Araki, Naoto Kubota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHeat shock (HS) and mild electrical stimulation (MES) induce the expression of heat shock protein 72 (HSP72), which suppresses endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress, and c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase (JNK) activity, thereby ameliorating pathophysiologic abnormalities associated with diabetes.AimsThis study aims to investigate whether HS + MES influences the development of atherosclerosis.MethodsHSP72+/+/ApoE−/− and HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice were fed a high‐fat diet and treated with either sham or HS + MES. Atherosclerotic lesion formation and oxidative stress were evaluated histologically, while HSP72 expression, macrophage markers, and proinflammatory mediators were analyzed in bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMMs).ResultsIn HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− mice, HS + MES reduced atherosclerotic lesion size and necrotic core formation, whereas these effects were absent in HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice. Plaque immunostaining revealed significantly lower 4‐hydroxynonenal and F4/80 levels in HS + MES‐treated HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− mice. In vitro, HS + MES increased HSP72 mRNA in BMMs, suppressed lipopolysaccharide‐induced monocyte chemotactic protein‐1 (MCP‐1) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression, inhibited JNK phosphorylation, and promoted macrophage polarization from M1 to M2.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings indicate that induction of HSP72 expression by HS + MES prevents atherogenesis independently of glucose homeostasis.

INTRODUCTION

Atherosclerosis is a prevalent cardiovascular disease characterized by a complex pathogenesis involving endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation. Local macrophage infiltration plays a central role in lesion development, and macrophage proliferation within plaques further drives disease progression 1 , 2 . Several proinflammatory cytokines, including monocyte chemotactic protein‐1 (MCP‐1), contribute to macrophage recruitment and activation 3 . Activated macrophages are generally classified into M1 (proinflammatory) and M2 (anti‐inflammatory) subsets, with an imbalance between these phenotypes influencing lesion dynamics 4 . M2 macrophages exert plaque‐stabilizing and athero‐protective effects, whereas impaired induction of the M2 phenotype exacerbates atherosclerosis 5 . Thus, therapeutic strategies that suppress ER stress and inflammation while promoting M2 polarization may provide vascular protection. We previously demonstrated that heat shock protein 72 (HSP72), which can be induced by heat shock (HS) combined with mild electrical stimulation (MES), suppresses ER stress, oxidative stress, and c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase (JNK) activation, thereby improving insulin resistance and glucose homeostasis in diet‐induced 6 and db/db mouse models of diabetes 7 .

Stress responses are evolutionarily conserved across species, with heat shock proteins (HSPs) being among the most prominent mediators. HSP72, in particular, is a major inducible chaperone that is rapidly upregulated by a range of stressors including HS, ultraviolet radiation, toxins, infection, oxidative stress, and inflammation 8 , 9 , 10 . Persistent ER stress and prolonged activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) promote vascular cell apoptosis and atherogenesis 11 . ER stress also results in JNK activation, which is associated with atherogenesis. HSP72 counteracts these processes by binding to inositol‐requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α), enhancing IRE1α/X‐box binding protein 1 (XBP1) signaling, and inhibiting ER stress‐induced apoptosis 12 . HSP72 also attenuates JNK activation through multiple pathways 13 .

Given the overlapping roles of ER stress and JNK activation in both diabetes and atherosclerosis, we hypothesized that HS + MES‐induced HSP72 expression may protect against atherogenesis. While temporal patterns of HSP72 expression have been described in atherosclerotic lesions 14 , the impact of HS + MES and the contribution of HSP72 to lesion development remain unclear.

In this study, we evaluated the effects of HS + MES on atherosclerosis in apolipoprotein E‐deficient (ApoE−/−) mice, focusing on macrophage polarization, infiltration, and plaque composition. We further compared responses in HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− and HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice to clarify the HSP72 dependence of these effects.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell preparation and HS + MES treatment

Femora and tibiae were isolated from anesthetized 6–8‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice. Bone marrow was flushed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Nakalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min, and resuspended in medium A [Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (25 mM glucose; Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 20% L‐cell–conditioned medium, 10% FBS, and antibiotics/antifungals (Nakalai Tesque)]. Cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2, with medium changes every 2–3 days. After one week, mature bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMMs) were seeded in 60‐mm dishes and treated once they reached ~80% confluence.

For MES, flat rubber electrodes were attached to the culture plate wall and immersed in the medium, connected to a Biometronome™ (Tsuchiya Rubber Co., Kumamoto, Japan). Cells received direct current at 5 V, 55 pulses/s, with a pulse width of 0.1 ms. Plates were sealed and immersed in a 42°C water bath, applying simultaneous HS + MES for 90 min. Control cells underwent sham treatment with electrodes placed but no current applied. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 ng/mL) was added after treatment, and cells were incubated for 6 h before collection for RNA extraction or protein analysis.

Animals and HS + MES treatment

ApoE−/− mice on a C57BL/6 background were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). HSP72−/− mice, carrying global deletions of Hspa1a and Hspa1b, were purchased from the Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Centers and crossed with ApoE−/− mice to generate HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice. All animals were housed under standard conditions with free access to water and a high‐fat diet (14% lard, 14% beef tallow, 25% casein, 20% sucrose, 15% cornstarch, 5% cellulose).

HS + MES was applied in a ventilated 12 × 10 cm chamber using conductive thermogenic rubber electrodes (10 cm diameter, moistened cotton covering). Electrodes were connected to a Biometronome™ delivering 12 V DC at 55 pulses/s, 0.1 ms per pulse. Electrode surface temperature was maintained at 42°C. Sham controls were placed in the chamber for 10 min without heat or electrical current. Procedures were approved by the Kumamoto University Animal Experimentation Committee (approval no. A2023‐012).

At 20 weeks of age, mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia, and serum and aortic tissues were collected. Frozen sections (6 μm) of the aortic bifurcation and sinus were stained with Oil Red O, and immunostaining was performed. Lesion areas were quantified using a BZ‐X700 microscope (Keyence, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunostaining

Frozen aortic sinus sections were incubated with antibodies against HSP72 (Enzo, ADI‐SPA‐810‐F), F4/80 (Santa Cruz, sc‐25,830), or 4‐hydroxynonenal (4‐HNE; Abcam, ab46544). Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 546 secondary antibodies (Molecular Probes) were used. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (1:1000), followed by secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. After washing, slides were mounted with Fluoromount (Sigma‐Aldrich) and imaged by fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Western blotting

Protein lysates (20 μg) were separated by SDS‐PAGE and blotted using antibodies against MCP‐1 (Proteintech, 29547‐1‐AP), α‐tubulin (Proteintech, HRP‐66031), phosphorylated JNK (Thr183/Tyr185; Cell Signaling, 9251S), total JNK (Cell Signaling, 9252S), and HSP72 (StressMarq, SMC‐100B). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using chemiluminescent substrate (Roche Diagnostics) and quantified with NIH image analysis software.

PCR analysis

RNA was extracted with an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74,104) and reverse‐transcribed using ReverTraAce‐alpha (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Quantitative real‐time PCR was performed with LightCycler SYBR Green (Roche) following manufacturer instructions. mRNA expression was normalized to 18S.

The primer sequences used for qRT‐PCR were as follows:

HSP72 upstream primer (5′‐TGGTGCTGACGAAGATGAAG‐3′) and downstream primer (5′‐AGGTCGAAGATGAGCACGTT‐3′).

F4/80 upstream primer (5′‐TCTGGGGAGCTTACGATGGA‐3′) and downstream primer (5′‐TAGGAATCCCGCAATGATGG‐3′).

MCP‐1 upstream primer (5′‐GGTCCCTGTCATGCTTCTGG‐3′) and downstream primer (5′‐CCTTCTTGGGGTCAGCACAG‐3′).

iNOS upstream primer (5′‐ACTGGCCTCCCTCTGGAAAG‐3′) and downstream primers (5′‐CTGCCAGATGTGGGGTCTTCC‐3′).

Arginase‐1 upstream primer (5′‐GCTGGAACCCAGAGAGAGAGCA‐3′) and downstream primer (5′‐CCACAGACCGTGGGTTCTTC‐3′).

Interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) upstream primer (5′‐CTCTCTCACCTCTCCTACTCAC ‐3′) and downstream primer (5′‐ACACTGCTACTTCTTGCCCC‐3′).

Tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) upstream primer (5′‐CAGCCAACCAGGCAGGTTCT −3′) and downstream primer (5′‐ACCGATCACCCCGAAGTTCA‐3′).

Interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) upstream primer (5′‐CCATCCAGTTGCCTTCTTGG‐3′) and downstream primer (5′‐TGCAAGTGCATCATCGTTGT‐3′).

18S upstream primer (5′‐AGTCCCTGCCCTTTGTACACA‐3′) and downstream primer (5′‐CGATCCGAGGGCCTCACTA‐3′).

Statistics

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t‐test, and multiple groups by one‐way anova with Bonferroni's post hoc test. Analyses were performed using Prism v10 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

HS + MES does not alter metabolic parameters in ApoE −/− mice

HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− and HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice were fed a high‐fat diet starting at 6 weeks of age. After 4 weeks, they were subjected to either sham treatment or HS + MES (10 min, twice weekly) for 10 weeks. The mild electrical stimulation (12 V, 0.1 ms, 55 pps) was well tolerated and caused no apparent discomfort. HS + MES did not affect body weight gain (Figure 1a), fasting or random blood glucose (Figure 1b,c), or food intake (Figure 1d), indicating that treatment did not alter systemic metabolic parameters. Serum lipid profiles, including total cholesterol (TC), very low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and triglyceride (TG), were summarized in Figure S1.

Metabolic characteristics of HSP+/+/ApoE−/− and HSP−/−/ApoE−/− mice that were sham‐treated (n = 8) or subjected to HS + MES treatment (n = 8) during high‐fat diet feeding. Weekly increment in body weight (a), fasting blood glucose (b), random blood glucose (c), and food intake (d). Data are presented as mean ± SE.

HS + MES reduces atherosclerotic lesions in HSP72 +/+/ApoE −/− but not HSP72 −/−/ApoE −/− mice

Oil Red O staining showed significantly reduced plaque coverage in the aortic arch of HS + MES‐treated HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− mice compared with sham controls (42% reduction; Figure 2a,b,i). This effect was attenuated in HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice (Figure 2c,d,i). Similarly, cross‐sectional analysis of the aortic sinus revealed a 62% reduction in lesion size in HS + MES‐treated HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− mice, but no reduction in HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice (Figure 2e–h,j). These findings indicate that the anti‐atherogenic effect of HS + MES requires HSP72.

Oil Red O stained sections of the aortic bifurcation (a–d) and aortic sinus (e–h) of HSP +/+ /ApoE−/− and HSP−/−/ApoE−/− mice that had been sham (n = 8) or HS + MES (n = 8) treated at 20 weeks of age. Quantification of the atherosclerotic areas at the aortic bifurcation (i) and in the aortic sinus (j). Data are presented as mean ± SE. **P < 0.01 (one‐way anova with post hoc Bonferroni correction).

HS + MES limits necrotic core formation in HSP72 +/+/ApoE −/− mice

Hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrated significantly smaller necrotic cores (−43%) in HS + MES‐treated HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− mice compared with sham‐treated controls (Figure 3a,b). This effect was abolished in HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice (Figure 3c,e), suggesting that HSP72 mediates the reduction of necrotic core formation.

Hematoxylin and eosin‐stained aortic sinuses of sham (n = 8) or HS + MES (n = 8)‐treated HSP+/+/ApoE−/− and HSP−/−/ApoE−/− mice at 20 weeks of age (a–d). Necrotic cores are indicated by yellow contours. Areas of necrotic cores in the aortic sinuses (e). Data are presented as mean ± SE. ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001 (one‐way anova with post hoc Bonferroni correction).

HS + MES increases HSP72 expression and reduces macrophage infiltration and oxidative stress

Immunostaining revealed stronger HSP72 expression in plaques from HS + MES‐treated HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− mice compared with sham controls (Figure 4a,c). Expression of the macrophage marker F4/80, a well‐established marker of murine macrophages 15 , 16 , was significantly reduced by HS + MES in HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− mice but remained unchanged in HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice (Figure 4a,b,d). Similarly, 4‐hydroxynonenal (4‐HNE), a lipid peroxidation–derived marker of oxidative stress 17 , 18 , was significantly suppressed by HS + MES in HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− mice, but this effect was absent in HSP72‐deficient mice (Figure 4a,b,e). These results indicate that HS + MES enhances HSP72 expression while limiting macrophage infiltration and oxidative stress in an HSP72‐dependent manner.

Representative immunofluorescence staining of aortic sinus lesions from HSP+/+/ApoE−/− mice treated with sham (n = 8) or HS + MES (n = 8) (a), and HSP−/−/ApoE−/− mice treated with sham (n = 8) or HS + MES (n = 8) (b) at 20 weeks of age. Sections were immunostained for HSP72 (red), F4/80 (green), or 4‐hydroxynonenal (4‐HNE; green). White bars indicate 100 μm. Quantification of HSP72‐positive area (c), F4/80‐positive area (d), and 4‐HNE‐positive area (e). Data are presented as mean ± SE. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ****P < 0.0001 (one‐way anova with post hoc Bonferroni correction).

HS + MES alters macrophage polarization in vitro

In BMMs, HS + MES significantly increased HSP72 mRNA expression, regardless of LPS stimulation (Figure 5a). F4/80 mRNA was unaffected (Figure 5b). LPS markedly induced MCP‐1 expression, which was reduced by HS + MES (−29%; Figure 5c). Inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), a representative marker of proinflammatory M1 macrophages 19 , 20 , was strongly induced by LPS but attenuated by HS + MES (−47%; Figure 5d). In contrast, arginase‐1, an M2 marker, was upregulated by HS + MES (Figure 5e). Furthermore, Interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), proinflammatory cytokines associated with M1 macrophage polarization, were markedly induced by LPS and attenuated by HS + MES (Figure 5g–i). Consequently, the iNOS‐to‐arginase‐1 ratio, elevated by LPS, was significantly reduced by HS + MES (Figure 5f), suggesting a shift in macrophage polarization from the M1 toward M2 phenotype.

Mouse‐derived BMMs underwent HS + MES treatment (n = 10) for 90 min, then 1 ng/mL LPS was added and the cells were incubated for 6 h. RNA was extracted and analyzed for HSP72 (a), F4/80 (b), MCP‐1 (c), iNOS (d), arginase‐1 (e), ratio of iNOS to arginase‐1 mRNA (f), IL‐1β (g), TNF‐α (h), and IL‐6 (i) mRNA expression by qRT‐PCR. Data are presented as mean ± SE. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001 (one‐way anova with post hoc Bonferroni correction).

HS + MES reduces inflammatory signaling at the protein level

Western blot analysis showed that HSP72 protein expression was increased by LPS and further enhanced by HS + MES (Figure 6a,b). MCP‐1 expression and JNK phosphorylation, both elevated by LPS, were significantly reduced by HS + MES (Figure 6a,c,d). These data demonstrate that HS + MES upregulates HSP72 while suppressing inflammatory and stress‐related signaling in macrophages.

Mouse‐derived BMMs were treated with HS + MES (n = 10) for 90 min, then 1 ng/mL LPS was added and the cells were incubated for 6 h. Protein lysates were prepared and HSP72 (b), MCP‐1 (c), and p‐JNK/JNK (d) protein expression was evaluated using Western blotting (a). Data are presented as mean ± SE. **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001 (one‐way anova with post hoc Bonferroni correction).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated that induction of HSP72 by combined HS and MES exerts anti‐atherogenic effects in ApoE‐deficient mice. These effects include reduced necrotic core formation, decreased macrophage infiltration, suppressed oxidative stress, and altered macrophage polarization. Importantly, these protective effects were observed only in HSP72+/+/ApoE−/− mice and were absent in HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice, indicating that the anti‐atherogenic action of HS + MES is HSP72‐dependent. Atherogenesis is closely linked to metabolic disturbances such as hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, but it is also driven by oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ER stress, which amplify inflammation 21 . We previously reported that HS + MES‐induced HSP72 improves glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity in diabetic models 6 , 7 . In contrast, in the present study, HS + MES did not alter systemic metabolic parameters, including in lipid profiles (Figure S1) in ApoE−/− mice, suggesting that its anti‐atherogenic effects are independent of glucose metabolism.

Our findings are consistent with earlier reports showing that heat treatment upregulates protective HSPs and attenuates atherosclerosis. For example, chronic whole‐body heat treatment suppressed plaque progression in LDL receptor‐deficient mice by restoring HSP expression and reducing NF‐κB activity 22 . Similarly, elevated HSP70 levels have been shown to inhibit NF‐κB signaling during ischemia/reperfusion injury 23 . Together, these studies suggest that thermal stress activates an HSP‐mediated defense mechanism against vascular injury.

HSP72 is a highly inducible molecular chaperone that prevents protein aggregation and promotes refolding of misfolded proteins 24 . Beyond its chaperone role, HSP72 modulates stress and inflammatory pathways. It directly inhibits JNK activation at multiple levels 13 , suppresses upstream kinases 25 , 26 , and activates phosphatases such as MAPK phosphatase‐1/3 27 . HSP72 also binds to IRE1α, enhancing IRE1α/XBP1 signaling and preventing ER stress‐induced apoptosis 12 . Because JNK and ER stress signaling drive inflammation and plaque instability, the ability of HSP72 to suppress these pathways likely contributes to its anti‐atherogenic effects.

We also found that HS + MES reduced necrotic core formation, a critical determinant of plaque vulnerability. Necrotic core expansion results from foam cell death, matrix degradation, and uncontrolled inflammation, and it predisposes plaques to rupture 28 . By suppressing macrophage infiltration, oxidative stress, and M1 polarization, HS + MES‐induced HSP72 may limit necrotic core progression and thereby enhance plaque stability. Our in vitro studies further support this mechanism. HS + MES promoted HSP72 expression in BMMs, attenuated LPS‐induced MCP‐1 and iNOS expression, suppressed proinflammatory cytokines associated with M1 macrophage polarization, including IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and IL‐6, and enhanced arginase‐1 expression, collectively shifting macrophage polarization from M1 toward M2. Because macrophage polarization strongly influences plaque development, this HSP72‐dependent modulation provides a plausible explanation for the reduced lesion burden in vivo.

Clinical studies have also suggested a role for HSPs in atherosclerosis. People with advanced cerebrovascular atherosclerosis exhibit reduced serum HSP70, whereas healthy controls show higher levels 29 . Moreover, we have reported that HS + MES safely reduces visceral adiposity, inflammation, and glucose intolerance in humans with diabetes or metabolic syndrome 7 , 30 , 31 , 32 . These findings raise the possibility that HS + MES could serve as a non‐invasive therapeutic strategy for cardiovascular disease, complementing its metabolic benefits.

This study has limitations. First, the duration of HS + MES treatment and the age at treatment initiation may influence metabolic outcomes, which could explain why we did not observe changes in glucose or body weight in ApoE−/− mice. Second, because we used global HSP72 knockout mice, systemic effects cannot be excluded. Although our in vivo findings using HSP72−/−/ApoE−/− mice strongly support the involvement of HSP72 in the anti‐atherogenic effects of HS + MES, we were unable to perform complementary in vitro experiments using bone marrow‐derived macrophages from HSP72‐deficient mice. Such experiments would help further clarify the cell‐autonomous role of HSP72 in macrophage polarization and inflammatory signaling. Future studies using tissue‐specific knockouts, bone marrow transplantation, and HSP72‐deficient macrophage will be necessary to clarify whether vascular or immune cell–specific HSP72 is primarily responsible for the observed benefits.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that HS + MES‐induced HSP72 expression suppresses atherogenesis in ApoE‐deficient mice by reducing inflammation, oxidative stress, and macrophage M1 polarization, independently of systemic glucose regulation. Induction of HSP72 may therefore represent a promising non‐invasive strategy to prevent atherosclerosis and improve vascular health.
