Artificial Intelligence and Humans
As anyone even vaguely aware of current technology can tell you, machine learning (ML) and
artificial intelligence (AI) have made exceptional breakthroughs in recent years. Generative
artificial intelligence (GAI) emerged circa 2022 dominated by Large Language Models (LLMs)
and generative tools for images emerged at about the same time.

It is an understatement to say that progress in the field of AI has been prodigious. Experts have
predicted that artificial general intelligence (AGI), AI capabilities rivaling or surpassing human
experts, are just around the corner. However, given the pace and complexity of AI development
humans, including data scientists, sometimes have trouble keeping up.

The goal of this report is to explore the use of ontologies, data schemas, and knowledge graphs
to help humans better understand and work with AI and machine learning (ML).

After a preliminary exploration of available schemas, we believe that it is useful to consider ML
separately from AI, even though conceptually ML can be considered a subset of AI. That is
because the current use cases for ML are a bit different from AI generally, especially when
employing generative AI products like LLMs.

At the present time, ML workflows are typically designed by humans with the aim of performing
numerical ML experiments, while generative AI use cases are often less structured and more
interactive. Agentic AI will likely obliterate that distinction if it has not already. Agentic AI is an
autonomous AI strategy capable of enlisting generative AI tools as well as ML methods
alongside web-based services, to accomplish very complex goals.

Our reason for including ML ontologies in this report, is to help data scientists craft their own ML
workflows in the near term, while preparing them for collaborating with agentic AI as those
systems mature. As capable as agentic AI may eventually come to be, we anticipate that
collaboration between humans and AI systems on machine learning tasks will nonetheless be of
value.

A Brief Tutorial on Ontologies and Knowledge
Graphs
Ontologies are logical models of knowledge. The term “ontology” comes from philosophy and
refers to the totality of what one knows about a given subject while “epistemology” is how you
know it to be true. What we will discuss in this section is the current methodology for
representing knowledge in a form that is accessible to both machines and humans. One might
well call such representations “computational ontologies.” The enabling technology for creating
computational ontologies is called semantic technology because it captures meaning.

The foundations of knowledge representation originated in the70s and 80s when the field of
cognitive science was coming into its own with and eye to building expert systems1.

Semantic technology became an integral part of the Worldwide Web in 1998 when Tim Berners-
Lee proposed a roadmap for what he called the semantic web2. His idea was later popularized
in a Scientific American article in 20013.

The knowledge representation for semantic technology is very close to the conceptual graph as
originally proposed by Sowa4. The implementation of conceptual graphs was later standardized
in the 2000s in XML protocols like RDF (the Resource Description Framework), RDFS (the RDF
Schema), and OWL (the Web Ontology Language)5.

In summary, a computational ontology is a knowledge model encoded in the style of Sowa’s
conceptual graph using concepts germane to the knowledge domain of interest. Concepts are
represented as a vocabulary of relevant terms alongside a vocabulary of relationships that bind
concepts to one another.

Specific concepts are placed under more general categories to form a hierarchy of concepts
called a “taxonomy.” Since relationships can also be organized into categories, they, too, form a
taxonomy.

The network formed by binding concept nodes with relationship edges is called a knowledge
graph.

A word about vernacular is in order here. In RDF-based ontologies, a concept is called a “class”
while an instance of a class is called an “individual.” Relationships between concepts are called
“properties.”

There are two kinds of properties: object properties and data properties. Object properties
connect class instances to one another, while data properties connect individuals to standard
computational data types.

Computational ontologies are also logical. Formally, a knowledge graph is a construct of first-
order logic (aka predicate logic). A complete edge of a knowledge graph is a logical “fact”
having the form (subject, predicate, object). Such facts are called “triples.” Hence, a knowledge
graph is a collection of triples.

There are two kinds of relationships in knowledge graphs that have especially important roles.
One is the inheritance relationship “this is a kind of that.” Without it one could not generalize
concepts into categories. In RDFS the predicates “subClassOf” and “subPropertyOf” are used to
build taxonomies of classes and properties respectively.

The other especially important relationship “this is a part of that.” This property is indispensable
to knowing how complex things are composed from simpler things.

In RDFS, there is no predefined “part-of” predicate, but there are well-known “top ontologies”
(as they are called) that include it. A top ontology is one that provides a foundation of high-level
concepts and relationships on which to build other ontologies. Top ontologies ensure that logical
inconsistencies will not creep into the design of an ontology intended for general use. A famous
top ontology is “The Basic Formal Ontology” or BFO.6 The BFO includes a “part-of” predicate.

Inheritance and composition are fundamental ways that humans relate to what they know of the
world. Without those two strategies, we would all be overwhelmed by complexity.

With some basics in place, we now offer an example of how ontologies are built using social
networks as the context.

Let’s say we know of a person named Alice and that Alice is a friend of Bob. There is an RDF
framework called the Friend of a Friend (FOAF) vocabulary7 intended for social network
modeling. In FOAF, we would first make use of the Person class to represent individuals in the
social network and then identify them by name as Bob and Alice. Then we would create a triple
expressing the fact that Alice knows Bob.

Here is some code that accomplishes that construction using “Turtle” which is shorthand for The
Terse Triple Language. Files that contain Turtle statements customarily use the extension “ttl.”

Table 1. Terse Triple Language (Turtle) specification of triples in a social network.

 @prefix foaf: <http://xmlns.com> .
                @prefix rdfs: <http://www.w3.org/2000/01/rdf-schema#> .

 _:p1 a foaf:Person ;
 foaf:name "Alice" .

 _:p2 a foaf:Person ;
 foaf:name "Bob" .

                _:p1 foaf:knows _:p2 .

The first two lines of the example declare namespaces for the FOAF vocabulary and RDF
Schema respectively. Then using “anonymous” URI nodes as subjects we declare that there is
an individual instance of Person named Alice, another individual person named Bob and that
Alice knows Bob.

The predicate denoted by “a” is just a shorthand in RDFS for subClassOf. The “prefixes” in the
first two lines declare that we intend to use elements defined in both the FOAF and RDFS
vocabularies.

This is how knowledge graphs are made.

In database terminology, an ontology is a schema for expressing knowledge as a knowledge
graph, while the graph itself is a particular expression of that schema. In their essential form,
both the ontology and its expression as a knowledge graph are first-order logical facts
expressed as triples.

What do we do with triples once we have made them? They go into a data management system
called a “triple store,” and because all the triples in storage taken together constitute a graph, a
triple store is sometimes called a “graph database.”

There is a query language for retrieving knowledge in in a triple store (aka graph database). The
language is called SPARQL (pronounced “sparkle”) which is a recursive acronym standing for
"SPARQL Protocol and RDF Query Language."

This introduction to semantic technology would be incomplete without mentioning the
technology available for developing and validating an ontology, and the tools available to aid in
those tasks. One famous and commonly used tool for developing ontologies is Protégé from
Stanford University8.

Ontologies for Machine Learning and Artificial
Intelligence
As we suggested in our introduction to this report, we believe it is useful to distinguish between
machine learning and artificial intelligence even though technically the former is a subcategory
of the latter. That is because, at least at this stage, use cases for ML experiments and
generative AI are sufficiently different to distinguish between them in terms of the ontologies we
would recommend for them.

ML experiments are mainly designed and executed by human experts just like experiments
conducted in other areas of science.

In contrast, the use cases for generative AI tools like LLMs are typically more interactive and
prompt-driven on the part of the user.

The net effect is that ML experiments are workflows that employ deliberate process steps, while
explorations based on generative AI typically do not expose their internal decisions and
reasoning to the user. Often, one only has prompt histories to go by.

Agentic AI can be even more opaque. AI agents, being genuinely autonomous, can employ
arbitrary numbers and varieties of process steps in response to just a few goal-oriented
prompts.

To explore what ontologies might be available for ML and AI we consulted several chatbots from
the LANL AI portal including ChatGPT, LLAMA, and Claude.

Some things we kept in mind as we considered candidate ontologies were our impression of
their basic design and whether the ontology was adequately documented. We also considered
how well the ontology appeared to be supported. The degree of support can be inferred by
looking at publications, release dates, and manner of distribution.

An Ontology for Artificial Intelligence
Our motivation to find a good, general ontology for AI is that we would like to understand AI
better. Ontologies are formal models of knowledge, and we believe codifying knowledge about
AI, in all its rich and varied complexity, to be a worthwhile goal.

To search for a good AI ontology, we queried several chatbots, while bearing in mind the same
criteria used in evaluating ML ontologies (approach, design, thoroughness of documentation,
evidence of continuing support, and so on). Based on these considerations, we quickly
converged on an ontology for AI called AIO20,21.

The first few lines from the abstract of the paper by Joachimiak, et al introducing AIO indicate
why this particular ontology caught our attention:

 The Artificial Intelligence Ontology (AIO) is a systematization of artificial intelligence (AI)
 concepts, methodologies, and their interrelations. Developed via manual curation, with
 the additional assistance of large language models (LLMs), AIO aims to address the
 rapidly evolving landscape of AI by providing a comprehensive framework that
 encompasses both technical and ethical aspects of AI technologies. The primary
 audience for AIO includes AI researchers, developers, and educators seeking
 standardized terminology and concepts within the AI domain.

AIO is partly LLM-generated and partly human-curated, leveraging powerful software tools
designed for automated ontology development. Many of these tools come from the
bioinformatics community which has long maintained an interest in semantic technology. One
tool that, according to the paper, proved especially helpful for the development of AIO is called
ROBOT22.

Let us begin our exploration of AIO by looking at its taxonomy.

Of particular interest are the Network, Layer, and Model classes since these are principal
constituents of modern AI tools. AI tools are models, models are often networks, and networks
have layers.

From this one may infer that AIO could in principle support specifications of ML tasks in a
manner equivalent to ML-Schema and OntoDM-core perhaps obviating the need for those
ontologies.

Another idea that suggests itself is that, with a modest alignment of shared concepts, AIO could
permit ML experiments and their data provenance to be referenced in within the larger scope of
studies involving Agentic AI. One could, in principle, use the ML task taxonomy in AIO to equip
AI agents to self-document the ML methods they employ when pursuing goals that require ML
tasking.

Turning to AIO properties, we were at first be surprised to discover that there are only two object
properties in AIO, both borrowed from The Basic Formal Ontology: BFO:has part, and its
inverse BFO:part of. We believe this bare-bones set of properties reflects the designers’
intention to keep the ontology as simple as possible.

Furthermore, we note that the predicates part of and has part are the relationships essential for
mereology. That is, these predicates are the ones that tell us how things are put together.
Knowing how AI models are put together is something we would likely want to know.

To illustrate the point, we constructed a SPARQL query to reveal what layers are employed in
different networks to enable them to function as required.

 15

Table 2 shows the SPARQL query we used:

Table 2. A SPARQL query to reveal the layers used in different neural networks.

 PREFIX : https://w3id.org/aio/aio-full.owl#
 PREFIX rdfs: http://www.w3.org/2000/01/rdf-schema#
 PREFIX aio: https://w3id.org/aio/
 PREFIX owl: http://www.w3.org/2002/07/owl#
 PREFIX BFO: http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/BFO_
 SELECT ?class ?target
 WHERE {
 ?target rdfs:subClassOf aio:Layer .
 ?class rdfs:subClassOf aio:Network .
 ?class rdfs:subClassOf ?restriction .
 ?restriction rdfs:subClassOf owl:Restriction ;
 owl:onProperty BFO:0000051 ; #has part
 owl:someValuesFrom ?target .
 }

The query makes use of the class owl:Restriction. Usually, object properties relate individuals of
different classes to one another. However, in our case we want to relate classes to classes and
that requires a restriction of the query to find some values from a particular class. Within the
restriction class we also specify the property of interest. Here the property is has part aka
BFO:0000051. In the query we also specify the source and target classes of interest, namely
the Network and Layer classes respectively.

The above query returned about 90 matches connecting Network to Layer classes using the has
part property.

Figure 11 is a diagram showing Network-Layer connections for two selected Network classes:

Figure 11. Connections between Networks and Layers for two classes of neural networks.

These results make sense. We would expect each network to have an input and output layer.
We can also see that the Deep Convolutional Network has some specialized layers like a

Pooling Layer (that would typically compute averages of some kind), a Convolutional Layer (as
we expect a convolutional network would), a Kernel Layer (often used to compute vector
products), and a Hidden Layer (whose role here is unspecified). The Recursive Language
Model network naturally has input and output layers as well and has a Memory Cell Layer (to
maintain internal states). These layers seem to represent plausible constituents of these
networks.

Thus, we have a practical example of how AIO can help to understand AI better.

As for support, AIO is the product of a research team composed of researchers from Lawrence
Berkeley National Laboratory and the University of California with a strong commitment behind
it.

The development and ongoing maintenance of the AIO is explicitly backed by federal and
institutional research grants administered through LBNL and DOE Office of Science. It is
supported by the Advanced Scientific Computing Research (ASCR) program and the Office of
Biological and Environmental Research (BER). The ontology's creation was directly funded
under the LBNL-administered ENDURABLE project ensuring financial backing for the
researchers building the infrastructure.

LBNL provides the core scientific personnel driving AIO. Principal investigators, data scientists,
and ontology editors are staff members within LBNL's Biosciences Area. LBNL commits
personnel to lead the curation and oversee the Large Language Model (LLM) workflows that
scale the ontology.

The UC system and LBNL support the AIO by nesting it within their broader, long-term open-
science infrastructure:

The project is a core output of the Berkeley Bioinformatics Open-source Projects (Berkeley
BOP) group, a long-standing LBNL initiative dedicated to standardizing biological and
computational knowledge.

The University of California permanently archives and open-publishes the foundational research
and methodology of the AIO through the UC eScholarship institutional repository.

The University of California AI Council is actively working to integrate responsible AI principles
across all UC campuses and labs. AIO development includes an entire top-level branch for

enforce ethical standards programmatically.

In other words, AIO is extraordinarily well supported, and that is likely to remain so for some
time.

Summary and Conclusion
Progress in the field of AI has been prodigious. However, given the pace and complexity of AI
development, humans, including data scientists, may find it difficult to avoid being overwhelmed.
Our goal of this report has been to explore the use of ontologies manage the complexity of
knowledge relevant to understanding and using AI.

Following an initial assessment, we decided to address machine learning separately from AI,
even though ML is formally a subset of AI. That is because use cases for ML are qualitatively
different from AI use cases. ML use cases are mainly concerned with designing and executing
machine learning experiments, whereas generative AI use cases expose less internal structure
and are largely interactive.

We are aware that because Agentic AI systems can enlist generative AI tools in combination
with ML methods to accomplish complex goals, any current distinctions between ML and
generative AI use cases may soon become blurred. However, we anticipate that collaboration
between humans and agentic AI systems will eventually become commonplace. When that
happens, ontologies that capture the full range of AI services, including ML tasks, will increase
in value. We imagine, for instance, that sometime soon, an AI agent may be able to report the
tasks and strategies used in solving a problem, including ML tasks performed, and then consult
a human data scientist on what they would like them to try next.

In the meantime, for machine learning, we have explored an ontology called ML-Schema which,
it is intended to be a high-level, harmonizing ontology, must be supplemented by more
specialized ontologies to fully characterize ML workflows. That is, ML-Schema needs a stack of
ontologies to fulfill its purpose. Recommendations the ML-Schema ontology stack include:

 • ML-Schema – to represent top-level ML concepts;
 • OntoDM – for intermediate ML classes;
 • PROV-O and MEX – for concepts relating to data provenance and data exchange; and
 • Exposé – for specifying the details of ML workflows.

That this stack works is evidenced by its routine use in support of a collaborative, integrated ML
environment called Open-ML. In passing, we suggest that the LANL may want to consider some
way of permitting Open-ML to be used as a platform for ML experiments, perhaps as a
sanctioned cloud-based service.

For AI systems generally, we recommend AIO, a well-supported, well documented ontology that
includes a wide range concepts and relationships essential for knowledge concerning AI,
including those necessary for specifying ML tasks.

As we discussed, top-level classes in AIO include key concepts like:

 18

 • Bias – Concepts related to performance and accuracy, as well as those related to the
 safe and equitable uses of AI.
 • Machine Learning Task – Algorithms that learn.
 • Model – The result of training an algorithm.
 • Training Strategy – A method by which an algorithm is trained.
 • Preprocessing – Whatever conditioning is necessary to train an algorithm.
 • Network – Algorithms that learn based on artificial neurons arranged in layers.
 • Layer – The key functioning components of artificial neural networks.
 • Mathematical Function – Any mathematical assignment of one set of things to a unique
 value in another set of things.

AIO has very extensive taxonomies based on each of these concepts, several of which we have
explored in some detail. As AI continues to develop, we expect that these concepts will continue
to grow in expressiveness.

Interestingly, AIO only has two object properties: has part, and its inverse part of. These are the
essential predicates for mereology, the study of how complex things may be constructed from
simpler things. We believe this kind of knowledge to be of enormous value in understanding
something as complex as AI.

We showed the value of knowing how the different pieces of AI come together with a query to
the AIO ontology concerning its Layer and Network classes. Layers are the essential
components that enable neural networks to function as intended. As a demonstration, we
constructed a query to find connections between Network and Layer classes using the has part
property. The query returned 90 connections from which we selected the connections related to
two kinds of networks: The Deep Convolutional Network and the Recursive Language Model.
Although verifying these connections was beyond the scope of this report, we nonetheless
found the connections we examined to be sensible.

Looking into support, we found AIO to be extraordinarily well-supported ontology. It is the
product of a research team composed of researchers from Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory and the University of California with very strong commitments from established
research programs and institutions behind it.

Finally, we summarize our findings by saying that we have explored ontologies for artificial
intelligence and have found two attractive solutions.

One is intended to aid data scientists who routinely perform machine learning experiments that
they largely design and perform themselves. The solution we recommend for ML that is a stack
of ML ontologies with one called ML-Schema at the top.

The other is intended to promote the understanding and use of AI generally and is called AIO.
We found AIO to be a remarkably well-supported ontology with an enormous taxonomy of
concepts covering virtually every aspect of AI development including safety and equitable usage
