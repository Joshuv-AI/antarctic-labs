Cowpea production systems and determinants of adopting improved cultivars by farmers across western highlands and Sudano Sahelian zones of Cameroon
Authors: Eric Bertrand Kouam, Toscani Ngompe Deffo, Maxime Merlin Djoufack Tonfack, Moise Harnold Fotso Ngangoua, Rogil Kuete Fokou, Astride Stéphanie Djabou Mouafi, Mariette Anoumaa, Marie Solange Mandou, Remy Stephane Pasquet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata) is an essential crop in tropical Africa with its production facing numerous challenges. Identifying technologies and policies allowing to improve cowpea production are required to confront increasing food insecurity. This study investigated cowpea production systems, identified farming constraints, traits preference and determinants for the adoption of improved cultivars among 300 farmers surveyed across 15 divisions in two distinct agroecological zones of Cameroon. Participatory research through questionnaires and interviews were used to collect data, analysed with IBM SPSS and GraphPad prism softwares. Farmers grow minorly improved varieties (21.66%) compared to landraces (78.44%). These improved varieties are essentially grown in the Sudano-Sahelian zone. Farmers there are mainly males (85%), cultivating on large farm size, using animal traction (74.29%) with higher harvest that are sold. The western highlands zone in contrary is highly characterized by female farmers (98.75%), practicing sole cropping (95.63%) entirely on an area of < 0.5 ha with no use of chemicals, not belonging to farmers’ organisation and minimum harvest product sold (96.87%). Farmers preferred traits are high yielding varieties, longer storage duration, drought tolerance and early maturing. Major production constraints across the two zones are drought, insect pests, lack of improved varieties and poor soil fertility. Results showed that purchasing land, using animal traction, having certified seed company and improved varieties seed stockists as seed sources, targeting large benefits, belonging to a famer organisation and larger farm size with more workforce positively affected the adoption of improved varieties. To increase adoption, breeders should address the identified farming constraints and take into account farmers’ preferred traits in their research. There is huge recommendation to enhance availability of improved varieties to farmers once developed, through strengthening extensionists training and services, and encouraging farmer-to-farmers experience exchanges.

Introduction

Africa's population is the fastest growing worldwide with 2.8 billion people and 57 inhabitant/square kilometres population density expected by 2050 against 1.34 billion people and 45 inhabitant/square kilometres population density observed in 20201. Because of this exponential growth, it is crucial to put in place sustainable food policies, required to combat food insecurity that could set-in in many households by then. The rapid growth of African population is directly associated with high food demand and represent serious challenge for achieving the United Nation Sustainable Development Goal 2 regarding the eradication of hunger by the year 20302,3. Among sectors susceptible to impact in addressing this concern, agriculture is the most prominent and sharing ideas is imperative for the future of this sector of activity, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa where 60% of the population is made up of smallholder farmers with strong dependence on agriculture for food and income4. In addition to high growth rate, sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) currently faces multiple challenges, including hunger3, undernourished population5, and climate change4. Today, ensuring Africa's food security largely relies on many staple crops that are cereals (maize, pearl millet, rice, and sorghum), tubers (cassava, yam and potato), and legumes (cowpea, groundnuts and beans). However, the average production of each of these crops is low compared to global average because of low soil fertility6, rain-fed agriculture7, insect pest attacks8, diseases9 as well as many other biotic and abiotic stresses.

Therefore, in response to tackle the increasing food insecurity in the African continent, legumes are prominent. Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata) is a legume native to Africa and popular in many sub-Saharan African countries where it is used for human and animal feeding10. Cytological characterisation of cowpea revealed its diploid structure: 2n = 2x = 2211. The crop is a self-pollinating annual plant that has been classified as an economically important indigenous African crop12,13. Eaten as a leafy vegetable, fresh pod or grain, cowpea is considered as cheap source of protein for resource-poor farmers whose diets rely heavily on starchy crops such as maize, potato, millet, sorghum and cassava. Several varieties of cowpeas are cultivated for different purposes. The most widely cultivated cowpeas belong to the unguiculata subspecies, divided into five cultivar groups, namely Unguiculata, Sesquipedalis, Textilis, Biflora, and Melanophthalmus14. Cowpea global production is estimated at over 9 million metric tons yearly from approximately 15 million hectares13. More than 95% of this production occurs in West Africa, with Nigeria and Niger being the top largest producers with respectively 2 million and 641,000 metric tons15. Other African producer countries are Burkina-Faso, Mali, Ghana, Tanzania, Kenya Uganda and Cameroon16. In sub-Saharan Africa, the average yield in farmers' fields is 300 kg/ha on average, far below the genetic potential, estimated at 1500–2500 kg/ha in experimental stations17. Cowpea production in Cameroon is estimated at 152,000 tons yearly, with more than 80% from the Sudano-Sahelian region18. The western highlands as well contribute significantly to the national production19. In addition to its food role, cowpea has become a cash crop for poor farmers cultivating different varieties with significant genetic background18. Many of these varieties possess interesting traits for which genes could be exploited by breeders in cowpea improvement programs19,20.

According to Horn et al.21, the low yield usually obtained is heavily due to the use of unimproved varieties, coupled with inadequate application of inputs and poor agronomic practices. Furthermore, cowpea production is also limited by numerous biotic and abiotic factors, including low soil fertility, insect pests, diseases and parasitic weeds22–24. Cowpea production constraints and farmers desired characteristics in cultivars have not yet been documented in Cameroon. Many improved cowpea cultivars are cultivated in the country including WILIBALI, known as drought tolerant and resistant to striga25; KOMCALLE and TILIGRE that are resistant to striga25; MTA-22, identified as drought tolerant26; LORI, known as resistant to cowpea weevils27; VYA, recognised as resistant to Cowpea Aphid Borne Mosaic Virus28; IRI5-MA02 and ITI5-MA33 that are identified as resistant to various pests and diseases29. A good understanding of the determinants of the adoption of these improved cultivars across agroecological zones in Cameroon will allow developing operative approaches in order to increase cowpea productivity that will end at improving the livelihood of many farmers in several developing countries. From all these above, the present study was undertaken to characterize cowpea production systems, identify farming constraints, traits preference and determinants guiding the adoption of improved cultivars across the western highlands and sudano-sahelian agroecological zones of Cameroon and will end up enlarging the foundation for cowpea improvement and promotion in the country.

The studied population belongs to two distinct agro-ecological zones in Cameroon where cowpea is majorly cultivated: the western highlands zone in the western region, 1007–1490 masl, 05° 08′ 29″–05° 43′ 36″ North latitude and 10° 03′ 16″– 10° 52′ 57″ East longitude and the Sudano-sahelian zone in the northern region, 312–599 masl altitude, 08° 24′ 01′′–11° 02′ 52′′ North latitude and 13° 23′ 39′′–15° 14′ 03′′ East longitude (Fig. 1, Table 1). Sampling procedure

A multiple step random sampling method according to Semahegn et al.30 was applied to select farmers to be used for the study. The first step allowed the selection of the two agroecological zones: the Western highlands and the Sudano-sahelian zones which represent two major cowpea production sites in Cameroon. The second step was the selection of divisions in each agro-ecological zone. Eight divisions from western highlands and seven from the sudano-sahelian zone were selected the way to be the most closed to each other while ensuring a better representativeness of the study area and capturing the maximum diversity. Many of these divisions are operational sites of the Institute for Agricultural Research for Development, the national research centre highly involved in the dissemination of improved cultivar of several crops, including cowpea. The third and final step involved the random selection of cowpea farming households within each division. About 20 households were selected from each division, giving a total of 300 cowpea farming households from the 15 Divisions for the whole study area.

Data collection

Investigations were conducted from November 2022 to February 2023 in the western highlands zone and from December 2023 to January 2024 in the sudano-sahelian site. Data collection used household survey method with heads of households interviewed by means of a semi-structured questionnaire. Field observation was also employed for data collection. Before the survey, consultations with key individuals in each Division including traditional authorities, agronomists from the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development and leaders of farmer-based organisation were carried out. This permitted us to precisely gather information on the cowpea production in the area. The data were collected from 300 households across the two sites. Because of cultural diversity, local interpreters were employed in order to facilitate exchanges with farmers. Data collection involved: (1) socio-demographic and economic characteristics of each cowpea farmer respondent, (2) cowpea production systems, farm’s characteristics and cultural practices, (3) farming constraints and traits preferences.

Data analysis

Data obtained during investigations were analysed by descriptive and multivariate statistics by the help of Microsoft Excel 2019, SPSS 20.0, XLSTAT 2018 and GraphPad Prism 7.0 statistical software packages. Descriptive statistics involving frequency counts to describe variables were presented using tables and charts. Data on the socio-demographic profile of farmers and the characteristics of production and the farms were subjected to Pearson chi-square tests to compare agroecological zones and clusters. The cross sorting between one variable of interest (Adoption of improved cultivar) allowed comparison between adopters and non-adopters with frequency counts of each variable using z-test. Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) was used to highlight the existing relationships between variables through coefficient of correlation. Factor analysis (FA) and Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) were performed to classify the cowpea farming system in the study area. Information connected to constraints of cowpea production was provided by the method of Rank Based Quotient (RBQ) as proposed by Sabarathnam and Vennila31. Nine production constraints already predefined were subjected for severity evaluation by farmers. Four levels of severity were used (High, Medium, Low and No impact) according to their perception and were respectively ranked as first (1), second (2), third (3) and fourth (4). Cowpea production systems

The majority of cowpea farmers (65.30%) performed manual land preparation for producing local varieties (78.33%). This tradition was fully respected in WHZ in contrary of the SSZ where farmer used minorly manual land preparation (21.66%) and majorly animal traction (74.29%) to produce both local (53.60%) and improved (46.41%) cowpea varieties (Table 3). The majority of farmers in both agroecological zones produce cowpea once a year with no use of chemicals (Table 3). Figure 2 shows that cowpea is cultivated in monoculture in almost all production areas in WHZ (95.63%) and very few were found cultivating cowpea in association with maize (2.50%). In SSZ in contrary, there was equal distribution of sole cropping (35.0%) and intercropping (33.57%) with cowpea associated either with maize or cotton (Fig. 2). Four uses of cowpea (Fried grains, sauce, cake and donuts) were significantly observed in SSZ against only cake and donuts largely observed in WHZ (Fig. 3). Factor analysis revealed the first two axes globally explaining 34.88% of the total variation with 28.89% for the first axis and 5.99% for the second (Fig. 4). These two axes help identifying traits that differentiate the cowpea farming systems in the study area. The Agroecological zone, gender, farm size, animal traction possession, cropping system, proportion of yield sold, selling benefits, food use and production constraints were significantly correlated with the first axis (Table 4). The second axis were significantly associated with the adoption of improved cultivars, purchasing of the land, certified seed companies and improved varieties seed stockists as seed sources. The hierarchical cluster analysis showed three distinct clusters, defining the cowpea farming systems (Table 5). The first cluster presented 129 (44.33%) surveyed farmers spread across SSZ. They are majorly males (87.60%) and Chief (69.77%) or senior children (20.16%) producing cowpea on 0.5–2 ha (84.50%) farm size with chemical (4.65%). They belong to a farmer organisation (15.50%) and produce cowpeas once (89.15) or twice (10.85%) yearly as a monoculture (37%) or in association with other crops (36%). They use animal traction (73.64%) and seeds can be from NGO (4.6%). The second group, of 11 farmers are also from the SSZ, constituted equally of males and females, family chief (90.91%) producing on 0.5–2 ha (81.81%) with no chemicals and using animal traction (81.82%). Their seeds are from certified seed companies or improved varieties seeds stockists. The clusters 3 are all farmers from WHZ, majorly females (98.75%) and spouse (98.75%) not belonging to any farmers’ organisation, practicing monoculture (95.63%) with seeds highly from local market Constraints of cowpea farming

Nine cowpea production constraints were identified as obstacles to cowpea production during the survey. Table 6 presents the perception of each production constraints by farmers in the different AEZs, according to gender and the study area. The perception differs for each constraint according to AEZ as well as for gender as chi square test was always significant. Constraints with important impact in both AEZs and independently of the gender were lack of improved varieties, drought and pests (Table 6). High impact of lacking improved cultivars was only observed in SSZ (21.42%). Disease as major constraint was only observed in SSZ: High impact (6.49%) and medium impact (38.56%), respectively. High (79.21%) and medium (20.71%) impact was observed in SSZ against High (43.19%) and medium (56.88%) in WHZ for drought (Table 6). Pest majorly had high impact in SSZ (73.57%) and medium impact in WHZ (94.38%). For cowpea production, more men (21.49%) perceived the lack of improved varieties with high impact in contrary of women (2,23%). Most men revealed the presence of pests impacting highly on their production (71.90%) while these pests rather had medium impact for women (84.92%). Drought equally had high (49.16%) and medium impact (50.14%) according to women and men saw drought as highly impacting (76.03%) cowpea production (Table 6). Table 7 presents the ranking and the grading of production constraints according to their degree of importance. The RBQ result indicated that drought, insect and pest, lack of improved cultivars, poor soil fertility and diseases were the first, second, third, fourth and fifth ranked production constraints as documented by farmers in the study area. Other perceived constraints by farmers by the order of importance were high cost of land rent, high cost of labour, flood and inadequate marketing (Table 7). The order was almost similar in both AEZs. Adoption determinants of improved cultivars

Significant differences amongst adopters and non-adopters of improved cultivars for various variables of the cowpea production system were observed (Table 10). Adopters were dominantly males (63.64%) from 45 to 55 years old (36.36%) with university backgrounds (40.91%). They were all from the SSZ and family chiefs (81.82%) producing cowpeas on 0.5 to 2 ha farm (90.91%). Differences between adopters and non-adopters were shown in terms of land holdings, seed source and the use of animal traction in the farm. Adopters of improved cultivars owned their land through purchase (68.18%), have certified seed companies as seed source (59.08%) and use animal traction (86.36%) in their farm, respectively against 9.35%, 1.44% and 30.58% for non-adopters. Significant farmers using improved cultivar were from a farmer organisation (22.73%) and had a maximum workforce size (36.36%) in contrast for non-adopters’ farmers with respectively 5.76 and 5.04%. The majority of non-adopter farmers (55.76%) had < 20% of their yield sold while adopters majorly (68.18%) had 50 – 80% of their yield sold (Table 10). Most non-adopters had medium benefits (60.79%) compared to adopters with large benefits (86.36%) for the majority. Results revealed also significant differences between adopters and non-adopter farmers’ contact with NGO. Adopters (9.09%) had more farming experience with NGO against 1.44% for non-adopters. No selling difficulties exist for adopters on cowpea improved cultivars (Table 10). Most farming constraints including lack of improved cultivars, drought and insect pests were more experienced by non-adapters compared to adopters (Fig. 5). Discussion

The present research provided indications of cowpea production characteristics, constraints, farmers’ favourites credits for improved cultivars and factors influencing their adoption across two distinct agro-ecological zones of Cameroon. It was observed that males predominantly engaged (85%) in cowpea farming in the SSZ while it is the contrary in WHZ with more females (98.75%) involved (Table 2). This gender disproportion between these agroecological zones is certainly due to cultural differences existing between both zones. Indeed, in the sudano-sahelian zone, which is predominantly Muslim in culture, tradition and laws would have it that women are the guardians of the home and are not to carry out income generating activities that are reserved to men32. In contrast with western highlands zone, women are mainly interested in ensuring food self-sufficiency in households and are therefore involved in crop production, even on very small areas, close to the house contrary to men who usually seek employment outside agriculture to supplement household needs33,34. Farming was recorded as the primary activity of respondents in SSZ (90.72%), WHZ (69.37%) and combining 79.30% in the whole study areas. These sampling sites cover large agricultural lands and to avoid overrun of weeds, population majorly are to partly or fully practice agricultural activities in order to meet their daily needs. This result of farming as main activity is similar to those obtained for cowpea farming in Ghana by Karikari et al.35 and rice farming in Benin by Loko et al.36.

Cowpea production constraints were highlighted by farmers in both agro-ecological zones with ranking being closely the same. Hierarchically, farmers are faced with constraints such as susceptibility to drought, insects and pests, lack of improved cultivars, poor soil fertility and high disease incidence (Tables 6 and 7). These conclusions agree with previous studies on cowpea production constraints conducted in Niger37, Ghana35 and in sub-Saharan Africa23. Defencelessness against drought and insect pests were found to be the main constraints in the whole study area. The increasing drought because of climate change is progressively perceived by farmers in crop production sites38,39. As emphasized by Vadez et al.40, drought impairs crop production by disrupting plant development and physiological processes, leading to reduced yields, and this include cowpea16,41. Yadav et al.42 and Togola et al.43 pointed out insect and pests as serious problems facing cowpea farmers during plant growth as well as during storage of grains. As example, Legume pod borer (Maruca vitrata) destroys cowpea by making holes on pods while cowpea weevil (Callosobruchus maculatus) is a pest of stored cowpea grains.

The results of preferences showed that farmers ranked several attributes. These results are consistent with recent studies on farmers’ preferences on cowpea traits44,45. The majority of farmers in this study prefer high yielding cowpea varieties (Table 8). Farmers are coherent people and, hence will be primarily focus in increasing their yield, leading to large benefits. This is consistent with Yengoh46 and Laajaj et al.47 who described yield as an important agronomic trait for smallholder farmers during the choice of the variety to be sown. The present study pointed that yield is not the only criterion for farmers while selecting a variety. Other criteria included early maturity, diseases and pests’ resistance, good taste desire, high market value, reduced cooking time and drought tolerance were observed as also highlighted several authors35,48,49. Reports point cowpea as a fairly drought-tolerant crop and prospers well in poor soil conditions compared to others crops50,51. However, drought can occur anytime during the agricultural cycle with significantly different impact according to the crop’s developmental phase41,52. Drought typically has more negative impact on cowpea production when it occurs at the reproductive compared to the vegetative phase according to these authors.

The use of improved cowpea cultivars is a reality in agro-systems in Cameroon although farmers still rely largely on landraces (78.33%). The strong existence these landraces in the study area underlines their durable cultural anchoring. The low adoption of improved cowpea (21.66%) is likely due to the poor living conditions of farmers and deprived work of extensionists not favouring accessibility to improved seeds by farmers as pointed Mafouasson et al.53. Beye et al.54 also reported low adoption of improved cowpea (33%) compared to landraces in Senegal. Many variables related to farmers’ socio-economic attributes and farming systems characteristics were revealed to influence the adoption of improved varieties of cowpea as shown by Tables 9 and 10. This study showed positive relationship between adoption of improved cultivars and each of the following variables: farm size, family size, workforce size, proportion of yield sold and selling benefits. The surveyed farmers using significant workforce size adopted more improved cowpea seeds probably because improved varieties are prioritized by farmers cultivating cowpea as a lucrative activity on large areas in order to expect more benefits. These results fit conclusions of Ruzzante et al.55 from regression models over the published literature showing family size, workforce size, farm size and profits correlating each with adoption of new agricultural technologies. Membership of farmers’ organization was positively correlated with the adoption of improved cowpea cultivars. In their mission, agricultural development projects, NGO and extensionists are to collaborate with Farmers’ based organisations. The organisation’ members therefore receive training and seminars on best agricultural practices and have increased contact with extension services and consequently, are the first to know and obtain newly released varieties. Rabé et al.56 and Beye et al.54 reported the significant association between Farmers’ organisation membership and the purchase or adoption of cowpea improved cultivar in Niger and Senegal respectively. Similar observations were observed with maize farmers in D. R Congo57. Land ownership positively influences the adoption of improved cowpea cultivars by Cameroonian farmers. Indeed, Beye et al.54 reported that Senegalese cowpea farmers with secured land tenure adopt improved varieties. Our results are also consistent with maize57 and rice36 farmers who showed that land ownership security is a very important variable in the adoption of improved seeds. Owning land also is likely to stimulate the farmer to invest in more demanding agricultural technologies involving machinery farming and use of improved varieties. Seed source was found as a critical determinant of the adoption of improved varieties as also highlighted Begho58. Farmers are more likely to adopt and benefit from novel varieties once they are established. Adopters mainly rely of certified seed compagnies, efficient and ideal distributors channels of improved seeds59.

Conclusion

Cowpea production systems, farming constraints in main growing agroecological sites, and major determinants influencing the adoption of improved cultivars by Cameroonian farmers were identified for the first time. Valuable insights are therefore provided on drivers of improved cultivars choices and uptakes, essential for guiding improvement programmes and agricultural strategies. The results showed that cowpea production is characterized majorly by sole cropping once a year on a less than 0.5 ha, with no chemical use by farmers not belonging to any organisation group and cultivating for food and income generation. Top constraints include drought, insect pests and lack of improved varieties. Results showed that improved cowpea cultivars are still poorly adopted in Cameroon because of some socio-economic features that rapidly need to be addressed. Agro-ecological zone, farm and workforce size, belonging to a farmer organisation, land ownership, seed source yield proportion sold and selling benefits were pointed as affecting positively the adoption of improved cowpea cultivars in the study area. Preferred traits like high yielding, longer storage duration, drought tolerance, early maturing, resistance to pests and disease should be prioritized when breeding for new cultivars. Strategies should be planned to ensure to farmers seed accessibility by strengthening seed distribution system and alertness of all already disseminated varieties by extension services. As limitations, this study uses surveys of smallholder farmers who potentially can be ignoring the diverse challenges of cowpea production. Also, perceptions of these farmers can lead to memory bias. As prospective research, studies should be done in order to understand how cowpea as a pulse crop could enhance the profitability in intercropping with cereals crops such as maize and sorghum in a mixing cropping farming system.
