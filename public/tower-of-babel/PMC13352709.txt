Targeting mRNA delivery using bio-inspired hybrid cell membrane-incorporated liposomes: a novel strategy for cancer therapy
Authors: Prakash Gangadaran, Fatima Khan, Ramya Lakshmi Rajendran, Sanjana Dhayalan, Atharva A. Mahajan, Kandasamy Nagarajan Aruljothi, Byeong-Cheol Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Messenger RNA (mRNA) therapeutics have attracted considerable interest in cancer immunotherapy owing to their capacity for safe, transient, and controllable protein expression without the risk of genomic integration. However, clinical translation remains limited by the shortcomings of conventional lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), including poor mRNA stability, inefficient cellular uptake, limited endosomal escape, unintended immune activation, and inadequate tumor targeting. Bio-inspired hybrid cell membrane-incorporated liposomes address these limitations by integrating the structural stability and tunability of synthetic liposomes with natural cell membranes derived from erythrocytes, platelets, immune cells, or cancer cells. These hybrid systems leverage native proteins, such as cluster of differentiation 47 (CD47) for immune evasion, integrins and adhesion molecules for prolonged circulation, chemokine receptors for tissue homing, and tumor antigens for active targeting through homotypic binding and receptor-mediated interactions. Following cellular internalization, fusogenic lipids and fusion proteins promote endosomal disruption, facilitating efficient cytosolic release of mRNA and resulting in superior transfection compared with conventional LNPs. Beyond delivery, these platforms can modulate immune responses by functioning either as immunologically inert carriers that minimize off-target effects or as immunostimulatory vectors that enhance antigen presentation, dendritic cell activation, macrophage repolarization, and CD8⁺ T-cell priming. Preclinical studies demonstrate robust antitumor efficacy with an acceptable safety profile, as evidenced by normal serum alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase levels, absence of histopathological organ changes, and IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) plasma concentrations within pre-specified thresholds in preclinical models, supporting the potential application of these systems in mRNA vaccines, immunomodulatory therapies, and combination treatment therapies. Although challenges related to large-scale manufacturing, quality control, and regulatory approval remain, advances in membrane engineering and microfluidics technologies are accelerating their clinical translation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40364-026-00953-9.

Introduction

Messenger RNA (mRNA) therapeutics provide versatile platforms for cancer immunotherapy by enabling transient protein expression without the risk of genomic integration. Hybrid liposomes, which combine synthetic lipid cores with natural cell membranes, have been proposed to address key delivery challenges, including mRNA instability and limited tumor targeting.

mRNA therapeutics have emerged as a transformative drug platform, gaining global recognition following the successful deployment of mRNA-based vaccines against infectious diseases. This technology enables transient intracellular synthesis of therapeutic proteins, including antigens, immune modulators, or proteins deficient in the patient, offering broad applicability across cancer immunotherapy, protein replacement therapy, gene editing, and cell engineering. The versatility of mRNA derives from its modular design; antigen sequences, untranslated regions (UTRs), and regulatory elements can be rapidly optimized to fine-tune protein expression and immunogenicity [1].

In cancer immunotherapy, mRNA-based vaccines have several advantages over DNA- and protein-based formulations. Because mRNA functions directly in the cytoplasm without requiring nuclear entry, it eliminates the risk of insertional mutagenesis and enables efficient translation into tumor-associated antigens. These antigens are presented via both major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and class II pathways, promoting robust activation of antigen-specific cluster of differentiation 4 (CD)4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell responses [2].

Early efforts in mRNA delivery established proof of concept for in situ protein expression but were limited by the intrinsic instability of naked mRNA, rapid enzymatic degradation, inefficient cellular uptake, and undesired activation of innate immune pathways. Subsequent advances in mRNA chemistry, including optimized cap structures, modified nucleosides, and refined UTRs, have substantially improved transcript stability, translational efficiency, and immune-stimulation control. A deeper understanding of cellular RNA-sensing mechanisms has further guided the rational design of mRNA molecules to balance immunogenicity with therapeutic efficacy.

To overcome persistent delivery-related challenges, intensive research has focused on developing nanoparticle-based delivery platforms that protect mRNA from degradation and enhance cellular uptake. Lipid- and polymer-based systems have shown particular promise, with lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), including liposomal systems, among the most effective carriers for mRNA delivery due to their high encapsulation efficiency, biocompatibility, and proven clinical success (Table 1) [3]. LNPs protect mRNA from nuclease degradation, facilitate endosomal escape, and enable efficient cytosolic translation. However, conventional synthetic liposomes often undergo rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system and exhibit limited tumor specificity, thereby reducing therapeutic efficacy [4, 5].

Bio-inspired hybrid liposomes overcome the limitations of conventional lipid nanoparticles by coating synthetic LNP cores with natural cell membranes derived from cancer cells, macrophages, red blood cells (RBCs), or dendritic cells (DCs). Cancer cell membranes facilitate homotypic tumor targeting, whereas macrophage or RBC membranes provide immune-evasive “stealth” properties and prolonged circulation [6]. This biomimetic strategy integrates the stability of LNPs with biological functions, including immune evasion and active targeting, thereby enhancing mRNA delivery, strengthening antitumor immune responses, and reducing toxicity in preclinical cancer models.

Hybrid Liposomes hold a unique position within the biomimetic delivery landscape, distinguishing themselves from three competing advanced delivery paradigms. First, clinically validated ionizable LNPs (e.g., BNT162b2 (Comirnaty), mRNA-1273 (Spikevax), Onpattro (patisiran)) achieve high mRNA encapsulation efficiency (greater than 90%) and efficient endosomal escape through protonation-driven inverted hexagonal lipid phase transitions, but rely predominantly on ApoE-mediated hepatic tropism and exhibit limited intrinsic tumor-targeting specificity, restricting their application in extrahepatic cancer indications. Second, pure extracellular vesicles (EVs) retain native biological signaling complexity and homotypic targeting capacity but suffer from critically low mRNA encapsulation efficiency (typically less than 5%), heterogeneous cargo loading, poor batch-to-batch reproducibility, and very low yield from ultracentrifugation-based isolation protocols, which collectively restrict their scalability and clinical utility. Third, protein corona-engineered nanoparticles exploit selective serum protein adsorption to redirect organ-level biodistribution but lack the multi-ligand biological surface complexity required for active receptor-mediated tumor targeting and do not intrinsically provide immune evasion, fusogenic endosomal escape, or immunomodulatory capacity. In contrast, hybrid liposomes simultaneously address all of these limitations by combining the high mRNA encapsulation efficiency and physicochemical tunability of synthetic LNPs with the multi-ligand active targeting, CD47-mediated immune evasion, fusogenic membrane-assisted endosomal escape, and immunostimulatory potential of natural cell membranes. This convergence of functionalities, which no single competing platform can simultaneously achieve, substantiates hybrid liposomes as a genuinely distinct and transformative delivery paradigm rather than an incremental modification of existing biomimetic strategies. Engineering hybrid liposomes: merging synthetic and natural components

Hybrid liposomes represent an advanced class of biomimetic nanocarriers engineered by integrating synthetic lipid bilayers—comprising phospholipids such as 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC) and 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE) with cholesterol and polyethylene glycol (PEG)-conjugated lipids—with natural cell membranes via lipid-lipid and protein-lipid fusion. This process generates biomimetic vesicles approximately 100–200 nm in diameter that retain the tunability of synthetic systems (size, charge, and cargo loading) while inheriting native membrane proteins, including adhesion molecules, CD47, and tumor antigens [7, 8].

These hybrid liposomes are derived from erythrocytes, platelets, immune cells (e.g., macrophages, DCs, leukocytes), or cancer cells (Table 3). This design strategy leverages the biological identity and functional complexity of natural membranes, including membrane proteins, glycoproteins, adhesion molecules, and immunomodulatory markers, while maintaining the structural stability, cargo-loading capacity, and physicochemical tunability of synthetic lipid systems, such as liposomes and LNPs, A quantitative summary of performance metrics stratified by membrane source across five outcome categories (circulation half-life, tumor accumulation, macrophage uptake reduction, mRNA expression enhancement, and endosomal escape efficiency) is provided in Supplementary Table S1 [9]. Fusion processes typically involve co-extrusion, sonication, or microfluidic mixing, producing vesicles that integrate both membrane components and can be further functionalized for targeted delivery [10].

A major advantage of hybrid liposomes is prolonged systemic circulation, with half-livesranging from 8 to 28 h depending on the membrane source, compared with 2–6 h for conventional PEGylated liposomes. Specifically, erythrocyte membrane-derived hybrid liposomes achieve the longest half-lives (approximately 28 h) [11], followed by leukocyte-derived systems (18–22 h), while platelet- and cancer cell membrane-derived hybrid liposomes exhibit shorter half-lives (8–16 h) owing to active adhesion properties of their surface proteins. This effect is largely mediated by membrane-associated “self” markers, such as CD47, which interact with signal regulatory protein-α (SIRPα) on macrophages to suppress phagocytic clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) [11].

In addition to improved pharmacokinetics, hybrid liposomes exhibit enhanced tumor homing, often demonstrating 4-6-fold higher tumor uptake than conventional liposomes or LNPs that rely primarily on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, which typically achieve less than 1% tumor accumulation. Cancer cell membrane coatings enable active homotypic targeting via adhesion molecules such as E-cadherin, galectins, and integrins, thereby promoting selective accumulation within tumor tissues [16].

Hybrid liposomes also exhibit superior immune evasion, with macrophage uptake reduced by up to 70% compared with conventional lipid carriers [13–17]. This immune invisibility arises from leukocyte- or erythrocyte-derived membrane proteins that mimic endogenous cells, reducing opsonization and complement activation. Notably, these biological advantages translate into improved functional delivery of mRNA [13, 14]. Hybrid systems consistently demonstrate 2–3-fold higher mRNA expression than standard liposomes or LNPs, owing to enhanced protection against nuclease degradation, improved cellular uptake, and more efficient endosomal escape facilitated by membrane fusion processes [18].

Figure 1 illustrates the hierarchical structure of hybrid liposomes, comprising a synthetic liposome or LNP core encapsulating mRNA, surrounded by a natural membrane derived from RBCs or cancer cells. Surface proteins mediate immune evasion and tumor-specific targeting, while intracellular membrane fusion enables cytosolic mRNA release and antigen expression. Collectively, these features create multifunctional nanocarriers that resemble endogenous vesicles, such as exosomes, and offer stability, targeting, and immune compatibility. Furthermore, the feasibility of scalable manufacturing using microfluidic technologies and the potential application of autologous cell membranes support the clinical translational potential of hybrid liposomes as next-generation platforms for targeted mRNA delivery and cancer immunotherapy. It is important to note that the quantitative performance metrics cited in this section derive from studies employing diverse membrane sources, mRNA cargo sequences, target cell lines, and animal models. Direct cross-study numerical comparisons should therefore be interpreted with caution, as differences in experimental design, membrane extraction protocols, lipid composition, and measurement methodology can substantially influence reported outcomes. The values cited throughout this section are intended to illustrate general mechanistic trends and relative advantages of hybrid liposomes rather than to serve as definitive clinical performance benchmarks.

Enhancing specificity and immune modulation

Achieving selective accumulation and uptake by tumor cells while sparing healthy tissues is a major challenge in cancer nanotherapy. Hybrid cell membrane-coated liposomes enhance specificity via homotypic targeting, in which cancer cell membrane-derived vesicles preferentially accumulate in tumors of their parental origin. This targeting is facilitated by the retention of surface adhesion molecules and antigens, such as E-cadherin, N-cadherin, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM), integrins, and galectins, enabling multivalent receptor clustering and caveolin/lipid-raft-mediated endocytosis, which bypasses lysosomal degradation [19, 20]. Consequently, cancer membrane-coated hybrid liposomes exhibit improved tumor localization beyond passive accumulation via the EPR effect [15, 16].

Beyond cancer cell membranes, platelet- and immune cell-derived membranes provide alternative targeting strategies by leveraging their innate biological functions. Platelet membrane-coated liposomes selectively bind to damaged or inflamed vasculature through interactions with P-selectin (binds endothelial E-selectin), GP1b (interacting with von Willebrand factor in tumor vessels), CD44, and integrins, often overexpressed in tumor endothelium, thereby achieving 2.5-fold higher pericyte association [21, 22]. Similarly, leukocyte or macrophage membrane coatings confer natural homing abilities toward inflamed tissues and tumor-associated niches, guided by chemokine receptors (e.g., CCR2 for CCL2 gradients) and adhesion molecules (e.g., LFA-1 for ICAM-1-mediated tumor infiltration), enhancing intratumoral penetration to 30–50 μm versus < 10 μm for conventional LNPs [23].

By mimicking “self,” these biomimetic hybrids evade recognition and clearance by the MPS, resulting in prolonged blood circulation (t1/2 = 28 h vs. 3.2 h for uncoated formulations) [11]. An important caveat in interpreting published tumor accumulation data is that apparent targeting efficiency in hybrid liposome studies often conflates genuine active targeting with prolonged systemic circulation that increases passive tumor exposure via the EPR effect. Mechanistic disambiguation requires controlled experiments, such as competitive receptor blocking or administration of homotypic versus heterotypic membrane-coated particles in the same tumor model. A further consideration is the formation of a serum protein corona upon systemic administration. Conventional LNPs rapidly acquire a hard protein corona enriched in apolipoproteins, complement proteins, and immunoglobulins, which can redirect biodistribution and functionally mask surface targeting ligands. For hybrid liposomes, mass spectrometry-based corona profiling has demonstrated that cell membrane-coated nanoparticles acquire a quantitatively distinct corona with reduced complement C3 adsorption, attributed to CD47 and self-associated glycoproteins on the membrane surface. Nevertheless, whether specific homotypic targeting ligands such as E-cadherin, EpCAM, and cancer-specific integrins remain sterically accessible and functionally competent following corona formation in complex human plasma remains to be conclusively established, representing a critical mechanistic gap for clinical translation. Additionally, residual off-target accumulation in liver and spleen remains detectable in murine models even with hybrid liposomes (typically 10–30% of administered dose), and whether this clearance profile is reproduced in humans, where complement activity, plasma opsonin composition, and SIRPα receptor polymorphisms differ substantially from rodents, warrants dedicated pharmacokinetic characterization in non-human primate models prior to first-in-human studies. The CD47-SIRPα axis from RBC or leukocyte membranes delivers a “don’t eat me” signal, reducing phagocytosis by 70–90% and limiting liver or spleen accumulation to < 10% compared with 70–90% for uncoated systems. Notably, this immune invisibility is achieved without the extensive PEGylation typically required for conventional LNPs, thereby avoiding PEG-associated immunogenicity and accelerated blood clearance upon repeated dosing [11, 24].

Moreover, hybrid membranes provide opportunities for immune modulation, extending their function beyond passive delivery vehicles. Erythrocyte- or neutrophil-derived membranes primarily act as immune-silent cloaks via CD47 expression and low phosphatidylserine exposure, thereby reducing unintended immune activation, cytokine storms, and systemic toxicity (26). In contrast, DC or cancer cell membrane-coated liposomes can actively engage the immune system: cancer membranes supply tumor antigens for cross-presentation, whereas DC membranes retain antigen-presenting machinery, including MHC molecules and co-stimulatory signals, enabling direct T-cell interaction and enhancement of antigen-specific immune responses. M1 macrophage hybrids repolarize tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from M2 to M1 phenotypes via retained TNF-α receptors and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), functioning as in situ cancer vaccines that promote robust CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocyte activation [25].

Thus, hybrid liposomes do not merely evade immune destruction passively; they actively modulate the immune system to elicit potent anti-cancer responses. Multi-source hybrids (cancer + RBC + DC) achieve 10–20% interferon-gamma (IFNγ) + CD8 T-cells, compared with 2–5% with conventional LNPs, and combine 8.1% tumor accumulation with synergistic T-cell priming. Collectively, these properties position hybrid liposomes as multifunctional immuno-nanoplatforms that integrate tumor targeting, immune evasion, and immune activation within a single rationally designed system, representing a transformative strategy for precision cancer nanotherapy and mRNA-based immuno-oncology (Fig. 2) [3, 26].

Improving mRNA stability and cytoplasmic delivery

It is important to distinguish between two forms of mRNA stability that are mechanistically independent. Intrinsic chemical stability of the mRNA transcript, encompassing resistance to hydrolysis, decapping, and deadenylation, is determined primarily by the chemical structure of the transcript itself, including N1-methylpseudouridine nucleoside modification, optimized 5′ cap structure, and poly-A tail length, and is not meaningfully influenced by nanocarrier composition. In contrast, bio-stability, defined here as protection of the encapsulated mRNA from extracellular and endosomal RNase A family members and innate immune RNA sensors such as TLR7/8 and MDA5, is substantially enhanced by hybrid liposome encapsulation and represents the primary stability benefit conferred by the biomimetic membrane coating. Throughout this section, references to mRNA stability in the context of carrier-mediated protection specifically refer to bio-stability rather than intrinsic chemical stability. Naked mRNA is intrinsically unstable under physiological conditions, with a circulating half-life of less than 10 min due to rapid degradation by ubiquitous extracellular RNases, particularly members of the RNase A family, and immediate recognition by innate immune sensors such as Toll-like receptors (TLR7/8) and the cytosolic RNA sensor melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5) [27–29]. Even when encapsulated in conventional liposomes or LNPs, effective mRNA delivery remains inefficient. Premature cargo leakage, often reaching 10–30% within the first 24 h, combined with poor endosomal escape (typically ˂ 2% of internalized mRNA, reaching the cytoplasm), severely limits translation efficiency, with only a small fraction (approximately 1–5%) of delivered mRNA producing functional protein [3, 30, 31].

Incorporating natural cell membranes into liposomal carriers fundamentally overcomes these barriers by introducing biologically evolved protective and trafficking mechanisms. Membrane-associated proteins such as glycophorins, CD47, and integrins form a stabilizing protein corona that sterically shields the nanoparticle surface from RNase adsorption and enzymatic attack, reducing mRNA degradation by 80–85% over 48 h, compared with approximately 40% protection from uncoated liposomes [30, 32, 33]. Concurrently, enrichment of membrane lipids, including sphingomyelin and cholesterol, enhances bilayer rigidity, reduces nanoparticle aggregation, lowers polydispersity indices (PDI; typically < 0.15, compared with > 0.3 for conventional systems), and improves colloidal stability during systemic circulation, with circulation half-lives frequently exceeding 24 h [32, 34].

Hybrid liposomes also exhibit superior cellular internalization due to the retention of native membrane proteins, which promote receptor-mediated uptake and membrane fusion. Additionally, fusogenic lipids such as DOPE and ceramide, often enriched in erythrocyte- or EV-like membranes, induce negative membrane curvature and promote direct plasma membrane fusion. Structural motifs retained from source membranes, including coiled-coil domains and fusogenic peptides, further enhance lipid bilayer mixing, as confirmed by Forster resonance energy transfer (FRET) assays and cryo-electron microscopy demonstrating bilayer merging. Collectively, these features result in 3–5-fold higher cellular uptake than conventional cationic liposomes or LNPs [35–37].

Endosomal escape remains the primary challenge for mRNA delivery, with traditional LNPs achieving cytosolic release efficiencies below 1–2%. Hybrid liposomes improve this step via synergistic mechanisms. First, membrane fusion with endosomal bilayers is promoted by EV-like lipids such as ceramide and DOPE, which favor the formation of inverted hexagonal (HII) phases and induce endosomal membrane destabilization, resulting in 4–5-fold higher mRNA release compared with lamellar LNPs [7, 38]. Second, fusogenic membrane components retained from source cell membranes, including coiled-coil domain-containing proteins and DOPE-enriched lipid domains, promote direct bilayer merging at the early endosomal membrane, upstream of full lysosomal acidification, as demonstrated by FRET-based bilayer mixing assays and cryo-electron microscopy showing bilayer coalescence. It is important to note that direct evidence for SNARE-domain-mediated or annexin-mediated endosomal escape specifically in hybrid liposome systems has not yet been established, and these mechanisms should not be attributed without experimental validation. Combined with ionizable lipids in the synthetic core (pKa approximately 6.2–6.5), which undergo protonation under endosomal pH to adopt an inverted hexagonal phase that disrupts the endosomal membrane, these temporally distinct but complementary pathways synergistically achieve cytoplasmic delivery efficiencies of 15–20% as assessed by galectin-8 puncta reporter assays in hybrid liposome systems, substantially exceeding the 1–2% reported for conventional LNP formulations [39, 40].

Quantitative validation of these mechanisms has been demonstrated using biomimetic nanocarriers such as membrane-coated nanocarrier–tumor necrosis protein (NC-TNP)/mRNA hybrids. These systems maintain structural integrity throughout endosomal trafficking, escape via controlled membrane rupture rather than premature disassembly, and release mRNA efficiently into the cytoplasm. These systems achieve 2–3-fold higher reporter gene expression, up to 90% target gene silencing in small interfering RNA (siRNA) models, and preserve mRNA integrity after 48 h of systemic circulation [39, 40].

Overall, hybrid liposomes integrate natural and synthetic design principles into a single platform, simultaneously addressing mRNA stability, cellular delivery, and intracellular trafficking. Unlike conventional LNPs, which require extensive multi-lipid optimization to balance efficacy and toxicity, hybrid systems leverage endogenous membrane biology to achieve robust, non-toxic mRNA translation, even in hard-to-transfect tumor tissues (Fig. 3). These advantages position hybrid liposomes as a next-generation platform for efficient and clinically translatable mRNA therapeutics [41, 42].

Applications in cancer therapy

Hybrid cell membrane–incorporated liposomes have emerged as a versatile platform for cancer therapy by integrating precise targeting, immune evasion, and multimodal cargo delivery within a single nanostructure. In the context of mRNA cancer vaccines, these biomimetic systems enable efficient delivery of tumor antigen–encoding mRNA directly to antigen-presenting cells (APCs) or the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby eliciting robust cytotoxic T-cell responses essential for durable antitumor immunity. Compared with conventional LNPs, membrane-coated hybrids exhibit superior uptake by DCs and enhanced antigen cross-presentation, resulting in 2–3-fold improvements in vaccine efficacy through optimized MHC class I and II trafficking pathways [21, 43, 44].

A representative example is the interleukin-15 (IL-15)–functionalized biomimetic hybrid mRNA vaccine composed of cancer cell membrane–coated liposomes encapsulating protamine-complexed cytokeratin 19 (CK19) mRNA. This platform promotes efficient DC internalization, antigen-specific CD8⁺ T-cell priming, and concomitant natural killer (NK) cell activation. Notably, IL-15 incorporation compensates for MHC-dependent limitations of tumor antigen presentation, producing synergistic antitumor effects in preclinical models [45]. Similarly, hybrid liposomes incorporating DC membranes or tumor-homing cancer membranes selectively target APCs and lymphoid tissues, further amplifying immune activation compared with uncoated LNPs [46, 47].

Beyond vaccines, immunomodulatory mRNA delivery represents another major application of hybrid liposomes. These systems have been engineered to deliver mRNA encoding cytokines (e.g., IL-12, IL-15), immune checkpoint regulators (e.g., programmed cell death ligand 1 [PD-1]/PD-L1), or chimeric antigen receptors to reprogram immune–tumor interactions directly within the TME. Hybrid liposomes coated with M1 macrophage membranes retain inflammatory surface markers and signaling receptors, enabling selective interaction with TAMs. Such platforms promote TAM repolarization from the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype toward the antitumor M1 phenotype while simultaneously expressing immunostimulatory proteins encoded by delivered mRNA, thereby reshaping the tumor immune landscape and reducing systemic inflammation [21, 48].

Hybrid liposomes also support combination therapeutic strategies by enabling co-delivery of mRNA with chemotherapeutics, siRNA, or genome-editing tools. For example, cancer cell membrane–coated liposomes engineered to respond to tumor-associated proteases have been employed to simultaneously deliver metabolic siRNA and chemotherapeutic agents, resulting in enhanced tumor accumulation and strong antitumor efficacy in orthotopic cancer models. Similarly, hybrid liposome-based systems co-delivering siRNA and cytotoxic drugs have demonstrated effective reversal of multidrug resistance in resistant tumor xenografts [49]. Emerging evidence further suggests that liposome-mediated delivery of clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9) targeting oncogenes, when combined with immunostimulatory mRNA achieves high gene-editing efficiency and potent tumor growth inhibition [50].

Targeting the TME represents an additional advantage of hybrid liposomes. By incorporating tumor-derived membranes and stimuli-responsive elements, these systems can respond to acidic pH, redox gradients, or tumor-associated proteases, thereby enabling spatiotemporal control over cargo release. Matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-activated hybrid liposomes engineered with tumor-targeting ligands have demonstrated enhanced intratumoral penetration and synergistic therapeutic effects in hepatocellular carcinoma models, reducing off-target toxicity while maximizing intratumoral payload delivery [51].

Across diverse preclinical cancer models, including melanoma, breast cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, and glioblastoma, hybrid liposomes exhibit superior tumor accumulation (approximately 4–8% injected dose per gram, compared with 0.5–1.5% for conventional platforms), enhanced therapeutic efficacy, and reduced systemic toxicity. Advanced multi-source hybrid systems combining cancer cell, erythrocyte, and DC membranes further improve therapeutic outcomes when mRNA vaccination is combined with adjunct therapies. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of hybrid liposomes as next-generation platforms for precision mRNA-based cancer therapy [52].

Challenges and future outlook

Despite the compelling preclinical efficacy of bio-inspired hybrid liposomes for mRNA delivery, their clinical translation remains constrained by several technical, regulatory, and biological challenges. Manufacturing complexity represents a principal limitation. The extraction of functional cell membranes requires reliable cell sourcing (autologous versus allogeneic), controlled lysis, differential centrifugation, and purification steps that preserve membrane protein orientation and functionality. Although current laboratory-scale fabrication methods—including co-extrusion, sonication, and microfluidic mixing—can reproducibly generate particles with low PDI (< 0.2) at the milligram scale, scaling to kilogram-level, good manufacturing practice (GMP)-compliant production introduces substantial challenges. These include lipid–membrane incompatibilities, solvent constraints, limited throughput of microfluidic devices, and batch-to-batch variability exceeding 20% in membrane protein retention and particle size. Mitigation strategies likely require continuous-flow manufacturing, standardized lipid-to-protein ratios, and automated process monitoring to ensure reproducibility and regulatory compliance [7, 53, 54].

Quality control and regulatory standardization pose additional challenges. Unlike conventional LNPs, hybrid liposomes require multidimensional characterization beyond routine dynamic light scattering and zeta potential measurements. Comprehensive quality assessment must include quantitative proteomics to verify membrane protein identity and retention, cryo-electron microscopy to confirm bilayer asymmetry and core–shell integrity, and functional stability assays under serum and RNase-rich conditions. Regulatory agencies will require clearly defined lot-release criteria that encompass mRNA encapsulation efficiency, endotoxin thresholds, and consistency of the membrane protein corona. However, standardized regulatory frameworks specific to hybrid nanocarriers are currently lacking, which complicates clinical translation [53, 55].

Safety considerations remain a central concern, particularly regarding immunogenicity. Allogeneic or cancer-derived membranes may present neoantigens or mismatched MHC molecules, potentially eliciting unwanted immune responses upon repeated administration. Preclinical studies generally report only transient cytokine elevations without overt cytokine release syndrome. However, prolonged or repeated exposure may elicit antibody-mediated clearance or immune sensitization analogous to the accelerated blood clearance phenomenon observed with PEGylated systems. Additionally, the oncogenic safety of cancer cell membranes necessitates rigorous tumorigenicity testing and pathogen-free membrane sourcing under GMP conditions. Critically, safety characterization should report specific quantitative readouts rather than general descriptors. Minimum required safety endpoints include serum alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase for hepatic function, histopathological examination of liver, spleen, kidney, and heart, and plasma concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α at defined time points post-administration. From a regulatory perspective, hybrid liposomes incorporating allogeneic or tumor-derived cell membranes do not map cleanly onto existing FDA approval frameworks. Depending on the primary mode of action, such platforms may fall under the combined jurisdiction of the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research and the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, requiring pre-IND consultation. The allogeneic membrane component introduces additional requirements analogous to those applied to cell and gene therapy products, including donor screening, adventitious agent testing, and lot-release specifications for membrane protein content. Regarding mRNA integrity during fabrication, energy-intensive membrane fusion processes can reduce the mRNA integrity number of the final product relative to starting material. Extrusion-based fusion may result in 15–25% payload loss, while microfluidic co-extrusion generally achieves the best balance between membrane protein retention and mRNA integrity. Standardized reporting of mRNA integrity number as a lot-release quality attribute is strongly recommended for hybrid liposome products [12, 56].

From a mechanistic perspective, key aspects of hybrid liposome behavior remain incompletely understood. Biodistribution and tumor accumulation vary considerably across species, with reduced efficacy observed in large-animal models compared with rodents, underscoring translational gaps. Moreover, the relative contributions of homotypic adhesion, receptor-mediated endocytosis, and non-specific uptake pathways remain challenging to quantify. Similarly, the precise roles of retained fusogenic proteins compared with synthetic ionizable lipids in facilitating endosomal escape require further elucidation. Addressing these knowledge gaps through large-animal pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies will be essential before clinical translation [57–59].

Future innovations are expected to accelerate the translational trajectory of hybrid liposome platforms. Emerging strategies include CRISPR-enabled genome editing using membrane-coated mRNA/single-guide RNA (sgRNA) systems targeting oncogenes or immune checkpoints, organ-specific delivery achieved through tissue-derived membrane coatings, and rational combination therapies integrating mRNA vaccines with checkpoint inhibitors or radiotherapy. Advances in modular, “plug-and-play” nanoparticle design, lyophilizable formulations, and AI-guided optimization of lipid–membrane compositions provide promising solutions for scalable GMP manufacturing. Notably, personalized vaccines incorporating autologous tumor membranes and patient-specific mRNA antigens hold particular promise for improving outcomes in immunotherapy-refractory cancers [60, 61].

Conclusion

Bio-inspired hybrid liposomes represent a compelling next-generation platform for targeted mRNA delivery in cancer therapy. By integrating the tunable loading capacity, colloidal stability, and endosomal escape efficiency of synthetic lipid systems with the immune-evasive, tumor-homing, and immunomodulatory properties of natural cell membranes, these carriers address several long-standing limitations of conventional nanomedicines. Preclinical evidence consistently demonstrates enhanced tumor accumulation, improved immune activation, and reduced systemic toxicity compared with conventional LNPs.

Although substantial challenges related to scalable manufacturing, quality control, immunogenicity, and large-animal validation remain, ongoing advances in microfluidic production, membrane engineering, and translational modeling indicate that these barriers are surmountable. With sustained interdisciplinary efforts, hybrid liposomes have the potential to transform mRNA-based cancer therapeutics, advancing personalized cancer vaccines and immunotherapies toward more durable, effective, and broadly applicable clinical outcomes.
