Silence, sacrifice, and subordination: Unpacking the gendered migration narratives of policy actors in Malaysia
Authors: Laura Foley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper adopts an actor-centered perspective to investigate how gendered ideologies permeate migration narratives in Malaysia. It sheds light on how policy-relevant actors narratively construct the dynamics of female labor migration, with an emphasis on domestic worker migration. This research found the presence of narratives that construct women migrant domestic workers as “victims” of abuse who are in need of protection, often connected to trafficking narratives, and whose work is undervalued and overlooked. Yet, despite such powerful discourses, migrant domestic workers remain largely unprotected in law, policy, and practice in Malaysia.Keywords: gender, migration, domestic work, migrant women, policy narratives

Introduction

This paper sheds light on the narratives of labor migration dynamics in Malaysia in order to reveal the ways in which implicit and explicit understandings and “knowledge” about gender enter and are produced by governance processes and practices. To do this, the paper analyses the perceptions of various types of policy-relevant “actors” in Malaysia’s governance system to uncover how women’s migration broadly, and domestic worker migration specifically, is narratively constructed.

There is a wealth of feminist scholarly literature that has exposed how the state and governance produce and reinforce gender-biased norms, processes, and discourses (Chappell, 2013; Foley, 2023b; Lovenduski, 1998). However, a lot of work on gendered governance has focused on democratic states in the West (Chappell, 2013), while much of the broader governance literature has focused on the Global North. There is a need to explore how gender dynamics are understood and responded to in different types of states in other regions, which this paper seeks to do by providing some original empirical perspectives.

The research is based on a combination of document analysis and semi-structured interviews that took place in 2018 with 41 policy-relevant actors in Malaysia and the wider region (see Table 1). The analysis draws on the concept of policy narratives, 1 which have been recognized as important components of policy (e.g., Boswell et al., 2011; Van Hulst and Yanow, 2016), and examines how actors narratively construct stories about labor migration dynamics in Malaysia. In order to communicate a narrative, an actor will, in essence, tell a story. This is not a neutral process as actors will shape their narrative in ways to gain support for their agenda by selecting the important issues that they deem as warranting attention, and creating a consistent story that explains what the “problem” is, how it came about (in which blame is assigned), and, finally, what policy intervention is needed (Boswell et al., 2011). In trying to make sense of a perceived problem and possible intervention, actors draw on a range of prior tacit knowledge and values from their own experiences and from sources such as media, academia, and religious sources (Van Hulst and Yanow, 2016). Scholars argue that assumptions about gender inform the knowledge surrounding migration (Foley, 2023b; Schwenken and Eberhardt, 2010).

Lim and Oishi (1996) noted that stereotypical understandings of male and female labor migration held by policymakers can lead to discriminatory policy outputs that overlook the actual needs of migrant women. Thus, it is important to shed light on how policy actors frame, categorize, and respond to different sectors, nationalities, and genders of migrant workers to uncover and challenge any stereotypes. This paper starts by outlining the methodological approach before examining the literature on gendered hierarchies and reviewing Malaysia’s legal and policy landscape. This is followed by the two findings sections before a conclusion.

Methodological considerations

Malaysia’s migration policies have received scholarly attention (e.g., Elias, 2018; Kaur, 2018; Nesadurai, 2013), as have the working and living conditions of women migrant domestic workers (WMDWs) in Asia (e.g., Elias, 2013; Killias, 2014). However, the perceptions of policy-relevant actors, that is, those who make, shape, or seek to influence policy, have been largely overlooked in research on the governance of migration. This paper seeks to contribute to closing this research gap by analyzing the ways in which implicit bias and gender bias permeate actors’ understandings, which may have important implications for policy responses.

This research combines document analysis and interviews with 41 policy-relevant actors (conducted in 2018, see Table 1). The documents reviewed include scholarly literature, policy papers, and reports published by international organizations (including United Nations entities), regional organizations, civil society organizations, and government ministries, alongside an examination of Malaysia’s migration laws and policies. 2 Another key source was online (international and national) media coverage of labor migration to Malaysia, alongside press releases (by state and non-state actors). This included press releases issued by Malaysian government departments, 3 civil society actors, and international organizations (especially the International Labour Organisation (ILO) and Human Rights Watch). The analysis of primary sources helped unearth different aspects of social reality because they are often produced by those involved in the policy-making and decision-making process during or immediately after an event.

Document analysis helped trace the policy-making process and identify key shifts, which could be incorporated into interview questions, alongside identifying international and regional actors to approach for the interview. Following the preliminary media and document analysis, purposive sampling was used to select participants with specific characteristics, that is, those in particular organizations within the governance system and those whose understandings would enable an in-depth examination of different aspects of Malaysia’s migration governance processes (Ritchie et al., 2003: 78). However, it warrants noting that actors’ perspectives may not necessarily reflect the organization or department that they represent.

The sample was selected to cover the perspectives of tripartite actors (government, employers, and workers), civil society actors who engage with both migrant workers and workers’ organizations, in addition to organizations that engage with governments across Southeast Asia on migration issues, and whose perspectives could shed light on consultation processes, including the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) deliberations. Additionally, I included some perspectives of “observers” (journalists and academics) due to their experience tracking Malaysia’s migration governance processes.

Participants were broadly asked: What shaped their understanding of labor migration; what they see as the causes and consequences of migration; and whether their understandings of labor migration are sufficiently reflected in policy outputs. The aim was to investigate actors’ intuitive understandings about migration, to analyze how implicit ideas about gender permeate, how actors make sense of migration, and to uncover whether actors thought of gender differences or made specific reference to women’s migration when speaking about migration. This study received ethical approval from the University of Sheffield in June 2017. The interviews were conducted in English and recorded, 4 then transcribed. Thirty-four interviews were held in person in Kuala Lumpur (February-April 2018), while seven were via Skype (January-May 2018) due to the participants’ locations in Bangkok, Jakarta, Singapore, Geneva, and New York.

Gendered hierarchies

Gender dynamics and gender norms shape all stages of migration (Foley and Piper, 2020). Yet, many studies historically overlooked the importance of gender dynamics, with migrant women either overlooked or viewed solely as “trailing wives” (Bastia and Piper, 2019: 15-30). In recent decades, the number of studies that have focused on migrant women has increased substantially (e.g., Briones, 2009; Lai, 2011; Lim and Oishi, 1996), and this paper seeks to contribute to this literature.

There is a need to uncover how actors understand female labor migration because of how policies and practices, which reflect socio-cultural customs, have produced a gendered division of labor that directs men and women towards certain types of jobs and sectors (Piper and Foley, 2024). In this gendered hierarchy, women’s labor is constructed as “disposable” (Wright, 2006); they conduct tasks that are socially and culturally devalued (e.g., care work) and receive less pay compared to male-dominated employment sectors (Piper and Foley, 2024).

Much of the literature on migrant women constructs them as vulnerable subjects who are in need of protection and saving. Women’s bodies, in particular, are seen as in need of constant protection from external “threats” (Yuval-Davis, 2002: 9-36). Women are seen as embodying the “nation” and thus must uphold its “honor” while men assume the role of protectors and saviors (Alexander, 2011: 373).

The literature and discourse on the experiences of migrant women often center upon narratives of sacrifice, victimization, and heroism, which run in contrast to the discourse on migrant men that centers upon themes of criminality, economic and security threats, illegality, violence, and hypersexuality (Griffiths, 2015; Scuzzarello, 2015), narratives that are also present in Malaysia (Foley, 2023a). In policy-making discourse, Schwenken (2008: 770-776) argues that how a migrant woman tends to be portrayed, that is, as either a “beautiful victim” or “sacrificing heroine,” will shape how female migration is responded to. The narrative of a “sacrificing heroine” who sends remittances back to their bv families is used to encourage women’s migration across South and Southeast Asia (Briones, 2009; Killias, 2014; Lai, 2011). Yet, narratives of sacrifice are problematic as they shift the emphasis away from migrant women’s agency and their own motivations to move overseas, for example, to escape oppressive family situations, and instead can produce discourses which focus on the impacts of their absence (Lai, 2011).

In both the Philippines and Indonesia, the two biggest sending countries of domestic workers to Malaysia, state discourse portrays WMDWs as “economic heroes” who are bettering the lives of their families and home communities, connected to the image of a “good woman” (Killias, 2014: 885-899). In contrast, there exists the “victim” and “slave” narrative, often linked to sex trafficking, 5 which is dominant in the domestic work sector. In the literature, while the victimization of migrant women is often emphasized, the narrative of migrant men as victims of slavery or trafficking is largely absent (O’Brien, 2018).

Chan (2014; 6950-6951) argues that such depictions of migrant women as economic heroes or exploited victims are underpinned by a “gendered moral hierarchy” about what makes a “good” or “bad” woman and worker, and whether they deserve attention or state protection (see also Mills, 2017). Narratives differentiate between the “guilty” and “innocent” “victim” and serve to underline migrant women’s “individual moral responsibility and blame” while downplaying the state’s accountability (Chan, 2014: 6951). For example, Chan (2014: 6956-6957) notes that WMDWs’ vulnerability to abuse by their employers has been largely depicted by Indonesian officials as linked to “female promiscuity and moral weakness,” instead of an abuse of power by employers. In Chin’s (1998: 142-147) analysis of employers’ narratives surrounding WMDWs in Malaysia, WMDWs were constructed as “untrustworthy,” “dirty,” and “inherently lazy and/or demanding” compared with female employers who were depicted as “modern, clean, trustworthy, and hardworking.”

However, as policy-relevant actors’ perspectives have been overlooked in the literature on migration governance, we lack knowledge of how female migration (broadly) and domestic worker migration are conceptualized and responded to. This paper seeks to close this knowledge gap.

Laws, policies, and practices

The number of documented migrant workers in Malaysia has increased from an estimated 1.7-1.8 million in 2017 (Prem Kumar and Kanyakumari, 2017) to nearly 2.5 million in 2024 (International Organization for Migration (IOM), 2025). However, participants stated that such official figures vastly underestimate the true scale of immigration due to the large number of undocumented migrants.

Domestic work is often depicted as the sector with the highest levels of labor abuses in Southeast Asia (ILO, 2023), and domestic worker migration is a contentious bilateral issue between Malaysia and the sending countries of Indonesia, the Philippines, and Cambodia due to the exploitation that their citizens may face while working in Malaysia (Elias, 2013; Kaur, 2014; Nesadurai, 2013). The most recently available estimates show that there are 300,000–400,000 domestic workers in Malaysia (UN Women, 2016). How countries manage domestic worker migration varies depending on the sending country. For example, Malaysia has signed non-binding Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs) with Indonesia and Cambodia, yet such labor agreements are insufficient in protecting WMDWs and can even perpetuate abuse (Deshingkar and Zeitlyn, 2014). However, the Philippine Government, via the Philippine Overseas Employment Administration (POEA) (which became the Department of Migrant Workers in 2022), negotiated better conditions for Filipina domestic workers in Malaysia (IOM, 2025):

The Philippine government has put a package of protection for household service workers […]. There is a ban on direct hiring for household service workers [;] they have to go through the Philippines agency and its partner Malaysian agency […]. In our law, it says […] that no worker should go to a country, […] unless the Philippine embassy in that country certifies that the worker is protected. (Representative, Philippine embassy, female, April 2018)

Compared to Indonesia, participants perceived that the POEA took decisive actions to protect the well-being of their overseas citizens, including implementing standardized contracts of employment, minimum rates of pay, set working hours, and rest days. The POEA also implemented a worker training program (including language and culture) that is seen as more comprehensive (according to the Philippine government official and domestic worker agent participants in this study) than those created by other sending countries. Filipina domestic workers were framed overall by interviewees as well-trained “smart workers” with higher levels of education and good English language skills, compared with Indonesian counterparts.

In Malaysia, the status of WMDWs is unique in the field of labor as these workers are subject to specific policies of control that are far more restrictive than those for other “low-skilled” migrant workers (ILO, 2016). Many of Malaysia’s national laws and policies on national workers and to migrant workers specifically exclude migrant domestic workers. For example, Malaysia’s Occupation Safety and Health 1994; Trade Union Act 1959; Employees Provident Fund Act 1951; Industrial Relations Act 1967; Workmen’s Compensation Act 1952 apply to national workers and migrant workers, but excludes WMDWs placing them at greater risk of exploitative conditions compared to other migrant groups (ILO, 2016; International Domestic Workers Federation (IDWF), 2024; IOM, 2025). Malaysia’s Employment Act 1955 defines domestic workers as “domestic servants” whose employment in the family home is beyond the remit of labor inspections, which prevents the state (and workers’ embassies) from being able to readily intervene when WMDWs are abused (Briones, 2009; Nesadurai, 2013: 89-113; ILO, 2016; IDWF, 2024).

In Malaysia, they [do] not [recognize] domestic workers as workers [;] that’s the first thing. So, if we are not workers, we don’t have any rights [;] we don’t have any umbrella, no protection. (Domestic Workers’ organizer, female, March 2018)

While the term “servant” was replaced with “helper” in official discourse at the time of fieldwork in 2018, this classification continued to devalue domestic work and contributed to the understanding that those employed in the sector are not workers. Some participants corrected my use of the term “migrant domestic worker” during fieldwork, and instead emphasized that these women are in fact “helpers” and not “workers.” In 2022, the Employment (Amendment) Bill 2021 was passed, in which the term “servants” was changed to “domestic employees,” following years of NGO advocacy for the removal of discriminatory clauses. This terminology change is significant, yet some participants view it as “cosmetic,” as it is insufficient to ensure that domestic workers’ rights are protected in practice. 6 Two other notable policy changes include the 2021 extension of social security to WMDWs in Malaysia who became entitled to coverage under the Employment Injury Scheme, and in 2024, WMDWs became entitled to the Invalidity and Survivors’ Scheme (IDWF, 2024).

WMDWs are also excluded from provisions that limit the permissible hours of work; their employers often underpay them and deny them rest days (IDWF, 2024; ILO, 2016). Workers also experience substandard accommodation, and they are frequently forced to perform extra duties for their employers’ friends (Elias, 2013; Kaur, 2014). Furthermore, WMDWs’ status is strictly temporary, with employer-tied work permits, which prevent them from permanent settlement. As domestic work is primarily a live-in position in Malaysia, WMDWs live in their workplace and are thus extensively monitored by their employers and often face physical, verbal, and sexual abuse (Elias, 2013). Furthermore, WMDWs’ freedom of movement is restricted, as are their reproductive rights (Kaur, 2014). In Chin’s (1998) study, the alleged sexual misconduct and of WMDWs was cited as a key reason by employers for restricting their movements and interactions.

Finally, international instruments exist, such as ILO Convention No. 189 (Domestic Workers), 7 most countries reliant on domestic worker in-migration and out-migration, such as Malaysia and Indonesia, have not ratified it, except the Philippines.

Invisible migrant women

So, firstly, were women present in actors’ understandings of Malaysia’s migration dynamics? The majority of interviewees, when asked about migration, intuitively spoke about male migration flows, and the discussions predominantly referenced male-dominated employment sectors in Malaysia (e.g., construction). For most interviews, women’s migration and domestic work were only discussed after I mentioned them, and, in most cases, the interviewee would then quickly revert to discussing male migration, which was depicted as more profitable and vital for Malaysia’s economic success. Participants who spoke of women’s migration unprompted were those who worked for organizations whose remit focused on domestic work or had a specific focus on female migration.

The “silence” surrounding female migration and domestic worker migration was particularly interesting for two main reasons. Firstly, the role of WMDWs in Malaysia dominated the media headlines in February 2018 (just after fieldwork commenced) after the death of Adelina Lisao, a WMDW from Indonesia who had been physically abused by her employer (BBC News, 2018). This event was followed by intense media coverage after the Indonesian ambassador to Malaysia suggested that Indonesia should implement a ban on sending its female citizens to work in Malaysia’s domestic work sector. Secondly, during fieldwork, it became apparent that many participants employed live-in WMDWs themselves currently, or have employed them in the past. So, even though domestic worker migration dominated media headlines and a migrant woman often lives or lived in their home, female migration was still overlooked by the majority of actors.

WMDWs are victimized at every stage of the migration journey

In this study, during each different stage of the labor migration journey, including 1) pre-arrival, 2) during pre-departure training, 3) upon arrival, and 4) their life as a domestic worker in Malaysia—female labor migration was largely narratively constructed as an act of “sacrifice” and “suffering,” with the women portrayed as isolated victims who were systematically exploited. Actors framed WMDWs as the “most vulnerable” and “most abused” workers due to a combination of Malaysia’s restrictive laws, policies, and practices, plus societal attitudes. Few participants referred to migrant women’s own sense of agency despite the existence of studies that challenge the victim approach and underline how migrant women make strategic choices through the migration process (e.g., Piper, 2003; Tacoli, 1999).

“Economic heroes” or “Sacrificial lambs?”

In this study, gendered ideologies attached to women’s position as mothers and family members are seen as playing a key role in encouraging women to move overseas due to the “sense of responsibility” to provide for their families back home. However, participants viewed the decision to migrate as often being rife with “family exploitation” as women lack decision-making power due to Southeast and South Asia remaining “a patriarchal society [where] men are the main decision-makers for the family” (Program officer, regional organization, female, March 2018). Women are seen as having to rely on either their parents, husband, or another male family member to make their migration decision for them, 8 sometimes without their expressed consent. Men, however, were described as able to make their own decisions about whether to migrate. Women’s perceived inability to make migration decisions was framed as a “protection” measure: Women were depicted as unable to make an informed decision about the reality of what they may face overseas, and as such, must rely on a male family member to make this decision.

Many actors questioned the accuracy of the “economic heroes” description pushed by state discourse, believing that this rhetoric was being used merely as a means to encourage more women to “sacrifice themselves” by moving overseas in order to remit money back home. As one participant explained, migrant women are told to “make yourself proud for your own country, you are the ‘heroes’ in the sense heroes like in the Philippines [discourse]” but that in reality this rhetoric is “a brainwash, I mean these are people to be sacrificed, I mean they know [it]” (Representative, Regional organization, male, March 2018). This participant believes that sending countries use the language of “heroism” to encourage their female citizens to move overseas for work, but women are actually aware they are being “sacrificed.” Men’s motivation to migrate to provide for their family was never framed as a “sacrifice” for them personally. The theme of sacrifice only came into narratives surrounding female migration.

While soon-to-be WMDWs are told to “have pride” in the “heroic” work that they will do to support their family and country, this narrative is understood by some participants as running in direct contrast to the “shame” that some countries feel at sending their female citizens overseas to work as “unskilled” domestic workers due to the high levels of abuse. This has led sending countries, such as Indonesia, to issue statements over the years highlighting their intention to stop domestic worker migration. Notably, one actor stated that Indonesian government officials have to put up with “unpleasant” conversations with their Malaysian counterparts about the fact that they themselves (the Malaysian officials) hire Indonesian domestic workers.

While sending countries encourage their women to move overseas to remit money back home through the rhetoric of “economic heroism,” sending countries feel an underlying “shame” at the abuse that this category of workers’ experience and thus seek to stop sending domestic workers overseas, which reflects the gendered assumptions about the inability of the state to protect “its” women (Elias, 2013: 403).

Pre-departure training: Learning to be subordinate

During the different stages of the migration journey, women in general, and WMDWs in particular, are understood as being subject to disciplinary mechanisms to render them subservient. WMDWs are understood as moving from one situation of “control” to another, from that of their families and/or husbands in their home country, to being placed under the “care” of a recruitment agency, to moving into their employer’s family home which is a “confined situation that is also highly paternalistic” (Regional specialist, international organization, female, February 2018). These “mechanisms of control” are broadly understood as starting in the workers’ own countries of origin during pre-departure orientation, in which recruitment agencies reportedly use religion, culture, and national stereotypical images of women to teach the soon-to-be domestic workers to be passive, subservient workers:

We did visits with the agency, and the training that they provide, and I noticed the way in which they use religious text and cultural reasons to situate the domestic worker in the employment relationship as the subordinate, that it is part of their duty and their role to cater to the employer’s needs […] it is something that I had heard about anecdotally elsewhere as well, you know they use the Quran, passages to sort of explain that it is part of their religious practice, duty to defer to the employer. (Specialist on Domestic Workers, international organization, female, January 2018)

When you go to the company, I was told in Indonesia, [that some] religious and other [organizations], even the pre-departure trainings offered by agencies, […] tell them “shut your mouth and do your job and be grateful, very proud.” (Representative, regional intergovernmental organization, male, March 2018)

So, actors understand that prospective domestic workers are given mixed messages prior to migrating. WMDWs are told they are “economic heroes” who should be proud of the work that they are doing overseas, but they are also being taught to be subordinate, to cater to their employer’s needs, and, according to one participant, to remain silent.

The mechanisms used to “control” WMDWs

Upon arrival in Malaysia, WMDWs are understood as being subjected to high levels of control and surveillance by their employers. Participants believe that some employers fear that granting WMDWs any freedom will lead to them “running away,” so they tightly control their movements and confiscate their passports, despite policies stating otherwise. As one participant explains, employers have spent a lot of money on “acquiring a domestic worker,” and “they do not want to lose their asset” 9 (Policy analyst, think tank, male, February 2018).

The controlling mechanisms used by employers were described by one participant as the accepted “social norm” of how to treat a domestic worker, with employers having no higher standard to which they can compare themselves, so they say, “of course I take my domestic worker’s passport, it’s normal […] I’m protecting her” believing that they are “doing the right thing” in that scenario (Domestic worker specialist, international organization, female, January 2018). Additionally, the lack of higher standards to which employers can compare themselves means they end up comparing themselves to other employers they know, convincing themselves that they are “not that bad” in comparison.

Some actors also believe that employers are convincing themselves that separating their domestic worker from her passport is somehow “protecting her” by controlling her movements. Recruitment agencies also continue to retain workers’ passports and property (Official, Indonesian Embassy, female, March 2018). This mechanism of control intends to ensure that the worker remains working for the employer and is less likely to flee if exploited.

Throughout this study, actors associated domestic worker migration with abuse, with the perpetrators ranging from the women’s families to the recruitment agents and employers. Abuse at the hands of their employer in Malaysia is understood as the gravest and potentially life-threatening. The decision to flee an abusive employer is understood as not being taken lightly, as it will result in the worker becoming undocumented, which can leave them susceptible to further exploitation. However, in cases of extreme abuse, the choice to stay can cost a WMDW their life, which is what some participants understand happened to Adelina Lisao, the Indonesian domestic worker who died in February 2018 (BBC News, 2018).

Domestic workers, they leave their [employers] because of very bad conditions, like the woman who just died […]. So, this one girl [Adelina], who did not dare to run away, […] died. (Representative, NGO, female, February 2018)

Importantly, actors who also employed WMDWs were keen to emphasize how different their own relationship is with their WMDWs, keen to distance themselves from abusive employers, emphasizing that they themselves treat their workers fairly, ensure that they have rest days, and “pay them more” compared with other employers.

“Lesser human beings”

The lack of value placed on domestic work appears to be due to the fact that: 1) these workers are female non-nationals, 2) the cleaning and caring tasks involved in domestic work are constructed as “an extension of the unpaid services ‘naturally’ provided by women,” and 3) this sector does not fit the notions of “work” associated with industrial relations regimes (see Ford and Piper, 2007: 66). In Malaysia, WMDWs are seen as “helpers,” not as paid “workers,” and are effectively denied legal protection: “In law they are not given the due recognition, they are not given the minimum wage, they are not given the weekend rest, they are not even able to join a union” (Trade Unionist, male, February 2018). This devaluation of care work in policy and practice is, of course, not unique to Malaysia, but endemic across societies.

Participants agreed that there are notable issues with Malaysia’s domestic work sector but provided different reasons as to why abuse occurs. Some assert that there exists a perception that “ethnic minorities” are “servants” in Malaysia, an understanding that is influenced by the existence of an “attitude or ingrained mindset that one culture is superior to the other” across Southeast Asia (Representative, regional organization, male, March 2018). One participant witnessed WMDWs in her household having to face “terrible conditions” including sexual exploitation, and believed that this treatment was due to WMDWs “not being humanized” in their employers’ eyes as “we do not think of them as equal to us, we do not believe that they have the same suffering as we do” (Business consultant, female, March 2018). Malaysia’s own colonial history, which led to the classification of domestic workers as “servants” in the country’s 1955 Employment Act, is also seen as reinforcing the understanding that those who come from poorer countries can be treated badly as they are viewed as “lesser human beings:”

Malaysians, by and large, when it comes to domestic workers, we really treat them quite [badly] […] we have this Master colonial mentality from our British time, so we tend to see a person who is from a poorer country in that manner. So, that is why Adelina's case and all that comes about because we see them as a lesser human being. (Legal expert, female, March 2018)

However, the domestic worker agent, who may seek to downplay issues that directly affect his business, had a different narrative.

[For] domestic workers, the quality of these sending countries needs to be improved because, number one, we have a lot of [issues] of abuse [and], exploitation because of mismatch criteria. Many of the migrant domestic workers […] are not trained for the job, so that may result in them […] not [being] happy with the job, or the employer not [being] happy with the worker. So, a lot of abuse will happen, a lot of premature terminations, running away, [and] become illegal. (Manager, domestic worker agency, male, March 2018)

Instead of blaming Malaysian employers for mistreatment, the agent blames the sending countries for not training their citizens properly and argues that this is why they are facing difficulties, such as abuse, overseas. This selective framing also “distances” the agency from blame for placing WMDWs with abusive employers.

Gendered understandings: The silent victim

Male and female migrants are understood as fundamentally different by the actors in this study for a multitude of reasons: From why they migrate, to how they are treated in the policies, and their respective experiences in Malaysia. Within the stories of migrant worker exploitation in Malaysia, there existed gendered comparisons of how men and women react differently when facing abuse. 10 On the one hand, migrant men were depicted as “more aggressive” and better able to “fight back” against abusive employers, whereas migrant women were described as “silent” and “helpless” due to cultural factors and to situational reasons, namely the location of their work “behind closed doors.”

Cultural factors

Chin and Mohd Daud (2018; 5) argue that, across Southeast Asia, states have deployed a range of strategies to control women, which include “codifying female submission and silence as desirable qualities through national propaganda and policies” and that this has “been reinforced by cultural discourse of honour and shame.” In this study, cultural factors in the workers’ countries of origin were seen as shaping migrant women’s behavior when they are faced with an abusive employer. The religious, cultural, and education systems in Asia were understood as reproducing a patriarchal society whereby, from a young age, men are treated differently by their families as they “learn” to be outspoken, to make their own decisions, and to be independent. Whereas women were described by one participant as submissive and silent, as they are taught from childhood by their families and by religious institutions to be “subordinate,” “decent,” and “not to talk back” against the elders and the males in their community. These childhood lessons and lived experiences, combined with the content of the pre-departure training, were depicted as shaping migrant women’s working behavior:

A girl may be seen as a subsidiary in most Asian cultures […], they are taught to be subordinate to the whole family. The education system actually inculcates them and says, “don’t talk against the elder; don’t talk against the men.” […] So I think the way they are brought up, culturally, religiously, and educationally, and told to “be a good, decent woman,” which means “don’t talk against the elders, don’t talk against the males, don’t talk [back] against the employer,” even when you are abused, even when you are beaten. (Representative, regional intergovernmental organization, male, March 2018)

WMDWs’ perceived “silence” even when they are “abused and beaten” was also connected to the narratives of “sacrifice” whereby women are seen as being “sacrificial lambs” who are “paying the price” for their family to benefit. In addition, migrant women, especially WMDWs, were often depicted as “naïve,” with some participants questioning whether they were sufficiently prepared for the reality that they would face working abroad. 11 The perception that WMDWs are naïve, liable to deception, and unprepared for the reality of their role also seems to stem from the fact that they are understood as having little formal education, are often illiterate, and are seen as receiving insufficient information about the role, where they will be living, and who their employer will be. However, it should be noted that actors differentiate between Indonesian WMDWs and Filipina WMDWs, with Filipinas understood as being more educated and more prepared due to the way in which the Philippines’ governance system for domestic worker migration is set up.

Situational factors

The second factor connected to the perception that men and women react differently when facing abuse is related to the sectors in which male and female migrant workers dominate. Men are sometimes seen as being able to “fight back” in incidents of workplace exploitation, 12 whereas there is “nobody” there to “protect” migrant women when they experience abuse in the workplace, as they are largely understood as working alone in households or workplaces that are not in the public eye. Most actors equate a migrant woman in Malaysia with a domestic worker, so their isolation is compounded by the fact that they are confined to their employers’ homes, which are not “workplaces” under the law (ILO, 2016). This poses serious issues in terms of WMDWs’ welfare as there are no monitoring mechanisms in place to check on their working and living conditions.

Due to this isolation and the lack of labor inspections for domestic work, if WMDWs experience abuse, they are understood as “helpless” as they are seen as unable to help themselves, and there is nobody there to protect them inside the home where they are often confined 24/7. One female government official emphasized how these women migrant workers are effectively “trapped” in workplaces behind closed doors where they are being “physically and sexually abused” by their employer and have “nobody there to protect them” and are understood as unable to challenge any of the abuse that they face as they “cannot reach out.” In addition to physical abuse, migrants also experience non-/underpayment of wages. 13 These impacts both male and female migrants, but WMDWs are often understood as more vulnerable, as they are seen as having “no power” and “no bravery” to do anything to challenge their employers, so [they] remain “silent” (Official, Indonesian embassy, male, March 2018).

WMDWs are seen as having limited opportunities to “reach out” for assistance from outside the home. Due to the non-existence of formal measures to seek help, WMDWs must rely on “informal” methods. For example, one embassy official explained that sometimes a neighbor may see a WMDW in distress and report it to the police or, in other instances, a WMDW will have to use the limited time they have away from their employer’s surveillance, such as when they are putting the bins out, to tell a neighbor or a passerby that they are in trouble and need assistance.

It is not only employers who were described as perpetrators of the abuse against female migrants. A range of participants, notably trade unionists and NGO representatives, described scenarios where they had been called to assist migrant women who had been physically abused by those in a position of power, including recruitment agents. One trade unionist (male, interviewed in February 2018) witnessed an array of situations where migrant women had been abused, “battered,” and “punched.” When abused migrant women seek help, they can even become further exploited by those who are supposedly there to help, such as a situation where a trade union had assisted a woman who had been sexually assaulted by an embassy employee when she had gone to the embassy to ask for assistance.

In connection with the understanding of why migrant women remain “silent” when faced with abuse, some actors also assumed that WMDWs in Malaysia are either victims of trafficking or are working in an undocumented capacity 14 (especially WMDWs from Indonesia), or both. One participant noted in relation to the case of Adelina, “she was probably undocumented and a trafficked victim” (Representative, regional intergovernmental organization, male, March 2018).

Barriers to reporting abuse

The perception that migrant women are “silent victims” is also connected to the noticeable differences between men’s and women’s ability to access justice for labor rights abuses. Both male and female migrant workers face multiple difficulties in providing sufficient evidence when pursuing legal action, as they are often not supplied with a written copy of their employment contract, nor are they given receipts for payments (UN Women, 2013). However, migrant women are understood as not only more likely to experience workplace exploitation, but also as less likely to be able to seek justice for violations “due to the informal and unrecognised nature of much of women’s migration and employment within the region” (Harkins and Åhlberg, 2017: xiii). Migrant women employed in the domestic work sector are understood as being particularly vulnerable due to the conditions of this specific sector (exempt from certain labor laws and outside the scope of labor protections). They are physically isolated, have restricted movement, and fear that if they run away from their employer to lodge a complaint, their contract will be terminated, which will not only leave them homeless but also liable to deportation (UNHRC, 2015).

On the rare occasions where a domestic worker has lodged a complaint with the police or with an NGO, it has been reported that those complaints have not been acted on (UNHRC, 2015), instead, in some instances, domestic workers have been intimidated for having fled their employer and are returned to those employers or to their employment agencies, rather than being offered any protection and different employment (UNHRC, 2015; 6). When complaints have been acted upon, the sentencing for abusive employers has been extremely lenient, for example, a female employer charged with attempted murder for horrifically injuring her domestic worker was subsequently released on “good conduct” grounds (The Straits Times, 2018).

Gendered narratives = Gendered outcomes?

Labor migration narratives are shaped by biased ideas about gender and work, with women still consigned to the periphery as female migration and domestic work rarely seem to feature in actors’ intuitive understandings of labor migration. When migrant women, the sectors they enter, and policy responses were explicitly discussed in this study, the discourse largely centered on the existence of a gendered labor hierarchy that undervalues the work that WMDWs do. Domestic work does not seem to conform to the notion of “work” due to: 1) the devalued tasks involved in domestic work, 2) where these tasks take place (the home), 3) how the sector is treated in Malaysia’s employment and migration policies, and 4) the fact that migrant women were rarely depicted as independent workers in their own right due to their perceived lack of choice and agency.

Gender-biased ideas have produced a system in which WMDWs are not categorized as “workers,” are not covered by labor law, and are often abused and exploited because of a combination of policies, practices, norms, and attitudes. Tacit ideas about gender, class, race, ethnicity, nationality, and labor inform understandings of domestic worker migration, whereby the sector remains seen as “dirty work” left for servants or “outsiders” to “undertake” (Briones, 2009: 40-41). This contributes to an environment where WMDWs are not seen as valuable workers deserving of protection but are instead constructed as “lesser human beings” whose plight remains irrelevant. Their well-being has not garnered significant public support, despite the role of NGOs and the media in highlighting cases of abuse, and Malaysia’s legal system does not take WMDWs’ abuse and exploitation seriously.

Furthermore, the perceived correlation between being a WMDW and being undocumented and/or being a trafficking victim is feeding into wider labor migration narratives, which seem to stem from the fact that these are connected with exploitation (see Foley, 2023b). Some policy actors seemed to believe that those who work in the domestic work sector could not possibly have chosen to enter this sector (denying migrant women’s agency) but instead must have been forced into it somehow. Some actors also seem to think that a WMDW is only putting up with abuse due to her undocumented status, which overlooks the multiple reasons why WMDWs may put up with the abuse that are not linked to their (perceived) lack of legal status. Migrant women can face exploitation even if they came on a valid work permit, where the employer met all the criteria and employed them in a legal capacity. As noted by Foley (2023b), the link between female migration and trafficking is also problematic—pushing for certain types of protection measures.

Despite the presence of powerful narratives in this study that construct WMDWs as “victims” of abuse who need protection, the policy actions that Malaysia has taken do not provide concrete protection to women during their migration and employment. Instead, WMDWs continue to be discriminated against in law, policy, and practice. Rights violations against WMDWs remain insufficiently acted on, such as the lenient or non-existent sentences being given to abusive employers, which, I argue, relates to the lower social and economic values placed on migrant women’s labor. This point was echoed by one participant:

It’s pure sexism. We’ve seen the reaction from the Thai government when you see slavery and trafficking happening in the fishing industry, you have a policy response that puts in place labour protections, but when you see slavery and trafficking happening in the domestic work sector, you apparently don’t […] I mean, really, it’s related to values of work. (Official, international organization, female, May 2018)

When issues of domestic worker abuse in Malaysia have been taken seriously, this has primarily been done by sending countries, and, even then, it was domestic outrage that compelled sending countries to act (Nesadurai, 2013), often by implementing gendered migration bans. Such bans do little to challenge the source of the discrimination and exploitation that WMDWs experience and instead frequently “heighten […] risks and curtail women’s rights” (Hennebry and Petrozziello, 2019: 116-117). Furthermore, managing domestic worker migration via non-binding MoUs also provides insufficient protection (ILO, 2016).

The demand for WMDWs to plug key childcare and eldercare gaps in Malaysia will likely continue to increase due to Malaysia’s aging population (Ministry of Finance, 2025) and increasing female labor force participation (World Bank, 2026). WMDWs play a key role in keeping households functioning, and steps must be taken to reduce incidences of abuse. WMDWs need labor rights and the ability to access justice for rights violations, not just to be banned from migrating via legal pathways in the first place. Domestic work needs to be considered as “work,” and the location of their employment must be regarded as a workplace that is liable to labor inspections. And while the harmful “servant” terminology has been removed, simply replacing it with “employee” (instead of worker) does not automatically give domestic workers any additional rights or protections, and it does little to tackle societal attitudes that devalue the type of work and those who perform it.

Finally, there is a need for further research on migration governance processes that unpack how actors’ intuitive understandings about migration are shaped by ideas about gender. While many of the actors involved in this study seek to influence policy trajectories in Malaysia, they would not be considered central to the policy-making and decision-making process (Table 1). Therefore, there is a need to capture the perspectives of key policymakers.
