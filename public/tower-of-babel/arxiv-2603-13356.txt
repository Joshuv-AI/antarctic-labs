Learning When to Trust in Contextual Social Bandits
Majid Ghasemi majid.ghasemi@uwaterloo.ca, Mark Crowley mark.crowley@uwaterloo.ca

Abstract
Standard approaches to Robust Reinforcement Learning assume that feedback sources are either globally trustworthy or globally adversarial. In this paper, we challenge this assumption and we identify a more subtle failure mode. We term this mode as Contextual Sycophancy , where evaluators are truthful in benign contexts but strategically biased in critical ones. We prove that standard robust methods fail in this setting, suffering from Contextual Objective Decoupling . To address this, we propose CESA-LinUCB , which learns a high-dimensional Trust Boundary for each evaluator. We prove that CESA-LinUCB achieves sublinear regret O ~ ​ ( T ) \tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}) against contextual adversaries, recovering the ground truth even when no evaluator is globally reliable.

Related Work

Contextual Bandits.

Research on bandit algorithms has progressed in several directions. Early work on linear bandits focused on developing exploration strategies with theoretical regret bounds.
2
,
12
, and
1
established the foundations for linear models, designing algorithms that perform well across diverse settings. While
3
later extended formal regret guarantees to Thompson Sampling, subsequent research expanded beyond linearity.
15
and
23
introduced Generalized Linear Bandits to handle non-linearity via link functions. More recently, the field has moved toward deep learning, with
40
introducing NeuralUCB and
39
extending the analysis to Neural Thompson Sampling. Our work revisits the foundational linear setting established by
1
, but challenges the implicit assumption of data reliability common to all these approaches.

Linear Bandits and UCB.

To handle high-dimensional or infinite state spaces, standard approaches assume a linear relationship between the context-action features and the expected reward. The Linear Upper Confidence Bound (LinUCB) algorithm
(
22
;
1
)
employs the principle of
optimism in the face of uncertainty
. It maintains a confidence ellipsoid around the estimated reward parameter
\hat{\theta}_{t}
and selects the action that maximizes the upper bound of the predicted reward.
1
provided the foundational self-normalized martingale analysis for this setting, proving
\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T})
regret bounds. We refine this estimator to account for epistemic trust weights within the confidence ellipsoid, avoiding the standard assumption that all data is equally valid.

Robust and Adversarial Bandits.

While LinUCB is efficient, it is brittle to corruption. The problem of learning with corrupted rewards has been extensively studied.
26
and
18
introduced algorithms robust to adversarial corruptions, assuming a global corruption budget
C
. In the contextual linear setting,
6
and
14
provided algorithms that tolerate adversarial attacks, but their regret bounds degrade linearly with the corruption level (
O(C\sqrt{T})
).
19
proposed using uncertainty weighting to filter corrupted data. However, all these methods fundamentally rely on the assumption that the "majority" of the data remains clean or that the corruption is sparse. Our work addresses the harder
hostile majority
setting, where corruption is dense in specific contexts.

Sycophancy and Alignment.

The phenomenon of sycophancy in LLMs, where models repeat user errors or flatter user biases was formalized by
31
and
29
. Recent studies suggest this behavior stems from both RLHF training dynamics
32
and reward gaming
28
. While
36
and
10
demonstrated that synthetic data interventions can reduce this behavior, these methods often lack theoretical robustness guarantees.

Truth Discovery and Crowdsourcing.

Recovering ground truth from noisy annotators is a classical problem in the domain. The Dawid-Skene model
(
13
)
and its modern variants
(
16
;
24
)
estimate a confusion matrix for each annotator to weight their feedback. However, these methods assume
static
reliability. The assumption is annotators are either reliable or not. They cannot handle "Jekyll & Hyde" evaluators whose reliability is a function of the context
x_{t}
.

Problem Formulation

We define the
Contextual Social Bandit
as a tuple
\mathcal{B}=\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{E},R^{*},\mathcal{P}_{soc}\rangle
, where:

•

\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the set of observable contexts.

•

\mathcal{A}
is the finite set of
K
available actions.

•

\mathcal{E}=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{M}\}
is a set of
M
social evaluators (annotators or AI feedback models).

•

R^{*}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
is the unknown ground-truth reward function (the
latent
objective).

•

\mathcal{P}_{soc}
is the social feedback distribution. At each time
t
, the agent receives a feedback vector
\mathbf{y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
drawn from
\mathcal{P}_{soc}(\cdot|x_{t},a_{t})
, where each entry
y_{t,m}
represents the noisy, potentially biased score from evaluator
e_{m}
.

The agent’s goal is to minimize latent regret with respect to
R^{*}
, despite only observing samples from
\mathcal{P}_{soc}
.

Definition 1

(Context-Dependent Bias)
.

The feedback signal from evaluator
m
at time
t
is given by:

y_{t,m}=R^{*}(x_{t},a_{t})+\beta_{m}(x_{t},a_{t})+\eta_{t}

(1)

where
R^{*}(x_{t},a_{t})
is the latent ground-truth reward,
\eta_{t}
is
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian noise, and
\beta_{m}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is a deterministic bias function.
Unlike standard robust bandits where
\beta_{m}\in\{0,\infty\}
(global corruption), here
\beta_{m}
is continuous and state-dependent. This term captures two distinct failure modes:

1.

Sycophancy:

\beta_{m}(x,a)\propto\pi_{soc}(a|x)
, where the evaluator aligns with the social consensus rather than the objective truth.

2.

Incompetence:

\beta_{m}(x,a)
is high in regions where the evaluator lacks domain expertise (e.g., a math evaluator judging politics, acknowledging this is not the case for LLMs alignment most of the times).

Definition 2

(The Trust Function)
.

We define the binary trust function
\tau^{*}_{m}:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
which indicates the decision boundary separating reliable feedback from corruption:

\tau^{*}_{m}(x)=\mathbb{I}\left(\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\beta_{m}(x,a)|\leq\epsilon_{tol}\right)

(2)

This function partitions the context space
\mathcal{X}
into a
Trust Region

\mathcal{T}_{m}=\{x\mid\tau^{*}_{m}(x)=1\}
and an
Untrustworthy Region

\mathcal{U}_{m}
. The difficulty of the learning problem is determined by the complexity of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{T}_{m}
. If
\tau^{*}_{m}
is realizable by a linear classifier (i.e.,
\mathcal{T}_{m}
is a half-space), the problem reduces to learning a hyperplane separation; otherwise, it requires a kernelized or neural approximation.

The Inevitability of Decoupling.

Standard approaches minimize regret against the aggregated social signal
\bar{y}_{t}
. Theorem
1
provides the formal lower bound, proving that any estimator relying on central tendency (mean/median) suffers linear regret
\Omega(T)
when the adversary controls the majority mass, leading to structural failure.

Theorem 1

(Contextual Objective Decoupling (COD))
.

Let
\pi_{soc}(x)
be the optimal policy under social feedback, and
\pi^{*}(x)
be the ground-truth optimal policy.
If there exists a sub-region
\mathcal{X}_{dec}\subset\mathcal{X}
with measure
\mu(\mathcal{X}_{dec})>0
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{dec}
:

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\beta_{m}(x,\pi_{soc}(x))>R^{*}(x,\pi^{*}(x))-R^{*}(x,\pi_{soc}(x))

(3)

Then, any algorithm
\mathfrak{A}
with sublinear regret on
\bar{y}
suffers linear regret on
R^{*}
. Specifically,
\mathcal{R}_{T}^{latent}(\mathfrak{A})=\Omega(T\cdot\mu(\mathcal{X}_{dec}))
.

Proof.

Let the observed social reward signal be
\bar{y}_{t}(a)=\text{Agg}(\{y_{t,m}\}_{m=1}^{M})
, where
Agg
is any aggregation function (mean or median). By assumption, the algorithm
\mathfrak{A}
achieves sublinear regret with respect to this observed signal. Let
r_{soc}(x,a)=\mathbb{E}[\bar{y}(a)\mid x]
be the expected social reward. The definition of sublinear observed regret is:

\mathcal{R}^{obs}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(r_{soc}(x_{t},\pi_{soc}(x_{t}))-r_{soc}(x_{t},a_{t})\right)=o(T)

(4)

This implies that the algorithm selects the social optimal action
\pi_{soc}(x_{t})
for the overwhelming majority of rounds. Formally, let
N_{T}(a\neq\pi_{soc})
be the number of rounds where the agent pulls a suboptimal arm (relative to the social objective). Since the regret is
o(T)
, we must have
\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{N_{T}(a\neq\pi_{soc})}{T}=0
.

Now, consider the latent regret
\mathcal{R}^{latent}_{T}
with respect to the ground truth
R^{*}(x,a)
.

\mathcal{R}^{latent}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(R^{*}(x_{t},\pi^{*}(x_{t}))-R^{*}(x_{t},a_{t})\right)

(5)

We lower bound this regret by restricting the summation to the decoupling region
\mathcal{X}_{dec}
:

\mathcal{R}^{latent}_{T}\geq\sum_{t:x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}_{dec}}\left(R^{*}(x_{t},\pi^{*}(x_{t}))-R^{*}(x_{t},a_{t})\right)

(6)

By the definition of the decoupling region
\mathcal{X}_{dec}
, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}_{dec}
, the social optimal action is strictly suboptimal with respect to the ground truth. That is,
\pi_{soc}(x)\neq\pi^{*}(x)
. Let the minimum suboptimality gap in this region be:

\Delta_{min}=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{dec}}\left(R^{*}(x,\pi^{*}(x))-R^{*}(x,\pi_{soc}(x))\right)>0

(7)

Since the algorithm converges to
\pi_{soc}
(due to the sublinear observed regret condition), for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a time
T_{0}
such that for all
t>T_{0}
, the probability of playing the social optimal action
\mathbb{P}(a_{t}=\pi_{soc}(x_{t}))>1-\epsilon
.

Therefore, for large
T
, the expected latent regret is bounded by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}^{latent}_{T}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}_{dec})\cdot\mathbb{I}(a_{t}=\pi_{soc}(x_{t}))\cdot\Delta_{min}\right]

(8)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{min}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}(x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}_{dec})\cdot\mathbb{P}(a_{t}=\pi_{soc}(x_{t})\mid x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}_{dec})

(9)

Assuming contexts are sampled i.i.d. from a distribution with measure
\mu(\mathcal{X}_{dec})
, and substituting the convergence probability:

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}^{latent}_{T}]\geq\Delta_{min}\cdot T\cdot\mu(\mathcal{X}_{dec})\cdot(1-\epsilon)

(10)

Taking the limit as
T\to\infty
, we have
\mathcal{R}^{latent}_{T}=\Omega(T\cdot\mu(\mathcal{X}_{dec}))
.
This proves that any algorithm successfully optimizing the observed (corrupted) objective must fail on the latent objective in the decoupling regions.
∎

(B) The Solution (

We introduce

\mathcal{L}(\theta_{a})=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_{\tau,m}(y_{\tau,m}-\theta_{a}^{\top}x_{\tau})^{2}+\lambda\|\theta_{a}\|_{2}^{2}

(11)

This formulation ensures that the influence of a data point on the learned policy is proportional to its predicted trustworthiness, effectively down-weighting adversarial feedback before it corrupts the reward estimate. Finally, to prevent the trust models from collapsing into the majority consensus, the agent performs a

p_{axiom}

5
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we provide the formal guarantees for
CESA-LinUCB
. We first prove that standard robust methods
(
19
)
are mathematically guaranteed to fail in the "Jekyll & Hyde" setting (Theorem
1
). We then derive the sublinear regret bound for our algorithm, explicitly characterizing the dependency on the complexity of the adversary’s strategy.

5.1
Consistency of Trust-Weighted Estimation

We first establish that our trust-weighted ridge regression estimator is consistent.

Lemma 2

(The Price of Distrust)
.

Let
\hat{\theta}_{t}
be the weighted estimator. A Type I error (False Trust:
\hat{w}\approx 1,\tau^{*}=0
) introduces non-vanishing bias
\beta
, rendering the estimator inconsistent. A Type II error (False Distrust:
\hat{w}\approx 0,\tau^{*}=1
) increases the variance of
\hat{\theta}_{t}
but preserves consistency.

Proof.

Consider the standard linear reward model where
y_{t,m}=x_{t}^{\top}\theta^{*}+\beta_{t,m}+\eta_{t}
. The agent minimizes the trust-weighted squared loss:

\hat{\theta}=\arg\min_{\theta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{t,m}(y_{t,m}-x_{t}^{\top}\theta)^{2}+\lambda\|\theta\|^{2}

(12)

The closed-form solution for this weighted ridge regression is:

\hat{\theta}=\left(\sum_{t,m}w_{t,m}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}+\lambda I\right)^{-1}\sum_{t,m}w_{t,m}y_{t,m}x_{t}

(13)

Let
\mathbf{X}_{w}
be the weighted design matrix and
\mathbf{y}_{w}
be the weighted target. We analyze the expectation of the estimator:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{X}_{w}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{w}+\lambda I\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{w}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{w}]

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{X}_{w}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{w}+\lambda I\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{w}^{\top}(\mathbf{X}_{w}\theta^{*}+\mathbf{b}_{w})

\displaystyle=\theta^{*}-\underbrace{\lambda(\mathbf{X}_{w}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{w}+\lambda I)^{-1}\theta^{*}}_{\text{Regularization Bias}}+\underbrace{(\mathbf{X}_{w}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{w}+\lambda I)^{-1}\sum_{t,m}w_{t,m}\beta_{t,m}x_{t}}_{\text{Adversarial Bias}}

Case 1: False Trust (Type I Error).
Suppose
w_{t,m}\approx 1
but
\beta_{t,m}\neq 0
(the source is lying but we trust them). The adversarial bias term becomes non-zero. Since
x_{t}
spans the feature space, the term
\sum w_{t,m}\beta_{t,m}x_{t}
does not vanish as
T\to\infty
. The estimator converges to
\theta^{*}+\Delta
, where
\Delta
is determined by the projection of the bias vector onto the feature space. This constant bias leads to linear regret.

Case 2: False Distrust (Type II Error).
Suppose
w_{t,m}\approx 0
but
\beta_{t,m}=0
(the source is honest but we ignore them). In this case, the Adversarial Bias term is strictly zero (since
w=0
). The estimator remains unbiased (asymptotically, as
\lambda\to 0
). However, the effective sample size decreases. The covariance matrix
\mathbf{V}_{t}=\sum w_{t,m}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}
grows slower. Since the variance of the estimator scales with
\mathbf{V}_{t}^{-1}
, False Distrust inflates the confidence ellipsoid, leading to increased (but sublinear) exploration regret
\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T/\rho})
, where
\rho
is the retention rate.
∎

5.2
Regret Bounds

We now analyze the convergence of
CESA-LinUCB
. The analysis relies on decomposing the regret into two phases: the
Epistemic Phase
(where the agent learns who to trust) and the
Bandit Phase
(where the agent learns the task using trusted data).

Theorem 3

(Regret Upper Bound)
.

Assume the true trust boundary
\tau^{*}
belongs to a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
with VC-dimension
d_{VC}
. With probability
1-\delta
, the cumulative latent regret of
CESA-LinUCB
is bounded by:

\mathcal{R}_{T}^{latent}\leq\tilde{O}\left(\sqrt{T\cdot d_{VC}}\right)+\tilde{O}\left(d\sqrt{T}\right)

where
d
is the context dimension.

Proof.

We decompose the regret into two components: rounds where the trust model makes a mistake (Epistemic Regret) and rounds where the trust model is accurate (Bandit Regret).
Let
\hat{\tau}_{t}(x)
be the predicted trust boundary and
\tau^{*}(x)
be the ground truth. We define a "Mistake" event
M_{t}
as any round where the predicted trust region significantly deviates from the ground truth, allowing adversarial data to enter the active set with weight
w>\epsilon
.

Step 1: Bounding Epistemic Regret.

The learning of the trust boundary
\tau^{*}
is a supervised classification problem with sparse labels (provided by the axioms). We employ the standard mistake bound analysis for online classification. For a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
with VC-dimension
d_{VC}
, the number of mistakes
M_{T}
made by an optimal online learner (e.g., Halving Algorithm or randomized weighted majority) is bounded by:

M_{T}\leq O\left(\sqrt{T\cdot d_{VC}\cdot\log T}\right)

(14)

In the realizable setting, this can be improved to
O(d_{VC}\log T)
, but we assume the agnostic setting (noise) for robustness.
During a mistake round (
M_{t}=1
), the agent may accept adversarial data. In the worst case, this leads to an arbitrary action. Since rewards are bounded in
[0,1]
, the regret per mistake is at most 1.

\mathcal{R}_{epistemic}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(M_{t})\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T\cdot d_{VC}})

Step 2: Bounding Bandit Regret.

Consider the rounds where
M_{t}=0
(the "Clean" rounds). In these rounds, the trust weights
w_{t,m}
correctly filter out the adversarial bias (up to a tolerance
\epsilon
). The problem reduces to a stochastic linear bandit problem on the subset of honest contexts.
We invoke the self-normalized martingale bound for linear bandits
(
1
)
. For the weighted ridge estimator
\hat{\theta}_{t}
, with probability
1-\delta
, the estimation error is bounded by:

\|\hat{\theta}_{t}-\theta^{*}\|_{\mathbf{V}_{t}}\leq\beta_{t}=\sigma\sqrt{d\log\left(\frac{1+TL^{2}/\lambda}{\delta}\right)}+\lambda^{1/2}\|\theta^{*}\|

(15)

where
\mathbf{V}_{t}
is the weighted covariance matrix. The cumulative regret for the LinUCB algorithm using this confidence bound is:

\mathcal{R}_{bandit}=\sum_{t\notin M_{T}}r_{t}\leq\sqrt{Td\log T}\cdot\beta_{T}=\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T})

(16)

Step 3: Conclusion.

The total regret is the sum of the regret during mistake rounds and clean rounds:

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{R}_{epistemic}+\mathcal{R}_{bandit}\leq\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T\cdot d_{VC}})+\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T})

(17)

This confirms that the regret scales with the square root of the complexity of the adversary’s lying strategy (
d_{VC}
) and the standard bandit complexity (
d
).
∎

Proposition 4

(Sycophantic Complexity)
.

Let
\kappa=\inf\{\text{VC-dim}(\mathcal{H})\mid\tau^{*}\in\mathcal{H}\}
be the complexity of the minimal hypothesis class capable of representing the adversary’s bias strategy. The cumulative latent regret of
CESA-LinUCB
scales as
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\kappa T})
.

Proof.

This result follows directly from substituting
d_{VC}=\kappa
into the Mistake Bound term (
M_{T}
) of Theorem
3
.
It provides the formal intuition for "Jekyll & Hyde" robustness: complex liars (high
\kappa
) force the agent to spend more time in the exploration phase (higher
M_{T}
), whereas simple liars (low
\kappa
) are identified and quarantined quickly.
∎

7
Conclusion

In this work, we challenged the "Global Trust Assumption" in robust reinforcement learning. We identified
Contextual Objective Decoupling
as a structural failure mode where even robust aggregators align with a hostile majority. To resolve this, we introduced
CESA-LinUCB
. A framework that learns high-dimensional trust boundaries to disentangle competence from adversarial intent.

Our theoretical analysis established that
CESA-LinUCB
achieves sublinear regret
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T\cdot\kappa})
, paying a price proportional only to the complexity of the adversary’s lying strategy (
\kappa
) rather than the volume of corruption. Empirically, we demonstrated that while standard and robust baselines collapse under a 50% sycophantic majority, our approach recovers the latent ground truth with negligible regret. Furthermore, the successful extension to Thompson Sampling confirms that
Epistemic Source Alignment
acts as a universal data sanitation layer, applicable across diverse exploration mechanisms.
