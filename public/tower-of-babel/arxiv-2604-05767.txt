Beyond the Beep: Scalable Collision Anticipation and Real-Time Explainability with BADAS-2.0
Roni Goldshmidt, Hamish Scott, Lorenzo Niccolini, Hernan Matzner

Abstract
We present BADAS-2.0, the second generation of our collision anticipation system, building on BADAS-1.0 7 , which established that fine-tuning V-JEPA2 1 on large-scale, ego-relevant dashcam data dramatically outperforms both academic baselines and ADAS systems. BADAS-2.0 advances the state of the art along three coherent axes. (i) Long-tail benchmark and accuracy: We introduce a new 10-group long-tail benchmark targeting rare and safety-critical driving scenarios — the primary testbed for evaluating model robustness on underrepresented collision types. To populate it, we employ an pipeline in which BADAS-1.0 serves as a deployed active oracle, continuously scoring millions of unlabeled drives to surface high-risk and informative candidates for annotation. Combined with Nexar’s geospatial Atlas platform 13 for targeted harvesting of rare long-tail scenarios, this strategy grows the training corpus from 40k to 178,500 labeled videos ( ∼ \sim 2M clips ), achieving consistent state-of-the-art gains across all benchmark subgroups, with the most pronounced improvements on the hardest long-tail categories. (ii) Knowledge distillation to edge: Domain-specific self-supervised pre-training on 2.25M unlabeled driving videos enables effective distillation into BADAS-2.0-Flash (86M) and BADAS-2.0-Flash-Lite (22M) — delivering a 7–12 × \times runtime speedup while retaining near-parity accuracy, making real-time collision anticipation practical for edge deployment. (iii) Explainability: BADAS-2.0 produces real-time, object-focused attention heatmaps that localize which scene elements drive each prediction. BADAS-Reason 17 extends this into language: a fine-tuned vision-language model that ingests the last video frame together with its heatmap and generates the required driver action alongside structured textual reasoning for avoiding the detected hazard. Inference code and evaluation benchmarks are publicly available.

Introduction

Ego-centric collision anticipation — predicting whether the
filming
vehicle
will be involved in a collision — is a distinct and harder task than
general accident detection. BADAS-1.0
7
established this
distinction: it re-annotated public benchmarks (DAD
3
,
DADA-2000
6
, DoTA
18
) to isolate ego-relevant events,
and demonstrated that fine-tuning V-JEPA2
1
on 40k ego-centric
dashcam clips outperforms both RNN- and transformer-based accident
detectors
16
;
10
and non-commercial forward-collision warning
systems
12
. The key insight behind this result is that
V-JEPA2’s masked spatiotemporal prediction objective learns to represent the

dynamics
of an unfolding scene — capturing the motion patterns that
precede a collision — whereas prior methods rely on appearance-based features
or semantic priors that transfer poorly to the ego-centric dashcam distribution.

BADAS-2.0 addresses three unsolved constraints from BADAS-1.0: the

training corpus
(40k clips) lacked rare long-tail scenarios,
leading to fragile performance;
inference cost
(2.5 s/window)
precluded on-device deployment; and the
scalar risk score
provided
no explanation of which object triggered an alert.

We address all three through an integrated arc: collect
more data,
intelligently
; train a stronger model; distill to edge latency; and add
explainability via heatmaps and VLM fine-tuning. Each stage builds on the
previous: the dataset is assembled using BADAS-1.0 as an oracle with a
human-in-the-loop (HITL) annotation process; the distilled models start from
SSL weights pre-trained on millions of unlabeled driving videos; and the VLM
pipeline is trained on frames selected by the BADAS attention mechanism.

Related Work

Collision anticipation.

Existing methods range from RNN-based adaptive loss functions
16

to spatial-temporal attention transformers
10
and graph networks
modelling agent relationships
5
. BADAS-1.0
7

showed the primary bottleneck is data alignment — ego-relevant events rather
than general accident footage — rather than architecture.

Active learning and model-assisted mining.

Self-training pipelines that use model scores to prioritise unlabeled data
for annotation are established in image classification
11
.
We apply this to collision anticipation: BADAS-1.0’s risk scores surface
high-risk clips for human review, scaling the corpus without exhaustive search.

Knowledge distillation for video.

Feature- and response-based distillation
9
are well-established.
For video, domain-specific SSL pre-training via V-JEPA-style masked
prediction
2
;
1
is a prerequisite: V-JEPA2 was released
only at ViT-L scale, and the domain gap from hand-gesture alternatives is
too large for supervised fine-tuning alone.

VLMs for driving.

Large VLMs are increasingly applied to driving
15
.
BADAS-Reason uses a fine-tuned VLM as an explanation generator consuming
BADAS attention outputs, adding natural-language reasoning at real-time speed.

3
BADAS-2.0 Dataset

3.1
Intelligent Data Collection

BADAS-1.0 was trained on
\approx
40k clips. Its primary weakness was
long-tail coverage: animal crossings, nighttime intersections, and adverse
weather were underrepresented, leading to fragile recall on these groups.
BADAS-2.0 assembles 178,500 labeled videos through two strategies executed
after BADAS-1.0 was trained.

Active mining with BADAS-1.0 (Oracle + HITL).

We run BADAS-1.0 over millions of unlabeled Nexar drives; clips exceeding
a risk threshold are surfaced for human review. Professional annotators
independently confirm or reject each label — the oracle acts purely as a
retrieval filter, and every clip entering training carries a human-verified
label. High-confidence false positives are retained as hard negatives to
directly reduce false alarm rates.

Rare-event harvesting via Nexar Atlas
13
.

Atlas is Nexar’s geospatial intelligence platform, which indexes billions of
road miles by scene semantics, weather, road type, time of day, and detected
objects. Targeted queries extracted clips matching underrepresented
categories — animal crossings, nighttime intersections, rain, snow, fog,
passing/overtaking scenarios — and sent them for expert annotation. This
directly addresses the long-tail gaps measured in BADAS-1.0.

3.2
Annotation and Labeling

Professional annotators applied a seven-type ontology (collision,
near-collision, hard brake, normal driving, intersection,
passing/overtaking, other), recording direction, third-party involvement,
and event timestamps.

4
Improved Core Model

4.1
Architecture

BADAS-2.0 fine-tunes a V-JEPA2 ViT-L backbone
1
(300M
parameters,
D
=1024, 24 transformer layers) end-to-end on 16-frame clips
at
256{\times}256
resolution and 8 fps. Spatial patch tokens are
mean-pooled across frames to produce a compact clip-level representation.
A future-prediction branch generates the backbone’s estimated scene
representation 1 s ahead and concatenates it with the current-clip
features, giving the prediction head access to both present evidence and
near-future dynamics. A three-layer GELU MLP (hidden dim 768, dropout 0.1)
maps this combined vector to a collision probability.

Training changes over BADAS-1.0: (i) gradient checkpointing for
memory-efficient full fine-tuning; (ii) a 2:1 negative-to-positive
oversampling ratio counteracting the
\approx
88% negative prevalence in the
mined corpus; (iii) cosine learning-rate annealing with label smoothing, gradient
clipping, and mixed-precision training. The enlarged dataset provides 4.5
\times

more labeled windows for learning rare scenarios.

5
Knowledge Distillation to Edge Models

5.1
The Domain Gap Problem

Meta released V-JEPA2 only at ViT-L scale; no smaller V-JEPA2 checkpoints
are publicly available. Training a randomly initialized ViT-S or ViT-B
directly on the BADAS supervision signal fails: without a strong video
prior the backbone never converges to reliable early-warning behavior.
Our SSL ablation (Table
3
) quantifies this gap precisely.

5.2
Domain-Specific SSL Pre-Training

Before any supervised training, we pre-train a ViT-B and ViT-S backbone on

2.25 million unlabeled Nexar dashcam videos
(
\approx
5M clips)
using V-JEPA-style masked feature prediction
1
. The SSL
objective trains the backbone to predict abstract spatiotemporal representations
of masked video regions from visible context, adapting positional embeddings,
motion representations, and attention patterns to the dashcam distribution.
This phase introduces no labeled data — only the raw video stream.

5.3
Two-Phase Knowledge Distillation

After SSL pre-training, we distill from the frozen BADAS-2.0 ViT-L teacher into
the domain-adapted student via a composite loss:

\mathcal{L}=\alpha_{\text{hard}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}+\alpha_{\text{logit}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}+\alpha_{\text{feat}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{feat}}

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}
is the KL divergence between
student and teacher soft probability distributions at temperature
\tau=4.0
,
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{feat}}
matches intermediate feature representations
(4 selected layers). Loss weights are
\alpha_{\text{hard}}=0.3
,

\alpha_{\text{logit}}=0.6
,
\alpha_{\text{feat}}=0.1
.

Training proceeds in two phases over 4,000 steps (effective batch 512, 8 GPUs):

Phase 1
(steps 0–3,000) trains with the full composite loss, allowing
the student to absorb the teacher’s calibrated uncertainty on borderline cases;

Phase 2
(steps 3,000–4,000) drops the teacher and trains only on hard
ground-truth BCE, sharpening probability outputs for deployment.

5.5
EWR and MTTA Dynamics Across Model Sizes

Two complementary metrics capture the temporal behaviour of each model variant.

Early Warning Recall (EWR)
measures what fraction of events the model
detects
before
they occur, and follows a clear capacity ordering:
BADAS-2.0 (91.3%)
>
BADAS-2.0-Flash (89.9%)
>
BADAS-2.0-Flash-Lite (85.5%).
Larger models detect a greater share of events, including subtle late-developing
collisions where only brief visual evidence is available; capacity-constrained
models require stronger proximate cues and miss these harder cases entirely.

Mean Time to Alert (MTTA)
measures the average lead time

among detected events only
, and exhibits the opposite ordering:
BADAS-2.0-Flash-Lite (1.46 s)
>
BADAS-2.0-Flash (1.42 s)
>
BADAS-2.0 (1.31 s).
This is a selection effect: smaller models only alert on unambiguous,
visually clear events — which tend to develop over longer time horizons
— while BADAS-2.0 also catches harder late-onset events where minimal warning
time is available, lowering its average lead time among detected cases.
The practical implication is that BADAS-2.0 provides the most total early
warnings across the broadest range of collision dynamics.

6
Extensibility: Heatmaps and BADAS-Reason

6.1
Training-Free Attention Heatmaps

BADAS produces spatial attribution heatmaps using a
training-free

method that requires no additional modules. For each prediction window, we
extract self-attention weights from late encoder layers (layers 12–20 for
ViT-L; 8–12 for ViT-B), apply exponential temporal weighting to emphasize
frames close to the alert:

w_{t}=\frac{\exp\!\bigl((t-7)/T\bigr)}{\sum_{t^{\prime}}\exp\!\bigl((t^{\prime}-7)/T\bigr)},\quad T=2.0,

(2)

spatially aggregate to a 16
\times
16 map per layer, average across layers,
upsample to 256
\times
256, and normalize to
[0,1]
. The only runtime
requirement is eager attention mode (disabling FlashAttention) to expose
weight tensors; a single forward pass suffices.

We evaluate localization via
Pointing Game Accuracy (PGA)
: whether
the peak activation pixel falls inside a ground-truth bounding box on 1,894
annotated danger clips.

6.2
BADAS-Reason: End-to-End VLM Fine-Tuning

Beyond binary risk scores and heatmaps, downstream ADAS applications need

natural-language explanations
:
“A pedestrian is stepping into
the lane from the right sidewalk. Brake immediately.”
We introduce
BADAS-Reason, a four-stage pipeline for producing such explanations via a
fine-tuned VLM.

Stage 1 — BADAS attribution.

We run BADAS-2.0 with attention attribution enabled on 8,680 dashcam videos
with human-authored scene descriptions. For each video we extract the
peak-risk frame (highest predicted probability) and its associated attention
bounding boxes.

Stage 2 — Structured label generation.

Each peak frame (with bounding boxes drawn) is passed to
Gemini
8
alongside the human-authored free-language description
written by the original annotator. Gemini converts each human description into the structured

{reasoning, action}
JSON schema — a one-sentence hazard
description and a 2–4 word driver command — yielding 6,862 valid samples
at 99.6% parse rate.

Stage 3 — Dataset assembly.

Images are prepared as
bounding-box-cropped frames
(256
\times
256, JPEG) with
bounding boxes drawn directly on the frame. An ablation study
(Table
5
) shows that adding heatmap overlays
hurts

performance (validation loss 0.628 vs. 0.612), likely because the colored overlay
occludes scene details the VLM needs for semantic reasoning. Samples are
split 80/10/10 via deterministic hash-based assignment for reproducibility.

Stage 4 — QLoRA fine-tuning.

We fine-tune Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct
17
with QLoRA
4

(rank 16, 11.8M of 4,400M parameters trainable) using supervised loss on
assistant-turn tokens, cosine LR (
1\times 10^{-4}
), batch 16, 3 epochs
with early stopping, on a single NVIDIA L4 GPU (24 GB).

Experiments

7.1
Setup

All models use 16 frames at 8 fps and
256\times 256
resolution. Sliding
window evaluation uses stride 1 (every sampled frame) for all reported
numbers. Unless stated otherwise, classification threshold is 0.75.
GPU evaluation uses FP16 precision with ahead-of-time operator-fusion compilation. TensorRT evaluations
on embedded platforms use FP16 engine compilation.

7.2
Long-Tail Benchmark

Table
6
reports per-group results on the 10-group long-tail
benchmark for all model variants. Several group-level findings are notable.

Infrastructure and intersection
are near-perfect for all
second-generation models (F1
\geq
0.93, EWR
\geq
0.93), indicating robust
coverage in the expanded dataset.

Animal
remains the most challenging group: BADAS-2.0
improves EWR from 66.1% (BADAS-1.0) to 78.9%, while simultaneously
reducing Animal FPR from 7.4% to 3.7% — the model detects more events
and generates fewer false alarms. This group requires integrating subtle

Weather groups
(fog, rain, snow) show strong performance
across all models (F1
\geq
0.90), benefiting directly from the Atlas-harvested
adverse-weather clips added to the training corpus.

False positive reduction.

A critical practical gain of BADAS-2.0 is its dramatically lower false alarm
rate. On the Kaggle benchmark, FPR drops from 10.9% (BADAS-1.0) to

4.6%
(BADAS-2.0) — a 58% reduction — with no loss of recall.
The long-tail table shows where this matters most: infrastructure scenarios
(AUC+F1 both reach 1.000) and passing/overtaking maneuvers (100% EWR)
were previously the two largest sources of missed detections and false
alarms. These are precisely the scenarios enriched by the Atlas-targeted
data collection, confirming that expanding hard-negative coverage directly
translates to false positive suppression in deployment.

8
Conclusion

BADAS-2.0 demonstrates that collision anticipation is, at its core, a data
and representation problem rather than an architectural one. The gains
reported here did not come from a novel loss function or a larger backbone
— they came from closing three systematic gaps that BADAS-1.0 left open:
a training distribution blind to rare events, an inference cost incompatible
with edge hardware, and a risk score that told operators
when
but
not
why
.

The most transferable lesson is the compounding value of a deployed model.
Once BADAS-1.0 was in production, it became the cheapest annotator in the
pipeline: its risk scores surfaced hard cases that human labelers would never
encounter through random sampling, its attention maps provided ready-made
supervision for BADAS-Reason, and its calibrated uncertainty transferred directly
to the distilled students through soft targets. That last point has a
prerequisite, however: the students could only absorb that uncertainty because
domain-specific SSL had already aligned their representations to the dashcam
distribution. The gap between a randomly initialised ViT-S (AP 0.693) and
its SSL-pretrained counterpart (AP 0.974) is larger than the gap between any
two supervised models in this paper — a result with direct implications for
how edge variants of future systems should be developed. Future systems that
neglect either half of this loop — the deployed oracle or the domain-adapted
student — leave substantial efficiency on the table.

Inference code and evaluation benchmarks are publicly available at the
project page.

Limitations and future work.

Animal EWR remains below 80% even for the largest model. A share of this
gap is irreducible: animals frequently enter the roadway with near-zero
warning time regardless of detection quality, and the fundamental constraint
is scenario geometry rather than model capacity. The remaining gap points
toward richer temporal context and multi-frame feature aggregation as the
most promising directions.

Beyond accuracy, three architectural directions stand out.

Semantic spatial grounding.
The current attention heatmaps
operate at patch resolution and are extracted as a post-hoc proxy.
Replacing them with a pixel-aligned segmentation head — trained jointly
with the collision objective — would promote each predicted region from
a saliency highlight to a typed object instance. Downstream components
could then condition on object class and extent rather than on a continuous
activation map, enabling more precise handoff to planning and intervention
modules.

Trajectory-conditioned risk.
BADAS-2.0 outputs a scalar
collision probability but does not model
which evasive action removes
the risk
. Augmenting the prediction head to jointly estimate a
safety-restoring trajectory — braking profile, steering correction, or
lane change — would close the loop between anticipation and action,
making the system directly actionable rather than purely alerting.

Intrinsic reasoning from model representations.

BADAS-Reason currently externalises explanation to a separate VLM that consumes
attention heatmaps as a proxy for model state. A tighter design would
extract textual reasoning directly from the encoder’s latent
representations — bypassing the heatmap intermediary and the modality
gap it introduces — yielding explanations that are causally grounded in
the same features that drove the risk score.
