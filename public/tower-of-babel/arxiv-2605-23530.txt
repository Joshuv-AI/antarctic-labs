Randomly twisted transfer operators and singular values statistics.
Frédéric Naud

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the singular values of a natural family of transfer operators twisted by large random permutation matrices. In the large N N limit, we obtain a Weyl law for its singular values, valid asymptotically almost surely with rapid decay. We also extend the so-called polynomial method to an infinite dimensional setting which implies a ”smooth” probabilistic Weyl law for singular values.

37C30, 37D20

1.
Setting and introduction

Transfer operators are ubiquitous objects in the study of hyperbolic dynamical systems and their asymptotic statistics. In the early works of the pioneers, they arised through Markov codings and where used as a basic tool to construct invariant measures and establish decay of correlations, see the classics
[
11
,
2
,
27
]
. For analytic dynamical systems, general transfer operators can be described as weighted sums of composition operators acting on suitable holomorphic function spaces. They were pioneered by David Ruelle in
[
32
]
. More recently, we mention the fairly general papers of Bandtlow-Jenkinson
[
4
,
5
]
, see also
[
6
]
, for sharp estimates on the eigenvalues.

Let us be more specific on the models we want to study here.
Let
\Omega_{0}
be a bounded, simply connected, bounded open set in
\mathbb{C}
. We assume that the boundary
\partial\Omega_{0}
is at least
C^{2}
regular.
Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{d}:\Omega_{0}\rightarrow\Omega_{0}
be holomorphic mappings, and
G:\Omega_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a weight function. We assume that the following holds.

(1)

Each map
\gamma_{j}
extends continuously to
\overline{\Omega_{0}}
.

(2)

For all
j=1,\ldots,d
, we have
\overline{\gamma_{j}(\Omega_{0})}\subset\Omega_{0}
.

(3)

The weight
G
extends continuously to
\overline{\Omega_{0}}
and is holomorphic on
\Omega_{0}
.

In addition, we pick
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\in U_{N}(\mathbb{C})
to be
permutation matrices
, chosen uniformly and independently in the symetric group
\mathcal{S}_{N}
. The twisted transfer operator
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
acts on holomorphic, vector valued functions
F:\Omega_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N}
by

{\mathscr{L}}_{N}(F)(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}e^{G(\gamma_{j}z)}U_{j}F(\gamma_{j}z).

These type of operators appear naturally in the geometric context of covers of hyperbolic surfaces, see for example in
[
20
,
12
,
29
]
, where their spectral properties are closely related to Laplace spectrum.
We will let
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
act on the Bergman space

\mathcal{H}_{N}:=H^{2}(\Omega_{0},\mathbb{C}^{N}),

defined by

\mathcal{H}_{N}:=\left\{F:\Omega_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N}\ :\ F\ \mathrm{is\ holomorphic\ and}\ \int_{\Omega_{0}}\|F(z)\|^{2}dm(z)<\infty\right\},

where
m
stands for Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}
and
\|.\|
is the usual Hermitian norm on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. The operator
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
is then compact and trace class, as composition operators by strict holomorphic contractions have this property, see
[
4
]
. The spectrum of
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
is therefore discrete outside
\{0\}
and we denote by
(\lambda_{j}({\mathscr{L}}_{N}))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
this sequence of eigenvalues with

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{j}({\mathscr{L}}_{N})=0
.

A typical example
.
Let
\Omega_{0}
be the open disc given by

\Omega_{0}=D\left(1,\frac{3}{2}\right)=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ :\ |z-1|<\frac{3}{2}\right\}.

For all
j=1,\ldots,d\leq\infty
, set
\gamma_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{j+z}
. Then it is easy to see that each Möbius transformation
\gamma_{j}
satisfies

\gamma_{j}(\Omega_{0})\subset D(1,1).

The associated transfer operator with weights
e^{G_{j}(z)}=\frac{1}{(j+z)^{2\sigma}}
is often called the Mayer-Perron-Frobenius operator, see
[
21
]
, and acts on scalar functions by the popular formula

{\mathscr{L}}_{\sigma}(f)(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(j+z)^{2\sigma}}f\circ\gamma_{j}(x).

When
d
is finite, which is the case we only consider in this paper, it is often associated to the dynamics of continued fractions with bounded entries and the ”open” Gauss map. The limit set generated by
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{d}
is a Cantor subset of
[0,1]
with positive Hausdorff dimension. These transfer operators are standard models of ”open chaotic systems” and are useful toy-models in quantum chaos, see for example the paper of Arnoldi-Faure-Weich
[
1
]
.

In this paper we will focus on the behaviour of spectra and singular values of general operators
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
in the large
N
regime, when permutation matrices
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}
are chosen uniformly independently,
d
being fixed. For all probabilistic statements,
{\mathbb{P}}_{N}
will denote the probability of an event while
{\mathbb{E}}_{N}
will denote the expectation. An event
\mathcal{E}_{N}
is said to occur asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.) if we have
\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{N}(\mathcal{E}_{N})=1
.

A first basic result that we will prove is a deterministic upper bound on the number of eigenvalues as
N
goes to infinity.

Theorem 1.1
.

Using the above notations, there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that for all
r>0
,

\#\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\ |\lambda_{j}({\mathscr{L}}_{N})|\geq r^{-1}\}\leq C_{0}rN.

This upper bound follows straightforwardly from an estimate of the trace norm of
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
and Weyl inequalities, see
\lx@sectionsign 2
. A natural and difficult problem is the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.2
.

Assume that
d\geq 2
. There exists an absolutely continuous measure
\nu
, compactly supported in
\mathbb{C}
, locally finite in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
, such that for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{*})
, we have

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\mathrm{Sp}({\mathscr{L}}_{N})}\varphi(\lambda)\right)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\varphi d\nu.

In the case when
d=1
and
G=0
, this is not true because spectra of single composition operators
T_{\gamma}
can be explicitely computed and involve powers of the derivative at the unique fixed point of
\gamma
in
\Omega_{0}
. The spectrum of
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}

is therefore the spectrum of the tensor product
T_{\gamma}\otimes U
where
U
is a random permutation matrix, and the limit distribution is not absolutely continous. We will assume that
d\geq 2
in the whole paper.

Should this conjecture be proved for
d\geq 2
, it could be considered as an infinite dimensional variant of the ”single ring theorem”, see
[
17
,
8
]
. To understand why this is a hard problem (due to the non-self adjointness), one has to be aware that for the finite dimensional problem of sums of
d
i.i.d random permutation matrices, the limit law is currently still not known when
d
is fixed, see in
[
7
]
for more insights. The problem is likely to be slightly easier if
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}
are chosen as independent, Haar distributed, unitary matrices, see
[
8
]
, but the techniques used in
[
8
]
should be replaced by a softer approach to allow an extension to infinite dimensional operators.

A fist step toward the analysis of non-self adjoint spectra is to study
singular values
instead. We recall that if
T:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is a compact operator acting on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, the
singular values sequence

\mu_{0}(T)=\|T\|\geq\mu_{1}(T)\geq\ldots\geq\mu_{j}(T)\geq\ldots>0

is by definition the eigenvalue sequence of the positive compact self-adjoint operator
\sqrt{T^{*}T}
. We prove the following fact.

Theorem 1.3
.

Set for all
r>0
,

\mathcal{N}(r):=\#\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ \mu_{j}({\mathscr{L}}_{N})\geq\frac{1}{r}\right\}.

Using the above notations, there exist constants
C_{2}>0
and
r_{0}>0
with the following property. For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, one can find
A_{k}>0
such that as
N\rightarrow+\infty
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(C_{2}^{-1}N\leq\mathcal{N}(r_{0})\leq C_{2}N\right)\geq 1-\frac{A_{k}}{N^{k}}.

In other words, with rapid convergence as
N\rightarrow+\infty
, we have an a.a.s Weyl law for the singular values of
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
.
The constant
r_{0}
can be made effective, for example if
\Omega_{0}
is a disc. The proof of Theorem
1.3
actually follows readily from the following moments result, which is of independent interest.

Proposition 1.4
.

Set
\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mu_{j}^{2}({\mathscr{L}}_{N})
.

(1)

There exists an explicit
L_{1}=L_{1}(G,\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{d})>0
such that we have

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2})}{N}=L_{1}.

(2)

Consider the moments

\mathbb{V}_{N}^{(k)}:={\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\left|\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2})\right|^{2k}\right),

then for all
k\geq 1
,
L_{2k}:=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{V}_{N}^{(k)}
exists and is finite.

(3)

Moreover, set for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

H_{i,j}:=\int_{\Omega_{0}}e^{G\circ\gamma_{i}(z)}e^{\overline{G\circ\gamma_{j}(z)}}B_{\Omega_{0}}(\gamma_{i}z,\gamma_{j}z)dm(z),

where
B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,w)
denotes the Bergman kernel of the space
H^{2}(\Omega_{0},\mathbb{C})
.
Consider the random variable

X_{N}:=\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}).

Then as
N\rightarrow+\infty
, we have convergence in distribution

X_{N}\overset{\mathrm{dist}}{\longrightarrow}2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(Z_{i,j}-1){\rm Re}(H_{i,j}),

where for all
i<j
,
Z_{i,j}
are independent Poisson variables with parameter
1
.

The fact that all the moments have a limit is by itself non-trivial.
Let us show how to derive Theorem
1.3
from Proposition
1.4
. First observe that the upper bound is actually valid deterministically, see Proposition
2.3
, so we only need to worry about the lower bound. Let
t>0
to be adjusted later on. By Markov inequality we have for all
N
large and a constant
A_{k}>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\left|\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2})\right|\geq t\right)

={\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\left|\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2})\right|^{2k}\geq t^{2k}\right)\leq\frac{1}{t^{2k}}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\left|\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2})\right|^{2k}\right)

\leq\frac{A_{k}}{t^{2k}}.

Taking
t=\sqrt{N}
and dividing by
N
we get

{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\frac{\left|\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2})\right|}{N}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\leq\frac{A_{k}}{N^{k}}.

Therefore using
(1)
we get for all
N
large

{\mathbb{P}}_{N}\left(\frac{\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}}{N}\geq\frac{L}{2}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\geq 1-\frac{A_{k}}{N^{k}},

which for all
N
large enough gives

\frac{\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}}{N}\geq\frac{L}{3},

with probability greater than
1-\frac{A_{k}}{N^{k}}
. Let us consider the counting function

\mathcal{N}(r):=\#\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ \mu_{j}({\mathscr{L}}_{N})\geq\frac{1}{r}\right\},

we can write

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mu_{j}({\mathscr{L}}_{h})^{2}=\sum_{\mu_{j}>1/r}\mu_{j}^{2}+\sum_{\mu_{j}\leq 1/r}\mu_{j}^{2},

which can be bounded as

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\leq M_{0}^{2}\mathcal{N}(r)+\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{d\mathcal{N}(\lambda)}{\lambda^{2}},

by using the fact that
\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}\leq M_{0}
uniformly in
N
, see below. Using the deterministic upper bound
\mathcal{N}(r)\leq C_{3}rN
from section 2, (
2
), we get by summation by parts

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\leq M_{0}^{2}\mathcal{N}(r)+3\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{N}(\lambda)d\lambda}{\lambda^{3}}\leq M_{0}^{2}\mathcal{N}(r)+3C_{3}\frac{N}{r}.

Therefore with probability greater than
1-\frac{A_{k}}{N^{k}}
, we have

\mathcal{N}(r)\geq M_{0}^{-2}\left(\frac{L}{3}-\frac{3C_{3}}{r}\right)N,

and we are done as long as
r>9C_{3}/L
.

Although Theorem
1.3
gives the exact growth rate of the counting function
\mathcal{N}(r)
as
N\rightarrow+\infty
, it does not provide a precise asymptotic description of the location of singular values. We will prove a complementary result, based on a different set of techniques, which we state below. Let
\mathbb{F}^{d}
be the
abstract free group on
d
generators

\mathbb{F}^{d}:=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d};a_{1}^{-1},\ldots,a_{d}^{-1}\rangle.

The left regular representation
\lambda:\mathbb{F}^{d}\rightarrow U(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d}))
is defined for all
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})
and all
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
by

\lambda(\gamma)(f):=f\circ\gamma^{-1}.

We define a bounded operator
\mathscr{M}_{\infty}
, acting on the tensor product
H^{2}(\Omega_{0})\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})
, by the formula

\mathscr{M}_{\infty}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}e^{G\circ\gamma_{i}}T_{\gamma_{i}}\otimes\lambda(a_{i})\right)^{*}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}e^{G\circ\gamma_{j}}T_{\gamma_{j}}\otimes\lambda(a_{j})\right),

where
T_{\gamma_{i}}:H^{2}(\Omega_{0})\rightarrow H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
is the composition operator associated to the contraction
\gamma_{i}
. Note that we have also the formula

\mathscr{M}_{\infty}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|e^{G\circ\gamma_{j}}|^{2}T_{\gamma_{j}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j}}\right)\otimes Id+\sum_{i\neq j}e^{\overline{G\circ\gamma_{i}}}e^{G\circ\gamma_{j}}T_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j}}\otimes\lambda(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}).

By definition,
\mathscr{M}_{\infty}
is a positive self-adjoint operator with non-negative spectrum.
We denote by
V_{N}
the subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{N}=H^{2}(\Omega_{0},\mathbb{C}^{N})
defined by

V_{N}:=\left\{F=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{N})\in\mathcal{H}_{N}\ :\ \forall\ z\in\Omega_{0},\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{i}(z)=0\right\}.

The subspace
V_{N}
is exactly the orthogonal to the subspace of functions
F\in\mathcal{H}_{N}
whose coordinates are all the same i.e.
F(z)=f(z)\otimes(1,\ldots,1)
. Note that both
V_{N}
and

V_{N}^{\perp}
are invariant by
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
since permutations matrices leave constant vectors invariants.

Theorem 1.5
.

There exists a finite positive Radon measure
\mu_{\infty}
, whose support satisfies

\mathrm{Sp}(\mathscr{M}_{\infty})\setminus\{0\}\subset\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\infty})\subset\mathrm{Sp}(\mathscr{M}_{\infty}),

such that we have for all
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
function
\varphi
with
\varphi(0)=0
,

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(\varphi({\mathscr{L}}_{N}^{*}{\mathscr{L}}_{N}|_{V_{N}}))\right)}{N}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varphi(x)}{x}d\mu_{\infty}(x).

Note that since
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}^{*}{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
is self-adjoint,
\varphi({\mathscr{L}}_{N}^{*}{\mathscr{L}}_{N})
is well defined for all smooth function, the hypothesis
\varphi(0)=0
is here to ensure that
\varphi({\mathscr{L}}_{N}^{*}{\mathscr{L}}_{N})
remains trace class. In the above statement, it is important to
observe that
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}^{*}{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
is restricted to
V_{N}
, to avoid the obvious spectra coming from constant invariant vectors.
Let us comment more on the result: the spectrum of the limit operator
\mathscr{M}_{\infty}
and the measure
\mu_{\infty}
describe the asymptotic distribution of singular values of
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
in the large
N
limit, and the above result shows that a smooth Weyl asymptotic for singular values holds on average. A straightforward

In

2.
Trace norm estimate and deterministic upper bounds

Most of the estimates pertaining to composition operators on
H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
will be achieved via Bergman kernel techniques, and we include an overview of the tool box
below.

2.1.
On Bergman kernels

We need to recall key facts about Bergman kernels which we summarize here and can all be derived from the elementary properties as explained in

[
19
]
. Let
\Omega
be an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{C}
, and
H^{2}(\Omega)
the associated Bergman space of holomorphic functions.
The
Bergman kernel
is the unique reproducing kernel
B_{\Omega}(z,w)
such that for all
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)
we have for all
z\in\Omega
,

f(z)=\int_{\Omega}B_{\Omega}(z,w)f(w)dm(w).

Given any Hilbert basis
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the (separable) Hilbert space
H^{2}(\Omega)
, we have (convergence is uniform on compact subsets of
\Omega
),

B_{\Omega}(z,w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\varphi_{n}(z)\overline{\varphi_{n}(w)}.

In addition, we have the following properties.

(1)

For all
z,w\in\Omega
, we have
|B_{\Omega}(z,w)|^{2}\leq|B_{\Omega}(z,z)||B_{\Omega}(w,w)|
.

(2)

For all
z\in\Omega
,
B_{\Omega}(z,z)=\sup\{|f(z)|^{2}\ :\ \|f\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 1\}
.

(3)

If
z\in\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega_{2}
, then
B_{\Omega_{2}}(z,z)\leq B_{\Omega_{1}}(z,z)
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{C}
and
r>0
. We also recall that in the case of a disc,
\Omega=D(x,r)
, a Hilbert basis of
H^{2}(D(x,r))
is given by the family of functions

{\bf e}_{\ell}(z):=\sqrt{\frac{\ell+1}{\pi}}\left(\frac{z-x}{r}\right)^{\ell},

while the Bergman reproducing kernel of
H^{2}(D(x,r))
is explicitly given by

B_{D(x,r)}(z,w)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{1}{r^{2}\left(1-\frac{(z-x)\overline{(w-x)}}{r^{2}}\right)^{2}},

see for example
[
19
]
.
From these elementary facts we will mainly use the following practical consequence.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
\Omega\subset B(x,r)
with
x\in\Omega
. Set

\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)=\inf_{w\in\Omega^{c}}|z-w|.

Then we have for all
z\in\Omega
,

\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}}\leq B_{\Omega}(z,z)\leq\frac{1}{\pi\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)^{2}}

Proof
. We have
D(z,\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega))\subset\Omega
therefore setting
\alpha=\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)
, we have

B_{\Omega}(z,z)\leq B_{D(z,\alpha)}(z,z)=\frac{1}{\pi\alpha^{2}}.

On the other hand we have

B_{\Omega}(z,z)\geq B_{D(x,r)}(z,z)=\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}(1-|z-x|^{2}/r^{2})^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\pi r^{2}},

the proof is done.
\square

Stronger estimates can be derived by using the conformal mapping theorem.
Indeed, since
\Omega_{0}
has a
C^{2}
-regular boundary, it is a ”Dini smooth” domain and there exists a conformal mapping
\psi:\Omega_{0}\rightarrow{\mathbb{D}}
,
where
{\mathbb{D}}
is the unit disc, such that
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
extend continuously to
\partial\Omega_{0}
. See for example in the book by Pommerenke
[
30
]
, Theorem 3.5. It allows to define an isometry
\mathscr{J}:H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})\rightarrow H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
via the formula:

\mathscr{J}(f):=\phi^{\prime}(f\circ\psi).

As an application, the Bergman kernel of
H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
is then given by

B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,w)=\frac{\psi^{\prime}(z)\overline{\psi^{\prime}(w)}}{\pi\left(1-\psi(z)\overline{\psi(w)}\right)^{2}}.

In particular, if
K
is any compact subset of
\Omega_{0}
, setting
R=\max_{z\in K}|\psi(z)|<1
, we have for all
z\in K
and all
w\in\Omega_{0}
,

|B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,w)|\leq\sup_{\Omega_{0}}|\psi^{\prime}|^{2}\frac{1}{\pi(1-R)^{2}}.

2.2.
A crude operator norm bound

In this subsection we prove the following crude bound.

Proposition 2.2
.

There exists a constant
M_{0}>0
such that for all
N
we have

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}\leq M_{0}.

Proof
. Denote by
T_{\gamma_{j}}:\mathcal{H}_{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{N}
the composition operator defined by

T_{\gamma_{j}}(F)=F\circ\gamma_{j}.

Since we can write

{\mathscr{L}}_{N}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}e^{G\circ\gamma_{j}}U_{j}\otimes T_{\gamma_{j}},

it is enough to bound each

\|e^{G\circ\gamma_{j}}U_{j}\otimes T_{\gamma_{j}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}.

Writing by unitarity of
U_{j}

\|e^{G\circ\gamma_{j}}U_{j}\otimes T_{\gamma_{j}}(F)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\|e^{G\circ\gamma_{j}(z)}U_{j}F\circ\gamma_{j}\|^{2}dm(z)

\leq\sup_{\Omega_{0}}e^{2|G|}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\|F\circ\gamma_{j}\|^{2}dm(z).

Using the reproducing property of the Bergman kernel we have for all
z\in\Omega_{0}
,

F(z)=\int_{\Omega_{0}}Id_{N}\otimes B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,w)F(w)dm(w),

and therefore by (
1
) and Cauchy-Schwarz we have

\sup_{z\in\Omega_{0}}\|F(\gamma_{j}(z))\|\leq\int_{\Omega_{0}}|B_{\Omega_{0}}(\gamma_{j}z,w)|\|F(w)\|dm(w)

\leq\sup_{\Omega_{0}}|\psi^{\prime}|^{2}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega_{0})}{\pi(1-R_{j})^{2}}\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}

with
R_{j}=\sup_{z\in\Omega_{0}}|\psi(\gamma_{j}z)|
. All these estimates are uniform in
N
and we are done.
\square

2.3.
Bounding the trace norm of
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}

In this subsection we will prove the following fact. We recall that given a trace class operator
T:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, the Schatten norms are defined for
p=1,\ldots,\infty
by

\|T\|_{p}:=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mu_{j}(T)^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

The norm for
p=1
is called the trace norm while for
p=2
it is nothing but the Hilbert-Schmidt norm.

Proposition 2.3
.

There exists
C_{3}>0
such that for all
N
large we have

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{1}\leq C_{3}N.

Proof
. Recall that by definition we have

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{1}=\sum_{j}\mu_{j}=\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{{\mathscr{L}}^{*}_{N}{\mathscr{L}}_{N}}e_{\ell})=\sum_{\ell}\langle\sqrt{{\mathscr{L}}^{*}_{N}{\mathscr{L}}_{N}}e_{\ell},e_{\ell}\rangle,

where
(e_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is any Hilbert basis of
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. By Cauchy-Schwarz this is bounded as

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{\ell}\|\sqrt{{\mathscr{L}}^{*}_{N}{\mathscr{L}}_{N}}e_{\ell}\|=\sum_{\ell}\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}e_{\ell}\|.

We will carry this estimate by using a rather ”explicit” basis of
H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
via the Riemann mapping Theorem (since
\Omega_{0}
is simply connected).
Recall the
\psi
is a conformal mapping
\psi:\Omega_{0}\rightarrow{\mathbb{D}}
,
where
{\mathbb{D}}
is the unit disc, such that
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
extend continuously to
\partial\Omega_{0}
.
A basis of
H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
is obtained for
\ell=0,\ldots,\infty
as

{\mathbf{g}}_{\ell}(z):=\psi^{\prime}(z)\sqrt{\frac{\ell+1}{\pi}}\left(\psi(z)\right)^{\ell}.

A full Hilbert basis of
\mathcal{H}
is then given for
j=1,\ldots,N
and
\ell=0,\ldots,\infty
by

\delta_{j}\otimes{\mathbf{g}}_{\ell},

where
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{N}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. We have therefore to estimate

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}(\delta_{j}\otimes{\mathbf{g}}_{\ell})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}.

Let us bound

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}(\delta_{j}\otimes{\mathbf{g}}_{\ell})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}(\delta_{j}\otimes{\mathbf{g}}_{\ell})\|^{2}dm(z)

\leq d\sup_{z\in\Omega_{0}}e^{2|G(z)|}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\|U_{k}(\delta_{j}){\mathbf{g}}_{\ell}(\gamma_{k}z)\|^{2}dm(z),

where we have used the standard inequality

\|Z_{1}+\ldots+Z_{d}\|^{2}\leq d\sum_{k=1}^{d}\|Z_{k}\|^{2},

valid for all
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, which follows directly from Cauchy-Schwarz. Using unitarity of each
U_{k}
, we are left with estimating

\int_{\Omega_{0}}|{\mathbf{g}}_{\ell}(\gamma_{k}z)|^{2}dm(z)=\frac{\ell+1}{\pi}\int_{\Omega_{0}}|\psi^{\prime}(\gamma_{k}z)(\psi(\gamma_{k}z))^{\ell}|^{2}dm(z),

\leq\frac{\ell+1}{\pi}\sup_{\Omega_{0}}|\psi^{\prime}|^{2}\int_{\Omega_{0}}|\psi(\gamma_{k}z))|^{2\ell}dm(z).

By assumption each
\gamma_{k}(\Omega_{0})
has a
compact closure
inside
\Omega_{0}
, therefore there exists
0<\rho<1
, independent of
N
such that

\max_{k}\sup_{z\in\Omega_{0}}|\psi(\gamma_{k}z))|^{2\ell}\leq\rho^{2\ell}.

As a conclusion, there exists a large constant
C>0
such that we have (uniformly in
N
)

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}(\delta_{j}\otimes{\mathbf{g}}_{\ell})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\ell+1}\rho^{\ell}=\widetilde{C}N,

and the proof is done.
\square

Note that in general,
\psi
is not explicit and therefore
\widetilde{C}
is not fully explicit, unless
\Omega_{0}
is a disc, in which case it is fully computable.

Let us end this section by giving a very short proof of Theorem
1.1
. By writing in the style of Markov inequality

\#\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\ |\lambda_{j}({\mathscr{L}}_{N})|\geq r^{-1}\}=\sum_{|\lambda_{j}|\geq 1/r}1\leq r\sum_{|\lambda_{j}|\geq 1/r}|\lambda_{j}|\leq r\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}|,

one can then use Weyl inequalities, see
[
33
]
1.6, to get

\#\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\ |\lambda_{j}({\mathscr{L}}_{N})|\geq r^{-1}\}\leq r\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{1}\leq C_{3}rN,

and the proof is now complete.

Note that we have also obtained, by the exact same idea, the deterministic upper bound for all
r>0
,

\mathcal{N}(r):=\#\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ \mu_{j}({\mathscr{L}}_{N})\geq\frac{1}{r}\right\}\leq C_{3}rN,

(2)

which will be useful below (and was used at the end of
\lx@sectionsign 1
).

3.
Expectation and variance of
\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}

The lower bound of
\mathcal{N}(r)
from Theorem
1.3
will follow from the asymptotic analysis of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm (squared)
\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|^{2}_{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mu_{j}^{2}({\mathscr{L}}_{N}).

We first need to introduce the necessary probabilistic tools involved in the proofs.

3.1.
Random permutations and fixed points statistics

As stated in the introduction, permutations matrices
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}
are chosen randomly, according to the uniform measure on
\mathcal{S}_{N}
, and independently. Alternatively, we can view it as choosing a random homomorphism
\phi_{N}:\mathbb{F}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{N}
, where
\mathbb{F}^{d}
is the
abstract free group on
d
generators

\mathbb{F}^{d}:=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d};a_{1}^{-1},\ldots,a_{d}^{-1}\rangle.

Indeed, for each generator
a_{j}
we choose
\phi_{N}(a_{j})
to be a permutation
\sigma_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
taken uniformly among
N!
possible choices. Since there are no non-trivial relations in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
, the homomorphism
\phi_{N}
is then uniquely defined. This leads to a natural probability measure on
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{F}^{d},\mathcal{S}_{N})
, denoted by
{\mathbb{P}}_{N}
.
According to this notations, we have that for all
j=1,\ldots,d
,
U_{j}
is the permutation matrix associated to
\sigma_{j}:=\phi_{N}(a_{j})
. We will clarify notations by denoting
U_{j}
by
U_{\sigma_{j}}
instead.
Given a word
\gamma=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\ldots\gamma_{p}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
with
\gamma_{i}\in\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{d},a_{1}^{-1},\ldots,a_{d}^{-1}\}
, we will denote by
U_{\phi_{N}(\gamma)}
the permutation matrix associated to
\phi_{N}(b)\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
. Note that we always have the identity

\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\phi_{N}(\gamma)})=F_{N}(\gamma),

where
F_{N}(\gamma)
is the
number of fixed points
of the permutation
\phi_{N}(\gamma)\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
. It is classical exercise in combinatorics to show that, when taken uniformly, the expectation of the number of fixed points of a random permutation is exactly
1
. In our situation, for longer words
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
, the distribution of
F_{N}(\gamma)
is in general not uniform.
Our main tool is the following result. We will denote by

\mathbb{F}^{d}_{0}
the set of
primitive words
in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
, i.e. words which are not a non-trivial power of another element. The identity word does not belong to
\mathbb{F}^{d}_{0}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Under the above notations, the following holds.

(1)

For all
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}^{d}_{0}
and
k\geq 1
, we have as
N
goes to
\infty
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{n}(\gamma^{k}))=d(k)+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

where
d(k)
denotes the number of divisors of
k
.

(2)

For all
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}_{0}
such that
\gamma_{1}\not\in\{\gamma_{2},\gamma_{2}^{-1}\}
, for all
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1
, we have as
N\rightarrow\infty
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(\gamma_{1}^{k_{1}})F_{N}(\gamma_{2}^{k_{2}}))={\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{n}(\gamma_{1}^{k_{1}})){\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{n}(\gamma_{2}^{k_{2}}))+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

(3)

For all
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 1
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}
, the limit

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left[\left(F_{N}(\gamma^{k_{1}})-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(\gamma^{k_{1}}))\right)\left(F_{N}(\gamma^{k_{2}})-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(\gamma^{k_{2}}))\right)\right]

:=\mathcal{V}(k_{1},k_{2})

exists. If
k_{1}=k_{2}=1
then
\mathcal{V}(1,1)=1
. Moreover we have the formula

\mathcal{V}(k_{1},k_{2})=\sum_{k|\gcd(k_{1},k_{2})}k.

Let us comment on this above Proposition. In
(2)
, the remainder is not uniform with respect to
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
and
k_{1},k_{2}
. Notice that since
F_{n}(\gamma^{k})=F_{n}(\gamma^{-k})
, one can also
use
(3)
if
k_{1},k_{2}
are not both positive. The major output of the above proposition is that, in the large
N
limit, the averages and covariances of the random variables
F_{n}(\gamma^{k})
asymptotically depend only on the powers
k
’s. This statement follows from the work of Nica
[
25
]
, see also the more general paper of Puder and Zimhoni
[
31
]
. For the exact derivation of the above Proposition from
[
31
]
, see
[
23
]
.

3.2.
Trace formula and expectation of
\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}

Let us compute
\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}
deterministically. Let
(\delta_{j}\otimes{\bf g}_{\ell})
, with
j=1,\ldots,N
and
\ell=0,\ldots,\infty
be a Hilbert basis of
\mathcal{H}_{N}
, as in the proof of Proposition
2.3
.
We know that

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}({\mathscr{L}}_{N}^{*}{\mathscr{L}}_{N})=\sum_{\ell,j}\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}(\delta_{j}\otimes{\bf g}_{\ell})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}^{2}.

Writing

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}(\delta_{j}\otimes{\bf g}_{\ell})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}^{2}=\sum_{1\leq k_{1},k_{2}\leq d}\int_{\Omega_{0}}e^{G\circ\gamma_{k_{1}}}e^{\overline{G\circ\gamma_{k_{2}}}}\langle U_{k_{2}}^{*}U_{k_{1}}\delta_{j},\delta_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}{\bf g}_{\ell}\circ\gamma_{k_{1}}\overline{{\bf g}_{\ell}\circ\gamma_{k_{2}}}dm,

we can then use Fubini and uniform convergence of
\sum_{\ell}{\bf g}_{\ell}(z)\overline{{\bf g}_{\ell}(w)}
to
B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,w)
on compact subsets of
\Omega_{0}
to get the trace formula:

\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{1\leq k_{1},k_{2}\leq d}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{k_{2}}^{*}U_{k_{1}})\int_{\Omega_{0}}e^{G\circ\gamma_{k_{1}}(z)}e^{\overline{G\circ\gamma_{k_{2}}(z)}}B_{\Omega_{0}}(\gamma_{k_{1}}z,\gamma_{k_{2}}z)dm(z).

(3)

We can now prove the first claim of Proposition
1.4
. Observe that according to our notations, we have

\mathrm{Tr}(U_{k_{2}}^{*}U_{k_{1}})=\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\phi_{N}(a_{k_{2}}^{-1}a_{k_{1}})})=F_{N}(a_{k_{2}}^{-1}a_{k_{1}}),

and therefore for all
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
we have by Proposition
3.1

(1)
,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\mathrm{Tr}(U_{k_{2}}^{*}U_{k_{1}}))}{N}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{k_{2}}^{-1}a_{k_{1}}))}{N}=0.

This leads straightforwardly to the formula

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2})}{N}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left|e^{G\circ\gamma_{j}(z)}\right|^{2}B_{\Omega_{0}}(\gamma_{j}z,\gamma_{j}z)dm(z).

Note that the constant

L_{1}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left|e^{G\circ\gamma_{j}(z)}\right|^{2}B_{\Omega_{0}}(\gamma_{j}z,\gamma_{j}z)dm(z),

is indeed positive, by positivity of the Bergman kernel on the diagonal, see Proposition
2.1
. We have actually the lower bound

L_{1}\geq de^{-2\sup_{\Omega_{0}}|G|}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega_{0})}{\pi\mathbf{R_{0}}^{2}},

where
\mathbf{R_{0}}
is such that
\Omega_{0}\subset D(x_{0},\mathbf{R_{0}})
, by the lower bound from Proposition
2.1
.

3.3.
Limit Variance of
\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}

We now turn our attention to the variance

\mathbb{V}_{N}^{(2)}:={\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\left|\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2})\right|^{2}\right)

={\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{4}\right)-\left({\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right)^{2}.

We will actually show, using Proposition
3.1
, that
\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{V}_{N}^{(2)}
exists and has an explicit formula.
Let us set for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

H_{i,j}:=\int_{\Omega_{0}}e^{G\circ\gamma_{i}(z)}e^{\overline{G\circ\gamma_{j}(z)}}B_{\Omega_{0}}(\gamma_{i}z,\gamma_{j}z)dm(z).

Note that we always have
H_{j,i}=\overline{H_{i,j}}
. By the trace formula (
5.4
), we have

\mathbb{V}_{N}^{(2)}=

\sum_{1\leq i_{1},i_{2}\leq d\atop 1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq d}\left\{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}})F_{N}(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}))-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}})){\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}))\right\}H_{i_{1},i_{2}}\overline{H_{j_{1},j_{2}}}.

We observe first that if
i_{1}=i_{2}
then we get

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}})F_{N}(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}))-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}})){\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}))

={\mathbb{E}}_{N}(NF_{N}(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}))-N{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}))=0.

Same outcome if
j_{1}=j_{2}
. We are thus left with the sum

\mathbb{V}_{N}^{(2)}=

\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}\atop j_{1}\neq j_{2}}\left\{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}})F_{N}(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}))-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}})){\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}))\right\}H_{i_{1},i_{2}}\overline{H_{j_{1},j_{2}}}.

Assuming that
(i_{1},i_{2})\not\in\{(j_{1},j_{2}),(j_{2},j_{1})\}
then since both words
a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}}
and
a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}
are reduced in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
,
we know that
a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}}\not\in\{a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}},(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}})^{-1}\}
. We can therefore apply Proposition
3.1

(2)
to get

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}})F_{N}(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}))-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}})){\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}}))\right)=0.

On the other hand, if
(i_{1},i_{2})\in\{(j_{1},j_{2}),(j_{2},j_{1})\}
, noticing that the words
a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}}
are primitive (not proper powers), and using the fact that

F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}})=F_{N}((a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}})^{-1}),

we can apply
Proposition
3.1

(3)
which yields in both cases
(i_{1},i_{2})=(j_{1},j_{2})
,
(i_{1},i_{2})=(j_{2},j_{1})

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left((F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}}))^{2}\right)-\left({\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}}))\right)^{2}=\mathcal{V}(1,1)=1.

Therefore we have obtained

L_{2}:=\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{V}_{N}^{(2)}=\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}}H_{i_{1},i_{2}}(\overline{H_{i_{1},i_{2}}}+\overline{H_{i_{2},i_{1}}})

=\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}}H_{i_{1},i_{2}}(\overline{H_{i_{1},i_{2}}}+H_{i_{1},i_{2}})=2\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}}H_{i_{1},i_{2}}{\rm Re}(H_{i_{1},i_{2}})

=4\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}({\rm Re}(H_{i_{1},i_{2}}))^{2}.

One may wonder at this point if
L_{2}
is in general positive. Going back to the example of the introduction, we have in this case

H_{j,j+1}=\frac{4}{9\pi}\int_{D(1,3/2)}\frac{dm(z)}{\left(1-\frac{4}{9}(\gamma_{j}(z)-1)(\overline{\gamma_{j+1}(z)}-1)\right)^{2}}.

We have the bound

\sup_{z\in\Omega_{0}}|\gamma_{j}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{j-1/2},

hence uniform convergence on
\Omega_{0}=D(1,3/2)
of
\gamma_{j}(z)
to
0
as
j\rightarrow+\infty
. This allows us to obtain

\lim_{j\rightarrow+\infty}H_{j,j+1}=\frac{4}{9\pi}\int_{D(1,3/2)}\frac{dm(z)}{\left(1-\frac{4}{9}\right)^{2}}=\frac{81}{25}.

Therefore if

is taken large enough, for the example stated in the introduction, we have

.

4.
Higher moments and Poisson distributions

Let us set in the following

X_{N}:=\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\|{\mathscr{L}}_{N}\|_{2}^{2}).

We recall that we have, using the above notations

X_{N}=2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}\left(F_{N}(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j})-{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}))\right){\rm Re}(H_{i,j}),

and using Proposition
3.1
(each word
a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}
is primitive), we know that for all
i<j
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(F_{N}(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}))=1+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

therefore we have as
N\rightarrow+\infty
,

X_{N}=2Y_{N}-2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}{\rm Re}(H_{i,j})+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

where we have set

Y_{N}:=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}F_{N}(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}){\rm Re}(H_{i,j}).

We will
compute below the limit of each moments of
Y_{N}
, which imply convergence of all moments of
X_{N}
, and then use a classical result to obtain convergence in distribution
to a linear combination of independent Poisson variables, which is part
(3)
of Proposition
1.4
. We first recall basic facts about linear combinations of independent Poisson variables.

4.1.
Poisson variables

A Poisson variable
Z
with parameter
\lambda
obeys the probability law for
k=0,\ldots,\infty

{\mathbb{P}}(Z=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{k}}{k!}.

The (non-centered) moments are given by

{\mathbb{E}}\left(Z^{k}\right)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}S(k,\ell)\lambda^{\ell},

where
S(k,\ell)
are so-called Stirling numbers, which is the number of ways to partition a set of
k
objects into
\ell
non-empty subsets.

The case
\lambda=1
, which is especially relevant for us, gives

{\mathbb{E}}\left(Z^{k}\right)=B_{k},

where
B_{k}
is the Bell number (which counts the total number of partitions of set of
k
objects) defined by the recursion formula

B_{k+1}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\binom{k}{\ell}B_{\ell},

and do satisfy the classical Dobinski formula:

B_{k}=e^{-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\ell^{k}}{\ell!},

which is just another way of writing

{\mathbb{E}}(Z^{k})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(Z=\ell)\ell^{k}.

It is also important to recall that the moment generating function is known

{\mathbb{E}}(e^{tZ})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{\ell}{\mathbb{E}}(Z^{\ell})}{\ell!}=e^{e^{t}-1},

and is in particular finite for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
In this section, we are interested in identifying the moments of a linear combination

Y=\sum_{p=1}^{m}\alpha_{p}Z_{p},

where
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}
are independent Poisson variables with parameter
\lambda=1
and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
.
By the multinomial formula and independence of
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{m}
, we clearly have

{\mathbb{E}}(Y^{k})=\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ldots+\ell_{m}=k}\frac{k!}{\ell_{1}!\ldots\ell_{m}!}\alpha_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\ldots\alpha_{m}^{\ell_{m}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(Z_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\ldots Z_{m}^{\ell_{m}}\right)

=k!\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ldots+\ell_{m}=k}\frac{\alpha_{1}^{\ell_{1}}}{\ell_{1}!}\ldots\frac{\alpha_{m}^{\ell_{m}}}{\ell_{m}!}B_{\ell_{1}}\ldots B_{\ell_{m}}.

(4)

Note that we have the bound for each
k\geq 0
and
t\geq 0
,

\frac{|{\mathbb{E}}(Y^{k})|t^{k}}{k!}\leq\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ldots+\ell_{m}=k}\frac{(|\alpha_{1}|t)^{\ell_{1}}}{\ell_{1}!}\ldots\frac{(|\alpha_{m}|t)^{\ell_{m}}}{\ell_{m}!}B_{\ell_{1}}\ldots B_{\ell_{m}},

and thus

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{|{\mathbb{E}}(Y^{k})|t^{k}}{k!}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{(|\alpha_{j}|t)^{\ell}}{\ell!}B_{\ell}\right)=e^{-m}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}e^{t|\alpha_{j}|}\right).

(5)

We therefore have convergence of

{\mathbb{E}}(e^{tY})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(Y^{k})t^{k}}{k!}

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

4.2.
The method of moments

We will apply below the following classical facts on moments of random variables. A basic reference is for example the book of Billingsley
[
9
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

•

A Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
is said to be determined by its moments if for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

a_{k}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{k}d\mu(x)

exists and
\mu
is the only probability measure with this sequence of moments
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.

•

A sufficient condition for a probability measure
\mu
to be determined by its moments is the absolute convergence of the series

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}

for some
t>0
.

•

Let
(W_{N})
be a sequence of real random variables, defined on probability spaces
(\Omega_{N},{\mathbb{P}}_{N})
and
W
a real random variable, defined on
(\Omega,{\mathbb{P}})
, whose distribution is determined by its moments.
If we have for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(W_{N}^{k})={\mathbb{E}}(W^{k}),

then
W_{N}
converges as
N\rightarrow\infty
to
W
in distribution.

Convergence in distribution means that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
x
is a point of continuity of the distribution function
x\mapsto{\mathbb{P}}(W\leq x)
, we have

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{N}(W_{N}\leq x)={\mathbb{P}}(Z\leq x).

It is also equivalent to say that for all
bounded continuous
function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we have

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(f(W_{N}))={\mathbb{E}}(f(W)).

Note that by (
5
), it is clear that any distribution of the type

Z=D_{0}+\sum_{p=1}^{m}\alpha_{p}Z_{p},

where
D_{0}
is a constant,
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p}
are real constant coefficients and
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}
are independent Poisson variables with parameter
1
, is uniquely determined by its moments.
Indeed we have for all
t>0
, and setting
Y=\sum_{p=1}^{m}\alpha_{p}Z_{p}
,

\sum_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}|Y+D_{0}|^{k}\leq\sum_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}(|Y|+|D_{0}|)^{k}=e^{t|Y|}e^{t|D_{0}|},

and we can use the fact that
D_{0}
is a constant and
|Y|\leq\sum_{p}|\alpha_{p}|Z_{p}
to justify, using (
5
), that for any
t>0
,

{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}|Y+D_{0}|^{k}\right)\leq{\mathbb{E}}(e^{t|Y|})e^{t|D_{0}|}<\infty.

4.3.
Convergence of moments and end of proof of Proposition
1.4

Our analysis will rely on the following facts, which are essentially due to Nica
[
25
]
(see also the related paper
[
16
]
) for free groups. We also point out the work of Y. Moaz
[
22
]
for the case of surface groups. A general statement
for more general groups is also given in
[
31
]
. We state below the bare minimum required for our needs.

Proposition 4.2
.

Using the preceding notations, the following holds.

(1)

Given a primitive word
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{0}^{d}
, we have for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}((F_{N}(\gamma))^{k})=B_{k},

where
B_{k}
is as above the
k
-th Bell number.

(2)

Consider a finite set of distincts primitive words
(\gamma_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{F}}\in\mathbb{F}_{0}^{d}
such that for all
i\neq j
we have
\gamma_{i}\neq\gamma_{j}^{-1}
.
Fix a set of non-negative integers
(k_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{F}}
. Then we have as
N
goes to infinity,

{\mathbb{E}}\left(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{F}}(F_{N}(\gamma_{j}))^{k_{j}}\right)=\prod_{j\in\mathcal{F}}{\mathbb{E}}\left((F_{N}(\gamma_{j}))^{k_{j}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

Clearly by the moment method,
(1)
implies convergence in distribution of
F_{N}(\gamma)
to a Poisson variable with parameter
1
, which is the main result in
[
25
]
. Statement

(2)
is an asymptotic multiple decorrelation result for distincts words in
\mathbb{F}_{0}^{d}
which extends Proposition
3.1

(2)
.

Let us recall that we have set

X_{N}=2Y_{N}+D_{N},

where

D_{N}=-2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}{\rm Re}(H_{i,j})+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)

and

Y_{N}:=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}F_{N}(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}){\rm Re}(H_{i,j}).

We first write for
k
fixed, by the multinomial formula

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(Y_{N}^{k}\right)={{{\sum}}}_{\sum_{i<j}\ell_{i,j}=k}\frac{k!}{\prod_{i<j}\ell_{i,j}!}\left(\prod_{i<j}({\rm Re}(H_{i,j}))^{\ell_{i,j}}\right){\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\prod_{i<j}(F_{N}(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}))^{\ell_{i,j}}\right).

Since the summation is over indices
(i,j)
such that
1\leq i<j\leq d
, whenever
(i_{1},j_{1})\neq(i_{2},j_{2})
, we can’t have
a_{i_{1}}^{-1}a_{i_{2}}=(a_{j_{1}}^{-1}a_{j_{2}})^{-1}
, hence we can actually apply Proposition
4.2

(2)
which yields

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(Y_{N}^{k}\right)=

{{{\sum}}}_{\sum_{i<j}\ell_{i,j}=k}\frac{k!}{\prod_{i<j}\ell_{i,j}!}\left(\prod_{i<j}({\rm Re}(H_{i,j}))^{\ell_{i,j}}\right)\left(\prod_{i<j}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left((F_{N}(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}))^{\ell_{i,j}}\right)\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

Applying Proposition
4.2

(1)
for each term
{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left((F_{N}(a_{i}^{-1}a_{j}))^{\ell_{i,j}}\right),
individually we get immediately (
k
being fixed),

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(Y_{N}^{k}\right)={{{\sum}}}_{\sum_{i<j}\ell_{i,j}=k}\frac{k!}{\prod_{i<j}\ell_{i,j}!}\left(\prod_{i<j}({\rm Re}(H_{i,j}))^{\ell_{i,j}}\right)\left(\prod_{i<j}B_{\ell_{i,j}}\right).

We recognize from (
4
) the formula for
{\mathbb{E}}(Z^{k})
where
Z
is the random variable

Z=\sum_{i<j}{\rm Re}(H_{i,j})Z_{i,j},

with
Z_{i,j}
being independent Poisson variables with parameter
1
. To prove part
(2)
and
(3)
of Proposition
1.4
, we just observe that for all
k
we have

{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(X_{N}^{k}\right)={\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left((2Y_{N}+D_{N})^{k}\right)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\binom{k}{\ell}2^{\ell}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(Y_{N}^{\ell}\right)(D_{N})^{k-\ell},

so that

L_{k}:=\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(X_{N}^{k}\right)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\binom{k}{\ell}2^{\ell}{\mathbb{E}}\left(Z^{\ell}\right)(D_{0})^{k-\ell}={\mathbb{E}}\left((2Z+D_{0})^{k}\right),

with

D_{0}=\lim_{N\rightarrow=\infty}D_{N}=-2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq d}{\rm Re}(H_{i,j}).

Since
2Z+D_{0}
is uniquely determined by its moments, the method of moments implies convergence in distribution of
X_{N}
to
2Z+D_{0}
, thus finishing the proof of Proposition
1.4
.

For the record, we have already computed

L_{2}=4\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}({\rm Re}(H_{i_{1},i_{2}}))^{2}.

Using the above formula (or alternative formulas for moments of centered Poisson distributions) we can also compute:

L_{3}=8\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}({\rm Re}(H_{i_{1},i_{2}}))^{3},\ L_{4}=16\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}({\rm Re}(H_{i_{1},i_{2}}))^{4}+48\left(\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}({\rm Re}(H_{i_{1},i_{2}}))^{2}\right)^{2}.

5.
The polynomial method and trace asymptotics

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.5
. We will rely on an adaptation (to the infinite dimensional case) of one the main inequalities that appear in the so-called ”polynomial method” as used in
[
13
]
for free groups. We start with some functional analytic preliminaries.

5.1.
Exponential classes

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex separable Hilbert space. We denote by
B(\mathcal{H})
the
C^{*}
-algebra of bounded linear operator on
\mathcal{H}
. We denote by
S_{1}(\mathcal{H})\subset B(\mathcal{H})
the
subspace of
trace class operators
. Let
a>0
. We define the exponential class
E(a,\mathcal{H})
by

E(a,\mathcal{H})=\left\{A\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})\ :\ \sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{j}(A)e^{aj}<\infty\right\}.

Note that
E(a,\mathcal{H})
is not a linear subspace of
S_{1}(\mathcal{H})
. Exponential classes have nevertheless some interesting stability properties that we summarize below.

Proposition 5.1
.

(1)

If
A\in E(a,\mathcal{H})
and
B\in B(\mathcal{H})
, then both
AB
and
BA
belong to
E(a,\mathcal{H})
.

(2)

If
A\in E(a,\mathcal{H})
, so does its adjoint
A^{*}
.

(3)

Assume that
A_{k}\in E(a_{k},\mathcal{H})
for
k=1,\ldots,r
, then we have

\sum_{k=1}^{r}A_{k}\in E(a^{\prime},\mathcal{H}),\ \mathrm{with}\ a^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{r}a_{k}^{-1}}.

Fact
(1)
follows from
\mu_{j}(AB)\leq\|B\|\mu_{j}(A)
, see Simon
[
33
]
, Theorem 1.5. Claim
(2)
is due to the fact that
A
and
A^{*}
have the same singular value sequence. Indeed,
for all
\lambda\neq 0
the map
A
is an isomorphism

A:\mathrm{Ker}(A^{*}A-\lambda)\rightarrow\mathrm{Ker}(AA^{*}-\lambda).

For a proof of
(3)
, based on rearrangement inequalities, see Bandtlow in
[
3
]
.

Since we have the well known fact that for compact operators
A
, singular values coincide with approximation numbers i.e.,

\mu_{j}(A)=\inf\{\|A-F\|\ :\ F\in B(\mathcal{H}),\ \mathrm{rank}(B)<j\},

it follows that if
A\in E(a,\mathcal{H})
, for all
\epsilon>0
, one can find a sequence
(F_{j})_{j}
of finite rank operators with
\mathrm{rank}(F_{j})\leq j
such that

\|A-F_{j}\|\leq Ce^{-(a-\epsilon)j},

for some constant
C>0
. We will need a slightly stronger approximation result, for the trace norm.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
A\in E(a,\mathcal{H})
, then there exist
C,\widetilde{a}>0
and a sequence of finite rank operators
(A_{k})_{k}
with
\mathrm{rank}(A_{k})\leq k
such that for all
k
we have

\|A-A_{k}\|_{1}\leq Ce^{-\widetilde{a}k}.

Note that since
\|A-A_{k}\|=\mu_{0}(A-A_{k})\leq\|A-A_{k}\|_{1}
, the same estimate is also implied for the operator norm.

Proof
. By writing

A=\frac{A+A^{*}}{2}+i\left(\frac{A-A^{*}}{2i}\right),

we have
U=\frac{A+A^{*}}{2}
and
S=\frac{A-A^{*}}{2i}
which are both self-adjoint and belong to
E(a/2,\mathcal{H})
by Proposition
5.1
. We are therefore left with the problem of approximating a compact
self-adjoint
operator, say
B
, belonging to some exponential class
E(a,\mathcal{H})
. By the usual spectral theorem, denoting by

(\lambda_{j})_{j}
the eigenvalue sequence of
B
, ordered so that
|\lambda_{1}|\geq|\lambda_{2}|\geq\ldots\geq|\lambda_{j}|\geq\ldots
, there exists a Hilbert basis
(e_{j})_{j}
of
\overline{\mathrm{Range}(B)}
so that for all
j\geq 1
, we have
B(e_{j})=\lambda_{j}e_{j}
. Define then
B_{k}
to be given for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
by

B_{k}(f):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\langle f,e_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}e_{j}.

Clearly
\mathrm{rank}(B_{k})\leq k
and since
B-B_{k}
is self-adjoint we have

\|B-B_{k}\|_{1}=\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{j}|,

with
|\lambda_{j}|=\mu_{j}(B)
. Since we have the upper bound
\mu_{j}(B)\leq Ce^{-aj}
, we deduce that

\|B-B_{k}\|_{1}\leq C\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}e^{-aj}=C\frac{e^{-a(k+1)}}{1-e^{-a}},

and the proof is done.
\square

A fundamental fact for us, which we have already proved in disguise, is the following.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\gamma:\Omega_{0}\rightarrow\Omega_{0}
be a holomorphic contraction such that

\overline{\gamma(\Omega_{0})}\subset\Omega_{0},

then the composition operator
T_{\gamma}
acting
on
H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
is in an exponential class.

Outline of proof
. By the min-max theorem applied to
\sqrt{T_{\gamma}^{*}T_{\gamma}}
, we have

\mu_{j}(T_{\gamma})\leq\sum_{k=j}^{\infty}\|T_{\gamma}(e_{k})\|_{H^{2}(\Omega_{0})},

for all Hilbert basis
(e_{k})
of
H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
. By using an ”explicit” basis, exactly as in the proof of Proposition
2.3
, we obtain existence of
C>0
and
0<\rho<1
such that
for all
k
,

\|T_{\gamma}(e_{k})\|_{H^{2}(\Omega_{0})}\leq C\rho^{k},

and the proof is complete since we get immediately

\mu_{j}(T_{\gamma})\leq C\frac{\rho^{j}}{1-\rho}.

\square

5.2.
Tracial states and group algebras

Let
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
be the group algebra of the free group
\mathbb{F}^{d}
, that is the set of formal sums

x=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{F}^{d}}a_{\gamma}.\gamma

where
a_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\gamma\mapsto a_{\gamma}
has finite support in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. We recall that
\lambda:\mathbb{F}^{d}\rightarrow U(\mathbb{F}^{d})
is the left regular representation. We will denote by

\lambda(x):=\sum_{\gamma}a_{\gamma}\lambda(\gamma)
which is a bounded operator acting on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})
.

Recall also that
\phi_{N}:\mathbb{F}^{d}\rightarrow S_{N}
is a random permutation representation of the free group
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Each permutation
\phi_{N}(\gamma)
acts on
\ell^{2}(\{1,\ldots,N\})
and we denote

\mathcal{V}_{N}
the orthogonal subspace to constant functions in
\ell^{2}(\{1,\ldots,N\})
. The representation
\phi_{N}:\mathbb{F}^{d}\rightarrow U(\mathcal{V}_{N})
is then denoted by
\pi_{N}
. For all

x=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{F}^{d}}a_{\gamma}.\gamma\in\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
we will use the notation
\pi_{N}(x)=\sum_{\gamma}a_{\gamma}\pi_{N}(\gamma)
.

Of primary interest for us will be the group algebras
\mathcal{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
and
S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
which consists of formal sums

\mathscr{X}=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}.\gamma,

where
A_{\gamma}
are respectively
D\times D
complex matrices and trace class operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. We say that such an element
\mathscr{X}

is
self-adjoint
iff

A_{\gamma^{-1}}=A_{\gamma}^{*}
for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
.

In Both cases we define
\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})
and

\lambda(\mathscr{X})
by

\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}\otimes\pi_{N}(\gamma),\ \lambda(\mathscr{X})=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}\otimes\lambda(\gamma),

acting respectively on the tensor products
\mathbb{C}^{D}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{N}
and
\mathbb{C}^{D}\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})
or
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{N}
and
\mathcal{H}\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})
.
We then define the ”tracial state”
\tau
to be

\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})):=\sum_{\gamma}\mathrm{Tr}(A_{\gamma})\langle\lambda(\gamma)\chi_{e},\chi_{e}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})}=\mathrm{Tr}(A_{Id}),

where
\chi_{e}(\gamma)=1
if
\gamma=Id
, and
0
elsewhere.

Classical results in free probability (namely asymptotic freeness of independent random permutation matrices) imply that for all
\mathscr{X}\in\mathcal{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
, with
D
fixed, we have

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})))}{N}=\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})).

This result was first shown by Nica in
[
24
]
. The main idea of the polynomial method as explained in
[
13
]
is to obtain similar asymptotics for self-adjoint elements
\mathscr{X}
, when
\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})
is replaced by
\varphi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}))
where
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is an arbitrary smooth, real valued, function. Achieving this task implies a soft proof of ”strong asymptotic freeness”, also called strong convergence in a wider context, which was first proved for random permutation matrices by Bordenave and Collins in
[
10
]
.

The first basic question is to understand what
\tau(\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))
means when
\varphi
is a smooth function. Assume first that

\varphi
is a real polynomial and write

\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))=\sum_{\gamma}B_{\gamma}\otimes\lambda(\gamma),

with
B_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})
. We have by definition

\tau(\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\mathrm{Tr}(B_{\gamma})\langle\lambda(\gamma)\chi_{e},\chi_{e}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})},

which by picking an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{D}
can be written as

\tau(\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\langle B_{\gamma}e_{i},e_{i}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{D}}\langle\lambda(\gamma)\chi_{e},\chi_{e}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})}

=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\langle\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e},e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{D}\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})}.

By Cauchy-Schwarz we simply get

|\tau(\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))|\leq D\|\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))\|.

Since
\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
is a bounded self-adjoint operator, its norms equals its spectral radius and applying the spectral mapping theorem gives

|\tau(\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))|\leq D\sup_{z\in\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))}|\varphi(z)|.

The linear functional
\varphi\mapsto\tau(\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))
is therefore continuous on the Banach space
C^{0}(\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))
and therefore extends by density from polynomials to any continuous function. Since it is also positive, Riesz theorem says that there exists a finite Radon measure
\mu
, supported in
\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
such that

\tau(\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))=\int\varphi d\mu.

We need an infinite-dimensional extension of this line of ideas.
We first make the following basic remark.

Proposition 5.4
.

The tracial state
\tau
is ”faithfull” on
S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
i.e. for all

\mathscr{Y}\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
with

\mathscr{Y}=\sum_{\gamma}C_{\gamma}.\gamma,

we have for any Hilbert basis
(e_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{H}
,

\tau((\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))^{*}\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))=\sum_{\gamma}\mathrm{Tr}(C_{\gamma}^{*}C_{\gamma})=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\|\lambda(\mathscr{Y})e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e}\|^{2}.

Proof
. Write

\mathscr{Y}=\sum_{\gamma}C_{\gamma}.\gamma,

so that

(\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))^{*}\lambda(\mathscr{Y})=\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}C_{\gamma}^{*}C_{\gamma^{\prime}}\otimes\lambda(\gamma^{-1}\gamma^{\prime}),

and therefore

\tau((\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))^{*}\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))=\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}\mathrm{Tr}(C_{\gamma}^{*}C_{\gamma^{\prime}})\langle\lambda(\gamma^{-1}\gamma^{\prime})\chi_{e},\chi_{e}\rangle=\sum_{\gamma}\mathrm{Tr}(C_{\gamma}^{*}C_{\gamma}).

On the other hand we have by definition of the trace,

\mathrm{Tr}(C_{\gamma}^{*}C_{\gamma^{\prime}})=\sum_{i}\langle C_{\gamma}^{*}C_{\gamma^{\prime}}e_{i},e_{i}\rangle,

which also gives

\tau((\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))^{*}\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))=\sum_{i}\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}}\langle[C_{\gamma}^{*}\otimes\lambda(\gamma^{-1})][C_{\gamma^{\prime}}\otimes\lambda(\gamma^{\prime})]e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e},e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e}\rangle

=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\|\lambda(\mathscr{Y})e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e}\|^{2},

and the result is proved.
\square

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
P
be a real polynomial, and assume that

\mathscr{X}=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}.\gamma\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]

is self-adjoint and positive. Then we have

|\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})P(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))|\leq\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sqrt{B}\right)\sup_{z\in\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))}|P(z)|,

where
B=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}^{*}A_{\gamma}
. Moreover, the functional

P\mapsto\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})P(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))

extends to a finite positive Radon measure
\mu_{\mathscr{X}}
whose support is included in
\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
and contains

\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof
. We first need to justify that
\sqrt{B}
is indeed trace class. Let
r
denote the cardinality of the support of
\gamma\mapsto A_{\gamma}
.
By Fan inequality, see Simon
[
33
]
, Theorem 1.7, we have

\mu_{rj+1}(B)\leq\sum_{\gamma}\mu_{j}(A_{\gamma}^{*}A_{\gamma})=\sum_{\gamma}\mu_{j}^{2}(A_{\gamma}).

Since
B
is self-adjoint positive we have actually

\lambda_{rj+1}(B)=\mu_{rj+1}(B)\leq\sum_{\gamma}\mu_{j}^{2}(A_{\gamma})\leq\left(\sum_{\gamma}\mu_{j}(A_{\gamma})\right)^{2}.

By spectral mapping and Lidskii’s theorem, we have therefore

\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{B})=\sum_{\ell}\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}(B)},

which implies (recall that the sequence of eigenvalues
(\lambda_{\ell})_{\ell}
is decreasing)

\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{r[\ell/r]+1}}\leq\ \sum_{\gamma}\mu_{[\ell/r]}(A_{\gamma})

and therefore
\sum_{\ell}\sqrt{\lambda_{\ell}(B)}<\infty
since each series
\sum_{j}\mu_{j}(A_{\gamma})
is finite.
Note that we have actually obtained the estimate

\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{B})\leq\sum_{\gamma}\|A_{\gamma}\|_{1}

(6)

which will be helpful further on.
We now refocus on the calculation of
\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})P(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))
, trying to mimic the finite-dimensional case.
Let
P\in\mathbb{R}[X]
, we have

\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})P(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\langle\lambda(\mathscr{X})P(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e},e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})},

(7)

for any Hilbert basis
(e_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{H}
. Therefore by Cauchy-Schwarz we get

|\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})P(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))|\leq\|P(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))\|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\|\lambda(\mathscr{X}))e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e}\|

Since
\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
is a bounded self-adjoint operator, we have actually by the spectral mapping theorem

\|P(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))\|=\sup_{z\in\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))}|P(z)|.

We also observe that

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\|\lambda(\mathscr{X}))e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e}\|=\sum_{i}\left(\langle\lambda^{*}(\mathscr{X}))\lambda(\mathscr{X}))e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e},e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e}\rangle\right)^{1/2}

=\sum_{i}\left(\sum_{\gamma}\langle A_{\gamma}^{*}A_{\gamma}e_{i},e_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)^{1/2}=\sum_{i}\left(\langle Be_{i},e_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)^{1/2}

But
B
is self-adjoint positive, trace class, and thus by the above discussion we have exactly (by taking
(e_{i})
to be an orthonormal basis diagonalizing
B
)

\sum_{i}\left(\langle Be_{i},e_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)^{1/2}=\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{B}).

We have obtained the inequality

|\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})P(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))|\leq\mathrm{Tr}\left(\sqrt{B}\right)\sup_{z\in\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))}|P(z)|,

The linear functional

P\mapsto\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})P(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))

is therefore continous on
\mathbb{R}[X]\cap C^{0}(\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))
, and extends by Stone-Weierstrass to a continous linear functional on
C^{0}(\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))
.
By Riesz theorem, there exists a finite (a priori signed) Radon measure
\mu_{\mathscr{X}}
,
supported inside
\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
, such that for all continuous function
\varphi

\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))=\int\varphi d\mu_{\mathscr{X}}.

We also remark that if
P\in\mathbb{R}[X]
is such that
XP(X)
is positive on
\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
, then the operator

\lambda(\mathscr{X})P(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
is also positive and thus by (
7
)

\int Pd\mu_{\mathscr{X}}\geq 0.

Since we have assumed that
\lambda(\mathscr{X})
is a positive operator, by a easy density argument,
\mu_{\mathscr{X}}
is therefore a positive measure.
It remains to prove that the support of
\mu_{\mathscr{X}}
contains
\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))\setminus\{0\}
. We assume
\lambda(\mathscr{X})\geq 0
. Let
x_{0}\in\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
with
x_{0}>0
.
Assume that there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
0<x_{0}-\epsilon
and

\mu_{\mathscr{X}}((x_{0}-\epsilon,x_{0}+\epsilon))=0.

We pick
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
to be a non-negative function such that

\varphi(x_{0})=1
, and
\varphi\leq 1
with
\mathrm{Supp}(\varphi)\subset(x_{0}-\epsilon,x_{0}+\epsilon)
.
Write
\varphi(x)=x\psi(x)
, where
\psi
is also obviously smooth. Note that
\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))
, defined by functional calculus, is a non zero operator since by spectral mapping and self-adjointness

\|\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))\|\geq 1
.
On the other hand we have

\tau(\varphi^{2}(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))=\tau(\lambda^{2}(\mathscr{X})\psi^{2}(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))=\int u\psi^{2}(u)d\mu_{\mathscr{X}}(u)

=\int\varphi^{2}(u)\frac{d\mu_{\mathscr{X}}(u)}{u}\leq\int_{(x_{0}-\epsilon,x_{0}+\epsilon)}\frac{d\mu_{\mathscr{X}}(u)}{u}=0.

To get a contradiction, we need to show that
\tau(\varphi^{2}(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))
is indeed positive. Note that
\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
is a priori no longer in

S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
but rather is a limit in operator norm of a sequence of self-adjoint operators

C_{n}=\lambda(\mathscr{X})P_{n}(\lambda(\mathscr{X})),

where
P_{n}
are real polynomials. We have therefore

0=\tau(\varphi^{2}(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i}\|\lambda(\mathscr{X})P_{n}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e}\|^{2},

in particular we get for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))(e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e})=0.

The end of the proof is then a standard trick, see for example in
[
26
]
, Chapter 3.
Let
\delta_{h}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})
, with
h\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
be defined by

\delta_{h}(f)(g):=f(gh^{-1}),

then for any
\gamma,h\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
,
\lambda(\gamma)
and
\delta_{h}
commute. As a consequence
Id\otimes\delta_{h}
commutes with any element of the type
\lambda(\mathscr{Y})
with
\mathscr{Y}=\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
. As a limit in operator norm of such elements,
\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
must commute with
Id\otimes\delta_{h}
also.
Therefore we have for any
h
,

\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))(e_{i}\otimes\chi_{h})=\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))(Id\otimes\delta_{h})(e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e})=(Id\otimes\delta_{h})\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))(e_{i}\otimes\chi_{e})=0,

and hence
\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
must be
0
since it is vanishing on a Hilbert basis of
\mathcal{H}\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})
.
A contradiction. We have therefore shown that

\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))\setminus\{0\}\subset\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\mathscr{X}})\subset\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X})).

The proof is done.
\square

Note that if we know that
0
is not isolated in the spectrum of
\lambda(\mathscr{X})
, then we have actually
\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{\mathscr{X}})=\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
.
Under the above hypotheses, we always have
0=\inf\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))
. Indeed, we have by direct computation

\langle\lambda(\mathscr{X})f\otimes\chi_{g},f\otimes\chi_{g}\rangle=\langle A_{e}f,f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

and since
A_{e}
is assumed to be a self-adjoint positive compact operator,
0
is in its spectrum which implies

\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H},\atop\|f\|=1}\langle A_{e}f,f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0.

However at this level of generality, we cannot rule out the fact that
0
could be an isolated point of the spectrum of
\lambda(\mathscr{X})
: for example if
\lambda(\mathscr{X})=A_{e}\otimes Id
,
with
A
a finite rank operator, then this is the case.

We also point out to the reader that the ”tracial state”
\tau
, unlike in the finite dimensional case of
\mathcal{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
, does not make sense in the operator norm closure of

S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
, simply because operator norm limits of trace class operators are in general not trace class.

We end this subsection by a key inequality.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\mathscr{X},\mathscr{Y}\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
, with

\mathscr{X}=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}.\gamma,\ \mathscr{Y}=\sum_{\gamma}C_{\gamma}.\gamma.

Let
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, then there exists
C_{\psi}>0
such that

|\tau\left\{\lambda(\mathscr{X})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))\right\}-\tau\left\{\lambda(\mathscr{Y})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))\right\}|\leq

\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{B})C_{\psi}\|\lambda(\mathscr{X})-\lambda(\mathscr{Y})\|+\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\psi(x)|\right)\sum_{\gamma}\|A_{\gamma}-C_{\gamma}\|_{1},

where as before,
B=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}^{*}A_{\gamma}
, and
C_{\psi}
is a constant involving only
\psi
, see below.

Proof
. Assume first that
\psi
is a real polynomial. We write

\tau\left\{\lambda(\mathscr{X})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))\right\}-\tau\left\{\lambda(\mathscr{Y})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))\right\}=

\tau\left\{(\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))-\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{Y})))\lambda(\mathscr{X})\right\}+\tau\left\{(\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))-\lambda(\mathscr{Y})))\right\}.

Proceeding as in the preceding proof, we get

|\tau\left\{(\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))-\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{Y})))\lambda(\mathscr{X})\right\}|\leq\|\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))-\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))\|\mathrm{Tr}(\sqrt{B}),

while by (
6
) we obtain

|\tau\left\{(\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))-\lambda(\mathscr{Y})))\right\}|\leq\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\psi(x)|\right)\sum_{\gamma}\|A_{\gamma}-C_{\gamma}\|_{1}.

The inequality extends by density of polynomials in
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
(endowed with the Frechet topology) to any test function
\psi
. What remains to be controled is the term

\|\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))-\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{Y}))\|.

There are several possible ways to bound it via the operator norm
\|\lambda(\mathscr{X})-\lambda(\mathscr{Y})\|
, but none are straightforward. We give an outline below based on the
Helffer-Sjöstrand formula from
[
18
,
15
]
. In what follows we denote for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\langle x\rangle:=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}
. Let
g_{0}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

be such that
1\geq g_{0}\geq 0
,
g(x)\equiv 1
if
|x|\leq 1
and
\mathrm{supp}(g_{0})\subset[-2,+2]
, and set

\sigma(x,y):=g_{0}\left(\frac{y}{\langle x\rangle}\right).

We assume that
\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset[-K,+K]
, with
K\geq 1
. We define an almost-analytic extension
\widetilde{\psi}
of
\psi
to
\mathbb{C}
as follows (here
n
is arbitrary). Set for all
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}
,

\widetilde{\psi}(z):=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\psi^{(k)}(x)(iy)^{k}}{k!}\right)\sigma(x,y).

Recall that we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+i\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right).

A direct calculation gives

\frac{\partial\widetilde{\psi}}{\partial\overline{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\psi^{(n+1)}(x)(iy)^{n}}{n!}\sigma(x,y)+\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\psi^{(k)}(x)(iy)^{k}}{k!}\right)\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial\overline{z}}(x,y).

Set

U_{K}:=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\ :\ |x|\leq K\ \mathrm{and}\ |y|\leq 2\langle x\rangle\}

and

W_{K}:=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\ :\ |x|\leq K\ \mathrm{and}\ \langle x\rangle\leq|y|\leq 2\langle x\rangle\}.

Then we have

\mathrm{supp}\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{\psi}}{\partial\overline{z}}\right)\subset U_{K},\ \mathrm{supp}\left(\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial\overline{z}}\right)\subset W_{K},

and for all
z\in U_{K}
we have the estimate

\left|\frac{\partial\widetilde{\psi}}{\partial\overline{z}}(z)\right|\leq|y|^{n}\sup_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi^{(n+1)}|+C_{\sigma}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sup_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi^{(k)}|\right)(2\langle K\rangle)^{n}\chi_{W_{K}}(z),

where we have set
C_{\sigma}:=\sup_{U_{K}}\left|\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial\overline{z}}\right|
. Setting
n=2
and onserving that whenever
z\in W_{K}
we have
|{\rm Im}(z)|\geq 1
, we get

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left|\frac{\partial\widetilde{\psi}}{\partial\overline{z}}\right||{\rm Im}(z)|^{-2}dm(z)\leq\sup_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi^{(3)}|\mathrm{Vol}(U_{K})+C_{\sigma}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{2}\sup_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi^{(k)}|\right)(2\langle K\rangle)^{2}\mathrm{Vol}(W_{K}).

(8)

\leq M(\sigma,K)\|\psi\|_{C^{3}([-K,+K])},

where
M(\sigma,K)>0
depends only on
K
and the choice of
\sigma
i.e.
g_{0}
and

\|\psi\|_{C^{3}([-K,+K])}=\max_{0\leq j\leq 3}\sup_{[-K,+K]}|\psi^{(j)}|.

Let
T:\mathscr{H}\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
be a bounded self-adjoint operator acting on a complex separable Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
. Helffer-Sjöstrand formula, see
[
15
]
chapter 2, says that we have for all
n\geq 1
,

\psi(T)=\frac{-1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{\psi}}{\partial\overline{z}}R_{T}(z)dm(z),

where
R_{T}(z):=(zId-T)^{-1}
is the resolvent, which is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathrm{Sp}(T)
.
As a consequence, if
T_{1},T_{2}
are bounded self-adjont operators we have by the resolvent formula

\psi(T_{1})-\psi(T_{2})=\frac{-1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{\psi}}{\partial\overline{z}}R_{T_{1}}(z)(T_{1}-T_{2})R_{T_{2}}(z)dm(z)

and thus using the fact that
\|R_{T}(z)\|\leq|{\rm Im}(z)|^{-1}
for all
z\not\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\|\psi(T_{1})-\psi(T_{2})\|\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\left|\frac{\partial\widetilde{\psi}}{\partial\overline{z}}\right||{\rm Im}(z)|^{-2}\|T_{1}-T_{2}\|dm(z).

Inequality (
8
) gives

\|\psi(T_{1})-\psi(T_{2})\|\leq C_{\psi}\|T_{1}-T_{2}\|,

with

C_{\psi}:=\frac{1}{\pi}M(\sigma,K)\|\psi\|_{C^{3}([-K,+K])}.

We record the above discussion in the following Lemma, to be used again in the next subsection.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}([-K,+K])
,
then there exists
M_{0}(K)>0
, such that for all
T_{1},T_{2}:\mathscr{H}\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
bounded self-adjoints operators, we have in operator norm,

\|\psi(T_{1})-\psi(T_{2})\|\leq M_{0}(K)\|\psi\|_{C^{3}([-K,+K])}\|T_{1}-T_{2}\|.

For a more sophisticated proof based on the notion of ”operator differentiable functions”, we refer the reader to
[
28
]
. Unlike what a naive approach would suggest,
C^{1}
-regularity of
\psi
is not enough to guarantee that such an estimate holds, see in
[
28
]
for counter examples, while
C^{2}
is enough. In our soft proof above, we essentially need
\psi
to be
C^{3}
which is non-optimal but enough for our purpose. The end of the proof of Proposition
5.6
follows by applying directly the above bound to
T_{1}=\lambda(\mathscr{X})
and
T_{2}=\lambda(\mathscr{Y}).

5.3.
Extending the polynomial method to
S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]

We start by another useful Lemma.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\mathscr{X}\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
, say

\mathscr{X}=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}.\gamma,

then we have for all
N
,

\|\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\|_{1}\leq N\sum_{\gamma}\|A_{\gamma}\|_{1}.

Proof.
Since we have

\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}\otimes\pi_{N}(\gamma),

it is enough to estimate
\|B\otimes\pi_{N}(\gamma)\|_{1}
for any
B\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})
.
Picking an orthonormal Hilbert basis
(e_{i}\otimes f_{j})_{i\in\mathbb{N}\atop 1\leq j\leq N-1}
of
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{N}
,
we have

(B\otimes\pi_{N}(\gamma))^{*}(B\otimes\pi_{N}(\gamma))=B^{*}B\otimes I_{N-1},

and therefore

\|B\otimes\pi_{N}(\gamma)\|_{1}=\sum_{i,j}\langle(\sqrt{B^{*}B}\otimes I_{N-1})e_{i}\otimes f_{j},e_{i}\otimes f_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{N}}

=\sum_{i,j}\langle\sqrt{B^{*}B}e_{i},e_{i}\rangle\langle f_{j},f_{j}\rangle=(N-1)\|B\|_{1},

and the proof is done.
\square

We will also use the following easy fact.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
A
be in
B(\mathcal{H})
, then for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
, we have

\|A\otimes\pi_{N}(\gamma)\|\leq\|A\|.

Similarly we have also for all
\gamma
,

\|A\otimes\lambda(\gamma)\|\leq\|A\|.

Proof
. Write for any
F\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{N}
,

F=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}f_{j}\otimes e_{j}

where
(e_{j})
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{V}_{N}
and
\|F\|^{2}=\sum_{j}\|f_{j}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}
.
By unitarity of
\pi_{N}(\gamma)
we have immediately

\|A\otimes\pi_{N}(\gamma)F\|^{2}=\left\|\sum_{j}A(f_{j})\otimes\pi_{N}(\gamma)(e_{j})\right\|^{2}=\sum_{j}\|Af_{j}\|^{2}\leq\|A\|^{2}\|F\|^{2}.

When
\mathcal{V}_{N}
is replaced by
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})
, we can proceed in the same way, replacing the finite basis of
\mathcal{V}_{N}
by a Hilbert basis of
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{d})
and using unitarity of
\lambda(\gamma)
.
The proof is done.
\square

Our aim is to prove the following fact, from which Theorem
1.5
can be deduced readily by taking
\mathcal{H}=H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
and observing that
V_{N}\simeq\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{N}
.
The properties of exponential classes ensure that
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}^{*}{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
can indeed be written as
\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})
for some self-adjoint positive element
\mathscr{X}\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[{\mathbb{F}}^{d}]
, whose ”coefficients” are all in the same exponential class.
Applying the next result below combined with Proposition
5.5
ends the proof.

Theorem 5.10
.

Assume that
\mathscr{X}=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}.\gamma\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
is a self-adjoint positive element. Assume in addition that all coefficients
A_{\gamma}
belong to some exponential class
E(a_{0},\mathcal{H})
, for some
a_{0}>0
. Then there exists
C_{\mathscr{X}},K_{\mathscr{X}}>0
independent of
\psi
such that for all
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we have for all
N
large

\left|\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})))\right)}{N}-\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))\right|\leq C\frac{\log(N)}{N}\|\psi\|_{C^{5}([-K,+K])}.

Proof
. In this proof,
C>0
will denote a constant that does not depend on
m,N,\psi
but may depend on
\mathscr{X}
. This generic constant
C
will change from line to line. We start by using Proposition
5.2
to approximate each operator
A_{\gamma}
by a finite rank operator
A_{\gamma}^{(m)}
with
\mathrm{rank}(A_{\gamma}^{(m)})\leq m
in such a way that

\max_{\gamma}\|A_{\gamma}-A^{(m)}_{\gamma}\|_{1}\leq Ce^{-am},

for some
0<a\leq a_{0}
which depends obviously on
\mathscr{X}
. We then set

\mathscr{X}^{(m)}:=\sum_{\gamma}A^{(m)}_{\gamma}.\gamma.

Note that since for any trace class operator
T
, we have
\|T^{*}\|_{1}=\|T\|_{1}
,
and using the fact that the adjoint of a finite rank operator is also finite rank with the same rank, we can also assume that each
\mathscr{X}^{(m)}
is also self-adjoint.

In practice, we will set

m=[\alpha\log(N)]

for a suitably chosen
\alpha>0
, see at the end of the proof. Let
K>0
be large enough so that for all
N
and
m
we have

\|\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})|\leq K,\ \|\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})|\leq K,\ \mathrm{and}\ \|\lambda(\mathscr{X})\|\leq K,\ \|\lambda(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})\|\leq K.

Such a
K
does exist obviously by Lemma
5.9
.
In the following we will also set

r:=\#\{\gamma\ :\ A_{\gamma}\neq 0\}.

Let us write

\left|\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})))\right)}{N}-\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))\right|

\leq\mathcal{E}_{1}(m,N)+\mathcal{E}_{2}(m,N)+\mathcal{E}_{3}(m,N),

where

\mathcal{E}_{1}(m,N)=\frac{1}{N}{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\left|\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}))))-\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})))\right|\right),

\mathcal{E}_{2}(m,N)=\left|\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)}))))}{N}-\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})))\right|,

\mathcal{E}_{3}(m,N)=\left|\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}^{(m)}))-\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))\right|.

We will now bound each error term
\mathcal{E}_{j}(m,N)
for
j=1,2,3
separately.

Bounding
\mathcal{E}_{1}(m,N)
.
Writing

S_{m,N}:=\frac{1}{N}\left|\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}))))-\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})))\right|

\leq\frac{1}{N}\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)}-\mathscr{X})\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)}))\right)\right|

+\frac{1}{N}\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})(\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)}))-\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})))\right)\right|,

which by applying the standard inequality
|\mathrm{Tr}(AB)|\leq\|A\|\|B\|_{1}
for any operators
A,B
with
A
bounded and
B
trace class gives

S_{m,N}\leq\frac{\|\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})-\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\|_{1}}{N}\|\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)}))\|

+\frac{\|\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\|_{1}}{N}\|\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)}))-\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}))\|.

Applying Lemma
5.8
with Lemma
5.7
we end up with

S_{m,N}\leq\|\psi\|_{C^{0}([-K,+K])}Cre^{-am}

+M_{0}(K)\|\psi\|_{C^{3}([-K,+K])}\left(\sum_{\gamma}\|A_{\gamma}\|_{1}\right)\|\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})-\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\|.

Note that to bound
\|\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})-\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\|
, we write (using Lemma
5.9
)

\|\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})-\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\|\leq\sum_{\gamma}\|(A_{\gamma}^{(m)}-A_{\gamma})\otimes\pi_{N}(\gamma)\|\leq\sum_{\gamma}\|(A_{\gamma}^{(m)}-A_{\gamma})\|\leq rCe^{-am}.

We have obtained

\mathcal{E}_{1}(m,N)\leq C\|\psi\|_{C^{3}([-K,+K])}e^{-am}.

Bounding
\mathcal{E}_{3}(m,N)
.
Applying Proposition
5.6
combined with Lemma
5.7
, we have

\mathcal{E}_{3}(m,N)=\left|\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}^{(m)}))-\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X})))\right|

\leq C\|\psi\|_{C^{3}([-K,+K])}\|\lambda(\mathscr{X})-\lambda(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})\|+C\|\psi\|_{C^{0}([-K,+K])}\sum_{\gamma}\|A_{\gamma}-A_{\gamma}^{(m)}\|_{1}.

We then apply Lemma
5.9
as above to bound
\|\lambda(\mathscr{X})-\lambda(\mathscr{X}^{(m)})\|
and we finally get

\mathcal{E}_{3}(m,N)\leq C\|\psi\|_{C^{3}([-K,+K])}e^{-am}.

Bounding
\mathcal{E}_{2}(m,N)
.
This is where we will use the main input from the polynomial method as detailed in
[
13
]
, which we rephrase here according to our notations.

Theorem 5.11
.

[
13
]
, Corollary 7.2.
Assume that
\mathscr{Y}=\sum_{\gamma}C_{\gamma}.\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
is self-adjoint. Let
q_{0}=\max\{|\gamma|\ :\ C_{\gamma}\neq 0\}
. Then there exists

\left|\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\mathrm{Tr}(h(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{Y}))))}{N}-\tau(h(\lambda(\mathscr{Y})))\right|\leq\widetilde{C}\frac{D(q_{0}(1+\log(d)))^{4}}{N}\|f^{(5)}\|_{L^{2}([0,2\pi])},

\theta\in\mathbb{R}

,
K
and

\mathscr{Y}_{m}

\mathrm{supp}(\mathscr{X})=\{\gamma\ :\ A_{\gamma}\neq 0\}

\mathcal{F}_{m}:=\left(\bigcap_{\gamma\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathscr{X})}\mathrm{Ker}(A_{\gamma}^{(m)})\right)^{\perp}\subset\mathcal{H}.

\mathscr{X}^{(m)}

\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{F}_{m})\leq\sum_{\gamma}\mathrm{rank}(A_{\gamma}^{(m)})\leq rm,

which is obviously invariant by all operators
A_{\gamma}^{(m)}
. We now choose a basis
\mathscr{B}_{m}
of
\mathcal{F}_{m}

\mathscr{Y}_{m}:=\sum_{\gamma}M_{\gamma}^{(m)}.\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{D}(\mathbb{C})[\mathbb{F}^{d}],

with

A:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}

•

A(\mathcal{F}_{M})\subset\mathcal{F}_{m}

•

\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\perp}\subset\mathrm{Ker}(A)
,

then we have
\mathrm{Tr}(A)=\mathrm{Tr}(A|_{\mathcal{F}_{m}})
.
Since for each
\gamma
,
A_{\gamma}^{(m)}(\mathcal{F}_{m})\subset\mathcal{F}_{m}
and
A_{\gamma}^{(m)}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{\perp})=\{0\}
,
we have therefore

\mathrm{Tr}(A_{\gamma}^{(m)})=\mathrm{Tr}(A_{\gamma}^{(m)}|_{\mathcal{F}_{m}})=\mathrm{Tr}(M_{\gamma}^{(m)}).

This properties are preserved by composition and consequently we get for all
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p}
,

\mathrm{Tr}(A_{\gamma_{1}}^{(m)}\ldots A_{\gamma_{p}}^{(m)})=\mathrm{Tr}(A_{\gamma_{1}}^{(m)}\ldots A_{\gamma_{p}}^{(m)}|_{\mathcal{F}_{m}})=\mathrm{Tr}(M_{\gamma_{1}}^{(m)}\ldots M_{\gamma_{p}}^{(m)}).

It is now not difficult to see that for any polynomial which vanishes at
0
, say
P(X)=XQ(X)
we have

\mathrm{Tr}(P(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{Y}_{m})))=\mathrm{Tr}(P(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}^{(m)}))).

By density of polynomials and continuity of the functionals

Q\mapsto\tau(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{Y}_{m})Q(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{Y}_{m}))),\ Q\mapsto\tau(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}_{m})Q(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X}_{m}))),

we can extend this property to any
h\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
which vanishes at
0
.
Recalling that if
\mathscr{Z}=\sum_{\gamma}D_{\gamma}.\gamma\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
,
we have

\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{Z}))=\mathrm{Tr}(D_{Id}),

we can prove similarly that for all polynomial
P
which vanishes at
0
we have

\tau(P(\lambda(\mathscr{Y}_{m})))=\tau(P(\lambda(\mathscr{X}^{(m)}))),

and extend similarly the result to any
h\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
h(0)=0
.

We can now apply the above theorem to get (with
h=x\psi(x)
,
\mathscr{Y}=\mathscr{Y}_{m}
,
D=\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{F}_{m})
)

\mathcal{E}_{2}(m,N)\leq C\frac{m}{N}\|f^{(5)}\|_{L^{2}([0,2\pi])}\leq C\alpha\frac{\log(N)}{N}\|f^{(5)}\|_{L^{2}([0,2\pi])}.

It is elementary to check that in our situation, by Faà di Bruno’s formula,

\|f^{(5)}\|_{L^{2}([0,2\pi])}\leq C_{K}\|\psi\|_{C^{5}([-K,+K])},

where
C_{K}
depends only on
K
. We now choose
\alpha
large enough such that

e^{-am}=e^{-a[\alpha\log(N)]}\leq\frac{C}{N},

and the proof is done.
\square

We conclude this section by some remarks.

•

If
\mathscr{X}=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}.\gamma\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
but the
A_{\gamma}
’s are not in an exponential class but enjoy polynomial decay say

\mu_{j}(A_{\gamma})\leq C(1+j)^{2+2\beta},

for some
\beta>0
, then we can still get an effective error term in Theorem
5.10
of size
O\left(N^{\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}}\right)
, by using similar ideas.

•

If
\mathscr{X}=\sum_{\gamma}A_{\gamma}.\gamma\in S_{1}(\mathcal{H})[\mathbb{F}^{d}]
but no effective estimate on the singular value sequences of
A_{\gamma}
’s is available,
we can still obtain the non effective convergence

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})\psi(\pi_{N}(\mathscr{X})))\right)}{N}=\tau(\lambda(\mathscr{X})\psi(\lambda(\mathscr{X}))).

6.
An effective example

As in the introduction, let
\Omega_{0}
be the open disc given by

\Omega_{0}=D\left(1,\frac{3}{2}\right)=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ :\ |z-1|<\frac{3}{2}\right\}.

For all
j=1,\ldots,\infty
, set
\gamma_{j}(z)=\frac{1}{j+z}
. Then for all
j
,
\gamma_{j}
satisfies

\gamma_{j}(\Omega_{0})\subset D(1,1).

Let
m_{0}\leq j_{1}<j_{2}
be integers. Here the free group on two generators is

\mathbb{F}^{2}=\langle a_{1},a_{2};a_{1}^{-1},a_{2}^{-1}\rangle.

We will look at the twisted transfer operators

{\mathscr{L}}_{N}=T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}\otimes\pi_{N}(a_{1})+T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}\otimes\pi_{N}(a_{2}),

acting on
H^{2}(\Omega_{0})\otimes\mathcal{V}_{N}
, and where
\pi_{N}(a_{1})
,
\pi_{N}(a_{2})
are independent uniform random permutation matrices.
Here we have

{\mathscr{L}}_{N}^{*}{\mathscr{L}}_{N}=\left(T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}+T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}\right)\otimes I_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}+T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}\otimes\pi_{N}(a_{1}^{-1}a_{2})+T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}\otimes\pi_{N}(a_{2}^{-1}a_{1}).

The limit operator acting on
H^{2}(\Omega_{0})\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{F}^{2})
is

\mathscr{M}_{\infty}=\left(T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}+T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}\right)\otimes Id+T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}\otimes\lambda(a_{1}^{-1}a_{2})+T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}\otimes\lambda(a_{2}^{-1}a_{1})=\lambda(\mathscr{X}),

with
\mathscr{X}=\left(T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}+T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}\right)Id+T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}.a_{1}^{-1}a_{2}+T_{\gamma_{j_{2}}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j_{1}}}.a_{2}^{-1}a_{1}
.
The goal of this section is to describe the measure
\mu_{\mathscr{X}}
in the limit regime
m_{0}\rightarrow+\infty
and deduce an effective Weyl law for the singular values of
{\mathscr{L}}_{N}
.

6.1.
Integral kernels and trace norm estimates

Using the Bergman kernel, we know that we have for all
z\in\Omega_{0}
, for all
f\in H^{2}(\Omega_{0})

T_{\gamma_{i}}(f)(z)=\int_{\Omega_{0}}B_{\Omega_{0}}(\gamma_{i}(z),w)f(w)dm(w).

A direct application of Fubini gives an integral representation for
T_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}(f)
as

T_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}(f)(z)=\int_{\Omega_{0}}B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,\gamma_{i}(w))f(w)dm(w).

As a consequence we get for all
i,j

T_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j}}(f)(z)=\int_{\Omega_{0}}B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,\gamma_{i}(w))f(\gamma_{j}(w))dm(w).

Recall that
\gamma_{\ell}(z)=\frac{1}{\ell+z}
, so uniformly on compact sets of
\Omega_{0}
as
\ell\rightarrow\infty
we have
\gamma_{\ell}(z)\rightarrow 0
. In particular we obtain that
as
i,j\rightarrow+\infty
, uniformly on compact sets, we have

T_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j}}(f)(z)\rightarrow f(0)\int_{\Omega_{0}}B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,0)dm(w)=f(0)\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega_{0})B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,0)=\frac{f(0)}{\left(1+\frac{4}{9}(z-1)\right)^{2}}.

Here we have used the explicit formula

B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,w)=\frac{4}{9\pi}\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{4}{9}(z-1)\overline{(w-1)}\right)^{2}}.

We will need the following fact.

Proposition 6.1
.

Assume that
i,j\geq m_{0}
, then we have

\lim_{m_{0}\rightarrow+\infty}\|T_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j}}-\delta_{0}\|_{1}=0,

where
\delta_{0}:H^{2}(\Omega_{0})\rightarrow H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
is the rank one operator defined by

\delta_{0}(f)=\frac{f(0)}{\left(1+\frac{4}{9}(z-1)\right)^{2}}.

Proof
. As already explained in the proof of Proposition
2.3
, we have for any Hilbert basis
(e_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
of
H^{2}(\Omega_{0})
,

\|T_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j}}-\delta_{0}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\|T_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j}}(e_{\ell})-\delta_{0}(e_{\ell})\|_{H^{2}(\Omega_{0})}.

Here we will use the effective basis given by

e_{\ell}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{\ell+1}{\pi}}\left(\frac{z-1}{3/2}\right)^{\ell}.

For us the following fact will be enough.

Lemma 6.2
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for all
\ell
, for all
m_{0}\geq 1
,

\|T_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j}}(e_{\ell})-\delta_{0}(e_{\ell})\|_{H^{2}(\Omega_{0})}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\ell+1}{\pi}}\ell\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{\ell}\frac{1}{m_{0}}\left(1+m_{0}^{-1}\right)^{\ell}.

Proof
. This is just a brute force estimate using the explicit formulas for
e_{\ell}(z)
and
B_{\Omega}(z,w)
. We omit it.
\square

As a consequence, for all
m_{0}>8
we have the bound

\|T_{\gamma_{i}}^{*}T_{\gamma_{j}}-\delta_{0}\|_{1}\leq C\frac{1}{m_{0}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\ell\sqrt{\ell+1}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\ell},

and clearly we have the desired result.
\square

We point out that we have the identity

\delta_{0}(f)=\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega_{0})B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,0)\langle f,B_{\Omega_{0}}(.,0)\rangle_{H^{2}(\Omega_{0})},

from which we can observe that
\delta_{0}
is self-adjoint and his spectrum is precisely

\mathrm{Sp}(\delta_{0})=\{0\}\cup\left\{\frac{81}{25}\right\}.

Indeed, this is a rank one operator and we have

\delta_{0}(B_{\Omega_{0}}(.,0))=B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,0)\mathrm{Vol}(\Omega_{0})B_{\Omega_{0}}(0,0)=\left(\frac{9}{5}\right)^{2}B_{\Omega_{0}}(z,0).

As a simple consequence (by Lidskii’s formula) we have for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathrm{Tr}(\delta_{0}^{p})=\left(\frac{81}{25}\right)^{p}.

6.2.
Limit spectrum of
\mathscr{M}_{\infty}
as
m_{0}\rightarrow\infty
.

By Lemma
5.9
and Proposition
6.1
we now that in operator norm, we have the limit

\lim_{m_{0}\rightarrow+\infty}\|\mathscr{M}_{\infty}(m_{0})-\lambda(\mathscr{Z}_{0})\|=0,

where
\mathscr{Z}_{0}\in S_{1}(H^{2}(\Omega_{0}))[\mathbb{F}^{2}]
is such that

\lambda(\mathscr{Z}_{0})=\delta_{0}\otimes\left(2Id+U+U^{*}\right),

with
U=\lambda(a_{1}^{-1}a_{2})
. Moreover, by combining Proposition
5.6
with Proposition
6.1
, we now that for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\varphi(0)=0

\lim_{m_{0}\rightarrow+\infty}\tau(\varphi(\mathscr{M}_{\infty}(m_{0})))=\tau(\varphi(\lambda(\mathscr{Z}_{0})))=\int\frac{\varphi(x)}{x}d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x).

Note that this convergence is not uniform with respect to
\varphi
.
It turns out that
\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}
is computable. Let
p\geq 1
(recall we are only allowing test functions which vanish at
0
) we have by the binomial formula

\tau\left((\lambda(\mathscr{Z}_{0}))^{p}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}(\delta_{0}^{p})\tau\left((2Id+U+U^{*})^{p}\right)

=\left(\frac{81}{25}\right)^{p}\sum_{k=0}^{p}\binom{p}{k}2^{p-k}\tau\left((U+U^{*})^{k}\right).

It is a classical fact that the moments of a ”Haar unitary element” plus its adjoint can be explicitely computed, see for example
[
26
]
page 12-13. We have actually

\tau\left((U+U^{*})^{k}\right)=\left\{0\ \mathrm{if}\ k\ \mathrm{is\ odd,}\atop\binom{k}{k/2}\ \mathrm{if}\ k\ \mathrm{is\ even.}\right.

Moreover we have also the so-called ”arcsine law”

\tau\left((U+U^{*})^{k}\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-2}^{+2}t^{k}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{4-t^{2}}}.

We get therefore by using the binomial formula again

\tau\left((\lambda(\mathscr{Z}_{0}))^{p}\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-2}^{+2}\left(\frac{81}{25}(2+t)\right)^{p}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{4-t^{2}}},

which by an obvious affine change of variable gives

\tau\left((\lambda(\mathscr{Z}_{0}))^{p}\right)=\frac{25}{81\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{324}{25}}u^{p}\frac{du}{\sqrt{4-(\frac{25}{81}u-2)^{2}}}.

By Stone-Weierstrass theorem we end up with the final formula

d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x)=\frac{25}{81\pi}\chi_{[0,\frac{324}{25}]}(x)\frac{xdx}{\sqrt{4-(\frac{25}{81}x-2)^{2}}}.

Among other interesting facts, this formula shows that the spectrum of
\lambda(\mathscr{Z}_{0})
is the full interval

\mathrm{Sp}(\lambda(\mathscr{Z}_{0}))=\left[0,\frac{324}{25}\right],

with

\frac{324}{25}=12,96
. In particular the spectrum of
\mathscr{M}_{\infty}(m_{0})
converges
in Hausdorff distance
to this interval as
m_{0}\rightarrow+\infty
.
We have the following statement which is direct consequence of the above analysis and Theorem
5.10
.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
I=[a,b]
with
0<a<b
be any closed interval such that
I\subset(0,\frac{324}{25}]
. Then there exists
M_{0},C_{0},C_{1}>0
such that for all
m_{0}\geq M_{0}
fixed, for all
N
large,

C_{0}N\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\mathcal{N}_{I})\leq C_{1}N,

where as before,
\mathcal{N}_{I}:=\#\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}\ :\ \mu_{j}^{2}({\mathscr{L}}_{N})\in I\right\}
.
Both constants
C_{0},C_{1}
above can be taken arbitrarily close to

\int_{I}\frac{d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x)}{x}=\frac{25}{81\pi}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{4-(\frac{25}{81}x-2)^{2}}}

=\frac{1}{\pi}\left[\arcsin\left(\frac{25b-162}{162}\right)-\arcsin\left(\frac{25a-162}{162}\right)\right],

provided
m_{0}
is large enough.

Proof
. Fix
\epsilon>0
. For all
\alpha>0
small enough, there exist
\varphi^{+}_{\alpha},\varphi^{-}_{\alpha}\in C_{0}^{\infty}((0,\infty))
such that

0\leq\varphi_{\alpha}^{-}\leq\chi_{I}\leq\varphi^{+}_{\alpha}\leq 1,

with
\mathrm{Supp}(\varphi^{+}_{\alpha})\subset[a-\alpha,b+\alpha]
,
\mathrm{Supp}(\varphi^{-}_{\alpha})\subset[a,b]
and
\varphi_{\alpha}^{+}\equiv 1
on
I
while

\varphi_{\alpha}^{-}\equiv 1
on
[a+\alpha,b-\alpha]
. Fix then
\alpha>0
small enough so that we have

-\epsilon+\int_{I}\frac{d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x)}{x}\leq\int\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}^{-}(x)}{x}d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x)\leq\int\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}^{+}(x)}{x}d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x)\leq\int_{I}\frac{d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x)}{x}+\epsilon,

which is possible due to the fact that
\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue and hence non-atomic.
By weak convergence of
\mu_{\infty}(m_{0})
to
\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}
, we know that for all
m_{0}
large enough we have

-\epsilon+\int\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}^{-}(x)}{x}d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x)\leq\int\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}^{-}(x)}{x}d\mu_{\infty}(x)\leq\int\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}^{+}(x)}{x}d\mu_{\infty}(x)\leq\int\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}^{+}(x)}{x}d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x)+\epsilon.

On the other hand we have for all
N
large enough by Theorem
5.10
,

-\epsilon+\int\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}^{-}(x)}{x}d\mu_{\infty}(x)\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\mathrm{Tr}(\varphi^{-}_{\alpha}({\mathscr{L}}_{N}^{*}{\mathscr{L}}_{N})))}{N}

\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\mathcal{N}_{I})}{N}\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\mathrm{Tr}(\varphi^{+}_{\alpha}({\mathscr{L}}_{N}^{*}{\mathscr{L}}_{N})))}{N}\leq\int\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}^{+}(x)}{x}d\mu_{\infty}(x)+\epsilon.

The above inequalities show that for all
N
large we have indeed

-3\epsilon+\int_{I}\frac{d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x)}{x}\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{N}(\mathcal{N}_{I})}{N}\leq\int_{I}\frac{d\mu_{\mathscr{Z}_{0}}(x)}{x}+3\epsilon,

the claim is proved.
\square
