Clinical dose of enoxaparin enhances oncolytic virus M1 infection and antitumor efficacy in human bladder cancer
Authors: Xiaoting Xiao, Xudong Jia, Runling Shen, Deli Song, Chenxi Yan, Yongqi Yi, Yang Liu, Cheng Hu, Jun Hu, Wenbo Zhu, Jiankai Liang, Jing Cai, Guangmei Yan, Ying Liu, Yuan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCancer-associated thrombosis (CAT) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in cancer patients, commonly treated with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH). LMWH is structurally similar to cell-surface heparan sulfate (HS), a key mediator of viral attachment, raising concerns that anticoagulation might interfere with oncolytic virotherapy. Here, we found that heparin directly binds oncolytic virus M1 (OVM). While high-dose enoxaparin inhibited viral infection, clinically relevant plasma concentrations (0.52–1.2 IU/mL) unexpectedly enhanced OVM infection and oncolysis in human bladder cancer cells. Mechanistically, enoxaparin at these concentrations facilitates viral attachment via HS proteoglycans (HSPGs). Crucially, this potentiation depends on the host innate immune landscape and is restricted to cells with attenuated interferon-α (IFN-α) responses. In vivo, clinically relevant enoxaparin significantly enhanced the antitumor efficacy of OVM without added toxicity, highlighting a novel and feasible strategy to potentiate oncolytic virotherapy using a standard anticoagulant in bladder cancer.

Introduction

The incidence of venous thromboembolism (VTE) and arterial thrombosis is higher in patients with cancer (4%–20% and 2%–5%, respectively) than in the general population, a phenomenon potentially attributed to leukocytosis or thrombocytosis. These conditions are especially prevalent in patients with pancreatic cancer, lung cancer, or bladder cancer.1 Moreover, the presence of VTE exacerbates the prognosis of cancer patients, leading to a lower survival rate compared to those without thrombosis.2 Studies have found that VTE is significantly associated with a more than 2-fold increase in mortality risk among patients with cancer.3,4,5 Anticoagulants are standard therapy for treating cancer-associated thrombosis (CAT) and for prophylaxis in high-risk patients.6,7 Among these, enoxaparin, a low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), is frequently used due to its advantages, such as a lower bleeding risk and higher bioavailability than other anticoagulants. For the treatment of venous thrombosis, enoxaparin is administered subcutaneously at 100 IU/kg every 12 h. At steady state, the peak and trough plasma concentrations of enoxaparin in patients are approximately 1.2 and 0.52 IU/mL, respectively.8

The mechanism of LMWH is relevant to virology due to its structural similarity to heparan sulfate (HS). HS proteoglycans (HSPGs), expressed on the surface of most mammalian cells, including tumor cells, act as attachment factors for numerous viruses. HSPGs facilitate viral entry by concentrating virions on the cell surface or by serving as primary entry receptors for viruses such as herpes simplex virus 1,9 eastern equine encephalitis virus,10 and Chikungunya virus.11 Consequently, exogenous heparin can competitively inhibit this interaction, thereby blocking viral entry into host cells.12

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) are a class of therapeutic agents that selectively replicate in and lyse cancer cells while sparing normal tissue, emerging as a promising cancer treatment modality. In 2015, the FDA-approved Talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC), an OV derived from herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), as the first such agent for treating unresectable melanoma. Many other OVs are currently in clinical development.13,14,15,16 One such agent, previously identified by our group, is oncolytic virus M1 (OVM), a single-stranded RNA alphavirus. OVM selectively induces apoptosis in cancer cells that express the MXRA8 receptor and lack the zinc finger antiviral protein by mediating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress.17 Preclinical studies have demonstrated the significant therapeutic efficacy and favorable safety profile of OVM,18 leading to its approval for a phase I clinical trial in Japan and China for malignant solid tumors, as well as orphan drug designation from the US FDA for liver cancer and malignant glioma. However, the LMWH used to treat CAT may interfere with OV infection and reduce its oncolytic efficacy.

This study reveals a paradoxical, dose-dependent effect of enoxaparin on OVM activity: while high concentrations inhibited OVM infection and oncolysis, clinically relevant plasma concentrations promoted these effects. This enhancement occurred during the early stages of infection, was driven by increased viral attachment through HSPGs and translated into productive infection only in cells with a relatively weak IFN-α antiviral response.

Furthermore, in a human bladder cancer xenograft model in NCG mice, a clinically achievable peak plasma dose of enoxaparin (0.64 IU/mL) augmented the antitumor efficacy of OVM, prolonged survival, and maintained a favorable safety profile. These findings suggest that enoxaparin, when used for CAT, may not compromise but instead augment OVM therapy, providing a strong rationale for the clinical combination of these two treatments.

Given its limited clinical applicability of unfractionated heparin, we next investigated its derivative, the LMWH enoxaparin. We used cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) to visualize a potential interaction between enoxaparin and OVM. The maps clearly show enoxaparin bound to the surface of OVM, indicating that enoxaparin directly associates with the virion surface (Figure 1C).

Enoxaparin promotes the infection and oncolysis of OVM

Enoxaparin is frequently prescribed during bladder cancer therapy, as bladder cancer is associated with a high risk of VTE.2 Notably, bladder cancer is also highly permissive to OVM infection. Given that enoxaparin can bind directly to OVM, we investigated its effect on the infectivity and oncolytic efficacy of OVM in bladder cancer cells.

To evaluate the effect of enoxaparin on OVM infection, KU-19-19 cells were treated with OVM (multiplicity of infection [MOI] = 1) together with a range of enoxaparin concentrations. Flow cytometry revealed a clear biphasic, dose-dependent pattern: high concentrations of enoxaparin (2.56–164 IU/mL) markedly inhibited viral infection, whereas lower concentrations (0.01–2.56 IU/mL) enhanced OVM infection (Figure 2A). A similar concentration-dependent enhancement was observed at lower MOIs (0.1 and 0.5) (Figures 2B–2D). Pharmacokinetic studies show that standard subcutaneous administration of enoxaparin (1 mg/kg) results in peak and trough plasma concentrations of approximately 1.2 and 0.52 IU/mL, respectively.8 Notably, this clinically established therapeutic window falls within the concentration range that enhanced OVM infection in vitro. It is important to note that at concentrations of 2.56 IU/mL and higher, enoxaparin inhibited KU-19-19 cell growth, which could confound infection rate measurements. Therefore, subsequent experiments were conducted using enoxaparin concentrations that maintained cell viability above 80%.

To further verify the promoting effect of enoxaparin, we assessed viral protein expression and replication. Cells were treated with OVM at an MOI of 0.5 and enoxaparin (0.16–2.56 IU/mL) for 36 h. Western blot analysis showed that enoxaparin increased the expression of the viral E1 protein (Figures 2H and 2I). Correspondingly, qPCR analysis of the supernatant confirmed that enoxaparin significantly increased the viral RNA copy number (Figure 2J).

In summary, our results demonstrate that enoxaparin enhances OVM infection, replication, and oncolysis across an in vitro-effective concentration range that includes clinically relevant plasma levels (0.52–1.2 IU/mL).

Enoxaparin but not other oral anticoagulants enhances OVM infection and oncolysis

We next evaluated the effects of enoxaparin on OVM infection and oncolytic effect in four human bladder cancer cell lines. In BFTC-905 and UM-UC-3 cells, enoxaparin at concentrations of 0.16–2.56 IU/mL enhanced OVM-mediated infection and oncolysis (Figures 3A–3D). Conversely, in highly susceptible 5637 cells, enoxaparin led to a dose-dependent decrease in OVM-mediated effects (Figures 3E and 3F). The 647-V cell line, known for its resistance to OVM, showed a near-zero infection rate even at a high MOI of 100 (Figures S1A and S1B), and varying enoxaparin concentrations did not affect OVM infection or cytotoxicity in 647-V cells (Figures S1A and S1B). To compare these findings with other common anticoagulants, we investigated the effects of two direct oral anticoagulants, rivaroxaban and dabigatran.19,20 Concentrations were selected to reflect therapeutic plasma levels: 127.3–235.4 ng/mL for rivaroxaban21 and approximately 100 ng/mL for dabigatran.22 Rivaroxaban had no significant impact on M1 virus-mediated cytotoxicity, whereas dabigatran showed a slight inhibitory effect (Figure 3G).

Additionally, for safety consideration, we assessed whether enoxaparin affects OVM infection in non-malignant cells using the human ureteral epithelial cell line SV-HUC-1. The OVM was largely non-infectious in SV-HUC-1 cells, and its infectivity was not significantly altered by enoxaparin at any concentration tested (Figures S1C and S1D).

In summary, enoxaparin at therapeutic plasma concentrations (0.16–2.56 IU/mL) enhanced OVM-mediated oncolysis in several bladder cancer cell lines but not in normal ureteral epithelial cells. Among the oral anticoagulants tested, rivaroxaban did not alter OVM cytotoxicity, while dabigatran was slightly inhibitory.

Enoxaparin enhances OVM infectivity by promoting viral attachment

To investigate the mechanism by which low-dose enoxaparin enhances OVM infection, we first sought to identify the affected stage of the viral life cycle. The OVM life cycle, typical of positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses, comprises six key stages: attachment, penetration, uncoating, gene expression and replication, assembly, and release.23,24

We used a fluorescent viral plaque assay to distinguish between effects on early versus late stages of infection (Figure 4A). In this assay, cells were treated with OVM and enoxaparin for varying durations (0.5, 1, 2, 4, or 6 h), after which an agarose overlay was added to restrict viral spread. Plaques were imaged 48 h post-infection. An increase in the number of plaques indicates enhancement of early events (e.g., attachment and entry), while an increase in plaque size suggests acceleration of later processes (e.g., replication and cell-to-cell spread). Treatment with enoxaparin (0.64 IU/mL) resulted in a significantly greater number of viral plaques compared to the untreated control group (Figures 4B and 4C). However, there was no significant difference in plaque size between the two groups (Figure 4D). To confirm this hypothesis, we performed a viral binding assay. Consistently, enoxaparin at 0.16–0.64 IU/mL significantly enhanced OVM attachment to host cells (Figure 4E), supporting the conclusion that enoxaparin promotes infection by increasing initial viral binding. Conversely, higher concentrations of enoxaparin significantly inhibited viral binding (Figure S2), aligning with the observed reduction in infectivity at high doses. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that enoxaparin increases OVM infectivity by promoting the initial attachment of the virus to the cell surface.

Enoxaparin enhances OVM infection via HSPG interactions and is restricted by intrinsic interferon responses

Given that HS is a known attachment factor for alphaviruses, we investigated its role in the enoxaparin-mediated enhancement of OVM infection. While a low concentration of anti-HS antibody (0.5 μg/mL) did not markedly affect baseline OVM infection, it significantly ablated the pro-infective effect of enoxaparin. Higher concentrations of the anti-HS antibody (2.0 and 8.0 μg/mL) dose-dependently inhibited OVM infection, both in the presence and absence of enoxaparin. Notably, this inhibition was significantly more pronounced in the enoxaparin-treated group (Figures 5A and 5B). Based on the differential effect of enoxaparin on OVM infection, we categorized the cell lines. KU-19-19, BFTC-905, and UM-UC-3 cells, in which enoxaparin enhanced OVM infection, were designated “promoting cells.” In contrast, 5637 and 647-V cells, where enoxaparin was either inhibitory or had no effect, were designated “non-promoting cells.” We then compared the cell-surface HSPG expression levels between these cells using the 10E4 antibody, which recognizes sulfated HS epitopes, and found that the median HSPG expression on promoting cells was slightly but not significantly higher than on non-promoting cells (Figure 5C). Thus, baseline HSPG abundance alone cannot fully explain the differential responsiveness to enoxaparin.

To determine if qualitative differences in HSPG interactions contribute to this phenotype, we utilized a HS microarray to analyze OVM binding specificity. Compared to OVM alone, OVM pre-mixed with enoxaparin (0.64 IU/mL) displayed significantly amplified binding signals across multiple sulfated oligosaccharide structures (Figure 5D). This suggests that enoxaparin enhances OVM-glycan interactions by mimicking or providing densely sulfated binding motifs that increase viral attachment efficiency.

To identify downstream host factors governing cellular permissiveness, we performed transcriptomic profiling of promoting versus non-promoting cells. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment of the Interferon Alpha Response pathway in non-promoting cells (Figure 5E). This signature is consistent with a heightened intrinsic antiviral state in non-promoting cells that likely counteracts the attachment advantages conferred by enoxaparin. These findings suggest that stronger intrinsic antiviral signaling may limit infection despite enhanced viral attachment. In promoting cells, the absence of this robust antiviral barrier allows enoxaparin-facilitated attachment to drive efficient infection and oncolysis.

Finally, we assessed two established determinants of alphavirus susceptibility, the entry receptor MXRA8 and the antiviral restriction factor ZAP.17,25 Western blot analysis demonstrated that expression levels of neither protein correlated with the enoxaparin-enhancement phenotype across the panel of cell lines (Figure S3A). Consequently, these specific host factors do not appear to drive the differential response to enoxaparin.

Collectively, our findings support a model in which enoxaparin potentiates OVM infection by facilitating interaction with highly sulfated motifs during entry. However, the transition from enhanced attachment to productive replication is gated by the target cell’s intrinsic antiviral state, specifically the integrity of the interferon-α response.

Enoxaparin enhances the therapeutic efficacy of OVM in vivo with a favorable safety profile

To investigate the effect of enoxaparin on the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the OVM, we established a KU-19-19 subcutaneous xenograft model in NCG mice. First, a dose-ranging study was conducted to determine the optimal dosing strategy. NCG mice were administered varying subcutaneous doses of enoxaparin, and plasma concentrations were quantified 5 h post-injection, a time point corresponding to the approximate peak concentration (Tmax ≈ 4–5 h26) (Figure 6A). A dose of 0.8 mg resulted in a peak plasma concentration of approximately 1 IU/mL. To simulate the peak steady-state concentration achieved in human therapy,27 we adopted a dosing regimen consisting of a 0.8 mg loading dose followed by 0.4 mg maintenance doses. The administration protocol is depicted in Figure 6B. The combination of the OVM and enoxaparin significantly delayed tumor growth and extended mouse survival compared to OVM monotherapy, demonstrating an enhanced antitumor effect (Figures 6C–6E). Finally, we investigated whether this synergistic effect and favorable safety profile was related to the tumor-selective viral biodistribution. Following the sampling schedule in Figure 7C, viral RNA levels were quantified in tumors and major organs at 24, 72, and 96 h post-treatment. Enoxaparin administration did not significantly alter viral distribution in major organs at any time point. In contrast, the combination group exhibited a significantly elevated viral load within the tumor tissue (Figure 7E). This tumor-restricted increase in viral burden provides a mechanistic basis for the enhanced efficacy observed in vivo while preserving systemic safety.

In summary, combining enoxaparin with the OVM significantly delays bladder tumor progression and extends survival in a preclinical model. This therapeutic enhancement is associated with an increased intratumoral viral load. Furthermore, the combination therapy demonstrated a favorable safety profile, supporting its potential for future clinical evaluation.

Discussion

VTE is a frequent and serious complication in oncological patients, contributing to poor prognosis and increased mortality across major cancer types.3,28,29 Consequently, clinical guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and other organizations recommend anticoagulant prophylaxis, often with LMWH, for at-risk patients.30,31 While direct oral anticoagulants are emerging as a preferred option, LMWH remains a standard of care, particularly for patients at high risk of bleeding.19,20 Paradoxically, heparin and its derivatives are widely reported to inhibit the adhesion and invasion of various viruses.12,32,33 This apparent contradiction raises a critical question: does anticoagulant therapy with LMWH interfere with the efficacy of OV treatments?

This study sought to address this question by investigating the interaction between enoxaparin, a widely used LMWH, and OVM. Our results reveal a direct, concentration-dependent effect. At high concentrations (>10 IU/mL), enoxaparin inhibited OVM activity, a finding consistent with previous reports on other viruses.34,35,36,37 However, at clinically relevant plasma concentrations (0.5–1.2 IU/mL), we observed a significant enhancement of OVM infection and oncolysis. This suggests that, contrary to initial concerns, the standard use of enoxaparin in cancer therapy may not only be compatible with OVM treatment but could potentiate its antitumor effects.

Our finding is not without precedent. A study by Choijilsuren et al. reported that heparin at low, physiological concentrations (≈0.2–0.6 IU/mL) strongly boosted hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection.38 Our preliminary mechanistic investigation suggests that the enhancement of OVM infection by enoxaparin is similarly dependent on cell surface HSPGs. However, we noted that this pro-viral effect was cell-line specific. Elucidating the precise molecular mechanism is therefore critical for identifying the patient populations in which this synergy is most likely to occur, paving the way for more precise therapeutic strategies.

To validate these findings in vivo, we used a human bladder cancer xenograft model in NCG mice. The results showed that enoxaparin did not negatively impact the therapeutic efficacy of OVM, but rather significantly augmented its antitumor activity. OVM has already entered phase I clinical trials in Japan and China and also received orphan drug designation from the FDA for treating glioma and liver cancer, highlighting its therapeutic promise. Our findings broaden the potential clinical applications of OVM, particularly for the large cohort of cancer patients who require anticoagulant therapy for VTE.

Our animal experiments were conducted in severely immunodeficient NCG mice, in which adaptive immune responses are largely absent. Accordingly, the enhanced therapeutic efficacy observed with enoxaparin co-administration most likely reflects increased viral infection and replication within tumor tissues. Consistent with this interpretation, enoxaparin co-treatment led to a higher viral burden in tumors without a corresponding increase in viral accumulation in normal organs (Figure 7E).

Our findings suggest that the ability of enoxaparin to enhance OVM infection is ultimately constrained by the intrinsic antiviral state of the target cell, particularly the activity of the interferon-α (IFN-α) pathway.39 IFN-α is a key component of the innate antiviral defense against RNA viruses and establishes a broad antiviral state in infected and neighboring cells.40,41 Upon receptor activation, IFN-α triggers the JAK-STAT signaling cascade, inducing the transcription of hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs). These ISGs suppress viral RNA synthesis and translation and disrupt replication-complex formation, thereby preventing efficient amplification of viral genomes—even after the virus has successfully entered the cell.42

For alphaviruses, IFN-α-mediated restriction occurs predominantly during the early intracellular replication phase.43,44 This mechanistic framework aligns well with our observations. In cell lines with comparatively weak IFN-α activity (promoting cells), the increase in viral attachment mediated by enoxaparin is sufficient to support productive viral replication and oncolysis. In contrast, in cell lines with a stronger IFN-α-driven antiviral state (non-promoting cells), intracellular restriction mechanisms effectively suppress viral replication after entry, thereby negating any attachment advantage conferred by enoxaparin. Thus, the activity of the IFN-α pathway emerges as a critical determinant of whether enoxaparin can potentiate OVM infection.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that clinically relevant concentrations of enoxaparin can enhance the infectivity and antitumor efficacy of OVM without compromising safety. This effect appears to be determined by the balance between enoxaparin-mediated enhancement of viral attachment and the intrinsic antiviral state of the target cell, particularly the IFN-α pathway. These findings provide a mechanistic basis supporting the compatibility and potential therapeutic benefit of combining LMWH with oncolytic virotherapy in cancer patients and warrant further evaluation in additional oncolytic platforms and tumor settings.

Limitations of the study

Restricted tumor type validation: in vivo efficacy was demonstrated solely in a human bladder cancer xenograft model; translational relevance across a broader spectrum of tumor histologies requires further investigation.

Immunodeficient in vivo model: all animal experiments were performed in severely immunodeficient NCG mice, precluding evaluation of potential adaptive immune contributions to the observed therapeutic enhancement. In addition, only male mice were used in this study; the potential influence of sex on therapeutic efficacy was not evaluated and warrants further investigation.

Preliminary mechanistic insight: the molecular mechanism underlying the enoxaparin-OVM synergy was not fully elucidated; the investigation was limited to implicating HSPG engagement and the IFN-α pathway without defining precise downstream effectors.

Cell-line specificity and undefined biomarkers: the proviral effect of enoxaparin was restricted to specific cell lines, and reliable predictive biomarkers for identifying responsive patient populations remain unidentified.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

Cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Vero, UM-UC-3), Guangzhou Cellcook Biotech (5637) and Nanjing Foksay Biotechnology Company Limited (KU-19-19, 647-V, BFTC-905). Vero cells and UM-UC-3 cells were cultured in Eagle’s minimum essential medium (Corning, 10-010-CV) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 10099–141) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, SV30010). KU-19-19 cells and 5637 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Corning, 10-040-CV) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 10099–141) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, SV30010). 647-V cells and BFTC-905 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (Corning, 10-013-CV) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 10099–141) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, SV30010). All cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and routinely tested to confirm the absence of mycoplasma contamination.

Animal model

Animal studies were conducted after approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Lai’an Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (ethical approval number: G2025081). All animal experiments were performed by Lai’an Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All mice used in this study were male. 6–8 weeks old male NCG mice were purchased from GuangDong GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. For evaluation of systemic antitumor effect, 2×106 KU-19-19 cells were subcutaneously injected into the hind flank of male NCG mice. When the palpable tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to different treatment groups. In each regimen, treatments were administered as single agents or in combination with the drugs listed in the corresponding figure legends. OVM (1 × 107 pfu/mouse) was intravenously injected daily for a total of 5 administrations. Enoxaparin sodium injection (Sanofi-Synthelabo Limited) was diluted with sterile phosphate-buffered saline to the desired concentration and administered via subcutaneous injection (0.4 mg/mouse). Tumor length and width were measured and tumor volume was calculated using the formula (length × width2/2). Tumor measurements were performed by researchers blinded to the group allocations. Mice were sacrificed when the maximum tumor diameter exceeded 2 cm or tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3, and their survival rate was recorded. Blood samples were collected from mice using the retro-orbital bleeding method. Hematological and biochemical analyses were performed by the Guangdong Experimental Animal Testing Institute. After euthanasia with carbon dioxide, tissues and organs were harvested for histopathological examination. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was conducted by The GBA National Institute for Nanotechnology Innovation.

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulatory requirements.

Method details

Production and quantification of oncolytic M1 virus (OVM)

Oncolytic virus M1 (OVM) was grown in Vero cells, and was provided by Guangzhou Virotech Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. M1-GFP is a recombinant derivative of OVM expressing jellyfish green fluorescent protein 61. Virus titer was determined by CCID50 assay using BHK-21 cells. The virus titer was determined by a CCID50 assay in BHK-21 cells.

Heparin-sepharose affinity chromatography

Purified M1-GFP virus in dPBS was applied to a 1 mL HiTrap heparin-Sepharose column (GE Healthcare, 17040601). The column was washed with 10 mL of dPBS and bound protein was eluted with a gradient of NaCl from 100 mM to 900 mM in dPBS. The eluents with different concentrations of NaCl were collected, and 100 μL of them were taken for RNA extraction and detected by qRT-PCR.

Cryo-EM

The cryo-EM map of OVM was resolved in previous study.25 The excess enoxaparin were incubated with purified OVM at 4°C overnight. Cryo-EM datasets were collected under Talos F200C election microscope (Thermo Fisher) with elsa cryo-transfer holder (Gatan). Contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using Gctf (Real-time CTF determination and correction). Virion particles were picked and subjected to reference-free 2D classification by relion-3. Selected particles were refined by relion-3, yielding the final reconstructions. Structures were visualized and compared using UCSF Chimera.

Cell infection assay

Cells were seeded in 48-well plates and cultured overnight. M1-GFP, known to have the same infection rate and cytotoxicity as the M1 virus, was used for treatment.17 Cells were treated with a premixed solution of enoxaparin and OVM. The specific virus MOIs and incubation times for each cell line are provided in the figure legends. Following treatment, the cells were digested with trypsin to create a single-cell suspension. Flow cytometry(CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter) was employed to quantify the intensity of GFP expression and determine the infection rate.

RNA sequencing

Human bladder cancer cell lines (KU-19-19, BFTC-905, UM-UC-3, 5637, and 647-V) were submitted for transcriptome sequencing, which was carried out by GENEWIZ (Suzhou Genewiz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) using the provider’s standard workflow for RNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing, and primary data processing.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded in 48-well plates and cultured overnight. After treatment, 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT, MP Biomedicals, 102227) was added to the well (0.5 mg/mL to final concentration), and the cells were incubated at 37°C for another 3 h. MTT-containing medium was removed and the MTT precipitate was dissolved in 200 μL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, 67-68-5). The optical absorbance was determined at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek).

Western blotting

Cells were lysed using the M-PER Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Scientific), and sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was performed. Membranes were visualized on a ChemiDoc XRS+System (Bio-Rad) using ImmobilonWestern Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore).

Heparan sulfate microarray binding assay

Heparan sulfate microarray analysis was performed by Nanjing Kreyxin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. using their standardized glycan microarray platform. (https://ncfg.hms.harvard.edu/protocols). Two independent assays were performed: one using purified OVM (12 μg/mL) to assess its baseline binding profile, and another comparing OVM alone with OVM pre-mixed with enoxaparin (0.64 IU/mL) to evaluate the effect of enoxaparin on OVM–glycan interactions.

For each experiment, protein samples were fluorescently labeled with Cy3 dye and incubated with the microarray for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After incubation, slides were washed to remove unbound proteins and scanned using a LuxScan-10 K/A microarray scanner. Fluorescence intensities were quantified with Microarray Image Analyzer software (Creative Biochip Ltd).

Binding signals were compared across oligosaccharides with different sulfation patterns to characterize the sulfation-dependent binding of OVM and the modulatory effect of enoxaparin.

Viral quantification using RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Life Technologies). Quantitative PCR was performed using the Real Time One Step RT-qPCR (TIANGEN, FP314) in the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (LifeTechnologies).

Fluorescent virus plaque formation assay

Cells (3∗103) were seeded in 96-well plates and, after overnight, M1-GFP(1MOI) and enoxaparin (0.64 IU/mL) were added. After 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h of incubation, the plate culture supernatant was discarded and washed three times with PBS, then added the mixed agarose medium. After 48 h of infection, the virus infection was observed by imaging analysis system (Operetta CLS High Content Analysis System), and the size and number were recorded by photographing the whole well.

Binding experiment

The adherent cells were washed with ice-cold medium containing 2% FBS for three times. For the binding test, cells (5∗105), enoxaparin (0.16, 0.64 IU/mL) and OVM (MOI 50) were mixed in 6-well plates and incubated on ice for 2 h. After the cells were washed with PBS for 3 times, the RNA of the cells was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Life technologies, 15596018).

Anti-heparan sulfate-mediated inhibition of enoxaparin effect

KU-19-19 cells were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells per well. After cell adherence, Anti-Heparan Sulfate (F58-10E4, Amsbio) was added at concentrations of 0, 0.5, 2.0, and 8.0 μg/mL. Following a 30-min incubation, a premixed solution of enoxaparin (0.64 IU/mL) and OVM (1 MOI) was added. After 30 h, GFP expression intensity and infection rate were quantified using flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter).

Cell surface heparan sulfate expression in different cell lines

Different cell lines (KU-1919, BFTC-905, UM-UC-3, 5637, 647-V) were seeded into flow cytometry tubes at a density of 1×105 cells per tube. Each tube was incubated with 1 μg of Anti-Heparan Sulfate (F58-10E4, Amsbio) for 1 h. After three washes with PBS, a fluorescently conjugated secondary antibody (APC channel) was added and incubated for 40 min. Following three additional PBS washes, cell surface heparan sulfate expression was assessed by measuring the APC channel signal intensity using flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter).

Determination of enoxaparin plasma concentration in mice using coagulation analyzer

NCG mice were subcutaneously injected with PBS or different doses of enoxaparin (0.1 mg, 0.4 mg, and 0.8 mg). After 5 h, blood samples were collected from the mice’s orbit. The blood samples were then treated with an anti-Xa assay kit (Stago, STA LIQUIDANTI XA 4) and analyzed for enoxaparin concentration using an automatic coagulation analyzer (Stago, STA R Max).

Viral load measurement in mouse tissues

Tissues were washed with phosphate-buffered saline and weighed, then placed into a gentleMACS M tube (Miltenyi Biotec). The volume of TRIzol added was calculated based on the tissue weight (1 mL/100 mg). The tissue was minced using scissors, and the tube lid was securely closed. The M tube was inverted and placed into the gentleMACS Dissociator for dissociation. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method. The subsequent viral content determination was performed as described in “Viral Quantification Using RT-qPCR.”

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (versions 8.0 and 9.0). Sample sizes and specific statistical tests used for each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size, and no data points were excluded from analysis.

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Bartlett’s test. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used when normality and equal-variance assumptions were met. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc test or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was applied, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Levels of significance are indicated as follows: ns, not significant; p < 0.05; ∗p < 0.01; ∗∗p < 0.001.
