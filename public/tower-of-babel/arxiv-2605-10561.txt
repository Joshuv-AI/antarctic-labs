The diffusion equation for non-Markovian Gaussian stochastic processes Preprint: APS/123-QED
Alessandro Taloni, Gianni Pagnini, Aleksei Chechkin

Abstract
We derive the exact evolution equation for the probability density function of particle displacements generated by arbitrary Gaussian velocity processes, when neither Markovianity and nor stationarity are assumed. Starting from the characteristic function of the density of the position, we construct a systematic hierarchy of equations based on Wick’s theorem, in which the dynamics is governed by sums of geometrically connected Wick contractions. This approach yields a closed non‑Markovian diffusion equation that generalizes the Fokker–Planck description and preserves Gaussianity only in the infinite‑order limit.



The Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{\partial P(v,t)}{\partial t}=\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial v}(vP(v,t))+\frac{k_{B}T\gamma}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}P(v,t)}{\partial v^{2}}

(1)

was independently derived by
Fokker
11
;
12
, Planck
31
, and Smoluchowski
34
,
building on the earlier insight of Lord Rayleigh, who investigated the velocity dynamics of a massive particle
m

colliding with a bath of lighter particles in thermal equilibrium at temperature
T
. This setting, later
known as the Rayleigh piston
20
;
10
, leads at the macroscopic level to linear damping,

\dot{v}=-\gamma v

35
, and, upon Langevin’s introduction of a stochastic force
\xi(t)
, to the
classical description of Brownian motion
24
:

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=v(t)

(2a)

\displaystyle\dot{v}(t)

\displaystyle=-\gamma v+\xi(t)\,.

(2b)

The statistical properties of the velocity
v(t)
are entirely determined by
those of the stochastic force
\xi(t)
. In most physical applications one
specifies only
\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0
and

\langle\xi(t_{1})\xi(t_{2})\rangle=2\gamma k_{B}T\,\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})
, thereby ensuring
consistency with the fluctuation–dissipation theorem. However, full
equivalence between the Langevin description (
2b
) and the
associated Fokker–Planck equation (
1
) requires the process
v(t)
to
be Gaussian at all times. This, in turn, demands that
\xi(t)
be Gaussian white
noise, implying an additional postulate beyond the first two moments

37
(see the Supplementary Material – SM).

As a Gaussian process, all statistical properties of
v(t)
are completely
determined by its two-time correlation function. In particular, any
2n
-point
velocity correlation admits a Wick decomposition
39
,

\langle v(t_{1})\cdots v(t_{2n})\rangle=\sum_{\text{all pairings}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i<j\leq 2n\\
(i,j)\,\text{paired}\end{subarray}}\langle v(t_{i})v(t_{j})\rangle\,,

(3)

with
(2n-1)!!
distinct pairings. In the long-time limit or under thermal
initial conditions,
v(t)
reduces to the unique stationary Gaussian Markov
process with exponential autocorrelation,

\langle v(t_{1})v(t_{2})\rangle=\frac{k_{B}T}{m}\rm{e}^{-\gamma\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|}\,,

(4)

namely the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

36
;
9
;
8
.

Therefore, also the position variable
x(t)
, defined by the linear kinematic relation
(
2a
), is Gaussian. Its even moments follow
directly from Wick’s theorem and obey the identity

\langle x^{2n}(t)\rangle=(2n-1)!!\,\langle x^{2}(t)\rangle^{n}
. However, unlike

v(t)
,
x(t)
exhibits non-Markovian dynamics due to the finite temporal
correlations of the velocity (
4
)

1
1

1

In general, time integration of a Markov process generates non‑Markovian dynamics, except in the singular case of white noise. We recall that Markovianity, as well as stationarity or ergodicity, is a property of a chosen
observable, not an intrinsic property of the underlying stochastic process.
.

On long time scales,
t\gg 1/\gamma
, or in the large damping limit, the probability density
P(x,t)
obeys the
standard Smoluchowski diffusion equation

34
;
37
;
33
. At shorter time
scales, however, the correct evolution equation remains nontrivial.

The coupled system defined by Eqs. (
2a
) and (
4
)
thus provides a prototypical example of a non-Markovian Gaussian process. In
this work we derive the
exact diffusion equation
governing
P(x,t)
for an
arbitrary Gaussian non-Markovian velocity process, not necessarily stationary.

Rather than restricting to Ornstein–Uhlenbeck statistics, we consider a general
Gaussian velocity process
v(t)
fully characterized by its two-time
correlation function
\langle v(t_{1})v(t_{2})\rangle
, thereby encompassing a wide
class of internal and external colored noises

37
;
27
;
19
.
Although linear systems driven by Gaussian colored noise have been extensively
studied

19
;
37
;
42
;
33
,
our starting point follows Balescu’s
physicist’s compromise

2
;
3
;
7
, whereby the
velocity
v(t)
is treated as a prescribed Gaussian process, rather than being
generated explicitly by an underlying Langevin equation, i.e., Eq. (
2b
).

Within the Balescu’s hybrid kinetic framework, the quasi-linear approximation leads to
the diffusion equation

\frac{\partial P(x,t)}{\partial t}=\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\,\langle v(t)v(t_{1})\rangle\frac{\partial^{2}P(x,t_{1})}{\partial x^{2}}\,,

(5)

which first emerged from projection-operator coarse-graining of Hamiltonian
systems in the work of Zwanzig

41
;
28
;
42
.
We therefore refer to Eq. (
5
) as the
Zwanzig–Balescu equation

(ZBE).

An alternative route, based on a non-Markovian generalization of the
Chapman–Kolmogorov equation, was developed by Hänggi and leads to

17
;
18
;
16
;
19

\frac{\partial P(x,t)}{\partial t}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt_{1}\,\langle v(t)v(t_{1})\rangle\frac{\partial^{2}P(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}.

(6)

This equation, also referred to as a
bona fide
Fokker–Planck equation

1
;
13
, was originally introduced by Batchelor
in the context of turbulent diffusion
4
.
We therefore refer to Eq. (
6
) as the

Batchelor–Hänggi equation
(BHE).

Although both equations apply to Gaussian stationary processes, the BHE is
explicitly dependent on the initial time
t_{0}
and may violate positivity

14
;
19
. More fundamentally,
neither
equation is exact
: both fail to reproduce Gaussian position moments beyond the
second order, as shown in the SM.
This structural shortcoming motivates the exact diffusion equation
derived in the next section.

The truncated characteristic function hierarchy.

We introduce the characteristic function as
\hat{P}(q,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!dx\,P(x,t)\,e^{iqx},

whose Taylor expansion around
q=0
reads

\hat{P}(q,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(iq)^{2n}}{(2n)!}\,\langle x^{2n}(t)\rangle.

(7)

Taking the time derivative of Eq. (
7
) yields the exact
identity

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{P}(q,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(iq)^{2n}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\cdots

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{2n-2}}dt_{2n-1}\langle v(t)v(t_{1})\cdots v(t_{2n-1})\rangle,

(8)

with
t_{0}\equiv t
(see SM). Eq.(
8
) holds for any stochastic process, independently of Markovianity,
Gaussianity, or stationarity (with the exception of Lévy–stable processes).
We now define the
moment–truncated characteristic function

(which coincides with the truncated cumulant expansion in the present Gaussian
setting) as

\hat{\rho}_{N}(q,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{(iq)^{2n}}{(2n)!}\langle x^{2n}(t)\rangle,

(9)

with the consistency condition

\lim_{N\to\infty}\hat{\rho}_{N}(q,t)=\hat{P}(q,t).

Finally, making use of the Wick decomposition (
3
), the time evolution of
the truncated characteristic function can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\rho}_{N}(q,t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}(iq)^{2n}\sum_{\text{all pairings}}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\cdots

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{2n-2}}dt_{2n-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<j\leq 2n-1\\
(i,j)\,\text{paired}\end{subarray}}\langle v(t_{i})v(t_{j})\rangle.

(10)

3

2n
‑point correlation function is given by the sum over all possible pairwise connections among the points
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{2n}

\langle v(t_{i})v(t_{j})\rangle

The diffusion equation for non-Markovian Gaussian processes
.
In the infinite truncation limit, the evolution equation
for the characteristic function is recovered in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{P}(q,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(iq)^{2k}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{2k-2}}dt_{2k-1}\,R_{k}\,\hat{P}(q,t_{2k-1}).

(15)

By inverting the Fourier transform, we finally obtain the exact evolution
equation for the probability density function of a non‑Markovian Gaussian
process:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\cdots

\displaystyle\qquad\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{2k-2}}dt_{2k-1}\,R_{k}\,\frac{\partial^{2k}}{\partial x^{2k}}P(x,t_{2k-1}).

(16)

The first term of the summation (
k=1
) coincides with the
ZBE, showing explicitly that any finite truncation
destroys the Gaussian character of the solution.
Only the full infinite series restores Gaussianity exactly.

Remarkably, no assumption has been made on the stationarity of the velocity
process. Therefore, Eqs. (
10
) and
(
16
) are valid for both stationary and non‑stationary
kinetics defined by Eq. (
2a
).
In fact, these equations hold for
any
velocity process
v(t)
whose
single‑time probability density is Gaussian,

P(v,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\langle v^{2}(t)\rangle}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{v^{2}}{2\langle v^{2}(t)\rangle}\right],

independently of the explicit temporal structure of its correlations.

Fractional Brownian motion.

Fractional Brownian motion is a Gaussian process characterized by long–range,
stationary velocity correlations of the form
\langle v(t_{1})v(t_{2})\rangle=2\chi H(2H-1)\,|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{2H-2}
,
where
0<H<1
is the Hurst exponent and
\chi>0

25
.
Contrary to the conclusions of Ref.
38
, coinciding with a BHE, Eq. (
16
) provides the correct evolution
equation for the displacement probability density
P(x,t)
.

In the persistent regime,
1/2<H<1
, the leading term in the sum
(
16
) can be derived explicitly, as shown in the SM,
yielding

\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial t^{2}}\simeq\chi\,\Gamma(2H+1)\,\mathcal{D}^{2H-2}\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial x^{2}},\qquad\tfrac{1}{2}<H<1,

where
\mathcal{D}^{\mu}
, with
\mu>0
, denotes the Riemann–Liouville fractional derivative
of order
\mu

32
.
To the best of our knowledge, this fractional differential equation has not been previously
studied in the literature. For
H=1/2
, the standard diffusion equation with diffusion coefficient
\chi
is recovered,
since
\mathcal{D}^{1}\equiv d/dt
.
In the antipersistent regime,
0<H<1/2
, the velocity correlation kernel becomes
non–integrable at coinciding times, and the derivation of an analogous leading–order
equation remains an open problem that we will address in a forthcoming paper.
