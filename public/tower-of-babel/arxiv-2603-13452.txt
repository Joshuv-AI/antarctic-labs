MESD: Detecting and Mitigating Procedural Bias in Intersectional Groups
Gideon Popoola and John Sheppard

Abstract
Research about bias in machine learning has mostly focused on outcome-oriented fairness metrics (e.g., equalized odds) and on a single protected category. Although these approaches offer great insight into bias in ML, they provide limited insight into model procedure bias. To address this gap, we proposed multi-category explanation stability disparity (MESD), an intersectional, procedurally oriented metric that measures the disparity in the quality of explanations across intersectional subgroups in multiple protected categories. MESD serves as a complementary metric to outcome-oriented metrics, providing detailed insight into the procedure of a model. To further extend the scope of the holistic selection model, we also propose a multi-objective optimization framework, UEF (Utility-Explanation-Fairness), that jointly optimizes three objectives. Experimental results across multiple datasets show that UEF effectively balances objectives. Also, the results show that MESD can effectively capture the explanation difference between intersectional groups. This research addresses an important gap by examining explainability with respect to fairness across multiple protected categories.



I
Introduction

Machine learning (ML) systems have increasingly been used in day-to-day human decision-making, such as criminal justice, credit scoring, hiring, and healthcare
[
12
]
. As these ML models become more common, concerns about bias have become essential to responsible use of these models
[
3
]
. Most of the previous fairness research focuses on outcome-oriented fairness, which measures whether predictions
\hat{Y}
satisfy group-level statistical constraints relative to the true label
Y
and a protected attribute
A

[
17
]
. Examples of these outcome-oriented fairness metrics include Demographic Parity, Equal Opportunity, and Equalized Odds
[
10
]
. Recently, a novel body of research proposes that fair outcomes alone are insufficient, and that fairness must also attend to the procedural aspect of model reasoning

[
9
]
. A model may satisfy outcome-oriented fairness metrics such as Equalized Odds while relying on unstable or group-dependent reasoning processes for individual predictions, which can cause the model to be nontransparent or violate fundamental principles of procedural fairness
[
28
]
.

Beyond fairness, the field of explainability has produced several tools for analyzing models, including Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP)
[
16
]
and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanation (LIME)
[
20
]
. These post-hoc explanation methods have the capability to decompose predictions into feature-level attributions, helping users to explain how a model arrives at a decision. However, the explainability literature has historically operated independently of the fairness literature.

Recent work has begun to formalize how to bridge explanation research and fairness research to assess the stability, robustness, and fidelity of explanations derived from an ML model
[
6
]
. These works have demonstrated that two models with identical outcome-oriented fairness (e.g., equalized odds) can differ widely in whether they provide stable, consistent explanations across groups
[
2
]
. However, most of these works are limited in scope because they handle only single protected attributes, not intersectional identities (e.g., race
\times
gender). Also, most fairness optimization studies balance only utility and outcome-fairness
[
25
]
,

This paper addresses these gaps by developing the Multi-category Explanation Stability Disparity (MESD), a procedural fairness metric designed to operate across intersectional groups using multiple post-hoc explainers and label-aware aggregation. MESD assesses whether individuals across different intersectional groups receive explanations of comparable stability, consistency, and robustness when subjected to small perturbations. To the best of our knowledge, MESD is the first procedural metric that addresses the issue of multiple protected attributes. Also, we integrate MESD into a multi-objective optimization framework that optimizes utility, fairness, and explanation.
This optimization framework reveals trade-offs that are invisible under outcome-oriented fairness alone and provides models that are simultaneously accurate, statistically fair, and procedurally fair. The contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

1.

We formulate the problem of detecting intersectional group-level disparities in explanation quantity and propose a new procedural metric named MESD to quantify disparities in explanation robustness, stability, and robustness.

2.

We proposed label-aware aggregation to align MESD with the label-conditional nature of outcome fairness, empirical-Bayes shrinkage to stabilize estimates for small intersectional groups, and a Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)-based tail formulation to emphasize worst-case subgroup disparities, addressing fairness gerrymandering
[
14
]
.

3.

We proposed a multi-objective optimization framework that optimizes utility, explainability, and fairness, coupled with Chebyshev scalarization for Pareto front selection.

4.

We demonstrate empirically that MESD reveals disparities and mitigation opportunities overlooked by traditional fairness metrics alone.

II
Related Work

Fairness in ML has become a rapidly expanding research area, motivated by the increasing reliance on ML systems in socially consequential domains
[
18
]
.
The majority of the existing fairness research work is centered on group fairness, wherein predictions must satisfy statistical criteria across protected groups (e.g., race, gender). Among the most common definitions is Demographic Parity (DP)
[
15
]
, which requires independence between the positive predicted label and the protected attribute; however, DP disregards the ground truth
(Y)
, often leading to undesirable incentives.

To address these drawbacks, label-conditional metrics such as
Equalized Odds Difference(EOD)
[
10
]
were introduced. EOD requires equal false-positive and true-positive rates across groups. Several algorithms have been proposed to enforce DP or EOD, including re-weighting
[
13
]
, adversarial debiasing
[
27
]
, and calibrated score adjustments
[
10
]
. However, most of these metrics and the algorithms share a core limitation: they evaluate only the distributions of outcomes, not the stability of explanations that justify them, and thus cannot mitigate bias arising from these explanations.

The fairness community has increasingly recognized that evaluating fairness across single protected attributes overlooks disparities affecting individuals who belong to multiple protected categories simultaneously (e.g., black women). This problem can lead to a phenomenon known as fairness gerrymandering, which occurs when models appear fair on each individual attribute but remain unfair for intersections
[
14
]
. Approaches to intersectional fairness include distributionally robust optimization
[
11
]
, probabilistic overlapping-group fairness
[
26
]
, and resampling
[
19
]
.

Beyond outcome-oriented fairness, recent research emphasizes that fairness should extend beyond model outcome to explain the procedures by which decisions are made
[
8
]
. Procedural fairness argues that fairness should be evaluated not only on the basis of outcomes but also on transparency, consistency, and the quality of reasoning behind the model decision
[
21
]
. In ML, this means to determine whether feature attributions, gradients, saliency maps, or surrogate models behave coherently across demographic groups.
Recent research has uncovered substantial procedural disparities in model explanation that models can satisfy Equalized Odds yet use qualitatively different reasoning for protected groups
[
28
,
6
]
.

To address inconsistencies in model reasoning across demographic groups, researchers have used post-hoc explanation algorithms that aim to explain how a trained model arrives at a given prediction

[
7
]
. However, a common challenge here
is explanation instability, where small input perturbations can produce substantially different explanations
[
22
]
.

From a model selection perspective, recent research has begun treating the context of model selection as a multi-objective optimization problem, with objectives including utility, outcome-oriented fairness, and procedural-oriented fairness
[
24
,
28
]
. However, previous work focuses mostly on two objectives, which are utility vs. fairness

[
25
]
.

III
The MESD Method

We proposed Multi-category Explanation Stability Disparity (MESD) as a procedural fairness metric that detects and mitigates procedural bias across multiple protected categories, making it, to the best of our knowledge, the first of its kind. MESD builds on earlier work on the definitions of explanation stability, robustness, and sensitivity
[
4
]
, while also incorporating novel concepts such as intersectional subgroups, label-aware aggregation, empirical-Bayes shrinkage, and distributionally robust CVaR-based measurement of worst-case disparities. The steps of MESD are explained as follows:

III-A

Intersectional Subgroups

In the real world, humans can belong to more than one protected category (e.g, race and gender)
[
19
]
. To capture this more detailed bias in our metric and to prevent fairness gerrymandering, MESD constructs multi-category protected groups via the Cartesian product of protected features. Let
\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{m}
represent
m
protected features. Let

\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{A}_{m}.

Each element
g\in\mathcal{G}
corresponds to a unique intersectional subgroup, and every individual is mapped to exactly one such subgroup via the protected attributes vector:

g(x)=(A_{1}(x),A_{2}(x),\ldots,A_{m}(x))

(1)

However, such decomposition introduces statistical challenges. As
(|\mathcal{G}|)
grows with the number of protected features, many intersectional subgroups may contain few samples, producing high-variance estimates of explanation stability.

III-B

Aggregation of Explainers

Let
\phi^{(e)}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the normalized attribution vector returned by explainer
(e\in\mathcal{E})
, where
(\mathcal{E})
is the set of chosen explainers (e.g., SHAP, LIME). Each attribution vector is normalized using signed
(L_{1})
-normalization, ensuring comparability across explainers despite differing scales or sparsity patterns.
We then define an ensemble attribution vector as a convex combination as proposed by
[
5
]
:

\phi_{\mathrm{ens}}(x)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}w_{e}{\phi}^{(e)}(x),

(2)

where
(w_{e}\geq 0)
and
(\sum_{e}w_{e}=1)
. The weights
(w_{e})
can be user-specified
or optimized to reflect the reliability of each explainer.
This serves as a stabilizing mechanism to address inconsistencies that may arise from an explainer
[
22
]
.
Prior work has shown that explanation aggregation can improve consistency and reduce adversarial vulnerability
[
5
]
.

III-C

Perturbation

To evaluate the robustness, stability, and consistency of an explanation, we generate several perturbed samples of the inputs to tests whether explanations generated by a model remain stable under small perturbations.
Given an instance
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we generate a set of perturbed variants
x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(K)}
.
We adopt a hybrid perturbation framework that combines Gaussian noise and stochastic feature masking. For Gaussian perturbations, we sample

x^{(k)}=x+\eta^{(k)},\qquad\eta^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})

The masking perturbation replaces randomly selected features with a baseline value
b_{j}
:

x^{(k)}_{j}=\begin{cases}x_{j},\text{with probability }1-p_{m},\\
b_{j},\text{with probability }p_{m},\end{cases}

where
p_{m}
is the masking probability and
b_{j}
is a baseline value (e.g., the feature mean).

III-D

Stability Evaluation

To quantify the stability of each instance, we used Euclidean distance to measure the distance between the explanations of the original and perturbed instances,
under the assumption that
the distance between the original and perturbed explanation should be insignificant.

Formally, consider an instance
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with ensemble explanation
(\phi_{\mathrm{ens}}(x))
defined in Equation
2
. We construct a local perturbation neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(x)
consisting of (K) perturbed variants of
x
, denoted
{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(K)}}
. Perturbations are generated with Gaussian perturbations and feature masking:

x^{(k)}=x+\eta^{(k)}-m^{(k)}\odot x+m^{(k)}\odot b

For each perturbed instance, a corresponding ensemble explanation is computed, then the
stability is quantified by measuring how similar the explanation of the original instance is to the explanations of its perturbed counterparts.
We then invert the instability to obtain a stability score.

S(x_{i})={\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}||\phi(x_{i})-\phi(x_{i}^{(k)})||_{2}}

(3)

A perfectly stable explanation yields
S(x)\approx 1
, while highly inconsistent explanations approach zero.

III-E

Label-Aware Group Aggregation

To eliminate the risk of misleading disparity estimates, we aggregate group stability using label-aware aggregation. That is, we explicitly condition stability on the true class label before performing any group-level comparisons. Formally, let the complete set of intersectional subgroups be denoted by
\mathcal{G}
,
and let
y\in\{0,1\}
denote the binary ground-truth label.
We first partition the dataset into disjoint label-conditional subsets:

\mathcal{D}_{g,y}={x:A(x)=g,;Y(x)=y},

where
A(x)
denotes the protected group membership of instance
x
. For each subgroup–label cell
(g,y)
, We compute the average stability:

S(g,y)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{g,y}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{g,y}}S(x),\quad\text{for all }g\in\mathcal{G},;y\in{0,1}.

We address noisiness that results from such aggregation via empirical-Bayes shrinkage, pulling unstable small-sample estimates toward the label-wise mean:

\tilde{S}(g,y)=\alpha_{g,y}S(g,y)+(1-\alpha_{g,y})\bar{S}(y)

where

\alpha_{g,y}=\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{g,y}|}{|\mathcal{D}_{g,y}|+\lambda},\quad\bar{S}(y)\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{y}|}\sum_{x:Y(x)=y}S(x),

(4)

and
(\lambda>0)
controls the degree of shrinkage.

Finally, we aggregate these label-conditioned scores to produce a single group-level measure:

S(g)=\sum_{y\in{0,1}}w_{y},\tilde{S}(g,y),

(5)

where

w_{y}=\frac{|\mathcal{D}_{y}|}{|\mathcal{D}|}

are label prevalence weights, ensuring that each label influences group-level stability in proportion to its frequency.

III-F

CVaR-Weighted Group Disparity (MESD)

To extract the final MESD metric from the group-level stability
S(g)
, we use Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), which
gives greater weight to the worst-performing group-level disparities. This reveals subgroups that may exhibit disproportionately low explanation stability.
Formally, for any pair of intersectional groups
(g_{i},g_{j}\in\mathcal{G})
, define the pairwise stability disparity as

D_{ij}=\left|S(g_{i})-S(g_{j})\right|,

where
(S(g))
is the aggregated group-level stability defined in Equation
5
. To incorporate the severity of instability, we define a risk score for each pair:

R_{ij}=1-\min{S(g_{i}),S(g_{j})},

so that pairs involving groups with low procedural fairness receive higher risk values. The collection of risks
{R_{ij}}
induces a distribution from which MESD computes the
(\alpha)
-tail risk threshold

\tau_{\alpha}=\mathrm{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}R_{ij},

identifying the set of “worst-case” disparities. CVaR then assigns weights only to disparities whose risk exceeds this threshold:

w_{ij}=\frac{\{\max{R_{ij}-\tau_{\alpha},0\}}}{\sum_{k,\ell}\{\max{R_{k\ell}-\tau_{\alpha},0}\}+\varepsilon}.

The CVaR-weighted MESD measure is therefore given by:

\mathrm{MESD}_{\mathrm{CVaR}}(\alpha)=\sum_{i<j}w_{ij},D_{ij},

(6)

which summarizes the severity of tail disparities under explanation perturbations.

IV
Optimizing Fairness and Explainability

To select a fair model with high utility and high explainability, we propose a multi-objective optimization framework, Utility, Explainability, and Fairness (UEF), that optimizes these three objectives using Non-Dominating Sorting Genetic Algorithm (NSGA-II). The goal of our UEF is to achieve a better trade-off between these objectives. In our modeling, each individual in the population encodes a specific model configuration.

Let
\theta
denote the decision variables. We define three objectives
F_{\text{perf}}(\theta),F_{\text{out}}(\theta),F_{\text{proc}}(\theta)
, which represent performance (utility), outcome-oriented fairness, and procedural-oriented fairness, respectively.
The multi-objective optimization problem is stated as follows:

\min_{\mathbf{\theta}\in{\Theta}}\big(F_{\text{perf}}(\theta),;F_{\text{out}}(\theta),;F_{\text{proc}}(\theta)\big)

(7)

where
\Theta
is the space of all valid model configurations.

The first objective measures the predictive utility of the model. Let
(f_{\theta}(x)\in[0,1])
denote the predicted probability of the positive class. Then

F_{\text{perf}}(\theta)=-\mathrm{AUC}(f_{\theta};\mathcal{D}),

where AUC denotes the area under the ROC curve evaluated on a validation set. The negative sign ensures that all objectives are minimized.

The second objective captures traditional outcome-oriented fairness, we selected Demographic Parity (DP), which requires that the positive prediction of the model to be invariant across protected groups. For multiple protected categories, demographic parity is defined as:

P(\hat{Y}=1\mid A=a)=P(\hat{Y}=1\mid A=a^{\prime})\quad\forall a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}.

In practice, we quantify deviations from this ideal using the maximum pairwise disparity:

F_{\mathrm{out}}(\theta)\max_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\big|P(\hat{Y}=1\mid A=a)-P(\hat{Y}=1\mid A=a^{\prime})\big|.

This formulation naturally generalizes binary demographic parity to intersectional settings.

The third objective,
F_{proc}(\theta)
, is our MESD procedural metric defined in Section
III
. The aim of MESD is to quantify the difference in explanation disparity between intersectional groups within protected attributes.

To solve the above multi-objective problem, we used NSGA-II.
In our optimization setup, each population represents a model configuration
\theta
as defined earlier. We begin by initializing a population
P_{0}
of
N
random solutions, sampling each hyperparameter from its specified range. We evaluate every solution
\theta
in the population on all the objectives, which means we use the hyperparameter set returned by the objective to train a model, and we evaluate the performance of the model using AUC, DP, and MESD on a test dataset.

The Pareto front returned by NSGA-II comprises several model configurations.
To select a single model for final deployment, we adopt Chebyshev scalarization as a post-optimization decision rule. Chebyshev scalarization minimizes the maximum deviation from an ideal point.

Let
\mathbf{F}(\theta)=(F_{\text{perf}}(\theta),F_{\text{out}}(\theta),F_{\text{proc}}(\theta))
denote the vector of objectives
Let
(\mathbf{z}^{\star}=(z_{\text{perf}}^{\star},z_{\text{out}}^{\star},z_{\text{proc}}^{\star}))
be an ideal point. Chebyshev scalarization selects the solution

\theta^{\star}=\underset{\theta\in\mathcal{P}}{\text{argmin}}\max_{k\in\{\text{perf,out,proc}\}}\lambda_{k},\lvert F_{k}(\theta)-z_{k}^{\star}\rvert,

where
(\lambda_{k}>0)
are optional scaling coefficients used to normalize objectives or encode relative importance.

V
Experimental Evaluation

In this section, we report several experiments to test the effectiveness of MESD and UEF. In particular, we seek to answer the following questions:

1.

How effectively can UEF mitigate both outcome-oriented and procedural-oriented bias in comparison to other baselines?

2.

How does CVaR-weighted MESD improve the detection and prioritization of worst-case procedural fairness violations among underrepresented or high-risk groups?

V-A

Experimental Settings

We used three real-world datasets to evaluate our proposed framework: German Credit, Recidivism, and Adult Income, all of which are state-of-the-art for measuring algorithmic bias. Both German credit and Recidivism have two protected attributes each, while Adult income has three.

Because no prior work has explored explanation fairness from a multiple protected categories perspective, we selected three baseline fair ML methods: Adversarial Learning (ADL)
[
27
]
, Reduction (RD)
[
1
]
, and Reweighting (RW)
[
13
]
. It is worth noting that ADL uses a neural network (NN), while RD and RW use logistic regression as their base model.

We measured model performance from three variants of metrics: (1) model utility (AUC and F1), (2) outcome-oriented fairness (
\Delta_{DP}
and
\Delta_{EOD}
), and (3) procedural-oriented fairness (MESD in Equation
6
).

For MESD, we used 200 samples to calculate the group stability. We used a neural network with two hidden layers as the underlying model for UEF, which uses five hyperparameters: threshold, L2 regularizer, learning rate, epoch, and dropout.

V-B

Performance Comparison

To test our first question, we evaluate UEF and other fairness models across the three datasets and report results for utility, procedural, and outcome-oriented fairness. The results of our experiments are summarized in Table

First, UEF outperformed other baseline algorithms on two out of the three datasets with respect to utility (AUC and F1). On Recidivism, UEF still yields competitive results, indicating that it can balance multiple objectives without sacrificing any of them. The results of UEF further demonstrate that it is possible to improve both outcome-oriented and procedural fairness without sacrificing utility, which supports the findings of several studies in the literature that tackle the accuracy-fairness trade-off
[
25

. Also, ADL, which used a neural network, shows superior utility performance compared to RW and RD, both of which use logistic regression.

Second, on outcome-oriented fairness (EOD and DP), UEF yielded superior performance compared to other baselines. However, in the Adult Income dataset, EOD seems a bit high across all the algorithms because the dataset contains three protected categories,
which leads to some intersectional groups containing very small samples.
This resulted in skewed EOD results in less sampled groups.
DP produces very low results across all three datasets because it focuses only on positive predictions, which can sometimes distort the original distribution of the dataset.

Third, UEF outperformed the baselines in MESD on all datasets.
This shows that UEF can effectively handle a multi-objective problem without sacrificing any objective. The addition of UEF to a model selection process should be complementary, since a model may yield a better MESD but a worse EOD, as we saw in the Adult Income dataset.

Evaluating CVaR on MESD

To calculate the final MESD metric, several approaches can be used, such as calculating the variance of each group’s stability, the difference between the smallest and largest group stabilities, or the CVaR-weighted MESD. We used CVaR-weighted adjustment to align MESD sensitivity with the worst group, so that MESD would not seem fair on the surface, but one group still suffers from extreme procedural bias.

The instability arises from the small sample size of the group, which ultimately increases the variance of the group stability. VI
Discussion

From Table
I
, we see that UEF outperforms other baselines across all objectives on two of the three datasets, and on the third, it produces competitive results that do not sacrifice accuracy for fairness or explainability. This arises from the multi-objective optimization approach, which has been shown to balance exploration and exploitation very well during optimization
[
24
]
.

UEF underperformed on the Recidivism dataset because of a high level of bias that can lead to premature convergence in optimization. On the other hand, UEF on Adult Income and German show that the optimization is not needing to trade one objective for another.
Across all the algorithms, however, we can see several instances where all algorithms produced a lower DP with higher EOD and vice versa. This happened for two reasons. First, DP focuses only on the positive class, which means it cannot balance the true positive and false positive rates across intersectional groups. This can distort the ground-truth distribution in the data. Second, there is often an inherent trade-off across multiple fairness metrics, which can make it impossible to optimize these metrics simultaneously.
[
23
]
.

Also, In Figure
1
, we show how each algorithm is comparable to the others with respect to how much of their solutions are non-dominated on the Adult Income dataset. The Figure also shows the superiority of UEF across the objectives, as most of the UEF solutions are non-dominated.

The results obtained in Figure
1
will be slightly different on the Recidivism dataset because the dataset has a small sample (around 1000), which is not favorable for our algorithm. Also, the amount of bias embedded in each dataset is different, and this can skew the outcome-oriented and procedural-oriented results for a method that does not explicitly include a bias-tackling method.

Finally, Table
II
shows that there can be cases where the group stability of one group is substantially lower than that of other groups. This can happen due to various reasons, such as the distribution of favorable and unfavorable classes in that particular group, or the number of samples in that particular intersectional group can be very small.
This means that special attention needs to be paid to the worst-case tail group to prevent fairness gerrymandering, which can make an algorithm seemingly procedurally fair on the surface, but internally, some of the groups suffer from instability in reasoning.
Lastly, we see several models on Adult Income that yield lower MESD but higher EOD or DP, which validates the motivation behind MESD: to serve as a complementary role to outcome-oriented fairness metrics rather than replace them.

VII
Conclusion and Future Work

We proposed MESD, which is a procedural fairness metric for intersectional subgroups. MESD was designed to measure the quality of explanation generated for each intersectional subgroup.
We incorporated CVaR to MESD to make it robust to the worst group to prevent fairness gerrymandering. To holistically optimize this new metric alongside outcome-oriented metrics and utility, we use multi-objective optimization that optimizes three objectives, utility, outcome-oriented fairness, and procedural-oriented fairness, in order to generate a final model that balances the trade-offs between three objectives effectively. The results show that our optimization framework outperforms other baselines, and the inclusion of CVaR in MESD is an important addition.

In this work, we approached the problem of detecting procedural bias from a pre-processing perspective, i.e., we focus on the input to the model, not the model itself. In the future, we plan to shift to an in-processing explainability method, such as layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP), which can explain how the model makes decisions for each intersectional subgroup.
Additional future work will be to include more procedural metrics in the multi-objective optimization and move it to many-objective optimization.
