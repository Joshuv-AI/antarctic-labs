The Extended Real Line with Reentry: Separating US from KC in the Clontz Hierarchy
Damián Rafael Lattenero

Abstract
We construct the Extended Real Line with Reentry (ERI), a quotient of ℝ ¯ = [ − ∞ , + ∞ ] \overline{\mathbb{R}}=[-\infty,+\infty] that is compact, path-connected, sober, US (uniquely sequential), and not KC (kompacts closed)—the first compact path-connected space known to separate US from KC (see Remark 1.3 for a systematic verification against the pi-Base database [ 17 ] ). In the Clontz hierarchy [ 2 ] , ERI is k 2 k_{2} -Hausdorff but not weakly Hausdorff. The construction generalizes to a Filter-Modified Quotient (FMQ) framework that produces US-not-KC spaces from any compact Hausdorff base without isolated points. A modifier spectrum identifies an optimal “US zone,” and an extended rigidity analysis shows that the hierarchy level k 2 ​ H k_{2}\mathrm{H} -not- wH \mathrm{wH} is preserved under arbitrary closed nowhere-dense collapse sets, iterated FMQ, and products—with first-countability of the base as the precise frontier for potential new levels.

Introduction

1.1
The Wilansky hierarchy

The study of separation axioms strictly between
T_{1}
and
T_{2}

(Hausdorff) was initiated by Wilansky
[
1
]
, who introduced
the following properties for a topological space

:

Definition 1.1

(Wilansky
[
1
]
)
.

(i)

X
is a
US-space
(uniquely sequential) if every convergent
sequence in
X
has a unique limit.

(ii)

X
is a
KC-space
(kompacts closed) if every compact subset
of
X
is closed.

The implications

T_{2}\;\Longrightarrow\;\mathrm{KC}\;\Longrightarrow\;\mathrm{US}\;\Longrightarrow\;T_{1}

(1)

are all strict
[
1
]
; see also
[
16
]
.
A refined hierarchy with additional intermediate axioms has been
developed by Clontz
[
2
]
:

T_{2}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{k_{1}H}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{KC}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{wH}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{k_{2}H}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{US}\;\Rightarrow\;T_{1},

(2)

where a space is
weakly Hausdorff
(wH) if every continuous image
of a compact Hausdorff space is closed, and

k_{2}
-Hausdorff
(
k_{2}
H) if for every continuous

f\colon K\to X
with
K
compact Hausdorff, the kernel

\{(a,b)\in K\times K:f(a)=f(b)\}
is closed in
K\times K

[
12
]

(see Section
7
for the full treatment).
The role of first-countability is clarified by the classical equivalence:

Fact 1.2

(Folklore; see e.g.
[
5
, p. 60]
)
.

A first-countable space is Hausdorff if and only if it is US.

Thus in first-countable spaces, the four levels of (
1
)
collapse to two (
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
), and only in the absence of
first-countability can US and
T_{2}
genuinely separate.

2
The ERI Construction

2.1
Definition and notation

Definition 2.1

(ERI space)
.

Let
\overline{\mathbb{R}}=[-\infty,+\infty]
denote the extended real
line with its standard order topology. Define the equivalence relation

\sim
on
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
whose sole non-singleton class is

\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
, and let

X_{0}:=\overline{\mathbb{R}}/{\sim}

be the quotient set. Write
q:\overline{\mathbb{R}}\to X_{0}
for the
projection,
\ast:=q(-\infty)=q(0)=q(+\infty)
for the collapsed
point, and
\hat{x}:=q(x)
for all

x\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
The map
x\mapsto\hat{x}
is a bijection from

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
onto
X_{0}\setminus\{\ast\}
.

The
ERI topology
on
X_{0}
is

\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}\;:=\;\bigl\{\,U\subseteq X_{0}:\text{(a) }q^{-1}(U)\text{ is open in }\overline{\mathbb{R}},\;\text{and (b) }\ast\in U\Rightarrow\overline{q^{-1}(U)}=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\,\bigr\}.

We write
X:=(X_{0},\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}})
.

Remark 2.2

(Intuitive picture)
.

Think of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
as a closed interval with endpoints

-\infty
and
+\infty
and midpoint
0
.
ERI is obtained by gluing these three points into a single
point
\ast
, creating a “figure-eight” topology where the two
loops share the vertex
\ast
.
The density condition means that a neighborhood of
\ast
must be
“almost all” of this figure-eight: the complement of a
neighborhood (relative to the figure-eight) must be a closed
nowhere-dense set—such as a Cantor set, a convergent sequence
with its limit, or finitely many points—but never a set with
interior (e.g., an interval).

Remark 2.3

(Standing notation)
.

Throughout this paper:

\overline{\mathbb{R}}=[-\infty,+\infty]
denotes the extended real
line (compact, Hausdorff, homeomorphic to
[-1,1]
);

\mathbb{R}=(-\infty,+\infty)
the usual real line.
A subset
D
of a topological space
Y
is
dense
if

\overline{D}=Y
.
A set
A
is
nowhere dense
if

\mathrm{int}(\overline{A})=\varnothing
;
for closed
A
this reduces to
\mathrm{int}(A)=\varnothing
.

Convention 2.4

(Closure convention)
.

Throughout this paper, when we say that
F\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

is
closed
, we mean closed in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
Since
F
avoids
\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
, this is equivalent to

F
being a closed and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}
that does not
contain
0
, i.e., a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

2.2
Preliminary facts

We collect standard facts used throughout.

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
has no isolated points (every basic open set is
an infinite interval), so for any finite
A\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}}
,

\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus A
is dense.
A subset
S\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is
saturated

if
q^{-1}(q(S))=S
, equivalently

S\cap\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}\in\{\varnothing,\;\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}\}
.
Every subset of
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and its complement in

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
are both saturated (immediate from

\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
being the unique non-singleton equivalence class).

Lemma 2.5

(Dense meets open)
.

In any topological space, if
D
is dense and
V
is non-empty and open,
then
D\cap V\neq\varnothing

(
[
10
]
, 1.3.5)
.
Finite intersections of dense open sets are dense; the Baire Category
Theorem extends this to countable intersections in compact Hausdorff
spaces
(
[
7
]
, §48)
.

2.3
Topology axioms and basic properties

Proposition 2.6

(
\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
is a topology)
.

The collection
\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
satisfies the topology axioms.

Proof.

(i)

q^{-1}(\varnothing)=\varnothing
is open; condition (b) is
vacuous.

q^{-1}(X_{0})=\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is open and dense.

(ii) Arbitrary unions.

If
\{U_{\alpha}\}\subseteq\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
and

U=\bigcup_{\alpha}U_{\alpha}
, then
q^{-1}(U)
is an open union.
If
\ast\in U
, some
q^{-1}(U_{\beta})
is dense, and

q^{-1}(U_{\beta})\subseteq q^{-1}(U)
.

(iii) Finite intersections.

If
G_{1},G_{2}\in\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
, then

q^{-1}(G_{1}\cap G_{2})=q^{-1}(G_{1})\cap q^{-1}(G_{2})
is open.
If
\ast\in G_{1}\cap G_{2}
, both preimages are dense and open;
their intersection is dense (Lemma
2.5
).
∎

Proposition 2.7

(Continuity)
.

q:\overline{\mathbb{R}}\to(X,\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}})
is continuous.

Proof.

Condition (a) states
q^{-1}(U)
is open for every

U\in\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
.
∎

Proposition 2.8

(Strict inclusion)
.

\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}\subsetneq\tau_{q}
.
The standard quotient
(X_{0},\tau_{q})
is Hausdorff.

Proof.

The inclusion
\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}\subseteq\tau_{q}
is immediate.
For strictness, given
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, set

\varepsilon:=|x|/2
and

V_{\ast}:=q([-\infty,x-\varepsilon)\cup(x+\varepsilon,+\infty])
.
Since
\varepsilon<|x|
, the interval

[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon]
does not contain
0
, so

\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}\subseteq q^{-1}(V_{\ast})

and
\ast\in V_{\ast}
.
The preimage
q^{-1}(V_{\ast})
is open, so
V_{\ast}\in\tau_{q}
.
However, the complement

[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon]
has nonempty interior

(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)
, so
q^{-1}(V_{\ast})
is not dense
and condition (b) fails:
V_{\ast}\notin\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
.
The Hausdorff property of
\tau_{q}
follows by explicit

\varepsilon
-separation: given distinct
\hat{a},\hat{b}\in X_{0}
,
their preimages under
q
are singletons or
\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
,
all of which can be separated by disjoint open intervals in

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
∎

2.4
Neighborhoods of
\ast

Definition 2.9

(The family
\mathcal{W}
)
.

For any closed nowhere-dense
F\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
define
W_{F}:=q(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus F)
.

Lemma 2.10

(Canonical neighborhoods)
.

(i)

If
U\in\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
and
\ast\in U
, then

F_{U}:=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus q^{-1}(U)
is a closed
nowhere-dense subset of
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, and
U=W_{F_{U}}
.

(ii)

For every closed nowhere-dense
F\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,

W_{F}
is an open neighborhood of
\ast
in
\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
, with

q^{-1}(W_{F})=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus F
.

Proof.

(i)
.
Set
V:=q^{-1}(U)
. Conditions (a) and (b) give
V
open and dense.
Since
\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}\subseteq V
, we get

F_{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then
F_{U}
is closed (
V
is open) with
\mathrm{int}(F_{U})=\varnothing

(
V
is dense), hence nowhere dense.
Since
F_{U}\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,

\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus F_{U}
is saturated
(saturation), giving
U=W_{F_{U}}
.

(ii)
.
Since
F\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, the set

\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus F
contains
\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}

and is therefore saturated, so

q^{-1}(W_{F})=q^{-1}(q(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus F))=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus F
.
This set is open (
F
closed) and dense (
F
nowhere dense),
so both conditions hold;
\ast\in W_{F}
.
∎

2.5
The key intersection principle

Proposition 2.11

(Key intersection principle (KIP))
.

If
U\in\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
with
\ast\in U
and

V\in\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
with
V\neq\varnothing
, then

U\cap V\neq\varnothing
.
In particular, every open neighborhood of
\ast
is dense in
X
.

Proof.

q^{-1}(U)
is dense (condition (b)) and
q^{-1}(V)
is non-empty open
(condition (a)).
By Lemma
2.5
,
q^{-1}(U)\cap q^{-1}(V)\neq\varnothing
;
hence
U\cap V\neq\varnothing
.
∎

Corollary 2.12
.

\ast\in\overline{O}
for every non-empty open

O\subseteq X
.

Proof.

Every open neighborhood
W
of
\ast
satisfies

W\cap O\neq\varnothing
by the KIP.
∎

3
Separation and Topological Properties

Proposition 3.1

(
X
is
T_{1}
)
.

Every singleton in
X
is closed.

Proof.

Case
y=\ast
:

q^{-1}(X\setminus\{\ast\})=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
is open
(finite sets are closed in the Hausdorff
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
).
Since
\ast\notin X\setminus\{\ast\}
, condition (b) is vacuous.

Case
y=\hat{x}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
:

q^{-1}(X\setminus\{\hat{x}\})=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{x\}

is open and dense (no isolated points).
Since
\ast\in X\setminus\{\hat{x}\}
, condition (b) is satisfied.
∎

Theorem 3.2

(Failure of Hausdorff)
.

(X,\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}})
is not Hausdorff:
\ast
cannot be separated
from any other point by disjoint open sets.

Proof.

Let
U,V\in\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
with
\ast\in U
and

\hat{x}\in V
.
Since
V\neq\varnothing
, the KIP
(Proposition
2.11
) gives
U\cap V\neq\varnothing
.
∎

Proposition 3.3

(Compactness)
.

X
is compact.

Proof.

(X_{0},\tau_{q})
is compact (continuous image of compact

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
) and
\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}\subseteq\tau_{q}
.
∎

Proposition 3.4

(Connectedness)
.

X
is connected.

Proof.

If
U,V
partition
X
with
\ast\in U
, then
V
is non-empty open and
the KIP gives
U\cap V\neq\varnothing
, a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 3.5

(Path-connectedness)
.

X
is path-connected.

Proof.

If
\hat{a}=\hat{b}
, the constant path suffices.
Otherwise, choose representatives

a,b\in\mathbb{R}
(when
\hat{a}=\ast
or
\hat{b}=\ast
,
we choose the representative
0\in\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
;
since
0\in\mathbb{R}
, the linear path below is well-defined).
The linear path
\tilde{\gamma}(t)=(1-t)a+tb
lies in

\mathbb{R}\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and is continuous.
Since
q\colon\overline{\mathbb{R}}\to X
is continuous
(Proposition
2.7
), the composition

\gamma:=q\circ\tilde{\gamma}\colon[0,1]\to X
is continuous.
We verify the endpoints:

\gamma(0)=q(a)=\hat{a}
and
\gamma(1)=q(b)=\hat{b}

(with the convention
q(0)=\ast
).
If
a
and
b
have the same sign, then
(1-t)a+tb
has the same
sign for all
t\in[0,1]
, so
\tilde{\gamma}
avoids
0
and
\gamma

avoids
\ast
.
If
a
and
b
have opposite signs, the path

\tilde{\gamma}
passes through
0
, so
\gamma
passes
through
\ast
; this is harmless since
q
is continuous.
∎

Remark 3.6

(Submetrizability)
.

ERI admits no coarser Hausdorff topology; in particular, it is

not
submetrizable.
Indeed, if
\tau^{\prime}\subseteq\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
is any
T_{2}

topology on
X_{0}
, then for every pair of distinct points there
exist disjoint
\tau^{\prime}
-open sets separating them; since

\tau^{\prime}\subseteq\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
, these sets are also

\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
-open, so
\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
would
separate every pair of distinct points—contradicting the
failure of
T_{2}
(Theorem
3.2
).
Since every metrizable topology is Hausdorff, no metrizable

\tau_{m}\subseteq\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
can exist.
The unique obstruction to metrizability is
\ast
: the subspace

X\setminus\{\ast\}\cong\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is metrizable.

Proposition 3.7

(Small inductive dimension)
.

\mathrm{ind}(X)=1
.

Proof.

Upper bound.

We verify
\mathrm{ind}(x,X)\leq 1
at each point.

For
\hat{x}\neq\ast
: the subspace

X\setminus\{\ast\}\cong\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is
metrizable with
\mathrm{ind}=1
; basic neighborhoods are open
intervals whose boundaries are finite sets (hence
zero-dimensional).

For
\ast
: a basic neighborhood is

W_{F}=q(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus F)
for closed
nowhere-dense
F\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

(Lemma
2.10
).
We claim
\partial(W_{F})=q(F)
and
\mathrm{ind}(q(F))=0
.
Since
W_{F}
is an open neighborhood of
\ast
, it is dense in
X

(Proposition
2.11
), so

\overline{W_{F}}=X
and

\partial(W_{F})=\overline{W_{F}}\setminus W_{F}=X\setminus W_{F}=q(F)
.
Now
q|_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}
is a homeomorphism, so

q(F)\cong F
.
The set
F
, being a closed subset of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with
empty interior, is totally disconnected: any connected subset
of
\mathbb{R}
with more than one point contains an interval,
which would give
F
nonempty interior.
Since
F
is also compact (closed in
compact
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
), it is zero-dimensional:

\mathrm{ind}(F)=0

(
[
10
]
, Theorem 1.3.4: compact totally disconnected
metrizable spaces are zero-dimensional).
Hence
\mathrm{ind}(\partial(W_{F}))=0
, confirming

\mathrm{ind}(\ast,X)\leq 1
.

Lower bound.

For any interval
(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,

q((a,b))\cong(a,b)
is a subspace of
X
with

\mathrm{ind}((a,b))=1
.
Since
\mathrm{ind}
is monotone
(
\mathrm{ind}(A)\leq\mathrm{ind}(X)
for every
subspace
A

[
10
]
), we obtain

\mathrm{ind}(X)\geq 1
.
∎

Remark 3.8

(Dimension functions)
.

For metrizable spaces the three classical dimension functions
coincide:
\mathrm{ind}=\mathrm{Ind}=\dim

(
[
10
]
, Theorem 1.7.7).
Since ERI is not metrizable (Remark
3.6
),

\mathrm{Ind}(X)
and
\dim(X)
may differ from

\mathrm{ind}(X)=1
.
The covering dimension satisfies
\dim(X)\geq 1
: a space with

\dim(X)=0
is totally disconnected
(
[

]
, Theorem 1.6.4), but ERI is connected with

.
The exact value of

in the non-metrizable, non-normal
setting of ERI remains open.

Proposition 3.9

(Baire property)
.

(X,\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}})
is a Baire space: the intersection of
countably many dense open sets is dense.

Proof.

Let
\{U_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a countable family of dense open subsets
of
X
.
We show each
q^{-1}(U_{n})
is open and dense in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
If
\ast\in U_{n}
, density follows from condition (b).
If
\ast\notin U_{n}
, then
q^{-1}(U_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is open.
Since
q|_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}
is a homeomorphism and

U_{n}
is dense in
X
(by hypothesis),
q^{-1}(U_{n})
is dense in

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, hence in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}

(as
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is itself dense
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
).
Since
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is compact Hausdorff, it is a Baire
space (
[
7
]
, §48), so

D:=\bigcap_{n}q^{-1}(U_{n})
is dense
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
For any nonempty open
V\subseteq X
,
q^{-1}(V)
is nonempty
open, so
D\cap q^{-1}(V)\neq\varnothing
and

\bigcap_{n}U_{n}
is dense in
X
.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

Compact spaces need not be Baire without Hausdorff separation;
ERI’s Baire property is inherited from
\overline{\mathbb{R}}

via the density condition, which ensures dense open subsets of
X

lift to dense open subsets of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
The same argument gives
\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})

Baire whenever
Y
is compact Hausdorff without isolated points.

4
Countability and Sequential Properties

4.1
First countability fails at

\ast

Proposition 4.1

(Not first-countable at
\ast
)
.

\ast
has no countable neighborhood base in
X
.
In particular,
X
is neither first-countable nor second-countable.

Proof.

Suppose
\{U_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is a countable neighborhood base at

\ast
.
By Lemma
2.10
(i)
,
U_{n}=W_{F_{n}}
for
closed nowhere-dense
F_{n}
, and

V_{n}:=q^{-1}(U_{n})=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus F_{n}
is dense
and open.

Since
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is compact Hausdorff, it is a Baire space
(
[
7
]
, §48).
Hence
D:=\bigcap_{n\geq 1}V_{n}
is dense.
Pick
t\in D\cap(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
(using that

(-1,0)\cup(0,1)
is non-empty open).
Then
W_{\{t\}}
is an open neighborhood of
\ast

(Lemma
2.10
(ii)
), so some

U_{n_{0}}\subseteq W_{\{t\}}
, giving

q^{-1}(U_{n_{0}})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{t\}
.
But
t\in D\subseteq V_{n_{0}}=q^{-1}(U_{n_{0}})
, a contradiction.
∎

Remark 4.2

(Character at
\ast
)
.

The neighborhoods of
\ast
are parametrized by closed nowhere-dense
subsets
F\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

(Lemma
2.10
).
The family of such sets has cardinality
\mathfrak{c}

(the continuum), so the character

\chi(\ast,X)\leq\mathfrak{c}
.
Since the character is uncountable
(Proposition
4.1
), we have

\aleph_{1}\leq\chi(\ast,X)\leq\mathfrak{c}
.
Under CH,
\chi(\ast,X)=\mathfrak{c}
.
At all other points, the character is
\aleph_{0}

(Corollary
5.6
).

Remark 4.3

(Topological weight)
.

The weight of
X
equals
\mathfrak{c}
.
The subspace
X\setminus\{\ast\}\cong\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is
second-countable (weight
\aleph_{0}
), so the weight of
X
is determined
by the neighborhoods of
\ast
: a base for these is

\{W_{F}:F\text{ closed nowhere dense in }\overline{\mathbb{R}}\}
,
which has cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
, and no smaller family suffices
(Proposition
4.1
).

Remark 4.4

(Separability)
.

ERI is separable:
\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
(identified via
q
) is a
countable dense subset of
X
, since
X
is the continuous image
of the second-countable space
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proposition 4.5

(Cardinal invariants)
.

c(X)=\aleph_{0}

(
cellularity
)
and

s(X)=\aleph_{0}

(
spread
)
.

Proof.

Every family of pairwise disjoint nonempty open subsets of
X

is countable, since each member meets the countable dense subset

q(\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\})
(Remark
4.4
).
Every discrete subspace of
X
avoids
\ast
(whose every
neighborhood is dense and hence meets any nonempty open set), so it
lies in
X\setminus\{\ast\}\cong\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
where discrete subspaces are countable (separable metrizable).
∎

Remark 4.6

(Local compactness)
.

ERI is locally compact in both the weak and strong senses.
At
\hat{x}\neq\ast
, compact neighborhoods are inherited from

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
At
\ast
: each basic neighborhood
W_{F}
satisfies

\overline{W_{F}}=X
(since
W_{F}
is dense by the KIP), and
X
is
compact, so
\overline{W_{F}}
is a compact neighborhood of
\ast
.
Thus
\ast
has a local base of neighborhoods with compact closure.

Proposition 4.7

(Local connectedness)
.

(X,\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}})
is locally connected.

Proof.

At points
\hat{x}\neq\ast
, basic neighborhoods are open intervals,
which are connected.
At
\ast
: every
W_{F}
is connected.
Indeed, if
W_{F}=U\cup V
with
U,V
disjoint and open in the
subspace
W_{F}
, and
\ast\in U
, then both
U
and
V
are open
in
W_{F}
, hence open in
X
(since
W_{F}
is open in
X
).
In particular,
U
is an open neighborhood of
\ast
in
X
and
V

is a nonempty open subset of
X
.
The KIP (Proposition
2.11
) gives
U\cap V\neq\varnothing
,
a contradiction.
∎

Remark 4.8

(Semiregularity)
.

ERI is
not
semiregular.
A set
U
is
regular open
if

U=\mathrm{int}(\overline{U})
.
For every nonempty
U\in\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
with
\ast\in U
,

U
is dense (Proposition
2.11
), so

\overline{U}=X
and
\mathrm{int}(\overline{U})=X
.
Hence the only regular open set containing
\ast
is
X
itself,
and the regular open sets do not form a base at
\ast
.
This is consistent with the spectral collapse
(Remark
9.28
): the semiregularization topology at

\ast
is trivial.

Remark 4.9

(Normality of subspaces)
.

X
is not normal (Corollary
8.12
), but every
subspace not containing
\ast
is normal: the subspace

X\setminus\{\ast\}\cong\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is
metrizable, hence normal, and normality is hereditary to
closed subspaces.
Thus
\ast
is the unique “obstruction” to normality.

Remark 4.10

(Paracompactness)
.

Every compact space is paracompact (every open cover has a finite,
hence locally finite, subcover).
Thus ERI is
T_{1}
, paracompact, and not normal—showing that
the hypothesis of regularity in Dieudonné’s theorem
(paracompact
+
regular
+

T_{1}

\Rightarrow
normal) cannot
be dropped.

4.2
Sequential convergence

Throughout this subsection,
(x_{n})
denotes a sequence in

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
\hat{x}_{n}:=q(x_{n})\in X
.

Proposition 4.11

(Convergence criterion)
.

\hat{x}_{n}\to\ast
in
X
if and only if

(x_{n})
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
).

Suppose
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is an accumulation point.
A subsequence satisfies
x_{n_{k}}\to t
.
Let
C:=\{x_{n_{k}}:k\geq 1\}\cup\{t\}
.
Since
x_{n_{k}}\to t
, the only accumulation point of
C
in

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is
t
, and
t\in C
, so
C
is closed.

C
is countable, hence nowhere dense
(
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\cong[-1,1]
, so every non-empty open set is
uncountable).
Since
C\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,

W_{C}=q(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus C)\in\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}

contains
\ast
(Lemma
2.10
(ii)
).
But
\hat{x}_{n_{k}}\notin W_{C}
for all
k
.
If
\hat{x}_{n}\to\ast
, then
(\hat{x}_{n})
would be eventually in

W_{C}
, forcing
\hat{x}_{n_{k}}\in W_{C}
for large
k
—a
contradiction.

(
\Leftarrow
).

Let
U
be an open neighborhood of
\ast
.
By Lemma
2.10
(i)
,

F:=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus q^{-1}(U)
is a compact subset of

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
(closed in the compact space
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
).
If infinitely many
x_{n}
belonged to
F
, the
Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem yields a convergent subsequence whose limit
lies in
F
(since
F
is closed in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
),
hence in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, contradicting the hypothesis.
Hence eventually
\hat{x}_{n}\in U
.
∎

Remark 4.12

(Equivalent formulations)
.

The condition “
(x_{n})
has no accumulation point in

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
” is equivalent to:

(a)

every accumulation point of
(x_{n})
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}

belongs to
\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
;

(b)

(x_{n})
eventually leaves every compact subset of

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

In particular, the sequence need not converge in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
:
different subsequences may tend to different elements of

\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
.

4.3
Uniqueness of sequential limits

Proposition 4.13

(Unique sequential limits)
.

Limits of convergent sequences in
X
are unique.

Proof.

Let
(y_{n})
be a sequence in
X
converging to
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
with

L_{1}\neq L_{2}
.

Reduction to sequences in
X\setminus\{\ast\}
.

If
y_{n}=\ast
for infinitely many
n
, then every open
U\ni L

must contain
\ast
for any limit
L
.
Since
\{\ast\}
is closed (Proposition
3.1
),

X\setminus\{\ast\}
is an open set containing
L
but not
\ast

whenever
L\neq\ast
—a contradiction.
Hence
L=\ast
is the only possible limit.
If
y_{n}=\ast
for only finitely many
n
, the tail lies in

X\setminus\{\ast\}
.
In either case we reduce to
\hat{x}_{n}
with

x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Case 1:
\hat{x}_{n}\to\ast
and
\hat{x}_{n}\to\hat{c}
,

c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
For small
\varepsilon>0
,

q((c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon))
is an open neighborhood of

\hat{c}
not containing
\ast
.
Convergence to
\hat{c}
forces
x_{n}
eventually into

(c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon)
, making
c
an accumulation point
in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
—contradicting
Proposition
4.11
.

Case 2:
\hat{x}_{n}\to\hat{c}
and
\hat{x}_{n}\to\hat{d}
,

c\neq d
both in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
Disjoint neighborhoods

q((c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon))
and

q((d-\varepsilon,d+\varepsilon))
cannot both eventually contain

\hat{x}_{n}
.
∎

4.4
Nets witness non-Hausdorff

Example 4.14

(A net with two limits)
.

Fix
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
Let
\mathcal{D}:=\mathcal{N}_{\ast}\times\mathcal{N}_{\hat{c}}

(products of neighborhood filters), directed by reverse inclusion.
For each
(U,V)\in\mathcal{D}
, the KIP gives

U\cap V\neq\varnothing
; select

z_{(U,V)}\in U\cap V
(Axiom of Choice).
Then
z_{(U,V)}\to\ast
(given
W\ni\ast
,

(U,V)\succeq(W,X_{0})
implies
z_{(U,V)}\in U\subseteq W
)
and
z_{(U,V)}\to\hat{c}
(similarly).

4.5
Non-sequentiality

Recall that a topological space is
sequential

(Franklin
[
6
]
) if every sequentially closed set is
closed; equivalently, if the topology is completely determined by
convergent sequences.

Proposition 4.15
.

(X,\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}})
is not a sequential space.

Proof.

The set
K=q([1,2])
is sequentially closed but not closed.
It is not closed because
\ast\in X\setminus K
(since

[1,2]\cap\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}=\varnothing
) and

q^{-1}(X\setminus K)=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[1,2]
is not
dense (the complement
[1,2]
has interior
(1,2)
), so condition (b)
fails; see Theorem
5.2
below for the general statement.
To see that
K
is sequentially closed, suppose

\hat{x}_{n}\in K
(i.e.,
x_{n}\in[1,2]
) and
\hat{x}_{n}\to L

in
X
.

•

If
L=\hat{c}
with
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, then

x_{n}\to c
, forcing
c\in[1,2]
(closed in

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
), so
L\in K
.

•

L=\ast
is impossible: by
Proposition
4.11
,
\hat{x}_{n}\to\ast
requires

(x_{n})
to have no accumulation point in

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, but
(x_{n})\subseteq[1,2]
is bounded
and the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem yields an accumulation point
in
[1,2]\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Thus
K
contains all its sequential limits but is not closed.
∎

4.6
Tightness at
\ast

Recall that the
tightness
of a space
X
at a point
x
is

t(x,X):=\min\{\kappa:\text{if }x\in\overline{S},\text{ then }x\in\overline{S_{0}}\text{ for some }S_{0}\subseteq S\text{ with }|S_{0}|\leq\kappa\}
.

Proposition 4.16

(Countable tightness at
\ast
)
.

t(\ast,X)=\aleph_{0}
, despite

\chi(\ast,X)\geq\aleph_{1}

(Proposition
4.1
)
.

Proof.

At points
\hat{x}\neq\ast
, the subspace topology is Euclidean,
so tightness is
\aleph_{0}
.
For
\ast
: let
S\subseteq X
with
\ast\in\overline{S}
.
We may assume
S\subseteq X\setminus\{\ast\}
(if

\ast\in S
, take
S_{0}=\{\ast\}
).
Let
D:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}:q(x)\in S\}
.
The condition
\ast\in\overline{S}
means that for every closed
nowhere-dense
F\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,

D\not\subseteq F
(otherwise
W_{F}
is a neighborhood of
\ast

missing
S
).
Since
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is second-countable (metrizable),
D

has a countable dense subset
D_{0}\subseteq D
(every subspace of a
second-countable space is second-countable, hence separable).
We claim
D_{0}
is not contained in any closed nowhere-dense

F\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
:
if
D_{0}\subseteq F
with
F
closed, then

D\subseteq\overline{D_{0}}^{\,\overline{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq F
,
contradicting
D\not\subseteq F
.
Hence
S_{0}:=q(D_{0})
is countable and
\ast\in\overline{S_{0}}
.
∎

Remark 4.17
.

The coexistence
t(\ast,X)=\aleph_{0}
with

\chi(\ast,X)\geq\aleph_{1}
and the failure of sequentiality
(Proposition
4.15
) makes ERI a natural example of
a compact space where countable tightness does not imply
sequentiality.
The non-sequentiality is witnessed by
q([1,2])
: it is sequentially
closed (no sequence in
[1,2]
escapes to
\ast
by
Proposition
4.11
), yet
\ast\in\overline{q([1,2])}

by the KIP.
Together with sequential compactness
(Proposition
8.3
), ERI exhibits the rare
combination: countable tightness, sequentially compact, and not
sequential.

5
ERI in the Wilansky Hierarchy

This section contains the main results of the paper.

5.1
ERI is US

Theorem 5.1

(ERI is US)
.

(X,\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}})
is a US-space:
every convergent sequence in
X
has a unique limit.
In particular, ERI is not Fréchet–Urysohn

(
since Fréchet–Urysohn implies sequential,
contradicting Proposition
4.15
)
.

Proof.

Immediate from Proposition
4.13
.
∎

5.2
ERI is not KC

Theorem 5.2
.

(X,\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}})
is not a KC-space.

Proof.

Let
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
with
a<b

(e.g.,
[a,b]=[1,2]
).
Set
K:=q([a,b])
.

K
is compact.

The map
q
restricted to
[a,b]
is continuous
(Proposition
2.7
) and
[a,b]
is compact in

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, so
K=q([a,b])
is compact in
X
.

K
is not closed.

We show
X\setminus K\notin\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
.
Since
\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}\cap[a,b]=\varnothing

(as
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
), we have

\ast\notin K
, so
\ast\in X\setminus K
.
The preimage is

q^{-1}(X\setminus K)\;=\;\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[a,b],

which is open in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
(complement of a closed set).
However,
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[a,b]
is
not dense
:
its complement
[a,b]
has non-empty interior
(a,b)
.
Therefore condition (b) fails, and

X\setminus K\notin\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}}
.

Since
K
is compact but not closed,
X
is not KC.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

More generally, for
any
closed subset

F\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
with
\mathrm{int}(F)\neq\varnothing
(i.e.,
F
is not nowhere dense), the set
q(F)
is compact
but not closed in
X
.
The KC failure is thus pervasive: the only compact subsets that are
closed are those whose preimage complement is dense—precisely the
nowhere-dense ones and those not involving
\ast
.

Remark 5.4

(ERI is a k-space)
.

A topological space is a
k-space
if a set is closed whenever
its intersection with every compact subset is closed in that compact
subset.
Every compact space is a k-space: if
A\cap K
is closed in
K

for every compact
K\subseteq X
, taking
K=X
gives
A
closed.
Hence ERI, being compact, is automatically a k-space.
Note, however, that ERI is not a
k_{H}
-space (compactly generated
with respect to compact
Hausdorff
subspaces), since it is
not weakly Hausdorff (Proposition
8.1
).

5.3
The role of first-countability

The following classical result (Fact
1.2
from the
Introduction) explains why ERI can be US without being
T_{2}
:

Proposition 5.5

(Classical)
.

A first-countable space is Hausdorff if and only if it is US.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) is standard (
[
7
]
, Th. 17.10).
(
\Leftarrow

X
is first-countable and not Hausdorff.
Let
x\neq y
be inseparable.
Let
\{U_{n}\}
and
\{V_{n}\}
be nested countable bases at
x
and
y
.
For each

, pick
z_{n}\in U_{n}\cap V_{n}
(non-empty by inseparability).
Then
z_{n}\to x
and
z_{n}\to y
, so
X
is not US.
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

The failure of first-countability at
\ast

(Proposition
4.1
)
is the precise
obstruction that allows

to be US without being
T_{2}
.
More precisely:

(i)

At every point
\hat{x}\neq\ast
, the space
X
is
first-countable

(
the sets

for

form a
countable base
)
.

(ii)

If
X
were first-countable at
\ast
, then
\mathrm{US}+\text{first-countable}
would force
T_{2}
by
Proposition
5.5
—contradicting
Theorem
3.2
.

(iii)

The failure of first-countability at
\ast
is therefore

necessary
for the coexistence of US and non-Hausdorff.

Definition 7.1

(

A space

[
12
]
; we use the kernel
formulation (equivalent by
[

, Prop. 2.3]
)
throughout.
The implications

hold in general
[
2
]
.

We prove the

and all FMQ spaces over first-countable base spaces.

Section

.

Lemma 7.3

(Closed fibers principle)
.

Let
f\colon K\to X
be continuous with
K
compact Hausdorff. Set

N:=f^{-1}(\ast)
and let

be the
continuous “coordinate map”

.
Then for every closed nowhere-dense

g^{-1}(F)
is closed in
K

(
not merely in
K\setminus N
)
.

Proof.

Since
F
is closed and nowhere dense,

is open and dense.
Since
F\subset Y\setminus A
, we have
A\subseteq Y\setminus F
.
For
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{D}_{Y}
:
Y\setminus F
is dense, hence

Y\setminus F\in\mathcal{D}_{Y}
.
More generally, closed nowhere-dense sets are meager, so when

,

is comeager, hence in

.

Set

. Then

and

, so

is open in

Its complement is

K\setminus f^{-1}(W_{F})=\{k\in K\setminus N:g(k)\in F\}=g^{-1}(F)
, which is therefore closed. ∎∎

Proposition 7.4

(Accumulation structure)
.

Suppose every point of

has a countable neighborhood base
in
Y

(
automatic if
Y
is metrizable
)
.
Let

,
K
,

,

be as in Lemma

.
If
(k_{\alpha})
is a net in

with

(g(k_{\alpha}))

Proof.

Suppose a subnet

;
we derive a contradiction.

Step 1.

Since
\{z\}
is closed and nowhere dense (

gives

Because

Step 2 (sequence extraction).

Since

Pick

Step 3.

Define

The values are distinct and

is countable, hence meager (each singleton is

).

Step 4.

By Lemma

is closed in

Since

Theorem 7.5

(
k_{2}
-Hausdorff for FMQ spaces)
.

Let
Y
be a compact Hausdorff space without isolated points,

finite, and
\mathcal{F}
an admissible modifier with

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{Y}
.
Suppose every point of

has a countable neighborhood base
in
Y

(
automatic if
Y
is metrizable
)

. Then

-Hausdorff.

Proof.

We show that

Case 3:

Since

The injectivity of

Since

But

Theorem 8.5

(Sobriety)
.

(X,\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}})
is sober.

Proof.

A space is
sober
if every irreducible closed subset has a unique
generic point. A closed set
F
is
irreducible
if whenever

F=F_{1}\cup F_{2}
with
F_{1},F_{2}
closed, then
F_{1}=F
or
F_{2}=F
.
A point
x
is
generic
for
F
if
\overline{\{x\}}=F
.

Since
X
is
T_{1}
(Proposition
3.1
), every singleton is
closed, so
\overline{\{x\}}=\{x\}
for all
x\in X
. Hence a closed
set can have a generic point only if it is a singleton, and each
singleton
\{x\}
has the unique generic point
x
. Sobriety therefore
reduces to the following claim.

Claim.

Every closed subset
F\subseteq X

with
|F|\geq 2
is reducible
(i.e., expressible as a union of two
proper closed subsets).

Case 1:
\ast\notin F
.

Then
F\subseteq X\setminus\{\ast\}
. The subspace

X\setminus\{\ast\}
carries the Euclidean topology of

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
(since
q
restricts to a homeomorphism

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}X\setminus\{\ast\}
),
hence is Hausdorff. Every Hausdorff space is sober: if
|F|\geq 2
,
pick distinct

and separate them by disjoint open sets

in the subspace; then
F=(F\setminus U_{a})\cup(F\setminus U_{b})

is a decomposition into two proper closed subsets of
F
.
Since irreducibility is intrinsic to the subspace topology on
F
,
the set

is reducible.

Case 2:
\ast\in F
and
|F|\geq 2
.

Pick
\hat{c}\in F\setminus\{\ast\}
with

c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Choose
\varepsilon>0
small
enough that

(possible since
c\neq 0
). Define

We verify that
K
and
L
are closed in
X
.

K
is closed.

Its complement satisfies

q^{-1}(X\setminus K)=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon]
, which is open in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
Moreover,
\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon]

(since

is dense in

because it contains

(-\infty,c-\varepsilon)\cup(c+\varepsilon,+\infty)
.
Since
\ast\in X\setminus K
, condition (b) requires this density,
which holds. So

.

L
is closed.

Its complement satisfies

q^{-1}(X\setminus L)=(c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon)
, which is open
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
Since
\ast\notin X\setminus L
(as

),
condition (b) is vacuous. So

.

Now set
F_{1}:=F\cap K
and
F_{2}:=F\cap L
. Both are closed
(intersections of closed sets). We check the three required properties.

•

F_{1}\subsetneq F
:

Since
q^{-1}(K)=[c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon]
does not meet

, we have
\ast\notin K
, hence

\ast\notin F_{1}
. But
\ast\in F
, so
F_{1}\subsetneq F
.
Also
\hat{c}\in K\cap F
gives
F_{1}\neq\varnothing
.

•

F_{2}\subsetneq F
:

Since
c\in(c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon)
, we have

\hat{c}\in q\bigl((c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon)\bigr)
, hence

\hat{c}\notin L
and
\hat{c}\notin F_{2}
. But
\hat{c}\in F
,
so
F_{2}\subsetneq F
.
Also
\ast\notin q\bigl((c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon)\bigr)
gives

\ast\in L
, so
\ast\in F_{2}\neq\varnothing
.

•

F_{1}\cup F_{2}=F
:

It suffices to show
K\cup L=X
. Let
\hat{x}\in X
. If

\hat{x}\in K

\hat{x}\notin K
, then

x\notin[c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon]

\hat{x}\notin q\bigl((c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon)\bigr)

\hat{x}\in L

Thus
F=F_{1}\cup F_{2}
with both
F_{i}
proper closed subsets
of

; hence

is reducible.

In both cases every closed set with
|F|\geq 2
is reducible.
The only irreducible closed sets are the singletons

, each with
unique generic point

. Hence
(X,\tau_{\mathrm{ERI}})
is sober.
∎

—so the decomposition

does not contradict connectedness.

9
The FMQ Framework and Generalized Construction

The preceding sections establish ERI as a concrete example with a
specific base space (
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
) and collapse set
(
\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
).
A natural question is whether the construction is ad hoc—tied to the
peculiarities of the real line—or reflects a deeper structural
phenomenon.
We now develop the
Filter-Modified Quotient
(FMQ)
framework, which shows that the density condition is a calibrated
mechanism producing the same hierarchy level for
any

compact Hausdorff base without isolated points.
Specifically, this framework yields three results not
visible from ERI alone: a modifier spectrum revealing the density
condition as an optimal equilibrium
(Sections
10
–
11
), an abstract
characterization of the US zone (Section
11.2
),
and an extended rigidity analysis showing that the hierarchy level
is invariant under infinite collapse sets, iteration, and products
(Section
13
).
Several ERI-specific results
(convergence criterion, SC, sobriety, functional triviality) are
special cases of their FMQ counterparts below; we state the general
versions and indicate where the ERI proofs carry over

verbatim
.

9.1
Admissible modifiers and the FMQ construction

Definition 9.1

(Admissible modifier)
.

Let
(X,\tau)
be a topological space. A collection

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
is a

\tau
-admissible modifier
if:

(F1)

X\in\mathcal{F}
.

(F2)

\mathcal{F}
is upward closed:
F\in\mathcal{F}

and
F\subseteq G
implies
G\in\mathcal{F}
.

(F3)

If
U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathcal{F}\cap\tau
, then

U_{1}\cap U_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 9.2

(Filter-Modified Quotient)
.

Let
(X,\tau)
be a topological space,
A\subseteq X
nonempty,

q\colon X\to X/A
the projection collapsing
A
to

\ast:=q(A)
, and
\mathcal{F}
a
\tau
-admissible modifier.
The
\mathcal{F}
-modified quotient topology
is

\tau_{\mathcal{F}}\;:=\;\bigl\{\,U\subseteq X/A:q^{-1}(U)\in\tau\;\;\text{and}\;\;\bigl(\ast\in U\Longrightarrow q^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal{F}\bigr)\,\bigr\}.

We write
\mathrm{FMQ}(X,A,\mathcal{F}):=(X/A,\,\tau_{\mathcal{F}})
.

Proposition 9.3
.

\tau_{\mathcal{F}}
is a topology on
X/A
, with

\tau_{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\tau_{q}

(
the standard quotient
topology
)
and equality on subsets not containing
\ast
.
Moreover,
q\colon(X,\tau)\to(X/A,\tau_{\mathcal{F}})
is
continuous.

Proof.

\varnothing\in\tau_{\mathcal{F}}
(vacuous) and

X/A\in\tau_{\mathcal{F}}
(since
X\in\mathcal{F}

by
(F1)
).
For finite intersections: if
\ast\in U_{1}\cap U_{2}
, then

q^{-1}(U_{i})\in\mathcal{F}\cap\tau
, and
(F3)
gives

q^{-1}(U_{1})\cap q^{-1}(U_{2})\in\mathcal{F}
.
For arbitrary unions: if
\ast\in\bigcup_{i}U_{i}
, pick
j
with

\ast\in U_{j}
; then
q^{-1}(U_{j})\in\mathcal{F}
and

q^{-1}(U_{j})\subseteq\bigcup_{i}q^{-1}(U_{i})
, so
(F2)
gives

\bigcup_{i}q^{-1}(U_{i})\in\mathcal{F}
.
The remaining assertions are immediate from the definition.
∎

Proposition 9.4
.

The collection of dense subsets

\mathcal{D}_{X}:=\{D\subseteq X:\overline{D}=X\}

is a
\tau
-admissible modifier on any topological space
(X,\tau)
.

(
Note:
\mathcal{D}_{X}
is not a filter in general—
\mathbb{Q}

and
\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
are both dense with empty
intersection—but the weaker axiom
(F3)
suffices.
)

Proof.

(F1)
:
X
is dense.

(F2)
: supersets of dense sets are dense.

(F3)
: finite intersections of dense open sets are dense
(Lemma
2.5
).
∎

Remark 9.5
.

The requirement that
A
be closed is essential for
T_{1}

(see Example 9 in §
12
).
The original ERI is

\mathrm{FMQ}(\overline{\mathbb{R}},\,\{-\infty,0,+\infty\},\,\mathcal{D}_{\overline{\mathbb{R}}})
;
the generalized ERI of Theorem
9.8
is

\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
.

9.2
Basic properties of FMQ spaces

Proposition 9.6
.

Let
Y
be a topological space,
A\subseteq Y
finite nonempty, and

\mathcal{F}
an admissible modifier.

(i)

If
Y
is compact, then
\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F})
is compact.

(ii)

\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F})
is
T_{1}
if and only if:
Y
is
T_{1}
,

A
is closed in
Y
, and for every

y\in Y\setminus A
,
Y\setminus\{y\}\in\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

(i) follows from compactness of
Y
and continuity of
q
.
For (ii): the singleton
\{\ast\}
is closed iff

X\setminus\{\ast\}
is open; its preimage is
Y\setminus A
,
and
\ast\notin X\setminus\{\ast\}
, so the
\mathcal{F}
-condition
is vacuous. Hence
\{\ast\}
is closed iff
Y\setminus A
is open,
i.e., iff
A
is closed in
Y
.
For
y\notin A
:
\{\hat{y}\}
is closed iff
X\setminus\{\hat{y}\}
is open; the preimage is
Y\setminus\{y\}
which contains
A
, so both conditions
(open
+

\mathcal{F}
-membership) must hold.
∎

9.3
The generalized ERI construction

The density modifier
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
applied to any compact Hausdorff
base without isolated points yields a US-not-KC space:

Lemma 9.7
.

A Hausdorff space with no isolated points and at least one point is
infinite.

Proof.

If
X
is finite and Hausdorff, every singleton is open (finite
T_{1}

spaces are discrete), so every point is isolated.
∎

Theorem 9.8

(Generalized ERI construction)
.

Let
Y
be a compact Hausdorff space with no isolated points, and let

A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}\subset Y
(
k\geq 1
) be a finite non-empty
subset.
Then
X:=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
is:

(a)

compact;

(b)

T_{1}
;

(c)

not Hausdorff;

(d)

connected;

(e)

US;

(f)

not KC.

Proof.

(a)–(b).
Follow from
Proposition
9.6
.

(c).
The density modifier satisfies
(KIP
F
) (Remark
9.11
below),
so every neighborhood of
\ast
meets every nonempty open set.

(d) Connected.

Suppose
X=U\cup V
is a disconnection with
\ast\in U
.
Then
q^{-1}(U)
is open and dense, and
q^{-1}(V)
is open and nonempty.
A dense set meets every nonempty open set, so

q^{-1}(U)\cap q^{-1}(V)\neq\varnothing
, contradicting

U\cap V=\varnothing
.

(e) US.

This follows from Lemma
9.14
(Baire exclusion) below.

(f) Not KC.

By Lemma
9.7
,
Y
is infinite, so
Y\setminus A
is
dense in
Y
.
Since
Y
is compact Hausdorff (hence
regular
[
8
]
), for any
y\in Y\setminus A
there exists
an open set
W
with

y\in W\subseteq\overline{W}\subseteq Y\setminus A
.
Set
C:=\overline{W}
; then
\mathrm{int}(C)\neq\varnothing

and
C\subset Y\setminus A
.
Now
q(C)
is compact and

\ast\in X\setminus q(C)
, but

q^{-1}(X\setminus q(C))=Y\setminus C
is not dense, so
q(C)

is not closed.
∎

Remark 9.9
.

ERI is the instance
Y=\overline{\mathbb{R}}
,

A=\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
.
Connectedness of
Y
is not required; the density condition forces

X
to be connected even when
Y
is disconnected
(e.g.,
Y=[0,1]\sqcup[2,3]
).
Path-connectedness does not generalize without further hypotheses on
Y
.

9.4
The Key Intersection Principle (generalized)

Proposition 9.10

(Generalized KIP)
.

If
\mathcal{F}
satisfies

\text{for all }F\in\mathcal{F}\cap\tau\text{ and all nonempty }V\in\tau,\quad F\cap V\neq\varnothing,

(KIP
F
)

then every open neighborhood of
\ast
in
\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F})

meets every nonempty open set.
In particular,
\ast
cannot be separated from any point by disjoint open sets,
so the FMQ space is not Hausdorff (unless it is a singleton).

Proof.

If
U\ni\ast
and
V\neq\varnothing
are open in
X
, then

q^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal{F}\cap\tau
and
q^{-1}(V)\in\tau
is nonempty. By
(KIP
F
),
q^{-1}(U)\cap q^{-1}(V)\neq\varnothing
, so

U\cap V\neq\varnothing
.
∎

Remark 9.11
.

The density modifier
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
satisfies
(KIP
F
) by definition: a dense
open set meets every nonempty open set.

9.5
General convergence criterion and Baire exclusion

The following auxiliary fact is used repeatedly.

Lemma 9.12

(Countable Baire)
.

In a compact Hausdorff space without isolated points, every countable
subset has empty interior.

Proof.

Let
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots\}
be countable and suppose

\mathrm{int}(S)\neq\varnothing
; let
U\subseteq S
be nonempty
open. Since
X
has no isolated points, each
U_{n}:=U\setminus\{s_{n}\}
is
open and dense in
U
. Since compact Hausdorff spaces are
Baire
[
7
]
, and open subsets of Baire spaces are Baire,

\bigcap_{n}U_{n}=U\setminus S
is dense in
U
. But

U\subseteq S
gives
U\setminus S=\varnothing
, which cannot be
dense in the nonempty space
U
.
∎

Proposition 9.13

(General convergence criterion)
.

Let
Y
be a compact Hausdorff space without isolated points,

A\subseteq Y
nonempty and closed with
\mathrm{int}(A)=\varnothing

(
in particular,
A
finite
)
, and

X=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
.
For any sequence
(z_{n})
in
Y\setminus A
,

\hat{z}_{n}\to\ast\;\text{ in }X\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(z_{n})\text{ has no accumulation point in }Y\setminus A.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
): Suppose
t\in Y\setminus A
is an accumulation
point. Pass to a subsequence
z_{n_{k}}\to t
.
The set
C:=\{z_{n_{k}}\}\cup\{t\}
is closed, countable,
and nowhere dense (Lemma
9.12
).
Since
C\subset Y\setminus A
, the set
Y\setminus C
is open
and dense, so
q(Y\setminus C)
is a neighborhood of
\ast

not containing
\hat{z}_{n_{k}}
for any
k
—contradicting

\hat{z}_{n}\to\ast
.

(
\Leftarrow
): If
U
is a neighborhood of
\ast
, then

F:=Y\setminus q^{-1}(U)
is closed in
Y
(hence compact)
and contained in
Y\setminus A
.
If infinitely many
z_{n}
belonged to
F
, compactness yields
a convergent subsequence with limit in
F\subseteq Y\setminus A
,
contradicting the hypothesis.
Hence eventually
\hat{z}_{n}\in U
.
∎

For the original ERI space, this reduces to
Proposition
4.11
:
\hat{x}_{n}\to\ast
iff

(x_{n})
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Lemma 9.14

(Baire exclusion)
.

Let
Y
be compact Hausdorff,
A\subseteq Y
finite, and suppose no point of

Y\setminus A
is isolated in
Y
. Then
\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
is US.

Proof.

By Proposition
9.13
,
\hat{z}_{n}\to\ast
iff

(z_{n})
has no accumulation point in
Y\setminus A
.
If also
\hat{z}_{n}\to\hat{c}
with
c\in Y\setminus A
,
then
z_{n}\to c
, making
c
an accumulation point—contradiction.
If
\hat{z}_{n}\to\hat{c}
and
\hat{z}_{n}\to\hat{d}
with

c\neq d
in
Y\setminus A
, Hausdorff of
Y
separates them.
∎

9.6
US and KC criteria for general modifiers

Theorem 9.15

(US criterion)
.

Let
Y
be compact Hausdorff,
A\subseteq Y
finite, and
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{D}_{Y}
.
Then
X=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
is US if and only if no point of

Y\setminus A
is isolated in
Y
.

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
). This is Lemma
9.14
(Baire exclusion).

(
\Rightarrow
). Suppose
p\in Y\setminus A
is isolated in
Y
. Then
\{p\}
is
open, so every dense subset of
Y
must contain
p
. Every open neighborhood
U
of
\ast

in
\tau_{\mathcal{D}_{Y}}

has preimage
q^{-1}(U)
that is dense in
Y
, hence contains
p
, so
\hat{p}\in U
.
Therefore
\hat{p}
belongs to every neighborhood of
\ast
. The constant sequence

(\hat{p},\hat{p},\ldots)
converges to both
\hat{p}
and
\ast
, so
X
is not US.
∎

Theorem 9.16

(KC failure criterion)
.

Let
Y
be compact Hausdorff without isolated points,
A\subseteq Y
finite, and

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{D}_{Y}
. Then
X=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
is not KC
if and only if there exists a closed
C\subset Y\setminus A
with

\mathrm{int}_{Y}(C)\neq\varnothing
.

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
). Such a
C
is compact (closed in compact
Y
), so
q(C)
is compact.
But
X\setminus q(C)\ni\ast
(since
C\cap A=\varnothing
) and

q^{-1}(X\setminus q(C))=Y\setminus C
is not dense (
C
has nonempty interior),
so
q(C)
is not closed.

(
\Rightarrow
). If no such
C
exists, then every closed subset of
Y\setminus A
is
nowhere dense. For every compact
K\subseteq X
with
\ast\notin K
:

q^{-1}(K)
is compact in
Y
, hence
closed (Hausdorff), hence a closed subset of
Y\setminus A
, hence
nowhere dense by hypothesis, so
Y\setminus q^{-1}(K)
is open and dense.
Thus
X\setminus K
is open. For compact
K\ni\ast
: the preimage is closed, and

\ast\notin X\setminus K
, so the
\mathcal{F}
-condition is vacuous.
∎

Corollary 9.17
.

If
Y
is compact Hausdorff without isolated points and
|Y\setminus A|\geq 2
, then

\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
is always not KC.

Proof.

Since
Y
is compact Hausdorff (hence regular) and
A
is finite, for any

y\in Y\setminus A
there exists an open set
W
with

y\in W\subseteq\overline{W}\subseteq Y\setminus A
.
Then
C:=\overline{W}
is closed,
C\subset Y\setminus A
, and

\mathrm{int}_{Y}(C)\supseteq W\neq\varnothing
.
The result follows from Theorem
9.16
.
∎

9.7
SC property for general FMQ spaces

Proposition 9.18

(SC for FMQ spaces)
.

Let
Y
be compact Hausdorff without isolated points,
A
finite, and

\mathcal{F}
admissible with

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{com}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{Y}
.
Then
X=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F})
is SC
(sequentially closed):

every convergent sequence together with its limit is a closed set.

Proof.

The proof follows the same scheme as Proposition
8.2
,
replacing
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with
Y
,

\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
with
A
, and the density condition with
the comeager hypothesis: in the case
L=\hat{c}
, the set

C:=\{c\}\cup\{z_{n}\}
is closed, countable, and meager
(each singleton is nowhere dense), hence nowhere dense; its
complement is dense and comeager, so belongs to
\mathcal{F}
.
The case
L=\ast
is identical.
∎

Proposition 9.19

(US is hereditary)
.

Every subspace of a US space is US. In particular, every
subspace of
\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
has unique sequential limits.

Proof.

Let
X
be US and
S\subseteq X
. If
(x_{n})
in
S
converges to both
a

and
b
in the subspace topology, then
x_{n}\to a
and
x_{n}\to b
in
X
. By US,
a=b
.
∎

9.9
Sobriety of FMQ spaces

Proposition 9.20

(Sobriety for FMQ spaces)
.

Let
Y
be a compact Hausdorff space without isolated points,

finite nonempty, and
\mathcal{F}

Then

is

.

Proof.

Same scheme as Theorem

: since
X
is

, sobriety
reduces to showing every closed

If
\ast\notin F

gives reducibility.
If
\ast\in F

|F\setminus\{\ast\}|=1

is a union of two closed singletons.
If
|F\setminus\{\ast\}|\geq 2
, separate two points of

F\setminus\{\ast\}
in
Y\setminus A
by Hausdorff; the
resulting decomposition has vacuous modifier condition since

\ast\notin q(U_{a})\cup q(U_{b})
.
∎

Remark 9.21
.

Sobriety of FMQ spaces contrasts sharply with the cocountable
topology, which is
T_{1}
and connected but
not
sober (the
whole space is irreducible with no generic point).
The difference is that in FMQ spaces, the Hausdorff structure of

Y\setminus A
provides enough closed sets to decompose any
non-singleton closed subset.

9.10
The Hausdorff barrier

Theorem 9.22

(Hausdorff barrier)
.

Let
Y
be compact Hausdorff,
A\subseteq Y
finite nonempty, and

\mathcal{F}
any
\tau
-admissible modifier.
If
X:=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F})
is KC, then
X
is Hausdorff.

Proof.

In a KC space every compact set is closed.
Since
Y
is compact Hausdorff and
q\colon Y\to X
is continuous,
for every closed
C\subseteq Y
the image
q(C)
is compact, hence
closed in the KC space
X
.
Thus
q
is a closed map.
A continuous, surjective, closed map is a quotient map, so the
topology of
X
equals the standard quotient topology
\tau_{q}
.
By Willard
[
8
]
(Theorem 9.2), a quotient map out of a
compact Hausdorff space yields a Hausdorff quotient if and only if it
is closed; since
q
is closed,
X
is Hausdorff.
∎

Corollary 9.23
.

For single-point-collapse FMQ spaces over compact Hausdorff base
spaces, the Wilansky hierarchy collapses at KC:

\mathrm{KC}\Rightarrow T_{2}
.
The density modifier
\mathcal{D}_{X}
achieves the finest
non-Hausdorff level, namely
\mathrm{US}
-not-
\mathrm{KC}
.

9.11
Functional triviality

Proposition 9.24

(
C(X,\mathbb{R})
is trivial)
.

For any compact Hausdorff
Y
without isolated points, finite
A
, and

X=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
, every continuous function

f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is constant.

Proof.

Identical to Proposition
8.8
, replacing

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with
Y
and

\{-\infty,0,+\infty\}
with
A
: the key point is that

g:=f\circ q
is constant on
A
(say value
c
), and if

g(t)\neq c
for some
t\in Y\setminus A
, then

f^{-1}((c-\varepsilon,c+\varepsilon))
is a neighborhood of

\ast
whose preimage is not dense.
∎

Corollary 9.25

(Pseudocompactness)
.

Every FMQ space
X=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
is pseudocompact:
every continuous
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded

(
indeed constant, by Proposition
9.24
)
.

Corollary 9.26
.

No FMQ space
X=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
is completely regular.

Proof.

A completely regular space with more than one point admits a non-constant
continuous function to
\mathbb{R}
; Proposition
9.24
shows

C(X,\mathbb{R})
consists of constants only.
∎

Corollary 9.27
.

No FMQ space
X=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
is normal.

Proof.

By the Urysohn lemma, every normal
T_{1}
space is completely regular.
Since
X
is
T_{1}
(Theorem
9.8
) but not completely regular
(Corollary
9.26
), it cannot be normal.
∎

Remark 9.28

(Spectral collapse)
.

The triviality of
C(X,\mathbb{R})
for spaces where every
open neighborhood of a point is dense was studied by
Herrlich
[
15
]
.
In the language of
C^{*}
-algebras, the ring of bounded continuous
real-valued functions
C^{*}(X,\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}

(constants only), so its Gelfand spectrum (maximal ideal space) is a
single point.
(Since
X
is not completely regular, the classical Stone–Čech
compactification
\beta X
is not defined; by
\beta X
we mean
the Gelfand spectrum of
C^{*}(X)
, which is a singleton.)
This “spectral collapse” is intrinsic to the density modifier:
the topology is rich enough to support non-trivial
convergence (Proposition
4.11
), yet too coarse for
continuous real-valued functions to detect it.
The result extends to any admissible modifier

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{Y}
satisfying
(KIP
F
), since the KIP forces every
continuous
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
to be constant by the same
argument as Proposition
9.24
.

The Modifier Spectrum

The density modifier
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
is one choice among many. In this section we
systematically explore what happens when
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
is replaced by other natural
modifiers, revealing the density condition as a precise equilibrium point.
The abstract criterion characterizing the “US zone” will be given in
Section
11.2
.

10.1
The cofinite modifier

Definition 10.1
.

The
cofinite modifier
on
Y
is

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cof}}:=\{S\subseteq Y:Y\setminus S\text{ is finite}\}
.

Every cofinite set in a space without isolated points is dense,
so
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cof}}\subsetneq\mathcal{D}_{Y}
, and

\tau_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cof}}}\subsetneq\tau_{\mathcal{D}_{Y}}
: the cofinite-modified
topology is strictly coarser.

Proposition 10.2
.

Let
Y=[0,1]
,
A=\{0,1\}
. Then
\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cof}})
is

T_{1}
but not US.

Proof.

T_{1}
: for
y\in(0,1)
,
Y\setminus\{y\}
is cofinite, so

Y\setminus\{y\}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cof}}
; this gives
\{\hat{y}\}
closed.

Not US: consider
x_{n}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{n}
for
n\geq 3
. Then
x_{n}\to\frac{1}{2}

in
[0,1]
, so
\hat{x}_{n}\to\widehat{1/2}
in
X
. We claim
\hat{x}_{n}\to\ast
as well.
Let
U
be a neighborhood of
\ast
in
\tau_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cof}}}
. Then
q^{-1}(U)

is cofinite, i.e.,
[0,1]\setminus q^{-1}(U)
is a finite set
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}
.
Since
x_{n}\to\frac{1}{2}
and
x_{n}\neq y_{j}
for all large
n
,
eventually
x_{n}\in q^{-1}(U)
, hence
\hat{x}_{n}\in U
. So
\hat{x}_{n}
converges to both

\widehat{1/2}
and
\ast
: US fails.
∎

10.2
The comeager modifier

Definition 10.3
.

The
comeager modifier
on a topological space
Y
is

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{com}}:=\{S\subseteq Y:S\text{ is comeager (complement is meager)}\}
.

In a Baire space, every comeager set is dense, so

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{com}}\subsetneq\mathcal{D}_{Y}
.

Lemma 10.4
.

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{com}}
is an admissible modifier on any topological space
Y
.

Proof.

(F1):
Y
is comeager (
Y\setminus Y=\varnothing
is meager).
(F2): if
S
is comeager and
S\subseteq T
, then
Y\setminus T\subseteq Y\setminus S
;
a subset of a meager set is meager, so
T
is comeager.
(F3): if
U_{1},U_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{com}}\cap\tau
, then

Y\setminus(U_{1}\cap U_{2})=(Y\setminus U_{1})\cup(Y\setminus U_{2})
; a finite
union of meager sets is meager, so
U_{1}\cap U_{2}
is comeager.
∎

Proposition 10.5
.

Let
Y
be compact Hausdorff without isolated points and
A
finite. Then

\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{com}})
is
T_{1}
, compact, not Hausdorff, and

US
.

Proof.

T_{1}
: singletons in
Y\setminus A
are nowhere dense, hence meager,
so their complements are comeager.
US: closed countable sets are meager, so
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{com}}

satisfies hypothesis (ii) of Theorem
11.3
.
Not Hausdorff: every comeager set in a Baire space is dense, so
(KIP
F
) holds.
∎

10.3
The accumulation-at-
A
modifier

Definition 10.6
.

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{acc}}:=\{S\subseteq Y:\text{every }a\in A\text{ is an accumulation point of }S\}
.

Proposition 10.7
.

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{acc}}
satisfies
(F1)
and
(F2)
.
It satisfies
(F3)
for all pairs

: indeed, if every
a\in A
is an
accumulation point of both
U_{1}
and
U_{2}

is an accumulation

U_{1}\cap U_{2}
(since

is an open neighborhood
of

This restricted form of

suffices for the FMQ construction:
in the proof that

(Proposition

)

(F3)
is
invoked only when

, which forces

; hence the
hypothesis

in the restricted form is
automatically satisfied.
Moreover,
\mathcal{D}_{Y}\subsetneq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{acc}}
:
for

,

, the set

(

and

)
but not to
\mathcal{D}_{Y}

(
since
\overline{S}=[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]\cup[\tfrac{3}{4},1]\neq[0,1]

.

Proposition 10.8

(Accumulation collapses to standard quotient)
.

Let

be compact Hausdorff without isolated points,

finite. Then

).

Every open

∎

10.6
The US zone is uniformly not wH

Theorem 10.9
.

Let
Y
be compact Hausdorff without isolated points,
A
finite, and
\mathcal{F}
any
admissible modifier with
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{com}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{Y}
.
Then
\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F})
is
not
weakly Hausdorff.

Proof.

Let
C\subseteq Y\setminus A
be a closed set with nonempty interior (exists by regularity).
The inclusion
\iota\colon C\hookrightarrow Y
composed with
q
gives a continuous map

f=q\circ\iota\colon C\to X
from the compact Hausdorff space
C
.
The image
f(C)=q(C)
is compact.
We claim
q(C)
is not closed:
X\setminus q(C)\ni\ast
, so
q(C)
is closed iff

q^{-1}(X\setminus q(C))=Y\setminus C\in\mathcal{F}
. But
C
has nonempty interior,
so
Y\setminus C
is not dense, and since

\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{Y}
, every element of
\mathcal{F}
is dense.
Hence
Y\setminus C\notin\mathcal{F}
, so
q(C)
is not closed.
Since
f
is continuous from compact Hausdorff with non-closed image,
X
is not wH.
∎

(Uniform hierarchy of the US zone)
.

For any admissible modifier
\mathcal{F}
in the US zone
(
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{com}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{Y}
), the space

\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F})
sits at exactly the position

k_{2}\text{H},\quad\text{not wH},\quad\text{not KC},\quad\text{not }T_{2}

in the Clontz hierarchy
[
2
]
. The entire US zone collapses to a
single
hierarchy level.

(
This extends to infinite closed nowhere-dense

by
Theorem

.

11
Optimality and Abstract US Criterion

We now show that
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
is the least restrictive admissible
modifier preserving the KIP, and give an abstract characterization of
the US zone.

11.1
Optimality of the density modifier

Theorem 11.1

(Optimality of density)
.

Let
Y
be compact Hausdorff without isolated points and
A
finite.

(i)

(KIP holds for
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
)

Every
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
-open neighborhood of
\ast
is dense, hence meets every nonempty
open set.

(ii)

(KIP fails beyond
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
)

For any admissible
\mathcal{F}\supsetneq\mathcal{D}_{Y}
, the KIP fails: there exists a
non-dense open
\mathcal{F}
-neighborhood of
\ast
.

(iii)

(Hausdorff recovery)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{acc}}
and
\mathcal{P}(Y)
both produce Hausdorff spaces
(Proposition
10.8
).

(iv)

(US zone boundary)

Below
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
, US may or may not hold. The comeager modifier satisfies
(US
F
); the cofinite does not
(Propositions
10.5
and
10.2
).

In summary,
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
is the least restrictive admissible modifier for which the KIP
holds.

Proof.

(i) is immediate from the definitions.

(ii): There exists
S\in\mathcal{F}
with

\overline{S}\neq Y
. Pick

p\in Y\setminus(\overline{S}\cup A)
.
By normality,
separate
\{p\}
from
A\cup\overline{S}
by disjoint open sets
V_{p}\ni p

and
W\supseteq A\cup\overline{S}
. Then
W\in\mathcal{F}
(upward closure from
S
)
is not dense (
p\notin\overline{W}
), so
q(W)
is a non-dense
\tau_{\mathcal{F}}
-open
neighborhood of
\ast
disjoint from
q(V_{p})
.

(iii)–(iv): Already established.
∎

Remark 11.2
.

The density modifier
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
sits at the boundary between
the non-Hausdorff US zone and the Hausdorff regime. The equilibrium is
between two competing requirements:

all neighborhoods of
\ast
must be dense
(to block
T_{2}
;
requires
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{Y}
), and

\ast

must have enough neighborhoods to block double limits

(to preserve US; requires
\mathcal{F}
to contain complements of
closed countable sets).

11.2
Abstract US criterion

Theorem 11.3

(Abstract US criterion)
.

Let
Y
be compact Hausdorff,
A\subseteq Y
finite nonempty, and
\mathcal{F}
an
admissible modifier. Suppose:

(i)

No point of
Y\setminus A
is isolated in
Y
.

(ii)

Every closed countable
C\subset Y\setminus A
satisfies

Y\setminus C\in\mathcal{F}
.

Then
\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F})
is US.

Equivalently,
\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F})
is US whenever

\text{for every convergent sequence }z_{n}\to c\in Y\setminus A,\quad Y\setminus(\{z_{n}\}\cup\{c\})\in\mathcal{F}.

(US
F
)

Hypothesis
(ii)
is satisfied by
\mathcal{D}_{Y}

(closed countable sets are nowhere dense, so their complements are dense),
by
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{com}}
(closed countable sets are meager, so
their complements are comeager), and fails for
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cof}}

(closed countable sets need not be finite).

Proof.

Hypothesis (i) ensures
X
is
T_{1}
(Proposition
9.6
(ii)).
Since
X\setminus\{\ast\}\cong Y\setminus A
is Hausdorff, it suffices to
show no sequence converges to both an ordinary point and
\ast
.
Suppose
\hat{z}_{n}\to\hat{c}
with
c\in Y\setminus A
and also
\hat{z}_{n}\to\ast
.
Then
z_{n}\to c
in
Y
; passing to a tail,
z_{n}\notin A
for all
n
. The set

C:=\{z_{n}:n\geq 1\}\cup\{c\}
is closed and countable in
Y\setminus A
. By (ii),

Y\setminus C\in\mathcal{F}
. Since
C
is closed,
Y\setminus C
is open; since

C\cap A=\varnothing
, we have
A\subseteq Y\setminus C
. Hence
q(Y\setminus C)
is
a
\tau_{\mathcal{F}}
-open neighborhood of
\ast
. But
\hat{z}_{n}\notin q(Y\setminus C)

for all
n
, contradicting
\hat{z}_{n}\to\ast
.
∎

11.3
The neighborhood filter at
\ast

The entire non-Hausdorff character of the space is encoded in the
neighborhood filter of
\ast
: at all other points, the topology is the
standard Hausdorff quotient topology. Thus:

\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{F})
is a standard Hausdorff space with a single
“deformed” point
\ast
whose neighborhood filter has been replaced by
\mathcal{F}
.

For
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{D}_{Y}
, the neighborhood filter of
\ast
is generated by the sets

where
F
ranges over closed nowhere-dense subsets of

Y\setminus A
.

Remark 11.4

(Convergence-space embedding)
.

The sequential coreflection
\sigma X
(Dolecki–Mynard
[
13
]
)
is strictly finer than
X
and Hausdorff, with

\tau_{\sigma X}\supsetneq\tau_{X}
; the identity

\sigma X\to X
is a homeomorphism on
X\setminus\{\ast\}
.

Remark 11.5

(Frame-theoretic perspective)
.

When
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{D}_{Y}
, the density condition
corresponds to the
double-negation nucleus

j(U)=\mathrm{int}(\overline{U})
on
\mathcal{O}(Y)

[
14
]
: an open set
U
is in
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
iff

j(U)=Y
.
The FMQ construction thus replaces the neighborhood filter
of
\ast
with the filter of dense opens—a point-set manifestation
of
\neg\neg
-sheafification.

13.2
Iterated FMQ: absorption into multi-point collapse

A natural attempt to produce new hierarchy levels is to iterate
the FMQ construction: apply FMQ to an FMQ space.
We show this reduces to a
multi-point
FMQ over the
original base, producing the same hierarchy level.

Definition 13.4

(Multi-point FMQ)
.

Let
Y
be a topological space,
A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}\subseteq Y

pairwise disjoint nonempty subsets, and
\mathcal{F}
a

\tau_{Y}
-admissible modifier.
The
multi-point FMQ
collapses each
A_{i}
to a distinct
point
\ast_{i}
and defines:
U
is open iff
q^{-1}(U)
is open
in
Y
and
\ast_{i}\in U\Rightarrow q^{-1}(U)\in\mathcal{F}

for each
i
.

Proposition 13.5

(Iterated FMQ absorption)
.

Let
Y
be compact Hausdorff without isolated points,

A\subset Y
finite, and

X_{1}=\mathrm{FMQ}(Y,A,\mathcal{D}_{Y})
.
Let
B=\{\hat{b}_{1},\ldots,\hat{b}_{m}\}\subset X_{1}\setminus\{\ast_{1}\}

(
corresponding to

b_{i}\in Y\setminus A
)
be finite.
Then
X_{2}:=\mathrm{FMQ}(X_{1},B,\mathcal{D}_{X_{1}})
is
canonically homeomorphic to the multi-point FMQ of
Y

collapsing
A
to
\ast_{1}
and
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\}

to
\ast_{2}
, with the density modifier
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
applied
at both collapsed points.

Proof.

Write
q_{1}\colon Y\to X_{1}
and
q_{2}\colon X_{1}\to X_{2}
for
the projections, and
\pi:=q_{2}\circ q_{1}\colon Y\to X_{2}

for their composition.
The set-level description of

is:

,
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\}\mapsto\ast_{2}
,

for

.

Key lemma:

V\subseteq X_{1}
is dense in
X_{1}
if
and only if
q_{1}^{-1}(V)
is dense in
Y
.

Proof of lemma.

V
is dense in
X_{1}
iff

meets every nonempty open set.
The open sets in
X_{1}
are of two kinds:
(i) those not containing
\ast_{1}
, which are exactly

q_{1}(W)
for

; and
(ii) those containing
\ast_{1}
, whose preimages are open
and

dense in
Y
.
For type (i):

meets

iff

q_{1}^{-1}(V)\cap W\neq\varnothing

q_{1}^{-1}(V)\cap(Y\setminus A)
is dense in

, hence in

(since

is dense
in
Y
:

is finite and
Y
has no isolated points).
For type (ii): if

is open, then
q_{1}^{-1}(U)

is open and dense in
Y
; any set whose preimage is dense in
Y

automatically meets
q_{1}^{-1}(U)
, so
V
meets
U
.
Thus
V
is dense in
X_{1}
iff
q_{1}^{-1}(V)
is dense in
Y
. ∎

Now let
U\subseteq X_{2}
.
The topology of
X_{2}
requires:

(i)

q_{2}^{-1}(U)
is open in
X_{1}
, and

(ii)

\ast_{2}\in U\Rightarrow q_{2}^{-1}(U)
is dense in
X_{1}
.

Condition (i) unpacks (via the
X_{1}
topology) to:

\pi^{-1}(U)
is open in
Y
, and

\ast_{1}\in U\Rightarrow\pi^{-1}(U)
is dense in
Y

(since
\ast_{1}\in q_{2}^{-1}(U)
iff
\ast_{1}\in U
,
because
\ast_{1}\notin B
).
Condition (ii) becomes, by the Key Lemma:

\ast_{2}\in U\Rightarrow\pi^{-1}(U)
is dense in
Y
.
Combining:
U\in\tau_{X_{2}}
iff
\pi^{-1}(U)
is open in
Y
,
and
(\ast_{1}\in U\text{ or }\ast_{2}\in U)\Rightarrow\pi^{-1}(U)
is dense in

.
This is exactly the multi-point FMQ topology with density
modifier
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
.
∎

14.2
Concluding remarks

We summarize the main contributions and their interplay.

The ERI example.

The Extended Real Line with Reentry is the first compact path-connected
space known to separate US from KC.
Its mechanism is elementary—a single density condition on
neighborhoods of
\ast
—yet its consequences are rich:
a complete convergence criterion
(Proposition
4.11
), the rare combination of countable
tightness without sequentiality
(Proposition
4.16
), and trivial continuous function
space (Proposition
8.8
).
These properties are robust: the independent one-point
extension
Z
(§
12.3
) achieves the same hierarchy
position, confirming that this separation is a genuine topological
phenomenon.

The FMQ framework.

Combining the modifier spectrum (§
10
) with the
extended rigidity results (§
13
), the FMQ framework
accesses exactly three hierarchy levels via the modifier:

T_{1}
-not-US (cofinite),
k_{2}
H-not-wH (comeager through density),
and
T_{2}
(accumulation and beyond).
The
(Y,A)
inputs affect properties orthogonal to the
hierarchy level (path-connectedness, sobriety, tightness, cardinal
functions), and the hierarchy level is invariant under infinite
collapse sets (Theorem
13.1
), iteration
(Proposition
13.5
), and products
(Proposition
13.7
).

Frontiers.

Two principal directions remain open.
First, a conjectural fourth level (US-not-
k_{2}
H) would require a
non-first-countable base space (Open Problem
14.1
):
the only known path toward a second FMQ hierarchy level
is
Y=\beta\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathbb{N}
, where
the sequential extraction in Proposition
7.4
breaks down.
Second, the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(X,\ast)
is conjecturally
trivial (Remark
14.5
(c)
): every continuous map to
a Hausdorff space is constant
(Proposition
8.13
), but a
complete proof requires verifying semilocal simple-connectedness
at
\ast
.
The gap between US and Hausdorff in ERI is witnessed by nets
(Example
4.14
) but
not
by sequences: this is a structural
necessity, since first-countable US spaces are Hausdorff
(Proposition
5.5
).
