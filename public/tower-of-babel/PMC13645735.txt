Indocyanine green angiography and machine learning analysis determine topographic distribution of peripheral hard drusen in a Chinese cohort
Authors: Jiaxin Pu, Yuying Ji, Miaoling Li, Xiongze Zhang, Chengguo Zuo, Xuenan Zhuang, Xinlei Hao, Guiqin He, Yuhong Gan, Yongyue Su, Yining Zhang, Ruijun Yang, Xuelin Chen, Lan Mi, Feng Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHard drusen appear as hyperfluorescent dots on indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) due to their high phospholipid content. This study aims to explore the distribution of peripheral hard drusen across age groups and retinal regions, and their correlation with macular pathology including hard drusen, soft drusen, and progression to neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD).MethodsA hospital-based retrospective study was conducted at Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center. Patients who underwent ICGA over a five-year period were included and categorised into seven age groups. Hard drusen were quantified across nine retinal quadrants using the Trainable Weka Segmentation machine learning plugin. A subgroup of patients underwent imaging follow-up to assess progression to nAMD.ResultsHere we show, across 1562 normal eyes (14,058 ICGA images) quantified using a validated machine learning model (Dice coefficient: 0.697; sensitivity: 82.93%; ICC: 0.944), that age 50 represents a critical threshold for drusen accumulation, with significant increases observed across all nine quadrants. The nasal quadrants demonstrate earlier and more prominent drusen accumulation compared to the temporal quadrants (P < 0.001 across ages 20–69 years). Notably, the central superior quadrant shows consistent associations with macular hard drusen counts (Stdβ = 0.104, P = 0.010), soft drusen volume (ρ = 0.657, P = 0.020), and progression to nAMD (OR = 1.324, P = 0.040).ConclusionsPeripheral hard drusen accumulate preferentially in the nasal quadrants and increase markedly after age 50. Comprehensive peripheral retinal evaluation during ICGA examination may hold potential clinical value for AMD monitoring, pending further prospective validation.Subject terms: Retinal diseases, Medical imaging

Introduction

Drusen refers to localized accumulation of extracellular material located between retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) basal lamina and the inner collagenous layer of Bruch’s membrane, which is commonly observed in both normally aging eyes and in cases associated with retinal disease1,2. Lipids, primarily esterified and unesterified cholesterol, along with phosphatidylcholine, comprise the main component of drusen3,4. Biochemical process linked to aging, influenced or altered by genetic and environmental variables, may lead to the formation of drusen and disease progression5. Over the past decade, considerable progress has been achieved in comprehending drusen, particularly through the emergence of high-resolution in vivo fundus imaging5,6.

Small hard drusen are uniformly bright white in color with a sharp outline and, by conventional classification, are smaller than 63 µm in diameter5. They often appear as common incidental findings of unclear significance during fundus examinations, with their presence visible in fundus photographs for over 90% of adults aged 20 to 46 years7. Previous research has shown that the presence of numerous small hard drusen in adults is associated with the later development of soft drusen, indicative of early stages of age-related macular degeneration (AMD)8–10. Most research has focused on hard drusen in the macular area, yet these lesions also occur extensively in the peripheral retina. Recent studies have begun investigating peripheral hard drusen using ultra-widefield (UWF) imaging, but results are often limited by the subjective nature of drusen assessment11–13. This is due to the low contrast between drusen and surrounding retinal tissue in pseudocolor and autofluorescence assessments, as well as poor visualization of drusen in the superior and inferior quadrants caused by eyelash obstruction.

Hard drusen may exhibit mild to intense hyperfluorescence on fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) and indocyanine green angiography (ICGA), with intensity determined by their biochemical composition—high phospholipid content binding more fluorescein while neutral lipid-rich deposits show less binding5,14. This fluorescence binding property creates higher contrast with surrounding tissue15, particularly in late-phase ICGA, which provides an ideal foundation for machine learning-based automated quantification. Here, leveraging this advantage, we conducted a hospital-based retrospective study of 1562 normal eyes using nine-quadrant late-phase ICGA and automated machine learning segmentation to characterise the topographic distribution of peripheral hard drusen across age groups and its associations with macular pathology. We show that nasal quadrants accumulate drusen earlier than temporal quadrants, with age 50 as a critical threshold across all quadrants, and that the central superior quadrant is independently associated with macular hard drusen burden, soft drusen volume, and progression to neovascular AMD (nAMD).

Methods

Study participants

This is a hospital-based retrospective study conducted in Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center. This study reviewed consecutive patients who underwent ICGA and were diagnosed with monocular fundus disease or angiographically normal eyes over a five-year period (May 2019 to May 2024). Follow-up data were obtained through systematic review of existing records and active patient recall (April-October 2025). The research adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (2022KYPJ117 and 2024KYPJ099). Given the retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent for research participation was waived by the IRB. Written informed consent obtained from patients pertained solely to the clinical ICGA procedure, covering associated procedural risks, and was not specific to inclusion in this research study.

Each patient underwent multimodal fundus imaging as assessed by the initial consulting ophthalmologist to confirm the diagnosis. Only late-phase ICGA images (Spectralis; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), taken more than 25 minutes after dye injection and following full mydriasis induced by tropicamide, were selected for hard drusen analysis. To obtain a thorough assessment of the fundus, ICGA images of nine retinal quadrants were captured by having patients fixate on nine directional lights within the imaging device. These quadrants include superior temporal, central superior, superior nasal, central temporal, central, central nasal, inferior temporal, central inferior, and inferior nasal (Supplementary Figs. 1-2). A circle centered on the fovea with a radius stretching to the temporal rim of the optic disc was used to define the macular area for further macular drusen identification16,17.

To avoid interference from other lesions, only fellow eyes with normal ICGA appearances were included in the study (study eye), including eyes exhibiting solely hard drusen or age-related scattered hypofluorescent spots on late-phase ICGA (ASHS-LIA)18. If both eyes were found to be angiographically normal, the eye with the higher imaging quality was included. Eyes with poor cooperation during the examination, insufficient ocular movement for peripheral image acquisition, or severe refractive media opacities were excluded from the study. Additionally, considering the potential differences in formation mechanisms of pachydrusen, optic disk drusen (ODD), and subretinal drusenoid deposits (SDD) compared to conventional hard drusen19,20, eyes with these drusen subtypes were excluded. Drusen located over choroidal nevi were omitted from analysis. The data selection workflow is presented in Fig. 1.

Eligible patients in the study were divided into seven age groups based on their actual age at the time of examination: under 20 years, 20−29 years, 30−39 years, 40−49 years, 50−59 years, 60−69 years, and over 70 years. For ease of reference in subsequent description, these groups will be referred to as ‘under 20 s,’ ‘20 s,’ ‘30 s,’ ‘40 s,’ ‘50 s,’ ‘60 s,’ and ‘70 s and over’ respectively. The distribution of drusen across different quadrants in each age group was studied further.

Imaging processing

Hard drusen are defined as yellow, round deposits with a diameter smaller than 63 µm at the sub-RPE level in color photography, and as dotted highly fluorescent in the late-phase ICGA, and sometimes in FFA. Drusen diameter was assessed by visual grading, and those with a diameter less than half the width of a large arcade vein near the optic disk on ICGA were identified21. During image selection, we observed that hard drusen in some elderly participants merged into confluent patches appearing as intense hyperfluorescence on late-phase ICGA (Supplementary Fig 2). This confluence occurred in peripheral, peripapillary, and macular regions, obscuring individual drusen contours. To ensure accurate automated counting, eyes with confluent hard drusen were analyzed separately. Cases were classified as either having confluent hard drusen or only scattered hard drusen by two well-trained retinal specialists (J. P. and Y. J.). In instances of disagreement, the case was publicly discussed, and a third retinal specialist (F. W.) made the final determination. Cases with only scattered hard drusen were included in the subsequent machine training for image segmentation and counting.

The Fiji-ImageJ plugin: trainable weka segmentation (TWS) was used to analyse ICGA images of the hard drusen sections (ImageJ, version 1.8.0_322; National Institutes of Health, USA, available at https://imagej.nih.gov/ij). To achieve optimal recognition and segmentation of regions of interest (ROI), we prepared four training sets (A-D), each containing 20 late-phase ICGA images covering all nine quadrants. The images in these training sets displayed an increasing gradient of drusen count: training set A contained images with fewer than 5 drusen, training set B with 6-10 drusen, training set C with 11-20 drusen, and training set D with more than 20 drusen. The image stacks from A to D were sequentially trained on the TWS interface until all drusen in the training sets were clearly identifiable.

The training steps were as follows: (i) Stack Creation: The Stacking tool in ImageJ was utilized to create four training stacks, each containing 20 images. (ii) Opening TWS: The TWS plugin was launched, and training stack A was initially loaded. (iii) Algorithm Selection: The classifier was configured to use the following algorithms: Gaussian blur, Sobel filter, Hessian, difference of Gaussians, and membrane projections. Membrane Thickness was set to 1, Membrane Patch Size to 19, and Minimum and Maximum Sigma values were set to 1.0 and 16.0, respectively. (iv) ROI Segmentation: Selection tools, including circular, rectangular, and freehand line tools, were employed to delineate the drusen area in the 20 ICGA images. A line tool was used to separate overlapping drusen. The samples were manually classified into two classes: class one (drusen) and class two (background). (v) Classifier Training: The classifier was trained with the selected samples until it could recognize all drusen particles in the training stack A. Subsequently, stacks B through D were sequentially loaded and trained further on the existing model. Finally, the classifier was saved, and a probability map was generated. (vi) Particle Counting: The probability map was converted from a 32-bit to an 8-bit image, and the threshold was adjusted to above 90% to obtain a binary image, where drusen appeared as white particles against a black background. The ‘Analyze Particles’ tool in ImageJ was employed for counting the drusen, with a size threshold set from 30 to infinity and a circularity threshold from 0.2 to infinity. (vii) Workflow Standardization: The segmentation and quantification steps were recorded and compiled into a batch of commands using macros in ImageJ, facilitating easy execution in subsequent large-sample analyses.

A separate validation set comprising 80 ICGA images was obtained from an independent retrospective cohort examining ICGA-based choroidal features in pachychoroid spectrum diseases (IRB approval: 2023KYPJ114, Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center). As with the main study, informed consent for research use of images was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of the study. Drusen counts in these 80 ICGA images were automatically quantified by the trained machine learning program, as well as manually by a trained observer (J. P.). The consistency between the model’s output and manual measurements was evaluated to assess the model’s performance. Follow-up data collection

Systematic follow-up was conducted for participants aged ≥50 years through review of existing medical records and active patient recall (April–October 2025). Follow-up examinations included optical coherence tomography (OCT), with OCT angiography (OCTA) or FFA/ICGA performed when clinically indicated. To investigate the relationship between peripheral hard drusen and macular soft drusen (or drusenoid pigment epithelial detachment [PED]), eyes with soft drusen (or drusenoid PED) identified at baseline or developed during follow-up were combined for analysis. All eyes with soft drusen (or drusenoid PED) had volumetric OCT imaging available. Soft drusen volumes were quantified using manual three-dimensional segmentation on OCT as we previously described22–24. Statistics and reproducibility

Model performance was evaluated using pixel-level metrics (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, Dice coefficient, and intersection over union [IoU/Jaccard Index]) and object-level metrics (mean absolute error [MAE] for drusen counting). Inter-observer reliability for manual drusen counting in the validation set was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Pearson correlation analysis. Bland-Altman analysis was performed to evaluate agreement between automated and manual drusen counts. For clinical data analysis, descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare categorical variables (presence of scattered and confluent hard drusen across age groups). Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare continuous variables between groups (drusen counts between age groups, drusen counts between progressors and non-progressors). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to evaluate differences in drusen counts across different retinal quadrants. Linear regression models assessed the relationship of peripheral and macular hard drusen counts. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between peripheral hard drusen counts and macular soft drusen volume. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with progression to neovascular AMD, with age, sex, follow-up duration, and fellow eye nAMD status as candidate variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-sided).

Results

Demographic data for study population

ICGA images from a total of 6175 patients were collected and screened. After applying stringent exclusion criteria, images from 1562 normal eyes—totaling 14,058 late-phase ICGA images across nine quadrants in 1562 patients—were included in the study, as depicted in the flowchart of Fig. 1. In this cohort, males accounted for 64.21% of the subjects, with an almost equal distribution between left and right eyes (50.19% for right eyes). The age distribution across the seven groups was as follows: 132 eyes in the under 20 s group, 132 in the 20 s group, 237 in the 30 s group, 302 in the 40 s group, 388 in the 50 s group, 299 in the 60 s group, and 72 in the 70 s and over group. Regarding the diagnosis of the contralateral symptomatic eyes, the electronic medical records system was queried, with uveitis (379 cases, 24.26%) being the most common diagnosis, including infectious and non-infectious types, followed by nAMD (244 cases, 15.62%), central serous chorioretinopathy (CSC) (222 cases, 14.21%), and angiographically normal eyes (170 cases, 10.88%). Hard drusen presence across different age groups

Initially, we focused on the presence of hard drusen across different age groups, as demonstrated in Table 2 and visualized in Fig. 5. As age increases, the proportion of eyes with hard drusen rises, with the percentages in ascending age groups being 28.79%, 53.03%, 54.21%, 72.52%, 90.47%, 99.67%, and 100%, respectively. Significant differences were observed between most adjacent age groups (P < 0.001 for all except for the 20 s vs. 30 s, and 60 s vs. 70 s and over, with P-values of 0.857 and 0.623, respectively). When focusing solely on the confluent type of hard drusen, we found only 2 cases in the 40 s group. With each advancing decade, the proportion of such cases significantly increased (P < 0.001 for all). Age group was found to be significantly correlated with the presence of drusen in regression models adjusted for sex (P < 0.001 for both drusen and the confluent subtype). However, sex was not recognized as a significant factor for drusen presence when adjusted for age group (P = 0.569 and 0.517 for drusen and the confluent subtype, respectively).

Model performance and validation

The Trainable Weka Segmentation model demonstrated good performance for automated drusen quantification on the 80-image validation set. Pixel-level metrics revealed a sensitivity of 82.93% ± 12.28%, specificity of 99.95% ± 0.07%, overall accuracy of 99.93% ± 0.09%, and precision of 61.83% ± 10.77%. The Dice coefficient was 0.697 ± 0.069, and intersection over union (IoU) was 0.539 ± 0.087. Object-level performance showed a mean absolute error of 2.19 ± 2.98 drusen per image, with 73.1% of images showing errors within ±2 drusen, indicating good spatial agreement between automated and manual segmentations. The high specificity and accuracy primarily reflect the extreme class imbalance, where drusen pixels constitute <0.1% of total image area. Bland-Altman analysis demonstrated excellent agreement between automated and manual quantification, with a mean bias of 0.47 drusen (95% limits of agreement: −8.32 to 7.37 drusen), and 97.5% (78/80) of measurements fell within the limits of agreement (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Inter-observer reliability for manual drusen counting was excellent, with an ICC of 0.944 (95% CI: 0.917−0.963) and Pearson correlation of r = 0.946 (P < 0.0001) between two independent raters (Supplementary Fig 5B), confirming high reproducibility of manual drusen quantification in late-phase ICGA images.

Topographic distribution differences of hard drusen

By applying such TWS classifier, we obtained drusen counts in nine quadrants across different age groups, the results are presented in Table 3 and visualized in a heatmap in Fig. 5. A significant increase in drusen counts was observed at ages of 50 s and 60 s in each quadrant (P < 0.05 for all). Interestingly, in the nasal and inferior quadrants, the onset of significant increases in drusen counts occurred earlier, with the first significant increase observed at the age of 40 s in the three inferior quadrants, including the inferior temporal, central inferior, and inferior nasal quadrants (P < 0.05 for all). The superior nasal quadrant showed its first significant increase at the age of 30 s (P = 0.013). Additionally, there was a trend of increasing drusen in the central nasal quadrant at the age of 40 s, although the P-value approached but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.092).

Correlation between baseline periphery drusen counts and nAMD progression

Among the 246 participants aged ≥50 years who completed imaging follow-up (mean follow-up duration: 26 ± 18 months), 49 eyes (19.9%) developed newly diagnosed nAMD. Comparison between progressors (n = 49) and non-progressors (n = 197) revealed significantly greater hard drusen counts in the central superior (P = 0.001) and superior nasal (P = 0.001), as shown in Table 6. Moreover, confluent hard drusen in the central superior quadrant were more frequent in progressors than in non‑progressors (22/54 vs 1/13, P = 0.024). Logistic regression analysis (Table 7) demonstrated that central superior hard drusen count was the only factor independently associated with progression to nAMD (OR: 1.324 per drusen, 95% CI: 1.013−1.731, P = 0.040), whereas age, sex, follow-up duration, and fellow-eye nAMD status at baseline showed no significant associations in this cohort. A sensitivity analysis was conducted in the subgroup of 198 eyes with complete data on clinical covariates, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, smoking history, and alcohol consumption (Supplementary Table 2). Among these 198 eyes, 40 (20.2%) progressed to nAMD. After forcing all available clinical covariates into the logistic regression model, both central superior and superior nasal hard drusen counts remained independently associated with nAMD progression (CS: OR = 1.455, 95% CI: 1.031–2.052, P = 0.033; SN: OR = 1.398, 95% CI: 1.043–1.874, P = 0.025).

Discussion

By integrating manual identification with machine learning, this hospital-based retrospective study of ICGA images from 1562 fellow eyes revealed that the prevalence and count of hard drusen increased with age. The most significant increase in drusen counts was commonly observed in the 50 s age group across all nine quadrants, with earlier occurrences in the nasal and inferior quadrants. Notably, the nasal quadrants consistently showed significantly higher drusen counts and the frequency of confluent type compared to the temporal quadrants, whereas no substantial differences were observed between the superior and inferior quadrants. Among all quadrants, central superior hard drusen counts consistently emerged as an independent correlate in all three analyses (macular hard drusen counts, macular soft drusen volume, and nAMD progression). It should be noted that the observed association with nAMD progression is exploratory and hypothesis-generating, constrained by the retrospective design, limited longitudinal subgroup, and lack of multimodal confirmation (e.g., colour fundus photography); whether peripheral drusen burden in particular quadrants constitutes a reliable prognostic marker merits prospective investigation in larger cohorts.

Drusen were first described by Franciscus Donders in 1855 and termed “drusen” by Heinrich Müller in 18565. It is now widely recognized that age-related declines in Bruch’s membrane hydraulic conductivity impair RPE-choroid nutrient and waste exchange, resulting in drusen formation26. Since larger drusen (>63−125 μm) serve as AMD diagnostic criteria, subjects with only small hard drusen have been consistently excluded or used as controls in previous research27–29. Such study designs provide limited information about the age-related and topographic distribution of hard drusen in otherwise normal eyes. Although small hard drusen may not significantly affect vision, their presence may signal early AMD risk, as previous research indicates that having 20 or more small hard drusen in the macular area is associated with a higher probability of developing soft drusen16,17. Due to the lack of normal eye samples with comprehensive fundus examinations, as well as the subjectivity of drusen assessment on fundus photography and autofluorescence imaging, no studies have yet demonstrated the correlation between peripheral and macular drusen in normal eyes.

Advances in machine learning and tools like ImageJ have improved image quantification efficiency and consistency. By marking objects in images to create a training dataset, a classifier can be refined through direct feedback and then applied to classify similar images30–32. The Trainable Weka Segmentation (TWS) plugin in FIJI has been widely applied across diverse fields, including mineralogy and microbiology33,34. In late-phase ICGA images, hard drusen, due to their high phospholipid content, bind effectively with ICG molecules, displaying well-defined hyperfluorescent regions5,14. This creates a stark contrast against the background of the surrounding normal chorioretinal tissue, which to some extent simplifies the training process, allowing for improved ROI segmentation results with relatively smaller sample sizes. We pioneered the application of TWS methodology to ophthalmic imaging, with comprehensive procedural details provided in the Methods section as a reference for future studies. Validation on 80 images yielded acceptable performance (Dice coefficient: 0.697), which is consistent with expectations for small lesion segmentation and supports its applicability for large-scale population-level drusen quantification in ICGA images acquired under standardised conditions. Conclusions drawn from this study reflect the performance of TWS on single-centre ICGA data and should be interpreted within this methodological context.

We observed age-dependent increases in peripheral hard drusen across all quadrants, with age 50 serving as a critical threshold. This finding parallels the current age-based AMD diagnostic criteria25. However, the age at which significant drusen accumulation occurs varied across peripheral quadrants, suggesting regional differences in aging rates. This indicates that drusen formation is influenced not only by age and systemic risk factors (including elevated serum triglycerides, reduced high-density lipoprotein [HDL] cholesterol16, and genetic variants such as CFH and ARMS2)35–38, but also by quadrant-specific anatomical features. Our study suggested that the nasal side is likely the earliest area to show drusen, while the temporal side is the latest. This nasal-predominant, temporal-sparing pattern is consistent with findings from several studies. Corbelli et al. and Lengyel et al. both reported that peripheral drusen frequently spare the temporal region11,39, while Munch et al. found drusen prevalence primarily on the nasal side of the optic disc17. However, Sharma et al., using ultra-widefield fundus imaging, reported no significant quadrant-specific distribution differences40. These discrepancies likely reflect methodological differences in imaging modalities and drusen definitions across studies. We employed late-phase ICGA, which offers superior sensitivity for drusen detection compared to conventional color fundus imaging15, potentially revealing early nasal drusen distribution patterns before they become visible on fundoscopy. Additionally, color fundus imaging often suffers from lower resolution in the nasal periphery due to stretching artifacts and defocus effects, which may underestimate nasal drusen burden.

We hypothesize that the greater distribution of hard drusen on the nasal side compared to the temporal side may reflect regional variations in lipoprotein clearance capacity. The widely accepted mechanism of drusen formation posits that RPE-secreted lipoproteins accumulate in the sub-RPE-basal lamina space due to impaired clearance across aged Bruch’s membrane (BrM)-choriocapillaris complex41,42. Therefore, structural and functional differences in the BrM-choriocapillaris complex across peripheral quadrants may be key determinants of regional drusen distribution patterns. BrM surface roughness around the optic disc increases significantly with age in the nasal, while temporal changes remain modest43, suggesting earlier structural remodeling. Furthermore, the landmark Beaver Dam Eye Study reported that pigmentary abnormalities were most likely to occur in the nasal or superior quadrants44, findings corroborated by Heussen et al.,45 indicating earlier metabolic stress in the nasal RPE-Bruch’s membrane complex. OCTA has enabled non-invasive assessment of peripheral choriocapillaris perfusion. Using 12 × 12 mm swept-source OCTA, Borrelli et al. demonstrated that the temporal region exhibited the highest perfusion in the near/mid periphery46. Studies employing wider scanning protocols (20 × 23 mm SS-OCTA) further revealed significantly higher choroidal thickness in the temporal versus nasal field47,48. Collectively, we propose that the nasal-predominant distribution of peripheral hard drusen observed in our study may result from the synergistic effects of (1) reduced choriocapillaris perfusion compromising metabolic waste clearance, and (2) earlier Bruch’s membrane structural remodeling disrupting trans-membrane lipoprotein transport. However, direct histopathological evidence is needed to validate these proposed mechanisms.

Prior studies have preliminarily investigated the correlation between peripheral hard drusen and AMD using subjective assessment methods. Sharma et al. and Seddon et al. reported significant associations between peripheral hard drusen and AMD presence based on UWF imaging and SD-OCT analyses38,40. Heussen et al. found a higher prevalence of abnormal peripheral autofluorescence patterns in AMD patients versus controls (70% vs. 18%)45. However, these studies focused on AMD eyes rather than normal eyes. The correlation between peripheral hard drusen and several macular pathologies was further explored in current study. Our results revealed that the central superior quadrant showed consistent associations with all forms of macular changes, including hard drusen counts, soft drusen (or drusenoid PED) volume, and progression to nAMD. This finding is difficult to explain given the scarcity of relevant mechanistic studies. Whether the superior quadrant shares similar pathophysiological mechanisms with the macula, or whether superior images provide better quality for drusen quantification remains uncertain. Nevertheless, our findings provide preliminary evidence that superior peripheral hard drusen counts may be associated with subsequent nAMD progression, suggesting the superior quadrant merits further investigation. A long-term prospective follow-up study is underway to track peripheral drusen evolution and their correlation with macular changes. We believe these longitudinal data will provide deeper insights into the prognostic significance of the superior quadrant and help elucidate the mechanisms linking peripheral and macular drusen patterns.

Multiple limitations need to be acknowledged. First, although longitudinal analysis was performed, the retrospective study design is subject to selection bias, and the limited follow-up data cannot capture the complete disease progression profile of the entire study population. Particularly in the soft drusen analysis, the small sample size with countable scattered hard drusen (n = 12) precluded further subgroup analyses or adjustment for potential confounding factors. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied across the nine quadrant-wise analyses. Accordingly, the reported associations should be regarded as exploratory and interpreted with caution. Second, the counting of hard drusen may not perfectly align with their actual quantity due to variations in fluorescence intensity of drusen with different internal components, potentially leading to the omission of some drusen with lower fluorescence intensity during machine recognition. However, the consistency between manual and machine counting reinforces the reliability of our data. Furthermore, the TWS model was trained and validated on single-centre ICGA images; performance on images from other acquisition systems or protocols has not been assessed, and quantitative findings should be interpreted within this methodological context. Additionally, eyes with confluent hard drusen were excluded from quantitative counting analyses, which may result in conservative estimates of the drusen–nAMD associations. Third, the older subjects in this study were limited due to significant refractive media opacities and low cooperation. Future studies should employ advanced imaging techniques with improved signal quality to include elderly patients who were excluded from our current analysis due to suboptimal ICGA image quality. Fourth, the longitudinal subgroup was limited to 246 followed eyes with 49 progression events and was retrospectively assembled. The association between superior peripheral hard drusen and nAMD progression thus warrants validation in well-designed prospective studies before its prognostic value can be fully established. Future prospective studies with comprehensive systemic and genetic profiling would provide more comprehensive insights. Fifth, the absence of matched color fundus or ultra-widefield retinal photographs represents an additional limitation. Although late-phase ICGA provides high-contrast visualization of hard drusen, the possibility that some hyperfluorescent spots detected by the algorithm may represent other retinal lesions cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies incorporating multimodal imaging would help validate the specificity of ICGA-based drusen detection across different quadrants and age groups. Despite these limitations, the strength of this research lies in its pioneering use of a TWS-based counting method to quantify hard drusen in normal eyes via ICGA images, revealing distribution characteristics across ages and quadrants, and providing valuable population-based evidence for future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, by training a machine learning model for drusen segmentation in late-phase ICGA using the TWS plugin, our study revealed three key findings. First, age 50 represents a critical demarcation point, with significant increases in drusen counts commonly observed across all quadrants. Second, the nasal quadrants demonstrated earlier and more prominent drusen accumulation compared to the temporal quadrants, suggesting regional differences in the aging process of the RPE-Bruch’s membrane-choriocapillaris complex. Third, the superior quadrant showed consistent associations with macular pathology. This exploratory finding, however, requires confirmation in larger prospective studies before any clinical utility can be established. In summary, these findings provide insights into the age-related and topographic distribution patterns of peripheral hard drusen and highlight the potential value of comprehensive peripheral retinal evaluation during ICGA examination.
