The interplay between ruminative thinking, stress and emotional eating
Authors: Tuba Yalçın, Seda Çiftçi, Elif Esra Ozturk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRuminative thinking, a repetitive focus on negative emotions, may prolong stress responses and promote maladaptive eating, yet stress, ruminative thinking, and eating behaviour are usually studied in isolation. This study examined factors independently associated with ruminative thinking, focusing on perceived stress, emotional eating, and emotional appetite in community-based adults.This cross-sectional study recruited 626 adults aged 18 years or older from public settings in Izmir, Türkiye, from January to May 2023. Face-to-face questionnaires used four validated instruments, the Ruminative Thought Scale (RTS), the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), the Turkish Emotional Eating Scale (TR-EES), and the Emotional Appetite Questionnaire (EMAQ), alongside sociodemographic and lifestyle data. Participants were categorised as ruminative or non-ruminative by the median RTS score. Multivariable binary logistic regression showed independent correlates of ruminative thinking, adjusting for gender, body mass index, smoking, alcohol use, perceived stress, emotional eating, and EMAQ subscale scores, with age included as a covariate rather than a primary variable of interest.Overall, 48.2% were ruminative and 35.5% emotional eaters. Both perceived stress and emotional eating were significantly associated with ruminative thinking in bivariate analyses. After adjustment, emotional eating stayed the strongest correlate, with emotional eaters showing over threefold higher odds (odds ratio 3.44, 95% confidence interval 2.28–5.19; p < 0.001), while perceived stress was no longer significant (odds ratio 1.36, 95% confidence interval 0.93–1.97; p = 0.111). Among the covariates, older age was independently associated with lower odds of ruminative thinking (odds ratio 0.96, 95% confidence interval 0.94–0.98; p = 0.001). Eating in positive situations was positively associated (odds ratio 1.09, 95% confidence interval 1.05–1.15; p < 0.001) and eating in negative situations inversely associated (odds ratio 0.96, 95% confidence interval 0.92–0.99; p = 0.047) with ruminative thinking.Emotional eating, rather than perceived stress, was the principal factor independently associated with ruminative thinking. Because the cross-sectional design cannot prove temporal order, longitudinal studies should figure out whether emotional eating precedes or follows ruminative thinking and clarify the unexpected inverse association between eating in negative situations and ruminative thinking. Such studies should add direct dietary intake assessment, which body mass index cannot capture, and include clinical populations to test whether targeting emotional eating interrupts the cycle linking stress to disordered eating.Keywords: Perceived stress, Nutritional status, Emotional appetite, Emotional eating behaviours, Ruminative thinking

Introduction

Stress is widely recognized as a pervasive challenge of modern life, encompassing complex emotional, cognitive, and behavioural responses to internal and external demands [1]. Although traditionally conceptualized through the “fight or flight” response, contemporary models emphasize the central role of cognitive appraisal in deciding whether stress is experienced as adaptive (eustress) or harmful [2]. However, the long-term psychological and physiological consequences of stress are not solely decided by first appraisal; rather, they may be influenced by the cognitive processes that follow the stressful event [3, 4].

One such cognitive process is ruminative thinking. Ruminative thinking refers to a repetitive and passive focus on the causes, meanings, and consequences of negative emotions [5–7]. Instead of facilitating problem-solving, ruminative thinking tends to prolong negative affect, impair effective coping strategies, and is consistently associated with depressive symptomatology [8]. In this context, ruminative thinking may function as a cognitive mechanism that sustains the stress response beyond the first triggering event.

Prolonged cognitive-emotional activation may manifest behaviourally, including through alterations in eating patterns. Emotional eating and emotional appetite describe the tendency to modify food intake in response to emotional states rather than physiological hunger cues [9]. Stress has been identified as a significant contributor to such eating behaviours, and emerging evidence suggests that maladaptive cognitive styles, including ruminative thinking, may be associated with dysregulated eating patterns [10, 11]. Individuals who engage in persistent ruminative thinking may be more likely to use eating as an emotion regulation strategy, potentially contributing to changes in nutritional status over time [12].

For conceptual clarity, it is important to distinguish these constructs. Stress refers to the perceived imbalance between environmental demands and personal coping resources [13]. Ruminative thinking represents a repetitive cognitive process characterized by persistent focus on distress and its possible causes and consequences [14]. Emotional eating describes the behavioural tendency to consume food in response to emotional states rather than physiological hunger [15], whereas emotional appetite reflects changes in appetite (increase or decrease) triggered by positive or negative emotional experiences [16]. Although these constructs are interrelated, they are distinct cognitive, emotional, behavioural, and physiological processes examined within an integrated framework in the present study.

Despite theoretical links between stress, ruminative thinking, and emotional eating, existing research has largely examined these constructs independently [17]. Empirical studies simultaneously evaluating cognitive processing patterns, perceived stress, and eating-related outcomes in adult population remain limited. Understanding these variables as part of an interconnected system may provide a more comprehensive perspective on how cognitive-emotional processes relate to health-related behaviours [18].

Although previous studies have documented associations between ruminative thinking and emotional eating, as well as between perceived stress and eating behaviours, these constructs have predominantly been examined in isolation [12]. Less attention has been given to their simultaneous and comparative contributions within a unified analytical framework. Examining these variables collectively allows for the identification of their independent predictive effects while accounting for potential overlap. Such an approach provides a more integrative understanding of whether emotional eating is more strongly associated with cognitive processes (ruminative thinking), contextual stress perception, or their combined influence.

The present study is conceptually grounded in cognitive-behavioural and emotion regulation frameworks. According to perseverative cognition theory, repetitive negative thinking such as ruminative thinking prolongs physiological and psychological stress responses beyond the initial stressor [19]. This sustained cognitive activation may increase vulnerability to maladaptive coping behaviours, including emotional eating [20]. From an emotion regulation perspective, individuals who engage in ruminative thinking may experience heightened negative affect and reduced regulatory flexibility, thereby increasing the likelihood of using food as a strategy to modulate emotional distress [21]. Within this framework, perceived stress is conceptualized as a contextual trigger, ruminative thinking as a maladaptive cognitive process that amplifies stress experience, and emotional eating as a behavioural outcome of impaired emotion regulation [18].

So, the present study aimed to examine the factors independently associated with ruminative thinking within a cognitive-emotional framework. Ruminative thinking was operationalized as the primary outcome variable. It was hypothesized that: (H1) Individuals with higher levels of perceived stress would be more likely to show ruminative thinking. (H2) Individuals with emotional eating tendencies would be more likely to show ruminative thinking. (H3) Emotional appetite characteristics (EMAQ subdimensions) would be significantly associated with ruminative thinking. Age, gender, BMI, smoking status, and alcohol use were included as covariates to control for potential confounding effects.

Material & methods

Participants

This study is intended as a cross-sectional, descriptive research. Prior to beginning the study, our primary aim was to reach a minimum sample size of 385. This calculation was figured out with a margin of error set at 0.05 within a 95% confidence interval, using the G Power software. The sample size estimation was conducted with reference to an unknown population sample. The study was carried out in the province of İzmir and its districts from January to May 2023 with 626 adult individuals aged 18 and older. People were randomly selected from various public places, such as universities, parks and shopping centres and a face-to-face questionnaire was used to collect data. No financial or other benefits were offered to participants, and their participation was completely voluntary. People with disabilities or chronic illnesses, pregnant and lactating women, people with psychiatric diagnosis or using psychiatric medication were excluded. Individuals with diagnosed psychiatric disorders and clinically diagnosed eating disorders were excluded to reduce potential confounding effects and to examine the associations among ruminative thinking, perceived stress, and emotional eating within a non-clinical community sample. Participants were recruited from public areas within a single metropolitan city. Therefore, the sample reflects a community-based urban adult population rather than a nationally representative sample.

Procedure and measures

Sociodemographic data was collected from the participants in order to understand their characteristics. The Ruminative Thought Scale was used to assess ruminative thinking styles. The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) was employed to measure participants’ stress levels. Additionally, emotional eating behaviours were examined using the Turkish Emotional Eating Scale (TR-EES), and the impact of emotional states on nutritional habits was evaluated using the Emotional Appetite Questionnaire (EMAQ).

The research meticulously established inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure the study’s findings’ reliability and validity. The study enrolled volunteers aged 19 to 64 with no psychiatric diagnosis and not using psychiatric medication (this condition was asked participants by a question in section one), narrowing the focus to the among adults. To support sample homogeneity and minimize potential confounding factors, individuals with psychiatric diagnoses such as bulimia nervosa, anorexia nervosa, and binge eating disorder were excluded. Additionally, pregnant and lactating women were excluded due to the potential influence of hormonal and physiological changes on emotional eating behaviours and nutritional states. On the other hand, we included all the voluntarily participants who did not have exclusion criteria.

Researchers collected study data using a comprehensive, in-person questionnaire consisting of five sections. The first section encompassed inquiries aimed at finding the general information of the individuals, including their psychiatric illness status, medication use, gender, age, education level, weight, height, and habits related to smoking, alcohol consumption.

In section two, The Ruminative Thought Scale (RTS), developed by Brinker and Dozois [22], served as a measurement tool specifically designed to assess ruminative thinking as a distinct cognitive process, independent of psychopathologies. Karatepe et al. [23] conducted a validity and reliability study of the Turkish version. A Likert-type scale with seven response options, ranging from ‘1’ (not describing me at all) to ‘7’ (describing me very well), was used, and the scale consisted of twenty items. The total score did not write down a particular psychopathological condition but rather highlighted individuals’ tendencies towards ruminative thinking. In the validated study, Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was reported as 0.907; in the present study, it was calculated as 0.961, writing down excellent internal consistency.

In part three, the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) was used to gauge stress levels experienced by the participants. Developed by Cohen et al., PSS consisted of a total of fourteen items and was designed to assess an individual’s perceived stress in various situations. Eskin et al. established Turkish validity and reliability of scale [24, 25]. Among items, 7 were positively worded (4th -7th, 9th -10th, and 13th ), and their scores were reverse-coded. The scores on PSS ranged from zero to fifty-six points, with higher scores signify increased levels of perceived stress. In the validated study, Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was reported as 0.870; in the present study, it was calculated as 0.888, showing excellent internal consistency.

In part four, Turkish Emotional Eating Scale (TR-EES) was used to examine the emotional eating behaviours of the study participants. The ‘Turkish Emotional Eating Scale,’ developed by Bilgen [26] in 2018, was employed. This Likert-type scale formed 30 items, with participants responding on a 5-point scale. The scale was structured into four factors: ‘Eating in Situations of Tension,’ ‘Eating to Cope with Negative Emotions,’ ‘Self-Control,’ and ‘Control Over Stimulus.’ The scale yielded scores ranging from 30 to 150, with higher scores showing more pronounced emotional eating behaviours. Following reliability analysis, reverse scoring was applied to three items (26th, 28th, and 29th). In the validated study, Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was reported as 0.959; in the present study, it was calculated as 0.951, showing excellent internal consistency.

In section five, Emotional Appetite Questionnaire (EMAQ) was used to calculate the influence of individuals’ emotional states on their nutritional status. The study employed the EMAQ developed by Nolan et al. [27]. The questionnaire gauged the impact of emotions on appetite status using a scale ranging from ‘less’ (scored as 1–4) to ‘the same’ (scored as 5) to ‘more’ (scored as 6–9). The EMAQ comprised two subscales: one assessing emotional eating in response to negative/positive emotions (14 items) and the other evaluating emotional eating in negative/positive situations (8 items). The scale did not have a cut-off score for emotional eating; instead, it explored the emotions and situations in which emotional eating may be influential. The Turkish validity and reliability of the EMAQ were examined by Demirel et al. [28]. In the validated study, Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was reported as 0.730; in the present study, it was calculated as 0.944, writing down excellent internal consistency.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from İzmir Katip Çelebi University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee before the application (22.12.2022/0606). Participants were provided with information about the study, and those who willingly chose to take part were requested to endorse a written consent form in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. As several variables deviated from normal distribution, non-parametric tests were applied for group comparisons. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two independent groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis H test was employed for comparisons among three or more groups. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), as proper. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

For analytical purposes, participants were categorized into ruminative and non-ruminative groups based on the median RTS score, and into excessive and normal stress groups based on the median PSS score. Although dichotomization of continuous variables has methodological limitations, it may be justified in specific research contexts when transparently reported and theoretically supported [29, 30].

To find factors independently associated with ruminative thinking, multivariate binary logistic regression analysis was performed using the enter method. Since ruminative thinking was operationalized as a dichotomous outcome variable (ruminative vs. non-ruminative), logistic regression was considered statistically proper to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Logistic regression does not assume normality of predictor variables or homoscedasticity, as parameter estimation is based on maximum likelihood rather than ordinary least squares [31].

The selection of independent variables was guided by the theoretical framework of the study and earlier literature addressing the relationships between ruminative thinking, perceived stress, emotional eating, and sociodemographic factors. Age, gender, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, perceived stress, emotional eating classification (TR-EES), and EMAQ subscale scores were entered into the model as independent variables.

Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) prior to the regression analysis. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and the Nagelkerke R² statistic. Given the cross-sectional design, the findings reflect statistical associations rather than causal or predictive relationships.

A total of 626 individuals took part in the research. Among whom 302 (48.2%) were identified as ruminative (RTS score ≥ 64), while 324 (51.8%) were classified as non-ruminative (RTS score < 64) in sample-based categorisation. Among all the participants, 379 (60.5%) were male, and 247 (39.5%) were female. In the ruminative group (RTS ≥ 64), the prevalence of excessive stress was found to be 56.0% (n = 169), compared to 44.0% (n = 133) for normal stress. No significant differences were found between stress status and gender or education level in this group (p < 0.05). However, a significant difference was found between stress status and smoking (p = 0.030) and alcohol use (p = 0.022). The rate of smokers and alcohol users was higher in the extremely stressed group. In the non-ruminative group (RTS < 64), the prevalence of excessive stress was 46.0% (n = 149), while the prevalence of normal stress was 54.0% (n = 175). In this group, a significant difference was found between gender and stress level (p = 0.001) the rate of excessive stress was higher in men. No significant difference was found between educational level and stress status (p < 0.05). While no significant difference was seen between smoking and stress, a significant difference was seen between alcohol consumption and stress.

Table 2 presents the age and anthropometric measurements of the study participants. The median age of the participants was 28.31 years, and the median BMI was 23.18 kg/m². According to the BMI classification, 6.1% of participants in the general sample were underweight, 60.1% were of normal weight, 24.9% were overweight and 8.9% were obese. In the ruminative group (RTS ≥ 64), the median age of individuals with high stress levels (PSS ≥ 31) was 24 years, compared to 23 years for those with low stress levels (PSS < 31). A statistically significant difference in age was found between the two groups (p = 0.031). In the same group, the median BMI was 24.03 kg/m² in the high-stress group and 22.03 kg/m² in the low-stress group; this difference was also significant (p = 0.004). However, when the distribution according to BMI categories was examined, no significant difference was found between the high- and low-stress groups (p = 0.090). In the non-ruminative group (RTS < 64), no significant difference was found between individuals with high and low stress levels (p = 0.773).

In the study, 35.5% of the participants were identified as emotional eaters. In the ruminative group, 50.9% of individuals with high stress levels showed emotional eating behaviour. In the low stress group, this figure was 46.6%. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.267). Similarly, no significant difference in emotional eating behaviour was found between the high- and low-stress groups in the non-ruminative group (p = 0.550). However, there was a highly significant difference in emotional eating behaviour between the high and normal stress groups in both the ruminative and non-ruminative groups (p < 0.001 for both comparisons). Among ruminative individuals, the rate of emotional eating was higher in the high stress group, while non-emotional eating behaviour was more common among non-ruminative individuals.

No significant difference was found in the total and subscale scores of the TR-EES according to stress level in the ruminative group (p < 0.05). Similarly, no significant difference was seen in the TR-EES total and subscale scores according to stress level in the non-ruminative group (p < 0.05). However, in both the ruminative and non-ruminative groups, there was a highly significant difference in TR-EES total and subscale scores between emotional and non-emotional eaters (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). Emotional eaters had significantly higher TR-EES total and subscale scores.

Examining the EMAQ subscales revealed that individuals in the ruminative group with high stress levels had significantly different EMAQ-NE (p = 0.048) and EMAQ-PE (p = 0.028) scores compared to the low stress group. However, no significant differences were found in the EMAQ-NS (p = 0.284) and EMAQ-PS (p = 0.301) scores. In the non-ruminative group, significant differences were found in EMAQ-PE (p = 0.001) and EMAQ-PS (p = 0.048) scores according to stress level. No significant differences were found in EMAQ-NE (p = 0.414) or EMAQ-NS (p = 0.734). Furthermore, in both ruminative groups, there were highly significant differences between emotional and non-emotional eaters in terms of total EMAQ and subscale scores (Table 3).

Discussion

Our research explored the relationship between perceived stress, nutritional status, emotional appetite, and emotional eating behaviours in an adult population, with a specific focus on ruminative thinking levels. In our study, 48.2% of participants were classified as ruminative, a proportion slightly lower than non-ruminative individuals (51.8%). Although earlier studies suggest that ruminative individuals may form approximately 20–30% of the general population [32, 33], prevalence estimates may vary depending on population characteristics and conceptual definitions.

Consistent with prior literature, descriptive findings shown that females were more likely to engage in ruminative thinking than males [33, 34]. Among participants with normal stress levels, a greater proportion of females were classified as non-ruminative, while among non-ruminators, excess stress appeared more frequent in males. These gender-related patterns are broadly consistent with research suggesting that women tend to report higher levels of internalizing symptoms [33, 35]. However, these observations were based on descriptive and bivariate analyses. It is important to note that formal interaction effects between gender and other predictors were not examined in the regression model. Gender was included as a covariate to adjust for potential confounding effects, but the present study was not designed to test moderating effects. Therefore, interpretations of gender differences should be considered exploratory. Future research may receive help from explicitly testing interaction terms to examine potential gender-specific pathways.

Additionally, a higher proportion of non-ruminative individuals with normal stress levels had higher education levels, suggesting an association between education and adaptive coping patterns. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of causal relationships.

In this study, individuals with a ruminative tendency were found to be more likely to experience excessive stress (p < 0.05). However, among individuals reporting high levels of stress, a substantial proportion of alcohol consumers were non-ruminative. This finding suggests that some individuals may rely on behavioural coping strategies, particularly alcohol use, rather than cognitive strategies such as rumination when dealing with stress [36]. The finding of this study is that high levels of ruminative thinking exert a strong and pervasive influence on both emotional eating patterns and emotional appetite, largely overshadowing the independent effect of perceived stress. Regardless of whether participants experienced high or normal levels of stress, the ruminative group consistently demonstrated significantly higher TR-EES and EMAQ scores than their non-ruminative counterparts. This finding can be explained within the framework of cognitive load and maladaptive emotion regulation theories. The ruminative thought process leads to the depletion of cognitive resources required for self-control and emotion regulation, thereby increasing the likelihood that individuals will engage in emotional eating as a means of coping with negative emotions [37]. Our findings indicate that the emergence of dysfunctional eating behaviours is determined not only by the presence of stress itself but also by the individual’s repetitive and negative thinking patterns in response to stress. Therefore, the observed association between ruminative thinking and emotional eating suggests that cognitive-emotional processes may be relevant factors to consider when examining emotional eating behaviours, in addition to perceived stress. Future longitudinal studies are needed to determine the temporal direction of this relationship and whether one process contributes to the development or maintenance of the other.

Ruminative thinking, characterized by the persistent and intrusive fixation on negative thoughts and emotions, has been identified as a factor linked to problematic eating behaviours, notably emotional eating [18]. Additionally, individuals’ perceived stress levels have been shown to influence their eating habits, with emotional responses rather than physiological hunger often guiding dietary choices, potentially leading to long-term effects on nutritional status [38]. Recent research has underscored the significance of investigating the day-to-day connection between stress and different sides of eating behaviour, particularly in young individuals. Daily stress levels have been found to have a significant association with motivations related to eating and feelings of hunger, emphasizing the need for a nuanced examination of stress’s impact on eating behaviour parameters [39]. Although there is a positive association between perceived stress and emotional eating, it is important to note that high stress levels are not directly linked to an individual’s BMI through eating behaviours [40]. In line with these findings, perceived stress showed significant associations with ruminative thinking in bivariate analyses in the present study. However, it did not keep statistical significance in the multivariate regression model after adjustment for emotional eating and other covariates. This suggests that the effect of perceived stress may overlap with emotional eating or related affective constructs included in the model. It is possible that perceived stress runs indirectly or shares variance with other psychological variables rather than functioning as an independent predictor of ruminative thinking. Future studies should examine the potential mechanisms and pathways underlying these associations to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the observed relationships.

We found that participants who showed both ruminative and excess stress had higher BMI values compared to those with normal stress levels (p < 0.05). It showed that those with a higher level of ruminative thinking had an increased total calorie intake [41]. This finding suggests that individuals experiencing both ruminative thinking and high stress may be at greater risk of weight-related issues, which could call for further investigation and targeted interventions.

This study revealed that individuals characterized as ruminative displayed a higher propensity for emotional eating in comparison to their non-ruminative counterparts, irrespective of their stress levels. Furthermore, this research uncovered that ruminative exhibited elevated scores across all the subscales of the TR-EES when contrasted with non-ruminative. These results are in agreement with earlier studies that have explored link between ruminative thinking and emotional eating [18]. The heightened perceived stress has been associated with unfavourable eating behaviours, particularly in individuals who are overweight or obese [42]. Emotional eating, a common response to strong emotions, can have detrimental effects on weight management efforts [43]. Furthermore, it’s worth acknowledging that eating disorders, characterized by severe and persistent disruptions in eating behaviours, can be influenced by emotional factors and stress [44]. These findings emphasize the complex interplay between emotions, stress, and eating behaviours, highlighting the need for a comprehensive understanding of these relationships in the context of overall health and well-being. Consequently, these results shed light on how ruminative thinking, stress, and emotional eating are interconnected and contribute to our understanding of their impact on individuals’ eating habits and health outcomes.

Table 4 presents the outcomes of a binary logistic regression analysis aimed at finding variables that predict ruminative thinking. The results show that, of the variables included in the model, age, the total score on the TR-EES, the EMAQ-NS, and the EMAQ-PS were significant independent predictors of ruminative thinking. Higher TR-EES and EMAQ-PS scores significantly increased the likelihood of ruminative thinking, whereas older age and higher EMAQ-NS scores were associated with a lower likelihood. These findings are consistent with literature indicating that emotional eating may be associated with both negative and positive emotions [45]. It also aligns with meta-analysis findings that ruminative thinking is related to eating behaviours within the context of emotion regulation processes [46]. However, it was decided that gender, smoking, alcohol consumption, perceived stress level, BMI, EMAQ-NE (negative emotions), and EMAQ-PE (positive emotions) did not have a statistically significant effect on ruminative thinking. These results suggest that ruminative thinking is associated with emotional eating tendencies rather than with demographic characteristics or general stress levels. Overall, the model findings suggest that emotional eating behaviour (including eating in response to positive situations) is related to ruminative thinking.

This observation aligns with prior research highlighting the association between ruminative thinking and emotional eating. The findings may have potential implications for preventive and supportive psychological approaches. In particular, the observed relationship between emotional eating and ruminative thinking suggests that maladaptive eating patterns may coexist with repetitive cognitive processes. However, given the cross-sectional design, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that interventions targeting emotional eating would directly reduce ruminative thinking. Longitudinal and experimental research is needed before clinical implications can be set up.

The present study has several strengths. The relatively large sample size (n = 626) allowed for a comprehensive examination of perceived stress, ruminative thinking, emotional appetite, and emotional eating behaviours. Ethical approval was obtained from a recognized ethics committee, and validated measurement instruments (RTS, PSS, TR-EES) were used. Statistical procedures proper to the study design were applied to examine the associations among variables.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow conclusions on temporal directionality. Although logistic regression models were used to estimate statistical associations, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of prediction, risk, or causal influence. Longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify the temporal and causal relationships among ruminative thinking, perceived stress, and emotional eating.

Second, the study did not directly assess dietary intake or nutrient consumption. Although BMI was examined in relation to psychological variables, BMI is an anthropometric indicator and does not provide detailed information about dietary patterns or nutrient intake. Therefore, conclusions on specific dietary mechanisms linking emotional processes to weight-related outcomes should be interpreted cautiously.

Third, participants were recruited from public spaces within a single city, which may limit generalizability to broader adult populations. Cultural, regional, and socio-demographic differences may influence stress feeling, ruminative thinking, and eating behaviours. Therefore, findings should be interpreted within the context of this community-based sample. Future multi-centre or nationally representative studies would enhance external validity.

Fourth, individuals with psychiatric diagnoses and eating disorders were excluded to increase sample homogeneity and reduce clinical confounding. However, this limits ecological validity, as ruminative thinking, perceived stress, and emotional eating are highly prevalent in clinical populations. Therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to psychiatric or eating disorder groups.

Fifth, the cut-off points used for RTS and PSS were derived from the sample distribution rather than set up clinical thresholds. These categories should be interpreted as analytical groupings rather than diagnostic classifications.

Finally, the use of median splits to categorize continuous variables may have reduced variability and statistical precision. Although this approach supported logistic regression modelling and interpretability, future studies may receive help from keeping continuous data structures to preserve statistical power and analytical accuracy.

Conclusion

Ruminative thinking was statistically associated with emotional eating behaviours in this community-based adult sample. Furthermore, although perceived stress was significant in bivariate analyses, its effect diminished when emotional eating was included in the model, suggesting that part of the association attributed to rumination may be explained through emotional eating behaviours rather than stress per se, thereby offering a novel perspective to the existing literature. These findings contribute to the growing body of literature examining the interplay between cognitive and emotional processes and eating behaviours. However, given the cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported measures, the results should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Although BMI and nutritional status were examined in relation to psychological variables, the absence of direct dietary intake assessment limits conclusions on specific nutritional mechanisms. Future longitudinal and experimental research is needed to clarify temporal relationships and potential underlying pathways among perceived stress, ruminative thinking, emotional eating, and weight-related outcomes.

The complex relationships among stress, ruminative thinking, and eating behaviours highlight the importance of interdisciplinary research approaches involving psychology, nutrition, and public health. Although these findings may inform preventive considerations in non-clinical adult populations, further research is needed before their clinical relevance can be established.
