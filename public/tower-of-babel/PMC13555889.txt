Reduced metabolic energy cost in bounce squats driven by lower vasti contribution during the eccentric phase
Authors: Hoon Kim, Taewoong Kong, Basil B. Achermann, Gyeongeun Lee, Silvio R. Lorenzetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe back squat is a fundamental resistance exercise, yet technical variations such as bounce squats may substantially alter energetic demands. While previous research highlighted mechanical and neuromuscular effects of the stretch-shortening cycle, the phase-specific and muscle-specific metabolic consequences of bounce squats remain unclear. To inform training strategies aimed at improving movement economy or managing fatigue during high-volume training, this study examined how bounce influences whole-body and muscle-level metabolic energy cost across different loads using musculoskeletal simulation.MethodsNine resistance-trained adults performed bounce and standard squats under five loading conditions (0-100% body weight) while motion capture, ground reaction forces, and surface EMG were recorded. Musculoskeletal modeling was used to estimate instantaneous metabolic power, integrated to compute phase-specific energy costs.ResultsTwo-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that bounce squats reduced total metabolic energy cost by approximately 10.5% compared with standard squats (p < 0.001), with the most pronounced effect during the eccentric phase (25.5% lower, p < 0.001). Muscle-level analyses indicated that the vasti (VAS) consistently exhibited lower metabolic cost in bounce squats across loads during descent, highlighting their central role in energetic savings. In contrast, during the concentric phase, VAS and gluteus maximus costs scaled with external load, with no detectable benefit of bounce. Other lower body muscles showed statistical effects of squat type, but their absolute contributions were minimal.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that the energetic benefit of bounce squats is phase- and muscle-specific, reflecting the reduced metabolic cost of eccentric VAS control and tendon recoil during rapid reversal. Bounce squats may therefore enhance movement economy without diminishing mechanical loading, offering practical value for athletic performance and rehabilitation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01819-1.

Background

The back squat is one of the most widely prescribed resistance exercises in both athletic training and rehabilitation because it simultaneously loads the ankle, knee, and hip in a closed-chain movement that develops strength, power, and functional performance [1]. Variations in squat execution, including stance width, foot angle, depth, and bar position, substantially alter joint moments and muscle recruitment [1, 2].

Although research has mainly emphasized mechanical output, technical choices also affect metabolic demand, which can exceed 20 kcal/min near 80% of 1 repetition maximum (RM) [3], underscoring the need to understand how technique influences economy. Squats repeatedly can elicit oxygen uptake values approaching or even matching an individual’s VO2max, highlighting the prominent contribution of aerobic metabolism during sustained efforts [4, 5]. Moreover, previous studies demonstrated that modifying movement speed or eccentric phase duration can shift aerobic and anaerobic energy balance [6]. Similarly, recent work highlights that changes in exercise execution and loading strategy can meaningfully alter neuromuscular control and internal demands, even when external mechanical conditions are comparable [7]. These findings suggest that small adjustments in squat execution may meaningfully affect energetic costs.

One technique that may influence these energetic differences is the ‘bounce’ squat, in which the lifter transitions rapidly from the eccentric to concentric phase utilizing a spring-like effect [8]. Early experimental comparisons indicated lower oxygen consumption and higher efficiency when bounce was used [9]. The proposed rationale aligns with the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC), where rapid eccentric-concentric coupling enhances performance through elastic recoil from tendons and passive muscle components (e.g., aponeuroses, fascia), cross-bridge dynamics, and reflex contributions [10]. In vivo studies confirmed that tendons store and return energy during walking [11], and that tendon stiffness is strongly associated with individual differences in SSC [12]. By utilizing this passive elastic recoil to contribute to mechanical work, the active metabolic demand on contractile elements is theoretically reduced. Together, these findings suggest that incorporating bounce at the bottom of a squat may reduce energy requirements. However, SSC benefits are not uniform, as studies of jump and countermovement squats showed that load alters both mechanical and energetic outcomes, with optimal benefits shifting across loads [13–15].

At the muscle level, contraction type and activation also affect metabolic cost. Eccentric contractions produce high force at lower oxygen cost than concentric contraction [16] with eccentric work elongating fascicles and concentric work increasing pennation angle [17] reflecting different sarcomere adaptations. These characteristics suggest that bounce squats may reduce metabolic energy cost in muscles that are mostly active during squats, such as the quadriceps. Importantly, analyzing eccentric, concentric, and total metabolic costs separately offers clearer insight into phase-specific energetics essential for training and rehabilitation. However, there is no study directly quantifying how bounce versus standard squats influence whole-body and muscle- and phase-specific energy expenditure across loads. Musculoskeletal modeling offers a powerful solution by integrating motion and force data with energy models [18, 19] to estimate metabolic cost [20]. These tools have revealed how task constraints, assistance, and strategy affect energy use [21–23].

Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to determine whether bounce squats reduce whole-body metabolic cost compared with standard squats, to examine how that effect varies with external load, and to identify which phases and muscles contribute to any energy savings. We tested three specific hypotheses: (1) bounce squats will lower whole-body metabolic energy expenditure compared with standard squats; (2) the magnitude of this reduction will vary depending on the external load condition; and (3) these energy savings will be muscle-specific, concentrated in the muscles dominant during the bottom phase, rather than being uniformly distributed across the lower limb.

Methods

Participants

We recruited nine resistance-trained adults from the university community and local training facilities (Table 1). Inclusion criteria were: (1) at least 3 years of resistance training experience, (2) a minimum training frequency of 3 sessions per week, (3) a 1RM back squat of at least 1.5 times body mass, and (4) participation in bodybuilding or weightlifting competitions within the previous year. All had more than 3 years of resistance training experience and competed in bodybuilding or weightlifting within the previous year. Participants reported Tegner activity scores less than 4 (scale 0–10), indicating participation primarily in low-impact activities without pivoting or contact sports [24]. This criterion was chosen to minimize the risk of underlying joint pathology often associated with high-impact sports, while targeting individuals specialized in sagittal-plane resistance training. They had no lower-limb injury in the prior six months, and had no history of steroid injections. They were asked to avoid strenuous lower body exercises for at least 72 h before the experiment. This study employed a cross-sectional, within-subject repeated measures design. Data collection was conducted at the University’s laboratory between October 2024 and January 2025. The protocol was approved by the University’s Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants via Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental setup

Whole body kinematics were captured with 9 motion capture cameras (Prime13W, OptiTrack, USA) sampling at 200 Hz. A 61-marker set was placed over standard anatomical landmarks and on the barbell. Ground reaction forces (GRFs) were recorded from two embedded force plates at 1000 Hz (BMS400600, AMTI). Marker trajectories and GRFs were low-pass filtered using a 4th order Butterworth filter with 14 Hz cutoff chosen by residual analysis [25]. Electromyography (EMG) data was collected at 3000 Hz (DTS, Noraxon) from gluteus maximus, gluteus medius, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, biceps femoris, tibialis anterior, and medial gastrocnemius following SENIAM guidelines after skin preparation [26]. Kinematic and kinetic data were recorded using the motion capture software (Motive, OptiTrack, USA), while EMG data were recorded using MR3 software (Noraxon, USA). Synchronization between systems was achieved via a hardware trigger signal sent from the motion capture system to the EMG system. The EMG signal was detrended, subtracted by a mean value (i.e., offset removal), filtered with 4th order Butterworth bandpass filter with cutoff frequencies of 20–450 Hz, and smoothed with a 4th order Butterworth low-pass filter with cutoff frequency of 10 Hz.

Experimental protocols

First, participants performed maximum voluntary isometric contraction of muscles in which EMG sensors were attached to record each muscle’s maximum activation. The activation values were used to normalize the EMG signals and the normalized EMG signals were compared to the muscle activations driven by musculoskeletal simulation to qualitatively assess the plausibility and temporal agreement of activation patterns [27]. After a warm-up, participants performed standard and bounce squats across five load conditions (0, 25, 50, 75, and 100% body weight (BW)) with self-selected stance and depth established during the warm-up. Foot positions were preserved using tape markers on the force plates, and participants were instructed to consistently replicate the practiced depth across all trials. In the bounce condition, participants descended normally but, upon reaching the bottom of the squat, reversed direction as quickly as possible without pausing, thereby performing a bouncing motion. This distinction was standardized behaviorally through verbal instruction rather than by enforcing specific kinematic thresholds (e.g., velocity, acceleration, or joint angle criteria). This approach was chosen to maximize ecological validity and to reflect natural training strategies commonly used by resistance-trained athletes. Squat depth was self-selected and instructed to be as deep as possible during familiarization and warm-up trials, and participants were instructed to maintain a consistent depth across all load conditions and squat types. No explicit kinematic constraints (e.g., hip flexion angle or knee angle thresholds) were imposed to regulate squat depth, and depth consistency was monitored visually. In contrast, during standard squats, participants were instructed to descend in a controlled manner to maximum depth, pause briefly at the bottom, and then ascend without using any bouncing motion. For each squat type and load condition, participants completed two sets of two trials (four trials in total) in randomized order, with a 2-minute rest between trials. A metronome (0.5 Hz) was used to standardize movement velocity, with 2 s each for descent and ascent. Compliance with this cadence was visually monitored during data collection and subsequently verified using the temporal data from the barbell marker trajectory, which confirmed a distinct, rapid reversal in the bounce condition compared to the standard condition. Trials deviating by more than 0.25 s from the target phase duration were excluded from analysis.

Data processing

The 12.5th percentile of barbell height data was used to define the start and end frames of each squat cycle. This position-based threshold was selected over a velocity-based threshold to ensure consistent and robust detection of the movement cycle across all trials, minimizing potential errors caused by postural sway or noise during the standing phase. The descent phase was defined as the eccentric phase, and the ascent phase was defined as the concentric phase, based on the vertical trajectory of the barbell, separated by the transition frame corresponding to the lowest vertical position of the barbell. We analyzed the data in OpenSim [20]. We first scaled the generic model [28], developed for larger flexion motions, to create subject-specific model with anthropometric data and marker data recorded during static trial. To address the discrepancy between the strength of generic musculoskeletal models (often derived from cadaveric data) and the high force requirements of weighted squats in resistance-trained adults, we adjusted maximum isometric muscle forces. We increased maximum isometric muscle forces by multiplying a constant multiplier of “2” and a regression-driven subject-specific multiplier using ratio of muscle volume between a generic model and each subject [29]. This scaling approach was necessary to prevent unrealistic muscle saturation and ensure simulation convergence, as generic force values were insufficient to generate the required joint moments for the heavy loading conditions (up to 100% BW). Also, we added the bar segment in the model with a “cylinder” inertia property calculation. We used inverse kinematics to calculate joint angles, residual reduction algorithm to improve dynamic consistency by adjusting kinematics and model properties, and computed muscle control (CMC) to estimate muscle excitations/activations and forces. To ensure our simulation results, simulated muscle activations obtained from CMC were qualitatively compared with normalized EMG signals. Finally, we used the Analyze tool with Umberger’s metabolic cost model to estimate instantaneous muscle power [18]. This model was selected over others (e.g., Bhargava et al.) because it explicitly accounts for the distinct metabolic energy expenditure of concentric (positive work) versus eccentric (negative work) muscle actions, which is critical for testing our hypothesis regarding energy savings during the descent phase. All outcomes were normalized to body mass (excluding external load), and muscle powers were summed to obtain total (i.e., whole-body) metabolic power. Similarly, for muscle groups reported in the results (e.g., VAS), the metabolic costs of the constituent muscles (vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, and vastus intermedius) were summed. Finally, we numerically integrated both individual muscle and total power to derive corresponding energy costs (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

The metabolic energetic outcome measures were analyzed in three phases: entire, eccentric, and concentric phases. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test with Marsaglia and Lilliefors corrections [30]. All variables satisfied the normality assumption (p > 0.05). Multiple two-way repeated-measures (ANOVAs) were conducted for total and muscle-specific metabolic energy costs across squat type and load conditions. The independent variables were squat types and load conditions. When interaction effects or main effects for load conditions were found, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni correction [31]. Statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB R2024b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). We utilized the “fitrm” and “ranova” functions from the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox to conduct the repeated measures ANOVA and “multcompare” for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Alpha value was set to 0.05. In addition, we calculated effect sizes (partial eta-squared, η2p, and Cohen’s d) for two-way repeated measures ANOVA and post-hoc test.

Results

Total metabolic energy cost

A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA on total metabolic energy cost during entire phase of squats showed a non-significant interaction (F(4,32) = 1.85, p = 0.182, η2p = 0.188, mean square error (MSE) = 4.15), a significant main effect for the squat type (F(1,8) = 38.12, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.827, MSE = 10.09), and a significant main effect for the load condition (F(4,32) = 16.96, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.680, MSE = 22.88). Across all conditions, bounce squats required approximately 10.5% less energy than standard squats (Cohen’s d range = 0.35–1.38). In addition, total metabolic energy cost during entire phase of squats increased with loads, with descriptive grand means of 35.34, 33.21, 35.88, 38.63, and 45.22 J/kg for BW00, BW25, BW50, BW75, and BW100, respectively (e.g., BW100 > BW00 by 27.9%) (Fig. 2).

Analysis of eccentric-phase energy cost showed no interaction (F(4,32) = 0.73, p = 0.533, η2p = 0.083, MSE = 1.29), a significant main effect for the squat type (F(1,8) = 61.87, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.885, MSE = 7.28), and a significant main effect for the load condition (F(4,32) = 3.64, p = 0.047, η2p = 0.313, MSE = 6.72). Across load conditions, bounce squats required approximately 25.5% lower eccentric energy than standard squats (Cohen’s d range = 0.99–1.89). Although the main effect for the load condition showed significance, post-hoc with Bonferroni correction did not exhibit significant differences in any of pairs of load conditions (Fig. 2).

For the concentric phase, statistical testing showed no interaction (F(4,32) = 1.67, p = 0.212, η2p = 0.172, MSE = 2.16) and no main effect for the squat Type (F(1,8) = 0.58, p = 0.470, η2p = 0.067, MSE = 5.57), but a main effect for the load condition (F(4,32) = 39.24, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.830, MSE = 7.33). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons confirmed that the concentric energy cost increased as the loads increased regardless of the squat type (Fig. 2).

Muscle metabolic energy cost during the entire phase of squats

VAS (F(4,32) = 14.73, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.648, MSE = 0.17) and TibAnt (F(4,32) = 3.27, p = 0.023, η2p = 0.290, MSE = 0.02) exhibited significant interactions. Post-hoc analyses showed that metabolic cost was consistently lower during bounce squats compared with standard squats across all load conditions for VAS (p = 0.001–0.018). TibAnt, metabolic cost was significantly lower at 00% BW (p = 0.001), 25% BW (p = 0.016), and 75% BW (p = 0.033). RectFem also showed a significant interaction (F(4,32) = 3.82, p = 0.012, η2p = 0.323, MSE = 0.08). This interaction was driven by a load-dependent pattern where bounce squats elicited lower metabolic costs at lighter loads (0–25% BW), whereas these differences diminished and became negligible at heavier loads (75–100% BW), resulting in no statistically significant pairwise differences after Bonferroni correction (p > 0.05). Hamstring (F(4,32) = 8.04, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.50, MSE = 0.15) and GlutMax (F(4,32) = 26.94, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.77, MSE = 0.20) exhibited significant main effects of load. Both muscles showed progressively greater metabolic cost as external load increased, while no effect of squat type was observed. GAS, SOL and Adductors contributed negligibly to the metabolic cost (< 2 J/kg) and are reported in Table 2 (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study showed that bounce squats required less total metabolic energy than standard squats across all conditions, confirming the first hypothesis. The second hypothesis was partially supported. Total metabolic energy increased with external load, and although the interaction for the entire phase was not significant, the phase-specific and muscle-specific analyses indicated that the energetic advantage of bounce was concentrated in the eccentric portion and varied with load and with specific muscles. The third hypothesis was supported. The reductions were muscle specific, with the most consistent decreases observed in the knee extensors, particularly VAS. Together, these results indicate that the bounce technique influences energetic expenditure and that the effect is greatest for muscles that dominate the bottom position of the squat.

Across all tested loads, bounce squats required approximately 10.5% less total metabolic energy than standard squats, indicating that execution at the bottom of the movement exerts a meaningful influence on whole-task economy. This result agrees with earlier comparisons showing lower oxygen cost and higher efficiency when a rebound is used rather than a pause at the bottom [9] and aligns with work demonstrating that modest changes in execution, such as movement timing or eccentric duration, can shift internal energetic demands at the same external load [32]. The practical relevance is nontrivial because lower-body resistance tasks can reach approximately 80% 1 RM [3] and repeated squat sets can approach high aerobic demand in trained individuals [4] Mechanistically, a controlled bounce shortens the brief transition at the bottom of the squat, allowing tendons to return stored elastic energy and reducing the contractile work and activation required during the change of direction. This mechanism is consistent with the general principles of the SSC and muscle-tendon interaction observed in vivo [10, 11] Furthermore, recent ultrasound evidence during loaded squats specifically demonstrates that faster descent velocities increase tendon strain and recoil, allowing muscle fascicles to operate under more favorable conditions for metabolic efficiency [33]. In addition to tendon dynamics, the reduction in vasti metabolic cost may also relate to reduced co-contraction around the knee. By leveraging passive tissue constraints for stability during the rapid reversal, the bounce technique may minimize the need for energetically expensive agonist-antagonist co-activation compared to the slower, more controlled standard squat.

Phase-specific analyses show that energy savings with bounce arise in the eccentric portion, whereas concentric energy is governed primarily by external load rather than technique. This pattern is coherent with two possible interpretations. First, eccentric actions can generate high force at comparatively low oxygen cost [16], and a short and well-timed reversal allows tendons to store and return energy while limiting fascicle shortening near the bottom [11]. In other words, lowering the bar uses less oxygen than lifting, and if the change of direction at the bottom is quick, the stretched tendons return elastic energy and thus the muscle fibers do not need to shorten as much or as fast at that instant, which reduces the metabolic energy required [34]. Second, in the concentric phase, the influence of load was more pronounced than the effect of bounce. As external load increases, lifters typically spend longer at the bottom, which diminishes tendon elastic return and reduces the spring-like benefit of the bounce [13]. Heavier loads also require greater co-contraction for stability, raising metabolic expenditure and damping the pre-stretch effect [14]. In parallel, work is redistributed among synergists, often shifting demand toward the hip extensors during ascent, so the knee-extensor-focused savings around the transition decreased [15]. Thus, in the concentric phase, metabolic cost was governed primarily by load, with no detectable benefit of the bounce across the tested conditions.

The most robust and consistent reductions of metabolic energy cost in bounce squats were observed in the VAS across loads and especially during the eccentric phase, indicating that the energetic benefit is closely tied to the period when these muscles lengthen to control knee flexion. This interpretation is consistent with the primary functional role of the quadriceps during descent, where they act eccentrically to resist knee flexion while generating force [1]. Eccentric contractions are known to produce high forces at comparatively low metabolic cost, and the bounce condition likely amplifies this economy by emphasizing controlled lengthening just before the change of direction [16]. In addition, a brief bottom transition allows tendons to return stored elastic energy at the onset of ascent, limiting fascicle shortening and contractile work precisely where VAS exert the greatest control demand [11]. In contrast, during the concentric phase VAS did not exhibit a bounce-related advantage. Instead, their metabolic cost increased with external load. This pattern reflects the fundamental difference between contraction types and tasks. Once the movement proceeds upward, VAS must perform positive mechanical work through active shortening, which carries a higher metabolic cost than force production during lengthening. As ascent progresses and joint angles change, the transient assistance from tendon recoil diminishes, leaving contractile elements to supply the majority of work. Accordingly, concentric energetics were governed primarily by load rather than by the presence of a bounce [35]. However, it is important to distinguish between metabolic and mechanical outcomes. While the bounce provided no metabolic advantage during ascent, it may still enhance performance characteristics such as peak power or rate of force development.

RectFem and TibAnt showed interaction effects, but their patterns were less consistent. For the RectFem, differences between squat types emerged only at lower loads during the eccentric phase, while no robust effect was observed in the concentric phase. This outcome may reflect the biarticular function of rectus femoris, which splits its role between hip and knee actions, thereby diluting the magnitude of bounce-related savings [36]. Similarly, TibAnt showed reductions in energy cost during bounce squats at selected loads in the eccentric phase, consistent with its role in stabilizing the ankle during descent. However, this effect diminished under heavier loading and was absent during ascent, suggesting that the contribution of ankle dorsiflexors to whole-body energy cost is modest and load-sensitive.

The hip extensors showed a distinct pattern compared with the knee extensors. GlutMax exhibited a consistent increase in the metabolic cost with rising external load across all phases, reflecting its fundamental role in generating hip extension torque as resistance increases. During the eccentric phase, both squat type and load significantly influenced energy demand, with bounce squats requiring less energy than standard squats. In contrast, in the concentric and entire phases, only the effect of load persisted, with no detectable bounce advantage. These findings indicate that any bounce-related energetic savings for GlutMax are confined to the lowering phase, whereas during ascent and overall performance its metabolic cost is determined primarily by the magnitude of the load.

Adductor, GAS, and SOL showed significant main effects of squat type, yet their absolute metabolic costs were minimal across all loads. This is likely to reflect their functional roles. Adductor act primarily as stabilizers, while the GAS and SOL function largely in isometric or quasi-isometric contraction, relying on tendon elasticity to manage load. This reliance on elastic mechanisms explains why modeled contractile costs remained minimal regardless of squat type [37, 38]. As a result, bounce-related changes in these muscles appear to represent minor shifts in coordination rather than meaningful contributions to overall metabolic economy.

In a training context, minimizing the metabolic energy required for each individual squat can significantly reduce the overall metabolic demand of a full squat set. This reduction could facilitate faster recovery between sets or enable a greater number of repetitions, particularly when metabolic capacity is the primary limiting factor. In competitive settings, the bounce, where elastic energy is utilized during the transition from the eccentric to concentric phase, can enhance performance in disciplines that permit such methods, including high-intensity interval training and weightlifting. However, in other strength sports like powerlifting, the use of bounce techniques may be restricted or explicitly prohibited by sport-specific regulations, necessitating careful consideration of rule compliance when applying biomechanical optimizations.

From a practical perspective, the reduction of about 10.5% in overall cost suggests that bounce squats may allow lifters to perform higher training volumes at a lower energetic demand. This aligns with recent evidence emphasizing that movement efficiency can be optimized to modulate internal metabolic loads without compromising the necessary mechanical stimulus [39]. Thus, this technique potentially facilitates greater mechanical work with reduced fatigue accumulation. For athletes, this could translate into improved efficiency during sessions emphasizing repeated lower-body efforts, while in rehabilitation settings it may offer a strategy to preserve functional loading with lower cardiometabolic strain. Importantly, the energetic advantage was phase-specific and muscle-specific, with the bounce technique showing clear benefits during the eccentric portion while concentric costs scaled primarily with the external load. Practitioners should therefore consider bounce squats as a technique to strategically modulate energy demands, particularly when the goal is to maximize training economy without compromising mechanical stimulus. However, practitioners should exercise caution, as bounce squats may increase joint loading rates due to the rapid reversal. Consequently, this technique could be contraindicated in some populations, such as individuals with compromised joint integrity or those in early stages of rehabilitation.

Our study has limitations, though most are practical and addressable. First, the in-lab protocol may modestly limit ecological validity because the cadence was metronome-guided and depth was standardized. While necessary for experimental control, these constraints may alter natural muscle activation timing or tendon loading rates compared to self-paced movements, potentially influencing metabolic estimates. Future studies should examine whether self-paced or sport-specific tempos modify the magnitude or muscle specificity of the energetic benefits observed with bounce squats. Second, the loading protocol was scaled to body mass rather than percentages of individual 1RM. While this approach standardizes external load relative to anthropometry, it may not fully capture the physiological intensity associated with heavy lifting (e.g., > 80% 1RM). Therefore, the observed load-dependent effects should be interpreted within this context, as neuromuscular strategies may differ under relative intensity constraints. Future research should utilize %1RM-based protocols to better ensure ecological validity for strength-trained populations. Third, musculoskeletal outputs were mode-based using OpenSim and CMC with subject-specific strength scaling, and metabolic cost was estimated with a standard metabolic model rather than direct calorimetry. Although this generic model may not fully capture inter-individual variability in muscle efficiency, the within-subject design ensures that the relative comparisons between squat techniques remain valid. These choices are common in controlled biomechanics and future experiments could add indirect calorimetry or tendon ultrasound to validate energy storage and expenditure. Fourth, the sample size (n = 9) was relatively small, which is characteristic of computationally intensive musculoskeletal simulation studies. This limited sample size constrains statistical power, potentially increasing the risk of Type II errors. Consequently, non-significant interaction effects or post-hoc comparisons should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, prior musculoskeletal simulation studies investigating dynamic tasks, including squats, have commonly employed similarly small sample sizes [40, 41]. Importantly, in musculoskeletal simulation research, the validity of findings depends not only on sample size but also on rigorous model verification and validation procedures [27] Although the within-subject design supports the validity of the biomechanical comparisons within this cohort, extrapolation of these findings to other populations (e.g., novice lifters, older adults, or individuals with pathology) should be done cautiously, as their neuromuscular control and tendon properties may differ from those of the resistance-trained cohort in this study. Finally, while EMG data verified the temporal patterns of muscle activation, the simulations were driven by CMC rather than EMG data directly. Therefore, muscle-specific metabolic estimates reflect optimization criteria and may not fully capture individual variability in recruitment strategies compared to EMG-driven methods. Similarly, the use of generic tendon properties limits individual-specific elastic estimates, though the within-subject design supports valid relative comparisons.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that bounce squats lower whole-body metabolic energy cost by 10.5%, with the most consistent savings in VAS during the eccentric phase. The benefit reflects the low energetic demand of eccentric contractions and tendon recoil during rapid reversal, whereas concentric cost was mainly determined by external load. Other muscles showed only minor effects, so their overall impact was negligible. Together, these results suggest bounce squats can reduce energy use without reducing mechanical loading, with practical value for training.
