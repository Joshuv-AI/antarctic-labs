Laplace Approximation for Bayesian Tensor Network Kernel Machines
Albert Saiapin, Kim Batselier

Abstract
Uncertainty estimation is essential for robust decision-making in the presence of ambiguous or out-of-distribution inputs. Gaussian Processes (GPs) are classical kernel-based models that offer principled uncertainty quantification and perform well on small- to medium-scale datasets. Alternatively, formulating the weight space learning problem under tensor network assumptions yields scalable tensor network kernel machines. However, these assumptions break Gaussianity, complicating standard probabilistic inference. This raises a fundamental question: how can tensor network kernel machines provide principled uncertainty estimates? We propose a novel Bayesian Tensor Network Kernel Machine (LA-TNKM) that employs a (linearized) Laplace approximation for Bayesian inference. A comprehensive set of numerical experiments shows that the proposed method consistently matches or surpasses Gaussian Processes and Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) across diverse UCI regression benchmarks, highlighting both its effectiveness and practical relevance.

INTRODUCTION

The ability of machine learning (ML) systems to discriminate between different objects of interest provides a good value in various fields: from financial
[
11
]
and medical
[
41
]
applications to natural language processing
[
33
]
and recommender systems
[
37
]
.
For example, a portable personalized medical assistant could leverage physiological measurements — such as heart rate, age, height, and weight — as input features to generate diagnostic predictions or disease risk assessments. However, in practical applications, a critical component is often overlooked: uncertainty estimation (UE)
[
26
,
1
]
.
One of the methods to construct uncertainty-aware models is through probabilistic modeling
[
32
]
, where the goal is to estimate the following predictive distribution:

p(y^{*}|\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\mathcal{D}),

(1)

where
\mathcal{D}\coloneq\{\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{i}\right)|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}^{D},y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y},i=1,\dots,N\}=\{\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{X}^{N\times D},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{N}\}
is the training data and
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},y^{*})
is a test sample.
In this case, the model produces not only mean of the distribution (predictions, e.g. cat or dog label) but also variance, reflecting its confidence. High variance indicates uncertainty, arising from limited training data, missing or noisy features, or out-of-distribution inputs. Accounting for this uncertainty enables more robust decision-making and can guide active data acquisition when the model is unsure.

A well-known example of this idea is the Gaussian Process (GP) model
[
36
]
, which provides a principled probabilistic framework for kernel-based learning from a function-space perspective.
The main training step of a standard GP model consists of inverting the Gram matrix
k_{ij}\coloneq k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
, which encodes the pairwise relations between all training data points. As a result, the storage complexity is
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
and the computational complexity is
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
, which limits the applicability of conventional GP models to small- or medium-scale datasets (up to
10^{5}
–
10^{6}
observations).

On the other hand, an equivalent weight-space formulation of the GP learning problem,
f(\boldsymbol{x}):=\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}
with deterministic features
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
, allows the training complexity to be reduced from cubic to linear in the number of data samples
N
, under specific constraints.
Following
[
43
,
3
,
34
]
, model weights
\boldsymbol{w}
can be parameterized as a tensor network, thereby learning a multilinear (nonlinear) data-dependent representation from an exponentially large number of fixed features using only a linear number of parameters. However, as multilinearity precludes closed-form Bayesian inference, most of the previous works focus mainly on training a maximum a posteriori (MAP) parameter estimates
[
46
,
38
]
.

In this work, we introduce the Bayesian Tensor Network Kernel Machine (LA-TNKM), which unifies variational inference and (linearized) Laplace approximation within a single framework.
The key idea is to locally approximate the posterior over model parameters with a Gaussian centered at a local maximum, where the covariance captures the local curvature. This approximation enables estimation of the predictive distribution (
1
) and facilitates hyperparameter evaluation via a variational inference framework. Building on this, the main contributions of this work are:

•

We introduce LA-TNKM, a novel probabilistic tensor network model that enables uncertainty-aware predictions while maintaining a computational cost comparable to that of a standard MAP-based tensor network kernel machine.

•

We systematically evaluate various Hessian approximation techniques — Full, Generalized Gauss–Newton, Block-Diagonal, Diagonal, and Last Core — within the tensor network framework, highlighting their impact on memory requirements, computational efficiency, and predictive performance.

•

We demonstrate the competitive performance of LA-TNKM on UCI regression benchmarks, where it consistently matches or outperforms Gaussian Processes, Bayesian Neural Networks, and other tensor network-based regression methods, highlighting its robustness and practical utility.

2.1
Tensor Networks

The fundamental idea underlying tensor networks (TNs)
[
20
,
8
]
is to efficiently represent and manipulate high-dimensional tensors
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}
by decomposing them into a network of smaller, low-rank tensors (cores) connected by shared indices.
In this work, we focus on the canonical polyadic decomposition.

Definition 2.2

(Canonical polyadic decomposition (CPD)
[
20
]
)
.

A
D
th-order tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times\dots\times I_{D}}
has a rank-
R
CPD if

\operatorname{vec}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{W}}\right)\coloneq\sum_{r=1}^{R}\boldsymbol{v}^{(D)}_{r}\otimes\dots\otimes\boldsymbol{v}^{(1)}_{r},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{V}^{(d)}\coloneq\left[\boldsymbol{v}^{(d)}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{v}^{(d)}_{R}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{d}\times R}
are called the cores of the CPD.

From Definition
2.2
, the storage complexity of a
D
th-order tensor can be reduced from exponential
\mathcal{O}(I^{D})
to linear
\mathcal{O}(DIR)
that is beneficial when
D
is large and
I\coloneq\max(I_{1},\dots,I_{D})
.
An additional advantage of the CPD is its simplicity, as it is governed by a single hyperparameter — the scalar rank-
R
. In contrast, Tensor Train (TT)
[
35
]
and Hierarchical Tucker (HT)
[
13
]
decompositions involve multiple rank parameters, making hyperparameter tuning more complex.

2.2
Tensor Network Kernel Machine

Consider the following model of linear regression:

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{w},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}I_{2}\dots I_{D}}
represents nonlinear features,
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}I_{2}\dots I_{D}}
is a vector of weights learned from the data
\mathcal{D}
.
The feature map
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
is important as it allows for modeling various nonlinear behaviors in the data.
In addition, in the context of tensor network kernel machines two assumptions are made:

1.

Features
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
have tensor-product structure:

\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneq\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(D)}(x_{D})\otimes\dots\otimes\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(1)}(x_{1}),

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(d)}:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{I_{d}}
is a feature map acting on the
d
th component of
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}
.

2.

Model weights
\boldsymbol{w}
are represented as a tensor network.

The tensor-product structure (
4
) is directly related to product kernels
[
15
,
42
]
, Fourier features
[
45
]
and polynomials
[
40
]
. At first glance, a disadvantage of the tensor-product structure in Equation (
4
) is that the input vector
\boldsymbol{x}
is mapped into an exponentially large feature vector
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{1}I_{2}\dots I_{D}}
. As a result, the model is described by an exponential number of model parameters
\boldsymbol{w}
.
This motivates the introduction of the second assumption. For the CPD, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 2.1

(CPD kernel machine
[
47
]
)
.

Suppose

\operatorname{ten}\left(\boldsymbol{w},I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{D}\right)

is a tensor in CPD form. The model responses and associated gradients of

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\left[\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(D)}(x_{D})\otimes\dots\otimes\boldsymbol{\phi}^{(1)}(x_{1})\right]^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}

can be computed in

\mathcal{O}(DIR)

rather than

\mathcal{O}(\prod_{d=1}^{D}I_{d})
, where

I=\max(I_{1},\dots,I_{D})
.

Training a CPD kernel machine means solving the following nonlinear nonconvex optimization problem:

\begin{split}&\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{2}^{2}\\
&\text{s.t. }\boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{v})=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\boldsymbol{v}^{(D)}_{r}\otimes\dots\otimes\boldsymbol{v}^{(1)}_{r},\end{split}

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{y}
is the target variable vector,
\Phi_{ni}\coloneq\phi_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
represents an
n
th row of data matrix
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times D}
,
\boldsymbol{v}^{(d)}_{r}
is the
r
th column of the
d
th CPD core
\boldsymbol{V}^{(d)}
,
\boldsymbol{v}
denotes the vector containing all entries of the CPD cores, and
\alpha
is a regularization hyperparameter.
Note that, to compute the model response
\boldsymbol{f}\coloneq\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{w}
, we implicitly take its real part, as the features
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and weights
\boldsymbol{w}
are generally complex.

Common approaches for optimizing tensor network models include specialized algorithms such as alternating least squares (ALS)
[
44
,
46
]
and Riemannian optimization
[
34
]
, which exploit the multilinear tensor structure for efficient convergence. Generic first- or second-order gradient-based methods can also be employed.

2.3
Bayesian Inference

Bayesian inference models uncertainty over parameters using probability distributions via Bayes’ rule
[
32
]
:

p(\boldsymbol{v}|\mathcal{D})=\dfrac{p(\mathcal{D}|\boldsymbol{v})p(\boldsymbol{v})}{p(\mathcal{D})},

(6)

where
p(\boldsymbol{v})
is the prior,
p(\boldsymbol{v}|\mathcal{D})
is the posterior, reflecting knowledge before and after observing the data, respectively;
p(\mathcal{D}|\boldsymbol{v})
is the likelihood of the data given the parameters;
p(\mathcal{D})
is the marginal likelihood, obtained by integrating out
\boldsymbol{v}
.
The posterior predictive distribution (
1
) can then be obtained by marginalizing over parameters:

p(y^{*}|\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\mathcal{D})=\int p(y^{*}|\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{v})p(\boldsymbol{v}|\mathcal{D})d\boldsymbol{v},

(7)

where
p(y^{*}|\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{v})
is the conditional model of outputs.
In the next section, we describe the assumptions and techniques enabling tractable Bayesian inference for tensor network kernel machines.

3
BAYESIAN TENSOR NETWORK KERNEL MACHINES

We consider the following discriminative model:

y=f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})+e=\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{v})+e,

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{g}(\cdot):\mathbb{C}^{DIR}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{I^{D}}
is the CPD parameterization mapping with
I\coloneq I_{d}
for all
d=1\dots D
;
\boldsymbol{v}\coloneq\operatorname{vec}\left(\left[\boldsymbol{v}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{v}^{(D)}\right]\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{DIR}
, with
\boldsymbol{v}^{(d)}\coloneq\operatorname{vec}\left(\boldsymbol{V}^{(d)}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{IR}
; and

e\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,\beta^{-1})
is a Gaussian noise term with precision
\beta
.
Based on the model formulation in Equation (
8
), the conditional distribution of the outputs is given by:

p(y|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v},\beta)=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{v}),\,\beta^{-1}).

(9)

Another key component of Bayesian inference is the prior distribution, which encodes initial beliefs and influences model uncertainty. Here, we define it as:

p(\boldsymbol{v}|\gamma)\coloneq\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\,\gamma^{-1}\boldsymbol{I}),

(10)

where
\gamma
denotes the precision hyperparameter.
For tractable Bayesian inference, we introduce a variational posterior under the mean-field assumption:

q(\boldsymbol{v},\beta,\gamma)=q(\boldsymbol{v})q(\beta)q(\gamma),

(11)

which allows efficient approximation of the true posterior.
Details on variational inference and the hyperprior are provided in Section
A.2
of the Appendix.

3.1
Tensor Network Parameters Posterior

A central element of Bayesian inference is the posterior distribution over model parameters (
6
), which captures both the model’s capacity and its uncertainty. Computing the posterior predictive distribution (
7
) requires evaluating a high-dimensional integral, which is often intractable.
To this end, we employ the Laplace approximation
[
4
]
to obtain a tractable posterior estimate, approximating
p(\boldsymbol{v}|\mathcal{D})
by
q(\boldsymbol{v})
as:

\begin{split}q(\boldsymbol{v})&=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v}|\boldsymbol{v}^{*},\,\boldsymbol{H}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}^{-1}),\\
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}&\coloneq\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v}),\\
\boldsymbol{H}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}&\coloneq\left[\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{J}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\top}\partial{\boldsymbol{v}}}\right]_{\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}},\\
\end{split}

(12)

using the loss function
\mathcal{J}
, defined as follows:

\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneq\dfrac{\beta}{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{2}^{2}+\dfrac{\gamma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}^{2},

(13)

where
\beta
and
\gamma
denote the precision hyperparameters.
The optimal model parameters
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
in the minimization problem (
12
) can be found using the ALS algorithm
[
46
]
, where each CPD core
\boldsymbol{V}^{(d)}
is updated sequentially for
d=1,\dots,D
while keeping the other cores fixed:

\begin{split}\operatorname{vec}\left(\boldsymbol{V}^{(d)}\right)&=\left(\boldsymbol{A}^{(d)\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{(d)}+\dfrac{\gamma}{\beta}\boldsymbol{I}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{(d)\top}\boldsymbol{y},\\
\boldsymbol{A}^{(d)}&\coloneq\left(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(d)}\odot_{R}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(d)}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times IR},\\
\boldsymbol{Z}^{(d)}&\coloneq\bigoasterisk_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq d\end{subarray}}^{D}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{V}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times R},\\
\Phi^{(d)}_{ni}&\coloneq\boldsymbol{\phi}_{i}^{(d)}(x_{nd}).\end{split}

(14)

This procedure is repeated for several epochs (sweeps).

GGN Hessian.

The primary advantage of the generalized Gauss–Newton approximation lies in its positive semi-definiteness, in contrast to the full Hessian, which may contain both positive and negative eigenvalues
[
17
]
. The formula for this approximation is given by:

\boldsymbol{H}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}=\beta\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}+\gamma\boldsymbol{I},

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{A}\coloneq\left[\boldsymbol{A}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{A}^{(D)}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times DIR}
and matrices
\boldsymbol{A}^{(d)}
are defined in Equation (
14
).

Block-Diagonal Hessian.

The use of block-diagonal Hessian approximations has been effective in BNNs literature
[
29
,
6
]
. The key assumption is that the CPD cores (analogous to separate layers in BNNs) are independent, allowing the posterior to be expressed as:

\begin{split}q(\boldsymbol{v})&=\prod_{d=1}^{D}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v}^{(d)}|(\boldsymbol{v}^{(d)})^{*},\,(\boldsymbol{H}^{(d)})^{-1}),\\
\boldsymbol{H}^{(d)}&=\beta\boldsymbol{A}^{(d)\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{(d)}+\gamma\boldsymbol{I}.\end{split}

(16)

Diagonal Hessian.

In this case, we assume that all model weights are independent (mean-field approach
[
21
]
), so the posterior is approximated by a diagonal multivariate Gaussian:

\begin{split}q(\boldsymbol{v})&=\prod_{d=1}^{D}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v}^{(d)}|(\boldsymbol{v}^{(d)})^{*},\,(\boldsymbol{H}^{(d)})^{-1}),\\
\boldsymbol{H}^{(d)}&=\operatorname{diag}\left(\beta\left[\boldsymbol{A}^{(d)}\oasterisk\boldsymbol{A}^{(d)}\right]^{\top}\boldsymbol{1}+\gamma\boldsymbol{1}\right),\end{split}

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{1}
represents a vector of ones.

Last Core Hessian.

Bayesian inference applied only to the last layer of a neural network was proposed by
22
and shown to be both theoretically well-founded and empirically effective. In our setting, this corresponds to the last CPD core,
\boldsymbol{v}^{(D)}=\operatorname{vec}\left(\boldsymbol{V}^{(D)}\right)
, with the posterior over the weights given by

\begin{split}q(\boldsymbol{v})&=\hat{q}(\boldsymbol{v})\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{v}^{(D)}|(\boldsymbol{v}^{(D)})^{*},\,(\boldsymbol{H}^{(D)})^{-1}),\\
\boldsymbol{H}^{(D)}&=\beta\boldsymbol{A}^{(D)\top}\boldsymbol{A}^{(D)}+\gamma\boldsymbol{I},\end{split}

(18)

where
\hat{q}(\boldsymbol{v})=\left(\prod_{d=1}^{D-1}\delta(\boldsymbol{v}^{(d)}-(\boldsymbol{v}^{(d)})^{*})\right)
and
\delta
denotes the Dirac delta function.

Eigenvalue Decomposition

Another challenge is the inversion of the Hessian matrix. Prior work
[
28
]
shows that the Hessian is often poorly conditioned, with many eigenvalues being zero or negative. Consequently, the optimization problem in Equation (
12
) is ill-conditioned and difficult to solve.
To address this, we apply a truncated eigenvalue decomposition to the Hessian,
\boldsymbol{H}=\boldsymbol{U}\operatorname{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}
, with eigenvalues sorted in descending order:

\hat{\boldsymbol{H}}\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{\hat{R}}\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\boldsymbol{u}_{j}^{\top},

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
is the
j
th column of
\boldsymbol{U}
,
\lambda_{j}\geq\hat{t}
for
j=1,\dots,\hat{R}
, and
\lambda_{\hat{R}+1}<\hat{t}
.
The thresholding hyperparameter
\hat{t}
is selected via cross-validation, with candidate values ranging from the smallest to the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian
\boldsymbol{H}
. One may ask why an additional hyperparameter is needed when
\gamma
already controls both the conditioning of the optimization problem and the Hessian eigenvalues. The issue is that excessively large
\gamma
can drive the CPD tensor-product structure toward degenerate (near-zero) solutions. Introducing
\hat{t}
decouples these roles:
\gamma
governs MAP estimation, while
\hat{t}
regulates Hessian inversion.

3.3
Predictive Distribution

Regardless of the chosen posterior approximation (e.g., the Hessian form), the predictive distribution is obtained by integrating the approximate posterior
q(\boldsymbol{v})
with the conditional model:

\begin{split}p_{LA}(y|\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{D})&\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{v})}\left[p(y|f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}))\right]\\
&\approx\dfrac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}p(y|f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}_{s}))\text{, }\boldsymbol{v}_{s}\sim q(\boldsymbol{v}),\end{split}

(20)

where the (intractable) expectation is approximated via Monte Carlo sampling, since
f
depends nonlinearly on
\boldsymbol{v}_{s}
.
When using the GGN approximation to the Hessian, the model
f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})
can be interpreted as a generalized linear model (GLM) with a local linearization
[
17
,
2
]
of the form:

f^{lin}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})\coloneq f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})+\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}),

(21)

where
\boldsymbol{g}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{C}^{DIR}
is the gradient of the function
f
evaluated at point
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
. Since the GGN approximation for posterior covariance corresponds to the posterior of the linearized model (
21
), we can use this model for prediction, consistent with GGN-based inference
[
17
]
:

p_{LLA}(y|\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{D})\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{q(\boldsymbol{v})}\left[p(y|f^{lin}_{\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}))\right].

(22)

Q1: Is Linearization Useful for LA-TNKM?

The short answer is yes. Figure

Q2: What Hessian Approximation to Choose?

There is no short answer here. As shown in Figure

This suggests that the approximated Hessian spectrum contains very small eigenvalues which, when inverted to form the covariance matrix, yield excessively large predictive variances. As a result, test performance degrades, as reflected in higher NLL. We recommend using the most efficient Hessian approximation (Last or Block, Table

\hat{t}

5
RELATED WORK

Our contributions lie at the intersection of BNNs, their connections to GPs, and probabilistic methods based on tensor networks (TNs).

18
establish a theoretical link between BNNs and GPs, showing that Gaussian posterior approximations — obtained via Laplace or variational inference — correspond to GP regression posteriors. Local linearization and its connection to the GGN approximation in BNNs is explored by
17
, and further extended in
2
, where the authors address mismatches between classical Laplace approximation assumptions and the behavior of modern neural networks. An alternative perspective is presented by
31
, who propose a scalable, matrix-free method for constructing Bayesian approximations in the Hessian’s null space to mitigate underfitting.

A related line of work focuses on probabilistic TN models.
14
apply the Laplace approximation to tensor train models with Bayesian priors over the network parameters. In contrast,
21
propose a scalable variational inference framework for CPD-based models, using low-rank and Kronecker-structured posteriors to balance expressiveness and tractability. Complementing these approaches,
30
approximate GPs by projecting the problem into a low-dimensional subspace defined by a TN, performing Bayesian inference there, and then projecting back to the original space for GP predictions.

6
CONCLUSION

In this paper, we developed a novel Bayesian Tensor Network Kernel Machine, LA-TNKM, which provides uncertainty estimates for its predictions.
We applied the Laplace approximation to the CPD weight posterior distribution to make Bayesian inference tractable and introduced several types of Hessian matrix approximations, highlighting their respective advantages and limitations.
We experimentally validated the benefits of local linearization of the prediction function and compared the proposed LA-TNKM model against GP- and BNN-based baselines. The results demonstrate the competitiveness and effectiveness of LA-TNKM across a diverse range of datasets and applications.
Future works may focus on improving the optimization strategy (finding MAP estimate), designing problem-dependent priors, and exploring alternative tensor network architectures (e.g., Hierarchical Tucker) to enhance flexibility and performance.

\boldsymbol{H}^{(k,m,r)}\coloneq\operatorname{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\right)\operatorname{diag}\left(\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k,m)}\right]_{:r}\right)
.

To conclude, we define new matrices as combinations of the previously introduced ones, leading to the final equations:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{T}^{(k,m,r,p)}&=\boldsymbol{D}^{(k,m,r,p)}+\delta_{rp}\boldsymbol{E}^{(k,m,r)},\\
\boldsymbol{E}^{(k,m,r)}&=\boldsymbol{G}^{(k,m,r)}-\boldsymbol{H}^{(k,m,r)}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{y}\right)\operatorname{diag}\left(\left[\boldsymbol{Z}^{(k,m)}\right]_{:r}\right).\end{split}

∎

A.2
Variational Inference and Hyperprior

Within a fully Bayesian framework, it is standard to place prior distributions over the unknown hyperparameters — specifically, the precision terms in our case. Following the approach outlined in
[
4
]
, we model these hyperparameters using Gamma distributions:

p(z)=\operatorname{Gam}(a^{(0)}_{z},b^{(0)}_{z}),z\in\{\beta,\gamma\}.

(37)

With these components in place, we are now able to define the variational posterior distribution under the mean-field assumption:

q(\boldsymbol{v},\beta,\gamma)=q(\boldsymbol{v})q(\beta)q(\gamma).

(38)

To determine the functional forms of the variational factors, we sequentially employ the standard coordinate ascent update rule
[
4
]
:

\ln{q(\boldsymbol{z}_{j})}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i\neq j}\left[\ln p(y,\boldsymbol{z})\right]+C\text{, }j=1,2,3,

(39)

where
\boldsymbol{z}\coloneq[\boldsymbol{z_{1}},z_{2},z_{3}]
, with
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}\coloneq\boldsymbol{v}
,
z_{2}\coloneq\beta
,
z_{3}\coloneq\gamma
;
p(y,\boldsymbol{z})=p(y|\boldsymbol{v},\beta)p(\boldsymbol{v}|\gamma)p(\beta)p(\gamma)
is the joint distribution;
q(\boldsymbol{z}_{j})
denotes the optimal variational distribution for the
j
-th variable, and the expectation is taken over all other variables.

The application of Equation (
39
) to the noise precision -
\beta
leads to the following parameter update for its variational Gamma posterior for
t=1,\dots,T
:

\begin{split}q(\beta)&=Gam(\beta|a_{\beta}^{(t)},b_{\beta}^{(t)}),\\
a_{\beta}^{(t)}&\coloneq a_{\beta}^{(t-1)}+\frac{N}{2}\text{, }\\
b_{\beta}^{(t)}&\coloneq b_{\beta}^{(t-1)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{v}}{\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{v})\|_{2}^{2}}.\end{split}

(40)

A similar line of reasoning yields the variational update of the Gamma posterior parameters for the weights precision
\gamma
:

\begin{split}q(\gamma)&=Gam(\gamma|a_{\gamma}^{(t)},b_{\gamma}^{(t)}),\\
a_{\gamma}^{(t)}&\coloneq a_{\gamma}^{(t-1)}+\frac{DIR}{2},\\
b_{\gamma}^{(t)}&\coloneq b_{\gamma}^{(t-1)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}{\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}}.\end{split}

(41)

The posterior distribution over the model weights
\boldsymbol{v}
can be obtained by applying Equations (
12
) and (
13
), where the precision hyperparameters
\beta
and
\gamma
are replaced with their expected values,
\mathbb{E}{\beta}
and
\mathbb{E}{\gamma}
, respectively.

B.1
Details of Computational Resources

All experiments were conducted on a Dell Inc. Latitude 7440 laptop equipped with a 13th Gen Intel Core i7-1365U CPU and 16 GB of RAM.

B.2
Details of Baseline Methods

LA-BNN.

The model is a standard multilayer perceptron (MLP) with a single hidden layer and tanh activation
[
16
]
, trained using the Adam optimizer
[
19
]
. The number of hidden units depends on the task: 32 for the synthetic experiments and 100 for the UCI datasets. To obtain probabilistic predictions, we apply the linearized Laplace approximation
[
17
]
, treating all network parameters as random variables and thus employing the full-Hessian approximation.

B.3
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate both predictive accuracy and uncertainty quality using the following regression metrics.
Given inputs
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, targets
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, predictive means
\{\hat{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and predictive distributions
p(y|\boldsymbol{x})
, we define:

Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE).

The RMSE measures point prediction accuracy and is defined as

\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}}.

(42)

Lower values indicate better predictive accuracy.

Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL).

To assess probabilistic predictions, we compute the negative log-likelihood metric
[
23
]
,

\mathrm{NLL}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p(y_{i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),

(43)

where
p(y_{i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
denotes the predictive distribution.
This metric jointly evaluates accuracy and uncertainty calibration, with lower values indicating better performance.
It is also commonly referred to as the negative log predictive density (NLPD).

For a Gaussian predictive distribution, the negative log-likelihood is given by

\mathrm{NLL}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\log\big(2\pi\sigma_{i}^{2}\big)+\frac{(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}},

(44)

where
\sigma_{i}^{2}
denotes the predictive variance for a single test point.

Empirical Coverage Probability (ECP-95).

The ECP-95 measures the fraction of targets lying within the predicted 95% credible intervals
[
24
]
,

\text{ECP-95}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\left[y_{i}\in\left[l_{i}^{95},u_{i}^{95}\right]\right].

(45)

where
\mathbb{I}[\cdot]
denotes the indicator function, which evaluates to
1
if its argument is true and
0
otherwise.
The quantities
l_{i}^{95}
and
u_{i}^{95}
denote the lower and upper bounds of the 95% predictive credible interval for the
i
-th test point.
Values of ECP-95 closer to
0.95
indicate better calibrated uncertainty.

In practice, the interval
\left[l_{i}^{95},u_{i}^{95}\right]
is obtained empirically as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the predictive samples.

Width of Credible Prediction Interval (WCPI-95).

The WCPI-95 quantifies the average width of the 95% predictive intervals
[
49
]
,

\text{WCPI-95}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(u_{i}^{95}-l_{i}^{95}),

(46)

where
l_{i}^{95}
and
u_{i}^{95}
denote the lower and upper bounds of the 95% predictive credible interval for the
i
-th test point.
Smaller values indicate sharper (less conservative) predictive uncertainty.

In practice, the values
l_{i}^{95}
and
u_{i}^{95}
are obtained empirically as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the predictive samples.

Relative Calibration Error (RCE).

The relative calibration error measures the deviation between nominal and empirical coverage across multiple confidence levels
[
24
]
.
It is defined as

\mathrm{RCE}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\hat{c}(\alpha_{k})-\alpha_{k}\right|,

(47)

where
K
is the number of nominal coverage levels considered,
\alpha_{k}
denotes the
k
-th nominal coverage level (e.g., 0.5, 0.6, …, 0.95), and
\hat{c}(\alpha_{k})
is the corresponding empirical coverage computed over the test set.

Empirically,
\hat{c}(\alpha_{k})
can be obtained in the same way as ECP: for each test point
i
, compute the
\alpha_{k}
predictive interval
[l_{i}^{\alpha_{k}},u_{i}^{\alpha_{k}}]
and evaluate the fraction of targets lying inside these intervals.
Lower RCE values indicate better overall calibration across the considered confidence levels. For reference, an RCE below 0.02–0.03 is typically considered well-calibrated, values around 0.05 indicate reasonable calibration, and values above 0.1 suggest poor calibration and under- or over-confident predictive intervals.

B.4
Ablation Study

In Table
3
, we compare several Hessian approximations for LA-TNKM, including the generalized Gauss–Newton (GGN), block-diagonal (Block), and last-core (Last) approximations, across UCI regression datasets. The results show that using the last-core Hessian consistently leads to improved data-fitting performance (lower RMSE) and better uncertainty estimation (lower NLL and RCE).

The superior performance of the last-core approximation suggests that concentrating curvature information on a subset of parameters can effectively capture the essential posterior structure, while reducing approximation error and numerical instability associated with more global or factorized Hessian estimates. This observation aligns with the findings of
39
, which demonstrate that modeling uncertainty in only a subset of layers is often enough to obtain well-calibrated and competitive Bayesian predictions.
