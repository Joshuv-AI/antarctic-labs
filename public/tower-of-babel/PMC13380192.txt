Arthroscopic Latarjet using double suture-button fixation: learning curve, graft positioning, and complications – A two-center study of 80 patients
Authors: Gabriel Cárdenas, Joaquín Ávila, Ignacio López, Christian Calderón, Bárbara Campos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe guided arthroscopic Latarjet procedure with double suture-button fixation has emerged as an alternative to traditional open or arthroscopic screw fixation technique for treating anterior shoulder instability in patients at high risk of recurrence. The purpose of this study was to analyze the learning curve (LC), clinical outcomes, computed tomography findings, and complications associated with this technique by evaluating the first 80 consecutive cases performed at 2 institutions.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed prospectively collected data from 80 patients (mean age 27.3 ± 7.7 years) who underwent this technique for traumatic anterior shoulder instability between 2017 and 2024. Patients were divided into 2 groups (Group A: first 40 cases; Group B: subsequent 40 cases) based on cumulative sum analysis of operative time. The LC was assessed by comparing operative time and complication rates between groups. Clinical evaluation included Subjective Shoulder Value and Rowe scores. Computed tomography scans were used to assess graft positioning and healing. Minimum clinical follow-up was 6 months (mean 27 ± 20 months).ResultsOperative time decreased significantly from 167 minutes in the first 40 cases to 110 minutes in the most recent 40 cases (P < .001). At final follow-up, mean Subjective Shoulder Value was 91.2% and mean Rowe score was 94 points. Optimal graft positioning was achieved in most cases in the sagittal and axial planes (subequatorial: 87.5%; flush: 92%). Graft rotation was observed in 29% of cases during the LC. Radiographic graft healing within the first 6 months was 68%. The main intraoperative problem was conversion to open procedure (5%), all in Group A. The rate of adverse events (problems and complications) decreased from 20% in the early cohort to 12.5% in the late cohort. Reoperation and redislocation rates were 3.7% and 2.5%, respectively. No hardware-related complications were observed.ConclusionThe arthroscopic Latarjet using double suture-button fixation provides satisfactory short- to medium-term clinical outcomes and reliable graft positioning, with operative time decreasing significantly after 20 cases. Although graft rotation and delayed healing may be observed in some cases, they do not appear to affect clinical outcomes. The guided nature of this technique and the use of a low-profile endobutton device reduce the adverse events rate (problems and complications) and eliminate hardware-related morbidity and reoperations. Based on these findings, surgeons with adequate arthroscopic experience who are considering adopting the arthroscopic Latarjet may regard this technique as an accurate, reproducible, and reliable alternative to traditional screw fixation.Keywords: Shoulder instability, Latarjet, Arthroscopic, Suture-button fixation, Cortical-button, Learning curve, Glenoid bone loss

The Latarjet procedure is a well-established approach for anterior shoulder instability, particularly in patients with substantial glenoid bone loss.17 Good clinical outcomes have been reported in short, medium, and long-term follow-up studies.19

Over time, advancements in surgical techniques have led to various enhancements in this procedure. Notably, in 2007, Lafosse et al16 introduced an all-arthroscopic Latarjet technique using screws. Subsequently, Boileau et al3,5 proposed an innovative arthroscopic guided approach using double suture-button fixation, as an alternative to screws, aiming to improve reproducibility while avoiding screw-related complications and hardware removal, which had been described in up to 16% and 18%, respectively.15

Biomechanically, suture-button fixation has demonstrated fixation properties equivalent or superior to the standard screw fixation technique.20,22 From a clinical perspective, both suture-button and screw fixation techniques in the arthroscopic Latarjet procedure have been associated with satisfactory clinical outcomes and low complication rates.28

Although the arthroscopic Latarjet is recognized as a technically demanding procedure with a substantial learning curve (LC) that may initially increase complication rates, emerging evidence suggests a different trajectory for the suture-button technique. Specifically, proficiency has been reported to be achievable after approximately 30 cases, with complication rates remaining low throughout the learning process.6

While previous studies have primarily investigated this technique in single-center settings involving its developers in France, the present study uniquely assesses LC progression across 2 independent centers outside France, offering broader insight into the reproducibility of the technique in diverse clinical contexts.

The purpose of this study was to describe the clinical and computed tomography (CT) outcomes, as well as the incidence of complications, during the LC of the arthroscopic Latarjet procedure using double suture-button fixation at 2 independent institutions.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective, two-center study analyzed prospectively enrolled patients from 2 hospitals, in Lisbon, Portugal, and Viña del Mar, Chile, between 2017 and 2024. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants and selection criteria

Patients with minimum 15 years with traumatic anterior shoulder instability and an Instability Severity Index Score (ISI) score2 ≥3 were included. At both centers, indication for the Latarjet procedure was based on the ISI score, a validated multifactorial risk-stratification tool, rather than on isolated glenoid bone loss measurements, in accordance with current evidence.18,24 The inclusion threshold of ISI ≥3 was selected based on the original study by Balg and Boileau2 and subsequently supported by Phadnis et al,21 who identified this cutoff as predictive of recurrence after arthroscopic Bankart repair and recommended considering alternative stabilization procedures, such as bone-block procedures. Importantly, the mean ISI in our cohort was 5.5 ± 1.5 (range, 4.0 to 10) points, indicating that most patients were well above the lower inclusion threshold. Complete pre-operative imaging, including radiographs and two-dimensional/three-dimensional (3D) CT scans, as well as a minimum 6-month follow-up with complete clinical and radiographic data, was required. Patients were excluded if they presented posterior or multidirectional instability, had undergone previous bone block procedures, presented a concomitant rotator cuff tear, or those with incomplete follow-up data. Of 97 patients who underwent surgery at both centers during the study period, 17 were excluded based on these criteria. The final cohort comprised the first 40 consecutive patients treated by each surgeon (n = 80).

Surgical technique

Surgery was performed by 2 fellowship-trained shoulder surgeons (B.C. and G.C.). Both surgeons completed equivalent fellowship training with the technique developer (P.B.) before independently initiating the procedure at their respective institutions, ensuring comparable baseline proficiency. The procedure used a 70° arthroscope and specific instruments (Latarjet Guiding System; Smith & Nephew Inc., Andover, MA, USA) through a standard posterior portal plus 5 anterior portals, following 5 consecutive steps as described by Boileau et al.3 (Fig. 1): (1) coracoid preparation and osteotomy (typically involving harvest of a 15–20 mm bone graft), (2) glenoid preparation and drilling, (3) subscapularis splitting with axillary nerve protection, (4) transfer and fixation, and (5) capsulolabral repair when tissue quality permitted. Capsulolabral repair was performed in 72 patients (90%). In the remaining 8 cases (10%), repair was not possible due to insufficient tissue quality.

The procedure follows 5 consecutive steps using a 70° arthroscope through a standard posterior portal plus 5 anterior portals: (A) step 1 - coracoid preparation, drilling, and osteotomy; (B) step 2 - glenoid preparation and drilling; (C) step 3 - subscapularis splitting with axillary nerve protection; (D) step 4 - coracoid transfer and fixation with double suture-button; (E) step 5 - capsulolabral repair when tissue quality permits.

Clinical assessment

Demographics (sex, age, dominant arm involvement, sports participation, tobacco use) and previous surgical history were recorded. Pre-operative and post-operative assessment included shoulder range of motion (ROM) and symmetry evaluated using the Walch-Duplay criteria,27 the ISI score,2 Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV),9 and Rowe score.23

Radiological assessment protocol

Pre-operative and post-operative evaluation included standard radiographs and two-dimensional/3D CT scans. Glenoid bone loss was quantified using the Sugaya method.25 Immediate post-operative CT was performed within 4 weeks after surgery to assess the graft positioning, and follow-up CT scans were obtained at 3 to 6 months to assess healing and positioning. In patients with nonhealed grafts, additional CT scan was performed at a minimum of 12 months when possible.

Image analysis

All CT-based assessments – graft positioning, healing status, graft rotation including GRA measurement, osteolysis, and migration – were performed by the same musculoskeletal radiologist (C.C.), who was blinded to clinical outcomes and to the chronological order of cases. Graft positioning and healing definitions are detailed in Table I and illustrated in Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4. To assess graft rotation, we introduced a new parameter, the Graft Rotation Angle, defined in the 3D coronal plane as the angle formed between a vertical line along the glenoid articular surface and the central longitudinal axis of the coracoid graft (Fig. 3 A). A Graft Rotation Angle ≥30° was considered nonoptimal positioning.

Problems and complications

We adopted the definitions provided by Athwal et al.1 A problem was defined as an unanticipated intraoperative or post-operative event that was unlikely to affect the patient's final outcome and did not require unplanned reoperation. A complication was defined as an anticipated intraoperative or post-operative event that negatively affected the outcome and/or required unplanned reoperation.

All recurrent shoulder instability episodes and reoperations were systematically documented and categorized according to these definitions.

Learning Curve Analysis

The LC was evaluated in 2 stages. First, cumulative sum analysis of operative time was performed for each surgeon. Consecutive cases were chronologically ordered and individual operative times were plotted together with a smoothed trend line to identify inflection points corresponding to changes in surgical performance over time. The cumulative sum analysis identified an inflection point at case 14 for surgeon 1 and case 16 for surgeon 2 (Fig. 5, A and B). Based on this analysis, patients were subsequently divided into 2 groups: Group A (the first 20 cases performed by each surgeon, n = 40) and Group B (the subsequent 20 cases performed by each surgeon, n = 40). The parameters analyzed included operative time, graft positioning accuracy, and intraoperative and post-operative adverse events (problems and complications). Operative time was defined as the interval from skin incision to skin closure, recorded by the circulating nurse and verified against the operative record.

Post-operative rehabilitation

All patients at both institutions followed a standardized post-operative rehabilitation protocol. A neutral rotation sling was used for 3–4 weeks. Pendulum exercises were initiated on the first post-operative day. Passive mobilization under physiotherapy supervision began at 3–4 weeks, followed by progressive active ROM exercises from 4 to 6 weeks. Strengthening exercises were initiated at 3 months. Return to noncontact sports was permitted after 3 months, whereas return to contact or overhead sports was allowed after 5–6 months, provided the shoulder was stable, pain-free, demonstrated full ROM, and post-operative CT confirmed maintained graft position.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using EasyMedStat software. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Student t-test for continuous variables and Fisher exact test for categorical variables. When appropriate, normality of distribution was assessed prior to analysis. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P value < .05.

Results

Patient population

Eighty patients (73 men, 7 women; mean age 27 ± 7.7 years, range 15 - 52 years) were included. The baseline demographic characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table II. The dominant arm was affected in 54% of cases. Surgery after first-time dislocation was performed in 3 patients (3.8%) due to high recurrence risk (ISI >6 points). Revision after failed Bankart repair was performed in 12 cases (15%). The mean ISI score was 5.5 ± 1.5 points and mean glenoid bone loss was 16 ± 8%.

Clinical outcomes

At mean follow-up of 27 ± 20 months (range 6 - 72 months), the mean SSV was 91.1% ± 10 and mean Rowe score was 94 ± 11 points. Excellent shoulder mobility was achieved in 90% of patients, who achieved a score of 25 points on the Walch-Duplay criteria for symmetric ROM.27 The mean forward flexion was 168° ± 12°, abduction 165° ± 15°, external rotation at 0° was 52° ± 12° (mean deficit of 8° compared to contralateral side), and internal rotation to T8 vertebral level on average. Return to previous sports activities was achieved in 66 patients (82.5%). Redislocation occurred in 2 patients (2.5%), both in Group A. One patient was managed nonoperatively, while the other required surgical intervention with a Hill-Sachs remplissage procedure.

Radiological outcomes

Graft positioning

Graft positioning results are shown in Figure 4. After excluding 2 patients with early graft migration, 78 patients were analyzed. In the sagittal plane, 99% (77/78) were satisfactory positioned at or below the equator. In the axial plane, 92% (72/78) were flush with the glenoid surface. Graft rotation was observed in 23 cases (29.4%): Type 1 in 13 cases (56%) and Type 2 in 10 cases (44%) (Fig. 3). Rotation was more frequent in Group A (33%) than Group B (25%), although not statistically significant difference was observed (P = .61).

We analyzed the rotation and its correlation with clinical and CT outcomes (Table III). Although no statistically significant clinical differences were observed between patients with and without graft rotation, the rotated group demonstrated nonsignificant trends toward lower healing rates, increased osteolysis and higher complication rates. In particular, type 1 graft rotation showed a clear trend toward higher rates of osteolysis and complications compared to Type 2 (Bristow-like) rotation.

Graft Healing

CT evaluation at 4-6 months was available for 65 patients (81%). Complete healing was observed in 44 cases (68%). Healing rates were similar between Group A and Group B (66.7% vs. 68.8%). Among 21 patients with initial nonunion, follow-up CT at ≥12 months was available for only 7 cases (33%) and delayed healing (Fig. 2E) was confirmed in 3 of these cases (43%). Comparison of the healing and nonhealing groups (Table IV) revealed no significant differences in pre-operative characteristics. However, the nonhealing group exhibited a significantly higher complication rate (28.5% vs. 0%, P < .001), primarily due to a higher rate of complete or near-complete osteolysis.

Problems and complications

All problems and complications are detailed in Table V. Conversion to mini-open surgery at the time of the subscapularis split was required in 4 cases (5%). The reasons for the conversion included the following: graft fracture during tensioning in 1 case (1.3%), impaired visualization due to bleeding in 1 case (1.3%), and prolonged surgical time in 2 cases (2.6%). Two cases (2.5%) required early reoperation for intraoperative complications. One patient (1.3%) had an unrecognized locked button in the posterior soft tissues, necessitating reoperation on the following day. Another case (1.3%) demonstrated severe graft malposition on immediate post-operative CT imaging and underwent revision surgery 6 days later. Post-operative complications included 1 case of transient neuropraxia (1.3%) affected both musculocutaneous and axillary nerves, with complete recovery occurring at 6 weeks and 6 months, respectively. Graft migration occurred in 2 cases (2.5%), one was due to progressive displacement of a nonhealed graft and the other occurred after a traumatic event. Both experienced persistent apprehension without true dislocation episodes and both were managed conservatively up to the last follow-up. Complete or near-complete graft osteolysis was observed in 5 patients (6.3%). One of these patients experienced redislocation, while the other maintained shoulder stability. None required additional surgery during the follow-up period.

Discussion

Main findings

This two-center study demonstrates that the arthroscopic Latarjet procedure using double suture-button fixation yields favorable clinical and radiological outcomes, with significant improvement in the LC after approximately 20 cases among adequately trained surgeons. Operative time decreased significantly from 167 to 110 minutes when comparing the early and the late groups (P < .001), and the overall rate of problems and complications declined from 20% to 12.5%. Optimal graft positioning was achieved in most cases, with 99% located in the equatorial or subequatorial position in the sagittal plane and 92% flush with the glenoid surface in the axial plane. Notably, graft rotation was observed in 29.4% of patients, a finding that, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported. Importantly, this phenomenon did not appear to affect clinical outcomes when graft healing was achieved.

Clinical outcomes

Our clinical outcomes (SSV 91%, Rowe score 94 points, and a redislocation rate of 2.5%) are consistent with previously reported suture-button series6 and comparable to those described in screw-based Latarjet procedures,14 supporting the equivalence of fixation methods in terms of clinical outcomes. These findings are further supported by recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses demonstrating similar clinical results between fixation techniques, with a trend favoring suture-button fixation in reducing hardware-related complications.11,26

Graft positioning

The high rate of optimal graft positioning in our series (92% flush, 87% subequatorial) is consistent with previously reported suture-button series and compares favorably with historical screw fixation cohorts. Among arthroscopic series using screw fixation, Kany et al13 achieved optimal positioning in 81% of cases using the "circle method" for axial evaluation, while Castricini et al7 achieved flush positioning in 76% of their arthroscopic Latarjet cases with screws. In contrast, arthroscopic series using guided suture-button fixation have shown consistently higher positioning accuracy, with Bonnevialle et al6 achieving 90% flush positioning in their later cases and Boileau et al4 reporting 96% of grafts congruent with the glenoid articular surface. These findings suggest that arthroscopic Latarjet using suture-button technique provides consistent graft positioning, with optimal positioning rates comparable to or better than those reported in historical screw fixation series, which may be related to the guided nature of the technique.

Graft rotation during the learning curve

Graft rotation was observed in 29% of our patients, representing a notable finding that has not been extensively reported in other series, whether using suture-buttons or screws. Although no significant differences in clinical outcomes were noted, rotated grafts showed trends toward higher nonunion and complication rates. This phenomenon may be explained by both biomechanical and technical factors. Biomechanically, the double suture-button construct works as a single-axis fixation system, which may allow rotation around this axis, in contrast to the rigid, multipoint fixation provided by 2 parallel screws. However, this phenomenon appears largely associated to LC-related technical factors, including inadequate coracoid/glenoid preparation (decortication), incomplete release of the pectoralis minor fascia, soft tissue interposition, suboptimal subscapularis splitting, and insufficient graft tensioning. Supporting this, Boileau et al3,4 did not report graft rotation after mastering the technique, and in our series, graft rotation decreased from 37% to 20% if we compare the first and last 20 cases, as surgical strategies to optimize graft positioning were refined. This suggest that proper technique can overcome the theoretical rotational tendency of this single-axis fixation system. Importantly, minor rotation did not appear to compromise clinical outcomes when graft union was achieved.

Graft healing may be delayed

Our graft healing rate of 68% at 4-6 months is comparable to other suture-button series with similar follow-up intervals, including 56% at 3 months reported by Dalmas et al8 and 71% at 6 months reported by Weick et al29 The similar healing rates observed at 6 months between Groups A and B (66.7% vs. 68.8%) suggest that time to graft healing may be more closely related to the nature of this flexible fixation system itself rather than surgeon experience. Although our data regarding delayed healing are limited by the availability of 12-month follow-up CT scans (7 of 21, 33%), this phenomenon has been previously reported and attributed to the flexible fixation of the suture-button construct. Dalmas et al8 demonstrated an increase in graft healing from 56% at 3 months to 96% at 12 months, whereas Girard et al10 reported an improvement from 70% at 6 months to 96% at 12 months. These findings further suggest that CT assessment at 12 months may be necessary to accurately determine final graft healing rates in patients who do not demonstrate graft healing at 6 months.

Regarding return to sport, patients were cleared from the 5 months if the shoulder was stable and with complete pain-free ROM, regardless of the graft healing if the graft remains still in place on the CT scan evaluation. The high return to sports rate (82.5%), despite 32% radiographic nonunion at 6 months, suggests that the fibrous union may provide adequate functional stability, although longer-term outcomes are needed to confirm this.

Learning curve and complications

Our LC findings showing significant improvement after 20 cases align with Bonnevialle et al,6 who specifically analyzed the LC of arthroscopic Latarjet with double suture-button fixation, reported improvements in operative time and graft positioning after approximately 30 cases.

When compared to arthroscopic screw fixation series, the number of cases required to achieve technical proficiency has been reported less consistently, ranging from 15 to 50 cases, suggesting that the guided suture-button technique provides a more predictable learning trajectory. Moreover, the suture-button technique appears to be safer during the LC period, as it avoids screw-related complications. Athwal et al1 conducted a short-term complications study evaluating arthroscopic Latarjet with screw fixation and reported an overall adverse event rate (problems and complications) of 24% and a secondary procedure rate of 8.5%, primarily due to screw-related issues, either directly (screw removal) or indirectly (graft fracture). In contrast, our series demonstrated rates of 17.5% and 3.7%, respectively, with no need for hardware removal.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design and lack of direct comparison group limit conclusions about superiority over other fixation methods. The relatively short follow-up (mean 27 months) may not capture late complications and recurrence. Moreover, the sample size may be underpowered to detect differences in less common complications. The limited availability of 12-month CT in patients with initial nonunion (33%) precludes definitive conclusions regarding final healing rates and their long-term clinical impact. Both surgeons were fellowship-trained who learned the procedure directly with the technique developer, which may limit generalizability. All CT assessments were performed by a single experienced musculoskeletal radiologist blinded to clinical outcomes and to the chronological order of cases. While this approach ensures internal consistency, it precludes intraobserver and interobserver reliability analysis, which is acknowledged as a limitation. Future prospective comparative studies are needed to further compare clinical outcomes, complications, and hardware-related problems between suture-button and screw fixation techniques.

Conclusion

The arthroscopic Latarjet using double suture-button fixation provides satisfactory short- to medium-term clinical outcomes and reliable graft positioning, with operative time decreasing significantly after 20 cases. Although graft rotation and delayed healing may be observed in some cases, they do not appear to affect clinical outcomes. The guided nature of this technique and the use of a low-profile endobutton device reduces the rate of adverse events (problems and complications) and eliminate hardware-related morbidity and reoperations.

Based on these findings, surgeons with adequate arthroscopic experience who are considering adopting the arthroscopic Latarjet may view this technique as an accurate, reproducible, and reliable alternative to traditional screw fixation, with the additional advantage of avoiding hardware-related complications.
