A Multicenter Registry for Rare Interstitial Lung Diseases in Korea: Baseline Characteristics and Clinical Outcomes
Authors: Ju Hyun Oh, Jae Ha Lee, Sung Woo Park, Yong Suk Jo, Jong Sun Park, Hongseok Yoo, Kyung Hoon Kim, Yangin Jegal, Joo Hun Park, Jinkyeong Park, Taehun Kim, Bo-Guen Kim, Hyung Koo Kang, Hyun-Kyung Lee, Ji Young Kang, Hee-Young Yoon, Hye Ju Yeo, Dong Won Park, Ala Woo, Jung-Wan Yoo, Jin Woo Song, on behalf of the Rare ILD Registry investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Rare interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) are a heterogeneous group of diffuse parenchymal lung disorders with low prevalence and diverse etiologies that pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. We aimed to establish a nationwide, multicenter registry of rare ILDs in South Korea to characterize their clinical features and outcomes.Methods We conducted a multicenter, observational cohort study involving 304 patients with rare ILDs including Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome (BHD), lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM), pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP), pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis (PLCH), pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis (PPFE), and hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) enrolled across 20 centers in Korea between September 2023 and December 2024. Data on clinical characteristics, outcomes, and treatment were collected.Results LAM (28.3%) and PAP (26.6%) were the most prevalent, followed by BHD (18.1%), PLCH (13.2%), HP (7.6%), and PPFE (6.3%). Dyspnea was common in HP and PPFE, whereas BHD and LAM were often asymptomatic. Pneumothorax was frequent in BHD (44.6%) and LAM (11.6%) at diagnosis. PPFE showed the most impaired lung function and the highest mortality (26.3%), during a median follow-up of 69.5 months, followed by PAP (6.2%) and BHD (1.8%). During follow-up, cancer was reported in 25.5% of patients with BHD. Treatment strategies varied by disease: corticosteroids were commonly used in HP (86.9%), PPFE (42.1%), and PLCH (32.5%); sirolimus in LAM (53.5%); pirfenidone in PPFE (42.1%); and whole-lung lavage in PAP (23.5%).Conclusion This is the first nationwide rare ILD registry in Korea, demonstrating heterogeneity in clinical features and outcomes and emphasizing the need for disease-specific diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) comprise a heterogeneous group of parenchymal lung disorders characterized by inflammation and fibrosis [1]. Among ILDs, relatively common subtypes such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and connective tissue disease-associated ILD have been widely studied through various cohort-based investigations, leading to the development of novel therapeutic agents, including antifibrotic drugs, which have been actively applied in these diseases [2-6]. However, rare ILDs, which are characterized by low incidence or diagnostic complexity that hinders an accurate estimation of their prevalence, have not been sufficiently studied and remain poorly understood despite their clinical significance and impact on patient outcomes [7]. Furthermore, owing to their rarity, most existing data on rare ILDs have been limited to single-center experiences, case series, or disease-specific international cohorts [8-11]. Prior to this study, no nationwide multicenter rare ILD registry had been established in South Korea. In this study, we established a nationwide multicenter registry of rare ILDs in South Korea and analyzed their characteristics based on real-world data to support improved disease recognition and management.

Materials and Methods

1. Study design and population

This nationwide, multicenter, observational cohort study aimed to establish the Korean Rare Interstitial Lung Disease registry. The six target diseases—Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome (BHD), lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM), pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP), pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis (PLCH), pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis (PPFE), and hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP)—were selected through consensus at the registry investigators’ meetings. Their selection was based not only on rarity, but also on the extent of available clinical data in Korea, the need for multicenter real-world data, and their clinical relevance in ILD practice. Accordingly, BHD, LAM, PAP, PLCH, and PPFE were included as representative rare ILDs described in the literature [7], whereas HP was included because, although it is not uniformly classified as a classic rare lung disease, multicenter clinical data remain limited in Korea and it represents an important disease entity in ILD practice.

Patients diagnosed with rare ILDs were enrolled across 20 institutions in Korea between September 2023 and December 2024. Patients were diagnosed based on the international guidelines available at each time point of diagnosis; the diagnoses were confirmed through multidisciplinary discussions involving pulmonologists, radiologists, and pathologists [12-17]. No specific exclusion criteria were applied for inclusion in the registry. Patients with rare ILDs were enrolled through both retrospective and prospective approaches: patients diagnosed prior to registry initiation were included as a retrospective cohort with clinical data collected from existing medical records, whereas patients newly diagnosed during the study period were prospectively enrolled and followed during routine clinical care with predefined data collection. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Asan Medical Center (2024-0134). The protocol was approved by the relevant IRBs prior to patient enrolment at each site, as listed in the Supplementary Table S1. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients in the prospective cohort. For the retrospective cohort, the requirement for informed consent was waived in accordance with the IRB-approved protocols.

2. Data collection and registry management

Patients who had been diagnosed prior to enrolment were included as a retrospective cohort, for whom clinical data were retrospectively collected from medical records, including demographic information (age, sex, and body mass index), smoking status, clinical symptoms, biopsy status, pulmonary function test results including forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), and diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO; expressed as percent predicted), 6-minute walking distance (6MWD), resting, and nadir peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) during the 6-minute walk test (6MWT), laboratory findings, and medications. Additionally, patients enrolled at the time of diagnosis were included as a prospective cohort, for whom clinical data, blood samples, and the EuroQol 5-Dimension (EQ-5D) questionnaire and King’s Brief Interstitial Lung Disease (K-BILD) for quality-of-life assessment at diagnosis were collected prospectively and who were followed during routine clinical care. The EQ-5D index score ranges from values below 0 to 1, whereas K-BILD scores range from 0 to 100; higher scores on both instruments indicate better health-related quality of life [18,19]. For all patients, a history of acute exacerbation, survival status, and use of home oxygen therapy were also collected. The registry database was managed in a secure web-based format (http://ipf.crf.kr/) and overseen by a central data team. Data integrity was ensured through periodic quality checks and center-level queries.

3. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation or median (interquartile range [IQR]), and categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. Comparative analysis between subgroups was performed using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. For comparisons across multiple disease groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for overall group comparisons, followed by Dunn's post-hoc test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons, as several variables violated the assumption of normality based on the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables with missing values were handled using complete-case analysis. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), MedCalc Statistical Software version 22.023 (MedCalc Software bvba, Ostend, Belgium) and R version 4.5.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Map boundary data were obtained from the GADM database version 4.1 (https://gadm.org) and all geospatial-data processing was performed using Python version 3.6.13.

Results

1. ILD types and demographics

In total, 304 patients with six types of rare ILDs, including BHD, HP, LAM, PAP, PLCH, and PPFE, were enrolled from 20 institutions across Korea (Supplementary Table S1). Of these, 25 (8.2%) were prospectively enrolled, and 279 (91.8%) were diagnosed prior to registry initiation and retrospectively enrolled. The regional distribution of the participating institutions is shown in Figure 1. Most patients (n=206, 67.8%) were recruited from hospitals in Seoul, followed by those in Gyeonggi/Incheon (n=55, 18.1%), Busan/Gyeongnam (n=36, 11.8%), Daegu/Gyeongbuk (n=5, 1.6%), and Jeju (n=2, 0.7%). The most common diagnosis was LAM (n=86, 28.3%), followed by PAP (n=81, 26.6%), BHD (n=55, 18.1%), PLCH (n=40, 13.2%), HP (n=23, 7.6%), and PPFE (n=19, 6.3%).

In 60 patients with PAP whose etiology was available, autoimmune PAP was the most common subtype (47/60, 78.3%), followed by secondary PAP (7/60, 11.7%) and congenital PAP (6/60, 10.0%). The mean age of all patients was 53.3 years at diagnosis, with considerable variation among the disease subgroups (Table 1). The oldest population was seen in the HP (64.0 years) and PPFE (63.8 years) groups, while patients in the LAM group were the youngest (47.3 years). The overall proportion of male patients was 42.8%, but male patients were predominant in the PAP (72.8%) and PLCH (77.5%) groups. As expected, all patients with LAM were female.

2. Symptom profiles and quality of life assessment

In the BHD group, most patients (89.3%) were asymptomatic, with dyspnea (8.9%) and cough (3.6%) infrequently reported (Figure 2). HP most commonly presented with dyspnea (78.3%), followed by cough (30.4%) and sputum (21.7%). Half of the patients with LAM were asymptomatic, whereas others reported dyspnea (36.0%), cough (20.9%), and sputum (9.3%). PAP commonly presented with cough, dyspnea, and sputum in 50.6%, 42.0%, and 35.8% of patients, respectively, whereas 42.0% were asymptomatic. In PLCH, 30.0% of patients were asymptomatic, and bone pain and rashes were observed in 2.2% of patients. In the PPFE group, dyspnea was reported in 52.6% patients, followed by cough (47.4%) and sputum (31.6%), while 15.8% had no symptoms at presentation.

Nineteen patients with rare ILDs responded to the EQ-5D questionnaire. The mean EQ-5D index was 0.976 in the LAM group (n=8) and 0.950 in the PPFE group (n=7). The individual scores were 0.957, 1.000, and 0.963 for the BHD, HP, and PLCH groups, respectively (n=1 for each).

3. Comorbidities

Representative comorbidities varied according to the rare ILD subtype (Figure 3). Hypertension was infrequently observed in the BHD group (5.4%). In the HP group, hypertension (43.5%) and chronic kidney disease (17.4%) were relatively common. The LAM group showed a high prevalence of cancer history at diagnosis (19.8%) and chronic kidney disease (19.8%). In the PAP group, hypertension (36.8%) and diabetes mellitus (14.5%) were notable. In the PLCH group, hypertension (7.5%) was the most frequent comorbidity. In the PPFE group, prior tuberculosis infection (21.1%) was the most prevalent, followed by hypertension and diabetes.

4. Lung function and exercise capacity

In patients with rare ILDs, the mean predicted values were 88.2% for FVC, 82.3% for FEV₁, and 68.3% for DLCO (Table 2). Among subtypes, PPFE demonstrated the most severe restrictive impairment, with the lowest FVC (58.5% predicted) among all diseases, which was significantly lower than that of BHD, LAM, PAP, and PLCH (all p<0.05) (Supplementary Table S2). PPFE also exhibited the lowest DLCO (48.2% predicted), which was significantly lower than BHD, PAP, and PLCH. In contrast, LAM showed the lowest FEV₁ among all subtypes (78.7% predicted) despite relatively preserved FVC (89.6% predicted), although no statistically significant pairwise differences were observed for FEV₁ across subgroups. Notably, BHD demonstrated relatively preserved DLCO (78.0% predicted), which was significantly higher than that of LAM (64.3% predicted; p=0.006) and PAP (68.4% predicted; p=0.043). The mean 6MWD across the cohort was 462 m, with the lowest distance observed in the HP group (408 m) and the highest in the BHD group (505 m). Baseline SpO₂ during the 6MWT was 97.0% in the overall cohort. The HP group showed the lowest nadir SpO₂ (88.3%), although the difference was not statistically significant.

5. Bronchoalveolar lavage analysis

BHD and LAM were excluded from the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) analysis because of the absence of tested cases. In the 19 patients with HP who underwent BAL, lymphocytes were the most abundant type of cell, comprising 49.7% of the BAL cells; 73.7% (14/19) of patients showed lymphocyte fractions ≥30% with a decreased CD4+/CD8+ ratio (Supplementary Table S2). Additionally, mildly elevated BAL neutrophil and eosinophil proportions were identified, with mean values of 14.2% and 3.3%, respectively, in the HP group. In the PAP group, lymphocytes were predominant (33.9%), with lymphocytosis (≥30%) observed in 53.8% (35/65) patients. In the PLCH and PPFE groups, neutrophil counts were elevated at 13.6% and 17.7%, respectively.

6. Serologic findings

In the LAM group, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF-D) levels were measured in six patients (7.0%) with a mean concentration of 381.2±504.1 pg/mL and only one patient had a level exceeding the diagnostic threshold of 800 pg/mL (Supplementary Table S2). Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6) levels were elevated in patients with PAP (n=7) and PLCH (n=9), with mean concentrations of 1,066.0±962.1 and 697.0±275.8 U/mL, respectively (reference range, <500 U/mL).

Genetic testing for folliculin (FLCN ) mutations was conducted in all BHD cases, and pathogenic variants were identified in 87.3% of cases (Figure 4). The most common FLCN mutations were c.469_471delTTC (16.4%), followed by c.31T>C (12.7%), and c.1285dupC (12.7%). Additional mutations included c.1557del (7.3%), c.1539-2A>G (5.5%), c.469_471del (5.5%), c.1429C>T (5.5%), and c.1285delC (3.6%).

7. Disease-associated complications

In the BHD group, pneumothorax was reported in 16 patients (29.0%) during follow-up (Table 3). Cancer was observed in 14 patients (25.5%) with BHD during follow-up, with renal cancer in six patients (10.9%) representing the highest proportion, followed by thyroid (n=4), colon (n=2), and breast cancers (n=2). In the LAM group, pneumothorax occurred in 28 patients (32.6%) during follow-up, tuberous sclerosis complex was identified in 13 patients (15.1%), angiomyolipoma in 27 patients (31.4%), lymphangioleiomyoma in 21 patients (24.4%), and chylous effusion in three patients (3.5%).

8. Treatment

Table 4 presented disease-specific treatments; management of disease-related complications was not included. All patients with BHD were managed by observation alone (Table 4). In the HP group, corticosteroids were used in 86.9% of patients; one patient (4.3%) received an immunosuppressant and another (4.3%) received home oxygen therapy. Among patients with LAM, sirolimus was administered to 53.5% of patients, bronchodilators to 25.6% of patients, everolimus to 2.3% of patients, and home oxygen therapy to 3.5% of patients. In the PAP group, whole-lung lavage was performed in 23.5% of patients and 4.9% were treated with granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF). In the PLCH group, 32.5% of patients were treated with corticosteroids, 5.0% of patients with chemotherapeutic agents, including vinblastine, 6-mercaptopurine, and cyclophosphamide, and 2.5% of patients received home oxygen therapy. In the PPFE group, 42.1% of patients received corticosteroids, 42.1% were treated with antifibrotic agents, and 21.1% required home oxygen therapy. No patients in the PPFE group received immunosuppressants.

9. Prognosis

The overall median follow-up duration was 69.5 months (IQR, 33.7 to 110.5), with prospective and retrospective cohorts having median follow-up durations of 3.0 months (IQR, 0.0 to 7.0) and 75.0 months (IQR, 38.5 to 120.0), respectively. During follow-up, PPFE showed the highest incidence of mortality and acute exacerbation among the six disease groups in this cohort (Supplementary Table S3). Among 19 patients with PPFE, acute exacerbation occurred in five patients (26.3%), lung transplantation was performed in one patient (5.3%), and five patients (26.3%) died, with causes of death including pneumonia (n=3) and unknown causes (n=2) (Figure 5 and Supplementary Table S4). In the 81 patients with PAP, five patients (6.2%) died; causes of death included pneumonia in one patient and unknown causes in four patients. In the BHD group (n=55), one patient (1.8%) died due to pneumonia.

Discussion

This is the first nationwide multicenter registry study of rare ILDs in South Korea, providing a comprehensive characterization of the six major ILD subtypes: BHD, HP, LAM, PAP, PLCH, and PPFE. Our findings revealed heterogeneity in the demographic and clinical characteristics among these rare ILDs.

Although male patients accounted for 42.8% of the overall cohort, the sex distribution varied across the rare ILD subtypes in this registry. In this study, LAM was observed exclusively in female patients, which is consistent with previous reports [20,21]. LAM typically affects women of reproductive age and is suggested to have an association with estrogen [20]. Although rare cases have been reported in males with tuberous sclerosis, such occurrences are extremely uncommon and have not been observed in sporadic LAM [22]. In contrast, HP showed pronounced male predominance in this registry. HP is reported more frequently in older women who do not smoke; however, epidemiological data on sex prevalence remain inconsistent, owing to conflicting findings across studies [23-27]. Some studies similar to our registry have demonstrated a male predominance among patients with HP [25,26]. These discrepancies may reflect geographical differences in occupational and environmental exposure profiles, with the possibility that this registry predominantly includes occupational HP cases from industrial regions with a high proportion of male workers. In our registry, patients with PAP were predominantly male. This pattern is consistent with previous studies that reported a higher prevalence of autoimmune PAP in males, often with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 2:1 [28]. PLCH also demonstrates a male predominance in this registry. However, PLCH is not inherently sex-predominant but is strongly associated with tobacco smoking, with 90% to 95% of patients being current or former smokers [29,30]. The male predominance in our registry likely reflects higher smoking rates among men in South Korea [31]. Our registry did not identify sex-related differences in PPFE. Some studies reported a female predominance, particularly in younger or non-transplant patients, whereas others found no difference [32-36]. The variable sex distribution across rare ILDs indicates that each subtype may be influenced by complex underlying mechanisms, including genetic, environmental, and hormonal factors.

Rare ILDs were often accompanied by significant comorbidities and complications in this cohort. The frequency of pneumothorax was notably high in the BHD and LAM groups at the time of diagnosis, with additional events reported during follow-up. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies that reported high recurrence rates of pneumothorax in cystic diseases, including LAM and BHD [8,37]. Although previous studies have reported that pneumothorax may occur in up to 75% of patients with PPFE over the course of the disease, no cases of pneumothorax were observed in the present registry [38]. Nevertheless, considering the potential risk of pneumothorax, close longitudinal monitoring remains essential in PPFE. Renal cancer was observed in 10.7% of patients with BHD. Despite a relatively lower prevalence than reported in previous studies, these findings are consistent with reports demonstrating an elevated renal cancer risk in BHD syndrome [39,40]. Pavlovich et al. [39] reported a 27% prevalence of renal cell carcinoma in BHD families. In a study by Zbar et al. [40] involving 223 individuals from 33 Caucasian BHD families, those with BHD had a 6.9-fold increased risk of developing renal tumors compared to unaffected individuals, after adjusting for age (95% confidence interval, 1.5 to 31.6; p=0.0025). Our findings support the need for active malignancy surveillance in patients with BHD, given the potential for multiorgan involvement.

In our registry, patients with PPFE demonstrated the most impaired pulmonary function, including the lowest mean FVC and DLCO values and the highest rates of acute exacerbation and mortality. Despite the variability in its clinical course, PPFE is generally associated with poor prognosis, with 5-year survival rates of 29%–38%, consistent with findings from the present registry [41-43]. In a study by Shioya et al. [36], which included 29 patients with PPFE and 67 with IPF, patients with PPFE showed a more rapid decline in FVC compared to the overall IPF group (−0.28 L/year vs. −0.11 L/year, p=0.02) and significantly worse survival compared to patients with IPF in Gender–Age–Physiology (GAP) stages II and III (log-rank p<0.001). Given the known risk factors for poor prognosis, including male sex, presence of a lower-lobe usual interstitial pneumonia pattern, older age, and reduced FVC and DLCO, patients with these features may require closer monitoring and more aggressive therapeutic intervention [42,43].

In addition to differences in prognosis across disease entities, prognosis may also vary according to the underlying etiology within the same disease category [44-46]. This is particularly evident in PAP, in which autoimmune PAP generally shows a more favorable clinical course than secondary PAP. Previous studies have reported a 5-year survival rate of approximately 95% in autoimmune PAP after the introduction of whole-lung lavage, whereas secondary PAP has been associated with substantially poorer outcomes because of the impact of the underlying disorder [45,46]. In a Japanese cohort, secondary PAP showed an overall survival rate of 46% and a median survival of 16 months, and in hematologic disease-associated secondary PAP, approximately half of the patients died within 15 months after diagnosis [44]. However, in our cohort, comparison of prognosis according to PAP etiology was limited by missing data and the small number of death events.

In this study, FLCN mutations were identified in 87.3% of patients with BHD, confirming that FLCN genetic analysis is a highly sensitive and essential tool for diagnosing BHD. This detection rate is consistent with previous studies, which reported mutation rates of approximately 84%–88% in patients with BHD [47,48]. The most common mutation identified in our cohort was c.469_471delTTC (16.4%), followed by c.31T>C and c.1285dupC, each accounting for 12.7% of cases. The c.469_471delTTC variant, which causes an in-frame deletion of phenylalanine at codon 157 (p.F157del), is listed in the Leiden Open Variation Database and has been reported in multiple cohort studies on BHD syndrome [47,49,50]. Despite this reproducibility, it has been observed at a relatively low frequency (approximately 3%) or in isolated case reports, highlighting potential differences across ethnic groups and study cohorts [47-50]. Additionally, the c.31T>C variant located in exon 4 results in the substitution of cysteine with arginine at codon 11 (p.C11R) of the folliculin protein. This missense mutation has also been reported in recent studies as a novel pathogenic variant found in patients who presented with typical clinical features of BHD, including multiple lung cysts, predominantly in the lower lobes, and facial angiofibromas [51,52].

In the present study, marked disease-specific differences were observed in the use of serum biomarkers and BAL fluid analysis, which likely reflect real-world diagnostic pathways for individual rare ILDs. BAL was performed at high rates in HP and PAP, specifically in 82.6% and 80.2% of patients, respectively, suggesting that BAL often provides diagnostically useful information in these conditions [17,53]. In contrast, in PLCH, cystic lung disease and PPFE, diagnosis is often based primarily on characteristic clinical and high-resolution computed tomography findings, and BAL may therefore have been performed mainly in selected cases requiring additional diagnostic support or exclusion of alternative diagnoses [29,35,54]. However, given the retrospective multicenter design, differences in institutional testing practices and medical record documentation may also have contributed to these patterns. Furthermore, the low rate of VEGF-D measurement in LAM may reflect the limited availability of this test in routine clinical practice in South Korea, despite its recognized diagnostic utility.

Because the present study was based on a multicenter clinical cohort rather than a nationwide population-based dataset, direct estimation and comparison of national prevalence or incidence across disease entities were not possible. In Korea, nationwide epidemiologic data have been reported for BHD, HP, and PAP, with a prevalence of 5.67 per 10 million population for BHD, 8,678 incident HP cases during 2011–2020 with an annual incidence of 1.14–2.16 per 100,000, and a 7-year prevalence and incidence of 4.44 and 0.56 per 106, respectively, for PAP [9,55,56]. By contrast, domestic epidemiologic data for LAM, PLCH, and PPFE remain limited, highlighting the need for more comprehensive nationwide data collection and collaborative multicenter efforts for rare or understudied ILDs.

The treatment approaches vary significantly across diseases. While all patients with BHD were monitored without pharmacological intervention, corticosteroids were commonly used in HP and PLCH, and sirolimus was the mainstay of therapy for LAM. Whole-lung lavage and GM-CSF therapy were utilized in PAP, reflecting evolving treatment paradigms for this disorder. In this study, corticosteroids and antifibrotic agents were administered to 42.1% of patients with PPFE. Evidence supporting the clinical efficacy of corticosteroids is limited, and data on antifibrotic therapies in PPFE remain scarce [57,58]. However, PPFE was included as a progressive fibrosing ILD in the INBUILD trial, which demonstrated that nintedanib reduced the annual rate of FVC decline (by 94.5 mL/year vs. placebo, p<0.001)5. Additionally, a recent study involving nine patients with PPFE treated with nintedanib for at least 3 months demonstrated a reduction in the annual rate of FVC decline (−13.6% predicted/year before treatment to −1.6% predicted/year during treatment, p=0.014), and a case report described a patient with idiopathic PPFE who exhibited stabilization of FVC during pirfenidone therapy (1.59 to 1.51 L over 8 months), followed by a marked decline after discontinuation (1.51 to 1.01 L within 10 weeks) [59,60]. These findings suggest the need for further investigations into the potential role of antifibrotic therapies in PPFE.

This study has some limitations. First, most patients were enrolled from academic centers in urban areas, particularly Seoul, which may have introduced a referral bias and limited the generalizability of the findings to broader clinical settings. Nevertheless, given the complexity of rare ILDs, diagnosis and follow-up are more frequently performed by experienced specialists in tertiary care centers. Therefore, the current study setting can reasonably be considered as a reflection of real-world clinical practice. Second, the small numbers of patients with certain diseases, particularly PPFE and HP, suggest that this cohort may represent only a limited subset of the overall Korean patient population for these disease entities, thereby limiting national representativeness and reducing the statistical power of subgroup analyses. In addition, prognostic outcomes such as lung transplantation may have been underrepresented in this retrospective cohort, as not all patients who underwent transplantation may have been fully captured. Third, although this registry involved institutions from multiple regions nationwide, some disease subgroups may have been disproportionately contributed by specific institutions or regions, which may limit the representativeness of the overall Korean population. In particular, 12 of 23 patients with HP (52.2%) were disproportionately enrolled at a limited number of centers, including those located in industrialized areas in southeastern Korea, raising the possibility of selection bias. Lastly, because most patients were included based on retrospectively collected data, comprehensive evaluations such as blood biomarker assessments and quality-of-life surveys at the time of diagnosis were not consistently performed. Accordingly, these data were available for only a small number of patients and may not be representative of the overall disease cohort. Therefore, future studies with larger prospective cohorts from a more diverse range of institutions and regions are required to improve our understanding of rare ILDs.

In conclusion, this nationwide registry offers valuable insights into the clinical spectrum of rare ILDs in South Korea. Continued longitudinal follow-ups will allow for further analyses of disease progression, treatment responses, and survival. Ultimately, this initiative will lay the groundwork for evidence-based strategies to improve the diagnosis and management of rare ILDs. In addition, future phases should consider the potential expansion of this multicenter registry to include other rare or understudied ILDs.
