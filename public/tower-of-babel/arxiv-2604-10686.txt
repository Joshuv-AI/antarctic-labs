Vector valued de Branges spaces, cnu contractions and functional models
Bharti Garg, Santanu Sarkar

Abstract
. In this paper, we study vector valued de Branges spaces associated with a de Branges operator, defined as a pair of Fredholm operator valued analytic functions on a domain symmetric with respect to the unit circle. Using a suitable direct sum decomposition of a Hilbert space, we construct a class of vector valued reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces and show that these are vector valued de Branges spaces. We further demonstrate that these spaces provide functional models for certain completely non-unitary contraction operators. We establish connections between the Sz.-Nagy-Foias characteristic function of the contraction operator, the projection operator valued function arising from the Hilbert space decomposition, and the reproducing kernel of the de Branges space. In particular, we show that the characteristic function coincides with the projection operator valued function on the unit disc. These results provide a new perspective on the role of vector valued de Branges spaces in operator model theory.

Introduction

L. de Branges introduced the Hilbert spaces of entire functions through three fundamental axioms, which are now known as de Branges spaces. Motivated by the classical scalar valued Paley-Wiener spaces, de Branges studied spaces of scalar valued entire functions, beginning with his seminal work

These spaces of entire functions have close connections with the M. G. Krein’s theory of entire operators with finite and equal, as well as infinite, deficiency indices (see

The de Branges spaces of

These spaces are found to have applications to various analytical problems, including direct and inverse problems for canonical differential systems and Dirac-Krein systems, prediction theory for stationary Gaussian processes and inverse spectral problems for Feller-Krein string equation (see

In the present paper, we study the vector valued de Branges spaces

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall all the basic definitions and preliminaries required for the paper.

First, we recall the definition of vector valued RKHS and of positive kernel functions.

A Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of
H
-valued functions defined on a nonempty set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is called a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) if there exists a
B(H)
-valued function
K_{\omega}(\lambda)
defined on
\Omega\times\Omega
satisfying the following properties:

•

For every
u\in H
and
\omega\in\Omega
, the function
K_{\omega}u
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
.

•

For every
f\in\mathcal{H}
,
u\in H
, and
\omega\in\Omega
, the reproducing property holds:

\langle f,K_{\omega}u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle f(\omega),u\rangle_{H}.

The function
K_{\omega}(\lambda)
is uniquely determined and is called the reproducing kernel (RK) of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Let
\delta_{\omega}
denote the evaluation operator at
\omega
, defined by
\delta_{\omega}(f)=f(\omega)
. Then the reproducing kernel admits the representation

K_{\omega}(\lambda)=\delta_{\lambda}\delta_{\omega}^{*}.

A function
K:\Omega\times\Omega\to B(H)
is called a positive kernel if, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, any choice of points
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\in\Omega
, and vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\in H
, the inequality

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle K_{\omega_{j}}(\omega_{i})u_{j},u_{i}\rangle\geq 0

holds.
It follows directly from the definition that the reproducing kernel of an RKHS is always a positive kernel. Moreover, the vector valued version of Moore’s theorem (see
[
34
, Theorem 6.12]
) states that every positive kernel determines a unique RKHS of vector valued functions for which it acts as the reproducing kernel.

For a detailed discussion on RKHS, see
[
2
]
and
[
34
]
.

Next, we recall the notion of Fredholm operators that will be used in the description of vector valued de Branges space
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{E})
. A bounded linear operator
A\in B(H)
is called Fredholm if
\dim(\ker(A))<\infty
,
\dim(\ker(A^{*}))<\infty
and
\mathrm{rng}(A)
is closed in
H
.
The index associated with a Fredholm operator is defined by

\text{index}(A)=\dim(\ker(A))-\dim(\ker(A^{*})).

(2.1)

Theorem 2.1
.

If
A\in B(H)
is a Fredholm operator. Then the following assertions are true.

(1)

A^{*}
is a Fredholm operator and
\text{index}(A)=-\text{index}(A^{*}).

(2)

A
is invertible iff

\text{index}(A)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\ker(A)\ (\text{or}\ \ker(A^{*}))=0.

Let us also recall the Fredholm analytic theorem that will be used below.

Theorem 2.2
.

(see
[
18
, Theorem 3.3]
,
[
19
, XI, Corollary 8.4]
) Let
T:\Omega\rightarrow B(\mathfrak{X})
be a Fredholm operator valued analytic function, where
\Omega
is an open connected subset of
\mathbb{C}
. Then, exactly one of the following two conditions holds true:

•

T(\lambda)
is not boundedly invertible for any
\lambda\in\Omega
.

•

T(\lambda)^{-1}
is an analytic Fredholm operator valued function for all
\lambda\in\Omega\setminus D
, where
D
in
\Omega
is a discrete set (a countable set with no accumulation point in
\Omega
) and
T(\lambda)^{-1}
is meromorphic on
\Omega
.

We now define the class of vector valued de Branges spaces, denoted by
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{E})
. Here we consider a domain
\Omega
which is symmetric with respect to the unit circle and contains the open unit disc. A de Branges operator with respect to the unit disc is defined as a pair of operator valued analytic functions
\mathfrak{E}(\lambda)=(E_{-}(\lambda),E_{+}(\lambda))
whose components satisfy the following conditions:

(1)

E_{+}
,
E_{-}:\Omega\rightarrow B(H)
both are Fredholm operators for all
\lambda\in\Omega
.

(2)

E_{+}
and
E_{-}
both are invertible at least at one point of
\Omega
.

(3)

E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
satisfy the following conditions:

\displaystyle(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})^{*}(\lambda)(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda)

\displaystyle\preceq

\displaystyle I\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{D},

\displaystyle(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})^{*}(\lambda)(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in\Omega\cap\mathbb{T},

\displaystyle(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda)(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})^{*}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in\Omega\cap\mathbb{T}.

We recall that the definition and construction of de Branges operator with respect to the upper half plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, where the domain
\Omega=\mathbb{C}
were studied in
[
30
,
31
]
.
Corresponding to a de Branges operator with respect to the unit disc, we define the kernel function by

K_{\xi}^{\mathfrak{E}}(z):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{E_{+}(z)E_{+}(\xi)^{*}-E_{-}(z)E_{-}(\xi)^{*}}{\rho_{\xi}(z)}&\mbox{if }z,~\xi\in\Omega~\mbox{and}~z\overline{\xi}\neq 1\\
\frac{E_{+}^{{}^{\prime}}(\frac{1}{\overline{\xi}})E_{+}(\xi)^{*}-E_{-}^{{}^{\prime}}(\frac{1}{\overline{\xi}})E_{-}(\xi)^{*}}{-\overline{\xi}}&\mbox{if }\xi\in\Omega~\mbox{and}~z\overline{\xi}=1.\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

The kernel is positive on
\Omega\times\Omega
, and
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{E})
denotes the corresponding unique RKHS of
H
-valued analytic functions on
\Omega
. The vector valued de Branges spaces
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{E})
were studied in
[
30

31
]
as Hilbert spaces of entire functions.

For clarity, we want to remark the following regarding the above definition of a de Branges operator. At first glance, condition (3) is unproblematic at those points where
E_{+}
is invertible, since the expression
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
is then defined in the usual sense. The only possible ambiguity arises at points where
E_{+}
fails to be invertible. In what follows, we show that
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
nevertheless admits a well-defined interpretation at such points in
\mathbb{D}\cup(\Omega\cap\mathbb{T})
, so that condition (3) is meaningful on the entire set under consideration. In particular, the stated inequalities and equalities in the respective domains continue to hold there as well.

By conditions (1) and (2), the analytic operator valued function
E_{+}
is Fredholm for every
\lambda\in\Omega
and invertible at least at one point of
\Omega
. Therefore, by the Fredholm analytic theorem,
E_{+}
is invertible at every point of
\Omega
except possibly on a discrete subset. Moreover, at each point of this discrete set where
E_{+}
is not invertible, the inverse
E_{+}^{-1}
has a pole. Since
E_{-}
is analytic on
\Omega
, it follows that the operator valued function
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
is analytic on
\Omega
except possibly at a discrete set, where it may have either poles or removable singularities.

We now show that, at those points of the discrete set in
\mathbb{D}\cup(\Omega\cap\mathbb{T})
, the function
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
has only removable singularities. Let
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
be an isolated singularity of
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
. Then
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
is analytic in some deleted neighbourhood of
\lambda_{0}
. On this deleted neighbourhood,
E_{+}
is invertible, and hence the contractivity condition (3) is valid on this deleted neighbourhood, i.e.,

\bigl\|(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda)\bigr\|\leq 1\qquad\text{for all }\lambda\text{ in a deleted neighbourhood of }\lambda_{0}.

Therefore, by Riemann’s theorem on removable singularities for operator valued functions
[
20
, Theorem 1.10.3]
,
\lambda_{0}
is a removable singularity of
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
. Redefining the function at
\lambda_{0}
by continuity, we obtain

\bigl\|(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda_{0})\bigr\|=\lim_{\lambda\to\lambda_{0}}\bigl\|(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda)\bigr\|\leq 1.

Hence,
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
extends analytically in a neighbourhood of
\lambda_{0}
and remains contractive there. Since
\lambda_{0}
was arbitrary, it follows that
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
extends analytically in a neighbourhood of every such singularity point in
\mathbb{D}
, and the contractive inequality continues to hold throughout the unit disc.

It remains to consider the singularity points of
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
lying on
\Omega\cap\mathbb{T}
. Let
\lambda_{0}\in\Omega\cap\mathbb{T}
be such an isolated singularity. Since
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
is meromorphic on
\Omega
, the point
\lambda_{0}
is either a pole or a removable singularity.

We claim that
\lambda_{0}
cannot be a pole. Indeed, let
\lambda\to\lambda_{0}
radially with
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. Then, by the contractivity condition in (3),

\bigl\|(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda)\bigr\|\leq 1.

Thus,
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
remains bounded as
\lambda\to\lambda_{0}
radially through points of the unit disc. Therefore, by
[
20
, Theorem 1.10.4]
, the point
\lambda_{0}
cannot be a pole. Consequently,
\lambda_{0}
is a removable singularity of
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
.

Hence,
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
extends analytically across a neighbourhood of
\lambda_{0}
. We continue to denote this extension by
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
. We now show that the equalities in condition (3) remain valid at
\lambda_{0}
. Since the set of singularity points of
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
is discrete in
\Omega
, there exists a sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}\subset(\Omega\cap\mathbb{T})\setminus\{\text{singularity points of }E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}\}
such that
\lambda_{n}\to\lambda_{0}
. For each
n
, condition (3) gives

(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})^{*}(\lambda_{n})(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda_{n})=I

and

(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda_{n})(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})^{*}(\lambda_{n})=I.

Since
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
is continuous at
\lambda_{0}
, passing to the limit as
n\to\infty
yields

(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})^{*}(\lambda_{0})(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda_{0})=I

and

(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda_{0})(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})^{*}(\lambda_{0})=I.

Thus, the extended value
(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda_{0})
is unitary. In particular,

\bigl\|(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda_{0})\bigr\|=1.

Since
\lambda_{0}
was arbitrary, it follows that every isolated singularity of
E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}
on
\Omega\cap\mathbb{T}
is removable, and after analytic extension at such points, the equalities in condition (3) continue to hold on
\Omega\cap\mathbb{T}
. Therefore, condition (3) is well defined on the whole of
\mathbb{D}\cup(\Omega\cap\mathbb{T})
.

We also record the following observation concerning the positivity of the kernel function (
2.2
). Since

is Fredholm for every

and invertible at at least one point of

Now consider the operator valued function

\lambda\longmapsto\left\{\bigl(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}\bigr)\!\left(\frac{1}{\overline{\lambda}}\right)^{*}\right\}^{-1}.

This function is well defined and analytic on

(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\{\bigl(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}\bigr)\!\left(\frac{1}{\overline{\lambda}}\right)^{*}\right\}^{-1}

Since

(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-})(\lambda)=\left\{\bigl(E_{+}^{-1}E_{-}\bigr)\!\left(\frac{1}{\overline{\lambda}}\right)^{*}\right\}^{-1}

Using this identity, one obtains the positivity of the kernel function (

) on the dense subset

Since the kernel function is continuous on

A bounded linear operator

D_{T}=(I-T^{*}T)^{1/2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{T^{*}}=(I-TT^{*})^{1/2},

and the defect spaces associated with

\mathcal{D}_{T}=\overline{\rng(D_{T})}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{D}_{T^{*}}=\overline{\rng(D_{T^{*}})}.

The characteristic operator valued function of

is defined for

\Theta_{T}(\lambda):=-T+zD_{T^{*}}(I-zT^{*})^{-1}D_{T}\big|_{\mathcal{D}_{T}}.

(2.3)

The function

is an analytic operator valued function from

3.
Direct sum decomposition of
H\oplus H

Let
T\in B(H)
be a cnu contraction. Throughout the paper, we also take the additional hypothesis that a point
a\in\mathbb{T}
belongs to the resolvent set
\rho(T)
of
T
.
Consider the operator
V_{0}:H\oplus\{0\}\to H\oplus H
by

V_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}T&0\\
D_{T}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(3.1)

Then
V_{0}
is an isometry. Indeed, for
h\in H
,

V_{0}(h,0)=(Th,D_{T}h),

and therefore

\|V_{0}(h,0)\|^{2}=\|Th\|^{2}+\|D_{T}h\|^{2}=\|Th\|^{2}+\langle(I-T^{*}T)h,h\rangle=\langle h,h\rangle=\|h\|^{2}.

Thus
V_{0}^{*}V_{0}=I
on
H\oplus\{0\}
, and hence
V_{0}
is an isometry.
Moreover, since
T
is cnu, it follows that
V_{0}
is also cnu. We also consider the operator
V:H\oplus H\to H\oplus H
by

V=\begin{bmatrix}T&0\\
D_{T}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(3.2)

Then
V
is a contraction and, moreover, a partial isometry and a dilation of
T
. Indeed,

V(h_{1},h_{2})=(Th_{1},D_{T}h_{1}),

and therefore the initial space of
V
is

(\ker V)^{\perp}=H\oplus\{0\}.

Thus
V
acts as an isometry on
(\ker V)^{\perp}
and vanishes on
\{0\}\oplus H
.
Since the restriction of
V
to its initial space coincides with the cnu isometry
V_{0}
, it follows that
V
is also cnu.

Moreover,

\ker V^{*}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}D_{T^{*}}g\\
-T^{*}g\end{bmatrix}:g\in H\right\}.

Remark 3.1
.

The operator
V
is not invertible and therefore
0\in\sigma(V)
.
We determine for which nonzero
\lambda
the operator
V-\lambda I
fails to be invertible. By
[
24
, Problem 71]
,

V-\lambda I=\begin{bmatrix}T-\lambda I&0\\
D_{T}&-\lambda I\end{bmatrix}

is invertible if and only if

\begin{bmatrix}T-\lambda I&0\\
0&-\lambda I\end{bmatrix}

is invertible. The latter holds if and only if
T-\lambda I
is invertible (since
\lambda\neq 0
). Consequently,

\lambda\notin\sigma(V)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lambda\notin\sigma(T),\ \lambda\neq 0.

Hence

\sigma(V)=\sigma(T)\cup\{0\}.

Remark 3.2
.

We have assumed that
T
is a cnu contraction with a point
a\in\rho(T)
. By the above remark, it follows that
a\in\rho({V})
. Hence there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\|(V-aI)(f+g)\|\geq c\|(f+g)\|

for all
f+g\in\mathcal{H}=H\oplus H
. In particular, for every
f\in H\oplus\{0\}
,

c\|f\|\leq\|(V-aI)f\|=\|(V_{0}-aI)f\|.

Thus
a
is a point of regular type of
V_{0}
. Note that this implies that
(V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}
is closed. In fact, the following result holds: if
z\in\mathbb{C}
is not an eigenvalue of
T
, then the closedness of
(V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}
is both necessary and sufficient for

to be a point of regular type of
T
. Moreover, the set of points of regular type is always open. For the proof , see
[
23
, Chapter 1, Section 3]

Remark 3.3
.

Since
T
is a cnu contraction,
T^{*}
is also a cnu contraction. Hence, any point
z\in\mathbb{T}
is not an eigenvalue of
T^{*}
as well. Thus,

\displaystyle\ker(T^{*}-zI)=\{0\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\overline{\rng(T-zI)}=H.

Now, if the point
z
is also a point of regular type of
T
, then
(T-zI)
is injective and has closed range. This implies that
z\in\rho(T)
.

In the forthcoming theorem, we establish a direct sum decomposition of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=H\oplus H
, where the second component is a fixed infinite dimensional closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let the operators
T
,
V
,
V_{0}
be as defined above, and assume
a\in\mathbb{T}
belongs to the resolvent set
\rho(T)
. Then for every
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{T}
, the following direct sum decomposition holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(V_{0}-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\dotplus((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp}

(3.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(V-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\dotplus((V-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp}

Proof.

Consider an extension
U_{a}
of
V_{0}
defined by

\mathcal{D}(U_{a})=(\ker V)^{\perp}\dotplus\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp},

and

U_{a}(f+\phi)=V_{0}f+a\phi,

where
f\in(\ker V)^{\perp}
and

\phi\in\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}
.
The proof is carried out in several steps. In Step 1, we show that

(\ker V)^{\perp}\cap\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}=\{0\}.

In Step 2, we prove that
U_{a}
is an isometry on its domain.
Steps 3 and 4 establish that
\mathcal{D}(U_{a})=\mathcal{H}
and

\rng(U_{a})=\mathcal{H}
, respectively.
Finally, we prove that the direct sum decomposition holds.

Step 1.

Let
h\in(\ker V)^{\perp}\cap\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}

such that
h\neq 0
. Then

0=U_{a}(0)=U_{a}(h-h)=V_{0}h-ah=(V_{0}-aI)h.

This is a contradiction, since
a
is a point of regular type for
V_{0}
.

Step 2.

Let
f,g\in(\ker V)^{\perp}
and

\phi,\psi\in\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}
.
Then

\displaystyle\langle U_{a}(f+\phi),U_{a}(g+\psi)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle V_{0}f+a\phi,V_{0}g+a\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle V_{0}f,V_{0}g\rangle+|a|^{2}\langle\phi,\psi\rangle+\bar{a}\langle V_{0}f,\psi\rangle+a\langle\phi,V_{0}g\rangle.

Since
V_{0}
is isometric on
(\ker V)^{\perp}
, we have

\langle V_{0}f,V_{0}g\rangle=\langle f,g\rangle.

Moreover, since
\psi\in\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}
, we have

0=\langle(V_{0}-aI)f,\psi\rangle,

which implies

\langle V_{0}f,\psi\rangle=a\langle f,\psi\rangle.

Similarly,

\langle\phi,V_{0}g\rangle=\bar{a}\langle\phi,g\rangle.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle U_{a}(f+\phi),U_{a}(g+\psi)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle+\langle\phi,\psi\rangle+|a|^{2}\langle f,\psi\rangle+|a|^{2}\langle\phi,g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle f+\phi,\,g+\psi\rangle.

Step 3.

Observe that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}(U_{a}))^{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big((\ker V)^{\perp}+((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}

\displaystyle\overset{\mbox{①}}{=}

\displaystyle(\ker V)^{\perp\perp}\cap\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp\perp}

\displaystyle\overset{\mbox{②}}{=}

\displaystyle\ker V\cap(V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\{0\}\oplus H)\cap\left\{\begin{bmatrix}(T-a)h\\
D_{T}h\end{bmatrix}:h\in H\right\}

\displaystyle\overset{\mbox{③}}{=}

\displaystyle\{0\}.

The equality
①
follows from the fact that if
M
and
N
are closed subspaces of a Banach space, then

(M+N)^{\perp}=M^{\perp}\cap N^{\perp}.

The equality
②
holds because
\ker(V)
is closed and

(V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}
is also closed, since
a
is a point of regular type of
V_{0}
.

For
③
, suppose there exists a nonzero
g\in H
such that

\begin{bmatrix}0\\
g\end{bmatrix}\in(\{0\}\oplus H)\cap\left\{\begin{bmatrix}(T-a)h\\
D_{T}h\end{bmatrix}:h\in H\right\}.

Then
(T-aI)h=0
, which implies
h=0
since
a\in\rho(T)
. Consequently,
g=D_{T}h=0
, a contradiction.

Step 4.

Since the range of an isometric operator is closed, it suffices to show that the range of
U_{a}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}
. Observe that

(V_{0}(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp}=(\rng V_{0})^{\perp}=(\rng V)^{\perp}=\ker V^{*}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}D_{T^{*}}g\\
-T^{*}g\end{bmatrix}:g\in H\right\},

and

((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp\perp}=(V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}(T-aI)h\\
D_{T}h\end{bmatrix}:h\in H\right\}.

Suppose, if possible, that

\left\{\begin{bmatrix}D_{T^{*}}g\\
-T^{*}g\end{bmatrix}:g\in H\right\}\cap\left\{\begin{bmatrix}(T-aI)h\\
D_{T}h\end{bmatrix}:h\in H\right\}\neq\{0\}.

Then there exist
g,h\in H
such that

D_{T^{*}}g=(T-aI)h,\qquad-T^{*}g=D_{T}h.

Hence

\displaystyle T^{*}D_{T^{*}}g=T^{*}(T-aI)h

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle D_{T}T^{*}g=T^{*}Th-aT^{*}h

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle-D_{T}^{2}h=T^{*}Th-aT^{*}h

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle(I-aT^{*})h=0.

Since
a\in\rho(T)
, it follows that
h=0
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle(V_{0}(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp}\cap((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp\perp}=\{0\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\big(V_{0}(\ker V)^{\perp}+((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}=\{0\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\overline{V_{0}(\ker V)^{\perp}+((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp}}=\overline{\rng(U_{a})}.

Step 5.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{T}
, the operator
(U_{a}-zI)
is invertible. Hence, for any such
z
and for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
, there exist unique elements

f\in(\ker V)^{\perp}
and

\phi\in\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}

such that

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(U_{a}-zI)(f+\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{0}f+a\phi-zf-z\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(V_{0}-zI)f+(a-z)\phi.

Therefore,

\mathcal{H}=(V_{0}-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}+\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}.

Since
f
and
\phi
are uniquely determined, the above decomposition is unique. Hence,

\mathcal{H}=(V_{0}-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\;\dotplus\;\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}.

The other direct sum decomposition follows directly from the definitions. ∎

Remark 3.5
.

Clearly, the space
\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.
Since
a
is a point of regular type of
V_{0}
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that the ball
B_{\varepsilon}(a)
consists entirely of points of regular type of
V_{0}
.
Since
V_{0}
is an isometry, it follows that

(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{T})\cup C_{\varepsilon}(a)

is a connected subset of the field of regularity of
V_{0}
.
Observe that

(V_{0}(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}D_{T^{*}}g\\
-\,T^{*}g\end{bmatrix}:g\in H\right\},

which is the range of the isometric operator

\begin{bmatrix}D_{T^{*}}&0\\
-\,T^{*}&0\end{bmatrix}

acting on
H\oplus\{0\}
. Hence
(V_{0}(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp}
is infinite dimensional.
It now follows from
[
1
, Section 78]
that
\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}
is also an infinite dimensional subspace.

It is shown in Theorem
3.4
that the decomposition (
3.3
) holds for all

z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{T}
. Trivially, it holds for the point
z=a
. In the following theorem, we show that it also holds for all

z\in C_{\varepsilon}(a)
for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let the operators
T
and
V_{0}
be as defined above, and suppose that

a\in\mathbb{T}
belongs to the resolvent set
\rho(T)
. Then the set of all
points
z\in\mathbb{C}
for which
M_{z}
is closed and the direct sum
decomposition (
3.3
) holds is an open set.

To prove the above theorem, we recall the following definitions and results
from
[
29
,
21
,
22
]
.

The gap between two linear manifolds
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
of a Hilbert space
H
,
denoted by
\delta(N_{1},N_{2})
, is defined by

\delta(N_{1},N_{2})=\max\left\{\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in N_{2}\\
\|x\|=1\end{subarray}}\rho(x,N_{1}),\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in N_{1}\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\rho(y,N_{2})\right\},

where

\rho(x,N_{1})=\inf_{y\in N_{1}}\|x-y\|.

We also define
\tilde{\delta}(N_{1},N_{2})
by

\tilde{\delta}(N_{1},N_{2})=\max\left\{\sup_{x\in S(N_{2})}\rho(x,S(N_{1})),\;\sup_{y\in S(N_{1})}\rho(y,S(N_{2}))\right\},

where
S(N_{1})
denotes the unit sphere of the subspace
N_{1}
.

Another equivalent definition of the gap (see
[
1
, Section 34]
) is

\delta(N_{1},N_{2})=\max\left\{\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\overline{N_{2}}\\
\|f\|=1\end{subarray}}\|(I-P_{1})f\|,\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\overline{N_{1}}\\
\|g\|=1\end{subarray}}\|(I-P_{2})g\|\right\},

where
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
denote the orthogonal projections onto

\overline{N_{1}}
and
\overline{N_{2}}
, respectively.

It follows from the definitions that

\delta(N_{1},N_{2})=\delta(N_{2},N_{1})=\delta(N_{1}^{\perp},N_{2}^{\perp}).

The relation between
\delta(N_{1},N_{2})
and
\tilde{\delta}(N_{1},N_{2})
is given by

\delta(N_{1},N_{2})\leq\tilde{\delta}(N_{1},N_{2})\leq 2\delta(N_{1},N_{2}).

The minimal angle
\alpha(L,N)
, where
0\leq\alpha(L,N)\leq\pi/2
, between
subspaces
L
and
N
of a Hilbert space
H
is defined by

\cos\alpha(L,N):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in L,\;y\in N\\
\|x\|=\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}|\langle x,y\rangle|.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
be subspaces of
H
. Then
L_{1}\cap L_{2}=\{0\}
and

L_{1}\dotplus L_{2}
is closed if and only if the minimal angle between them is positive.

Lemma 3.8
.

For any three subspaces
N_{1}
,
N_{2}
, and
N_{3}
of
H
, the following inequality holds:

\sin\alpha(N_{1},N_{3})\geq\sin\alpha(N_{1},N_{2})-\tilde{\delta}(N_{2},N_{3}).

Now we prove Theorem
3.6
.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Let

M_{z}=(V_{0}-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}.

From Theorem
3.4
and Remark
3.2
, we know that for all

z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{T}
, the space
M_{z}
is closed and the following
direct sum decomposition holds:

\mathcal{H}=M_{z}\dotplus M_{a}^{\perp}.

Since
a
is a point of regular type of
V_{0}
, there exists a constant
c_{a}>0

(depending on
a
) such that

\|(V_{0}-aI)f\|\geq c_{a}\|f\|.

Therefore, the space
M_{a}
is closed and the decomposition holds trivially for
z=a
.
Since the set of points of regular type is open, there exists
\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that
every
z\in B_{\varepsilon_{1}}(a)
is a point of regular type of
V_{0}
. Consequently,
for such
z
, the space
M_{z}
is closed.

Let
\varepsilon_{2}=\frac{c_{a}}{3}
. We will show that for all
z\in C_{\varepsilon_{2}}(a)
,
the direct sum decomposition holds. Let
z\in C_{\varepsilon_{2}}(a)
. Then for all

h\in M_{z}^{\perp}
with
\|h\|=1
, we have

\displaystyle\|(I-P_{a})h\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in H\oplus\{0\}}\frac{|\langle h,(V_{0}-aI)f\rangle|}{\|(V_{0}-aI)f\|}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in H\oplus\{0\}}\frac{|\langle h,(V_{0}-zI)f+(z-a)f\rangle|}{\|(V_{0}-aI)f\|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in H\oplus\{0\}}\frac{|\langle h,(z-a)f\rangle|}{\|(V_{0}-aI)f\|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in H\oplus\{0\}}\frac{c_{a}}{3}\frac{|\langle h,f\rangle|}{c_{a}\|f\|}\leq\frac{1}{3}.

Similarly, for all
h\in M_{a}^{\perp}
with
\|h\|=1
, we obtain

\|(I-P_{z})h\|\leq\frac{1}{3}.

(3.5)

Now, by the definition of the gap and by inequalities (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), we obtain

\delta(M_{a}^{\perp},M_{z}^{\perp})\leq\frac{1}{3}.

Hence,

\tilde{\delta}(M_{a},M_{z})\leq\frac{2}{3}.

Observe that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in M_{a},\;y\in M_{a}^{\perp}\\
\|x\|=\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}|\langle x,y\rangle|=0.

This implies that
\sin\alpha(M_{a},M_{a}^{\perp})=1.

Similarly,

\sin\alpha(M_{z},M_{z}^{\perp})=1

for all
z\in C_{\varepsilon_{2}}(a)
.
By Lemma
3.8
, we have

\displaystyle\sin\alpha(M_{z},M_{a}^{\perp})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sin\alpha(M_{a},M_{a}^{\perp})-\tilde{\delta}(M_{z},M_{a})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\tilde{\delta}(M_{z},M_{a})>0.

Hence,

\alpha(M_{z},M_{a}^{\perp})>0.

Therefore, by Lemma
3.7
, we obtain

M_{z}\cap M_{a}^{\perp}=\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{z}\dotplus M_{a}^{\perp}~\text{is closed}.

(3.6)

Interchanging
z
and
a
in the above arguments, and using the symmetry

\tilde{\delta}(M_{z},M_{a})=\tilde{\delta}(M_{a},M_{z}),

we also obtain

M_{a}\cap M_{z}^{\perp}=\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{a}\dotplus M_{z}^{\perp}~\text{is closed}.

(3.7)

We recall that for any two closed subspaces
M
and
N
of a Banach space,

(M+N)^{\perp}=M^{\perp}\cap N^{\perp}.

Now, from (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), we obtain

\mathcal{H}=\{0\}^{\perp}=(M_{a}\cap M_{z}^{\perp})^{\perp}=\overline{M_{z}+M_{a}^{\perp}}=M_{z}+M_{a}^{\perp}.

Hence, for all
z\in C_{\varepsilon}(a)
, where
\varepsilon=\min\{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\},

the space
M_{z}
is closed and the direct sum decomposition (
3.3
) holds.
∎

Throughout the rest of the paper, we will consider the following domain
\Omega_{a}
which is symmetric with respect to the unit circle and contains the open unit disc:

\Omega_{a}=(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{T})\cup C_{\varepsilon}(a)

(3.8)

Note that for
z\in\mathbb{T}
the following implications hold. These follow directly from Remarks
3.2
and

:

\displaystyle z\in\Omega_{a}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow z\text{ is a point of regular type of }V

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow z\in\rho(V)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow z\in\rho(T).

4.
Abstract construction of the vector valued RKHS and functional model

In this section, we construct a vector valued RKHS from the direct sum decomposition (
3.3
) given in the previous section, and show that under some assumptions, it is a vector valued de Branges space.

Denote

Y:=\big((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}.

By (
3.3
), for every
f\in\mathcal{H}
there exists a unique

g\in(\ker V)^{\perp}
(depending on
z
) and a projection operator

P_{Y}(z)
such that

f=(V_{0}-zI)g+P_{Y}(z)f,\qquad z\in\Omega_{a}.

(4.1)

The operator
P_{Y}(z)
is a bounded linear operator for all
z\in\Omega_{a}
.
Fix
f\in\mathcal{H}
and consider the map from
\Omega_{a}
to
Y
defined by

z\mapsto P_{Y}(z)f.

We denote this
Y
-valued function by
f_{Y}
, where

f_{Y}(z)=P_{Y}(z)f.

Define the space
\mathbf{H}
by

\mathbf{H}=\{f_{Y}:f\in\mathcal{H}\}.

Then
\mathbf{H}
consists of vector valued analytic functions defined on
\Omega_{a}
. Consider the map

\Psi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbf{H}
defined by

\Psi(f)=f_{Y}.

(4.2)

Suppose that
\Psi f=0
for some
f\in\mathcal{H}
. Then, for all
z\in\Omega_{a}
, we have

0=f_{Y}(z)=P_{Y}(z)f.

This implies that

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle(V_{0}-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}=(V-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\quad\text{for all }z\in\Omega_{a},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle f\in\bigcap_{z\in\Omega_{a}}(V-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}.

To prove that the map
\Psi
is injective, we first state and prove the following general result for cnu partial isometries.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
V
be a cnu partial isometry on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then

\bigcap_{z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{T}}(V-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}=\{0\}.

Proof.

Let if possible there exists a non zero
h
such that

h\in\bigcap_{z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{T}}(V-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}.

First, let
|z|>1
. Since
\|V\|\leq 1
, the operator
V-zI
is invertible and

(V-zI)^{-1}=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{-n-1}V^{n}.

Since,
h\in(V-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}
, there exists
x_{z}\in(\ker V)^{\perp}
such that

\displaystyle h=(V-zI)x_{z}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle x_{z}=(V-zI)^{-1}h.

Hence

0=\mathbf{P}_{\ker V}x_{z}=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{-n-1}\mathbf{P}_{\ker V}V^{n}h,\qquad|z|>1,

where
\mathbf{P}_{\ker V}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
\ker V
.
Since the above vector valued analytic function vanishes identically on
\{|z|>1\}
, all its coefficients must vanish. Therefore

\mathbf{P}_{\ker V}V^{n}h=0,\qquad n\geq 0,

that is,

V^{n}h\in(\ker V)^{\perp},\qquad n\geq 0.

Next, let
|z|<1
. Since
h\in(V-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}
, there exists
x_{z}\in(\ker V)^{\perp}
such that

h=(V-zI)x_{z}.

Applying
V^{*}
and using that
V^{*}V=I
on
(\ker V)^{\perp}
, we get

V^{*}h=V^{*}Vx_{z}-zV^{*}x_{z}=x_{z}-zV^{*}x_{z},

so that

x_{z}=(I-zV^{*})^{-1}V^{*}h=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}V^{*\,n+1}h.

Applying
\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}
, the orthogonal projection onto
\ker V^{*}
, to the identity
h=(V-zI)x_{z}
, we obtain

\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}h=\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}(Vx_{z}-zx_{z}).

Since,
Vx_{z}\in V(\ker V)^{\perp}=\rng V=(\ker V^{*})^{\perp}
, we get that
\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}Vx_{z}=0
. Hence,

\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}h=-z\,\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}x_{z}

Substituting the above expansion of
x_{z}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-z\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}V^{*\,n+1}h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n+1}\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}V^{*\,n+1}h

The right-hand side has no constant term, hence
\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}h=0
. It follows that all coefficients vanish, and thus

\mathbf{P}_{\ker V^{*}}V^{*\,n}h=0,\qquad n\geq 0.

Therefore

V^{*\,n}h\in(\ker V^{*})^{\perp}=\rng V,\qquad n\geq 0.

Now define

\mathcal{N}:=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\bigl(\{V^{n}h:n\geq 0\}\cup\{V^{*n}h:n\geq 1\}\bigr).

We claim that
\mathcal{N}
is reducing for
V
. Indeed, for
n\geq 0
,

V(V^{n}h)=V^{n+1}h\in\mathcal{N},

and for
m\geq 1
, since
V^{*m}h\in\rng V
, we have
VV^{*}=I
on these vectors, so

V(V^{*m}h)=VV^{*}V^{*(m-1)}h=V^{*(m-1)}h\in\mathcal{N}.

Thus
V\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
.

Similarly, for
m\geq 1
,

V^{*}(V^{*m}h)=V^{*(m+1)}h\in\mathcal{N},

and for
n\geq 1
, since
V^{n}h\in(\ker V)^{\perp}
, we have
V^{*}V=I
on these vectors, so

V^{*}(V^{n}h)=V^{n-1}h\in\mathcal{N}.

Also
V^{*}h\in\mathcal{N}
. Hence
V^{*}\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
, and therefore
\mathcal{N}
is reducing for
V
.

Finally, we show that
V|_{\mathcal{N}}
is unitary. Using the same reasoning as above, we get that for
m\geq 1
,

VV^{*}(V^{*m}h)=V^{*m}h,

and

V^{*}V(V^{*m}h)=V^{*}VV^{*}V^{*(m-1)}h=V^{*}V^{*(m-1)}h=V^{*m}h.

Similarly, for
n\geq 1
,

VV^{*}(V^{n}h)=VV^{*}V(V^{n-1}h)=V(V^{n-1}h)=V^{n}h,

and for
n\geq 0
,

V^{*}V(V^{n}h)=V^{n}h.

By linearity and continuity, we get that
\mathcal{N}
is a non-zero proper closed subspace reducing
V
and
V\big|_{\mathcal{N}}
is unitary. This contradicts the fact that
V
is cnu. Hence,

\bigcap_{z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{T}}(V-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}=\{0\}.

∎

Since

\bigcap_{z\in\Omega_{a}}(V-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\subseteq\bigcap_{z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{T}}(V-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp},

it follows from the above lemma that
f=0
. Hence, the map
\Psi
is injective.

Therefore,
\mathbf{H}
is a vector space of analytic vector valued functions with the usual pointwise addition and scalar multiplication. We define an inner product on
\mathbf{H}
by

\langle f_{Y},g_{Y}\rangle_{\mathbf{H}}:=\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

With this inner product, the map
\Psi
becomes a unitary operator from
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathbf{H}
. Consequently,
\mathbf{H}
is a Hilbert space. Moreover, it is also a RKHS, since the pointwise evaluations are bounded.
Indeed,

\|f_{Y}(z)\|=\|P_{Y}(z)f\|\leq\|P_{Y}(z)\|\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Observe that for all
f\in(\ker V)^{\perp}
,

\displaystyle V_{0}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{0}f-zf+zf

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(V_{0}-zI)f+zf

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(V_{0}-zI)f+z\big((V_{0}-zI)g+P_{Y}(z)f\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(V_{0}-zI)(f+zg)+zP_{Y}(z)f.

This implies that

P_{Y}(z)(V_{0}f)=z\,P_{Y}(z)f.

Hence the operator
V_{0}
is unitarily equivalent to the multiplication
operator
\mathfrak{T}
on its domain
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})
given by

(\mathfrak{T}f)(z)=zf(z),\quad f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}).

In particular,

T=\mathbf{P}_{H}V_{0}=\mathbf{P}_{H}V\big|_{H}\cong\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})}\mathfrak{T}\big|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})},

(4.3)

where
\mathbf{P}_{H}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the subspace
H
.

In the following theorem of this section, we show that the space
\mathbf{H}
is a vector valued
de Branges space
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{E})
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
T
be a cnu contraction operator on a Hilbert space
H
such that a point
a\in\mathbb{T}
belongs to
\rho(T)
.
Let
V_{0}
and
V
be the contraction operators defined in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), respectively, and let
\mathbf{H}
be the RKHS as described above. Suppose that there exists at least one
\beta\in\mathbb{D}
such that the following conditions hold:

i)

\dim(M_{z}^{\perp}\cap M_{\beta})<\infty
and
M_{z}^{\perp}+M_{\beta}
is closed for all
z\in\Omega_{a}\setminus\mathbb{T}
,

ii)

\dim(M_{z}^{\perp}\cap M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}})<\infty
and
M_{z}^{\perp}+M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}
is closed for all
z\in\Omega_{a}\setminus\mathbb{T}
,

iii)

\dim(M_{0}\cap M_{\beta}^{\perp})<\infty
and
\dim(M_{0}\cap M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}^{\perp})<\infty
,

where
M_{z}=(V_{0}-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}
.
Then the space
\mathbf{H}
is a vector valued de Branges space
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{E})
of analytic functions on
\Omega_{a}
.
Moreover, the operator
V_{0}
on
H\oplus\{0\}
is unitarily equivalent to the multiplication operator
\mathfrak{T}
on
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\subseteq\mathbf{H}
. Consequently, the operator
T
is unitarily equivalent to
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})}\mathfrak{T}\big|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})}
.

Remark 4.3
.

We want to remark here that the pairs
(M_{z}^{\perp},M_{\beta})
and
(M_{z}^{\perp},M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}})
satisfying the conditions i) and ii) given in the above theorem are semi-Fredholm. Recall that a pair
(A,B)
of closed subspaces of a Banach space is said to be semi-Fredholm if
A+B
is closed and at least one of the
\dim(A\cap B)
and
\operatorname{codim}(A+B)
is finite. For a detailed treatment of semi-Fredholm pairs of subspaces, we refer the reader to
[
27
, Chapter 4, Section 4]
.

In order to prove the above theorem, we will use the following results.

The following theorem generalizes a characterization given by de Branges for spaces of scalar valued functions (see
[
5
]
) to vector valued functions based on matrix valued RK and operator valued RK that are presented in
[
16
,
14
]
and
[
31
]
respectively.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{H}
be a non zero RKHS of
Y
-valued analytic functions defined on a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
which is symmetric with respect to the unit circle and contains the open unit disc. Let
K_{w}(z)
denote the RK of
\mathbf{H}
defined on
\Omega\times\Omega
. Suppose there exists a non zero
\beta\in\mathbb{D}
such that

K_{\beta}(z),~K_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}(z)~\mbox{are Fredholm operators for all}~z\in\Omega,

and

K_{\beta}(\beta),~K_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}(\tfrac{1}{\bar{\beta}})~\mbox{are invertible }.

Let
\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}:=\{f\in\mathbf{H}:f(\alpha)=0\}
for each point
\alpha\in\Omega
. Then the RKHS is same as a de Branges space
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{E})
, based on a de Branges operator
\mathfrak{E}(z)=(E_{-}(z),E_{+}(z))
with

K_{w}(z)=\frac{E_{+}(z)E_{+}(w)^{*}-E_{-}(z)E_{-}(w)^{*}}{\rho_{w}(z)}\quad{for}~z,w\in\Omega,z\bar{w}\neq 1,

if and only if

(1)

R_{\beta}\mathbf{H}_{\beta}\subseteq\mathbf{H}
,
R_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}\mathbf{H}_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}\subseteq\mathbf{H}

(2)

The linear transformation

S_{\beta}=-\bar{\beta}I+(1-|\beta|^{2})R_{\beta}:\mathbf{H}_{\beta}\rightarrow\mathbf{H}_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}

is an isometric isomorphism.

Moreover, in this case, the operator valued functions
E_{+}(z)
and
E_{-}(z)
may be specified by the formulas:

E_{+}(z)=\rho_{\beta}(z)\,K_{\beta}(z)\big(\rho_{\beta}(\beta)\,K_{\beta}(\beta)\big)^{-1/2}

and

E_{-}(z)=-\rho_{1/\overline{\beta}}(z)\,K_{1/\overline{\beta}}(z)\,\left(-\rho_{1/\overline{\beta}}(1/\overline{\beta})\,K_{1/\overline{\beta}}(1/\overline{\beta})\right)^{-1/2}.

Proof.

The result follows from the fact that the proof of Theorem
3.1
in
[
31
]
, originally established for spaces of entire vector valued functions, can be adapted easily to the spaces of vector valued functions that are holomorphic on the domain
\Omega
. Since, the proof technique is similar, we omit the proof details. For the matrix valued reproducing kernel setting, the analogous result may be found in
[
14
, Theorem 5.2]
.
∎

The following theorem characterizes when the multiplication operator
\mathfrak{T}
is an isometry on its domain.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathbf{H}
be a non zero RKHS of
Y
-valued analytic functions defined on a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
which is symmetric with respect to the unit circle and contains the open unit disc. Suppose that multiplication operator
\mathfrak{T}
has domain
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})
in
\mathbf{H}
and
R_{\beta}\mathbf{H}_{\beta}\subseteq\mathbf{H}
for some
\beta\in\Omega
,
\beta\notin\mathbb{T}
. Then
\mathfrak{T}
is an isometric operator on
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})
if and only if

\|(I-\bar{\beta}\mathfrak{T})R_{\beta}f\|_{\mathbf{H}}=\|f\|_{\mathbf{H}}

for all
f\in\mathbf{H}_{\beta}
.

Proof.

Let
f,g\in\mathbf{H}_{\beta}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\langle(I-\overline{\beta}\mathfrak{T})R_{\beta}f,(I-\overline{\beta}\mathfrak{T})R_{\beta}g\rangle=\langle f,g\rangle

(4.4)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle|\beta|^{2}\Big\langle\Big(\frac{\mathfrak{T}-\beta I}{\beta}-\mathfrak{T}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)+I\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\Big)R_{\beta}f,

\displaystyle\qquad\Big(\frac{\mathfrak{T}-\beta I}{\beta}-\mathfrak{T}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)+I\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\Big)R_{\beta}g\Big\rangle=\langle f,g\rangle

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\langle(\mathfrak{T}-\beta I)R_{\beta}f,(\mathfrak{T}-\beta I)R_{\beta}g\rangle

\displaystyle+|\beta|^{2}\Big\langle\Big(\frac{\mathfrak{T}-\beta I}{\beta}\Big)R_{\beta}f,\Big(-\mathfrak{T}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)+I\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\Big)R_{\beta}g\Big\rangle

\displaystyle+|\beta|^{2}\Big\langle\Big(-\mathfrak{T}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)+I\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\Big)R_{\beta}f,

\displaystyle\qquad\Big(-\mathfrak{T}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)+I\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\Big)R_{\beta}g\Big\rangle

\displaystyle+|\beta|^{2}\Big\langle\Big(-\mathfrak{T}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)+I\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\Big)R_{\beta}f,\Big(\frac{\mathfrak{T}-\beta I}{\beta}\Big)R_{\beta}g\Big\rangle=\langle f,g\rangle.

Observe that

(\mathfrak{T}-\beta I)R_{\beta}f=f.

(4.5)

Indeed, pointwise we have

((\mathfrak{T}-\beta I)R_{\beta}f)(z)=z(R_{\beta}f)(z)-\beta(R_{\beta}f)(z)=f(z).

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\Big\langle\Big(\frac{\mathfrak{T}-\beta I}{\beta}\Big)R_{\beta}f,\Big(-\mathfrak{T}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)+I\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\Big)R_{\beta}g\Big\rangle

(4.6)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\langle\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}f,\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}g\rangle

\displaystyle+\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\langle R_{\beta}f,\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}g\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)\langle\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}f,R_{\beta}g\rangle

\displaystyle-\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)\langle R_{\beta}f,R_{\beta}g\rangle.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\Big\langle\Big(-\mathfrak{T}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)+I\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\Big)R_{\beta}f,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\Big(-\mathfrak{T}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)+I\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\Big)R_{\beta}g\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|1+\frac{1}{\beta}\right|^{2}\langle\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}f,\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}g\rangle

\displaystyle-(1+\tfrac{1}{\beta})^{2}\langle\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}f,R_{\beta}g\rangle

\displaystyle-(1+\tfrac{1}{\overline{\beta}})^{2}\langle R_{\beta}f,\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}g\rangle

\displaystyle+\left|1+\frac{1}{\beta}\right|^{2}\langle R_{\beta}f,R_{\beta}g\rangle.

Also,

\displaystyle\Big\langle\Big(-\mathfrak{T}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)+I\Big(1+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}\Big)\Big)R_{\beta}f,\Big(\frac{\mathfrak{T}-\beta I}{\beta}\Big)R_{\beta}g\Big\rangle

(4.8)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}(1+\tfrac{1}{\beta})\langle\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}f,\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}g\rangle

\displaystyle+(1+\tfrac{1}{\beta})\langle\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}f,R_{\beta}g\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}(1+\tfrac{1}{\overline{\beta}})\langle R_{\beta}f,\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}g\rangle

\displaystyle-(1+\tfrac{1}{\overline{\beta}})\langle R_{\beta}f,R_{\beta}g\rangle.

Substituting (
4.5
)–(
4.8
) into (
4.4
), we obtain

\langle(I-\overline{\beta}\mathfrak{T})R_{\beta}f,(I-\overline{\beta}\mathfrak{T})R_{\beta}g\rangle=\langle f,g\rangle\iff\langle\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}f,\mathfrak{T}R_{\beta}g\rangle=\langle R_{\beta}f,R_{\beta}g\rangle.

Finally, we show that

\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})=R_{\beta}\mathbf{H}_{\beta}\iff R_{\beta}\mathbf{H}_{\beta}\subseteq\mathbf{H}.

The forward implication is immediate. For the converse, let

f\in\mathbf{H}_{\beta}
. Then

\displaystyle(R_{\beta}f)(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{f(z)}{z-\beta},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;z(R_{\beta}f)(z)

\displaystyle=f(z)+\beta(R_{\beta}f)(z),

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;R_{\beta}f

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}).

Thus,

R_{\beta}\mathbf{H}_{\beta}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}).

Now, to prove
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\subseteq R_{\beta}\mathbf{H}_{\beta}
, let
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})
. Then

R_{\beta}(\mathfrak{T}-\beta I)f=f

and

(\mathfrak{T}-\beta I)f(\beta)=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

The following is an easy corollary of the above theorem that will be used in the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{H}
be a non zero RKHS of
Y
-valued analytic functions defined on a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
which is symmetric with respect to the unit circle and contains the open unit disc. Suppose that multiplication operator
\mathfrak{T}
has domain
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})
in
\mathbf{H}
and
R_{\beta}\mathbf{H}_{\beta}\subseteq\mathbf{H}
,
R_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}\mathbf{H}_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}\subseteq\mathbf{H}
for some
\beta\in\mathbb{D}
. Then
\mathfrak{T}
is an isometric operator on
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})
if and only if the operator
(I-\bar{\beta}\mathfrak{T})R_{\beta}
maps
\mathbf{H}_{\beta}
isometrically onto
\mathbf{H}_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}
.

For the contraction operators
V_{0}
and
V
, defined in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), respectively, consider the transformation

U_{zw}=I+(z-w)(\widetilde{V}-zI)^{-1},

(4.9)

where
\widetilde{V}
is a unitary extension of the operator
V
. This transformation is analogous to the generalized Cayley transform for symmetric operators; see, for instance,
[
23
, Chapter 1, Section 2]
.

The following lemma records some basic properties of the transformation
U_{zw}
that will be used in the sequel.

Lemma 4.7
.

For all
z,w\in\Omega_{a}\setminus\mathbb{T}
with

z\neq 0
and
w\neq 0
, the following statements hold:

(1)

U_{zw}^{-1}=U_{wz}
.

(2)

U_{zw}
is a one-to-one and onto map from
M_{z}
onto
M_{w}
.

(3)

U_{zw}
is a one-to-one and onto map from

M_{\frac{1}{\overline{w}}}^{\perp}
onto
M_{\frac{1}{\overline{z}}}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

(1)

\displaystyle U_{zw}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(I+(z-w)(\widetilde{V}-zI)^{-1}\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big((\tilde{V}-z+z-w)(\tilde{V}-z)^{-1}\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big((\tilde{V}-w)(\tilde{V}-z)^{-1}\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\tilde{V}-z)(\tilde{V}-w)^{-1}=U_{wz}

(2)

Let
g\in(\ker V)^{\perp}
. Then
Vg=\widetilde{V}g
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle U_{zw}(V_{0}-zI)g

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(I+(z-w)(\widetilde{V}-zI)^{-1}\big)(V_{0}-zI)g

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(V_{0}-zI)g+(z-w)g

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(V_{0}-wI)g.

Let
h\in M_{w}
. Then there exists
g\in(\ker V)^{\perp}
such that
h=(V_{0}-wI)g
. If we set
f=(V_{0}-zI)g,
then from the above equality we obtain
U_{zw}f=h
. Therefore,
U_{zw}
is onto. Next, we show that
U_{zw}
is one-one. Let
f\in M_{z}
be such that
U_{zw}f=0
. Since
f\in M_{z}
, there exists
g\in H
such that
f=(V_{0}-zI)g
. Hence,

\displaystyle U_{zw}(V_{0}-zI)g=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle(V_{0}-wI)g=0.

Since
w
is a point of regular type of
V_{0}
, it follows that
g=0
. Consequently,
f=0
. Thus,
U_{zw}
is one-one.

(3)
Let
\phi\in M_{\frac{1}{\overline{w}}}^{\perp}
. Then for all
g\in M_{\frac{1}{\overline{z}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle U_{zw}\phi,g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\phi,U_{zw}^{*}g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\phi,(\tilde{V}^{-1}-\overline{w})(\tilde{V}^{-1}-\overline{z})^{-1}g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\phi,(\tilde{V}^{-1}-\overline{w})(\tilde{V}^{-1}-\overline{z})^{-1}(V_{0}-\tfrac{1}{\overline{z}})f\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\phi,(\tilde{V}^{-1}-\overline{w})\tilde{V}(I-\overline{z}\tilde{V})^{-1}(V_{0}-\tfrac{1}{\overline{z}})f\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-\overline{w}}{z}\langle\phi,(I-\overline{w}\tilde{V})f\rangle=0

where
f
is such that
g=(V_{0}-\tfrac{1}{\overline{z}})f
.
This implies that

U_{zw}M_{\frac{1}{\overline{w}}}^{\perp}\subset M_{\frac{1}{\overline{z}}}^{\perp}.

Similarly, we obtain

U_{wz}M_{\frac{1}{\overline{z}}}^{\perp}\subset M_{\frac{1}{\overline{w}}}^{\perp}.

By
(1)
, we get that
U_{zw}
is a one-to-one and onto map from

M_{\frac{1}{\overline{w}}}^{\perp}
onto
M_{\frac{1}{\overline{z}}}^{\perp}
.
∎

Now we prove Theorem
4.2
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

In order to prove the theorem, we will use the characterization of vector valued de Branges spaces
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{E})
given in Theorem
4.4
. The proof is divided into the following steps. In step
1
, we show that
K_{\beta}(z)
and
K_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}(z)
are Fredholm operators for all
z\in\Omega_{a}
. In step
2
, we show that the operators
K_{\beta}(\beta)
and
K_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}(\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}})
are invertible. In steps
3
and
4
, we verify conditions
(1)
and
(2)
of Theorem
4.4
, respectively.

Step 1.
Since
\mathbf{H}
is a RKHS, the RK is given by
K_{w}(z)=\delta_{z}\delta_{w}^{*}
, for all
z,w\in\Omega_{a}
. Here
\delta_{z}:\mathbf{H}\rightarrow Y
denotes the pointwise evaluation operator defined by

\delta_{z}(f_{Y})=f_{Y}(z)=P_{Y}(z)f.

Hence,

\rng(\delta_{z})=Y~\mbox{and}~\ker(\delta_{z})=\{f_{Y}:f\in(V_{0}-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\}.

(4.10)

This implies

\ker(\delta_{z}^{*})=\{0\}~\mbox{and}~\rng(\delta_{z}^{*})=\{f_{Y}:f\in H\ominus(V_{0}-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}\}.

(4.11)

Thus,

\displaystyle\dim(\ker K_{\beta}(z))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dim(\ker\delta_{z}\delta_{\beta}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dim(\ker\delta_{\beta}^{*})+\dim(\ker\delta_{z}\cap\rng\delta_{\beta}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dim(\ker\delta_{z}\cap\mathrm{rng}~\delta_{\beta}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dim(M_{z}\cap M_{\beta}^{\perp}).

Similarly,

\dim(\ker K_{\beta}(z)^{*})=\dim(\ker K_{z}(\beta))=\dim(M_{\beta}\cap M_{z}^{\perp}).

By, Lemma
4.7
, the map

U_{z\beta}:M_{z}\cap M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}^{\perp}\rightarrow M_{\beta}\cap M_{\frac{1}{\overline{z}}}^{\perp}

is bijective for all
z\in\Omega_{a}\setminus\mathbb{T}
,
z\neq 0
. Hence, for all such
z
, we have the following equality

\dim(M_{z}\cap M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}^{\perp})=\dim(M_{\beta}\cap M_{\frac{1}{\overline{z}}}^{\perp})

(4.12)

Similarly, the map

U_{z\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}:M_{z}\cap M_{\beta}^{\perp}\rightarrow M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}\cap M_{\frac{1}{\overline{z}}}^{\perp}

is bijective for all
z\in\Omega_{a}\setminus\mathbb{T}
,
z\neq 0
. Hence, for all such
z
, we have

\dim(M_{z}\cap M_{\beta}^{\perp})=\dim(M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}\cap M_{\frac{1}{\overline{z}}}^{\perp})

(4.13)

If
z\in\Omega_{a}\cap\mathbb{T}
, then by Theorem
3.4
, we have the decomposition

\mathcal{H}=M_{\beta}\dotplus M_{z}^{\perp}=M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}\dotplus M_{z}^{\perp}.

(4.14)

This implies

M_{\beta}\cap M_{z}^{\perp}=\{0\}~\mbox{and}~M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}\cap M_{z}^{\perp}=\{0\}~\mbox{for all}~z\in\Omega_{a}\cap\mathbb{T}.

(4.15)

Moreover, it is known that for closed subspaces
M
,
N
of a Banach space,

(M+N)^{\perp}=M^{\perp}\cap N^{\perp}.

Using this fact and (
4.14
), we obtain
\{0\}=\mathcal{H}^{\perp}=\big(M_{\beta}+M_{z}^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}=M_{\beta}^{\perp}\cap(M_{z}^{\perp})^{\perp}
and
\{0\}=\mathcal{H}^{\perp}=\big(M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}+M_{z}^{\perp}\big)^{\perp}=M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}^{\perp}\cap(M_{z}^{\perp})^{\perp}
. Hence

M_{\beta}^{\perp}\cap M_{z}=\{0\}~\mbox{and}~M_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}^{\perp}\cap M_{z}=\{0\}~\mbox{for all}~z\in\Omega_{a}\cap\mathbb{T}.

(4.16)

Now, by conditions i), ii), iii) of the hypothesis together with (
4.13
), (
4.15
) and (
4.16
), we conclude that
\dim(\ker K_{\beta}(z))
and
\dim(\ker K_{\beta}(z)^{*})
are finite for all
z\in\Omega_{a}
. To show that
K_{\beta}(z)
is Fredholm for all
z\in\Omega_{a}
, it remains to prove that
\rng{K_{\beta}(z)}
is closed for all
z\in\Omega_{a}
, or equivalently,
\rng{K_{z}(\beta)}
is closed for all
z\in\Omega_{a}
. By
[
26
, Corollary 2.5]
, we have that

\displaystyle\rng K_{z}(\beta)\text{ is closed}\iff\ker\delta_{\beta}+\rng\delta_{z}^{*}\text{ is closed}\iff M_{\beta}+M_{z}^{\perp}\text{ is closed}.

By the closedness condition in i) of hypothesis together with (
4.14
), we obtain that
\rng K_{\beta}(z)
is closed for all
z\in\Omega_{a}
. Similarly, using conditions i), ii), iii) of the hypothesis and by observation in (
4.12
), (
4.13
), (
4.14
), (
4.15
) and (
4.16
), it can be shown that
K_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}(z)
is Fredholm for all
z\in\Omega_{a}
.

Step 2.
By Step 1, the operator
K_{\beta}(\beta)
is Fredholm. Moreover, its index is zero since

\dim(\ker K_{\beta}(\beta)^{*})=\dim(\ker K_{\beta}(\beta))=\dim(M_{\beta}\cap M_{\beta}^{\perp})=0.

Now, by part
(2)
of Theorem
2.1
, we get that the operator
K_{\beta}(\beta)
is invertible. Similarly, it can be shown that
K_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}(\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}})
is also invertible.

Step 3.
The space
\mathbf{H}
is
R_{z}
invariant for all
z\in\Omega_{a}
. To see this, let
g
and
g^{\prime}
be such that

f=(V_{0}-wI)g+P_{Y}(w)f=(V_{0}-zI)g^{\prime}+P_{Y}(z)f,

which implies that

g^{\prime}=(V_{0}-wI)\frac{g-g^{\prime}}{w-z}+\frac{P_{Y}(w)f-P_{Y}(z)f}{w-z}.

Thus,

(R_{z}f_{Y})(w)=\frac{f_{Y}(w)-f_{Y}(z)}{w-z}=\frac{P_{Y}(w)f-P_{Y}(z)f}{w-z}=P_{Y}(w)g^{\prime}=g^{\prime}_{Y}(w).

Hence, in particular, for the points
\beta
and
\overline{\beta}
, and the spaces
\mathbf{H}_{\beta}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\frac{1}{\overline{\beta}}}
, condition
(1)
holds.

Step 4.
The operator
V_{0}
is isometry on
H\oplus\{0\}
and is unitarily equivalent to the multiplication operator
\mathfrak{T}
on
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})=\Psi(H\oplus\{0\})
, where
\Psi
is as given by
(
4.2
)
. Hence, the multiplication operator
\mathfrak{T}
is isometric operator on
\Psi(H\oplus\{0\})
. Now, using Corollary
4.6
, we have that the operator
(I-\bar{\beta}\mathfrak{T})R_{\beta}
maps
\mathbf{H}_{\beta}
isometrically onto
\mathbf{H}_{\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}}
. Since

S_{\beta}f=\big(-\bar{\beta}I+(1-|\beta|^{2})R_{\beta}\big)f=(I-\bar{\beta}\mathfrak{T})R_{\beta}f,

for all
f\in\mathbf{H}_{\beta}
, we get that the condition
(2)
holds.
∎

5.
Connections between the Sz.-Nagy-Foias characteristic function of
T
,
P_{Y}(z)
and the corresponding de Branges reproducing kernel

In this section, we show that the two operator valued analytic functions
\Theta_{T}(z)
and
P_{Y}(z)
coincide on
\mathbb{D}
.
First, we recall the definition of coincidence on an open set
\Omega
in
\mathbb{C}
. For each
\lambda\in\Omega
, let
R(\lambda)\in B(X_{1},Y_{1})
and
Q(\lambda)\in B(X_{2},Y_{2})
, be bounded linear operators, where
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
Y_{1}
, and
Y_{2}
are Hilbert spaces. The operator valued functions
R(.)
and
Q(.)
are said to coincide on
\Omega
if there exist unitary operators
A\in B(X_{1},X_{2})
and
B\in B(Y_{1},Y_{2})
such that:

R(\lambda)=AQ(\lambda)B^{-1},\quad\lambda\in\Omega.

For the cnu contraction operator
T
on a Hilbert space
H
such that
a\in\mathbb{T}\cap\rho(T)
, define the operator
S_{a}
on
H
by

S_{a}=-(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\Big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\Big)^{-1/2}.

Let us evaluate
I-S_{a}^{*}S_{a}
:

\displaystyle I-S_{a}^{*}S_{a}

\displaystyle=I-\Big((T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big)^{-1/2}\Big)^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad\times(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=I-\big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\times D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big)^{-1}.

Since

\big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\big)^{-1}\geq 0,

it follows that
I-S_{a}^{*}S_{a}\geq 0
. Therefore,

S_{a}^{*}S_{a}\leq I,

which shows that
S_{a}
is a contraction.
Consider the unitary operator matrix on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
given by the Julia operator matrix corresponding to the contraction
S_{a}
:

\Gamma_{a}=\begin{bmatrix}S_{a}&D_{S_{a}^{*}}\\
D_{S_{a}}&-S_{a}^{*}\end{bmatrix}.

(5.1)

We identify
H
with the subspace
H\oplus\{0\}\subset\mathcal{H}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{T}
,
\mathcal{D}_{T^{*}}\subseteq H
with
\mathcal{D}_{T}\oplus\{0\}
,
\mathcal{D}_{T^{*}}\oplus\{0\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
.

For every
f\in H
and
z\in\mathbb{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma_{a}\Theta_{T}(z)D_{T}f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{a}\begin{bmatrix}\Theta_{T}(z)D_{T}f\\
0\end{bmatrix}

(5.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{a}\begin{bmatrix}D_{T^{*}}(I-zT^{*})^{-1}(z-T)f\\
0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}S_{a}D_{T^{*}}(I-zT^{*})^{-1}(z-T)f\\
D_{S_{a}}D_{T^{*}}(I-zT^{*})^{-1}(z-T)f\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}h\\
h\end{bmatrix},

where

h=\Big(I+D_{T}(T-a)^{-1}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}\Big)^{-1/2}D_{T^{*}}(I-zT^{*})^{-1}(z-T)f.

(5.3)

The element

\begin{bmatrix}0\\
D_{T}f\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{H}

admits the following direct sum decomposition (see the proof of Theorem
5.1
):

\begin{bmatrix}0\\
D_{T}f\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(T-zI)j\\
D_{T}j\\
\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}-(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}h\\
h\\
\end{bmatrix},

(5.4)

where

j=(I+|z|^{2}-zT^{*}-\overline{z}T)^{-1}\Big((T^{*}-\overline{z})(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}h+D_{T}(D_{T}f-h)\Big).

(5.5)

The set

Y=M_{a}^{\perp}=((V_{0}-aI)(\ker V)^{\perp})^{\perp}

is given by

\displaystyle M_{a}^{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}\\
h_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{H}:\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}\\
h_{2}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}(T-aI)k\\
D_{T}k\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle=0,\ \text{for all }k\in H\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}\\
h_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{H}:\langle(T^{*}-\overline{a})h_{1}+D_{T}h_{2},\,k\rangle=0,\ \text{for all }k\in H\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{bmatrix}-(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}h_{2}\\
h_{2}\end{bmatrix}:\ h_{2}\in H\right\}.

Thus, in the decomposition (
5.4
), the element

\begin{bmatrix}(T-zI)j\\
D_{T}j\end{bmatrix}\in(V_{0}-zI)(\ker V)^{\perp}

and

\begin{bmatrix}-(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}h\\
h\end{bmatrix}\in Y.

By Theorem
3.4
, such a decomposition is unique. Hence,

P_{Y}(z)\begin{bmatrix}0\\
D_{T}f\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}h\\
h\end{bmatrix},

(5.6)

where
h
is as defined in (
5.3
).
Define a unitary operator
J
on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
by

J=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
I&0\end{bmatrix}.

(5.7)

Thus, by (
5.2
), (
5.6
), and (
5.7
), we obtain that for every
f\in H
,

\Gamma_{a}\Theta_{T}(z)D_{T}f=P_{Y}(z)J\begin{bmatrix}D_{T}f\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(5.8)

We summarize the above discussion in the following theorem of this section which provides the connection between the Sz.-Nagy-Foias characteristic function of
T
and
P_{Y}(z)
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
T
be a cnu contraction operator on a Hilbert space
H
such that a point
a\in\mathbb{T}
belongs to
\rho(T)
. Then, for every
z\in\mathbb{D}
, the characteristic operator valued function
\Theta_{T}(z)\in B(\mathcal{D}_{T},\mathcal{D}_{T^{*}})
coincides with the operator valued function
P_{Y}(z)\big|_{\{0\}\oplus\mathcal{D}_{T}}
, where
P_{Y}(z)
is as defined in (
4.1
).

Proof.

It remains to show that the decomposition given in (
5.4
) holds.
For this purpose, define the operator
F_{z}:H\rightarrow H\oplus H
, for each
z\in\mathbb{D}
given by

F_{z}j:=\begin{bmatrix}(T-zI)j\\
D_{T}j\end{bmatrix},\quad j\in H

The following equalities hold for all
j\in H
:

\displaystyle\left\langle j,F_{z}^{*}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle F_{z}j,\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}(T-zI)j\\
D_{T}j\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(T-zI)j,x\rangle+\langle D_{T}j,y\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle j,(T^{*}-\overline{z})x+D_{T}y\rangle.

This implies that the adjoint operator
F_{z}^{*}
is given by

F_{z}^{*}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
\end{bmatrix}=(T^{*}-\overline{z})x+D_{T}y.

Hence,

F_{z}^{*}F_{z}=I+|z|^{2}-\overline{z}T-zT^{*}.

Consider the following:

\displaystyle\langle F_{z}^{*}F_{z}j,j\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle(I+|z|^{2}-\overline{z}T-zT^{*})j,j\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(I+|z|^{2})\|j\|^{2}-\langle(\overline{z}T+zT^{*})j,j\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(I+|z|^{2})\|j\|^{2}-2\Re(\overline{z}\langle Tj,j\rangle)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(I+|z|^{2})\|j\|^{2}-2|z||\langle Tj,j\rangle|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(I+|z|^{2})\|j\|^{2}-2|z|\|j\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(I-|z|)^{2}\|j\|^{2}

Thus, for each
z\in\mathbb{D}
, the operator
F_{z}
is bounded below. Consequently, the operator
F_{z}^{*}F_{z}
is invertible. We now determine
j
for which the following equality holds

F_{z}j=\begin{bmatrix}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}h\\
D_{T}f-h\\
\end{bmatrix},

(5.9)

where
h
is as defined in (
5.3
). Premultiplying both sides of (
5.9
) by
(F_{z}^{*}F_{z})^{-1}F_{z}^{*}
, we obtain

j=(F_{z}^{*}F_{z})^{-1}F_{z}^{*}\begin{bmatrix}(T^{*}-\overline{a})^{-1}D_{T}h\\
D_{T}f-h\\
\end{bmatrix}

Since the decomposition in (
5.4
) is unique, this is precisely the element
j
given in (
5.5
). The remainder of the proof now follows from the above discussions.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Observe that for
f\in\mathcal{H}
and
z\in\Omega_{a}
, we have

P_{Y}(z)f=f_{Y}(z)=\delta_{z}(f_{Y})=\delta_{z}(\Psi f).

This implies that

P_{Y}(z)=\delta_{z}\Psi,\quad z\in\Omega_{a}.

Consequently, for all
z,w\in\Omega_{a}
, we have

\displaystyle K_{w}(z)=\delta_{z}\delta_{w}^{*}=P_{Y}(z)\Psi^{-1}\Psi P_{Y}(w)^{*}=P_{Y}(z)P_{Y}(w)^{*}.

Remark 5.3
.

From Theorem
5.1
and the expression of kernel function given in Theorem
4.4
, we obtain the following for all
z,w\in\mathbb{D}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{a}^{*}~\frac{E_{+}(z)E_{+}(w)^{*}-E_{-}(z)E_{-}(w)^{*}}{\rho_{w}(z)}~\Gamma_{a}\big|_{\mathcal{D}_{T^{*}}\oplus\{0\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{a}^{*}P_{Y}(z)P_{Y}(w)^{*}\Gamma_{a}\big|_{\mathcal{D}_{T^{*}}\oplus\{0\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta_{T}(z)\Theta_{T}(w)^{*}.

This establishes the connection between the operator valued functions
\Theta_{T}(z)
,
P_{Y}(z)
and
K_{w}(z)
.
