Infection Prevention and Control Practices Among United States Hemodialysis Facilities
Authors: Ibironke W Apata, Ana C Bardossy, Andrea Rodriguez, Lauren Hopkins, Lucy Fike, Stephanie Booth, Jesenia Angeles, Tamara Hoxworth, Lu Meng, Jeneita M Bell, Nicole R Gualandi, Shannon A Novosad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFrom 2014 through 2019, bloodstream infection (BSI) rates among US hemodialysis patients decreased by 40%. Several national efforts to address the burden of BSIs among hemodialysis patients were introduced spanning 2015 through 2019. We aimed to compare facility-reported infection prevention and control (IPC) practices in 2015 and 2021, to identify changes in practices nationally.MethodsOutpatient hemodialysis facilities complete the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) Outpatient Dialysis Practices Survey annually. Data from the Practices Survey pertaining to IPC practices were analyzed for 2015 and 2021. Counts, percentages, and medians with interquartile ranges were calculated, as appropriate. Percentage differences or points (% points) were calculated to compare specific variables from 2015 and 2021. Greater than 5% point difference was a priori selected as possibly suggesting a meaningful change.ResultsIn 2021, 7400 facilities completed the survey, comprising 503,530 dialysis patients. In 2021 compared with 2015, >5% point change was observed for several recommended catheter care practices such as exit site care (use of chlorhexidine with alcohol for skin antisepsis [90.8% uptake, +5.9% points] and antimicrobial ointment or chlorhexidine-impregnated dressing [29.0%, +6.4% points]), and catheter hub care (alcohol for hub antisepsis [84.3%, +9.9% points]). In 2021, 49.3% of facilities used chlorhexidine-impregnated catheter caps.DiscussionIncreased uptake of recommended IPC practices, and introduction of chlorhexidine-impregnated catheter caps may have contributed to decreased BSI rates among hemodialysis patients. Continued efforts are needed to increase adoption of Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)-recommended catheter exit site care practices and ensure maintenance of IPC practices with high uptake.Keywords: antibiotic stewardship, hemodialysis, infection control, infection prevention

National efforts have been made to address the burden of infections among patients receiving hemodialysis.1, 2, 3, 4 BSIs, pulmonary infections, and infections from multidrug-resistant organisms and bloodborne pathogens such as hepatitis B virus and hepatitis C virus, contribute to the high morbidity and mortality of patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 In 2021, infections were the second leading cause of both hospitalizations and deaths among patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, second to cardiovascular disease.6 Because of the burden of infections among dialysis patients, initiatives to optimize antibiotic prescribing and prevent vaccine-preventable diseases have received increased national attention among dialysis stakeholders.12, 13, 14, 15

In 2014, US outpatient hemodialysis facilities reported 29,516 BSIs to the CDC NHSN, an internet-based surveillance system that tracks health care–associated infections.16 In the same year, the Core Interventions for Preventing BSI in Outpatient Dialysis Facilities, a group of 9 evidence-based interventions shown to decrease BSIs in a small cohort of hemodialysis facilities, was published2 (Figure 1). Because catheters have a higher risk of BSIs compared with other types of hemodialysis vascular access, catheter care interventions (e.g., exit-site care, hub care) comprise a large focus of the Core Interventions.4,16 Following publication of the Core Interventions, there were several national efforts and initiatives aimed at improving IPC practices in outpatient hemodialysis facilities and decreasing BSIs.1, 2, 3, 4,17 For example, in 2016, the CDC launched the Making Dialysis Safer for Patients Coalition to increase uptake of the Core Interventions. The Coalition launch meeting was attended by dialysis stakeholders representing >50 organizations, including patient advocacy groups, dialysis organizations, academic institutions, and health departments. Other efforts have focused on engaging nephrologists in decreasing infections (i.e., the American Society of Nephrology Initiative launched the “Nephrologists Transforming Dialysis Safety” in 2016).3 Further, the 2019 vascular access guidelines from the National Kidney Foundation’s Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) provided guidance on preventing dialysis vascular access–related infections. Notably, from 2014 to 2019, the rates of BSIs among hemodialysis patients in the US decreased by 40%.18

Specific IPC practices adopted by dialysis facilities have not been reported on a national level since publication of the Core Interventions and the launch of national efforts targeting dialysis infection prevention.19 Understanding the uptake of IPC practices among facilities can help to identify areas for increased attention, highlight areas of progress, and potentially suggest practices that may have contributed to the decline in BSIs. Dialysis facilities report IPC and antibiotic stewardship practices annually as part of participation in the NHSN dialysis event surveillance.20 Since 2012, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services has required outpatient hemodialysis facilities who provide in-center hemodialysis to report these data to NHSN as outlined in the End-Stage Renal Disease Quality Incentive Program,21 and as such there is near universal reporting to NHSN from dialysis facilities across the US.16 We aimed to describe and compare IPC and antibiotic stewardship practices among dialysis facilities, in 2015 and 2021, to identify specific practices that have been adopted by facilities, changes in practices, and opportunities for improved implementation nationally.

Methods

Data Source

Participating facilities who provide in-center hemodialysis were required by Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services regulation to report monthly data into the electronic NHSN system on the following 3 reportable dialysis events: (i) i.v. antimicrobial starts; (ii) positive blood cultures; and (iii) pus, redness, or increased swelling at vascular access site.21 In addition, facilities complete an Outpatient Dialysis Practices Survey on enrollment and in February of every year thereafter.20 CDC updates the Practices Survey annually to ensure the survey captures relevant and current practices, resulting in some variations in survey questions over time. In 2015 through 2021, the survey consisted of approximately 70 questions including broad categories such as dialysis center information (e.g., general information and isolation and screening capabilities/practices), patient and staff census, vaccines, hepatitis B and C information, dialysis policies and practices (e.g., antibiotic use, infection prevention activities, and injection practices), and vascular access practices (e.g., hemodialysis catheter and arteriovenous fistula [AVF] or arteriovenous graft care). It encompassed questions aimed at evaluating the uptake of 6 of the 9 Core Interventions (Figure 1), as well as uptake of CDC-recommended practices, including appropriate antiseptics for catheter hub preparation,22,23 use of antimicrobial lock solutions for catheters,23 medication preparation location,24 and vaccinations.25 The survey additionally assessed recommended practices from professional organizations, including the form of catheter hub antiseptic packaging and AVF or arteriovenous graft cannulation technique,17 as well as practices reflecting standard of care.

Data Analyses

We selected 2015 because it represents the year before the launch meeting of the Making Dialysis Safer Coalition (i.e., the event that led to increased awareness of the Core Interventions by dialysis organizations in the US), and 2021 reflects the most recent year with data available at the time of our analysis. All facilities that completed all required components of the survey for the given year were included in our analysis. Facilities that had an incomplete survey were excluded. We limited our analyses to facility characteristics, IPC practices, vaccination uptake, and antibiotic stewardship activities. We calculated percentages and medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Percentage differences were calculated to compare specific variables from 2015 to 2021. We indicated instances when a percentage difference could not be calculated because of a change in the respective question between 2021 and 2015. We calculated percentage difference by subtracting the percentage for a given variable in 2015 from the percentage for the same variable in 2021. A cut-off of >5% points was a priori selected as potentially reflecting a meaningful change. We performed a sensitivity analysis using 2020 data on most variables to determine if there were major differences between results in 2020 and 2021, given the potential effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on 2021 data (i.e., data in 2020 reflects data collected in February 2020 before the COVID-19 pandemic). This activity was reviewed by CDC, deemed not research, and conducted consistent with applicable federal law and CDC policy (See e.g., 45 C.F.R. part 46, 21 C.F.R. part 56; 42 U.S.C. §241(d); 5 U.S.C. §552a; 44 U.S.C. §3501 et seq.)

Facility Characteristics and Personnel

In 2021, a total of 7400 facilities completed all required components of the Practices Survey; 9 facilities were excluded because of missing survey components. Most individuals (80.5%) completing the survey performed patient care at the respective facility. There was a total of 503,530 patients receiving maintenance dialysis, of which 449,734 patients (89.3%) received in-center hemodialysis, compared with 411,775 (91.1%) in 2015 (−1.8% points). There was a median of 54 in-center hemodialysis patients per facility (interquartile range: 33–81) (Table 1). Majority of facilities (87.0%, +0.6% points) were owned by for-profit organizations, and were free-standing (i.e., not hospital-based or hospital-owned) (92.3%, +1.3% points). Almost all facilities (>99%, +0.2% point) provided in-center daytime hemodialysis, and 2.5% (−2.8% points) provided in-center nocturnal hemodialysis care. Almost all facilities (> 97%) provided care to adult patients only, whereas <1% provided care to pediatric patients only and 2% provided care to both adult and pediatric patients.

More than 99% of facilities (+0.1% points) reported having a person in charge of IPC. The most common role of the designated person in charge of IPC was dialysis nurse or nurse manager (92.9%, +2.3% points). Further, 84.2% of facilities (2.4% points) reported having a dedicated vascular access nurse or coordinator.

IPC Practices

Core Interventions

In 2021, of the 6 Core Interventions captured by the survey,26 4 Interventions had a high uptake (>97%) and increased uptake compared with 2015 including the following: (i) hand hygiene audits (+4.5% points), (ii) vascular access practices observations (+2.5% points), (iii) competency assessments (+1.0% points), and (iv) catheter hub care (i.e., scrub the hub) (+3.1% points) (Table 2, Figure 1). In addition, there was increased use of chlorhexidine with alcohol for skin antisepsis during catheter exit-site dressing changes (90.8%, +5.9% points). Use of either antimicrobial ointment or chlorhexidine dressing for exit-site dressing (CDC considers chlorhexidine dressing an acceptable alternative to antibiotic ointment) was reported by 29.0% of facilities (+6.4% points), representing the Core Intervention with the lowest uptake but highest percent increase.26

Additional Catheter Care Practices

Of the CDC-recommended antiseptics for catheter hub preparation (i.e., alcohol, alcohol-based chlorhexidine, and povidone-iodine),22,23 alcohol was the most commonly used antiseptic in 2021 and 2015 (84.3% vs. 74.4%, +9.9% points) (Table 2). Conversely, there was a decline in the use of sodium hypochlorite for catheter hub prepping (4.3%, −5.7% points). KDOQI recommends single-use antiseptic solutions over multiuse solutions for catheter exit care17; there was an increase in the percentage of facilities using single-use antiseptic solution (swabstick/spongestick: 89.5%, +5.9% points, pad: 8.0%, +1.1% points) and a decrease in the use of multiuse solution (9.1%, −2.6% points). CDC suggests that showering be permitted if appropriate precautions are taken to prevent catheter contamination and KDOQI recommends that the catheter dressing should be protected from wet environments (e.g., showering), particularly when the exit site is not well-healed.17,23 Fifty-nine percent of facilities instructed their patients with catheters against showering (Supplementary Table S2). A small number of facilities (2.0%) routinely provided a protective catheter cover for showering (e.g., Shower Shield, Cath Dry) to all or most patients with catheters.

CDC recommends against routine use of antimicrobial lock solutions for preventing catheter-related BSIs (CRBSIs) but recommends its use for selected patients with a “history of multiple CRBSIs despite optimal use of maximal adherence to aseptic technique”23; approximately 5.4% of facilities (+2.3% points) reported routinely using antimicrobial lock solutions for all catheters to prevent BSIs (Table 2). Facilities reported on some catheter hub devices that do not have existing guidance from CDC and KDOQI; there was a decrease in the percentage of facilities routinely using needleless closed connector devices such as Tego and Q-Syte (26.5%, −25.2% points). Further, 56.3% of facilities reported using catheter caps impregnated with antiseptic (7.0% used 3M Curos, isopropyl alcohol disinfecting caps for needleless connectors, and 49.3% used ClearGuard HD, a chlorhexidine-impregnated cap that provides the catheter hub and port with chlorhexidine). Although a direct comparison between 2021 and 2015 was not possible because of changes in the survey questions, only 2.6% of facilities reported using antiseptic-impregnated catheter cap or port protectors in 2015.

AVF or Arteriovenous Graft Care Practices

CDC recommends either alcohol, alcohol-based chlorhexidine, or povidone-iodine for skin preparation before peripheral venous catheter insertion.23 For prepping the AVF or arteriovenous graft site for cannulation, there was increased use of alcohol (61.4%, +11.7% points), and a decrease in the use of povidone iodine (13.9%, −6.0% points) and chlorhexidine (14.3%, −1.3% points) (Supplementary Table S2). KDOQI considers it reasonable to limit buttonhole cannulation only to special circumstances because of the associated increased risks of infection.17 The percentage of facilities that reported using buttonhole cannulation for in-center patients decreased (23.9%, −30.2% points) (Table 2).

Medication Safety and Injection Practices

CDC recommends that medications be prepared in a designated medication preparation room or an area separated from the patient treatment area and designated only for medications.25 The most commonly used facility medication preparation locations were a fixed station within the treatment area (69.6%, +16.5% points), followed by a fixed location removed from the treatment area (15.2%, −15.8% points), and medication room (12.7%, +0.1% points) (Table 2). For erythropoiesis-stimulating agent formulations, 40.2% of facilities (−23.1% points) reported using multidose formulations.

Antibiotic Stewardship and Vaccination Practices

The American Society of Nephrology’s Nephrologists Transforming Dialysis Safety recommends blood culture collection before antibiotic administration for suspected BSI to optimize microorganism yield.27 Forty-one percent of facilities reported “never” administering antibiotics before drawing blood cultures for a suspected BSI. There was an increase in the percentage of facilities reporting specific processes to restrict or ensure appropriate antibiotic use (e.g., written policy for antibiotic use: 92.6%, +7.5% points, formulary restrictions: 89.2%, +6.1% points), except for automatic stop orders (52.5%, −7.5% points) (Table 3).

Facilities reported vaccine uptake among in-center hemodialysis patients, including completed hepatitis B vaccine series (70.2%, −0.4% points), influenza vaccine for the current or most recent flu season (85.4%, +3.0% points) and having ever received ≥1 dose of pneumococcal vaccine (78.5%). Sensitivity Analysis

A sensitivity analysis comparing data from 2020 and 2021 showed that data from each of these years were similar (Supplementary Tables S1–S3).

Discussion

This report summarizes comparison of IPC practices, reported by US outpatient hemodialysis facilities to NHSN via the Outpatient Dialysis Center Practices Survey, for 2015 and 2021. It is the first known report to summarize facility-reported IPC practices from most US outpatient dialysis facilities in 2 decades.19 Our analysis demonstrated an increase in uptake of most recommended IPC practices in 2021 compared with 2015. Notably, facility characteristics, including ownership and affiliation, remained stable in 2015 and 2021. In contrast, tunneled-catheter use appeared to increase, accompanied by a decrease in AVF use, a pattern that was also reported in the national data.28 Increased uptake of IPC practices and increased use of newly developed catheter devices to prevent BSIs potentially contributed to the decrease in national BSI rates observed from 2014 through 2019.18 Some recommended practices with the greatest change in uptake (>5% point change) included the following: (i) catheter care: increased use of chlorhexidine for skin antisepsis,26 single use antiseptic solution,17 antibiotic ointment or chlorhexidine-impregnated dressing,17,26 and increased use of alcohol antiseptic for catheter hub23; and (ii) AVF care: decreased use of buttonhole technique for cannulation.17 Further, use of chlorhexidine-impregnated catheter cap for catheters had a notable increase in uptake (>5% points).29 Continued efforts are needed to improve medication preparation and injection safety, vaccine uptake, and antibiotic stewardship. Although many recommended interventions and practices have high reported uptake (>97% of facilities), these results suggest that a considerable number of facilities (i.e., ≤200 facilities) may not be following recommended practices, and efforts are still needed to close these gaps.

Importantly, the use of antibiotic ointment or chlorhexidine dressing for catheter exit-site dressing remains an area where substantial progress is needed, because the results showed that approximately 70% of facilities have not adopted either practice. The CDC recommends the use of antimicrobial ointment for the catheter exit site at the end of each dialysis session, if compatible with the material of the catheter.23 The Core Interventions indicate that chlorhexidine-impregnated dressings are a reasonable alternative to polymicrobial ointment because several facilities that used chlorhexidine-impregnated foam dressings (in lieu of the antibiotic ointment) in the quality improvement project evaluating the Core Interventions had sustained reductions in BSIs.4 Studies examining the effectiveness of chlorhexidine-impregnated dressings for reducing CRBSIs among hemodialysis patients are limited. One study showed no difference in catheter-related infections with chlorhexidine-impregnated foam dressing compared with standard dressing, whereas 2 studies showed that use of a transparent chlorhexidine-impregnated dressing was associated with decreased catheter-related infections.30, 31, 32, 33 Chlorhexidine-impregnated dressings may address some of the concerns with ointments such as catheter compatibility, patient dissatisfaction with ointment stickiness, and impaired dressing adherence to the skin. However, it is not clear whether all facilities are aware that these dressings represent an alternative to antibiotic ointment; targeted education may help improve their uptake.

In 2021, 5% of facilities routinely used antimicrobial lock solutions for all catheters to prevent CRBSIs, an increase compared with 2015 (3.1%). Antimicrobial lock solutions have been found to reduce CRBSIs in hemodialysis patients using tunneled catheters.34 However, concerns have been raised about associated side effects, toxicity, and development of antimicrobial resistance.23,35,36 And until recently, there was no US Food and Drug Administration–approved lock formulation. In November 2023, taurolidine and heparin catheter lock solution (i.e., DefenCath) was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for use in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis regardless of CRBSI history.37 Exploring the reason for routine use of antimicrobial lock solutions for CRBSI prevention by some facilities may improve our understanding of the rationale and allow for implementation of solutions that could possibly mitigate potential patient safety issues.

Our analysis demonstrated widespread use (∼50% of facilities) of chlorhexidine-impregnated catheter caps (i.e., ClearGuard HD) whereas use of other catheter hub devices (i.e., needleless closed connectors) decreased. A chlorhexidine-impregnated catheter cap (i.e., ClearGuard HD) was compared with the combination of Tego and Curos in an open label randomized control trial in 2018. The trial, involving 40 dialysis facilities and >600 patients, revealed that the ClearGuard HD group had a lower BSI rate (0.28 vs. 0.75 positive blood cultures per 1000 CVC-days; P = 0.001).29,38 Currently, published guidelines do not address US Food and Drug Administration–approved antimicrobial lock solution or chlorhexidine-impregnated catheter caps, because the guidelines preceded marketing of these devices. As new products are released and become commonly used, future guideline updates will need to evaluate the evidence behind these new products and the incremental benefit above current evidence-based practices in combination with cost effectiveness.

Promoting safe medication preparation and injection safety practices in the outpatient hemodialysis setting should be a priority not only for BSI prevention, but also because breaches in these practices have been implicated in outbreaks of bloodborne pathogens in this setting.39,40 This analysis demonstrates that most facilities (>70%) are preparing medications in locations that are not recommended (e.g., fixed station in the treatment area, mobile carts, and individual dialysis stations). Dialysis facilities may face barriers in adopting these recommendations because of facility layout and spacing constraints; however, exploring recommended options such as a fixed area away from the treatment area when a dedicated medication room is not feasible remains important for patient safety. In addition, though there was a decline (−23%) in use of multidose erythropoiesis-stimulating agent vials, approximately 3000 facilities still used multidose vials in 2021. Contamination of erythropoiesis-stimulating agent medication vials during medication preparation was implicated in an outbreak of Serratia liquefaciens in 1999, in a dialysis facility that was inappropriately using single-use preparations for multiple doses.41 The CDC recommends dedicating a multiuse vial to a single patient when possible; or, if a vial is used for multiple patients, it should be accessed in a dedicated, clean medication-preparation area, away from the immediate patient-treatment area, to prevent inadvertent contamination of the vial.24 Given that multidose vials are still commonly used, efforts should be made to ensure facilities are adhering to these recommendations.

Other priorities for dialysis patient safety include the timely diagnosis and treatment of BSIs with antibiotics and prevention of infections through recommended vaccinations. Our findings (only 40% of facilities reported “never” administering antibiotics before obtaining blood cultures) are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that failure to obtain blood cultures before initiating antibiotics is a recognized challenge in the outpatient hemodialysis setting.42,43 Dialysis stakeholders should explore barriers to blood culture collection and strategies to overcome these barriers in their individual facilities. Staff training on blood culture collection, appropriate specimen processing and storage, and coordination of specimen transportation to the laboratory may be some barriers to target.27

We found a hepatitis B vaccine uptake of 70% among patients receiving in-center hemodialysis. This number may underestimate the uptake of hepatitis B vaccine by vaccine-eligible patients because facilities may not have documentation of vaccination, and the survey did not account for patients who may not be candidates for hepatitis B vaccine (e.g., patients who are considered vaccine nonresponders or who have chronic hepatitis B infection). Tracking of patient vaccinations, transfer of vaccination records between dialysis facilities and other health care facilities administering vaccines, patient engagement and education on vaccination, and collaboration among stakeholders involved in the care of patients with kidney disease are essential in promoting vaccine uptake among this very high-risk population. Further, we found a 69% hepatitis B vaccination among dialysis staff in 2021.25 Whereas hepatitis B vaccine uptake among in-center patients remained relatively stable in 2021 compared with 2015 (−0.4% points), we found a decrease in uptake among dialysis staff (−7.3% points). Exploring the reason for this concerning change and improving dialysis staff vaccination uptake is another important step in ensuring staff and patient safety.

This analysis is subject to some limitations. It relied on facility reporting and there was no validation to assess reporting accuracy. A recent report noted some discrepancies between outpatient dialysis facility leadership-reported IPC practices during interviews and observations of clinical staff IPC practices on the treatment floor, finding that leadership-reported IPC practices were superior to observed IPC practices.44 Further, though it is possible that the person completing the survey may not be knowledgeable about all facility practices, we suspect this would have minimal impact on our findings, because the vast majority (>80%) of personnel who completed the survey performed patient care in the facility. Another limitation is that we did not provide data on the years between 2015 and 2021, and practices in 2021 may have been impacted by the pandemic. However, for all reported variables, we looked at data from February 2020 (pre–COVID-19 pandemic period) to determine if practices in 2021 were different or reflect 2021 as an outlier. We found that results in 2020 were similar to those in 2021. Lastly, though we suggested that >5% point change may be a meaningful difference between 2015 and 2021 data, it is not clear exactly what percent change reflects a meaningful difference or how changes in adherence to individual interventions are related to BSI rates.

In conclusion, we provide a national-representative overview of key IPC practices, vaccination uptake, and antibiotic stewardship practices in US outpatient dialysis facilities. We demonstrate that the NHSN Practices Survey is an important tool to capture dialysis facility IPC practices across the country. Dialysis providers and front-line staff can compare their facility’s practices to guidelines or recommendations, potentially driving change in practices where gaps exist. Public health organizations, state health departments, dialysis professional organizations, and other dialysis stakeholders can use the data to prioritize and track efforts aimed at improving IPC practices through dialysis clinician education and partner engagement, including exploring barriers and solutions to implementing recommended IPC practices on a state and national scale.
