The word Dementia should be retired
Authors: Mike T Zuendel, Dani Cabral, Ana C Pereira, Eric M Reiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The term “dementia” carries a stigmatic burden that can undermine its clinical utility. Here we argue it should be retired because its continued use constitutes a public health harm by threatening personal identity, deterring care-seeking, discouraging research participation, and contributing to avoidable suffering.Subject terms: Diseases of the nervous system, Dementia

Introduction

Despite advances in neuroscience and a growing emphasis on early detection and intervention, the word “dementia” continues to evoke images of helplessness, loss, and inevitability. Language is not neutral, as the words we choose can shape perceptions, alter behaviors, and ultimately determine outcomes. Nowhere is this more apparent than in the terminology surrounding cognitive impairment during aging. The term “dementia,” with its etymological roots in the Latin for “without mind,” carries a historical and cultural burden that outweighs any clinical utility it may offer. Its use is outdated, originating in the 13th century and formalized as a medical diagnosis in 1797, and could be actively causing harm. Meanwhile, there have been significant advances in our understanding of the biology of Alzheimer’s and related diseases resulting in the progressive cognitive and functional decline, known as “dementia”. The CDC acknowledges that dementia is not a specific disease, defining it as an overall term that describes a decline in mental ability that interferes with daily life1. It affects memory, thinking, and behavior. In this Comment we argue that the continued use of the stigmatizing label “dementia” could be a threat to one’s identity, a barrier to care, a deterrent to research participation, and a contributor to avoidable suffering. Retiring the term is not a matter of semantics or political correctness but a public health necessity.

Impact of stigma

There is a body of evidence and growing consensus, including from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the World Health Organization, that stigma represents a major public health threat2. Stigma is a determinant of health as it shapes not only individual health behaviors and help-seeking patterns but also has direct biological effects on the body3. Evidence from across the spectrum of marginalized populations shows that those facing social stigma suffer disproportionately from chronic disease and premature mortality2. Thus, stigma shapes health outcomes in the same way as poverty or racism, as an insidious and pervasive social determinant.

Biologically, the effects of stigma are mediated through well-documented stress pathways. The experience of acute or chronic stigma activates pathophysiological responses, including elevated cortisol, epinephrine, and norepinephrine secretion, that are associated with cardiovascular strain and systemic inflammation2. Over time, these stress responses impact brain regions critical to memory, decision-making, and emotional regulation, by contributing to hippocampal atrophy and prefrontal dysregulation4. The stress associated with the stigma of “dementia” may therefore exacerbate the same cognitive symptoms that the word seeks to describe.

In the real world, the psychological and social consequences of stigma are deeply consequential. Individuals experiencing early signs of neurodegenerative disease often delay care due to fear of being labeled3. Families, too, may fall into patterns of denial or silence, interpreting cognitive changes as normal aging or concealing them to avoid shame5. These delays can result in crisis instead of planned actions, prevent clinical trial participation, and result in continued disease progression rather than beneficial therapy. By reducing stigma, individuals and families can access care earlier when it can make the most impact on progression of the disease.

Equally concerning is the negative impact on research into advances in disease understanding and treatment. The stigma of “dementia” discourages participation in clinical studies among both people living with the disease and their families6 and contributes to insufficient recruitment. This is particularly important among people of color, who are both at greater risk of Alzheimer’s disease and related diseases while being less likely to participate in clinical trials7. People of color also experience greater stigma associated with the disease8. This not only slows the pace of discovery but also results in less diverse and less generalizable datasets, undermining the very research efforts meant to advance treatment and understanding.

Most importantly, the stigma of “dementia” leads to a substantial loss of sense of self for those diagnosed, leading to self-stereotyping and loss of self-esteem9. Being diagnosed with “dementia” has a significant impact on one’s sense of identity, and is associated with negative emotions including loss, anger, fear, and frustration10. Psychological distress is associated with increased functional impairment, poor health behaviors and symptom management, reduced treatment adherence and lack of support through social withdrawal11. Moreover, consequences could include faster than expected functional decline related to the stigma-associated brain effects superimposed on the progressive brain disease. Mitigating this psychological distress through less stigmatizing language can help to lessen the burden of a diagnosis and enable better health outcomes.

Preferable clinical terminology

Clinical terminology should reflect both diagnostic precision and an ethical commitment to do no harm. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), has already adopted “mild” and “major neurocognitive disorder,” signaling a shift away from identity-based labels toward symptom-based descriptors. Yet in practice, the term “dementia” remains deeply entrenched in clinical, academic, and public discourse. Worse, its adjective form “demented” is still used in peer-reviewed literature and patient descriptions, inadvertently reinforcing stigma. The Initiative to Change the “D-Word” proposes more accurate and less stigmatizing alternatives, including “mild cognitive impairment,” “progressive cognitive impairment,” “debilitating cognitive impairment,” “neurodegenerative disease,” or specific disease names such as “Alzheimer’s disease.” Even broader, patient-centered language such as “brain health” and “memory and thinking issues” may serve to invite earlier engagement and reduce fear. Adopting such terminology is both scientifically sound and ethically responsible.

This is not an exercise in political correctness, nor is it a flippant proposal. We recognize that critics of the current DSM-5 terminology raised concerns about potential overdiagnosis due to the introduction of terminology for presymptomatic or prodromal stages of Alzheimer’s disease12. The inaccessibility of appropriately sensitive methods of testing might result in individuals receiving a diagnosis of “mild cognitive impairment” with the understanding that it preceded “dementia” and yet never develop more severe symptoms12. Others were concerned that early diagnosis, especially in newly established prodromal or preclinical stages using biomarkers, brought negative consequences, such as stigmatization and anxiety, with little positive benefit given the lack of reliable treatment13. Many, if not all, of these concerns have been mitigated with the availability of increasingly accurate testing and, most of all, disease-modifying therapies, which tip the scale towards the earliest possible screening and diagnosis. Without realizing it or intending, many of these arguments actually support the elimination of the term “dementia,” as they are based on an innate recognition that the diagnosis “dementia” could cause harm, including through stigmatization.

This kind of language change is not without precedent. Diagnoses such as “moron,” “idiot,” and “retarded” have been consigned to history books for their offensive and damaging connotations. “Dementia” must also be removed from the medical profession’s lexicon.

Concluding remarks

Thus, we call for an alignment of clinical practice with contemporary neuroscience, ethical standards, and public health objectives. As we seek to decrease shame and stigma, mobilize earlier detection, increase participation in research, and reduce the devastating burden of neurodegenerative disease, language must be part of our strategy.

The global medical and public health community must take up this challenge. By embracing more accurate, respectful, and motivating language, we can dismantle barriers to care, accelerate discovery, and promote dignity for millions affected by cognitive disorders. The science is ready. The ethics are clear. It’s time to change the “d-word”.
