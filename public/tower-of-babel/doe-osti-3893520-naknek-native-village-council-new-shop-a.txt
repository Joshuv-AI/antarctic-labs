1 Introduction
The Naknek Native Village Council (NNVC) has recently constructed a new workshop and
office building adjacent to the existing office building. The new construction represents a suite of
energy efficiency and building upgrades for community facilities; the adjacent village offices are
planning to be renovated in summer 2026.

This assessment identifies feasible solutions for on-site generation for the facility to support
reduced energy costs for both electricity and heat demand. Several photovoltaic (PV), solar
hydronic heating, and battery energy storage system combinations will be assessed for their
ability to meet either or both electric and projected heat loads for the new building and ride
through potential electric outages.

Naknek Native Village Council provided architectural drawings for the new building as well as
12 months of electric bills. The building shares a fuel tank with the nearby clinic, so the
building’s own fuel use could not be quantified based on actual fuel usage; the heating demand
was instead estimated via information in the architectural drawings and local heating degree
days.

This analysis was conducted as two tasks, described next.

Task 1
Task 1 compares the heat and electric load of the building to identify several appropriate PV
sizes that could fit on the roof of the building and supply either heat or electric load.

A key component of Task 1 is identifying the estimated heat load of the building and assessing
the electrical load profile by month, resulting in a comprehensive energy load assessment for the
building.

Two PV-to-heat methods are considered at a high level for comparison:

 • Hydronic solar heating: Solar collectors heat water, which is actively circulated
 throughout the building. For simplicity and mounting flexibility, flat plate collectors are
 considered with an assumed capacity of 1147 British thermal units Btu)/square feet (ft2)
 (TitanPower-Plus SU2.4).
 • Solar electric heating: Traditional solar PV directly powers an electric heating appliance
 when heat demand is present and electric load when not.
These two approaches represent the main ways solar energy can assist in meeting NNVC’s intent
to reduce heating fuel use. Hydronic systems make heat directly, whereas PV systems make
electricity that can run electric heaters such as electric boilers or heat pumps. The goal is to
understand if either of these options are feasible to consider further, based on potential size
requirements relative to the building size. Further analysis should be given to the heating
appliance to ensure the facility has adequate heating throughout the year and backup as needed.

 1

Task 2
Task 2 compares sitewide power generation and storage options for meeting electrical demands
and providing power during potential grid power outages. This task will use the REopt® tool,
developed by the National Laboratory of the Rockies, to estimate the cost-optimal installation
sizes for a variety of scenarios—including varying capital costs, utility escalation rates, and
outage durations.

2 Solar Heating Prefeasibility Analysis
2.1 Estimating Heat Load
From the architectural drawings, the area and insulation of each surface were recorded to
calculate the estimated heat loss by conduction. External walls are 3″ insulated metal panels, pre-
engineered with full-thickness batt insulation and 1-5/8″ metal stud furring. Roofs are 5″
insulated metal roof panels insulated to R-25. The garage doors are also insulated. Heating
degree days 1 were found using a base temperature of 65°F for the past year, which ranged from
just under 300 heating degree days in the summer to more than 1,800 heating degree days in
December. The goal of these calculations is to estimate how much heat the building loses to the
outdoors each month. These calculations represent a minimum estimate of heat loss because they
do not include infiltration (air leakage) or ventilation losses, though with new energy recovery
ventilators installed in the building, ventilation losses are not expected to be high. Actual heating
demand may be moderately higher, but the relative seasonal pattern will remain the same.

The heat flux by month, shown in Figure 1, represents the minimum heat demand that must be
met either by fuel or solar heating to compensate for heat loss through the building envelope.
Note that additional radiative or convective heat losses may apply but are not considered in this
analysis and that heat losses through windows may not be accurately reflected because the
architectural drawings did not have window specifications.

To meet estimated heat loads between April and September, up to 14,100 ft2 may be needed.
Because September loads are high and efficiencies are low, prioritizing heat loads between April
and August may be effective—balancing meeting heat loads with performance and potential
space constraints. Requiring more than 14,000 ft2 of collector area is far beyond what the site can
reasonably support. This reinforces that hydronic solar is not a feasible option for meeting
NNVC’s building heating needs. In addition, hydronic systems in Alaska may require freeze
protection, insulated piping, and careful placement of pumps and heat exchangers. These factors
could increase installation complexity in Alaska and further reduce feasibility compared to
standard PV systems.

Some important considerations for solar hydronic heating in Alaska include placement. Because
insulating and protecting working fluids in these systems may create challenges in mounting the
collectors on the roof as one might for traditional PV electric systems, ground-mounted systems
are popular. Practical demonstrations of solar collectors in cold and limited insolation climates
are limited primarily because of the low efficiencies.

50-kW PV System Assessment
A 50-kW PV system located on a west-facing roof in Naknek, Alaska, is expected to produce
35,680 kWh per year with a capacity factor of 8.1%. A west-facing array produces more energy
in the late afternoon, which may better align with building use patterns, though total annual
production is slightly lower than a south-facing system. The energy demands of the building,
both heat and electricity, are compared with the production of a 50 kW PV system in Figure 3,
below.

 7

 Figure 3. Monthly heat and electric demand vs. estimated production of a 50-kW PV system
Between March and September, the 50-kW PV system could likely meet significant portions of
the building’s net energy loads. However, even in February and October, some generation could
possibly offset some of the building’s load. Because the operation of the PV system is not reliant
on tank temperature like hydronic heating, the PV system can still produce usable energy even
when solar resource is low. In other words, the system performs best in the months when the sun
is available, and it can fully cover the building’s needs during summer. In winter, it still helps but
cannot replace fuel entirely. This seasonal mismatch is typical in high latitude communities:
Solar performs best when heating needs are lowest. However, even partial winter offset can
reduce fuel use and provide predictable long-term savings.

If we prioritize the PV system meeting heat loads first and electric loads second we would see a
2,000- to 11,000-kWh deficit between Jan–April and Sept–December, meaning the PV system
would meet between 7% and 55% of monthly heat load during these months. Between May and
August, the PV would meet 100% of the small heat load with 450–1750 kWh left over per month
to be used for electric loads.

To estimate gallons of heating fuel saved per month, the heating load served by the 50-kW PV
system was converted into British thermal units using a factor of 3,412 Btu/kWh. Then,
assuming 138,690 Btu per 1 gallon of heating oil, the demand served was converted into gallons
saved, not considering efficiencies of oil-based heating systems. See Table 5.

3 On-Site Power and Storage Options
This section identifies PV and possible battery options that can balance offsetting electric loads
with upfront costs. Additional scenarios will explore how to use battery storage to ride through
various duration grid power outages to provide facility-level resiliency.

3.1 Cost-Effective Systems
REopt is a techno-economic tool that helps users estimate the most cost-effective mix of energy
solutions (e.g., generators, batteries, wind turbines, PV) for a specific location based on its
unique goals and needs. REopt can simulate an off-grid energy system and estimate cost-optimal
PV, battery energy storage system, and generator sizes and dispatch—making it an ideal tool for
preliminary planning of on-site generation.

Because electricity prices, export rules, and installation costs in Naknek can vary, the analysis
explores several possible future scenarios. This approach allows the Council to see how system
recommendations change under different future conditions rather than relying on a single
assumed price or policy environment. The chosen system sizes are most sensitive to cost
parameters such as capital costs, electricity costs, and export credits provided by the utility for
excess generation of on-site systems because these parameters drive upfront costs and lifetime
cost savings. Naknek Electric Association does not have published documentation on its net-
metering program or apparent filings via the Regulatory Commission of Alaska, though the
community partner is aware of opportunities. Therefore, a range of possible net-metering
benefits are considered. Considering these parameters are uncertain, a sensitivity analysis was
run so the community can select an appropriate system size in the future when cost parameters
are known.

3.2 Outage Resiliency Planning
To identify system sizes that can support powering the facility in times of a systemwide grid
power outage, this section pursues additional REopt modeling using unchanging system costs
and outages of various lengths from 1 to 8 hours. The focus of these systems is not to deliver
maximum cost savings but to meet varying degrees of the facility’s load during outages.
However, it is worth noting that REopt will still estimate the most cost-effective way to survive
outages, even if that system size isn’t overall the most cost-effective when compared to a model
without grid outages. We will compare system sizes and total system costs for three outage
durations at both half load and full load, as shown in Table 10.

Conclusions
The analysis suggests the following key findings:
 • The feasibility of hydronic solar heating for this facility requires additional analysis, cost
 parameters, and additional details from potential installers. For the system analyzed, the
 collector efficiency was negative or low for significant portions of the year and the
 collector area required to meet even spring and summer heat loads (up to ~14,000 ft²) was
 estimated to exceed available space.
 • Traditional solar PV is a viable on-site renewable option for meeting partial electric heat
 loads and other electric loads at NNVC’s facility. A 50-kW system can fully meet
 summer electrical and heating loads and partially offset winter heating demand.
 However, the seasonal mismatch between solar availability and heating needs limits total
 fuel savings.
 • For this site, smaller PV systems could support increased cost savings by abating utility
 payments while also lowering upfront investment relative to other PV systems that are
 intended to additionally offset heating demands.
 o Financial returns for PV depend heavily on utility export rules.
 o With full net metering, systems up to ~100 kW become cost-effective.
 o With net billing or no export compensation, only very small systems (1–3 kW)
 show positive financial returns.
 o At current installation costs, a 50-kW system has a 25- to 31-year payback, driven
 mostly by summer electricity savings.
 • Battery storage improves resilience but increases cost. Small batteries can support short
 outages at the capital costs analyzed, but maintaining full building operation for longer
 outages (4–8 hours) requires significantly larger—and more expensive—systems.
 • Overall, one possible path forward is a modest PV system sized to offset summer
 electricity use, with optional small-scale storage if outage resilience is a priority. Larger
 PV systems should be pursued only if the utility confirms favorable net-metering
 policies.
 • Future decisions should be informed by:
 o Updated quotes from installers for pricing, installation limitations, and so on
 o Clarity from Naknek Electric Association on export compensation
 o Refined heating load estimates when the building is fully operational.
