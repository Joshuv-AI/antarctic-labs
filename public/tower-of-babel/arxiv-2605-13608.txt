Universal homogeneous two-sorted ultrametric spaces
Adam Bartoš 🖂 bartos@math.cas.cz, Wiesław Kubiś 🖂 kubis@math.cas.cz, Aleksandra Kwiatkowska 🖂 aleksandra.kwiatkowska@math.uni.wroc.pl, Maciej Malicki 🖂 mmalicki@mimuw.edu.pl

Abstract
We view ultrametric spaces as two-sorted structures consisting of a set of points and of a linearly ordered set of distances. We call the appropriate notion of embeddings distance-carrying (dc- for short). Those are obtained by combining isometries and linear order embeddings. We show that the class of all finite two-sorted ultrametric spaces with dc-embeddings is Fraïssé, and that the limit is the countable rational Urysohn ultrametric space 𝕌 \mathbb{U} . The space 𝕌 \mathbb{U} is dc-universal for all countable ultrametric spaces, and its Cauchy completion 𝕌 ¯ \overline{\mathbb{U}} is dc-universal for all separable ultrametric spaces, which is in contrast with the situation of classical ultrametric spaces and isometric embeddings, where no such universal space can exist. We study further properties of 𝕌 \mathbb{U} , of its variants, and of its automorphism group, which is richer than its group of isometries. In particular, we provide two types of tree representations of the two-sorted ultrametric spaces, discuss connections to valued fields, and characterize the automorphism group of 𝕌 \mathbb{U} as the semidirect product of a group of order preserving bijections and a group of isometries. Furthermore, we show universality of Aut ⁡ ( 𝕌 ) \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U}) and identify its universal minimal flow.



Keywords:

ultrametric spaces, representations, universality, Fraïssé theory, automorphism groups

Introduction

A metric space
(X,d)
is called an
ultrametric space
if for all
x,y,z\in X
, the ultrametric triangle inequality holds:
d(x,y)\leq\max\{d(x,z),d(y,z)\}
.
Consequently, for any
x,y,z\in X
, either
d(x,y)=d(x,z)=d(y,z)
(the triangle is equilateral),
or, if we assume without loss of generality that
d(x,y)
is the minimum, it holds that
d(x,y)<d(x,z)=d(y,z)
.

The
(open) ball
of center
x\in X
and radius
r>0
in an ultrametric space is the set
B_{r}(x)=\{y\in X\colon d(y,x)<r\}
. Analogously, we define the
closed ball
as
\bar{B}_{r}(x)=\{y\in X\colon d(y,x)\leq r\}
. Note that for any two balls
B
and
C
, they are either disjoint (
B\cap C=\emptyset
) or one is contained in the other (
B\subseteq C
or
C\subseteq B
). Moreover, every point in a ball can serve as its center.

Example 1.1
.

(1)

The Baire space
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}=\{x\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\}
equipped with the metric
d(x,y)=\max\{2^{-n}\colon x(n)\neq y(n)\}
, for
x\neq y
, is an ultrametric space.

(2)

A valued field (with a countable value group) gives rise to an ultrametric space. For example, consider the
p
-adic numbers
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, where
p
is a prime. Any rational number
x\neq 0
can be uniquely represented as
\frac{p^{k}r}{s}
, where
k,r,s\in\mathbb{Z}
,
s>0
, and
r,s
are not divisible by
p
. We define the norm
|x|_{p}=p^{-k}
and the distance
d(x,y)=|x-y|_{p}
, letting
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
be the completion of the rationals with respect to this metric
d
.

We are interested in
universal
ultrametric spaces. In the context of all metric spaces, there exists a separable metric space into which every separable metric space embeds isometrically.
Standard examples include the space of continuous real-valued functions
C([0,1])
equipped with the supremum norm or the Urysohn metric space, the latter being also
(ultra)homogeneous
, i.e. every isometry between its finite subspaces extends to an isometry of the whole space.

For ultrametric spaces, however, the situation is diametrically different: there is no separable ultrametric space that is universal for all separable, or even just finite, ultrametric spaces. In 1994, Vestfrid
[
33
]
constructed an ultrametric space universal for all separable ultrametric spaces, but that space is not separable. It is defined as

U=\{(x_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0})^{\mathbb{N}}\colon x_{n+1}\leq x_{n}\text{ for all }n\text{ and }(x_{n})\text{ converges to }0\},

where for
x\neq y
, we let
d(x,y)=\max\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
, with
k
being the least index such that
x_{k}\neq y_{k}
.

Furthermore, A. J. Lemin and V. A. Lemin
[
21
]
proved that there is no ultrametric space of weight
<\mathfrak{c}
into which every separable ultrametric space embeds isometrically. The authors also provided an example of a space with weight
\mathfrak{c}
:

L_{\mathbb{N}}=\{f\colon\mathbb{Q}^{>0}\to\mathbb{N}\colon f{\rm{\ is\ eventually\ }}0\}

equipped with the distance

\textstyle d(f,g)=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}}\{x\in\mathbb{Q}^{>0}\colon f(x)\neq g(x)\}

for
f\neq g
. The space
L_{\mathbb{N}}
is complete and universal for ultrametric spaces of cardinality
<\mathfrak{c}
.
Let us just mention that replacing
\mathbb{N}
with a cardinal number
\kappa
in the definition above,
Lemins obtained the space
L_{\kappa}
, which is universal for all ultrametric spaces of weight
\leq\kappa
, and
\lvert L_{\kappa}\rvert=w(L_{\kappa})=\kappa^{\aleph_{0}}
.

If we restrict ourselves to a countable set of distances
0\in E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
, a universal space does exist. In that case,

U_{E}=\{(x_{n})\in E^{\mathbb{N}}\colon x_{n+1}\leq x_{n}\text{ for all }n\text{ and }(x_{n})\text{ converges to }0\}

equipped with
d(x,y)=\max\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
, with
k
being the least index such that
x_{k}\neq y_{k}
, for
x\neq y
,
is a separable, complete
E
-valued ultrametric space into which all
E
-valued ultrametric spaces embed isometrically, as shown in the work of Gao and Shao
[
9
]
. This construction was inspired by that of Vestfrid.
All these examples admit a unified treatment, as sketched in Remark
3.25
.

The reason why there is no universal separable ultrametric space
X
lies in too strict treatment of distances: for every countable dense subspace
D\subseteq X
and two points
x\neq y\in X
there are points
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in D
with
d(x,x^{\prime}),d(y,y^{\prime})<d(x,y)
.
Hence, by the ultrametric triangle inequality we have
d(x,y)=d(x,y^{\prime})=d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, and so
d(x,y)
is one of the countably many distances attained by
D
.
Therefore,
X
does not contain even all two-point spaces, and if we wish to find a universal separable ultrametric space, we must disregard exact numerical values for distances.

We work with ultrametric spaces viewed as two-sorted structures where the metric takes values in a linearly ordered set of distances.
We also consider appropriate two-sorted embeddings, called
dc-embeddings
, preserving the orders of the distance sets.
Using Fraïssé theory, we will construct such a two-sorted ultrametric space. The Fraïssé limit
\mathbb{U}
will be countable and universal for all countable two-sorted ultrametric spaces.
Furthermore, since a (uniformly continuous) dc-embedding from one ultrametric space to another extends uniquely to a dc-embedding between their Cauchy completions, the completion
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
will be universal for all separable two-sorted ultrametric spaces.
More broadly, in the paper we embark on a systematic study of two-sorted ultrametric spaces, ways of representing them, methods of constructing universal objects, and properties of their automorphism groups.

In a recent related work by Gheysens, Pavlica, Pech, Pech, and Schneider
[
10
]
, the authors consider structures with a quaternary relation expressing when points in a pair are closer to each other than points in another pair, without explicitly referring to any distances.
They call such structures
echeloned spaces
, and among other results prove that finite echeloned spaces form a Fraïssé class, that their order expansions form a Ramsey class, and that the automorphism group of the Fraïssé limit is universal (via a Katětov functor).
Even though echeloned spaces are substantially different from ultrametric spaces, some of the results are parallel to ours.
We compare both structures in more detail in Remark
3.12
.

In a follow-up paper
[
2
]
we prove the existence of a generic dc-automorphism of
\mathbb{U}
and the non-existence of a generic pair of dc-automorphisms. We achieve the former result by showing cofinal amalgamation property of partial automorphisms and characterizing amalgamation bases. Moreover, we develop a general strategy for showing cofinal amalgamation property for a broad class of categories. Additionally, we carry out a detailed analysis of possible structures of an orbit of a point under a dc-automorphism.

1.1
Summary of the results

Initially motivated by the study of universal ultrametric spaces, this work provides a construction of a generic universal ultrametric space and investigates its completions and their automorphism groups.
Parallel to these results, we develop several tools of independent interest such as principles for transferring Fraïssé-theoretic properties between categories, and representations of ultrametric spaces via certain categories of trees and ball spaces and via spaces of functions.
While this is partially present in the mathematical folklore, we significantly extend the tools and provide a systematic and unified treatment.
We now summarize the variety of obtained results for the reader’s convenience.

Section
2
establishes the general framework for studying two-sorted ultrametric spaces, including two ways to represent them as trees, and for studying their automorphism groups. In Section
3
we construct the universal homogeneous two-sorted ultrametric space
\mathbb{U}
and several related Fraïssé limits, and we investigate its completions. Section
4
compares the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
with the isometry group
\operatorname{Iso}(\mathbb{U})
and discusses universality of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
via Katětov functors.

Preliminaries (Section
2
).

We introduce all key notions, present our setup, and collect general observations for later use.

•

Despite working with concrete possibly many-sorted structures, we use a basic category-theoretic framework to systematically organize and compare classes of structures we are interested in.
This includes notation for induced categories and of finite structures and of partial automorphisms.

•

Ultrametric spaces are introduced as two-sorted structures, where the first sort consists of points and the second of distances in a linear order. Maps, called dc-embeddings, are not necessarily isometric embeddings, we only require that they preserve the order between distances.
This does not always imply (uniform) continuity.
We provide a characterization (Lemma
2.13
).

•

The Cauchy completion
\overline{X}
of a two-sorted ultrametric space
X
behaves as expected, and in particular has the universal property for uniformly continuous dc-embeddings (Observation
2.14
).

•

For any ultrametric space
X
, we endow
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
with two topologies of pointwise convergence depending on whether we view
X
as a discrete or metric structure – the latter is denoted by
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
.
If
X
is separable without isolated points, then the adjacent balls functor
\mathscr{B}
(Proposition
2.7
) induces an isomorphism of Polish groups
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(\overline{X})\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{X})
(Proposition
2.19
).

Generic ultrametric spaces (Section
3
).
We construct the universal homogeneous two-sorted ultrametric space
\mathbb{U}
as a Fraïssé limit of the class of finite two-sorted ultrametric spaces.

•

We establish transfer principles (Propositions
3.7
and
3.8
) that allow us to transfer
Fraïssé-theoretic properties between different categories of structures, which we then apply to several classes related to ultrametric spaces.

•

We show that the class
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
of all finite two-sorted ultrametric spaces is a Fraïssé class (Lemma
3.9
). Its Fraïssé limit
\mathbb{U}
is dc-isomorphic to
\mathbb{U}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, the countable rational Urysohn ultrametric space, and so is both dc-homogeneous and iso-homogeneous (Theorem
3.11
).

•

The countable rational Urysohn ultrametric space
\mathbb{U}
is universal for countable ultrametric spaces even with respect to uniformly continuous dc-embeddings (Proposition
3.21
), and therefore its Cauchy completion
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
is universal for separable ultrametric spaces (Corollary
3.22
).

•

The class
\mathfrak{B}_{\operatorname{fin}}
of all finite leveled adjacency trees is Fraïssé, and its limits is the open ball structure
\mathscr{B}_{\mathbb{U}}
of the space
\mathbb{U}
(Proposition
3.24
).
Moreover, the Polish group
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{\mathbb{U}})
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(\overline{\mathbb{U}})
(Proposition
3.23
).

•

We study convexly ordered ultrametric spaces, i.e. linearly ordered in a way that each ball is convex. The resulting class
\mathfrak{U}^{\prec}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is a Fraïssé class whose limit is
\mathbb{U}
endowed with the generic convex order (Theorem
3.16
).

•

For
M\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\geq 2}\cup\{\infty\}
, we consider ultrametric spaces where sizes of maximal equilateral sets are bounded by elements of
M
. The resulting class
\mathfrak{U}^{M}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is Fraïssé and its limit
\mathbb{U}^{M}
is again both dc-homogeneous and iso-homogeneous (Theorem
3.20
).

•

We provide concrete representations of
\mathbb{U}
and of its Cauchy and spherical completions (Section
3.6
), and we summarize their homogeneity properties (Proposition
3.29
).
The concrete representation of
\mathbb{U}
will also give us a section for the canonical projection
\pi\colon\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
, and similarly for the completions (Proposition
3.26
).

•

We discuss connections to valued fields,
in particular which of the concrete representations admit a natural field structure (Corollary
3.31
).
We conclude that
\mathbb{U}
is the ultrametric reduct of every valued field of the form
k(t^{\mathbb{Q}})
for a countable field
k
(Corollary
3.33
).

Automorphism groups (Section
4
).

We investigate the relationship between the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
, the isometry group
\operatorname{Iso}(\mathbb{U})
, and the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
of the ordered set of non-negative rationals.

•

We realize the dc-automorphism groups as topological semidirect products, namely
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})\cong\operatorname{Iso}(\mathbb{U})\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
and
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(\overline{\mathbb{U}})\cong\operatorname{Iso_{m}}(\overline{\mathbb{U}})\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
(Theorem
4.4
).

•

Using results of Jahel–Zucker for short exact sequences, we establish extreme amenability of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U}^{\prec})
and amenability of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
(Theorem
4.7
).
Furthermore, using work of Kechris–Pestov–Todorčević, we describe the universal minimal flow of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
(Theorem
4.10
).

•

Finite ultrametric spaces admit a Katětov functor (Proposition
4.12
), and consequently
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
is a topologically universal automorphism group for countable ultrametric spaces (Corollary
4.13
).
Universality of
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(\overline{\mathbb{U}})
for automorphism groups of separable ultrametric spaces remains open (Question
4.14
).

2
Preliminaries

2.1
General framework

As we shall discuss several different (but related) types of structures as well as several types of structure-preserving maps, we shall adopt the language of category theory to treat the matter in a systematic way.
Recall that a category
\mathscr{C}
consists of a family of objects (sometimes called
\mathscr{C}
-objects) and of a composable family of morphisms (sometimes called arrows or
\mathscr{C}
-maps),
i.e. for every
\mathscr{C}
-maps between
\mathscr{C}
-objects
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to Z
, we have the composition
g\circ f\colon X\to Z
.
The composition is associative.
We also have the identity morphism
{\operatorname{id}_{X}}\colon X\to X
for every
\mathscr{C}
-object
X
that is neutral with respect to composition.

Recall that a
subcategory

\mathscr{D}\subseteq\mathscr{C}
is a category consisting of some
\mathscr{C}
-objects and some
\mathscr{C}
-maps between them, with the same composition.
A subcategory
\mathscr{D}\subseteq\mathscr{C}
is called
full
if we restrict only objects, i.e. if every
\mathscr{C}
-map between
\mathscr{D}
-objects is a
\mathscr{D}
-map, and a subcategory
\mathscr{D}\subseteq\mathscr{C}
is called
wide
if it has the same objects as
\mathscr{C}
, i.e. if we are restricting only the morphisms.
We will use some other standard category-theoretic notions such as functors, natural transformations, equivalences of categories, and colimits. See a standard reference such as
[
22
]
for details.

Our categories consist of model-theoretic structures in a fixed signature (sometimes many-sorted, but we use only finitely many sorts), and of all embeddings.
We call them
categories of structures
.
For every category of structures
\mathscr{C}
, the full subcategory of all finite structures is denoted by
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
Also, there is a notion of a substructure, i.e. of an embedding that is an inclusion on the level of sets.
The notation is
X\leq Y
, meaning that
X
is a substructure of
Y
and that
Y
is an extension of
X
.
Arguments are sometimes simplified by replacing embeddings with substructures. This is without loss of generality because, up to isomorphism, every embedding is an inclusion.

Recall that in general an
isomorphism
in a category
\mathscr{C}
is a
\mathscr{C}
-map
f\colon X\to Y
such that there is
\mathscr{C}
-map
f^{-1}\colon Y\to X
satisfying
f^{-1}\circ f={\operatorname{id}_{X}}
and
f\circ f^{-1}={\operatorname{id}_{Y}}
.
In a category of structures, it is equivalently a
\mathscr{C}
-map that is bijective (in every sort) as
\mathscr{C}
-maps are embeddings.
Isomorphisms
X\to X
are called
automorphisms
.
For every
\mathscr{C}
-object
X
the automorphism group of
X
is denoted by
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
.

Remark 2.1
.

Some general results we will use, such as the characterization of existence of a generic automorphism due to Kerchis–Rosendal
[
17
]
or the Kechris–Pestov–Todorčević correspondence
[
16
]
, are stated for classes of one-sorted structures (with both functions and relations).
This is not a problem for us as a many-sorted structure can be turned into a one-sorted structure while preserving the notion of embedding, and hence any category of structures is equivalent to a category of one-sorted structures.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a category of finite structures.
For every
\mathscr{C}
-object
A
a
partial automorphism
of
A
is a
\mathscr{C}
-isomorphism
p
such that
\operatorname{dom}(p),\operatorname{rng}(p)\leq A
, i.e. an isomorphism between substructures of
A
.
An embedding of partial automorphisms
f\colon\langle A,p\rangle\to\langle B,q\rangle
is a
\mathscr{C}
-map
f\colon A\to B
such that
f\circ p=q\circ f
on
\operatorname{dom}(p)
, from which it follows that
f
restricts to (unique)
\mathscr{C}
-maps
\operatorname{dom}(p)\to\operatorname{dom}(q)
and
\operatorname{rng}(p)\to\operatorname{rng}(q)
.
We denote the category of partial automorphisms in
\mathscr{C}
and their embeddings by
\mathscr{C}^{*}
.
We can view
\mathscr{C}^{*}
as a category of structures. Indeed, if structures in
\mathscr{C}
are one-sorted, we turn
\langle A,p\rangle
into a three-sorted structure with sorts
A,\operatorname{dom}(p),\operatorname{rng}(p)
, and we add the embeddings
\operatorname{dom}(p)\to A
and
\operatorname{rng}(p)\to A
as well as both
p
and
p^{-1}
as operations. If
\mathscr{C}
is many-sorted, we do this for every sort.

2.2
Two-sorted ultrametric spaces

By
\mathfrak{L}
we denote the category of all linear orders
\langle L,\leq_{L}\rangle
and all embeddings, i.e. maps
f\colon L\to L^{\prime}
such that
x\leq_{L}y
if and only if
f(x)\leq_{L^{\prime}}f(y)
.
It is well-known that
\mathfrak{L}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is a Fraïssé class, whose limit is the linearly ordered set of rationals
\mathbb{Q}
.
(We recall Fraïssé theory in Section
3
.)

We shall consider ultrametric spaces with linear orders as sets of distances.
First, let
\mathfrak{L}^{0}
denote the category of linear orders
\langle L,\leq_{L},0_{L}\rangle
with the least element
0_{L}
and of embeddings of linear orders preserving the least element.
As usual, the triple
\langle L,\leq_{L},0_{L}\rangle
is often denoted just by
L
, and
\leq_{L}
and
0_{L}
are often denoted just by
\leq
and
0
, respectively.
Clearly,
\mathfrak{L}^{0}
is a category equivalent to
\mathfrak{L}
.

We propose to consider ultrametric spaces as two-sorted structures of the form
\langle X,d_{X},D_{X}\rangle
,
where
X
is a set of points,
D_{X}
is a set of distances, i.e. an
\mathfrak{L}^{0}
-object, and
d_{X}\colon X\times X\to D_{X}
is an ultrametric.
To be more specific,
d=d_{X}
is symmetric,
d(x,x^{\prime})=0
if and only if
x=x^{\prime}
, and
d
satisfies the ultrametric triangle inequality

d(x,x^{\prime\prime})\leq\max\{d(x,x^{\prime}),d(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\}\qquad\text{for $x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in X$.}

Again, we shall denote
d_{X}
by
d
and
\langle X,d_{X},D_{X}\rangle
by
\langle X,D_{X}\rangle
or just
X
whenever convenient.
We shall refer to the two sorts as the
point sort
and the
distance sort
, respectively, when convenient.

A
distance-carrying embedding

f\colon\langle X,d_{X},D_{X}\rangle\to\langle Y,d_{Y},D_{Y}\rangle
(
dc-embedding
for short) is a one-to-one map
f\colon X\to Y
together with an
\mathfrak{L}^{0}
-map
D_{f}\colon D_{X}\to D_{Y}
such that

d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))=D_{f}(d_{X}(x,x^{\prime}))\qquad\text{for }x,x^{\prime}\in X.

Note that an
isometric embedding
corresponds to a dc-embedding
f\colon X\to Y
such that
D_{f}
is the inclusion
D_{X}\leq D_{Y}
, i.e. we have
d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))=d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
.

Let
\mathfrak{U}
denote the category of ultrametric spaces and distance-carrying embeddings.
Clearly the assignment
(f\colon X\to Y)\mapsto(D_{f}\colon D_{X}\to D_{Y})
yields a functor
D\colon\mathfrak{U}\to\mathfrak{L}^{0}
with the restriction
D_{\operatorname{fin}}\colon\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}
(which we may denote just by
D
).
Note that a
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-object is an ultrametric space
X
such that both the set of points
X
and the set of distances
D_{X}
are finite.

Let
\mathfrak{I}^{E}\subseteq\mathfrak{U}
for
E\in\mathfrak{L}^{0}
denote the subcategory of all ultrametric spaces
X
with
D_{X}\leq E
and all isometric embeddings.
We say that an ultrametric space
X
is
precise
if
d_{X}\colon X\times X\to D_{X}
is surjective, i.e. every distance is achieved.
By
\mathfrak{I}^{E}_{\rm prec}\subseteq\mathfrak{I}^{E}

we mean the full subcategory consisting of precise spaces.
In this view, the category of classical ultrametric spaces is just
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}}_{\rm prec}
.

Since every ultrametric space
X
can be viewed both as an
\mathfrak{U}
-object and an
\mathfrak{I}^{D_{X}}
-object, we make a convention that
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
denotes the dc-automorphism group, while
\operatorname{Iso}(X)
denotes the isometry group.

A
precise partial dc-automorphism

p
of
A
is a partial automorphism such that all
\operatorname{dom}(p),\operatorname{rng}(p)\leq A
are precise.
Then for every
r\in\operatorname{dom}(D_{p})
we have
D_{p}(r)=D_{p}(d(x,y))=d(p(x),p(y))
for some
x,y\in A
, i.e. we can ignore the distance part of
D_{p}
.

By the next lemma, we can often consider only precise ultrametric spaces without loss of generality as they form a cofinal subfamily in
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any space
X\in\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
there is a precise space
Y\in\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
with
X\leq Y
and
D_{X}=D_{Y}
.

Proof.

List all positive elements of
E=D_{X}
as
r_{1}<\cdots<r_{m}

2.2.1
Tree representation of ultrametric spaces by precise balls

It is well known that ultrametric spaces correspond to trees of balls. We will make it precise in our setup and we will introduce notation used later.

Let us fix an ultrametric space
X
.
By a
ball
in
X
, we mean any subset
B\subseteq X
that is an
open ball
, i.e. a set of the form
B_{r}(a)=\{x\in X:d(x,a)<r\}
for some
a\in X
and
r\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
, or a
closed ball
, i.e. a set of the form
\bar{B}_{r}(a)=\{x\in X:d(x,a)\leq r\}
for some
a\in X
and
r\in D_{X}
.
Note that neither the center nor the radius is unique for a general ball (in fact, every point of a ball works as its center in an ultrametric space).
A
precise ball
is a closed ball of the form
\bar{B}(a,b)=\bar{B}_{d(a,b)}(a)=\{x\in X:d(a,x)\leq d(a,b)\}
, or equivalently a ball
B
that attains its
diameter

\operatorname{diam}(B)=\max\{d(x,y):x,y\in B\}
.
In particular, every singleton
\{a\}
is a precise ball of diameter
0
.

Note that every two balls
B
,
B^{\prime}
are either disjoint, or one is contained in the other.
In the former case, we put
d(B,B^{\prime})=d(x,x^{\prime})
for any
x\in B
and
x^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
, which does not depend on the choice of the points.
In the latter case, we put
d(B,B^{\prime})=0
, so in both cases we have
d(B,B^{\prime})=\min\{d(x,x^{\prime}):x\in B,x^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}\}
.

By a
tree
we mean a partially ordered set
T
such that for every
t\in T
the
down-set

(\leftarrow,t]=\{x:x\leq t\}
is linearly ordered.
This differs from the classical definition in set theory, where one requires down intervals to be well-ordered.

T
is a
meet tree
if additionally for every
t,t^{\prime}\in T
the meet
t\wedge t^{\prime}=\inf\{t,t^{\prime}\}
exists.
Let
\mathfrak{T}
denote the category of meet trees and meet-preserving embeddings.
For every
t\in T
we have the equivalence relation
\approx_{t}
defined by
s\approx_{t}s^{\prime}
if
s\wedge s^{\prime}>t
that partitions the up-set
(t,\to)=\{x\colon x>t\}
into equivalence classes, which we call
branching classes
.
To see the transitivity of
s\approx_{t}s^{\prime}\approx_{t}s^{\prime\prime}
note that
s\wedge s^{\prime\prime}\geq\min\{s\wedge s^{\prime},s^{\prime}\wedge s^{\prime\prime}\}>t
since
(\leftarrow,s^{\prime}]
is linearly ordered.
If
T
is finite, then branching classes have immediate successors of
t
as smallest elements.

A
leveled meet tree
is a triple
\langle T,\ell_{T},\operatorname{Lev}(T)\rangle
such that
T
is a meet tree,
\operatorname{Lev}(T)
is a linear order, and
\ell_{T}\colon T\to\operatorname{Lev}(T)
, often denoted just by
\ell
, reverses the strict order, i.e.
t<t^{\prime}
implies
\ell_{T}(t)>\ell_{T}(t^{\prime})
, i.e. every restriction
\ell_{T}\colon(\leftarrow,t]\to\operatorname{Lev}(T)
is a reverse-embedding of linear orders.
Similarly to dc-embeddings of ultrametric spaces, a
level-carrying
\mathfrak{T}
-embedding
is a
\mathfrak{T}
-map
f\colon S\to T
together with an
\mathfrak{L}
-map
\operatorname{Lev}(f)\colon\operatorname{Lev}(S)\to\operatorname{Lev}(T)
such that
\ell_{T}(f(s))=\operatorname{Lev}(f)(\ell_{S}(s))
for every
s\in S
.
Let
\mathfrak{TL}
be the category of leveled meet trees and level-carrying
\mathfrak{T}
-embeddings.
Clearly, we have a forgetful functor
\operatorname{Lev}\colon\mathfrak{TL}\to\mathfrak{L}
.
Let
\mathfrak{TL}^{0}
be the subcategory of
\mathfrak{TL}
of leveled meet trees
T
such that
\operatorname{Lev}(T)
has the smallest element
0
and such that for every
t\in T
there are leaves
x,y\in\ell_{T}^{-1}(0)
such that
t=x\wedge y
.
As morphisms of
\mathfrak{TL}^{0}
we take
\mathfrak{TL}
-morphisms
f
such that
\operatorname{Lev}(f)
preserves
0
.
Then
\operatorname{Lev}
yields a functor
\mathfrak{TL}^{0}\to\mathfrak{L}^{0}
.

A connection between
\mathbb{R}
-valued ultrametric spaces and certain types of trees was exploited by Gao and Shao
[
9
]
in connection with studying the isometry groups, and later by Camerlo, Marcone, and Motto Ros
[
3
]
while describing the connection functorially.
Now we give an explicit equivalence of our categories of spaces and trees.

Observation 2.3
.

\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{TL}^{0}
are equivalent categories.
For every ultrametric space
X
we consider the meet tree
T=\langle\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{X},\supseteq\rangle
of all precise balls, and we turn it into a leveled tree by putting
\operatorname{Lev}(T)=D_{X}
and
\ell_{T}=\operatorname{diam}_{X}
, where
\operatorname{diam}_{X}\colon\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{X}\to D_{X}
is the diameter operation.
For every dc-embedding
f\colon X\to Y
let
\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{f}\colon\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{X}\to\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{Y}
be the map
\bar{B}(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto\bar{B}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))
together with the level-carrying part
\operatorname{Lev}(\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{f})=D_{f}
.
Then
\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}\colon\mathfrak{U}\to\mathfrak{TL}^{0}
is a functor.

Now for every leveled meet tree
T
in
\mathfrak{TL}^{0}
let
\operatorname{Max}(T)=\langle\ell^{-1}(0),d,\operatorname{Lev}(T)\rangle
be the ultrametric space of maximal elements of
T
where
d(x,y)=\ell(x\wedge y)
, and for every
\mathfrak{TL}^{0}
-map
f\colon S\to T
let
\operatorname{Max}(f)\colon\operatorname{Max}(S)\to\operatorname{Max}(T)
be the restriction
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{\ell^{-1}(0)}
together with the distance-carrying part
D_{\operatorname{Max}(f)}=\operatorname{Lev}(f)
.
Then
\operatorname{Max}\colon\mathfrak{TL}^{0}\to\mathfrak{U}
is a functor.

Together,
\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}
and
\operatorname{Max}
realize an equivalence between the categories
\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{TL}^{0}
that restricts to an equivalence between
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and
\mathfrak{TL}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
Moreover, we have
{\operatorname{Lev}}\circ\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}=D
and
D\circ\operatorname{Max}=\operatorname{Lev}
, i.e. the functors do nothing on the distance/level set.

Proof.

There are many thing to verify, most of which are very easy and left for the reader. We stress the following.

•

T=\langle\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{X},\supseteq\rangle
indeed has meets. If two balls
B,B^{\prime}\in\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{X}
are comparable, the meet is the larger one, and if they are disjoint,
B\wedge B^{\prime}=\bar{B}(x,x^{\prime})
for any
x\in B
and
x^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
.

•

We have
\bar{B}(x,x^{\prime})\subseteq\bar{B}(y,y^{\prime})
if and only if
\bar{B}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\subseteq\bar{B}(f(y),f(y^{\prime}))
.
Let
r=d(x,x^{\prime})
and
s=d(y,y^{\prime})
, so
\bar{B}(x,x^{\prime})=\bar{B}_{r}(x)
and
\bar{B}(y,y^{\prime})=\bar{B}_{s}(y)
.
Then
\bar{B}(x,x^{\prime})\subseteq\bar{B}(y,y^{\prime})
if and only if
d(x,y)\leq s
and
r\leq s
, which is equivalent to
d(f(x),f(y))\leq D_{f}(s)
and
D_{f}(r)\leq D_{f}(s)
as
f
is a dc-embedding.

•

The previous point implies that
\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{f}
is well-defined and that it preserves meets.

•

For every
X\in\mathfrak{U}
we have that
\operatorname{Max}(\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{X})
is a copy of
X
where every point
x
is replaced with the singleton precise ball
\{x\}
, which yields a natural isomorphism.

•

Similarly, for every
T\in\mathfrak{TL}^{0}
,
\mkern 3.0mu\overline{\mkern-3.0mu\mathscr{B}}_{\operatorname{Max}(T)}
is a copy of
T
where every node
t
is replaced with the family
[t,\to)\cap\ell^{-1}(0)
of all leaves above
t
.
This faithfully reconstructs the tree since we assumed that for every
t\in T
there are
x,y\in\ell^{-1}(0)
with
t=x\wedge y
.
∎

2.2.2
Tree representation of ultrametric spaces by adjacent balls

Replacing precise balls with so-called adjacent balls we arrive at the notion of a tree of adjacent balls.
In Proposition
2.19
and Section
3.5
we will use those trees to represent automorphism groups of Cauchy completions of ultrametric spaces, and we will show that there is a generic leveled adjacency tree.

Let
X
be an ultrametric space, let
B,B^{\prime}\subseteq X
be disjoint balls, and let
r=d(B,B^{\prime})
.
Let us put
E_{B,B^{\prime}}=B_{r}(x)
and
E_{B^{\prime},B}=B_{r}(x^{\prime})
for any
x\in B
and
x^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
.
Note that the open balls
E_{B,B^{\prime}}
and
E_{B^{\prime},B}
are the largest balls that are disjoint and contain
B
and
B^{\prime}
, respectively.
We say that
B,B^{\prime}
are
adjacent
, and we write
B\sim B^{\prime}
, if
B=E_{B,B^{\prime}}
and
B^{\prime}=E_{B^{\prime},B}
.
Note that the
\langle E_{B,B^{\prime}},E_{B^{\prime},B}\rangle
is the unique pair of adjacent balls containing
B
and
B^{\prime}
.
We call it the
adjacent envelope
of
\langle B,B^{\prime}\rangle
.
We shall call a single (necessarily open) ball
B

adjacent
if it is adjacent to some other ball
B^{\prime}
.

For every
x\neq y\in X
, similarly to
\bar{B}(x,y)
, we put
B(x,y)=B_{r}(x)
where
r=d(x,y)
.
Let
B,B^{\prime}\subseteq X
be disjoint balls as before, and let
x\in B
and
y\in B^{\prime}
be any points.
We have that
\langle B(x,y),B(y,x)\rangle
is the adjacency envelope of
\langle B,B^{\prime}\rangle
.
In particular, the ball
B(x,y)
is adjacent, and every adjacent ball is of this form.
Let
\mathscr{B}_{X}=\{B(x,y):x\neq y\in X\}
denote the family of all adjacent balls of
X
.

For every adjacent ball
B\subseteq X
we define its
radius

r(B)
as
d(x,y)
for any
x,y
such that
B=B(x,y)
, or equivalently
r(B)=\max\{r\in D_{X}:B=B_{r}(x)\}>0
where
x
is any point of
B
.
Clearly,
B\sim B^{\prime}
implies
r(B)=r(B^{\prime})
.
Note that in a finite space a ball can be simultaneously precise and adjacent, but the diameter and radius are different.

Observation 2.4
.

For any disjoint balls
B
,
B^{\prime}
,
B^{\prime\prime}
, if
B\sim B^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}\sim B^{\prime\prime}
, then
B\sim B^{\prime\prime}
, so the adjacency relation is the anti-reflexive modification of an equivalence relation on the family of all balls of
X
.
Adjacent balls form nondegenerate equivalence classes, while non-adjacent balls form singleton equivalence classes.
Moreover, nondegenerate precise balls (of diameter
r
) are exactly unions of nondegenerate adjacency equivalence classes (of adjacent balls of radius
r
), which correspond to the branching classes.

Remark 2.5
.

In a finite space
X
, every open ball
B\subsetneq X
is adjacent. But in infinite spaces there may exist nontrivial open balls that are not adjacent, e.g. a ball
B_{r}(x)
such that for every
r^{\prime}>r
there is
y
with
r<d(x,y)<r^{\prime}
, but there is no
y
with
d(x,y)=r
.

Motivated by the the concrete adjacency structure
\langle\mathscr{B}_{X},\supseteq,\sim\rangle
of balls, we define the
adjacency relation
on any tree
T
by

t\sim t^{\prime}\Longleftrightarrow(\leftarrow,t)=(\leftarrow,t^{\prime})\text{ and }t\neq t^{\prime}.

By an
adjacency tree
we mean a tree
T
such that:

•

For every
t\in T
there is
t^{\prime}\neq t
with
t^{\prime}\sim t
.

•

For any incomparable
t,t^{\prime}\in T
there are
e\leq t
and
e^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime}
in
T
such that
e\sim e^{\prime}
.
We call the pair
\langle e,e^{\prime}\rangle
the
adjacency envelope
of
\langle t,t^{\prime}\rangle
.

Note that for every incomparable pair
\langle t,t^{\prime}\rangle
, its adjacency envelope is unique.
Indeed, if
\langle e,e^{\prime}\rangle
and
\langle f,f^{\prime}\rangle
are two adjacency envelopes, then we have
e,f\leq t
, and so they are comparable.
If
e<f\sim f^{\prime}
, then
e<f^{\prime}
, which is a contradiction with
e^{\prime},f^{\prime}\leq t
being comparable.
Similarly, any of
e>f
,
e^{\prime}<f^{\prime}
, and
e^{\prime}>f^{\prime}
lead to a contradiction, so
\langle e,e^{\prime}\rangle=\langle f,f^{\prime}\rangle
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
denote the category of all adjacency trees and all adjacency preserving and adjacency reflecting tree embeddings, i.e. maps
f\colon S\to T
such that for every
s,s^{\prime}\in S
we have
s\leq s^{\prime}
if and only if
f(s)\leq f(s^{\prime})
, and
s\sim s^{\prime}
if and only if
f(s)\sim f(s^{\prime})
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For a map
f\colon S\to T
between adjacency trees to be an
\mathfrak{A}
-map it is enough that it preserves order and adjacency.

Proof.

The map
f
reflects adjacency:
if
f(s)\sim f(s^{\prime})
, then
s,s^{\prime}
are incomparable, and so the adjacency envelope
s\geq e\sim e^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime}
exists, and we have
f(s)\geq f(e)\sim f(e^{\prime})\leq f(s^{\prime})
.
Hence,
f(s)=f(e)
and
f(s^{\prime})=f(e^{\prime})
, and we have
s=e\sim e^{\prime}=s^{\prime}
.

The map
f
reflects order:
suppose
f(s)\leq f(s^{\prime})
.
We consider several cases.
If
s\leq s^{\prime}
, we are done.
If
s,s^{\prime}
are incomparable, we consider the adjacency envelope
s\geq e\sim e^{\prime}\leq s^{\prime}
, and we have
f(s)\geq f(e)\sim f(e^{\prime})\leq f(s^{\prime})
, and so
f(s),f(s^{\prime})
are incomparable, which is a contradiction.
If
s>s^{\prime}
, then
f(s)=f(s^{\prime})
, but there is
t\sim s
, and so
t>s^{\prime}
, and we have
f(s^{\prime})\leq f(t)\sim f(s)=f(s^{\prime})
, which is a contradiction.
∎

As with leveled meet trees, we consider
leveled adjacency trees

\langle T,\ell_{T},\operatorname{Lev}(T)\rangle
: we assume that
T
is an adjacency tree,
\operatorname{Lev}(T)\in\mathfrak{L}^{0}
is a linear order, and
\ell_{T}\colon T\to\operatorname{Lev}(T)
, often denoted just by
\ell
, is a map such that for every
t,t^{\prime}\in T
we have:

t<t^{\prime}\implies\ell(t)>\ell(t^{\prime}),\quad\qquad t\sim t^{\prime}\implies\ell(t)=\ell(t^{\prime}),\quad\qquad\ell^{-1}(0)=\emptyset.

We define the category
\mathfrak{B}
such that objects are leveled adjacency trees, and a
\mathfrak{B}
-map
f\colon\langle S,\ell_{S},\operatorname{Lev}(S)\rangle\to\langle T,\ell_{T},\operatorname{Lev}(T)\rangle
is an
\mathfrak{A}
-map
f\colon S\to T
together with an
\mathfrak{L}^{0}
-map
\operatorname{Lev}(f)\colon\operatorname{Lev}(S)\to\operatorname{Lev}(T)
such that
f
is
level-carrying
, i.e.
\ell_{T}(f(s))=\operatorname{Lev}(f)(\ell_{S}(s))
for every
s\in S
.
The projection to the level sort induces a functor
\operatorname{Lev}\colon\mathfrak{B}\to\mathfrak{L}^{0}
.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{U}
.
By the earlier observations
T=\langle\mathscr{B}_{X},\supseteq,\sim\rangle
is an adjacency tree.
If we put
\operatorname{Lev}(T)=D_{X}
and
\ell_{T}(B)=r(B)
, we obtain a leveled adjacency tree
\langle T,\ell_{T},\operatorname{Lev}(T)\rangle\in\mathfrak{B}
.
In this way we can view every
\mathscr{B}_{X}
as a
\mathfrak{B}
-object.

For every dc-embedding
f\colon X\to Y
we consider a
\mathfrak{B}
-map
\mathscr{B}_{f}\colon\mathscr{B}_{X}\to\mathscr{B}_{Y}
defined by

\mathscr{B}_{f}(B(x,y))=B(f(x),f(y))
,
or equivalently by
\mathscr{B}_{f}(B_{r}(x))=B_{D_{f}(r)}(f(x))
, where
r=d(x,y)
.

Proposition 2.7
.

\mathscr{B}_{f}\colon\mathscr{B}_{X}\to\mathscr{B}_{Y}
is a
\mathfrak{B}
-map with
\operatorname{Lev}(\mathscr{B}_{f})=D_{f}
, and so we have a functor
\mathscr{B}\colon\mathfrak{U}\to\mathfrak{B}
with
{\operatorname{Lev}}\circ\mathscr{B}=D
.

Proof.

•

\mathscr{B}_{f}
is well-defined since it maps an adjacent ball
B
of radius
r
to
B_{q}(f(x))
where
q=D_{f}(r)
and
x\in B
. This does not depend on the choice of
x
:
for
x,x^{\prime}\in B
we have
d(x,x^{\prime})<r
, and so
d(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))<q
, and so
B_{q}(f(x))=B_{q}(f(x^{\prime}))
.

•

\mathscr{B}_{f}
preserves order: if
x\in B\subseteq B^{\prime}
, then
r(B)\leq r(B^{\prime})
, and so

\mathscr{B}_{f}(B)=B_{D_{f}(r(B))}(f(x))\subseteq B_{D_{f}(r(B^{\prime}))}(f(x))=\mathscr{B}_{f}(B^{\prime}).

•

\mathscr{B}_{f}
preserves adjacency since every pair
B\sim B^{\prime}
is of the form
B(x,y)\sim B(y,x)
and so
\mathscr{B}_{f}(B)=B(f(x),f(y))\sim B(f(y),f(x))=\mathscr{B}_{f}(B^{\prime})
.

•

\mathscr{B}_{f}
is an
\mathfrak{A}
-map by Lemma
2.6
.

•

\mathscr{B}_{f}
is level-carrying with
\operatorname{Lev}(\mathscr{B}_{f})=D_{f}
since for every
x,y\in X
we have

\displaystyle\ell(\mathscr{B}_{f}(B(x,y)))=r(B(f(x),f(y)))

\displaystyle=d(f(x),f(y))

\displaystyle=D_{f}(d(x,y))=D_{f}(r(B(x,y))).\qed

Lemma 2.8
.

\mathscr{B}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is essentially surjective on objects, i.e. for every
T\in\mathfrak{B}_{\operatorname{fin}}
there is
X\in\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
such that
\mathscr{B}_{X}\cong T
.

Proof.

Note that
T
is a leveled adjacency tree
T=\langle T,\ell_{T},\operatorname{Lev}(T)\rangle
.
Let
X
be the set of all maximal elements of
T
(i.e. the leaves), and let
D_{X}=\operatorname{Lev}(T)
.
For
t\neq t^{\prime}\in X
we put
d_{X}(t,t^{\prime})=\ell_{T}(e)
where
\langle e,e^{\prime}\rangle
is the adjacency envelope of
\langle t,t^{\prime}\rangle
in
T
.

Clearly
d=d_{X}
is symmetric, and
d(x,x^{\prime})=0
if and only if
x=x^{\prime}
.
We put
\mathscr{B}_{X}=\{B_{X}(x,y):x\neq y\}
, where
B_{X}(x,y)=\{z:d(x,z)<d(x,y)\}
, even though we have not yet proved the ultrametric triangle inequality for
d_{X}
. That will follow once we show that
\mathscr{B}_{X}
is a tree.
First we show that
\Phi
mapping every
t\in T
to
B_{t}=\{x\in X:x\geq t\}
is an isomorphism of posets
T\to\mathscr{B}_{X}
.

Claim.

We have
B_{X}(x,x^{\prime})=B_{t}
wherever
x\geq t\sim t^{\prime}\leq x^{\prime}
for some
x,x^{\prime}\in X
and

.

Proof of Claim.

Whenever
y\in X\setminus\{x\}
, we let
\langle e_{x,y},e_{y,x}\rangle
denote the adjacency envelope.
If
y\in B_{t}
, we have
x,y\geq t
, and so either
y=x
, or
e_{x,y},e_{y,x}>t
and
d(x,y)=\ell(e_{x,y})<\ell(t)=d(x,x^{\prime})
.
In both cases
y\in B_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
.

If
y\in B_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
, then either
y=x
, or
\ell(e_{y,x})=d(y,x)<d(y,x^{\prime})=\ell(e_{y,x^{\prime}})
.
In the second case we have
e_{x,y}\sim e_{y,x}>e_{y,x^{\prime}}
and so
x>e_{y,x^{\prime}}\sim e_{x^{\prime},y}\leq x^{\prime}
.
Hence,
\langle e_{y,x^{\prime}},e_{x^{\prime},y}\rangle=\langle t,t^{\prime}\rangle
, and
y>t
.
In both cases,
y\geq t
, i.e.
y\in B_{t}
.

\qed_{\text{Claim}}

Given
t\in T
, let
t^{\prime}\sim t
and let
x\geq t
and
x^{\prime}\geq t^{\prime}
be any maximal elements of
T
, i.e.

x,x^{\prime}\in X
.
By the Claim,
B_{t}=B_{X}(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathscr{B}_{X}
.
On the other hand, given
B\in\mathscr{B}_{X}
, we know that
B
is of the form
B_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
for some
x,x^{\prime}\in X
.
Let
\langle t,t^{\prime}\rangle
be the adjacency envelope of
\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle
in
T
.
Then by the Claim, we have
B=B_{t}
, and
\Phi
is surjective.

Now let
t,t^{\prime}\in T
.
Clearly, if
t\leq t^{\prime}
, then
B_{t}\supseteq B_{t^{\prime}}
.
Also, if
t,t^{\prime}
are incomparable, then
B_{t}\cap B_{t^{\prime}}=\emptyset
, and so they are
\subseteq
-incomparable.
Moreover, if
t<t^{\prime}
, then
B_{t}\supsetneq B_{t^{\prime}}
since there is
t^{\prime\prime}\sim t^{\prime}
and
B_{t}\setminus B_{t^{\prime}}\supseteq B_{t^{\prime\prime}}
.
This together with surjectivity shows that
\Phi\colon T\to\mathscr{B}_{X}
is an isomorphism of posets.
Hence,
\mathscr{B}_{X}
is a tree, and both
\Phi
and
\Phi^{-1}
preserve the adjacency relation.

Since
\mathscr{B}_{X}
is a tree,
d
is an ultrametric:
If we had
d(x,y)>d(x,z),d(y,z)
, then
B_{X}(x,y)
and
B_{X}(y,x)
would be incomparable balls containing
z
, giving a contradiction.
Finally, for
t\in T
and
x,x^{\prime}\in X
as in the Claim, we have
r(B_{t})=r(B_{X}(x,x^{\prime}))=d(x,x^{\prime})=\ell_{T}(t)
, and so
\Phi
is level-preserving.
Altogether,
\Phi\colon T\to\mathscr{B}_{X}
is a
\mathfrak{B}
-isomorphism.
∎

Observation 2.9
.

Note that for every
X\in\mathfrak{U}
and
x\neq y\in X
we have that
\langle B(x,y),B(y,x)\rangle
is the unique pair
\langle B,B^{\prime}\rangle
of adjacent balls in
\mathscr{B}_{X}
such that
x\in B
and
y\in B^{\prime}
.

Let us for now denote by
\mathfrak{U}^{+}_{\operatorname{fin}}
denote the full subcategory of
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
consisting of spaces with nonempty point sort.

Lemma 2.10
.

For every
X\in\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and
Y\in\mathfrak{U}
(with the single exception of
X=\langle\{*\},D_{X}\rangle
and
Y=\langle\emptyset,D_{Y}\rangle
) and every
\mathfrak{B}
-map

there is a
\mathfrak{U}
-map

such that
\mathscr{B}_{f}=g
.
In particular,
\mathscr{B}_{\operatorname{fin}}\colon\mathfrak{U}^{+}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{B}_{\operatorname{fin}}

Proof.

If
X
has at most one point, then
\mathscr{B}_{X}=\emptyset
, and so
g
is the empty map, and any map
f\colon X\to Y
is a dc-embedding with
\mathscr{B}_{f}=g
.
In the following we suppose that
X
has at least two points.

For every
x\in X
let
r_{x}=\min\{d(x,y):y\in X\setminus\{x\}\}
, so that
B_{r_{x}}(x)
is the smallest adjacent ball in
X
containing
x
.
We let
f(x)
be any point of
g(B_{r_{x}}(x))
, and of course we put
D_{f}=\operatorname{Lev}(g)
.
We need to check that
f
is a dc-embedding and that
\mathscr{B}_{f}=g
.

Let
x\neq y\in X

g(B(x,y))=B(f(x),f(y))
.
In
\mathscr{B}_{X}
we have
B_{r_{x}}(x)\subseteq B(x,y)\sim B(y,x)\supseteq B_{r_{y}}(y)

f(x)\in g(B_{r_{x}}(x))\subseteq g(B(x,y))\sim g(B(y,x))\supseteq g(B_{r_{y}}(y))\ni f(y).

It follows from Observation
2.9
that

\langle g(B(x,y)),g(B(y,x))\rangle=\langle B(f(x),f(y)),B(f(y),f(x))\rangle.

In particular,
g(B(x,y))=B(f(x),f(y))
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle D_{f}(d(x,y))=\operatorname{Lev}(g)(r(B(x,y)))

\displaystyle=r(g(B(x,y)))

\displaystyle=r(B(f(x),f(y)))=d(f(x),f(y)),

and that

is a dc-embedding with

.
Finally, the fact that

for every

.
∎

2.3
Topologies on ultrametric spaces and their automorphism groups

As with classical ultrametric spaces, we have the metric topology and uniformity: a subset
U\subseteq X
is open if for every
a\in U
there is some
r\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
such that
B_{r}(a)=\{x\in X:d(x,a)<r\}\subseteq U
, and each
r\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
induces the partition
\{B_{r}(a):a\in X\}
. The family of these partitions forms a basis of a uniformity on
X
.
Hence, topological and uniform notions like a subset being dense or a space being uniformly complete are well-defined.

We note that a dc-embedding
f\colon X\to Y
(or even an isometric embedding with
D_{X}\subsetneq D_{Y}
) may not be continuous.
The key ingredient is that
D_{f}
is
continuous at
0
, i.e. there is no
\varepsilon\in D_{Y}
such that
0<\varepsilon<D_{f}[D_{X}\setminus\{0\}]
.
An ultrametric space
X
is
discrete
if for every
x\in X
there is
\varepsilon\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
such that
B_{\varepsilon}(x)=\{x\}
, and it is
uniformly discrete
if moreover
\varepsilon
can be chosen independently of
x
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a dc-embedding of ultrametric spaces.

(1)

f
is continuous if and only if
D_{f}
is continuous at
0
or
X
is discrete.

(2)

f
is uniformly continuous if and only if
D_{f}
is continuous at
0
or
X
is uniformly discrete.

In particular, a dc-isomorphism is always a (uniform) homeomorphism.

Proof.

Continuity means that for every
\varepsilon\in D_{Y}\setminus\{0\}
and
x\in X
there is
\delta\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
such that
f[B_{\delta}(x)]\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}(f(x))
, and uniform continuity means the same with
\delta
not depending on
x
.
If
D_{f}
is continuous at
0
, then we have
\delta\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
with
D_{f}(\delta)\leq\varepsilon
, and so
f
is uniformly continuous.

On the other hand, if
\varepsilon
witnesses that
D_{f}
is not continuous at
0
and
\delta
witnesses that
f
is (uniformly) continuous, then
B_{\delta}(x)=\{x\}
, and so
X
is (uniformly) discrete.
∎

We say that an ultrametric space
X
is
Cauchy complete
if it is complete with respect to the induced uniformity, which means that every
Cauchy chain
of balls, i.e. a chain of balls
\mathscr{C}
such that for every
r\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
there is
B\in\mathscr{C}
with
B\subseteq B_{r}(x)
for some
x
, has a non-empty intersection, which is necessarily a singleton.
It does not matter if we consider open, closed, precise, or adjacent balls: indeed a Cauchy chain
\mathscr{C}
either contains a singleton
\{x\}
, which is then its intersection,
or else for any
B\in\mathscr{C}
there is
B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}
such that
B\supsetneq B^{\prime}
.
Then for
x\in B^{\prime}
and
y\in B\setminus B^{\prime}
we have
B\supseteq\bar{B}(x,y)\supseteq B(x,y)\supseteq B^{\prime}
.

The
Cauchy-completion

\overline{X}
of an ultrametric space
X
, which is the unique Cauchy complete extension having
X
as a dense subspace and having the same distance set, can be constructed as follows.
The space
\langle\overline{X},d_{\overline{X}},D_{X}\rangle
consists of all maximal Cauchy chains
\mathscr{C}
of open balls.
The distance
d_{\overline{X}}:\overline{X}\times\overline{X}\to D_{X}
is defined as
d_{\overline{X}}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{C}^{\prime})=d(B,B^{\prime})
, where
B\in\mathscr{C}
,
B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}^{\prime}
are any disjoint balls. Note that by the ultrametric triangle inequality, this does not depend on the choice of
B
,
B^{\prime}
.
The mapping
x\mapsto\{B_{r}(x):r\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}\}
is an isometric embedding of
X
onto a dense subspace of its Cauchy-completion
\overline{X}
.

Observation 2.14
.

For every uniformly continuous dc-embedding
f\colon X\to Y
, the unique uniformly continuous map
\bar{f}\colon\overline{X}\to\overline{Y}
is a dc-embedding with
D_{\bar{f}}=D_{f}
since for every
\bar{x},\bar{y}\in\overline{X}
with
r=d(\bar{x},\bar{y})>0
there are
x\in B_{r}(\bar{x})\cap X
and
y\in B_{r}(\bar{y})\cap X
, and so we have

d(\bar{f}(\bar{x}),\bar{f}(\bar{y}))=d(f(x),f(y))=D_{f}(d(x,y))=D_{f}(d(\bar{x},\bar{y}))
.

It follows from the uniqueness that
\overline{g\circ f}=\bar{g}\circ\bar{f}
.
In particular, we have the canonical group embedding
\alpha\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(\overline{X})
mapping
f\mapsto\bar{f}
, and
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
can be identified with
\{f\in\operatorname{Aut}(\overline{X}):f[X]=X\}
.

On
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
we consider two topologies:

•

The topology of pointwise convergence, viewing
X
as a discrete two-sorted structure
X\cup D_{X}
, i.e. the neighborhood basis at every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
consists of the sets

N_{A}(f)=\{g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X):g(x)=f(x)\text{ for $x\in A$, and $D_{g}(r)=D_{f}(r)$ for $r\in D_{A}$}\},

where
A\subseteq X
is a finite subspace.

•

The topology of pointwise convergence, viewing
X
as a topological space with the topology induced by the ultrametric, i.e. the neighborhood basis at every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
consists of the sets

N_{A,r}(f)=\{g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X):d(g(x),f(x))<r\text{ for every $x\in A$}\},

where
A\subseteq X
is a finite subspace and
r\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
.

The first topology is a standard choice for any countable structure
M
, and turns
\operatorname{Aut}(M)
into a Polish group. We will use this topology without mention in cases like
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
and
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{X})
.
The second topology makes a good sense only for precise spaces
X
as there is no way how the distance part can be controlled on unused distances.

We make a convention that
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
denotes the group with the first topology, while
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
denotes the group with the second topology.
Similarly we denote their respective topological subgroups
\operatorname{Iso}(X)
and
\operatorname{Iso_{m}}(X)
.

Clearly, the topology of
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
is coarser than the topology of
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
.
It is a standard fact that
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is a Polish group if
X
is countable (and for general
X
,
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is a completely uniformizable topological group).
We will observe that
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
is a topological group.

Observation 2.15
.

Let
f\in\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
.
If
D_{f}(r)=q
, then also
D_{g}(r)=q
for every
g\in N_{\{x,y\},q}(f)
where
x,y\in X
are such that
d(x,y)=r
.
This follows from the ultrametric triangle inequality applied to
g(x),f(x),f(y),g(y)
.

Lemma 2.16
.

\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
is a topological group for any precise ultrametric space
X
.

Proof.

To prove continuity of the composition, let
f,f^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
,
A\subseteq X
be finite, and
q\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
.
For
r=D_{f^{\prime}}^{-1}(q)
there are points
x,y
with
d(x,y)=r
.
We put
B=f[A]\cup\{x,y\}
.
Then
N_{B,q}(f^{\prime})\circ N_{A,r}(f)\subseteq N_{A,q}(f^{\prime}\circ f)
:
for every
a\in A
,
g\in N_{A,r}(f)
, and
g^{\prime}\in N_{B,q}(f^{\prime})
we have

•

d(g(a),f(a))<r
since
a\in A
and
g\in N_{A,r}(f)
,

•

d(g^{\prime}(g(a)),g^{\prime}(f(a)))<q
since
D_{g^{\prime}}(r)=q
, by Observation
2.15
, and the fact that
x,y\in B
,

•

d(g^{\prime}(f(a)),f^{\prime}(f(a)))<q
since
f(a)\in B
and
g^{\prime}\in N_{B,q}(f^{\prime})
,

and altogether
d(g^{\prime}(g(a)),f^{\prime}(f(a)))<q
.

For continuity of the inverse we similarly have

N_{A,q}(f)^{-1}\subseteq N_{B,r}(f^{-1})
where
f,B,r
are given, and
q=D_{f}(r)
and
A=f^{-1}[B]\cup\{x,y\}
where
d(x,y)=r
.
This is because
d(g^{-1}(b),f^{-1}(b))<r
if and only if
d(f(a),g(a))<q
where
f(a)=b
(and
a=f^{-1}(b)
) and
g\in N_{A,q}(f)
and so
D_{g}(r)=q
(and
r=D_{g^{-1}}(q)
).
∎

For every ultrametric space
X
, by the
canonical projection

\pi\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(D_{X})
we mean the homomorphism defined by
\pi(f)=D_{f}
.

Observation 2.17
.

The canonical projection
\pi\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(D_{X})
is continuous for every space
X
as
D_{X}
is already part of the structure of
X
.
The canonical projection
\pi\colon\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(D_{X})
is continuous for every precise space
X
by Observation
2.15
.

It follows that
\operatorname{Iso}(X)
and
\operatorname{Iso_{m}}(X)
are closed normal subgroups of
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
and
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
, respectively.

Recall that for every ultrametric space
X
we denote by
\mathscr{B}_{X}
the family of all adjacent balls of
X
and that this gives a functor
\mathscr{B}\colon\mathfrak{U}\to\mathfrak{B}
with
\ell\circ\mathscr{B}=D
.

For every ultrametric space
X
we consider the homomorphism
\beta\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{X})
defined by
\beta(f)=\mathscr{B}_{f}
, with
\ell_{\beta_{f}}=D_{f}
.
Clearly,
\beta
is one-to-one: if
f(x)\neq x
, then
\mathscr{B}_{f}(B)\neq B
for
B=B(x,f(x))
.

Lemma 2.18
.

If
f\colon X\to Y
is a dc-embedding into a precise space such that
f[X]\subseteq Y
is dense, then
D_{f}\colon D_{X}\to D_{Y}
is an
\mathfrak{L}^{0}
-isomorphism, and
\mathscr{B}_{f}\colon\mathscr{B}_{X}\to\mathscr{B}_{Y}
is a
\mathfrak{B}
-isomorphism.

Proof.

For
r\in D_{Y}
there are points
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
with
d(y,y^{\prime})=r
, and, by density, there are points
x,x^{\prime}\in X
with
d(f(x),y),d(f(x^{\prime}),y^{\prime})<r
.
Hence,
d(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))=r
, and so
D_{f}
is surjective.

Similarly, for every
r\in D_{Y}\setminus\{0\}
and
B(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathscr{B}_{Y}
with
d(y,y^{\prime})=r
there are
x,x^{\prime}\in X
with
d(f(x),y),d(f(x^{\prime}),y^{\prime})<r
.
Hence,
B(y,y^{\prime})=B(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))=\mathscr{B}_{f}(B(x,x^{\prime}))
, and so
\mathscr{B}_{f}
is surjective.
∎

We say that a point
x\in X
in an ultrametric space is
precisely isolated
if there is
y\in X
such that
B(x,y)=\{x\}
.

Proposition 2.19
.

Let
X
be a precise ultrametric space such that every isolated point is precisely isolated.

(1)

The map
\beta\colon\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{X})
is an embedding of topological groups.

(2)

If
X
is separable, then
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{X})
is Polish, and so
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
is separable metrizable.

(3)

If
X
is separable and complete, then
\beta\colon\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{X})
is an isomorphism of Polish groups.

(4)

If
X
is separable, then
\bar{\beta}\colon\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(\overline{X})\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{X})
is an isomorphism of Polish groups.
Here
\bar{\beta}
denotes
\mathscr{B}_{i}^{-1}\circ\beta_{\overline{X}}\colon\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(\overline{X})\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{\overline{X}})\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{X})
where
i\colon X\to\overline{X}
is the inclusion.

Proof.

To prove (1) we have to show that
\beta
and its inverse are continuous. Let
f\in\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
and
B\in\mathscr{B}_{X}
.
For
q=D_{f}(r(B))
and any
x\in B
we have
\{g\in\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X):g[B]=f[B]\}=N_{\{x\},q}(f)
, and so
\beta
is continuous.
On the other hand, if
x\in X
and
q\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
, then there is
0<q^{\prime}\leq q
such that
B_{q^{\prime}}(f(x))\in\mathscr{B}_{X}
since every isolated point is precisely isolated.
For
r=D_{f}^{-1}(q^{\prime})
we have
B_{r}(x)=f^{-1}[B_{q^{\prime}}(f(x))]\in\mathscr{B}_{X}
, and so if
g[B_{r}(x)]=f[B_{r}(x)]
, then
g\in N_{\{x\},q}(f)
.
Hence,
\beta
is an embedding.

Claim (2): as
X
is separable, there is countable dense subset
A\subseteq X
, and so
\mathscr{B}_{X}=\{B(x,y):x\neq y\in A\}
is countable.
As
X
is precise,
\operatorname{Lev}(\mathscr{B}_{X})=D_{X}
is countable as well. Therefore
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{X})
as the automorphism group of a countable structure is Polish.

To prove (3) it is enough to show that
\beta
is surjective.
For every
x\in X
let
\mathscr{C}_{x}
be the maximal chain
\{B(x,y):y\in X\setminus\{x\}\}\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{X}
.
Since every isolated point of
X
is precisely isolated, either
\mathscr{C}_{x}
has a minimal element, or
\inf\{r(B):B\in\mathscr{C}_{x}\}=0
.
Since
X
is complete,
x\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{x}
is a bijective correspondence between points and such chains.
Hence, for
g\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{X})
and
x\in X
we have
\{g(B):B\in\mathscr{C}_{x}\}=\mathscr{C}_{f(x)}
for a unique point
f(x)
.
This defines a bijection
f\colon X\to X
such that
g(B)=f[B]
for every
B\in\mathscr{B}_{X}
since we have

x\in B\Longleftrightarrow B\in\mathscr{C}_{x}\Longleftrightarrow g(B)\in\mathscr{C}_{f(x)}\Longleftrightarrow f(x)\in g(B).

It remains to show that
f
is a dc-automorphism with
D_{f}=\ell_{g}
.
For every
x\neq y\in X
we have
d(x,y)=r(B(x,y))
, and
B(x,y)=\max_{\subseteq}(\mathscr{C}_{x}\setminus\mathscr{C}_{y})
, and

\max(\mathscr{C}_{f(x)}\setminus\mathscr{C}_{f(y)})=\max\{g(B):B\in\mathscr{C}_{x}\setminus\mathscr{C}_{y}\}=g(\max(\mathscr{C}_{x}\setminus\mathscr{C}_{y})).

Hence,
B(f(x),f(y))=g(B(x,y))
and
d(f(x),f(y))=\ell_{g}(d(x,y))
.

Claim (4):
by the first part of Lemma
2.18
we have that
D_{\overline{X}}=D_{X}
, so
\overline{X}
is precise.
By density, every isolated point of
\overline{X}
is in
X
and is precisely isolated.
Hence, the claim follows from (3) applied to
\overline{X}
and from the second part of Lemma
2.18
.
∎

3
Generic ultrametric spaces

We shall recall notions from Fraïssé theory and adapt them to our context.
Classical Fraïssé theory of countable one-sorted structures is well-known, see Fraïssé
[
8
]
and Hodges
[
12
, Section 7.1]
.
We intend to use the theory for categories of structures possibly with partial automorphisms.
We stress the category-theoretic aspects so that we can conveniently relate different classes by forgetful functors and use abstract principles to transfer Fraïssé-theoretic properties between them.
Category-theoretic Fraïssé theory was originally developed by Droste and Göbel
[
7
]
and Kubiś
[
19
]
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a category of structures. In particular,
\mathscr{K}
-objects are (many-sorted) structures, and
\mathscr{K}
-maps are all embeddings.
Fraïssé theory relates properties of the class
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
of finite structures and of countable structures that are colimits of countable direct sequences from
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
, i.e. up to isomorphism they are unions of countable increasing chains.
We denote this class by
\sigma\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
We assume that
\mathscr{K}
is
\sigma
-complete
in the sense that it is closed under countable unions and their isomorphic copies (i.e. under colimits of countable sequences taken in the ambient category of all structures of a given many-sorted signature).

A sequence
(A_{n},f_{n})
in
\mathscr{K}
consists of
\mathscr{K}
-objects
A_{n}
and
\mathscr{K}
-maps
f_{n}\colon A_{n}\to A_{n+1}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We also introduce notation for compositions of the bonding maps:
f_{m}^{n}\colon A_{m}\to A_{n}
for
m\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The colimit is denoted by
(A_{\infty},f^{\infty}_{n})
, i.e. it consists of a
\mathscr{K}
-object
A_{\infty}
and of
\mathscr{K}
-maps
f_{n}^{\infty}\colon A_{n}\to A_{\infty}
forming a cone.

Below we summarize the relevant properties of
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
such as variants of the amalgamation, properties of objects from
\sigma\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
such as homogeneity and extension property, and we formulate a general Fraïssé theorem in our context.

We define the following properties for
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

(HP)

The
hereditary property
: for every
A\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and
B\leq A
we have
B\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

(JEP)

The
joint embedding property
: for any
A,B\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
there is
C\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
, which embeds both
A
and
B
.

(AP)

The
amalgamation property
: for every
A\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
,
\alpha_{1}\colon A\to B
and
\alpha_{2}\colon A\to C
in
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
there is
D\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
together with
\beta_{1}\colon B\to D
and
\beta_{2}\colon C\to D
such that
\beta_{1}\circ\alpha_{1}=\beta_{2}\circ\alpha_{2}
.
In that case, we say that
A
is an
amalgamation base
in
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

(SAP)

The
strong amalgamation property
is a strengthening of the AP, where we additionally have
\beta_{1}(B)\cap\beta_{2}(C)=\beta_{1}(\alpha_{1}(A))(=\beta_{2}(\alpha_{2}(A)))
. In that case, we say that
A
is a
strong amalgamation base
.

(CAP)

The
cofinal amalgamation property
: any
A_{0}\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
embeds into
A\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
that is an amalgamation base in
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

(WAP)

The
weak amalgamation property
:
for every
A_{0}\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
there is an embedding
i\colon A_{0}\to A
in
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
that is an
amalgamable map
, i.e. for any
\alpha_{1}\colon A\to B
and
\alpha_{2}\colon A\to C
in
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
there is
D\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
together with
\beta_{1}\colon B\to D
and
\beta_{2}\colon C\to D
such that
\beta_{1}\circ\alpha_{1}\circ i=\beta_{2}\circ\alpha_{2}\circ i
holds.

We say
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}\neq\emptyset
is a
Fraïssé class
if it satisfies JEP and AP and has countably many isomorphism types.
Note that we do not require
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
to be hereditary.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a structure
U\in\mathscr{K}

•

is
universal
for
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
if for every
A\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
there is an embedding
f\colon A\to U
;

•

is
homogeneous
for
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
if for every
A,B\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
such that
A,B\leq U
and every isomorphism
f\colon A\to B
there is
h\in\operatorname{Aut}(U)
extending
f
, or equivalently, for every
A\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and all embeddings
f,g\colon A\to U
there is
h\in\operatorname{Aut}(U)
such that
h\circ g=f
;

•

has the
extension property
for
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
if for every
A,B\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and embeddings
f\colon A\to U
and
g\colon A\to B
there is an embedding
h\colon B\to U
such that
h\circ g=f
.

Similarly we say that a sequence
(A_{n},f_{n})
in
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}

•

is
cofinal
if for every
A\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
there is an embedding
f\colon A\to A_{n}
for some
n
,

•

has the
extension property
if for every
A,B\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and embeddings
f\colon A\to A_{m}
and
g\colon A\to B
there is an embedding
h\colon B\to A_{n}
for some
n\geq m
such that
h\circ g=f^{n}_{m}\circ f
,

•

is
absorbing
if for every
B\in\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and an embedding
g\colon A_{m}\to B
there is an embedding
h\colon B\to A_{n}
for some
n\geq m
such that
h\circ g=f^{n}_{m}
, i.e. it has the extension property for
f={\operatorname{id}_{A_{m}}}
,

•

is
Fraïssé
if it has the extension property (which under AP is equivalent to just being absorbing) and is cofinal.

The following two theorems are essentially well known.
For the classical setting of hereditary classes of one-sorted structures see Hodges
[
12
, Theorems 7.1.2 and 7.1.7]
.
For Theorem
3.2
, a construction explicitly using the notion of a Fraïssé sequence can be found in
[
19
, Corollary 3.8]
– we apply it to the special case of a category of finite structures, so the properties of being essentially countable, having a countable dominating subcategory, and having a cofinal subcategory are all equivalent to having countably many isomorphism types.
For a version of the theorem that states both implications in even more general setting see
[
1
, Theorem 4.46]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
has a Fraïssé sequence if and only if it is a Fraïssé class.

For the following theorem we sketch a proof.
A full proof in a more general setting can be found in
[
1
, Theorem 4.15]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a
\sigma
-complete category of structures, and let
U\in\sigma\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

U
is universal and homogeneous for
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

(2)

U
is universal and has the extension property for
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

(3)

U
is the colimit of a Fraïssé sequence in
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

Moreover, such object
U
is unique, universal for
\sigma\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
, and every sequence in
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
with colimit
U
is Fraïssé.

Proof sketch.

The fact that
\mathscr{K}
is
\sigma
-complete and that
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
consists of literally finite structures implies that there is an essentially one-to-one correspondence between sequences in
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and structures in
\sigma\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
– including morphisms between them.
The key property used is that every embedding from a
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-object to
U
factorizes through the sequence – while this factorization property is trivial in the classical case, it is an important assumption in abstract category-theoretic setup.
(For details see the notion of free completion
[
1
, Remarks 4.56 and 4.57]
.)

It follows that a sequence
(U_{n},f_{n})
in
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
has the extension property if and only if its colimit
(U,f^{\infty}_{n})
in
\sigma\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
has the extension property for
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
(and similarly for universality).
It also follows that two sequences having the same colimit are back-and-forth equivalent, and so if one sequence is Fraïssé, the other one is as well.

It is easy to see that a universal homogeneous structure for
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
has the extension property.
Homogeneity of the colimit of a Fraïssé sequence as well as uniquess of a Fraïssé sequence (and its colimit) up to isomorphism follow from a back-and-forth argument.
∎

The unique object
U
from Theorem
3.3
is called the
Fraïssé limit
of
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
(in
\mathscr{K}
), and by Theorem
3.2
it exists if and only if
\mathscr{K}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is a Fraïssé class.

3.1
Transfer principles

We formulate two propositions that will allow us to easily transfer amalgamation principles and preserve the Fraïssé limit by suitable functors between categories.
This captures the situation that generalized reducts (images under forgetful functors) of a homogeneous structure are homogeneous in their respective categories.

First we need to introduce the relevant properties of functors.
We say that a functor
F\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{D}
is

•

wide
/
surjective on objects
if for every
\mathscr{D}
-object
X
there is a
\mathscr{C}
-object
X^{\prime}
such that
F(X^{\prime})=X
;

•

essentially wide
/
essentially surjective on objects
if for every
\mathscr{D}
-object
X
there is a
\mathscr{C}
-object
X^{\prime}
with
F(X^{\prime})
isomorphic to

;

•

cofinal
if for every
\mathscr{D}
-object

there is a
\mathscr{C}
-object
X^{\prime}
and a
\mathscr{D}
-map

.

Remark 3.4
.

We have the following implications for a functor
F
:

wide
\implies
essentially wide
\implies
cofinal.

We say that a functor
F\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{D}

•

\mathscr{C}

X^{\prime}
and every
\mathscr{D}

-map

\mathscr{C}
-object
X^{\prime}
and every

\mathscr{C}
-object

-map

and an
\mathscr{D}
-isomorphism

;

•

absorbing
if for every
\mathscr{C}
-object
X^{\prime}
and every
\mathscr{D}
-map
f\colon F(X^{\prime})\to Y
there is a
\mathscr{C}
-object
Y^{\prime}
, a
\mathscr{C}
-map
f^{\prime}\colon X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
, and an
\mathscr{D}
-map

(wlog the map

).

Remark 3.5
.

We have the following implications for a functor

:

star-surjective
\Rightarrow
essentially star-surjective
\Rightarrow
absorbing
\Rightarrow

Remark 3.6
.

For every subcategory

we have the corresponding inclusion functor, and the properties listed in this section simplify, e.g. a subcategory
\mathscr{C}\subseteq\mathscr{D}
is absorbing if for every
\mathscr{C}
-object
X
,
\mathscr{D}
-object
Y
and a
\mathscr{D}
-map

there is a
\mathscr{C}
-object
Z
and a
\mathscr{D}
-map

such that

is a
\mathscr{C}
-map.

Similarly, when a sequence in
\mathscr{D}
is viewed as a functor

, the cofinality and absorption reduces to Definition
3.1
.

Note that definitions of AP, CAP, and WAP make sense for arbitrary categories, and we use them in the following general proposition.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
be categories and
F\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{D}
a functor.

(1)

If
\mathscr{C}
has AP and
F
is essentially wide and absorbing, then
\mathscr{D}
has AP.

(2)

If
\mathscr{C}
has CAP and
F
is cofinal and absorbing, then
\mathscr{D}
has CAP.

(3)

If
\mathscr{C}
has WAP and
F
is cofinal and weakly absorbing, then
\mathscr{D}
has WAP.

In particular, if
F
is wide and star-surjective, all the amalgamation properties are transferred forward.

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward diagram chasing.
If
A^{\prime}
is an amalgamation base in
\mathscr{C}
and
F
is absorbing, then
A=F(A^{\prime})
is an amalgamation base in
\mathscr{D}
.
Similarly, if
e^{\prime}\colon A^{\prime}_{0}\to A^{\prime}_{1}
is an amalgamable map, then
e=F(e^{\prime})
is an amalgamable map.
∎

A functor
F\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{D}
is
\sigma
-continuous
if for every sequence
(X_{n},f_{n})
in
\mathscr{C}
and its colimit
(X_{\infty},f^{\infty}_{n})
we have that
(F(X_{\infty}),F(f^{\infty}_{n}))
is a colimit of
(F(X_{n}),F(f_{n}))
in
\mathscr{D}
.
For categories of structures, where objects are (many-sorted) structures and morphisms are all embeddings, the colimit of a sequence is essentially the union of the structures, and so to check
\sigma
-continuity, it is enough to show that for every point
x\in F(X_{\infty})
there is
n\in\omega
and
x^{\prime}\in F(X_{n})
such that
f_{n}^{\infty}(x^{\prime})=x
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
be
\sigma
-complete categories of structures, and let
F\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{D}
be a
\sigma
-continuous functor that restricts to
F_{\operatorname{fin}}\colon\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathscr{D}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
If
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is Fraïssé with limit
U
and

(1)

F_{\operatorname{fin}}
is essentially wide and absorbing, or

(2)

F_{\operatorname{fin}}
is cofinal and absorbing, and
\mathscr{D}_{\operatorname{fin}}
has AP,

then
\mathscr{D}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is Fraïssé with limit
F(U)
.

Proof.

Since
U\in\sigma\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
, it is a
\mathscr{C}
-colimit of a
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-sequence
(U_{n},f_{n})
, which is necessarily Fraïssé by Theorem
3.3
.
By Proposition
3.7
(1) applied to
F_{\operatorname{fin}}
, the case (1) reduces to the case (2), which we now show.

By straightforward diagram chasing,
(F(U_{n}),F(f_{n}))
is cofinal in
\mathscr{D}_{\operatorname{fin}}
as
(U_{n},f_{n})
is cofinal in
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and
F_{\operatorname{fin}}
is cofinal.
Similarly,
(F(U_{n}),F(f_{n}))
is absorbing in
\mathscr{D}_{\operatorname{fin}}
as
(U_{n},f_{n})
is absorbing in
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and
F_{\operatorname{fin}}
is absorbing.
Altogether,
(F(U_{n}),F(f_{n}))
is a Fraïssé sequence in
\mathscr{D}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

Since
F
is
\sigma
-continuous,
F(U)
is the
\mathscr{D}
-colimit of
(F(U_{n}),F(f_{n}))
, and so it is the Fraïssé limit of
\mathscr{D}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
∎

3.2
The class of all finite ultrametric spaces

We will show that
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is a Fraïssé class and describe its Fraïssé limit
\mathbb{U}
.
To show the amalgamation property we will first assume that we work with a fixed ambient distance set
E\in\mathfrak{L}^{0}
and with isometric embeddings, i.e. in
\mathfrak{I}^{E}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
Then we will use a transfer principle to generalize to dc-embeddings.

The following are straightforward generalizations of amalgamation of one-point extensions of classical ultrametric spaces.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
E\in\mathfrak{L}^{0}
and let
X\in\mathfrak{I}^{E}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
Any two one-point extensions of
X
are strongly amalgamable, i.e. for all one-point extensions
X\cup\{a\},X\cup\{b\}\in\mathfrak{I}^{E}_{\operatorname{fin}}
with
a\neq b
there is
d(a,b)\in E\setminus\{0\}
such that
X\cup\{a,b\}\in\mathfrak{I}^{E}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

Moreover, if the extensions are non-isomorphic, i.e. if
d(a,x_{0})\neq d(b,x_{0})
for some
x_{0}\in X
, then such
d(a,b)
is unique.

Proof.

We put
Y=X\cup\{a,b\}
and
D_{Y}=D_{X\cup\{a\}}\cup D_{X\cup\{b\}}
, which is finite.
Since
d
is already specified on
X\cup\{a\}
and
X\cup\{b\}
, we just need to specify a suitable
d(a,b)
.

Suppose without loss of generality
s=d(a,x_{0})<d(b,x_{0})=r
for some
x_{0}\in X
. We are forced to define
d(a,b)=r
in order to
a,b,x_{0}
become an ultrametric triangle.
It still needs to be checked that all triangles
a,b,x
with
x\in X
are ultrametric.
This involves a case analysis in the tetrahedron
a,b,x_{0},x
depending on how
d(b,x)
compares to
r
, which is left to the reader.

Now suppose that
d(a,x)=d(b,x)
for every
x\in X
.
We put
d(a,b)=\min(D_{Y}\setminus\{0\})
.
Then for every
x\in X
we have
d(a,b)\leq d(a,x)=d(b,x)
, so
a,b,x
is an ultrametric triangle.
∎

Note the category of distance sets
\mathfrak{L}^{0}
is equivalent to the category of linear orders
\mathfrak{L}
, and so
\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is Fraïssé and its limit is
\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
.
The functor
D_{\operatorname{fin}}\colon\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is clearly wide and absorbing (since distances do not have to be attained), and
D\colon\mathfrak{U}\to\mathfrak{L}^{0}
is
\sigma
-continuous.
Hence, by the transfer principle Proposition
3.8
(1), if
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is Fraïssé with limit
\mathbb{U}
, then
D_{\mathbb{U}}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
.
Therefore we focus on
\mathfrak{I}^{E}
with
E=\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
.

In order to use the transfer principles to extend amalgamation to dc-embeddings, we need to prove the following.

Lemma 3.10
.

The inclusion functor
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}\subseteq\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is essentially wide and essentially star-surjective.

Proof.

Essentially we just observe that abstract distances can be identified with rational ones.
Specifically, let
X\in\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
,
Y\in\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and let
f\colon X\to Y
be a dc-embedding.
By the extension property of
\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
over
\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}
there is an
\mathfrak{L}^{0}
-isomorphism
\phi\colon D_{Y}\to E\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
such that
\phi\circ D_{f}
is the inclusion
D_{X}\subseteq E
.
We put
Y^{\prime}=\langle Y,d^{\prime}_{Y},E\rangle
where
d^{\prime}_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})=\phi(d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}))
.
Then
Y^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
,
and
g=\langle{\operatorname{id}_{Y}},\phi\rangle\colon Y\to Y^{\prime}
is a dc-isomorphism such that
g\circ f
is an isometric embedding.
This shows that the inclusion
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}\subseteq\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is essentially star-surjective.

We obtain essential surjectivity on objects as well since when we start with
Y\in\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and
X=\emptyset
with
D_{X}=\{0\}
, by the proof above, we obtain
Y^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
with
Y^{\prime}\cong Y
.
∎

Since every two-sorted ultrametric space
X
can be viewed both as a member of
\mathfrak{U}
and
\mathfrak{I}^{E}
(for any
E\in\mathfrak{L}^{0}
with
D_{X}\leq E
), we say that
X
is
dc-homogeneous
if it is homogeneous for
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
, and that
X
is
iso-homogeneous
if it is homogeneous for
\mathfrak{I}^{E}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

Recall that the class of classical finite rational ultrametric spaces with isometric embeddings, which in our setup is isomorphic to the category
(\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\rm prec})_{\operatorname{fin}}
(see Section
2.2
), is Fraïssé and that its Fraïssé limit is the
countable rational Urysohn ultrametric space

\mathbb{U}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Theorem 3.11
.

\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is a Fraïssé class, and its Fraïssé limit
\mathbb{U}
is dc-isomorphic to
\mathbb{U}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.
In particular,
\mathbb{U}
is both dc-homogeneous and iso-homogeneous.

Proof.

The class
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
has the amalgamation property by Lemma
3.9
since it is enough to check it for one-point extensions.
It follows that
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is a Fraïssé class. Indeed, clearly
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
has countably many isomorphism types.
Moreover, the empty space
\langle\emptyset,\{0\}\rangle
uniquely embeds into every ultrametric space, and so JEP follows from AP.

We apply Proposition
3.8
(2) to the inclusion functor of the full subcategory of precise spaces
(\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\rm prec})\subseteq\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}
to show that the Fraïssé limit of
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is
\mathbb{U}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.
By Lemma
2.2
, the subcategory
(\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\rm prec})_{\operatorname{fin}}\subseteq\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is cofinal, and since it is also full, it is absorbing.
Clearly, the inclusion functor is
\sigma
-continuous.

Finally we apply Proposition
3.8
(1) to the inclusion functor
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}\subseteq\mathfrak{U}
, which is also clearly
\sigma
-continuous and whose restriction
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}\subseteq\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is essentialy wide and absorbing by Lemma
3.10
.
Hence,
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is Fraïssé, and the Fraïssé limit is represented by
\mathbb{U}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

The alternative description of echeloned spaces
[
10
, Section 6]
exactly corresponds to precise two-sorted ultrametric spaces with the same notion of embeddings, but with the crucial difference that no form of triangle inequality is assumed.
Consequentially, the Fraïssé limits are different: as we showed the generic ultrametric space
\mathbb{U}
is represented by the countable rational Urysohn ultrametric space, while the generic echeloned space is represented by the countable
S
-valued Urysohn
metric

space for
S=\{0\}\cup((1,2)\cap\mathbb{Q})
.
Note that this is a
dull
metric space
[
10
, Definition 1.9]
in the sense that the distance set itself assures that the triangle inequality is trivially true.

3.3
Convexly ordered ultrametric spaces

A
convex order
on an ultrametric space
X
is a liner order
\preceq
on
X
such that all balls are convex, i.e. for every ball

3.4
Bounding the equilateral sets

We will also consider ultrametric spaces with bounds for the cardinalities of equilateral sets.
A subset
S\subseteq X
of an ultrametric space is
r

if
d(x,y)=r
for every
x\neq y\in S
.
For every
x\in X
and
r\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
, let
e_{X}(x,r)\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}\cup\{\infty\}
be the maximum cardinality of an
r
-equilateral subset of
X
containing
x
(where we do not distinguish between infinite cardinalities).
This is well-defined:
e_{X}(x,r)
is either
1
if the distance
r
is not attained at
x
, or is equal to the number of adjacent balls of radius
r
partitioning the precise ball
\bar{B}_{r}(x)
.

We say that an ultrametric space
X
is
\delta
-bounded
, where
\delta
is a map
E\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{N}^{\geq 2}\cup\{\infty\}
for some
D_{X}\leq E\in\mathfrak{L}^{0}
, if
e_{X}(x,r)\leq\delta_{X}(r)
for every
x\in X
and
r\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
.

Now fix a set
M\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\geq 2}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Let
\mathfrak{U}^{M}
denote the category whose objects are pairs
\langle X,\delta_{X}\rangle

X

\delta_{X}

\langle X,\delta_{X}\rangle\to\langle Y,\delta_{Y}\rangle
, i.e. dc-embeddings

such that
\delta_{Y}(D_{f}(r))=\delta_{X}(r)
for every

.

For a fixed distance set

and bounding map

Clearly, every space

.

Let
\mathfrak{L}^{M}
denote the category of

and

-map

such that
\delta_{F}(f(x))=\delta_{E}(x)
for every

.

defined by

.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{I}^{E,\delta}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
Every two one-point extensions
X\cup\{a\},X\cup\{b\}\in\mathfrak{I}^{E,\delta}_{\operatorname{fin}}
can be amalgamated.

Proof.

If the two extensions are isomorphic, i.e.
d(a,x)=d(b,x)
for every
x\in X

a
and
b
, so the amalgam is clearly
\delta
-bounded.
Otherwise, without loss of generality we have
s=d(a,x_{0})<d(b,x_{0})=r
for some
x_{0}\in X
.

By Lemma
3.9
, putting
d(a,b)=r
gives the unique amalgam

in
\mathfrak{I}^{E}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
We show that
X\cup\{a,b\}
is
\delta
-bounded.
Any equilateral set that is not already contained in
X\cup\{a\}
or

is an
r
-equilateral set of the form
\{a,b,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
.
For every
i\leq n

d(a,x_{0})=s<r=d(a,y_{i})

Hence,

∎

Lemma 3.19
.

The inclusion functor

Proof.

We proceed as in the proof of Lemma

.
Given
X\in\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{M}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and an
\mathfrak{U}^{M}_{\operatorname{fin}}

, we find an
\mathfrak{L}^{M}
-isomorphism

.

.

∎

Theorem 3.20
.

Let

.
Then
\mathfrak{U}^{M}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is a Fraïssé class, its Fraïssé limit
\mathbb{U}^{M}
is iso-homogeneous, and up to isomorphism we have
D^{M}(\mathbb{U}^{M})=\langle\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0},\delta_{\operatorname{gen}}\rangle
.
Moreover,
\mathbb{U}^{M}
is precise and for every

and
r\in\mathbb{Q}^{>0}
we have

.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem

, with the use of Lemma
3.18
, Lemma
3.19
, and Proposition
3.8
(1), we obtain that
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{M}}_{\operatorname{fin}}\subseteq\mathfrak{U}^{M}_{\operatorname{fin}}
are both Fraïssé classes with the same Fraïssé limit
\mathbb{U}^{M}
.
Since
\mathbb{U}^{M}
is Fraïssé limit of
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{M}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and since every isometry is bounds-preserving, we have that
\mathbb{U}^{M}
is iso-homogeneous and
D^{M}(\mathbb{U}^{M})=\langle\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0},\delta_{\operatorname{gen}}\rangle
.
For every
x\in\mathbb{U}^{M}
,
r\in\mathbb{Q}^{>0}
and finite
n\leq e_{\mathbb{U}^{M}}(x,r)
we consider an
r
-equilateral space

.
By the isometric extension property,

has an isomeric copy over

\mathbb{U}^{M}

∎

Note that if

, there are no restrictions on equilateral sets and hence
\mathbb{U}^{\{\infty\}}=\mathbb{U}
.
The other extreme is
\mathbb{U}^{\{2\}}
, where no equilateral triangles are allowed.

From now on we just work with
\mathbb{U}
rather than general
\mathbb{U}^{M}
.
We expect that most of results we prove for

(however, see Malicki

Note that we cannot combine adding convex order and bounds on equilateral sets as this does not lead to an amalgamation class: for one-point extensions

with

, the convex order

forces us to create an equilateral triangle in every amalgamation.

3.5
Properties of
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
and its ball structure

In Section
2.3
we introduced Cauchy completions. In that notation
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
is the Cauchy completion of
\mathbb{U}
; we can identify it with the Cauchy completion of the rational Urysohn ultrametric space. In this section, we prove universality of
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
and we identify
\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(\overline{\mathbb{U}})
as the automorphism group of the structure of balls of
\mathbb{U}
.

It follows directly from the fact that
\mathbb{U}
is the Fraïssé limit of
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
(Theorem
3.11
) and from general theory (Theorem
3.3
) that every countable ultrametric space can be dc-embedded into
\mathbb{U}
.
This can be improved.

Proposition 3.21
.

For every countable ultrametric space
X
there is a uniformly continuous dc-embedding
e\colon X\to\mathbb{U}
.

Proof.

If
X
is uniformly discrete, then every dc-embedding
X\to\mathbb{U}
is uniformly continuous.
Otherwise,
0
is not isolated in
D_{X}
, and there is an
\mathfrak{L}^{0}
-embedding
\phi\colon D_{X}\to\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
such that
\phi[D_{X}\setminus\{0\}]\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{>0}
is co-initial.
Hence, by Lemma
2.13
,
f=\langle{\operatorname{id}_{X}},\phi\rangle\colon X\to X^{\prime}=\langle X,\phi\circ d_{X},\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}\rangle
is a uniformly continuous dc-embedding.
We obtain
e
as
g\circ f
where
g
is any isometric embedding
X^{\prime}\to\mathbb{U}
.
∎

Corollary 3.22
.

For every precise separable ultrametric space
X
there is a uniformly continuous dc-embedding
e\colon X\to\overline{\mathbb{U}}
.

Proof.

Let
X_{0}\subseteq X
be a countable dense subspace.
By Proposition
3.21
, there is a uniformly continuous dc-embedding
e_{0}\colon X_{0}\to\mathbb{U}
.
By Observation
2.14
, it extends to the uniformly continuous dc-embedding
\overline{e_{0}}\colon\overline{X_{0}}\to\overline{\mathbb{U}}
.
Since
X
is precise, by Lemma
2.18
we have
D_{X_{0}}=D_{X}
, and we can identify
\overline{X}
with
\overline{X_{0}}
, and so there is a canonical isometric embedding
i\colon X\to\overline{X_{0}}
.
Finally we put
e=\overline{e_{0}}\circ i
.
∎

It turns out that the dc-automorphism group of
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
with the metric pointwise convergence topology is Polish and can be identified with the automorphism group of the countable structure of balls
\mathscr{B}_{\mathbb{U}}
.
Moreover,
\mathscr{B}_{\mathbb{U}}
is the Fraïssé limit of
\mathfrak{B}_{\operatorname{fin}}
, i.e. it is a generic countable leveled adjacency tree.
We refer to Section
2.2.2
for the definition of
\mathscr{B}_{X}
, the family of all adjacent balls of an ultrametric space
X\in\mathfrak{U}
, as well as for the definition of
\mathfrak{B}
, the category of leveled adjacency trees, viewed as ball structures of ultrametric spaces.

Proposition 3.23
.

The map
\bar{\beta}\colon\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(\overline{\mathbb{U}})\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathscr{B}_{\mathbb{U}})
is an isomorphism of Polish groups.

Proof.

Since
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
is precise and has no isolated points, the claim follows directly from Proposition
2.19
(4).
∎

Proposition 3.24
.

\mathfrak{B}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is Fraïssé with limit
\mathscr{B}_{\mathbb{U}}
.

Proof.

We apply the transfer principle Proposition
3.8
(1) to the ball functor
\mathscr{B}\colon\mathfrak{U}\to\mathfrak{B}
.
The functor
\mathscr{B}
is
\sigma
-continuous as for every increasing chain
(X_{n},f_{n})
with the union
X_{\infty}
and an adjacent ball
B_{X_{\infty}}(x,y)\in\mathscr{B}_{X_{\infty}}
, there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x,y\in X_{n}
, and so
B_{X_{\infty}}(x,y)=\mathscr{B}_{f^{\infty}_{n}}(B_{X_{n}}(x,y))
.
By Lemma
2.8
,
\mathscr{B}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is essentially wide.
To show that
\mathscr{B}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is absorbing let
X\in\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and

be an
\mathfrak{B}
-embedding.
We take any
\mathfrak{B}
-embedding

has at least one node.
By Lemma

3.6
Concrete representations and completions

In this section we revisit a concrete representation of the rational Urysohn ultrametric space
\mathbb{U}
and of its Cauchy completion
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
, and we describe a homogeneous spherical completion of
\mathbb{U}
and its connection to valued fields.
Moreover, we use this concrete representation of
\mathbb{U}
to obtain a continuous section to the canonical projection
\pi\colon\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
, which we use in Section
4.1
to represent
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
as a semidirect product
\operatorname{Iso}(\mathbb{U})\rtimes\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
.

Let
S\ni 0
be a set that will eventually represent splitting options in a tree.
In particular, it is natural to consider
S=\mathbb{N}
or
S=\mathbb{Q}
.
By a support of a map
f\colon X\to S
we mean the set
\operatorname{supp}(f)=\{x\in X:f(x)\neq 0\}
.

We consider the spaces

\displaystyle V_{S}

\displaystyle=\{f\colon\mathbb{Q}^{>0}\to S\mid\operatorname{supp}(f)\text{ is finite}\},

\displaystyle\overline{V_{S}}

\displaystyle=\{f\colon\mathbb{Q}^{>0}\to S\mid\operatorname{supp}(f)\text{ is finite or a decreasing sequence with limit }0\},

\displaystyle\widehat{V_{S}}

\displaystyle=\{f\colon\mathbb{Q}^{>0}\to S\mid\operatorname{supp}(f)\text{ is finite or a decreasing sequence}\},

\displaystyle\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}

\displaystyle=\{f\colon\mathbb{Q}^{>0}\to S\mid\operatorname{supp}(f)\text{ is a decreasing (countable) ordinal}\}

with the distance set
\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
and with the ultrametric

\textstyle d(f,g)=\max_{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}\{r\in\mathbb{Q}^{>0}:f(r)\neq g(r)\},

i.e. as
r
decreases,
f
and
g
agree up to
r=d(f,g)
, where the functions split.

If
S
is linearly ordered, we define a convex order by:

f\prec g\Longleftrightarrow f(r)<g(r)\quad\text{ for }r=d(f,g),

and we denote the corresponding expansions by
V_{S}^{\prec}
, etc.

We also define the trees of codes of balls:

\displaystyle T_{S}

\displaystyle=\{t\colon[r_{t},\infty)_{\mathbb{Q}}\to S\mid r_{t}\in\mathbb{Q}^{>0}\},

\displaystyle\bar{T}_{S}

\displaystyle=\{t\colon(r_{t},\infty)_{\mathbb{Q}}\to S\mid r_{t}\in\mathbb{Q}^{>0}\}.

It is easy to see that for every space
X\in\{V_{S},\overline{V_{S}},\widehat{V_{S}},\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}\}
,
f\in X
, and
r\in\mathbb{Q}^{>0}
we have

\displaystyle B_{r}(f)

\displaystyle=\{g\in X:g\supseteq t\}

\displaystyle\text{for $t=f\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r,\infty)}\in T_{S}$},

\displaystyle\bar{B}_{r}(f)

\displaystyle=\{g\in X:g\supseteq t\}=\textstyle\bigcup_{s\in S}B_{r}(f\cup\{r\mapsto s\})

\displaystyle\text{for $t=f\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{(r,\infty)}\in\bar{T}_{S}$},

so elements of
T_{S}
and
\bar{T}_{S}
indeed act as codes of balls in all the spaces considered.

Remark 3.25
.

Various versions of the above representation appear in literature, though mostly in isometric context.
One can be more general and replace
\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
with an arbitrary distance set
E\in\mathfrak{L}^{0}
and obtain spaces
V_{E,S}
,
\overline{V_{E,S}}
,
\widehat{V_{E,S}}
, and
\overline{\overline{V_{E,S}}}
.
The spaces
V_{E,\mathbb{Q}}
and
\overline{V_{E,\mathbb{Q}}}
for countable
0\in E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
appear in Nguyen Van Thé
[
25
, Sections 1.3.2 and 1.4.2]
, along with more references.
Spaces
V_{E,S}
with an abstract set of distances were already studied from a model-theoretic point of view by Delon
[
5
]
.
See also Delhomme–Laflamme–Pouzet–Sauer
[
4
]
for more on spaces
\overline{\overline{V_{E,S}}}
for
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
and their iso-homogeneity.

The universal spaces from the introduction also fit this representation.
The generalized Vestfrid’s space
U_{E}
of
E
-valued (non-strictly) decreasing sequences converging to zero, where
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
and
d((x_{n}),(y_{n}))=\max\{x_{k},y_{k}\}
for
k=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:x_{n}\neq y_{n}\}
, is isometric to
\overline{V_{E,\mathbb{N}}}
:
Every such sequence
(x_{n})\in E^{\mathbb{N}}
corresponds to the map
f\colon E^{>0}\to\mathbb{N}
defined by
f(r)=\lvert\{n\in\mathbb{N}:x_{n}=r\}\rvert
.
Vice versa, for
f\in\overline{V_{E,\mathbb{N}}}
we consider the sequence
(x_{n})
that is the decreasing enumeration of
\operatorname{supp}(f)
where every value
r\in\operatorname{supp}(f)
is repeated
f(r)
-many times (and where infinitely many zeros are added to the end if
\operatorname{supp}(f)
is finite).
It can be checked that the specified correspondence is indeed an isometry between
U_{E}
and
\overline{V_{E,\mathbb{N}}}
.

As for the Lemins’ spaces, we have
\overline{\overline{V_{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0},\kappa}}}\subseteq L_{\kappa}
.
The subtlety is that the distance formula for
L_{\kappa}
uses supremum instead of maximum:
d(f,g)=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}}\{r\in\mathbb{Q}^{>0}:f(r)\neq g(r)\}
.
So instead of requiring the functions
f\colon\mathbb{Q}^{>0}\to\kappa
to have co-well-ordered support, it is enough that they are eventually zero.
On the other hand, this means that the distance set extends to
\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
, and also that
\kappa
does not any more correspond to the splittings in the tree representation.
In fact, the splitting at
t\colon(r_{t},\infty)\to\kappa
corresponds to
\{e\colon(0,r_{t}]_{\mathbb{Q}}\to\kappa\}/{\sim}
, where
\sim
denotes the equivalence by eventual agreement.
In particular,
\overline{\overline{V_{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0},\mathbb{N}}}}\subseteq L_{\mathbb{N}}\hookrightarrow\overline{\overline{V_{\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0},\mathfrak{c}}}}
.

Proposition 3.26
.

For every space
X\in\{V_{S},\overline{V_{S}},\widehat{V_{S}},\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}\}
the canonical projection
\pi_{X}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
admits a homomorphism
s_{X}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})\to\operatorname{Aut}(X)
such that
\pi_{X}\circ s_{X}={\operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})}}
.
Moreover,
s_{X}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})\to\operatorname{Aut}(X)
is continuous for
X=V_{S}
, and
s_{X}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})\to\operatorname{Aut_{m}}(X)
is continuous for
X=\overline{V_{S}}
.

Proof.

We define
s_{X}(h)(f)=\langle f\circ h^{-1},h\rangle
.
Clearly, if
h\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
and
f\in X
, then
f\circ h^{-1}\in X
, and
s_{X}(h)
is a bijection.
Moreover for every
f,g\in X
we have,

\displaystyle d(s_{X}(h)(f),s_{X}(h)(g))

\displaystyle=\max\{x\colon f(h^{-1}(x))\neq g(h^{-1}(x))\}

\displaystyle=h(\max\{x\colon f(x)\neq g(x)\})=h(d(f,g)),

i.e.
s_{X}(h)
is a dc-embedding with
D_{s_{X}(h)}=h
, which concludes that
s_{X}(h)\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
.
Furthermore,
s_{X}
is a homomorphism, and we have
\pi_{X}\circ s_{X}={\operatorname{id}_{\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})}}
.

We now show that
s_{X}
, for
X=\overline{V_{S}}
, is continuous.
Fix
F=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}\subseteq X
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{>0}
, and consider the corresponding open neighbourhood of the identity

U=N_{F,q}({\operatorname{id}_{X}})=\{g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)\colon d(g(f_{i}),f_{i})<q,\ i=1,\ldots,n\}

in
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
. Let

P=\{a\in\mathbb{Q}\colon a>q\text{ and for some }i,\ f_{i}(a)\neq 0\}\cup\{q\},

and let
W=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})_{P}
be the pointwise stabilizer of
P
.
Clearly
P
is finite and hence
W
is an open neighbourhood of the identity in
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
. Moreover, as
q\in P
, for any
h\in W
we have
h([q,\infty))=[q,\infty)
. This implies that for any
i=1,\ldots,n
and
h\in W
,
(f_{i}\circ h^{-1})\restriction_{[q,\infty)}=f_{i}\restriction_{[q,\infty)}
, and so

s_{X}[W]\subseteq U
, which finishes the proof of continuity of
s_{X}
.

For
X=V_{S}
the proof of continuity of
s_{X}
is essentially the same with
U=N_{F}({\operatorname{id}_{X}})=\{g\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)\colon d(g(f_{i}),f_{i})=0,\ i=1,\ldots,n\}

and
q=0
in all other places.
∎

Lemma 3.27
.

Let
X
be an iso-homogeneous ultrametric space with
D_{X}=\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
.
If the canonical projection
\pi\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
is surjective, then
X
is dc-homogeneous.

Proof.

Let
p\colon A\to B
be a finite partial dc-automorphism of
X
. Take any
h\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
extending
D_{p}
and let
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
be such that
D_{f}=h^{-1}
.
Let
q\colon B\to f[B]
be the restriction of
f
.
Note that
q\circ p
is an isometry
A\to f[B]
, and so it can be extended to some
g\in\operatorname{Iso}(\mathbb{U})
.
Then
f^{-1}\circ g
is a dc-automorphism extending
p
.
∎

Besides the Cauchy completion we shall also consider spherical completions.
Recall that an ultrametric space
X
is said to be
spherically
complete

[
18
, Definition 5.1]
if every chain of balls in
X
has nonempty intersection.
An ultrametric space
Y
is said to be an
immediate extension

[
18
, page 34]
of an ultrametric space
X
if
X\subseteq Y
and if for each
y\in Y
and
x\in X
with
y\neq x
there exists
x^{\prime}\in X
such that
d(x^{\prime},y)<d(x,y)
.

It is known that an ultrametric space is spherically complete if and only if it has no proper immediate extension and that the relation of being an immediate extension is transitive (see
[
32
]
or
[
31
, 7.3, 7.9]
), hence a spherically complete immediate extension is the same thing as a maximal immediate extension and is called a
spherical completion
.
Unlike the Cauchy completion, a spherical completion is not unique in general.

We say that a ultrametric space
X
is
point iso-homogeneous
or
point dc-homogeneous
if for every
x,y\in X
there is
f\in\operatorname{Iso}(X)
or
\operatorname{Aut}(X)
, respectively, such that
f(x)=y
.
The following is known for real-valued ultrametric spaces, but the proof does not use any properties of the linearly ordered distance set.

Theorem 3.28

(
[
4
, Corollary 2]
)
.

An ultrametric space
X
is iso-homogeneous if and only if it is point iso-homogeneous.

We are ready to summarize properties of our concrete representations.

Proposition 3.29
.

Let
S\ni 0
be a any countable infinite set.

(1)

V_{S}
is iso-homogeneous and dc-homogeneous, and it is a concrete representation of
\mathbb{U}
.
Moreover,
V_{\mathbb{Q}}^{\prec}
is a concrete representation of the convexly ordered rational Urysohn ultrametric space
\mathbb{U}^{\prec}
.

(2)

\overline{V_{S}}
is the Cauchy completion of
V_{S}
, and so it is a concrete representation of
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
.
Moreover,
\overline{V_{S}}
is both iso-homogeneous and dc-homogeneous, but it is not spherically complete.

(3)

\widehat{V_{S}}
is a spherical completion of
V_{S}
, but it is not even point dc-homogeneous.

(4)

\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}
is a spherical completion of
V_{S}
that is both iso-homogeneous and dc-homogeneous.

Proof.

Concrete representations
in (1) and (2). To show that the countable space
V_{S}
is a concrete representation of
\mathbb{U}
it suffices to check that
V_{S}
is universal and homogeneous for
\mathfrak{I}^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}_{\operatorname{fin}}
. Both those properties will follow from the extension property. If
A\subseteq V_{S}
is finite and
i\colon A\to B=A\cup\{v\}
is an isometric embedding, let
r=\min\{d(v,a)\colon a\in A\}
, and let
A_{0}=\{a\in A\colon d(a,v)=r\}
. It is not hard to see that there is
v^{\prime}\in V_{S}
such that
d(v^{\prime},a)=r
for all
a\in A_{0}
. Indeed, take
v^{\prime}\in V_{S}
such that
v^{\prime}(t)=a(t)
for any
t>r
and
a\in A_{0}
,
v^{\prime}(t)=0
for
t<r
, and
v^{\prime}(r)
is distinct from all
a(r)
,
a\in A_{0}
– this is possible as
S
is infinite. By the ultrametric inequality, we also have
d(v^{\prime},a)=d(v,a)
for all
a\in A
.

For
V_{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}^{\prec}
and convexly ordered spaces
A^{\prec},B^{\prec}=A^{\prec}\cup\{v\}
, we additionally require for
v^{\prime}(r)
that
\{a\in A_{0}:a(r)<v^{\prime}(r)\}=\{a\in A_{0}:a\prec_{B}v\}
. Here we are using the fact that set of values
S=\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
is a dense linear order.

The fact that
\overline{V_{S}}
is a concrete representation of
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
follows from
\overline{V_{S}}
being the Cauchy completion of
V_{S}
, which is proved below.

Homogeneity
in (1), (2), (4):
We endow
S
with an abelian group operation
+
such that
0
becomes the neutral element.
Then every
X\in\{V_{S},\overline{V_{S}},\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}\}
becomes a subgroup of the product group
S^{\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}}
.
Indeed, if
x,a\in X
, then
x+a\in X
, as any subset of the union of the supports of two functions from
X
is still an allowed support for a function in
X
. Moreover, for a fixed
a\in X
, the map
x\mapsto x+a
is an isometry of
X
.
Therefore, as for any
x,y\in X
and
a=y-x
, we have
x+a=y
,
X
is point iso-homogeneous. By Theorem
3.28
,
X
is in fact iso-homogeneous.
By Proposition
3.26
, the canonical projection
\pi_{X}
is surjective, and so by Lemma
3.27
,
X
is dc-homogeneous.

Non-homogeneity
in (3):
Every point in
\widehat{V_{S}}\setminus\overline{V_{S}}
is isolated, while every point in
\overline{V_{S}}
is not isolated.
Since dc-automorphisms (unlike dc-embeddings) are continuous (Lemma
2.13
), this show that
\widehat{V_{S}}
is not point dc-homogeneous.

Cauchy completeness
in (2).
For
\{B_{r_{n}}(f_{n})\colon r_{n}\to 0\}
, a chain of open balls in
\overline{V_{S}}
, where
(r_{n})
is a strictly decreasing sequence, we have
f_{n+1}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)}=f_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)}
. Therefore

\bigcap_{n}B_{r_{n}}(f_{n})=\{f\}
, where
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)}=f_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)}
for all
n
, and so
f\in\overline{V_{S}}
, as the support of each
f_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)}
is finite. On the other hand, any
f\in\overline{V_{S}}
can be realized as the unique element in the intersection of a maximal chain of open balls in
V_{S}
. Therefore
\overline{V_{S}}
is the Cauchy completion of
V_{S}
.

Spherical completeness
in (4) and (3): We first show it for
\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}
. Every chain of open balls in
\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}
contains a cofinal subchain
\{B_{r_{n}}(f_{n})\colon r_{n}\to q\}
, for some non-negative real number
q
, a strictly decreasing sequence
(r_{n})
, and
f_{n}\in\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}
.
Then
f_{n+1}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)}=f_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)}
for every
n
.
Take
f
such that for every
n
,
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)}=f_{n}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)}
, and
f(t)=0
for
t\leq\inf\{r_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Then
f\in\bigcap_{n}B_{r_{n}}(f_{n})
. Since the support of each
f_{n}
is co-well-ordered, the same is true for
f
, and so
f\in\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}
. This shows the spherical completeness of
\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}
.

Spherical completeness of
\widehat{V_{S}}
is proved essentially as for
\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}
.
We have that either
\operatorname{supp}(f\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)})
is finite for every
n
, or
S=\operatorname{supp}(f\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n_{0}},\infty)})
is an infinite sequence for some
n_{0}
and
\operatorname{supp}(f\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{[r_{n},\infty)})=S
for every
n\geq n_{0}
.
In both cases,
f\in\widehat{V_{S}}
.

Non-spherical completeness
in (2):
If instead we take a chain of open balls in
\overline{V_{S}}
,
\{B_{r_{n}}(f_{n})\colon r_{n}\to q\}
, for some
q>0
, a strictly decreasing sequence
(r_{n})
, and
f_{n}\in V_{S}
with
f_{n}(r_{n})\neq 0
, then any
g\in\bigcap_{n}B_{r_{n}}(f_{n})
will be such that
g(r_{n})\neq 0
for all
n
. As
(r_{n})
does not converge to
0
,
g\notin\overline{V_{S}}
. This implies that
\overline{V_{S}}
is not spherically complete.

Immediate extensions
in (3) and (4):

\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}
and so
\widehat{V_{S}}
is an immediate extension of
V_{S}
: For any
f\in V_{S}
and
g\in\overline{\overline{V_{S}}}\setminus V_{S}
let
r=d(f,g)
.
Hence,
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{(r,\infty)}=g\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{(r,\infty)}
, but
f(r)\neq g(r)
.
Let
f^{\prime}(t)=f(t)
for
t>r
,
f^{\prime}(r)=g(r)
, and
f^{\prime}(t)=0
for
t<r
.
Then
f^{\prime}\in V_{S}
and
d(f^{\prime},g)<r
.
∎

3.6.1
Connection to valued fields

There is a natural connection between
\mathbb{U}
and valued fields, as can be demonstrated using the concrete representations above.
A discussion of ultrametric spaces in the context of valued fields and their spherical completions is present for example in
[
26
]
or
[
5
]
.

Recall that a
value group
is an abelian group
\langle\Gamma,+,0\rangle
with a linear order
\leq
that is preserved by addition.
Recall that a
valued field
is a field
K
together with a value group
\Gamma
and a
valuation

v\colon K\to\Gamma\cup\{\infty\}
(
+
and
\leq
are naturally extend to
\infty
) satisfying:

(1)

v(a)=\infty
if and only if
a=0
,

(2)

v(a\cdot b)=v(a)+v(b)
,

(3)

v(a+b)\geq\min\{v(a),v(b)\}
.

It is possible to re-interpret the value group
\Gamma
as a distance set
D_{\Gamma}\in\mathfrak{L}^{0}
by passing to the multiplicative notation and by reversing the order.
We let
D_{\Gamma}
be the set of all formal elements
e^{-x}
for
x\in\Gamma\cup\{\infty\}
(with
e^{-\infty}=0
and
e^{-0}=1
) endowed with linear order and multiplication defined by

e^{-x}\leq e^{-y}\Longleftrightarrow x\geq y\text{\qquad and\qquad}e^{-x}\cdot e^{-y}=e^{-(x+y)}.

The corresponding re-interpretation of the valuation
v
as a norm
\lvert a\rvert=e^{-v(a)}
gives

(1’)

\lvert a\rvert=0
if and only if
a=0
,

(2’)

\lvert a\cdot b\rvert=\lvert a\rvert\cdot\lvert b\rvert
,

(3’)

\lvert a+b\rvert\leq\max\{\lvert a\rvert,\lvert b\rvert\}
.

Then putting
d(a,b)=\lvert a-b\rvert
gives a
D_{\Gamma}
-valued translation invariant ultrametric.
The translation goes both ways, i.e. a translation invariant ultrametric induces a norm satisfying (1’) and (3’), and if it satisfies also (2’), it corresponds to a valuation.

In our case the value group
\Gamma
is
\langle\mathbb{Q},0,+,\leq\rangle
and the distance set
D_{\Gamma}
is
\{e^{-q}:q\in\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, which is (as a linear order) isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
.

Let
k
be a countable infinite field.
Recall that
k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]
is the Hahn field consisting of formal power series
a=\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}a_{q}t^{q}
in variable
t
with coefficients
a_{q}\in k
such that
\operatorname{supp}(a)=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:a_{q}\neq 0\}
is well-ordered.
The operations are extending addition and multiplication of polynomials:

\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}a_{q}t^{q}\Bigr)+\Bigl(\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}b_{q}t^{q}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}(a_{q}+b_{q})t^{q},

\displaystyle\Bigl(\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}a_{q}t^{q}\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(\sum_{q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}}b_{q^{\prime}}t^{q^{\prime}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}}a_{q}b_{q^{\prime}}t^{q+q^{\prime}}.

The multiplication is well-defined because of well-ordered supports.
Inverse elements for multiplication exist and
k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]
is indeed a field.
The valuation is defined by

\textstyle v(a)=\min_{\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}}(\operatorname{supp}(a)).

Proposition 3.30
.

The ultrametric structure of
k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]
can be identified with
\overline{\overline{V_{k}}}
, and therefore
\overline{\overline{V_{k}}}
can be endowed with a structure of a valued field.

Proof.

Using the multiplicative reformulation of
k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]
, we have

\textstyle\lvert a\rvert=\max_{D_{\mathbb{Q}}}(\operatorname{supp}(a))\qquad\text{and so}\qquad d(a,b)=\max_{D_{\mathbb{Q}}}\{e^{-q}:q\in\mathbb{Q},a_{q}\neq b_{q}\}.

We observe that this ultrametric matches the ultrametric of
\overline{\overline{V_{k}}}
, because the elements of
k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]
are functions
\mathbb{Q}\to k
with well-ordered support, which can be identified with functions
D_{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{0\}\to k
with decreasing well-ordered support, and
D_{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{0\}\cong\mathbb{Q}^{>0}
.
Altogether,
k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]
and
\overline{\overline{V_{k}}}
have mathching ultrametric structures, and hence the structure of a valued field can be transferred from
k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]
to
\overline{\overline{V_{k}}}
.
∎

In this view we have the following:

Corollary 3.31
.

(1)

V_{k}
corresponds to the valued ring
k[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]=\{a\in k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]:\operatorname{supp}(a)\text{ is finite}\}
.

(2)

\overline{V_{k}}
corresponds to
\overline{k[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]}:=\{a\in k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]:\operatorname{supp}(a)
is finite or a sequence converging to
\infty\}
, which is a subfield, and so
\overline{V_{k}}
becomes a valued subfield of
\overline{\overline{V_{k}}}
.

(3)

\widehat{V_{k}}
is spherically complete, but is not closed under addition and under multiplication.

(4)

The field of fractions
k(t^{\mathbb{Q}})
of
k[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]
corresponds to a countable space
W_{k}
such that
V_{k}\subseteq W_{k}\subseteq\overline{V_{k}}
, and hence
W_{k}
is isometric to
V_{k}
.

Proof.

Claim (1) is clear because we restrict functions on both sides to those with finite support.

Claim (2): Again the correspondence is clear.
Also clearly
\overline{k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]}
is an additive subgroup since the addition is defined coordinatewise.
It is closed under multiplication since

\operatorname{supp}(a\cdot b)\subseteq\bigcup_{q\in\operatorname{supp}(b)}(\operatorname{supp}(a)+q)
, and so
\operatorname{supp}(a\cdot b)
is finite or a sequence converging to
\infty
if the same is true for
a
and
b
.

If
a\in\overline{k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]}
is such that
\min(\operatorname{supp}(a))=0
and
a_{0}=1
, i.e.
a=1+a_{1}t^{q_{1}}+a_{2}t^{q_{2}}+\cdots
with
q_{1}<q_{2}<\cdots
, then the recursive procedure for computing
a^{-1}
in
k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]
shows that
\operatorname{supp}(a^{-1})
consists of finite sums of elements from
\operatorname{supp}(a)
, and therefore is again finite or a sequence converging to
\infty
.
Hence
a^{-1}\in\overline{k[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]}
.
Altogether,
\overline{V_{k}}\cong\overline{k[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]}
is a valued field.

Claim (3): The spherical completeness follows from Propositon
3.29
.
Now let
K\subseteq k[[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]]
be the subspace corresponding to
\widehat{V_{k}}
, i.e. it consists of functions whose support is finite or an increasing sequence.
Clearly if for example
a=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}t^{1-1/n}
, then
t\cdot a,(1+t)\in K
, but
a+t\cdot a=(1+t)\cdot a\notin K
as its support is of type
\omega+\omega
.

Claim (4): We have
V_{k}\subseteq W_{k}\subseteq\overline{V_{k}}
corresponding
k[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]\subseteq k(t^{\mathbb{Q}})\subseteq\overline{k[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]}
since
\overline{k[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]}
is a field by (2) and
k(t^{\mathbb{Q}})
is the smallest field extension of
k[t^{\mathbb{Q}}]
.
We have that
W_{k}
is isometric to
V_{k}
by Lemma
3.32
since
V_{k}
is a concrete representation of
\mathbb{U}
and
\overline{V_{k}}
is its Cauchy completion.
∎

Lemma 3.32
.

Let
M
be a countable ultrametric space such that
\mathbb{U}\subseteq M\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{U}}
, where
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
is the Cauchy completion of
\mathbb{U}
.
Then
M
is isometric to
\mathbb{U}
.

Proof.

Let
A\subseteq M
be finite, and let
A^{\prime}=A\cup\{x\}
be a one-point extension (using rational distances).
Let
r\in\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0}
be strictly smaller than every distance used in
A^{\prime}
.
For every
a\in A
we find a point
c(a)\in\mathbb{U}
with
d(a,c(a))\leq r
.
Then
c\colon A\to c[A]
is an isometry. By the extension property of
\mathbb{U}
applied to
c[A]\cup\{x\}
, there is
y\in\mathbb{U}
such that
d_{\mathbb{U}}(c(a),y)=d_{A^{\prime}}(a,x)
for every
a\in A
.
But we also have
d_{M}(a,y)=d_{M}(c(a),y)
since those distances are larger than
r
.
Hence, the countable space
M
has the extension property for one point extensions, and so is homogeneous.
∎

Corollary 3.33
.

The countable rational Urysohn ultrametric space
\mathbb{U}
is isometric to the reduct of the valued field
k(t^{\mathbb{Q}})
for any countable infinite field
k
.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.31
(4) and by Proposition
3.29
we have
k(t^{\mathbb{Q}})\cong W_{k}\cong V_{k}\cong\mathbb{U}
.
∎

Remark 3.34
.

, and if

[

Hence, we can view the ultrametric space

4
Automorphism groups

4.1
Comparing
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
with
\operatorname{Iso}(\mathbb{U})
and
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})

In this section we prove that
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
is an extension of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
by
\operatorname{Iso}(\mathbb{U})
, and similarly that
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U}^{\prec})
is an extension of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Q}^{\geq 0})
by
\operatorname{Iso}(\mathbb{U}^{\prec})
. Then we conclude that
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U}^{\prec})
is extremely amenable,
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
is amenable, and we identify the universal minimal flow of
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
G_{1},G_{2},H
be topological groups. Let

open surjective since it is a homomorphism and a quotient map.

4.2
Katětov functors and universality

While the Fraïssé limit
U
is universal for the class
\sigma\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
of countable structures, its automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(U)
can also be (topologically) universal for the collection of Polish groups
\{\operatorname{Aut}(X):X\in\sigma\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}\}
.
Furthermore, this universality is sometimes realized via an embedding of
X
into
U
.
Specifically, an embedding
\phi\colon\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(U)
can be an extension operator with respect to an embedding
e\colon X\to U
: we have
\phi(f)\circ e=e\circ f
for every
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
.
Such extension operators can be obtained using
Katětov functors
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a
\sigma
-complete category of structures and suppose that
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is generated by a family of morphisms
\mathscr{T}\subseteq\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
, i.e. every
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-map is a finite composition of
\mathscr{T}
-maps.
Typically,
\mathscr{T}
consists of all one-point extensions.

A
(small) Katětov functor
is an endofuctor
F\colon\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
together with a natural transformation
\eta\colon{\operatorname{id}_{\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}}}\to F
such that for every
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-object
X
,
\eta_{X}
absorbs
\mathscr{T}(X,{\to})
, i.e. for every
\mathscr{T}
-map
f\colon X\to Y
there is a
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-map
g\colon Y\to F(X)
such that
g\circ f=\eta_{X}
.

If
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
has a Katětov functor
F
, then
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
has the amalgamation property, and (if
\mathscr{C}
is also directed)
X\to^{\eta_{X}}F(X)\to^{\eta_{F(X)}}F^{2}(X)\to\cdots
is a Fraïssé sequence for every
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-object
X
.
Moreover, the colimit
U=F^{\omega}(X)\in\sigma\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is the Fraïssé limit, and for every
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-map
f\colon X\to Y
we have a functorial
\sigma\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-extension
F^{\omega}(f)\colon F^{\omega}(X)\to F^{\omega}(Y)
.
In particular, for every
\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-object
Y
we have a (topological) group embedding
\operatorname{Aut}(Y)\to\operatorname{Aut}(F^{\omega}(Y))\cong\operatorname{Aut}(U)
.
Moreover,
F
admits an essentially unique
\sigma
-continuous extension
\sigma\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\sigma\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
, and we can obtain
U
also as the colimit of
X\to^{\eta_{X}}F(X)\to^{\eta_{F(X)}}F^{2}(X)\to\cdots
for every
\sigma\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-object
X
.
Similarly, for every
\sigma\mathscr{C}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-object
Y
we have a (topological) group embedding
\operatorname{Aut}(Y)\to\operatorname{Aut}(U)
.
For more details see
[
20
]
.

Example 4.11
.

There is a Katětov functor

.
It is easy to see that for every linear order

the inclusion

absorbs every one-point extension of

For an embedding
f\colon X\to Y
we put
F_{\mathfrak{L}}(f)(x_{i})=f(x_{i})=:y_{g(i)}
so that
F_{\mathfrak{L}}(f)\circ\eta_{X}=\eta_{Y}\circ f
, and
F_{\mathfrak{L}}(f)(x^{\prime}_{i}):=y^{\prime}_{g(i)}=\max\{y\in Y:y<x
for every
x>x^{\prime}_{i}\}
for
i<k
and
F_{\mathfrak{L}}(f)(x^{\prime}_{k}):=\max(Y)
.
This definition turns
F_{\mathfrak{L}}
into a functor.

Similarly we obtain a Katětov functor
F_{\mathfrak{L}^{0}}\colon\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

Next we consider the category
\mathfrak{U}
of two-sorted ultrametric spaces and dc-embeddings.
For
\mathscr{T}
we take the class of all one-point extensions of finite spaces, i.e. of all dc-embeddings that either add a new distance while keeping the same set of points, or add a new point without introducing new distances.
Clearly,
\mathscr{T}
generates
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

Proposition 4.12
.

There is a Katětov functor
F\colon\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.

Proof.

We proceed in two steps.
First, we consider a functor
G\colon\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
accomodating all one-point extensions of the distance sets, i.e. for every finite space
\langle X,D_{X}\rangle
we put
G(\langle X,D_{X}\rangle))=\langle X,L(D_{X})\rangle
and
\eta^{G}_{X}:=\langle{\operatorname{id}_{X}},\eta^{L}_{D_{X}}\rangle\colon\langle X,D_{X}\rangle\to\langle X,L(D_{X})\rangle
where
L
is a Katětov functor
\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
For an
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-map
f\colon X\to Y
we put
G(f):=\langle f,L(D_{f})\rangle\colon\langle X,L(D_{X})\rangle\to\langle Y,L(D_{Y})\rangle
.
It is easy to see that
G
is a functor
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
and that
\eta^{G}\colon{\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}}}\to G
is a natural transformation.

Next, we define a functor
H\colon\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
accommodating all one-point extensions of the space while not changing the distance set.
Let
X
be a finite space.
For every
\langle x,r\rangle,\langle x^{\prime},r^{\prime}\rangle\in X\times D_{X}
we put

d(\langle x,r\rangle,\langle x^{\prime},r^{\prime}\rangle):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $r=r^{\prime}$ and $d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})\leq r$,}\\
\max\{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),r,r^{\prime}\}>0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Intutively, a pair
\langle x,r\rangle
represents a one-point extension with the closest point
x
at distance
r
.
We show that
d
is a
D_{X}
-valued pseudo-ultrametric on
X\times D_{X}
.
Clearly,
d
is symmetric and
d(\langle x,r\rangle,\langle x,r\rangle)=0
.
We show

q:=d(\langle x,r\rangle,\langle x^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\rangle)\leq\max\{d(\langle x,r\rangle,\langle x^{\prime},r^{\prime}\rangle),d(\langle x^{\prime},r^{\prime}\rangle,\langle x^{\prime\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\rangle)\}=:\max\{q_{1},q_{2}\}.

If
q_{1}=0=q_{2}
, we have
r=r^{\prime}=r^{\prime\prime}
and
d(x,x^{\prime\prime})\leq\max\{d(x,x^{\prime}),d(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\}\leq r
, and so
q=0
.
If
q_{1}\neq 0\neq q_{2}
, we have

q\leq\max\{d(x,x^{\prime\prime}),r,r^{\prime\prime}\}\leq\max\{d(x,x^{\prime}),d(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}),r,r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\}=\max\{q_{1},q_{2}\}.

If without loss of generality
q_{1}=0\neq q_{2}
, then
d(x,x^{\prime})\leq r=r^{\prime}
and so

q\leq\max\{d(x,x^{\prime\prime}),r,r^{\prime\prime}\}\leq\max\{d(x,x^{\prime}),d(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}),r,r^{\prime\prime}\}=\max\{d(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}),r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime}\}=q_{2}.

Let
X^{\prime}:=(X\times D_{X})/{\sim_{d}}
be the induced
D_{X}
-valued ultrametric space.
We put
H(X):=X^{\prime}
, and for any
\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-map
f\colon X\to Y
we put
H(f)(\langle x,r\rangle):=\langle f(x),D_{f}(r)\rangle
.
This is a valid definition of a functor
H\colon\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
.
We have

\displaystyle d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})\leq r=r^{\prime}\text{ if and only if }d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq D_{f}(r)=D_{f}(r^{\prime}),

\displaystyle D_{f}(\max\{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime}),r,r^{\prime}\})=\max\{d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime})),D_{f}(r),D_{f}(r^{\prime})\}.

Therefore,
H(f)
is well-defined on the pseudo-ultrametric spaces
X\times D_{X}\to Y\times D_{Y}
, and hence induces a dc-embedding
X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
with
D_{H(f)}=D_{f}
.
Moreover, we have an isometric embedding
\eta^{H}_{X}\colon X\to X^{\prime}
defined by
\eta^{H}_{X}(x)=\langle x,0\rangle
, and these embeddings together form a natural transformation
\eta^{H}\colon{\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}}}\to H
.

Finally, we put
F:=H\circ G
and
\eta^{F}:=\eta^{H}_{G}\circ\eta^{G}
(i.e. the horizontal composition of
\eta^{H}
and
\eta^{G}
).
It remains to show that
F\colon\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{U}_{\operatorname{fin}}
is a Katětov functor.
Consider a finite ultrametric space and its one-point extension (without loss of generality an inclusion)
i\colon X\to Y
.
We have either
Y=X
and
D_{Y}=D_{X}\cup\{q\}
, or
Y=X\cup\{y\}
and
D_{Y}=D_{X}
.
In the first case, there is an
\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}
-map
g\colon D_{Y}\to L(D_{X})
such that
g\circ D_{i}=\eta^{L}_{D_{X}}
since
L
is a Katětov functor
\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}\to\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{\operatorname{fin}}
, and so for
f=\langle{\operatorname{id}_{X}},g\rangle
we have
f\circ i=\eta^{G}_{X}
.
Hence,
\eta^{H}_{G(X)}\circ f\circ i=\eta^{F}_{X}
.

In the second case, let
r_{0}:=\min\{d_{Y}(x,y):x\in X\}\in D_{X}\setminus\{0\}
and let
x_{0}\in X
be any point such that
d_{Y}(x_{0},y)=r_{0}
.
Then
f\colon Y\to F(X)
defined by
f(x):=\eta^{F}_{X}(x)
for
x\in X
,
D_{f}(r):=\eta^{F}_{X}(r)
for
r\in D_{X}
, and
f(y):=\langle x_{0},L(r_{0})\rangle\in H(G(X))
clearly satisfies
f\circ i=\eta^{F}_{X}
.
It remains to show that
f
is a dc-embedding.
It is enough to check that
q_{1}:=d_{F(X)}(f(x),f(y))=D_{f}(d_{Y}(x,y))=:q_{2}
for every
x\in X
.
We have

q_{1}=d_{F(X)}(\langle x,0\rangle,\langle x_{0},L(r_{0})\rangle)=\max\{d_{G(X)}(x,x_{0}),L(r_{0})\}=L(\max\{d_{X}(x,x_{0}),r_{0}\}).

By the definition of
x_{0}
and
r_{0}
and by the ultrametric triangle inequality, we have
d_{Y}(x,y)=\max\{d_{X}(x,x_{0}),d_{X}(x_{0},y)\}
, and hence
q_{2}=D_{f}(\max\{d_{X}(x,x_{0}),r_{0})\})=q_{1}
.
∎

Corollary 4.13
.

There is a topological embedding
\operatorname{Aut}(X)\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{U})
for every countable ultrametric space
X
.

The analogous statement for the completion remains open.
